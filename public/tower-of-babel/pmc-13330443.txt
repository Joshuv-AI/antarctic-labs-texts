Strategies to support implementation of infectious disease decision support systems: a scoping review
Authors: Kaia M. Nielsen, Sara R. Packull-McCormick, Lauren E. Grant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDecision support systems (DSSs) are computerized tools that analyse data to guide actions and inform decision-making. Although DSSs are increasingly used in infectious disease contexts, their adoption remains inconsistent. Effective implementation is essential for integration of these tools into practice. Implementation strategies that promote sustainable uptake have not been comprehensively mapped for infectious disease DSSs. This scoping review aimed to identify and describe implementation strategies, associated outcomes, and use of theories, models, and frameworks (TMFs) in the implementation of infectious disease DSSs designed for early warning, detection, or prevention.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses. A limited search in MEDLINE was used to develop the search strategy based on relevant publications. The final search was applied across MEDLINE, CAB Direct, AGRICOLA, and Web of Science using a time limiter (2000–2025). Studies were screened against eligibility criteria by two independent reviewers. Data were charted on implementation strategies, outcomes, and TMFs. Strategies were mapped to the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy, and outcomes were mapped to Proctor’s implementation outcomes taxonomy. Data were synthesized by grouping DSSs according to their primary level of public health action: clinical/individual, population/program, or system/governance. Reported or codable strategies, outcomes, and TMFs were summarized within each level of action.ResultsOf the 18,708 records identified, 26 studies reported in 27 publications met the inclusion criteria. In total, 20 unique implementation strategies were identified through reviewer coding of narrative descriptions, 7 implementation outcomes were inferred from descriptive indicators, and 3 TMFs were reported in the literature. Clinical/individual-level DSSs included 13 strategies, 7 outcomes, and 2 TMFs; population/program-level DSSs included 13 strategies, 7 outcomes, and 1 TMF; and system/governance-level DSSs included 7 strategies, 3 outcomes, and no TMFs.ConclusionsImplementation of infectious disease DSSs most often involved activities that mapped to educational and stakeholder engagement strategies, with limited reported use of guiding theories or frameworks. Although outcome reporting was relatively common, outcome definitions and depth of outcome reporting varied widely. More deliberate use of TMFs and systematic outcome evaluation could strengthen the evidence base for DSS implementation. In policy and management contexts, better alignment between strategy selection, system design, and public health objectives may enhance sustainable DSS adoption and impact.RegistrationNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03515-z.

Background

Infectious disease outbreaks present urgent public health challenges that demand rapid, evidence-informed decision-making, often under high uncertainty [1]. Decision support systems (DSSs) are computerized tools that integrate, analyse, and interpret data to guide actions and inform decision-making [2, 3]. By combining data from surveillance, laboratory, and clinical sources with analytical models and expert knowledge, DSSs can support timely and coordinated public health actions [4]. These systems have been used across healthcare, environmental management, and public health contexts to improve outbreak detection, response efficiency, and resource allocation [5, 6]. Although DSSs have evolved since their early development in the 1980s, their integration into public health decision-making remains limited [7].

Despite their potential, the adoption and sustained use of infectious disease DSSs remain inconsistent [8]. Barriers such as limited adaptability, misalignment between developers and end users, integration challenges, and insufficient training are frequently reported [4, 6, 9–11]. To address these barriers, implementation science provides systematic approaches to facilitate the uptake, use, and sustainability of innovations in real-world contexts [12].

Implementation strategies, defined as the methods or techniques used to increase the adoption, implementation, and sustainability of evidence-based practices, can help bridge this gap [13]. Examples include stakeholder engagement, training, workflow adaptation, and auditing and providing feedback [14]. Theories, models, and frameworks (TMFs) further guide and evaluate the implementation process, with over 150 TMFs that vary in purpose, scope, and application [15, 16]. Understanding how implementation strategies and TMFs are applied within DSS contexts can strengthen adoption and long-term use.

While the implementation of digital health and information systems has been explored broadly, evidence specific to infectious disease DSSs is limited [17, 18]. Few studies have explicitly reported the implementation strategies used to integrate these systems into practice, and even fewer have evaluated associated outcomes [19, 20]. Strengthening this evidence base is essential for advancing preparedness and response capacity across public and animal health systems [21, 22].

To address this gap, the objectives of this scoping review were to (1) identify and map implementation strategies used to support the implementation of computerized DSSs for infectious disease early warning, detection, and prevention; (2) describe reported implementation outcomes and how they were assessed within studies; and (3) examine the use of TMFs. In doing so, this review highlights where evidence remains limited, particularly regarding the intentional study and evaluation of implementation itself, to inform future DSS research and practice. The findings from this review can also inform implementation planning and policy development for public health agencies aiming to strengthen preparedness, surveillance capacity, and data-driven decision-making. By characterizing how strategies, outcomes, and TMFs have been applied across different levels of public health decision-making, this review contributes a structured overview to inform implementation science efforts aimed at improving DSS uptake and sustainability.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [23, 24]. An a priori protocol was developed to guide the review and is provided in Additional file 1 but was not registered.

Search strategy

A limited exploratory search of MEDLINE was first conducted to inform the development of the full search strategy. The final strategy was applied to MEDLINE (Ovid), CAB Direct, AGRICOLA, and Web of Science. Searches were limited to English-language studies published from 2000 onwards to capture research reflective of modern digital DSSs and the evolution of implementation science [25]. The search combined terms related to implementation, infectious disease, and DSS. The full MEDLINE (Ovid) search strategy is provided in Additional file 2, and strategies for other databases were adapted accordingly.

Reference lists of the included studies were screened to identify additional sources. Grey literature sources were considered through targeted searches in Google and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global, but no additional eligible studies were identified. Final searches were completed on June 22, 2025.

Data collection

All citations were collated and deduplicated in Covidence, a review management software program [31]. Citations were first screened by title and abstract (Phase 1) and then by full text (Phase 2) according to the eligibility criteria. Reference lists of studies that passed Phase 2 were hand-searched for additional studies.

A pilot test was conducted before each screening phase to ensure consistent application of the eligibility criteria among reviewers, requiring a minimum of 80% agreement before proceeding. The screening was independently completed by two reviewers (KN and SP), and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Reasons for exclusion were documented at the Phase 2 level.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form was first developed by KN and included the following data items: author(s), publication year, country of origin, study aims or purpose, study methods, species, infectious disease, DSS type, implementation strategies, implementation outcomes, and TMFs. The extraction form was pilot tested and subsequently revised to include an additional variable capturing whether implementation was a stated objective of the study. Data extraction was completed by KN, and all extracted data were independently verified by SP for completeness, accuracy, and consistency. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus. The standardized extraction form is provided in Additional file 3.

If implementation strategies were described explicitly, the study authors’ categorization was retained. When strategies were described implicitly, they were coded by KN according to the closest corresponding category in the ERIC taxonomy [29]. Implementation outcomes were categorized according to Proctor’s implementation outcomes taxonomy [30], based on explicit definitions or descriptive indicators provided in the studies. TMFs were categorized following Nilsen’s taxonomy [15]. Plain-language definitions adapted from each taxonomy are provided in Additional file 4 (Tables S1–S3). When multiple studies reported on the same implementation project for a given DSS (i.e., involving the same setting, timeframe, and population), they were combined by KN and analysed as a single intervention to avoid duplicate extraction of implementation strategies.

The ERIC taxonomy [29], Proctor’s implementation outcomes taxonomy [30], and Nilsen’s taxonomy of TMFs [15] were selected to guide data extraction and synthesis because they represent widely used and complementary tools for characterizing implementation efforts. The ERIC taxonomy provides standardized terminology for implementation strategies, Proctor’s taxonomy defines core constructs used to assess implementation success, and Nilsen’s taxonomy offers a structured approach for identifying the theoretical foundations underpinning implementation studies. Together, these taxonomies support a comprehensive, theory-informed analysis of how DSS implementation has been planned, enacted, and evaluated.

Data synthesis and presentation

A descriptive synthesis approach was used to summarize and map the extracted data. The implementation strategies were categorized according to the ERIC taxonomy [29]. This taxonomy defines 73 discrete implementation strategies. Each reported activity was summarized on the basis of the study’s description and then assigned to one or more ERIC strategies to facilitate comparison across studies. Feedback-related activities were categorized under the ERIC strategy “Obtain and use patients/consumers and family feedback,” which involves using feedback from any intended end-user group. As this review includes both clinical and nonclinical contexts, this strategy was interpreted broadly to include feedback from any stakeholder group and is referred to as “Obtain and use feedback” in the results for simplicity.

The implementation outcomes were coded via Proctor’s implementation outcomes taxonomy [30]. The taxonomy includes eight core outcomes: acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, penetration, and sustainability. Each outcome described within a study was summarized and then assigned to its corresponding outcome within the taxonomy. The reported TMFs were recorded when explicitly named by the study authors and subsequently categorized according to Nilsen’s taxonomy [15]. This taxonomy distinguishes between process models, determinant frameworks, classic theories, implementation theories, and evaluation frameworks.

Following coding, the DSSs were grouped according to their primary level of public health action (clinical or individual, population or program, and system or governance) to capture differences in implementation focus and strategy application. This approach accounted for heterogeneity in context and intended users. This classification was informed by frameworks describing levels of decision-making within public health systems. Clinical or individual-level DSSs were defined as those supporting point-of-care or patient-specific decisions related to the detection, diagnosis, or prevention of infectious disease, such as screening, testing, or vaccination guidance [32]. Population or program-level DSSs were defined as systems that support surveillance, disease control, or program management within specific groups or jurisdictions [33]. System or governance-level DSSs were defined as tools that inform strategic planning, policy development, or resource allocation across organizations or regions [34]. When a DSS plausibly operated across more than one level, it was assigned based on its primary intended function or dominant use case. This grouping was conducted by KN and verified by SP, and any disagreements were discussed and resolved by consensus.

These categories also align conceptually with the levels of intervention described in the health impact pyramid. The pyramid illustrates how interventions range from those targeting individual behaviour and clinical care at the upper levels to those addressing contextual, policy, and social determinants of health at the base of the pyramid [35]. Notably, although treatment-focused DSSs can be classified as operating at the clinical or individual level, they were excluded to maintain focus on systems that support early warning, detection, and prevention of infectious diseases. This distinction aligns the review with public and animal health surveillance priorities, where DSSs are increasingly implemented to strengthen preparedness and response rather than just clinical management [4, 19, 21].

Within each level, the results were summarized narratively, highlighting the frequency and types of implementation strategies, outcomes, and TMFs identified. The findings were presented primarily in tables, with one visual element included to support study selection reporting. Implementation strategies, implementation outcomes, and TMFs within each level of public health action are summarized in tables, allowing for the comparison of implementation patterns across these levels. Synthesis by level of public health action served as the primary approach for exploring variation in implementation.

To capture the full scope of studies, no quantitative synthesis or formal study quality appraisal was performed. Reporting followed the PRISMA-ScR checklist, provided in Additional file 5 [23].

Results

Search results

The search strategy identified 18,708 records (treated as n = 18,707 studies as two publications were later extracted together as they reported on the same study). After 7,135 duplicates were removed, 11,572 records were screened by title and abstract. Following title and abstract screening, 124 records were included for full-text screening. Among these, 98 articles were excluded, most commonly for not discussing implementation strategies (n = 72). When full text was unavailable, corresponding authors were contacted to request copies; no additional full texts were obtained through author contact. In total, 26 studies reported in 27 publications met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. Characteristics of the included studies

Among the 26 included studies, 17 (65.4%) described a DSS for a specific infectious disease or pathogen, including COVID-19 (n = 5, 19.2%) and influenza (n = 5, 19.2%), followed by HIV (n = 2, 7.7%), malaria (n = 2, 7.7%), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (n = 1, 3.8%), monkeypox (n = 1, 3.8%), and hepatitis A (n = 1, 3.8%). The remaining nine studies (34.6%) described DSSs for either multiple or nonspecific infectious diseases, typically within broad surveillance systems encompassing zoonotic or vector-borne diseases. The 26 included studies represent 27 unique publications as two related publications describing the same DSS and implementation project were combined and treated as a single study for data extraction. Most studies were descriptive evaluations of system development, feasibility, or usability, rather than comparative or theory-driven implementation research.

Publication years ranged from 2000 to 2025. Only a small number of studies were published prior to 2019 (n = 9, 34.6%) with most published between 2020 and 2025 (n = 16, 61.5%), reflecting increased attention to DSS implementation following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. The highest number of publications occurred in 2023 (n = 4, 15.4%).

Geographically, all six World Health Organization regions were represented. Nearly half of the 26 studies were developed for use in the Americas (n = 12, 46.2%), followed by the Western Pacific (n = 6, 23.1%), European (n = 3, 11.5%), and African Regions (n = 3, 11.5%). Single studies originated from the Eastern Mediterranean (n = 1, 3.8%) and South-East Asia Region (n = 1, 3.8%).

Classification of included studies

Studies were grouped according to their primary level of public health action to illustrate variation in implementation contexts and intended system functions. Within each level, DSSs served distinct purposes reflecting the scope of decision-making they were designed to support. Over half of the included studies described DSSs operating at the clinical or individual level (n = 14, 53.8%), followed by population or program level systems (n = 9, 34.6%) and a small number at the system or governance level (n = 3, 11.5%) (Table 1). Very few studies described the implementation of a system or governance level DSS, indicating that this area remained relatively underrepresented in the literature. None of the included studies explicitly named implementation strategies; rather, all strategies were identified through reviewer coding of study descriptions and mapped to ERIC categories. Across all studies (n = 26), the most frequently coded strategies were conduct educational meetings (n = 15, 57.7%), obtain and use feedback (n = 13, 50.0%) and assess for readiness and identify barriers and facilitators (n = 6, 23.1%). Implementation outcomes

At least one implementation outcome were coded in 13 studies (92.9%) at the clinical/individual level, 8 studies (88.9%) at the population/program level, and 2 studies (66.7%) at the system/governance level. None of the included studies explicitly reported implementation outcomes; all outcomes were identified through reviewer coding of study indicators using Proctor’s taxonomy. Across all studies, the indicators most frequently coded as implementation outcomes corresponded to fidelity (n = 13, 50.0%), adoption (n = 11, 42.3%), and acceptability (n = 10, 38.5%) (Table 3). No indicators corresponding to implementation cost were described. At the clinical/individual level, indicators corresponding to adoption (n = 10, 71.4%) and fidelity (n = 9, 64.3%) were most frequently coded. At the population/program level, indicators corresponding to acceptability (n = 4, 44.4%) and feasibility (n = 4, 44.4%) were most frequently coded. Indicators coded as acceptability and feasibility were typically assessed through user surveys capturing perceived ease of use, fit with workflow, and usefulness of DSS recommendations. Indicators coded as penetration included the extent of DSS use across eligible users or settings and the proportion of patient visits in which the DSS was used. When identified, indicators corresponding to sustainability reflected ongoing DSS use or maintenance of vaccination delivery, rather than formal longitudinal evaluation.

At the population/program level, indicators that could be coded as implementation outcomes focused primarily on system performance and user experience. Adoption and penetration were both reflected in a single indicator, the proportion of clinics submitting daily case reports through the DSS. Fidelity and feasibility were represented by whether data entry, mapping, and reporting were completed as intended. Acceptability and appropriateness were described through qualitative feedback on perceived usefulness of the DSS and suitability for existing surveillance workflows.

At the system/governance level, indicators coded as implementation outcomes included whether decision-makers found the DSS suitable for use and whether the system functioned as intended during public health investigations. None of the studies explicitly referenced an established implementation outcome framework. Discussion

Overview of key findings

This scoping review identified 26 studies, representing 27 publications, describing the implementation of DSSs for infectious diseases. Across studies, 20 distinct implementation strategies were coded, most commonly those aligning with conduct educational meetings, obtain and use feedback, and assess for readiness and identify barriers and facilitators. Indicators that were coded as implementation outcomes were present in most studies, and all but one outcome, implementation cost, was represented at least once. The use of TMFs was limited, with only four studies reporting a TMF that were categorized according to Nilsen’s taxonomy.

Collectively, these findings highlight that while DSSs are increasingly being developed and applied in infectious disease contexts, systematic reporting of implementation processes remains limited. Current studies primarily describe educational and feedback-oriented activities, with little integration of theory-driven implementation planning and limited conceptualization or consistent measurement of implementation outcomes.

Implementation strategies

Implementation strategies that were identified were concentrated in a small subset of the 73 strategies defined by the ERIC taxonomy, with most describing activities that were coded as conduct educational meetings, obtain and use feedback, and assess for readiness and identify barriers and facilitators. These strategies reflect foundational capacity-building and stakeholder engagement approaches commonly used in the early stages of implementation [63].

Strategies that were least frequently coded, or not coded at all, such as build a coalition and change physical structure and equipment, can broadly be described as structural or policy-level approaches [29]. Their limited representation aligns with broader implementation research showing that outer-setting and policy-level determinants are often underexamined despite their importance for sustainability [64, 65]. Research has shown that strategies are often necessary to embed and sustain digital health interventions at scale [66]. These activities may occur in practice but are not explicitly studied or reported. This may reflect that system-level implementation efforts are often embedded within broader health or policy initiatives rather than being evaluated as stand-alone interventions [67, 68]. This may contribute to an incomplete understanding of the full range of implementation activities supporting DSS implementation.

This pattern mirrors findings from both broader implementation-science and digital-health literature where educational, feedback, and training-focused strategies are consistently the most frequently tested or reported [69]. Similarly, evidence from clinical DSS implementation research similarly indicates that individual, organizational, and system-level factors frequently influence adoption but are rarely addressed through coordinated implementation approaches [10]. Overall, short-term, user-focused activities aimed at building awareness, knowledge, and engagement were commonly described, whereas contextual or system-level processes required for long-term integration and sustainability were rarely described.

Implementation outcomes

Implementation outcomes were reported inconsistently across studies, with wide variation in both the type that were coded and level of detail provided. Few studies specified how outcomes were operationalized or over what time period outcomes were assessed, which limits the ability to compare results across systems or determine whether reported changes reflected initial adoption or sustained implementation.

The absence of standardized frameworks and definitions highlights an evidence gap in how implementation success is conceptualized, defined, measured and reported for infectious disease DSSs. In the included studies, outcomes were inferred from descriptive indicators rather than measured according to standardized constructs [30]. Notably, no studies measured implementation cost. This absence is important, as cost is a key determinant of digital health implementation [70]. Cost is frequently underreported in implementation research because reporting guidelines for economic outcomes are not standardized and implementation strategies are often insufficiently specific to enable cost assessment [69]. Cost is also inconsistently conceptualized and measured as an implementation outcome in the broader implementation science literature, even though it is included in established evaluation frameworks [15, 30].

The narrow focus on short-term, user-level outcomes observed in this review reflects a broader trend in digital health evaluation, where feasibility and early adoption are prioritized over systematic assessment of longer-term or system-level outcomes [70, 71]. Furthermore, the tendency to equate ongoing system use with sustainability overlooks key structural dimensions such as organizational support, policy alignment, and resource availability, which are essential for long-term success [72].

The continued absence of standardized outcome frameworks limits comparability across studies and makes it difficult to identify which implementation strategies most effectively drive sustained adoption or scale-up. Future research may benefit from linking outcome-related indicators to established frameworks, such as Proctor’s implementation outcome taxonomy, to strengthen conceptual clarity and comparability across studies [30].

Theories, models, and frameworks

Few studies explicitly reported applying or referencing a TMF to guide implementation or evaluation. The limited use of TMFs suggests that many evaluations prioritized practical application over theoretical guidance. Reviews of implementation-science TMFs similarly note that frameworks are often chosen and applied without clear direction, and that attention to context and sustainability is frequently incomplete [15, 73].

None of the studies reported using a TMF that was mappable to determinant frameworks or implementation theories, which are designed to explain how and why implementation succeeds or fails [15]. Evidence shows that determinant frameworks such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) can help identify contextual factors including barriers, facilitators, and organizational readiness that influence implementation success [64]. CFIR has been used across diverse health settings to systematically assess these determinants and explain variation in implementation outcomes [64, 74, 75]. Implementation theories such as the Normalization Process Theory provide complementary insight by identifying mechanisms that promote or inhibit the embedding and sustainment of innovations in practice [76–78].

The absence of such explanatory approaches limits the understanding of the processes driving the adoption and sustained use of infectious disease DSSs. The incorporation of determinant frameworks and implementation theories in future studies could enhance conceptual depth, link strategies to mechanisms of change, and promote theory-informed implementation research that moves beyond descriptive evaluation.

Nature of evidence

Most studies were descriptive evaluations of system development, feasibility, or usability, rather than comparative or theory-driven implementation research. This limits the strength and generalizability of findings related to implementation.

The majority focused on early stages of implementation such as developing, piloting, or assessing the usability of DSSs, often through qualitative or mixed-method designs. Quantitative evaluations were limited and typically relied on simple usage metrics or uptake rates, whereas qualitative approaches provided insight into user perspectives but offered limited exploration of contextual factors or mechanisms of change. Measures were rarely linked to implementation constructs, and few studies reported sustained follow-up or assessment of system integration.

Collectively, these characteristics suggest that current evidence provides useful preliminary insight into early implementation experiences but offers limited ability to draw conclusions about implementation effectiveness or sustainability across contexts. Future research would benefit from stronger study designs, clearer theoretical grounding, and more consistent outcome measurements to enhance the applicability of evidence for DSS implementation. In particular, only a small number (n = 3) explicitly identified implementation as a study objective. This highlights the need for research that purposefully investigates infectious disease DSS implementation processes, strategies, and outcomes, rather than treating them as secondary to system development or evaluation. Doing so would strengthen the evidence base needed to support the effective, scalable, and sustainable implementation of DSSs in real-world public health contexts.

Previous reviews have examined implementation of clinical DSSs [10], digital health interventions more broadly [69–71], and health information systems within public health and surveillance contexts [67, 68]. These reviews highlight important determinants of adoption and common challenges related to usability, workflow integration, and organizational readiness, but they do not systematically map implementation strategies, outcomes, or theoretical approaches specific to infectious disease DSSs. By contrast, this review provides the first comprehensive synthesis of implementation strategies, outcomes, and TMFs systematically coded across clinical, program, and system-level infectious disease DSSs. It places a particular emphasis on early warning, detection, and prevention contexts. This extends prior work by clarifying where implementation efforts are concentrated, where gaps persist, and how future research can build a more theory-informed and strategy-aligned evidence base for infectious disease DSS implementation.

Limitations

The included studies were not critically appraised, which limits the ability to draw conclusions about evidence quality and potential bias. Reporting detail varied across studies, and some implicit implementation strategies, outcomes, or theoretical influences may not have been captured during data extraction. To mitigate this, extractions and categorizations were verified by a second reviewer. Although the search was comprehensive, differences in terminology and indexing may have led to the omission of some relevant studies. Because terminology for both implementation concepts and computerized decision support systems is highly heterogeneous, the search strategy was intentionally broad. This may have reduced precision by retrieving some irrelevant records but was necessary to ensure that eligible studies using inconsistent or non-standard terminology were not missed. As per the review’s scope, treatment-focused DSSs were not included. As a result, implementation lessons specific to these systems may not be represented in the findings. Despite the review’s inclusion of all species, only one study addressed animal-health DSS, highlighting a substantial evidence gap and limiting the generalizability of these findings to animal-health contexts. DSS implementation needs and priorities are likely to vary across end users, disease contexts, and system settings, so findings should be interpreted with consideration of these contextual differences.

Evidence gaps and future directions

This review highlights several evidence gaps in the implementation of infectious disease DSSs. Most studies emphasized feasibility or short-term adoption but provided limited or no evaluation of long-term sustainability, cost, or system-level integration. Implementation strategies, as coded from study descriptions, were often narrowly focused on education and feedback, with few addressing structural or policy-level determinants. The inconsistent use of frameworks and the absence of standardized outcome constructs further limits the ability to compare findings across studies or to identify which strategies are most effective in specific contexts.

Future research should prioritize theory-informed implementation and evaluation that explicitly examine mechanisms of change, contextual influences, and sustainability. Comparative studies across settings and system types could clarify which strategies best support scale-up and integration of DSSs into routine decision-making. Incorporating specific TMFs would enhance conceptual consistency, whereas standardized reporting of implementation processes and outcomes would improve the synthesis and applicability of findings. Greater attention to governance-level factors, such as policy alignment and resource infrastructure, is also needed to ensure that DSSs are not only adopted but also sustained.

Conclusions

This scoping review contributes new insight into how DSSs for infectious diseases have been described, implemented, and evaluated. Mapping the strategies, outcomes, and frameworks reported or coded across studies revealed that few were designed with implementation as a primary objective. This is a key gap in the evidence base. Current implementation efforts are largely exploratory, with limited evaluation of factors that support the effective and long-term use of DSSs in practice.

For policy and management, the findings emphasize the need to incorporate implementation planning early in system design to ensure that DSSs are practical, acceptable, and scalable. For research, they point to the need for primary studies that explicitly examine implementation processes and outcomes to build stronger evidence for the effective integration of DSSs into infectious disease prevention and control.
