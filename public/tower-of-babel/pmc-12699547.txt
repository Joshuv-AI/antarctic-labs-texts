Comparison of Vyntus CPX and Vmax Encore 29N for indirect calorimetry: a randomised crossover study in participants of the German Diabetes Study with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Nina Trinks, Jennifer Pützer, Agnieszka Sutkowski, Volker Burkart, Michael Roden, Oliver Kuss
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study compared the reliability of two metabolic cart systems, Vyntus CPX and Vmax Encore 29N, to measure whole-body energy metabolism by indirect calorimetry (IC) in individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D).DesignRandomised, prospective, crossover study.SettingSingle-centre study conducted in the clinical research centre of the German Diabetes Study (GDS).ParticipantsFive participants (3 men, 2 women, mean age 49±6 years, body mass index (BMI) 32.62±4.2 kg/m²) with T2D completed the study protocol. Eligibility requires existing participation in GDS and availability for four consecutive study days.InterventionsParticipants underwent four IC measurements per day on four consecutive days, totalling 16 measurements per device. On each study day, two measurements with both devices, Vyntus CPX and Vmax Encore 29N, were performed in randomised order. Postcalorimetric gas calibration with normalisation was applied after each measurement.Primary outcome measuresReliability of respiratory quotient (RQ) and resting energy expenditure (REE), as assessed from the coefficient of variation (CV) and 95% CIs.ResultsDevice comparison showed minor differences in CV (95% CI) for carbon dioxide production (VCO₂) (3.5% vs 5.3%; 95% CI −8.2% to 8.0%), oxygen consumption (VO₂) (3.4% vs 5.7%; 95% CI −9.3% to 8.2%), RQ (3.6% vs 2.3%; 95% CI −3.5% to 3.7%) and REE (3.1% vs 5.6%; 95% CI −8.4% to 7.8%). Postcalorimetric calibration did not consistently affect RQ or REE. ConclusionsVyntus CPX provides reliable IC measurements comparable to Vmax Encore 29N and may serve as a suitable replacement in clinical settings.Trial registration numberClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01055093.

Introduction

A positive energy balance, that is, energy intake exceeding energy expenditure, leads to body weight gain and obesity,1 which contributes to insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes (T2D).2 Thus, weight loss, induced by a negative energy balance, is the primary treatment strategy to enhance insulin sensitivity in obese individuals with3 and without T2D.4 Energy needs are reflected by the measurement of resting energy expenditure (REE), as calculated from oxygen consumption (VO2) and carbon dioxide production (VCO2). In this context, oxygen (O2) is used to convert carbon-based nutrients into carbon dioxide (CO2) and water. During this reaction, energy-rich phosphate bonds (ie, ATP or ‘energy’) and heat are produced. Assuming that the total amount of inhaled O2 is used for this oxidation and the entire produced CO2 is expired, VO2 and VCO2 can be used for the indirect quantification of REE, which also supports nutritional management.5 6 In addition to REE, VO2 and VCO2 are used to evaluate the respiratory quotient (RQ), offering insights into the contribution of the oxidation of different macronutrients, carbohydrates, fats and proteins, to energy production.7 Furthermore, changes in the RQ during insulin stimulation allow us to describe the body’s ability to switch from lipid to glucose oxidation, which has been termed metabolic flexibility and can be impaired in insulin-resistant states.8 Indirect calorimetry (IC) with flow-through respirometry (ventilated hood/canopy mode) is the current gold standard non-invasive method to assess energy production in clinical and experimental settings.6 7 One of the most widely used devices among canopy-based metabolic carts is the Vmax Encore 29N.911 As this cart is no longer produced, there is a need for replacement with other metabolic monitors. This study aims to compare the reliability of the Vyntus CPX, the successor model of the Vmax Encore 29N (which has already been used in human studies1215), to that of the Vmax Encore 29N. This work was motivated by the German Diabetes Study (GDS), a prospective multicentre cohort study, which monitors tissue-specific metabolism in volunteers with or without recent-onset diabetes mellitus. In GDS, IC measurements are performed before and during the hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic clamp at baseline and every 5 years for up to 20 years.16

Methods

Study population

Five individuals with T2D from the GDS cohort were included in this study. The GDS comprises intensive phenotyping, including IC measurements at 5-year intervals for 20 years.16 To account for differences in clinical presentation and disease duration,3 a diverse cohort was selected for the current study. Diagnosis of diabetes mellitus is based on the criteria of the American Diabetes Association.3

Patient and public involvement

None.

Indirect calorimetry (IC)

IC measurements were performed according to in-house standard operating procedures based on best practice guidelines and under strictly controlled conditions6 17 on Vmax Encore 29N (SensorMedics; delivered by Cardinal Health, Höchberg, Germany) and Vyntus CPX (Vyaire Medical, Höchberg, Germany), with infrared CO2 and electrochemical O2 sensors and equipped with a variable mass flow. The carts were prepared and calibrated in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The postcalorimetric calibration procedure was assessed to control for cart-dependent inaccuracy and variability subsequently after each measurement.7 Data readout (VCO2 and VO2) was set at the highest available frequency (three readouts/min for Vmax Encore 29N and Vyntus CPX). Vmax Encore 29N provided VCO2 and VO2 in L/min, while Vyntus CPX provided both VCO2 and VO2 in mL/min. To ensure consistency, all data reported in this study are expressed in L/min.

Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA)

Measurements were done at 11–537 days before the IC study using BIA (Nutriguard MS, Data Input, Pöcking, Germany). In addition to fat mass (FM) and fat-free mass (FFM), we calculated the FM index (FMI) and FFM index (FFMI) as previously described.18

Experimental protocol

All participants followed the GDS study protocol.16 Briefly, they confirmed to follow a weight-maintaining diet throughout the study period, to refrain from alcohol and nicotine for 20 hours and 3 hours, respectively, to minimise physical activity and to fast for a minimum of 8 hours (with no food or drink intake other than water) prior to the metabolic test. All participants were instructed to maintain their weight-maintaining diet during the period of all four study days but were not required to record their individual dietary intake.

All participants completed four tests (two tests per cart) on four consecutive days, with the order of tests being randomised for each participant and day. For this task, a randomisation schedule was generated by using the RAND function in MS EXCEL, which generates uniformly distributed random numbers. Measurements were started at the same time of day and conducted in the same room. Participants had been asked to walk approximately the same distance each morning. Before the first IC measurement, participants’ anthropometry was measured at the beginning of each day as described before.16 Subsequently, participants remained supine throughout the whole study. In addition, venous blood sampling was conducted on the first study day. After a 10 min resting period, the canopy of the first cart was positioned over the participant’s head, and the first measurement was started. The total duration of each IC measurement comprised 30 min and consisted of an initial 10 min accommodation and equilibration period and a 20 min recording period. For analysis, only the 20 min recording period was used. During the test, participants were instructed to maintain strict resting conditions while remaining awake. After a 10 min break without the ventilated hood, the second measurement was initiated under identical conditions. This procedure was repeated until all four measurements were completed. The room temperature was consistent (~22°C), and noise was kept to a minimum throughout all tests. Finally, the participants received a standardised breakfast (figure 1).

Timeline of the experimental days. Four indirect calorimetry (IC) measurements were conducted per day over four consecutive days. The duration of one IC measurement was 30 min. Each day began with anthropometric measures (AM) and ended with a standardised breakfast. The total experiment duration was approximately 3 hours, with participants remaining under strictly resting conditions during all IC measurements.

Laboratory analyses

Venous blood samples were processed and stored under standardised, quality-assured conditions.16 Routine laboratory parameters were measured on a Cobas c311 analyser (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) was measured by high-pressure lipid chromatography on Variant II (Bio-Rad Laboratories) in the biomedical laboratory of the German Diabetes Centre as described previously.16

Calculations

The serial readouts of VCO2 and VO2 were used to calculate the RQ as VCO2/VO2. The RQ values for fat, protein and carbohydrates were set at 0.7, 0.8 and 1.0, respectively.5 19 In the absence of 24-hour urine collection for measuring nitrogen excretion, we used the Weir formula to calculate energy expenditure.20 VO2 and VCO2 were converted to kcal/day, and REE (kcal/day) was calculated as (3.941×VO2+1.11×VCO2)×1.44.20

Statistics

A formal sample size calculation was not performed, as no closed formulas are available for the estimation of variance components (VCs) in hierarchical mixed models. Thus, we adopted a pragmatic approach in five participants, who underwent four measurements per day across four consecutive days, resulting in 16 data points per participant and 80 measurements per device. This approach allows for the assessment of within-person reliability and between-device variability with sufficient precision, as reflected by the confidence intervals (table 1).

Continuous variables are summarised by means±SD. Linear mixed models with VCs were estimated separately for the four outcomes of VCO2, VO2, RQ and REE. As VCs, these models included, in addition to the residual error, random effects for the participant and for the day nested within the participant. The models further included a fixed effect for the device (Vmax Encore 29N vs Vyntus CPX) and estimated separate VCs for each device. Within-subject coefficient of variation (CV) was then calculated as the square root (residual error)/mean21 for the respective device. 95% CIs for all estimates were computed from the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles of a non-parametric bootstrap procedure with 1000 replications. Mixed models were computed in SAS, V.9.4, using the MIXED procedure.

For visual impression, line plots were drawn with a single line connecting the two measurements for the same device for a given participant on a given day. Horizontal lines in these plots would indicate identical readouts for both measurements taken with one device from the same participant on the same day (100% reliability for the respective device).

Reliability and variability of Vmax Encore 29N and Vyntus CPX

The IC results are summarised in table 1. In addition to the CV, intraclass correlation coefficients were calculated to assess measurement reliability across the four IC readouts (VCO₂, VO₂, RQ and REE). The CVs of all readouts were <6% precalorimetric and postcalorimetric calibration with both metabolic carts. Precalorimetric calibration, Vmax Encore 29N and Vyntus CPX featured no relevant differences in VCO2 (3.5% vs 5.3%, (95% CI for the difference −8.2 to 8.0)), VO2 (3.4% vs 5.7%, (95% CI for the difference −9.3 to 8.2)), RQ (3.6% vs 2.3%, (95% CI for the difference −3.5 to 3.7)) and REE (3.1% vs 5.6%, (95% CI for the difference −8.4 to 7.8)). The same holds true for all readouts postcalorimetric calibration. RQ values were generally stable across all participants and study days, with low intraindividual variability (as reflected by CVs) and no systematic time-dependent trends, supporting its robustness as a metabolic quality indicator. Postcalorimetric calibration failed to improve CVs of whole-body energy metabolism parameters when estimated with Vmax Encore 29N (RQ: 0.6% (95% CI for the difference −2.9 to 3.1); REE: 0.7% (95% CI for the difference −5.7 to 6.2)), as well as when estimated with Vyntus CPX (RQ: −0.1% (95% CI for the difference −2.4 to 2.3); REE: 0.2% (95% CI for the difference −6.1 to 6.5)).

In general, the results indicated high heterogeneity between the participants in all readouts for both devices. According to the CVs, all individuals, except for participant 3, showed similar intraindividual (high reliability) and inter-day (low variability) VCO2 and VO2 readouts with both metabolic carts. Participant 3 exhibited the largest intraindividual variation (lowest reliability) in VCO2 (0.243 L/min vs 0.199 L/min) and VO2 (0.268 L/min vs 0.211 L/min) during the Vyntus CPX assessments on experimental day 2 (figure 2A and B). This was accompanied by elevated ventilation during the first measurement (43 L/min) compared with the second (35 L/min), suggesting a higher initial breathing effort. Across all Vyntus CPX assessments, mean ventilation was 34.8±5.1 L/min, placing the first measurement of participant 3 clearly above the group average. With a difference of 0.09, this participant further exhibited the highest intraindividual variation (lowest reliability) in RQ within Vmax Encore 29N assessments on experimental day 4, while RQ was similar on the other days with Vmax Encore 29N and with Vyntus CPX on all days. Moreover, participant 3 featured the highest inter-day variation (highest variability) with Vyntus CPX (0.82 on experimental day 1 vs 1.00 on experimental day 3), which indicated a higher amount of carbohydrate oxidation on day 3. However, participant 2 showed the largest intraindividual variation (lowest reliability) on RQ with Vmax Encore 29N (0.85 vs 0.74, experimental day 2) compared with the other participants with both devices. Of note, an RQ of 0.85 indicates the oxidation of 50% fat and 50% carbohydrates, while an RQ of 0.74 reflects 87% fat and 13% carbohydrate oxidation (figure 2C). Again, except for participant 3, all participants had a high reliability and low variability in REE with both devices and on all four experimental days (figure 2D).

Overall, VCO2 and VO2 readouts, as well as the derived values for RQ and REE, did not improve with postcalorimetric calibration (online supplemental figure S1). To complement the comparison between devices, Bland-Altman plots were generated for VCO₂, VO₂, RQ and REE (online supplemental figure S2). These plots confirm the overall agreement between the two systems despite certain individual variability, particularly for RQ and REE in participants with values at the upper and lower ends of the observed measurement range.

Heart rates were recorded only during Vyntus CPX sessions and were overall stable across days and participants, except for consistently elevated heart rates in participant 3 on days 2 and 3, in line with previously observed variability in gas exchange parameters (table S3).

Discussion

This study demonstrated (1) the reliability of the Vyntus CPX to estimate RQ and REE in a cohort of middle-aged adults with T2D and (2) that all readouts of Vyntus CPX were comparable to readouts assessed with the Vmax Encore 29N. First, the study population was representative of the GDS cohort, as indicated by comparable anthropometric measures.16 Further, the cohort of this study reflected the current and planned study population of the GDS by encompassing individuals both shortly after their first visits and follow-up visits.16 The obtained CVs by both Vmax Encore 29N and Vyntus CPX indicate no metabolic or clinically relevant differences. Overall, intradevice comparisons demonstrated a low variability in REE estimates with both devices, as the CV of REE using different metabolic carts and methodology typically ranges between 4% and 8%.102224 Of note, CVs for RQ were within the range or even lower than those reported in previous studies that investigated the accuracy of Vmax Encore 29N and Vyntus CPX.7 10 13 14 Further, all RQ estimates remained within the range of 0.7–1.0 across the participants,25 demonstrating the physiologic validity of the measurements conducted in the current study. In line, both devices were comparable regarding their reliability in estimating RQ and REE. Although CVs, for both RQ and REE estimates, demonstrated no relevant differences between Vmax Encore 29N and Vyntus CPX, the means of RQ and REE differed by 0.5 and 106 kcal/day, respectively. Previous studies concluded from similar results that the comparison between studies using different metabolic carts should be taken with caution.7 10 13 To address this issue, Schadewaldt et al7 developed an in vitro postcalorimetric calibration for quantitative evaluation of each individual measurement. This comprises a precise simulation of the in vivo readouts (VCO2 and VO2) through a controlled infusion of pure gaseous CO2 and nitrogen into the canopy hose of the metabolic cart by using a high-precision mass-flow regulator.7 The current findings challenge the need for performing postcalorimetric calibration, as the CVs of VCO2 and VO2, as well as the derived measurements of RQ and REE, indicated clinically irrelevant differences compared with precalorimetric calibration readouts for both Vmax Encore 29N and Vyntus CPX.

One strength of the current study is the utilisation of a statistical model that explicitly separates the differences between participants as well as the daily fluctuations of outcomes from the reliability of devices, allowing the measurement of reliability as (within-participant) CVs. Conducting repeated measurements in the same individual on several consecutive days using two metabolic carts in a randomised order aimed at minimising systematic biases. By explicitly controlling for participant and day effects in the statistical models, the focus is on the variability attributed to the devices, while inter-individual and intraindividual variability in gas exchange may confound the measurement of reliability. Further, this type of analysis reflects the actual setting of a clinical study, where random fluctuations of measurements within the same participant on the same day are the norm and not the exception. Especially participant 3 illustrates this situation, as he showed both high intraindividual and inter-day variability. These findings underline the need for strict conditions and high compliance of the participants for quantified IC measurements.26

There are several limitations of this study. Blinding of the metabolic carts was not possible, as Vyntus CPX and Vmax Encore 29N use different hoods. In addition, participants were not admitted to the clinical research centre prior to the study, although we controlled for important variables known to affect resting energy metabolism in line with the GDS study protocol.16 Further, we opted for a short duration of each measurement because of the high number of measurements during a short time. While such short protocols are used in clinical trials, this will decrease the accuracy of our estimates of carbohydrate and fat utilisation,27 which should therefore not be used as reference values for persons with T2D. Of note, the primary aim was the comparison between carts and not the absolute accuracy, although the short protocol should not affect estimates of total REE. Heart rate data, available only from the Vyntus CPX device, were not included in the main statistical analysis due to their single-device origin. Body composition measurements were not obtained on the days of calorimetry, limiting the interpretability of derived parameters such as REE/FFM. This is particularly relevant as REE is not solely determined by FFM but also by its composition and metabolic activity.28 29 The use of indices such as FFMI and FMI has been proposed as more robust descriptors compared with percentage FM (%FM).18 Overall, the generalisability of our findings is limited because of the short study protocol and study population comprising only persons with T2D. Particularly, individuals with advanced age, undernutrition or cancer, in whom IC is widely used, were not represented in this study.30 Due to the small sample size, CIs, especially for CV differences, were quite large, sometimes also including relevant differences of 6–8 pp. Finally, this study did not intend to determine the superior validity of one device but compared the reliability and variability of Vmax Encore 29N and Vyntus CPX.

In conclusion, this study indicates low variability with Vmax Encore 29N and Vyntus CPX, despite observed heterogeneity across participants, attributable to a high degree of between-individual and intraindividual reliability. The Vyntus CPX further avoids previously used postcalorimetric calibration.
