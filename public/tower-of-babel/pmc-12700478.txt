Factors influencing patient satisfaction in postoperative pain management: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abhijit S. Nair, Gauhar Afshan, Viktor Kubricht, Sean Chetty, Balavenkatasubramanian Jagannathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:Effective postoperative pain management is pivotal not only for alleviating discomfort but also for enhancing overall patient satisfaction and recovery. This systematic review aims to investigate various factors influencing patient satisfaction due to the postoperative pain management offered.Methods:Using relevant keywords, we searched PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane database after registering the protocol in PROSPERO. A qualitative systematic review was planned using the Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS tool) to assess the risk of bias (RoB). Thereafter, a quantitative meta-analysis of the transformed proportions was performed using an inverse-variance random-effects model, and a Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied to all reported proportions.Results:From the 1234 articles available after the database search, 21 articles comprising 7289 patients met the inclusion criteria. Among the 21 studies, 12 (57.14%) had a moderate RoB, while nine (42.85%) had a low RoB. The random effects model, using the inverse variance method and the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation, revealed a summarised proportion of 0.8 (95% confidence interval: 0.74,0.85), with significant heterogeneity (P < 0.01 and I2 statistic of 97%). The various factors influencing postoperative patient satisfaction were summarised.Conclusions:Based on the results from this systematic review and meta-analysis, we conclude that the majority of the patients are satisfied with the postoperative pain management offered (a summarised proportion of 0.8 or 80%). Further studies need to be done to find out optimal strategies to improve patient satisfaction undergoing various surgeries. Having robust perioperative pain management policies and an audit of patient satisfaction for pain management could help in improving institutional practices.

INTRODUCTION

A crucial component of postoperative care is effective pain management, directly influencing patient satisfaction and the overall surgical outcome.[1] Despite advancements in anaesthesia techniques and the use of multi-modal analgesia, patients’ experiences with pain relief following various surgical procedures differ. Satisfaction with pain management is influenced by several factors, including the severity of pain, the efficacy of analgesic interventions, interactions with healthcare providers, and patients’ expectations. The overall hospital experience and patient recovery can be significantly improved by evaluating and improving pain management techniques. A patient-centred approach ultimately enhances overall patient satisfaction.[23]

Schwenkglenks et al.[4] analysed data from PAIN OUT, a European Union-funded acute pain registry, which included 16,838 patients from 11 hospitals across Europe and also comprised patients from Israel, Malaysia, and the United States. The study assessed patient satisfaction on the first postoperative day using a 0–10 scale with a median satisfaction score of 9 (interquartile range 7–10). The authors concluded that three key factors are responsible for patients’ satisfaction with postoperative pain management: (1) the patients’ actual pain experience, (2) perceived impressions of improvement, and (3) the perceived appropriateness of care. They also emphasised the importance of providing patients with adequate information and involving them in pain management decisions.

In 2000, Myles et al.[5] published the results of a survey involving 10,811 patients to assess patient satisfaction after anaesthesia and surgery. The findings showed a high overall satisfaction rate of 96.8%; however, 246 (2.3%) patients were somewhat dissatisfied, while 97 (0.9%) were dissatisfied with their anaesthetic care. On analysis, the authors concluded that the various factors responsible for patient satisfaction were intraoperative awareness, moderate or severe postoperative pain, severe nausea and vomiting, and any other postoperative complications. Studies suggest that patients receiving regional anaesthesia, such as spinal or epidural techniques, showed higher satisfaction due to better pain management and fewer systemic side effects compared to other methods.[67] However, managing postoperative pain remains challenging following major surgeries like abdominal, orthopaedic, and oncological surgeries.[891011]

This review aims to conduct a qualitative systematic review and a quantitative meta-analysis on patient satisfaction related to pain management and to investigate the factors responsible for this satisfaction.

METHODS

Search strategy and criteria

The protocol for this systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024576358). This study adheres to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines and follows the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions to ensure transparency.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, the Cochrane Reviews library (CENTRAL), and clinicaltrials.gov for studies published from January 2010 to January 2025. The search was restricted to English-language publications. Key search is presented in Supplementary Table 1. Inclusion criteria comprised studies that compared different types of pain management strategies and documented patient satisfaction expressed as a percentage. Exclusion criteria comprised categories like case reports, editorials, commentaries, reviews, and conference abstracts, articles not in the English language, and all other types of writings.

Study selection and data extraction:

Two independent reviewers (ASN and GA) screened the titles and abstracts manually, removing duplicates in the initial phase. The potentially eligible studies underwent full-text assessment by both reviewers to determine final inclusion. Any disagreement and inconsistency were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (SC). For studies in which data were not reported in the results or were not available in supplementary files, the corresponding authors were contacted via email to provide the missing information.

Two reviewers (ASN and GA) independently extracted pertinent data using a standardised form, including study characteristics (author details, publication year, country), participant demographics (sample size, age, sex), intervention details (different pain management strategies), and outcome measures (pain score, satisfaction rate, opioid consumption). Any disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third author (VK).

Methodological quality assessment

The included studies were evaluated for risk of bias (RoB) using the Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS).[13] This validated tool comprises 20 items assessing study quality with responses as YES, NO, or DON’T KNOW [Supplementary Table 2]. The studies’ quality was evaluated independently by two reviewers (ASN and GA). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consulting another author when consensus could not be reached (SC and VK). To measure the risk of bias, we implemented a scoring system.[14] A study was classified as having a very low risk of bias if it answered at least 19 of the 20 questions correctly as YES, a low risk of bias if it answered ‘YES’ 17 or 18 of the 20 questions, a moderate risk of bias if it answered 15 or 16 of the 20 questions, and a high risk of bias if it answered 14 or fewer.

Quantitative meta-analysis

Following a qualitative review, we performed a meta-analysis to address the quantitative findings. To account for disproportionate weightage of extreme proportions, particularly for studies with proportions near 0 or 1, and to stabilise variance estimates, a Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was applied to all reported proportions.[15] A logit transformation is used to convert probabilities into a continuous scale. It is particularly indicated for binary response data. Freeman–Tukey transformation produces narrower confidence interval (CI) and provides better coverage in meta-analyses. This offers superior meta-analysis performance. Freeman–Tukey transformation often outperforms the logit transformation in terms of bias, mean squared error, and confidence interval coverage. A meta-analysis of the transformed proportions was conducted using an inverse-variance random-effects model. For this, each study’s transformed proportion was weighted by the inverse of its variance. This inverse variance weighting ensures that studies with more precise estimates (smaller variance) contribute more to the pooled estimate.[16] Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Cochrane’s Q statistic and the I2 statistic, where I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were considered to represent low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.[1718] Finally, the pooled transformed proportion and its CI were back-transformed to the original proportion scale for ease of interpretation using the inverse Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation. A funnel plot was used to assess whether smaller studies disproportionately report certain outcomes, potentially distorting the summarised proportion, which is publication bias.[19]

RESULTS

The database search extracted 1234 articles. After removing duplicates and excluding titles that did not match the subject, 44 articles were selected for screening. After excluding another 11 titles, 33 articles were sought for retrieval, out of which 30 articles were considered eligible. After removing 9 titles (review article 7, unrelated primary outcome 2), 21 articles were included for qualitative systematic review and quantitative meta-analysis [Figure 1]. A total of 21 studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria.[202122232425262728293031323334353637383940] A summary of all the included articles is provided in Table 1. In all the studies that fulfilled the inclusion criteria, the type of surgeries performed was variable. In 17 studies, patients underwent various general surgeries, both open and laparoscopic.[2021222325262728293132333435363940] Obstetrics and gynaecological surgeries were performed in 14 studies.[2021222628303132333435363739] Orthopaedic patients were investigated in 14 studies.[2021222425262831323334353639] Five studies reported satisfaction for patients undergoing ENT surgeries.[2125283234] Four studies comprised patients undergoing neurosurgeries (including spine)[29303239] and urology,[20293239] and three studies with cardiothoracic surgeries.[293032] There was one study for ophthalmology,[32] vascular surgery,[32] maxillofacial,[21] breast,[30] bariatric,[38] and plastic surgery[32], each. In view of the high heterogeneity encountered after pooled analysis, we performed a subgroup analysis by analysing studies having general surgeries, orthopaedics, and obstetrics/gynaecological surgeries.[202122262831323334353639] Based on this criterion, we identified 12 studies. The pooled satisfaction proportion was 0.80 (95% CI: 0.69, 0.89) with a high heterogeneity (I² =98.18%), which is possible due to different settings or populations [Supplementary Figure 1].

Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot and Egger’s test. The scatter of points is asymmetric, with a relative paucity of lower-effect studies at smaller standard errors [Figure 3]. This hints at small-study effects/publication bias favouring higher proportions. The points spread wider at larger SE (smaller studies) and narrow as the standard error decreases.

Funnel plot depicting publication bias due to asymmetry

Several points lie outside the 95% limits, which can occur by chance but, combined with asymmetry, strengthen concern for small-study effects. The result of Egger’s test was also statistically significant with an intercept of 8.263 (95% CI: 1.166, 15.360; P = 0.0248), suggesting significant asymmetry and indicating potential publication bias or small-study effects. A trim-and-fill analysis was performed subsequently. This revealed orange imputed points appear on the high-proportion side, implying that symmetric “missing” counterparts would be on the lower-proportion side.

DISCUSSION

This review attempts to identify patient satisfaction across various studies and to perform a qualitative systematic review and synthesise the available data to conduct a quantitative meta-analysis. The type of anaesthesia provided was an important predictor of patient satisfaction. Regional anaesthesia was an important factor responsible for better satisfaction in 8 studies out of 21.[2021222428293134] Providing information about a pain management plan in the perioperative period was also an important predictor of better satisfaction (9 out of 21 studies).[212425273335363840] Another important factor was the interaction of the patients directly with the anaesthesiologist.[2835] Adverse events like nausea/vomiting[3234] and shivering following regional anaesthesia[2835] negatively influenced the overall patient satisfaction.

Although the patients were mostly satisfied with the pain management provided, the associated heterogeneity in the form of different surgeries and different methods used for assessing patient satisfaction is a hurdle in interpreting the results of this systematic review and meta-analysis. The real challenge lies in identifying the factors that lead to patient satisfaction, and thereafter, making policies and pathways to overcome those challenges and provide better patient satisfaction for all by incorporating evidence-based and frequently updated policies and pathways.

Several factors influence patient satisfaction regarding postoperative pain management. These factors include choice of analgesia and the pain relief methods, effective patient education and communication, consistent emotional and psychological support from admission onward, pre-existing and compliant pain management policies in the hospital, and improving hospital protocols for prioritising pain care. Additionally, well-trained nursing staff dedicated to postoperative care play a vital role in optimising patients’ outcomes.[2741]

Pain is a complex phenomenon that, when not treated adequately in the postoperative period, can significantly affect the psychological state of patients and lead to numerous complications. These adverse effects may compromise both physical and mental health, reduce patient satisfaction, and prolong hospital stay.[4243] Effective communication stands as a critical component in clinical care. In addition to recognising and promoting pain assessment techniques in clinical care, healthcare workers should help patients develop a proper understanding of postoperative pain and educate patients about the analgesic medications prescribed and also about pain management approaches that could be beneficial to them in early rehabilitation and discharge.[444546] Use of multi-modal analgesia can help in providing effective pain relief. Unfortunately, current evidence of well-designed studies investigating the efficacy of various non-pharmacological interventions remains limited. Consequently, the results of the pooled data in the systematic review and meta-analysis available should be interpreted with caution. There is a clear need for high-quality clinical trial studies to better establish its role in routine practice.[474849]

Patients’ experiences of pain seem to be significantly influenced by the attitudes and knowledge of both healthcare professionals and patients’ understanding and awareness.[50] Several factors influence patient satisfaction with postoperative pain management. Key factors include (1) decreased postoperative pain, (2) improved mood and sleep, and (3) fewer adverse events due to the analgesics prescribed. Other factors that could also lead to patient satisfaction are the patient–healthcare provider relationship, efforts taken by the healthcare provider to offer pain relief, and enquiring about pain scores and general well-being.[51]

Patient satisfaction is a complex and subjective concept that encompasses social, educational, cultural, expressive, psychological, and physical factors. In the context of postoperative pain, the dissatisfaction tends to be higher when patients are not informed about the plan of perioperative management and the available options. Benwu et al.[52] conducted a prospective study involving 120 patients undergoing various surgeries to assess patient satisfaction with anaesthesia services at a specialised hospital in Ethiopia. On analysis, the authors concluded that 88.33% of patients were satisfied with anaesthesia services. Factors that negatively affected satisfaction were events like postoperative nausea and vomiting, pain, shortness of breath, and feeling cold.

In a cross-sectional study by Kibru et al.,[53] involving 287 patients at a hospital in Addis Ababa, the authors found a relatively lower level of patient satisfaction, particularly regarding the information provided (69.7%) about medications, complications, and treatment options. These medications included not only antibiotics and anaesthetics but also pain management drugs. Sauaia et al.[54] conducted a prospective cohort study to evaluate satisfaction with pain management in elderly postoperative patients by enroling 322 patients from 8 urban hospitals. Analysis of the gathered data revealed that 62% of patients experienced severe postoperative pain, yet 87% reported being satisfied with the treatment, and these were the ones who were educated about the pain management plan. Svensson et al.[55] conducted a survey on 191 random surgical patients to investigate patient satisfaction due to postoperative pain management. The analysis revealed that patients generally expect moderate to severe pain in the postoperative period. Most patients were not aware of the pain management options that could be offered to them. Niemi-Murola et al.[56] used a 41-item questionnaire on 102 postoperative patients to examine the factors affecting patient satisfaction with postoperative pain management. On analysis, it was revealed that 80% of the patients were satisfied with the pain management they received. Their satisfaction was significantly correlated with the preoperative information provided to them (P < 0.01) and their preoperative well-being (P < 0.01).

Monitoring pain scores at regular intervals and instructing nurses in administering rescue analgesia can lead to better pain scores and enhanced patient satisfaction. Implementing patient satisfaction surveys and pain-management audits can help improve institutional and departmental practices.

This systematic review and meta-analysis have several limitations, which we acknowledge. Since cross-sectional studies only capture a single point in time, they are unable to determine causality or monitor changes over time. Furthermore, biases in the individual studies may affect meta-analyses of cross-sectional data. The high statistical heterogeneity in the studies analysed is also a concern, and therefore, the results of this review should be interpreted with caution. There was publication bias based on the funnel plot’s asymmetry and also the results of Egger’s test. Although we covered major databases, we included only articles published in English. By excluding pertinent research that has been published in other languages, the generalisability of our conclusions may be limited. At present, there are no universally validated, specific tools that can evaluate patient satisfaction due to pain management. The analysis and interpretation become more standardised and logical in the presence of a single tool that is used in all setups. Further studies need to emphasise patient education, both pre-operative and post-discharge, along with a defined timing of survey distribution for the responses. It is expected that the satisfaction due to pain management will be different based on the type of surgeries and anaesthesia administered, the time and the order of surgery performed in the list, age, gender, and also if it is a day-care or required in-patient postoperatively.

CONCLUSIONS

The results of this systematic review and meta-analysis conclude that in a variety of surgical settings, about 80% of patients express satisfaction with postoperative pain management. Important aspects impacting this include the type of anaesthesia (regional anaesthesia providing better satisfaction), the availability of clear patient education about pain management strategies, and efficient communication with the patients. Further studies are needed to investigate cultural, socio-economic, and other factors that could be identified and addressed to enhance patient satisfaction.
