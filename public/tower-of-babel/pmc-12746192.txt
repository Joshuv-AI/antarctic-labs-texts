A systematic review of transgender male rodent model methodology
Authors: Kai Robertson, Dylan Lane, Daniel Donner, Jason Peart, Eugene du Toit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRobust preclinical models of transgender male (TGM) gender‐affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) can inform clinicians of the isolated effects of GAHT; however existing models vary significantly in approach. We aimed to assess existing methodology and how it influences circulating sex‐hormone levels in rodent models of TGM GAHT to provide recommendations of best practise. PubMed, Embase, and Scopus databases were systematically searched for studies that investigated GAHT in rodent models and were published from inception to the 1st of August 2024. Study characteristics and methodology were extracted and compared. Post‐intervention circulating sex hormone concentrations were the primary outcome used to determine whether successful gender affirming hormone therapy had been achieved. Sixteen experimental rodent studies were included. Studies were performed on mice (n = 11) and rats (n = 5). Subcutaneous (SC) pellets and SC silastic implants were featured in some studies but weekly SC injections of testosterone enanthate was the preferred method. Sesame oil was the preferred solvent for injected testosterone formulations. Weekly doses of ~450 μg (mice) and ~420–900 μg (rats) consistently induced the testosterone levels of the male counterpart. Similarly, 10 mg of unesterified testosterone in a SC silastic implant in mice or 10 mg/100 g in rats were also successful methods. Most studies administered hormones for 6–8 weeks before performing post‐treatment assessments. This review demonstrates that methods largely varied across studies and successfully identifies the effective methodological approaches that improve the reproducibility and accuracy of preclinical models. Representing an integral step forward to bridging gaps in preclinical transgender healthcare research.Keywords: animal model, endocrinology, hormone therapy, methodology, transgender

INTRODUCTION

There are estimated to be 1.6 million transgender (TG) people in the United States as of 2022 1 and 7 to 35 million globally. 2 TG individuals are those whose gender identity does not align with their biological sex at birth. 3 This incongruence between their gender identities is referred to as gender dysphoria and can result in TG individuals seeking medical interventions such as hormone therapy in the pursuit of gender affirmation. Gender affirming hormone therapy (GAHT) has become the foundation of medical transition in transgender health care 4 and is reported to significantly alleviate gender dysphoria and improve mental health in these individuals. 5

TG males (TGMs) inject testosterone intramuscularly to induce masculine secondary sex characteristics. 6 For example, TGMs in Australia typically use intramuscular (IM) testosterone undecanoate injections every three months. 6 , 7 In contrast, in the United States of America (USA), TGMs use IM testosterone enanthate or testosterone cypionate injections. 8

Although testosterone is the first‐line treatment for medical transition in the growing TGM community, 9 there remains a significant gap in our understanding of the long‐term health effects. Researchers often rely on animal models to conduct preclinical studies to address knowledge gaps, leveraging their short life spans and lack of complex biological and psychosocial confounders that complicate the interpretation of the outcomes of human studies. Animal models can provide valuable insights into the long‐term effects of GAHT since their environment, diet, treatment type, dosage and treatment duration can be tightly controlled. Current literature, however, indicates that there are no consistent and well‐defined hormone treatment protocols and procedures for animal models of TGM GAHT.

This systematic review aims to evaluate the current literature reporting adult TGM GAHT modelling in rodents, specifically focusing on the experimental methods. To critically evaluate the efficacy of GAHT in these models, we assess the ability of these studies to achieve male testosterone levels in female rodents. Preclinical models of TGM GAHT are inaccurate if they are not using clinically relevant hormone formulations, administration routes, and dosages or do not achieve male testosterone levels. Inaccurate preclinical models complicate the design of clinical trials and therapeutic decision‐making for GAHT in humans. By synthesising evidence from preclinical model methodologies, this review identifies approaches that improve the accuracy and translation of preclinical transgender research. Through critical evaluation of the methods, results, and limitations of the studies included in the review, we set out to provide specific recommendations for future preclinical models of TGM GAHT to consider in their experimental design. We propose that by applying these recommendations, the consistency and reliability of preclinical transgender health research will improve and provide robust and reliable mechanistic data that can then be utilized to guide the design and implementation of human clinical trials and practice. Moreover, our critical evaluation can directly inform clinical trial design, by outlining the characteristics of a strong preclinical model to assist in their assessment of preclinical research.

METHODS

This systematic review was conducted using specified criteria in line with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The systematic search strategy was documented and is available as supplementary material.

Data sources and searches

KR formulated the search strategy and searched for studies published from the inception of each database to the 1st of August 2024 that investigated transgender hormone therapy in animals using the search terms: “transgender male” OR “transmale” OR “transmasculine” OR “transmen” AND “hormonal therapy” OR “gender affirming hormone therapy” OR “testosterone” OR “androgen” AND “animal model” OR “rodent model” OR “rodent” OR “mouse” OR “rat”. Our search strategy applied these terms to search MeSH terms, keywords, and title/abstract/keywords in PubMed, Scopus, and Embase databases. A detailed record of our search strings for each database is available as supplementary material.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they had performed research on rodents using sex‐steroid hormones such as testosterone or estrogen to model adult TGM GAHT. There were no restrictions placed on what species or strain, sex or age of animal was used in the study, nor the geographic location where the research had been conducted. KR and DL performed the screening of titles and abstracts of studies, and studies were subsequently excluded if they were performed on humans, did not use sex‐steroid hormones, or were duplicates. Full‐text manuscripts were retrieved and assessed for eligibility by KR and DL while EDT was responsible for managing discrepancies in screened articles. To be eligible for inclusion, studies must have used exogenous hormones or drugs to model adult TGM GAHT and reported testosterone levels.

The decision to only include studies that report post‐treatment testosterone levels was made to assess whether the methodologies used were effective and accurately mimicked an important aim of adult human TGM GAHT. Whether this aim was achieved in the animal models can only be determined if the study included post‐treatment testosterone levels for their animals. We assert that preclinical models of TGM GAHT must assess post‐treatment testosterone levels to confirm that the administered hormones increased testosterone levels and that these testosterone levels reflected normal male testosterone levels. We do, however, acknowledge the potential selection bias introduced by excluding studies that failed to report post‐treatment testosterone levels. There may have been studies that were excluded that may have added value to our understanding of the physiological effects of TGM GAHT.

Data extraction and quality assessment

KR performed data extraction for 16 eligible studies using a predefined template including study design, publication year, corresponding author, location, study aim, animal species, animal strain, animal sex, animal age, experimental groups coded into comparators, total number of animals in the study, sex‐steroid hormone investigated, ester of hormone, vehicle/solvent for hormone, route of administration, dose of hormone applied, frequency of hormone delivery, standardized dose per week, duration of hormone treatment, and post‐treatment hormone levels of research animal. Studies were subjected to SYRCLE's risk of bias tool for animal studies and were ranked either unclear, low, or high risk of bias.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure for this systematic review was post‐intervention circulating hormone concentrations and doses used to achieve these concentrations. Other measures include the drug type, drug esterification, vehicle suspension, administration route, and duration of treatment.

RESULTS

Literature search

Searches returned 252 studies of which 133 duplicates were subsequently removed. One hundred and nineteen studies remained of which 71 were removed after title and abstract screening. Forty‐eight full‐text papers were retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Sixteen experimental animal studies were published comprising 34 unique rodent models of human TGM GAHT (Figure 1).

Publication bias and internal validity

We conducted a detailed assessment for each study based on the SYRCLE risk of bias criteria. For example, Kinnear et al. 15 was assessed as ‘Low’ risk, reflecting their robust methodological design. However, there may be a disconnect between animal researchers and the criteria for a risk of bias assessment; for example, where authors have not reported whether they used allocation concealment or not. Under these circumstances an ‘Unclear’ grade for ‘allocation concealment’ was awarded. The methodology provided in many included studies lacked this detail resulting in an ‘Unclear’ risk grading. Risk of bias assessments informed the synthesis of the study results when considering the weighting of evidence. Insufficient detail preventing clear risk of bias assessments is a limitation of this study. We recommend that animal study researchers should be aware of the importance of providing a detailed methodological design when publishing their research.

Animal characteristics

Mice were the predominant species used (mice n = 11; rats n = 5). Of the 5 studies that used rats, most used Sprague Dawley rats (n = 3) followed by Wistar (n = 2). C57BL/6J mice were used in most studies (n = 8) followed by CF‐1 (n = 2) and Hsd:NSA (n = 1). Two studies used genetically modified mice: ApoE−/−; and Rag1−/−, IL‐17Ra−/−, and TCRδ−/−.

Apart from 3 studies where treatment was started at 6 weeks, all other studies commenced treatment when animals were 8–12 weeks old irrespective of whether mice or rats were used (animal characteristics data presented in Table 1).

Experimental aims of animal models

Fertility was a primary focus for many studies (n = 6), followed by bone structure (n = 1), brain structure (n = 1), renal function (n = 1), pharmacokinetics (n = 1), liver enzyme profile (n = 1), atherosclerosis risk (n = 1), vascular dysfunction (n = 1), toxicokinetics (n = 1), pain physiology (n = 1), and ovarian function after puberty blockade with subsequent testosterone therapy (n = 1) were assessed.

Drug formulations and protocols

Testosterone was esterified with enanthate (n = 8), cypionate (n = 4), benzoate (n = 2), or propionate (n = 1). Unesterified crystalline testosterone was used for SC silastic implants in three studies. SC injection (n = 10) was the preferred route of testosterone administration although SC silastic implants (n = 3), SC pellets (n = 3), and IM injection (n = 1) were also used. Esterified testosterone was suspended in either sesame oil (n = 9), sunflower oil (n = 1), peanut oil (n = 1), or olive oil (n = 1). Crystalline testosterone was dissolved in ethanol and placed in the silastic implant and left to evaporate (n = 2). Schwartz et al.'s 19 study was the only to dissolve testosterone enanthate in ethanol for silastic implants (n = 1). Administration frequency varied with formulation and administration route (drug formulation and protocol data presented in Table 2).

SC and IM injections

Testosterone enanthate and cypionate were injected SC once (n = 4) or twice (n = 3) per week whilst SC testosterone benzoate (n = 1) and propionate (n = 1) injections were administered once per week. One study used IM testosterone cypionate injections once every 10 days.

SC pellet implants

Hashim et al. 14 used SC pellets of testosterone enanthate with a release time of 60 and 90 days. While Kinnear et al. 16 also used SC pellets of testosterone enanthate, only a release time of 60 days was used. Zhang et al. 23 also used a SC pellet with a release time of 60 days but was the only study to use deuterized testosterone.

SC silastic implants

Crystalline testosterone (n = 3) or testosterone enanthate (n = 1) implants remained in situ between 5 and 13.5 weeks before removal.

Doses of hormones and drugs

In mouse models, testosterone was administered at vastly different doses ranging from as low as 31 μg per week to as high as 1800 μg per week. Duration of treatment in mice varied from 6 to 21 weeks. Similarly, rats were subjected to a large range of doses from 197 μg to 8000 μg and treatment duration varied from 2 to 17 weeks (dosage data presented in Table 2).

SC silastic implant models delivered 10 mg of crystalline testosterone or testosterone enanthate except for Craft et al. 21 who gave 10 mg/100 g bodyweight in rats. Hashim et al. 14 also gave testosterone enanthate via SC silastic implant but only gave 5 mg dissolved in sesame oil (Table 2). A standardized weekly delivery dose was not documented for the SC silastic implant models as it is not possible to determine a weekly dose without knowing the weight of the drug‐filled silastic implant before implantation and after removal. 26

Confirmation of successful transition and post‐intervention hormone levels

Circulating testosterone levels confirmed the success of the model for most studies. Kinnear et al., 15 , 16 , 17 Bartels et al., 20 and Dela Cruz et al., 10 provided the most robust evidence confirming successful model establishment by combining the presence of persistent diestrus with vaginal and ovarian morphological changes consistent with TGM. Schwartz et al. 19 confirmed their model with persistent diestrous and reduced measures of fertility. Hashim et al., 14 Lichtenecker et al., 24 Craft et al. 21 and Godiwala et al. 11 also confirmed their models with persistent diestrous to confirm their TGM models. Tassinari et al. 22 presented changes to uterine and ovarian morphology without estrous cycle data. Santos et al. 18 uniquely confirmed their TGM model with increased skeletal muscle mass in testosterone‐treated female mice. Goetz et al. 12 , 13 , Perez‐Laso et al. 25 and Zhang et al. 23 reported hormone levels only.

For this review, we converted the dose of drugs given across all studies to the same units and standardized the treatments to a weekly dose (Table 2) and final hormone concentrations were converted to ng/mL (Table 3). This process of unit conversion facilitated the comparison of these studies, and the evaluation of the methods used to achieve outcome hormone concentrations of the opposite sex.

Similarities between preclinical and clinical findings

There were distinct parallels between preclinical models included here and clinical outcomes observed in human TGMs. Bartels et al., 20 Godiwala et al., 11 Kinnear et al., 15 , 16 , 17 and Schwartz et al. 19 demonstrated that testosterone exposure impaired ovarian function in their preclinical models, which is consistent with ovarian function changes observed clinically with TGMs. 27 , 28 Moreover, these studies revealed that fertility can be restored upon cessation of testosterone treatment, a finding echoed by clinical studies that report restored fertility in TGMs after discontinuing GAHT. 29

Similar parallels of preclinical and clinical findings are evident in Goetz et al., 12 where the authors reported no change to bone mineral density, 30 Perez‐Laso et al.'s 25 finding of altered gray matter volumes in the brain, 31 and Lichtenecker et al.'s 24 discovery of increased creatinine concentrations and reduced creatinine clearance. 32 In addition, increased atherogenesis and vascular dysfunction were reported by Goetz et al. 13 and Santos et al., 18 which aligns with clinical data showing vascular dysfunction 33 and higher relative risk of myocardial infarction in TGMs compared to cisgender females. 34

In contrast, TGMs commonly display an increased lean mass with reductions in body fat 35 ; however, Goetz et al. 12 reported no differences in bodyweight, nor lean or fat mass with testosterone treatment. Tassinari et al. 22 reported dose‐dependent increases in body weight and weight gain, while Santos et al. 18 found that lean body mass and body mass index both increased and fat mass decreased. We discuss the doses used by different studies later in the review, which may provide insight as to why Goetz et al. 12 found little to no effect with testosterone treatment.

These findings emphasise the utility of preclinical models in predicting and simulating clinical outcomes, investigating hypothesized long‐term health outcomes of TGM GAHT, but also addressing less immediate concerns. For example, the Craft et al. 21 study found no change in pain responses with testosterone therapy and the Zhang et al. 23 study revealed greater clearance of gamma‐hydroxybutyrate acid otherwise known as GHB.

Clinical implications

The preclinical findings of the included studies support the need for tailored clinical surveillance in human TGMs. Namely, rodent evidence of an increased risk of atherosclerosis, 13 , 18 and vascular dysfunction 33 underscore the need for regular cardiovascular risk assessments. Similarly, the large body of work amassed by Dr. Hadrian Kinnear and the team at the University of Michigan 15 , 16 , 17 highlights the importance of baseline and periodic evaluations of ovarian reserve and fertility counselling in TGMs. Lastly, the reported attrition of animals receiving high dose testosterone in the included studies emphasises the need for careful monitoring of TGM testosterone levels.

DISCUSSION

This review assessed TGM rodent models' methods and their translation to appropriate sex hormone levels. The interpretation of our findings are presented below (summarised in Figure 4) and aim to provide methodological guidance for optimal modelling of TGM GAHT.

What drugs should be used, how should they be delivered, and how frequently?

Ester and administration frequency for injections routes (SC and IM)

SC injection was used most frequently; however, clinically GAHT is given to TGM via IM injection. Current literature surveys indicate that SC and IM injections produce similar serum hormone levels when delivery doses are the same. 36

The most frequently utilized ester was enanthate followed by cypionate, which are often used for GAHT in the USA. 7 , 37 This is likely why they were used in 9 of the 12 USA studies.

Hashim et al. 14 showed that weekly SC injections of testosterone enanthate at 0.9 mg/kg or 0.45 mg/kg twice/week achieved similar and stable elevations of circulating testosterone in mice. Dela Cruz et al., 10 Hashim et al., 14 Kinnear et al., 15 , 17 and Tassinari et al. 22 also injected testosterone enanthate but weekly and achieved appropriate testosterone elevations in mice. Bartels et al., 20 Godiwala et al., 11 and Santos et al. 18 injected testosterone cypionate once per week while Lichtenecker et al. 24 injected testosterone cypionate every 10 days. All four studies reported stable post‐treatment testosterone levels. We conclude that testosterone enanthate and cypionate can be injected once per week to stably elevate serum testosterone levels.

Goetz et al. 12 , 13 injected testosterone benzoate, an unusual choice since it has not been approved for human use, potentially impacting the translation of findings. Notably, it does have a relatively long elimination half‐life compared to other esters assessed in this review. This would confer relatively greater stability of circulating testosterone levels if delivered weekly. The circulating testosterone levels reached in the Goetz et al.'s study are interesting and will be revisited later in this discussion.

Finally, testosterone propionate was used by Perez‐Laso et al. 25 as a weekly injection. Propionate is known for its short elimination half‐life which necessitates its more frequent (every 3 days) administration in humans. 38 This makes it less attractive as a long‐term testosterone treatment option. This short half‐life and the necessity for regular injections would inevitably cause fluctuations in circulating testosterone levels compared to the comparatively longer acting enanthate or cypionate.

Esterification influences metabolic half‐life, which in turn dictates frequency of administration to ensure stable hormone levels are maintained. 39 Researchers should opt for longer acting esters over shorter ones.

Ester and administration frequency for SC implants (pellets and silastic)

SC silastic implants were the second most common delivery route in TGM studies and SC pellets were the least. Testopel® was also used and is a SC implant of crystalline testosterone for humans that is relatively new to the market but is effective for GAHT in TGM, albeit it is seldom chosen over injections.

For SC silastic implants, researchers used unesterified crystalline testosterone or testosterone enanthate powder. Hashim et al. 14 modified this delivery route by placing testosterone enanthate dissolved in sesame oil into the tubing. Crystalline testosterone in SC silastic implants was the most successful option here as it produced some of the most stable and in‐range post‐treatment testosterone levels featured in this review.

Testosterone enanthate was the preferred ester for SC pellet implants, followed by deuterized testosterone – a poor choice of formulation, since it has not yet reached the market. Post‐treatment testosterone levels achieved by SC testosterone enanthate pellets were less stable than SC silastic implants containing unesterified crystalline testosterone. Several studies using SC testosterone enanthate pellets found that testosterone levels increased markedly in the first two weeks of implantation and then slowly decreased over time. Large fluctuations like this are known to produce unwanted side effects in the human population and may also produce inconsistent findings in a research setting. For this reason, we would advise against the use of SC testosterone enanthate pellets.

In summary, we recommend the use of SC silastic implants with crystalline testosterone or weekly SC injections of testosterone enanthate or cypionate. We advise researchers to experiment with weekly injections of longer acting esters of testosterone to improve stability of post‐treatment hormone levels. Interestingly, longer acting formulations like testosterone undecanoate or ‘testosterone esters’ (aka Sustanon 250) were not used in any of these studies and are both clinically used for testosterone replacement therapy and TGM GAHT. 6 , 37

Implants vs. Injections

Implants require anaesthesia and surgery which adds significant risk to treatment procedures. Compared to injections, implants offer the benefit of reduced laboratory contact time. Studies included in this review demonstrated that SC silastic implants are effective and superior to SC pellets, producing stable and relevant hormone levels. 14 SC silastic implants with crystalline unesterified testosterone are unquestionably the best option if a ‘washout’ model is needed that requires timely cessation of hormone delivery and return to pre‐treatment hormone levels.

For adult GAHT models where therapy is intended to be lifelong, we would still recommend the use of SC injections over SC silastic implants. The consistent efficacy and non‐invasive delivery of testosterone enanthate or cypionate via SC injection suggest this method is preferable for most researchers. Injections also enable researchers to carefully control the dosage and provide flexibility to readily adjust dose.

What doses should be given?

The aim of these models should be to achieve male testosterone levels in the “transmale” animal that mimic clinical targets of GAHT. Testosterone and estradiol levels can vary between species and strains, so it is imperative to have the relevant male and female control or, at the least, a reference range when using a transgender animal model. In the sections below, we discuss weekly testosterone doses according to animal species and strain.

C57BL/6 mouse models

Only 6 of 16 TGM studies included a control male testosterone level as their target for GAHT treated females. From the control male data, we estimated that testosterone levels between 1 and 6 ng/mL are reflective of the levels in naive male C57BL/6 mice. Many studies reviewed here have successfully achieved post‐treatment testosterone levels within this range for their GAHT treated mice.

Models with strengths

Dela Cruz et al., 10 Hashim et al., 14 Kinnear et al., 16 and Santos et al., 18 all reported post‐treatment testosterone levels between 1 ng/mL and 6 ng/mL in C57BL/6 mice using SC injections. The weekly doses in these studies were, respectively, 450 μg, 450 μg, 450 μg, and 48 mg/kg (~1080 μg for a 20–25 g mouse). The studies delivering 450 μg used SC testosterone enanthate injection and the study delivering 1080 μg (48 mg/kg) was using twice weekly SC testosterone cypionate at ~540 μg (24 mg/kg). Considering the comparable half‐lives of enanthate and cypionate esters, it is interesting that the high doses used in Santos et al.'s 18 study did not reflect greater circulating testosterone levels. Kinnear et al. 17 and Hashim et al. 14 both gave testosterone enanthate SC injections at 900 μg a week and reported post‐treatment testosterone levels of ~7 ng/mL. These levels are marginally above what is commonly seen in naive C57BL/6 males but could still be considered successful. Surprisingly, Kinnear et al. 15 also used 900 μg/week and reported testosterone levels more than twice as high as those reported in the Kinnear et al. 17 study. It is possible that treating within the range of 450–900 μg a week produces stable and relevant post‐treatment testosterone levels. However, doses of 900 μg per week may also be associated with an increased risk of vaginal prolapse and subsequent animal losses, as seen with one animal in the Hashim et al. 14 study. To minimise incidence of vaginal prolapse, we recommend that 450 μg (~18 mg/kg for a 20–25 g mouse) of testosterone enanthate/week is appropriate for C57BL/6 mouse models.

Schwartz et al. 19 also achieved post‐treatment testosterone levels within the male range but used a 10 mg SC silastic implant. We recognise that 10 mg of unesterified crystalline testosterone used in female C57BL/6 mice produces very consistent post‐treatment testosterone levels that match those of naive male C57BL/6 mice. However, we cannot estimate a standardized weekly dose for this method from the data reported in the paper without knowing the weights of the drug‐filled tube before implantation and after removal. The absence of these data is a limitation in all SC silastic implant studies reviewed here.

Models with weaknesses

Kinnear et al. 15 was one of the first studies to employ increasing doses of testosterone (450 μg, 900 μg, and 1800 μg per week). Investigators saw vaginal prolapse in two of the five animals treated with 1800 μg/week. Post‐treatment hormone levels were not reported for this group. Doses at 1800 μg/week and above are excessive and should be avoided.

Goetz et al. 12 ovariectomized 6‐ and 10‐week‐old mice and then treated them with weekly SC testosterone benzoate injections at 31 μg/week for 14 and 10 weeks, respectively. These dosages were nearly 15 times lower than the dose of 450 μg considered to give a successful outcome. Despite this, they found that female mice that were ovariectomized at 6 and 10 weeks had post‐treatment testosterone levels of ~18–24 ng/mL and ~21–29 ng/mL, respectively, while the untreated female control mice had levels of 13–15 ng/mL and 13–19 ng/mL, respectively. In their later study, Goetz et al. 13 repeated these experiments in ovariectomized APO/E knockout mice and recorded testosterone levels 100 times lower than in the first study. If the post‐treatment testosterone levels in the Goetz et al. 12 study were incorrectly reported as ng/mL but were in fact ng/dL, as seen in their later study, 13 the very low dose of testosterone would have had a similar marginal impact and female control groups would have had normal testosterone levels. It is therefore likely that Goetz et al. 12 erroneously reported the testosterone units.

Even when the dose is correct, the method of delivery can weaken the model. Kinnear et al. 16 gave 580 μg per week as a 5 mg, 60‐day slow‐release SC testosterone enanthate pellet and saw relevant post‐treatment testosterone levels at 6 weeks. However, the SC pellets produced testosterone concentrations of ~20 ng/mL within the first week of treatment, which slowly declined to ~5 ng/mL by the end of the study. Interestingly, Hashim et al. 14 also used 5 mg in 60‐day (~580 μg/week) and 90‐day (~390 μg/week) slow‐release SC pellets, as well as a SC silastic tube with 5 mg of testosterone enanthate oil. In all cases, circulating testosterone increased to supraphysiologic levels, ~20 ng/mL in the 1st week before falling to naive male levels by the 6th week. For this reason, we do not recommend the use of SC pellets or SC silastic implants filled with oil.

CF‐1 mouse models

Studies that used CF‐1 mice largely succeeded in modelling TGM GAHT. Bartels et al. 20 and Godiwala et al. 11 used CF‐1 mice but used different mouse strains to determine the male testosterone target ranges. Bartels et al. 20 used CD1 males and Godiwala et al. 11 used B6SJLF1 males. The target testosterone ranges provided by these studies are similar to C57BL/6 at 1–6 ng/mL, Bartels et al. 20 gave 400 μg/week SC injections of testosterone cypionate and successfully achieved male testosterone levels in CF‐1 mice. Godiwala et al. 11 gave 200 μg/week for 4 weeks then 400 μg/week for 4 weeks using the same drug and delivery method as Bartels et al. 20 Likely due to Godiwala et al.'s 11 change of dose, investigators saw substantially less stable post‐treatment testosterone levels compared to Bartels et al., 20 though the mean values were still within naive male ranges. These data provide further support for SC injection dosages around 450 μg/week across different mouse strains.

Hashim et al. 14 used SC silastic implants loaded with 10 mg of crystalline testosterone in CF‐1 mice. Their observations corroborate those of Schwartz et al. 19 with this method of drug delivery successfully causing stable elevations in testosterone to 2.5 ± 1.0 ng/mL after 6 weeks and 3.0 ± 1.2 ng/mL after 12 weeks.

Sprague–Dawley Rat models

Craft et al., 21 Tassinari et al., 22 and Zhang et al. 23 all used Sprague Dawley Rats. Tassinari et al. 22 reported male control testosterone levels of 2.1 ± 0.6 ng/mL, Zhang et al. 23 reported 0.8 ± 0.5 ng/mL, and Craft et al. 21 did not report any. Naive male rat testosterone levels reported by each study are notably different. A study not covered in this review had measured testosterone in naive male Sprague Dawley rats and closely aligns with Tassinari et al. 22 rather than Zhang et al. 23 , 40 From Tassinari et al. 22 and supporting literature we conclude that testosterone levels between 1.5 and 4.5 ng/mL reflect levels in naive male Sprague Dawley rats.

Models with strengths

Tassinari et al. 22 gave SC testosterone enanthate injections at 900 μg, 1900 μg, and 4100 μg/week and documented post‐treatment testosterone levels in female rats of ~2.2 ± 0.3 ng/mL, ~3.0 ± 0.5 ng/mL, and ~3.5 ± 0.5 ng/mL, respectively. Surprisingly, large difference in the doses of testosterone used in this study did not translate to large differences in post‐treatment testosterone levels. These data suggest that injecting between 900 μg or ~4.5 mg/kg/week and 4100 μg or ~21.5 mg/kg produces stable male testosterone levels in female Sprague Dawley rats.

Craft et al. 21 also generated a strong model using a SC silastic implant containing 10 mg of crystalline testosterone/100 g bodyweight. Testosterone levels of treated female rats were ~3.1 ± 1.1 ng/mL, similar to levels achieved in Tassinari et al.'s 22 model. In this study, most rats weighed between 180 and 200 g and received two 10 mg implants.

Models with weaknesses

Zhang et al. 23 was the only study to use SC pellets in rats. We have previously discussed how SC pellets produced unstable testosterone levels in mice. At 10‐weeks of age, investigators administered a 7.5 mg, 60‐day release SC testosterone D3 pellet that raised testosterone levels to 6.2 ± 2.6 ng/mL after 3 weeks in female rats. Zhang et al. 23 only measured terminal testosterone levels after 3 weeks of treatment, which would not uncover fluctuations in testosterone levels caused by the SC pellet implants as seen in other studies in this review.

Wistar rat models

Only Lichtenecker et al. 24 and Perez‐Laso et al. 25 used Wistar rats, with Lichtenecker et al. 24 setting a target male testosterone range of 1.4 ± 0.2 ng/mL for their female animals. Lichtenecker et al. 24 gave ~2.1 mg/kg or 420 μg/week (200 g rat) IM injections of testosterone cypionate while Perez‐Laso et al. 25 injected 40 mg/kg or 8000 μg/week (200 g rat) of testosterone propionate but did not specify the injection route. Testosterone treated animals in the Lichtenecker et al. 24 study reached testosterone levels of 1.68 ± 0.4 ng/mL and successfully mimicked TGM GAHT. In contrast, in the Perez‐Laso et al. 25 study, treated animals had very high testosterone levels at 12.11 ± 1.07 ng/mL. The high doses used by Perez‐Laso et al. 25 were excessive and should not be used to model GAHT.

In summary, this review strongly recommends the use of weekly SC injections of testosterone enanthate or cypionate at 400‐450 μg or ~18 mg/kg (25 g mouse) per week for mice. IM injections of testosterone cypionate at 420 μg or ~2 mg/kg/week (200 g rat) successfully mimicked GAHT in Wistar rats and SC injections of testosterone enanthate at 900 μg/week or ~4.5 mg/kg/week (200 g rat) were sufficient in Sprague Dawley rats. SC silastic implants of crystalline testosterone at 10 mg in mice and two 10 mg implants in rats were effective in modelling GAHT and are also recommended methods.

How long should these treatments be given? Importance of treatment duration

Clinically, GAHT is required indefinitely to maintain secondary sex characteristics acquired through transition. The treatment duration in animal models should also be designed to mimic long term treatments in humans. Therefore, translatability of research findings would be stronger in a chronic treatment model where hormones are administered long‐term. Chronic treatment models, however, inherently increase staff workloads and require more hormones, serum hormone quantifications, and longer period of animal housing. For a given research question, researchers should consider utilising a chronic treatment model to accurately map the time course of any changes induced by the prolonged treatment. These data would inform the appropriate in‐procedure and end‐point measurements for future work.

LIMITATIONS OF CURRENT LITERATURE

Implications of unclear risk of bias rating on data reliability

Many of the included studies were assigned an ‘unclear risk of bias’ rating, most commonly due to missing detail regarding allocation concealment, random sequencing, and blinding. Without the appraisal of these domains, selection, performance or detection biases cannot be ruled out. The unclear risk of bias in these studies negatively impacts the reliability of the pooled findings of this review. The conclusions of this review should therefore be interpreted with caution since they may be biased due to missing methodological details in the studies included in the review.

Variability in reporting hormone outcomes and incomplete data

It is imperative that investigators report the post‐treatment testosterone and estradiol levels in their studies to confirm that their research methods effectively mimic human GAHT. Moreover, they should include both male and female control groups in their hormone assessments to reliably compare naive male and female hormone levels with the post‐treatment hormone levels of their treatment groups. Reporting of both, testosterone and estradiol levels was rare in the studies cited in this review.

Translational challenges of preclinical research

This review discusses the importance of mimicking human gender affirming‐hormone therapy in preclinical research to provide consistent and reliable insights for translation to clinical studies. The utility of preclinical research to inform the clinical field is well known; however, there are translational challenges that arise. There are studies that have reported changes in rodents that have not manifested in the human populations. One such example is the emergence of thyroid tumours in mice treated with glucagon‐like receptor‐1 agonists, 41 adverse changes that were, however, not evident in clinical trials or in the wider general population. 42 , 43 The limitations of translation should not be ignored regarding GAHT, particularly when considering long‐term health effects.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

To confirm the model, male and female control groups with naive hormone levels should be used to serve as a guide for post‐treatment circulating hormone target levels for testosterone and estradiol. Ancillary measurements like skeletal muscle or vaginal morphology changes should also be used to strengthen the confirmation of the model.

Testosterone enanthate and cypionate achieved stable testosterone levels but we recommend the use of long‐acting esters in formulations like Sustanon 250 or testosterone undecanoate that are clinically used in TGM GAHT. These should theoretically maintain more stable hormone levels than testosterone enanthate or cypionate when injected weekly.

Despite growing concerns about the cardiovascular health of the transgender population, only 2 of the 16 studies in this review had any cardiovascular focus. Preclinical cardiovascular research is key to the planning and design of clinical trials and practice. These studies provide the opportunity to utilise a variety of methods to assess cellular and subcellular signalling, cardiovascular risk, heart function, and tolerance to ischemia–reperfusion. Preclinical cardiovascular research is essential to the progression of TGM health research and healthcare, and the recommendations presented here could improve the impact of preclinical studies.

CONCLUSIONS

Preclinical studies have started to model TGM GAHT in rodents and we asked what methodological approaches are best to accurately mimic human TGM GAHT. Our systematic review addresses this question through an in‐depth assessment of the existing rodent TGM models published in the literature, highlighting both effective and ineffective methods used to model TGM GAHT to form methodological recommendations. Future preclinical research could adopt these recommendations but also leverage rodent models to compare how drug/hormone delivery systems themselves influence various health outcomes as seen with other hormone treatments. Moreover, future studies could significantly increase duration of exposure to assess the long‐term physiological impacts of these therapies in greater detail. The recommendations made here provide the foundation for consistent and reproducible study design and should ensure more applicable and reliable findings in TGM rodent models. This would also enable researchers to reliably determine any off‐target GAHT effects to guide future clinical trial design and clinical practice.
