Catatonia with prominent psychosocial factors: A case report
Authors: Hiroto Noguchi, Yuki Omiya, Masahiro Hishiyama, Mariko Nakau, Sadayuki Hashioka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCatatonia is a psychomotor syndrome historically linked to schizophrenia but now recognized across diverse psychiatric, medical, and psychosocial contexts. Detailed reports of catatonia arising amid pronounced psychosocial vulnerability remain scarce. We describe catatonia in a patient with co‐occurring somatic symptom disorder, functional neurological symptom disorder (FNSD), and dissociative disorder that remitted with pharmacotherapy.Case PresentationA man in his 40's with chronic physical illness and recurrent fears of abandonment by his partner developed functional and dissociative symptoms after his partner's surgery was scheduled and was admitted following the sudden onset of fragmented, childlike speech. No specific neurological or medical cause was identified, and no schizophrenia or mood disorder was present. He developed stupor, mutism, posturing, negativism, mannerisms, and stereotypy, meeting DSM‐5‐TR criteria for catatonia; the Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS) score was 22. Under lorazepam 3 mg/day and quetiapine 600 mg/day, the BFCRS improved to 3 by hospital Day 34, but worsened during dose reduction of either drug and improved on re‐escalation. He was discharged on hospital Day 98 with a BFCRS of 0, with no recurrence at 10 months.ConclusionCatatonia can arise in patients with co‐occurring somatic symptom disorder, FNSD, and dissociative disorder. In this patient, catatonia showed dose‐dependent re‐emergence during tapering of both lorazepam and quetiapine, with remission on re‐escalation. Even when psychosocial factors appear prominent, careful monitoring and gradual dose adjustment may be important for maintaining remission.

BACKGROUND

Catatonia is a psychomotor syndrome characterized by distinctive signs such as stupor, posturing, and waxy flexibility. Since Kahlbaum first described catatonia 1 and Kraepelin classified it as a subtype of schizophrenia, 2 the syndrome has historically been associated with schizophrenia. Current evidence, however, indicates a diverse etiology: catatonia occurs not only in psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia and mood disorders but also in association with medical conditions, medications, and psychosocial stressors. 3 , 4 Treatment centers on benzodiazepines (particularly lorazepam) and electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), both recommended as first‐line therapies by the British Association for Psychopharmacology (BAP) guidelines. 5 , 6 Antipsychotics are generally used with caution in catatonia owing to the risk of symptom exacerbation through dopaminergic blockade. 5

Only a small number of reports have linked catatonia to psychosocial stressors, including post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) 7 and dissociative disorders. 8 The present case adds to this literature through three intersecting features: (a) catatonia arising in the context of pronounced psychosocial vulnerability and abandonment anxiety; (b) functional neurological and dissociative symptoms that preceded—rather than followed—catatonia onset; and (c) a longitudinally documented Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS) course demonstrating dose‐dependent re‐emergence and remission during tapering and re‐escalation of both lorazepam and quetiapine.

CASE PRESENTATION

A man in his 40's was brought to our emergency department by his partner, who reported that he had suddenly begun speaking in short, fragmented sentences in a childlike tone. His medical history included cutaneous polyarteritis nodosa (onset in adolescence), left femoral head necrosis requiring osteotomy, lumbar compression fracture, and depression. He was the eldest of three siblings, with an unremarkable perinatal and developmental history, and was described premorbidly as competitive and emotionally reactive. Polyarteritis nodosa developed at age 18, and residual lower‐limb impairment led him to abandon his aspiration to train as a nurse; after a brief period of employment in his early twenties, he remained unemployed. Since adolescence, he had received continuous pharmacotherapy for chronic pain, including high‐dose opioids, from the hospital's Dermatology and Palliative Care departments. He was unmarried but had cohabited for approximately 20 years with a partner who became his principal attachment figure and caregiver, and he repeatedly expressed anxiety regarding his illness and fear of being abandoned.

Two months before admission, around the time his partner's surgery was scheduled, he developed rapid‐onset gait difficulty and became dependent on assistance for toileting. Two days before admission, he was transported by ambulance with head heaviness; no organic abnormalities were identified, and after being instructed to return home, he refused to leave the hospital restroom for two hours, insisting “I don't want to go home.” The following morning, urinary incontinence caused tension with his partner, and that same evening he suddenly began speaking in fragmented sentences with a childlike, infantile intonation, prompting another emergency transport and referral to our department. No medication changes had occurred during the six months preceding admission.

On admission, the patient was bedridden and hypokinetic. He responded with a relaxed expression, avoiding eye contact, and intermittently uttered contextually inappropriate words—such as expressions of negation and affection—in an infantile tone. The sensorium was clear, without diurnal fluctuation. Vital signs were stable, with no autonomic instability, muscle rigidity, tremor, or focal neurological deficit; a transient fever early in the admission was attributable to aspiration pneumonia and resolved promptly with antibiotics. Serum creatine kinase remained within normal limits throughout. Blood tests, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis—including negative anti‐NMDA receptor antibodies—and EEG revealed no significant abnormalities (Tables 1 and 2). Brain MRI showed no signal abnormalities in limbic structures, including the hippocampus and amygdala, arguing against autoimmune limbic encephalitis.

After admission, the patient gradually became mute, refused food and medication, and progressed to stupor with minimal response to external stimuli. Posturing, characterized by maintaining fixed positions for extended periods, was also observed following a lumbar puncture. On hospital Day 6, the patient exhibited stupor, mutism, posturing, negativism, mannerisms, and stereotypy, fulfilling DSM‐5‐TR criteria for catatonia 9 ; the BFCRS score was 22. No first‐rank symptoms of schizophrenia, persistent hallucinatory–delusional states, mood episode, or neurodevelopmental history were identified. These findings argued against an organic etiology or primary psychiatric disorder, and catatonia was best understood in the context of the pronounced psychosocial factors described above.

Lorazepam and quetiapine were administered via nasogastric tube and titrated to lorazepam 3 mg/day and quetiapine 600 mg/day, after which quetiapine was raised to 750 mg/day (Figure 1). Modified ECT was considered but not performed given a positive pharmacological response. Stupor and posturing gradually improved: oral intake resumed on hospital Day 19, single‐word speech on Day 25, and conversational speech on Day 33. The BFCRS score improved to three by hospital Day 34.

Lower panel: Bush–Francis Catatonia Rating Scale (BFCRS) total scores at successive assessments. The BFCRS fell from 22 to 3 under lorazepam 3 mg/day with quetiapine titrated to 600–750 mg/day, rose to 23 when lorazepam was tapered to 1 mg/day while quetiapine was held at 750 mg/day, decreased to 1 after lorazepam was restored to 3 mg/day, rose to 7 when quetiapine was reduced to 300 mg/day with lorazepam unchanged, and reached 0 by discharge on hospital Day 98.

With quetiapine held at 750 mg/day, stepwise tapering of lorazepam to 1 mg/day caused the BFCRS to rise to 23. After lorazepam was restored to 3 mg/day, supplemented by temporary intramuscular diazepam, the BFCRS decreased to 1. During this relapse, the patient also refused food and medication; nasogastric tube reinsertion was proposed on clinical‐necessity grounds to maintain nutrition, hydration, and pharmacotherapy, whereupon refusal resolved promptly. The tube was not actually reinserted.

Subsequently, with lorazepam held constant at 3 mg/day, stepwise reduction of quetiapine to 300 mg/day resulted in a BFCRS score of 7 and the return of bed‐bound urination. Re‐escalating quetiapine to 600 mg/day achieved symptomatic improvement; urinary catheterization was also proposed at this time on the same clinical‐necessity grounds, although it was likewise not performed. The patient was discharged on hospital Day 98 with a BFCRS score of 0, receiving lorazepam 3 mg/day and quetiapine 600 mg/day (Figure 1). Ten months after discharge, no recurrence of catatonia has been observed.

Following recovery, weekly 30 min supportive psychotherapy sessions were initiated, aiming to develop shared illness understanding, reduce anxiety, and help the patient verbalize his fear of losing his partner. Sessions included psychoeducation, explicit reassurance regarding ongoing care, and review of coping resources. The patient's repeated concerns about physical health and potential partner loss reinforced the impression that psychosocial factors had played a significant role in this episode.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

This case meets DSM‐5‐TR criteria for catatonia associated with another mental disorder—specifically, co‐occurring somatic symptom disorder, functional neurological symptom disorder (FNSD), and dissociative disorder—conditions that constituted the underlying clinical context, not prodromal phenomena. Somatic symptom disorder with predominant pain was a longitudinal formulation rather than a newly diagnosed acute condition: although polyarteritis nodosa, femoral head necrosis, and a lumbar compression fracture provided a genuine organic basis for pain, his health‐related anxiety and illness behavior persisted for more than two decades and appeared disproportionate to the objective disease burden, with repeated expressions of illness‐related fear, escalating help‐seeking, and behavior such as refusing to leave the hospital restroom for two hours when discharge was advised (DSM‐5‐TR Criterion B). The FNSD rested on rapid‐onset astasia–abasia without an organic basis, and the dissociative presentation, characterized by regressive, infantile speech that did not map onto a single defined subtype, was classified as unspecified dissociative disorder (ICD‐11: 6B6Z, dissociative disorders, unspecified). Although these categories can overlap clinically, characteristic catatonic motor signs, fulfillment of DSM‐5‐TR criteria, and the response to lorazepam identified catatonia as the principal syndrome requiring intervention.

Although the patient had a remote history of depression, no mood episode—depressive, manic, or mixed—was identified before, during, or after admission. His depression had been managed by the palliative care service with several antidepressants over the years, including escitalopram and amitriptyline; at the time of admission he was receiving duloxetine 40 mg/day and mirtazapine 15 mg/day. Both were discontinued on admission to reduce pharmacological confounding and clarify the underlying clinical picture, and were not resumed, as no depressive symptoms emerged during admission or the 10‐month follow‐up. Because catatonia was already present on admission, before antidepressants were withdrawn, antidepressant‐withdrawal catatonia was considered unlikely, as were antidepressant‐induced activation and a primary mood disorder. Delirium was unlikely without identifiable precipitants or diurnal fluctuation. 10 All opioids were gradually tapered and discontinued during admission without clinically significant recurrence of pain or behavioral disturbance, indicating that opioids were not the primary driver of the presentation. No additional specific cause was identified on laboratory studies, CSF analysis, brain MRI, and EEG, including the limbic MRI findings described above. 11 , 12

Because psychotic symptoms may be difficult to elicit during catatonic mutism, a schizophrenia‐spectrum disorder cannot be excluded on cross‐sectional examination alone, and we therefore relied on the longitudinal course: over more than two decades of continuous contact with our institution's dermatology and palliative care services no psychotic symptoms or unexplained functional deterioration had been documented, and once the patient became communicative during recovery, and throughout the 10‐month follow‐up, no hallucinations, delusions, formal thought disorder, or negative symptoms were observed. The improvement after antipsychotic treatment does not itself indicate a primary psychotic disorder here, because the earliest and most robust response tracked lorazepam rather than quetiapine: catatonia recurred when lorazepam was tapered while quetiapine was held at 750 mg/day, and remitted when lorazepam alone was restored. A schizophrenia‐spectrum disorder was therefore considered unlikely, although quetiapine was continued throughout the follow‐up period.

A biopsychosocial formulation plausibly accounts for the trajectory. Chronic somatic illness and persistent pain since adolescence had reinforced illness behavior and dependency over decades, and his cohabitation had become his central attachment relationship, with abandonment anxiety as a recurrent theme. His partner's scheduled surgery may have acted as a psychosocial precipitant, temporally associated with functional gait disturbance, dissociative‐like communication, and somatic symptoms culminating in catatonia. This trajectory is consistent with fear‐based models: Moskowitz conceptualized catatonia as an evolutionary defense response to overwhelming fear; 13 Northoff postulated that amygdala hyperactivity and reduced GABAergic prefrontal function play a central role; 14 and Fink and Shorter proposed that persisting fear sustains catatonic states. 15 The intense fear of loss and the marked response to lorazepam are consistent with these formulations. Anxiety and psychosocial stress are themselves common in catatonia, and prior reports of psychosocial‐stress‐related catatonia 7 , 8 , 16 similarly resolved with benzodiazepines or ECT. What appears distinctive here is not the presence of stress but its developmental and longitudinal character: a long‐standing attachment‐related vulnerability rather than a single acute event, and a sequence in which chronic abandonment anxiety gave rise first to functional and dissociative symptoms and only later to catatonia.

We did not consider lorazepam alone sufficient: anxiety, tension, regressive behavior, and dissociative‐like phenomena were clinically prominent, and GABAergic intervention alone seemed unlikely to stabilize these dimensions. Quetiapine was introduced early, when the underlying cause of the catatonia was not yet established, on the basis of a case series reporting benefit in catatonic schizophrenia 17 —a different diagnostic context with limited generalizability—and of its comparatively low D2‐receptor occupancy with potent 5‐HT2A antagonism, a profile chosen to limit the risk highlighted by BAP guidance. 5 Offered as a hypothesis rather than an established mechanism, proposed accounts implicate an imbalance among GABAergic, dopaminergic, and serotonergic transmission, 18 including a depolarization‐block model in which weak D2 blockade with 5‐HT2A antagonism might be less likely to worsen catatonia. 19 As the clinical picture clarified, lorazepam proved to be the primary effective agent, and quetiapine was continued as an adjunct whose independent anticatatonic contribution remains uncertain.

The BFCRS trajectory reflects two sequential, single‐agent dose manipulations. In the first, quetiapine was held constant at 750 mg/day while lorazepam was tapered from 3 to 1 mg/day, and the BFCRS rose from 3 to 23, indicating lorazepam dependence and showing that a high antipsychotic dose alone did not sustain remission. In the second, lorazepam was held constant at 3 mg/day while quetiapine was reduced from 750 to 300 mg/day, and the BFCRS rose from 1 to 7, improving again on re‐escalation. Because each agent was varied in isolation, these phases are not contradictory; the course instead documents a separate dose‐response for each drug, although a single case cannot establish causality.

Two behavioral observations merit comment. Neither nasogastric‐tube reinsertion nor urinary catheterization was actually performed; each was proposed on clinical‐necessity grounds, and the corresponding behavior—food and medication refusal, and bed‐bound urination—resolved after the proposal alone. These responses are consistent with the heightened suggestibility of dissociative states and may reflect anxiety reduction from the prospect of concrete clinical care. The proposals addressed nutritional, hydration, and elimination needs and were not designed as behavioral interventions; we do not interpret these changes as evidence of voluntary symptom production.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. We describe a single patient, and causal inferences cannot be drawn. Whether quetiapine exerted a direct effect on catatonia or stabilized co‐occurring affective and dissociative dimensions cannot be determined. Additional autoimmune antibody panels were not obtained, although the absence of limbic MRI signal abnormalities rendered autoimmune encephalitis unlikely. Despite these limitations, the longitudinally documented, dose‐responsive BFCRS course illustrates that benzodiazepine‐centered pharmacotherapy can be effective for catatonia arising amid pronounced psychosocial vulnerability, and that premature dose reduction carries a risk of relapse. Even when psychosocial factors predominate, careful monitoring and gradual dose adjustment may be important for achieving and maintaining remission.
