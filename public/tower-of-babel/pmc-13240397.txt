Factors Affecting Country Differences in the Incidence of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus Based on the Global Burden of Disease Data 2021: A Multilevel Longitudinal Ecological Study
Authors: Zahra Jaafari, Jafar Hassanzadeh, Mehrab Sayadi, Mohammad Hossein Sharifi, Alireza Mirahmadizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe variation in the incidence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in different countries is associated with various factors. This study aimed to examine the factors linked to the difference in the incidence of T2DM based on Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data and using a multilevel model.MethodsA two‐level longitudinal ecological study using GBD data examined annual observations nested within countries from 1990 to 2021. Level 1 predictors were high BMI, low physical activity (PA), and diet low in fiber (DLF), while Level 2 predictors included human development index (HDI) and current health expenditure (CHE) as a percentage of gross domestic product (GDP). The first model used fixed effects with a random country effect; the second added random slopes for time, high BMI, and low PA.ResultsThe results of the first model showed that high BMI and time had a positive and significant association with the incidence of T2DM, but the association between low PA, DLF, HDI and CHE (% of GDP) with the disease was inverse. In the second model, in addition to high BMI and time, low PA and CHE (% of GDP) also had a positive and significant association, but the HDI association was negative and significant, and DLF did not show a statistically significant association.ConclusionThe incidence of T2DM is linked to individual, lifestyle, and socioeconomic factors; thus, effective prevention requires programs that consider interactions between individual factors and broader structures.Keywords: global burden of disease, incidence, multilevel models, type 2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

In recent decades, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has been significantly associated with mortality, morbidity, and healthcare costs globally [1, 2], and changes in diet, exercise, and work have been linked to risk factors for T2DM, such as sedentary lifestyles, obesity, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension [3, 4, 5]. These behavioral and epidemiological changes are associated with a rise in the global burden of T2DM and show different temporal and geographical patterns. Estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 show that T2DM remains a leading factor in disability and mortality, with prevalence varying considerably between countries [6, 7, 8]. According to reports from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) (11th edition, 2025), this increasing trend is associated with obesity, inactivity, urbanization, and an aging population [9, 10].

In addition to behavioral factors, the global trend in T2DM is linked to a set of structural and socio‐economic factors. Evidence suggests that differences in lifestyle, nutrition, and urbanization can be associated with different patterns of incidence and prevalence of diabetes across countries [11, 12]. Also, access to disease prevention and management services, investment in the health system, and the level of human development of countries are among the major factors that correlate with heterogeneity in the burden of T2DM in different regions of the world [13, 14]. Examining the temporal trends and epidemiological changes of diabetes in diverse social and environmental contexts suggests that analyzing factors at multiple levels is essential to better understand the dynamics of the disease [6, 15].

Despite extensive evidence on the behavioral and structural factors associated with type 2 diabetes and its cross‐country differences, there is still a lack of a clear understanding of how these factors interact at different levels (individual, population, and national) [6, 16]. Many previous studies have examined individual or country factors only and have not been able to simultaneously examine within‐ and between‐country factors and correlations. Most studies have been cross‐sectional or individual‐level and have not been able to analyze differences between countries and changes over time [17, 18]. This limitation means that true differences between countries or changes over time in diabetes incidence are not fully understood. The use of longitudinal multilevel analysis allows for the simultaneous examination of individual, environmental, and country‐level factors and how these associations change over time, and can provide a more comprehensive view of the factors associated with cross‐country differences in T2DM incidence. Ignoring this structure often leads to underestimation of standard errors [19]. Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate factors associated with differences in T2DM incidence among adults aged 25 years and older between 1990 and 2021, using data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database and a multilevel longitudinal approach.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This a longitudinal ecological study was based on data from the GBD 2021 study, publicly available through the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) Global Health Data Exchange and Visualization Tools (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool) [20]. The study, focused on the incidence of T2DM among adults aged 25 years and older from 1990 to 2021, with the 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) were defined as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile values.

Case Definition

Based on data from the GBD 2021 study, the number of T2DM cases was calculated by subtracting estimates for type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) from the total diabetes burden across ages, genders, and regions. Diagnostic criteria are fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) or those currently treated with drugs or insulin [8].

Statistical Analyses

Multilevel Modeling of Repeated Measures

The assumption of independence in observations means that after considering the independent variables in the analysis, there is no relationship between the observations in the sample for the dependent variable. However, in many cases, the assumption of independence of observations is violated, and the method used to select the sample is associated with correlated responses between observations, creating a nested data structure. This structure means that lower‐level data is nested within higher‐level data [21]. Longitudinal data are correlated due to repeated measurements over time, making them a special case of multilevel data [22]. Given the multilevel nature of the data set, longitudinal multilevel modeling was used in this study. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study Levels

A two‐level model was constructed, with repeated measures of T2DM incidence over time (Level 1) nested within 188 countries (Level 2). These models include fixed effects and random effects. Fixed effects that are assumed to remain unchanged across the population, while random effects describe fluctuations and surface differences between groups, allowing for country‐specific characteristics to vary around the overall mean [21].

Independent Variables

In multilevel modeling using longitudinal data, independent variables can generally be classified into three categories:

Dedicated Time Predictor

A time predictor variable is included in the model to explain time‐related changes.

Time‐Varying Predictor

A time‐varying predictor is a variable whose values do not remain constant over time and is entered into the model at the first level of a multilevel longitudinal analysis to examine how individual or within‐unit changes are associated with the outcome of interest. In this study, time‐dependent variables included high BMI, low physical activity (PA), and a diet low in fiber (DLF). These macro‐level risk factors, retrieved from the GBD database, were measured using the Summary Exposure Value (SEV) rate. To ensure clarity and interpretability, it is important to note that the SEV is a robust, risk‐weighted metric quantifying a population's exposure to a risk factor. It operates on a continuous scale ranging from 0 (indicating no risk exposure in the population) to 100 (indicating the entire population is exposed to the maximum possible risk). The cross‐country comparability and temporal reliability of these estimates are inherent strengths of the GBD framework. The GBD study employs standardized analytical methods, including spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression, to adjust for differing data collection methods, missing data, and methodological biases across nations, thereby generating highly reliable and comparable global SEV estimates over time [23].

Time‐Invariant Predictor

Time‐invariant predictors are variables whose values do not change over the study period and are used to explain differences between individuals or units. In this study, these variables were used to explain differences between second‐tier countries. The time‐invariant predictors in this study's longitudinal multilevel models were the Human Development Index (HDI) and current health expenditure (CHE) as a percentage of gross domestic product (GDP). To create Level‐2 (country‐level) variables, annual data for each indicator from 1990 to 2021 were averaged for each country. In multilevel modeling, aggregating time‐varying observations into cluster means is an established approach to isolate “between‐country” contextual effects from “within‐country” temporal fluctuations. By doing so, these aggregated variables represent the long‐term, stable macro‐environment and the overall historical capacity of a country over the past three decades. While the absolute values of HDI and CHE fluctuate over 32 years, the relative global ranking of countries regarding these macro‐indicators remains highly stable. Therefore, these averages effectively serve as contextual proxies for the structural development and health investment tiers of nations [24, 25]. HDI, reported by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), was classified into low, medium, and high categories [26] and reflects gross national income (GNI) per capita, life expectancy, and years of schooling [27]. CHE (% of GDP) was similarly divided into three groups using the 33rd and 66th percentiles. Data were obtained from the Human Development Index database [28] and Gapminder [29], respectively.

In the development of the random slope model, we initially considered assigning random slopes to additional covariates, including DLF. However, incorporating DLF into the random‐effects structure led to model convergence issues due to excessive mathematical complexity. Therefore, to achieve a stable and parsimonious model, the final random slope specification was limited to time, high BMI, and low PA.

To assess the level of dependence between country observations, the intra‐class correlation coefficient (ICC) was utilized. The ICC measures of the proportion of variance in a given response variable that is explained by differences between countries, indicating the relative homogeneity within a group, with values ranging from 0 to 1. Larger ICC values suggest a stronger clustering effect and a closer relationship between observations in the same cluster. Where τ 2 denotes population variance between clusters and σ 2 indicates population variance within clusters. Previous research suggests that an ICC ≥ 2.0% indicates the necessity of using a multilevel approach [30, 31].

First, a null model (including intercept‐only) was run to estimate the ICC. Then, the independent variables were entered into the model in the fixed effects section to assess their overall effect on the incidence of T2DM. In the final step, a model was designed where the independent variables were included in the random effects section in addition to being present in the fixed effects section to allow for the examination of possible differences in the effect of these variables across countries as well as cross‐country heterogeneity in the incidence of T2DM. Model estimation was performed using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) approach in STATA14.0 software (Stata, College Station, TX, USA). To compare the models, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and log‐likelihood were used. Lower AIC and BIC values and higher log‐likelihood values indicated better model fit [21]. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was defined a priori at p < 0.05. To emphasize the precision and clinical relevance of our findings, all regression coefficients are reported alongside their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

The ICC was 0.80, indicating that approximately 80% of the variation in the incidence of T2DM was attributable to differences between countries. Discussion

Using GBD 2021 data, this study examined global changes in the incidence of T2DM among adults aged 25 years and older from 1990 to 2021. The ICC estimate obtained from the two‐level model showed a high degree of clustering, suggesting that a large proportion of the variation in the incidence of T2DM occurs at the country level. Approximately 80% of the total variance in repeated measures of incidence of the disease is attributable to differences between countries. These differences appear to be linked to several factors. Based on the results of the multilevel model, the time trend of T2DM incidence increased significantly during the study period, and high BMI showed the strongest association with the outcome time still had a significance in both models. In the first model, time and high BMI had a significant positive association with increased incidence, while low PA and DLF showed a negative but significant association. At the country level, CHE (% of GDP) at high and medium levels in the first model was associated with decreased incidence, but HDI was not statistically significant at this stage. Considering random slopes in the second model, the pattern of associations for some variables changed; time still had a significant positive association with incidence, and the strong association of high BMI remained, but low PA in this model showed a positive and significant association with incidence instead of an inverse association, and DLF was not statistically significant. At the macro level, high and moderate HDI were associated with a significant decrease in incidence, while CHE (% of GDP), unlike the first model, showed an increasing association with incidence at this stage. Additionally, variance estimation showed that a significant share of heterogeneity is accounted for by inter‐country differences, indicating that an important part of the dispersion of incidence is associated with the structural and economic factors of countries.

High BMI is one of the best‐known and most important modifiable risk factors for developing T2DM; increasing BMI increases body fat accumulation, especially visceral fat, which worsens insulin resistance and impairs glucose regulation [32]. In the present study, in both multilevel models, high BMI had a positive and significant association with the incidence of T2DM. This indicates that a higher BMI is strongly and independently associated with a higher incidence of this disease. This finding aligns with previous research; for instance, in a 10‐year cohort study, individuals with a BMI ≥ 27.5 were nearly four times more likely to develop diabetes compared to those with a normal BMI [33]. Due to this strong association, weight control and prevention of unnecessary increases in BMI are among the most effective preventive measures to reduce the burden of diabetes. It is recommended that public health policies focus on promoting a healthy weight, encouraging regular PA, and implementing targeted nutritional interventions; additionally, at the clinical level, regular screening and monitoring of BMI in individuals at moderate to high risk is crucial.

PA is not just a lifestyle behavior, it is also a crucial indicator for maintaining metabolic balance. A reduction in PA can disrupt the natural pathway of muscle glucose utilization and insulin function [34]. In the current analysis, it was noted that low PA was significantly associated with the incidence of T2DM in the first model, which seemed contradictory to scientific expectations. However, after considering random slopes and controlling for cross‐country heterogeneity, its association appeared to be positive and significant in the second model, aligning more with existing evidence. This change in direction could be attributed to the confounding effect of lifestyle variables, potential overlap with high BMI, or differences in PA among countries [35]. Our findings support recent research indicating that increasing moderate to vigorous PA can significantly reduce the risk of T2DM. Implementing exercise‐based interventions in high‐risk populations has also been shown to slow the progression of the disease [36, 37]. Given the significance of this factor, it is recommended that health policymakers prioritize the development of supportive environments, educate the public on the benefits of PA, and integrate regular exercise programs into the primary health care system to lessen the future burden of diabetes.

Dietary fiber is a key component of a healthy diet, and reduced fiber intake has long been considered a risk factor for T2DM. Recent clinical evidence firmly establishes that low‐fiber diets may increase the risk of diabetes by raising the glycemic index of foods, reducing satiety, and increasing systemic inflammation [38, 39]. However, in the present study, while DLF might show initial associations, it lost its statistical significance in the random slope model (p = 0.28). This lack of significance at the macro level, despite strong clinical evidence, can be explained by several methodological and ecological factors.

First, this discrepancy highlights the classic “ecological fallacy” and the inherent difficulty of aggregating dietary data compared to objective individual‐level predictors. Variables like BMI are objective, anthropometric measurements that can be aggregated relatively reliably into a national average. In contrast, ecological dietary data are highly susceptible to aggregation bias. Dietary habits are notoriously heterogeneous within populations and rely heavily on self‐reported survey data, which carry recall bias [40]. Consequently, national averages of fiber intake might become “noisy” and fail to capture the true individual‐level variance required to demonstrate its protective clinical association in an ecological model.

Second, there is the possibility of a food substitution pattern at the country level. In some nations, a low‐fiber diet might be concomitant with other protective dietary patterns (e.g., lower total caloric intake or lower saturated fat consumption), which could neutralize the expected adverse association impact of low fiber at the population level. Third, the GBD DLF index captures the quantity but may not fully capture the quality of complex carbohydrates or overall dietary patterns, whereas the role of fiber is usually interpreted synergistically with other dietary components [41]. Therefore, while individual‐level clinical studies have consistently linked adequate fiber intake with lower diabetes risk specifically through mechanisms such as modulating gut microbiota dysbiosis [42], our macro‐level findings should be interpreted with caution. Given this well‐documented physiological role, future research using individual‐level longitudinal data is needed to accurately disentangle the associations of fiber quality and complex dietary patterns on T2DM prevalence.

HDI is one of the most important macro‐socio‐economic indicators that includes the dimensions of education, income, and health. In the present study, HDI was not statistically significant in the first model, but after adding the structure of random slopes and controlling for cross‐country heterogeneity, it showed a significant inverse association with the incidence of T2DM in the second model. This difference may indicate that the association of HDI is not the same globally and that the magnitude and direction of the association are different in some countries. In the first model, the association of HDI may have been ignored due to the influence of development‐related variables (such as health expenditure, diet, or obesity), but in the second model, which included heterogeneity among countries, the independent association of HDI was revealed. Our finding is consistent with recent studies, as studies in recent years have shown that countries with higher HDI usually have a slower growth rate of diabetes and more favorable metabolic outcomes [43, 44]. However, some studies have also suggested that the association between HDI and diabetes can be positive [45]. According to the results, it seems that improving HDI, especially through improving education, access to primary care, and socio‐economic support, can play an important role in preventing diabetes, and it is recommended that future studies examine the association by separating geographical regions and the level of development of countries.

CHE (% of GDP) is considered an indicator for evaluating the level of investment of countries in the health sector. The theoretical expectation is that allocating a higher share to health will be associated with improved prevention, screening, and disease control services; however, the results of our study showed that although the association of this variable was negative in the first model, after considering random slopes and adjusting for other factors in the second model, its association became positive and significant; in other words, countries with a higher CHE (% of GDP) have experienced a higher incidence of T2DM. This unexpected pattern has been previously reported in some studies. Evidence suggests that this positive association is likely driven by two intertwined mechanisms. First, higher national health spending often serves as a proxy for more advanced demographic and epidemiological transitions. Countries with higher CHE generally have an aging population, a sedentary lifestyle, and the need for expensive treatments. For example, studies evaluating the epidemiological transition have shown that as countries develop, the burden of non‐communicable diseases increases dramatically alongside existing health challenges [46]. Also, in industrialized countries, demographic trends have shown that as life expectancy increases, more years are spent in poor health [47]. Furthermore, higher CHE reflects stronger healthcare infrastructures with better screening and diagnostic capabilities, which are linked to higher detection and reporting rates of T2DM compared to lower‐spending nations. Second, this association reflects a reactive fiscal policy (reverse causality); that is, it is possible that the increase in costs is a consequence of the increase in the prevalence and incidence of T2DM, rather than a predisposing factor. Countries increase health budgets in direct response to the escalating financial and health burden of noncommunicable diseases, particularly diabetes. These findings suggest that higher health spending in ecological models may reflect advanced detection capacities and the inevitable burden of epidemiological transition, rather than merely a failure of prevention programs.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, its longitudinal design with repeated measurements over a long period of time allows for the examination of changes over time. Second, the use of multilevel models allows for differences and fluctuations between levels to be accounted for and random effects to be included in the analysis, yielding more precise estimates and reduced standard errors. However, there are also several limitations. The data used were collected from a variety of sources, and it is possible that methodological heterogeneity or data collection biases may have influenced the results. Also, although the initial aim of the study was to cover approximately 204 countries, limitations in data availability meant that the final analysis included only 188 countries. Finally, a notable limitation is related to the treatment of HDI and CHE as time‐invariant predictors. While averaging these indicators over 32 years was a deliberate methodological choice to capture long‐term macro‐level (between‐country) differences, it inherently leads to a loss of temporal, within‐country dynamics. Consequently, this study cannot assess how short‐term fluctuations in a country's health expenditure or development might acutely impact T2DM incidence. Furthermore, as with all ecological studies, there is an inherent risk of the ecological fallacy; the associations observed at the aggregate macro‐level may not necessarily reflect individual‐level causal relationships.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the incidence of T2DM is associated with a complex combination of individual, lifestyle, and socioeconomic factors. Notably, our final model revealed a significant inverse association between HDI and T2DM incidence. These findings emphasize that for effective control and prevention of T2DM, it is not sufficient to focus solely on individual risk factors or merely increase health budgets. Instead, we highly recommend prioritizing primary structural interventions. Specifically, policymakers must focus on long‐term investments in human development, namely, enhancing educational opportunities and raising income levels. Ultimately, a multidimensional approach is required, and T2DM prevention programs must be designed by considering the interaction between individual lifestyles and macro‐level socioeconomic structures to maximize their effectiveness in reducing the burden of the disease.
