Modeling Narrative Archetypes in Conspiratorial Narratives: Insights from Singapore-Based Telegram Groups CCS: Human-centered computing Empirical studies in collaborative and social computing CCS: Applied computing Psychology CCS: Human-centered computing Social media
Soorya Ram Shimgekar, Abhay Goyal, Lam Yin Cheung, Roy Ka-Wei Lee, Koustuv Saha, Pi Zonooz, Navin Kumar

Abstract
. Conspiratorial discourse has become deeply embedded within digital communication ecosystems, yet understanding how these narratives are structured and propagated remains challenging. This study examines conspiratorial discourse within Singapore-based Telegram groups, revealing how such content integrates within broader everyday communication rather than existing in isolated echo chambers. We introduce a novel two-stage computational framework: first, we fine-tune RoBERTa-large to classify messages as conspiratorial or non-conspiratorial, achieving 0.866 F1-score on 2,000 expert-annotated messages. Second, we construct a signed belief graph where nodes represent messages and edge signs encode belief alignment (positive for shared labels, negative for opposing labels), weighted by textual similarity. We develop a Signed Belief Graph Neural Network (SiBeGNN) with custom Sign-Disentanglement Loss that learns disentangled message embeddings, separating ideological alignment from narrative stylistic traits. Hierarchical clustering of these embeddings identifies seven distinct narrative archetypes across 553,648 messages: General Legal Topics, Medical Concerns, Media Discussions, Banking and Finance, Contradictions in Authority, Group Moderation, and General Discussions. Our SiBeGNN approach achieves superior clustering quality (cDBI = 8.38) compared to standard methods (13.60–67.27), validated through 88% inter-rater agreement in expert annotation. Analysis reveals that conspiratorial content appears not only in skepticism-focused clusters but also within mundane discussions of finance, legal matters, and everyday life. These findings challenge assumptions about online radicalization by demonstrating that conspiratorial discourse operates within, rather than separate from, ordinary social interaction. Our framework advances computational methods for understanding belief-driven discourse in low-moderation platforms and offers methodological innovations applicable to stance detection, political discourse analysis, and content moderation policy design.



1.
Introduction

The Web has fundamentally transformed how information circulates, enabling rapid diffusion of narratives across digital platforms while simultaneously creating new challenges for information integrity and public trust. Conspiratorial content, narratives attributing major social, political, or health events to covert groups acting with hidden, malevolent intent, exemplifies these challenges
(
Zeng et al. 2022
)
. Such narratives position themselves in opposition to official or mainstream explanations, suggesting that institutions, governments, or corporations deliberately deceive the public for self-serving goals. Research demonstrates that conspiratorial discourse erodes confidence in science and governance, deepens polarization, and facilitates misinformation spread during crises
(
Douglas et al. 2017
;
Uscinski and Parent 2014
;
Zollo et al. 2017
)
. Understanding how these narratives emerge, circulate, and become embedded within digital ecosystems has become a critical concern for web science and computational social science.
Digital platforms vary substantially in their affordances for conspiratorial content circulation. Telegram has emerged as a particularly significant venue, especially during crises when users seek alternative or counter-mainstream perspectives. Its hybrid architecture, combining private messaging, large public groups, and frictionless forwarding, creates fertile conditions for conspiratorial and agenda-driven content proliferation
(
Urman and Katz 2020
)
. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Telegram gained substantial traction in Singapore as a space for discussing health policies, personal experiences, and political opinions, often reflecting skepticism toward institutional authority
(
Ng and Loke 2020
)
. This platform-specific dynamic exemplifies broader web science questions about how platform design shapes discourse patterns and belief formation in networked publics.
Singapore offers a distinctive socio-technical context for studying conspiratorial discourse online. With one of the world’s highest social media penetration rates and a government deeply invested in maintaining information integrity, Singapore’s media landscape embodies the tension between centralized information governance and increasingly decentralized, low-moderation platforms such as Telegram
(
van der Linden et al. 2023
;
Tandoc et al. 2021
)
. Prior studies demonstrate how these digital spaces serve as echo chambers for anti-vaccine sentiment, political discontent, and conspiratorial worldviews, particularly during public health crises
(
Walther and McCoy 2021
;
Nainani et al. 2022
)
. Yet limited empirical research examines how conspiratorial narratives are structured, disseminated, and reinforced within closed or semi-public messaging ecosystems in Southeast Asia
(
Alvern Cueco Ligo et al. 2025
;
Goyal et al. 2025
)
. This gap is critical given that the region is both highly digitally connected and politically diverse. Platforms like Telegram now mediate not only social mobilization but also the diffusion of narratives that shape public opinion, health behavior, and political stability.
Recent large-scale web research has begun revealing the complexity of Telegram’s conspiratorial content ecosystems. The
Schwurbelarchiv
project highlights how multimodal conspiratorial content circulates across German-language Telegram networks
(
Angermaier et al. 2025
)
, while the
TeleScope
dataset demonstrates longitudinal diffusion dynamics across millions of messages and channels
(
Gangopadhyay et al. 2025
)
. These studies underscore the necessity for geographically and culturally specific analyses that capture local contexts of meaning-making, policy discourse, and narrative co-construction.
In this study, we analyze Singapore-based Telegram groups to characterize the ecosystem of conspiratorial discourse in a low-moderation environment. We aim to understand not only the linguistic characteristics of conspiratorial messages but also how these messages propagate and interact within Telegram’s public group structures. To accomplish this, we categorize messages into
narrative archetypes
—recurring thematic patterns reflecting how meaning, intention, and stance are organized within messages. A narrative archetype captures the underlying narrative function a message performs, such as framing an event, asserting causality, or reinforcing group identity, rather than its specific linguistic content. By identifying these archetypes, we can abstract from surface-level variation and examine the deeper narrative mechanisms through which users construct, share, and sustain conspiratorial discourse.
We ask:
How is conspiratorial discourse structured within Singapore-based Telegram groups, and what narrative archetypes emerge from message-level patterns?

We adopt a two-stage computational approach to model conspiratorial discourse dynamics. First, we train a conspiratorial classification model to assign binary labels to each Telegram message. Second, we construct a Belief Graph-based narrative archetype clustering model, wherein each message is represented as a node and edges encode pairwise relationships between messages. An edge receives a positive sign if both messages share the same conspiratorial label, and a negative sign otherwise; edge magnitude corresponds to textual similarity. Using this structure, we train a Signed Belief Graph Neural Network (SiBeGNN) with a custom Sign-Disentanglement Loss to learn message-level embeddings, which are subsequently clustered using hierarchical clustering. By integrating message-level linguistic modeling with signed graph neural network architectures, our work advances understanding of how conspiratorial narratives emerge, evolve, and persist in semi-enclosed digital communities
(
O’Hanlon et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023a
)
, informing both computational methods for conspiratorial content detection and policy strategies for fostering more resilient digital information systems
(
Goyal et al. 2023
)
.

2.
Related Work

Research on conspiratorial discourse spans psychology, communication studies, and computational social science. Foundational work conceptualizes conspiracy theories as belief systems attributing major events to secret, malevolent groups, motivated by uncertainty, mistrust, or identity threat
(
Douglas et al. 2017
;
Uscinski and Parent 2014
)
. Such narratives persist due to cognitive biases, affective polarization, and resistance to correction
(
Abdou et al. 2021
;
Tandoc et al. 2021
)
. However, these theories often overlook platform-level affordances shaping collective conspiratorial dynamics
(
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Goyal et al. 2023
)
.

Telegram as a Platform for Conspiratorial Discourse:

Telegram has emerged as a critical venue for conspiratorial and extremist communication due to its hybrid public-private architecture, large groups, and minimal moderation
(
Urman and Katz 2020
;
Skarzauskiene et al. 2025
)
. Forwarding features and channel interconnectivity sustain narrative diffusion and create semi-private echo chambers
(
Nainani et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023b
)
. Large-scale corpora like
Schwurbelarchiv

(
Angermaier et al. 2025
)
and
TeleScope

(
Gangopadhyay et al. 2025
)
have advanced multimodal, longitudinal analyses. However, existing research is largely Euro-American, overlooking regional variations in linguistic practice and cultural context, particularly in Southeast Asia
(
Ng and Loke 2020
;
Goyal et al. 2025
)
. Our work addresses this gap by focusing on Singapore-based Telegram groups.

Computational Detection of Conspiratorial Content:

Automated detection has benefited from transformer-based models such as BERT, RoBERTa, and DeBERTa, alongside newer systems like LLaMA and SEA-LION
(
Urman and Katz 2020
;
Skarzauskiene et al. 2025
)
. While effective on benchmark corpora, these models degrade under domain shift and multilingual noise
(
Walther and McCoy 2021
;
Alvern Cueco Ligo et al. 2025
)
. Critically, existing approaches treat conspiracy detection as binary classification, without examining diverse communicative patterns and narrative structures within conspiratorial discourse itself. We extend beyond binary classification by discovering and characterizing distinct narrative archetypes.

Graph Neural Networks for Social Discourse:

Graph-based approaches naturally model relational structures in online discourse. Signed graph neural networks encode both positive and negative edges to capture agreement and opposition, consistent with structural balance theory
(
Derr et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
. However, prior signed-GNN applications focus on explicit social connections or simple sentiment polarity, rarely addressing complex belief alignment and ideological antagonism in text-centric networks. Furthermore, most architectures lack mechanisms for disentangling orthogonal behavioral dimensions—such as separating belief from expression style. Our SiBeGNN addresses this through a novel Sign-Disentanglement Loss.

Disentangled Representation Learning:

Models such as DisenGCN
(
Ma et al. 2019b
)
and DiGGR
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
demonstrate how orthogonal subspaces can isolate distinct generative processes in graph-structured data. However, they have not been adapted to signed graphs or belief-oriented text networks. We bridge this gap by integrating disentangled representation learning with signed belief graphs, enabling simultaneous capture of ideological alignment and communicative style.

Clustering and Narrative Archetype Discovery:

Clustering methods enable discovery of latent communities and thematic patterns from text-network embeddings
(
Li et al. 2021
)
. Standard approaches include hierarchical clustering, HDBScan, Gaussian Mixture Models, and topic modeling frameworks. While these group semantically similar content, they do not explicitly model belief polarity or antagonistic relations. Consequently, they struggle to uncover ideological structures in conspiratorial spaces, where messages may be thematically disparate yet united by shared skepticism
(
Uscinski and Parent 2014
)
.
No prior work has systematically clustered conspiratorial discourse to uncover narrative archetypes based on both semantic content and belief alignment. Existing studies either perform binary classification or conduct qualitative thematic analysis, without computationally discovering recurring narrative patterns.

Our work makes three contributions.
First
, we introduce a computational framework to cluster and characterize narrative archetypes, revealing internal diversity of conspiracy ecosystems.
Second
, we propose SiBeGNN with Sign-Disentanglement Loss that captures belief polarity and stylistic variation independently.
Third
, we develop a conspiratorial classification model for Singapore’s multilingual Telegram environment and apply hierarchical clustering with composite cDBI metric to identify seven coherent belief-driven archetypes.

3.
Data

Our two-stage methodology required distinct datasets: (1) an annotated subset for training the conspiratorial classification model (Stage 1), and (2) a large-scale corpus for narrative archetype discovery (Stage 2).

Annotated Training Set (Stage 1):

We randomly sampled 2,000 messages from the full Telegram corpus for expert annotation. Two content experts, each with over five peer-reviewed publications on conspiratorial content, independently labeled each message using the definition from
Diab et al. 2024
:
“A conspiracy theory is a set of narratives designed to accuse an agent(s) of committing a specific action(s), which is believed to be working towards a secretive and malevolent objective(s) (secret plot).”
Inter-annotator agreement reached 85%, with a third expert adjudicating disagreements. The resulting balanced dataset comprises 1,000 conspiratorial and 1,000 non-conspiratorial messages for fine-tuning RoBERTa-based classification.

Full Corpus Collection (Stage 2):

We selected six Singapore-based Telegram groups with diverse thematic orientations (Table
1
). Using Telegram’s data export functionality, we downloaded complete message histories from each group’s inception through the collection period. The unified corpus contains
553,648 messages
totaling
24,270,653 words
, with mean message length of 43.8 words (SD = 70). In Stage 2, the trained classifier labels all messages, enabling signed belief graph construction where edges encode semantic similarity and belief alignment. We apply SiBeGNN to learn disentangled embeddings, subsequently clustered to reveal narrative archetypes.

4.1.
Stage 1: Conspiratorial Content Classification

The first stage of our methodology involves training a binary classifier to distinguish conspiratorial from non-conspiratorial discourse.

Model Selection and Comparative Evaluation:

To identify the optimal language model for conspiratorial content classification, we conducted a systematic comparison of nine transformer-based architectures, selected based on their documented performance on discourse classification tasks and regional linguistic applicability. The evaluated models include: RoBERTa-large
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
, Gemini 2.0 Flash
(
Google DeepMind 2024
)
, LLaMA 3.2 3B
(
Meta AI 2024
)
, RoBERTa-base
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
, DeBERTa-base
(
He et al. 2021
)
, BERT-large-uncased
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
, DistilBERT-base-uncased
(
Sanh et al. 2019
)
, BERT-base-uncased
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
, and aisingapore/SEA-LION-v1-7B
(
AI Singapore 2024
)
. SEA-LION-v1-7B is a multilingual large language model specifically developed for Southeast Asian languages by AI Singapore, included to assess the potential advantages of region-specific pretraining.
All models were fine-tuned on the balanced 2,000-message manually annotated dataset using identical preprocessing and tokenization pipelines to ensure comparability. The manual annotation was done by our expert co-authors.
Training was conducted for three epochs to enable efficient comparative evaluation while avoiding overfitting on the limited training set. Fine-tuning employed the Hugging Face
transformers
framework with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-5}
, batch size of 16, and evaluation conducted after each epoch. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score on a held-out test set.

Classification Results and Model Selection.

Comparative results, presented in Table

Notably, SEA-LION-v1-7B exhibited the lowest performance (F1-score of 0.653), suggesting that region-specific pretraining does not necessarily confer advantages for this task. Based on these results, we selected RoBERTa-large as our primary classification model for all subsequent analyses.
The trained RoBERTa-large classifier was then applied to the full corpus of 553,648 messages to generate binary belief labels (conspiratorial vs. non-conspiratorial) for each message.

4.2.
Stage 2(a): Signed Belief Graph Neural Networks

Having obtained binary conspiratorial labels for all messages, we now construct a graph-based representation that captures both semantic similarity and belief polarity between messages. This stage introduces our novel Signed Belief Graph Neural Network (SiBeGNN), which learns disentangled embeddings separating ideological alignment from narrative stylistic traits.

4.2.1.
Graph Construction and Representation

Belief and Discourse Embedding Extraction:

Telegram messages were first cleaned and encoded using a fine-tuned RoBERTa-large model to obtain contextual semantic embeddings. To capture not only message content but also how beliefs are expressed, we augmented these embeddings with four discourse-level features: epistemic modality, agency, sentiment polarity, and emotion spectra.
Epistemic modality quantifies certainty by counting hedging expressions (e.g., “maybe,” “I think”) and certainty markers (e.g., “definitely,” “clearly”), then computing
\tanh(\#\text{certainties}-\#\text{hedges})\in[-1,1]
. Agency captures active versus passive framing by similarly counting active markers (e.g., “we must,” “take action”) and passive markers (e.g., “it is said,” “was done”), with
\tanh(\#\text{actives}-\#\text{passives})\in[-1,1]
. Sentiment polarity is obtained from a transformer-based classifier, with scores multiplied by
+1
for positive,
-1
for negative, and
0
for neutral messages, producing a continuous value in
[-1,1]
. Emotion spectra represent the likelihood of four affective states—joy, anger, fear, and sadness, predicted by a pretrained emotion model, resulting in a four-dimensional vector with values in
[0,1]
.
These seven discourse features are concatenated with RoBERTa embeddings to form enriched discourse-belief vectors, which are subsequently mean-centered and L2-normalized. This representation allows models to differentiate between the
content
and the
expression
of beliefs, supporting analyses of persuasion, conspiratorial discourse, and ideological alignment in online networks.

Signed Belief Graph Formation:

We construct a signed graph
G=(V,E,W,S)
where each node
v_{i}\in V
represents a message. Edges encode both semantic relatedness and belief polarity between message pairs. Edge weights
W_{ij}\in[0,1]
are defined as cosine similarity between discourse-belief embeddings. Edge signs
S_{ij}\in\{+1,-1\}
encode belief alignment: positive edges connect messages sharing the same conspiratorial label (both conspiratorial or both non-conspiratorial), while negative edges connect messages with opposing labels.
To ensure graph sparsity and computational tractability, we apply an empirically-derived threshold: edges are instantiated only when cosine similarity exceeds 0.5 (determined through cross-validation). Additionally, positive edges require similarity above
\mu+0.5\sigma
, and negative edges require similarity below
\mu-0.5\sigma
, where
\mu
and
\sigma
denote the mean and standard deviation of the similarity distribution. This construction yields a signed belief graph encoding the socio-semantic polarity structure of discourse, where positive connections represent ideological alignment and negative edges capture antagonistic or contrasting belief stances
(
Awal et al. 2022
;
Chin et al. 2024
)
.
Unlike unsigned networks treating all interactions equivalently, our signed formulation explicitly records interaction polarity (agreement/endorsement versus disagreement/critique), crucial for modeling conspiratorial ecosystems where cooperation and antagonism coexist and shape information propagation. By combining signed network analysis with linguistic signals, the framework distinguishes cooperative clusters mutually reinforcing conspiratorial narratives from adversarial clusters contesting or debunking them.

4.2.2.
Disentangled Sign-Aware Graph Neural Network

Our model learns distinct representations for belief polarity and narrative stylistic traits from the signed graph. Drawing on foundational work in signed network representation learning
(
Kumar et al. 2016
)
and disentangled graph embeddings
(
Ma et al. 2019a
)
, SiBeGNN maps each node
v_{i}
into two orthogonal subspaces: a belief subspace
z_{b,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{b}}
representing ideological alignment, and a persona subspace
z_{p,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}}
capturing stylistic and behavioral tendencies independent of belief. The combined embedding is
z_{i}=[z_{b,i};z_{p,i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{b}+d_{p}}
.

Architecture:

The model begins with learnable node embeddings
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_{\text{in}}}
, processed through two sign-specific graph convolutional layers, one for positive edges
E^{+}
and one for negative edges
E^{-}
. Message passing is defined as:

(1)

\displaystyle h^{+}

\displaystyle=\text{ReLU}(\text{GCNConv}^{+}(X,E^{+})),

\displaystyle h^{-}

\displaystyle=\text{ReLU}(\text{GCNConv}^{-}(X,E^{-})),

where
GCNConv
denotes graph convolutional operations. Hidden states are concatenated and projected:

(2)

h=\text{ReLU}(W_{p}[h^{+};h^{-}]),

where
W_{p}
is a learnable weight matrix. Finally, belief and persona embeddings emerge through separate linear projections, both L2-normalized for stability:

(3)

z_{b}=W_{b}h,\quad z_{p}=W_{p}^{\prime}h,

This architecture performs sign-aware message passing while explicitly disentangling ideological and behavioral signals.

Sign-Disentanglement Loss:

Training employs the Adam optimizer with weight decay, selecting the checkpoint with lowest total loss. Upon convergence, SiBeGNN produces two orthogonal embedding sets:
\{z_{b,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
for belief polarity and
\{z_{p,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
for narrative persona profiling, serving as inputs for hierarchical clustering in Stage 2(b).
We propose a composite loss function optimizing SiBeGNN through four complementary objectives enforcing structural fidelity, semantic grounding, and representational disentanglement:

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\text{recon}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}+\lambda_{\text{sign}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{sign}}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{\text{belief}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{belief}}+\lambda_{\text{orth}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{orth}}.

(1) Reconstruction Loss
preserves signed structural relationships. Predicted adjacency is:

(5)

\hat{A}_{ij}=\sigma(z_{i}^{\top}z_{j}),

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function. Target adjacency
A_{\text{target}}\in[0,1]^{N\times N}
maps positive edges to 1, negative edges to 0, and unobserved edges to 0.5:

(6)

A_{\text{target}}=\frac{A_{\text{signed}}+1}{2}.

The reconstruction loss is:

(7)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{M}}(\hat{A}_{ij}-A_{\text{target},ij})^{2},

where
\mathcal{M}=\{(i,j):A_{\text{target},ij}\neq 0.5\}
.

(2) Sign-Consistency Loss
regulates the persona subspace to respect social alignment. Positively linked pairs should have close embeddings; negatively linked pairs should be separated by margin
M
:

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{sign}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|E^{+}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in E^{+}}\|z_{p,i}-z_{p,j}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{|E^{-}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in E^{-}}[\max(0,M-\|z_{p,i}-z_{p,j}\|_{2})]^{2}.

(3) Belief Alignment Loss
trains a classification head on the belief subspace. Given predicted logits
s_{i}=f_{\text{belief}}(z_{b,i})
and labels
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
:

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{belief}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[

\displaystyle y_{i}\log\sigma(s_{i})

\displaystyle+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\sigma(s_{i}))\big].

(4) Orthogonality Loss
enforces disentanglement by minimizing cross-covariance:

(10)

\mathcal{L}_{\text{orth}}=\Big\|\frac{(z_{b}-\bar{z}_{b})^{\top}(z_{p}-\bar{z}_{p})}{N-1}\Big\|_{F}^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm and
\bar{z}_{b},\bar{z}_{p}
are mean-centered embeddings.

4.3.
Stage 2(b): Narrative Archetype Discovery via Hierarchical Clustering

Having obtained disentangled persona embeddings
\{z_{p,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from SiBeGNN, we now identify distinct narrative archetypes through hierarchical clustering. These archetypes represent recurring communicative patterns characterized by stylistic and behavioral traits rather than ideological content, enabling analysis of how different discourse modes coexist within the conspiratorial ecosystem.

4.3.1.
Clustering Pipeline

Each message
t_{i}
is represented by its persona embedding
z_{p,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}}
learned by SiBeGNN in Stage 2(a). These embeddings capture narrative stylistic traits independent of belief polarity, having been explicitly disentangled through the orthogonality constraint in the Sign-Disentanglement Loss. To reduce dimensionality while preserving semantic structure, we apply Principal Component Analysis (PCA), retaining components that explain
p\%
of the total variance. This preprocessing step balances noise suppression with structural fidelity, enhancing clustering stability while maintaining interpretability.
The reduced embedding space is clustered using agglomerative hierarchical clustering with Ward’s linkage criterion
(
Murtagh 1983
)
, which minimizes within-cluster variance while preserving hierarchical relationships. Ward’s method is particularly suited for this task as it naturally balances cluster compactness with separation, aligning with our goal of identifying distinct yet interpretable narrative archetypes.

4.3.2.
Optimal Cluster Selection

Determining the appropriate number of clusters is critical for discovering meaningful narrative arche types without over-segmentation. We evaluate candidate cluster counts
k\in[k_{\min},k_{\max}]
using the silhouette coefficient, a standard metric quantifying both cluster cohesion and separation
(
Shahapure and Nicholas 2020
)
. Higher silhouette scores indicate more compact, well-separated clusters. The configuration
k^{*}
yielding the maximum average silhouette score across all messages is selected as optimal.
To further refine cluster granularity and eliminate redundancy, we apply post-hoc cluster merging. Pairs of clusters whose centroid cosine similarity exceeds the threshold
\tau
are merged, ensuring that each final cluster corresponds to a semantically distinct narrative archetype rather than minor stylistic variations. This hierarchical refinement produces interpretable, non-overlapping archetypes while avoiding excessive fragmentation.

4.3.3.
Parameter Optimization and Validation

To ensure robustness and generalizability, we conducted a systematic ablation study varying key hyperparameters: PCA variance retention (
pca\_var\in\{0.5,0.6,0.7\}
), merge threshold (
merge\_th\in\{0.75,0.8\}
), and cluster range bounds (
k_{\min}\in\{2,3,4\}
,
k_{\max}=20
). For each configuration, we computed average coherence scores across multiple random initializations to assess stability. Table
4
presents the results of this hyperparameter search across 18 configurations. Coherence values remain remarkably stable, ranging from 0.360 to 0.386 (within 8% variation), demonstrating that our clustering approach exhibits robust performance across diverse parameter settings. This stability suggests that the narrative archetype structure emerges consistently from the disentangled SiBeGNN embeddings, rather than being an artifact of specific hyperparameter choices.

Clustering quality comparison:

Table 5
presents a comparative evaluation of clustering techniques using average topic coherence, silhouette score, and the Davies–Bouldin (DB) index. To integrate these metrics into a single interpretable score, we computed the cDBI for each model as the ratio of the DB index to coherence
(
Krasnov and Sen 2019
)
:

(11)

\displaystyle\text{cDBI}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{Davies--Bouldin Index}}{\text{Average Coherence}}.

Lower cDBI values indicate better joint coherence–compactness performance, with well-separated, semantically meaningful clusters.
Among the evaluated methods, hierarchical clustering on the SiBeGNN embeddings achieved the lowest cDBI (8.38), indicating the best balance between semantic coherence and cluster compactness. Standard Bertopic clusters exhibited strong semantic coherence (0.331) but a higher cDBI (13.60), suggesting thematically meaningful clusters with moderately higher overlap. By contrast, hierarchical clusters, HBScan clusters, and Gaussian mixture models on the latent graph produced much higher cDBI values (33.13–67.27), reflecting weaker inter-cluster separation and less stable partitions. These results demonstrate that the latent-graph hierarchical model most effectively captures the semantic and structural topology of user discourse, producing clusters that are both interpretable and well-separated.
To validate the contribution of each loss component, we conducted an ablation study removing L_orth, L_sign, and L_belief individually and in combination.
Table 6
shows that each component significantly contributes to clustering quality. Removing L_orth (orthogonality constraint) increases cDBI by 58.2%, indicating that disentanglement is critical for separating belief polarity from narrative style. Removing L_sign (sign consistency) causes a 94.6% increase in cDBI, demonstrating that respecting signed edge structure is essential for coherent persona embeddings. Removing L_belief (belief alignment) yields a 136.9% degradation, confirming that supervised grounding in conspiratorial labels is necessary for meaningful belief representations. The most severe degradation occurs when using only L_recon (271.4% increase), which approximates an unsupervised signed GNN and produces clusters that are structurally but not semantically coherent. These results validate our design choice of combining all four loss components.

Manual verification of narrative archetypes:

To establish validity, two independent content experts with extensive publication records on conspiratorial discourse evaluated cluster coherence by assessing 150 randomly sampled messages from each archetype. Experts independently evaluated topical consistency, stylistic coherence, and functional alignment, achieving 88% inter-rater agreement with disagreements resolved through structured discussion. These validation results demonstrate that our SiBeGNN-based clustering captures meaningful discourse structures rather than arbitrary statistical partitions, confirming the identified narrative archetypes reflect genuine communicative patterns within Telegram’s conspiratorial discourse ecosystem.

6.
Discussion and Implications

This study reveals that conspiratorial discourse in Singapore-based Telegram groups is deeply embedded within everyday communication ecosystems rather than existing in isolation, challenging conventional assumptions about online radicalization and suggesting that understanding such narratives requires examining their integration within broader social discourse.

Interpretation of narrative archetype clusters:

The seven identified archetypes reveal diverse user intents and conversational modes. Larger clusters such as
General Discussions
and
Banking and Finance
emphasize practical, everyday interaction—from casual conversation and shopping to financial literacy and economic critique—reflecting socially grounded communication rather than overtly ideological expression. In contrast,
Contradictions in Authority
and
Medical Concerns
demonstrate institutional skepticism and health-related anxieties, while
General Legal Topics
captures civic curiosity about rights and accountability. Smaller clusters like
Group Moderation
and
Media Discussions
represent meta-discourse about community norms and engagement with popular culture. Together, these archetypes illustrate that Telegram discourse is multifaceted, combining civic inquiry, social bonding, and episodic skepticism. Rather than being dominated by conspiratorial narratives, the platform reflects a continuum of everyday social engagement where critical commentary, entertainment, and community-building coexist (
Table 8
).

Conspiracy distribution across narrative archetypes:

Analysis of conspiratorial content distribution reveals that such discourse integrates within broader communication ecosystems rather than existing in isolation. The
Contradictions in Authority
and
Medical Concerns
clusters exhibit elevated conspiratorial message prevalence, consistent with their focus on institutional skepticism and health policy critique. However, conspiratorial discourse also appears within seemingly mundane clusters such as
Banking and Finance
and
General Legal Topics
, where discussions occasionally intersect with narratives about systemic manipulation. This distribution pattern supports our central finding that conspiratorial discourse operates within, rather than separate from, everyday communicative practices, with significant implications for platform governance strategies that must account for contextual variability rather than applying blanket removal policies.

Methodological implications:

Our signed belief graph-based hierarchical clustering outperformed standard techniques, achieving the lowest cDBI score (8.38) compared to alternatives (13.60–67.27), demonstrating that signed graph representations produce more interpretable narrative archetypes
(
Krasnov and Sen 2019
)
. The Sign-Disentanglement Loss effectively separates belief polarity from stylistic variation, offering methodological advantages beyond conspiracy detection with potential applications to stance detection, political discourse analysis, and any domain requiring joint modeling of relational polarity and content semantics.

Platform governance and generalizability:

The seven distinct archetypes, each with characteristic conspiracy prevalence patterns, suggest moderation policies should be archetype-aware. For instance,
Medical Concerns
may benefit from targeted health information interventions, while
Contradictions in Authority
may require strategies addressing institutional trust deficits. While our framework is platform- and region-agnostic, the specific archetypes reflect Singapore’s unique socio-political environment. Future comparative studies across platforms and regions are needed to identify universal versus context-specific patterns.

Ethical implications:

We implemented multiple privacy safeguards including message paraphrasing, anonymization, and focus on aggregate patterns. We acknowledge potential misuse of our classification model for surveillance and emphasize that institutional skepticism constitutes essential democratic discourse distinct from demonstrably false misinformation. We recognize that labeling health concerns as “conspiratorial” risks pathologizing legitimate grievances; our annotation protocols distinguish evidence-free claims from evidence-based criticism, though this boundary remains context-dependent. We commit to sharing methodology and code publicly while restricting raw message data access.

7.
Conclusion

This study demonstrates that conspiratorial discourse exists not in isolated echo chambers but within everyday communication ecosystems spanning legal discussions, health concerns, media commentary, and financial debates. Through our two-stage framework combining transformer-based classification with Signed Belief Graph Neural Networks (SiBeGNN), we identified seven distinct narrative archetypes in Singapore-based Telegram groups, revealing how conspiratorial content interweaves with ordinary social interaction rather than dominating separate ideological spaces.
Our findings challenge conventional assumptions about online radicalization. The narrative archetypes—from
General Legal Topics
to
Contradictions in Authority
—demonstrate that digital communities exhibit multifaceted engagement where pragmatic, affective, and ideological communication coexist. Even clusters marked by institutional skepticism operate within broader conversational contexts including civic inquiry, social bonding, and entertainment, suggesting interventions must account for conspiratorial discourse’s embeddedness in everyday digital life.
Methodologically, our Sign-Disentanglement Loss separates belief polarity from narrative style, validated through superior clustering quality (cDBI = 8.38). This disentangled representation approach offers a replicable framework for studying belief-driven discourse across platforms. The integration of signed network analysis with transformer embeddings provides a principled method for capturing semantic content and relational structure in low-moderation ecosystems, with applications to stance detection, political discourse analysis, and platform governance.

8.
Limitations

This study has several limitations presenting opportunities for future research. First, our analysis relies on textual embeddings from a specific transformer model; alternative architectures or multimodal data may reveal different patterns. Second, our normalization and weighting schemes, while theoretically justified, remain heuristic and could be optimized for specific objectives.
A critical limitation is our reliance on text-based features for archetype discovery. Narrative archetypes emerge from richer behavioral, temporal, and network-level dynamics beyond text alone. Notably,
lurkers
—users who consume content without contributing—remain invisible to text-based analyses yet constitute substantial portions of communities and indirectly influence information ecosystems. Capturing such latent participation requires incorporating posting frequency, temporal activity patterns, engagement metrics, response latency, and structural network position (e.g., central versus peripheral actors). Future extensions incorporating multimodal or interaction-based signals could better model latent behavioral dimensions underlying archetype formation.
Finally, our dataset is restricted to Singapore-based Telegram groups and may not generalize to other regions or platforms with different cultural contexts, regulatory environments, or community norms. Future work could incorporate temporal dynamics of cluster evolution
(
Shimgekar et al. 2025b
;
Shimgekar et al. 2025a
)
, integrate engagement metrics and social network structure, and conduct cross-platform comparative studies to identify universal versus context-specific patterns.
