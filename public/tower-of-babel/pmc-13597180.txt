Measuring intrinsic capacity among Canadian adults: a population-based study using the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA)
Authors: Vanessa De Rubeis, Jinhui Ma, Andrea Gonzalez, Marla Beauchamp, Lauren E Griffith, Parminder Raina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundIntrinsic capacity (IC) is the composite of an individual's physical and mental capacities, capturing underlying reserve and heterogeneity in the aging process. Despite growing recognition of the importance of IC, inconsistencies in measuring IC persist. The objective of this study was to develop a measure of IC in Canadian adults and to assess its psychometric properties.MethodsData from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (n = 30,097; 50.9% female; 96.0% White) were used. Candidate measures of IC were identified through key documentation and expert consultation. A total of 18 variables were identified to be suitable. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted, followed by a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using bifactor item response theory to compute the IC score. Reliability, construct validity and criterion validity were tested.FindingsEFA revealed a seven-factor structure including Locomotion, Vitality (body composition), Vitality (inflammation), Vitality (blood pressure), Psychological, Sensory/Balance and Cognition. Using CFA, a bifactor model produced an overall general IC score, which showed acceptable reliability, alongside subdomain scores, which showed low reliability and should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Construct validity was supported by expected correlations with related measures, including self-reported healthy aging (r = −0.24), frailty (r = −0.36), functional impairment (r = −0.29), and epigenetic age (r = −0.42). Known-groups validity was demonstrated by declining IC scores across age groups. IC scores demonstrated acceptable discrimination for mortality (AUC = 0.71–0.74).InterpretationThe overall IC score showed acceptable psychometric properties and can be used to characterize IC, whereas the subdomain scores remain exploratory and hypothesis-generating. By incorporating multiple physiological markers, this study advances the measurement of IC and supports earlier identification of individuals at risk of functional decline, with potential application to population-level monitoring of capacity. Longitudinal work is needed to establish its value for monitoring change over time.FundingThis research was conducted using data from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA), funded by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research and the Canada Foundation for Innovation. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Intrinsic capacity (IC), defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the composite of all the physical and mental capacities of an individual, is central to monitoring healthy aging. We conducted a targeted review of published systematic reviews, searching MEDLINE and Google Scholar from inception to April 1, 2026, using combinations of the terms “intrinsic capacity”, “ICOPE”, “measurement”, “operationalization”, “psychometric”, “validation”, “systematic review”, and “scoping review”. We identified eight reviews published since 2022 examining IC conceptualization, measurement, and associations with health outcomes. These reviews highlight rapid growth in IC research alongside substantial heterogeneity in its operationalization. Most studies adopt the five-domain WHO framework, or the six-item Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) screening tool, but apply diverse scoring approaches ranging from simple summative indices to latent variable models. A key limitation is the lack of consensus on domain indicators, with more than 100 measures used across studies despite WHO guidance on candidate measures. A recent consensus study identified 14 candidate biomarkers of aging to inform IC measurement but emphasized that the optimal combination remains unclear. Overall, existing evidence underscores the need for more rigorous, standardized, and biologically informed approaches to IC measurement.

Added value of this study

This study is the first in Canada to derive and validate IC in a large, population-based cohort of more than 30,000 participants from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging. Building on prior reviews, WHO guidance, and recent consensus work, we integrate functional, clinical, and biological indicators, including inflammatory biomarkers and DXA-derived muscle mass. Using a bifactor approach, we show that IC can be represented as a coherent general construct, while the domain-specific factors, though identifiable, showed modest reliability and remain exploratory and hypothesis-generating. We further test the IC score against established markers of aging, including physical functioning, frailty, and epigenetic age, demonstrating construct validity. IC declined significantly with increasing age, and demonstrated predictive validity with higher scores associated with significantly reduced mortality risk. Our findings highlight the multidimensional nature of vitality and identify novel domain patterns, including the clustering of balance with sensory function. By embedding biological measures within the IC construct, rather than treating them solely as correlates, this study advances an empirically grounded approach to measuring an individual's underlying IC.

Implications of all the available evidence

The value of IC as a measure of healthy aging depends on robust and standardized operationalization. We demonstrate that IC can be modelled as a multidimensional construct integrating biological and functional domains in large-scale population data. These findings advance measurement approaches aligned with the WHO and highlight priorities for harmonization, longitudinal validation, and cross-cohort comparability.

Introduction

Intrinsic capacity (IC), as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), refers to the composite of an individual's physical and mental capacities that can be drawn upon to maintain functioning across the life course.1,2 This construct reflects a shift from disease-centered and chronological age models toward functional ability and physiological reserve, emphasizing heterogeneity in aging by capturing both decline and resilience. Unlike biological aging, which reflects physiological deterioration at the cellular and molecular level, and frailty, which captures deficit accumulation and vulnerability to adverse outcomes, IC represents the composite of capacities at a given point in time that can be measured, monitored, and potentially modified across the life course. Critically, IC does not operate in isolation, it interacts dynamically with an individual's environment, and it is this interaction that ultimately determines functional ability.1,2 IC is comprised of multiple domains, including locomotion, cognition, psychological, vitality, and sensory.1,3 Evidence shows that lower IC, across diverse measurement approaches, is consistently associated with adverse outcomes including functional decline, frailty, disability, hospitalization, and mortality, supporting its validity as a marker of healthy aging.4

Since 2022, eight systematic reviews have examined IC, addressing its conceptualization, measurement, and associations with health outcomes.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Together, these reviews highlight the rapidly growing interest in IC as a measure of healthy aging and the ongoing need to refine its measurement. Among these, the review by Beyene et al. (2024) identified 84 studies aimed at operationalizing and developing IC measurement tools. Most studies followed the five domains as proposed by Cesari et al.3 and the Decade of healthy aging baseline report,1 or used the six domain Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework,13 which distinguishes hearing and vision as separate sensory domains. The statistical approaches used to develop these scores varied including simple summative or average approaches, to more complex statistical approaches including bifactor, correlated factors or hierarchical methods. One clear remaining gap is the lack of consensus on the optimal measures for representing individual domains of IC. The Decade of healthy aging baseline report has identified 25 candidate measures suggested by WHO experts for each domain of IC.1 Across the 84 studies operationalizing IC identified by Beyene et al., more than 100 measures were used in varying combinations. Many of these measures align with measures proposed by the WHO, such as hand grip strength, gait speed, vision, hearing, memory, and sleep. However, there remains no consensus on which measures should be included in IC scores.5 More recently, Perri et al., published a consensus statement identifying 14 biomarkers, or measures, of aging that could be used to measure change in IC in the context of intervention studies.14 These spanned physiological, inflammatory, functional and epigenetic domains. These biomarkers were identified to be relevant to aging, minimally invasive and reliable, found to be predictive of biological aspects of aging, and able to monitor aging. However, they noted that work is still required to test which combination of markers has the greatest utility. Many of the consensus-endorsed aging biomarkers closely align with those proposed in the Decade of healthy aging baseline report. Given the breadth of current literature aiming to measure IC, a comprehensive assessment considering all candidate measures based on available evidence is required, including the integration of biological markers that may capture underlying aspects of capacity. The objective of this study was to develop an IC score using a large population-based Canadian dataset, drawing on established methodological frameworks, and to evaluate its psychometric properties.

Methods

Study design and participants

The present study used data from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA), a nationally generalizable cohort of over 50,000 community-dwelling adults aged 45–85 years at recruitment (2011–2015).15 Participants were enrolled from all 10 Canadian provinces and are followed every three years for at least 20 years or until death. Baseline data collection occurred from 2011 to 2015, follow-up 1 from 2015 to 2018, and follow-up 2 from 2018 to 2022. By follow-up 2, 6.5% of participants had withdrawn and 5.9% had died since baseline. The CLSA consists of two complementary cohorts: the Comprehensive cohort (n = 30,097) and the Tracking cohort (n = 21,241). Both cohorts collect a common set of demographics, medical, psychosocial, lifestyle, and behavioural data through computer-assisted telephone interviews or in-home interviews. Participants in the Comprehensive cohort were recruited within 25–50 km of 11 data collection sites across seven provinces and completed in-person assessments that included detailed physical measures and biological sample collection. In contrast, the Tracking cohort was randomly selected from all 10 provinces and participated via telephone interviews only. For the present study, analyses were restricted to the Comprehensive cohort due to the availability of extensive biological and physical measures collected during in-person visits.

Ethics approval

Ethics approval was received from the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (HiREB) Project #16858. All participants provided informed written consent prior to data collection.

Measures

Intrinsic capacity (IC)

Several variables in the CLSA at baseline were considered for inclusion in the IC measure. All CLSA variables were mapped to the WHO Framework for Healthy Ageing,1 including functional ability, the environment and IC, however, only variables aligned with IC were included in this study. Other key documentation was consulted to inform this process.5,9,14 Discussion with experts to determine candidate measures for the score were also undertaken, with the final set of variables selected to provide a parsimonious set of measures capturing the most important aspects of IC. Based on the mapping exercise and expert consultations, a total of 18 variables were deemed suitable including adiposity, C-reactive protein (CRP), depression, executive functioning, gait speed, grip strength, hearing, interleukin-6 (IL-6), memory, muscle mass, muscle strength, sleep, standing balance, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, timed up and go (TUG), vision, white blood cell (WBC) count. Some measures identified from the consensus study14 were not included as a candidate measure for the IC score if they were unavailable in the CLSA (e.g., insulin-like growth factor, growth differentiating factor), or were identified to be better suited as comparator variables for evaluation of psychometric performance (e.g., DNA methylation-based measures, frailty index). For all measures, trained research assistants collected data from participants following standard procedures.16 Detailed information on measurement of each variable can be found in Supplementary Table S1. The distribution of continuous variables prior to dichotomization is presented in Supplementary Table S2. All variables were transformed if not normally distributed and standardized to improve comparability and interpretation. Variables were then dichotomized and coded, where a larger value equated to better health. A higher IC score indicates better health.

Other variables

Descriptive characteristics were categorized as follows: sex (male, female); age group (45–54, 55–64, 65–74, and 75–92 years); education (secondary school graduation or less, some postsecondary education, and postsecondary degree or diploma); and household income (≤$49,999, $50,000–$99,999, $100,000–$149,999, and ≥$150,000). Race and ethnicity were self-reported using the CLSA cultural and racial background measure, which asked participants “People living in Canada come from many different cultural and racial backgrounds. Are you…” and offered the following response options: White, Chinese, South Asian, Black, Filipino, Latin American, Southeast Asian, Arab, West Asian, Japanese, Korean, and an open-response option labeled “Other,” which allowed participants to self-describe their background in their own words. Since this item spans both cultural and racial dimensions, we use the term “race/ethnicity” throughout. Responses were grouped as White and non-White, as most individual categories contained too few participants to report separately. The non-White group combines heterogeneous backgrounds and does not represent a single category.

To test the psychometric properties of the IC score, other variables from the CLSA at baseline data collection were used. Functional impairment was operationalized using the Older Americans Resources and Services Multidimensional Assessment Questionnaire.17 Participants were asked about 14 Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) or Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs). Overall functional impairment was coded as present if the individual required assistance with at least one ADL or IADL. The frailty index used in this study was previously developed and validated.18 It includes 76 deficits which were summed and divided by the total number of deficits measured. A cut-off of >0.2 was applied to indicate high or severe frailty.19 To measure self-reported healthy aging, participants were asked, “In terms of your own healthy aging, would you say it is excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?”. Due to the distribution of responses, the tail ends were collapsed. Detail on DNA methylation analysis and derivation of biological age estimates from epigenetic clocks has been described in detail elsewhere.20 Briefly, DNA methylation age was calculated based on the Horvath 353-CpG Pan-Tissue clock sites and based on 71 CpG sites by Hannum et al.21 Mortality status was ascertained from baseline to the end of follow-up 2 (2022). Deaths were verified by multiple methods and included records from provincial governments where possible, obituaries, and by contacting family members.

Statistical analysis

Development of intrinsic capacity score

To develop the IC score, a multi-step process was undertaken. Following the variable identification process, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted to understand the structure of the selected variables. To aid in interpretation of results, a PROMAX rotation was used. To select the number of factors, eigenvalues and a scree plot were generated and inspected within each imputed dataset, retaining factors with an eigenvalue greater than 1.0.22 A factor-loading cut-off of 0.40 was used to determine relevance to the factor.23 As recommended in the literature, factor retention decisions drew on multiple sources of evidence rather than a single criterion, combining empirical evidence with theoretical considerations and recognizing that purely data-driven approaches may produce statistically defensible but theoretically incomplete solutions.22,24 Next, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using bifactor item response theory (IRT) to develop a score. A bifactor structure was selected to develop the score as it computes an overall score, and then individual scores for each identified subdomain. It has also been found to be the most suitable method when developing a score to measure IC.5 Since bifactor IRT only allows for categorical variables, all variables were dichotomized using established cut-offs or the median (See Supplementary Table S1). While ordinal representations were explored, the bifactor IRT model demonstrated better fit and convergence when variables were entered as binary. Since we were interested in the inclusion of additional biological measures, but IL-6 was only available for a subset of participants, the model building approach (EFA and confirmatory bifactor IRT) was conducted twice to derive two IC scores: 1) a core IC score and 2) an expanded IC score including IL-6. The core IC score included 17 measures and excluded IL-6 (n = 30,097). The expanded IC score included the same measures plus IL-6 and was estimated among participants with available IL-6 data (n = 9729). To assess potential selection bias, participants with and without IL-6 data were compared on key sociodemographic characteristics including age group, sex, education, race/ethnicity, and income. No significant differences were observed across any characteristic, suggesting the IL-6 subsample was representative of the full study sample. Prior to model estimation, bifactor IRT assumptions of multidimensionality, local independence, and monotonicity were evaluated in the core model. Assumption testing supported the bifactor approach, with significantly better fit than a unidimensional model (χ2 = 23,885.6, df = 17, p < 0.001), residual correlations above 0.20 mostly confined to items within the same domain, and monotonicity supported for 15 of 17 items. To determine model adequacy, model fit statistics were computed and compared to standard cut-offs, including Comparative Fit Index (CFI) (≥0.90 indicates acceptable fit), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) (≥0.90 indicates acceptable fit), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) (≤0.08 indicates acceptable fit) and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) (≤0.08 indicates acceptable fit).25

Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation with fully conditional specification. Ten imputed datasets were created. Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching, binary variables using logistic regression, and categorical variables with more than two levels using discriminant function methods. The imputation models included age group, sex and all variables used in the analysis. EFA and confirmatory bifactor IRT model estimation were conducted independently in each of the 10 imputed datasets. For EFA, a single scree plot could not be generated across the imputed datasets; we therefore based factor retention on the eigenvalue criterion in combination with theoretical considerations, consistent with recommendations to draw on multiple sources of evidence rather than any single rule.22 Model fit statistics were pooled by averaging across imputations to produce a single set of summary fit indices. Factor scores were estimated separately for each imputed dataset and subsequently averaged across the 10 imputations to yield a single pooled IC score per participant. Reliability, construct validity and criterion validity were assessed to understand the psychometric properties of the IC score. Reliability was assessed using model-based empirical reliability coefficients (R) for the general and subdomain factor scores, indicating the precision of the estimated latent factor scores relative to their measurement error. A higher value indicates better precision.26 Construct validity was assessed by examining correlations with self-reported healthy aging, frailty, functional impairment and epigenetic age. Based on prior literature, we hypothesized a priori weak correlations (r < −0.45), as these constructs are conceptually related to IC but capture distinct underlying constructs.27 To further establish construct validity, known-groups validity was assessed to examine whether the IC scores (general and subdomain-specific) could differentiate between groups expected to differ in IC, such as age groups.28 For criterion validity, predictive validity was tested.29 To examine predictive validity, two scores derived from the bifactor IRT model were compared: the general IC factor score, which captures shared variance across all domains as a single composite index, and the subdomain-specific factor scores modeled simultaneously, which allow each domain to contribute independently to the outcome while accounting for shared variance with other subdomains. ROC curves and AUC values were calculated to determine how well the general IC score, compared to the subdomain specific score, predicted mortality from baseline to follow-up 2. Additionally, Cox proportional hazard models with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusted for age and sex, were estimated to examine the association between the general IC score and mortality over the follow-up period. The proportional hazards assumption was tested prior to model estimation.

To evaluate the replicability of the factor structure, cross-validation was conducted on the core IC model. The sample was split into half to create a derivation (n = 15,050) and a validation (n = 15,047) sample using stratified random sampling by age group and sex. EFA was conducted on the derivation sample to confirm the factor structure, and a confirmatory bifactor IRT model was subsequently fitted to the validation sample. Model fit indices from the validation sample were reported.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 and R 4.1.0 (RStudio 2025.05.1).

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

The EFA revealed a seven-factor structure (Supplementary Table S3; Supplementary Figure S1). All seven factors had mean eigenvalues exceeding 1.0, therefore a seven-factor solution was retained. Factor loadings can be found in Supplementary Table S5. Briefly, the seven factors include: Factor 1: Locomotion (gait speed, TUG, muscle strength); Factor 2: Vitality–body composition (grip strength, muscle mass, adiposity); Factor 3: Vitality–inflammation (CRP, WBC count); Factor 4: Vitality–blood pressure (systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure); Factor 5: Psychological (depression, sleep); Factor 6: Sensory/Balance (hearing, vision, standing balance); Factor 7: Cognition (executive function, memory).

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

Since the bifactor IRT used a CFA approach, the seven-factor structure was fixed. The bifactor IRT model demonstrated adequate approximate fit to the data (RMSEA = 0.06), with incremental fit indices slightly below conventional thresholds (CFI = 0.86, TLI = 0.81, SRMSR = 0.13). Model fit statistics for individual imputed datasets can be found in Supplementary Table S7. Fig. 1A shows the IC bifactor model graphical representation, including item loadings onto the general and subdomain factors. The IC score varied by sociodemographic characteristics, with higher scores observed among males, those with higher educational attainment, and those in higher income categories (See Supplementary Table S9).

Psychometric properties

The general IC score demonstrated acceptable empirical reliability (mean R = 0.64), while domain-specific reliability estimates varied considerably; Vitality–body composition (mean R = 0.69), Vitality–blood pressure (mean R = 0.60), and Locomotion (mean R = 0.53) demonstrated positive reliability, whereas reliability estimates for the remaining domain-specific scores were insufficient for standalone interpretation (mean R < 0.25). Construct validity was supported by weak negative correlations between the general IC score and poor self-reported healthy aging (r = −0.24), frailty (r = −0.36), functional impairment (r = −0.29), and epigenetic age (r = −0.42) consistent with a priori expectations (Table 2). Correlations across the IC subdomains were similarly weak. Additionally, known-groups validity was supported by a decline in IC scores across increasing age groups for the overall score and most subdomains. For instance, the general IC score decreased from 0.47 in the youngest age group (45–54 years) to −0.60 in the oldest age group (75+ years), with comparable trends observed for Locomotion, Vitality-body composition, and Sensory/balance subdomains (Fig. 2). Both the general IC score and the combined subdomain-specific score demonstrated acceptable discrimination for mortality (AUC = 0.73; 95% CI: 0.72–0.74; and AUC = 0.74; 95% CI: 0.72–0.75, respectively) (Fig. 3A). A higher IC score was associated with significantly reduced risk of mortality after adjusting for age and sex (HR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.45–0.52). Given the low reliability of some subdomain scores, all subdomain-level results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating; subdomain-specific mortality associations are therefore not reported but are available on request.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

Similar to the core IC score, the seven-factor structure of the expanded IC model was fixed. The addition of IL-6 demonstrated modest improvements in fit indices (CFI = 0.87, TLI = 0.83) compared to the core IC score, while other indices remained similar (RMSEA = 0.06, SRMSR = 0.14) (Supplementary Table S8). Fig. 1B shows the expanded IC bifactor model graphical representation, including item loadings onto the general and subdomain factors. The expanded score showed consistent patterns across sex, education and income, whereby males, those of higher education and income levels had higher IC scores (Supplementary Table S9).

Psychometric properties

For most psychometric properties, the expanded bifactor IC model, performed similar to the core IC score. Reliability of the general factor slightly improved in the expanded model (mean R = 0.66), while domain-specific reliability estimates followed the same pattern as the core model. The most notable improvement was for Vitality–inflammation, which increased from 0.16 to 0.38, with the addition of IL-6. Construct validity was similar in the expanded model, with the general factor showing a weak negative correlation with self-reported healthy aging (r = −0.23), frailty (r = −0.33), functional impairment (r = −0.26), and epigenetic age measured using the Hannum DNA methylation clock (r = −0.42) and the Howarth DNA methylation clock (r = −0.42). Subdomains remained weakly correlated (Table 2). A clear downward trend in IC scores across increasing age groups was evident for the general IC score and most subdomains, supporting construct validity, known-groups validity. The general IC score decreased from 0.45 among those aged 45–54 years to −0.61 among those aged 75+ years, with comparable downward trends observed for Locomotion (0.12 to −0.29), Vitality—body composition (0.11 to −0.23), and Sensory/balance (0.30 to −0.42) subdomains (Fig. 4). The expanded general IC score and subdomain specific scores showed acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.69–0.74; and AUC = 0.73; 95% CI: 0.71–0.75, respectively) (Fig. 3B). Findings were consistent in the expanded model incorporating IL-6, where higher IC was similarly associated with reduced mortality risk, when adjusting for age and sex (HR = 0.51; 95% CI: 0.44–0.59). As in the core model, the low reliability of the subdomain scores means all subdomain-level results in the expanded model should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Cross-validation of factor structure

Cross-validation of the core IC model confirmed the replicability of the factor structure. EFA on the derivation sample replicated the same seven-factor structure (See Supplementary Tables S10 and S11), and the bifactor IRT model fitted to the validation sample demonstrated acceptable fit (RMSEA = 0.06, SRMSR = 0.13, CFI = 0.85, TLI = 0.80) (See Supplementary Tables S12 and S13).

Discussion

The concept of IC provides a valuable framework for conceptualizing health and well-being across the life course allowing for a better understanding of the aging process by shifting focus away from disease-based models toward the preservation and optimization of capacities. By emphasizing capacity rather than disease, IC offers opportunities for earlier and more targeted action to enhance health trajectories, potentially slowing decline or preventing loss of function over time.30 In this context, the present study makes a novel contribution by leveraging the breadth of data available in the CLSA to develop a conceptually driven IC score and evaluate its psychometric properties. Overall, the core and expanded IC models demonstrated similar psychometric properties, with the general IC score from both being highly correlated (r = 0.99). Reliability slightly improved in the expanded model for the general IC score, but subdomain-specific reliability varied considerably across both models, limiting the interpretability of individual domain scores. It is important to note that subdomain-specific reliability estimates were insufficient to support meaningful domain-level interpretation or application, and findings pertaining to subdomain structure—including the emergence of distinct vitality-related factors and the clustering of balance with sensory measures—should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The findings from this study suggest the general IC factor score is most appropriate for interpretation and application, while subdomain scores may serve to inform future research directions regarding the multidimensional structure of IC rather than for individual-level or clinical inference. Evidence for construct validity was consistent, with similar correlations between the general IC score and self-reported healthy aging, frailty, functional impairment, and epigenetic age, as well as comparable declines across increasing age groups. The moderate correlations observed with both epigenetic aging measures suggest that while IC captures meaningful variation in underlying biological aging processes, it also reflects dimensions of physical and mental capacity that are not fully explained by cellular and molecular aging alone, highlighting the complementary but distinct nature of these constructs. Predictive validity for mortality was comparable. Inclusion of IL-6 in a subset of participants had minimal impact on overall model performance.

A recent systematic review identified 84 studies that operationalized IC using heterogeneous combinations of measures and analytic approaches.5 Across these studies, more than 100 measures were used spanning the five IC domains, highlighting the diversity of approaches used to measure IC. In the present study, this review served as a foundation for identifying candidate measures. Several measures included in our IC score have been commonly used in prior studies, including gait speed, grip strength, TUG, chair rise, balance, cognitive impairment, depressive symptoms, sleep, hearing, vision, and waist circumference. Additional guidance from WHO documentation1 and a recent expert consensus study14 informed the inclusion of biological measures reflecting underlying physiological processes, including blood pressure and inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, IL-6, and WBC count), as well as gold-standard assessments of muscle mass measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). In previous studies, several of these biological measures have been examined primarily as correlates or outcomes of IC rather than as components of the score itself.31, 32, 33 Although some model fit indices did not meet conventional thresholds, variable selection was intentionally guided by the WHO Healthy Ageing Framework, an established consensus on biomarker hallmarks of aging, published IC studies, and expert consultation, prioritizing conceptual and empirical rigor over statistical optimization. In theory-informed models spanning conceptually heterogeneous domains such as IC, achieving conventional fit thresholds is inherently more challenging than in models where variables are selected or modified to maximize fit. Vitality emerged as a particularly complex and multifaceted component of IC in this study, consistent with recent evidence highlighting the conceptual and measurement complexity of this domain.34 Although often described as the physiological determinant of IC, there remains limited consensus regarding which measures best capture vitality.35 Our results reinforce this lack of conceptual clarity, as three distinct vitality-related factors were retained, reflecting the multidimensional nature of physiological functioning. Notably, the three vitality-related factors identified (body composition, blood pressure, and inflammation) can be conceptually mapped onto the WHO working definition of vitality as a composite of multiple interacting physiological systems, namely neuromuscular function, energy and metabolism, and immune and stress response.35 In the present study, these facets were treated as distinct and weighted equally, as no evidence currently supports weighting them differentially. Whether a higher-order vitality factor better represents the construct remains untested and is a priority for future work. It is also possible that the three facets are better represented as a single vitality factor; future research should test this directly, as a more parsimonious structure may prove the most suitable operationalization of this domain. Another notable difference was that balance loaded with sensory measures, whereas in previous studies it is typically grouped within the locomotion domain.5 Given the conceptual relationship between balance and sensory functions such as hearing and vision, it was retained within the sensory domain,36 though it is also possible that the correlation between these measures in this sample contributed to this pattern. These findings raise important questions about whether balance is most appropriately classified within the locomotion domain in future IC research. While the pattern was consistent across imputed datasets and replicated in a validation subsample, replication in other population-based samples would help clarify whether this represents a consistent structural feature of IC or a sample-specific finding. The remaining domains identified in this study aligned closely with the WHO Healthy Ageing Framework1 and were broadly consistent with domains reported across prior IC studies.5 To further evaluate the psychometric performance of the IC score, we examined its associations with established measures of aging operating at different biological and functional levels, including epigenetic aging markers, frailty, functional impairment, and mortality. While some measures of IC share conceptual overlap with constructs such as frailty and functional impairment, these represent theoretically and operationally distinct concepts, and the observed associations should be interpreted as evidence of convergent validity rather than circularity. This approach extends prior IC validation studies by evaluating associations across both biological aging markers and functional outcomes simultaneously. Importantly, these analyses address construct and criterion validity rather than incremental prognostic value.

This study benefits from the size and comprehensiveness of the CLSA, which includes a breadth of data on over 30,000 community-dwelling adults aged 45 years and older. This wide age range enables examination of IC across multiple stages of the aging process and addresses an important gap in the Canadian literature, where few studies have taken a comprehensive approach to IC measurement. While adults are often treated as a homogeneous group in the aging literature, this study recognizes that aging is a continuous process that begins before age 60, and that middle and older adulthood represent distinct life stages with meaningfully different capacity profiles, as reflected in the systematic decrease in IC scores observed across age groups. The use of a bifactor IRT framework represents a notable methodological strength, as it simultaneously yields a general IC factor score capturing IC as a single composite index and subdomain-specific factor scores. While the present psychometric analyses indicated that the subdomain-specific scores were unreliable, this approach remains valuable for exploring and generating hypotheses about the structure of the IC domains. See Supplementary Box S1 for illustrative vignettes demonstrating the practical significance of the general IC and subdomain scores. Additionally, most candidate measures identified in the expert consensus study14 were available in the CLSA, with the exception of insulin-like growth factor and growth differentiation factor. Although IL-6 was only available for a subset of participants, we were able to run a secondary analysis of almost 10,000 participants, to explore the addition of this inflammatory marker on model fit and psychometric properties, strengthening biological coverage.

This study has important limitations that should be acknowledged. At the time of this analysis, not all candidate measures were available longitudinally, precluding the examination of IC trajectories over time. For instance, epigenetic aging markers were available only at baseline, preventing longitudinal examination of associations between IC and biological aging over time. Future research should test longitudinal associations with IC and biological aging markers, as well as broader health system outcomes, such as hospitalizations or healthcare utilization, leveraging the linkage of the CLSA with administrative health databases across Canada. Longitudinal examination of IC trajectories would allow for the identification of critical periods of decline across the life course and assessment of whether changes in IC score are sensitive to aging-related change and responsive to intervention. Given that IC is conceptualized by the WHO as a dynamic construct that can be modified through targeted interventions, understanding how IC evolves over time and what factors predict divergent trajectories is essential for translating this measure into public health and clinical practice. As additional CLSA follow-up data become available, the current score can be replicated to examine change in IC. It is also important to note that although some characteristics of the CLSA participants are not representative of the Canadian population (e.g., limited racial and socioeconomic diversity), findings can be generalized to community-dwelling Canadians with similar sociodemographic characteristics. Beyond Canada, direct generalizability of this IC score to populations across the Americas may vary given the substantial differences in epidemiological, socioeconomic, and demographic profiles across settings. Nevertheless, the methodological approach employed, including the classification and operationalization of IC domains, the selection of candidate measures, and the psychometric evaluation framework, may offer a transferable template for IC score development in other contexts across the Americas. Similar methodological approaches have been applied in other regional contexts, including the validation of IC measures in upper middle-income countries such as Brazil using the ELSI-Brazil cohort,37 suggesting that findings from the current study may complement and inform ongoing efforts to operationalize IC across diverse populations in the Americas. Several additional limitations warrant consideration. The CLSA Comprehensive cohort, from which this sample is drawn, requires participants to travel to data collection sites, which may introduce selection bias whereby healthier and more mobile individuals are overrepresented. Although many measures were collected by trained research assistants using standardized and validated protocols, minimizing information bias, some self-reported measures remain subject to recall bias and social desirability effects. Additionally, the dichotomization of continuous variables into binary indicators, while necessary for the bifactor IRT model, may introduce misclassification that attenuates associations. Finally, some methodological limitations should be noted. Factor retention relied primarily on the eigenvalue criterion, as scree plots could only be inspected within each imputed dataset. The largest drops in eigenvalue occurred across the first three factors; however, factors four through seven retained eigenvalues above the conventional threshold of 1.0, indicating that they explained more variance than a single indicator. Although CFI, TLI, and SRMR values fell below conventional thresholds, variable selection was guided by the WHO Healthy Ageing Framework, an established consensus on biomarker hallmarks of aging, published IC studies, and consultation with content experts, rather than statistical optimization. In theory-informed models, achieving conventional fit thresholds is inherently more challenging than in models where variables are selected to maximize fit. Exploration of alternative model specifications, including ordinal variable classifications and additional indicators, did not improve fit. Muscle mass demonstrated extremely high item discrimination, largely driving the Vitality—body composition subdomain score, which should therefore be interpreted with caution. Subdomain-specific reliability estimates were also difficult to estimate, likely reflecting insufficient item-level variance within those domains. A further limitation is that we did not assess the incremental prognostic value of the IC score. While the score showed expected associations with related health measures, it remains untested whether it adds prognostic information beyond established measures such as frailty, disability, or routinely available clinical indicators. Establishing this is an important direction for future work.

This study advances the measurement of IC by demonstrating that its operationalization remains highly data-driven and dependent on population and data availability. These findings highlight the need for continued harmonization to improve comparability across studies and contexts. IC scores derived from routinely collected data could support earlier identification of declining capacity and interventions focused on function rather than disease, though the cross-sectional design of this study means the score's ability to detect change over time remains to be established. Overall, the findings of this study provide an example of an IC score, developed using a large Canadian population-based sample.
