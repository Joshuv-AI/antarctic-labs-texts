GREAT: Grassmannian REcursive Algorithm for Tracking & Online System Identification
András Sasfi, Alberto Padoan, Ivan Markovsky, Florian Dörfler

Abstract
This paper introduces an online approach for identifying time-varying subspaces defined by linear dynamical systems. The approach of representing linear systems by non-parametric subspace models has received significant interest in the field of data-driven control recently. This system representation enables us to provide rigorous guarantees for linear time-varying systems, which are difficult to obtain for parametric system models. The proposed method leverages optimization on the Grassmann manifold leading to the Grassmannian Recursive Algorithm for Tracking (GREAT) . We view subspaces as points on the Grassmann manifold and adapt the estimate based on online data by performing optimization on the manifold. At each time step, a single measurement from the current subspace corrupted by a bounded error is available. The subspace estimate is updated online using Grassmannian gradient descent on a cost function incorporating a window of the most recent data. Under suitable assumptions on the signal-to-noise ratio of the online data and the subspace’s rate of change, we establish theoretical guarantees for the resulting algorithm. More specifically, we prove an exponential convergence rate and provide an uncertainty quantification of the estimates in terms of an upper bound on their distance to the true subspace. The applicability of the proposed algorithm is demonstrated by means of numerical examples.

Given the literature’s focus on signal processing, an important objective of the available subspace tracking methods is compressing large amounts of data.
Therefore, the dimension of the embedding space is typically large, while the subspace is lower dimensional.
Most of the available algorithms are tailored to reduce the computation complexity by exploiting this property.
However, we focus on subspace representations of deterministic linear dynamical systems

For these systems, both the embedding space and the subspace are typically of moderate and comparable dimension.
Moreover, existing methods rely on assumptions of independence among samples, which do not hold in the case of dynamical systems where time series are inherently correlated.
Thus, these methods are inherently unsuitable for online system identification.

To address these issues, we adopt a geometric approach and view subspaces as points on the Grassmann manifold.
Tracking can be posed as an unconstrained optimization problem on the manifold

Contrary to parametric system identification methods, we optimize over non-parametric subspace models that uniquely define a dynamical system.
These models are coordinate-free, as they are independent of a basis.
By optimizing over subspaces directly in a coordinate-free manner, we obtain an unconstrained problem, avoiding non-convex orthogonality constraints that arise when explicitly optimizing over bases.

The contributions of our work are the following.
We introduce the
Grassmannian Recursive Algorithm for Tracking
(GREAT)

LTV

The method minimizes the projection error of an online data window onto the estimated subspace through gradient descent on the Grassmann manifold.
Assuming that the subspace’s rate of change is bounded, we provide finite-sample convergence certificates for the algorithm, in the form of guaranteed convergence rate and bias.
The derived bound explicitly depends on the subspace’s rate of change, the bound on the measurement error, and the persistency of excitation property of the online data.
In the special case of
LTI
systems and exact measurements, the estimates converge to the true subspace exponentially fast.

The applicability of the
GREAT
algorithm is demonstrated on a numerical example, in which an
LTV

We illustrate the predictive performance of the model obtained by our algorithm compared to two popular system identification methods.
First, we show that the
GREAT
algorithm performs similarly to the well-known subspace identification method N4SID
[
37
]
recomputed at each time step to allow for adaptation.
Second, we benchmark it against a standard online identification method, that estimates a parametric model via the recursive least squares technique
[
38
]

While the two methods achieve similar prediction error nominally, the proposed gradient-based method exhibits better robustness properties against large measurement errors.
Furthermore, we also compare our method to existing subspace tracking algorithms from the signal processing literature
[
27
,
25
]
.
Finally, we study the conservatism of the theoretical bounds on a synthetic example.

II-B

Subspaces

Throughout the paper, we denote subspaces by bold capital letters.
Any orthonormal matrix whose columns span the subspace is denoted by the same capital letter in regular font.
Consider a subspace
\mathbf{U}
of dimension
d

The orthogonal projection onto

Note that the projections are independent of the subspace representation

Note also that
P_{\mathbf{U}}=P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\top}=P_{\mathbf{U}}^{2}
and
\|P_{\mathbf{U}}M\|\leq\|M\|
for any matrix

and any matrix norm
\|\cdot\|

[
39
,

]
.

Now consider another subspace
\mathbf{V}
of the same dimension as
\mathbf{U}
, with representation
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},~V^{\top}V=I_{d}
.
The principal angles between
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
are denoted by
0\leq\theta_{1}\leq\dots\leq\theta_{d}\leq\pi/2
, and can be computed using, e.g., the

, see
[
39
, Sec. 6.4.3]
for details.
The following metrics will be used to quantify the distance between two
d
-dimensional subspaces
[
41
,
42
,
33
,
35
]

\displaystyle\text{Chordal:}\;d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}\right)^{1/2}=\left(\mathrm{tr}(P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\perp}P_{\mathbf{V}})\right)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\text{Gap:}\;d_{\infty}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})=\sin\theta_{d}=\|P_{\mathbf{U}}-P_{\mathbf{V}}\|_{2}.

The inequality
d_{\infty}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})\leq d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})\leq\sqrt{d}\cdot d_{\infty}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})
follows from the definition of the metrics immediately. Furthermore, the following property of the gap metric is useful.

For any subspaces

of dimension

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any

, the following relation holds

\displaystyle\|P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}u\|_{2}\leq d_{\infty}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})\cdot\|u\|_{2}.

Proof:

\displaystyle\|(I_{n}-P_{\mathbf{V}})u\|_{2}=\|(P_{\mathbf{U}}-P_{\mathbf{V}})u\|_{2}\leq\|P_{\mathbf{U}}-P_{\mathbf{V}}\|_{2}~\|u\|_{2}.

II-C

Grassmannian geometry

This section recalls some concepts related to the
Grassmann manifold
that are used throughout the paper.
Formal definitions are outside the scope of this paper, and the interested reader is referred to
[
34
]
for a thorough exposition.
Informally, the Grassmann manifold is the set of all subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of a given dimension
d
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)=\left\{\text{subspaces of dimension $d$ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

The tangent space associated to each point on the manifold
\mathbf{U}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
is denoted by
T_{\mathbf{U}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
(see
[
34
, Def. 8.33]
for a formal definition).
The tangent bundle of a manifold is the disjoint union of its tangent spaces, endowed with a smooth manifold structure, defined as

\displaystyle T\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)=\left\{(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})~|~\mathbf{U}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d),\mathbf{V}\in T_{\mathbf{U}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)\right\}.

Points on the Grassmann manifold are abstract objects.
For computation purposes, we represent a point on
\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
by a matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, whose columns are orthonormal and span the corresponding subspace
1
1

1

In fact, the set
\left\{U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}~|~U^{\top}U=I_{d}\right\}
endowed with a differentiable structure is called the Stiefel manifold, and it is an embedded submanifold of the linear space
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. The Grassmannian can be viewed as a quotient manifold of the Stiefel manifold. More details about quotient manifolds and the Grassmannian can be found in
[
34
, Chp. 9]
.
.
Furthermore, for any representation
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d},~U^{\top}U=I_{d}
of a point
\mathbf{U}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
, all tangent vectors
\mathbf{V}\in T_{\mathbf{U}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
admit a representation
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
that satisfies
U^{\top}V=0

[
34
]
.

The Grassmann manifold can be equipped with a Riemannian metric using the Frobenius inner product making it a Riemannian manifold
2
2

2

More precisely, the Frobenius inner product defines a Riemannian metric on the Stiefel manifold, which induces a Riemannian metric on the Grassmann manifold
[
34
, Sec. 9.7]
.
.
Given a differentiable function
f
that maps from
\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
to
\mathbb{R}
, the Riemannian gradient of
f
denoted by
\mathrm{grad}~f
is a vector field on the manifold.
The gradient associates a point
\mathbf{U}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
with a tangent vector
\mathbf{V}\in T_{\mathbf{U}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
, i.e.,
\mathbf{V}=\mathrm{grad}~f(\mathbf{U})
.
A representation of
\mathbf{V}
can be calculated as
[
34
, Sec. 9.16]

\displaystyle V=P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\perp}\nabla\bar{f}(U),

(1)

where
\bar{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
is such that
\bar{f}(U)=f(\mathbf{U})
for any orthonormal matrix representation
U
spanning the subspace
\mathbf{U}
, and
\nabla\bar{f}(U)
is the Euclidean gradient of
\bar{f}
evaluated at
U
.

In manifold optimization, various maps (called retractions) are used to move along the manifold in tangent directions.
In this work, we use the
exponential map
due to its desirable theoretical properties.
The exponential map
\mathrm{Exp}_{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{V})
maps an element of the tangent bundle
(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})\in T\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
to another element of the manifold
\mathbf{U}^{+}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
, i.e.,
\mathbf{U}^{+}=\mathrm{Exp}_{\mathbf{U}}(\mathbf{V})
.
Consider a representation
(U,V)
of
(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})
,
and let
V=Q_{1}SQ_{2}^{\top}
denote the compact
SVD
, where
Q_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
Q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and
S=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
\sigma_{i}
denoting the
i
-th singular value of
V
.
Note that
Q_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
only contains columns corresponding to the non-zero singular values.
Then, a representation of
\mathbf{U}^{+}
can be computed using the formula
[
33
, Eq. (2.65)]

\displaystyle U^{+}=[UQ_{2}~Q_{1}]\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(\cos(\sigma_{1}),\cos(\sigma_{2}),\dots,\cos(\sigma_{d}))\\
\mathrm{diag}(\sin(\sigma_{1}),\cos(\sigma_{2}),\dots,\cos(\sigma_{d}))\end{bmatrix}Q_{2}^{\top}.

In the remainder of the paper, with a slight abuse of notation, we provide formulas for points in
\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
and
T_{\mathbf{U}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
using an arbitrary orthonormal matrix representation of them.
However, this does not undermine consistency, since the quantities we work with, such as distances or projections, are coordinate-free notions defined for subspaces, not their representations.
Consequently, these quantities are invariant under the choice of representation.

Problem setup

LTV

III-A

Problem formulation

We consider the problem of recursively estimating an unknown and time-varying subspace
\mathbf{U}_{t}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
based on possibly noisy data, collected online.
We assume that a sample
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is available at each time, consisting of a nominal part
\bar{u}_{t}
from
\mathbf{U}_{t}
corrupted by some measurement error
e_{t}
. The measurement error is assumed to be bounded, as formalized below.

Assumption 1

Each sample
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle u_{t}=\bar{u}_{t}+e_{t},

with
\bar{u}_{t}\in\mathbf{U}_{t}
and
\|e_{t}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

Note that we do not make any (probabilistic) assumption on the distribution of the noise
e_{t}
, which is often unknown.
Instead, we use the upper bound
\epsilon
from Assumption
1
to provide worst-case (deterministic) guarantees for the estimate obtained by the algorithm proposed in the subsequent section.

In addition, we assume an upper bound on the subspace’s temporal variability.

Assumption 2

There exists some constant
c\geq 0
such that
d_{2}(\mathbf{U}_{t},\mathbf{U}_{t+1})\leq c
holds for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

In case
c=0
, the subspace is time-invariant, i.e.,
U_{t+1}=U_{t}
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
.

Our estimate of the subspace at time
t\in\mathbb{N}
is denoted by
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
.
The uncertainty of this estimate is quantified by an upper bound on its distance to the true subspace
\mathbf{U}_{t}
.
Therefore, uncertainty sets are in the form of metric balls of radius
r
centered around some subspace
\mathbf{U}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{r}(\mathbf{U}):=\left\{\hat{\mathbf{U}}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)~|~d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\hat{\mathbf{U}})\leq r\right\}.

The problem of interest can now be formulated as follows.

Problem 1

Given an initial estimate
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t_{0}}
at time
t_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\mathbf{U}_{t_{0}}\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{0}}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t_{0}})
for some
r_{0}
and data
u_{t}
,
t\in\mathbb{N}
, find an iterative algorithm that

•

provides
uncertainty quantification
in terms of an invariant tube, that is
\mathbf{U}_{t}\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{t}}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t})
for all
t>t_{0}
,

•

yields
diminishing bias
, that is,
\lim_{t\to\infty}r_{t}=0
if
c=0
and
\epsilon=0
, and

•

is
recursive
, that is,
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t+1}
depends only on
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t}
and the past length-
T
data window
u_{\tau},~\tau\in\{t-T+1,\dots,\\
t-1,t\}
for some
1\leq T\leq t_{0}+1
.

The uncertainty set is called an
invariant tube
, as the sequence of metric balls
\{\mathbb{B}_{r_{t}}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t})\}_{t>t_{0}}
defines a tubular neighborhood around the estimates, in which the true subspaces are guaranteed to remain for all times
t>t_{0}
.
In this work, we focus on methods that adapt the estimate based on a window of most recent data to simplify the analysis.
However, achieving adaptation by introducing a forgetting factor that discounts the past data is also possible in the proposed framework, see Remark
9
in Section
IV-C
.

In case the input is persistently exciting, the block Hankel matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{H}_{L+1}(v)\\
\mathcal{H}_{L+1}(y)\end{bmatrix}
spans the behavior
\mathcal{B}

[
5
]
.
However, the system may be time-varying in our setup, and therefore, the restricted behavior does not attain a direct data representation.

In light of the above, online identification of
LTV
systems in the behavioral setting is equivalent to tracking the time-varying subspace
\mathcal{B}_{[t,t+L]}
, and thus, it can be formulated as Problem
1
.
At each time
t
, a sample
u_{t}=[v_{[t-L,t]}^{\top}~y_{[t-L,t]}^{\top}]^{\top}
from the subspace
\mathbf{U}_{t}=\mathcal{B}_{[t-L,t]}
can be constructed from the most recent input-output measurements.
Note that
\mathcal{B}_{[t-L,t]}
is a special time-varying subspace that represents the restricted behavior of an LTV system.
However, we state the main results of our work in Section
IV
for general time-varying subspaces.

The proposed problem formulation allows us to handle additive measurement error on the inputs and outputs, c.f., Assumption
1
.
This error-in-variables formulation is used in many domains, such as identifying electrical circuits
[
44
]
or financial models
[
45
]
.
Considering this setup is also natural in the behavioral framework, since inputs and outputs are not distinguished in this setting
[
9
]
.
However, the input is often known exactly in the context of control for example.
The upper bound on the distance between two consecutive subspaces in Assumption
2
naturally quantifies how fast the behavior changes.
On the other hand, characterizing the time-variation of the system by the change in
A_{t},B_{t},C_{t}
is problematic, as these parameters are basis dependent.
Nevertheless, if the matrices
A_{t},B_{t},C_{t}
and
D_{t}
vary slowly with time, the change in the matrix on the right-hand side of equation (
3
) is small, and hence,
d_{2}(\mathcal{B}_{[t,t+L]},\mathcal{B}_{[t+1,t+L+1]})
can be bounded (c.f.,
[
46
]
).
The uncertainty characterization of the identified behavior is useful for various downstream tasks, such as prediction, estimation, or control.
For example, it can be used to quantify the uncertainty of predicted trajectories, or to robustify control formulations.
Besides describing time-varying systems,
LTV
models are also often used to approximate the behavior of nonlinear systems
[
14
,
15
]
.

Even though the state-space representation (
2
) can be related to the restricted behavior through the equation (
3
), we do not aim at recovering the matrices
A_{t},B_{t},C_{t}
and
D_{t}
to form an estimate of
\mathcal{B}_{[t,t+L]}
.
Instead, we use the subspace estimate and its uncertainty quantification directly to perform downstream tasks, such as prediction, which is described below and illustrated in Section
V-B
.
We divide the input-output trajectories into initial and future parts of length
T_{\mathrm{ini}}
and
T_{\mathrm{fut}}
, respectively, i.e.,
v_{[t-T_{\mathrm{ini}}+1,t+T_{\mathrm{fut}}]}=[v_{\mathrm{ini}}^{\top}~v_{\mathrm{fut}}^{\top}]^{\top}
and
y_{[t-T_{\mathrm{ini}}+1,t+T_{\mathrm{fut}}]}=[y_{\mathrm{ini}}^{\top}~y_{\mathrm{fut}}^{\top}]^{\top}
.
Let
\hat{U}
be a basis for the estimated behavior
\mathcal{B}_{[t-T_{\mathrm{ini}}+1,t+T_{\mathrm{fut}}]}
.
We denote the block-rows of
\hat{U}
corresponding to each trajectory component by
\hat{U}^{v_{\mathrm{ini}}},\hat{U}^{y_{\mathrm{ini}}},\hat{U}^{v_{\mathrm{fut}}}
, and
\hat{U}^{y_{\mathrm{fut}}}
.
Given
v_{\mathrm{ini}},y_{\mathrm{ini}},v_{\mathrm{fut}}
, the future outputs at times
t+1,\dots,t+T_{\mathrm{fut}}
are predicted as
\hat{y}_{\mathrm{fut}}=M[v_{\mathrm{ini}}^{\top}~y_{\mathrm{ini}}^{\top}~v_{\mathrm{fut}}^{\top}]^{\top}
, with
M
defined as

\displaystyle M:=\hat{U}^{y_{\mathrm{fut}}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{U}^{v_{\mathrm{ini}}}\\
\hat{U}^{y_{\mathrm{ini}}}\\
\hat{U}^{v_{\mathrm{fut}}}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger},

where
\dagger
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.
This linear multi-step predictor originates from the classical and widely adopted Subspace Predictive Control
[
47
,
12
]
.

IV
Subspace tracking with guarantees

Our main results are presented in this section.
In Section
IV-A
, we discuss how the signal in the data can be distinguished from noise.
Then, we introduce the gradient descent method in Section
IV-B
that serves as the main building block of the subspace tracking algorithm proposed in Section
IV-C
.

IV-A

Distinguishing between signal and noise

In this section, we define and analyze the signal and noise components of the online data.
We consider a data window of length
T\geq d
consisting of the most recent samples up to time
t
organized into a matrix

\displaystyle W_{t}=[u_{t-T+1}~u_{t-T+2}~\dots~u_{t}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T},\quad t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq T}.

Our goal is to estimate the current subspace
\mathbf{U}_{t}
using the data matrix
W_{t}
.
Note that in case the samples
u_{t}
are generated by an
LTI
system,
W_{t}
is a block Hankel matrix and its columns span the restricted behavior
\mathcal{B}_{[t,t+L]}
(see Section
III-B
and Remark

However, when the system is time-varying, only the last sample
u_{t}
originates from
\mathbf{U}_{t}
.
Therefore, instead of using the nominal sample
\bar{u}_{t}
and the measurement error

from Assumption

by introducing the following decomposition:

\displaystyle W_{t}=P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}+P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}W_{t}.

The first term lies in
\mathbf{U}_{t}
and is defined to be the signal, while the second term lies in the orthogonal complement of
\mathbf{U}_{t}
and is considered as noise (see Figure

for an illustration).
Under the stated assumptions, the noise term can be bounded as follows. The proof can be found in the Appendix.

To be able to guarantee positive convergence rate for the updates in the subsequent section, we also impose bounds on the singular values of the signal component

Remark 2

Both the noise bound

IV-B

Gradient descent on the Grassmannian

The main idea of our approach is to update a subspace estimate each time a new sample is available by performing
K
iterations of gradient descent on the Grassmann manifold.
Due to the simplicity of the gradient descent update rule, we are able to provide strong theoretical guarantees for the resulting algorithm.
We denote the estimate of the true subspace
\mathbf{U}_{t}
at iteration
k
by
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}
.
Since estimates are points on the Grassmann manifold, the optimization is carried out intrinsically on the manifold as described below.

As discussed in the previous section, the data matrix
W_{t}
serves as a surrogate representation to the true (unknown) subspace
\mathbf{U}_{t}
.
Therefore, we use the projection error of
W_{t}
onto
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}
as the cost for the gradient descent:

\displaystyle F_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}):=\sum_{i=0}^{T-1}\|P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}u_{t-i}\|_{2}^{2}=\|P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}W_{t}\|_{F}^{2}.

(6)

It is worth noting that if
W_{t}=U_{t}
, the cost function reduces to the squared chordal distance, i.e.,
F_{U_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k})=d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}
holds.
Using (
1
), the Riemannian gradient of the cost is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{grad}~F_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k})&=P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}\nabla F_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k})\\
&=P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}\nabla~\mathrm{tr}\left(W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}W_{t}\right)\\
&=-2P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}\Gamma_{k}.\end{split}

(7)

We use the exponential map to take a step of size
\alpha>0
towards the negative gradient direction, yielding the update for
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k+1}
and the formula to compute a representation
\Gamma_{k+1}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k+1}=\mathrm{Exp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}(-\alpha~\mathrm{grad}~F_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k})),\\
&\Gamma_{k+1}=[\Gamma_{k}Q_{2}~Q_{1}]\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(\cos(-\alpha\sigma_{1}),\dots,\cos(-\alpha\sigma_{d}))\\
\mathrm{diag}(\sin(-\alpha\sigma_{1}),\dots,\sin(-\alpha\sigma_{d}))\end{bmatrix}Q_{2}^{\top},\end{split}

(8)

where
\mathrm{grad}~F_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k})=Q_{1}\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{d})Q_{2}^{\top}
is the compact
SVD
.
We use a constant step size for the update to streamline the analysis.
However, the performance of the algorithm can be improved by optimizing the step size online (e.g., via line search), as illustrated in the numerical example in Section
V-B
.

Remark 3

Another subspace tracking algorithm, termed GROUSE
[
27
]
, also relies on gradient descent techniques on the Grassmann manifold.
In contrast to our formulation, the GROUSE algorithm minimizes the projection error of the most recent sample only, and thus, its cost
is a special case of (
6
) with
T=1
.
As a result, the gradient descent step boils down to a rank-one update of the subspace estimate yielding lower computational complexity than that of our algorithm.
In
[
32
]
, the authors analyze the GROUSE algorithm under the crucial assumption that the nominal samples
\bar{u}_{t}
are i.i.d. random variables with support on
\mathbf{U}_{t}
.
This condition allows them to prove convergence of the estimates in expectation.
However, the assumption does not hold if the subspace represents a dynamical system (c.f., Sec.
III-B
), where samples are correlated.
In our setup, on the other hand, a moving data window consisting of
T\geq d
samples is used in the cost.
This enables us to impose Assumption
3
, which can be satisfied by subspaces representing dynamical systems.
Under this assumption, we can guarantee monotonic improvement of the subspace estimates in Lemma
3
.

In
[
29
]
, the GROUSE algorithm was used to perform model reduction for nonlinear dynamical systems online.
The updated subspace estimate is guaranteed to contain the latest sample from the true subspace.
In contrast, by generalizing the GROUSE algorithm, we are able to provide an upper bound on the distance between the true and estimated subspaces in Lemma
3
, which is a much stronger result.
∎

Remark 4

Various subspace tracking algorithms were proposed in
[
25
]
to minimize a cost function similar to (5).
Contrary to our approach, these methods optimize over non-unique matrix representations of the subspace, which are not required to be orthonormal.
For example, the Projection Approximation Subspace Tracking (PAST) algorithm minimizes an approximation of the cost leading to an unconstrained problem that is quadratic in the decision variable.
Another approach proposed in
[
25
]
is to apply gradient descent on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
to minimize the original cost.
An analysis of the PAST algorithm was provided in
[
50
]
, yet the guarantees only hold for time-invariant subspaces and are asymptotic.
In contrast, we optimize the subspace directly by posing the problem on the Grassmann manifold, eliminating the issue of non-uniqueness of matrix representations.
This approach allows us to derive strong, non-asymptotic guarantees for a possibly time-varying reference subspace, which is the main advantage of our formulation compared to those in
[
25
,
50
]
.
∎

IV-C

Tracking of time-varying subspaces

The proposed approach for tracking
\mathbf{U}_{t}
called
Grassmannian Recursive Algorithm for Tracking
(GREAT)
is described by the pseudo-code in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

GREAT

Input:
initial estimate
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t_{0}}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
for some initial time
t_{0}\geq d
, sequence of samples
\{u_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
, window length
T\in\{d,\dots,t_{0}+1\}
, step size
\alpha\in\left(0,\dfrac{\underline{\sigma}^{2}}{2\overline{\sigma}^{4}}\right)
, gradient descent iteration number
K>0

for

t=t_{0}+1,t_{0}+2,\dots

Construct matrix
W_{t}=[u_{t-T+1}~u_{t-T+2}~\dots~u_{t}]

Initialize gradient descent
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0}=\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t-1}

for

k=0,\dots,K-1

Grad. descent step
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k+1}=\mathrm{Exp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}(-\alpha~\mathrm{grad}\,F_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}))

end

Update estimate
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t}=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{K}

end

Output:
sequence of estimates
\{\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\}_{t>t_{0}}

The
GREAT
algorithm recursively updates the subspace estimate
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t}
by performing
K
steps of gradient descent update (
8
) between sampling times
t
and
t+1
.
The initial estimate
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t_{0}}
can be constructed as the span of
d
singular vectors of an initializing data matrix
W_{\mathrm{ini}}=[u_{1}~u_{2}~\dots~u_{t_{0}}]
, corresponding to its largest singular values.
With this choice,
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t_{0}}
becomes the minimizer of the cost
F(\hat{\mathbf{U}})=\|P^{\perp}_{\hat{\mathbf{U}}}W_{\mathrm{ini}}\|_{F}^{2}
(c.f., Eq. (
6
)).
During online operation, the data matrix
W_{t}
is constructed from the most recent samples, and therefore, the subspace estimates adapt to the online data.

Remark 5

The computationally most expensive part of Algorithm
1
is calculating the gradient of the cost in (
7
) at each gradient descent iteration.
For an efficient implementation, one can exploit that the data matrix only appears as an inner product
W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}
(the empirical data covariance) in the expression.
This inner product at time
t
can be formulated as a rank-2 update , i.e.,
W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}=W_{t-1}W_{t-1}^{\top}-u_{t-T}u_{t-T}^{\top}+u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}
.
Therefore, the complexity of the algorithm does not depend on
T
.
With this implementation, the complexity of computing the gradient is
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}d)
, leading to overall complexity
\mathcal{O}(Kn^{2}d)
per sample.
The other expensive part of the algorithm is evaluating the exponential map in equation (
8
), which requires computing the compact
SVD
of the gradient.
However, that step is of complexity
\mathcal{O}(nd^{2})
only, and therefore it is not the bottleneck.
Similarly to Algorithm
1
, various data-driven control formulations depend only on the empirical data covariance, and this fact was exploited for computational reasons, e.g., in
[
51
]
.

Note that there exist computationally more efficient algorithms for subspace tracking in the literature.
For example, the GROUSE
[
27
]
and
Projection Approximation Subspace Tracking (PAST)

[
25
]
algorithms have
\mathcal{O}(nd^{2})
and
\mathcal{O}(nd)
complexity per iteration, respectively.
However, we are able to provide non-asymptotic guarantees for the
GREAT
algorithm, even when the subspace represents a dynamical system (see Remarks
3
and
4
).
∎

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}:=1-4(1-r_{\mathrm{b}}^{2})(\alpha\underline{\sigma}^{2}-2\alpha^{2}\overline{\sigma}^{4})\geq 0.

By contrast, the left-hand side is related to the noise bound

through the

Remark 6

In case only one gradient descent iteration is performed in the inner loop of Algorithm

Remark 8

Inequality (

Remark 9

We note that it is also possible to achieve adaptation by discounting the past data via an exponential forgetting factor
0<\gamma<1
.
In this case, we can utilize all past data by defining the data matrix
W_{t}
as

W_{t}=[\gamma^{t-1}u_{1}~\gamma^{t-2}u_{2}~\dots~\gamma^{0}u_{t}].

Even though the number of columns in

grows over time, the final computations are not affected, since the matrix
W_{t}
only appears as an inner product

5

This product can be computed by a rank-1 update as
W_{t+1}W_{t+1}^{\top}=\gamma^{2}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}+u_{t+1}u_{t+1}^{\top}

Alternatively, one can also combine the two approaches by considering a moving data window with discounted past data.
The theoretical analysis incorporating forgetting factors leads to a different bound in Lemma
2
, which can be derived following similar arguments.
We use a moving data window instead of a forgetting factor in this work, since this approach performed favorably in the online system identification problem considered in Section
V-B

VI
Conclusions

We introduced the
GREAT
algorithm that tracks time-varying subspaces based on gradient descent on the Grassmann manifold.
Under suitable assumptions on the online data, the uncertainty in the resulting estimates is quantified by an upper bound on their distance to the true subspace.
Furthermore, we guarantee exponential convergence to a stationary reference subspace in case of noise-free data.
In contrast to the existing literature, we are able to provide non-asymptotic guarantees for the
GREAT
algorithm, even when the subspace represents a dynamical system.
Hence, the proposed scheme is suitable for online identification of linear time-varying dynamical systems, which is also demonstrated in one of the numerical examples.

Future work includes developing an adaptive data-driven control framework using the online estimated subspace (c.f., Subspace Predictive Control
[
47
]
).
Furthermore, the uncertainty quantification of the estimate can be incorporated to robustify the resulting control formulation.
Another possible application of the proposed algorithm is fault detection based on the change in system behavior represented by the subspace.
Finally, extensions of the
GREAT
algorithm allowing for the adaptation of the subspace dimension could be explored
[
57
]
.

\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})^{2}
is Lipschitz continuous in the sense of
[
34
, Def. 10.40]
.

Lemma 5

The function
\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})^{2}
on the Grassmann manifold is
L
-Lipschitz continuous in the first argument in the sense of
[
34
, Def. 10.40]
with
L=4
.

Proof:

Note that by
[
34
, Cor. 10.47]
the gradient of a twice differentiable function is
L
-Lipschitz continuous if and only if its Hessian has operator norm bounded by
L
.
The Riemannian Hessian of
d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(U^{\top}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}U)
with respect to the first argument is given as (see
[
34
, Example 9.49, Eq. (9.69)]
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hess}~d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})[X]

\displaystyle=2P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\perp}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}X-2XU^{\top}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}U.

Note that we use the Frobenius inner product, and hence, the operator norm is defined using the Frobenius norm as

\displaystyle\|

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hess}~d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})\|=\max_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X\in T_{\mathbf{U}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)}}{{\|X\|_{F}=1}}}\|\mathrm{Hess}~d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})[X]\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\max_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X\in T_{\mathbf{U}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)}}{{\|X\|_{F}=1}}}\|2P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\perp}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}X-2XU^{\top}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}U\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X\in T_{\mathbf{U}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)}}{{\|X\|_{F}=1}}}\|2P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\perp}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}X\|_{F}+\|2XU^{\top}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}U\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X\in T_{\mathbf{U}}\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)}}{{\|X\|_{F}=1}}}2\|P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\perp}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}\|_{2}~\|X\|_{F}+2\|U^{\top}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}U\|_{2}~\|X\|_{F}

\displaystyle=2\|P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\perp}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}\|_{2}+2\|U^{\top}P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}U\|_{2}\leq 2+2\|P_{\mathbf{V}}^{\perp}U\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2+2\|U\|^{2}_{2}=4.

∎

In the following, we provide the proof of Lemma
3
using the result in Lemma
5
.

Proof:

We first exploit the Lipschitzness of
\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})^{2}
, and separate it into a nominal and a noise term in Part I.
The nominal part of the gradient contains the signal
P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}
only, and it is analyzed in Part II.
We bound all terms in the gradient that are affected by the noise
P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}W_{t}
in Part III.

Part I

By Lemma
5
, the Riemannian gradient of the squared chordal distance is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L=4
. Therefore, by
[
34
, Cor. 10.54]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k+1},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}&-d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\\
\leq&-\alpha\langle\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2},\mathrm{grad}~F_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k})\rangle_{F}\\
&+2\alpha^{2}\|\mathrm{grad}~F_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k})\|_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

(14)

The matrix
W_{t}
can be written as
W_{t}=P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}+P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}W_{t}
. Similarly, we decompose the gradient
\mathrm{grad}~F_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k})
as
\mathrm{grad}~F_{W_{t}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k})=\bar{G}_{t}+N_{t}
, with

\displaystyle\bar{G}_{t}:=

\displaystyle-2P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}\hat{U},

\displaystyle N_{t}:=

\displaystyle-2P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}\left(P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}+P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}\right)\hat{U}.

With this partitioning, (
14
) becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{2}&(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k+1},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}-d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\\
\leq&-\alpha\left(\langle\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2},\bar{G}_{t}\rangle_{F}+\langle\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2},N_{t}\rangle_{F}\right)\\
&+2\alpha^{2}\left(\|\bar{G}_{t}\|_{F}^{2}+2\langle\bar{G}_{t},N_{t}\rangle_{F}+\|N_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(15)

Part II
First, we analyze the nominal part of inequality (
15
). The gradient of the squared chordal distance on the Grassmann manifold with respect to
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}
is
\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}=-2P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}\hat{U}.
Furthermore, consider the compact
SVD

U_{t}^{\top}W_{t}=Q_{1}\Sigma Q_{2}^{\top}
. Then

\displaystyle\langle

\displaystyle\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2},\bar{G}_{t}\rangle_{F}

\displaystyle=4\mathrm{tr}(\hat{U}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}\hat{U})

\displaystyle=4\mathrm{tr}(U_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}U_{t}^{\top}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}U_{t})

\displaystyle=4\mathrm{tr}(Q_{1}^{\top}U_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}Q_{1}\Sigma^{2})

\displaystyle\geq\underline{\sigma}^{2}4\mathrm{tr}(Q_{1}^{\top}U_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}Q_{1})

\displaystyle=\underline{\sigma}^{2}4\mathrm{tr}(\hat{U}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}\hat{U})=\underline{\sigma}^{2}\|\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\|_{F}^{2},

where we used the fact that
\Sigma\geq\underline{\sigma}I_{d}
by Assumption
3
, and

\displaystyle Q_{1}^{\top}U_{t}^{\top}

\displaystyle P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}Q_{1}

\displaystyle=Q_{1}^{\top}\left(U_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}-U_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}\right)Q_{1}

\displaystyle=Q_{1}^{\top}\left(U_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}IP_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}-U_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}\right)Q_{1}

\displaystyle=Q_{1}^{\top}U_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}Q_{1}

\displaystyle=Q_{1}^{\top}U_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}Q_{1}\geq 0,

due to the properties of projection matrices.
Similarly, the following upper bound holds

\displaystyle\|\bar{G}_{t}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=4\mathrm{tr}(\hat{U}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}\hat{U})

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\sigma}^{2}4\mathrm{tr}(Q_{1}^{\top}U_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}Q_{1})

\displaystyle=\overline{\sigma}^{2}4\mathrm{tr}(U_{t}^{\top}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t}U_{t}^{\top}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}U_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\sigma}^{4}4\mathrm{tr}(U_{t}^{\top}P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}U_{t})

\displaystyle=\overline{\sigma}^{4}\|\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\|_{F}^{2}.

Part III
Now, we bound the terms containing
N_{t}
. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle|\langle\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2},N_{t}\rangle_{F}|\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\|_{F}\|N_{t}\|_{F},

\displaystyle|\langle\bar{G}_{t},N_{t}\rangle_{F}|\leq

\displaystyle\|\bar{G}_{t}\|_{F}\|N_{t}\|_{F}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|2P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}\hat{U}\|_{F}\leq\|2P_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}}^{\perp}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}\|_{F}\leq 2r,

\displaystyle\|\bar{G}_{t}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\sigma}^{2}\|\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\|_{F}\leq 2r\overline{\sigma}^{2}.

Combining the results of Part II and III with (
15
) yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{2}&(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k+1},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}-d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\\
\leq&\left(-\alpha\underline{\sigma}^{2}+2\alpha^{2}\overline{\sigma}^{4}\right)\|\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\|_{F}^{2}\\
&+2\alpha r\|N_{t}\|_{F}+8\alpha^{2}r\overline{\sigma}^{2}\|N_{t}\|_{F}+2\alpha^{2}\|N_{t}\|_{F}^{2}.\end{split}

Finally, the triangle inequality and Lemma
2
lead to

\displaystyle\|N_{t}\|_{F}\leq

\displaystyle 2\|P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}+P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}

\displaystyle+P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}\|_{F}+4\|P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}W_{t}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}W_{t}\|_{F}^{2}+4\|P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}W_{t}\|_{2}~\|P_{\mathbf{U}_{t}}^{\perp}W_{t}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\delta_{t}^{2}+4\overline{\sigma}\delta_{t}.

Together these yield the right-hand side bound in Lemma
3
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4

Proof:

For any
\mathbf{U},\tilde{\mathbf{U}}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,d)
such that
\tilde{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\mathbf{U})
, the squared chordal distance satisfies the gradient dominance property

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\mathrm{grad}~d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\tilde{\mathbf{U}})^{2}\|_{F}^{2}&=4\mathrm{tr}(P_{\mathbf{U}}P_{\tilde{\mathbf{U}}}P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\perp}P_{\tilde{\mathbf{U}}})\\
&=4\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{U}^{\top}UU^{\top}\tilde{U}\tilde{U}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\perp}\tilde{U})\\
&\geq 4\min_{i}\cos^{2}(\theta_{i})\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{U}^{\top}P_{\mathbf{U}}^{\perp}\tilde{U})\\
&=4\min_{i}(1-\sin^{2}(\theta_{i}))d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\tilde{\mathbf{U}})^{2}\\
&\geq 4(1-r_{\mathrm{b}}^{2})d_{2}(\mathbf{U},\tilde{\mathbf{U}})^{2},\end{split}

(16)

where
\theta_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,d
are the principal angles between
\mathbf{U}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{U}}
. Therefore, it is sufficient to show that
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\mathbf{U}_{t})
holds for all
t\geq T
and
k=0,\dots,K
.

First, we show by induction that the set
\mathbb{B}_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\mathbf{U}_{t})
is forward invariant under the gradient descent update (
8
). For a fixed
t\geq T
, assume that
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\mathbf{U}_{t})
for some
k=0,\dots,K-1
.
Note that with the choice of
\alpha
in Algorithm
1
, we have
\rho>0
, and hence,
\tilde{\rho}<1
.
Therefore, we can combine the results of Lemma
3
with
r=r_{\mathrm{b}}
, and inequality (
16
) yielding

\displaystyle d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k+1},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}+\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\delta_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}r_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}+\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\delta_{t}).

Furthermore, Assumption
4
implies

\displaystyle\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\delta_{t})\leq(1-\tilde{\rho})r_{\mathrm{b}}^{2},

and therefore,
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\mathbf{U}_{t})
holds.

Next, we show that for any
t\geq T
,
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t-1}=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0}\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\mathbf{U}_{t})
implies
\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t}=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{K}\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\mathbf{U}_{t+1})
, as illustrated in Figure
3
.
Due to the invariance of
\mathbb{B}_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\mathbf{U}_{t})
under the gradient descent update (
8
),
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{k}\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\mathbf{U}_{t})
holds at time
t-1
for all
k=0,\dots,K
, and hence, inequality (
16
) applies.
We apply the bound in Lemma
3
recursively to get

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{K},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\leq&\tilde{\rho}^{K}d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\tilde{\rho}^{k}\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\delta_{t})\\
\leq&\tilde{\rho}^{K}r_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}+\frac{1-\tilde{\rho}^{K}}{1-\tilde{\rho}}\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\delta_{t})\leq(r_{\mathrm{b}}-c)^{2},\end{split}

(17)

where we used Assumption
4
in the last step. Furthermore, due to the triangle inequality and Assumption
2
, we have

\displaystyle d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{K},\mathbf{U}_{t+1})\leq d_{2}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{K},\mathbf{U}_{t})+d_{2}(\mathbf{U}_{t},\mathbf{U}_{t+1})\leq r_{\mathrm{b}},

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1

Proof:

From the proof of Lemma
4
, we have

\displaystyle d_{2}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\leq\tilde{\rho}^{K}d_{2}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t-1},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}+\frac{1-\tilde{\rho}^{K}}{1-\tilde{\rho}}\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\delta_{t}).

Therefore, the following bound holds for any
t\geq T

\displaystyle d_{2}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}^{K}d_{2}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t-1},\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}+\frac{1-\tilde{\rho}^{K}}{1-\tilde{\rho}}\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\|\delta\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}^{K}(d_{2}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t-1},\mathbf{U}_{t-1})+c)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1-\tilde{\rho}^{K}}{1-\tilde{\rho}}\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\|\delta\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}^{K}d_{2}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t-1},\mathbf{U}_{t-1})^{2}+\tilde{\rho}^{K}(2r_{\mathrm{b}}-c)c

\displaystyle+\frac{1-\tilde{\rho}^{K}}{1-\tilde{\rho}}\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\|\delta\|_{\infty}),

where we used the facts that
d_{2}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t-1},\mathbf{U}_{t-1})\leq r_{\mathrm{b}}-c
from inequality (
17
), and
\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\delta_{t})\leq\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\|\delta\|_{\infty})
for any
t\geq T
.
We apply the above inequality recursively leading to

\displaystyle d_{2}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{t},

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{t})^{2}\leq\tilde{\rho}^{Kt}d_{2}(\hat{\mathbf{U}}_{0},\mathbf{U}_{0})^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}(\tilde{\rho}^{K})^{\tau}\left(\frac{1-\tilde{\rho}^{K}}{1-\tilde{\rho}}\gamma_{r_{\mathrm{b}}}(\|\delta\|_{\infty})+\tilde{\rho}^{K}(2r_{\mathrm{b}}-c)c\right).

Substituting the solution of the geometric series completes the proof.
∎
