Fermi’s golden rule in tunneling models with quantum waveguides perturbed by Kato class measures
Sylwia Kondej, Kacper Ślipko

Abstract
In this paper we consider two dimensional quantum system with an infinite waveguide of the width d d and a transversally invariant profile. Furthermore, we assume that at a distant ρ \rho there is a perturbation defined by the Kato measure. We show that, under certain conditions, the resolvent of the Hamiltonian has the second sheet pole which reproduces the resonance at z ⁡ ( ρ ) z(\rho) with the asymptotics z ⁡ ( ρ ) = ℰ β ; n + 𝒪 ⁡ ( exp ⁡ ( − 2 ​ | ℰ β ; n | ​ ρ ) ρ ) z(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\exp(-\sqrt{2|\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}|}\rho)}{\rho}\Big) for ρ \rho large and with the resonant energy ℰ β ; n \mathcal{E}_{\beta;n} . Moreover, we show that the imaginary component of z ⁡ ( ρ ) z(\rho) satisfies Fermi’s golden rule which we explicitly derive.



Keywords:
Resonances in quantum system, tunnelling, resolvent poles, Fermi’s golden rule.

Main results of the paper.
The main result of this paper can be formulated as follows.

•

From the physical point of view, the imaginary component of pole plays the main rule in the time evolution of metastable state.
Fermi’s golden rule plays an essential role in physics.
It describes the rate of transition from an energy eigenstate of a quantum system to an energy continuum as an effect of a weak perturbation.
The corresponding formula was, first time, obtained by Dirac
[
9
]
and then, after more than twenty years, it was analyzed and called as ‘golden rule’ by Fermi
[
20

. From the physical perspective, distantly located trap allows for controlling scattering processes and quantum transport in the waveguide, influencing tunneling and dissipative processes. The distance between the trap and the waveguide can be adjusted to impact the system dynamics.
Studying these decay mechanisms, especially through explicit derivations like Fermi’s golden rule, is crucial for optimizing such systems. This provides insights into key components of quantum technology
such as wires and waveguides, and facilitates the optimization of related processes.

Description of simplified model: influence of distant well on a straight waveguide

In this section, we consider a two dimensional quantum mechanical model with the potential supported on a straight, infinite planar waveguide and a distant well potential located on a compact set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the smooth boundaries
\partial\Omega
. We assume that the waveguide has a width
d
, therefore it can be defined as follows

\Sigma:=\{x=(x_{1},x_{2})\,:\,x_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\,x_{2}\in[0,d]\}\,.

(2.1)

Furthermore, we assume that
\Omega\cap\Sigma=\emptyset
and denote the distance

\rho:=\min_{x\in\Sigma,y\in\Omega}|x-y|\,.

(2.2)

Due to the orientation of
\Sigma
we can also write
\rho=\min_{x\in\Sigma,y\in\Omega}|x_{2}-y_{2}|
.
The parameter
\rho
will play the essential role in the further discussion.

Let
\alpha
and

be positive numbers, and using them, we define the Hamiltonian

(2.3)

where the Sobolev space
W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,,\,i,j\in\{1,2\}\}
determines the maximal domain. In fact, the above Hamiltonian depends on
\Sigma
and
\Omega
as well, however, to avoid over-indexing, we do not involve
\Sigma
and
\Omega
into the notation for the Hamiltonian.
H_{\alpha,\beta}
defined by (
2.3
) determines a particular example of (
1.1
).

(2.5)

(2.6)

2.1.1
Resolvent of
H_{\alpha,0}

In this section we are going to derive the resolvent of
H_{\alpha,0}
which will be the basic tool in the analysis of resonances.
Let us recall that the ’free’ resolvent is given by
R(z)=(-\Delta-z)^{-1}
, and it can be represented as an integral operator with a kernel defined by the Macdonald function
K_{0}(\cdot)
, also known as the modified Bessel function of the second kind.
2
2

2

Alternatively, the kernel of
R(z)
can be expressed using the Hankel function as
\frac{i}{4}H_{0}(\sqrt{z}|\cdot-\cdot|)
. Specifically:

R(z)f=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{0}(k_{z}|\cdot-y|)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\quad f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

(2.9)

where
k_{z}=-i\sqrt{z}
with
\Im\sqrt{z}>0
and the kernel of
R(z)
defines the Green’s function of the operator
-\Delta-z
.

In the present case, when the potential is defined by a negative coupling constant
-\alpha
, the Birman–Schwinger operator is given by
-\alpha\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}\,:\,L^{2}(\Sigma)\to L^{2}(\Sigma)\,.
Consequently, for

living in the resolvent set
\varrho(H_{\alpha,0})
the operator

\mathrm{I}-\alpha\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}\,:\,L^{2}(\Sigma)\to L^{2}(\Sigma)

(2.10)

is invertible.
Using the Birman–Schwinger concept we represent the resolvent
R_{\alpha,0}(z)=(H_{\alpha,0}-z)^{-1}
as

R_{\alpha,0}(z)=R(z)+\alpha R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}[\mathrm{I}-\alpha\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}]^{-1}\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\,,

(2.11)

cf.
[
6

7

23

36
]
.

To proceed further we are going to present the resolvent
R_{\alpha,0}(z)
in an alternative form that reveals transverse symmetry and reflexes the decomposition (
2.7
); a similar construction has been carried out in
[
15

26
]
. Note that the spectral family (equivalently, the resolution of the identity) associated to the operator

P_{\alpha;n}=(\phi_{\alpha;n},\cdot)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\phi_{\alpha;n}

\mathrm{h}_{\alpha}

Note that the longitudinal component of
H_{\alpha,0}

-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{1}^{2}}\otimes\mathrm{I}

E_{0}(\lambda)f=\frac{1}{(2\pi)}\int_{|\xi|^{2}\leq\lambda}f(\xi)e^{ix\cdot\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

(2.12)

By means of the introduced notation we can write

H_{\alpha,0}\,(\mathrm{I}\otimes P_{\alpha;n})=\int_{[0,\infty)}(\lambda+E_{\alpha;n})\,\mathrm{d}E_{0}(\lambda)\otimes P_{\alpha;n}\,.

(2.13)

This, consequently, allows to define components
of
R_{\alpha,0}(z)

related to the projection onto

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\otimes P_{\alpha}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, where
P_{\alpha}=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}}P_{\alpha,n}
, and

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\otimes P_{\alpha}^{\bot}L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, where
P_{\alpha}^{\bot}
stands for the orthogonal complement of

.
Precisely,

R^{d}_{\alpha,0}(z):=R_{\alpha,0}(z)\,(\,\mathrm{I}\otimes P_{\alpha}\,)=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}}\int_{[0,\infty)}\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{0}(\lambda)}{\lambda+E_{\alpha;n}-z}\otimes P_{\alpha;n}\,

(2.14)

R^{c}_{\alpha,0}(z):=R_{\alpha,0}(z)\,(\,\mathrm{I}\otimes P_{\alpha}^{\bot}\,=\int_{[0,\infty)}\int_{[0,\infty)}\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{0}(\lambda)\otimes\mathrm{d}E_{\alpha}(\lambda^{\prime})}{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}-z}\,.

(2.15)

Note that
E_{0}(\cdot)
can be also expanded by means of the plane waves
(2\pi)^{-1/2}\mathrm{e}^{ix_{1}p_{1}}
which yields

\Big(-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{1}^{2}}+E_{\alpha;n}-z\Big)^{-1}f(x_{1})=\int_{[0,\infty)}\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{0}(\lambda)}{\lambda+E_{\alpha;n}-z}f(x_{1})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{ip_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})}}{p_{1}^{2}+E_{\alpha;n}-z}f(y_{1})\,\mathrm{d}p_{1}\,\mathrm{d}y_{1}\,.

(2.16)

Summarizing, we get the decomposition

R_{0,\beta}(z)=R(z)+\beta R(z)\chi_{\Omega}(\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega})^{-1}\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\,,\quad z\in\varrho(H_{0,\beta})\,.

(2.23)

The Birman–Schwinger principle allows to associate zeros of
\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}\,:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
and eigenfunctions
\omega_{\beta;n}
of
H_{0,\beta}
in the following way

\omega_{\beta;n}=R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}w_{\beta;n}\,,\quad\mathrm{where}\quad w_{\beta;n}\in\ker[\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}]\,\quad\text{and}\quad n\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}\,,

(2.24)

cf.
[
6
,
7
,
23
,
32
]
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denote a sufficiently small neighbourhood of
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}
. With a view to further discussion we prove the following statement.

Lemma 2.1

Assume that
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}
is a simple discrete eigenvalue of
H_{0,\beta}
and
z\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\setminus\{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}\}
. Then
the operator
(\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega})^{-1}\,:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
admits the representation

(\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-z}\mathrm{P}_{\beta;n}+A_{n}(z)\,,

(2.25)

where
\mathrm{P}_{\beta;n}=(w_{\beta;n}\,,\cdot)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}w_{\beta;n}
and
A_{n}(z)
is analytic in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

Proof.
Note that
R_{0,\beta}(z)
is the resolvent of a self adjoint operator
with isolated spectral point at
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}
with corresponding eigenvector
\omega_{\beta;n}
and eigenprojector
P_{\beta;n}
.
Thus, for
z\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\setminus\{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}\}
we can write

R_{0,\beta}(z)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-z}P_{\beta;n}+B_{n}(z)\,:\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,,

(2.26)

where
B_{n}(z)
acts in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and it is analytic in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
; the above follows from the spectral resolution of the
R_{0,\beta}(z)
, cf.
[
24
, V-(3.21)]
. The singularities coming from the remaining isolated points contribute to
B_{n}(z)
.
On the other hand, since
(\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega})^{-1}
has the same pole of the multiplicity one, it can be decomposed as

(\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-z}\tilde{P}_{n}(z)+\tilde{B}_{n}(z)\,:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)\,,

(2.27)

where
\tilde{B}_{n}(z)
stands for an analytic component and
\tilde{P}_{n}(z)
is a projector acting in the space
L^{2}(\Omega)
, cf.
[
21
]
. Comparing the above with (
2.26
) and taking into account the resolvent formula (
2.23
),
we can write

P_{\beta;n}=\beta R(z)\chi_{\Omega}\tilde{P}_{n}(z)\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\,.

(2.28)

Furthermore, using the analyticity of the resolvent
R(z)
for
z\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, i.e.

R(z)=R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})+(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-z)R(z)R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\,

(2.29)

we obtain from (
2.23
) that
\beta R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}\tilde{P}_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})=P_{\beta;n}+\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{op.u.}}(z-\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})
, where the error term is understood in the norm operator sense.
Implementing
\mathrm{P}_{\beta;n}=(w_{\beta;n}\,,\cdot)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}w_{\beta;n}
which, by definition of
\omega_{\beta;n}
and
w_{\beta,n}
(see (
2.24
)) satisfies

P_{\beta;n}=R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\mathrm{P}_{\beta;n}R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})
, we state that
\tilde{P}_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})=\beta^{-1}\mathrm{P}_{\beta;n}
and
eq. (
2.27
) turns to

(\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{1}{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-z}\mathrm{P}_{\beta;n}+A_{n}(z)\,,

(2.30)

where
A_{n}(z)
acting in
L^{2}(\Omega)
is analytic in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. This completes the proof.

3
Resonances induced by the tunnelling

3.1
Analytic extension of
\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}

To analyze the resonances we will adopt the strategy of finding the poles of the resolvent (
2.31
) as zeros of
\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}
. Therefore, our first aim is to construct a second sheet continuation of
\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}
. We start our discussion from the analysis of
\tau_{\alpha;n}(z)
(contributing
\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}^{d}(z)\chi_{\Omega}
) which has been defined so far for the first Riemann sheet, i.e.
\Im\tau_{\alpha;n}(z)>0
.
For each
n\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}
, the function
z\mapsto\tau_{\alpha;n}(z)
admits a second sheet analytic continuation through
[E_{\alpha;n},\infty)
and the values of
\Im\tau_{\alpha;n}(z)

are negative in the lower complex half-plane. Consequently, for each
k\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}
,
we can construct the lower half-plane analytic continuation of

\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}^{d}(z)\chi_{\Omega}=\chi_{\Omega}(x)\Big[\frac{i}{2}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{i\tau_{\alpha;n}(z)|x_{1}-y_{1}|}}{\tau_{\alpha;n}(z)}\phi_{\alpha;n}(x_{2})\phi_{\alpha;n}(y_{2})^{\ast}\Big]\chi_{\Omega}(y)\,,\

(3.1)

via the segments
(E_{\alpha;k},E_{\alpha;k+1})
.
More precisely, assuming that
z=\lambda+i\varepsilon
, where
\lambda\in(E_{\alpha;k},E_{\alpha;k+1})
we move to the lower half-plane, i.e. varying with
\varepsilon
from positive to negative values and defining
\Im\,\tau_{\alpha;n}(z)<0
if
n\leq k
and
\Im\,\tau_{\alpha;n}(z)>0
if
n>k
. We insert it into (
3.1
) and, in this way, we construct the second sheet component of
\chi_{\Omega}R^{d}_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}\,:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
. On the other hand, the component
R^{c}_{\alpha,0}(z)
does not require any additional analysis, since it is analytic for
z=\lambda+i\varepsilon
, where
\lambda\in(E_{\alpha;k},E_{\alpha;k+1})
and
\varepsilon
belongs to a neighbourhood of
0
.
In view of (
2.18
), this constitutes, for any
k\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}
, the second sheet continuation of
\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}
via
(E_{\alpha;k},E_{\alpha;k+1})
to a certain set
\mathcal{C}_{k}\subset\mathbb{C}_{-}
. We remain the same
notation for the second sheet operator valued function
\mathcal{C}_{k}\ni z\mapsto\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}
. The above reasoning has also been adopted in
[
26
]
.

3.2
Zeros of Birman–Schwinger operator

To find out the poles of resolvent determined by zeros of
\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}\,:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
we
adopt (
2.11
) which allows to state that
z
satisfies
\ker[\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}]\neq\emptyset

if and only if

\ker[\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega}-G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)]\neq\emptyset\,,

(3.2)

where

G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\beta\,\alpha\,\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{I}-\alpha\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma})^{-1}\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\chi_{\Omega}\,:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)\,.

(3.3)

Our aim is to express the condition (
3.2
) in the terms of a scalar equation. Note that
\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega}
determines the Birman–Schwinger operator for
H_{0,\beta}
. For
z
living in a certain neighbourhood of the discrete eigenvalue
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}
but
z\neq\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}
the operator
\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega}
is invertible and its inverse has the representation (
2.25
).
Therefore one gets

\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega}-G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=(\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega})\Big[\mathrm{I}-(\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega})^{-1}G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\Big]\,.

(3.4)

To proceed further we estimate the norms involved in the above formula.

Lemma 3.1

The operator
\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}\,:\,L^{2}(\Sigma)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
has the Hilbert–Schmidt norm

\|\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq C\frac{|\Omega|\ }{|z|^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{z}}\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2}\mathcal{T}_{z}\rho}}{\rho}\,,

(3.5)

where
\mathcal{T}_{z}:=\Im\sqrt{z}>0
,
\rho
is defined by (
2.2
) and
C
is a positive constant.

Proof.
To prove the claim we will employ the representation (
2.9
) which implies

\|\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Sigma}\big|K_{0}(k_{z}|x-y|)\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,,

(3.6)

where
k_{z}=-i\sqrt{z}
; let us remind that
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
and the same notation is adopted for
y
.
Developing the above norm, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Sigma}\big|K_{0}(k_{z}(\,(x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2})^{1/2}\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=\frac{d}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|K_{0}(k_{z}(\,(x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+y_{2}^{2})^{1/2}\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}y\,.\end{split}

(3.7)

Using the asymptotics of the Macdonald function for the large argument,
[
1
, p. 378, 9.7.2]
,
we obtain

|K_{0}(k_{z}\,\xi)|^{2}\leq C^{\prime}\frac{1}{\xi|z|^{1/2}}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathcal{T}_{z}\xi}\,,

(3.8)

for
\xi>\xi_{0}
, where
C^{\prime}
and
\xi_{0}
are positive constants and
C^{\prime}
depends on
\xi_{0}
. Combining the above inequality
and (
3.7
) and employing
(
2.2
)
together with the monotonicity of the function
\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni x\mapsto x^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-cx}
,
c>0
we can continue with estimates for the Hilbert–Schmidt norm:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq&C^{\prime}\frac{d}{(2\pi)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{|z|^{1/2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathcal{T}_{z}(x_{1}^{2}+\rho^{2})^{1/2}}}{(x_{1}^{2}+\rho^{2})^{1/2}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=C^{\prime}\frac{d\,|\Omega|}{(2\pi)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{|z|^{1/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathcal{T}_{z}(x_{1}^{2}+\rho^{2})^{1/2}}}{(x_{1}^{2}+\rho^{2})^{1/2}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,.\end{split}

(3.9)

Applying again the monotonicity of the integrand function and
the inequality

\sqrt{2}(a^{2}+b^{2})^{1/2}\geq|a|+|b|\,,\quad\quad a\,,b\in\mathbb{R}\,,

we arrive at

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq&C^{\prime}\frac{\sqrt{2}d\,|\Omega|}{(2\pi)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{|z|^{1/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2}\mathcal{T}_{z}(|x_{1}|+\rho)}}{|x_{1}|+\rho}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\\
&\leq C\frac{|\Omega|}{|z|^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{z}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2}\mathcal{T}_{z}\rho}}{\rho}\,,\end{split}

(3.10)

where
C=C^{\prime}\frac{d}{2\pi^{2}}
.
This shows (
3.5
).

In fact the above reasoning shows that
the operator
\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\chi_{\Omega}\,:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Sigma)
admits the same Hilbert–Schmidt norm bound given by (
3.1
).
Proceeding in the same way as in (
3.9
) one can show that
\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma}
is Hilbert–Schmidt. Using the Fredholm theorem, cf.
[
34
]
, we can conclude that
(\mathrm{I}-\alpha\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma})^{-1}
exists and it is bounded for
z\in[E_{\alpha}\,,\infty)
apart from a set of isolated points.
Furthermore, applying (
3.3
) and the boundedness of
(\mathrm{I}-\alpha\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\chi_{\Sigma})^{-1}
we obtain
the following bound for operator
G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\,:\,L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
,

\|G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq C_{1}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2}\mathcal{T}_{z}\rho}}{\rho}\,,

(3.11)

where
C_{1}
is a positive constant which depends on
z
.

Remark 3.2

Analytic continuation of the resolvent
R_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
.
In Section
3.1
we constructed analytic continuation of
\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}
via the segments
(E_{\alpha;k},E_{\alpha;k+1})
. This means that
\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}

. Moreover, applying the estimates (

) and (

), we conclude that
\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}
is a Hilbert–Schmidt operator. Using again the Fredholm theorem, cf.
[
34
]

\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}
is invertible in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
apart from isolated points. For any
g,f\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
we can also construct the continuation of
gR_{\alpha,0}(z)f
to
\mathcal{C}_{k}
applying the reasoning from Section

of

fR_{\alpha,\beta}(z)g=fR_{\alpha,0}(z)g+\beta fR_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}(\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega})^{-1}\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)g\,,

(3.12)

via the segment
(E_{\alpha;k},E_{\alpha;k+1})
to
\mathcal{C}_{k}
apart from isolated points.

Let us back to the analysis of (
3.4
).
Combining the decomposition (
2.25
) and the fact that the operator valued function
z\mapsto A_{n}(z)
is analytic a certain neighbourhood
\mathcal{S}_{n}
of
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}
and taking into account the norm estimate
(
3.11
) we state that
\mathrm{I}-A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
is invertible for
\rho
large enough. Consequently, the operator contributing to (
3.4
) can be written

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}-(\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega})^{-1}G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\Big[\mathrm{I}-A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\Big]\,\Big[\mathrm{I}+\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{1}{z-\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}}\Big[\mathrm{I}-A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\Big]^{-1}\mathrm{P}_{\beta;n}G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\Big]\,.

(3.13)

Applying (
3.13
) and (
3.4
) we come to the conclusion that the condition (
3.2
)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\ker\Big[z-\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}+\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[\mathrm{I}-A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\Big]^{-1}\mathrm{P}_{\beta;n}G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\Big]\neq\emptyset\,.

(3.14)

Note that the operator
L_{n}(z):=[\mathrm{I}-A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)]^{-1}\mathrm{P}_{\beta;n}G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
acting in

is rank one and it acts as

L_{n}(z)f=(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)f)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big[\mathrm{I}-A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\big]^{-1}w_{\beta;n}\,.

(3.15)

Therefore, the condition (
3.14
) can be transited to the scalar equation

\displaystyle z-\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}+\frac{1}{\beta}(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\big[\,\mathrm{I}-A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\,\big]^{-1}w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0\,.

(3.16)

With the above results, we are ready to prove the following statement, which establishes the resonances.

Theorem 3.3

Assume that
H_{0,\beta}
has a simple, discrete eigenvalue
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}\in(E_{\alpha;j}\,,E_{\alpha;j+1})
.
Then the second sheet analytic continuation of
R_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
defined by (
3.12
)
has a unique pole
z_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, i.e.
\ker[\mathrm{I}-\beta\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z_{n})\chi_{\Omega}]\neq\emptyset
, with the asymptotics

z_{n}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2|\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}|}\rho}}{\rho}\Big)\,.

(3.17)

Proof.

Step 1. Auxiliary statement: the Neumann expansion.
We adopt the notation
b:=\frac{1}{\rho}
, which means that
b\to 0
for
\rho\to\infty
. Note that the operator
G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
depends on
b
because it is defined by the embeddings acting between spaces
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
L^{2}(\Sigma)
, see (
3.3
). Since the parameter
b
will play the essential rule in the further discussion we implement the notation

\displaystyle\eta(z,b):=(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)[\,\mathrm{I}-A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\,]^{-1}w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

(3.18)

In view of (
3.11
) we have
\lim_{b\to 0}\eta(z,b)=0
for any
z\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
. We continuously extend function
\eta
assuming that
\eta(z,0)=0
. Note that formula (
3.18
) can be rephrased in the terms of the Neumann series

[\mathrm{I}-A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)]^{-1}=\mathrm{I}+A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)+[\,A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\,]^{2}+...

(3.19)

which implies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta(z,b)=\Big[&\,(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
+&(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)[A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)]^{2}\,w_{\beta;n}\,)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+...\Big]=0\,.\end{split}

(3.20)

Step 2. Unique solution of the spectral equation.
To discuss further properties of
\eta
we will investigate analycity of
G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
, which is related to the resolvent
R_{\alpha,0}(z)
by the equivalence
G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\beta\,[\,\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}-\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega}\,]
, cf. (
3.3
) and (
2.31
).
As a consequence of the first resolvent identity

\chi_{\Omega}[\,R(z)-R(z^{\prime})\,]\chi_{\Omega}=(z^{\prime}-z)\chi_{\Omega}R(z)R(z^{\prime})\chi_{\Omega}\,,

(3.21)

and the analogous identity for
R_{\alpha,0}(z)
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)&-G_{\alpha,\beta}(z^{\prime})=\beta\,\chi_{\Omega}\big[\,R_{\alpha,0}(z)-R_{\alpha,0}(z^{\prime})-R(z)+R(z^{\prime})\,\big]\chi_{\Omega}\\
&=(z^{\prime}-z)\beta\,\big[\chi_{\Omega}\,R_{\alpha,0}(z)R_{\alpha,0}(z^{\prime})\chi_{\Omega}-\chi_{\Omega}\,R(z)R(z^{\prime})\chi_{\Omega}\,\big]\\
&=(z^{\prime}-z)\big[\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega}G_{\alpha,\beta}(z^{\prime})+G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\chi_{\Omega}R(z^{\prime})\chi_{\Omega}+\frac{1}{\beta}G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z^{\prime})\big]\,.\end{split}

(3.22)

Note that the operators in the last line of (
3.22
) can be bounded by means of (
3.11
). In view of the analyticity of
A_{n}(z)
, the same reasoning can be proceeded for operators appearing in further terms of (
3.20
).
Therefore we can state that
\eta(z,b)
is analytic as the function of
z\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, and

\eta(z,b)-\eta(z^{\prime},b)=(z-z^{\prime})f(z,z^{\prime};b)\,,

(3.23)

where
f(\cdot,\cdot\,;b)
is analytic and
\lim_{b\to 0}f(z,z^{\prime};b)=0
uniformly with respect to
z
and
z^{\prime}
. This gives
\partial_{z}\eta(z;b)|_{b=0}=0
, where we understand the symbol
|_{b=0}
as the limiting value for
b\to 0
. The latter follows directly from the estimate (
3.11
) and eq. (
3.22
). This means that

\frac{\partial\tilde{\eta}(z,b)}{\partial z}\Big|_{b=0}=1\,,\quad\mathrm{where}\quad\tilde{\eta}(z,b):=z-\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}+\beta^{-1}\eta(z,b)\,.

(3.24)

Note that the spectral equation (
3.16
) reads

\tilde{\eta}(z,b)=0\,.

(3.25)

Combining (
3.24
) with the implicit function theorem and the fact that
\tilde{\eta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n},0)=0
, we can conclude that for
b
small enough the equation (
3.25
) has a unique solution
z_{n}(b)
such that

\tilde{\eta}(z_{n}(b),b)=0
. The asymptotics of the solution can be recovered from (
3.20
). Since we are interested in a solutions for
b\neq 0
we again back to the variable
\rho
and the asymptotics
\rho\to\infty
. Without a danger of confusion, we keep the same notation for
z_{n}(\cdot)
expressed in the terms of
\rho
.

Step 3. Asymptotics of the solution.
Combining the spectral equation (
3.25
) and the expansion (
3.20
) together with the definition of
\tilde{\eta}(\cdot,\cdot)
from (
3.24
), we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}z_{n}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[&\,(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
+&(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)[A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)]^{2}\,w_{\beta;n}\,)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+...\Big]\,.\end{split}

(3.26)

As follows from the above discussion, the dominated term of
z_{n}(\rho)
comes from the term
-\beta^{-1}(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
z\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
.
Therefore we expand

(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(z-\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})(w_{\beta;n}\,T_{n}(z)w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

(3.27)

where
T_{n}(z)
has the norm that can be bounded by the norm of
G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)
, see (
3.22
).
Applying the expansions (
3.26
) and (
3.27
) we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}z_{n}(\rho)=&\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-\frac{1}{\beta}\Big[\,1+\beta^{-1}(w_{\beta;n}\,,T_{n}(z)w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\Big]^{-1}\Big[\,(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&+(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)[A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)]^{2}\,w_{\beta;n}\,)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+...\Big]\,.\end{split}

(3.28)

The above expansion shows that the dominated term is determined by

-\frac{1}{\beta}(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

(3.29)

which, in view of (
3.11
),
yields the asymptotics

z_{n}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2|\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}|}\rho}}{\rho}\Big)\,.

(3.30)

This completes the proof.

4
Fermi’s golden rule

The discussion of this section is devoted to a deeper analysis of the asymptotitcs of
\Im z_{n}(\rho)
.
First we analyze the component of
\Im z_{n}(\rho)
given by

\Gamma_{n}(\rho):=-\frac{1}{\beta}\Im(w_{\beta;n},G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,

(4.1)

which comes from (
3.29
); cf. (
3.28
).
Using the Birman-Schwinger principle (
2.24
) we get

\chi_{\Omega}R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}w_{\beta;n}=\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n}\,,\quad\chi_{\Omega}R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}w_{\beta;n}=\beta^{-1}w_{\beta;n}\,.

(4.2)

This yields

(w_{\beta;n},G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\beta^{2}(\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n},G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

(4.3)

On the other hand,
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}<0
belongs to the resolvent sets of
R_{\alpha,0}^{c}(\cdot)
and
R(\cdot)
, which are self adjoint operators (
R_{\alpha,0}^{c}(\cdot)
is defined by (
2.18
)). Therefore, we have

\Im(\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n},R(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\Im(\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n},R_{\alpha,0}^{c}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0\,.

(4.4)

Using again the equation

G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\beta[\,\chi_{\Omega}R_{\alpha,0}(z)\chi_{\Omega}-\chi_{\Omega}R(z)\chi_{\Omega}\,]\,,

(4.5)

together with (
4.4
),
we get

\Im(\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n},G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\beta\Im(\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n},R_{\alpha,0}^{d}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

(4.6)

Furthermore, applying (
2.19
) we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Im(\chi_{\Omega}&\omega_{\beta;n},R_{\alpha,0}^{d}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&=\Im\frac{1}{2\pi}\Big[\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{ip_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})}}{p_{1}^{2}+E_{\alpha;k}-(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}+i\varepsilon)}\phi_{\alpha;k}(x_{2})\phi_{\alpha;k}(y_{2})^{\ast}\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\omega_{\beta;n}(y)\mathrm{d}p_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\Big]\,.\end{split}

(4.7)

To deal with the integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{ip_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})}}{p_{1}^{2}-(b_{k,n}+i\varepsilon)}\mathrm{d}p_{1}\,,\quad\mathrm{where}\quad b_{k,n}:=-(E_{\alpha;k}-\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\,,

(4.8)

we distinguish two cases
b_{k,n}>0
and
b_{k,n}<0
. Assume first that
b_{k,n}>0
; then the above integrand has two poles
\pm(\tilde{b}_{\epsilon}+i\tilde{\varepsilon})
, where
\tilde{b}_{\epsilon}\to{\sqrt{b_{k,n}}}
and
\tilde{\varepsilon}\to
for
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Applying the
Sokhotski–Plemelj formula which can be
formally written
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{x\pm i\varepsilon}=\mathcal{P}\frac{1}{x}\mp i\pi\delta(x)
, where
\mathcal{P}
stands for the principle value,
we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{ip_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})}}{p_{1}^{2}-(b_{k,n}+i\varepsilon)}\mathrm{d}p_{1}=&\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{ip_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})}}{(p_{1}-(\tilde{b}_{\varepsilon}+i\tilde{\varepsilon}))(p_{1}+(\tilde{b}_{\varepsilon}+i\tilde{\varepsilon}))}\mathrm{d}p_{1}\\
=&\frac{i\pi}{2\sqrt{b_{k,n}}}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{i\sqrt{b_{k,n}}(x_{1}-y_{1})}+\mathrm{e}^{-i\sqrt{b_{k,n}}(x_{1}-y_{1})}\Big]+\mathcal{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{ip_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})}}{p_{1}^{2}-b_{k,n}}\mathrm{d}p_{1}\\
=&\frac{i\pi}{\sqrt{b_{k,n}}}\cos(\sqrt{b_{k,n}}|x_{1}-y_{1}|)+\mathcal{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{ip_{1}(x_{1}-y_{1})}}{p_{1}^{2}-b_{k,n}}\mathrm{d}p_{1}\,.\end{split}

(4.9)

For
b_{k,n}<0
the analogous limit reproduces
\pi\frac{\mathrm{exp}(-|b_{k,n}|^{1/2}|x_{1}-x_{2}|)}{|b_{k,n}|^{1/2}}
, which contributes to the real component of (
4.6
).
This, in view of (
4.7
) and (
4.9
) implies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Im(\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n}&,R_{\alpha,0}^{d}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&=\sum_{k\,:\,E_{\alpha;k}<\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{b_{k,n}}}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\cos(\sqrt{b_{k,n}}|x_{1}-y_{1}|)\phi_{\alpha;k}(x_{2})\phi_{\alpha;k}(y_{2})^{\ast}\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\omega_{\beta;n}(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\cos(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}|x_{1}-y_{1}|)\phi_{\alpha;k}(x_{2})\phi_{\alpha;k}(y_{2})^{\ast}\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\omega_{\beta;n}(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,,\end{split}

(4.10)

where, in the last step, we have changed the summation from
\sum_{k\,:\,E_{\alpha;k}<\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}}
to
\sum_{k=1}^{j}

since
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}\in(E_{\alpha;j}\,,E_{\alpha;j+1})
.
Using now (
4.1
), (
4.3
), (
4.6
) and (
4.10
) we can conclude

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma_{n}(\rho)=-\beta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}}\Big[\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}&\cos(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}|x_{1}-y_{1}|)\phi_{\alpha;k}(x_{2})\phi_{\alpha;k}(y_{2})^{\ast}\\
&\times\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\omega_{\beta;n}(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\Big]\,.\end{split}

(4.11)

In fact,
\Gamma_{n}(\rho)
can be expressed by means of the generalized eigenfunctions
\varphi_{k}(x,p_{1})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{ip_{1}x_{1}}\phi_{\alpha;k}(x_{2})
of
H_{\alpha,0}
.
Using the first term of the second line of (
4.9
) and
\varphi_{k}(x,p_{1})
we can write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma_{n}(\rho)=-\beta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}}\Big[&\Big|\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\varphi_{k}(x,p_{1})\mathrm{d}x\Big|^{2}_{p_{1}=(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k})^{1/2}}\\
+&\Big|\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\varphi_{k}(x,p_{1})\mathrm{d}x\Big|^{2}_{p_{1}=-(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k})^{1/2}}\,\Big]\,,\end{split}

(4.12)

where the lower indexes indicate that the values of functions are taken at specified points.
The above Formula reveals the well known Fermi’s golden rule, cf.
[
33
, XII. 6]
. In Appendix
Appendix: The asymptotics of futher terms contributing to
\Im z_{n}(\rho)
, we show that the further terms contributing to (
3.28
) possess the stronger asymptotics then (
4.12
).

Remark 4.1

Note that,
\varphi_{k}(\cdot,p_{1})\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, however, the function
\chi_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}(\cdot,p_{1})
is well defined as an element of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The expressions (
4.12
) can be reformulated in the form more familiar to physicists

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma_{n}(\rho)=-\beta^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{j}\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}}&\Big[|(\omega_{\beta;n},\chi_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}(\cdot,p_{1})\,)|^{2}_{p_{1}=(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k})^{1/2}}\\
&+|(\omega_{\beta;n},\chi_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}(\cdot,p_{1})\,)|^{2}_{p_{1}=-(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k})^{1/2}}\Big]\,,\end{split}

(4.13)

where the scalar product in the above formulae is defined in the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
The resonance width is determined by

\gamma_{n}(\rho):=2\,|\Im z_{n}(\rho)|\,,

(4.14)

and, therefore the dominated term of
\gamma_{n}(\cdot)
takes the form

\gamma_{n}(\rho)\approx 2\pi\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}}\Big[|(\omega_{\beta;n},V_{\beta}\varphi_{k}(\cdot,p_{1})\,)|^{2}_{p_{1}=p_{n,k}}+|(\omega_{\beta;n},V_{\beta}\varphi_{k}(\cdot,p_{1})\,)|^{2}_{p_{1}=-p_{n,k}}\Big]\,,

(4.15)

where
V_{\beta}=-\beta\chi_{\Omega}
and
p_{n,k}=(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k})^{1/2}
.
The above formula represents also
the decay rate of the resonant state in the first approximation. It reflexes the characteristic properties, namely the contribution of the transition amplitude
(\omega_{\beta;n},V_{\beta}\varphi_{k}(\cdot,p_{1})\,)
and spectral density function

(2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}})^{-1}
. The summation
\sum_{k=1}^{j}
comes from the fact that we have to take into account all modes which ‘catch’ the resonant energy
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}
.

4.1
Asymptotics of
\Gamma_{n}(\rho)

Let us analyze in more detail the asymptotic behaviour of
\Gamma_{n}(\rho)
for
\rho\to\infty
. For this aim we use the expression (
4.10
). Using the fact that for
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[0,d]
, we have

\phi_{\alpha;k}(x_{2})=M_{\alpha,k}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{|E_{\alpha;k}|}{|x_{2}|}}\,,

(4.16)

where
M_{\alpha}
is the normalization constant, we can estimate (
4.10
) by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{|M_{\alpha,k}|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\sqrt{|E_{\alpha;k}|}{\rho}}}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}}&\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\big|\cos(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}|x_{1}-y_{1}|)\,\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\omega_{\beta;n}(y)\big|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\leq jC_{j,n}\mathrm{e}^{-2\sqrt{|E_{\alpha;j}|}\rho}\,,\end{split}

(4.17)

where

C_{j,n}:=\mathrm{max}_{1\leq k\leq j}\Big[\frac{|M_{\alpha,k}|^{2}}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\big|\cos(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}|x_{1}-y_{1}|)\,\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\omega_{\beta;n}(y)\big|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\Big]\,;

(4.18)

in the above reasoning we used the fact that
\sqrt{|\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}|}<\sqrt{|E_{\alpha;k}|}
for any
k=1,...,j
. In fact it follows from (
4.12
) that all the components labeled by
1,..,j
contributing to
\Gamma_{n}(\rho)
have the negative sign (they can not vanish because the functions in the scalar products are not orthogonal). Using (
4.16
) we state

\Gamma_{n}(\rho)\approx\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\sqrt{|E_{\alpha;j}|}\rho}\,,\quad\mathrm{where}\quad\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}\in(E_{\alpha;j}\,,E_{\alpha;j+1})\,.

(4.19)

On the other hand, note that for
\rho
sufficiently large we have

\mathrm{e}^{-2\sqrt{|E_{\alpha;j}|}\rho}\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2|\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}|}\rho}}{\rho}\,;

(4.20)

therefore the asymptotics (
4.19
) enhances
(
3.17
) for the imaginary component of
z_{n}(\rho)
.

5
Generalization of the system: varying transverse profile and perturbation defined by the Kato class measure

In this section we consider a generalization of the model and assume that the transverse profile of the waveguide is given by a piecewise continuous function
\alpha\,:\,[0,d]\to\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, we generalize the well potential supported on
\Omega
by a Radon measure
\mu
belonging to the Kato class, i.e. satisfying

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\,\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{B(x,\varepsilon)}|\log(|x-y|)|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=0\,,

(5.1)

where
B(x,\varepsilon)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes a ball centered at
x
and with the radius
\varepsilon
.
Throughout this section we assume that the support
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
of
\mu
is compact; in this part of further discussion where support of
\mu
plays an essential role we will use the notation
\mu_{\Omega}\equiv\mu
. The examples of the Kato measures has been discussed in Introduction.

To maintain the consistency with previous discussion we define the remote perturbation by
-\beta\mu
, where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then the Hamiltonian is associated to the sesquilinear form

\mathcalligra{E}_{\alpha,\beta\mu}[f]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Sigma}\alpha(x_{2})|f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|I_{\mu}f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,,\quad f\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,,

(5.2)

where
I_{\mu}
denotes the space embedding
I_{\mu}\,:\,W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}_{\mu}\equiv L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\,\mathrm{d}\mu)
which is continuous, cf.
[
2
]
.
For any Kato class measure
\mu
and any small parameter
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C(\varepsilon)
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|I_{\mu}f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+C(\varepsilon)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\,,

(5.3)

cf.
[
7
]
. This yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|I_{\mu}f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leq\varepsilon\Big[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Sigma}\alpha(x_{2})|f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\Big]+C_{1}(\varepsilon)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\,,

(5.4)

where
C_{1}(\varepsilon)=C(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\sup\,|\alpha(x_{2})|
, which implies that the operator associated to
\mathcalligra E_{\alpha,\beta\mu}
has a unique self adjoint extension in view of the KLMN Theorem, cf.
[
34
]
.
The resulting operator defines a generalization of
H_{\alpha,\beta}
given by (
2.3
).

To reconstruct the resolvent of
H_{0,\beta\mu}
we define the embeddings of
R(z)
to
L^{2}_{\mu}
by

R(z)|_{0\mu}f=R(z)\ast f\mu=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{0}(k_{z}|x-y|)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\,:\,L^{2}_{\mu}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,,

(5.5)

where
k_{z}=-i\sqrt{z}
.
Furthermore, we define
R(z)|_{\mu 0}\,:\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}_{\mu}
which acts as

R(z)|_{\mu 0}f=I_{\mu}R(z)f\,

and, finally, we construct the bilateral embedding by

R(z)|_{\mu\mu}:=I_{\mu}R(z)|_{0\mu}\,:\,L^{2}_{\mu}\to L^{2}_{\mu}\,.

(5.6)

We are ready to construct the resolvent of
(H_{0,\beta}-z)^{-1}
by

R_{0,\beta\mu}(z)=R(z)+\beta R(z)|_{0\mu}(\mathrm{I}-\beta R(z)|_{\mu\mu})^{-1}R(z)|_{\mu 0}\,.

(5.7)

The similar types of Hamiltonian were discussed, for example in
[
7
,
28
]
.

On the other hand, the resolvent
R_{\alpha,0}(z)
represented in the Birman–Schwinger form is given by

R_{\alpha,0}(z)=R(z)+R(z)\alpha^{1/2}\chi_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{I}-|\alpha|^{1/2}\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\alpha^{1/2}\chi_{\Sigma})^{-1}|\alpha|^{1/2}\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\,,

(5.8)

where
\alpha(\cdot)
is now a function of
x_{2}
and
\alpha^{1/2}=\mathrm{sgn}(\alpha)|\alpha|^{1/2}
. Using the embeddings of
R(z)
to
L^{2}_{\mu}
and the strip resolvent (
5.8
) we can build the operator

\mathrm{I}-\beta R_{\alpha,0}(z)|_{\mu\mu}=\mathrm{I}-\beta\Big[R(z)|_{\mu\mu}+R(z)|_{\mu 0}\alpha^{1/2}\chi_{\Sigma}(\mathrm{I}-|\alpha|^{1/2}\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\alpha^{1/2}\chi_{\Sigma})^{-1}|\alpha|^{1/2}\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)|_{0\mu}\Big]\,:\,L^{2}_{\mu}\to L^{2}_{\mu}\,,

which contributes to
the Birman–Schwinger resolvent of
H_{\alpha,\beta}
.
Assuming now that
z
is such that the above operator is invertible,
we construct the resolvent of
H_{\alpha,\beta}
by

R_{\alpha,\beta\mu}(z)=R_{\alpha,0}(z)+\beta R_{\alpha,0}(z)|_{0\mu}(\mathrm{I}-\beta R_{\alpha,0}(z)|_{\mu\mu})^{-1}R_{\alpha,0}(z)|_{\mu 0}\,:\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,.

(5.9)

The stability of the essential spectrum

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(H_{\alpha,\beta\mu})=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(H_{\alpha,0})=[E_{\alpha},\infty\,)

(5.10)

is a consequence of the following reasoning. Note that the operators
R(z)|_{\mu 0}\,:\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}_{\mu}
and
R(z)|_{0\mu}\,:\,L^{2}_{\mu}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
are compact in view of
[
7
, Le. 3.1]
. Therefore, using (
5.8
)
we can state that both

R_{\alpha,0}(z)|_{0\mu}\,:\,L^{2}_{\mu}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\quad\mathrm{and}\quad R_{\alpha,0}(z)|_{\mu 0}\,:\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}_{\mu}

(5.11)

are also compact since the operator
(\mathrm{I}-|\alpha|^{1/2}\chi_{\Sigma}R(z)\alpha^{1/2}\chi_{\Sigma})^{-1}
is bounded. Consequently, we get the compactness of the second component of (
5.9
) and, in view of the Weyl’s theorem, we come to the conclusion that the essential spectra of
H_{\alpha,0}
and
H_{\alpha,\beta\mu}

coincide.

Assumptions on
H_{\alpha,0}
and
H_{0,\beta\mu}
.
As before, let
\mathrm{h}_{\alpha}
stand for the transverse component of the Hamiltonian
H_{\alpha,0}
. In the current framework it takes the form

\mathrm{h}_{\alpha}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{2}^{2}}-\alpha(x_{2})\chi_{[0,d]}(x_{2})\,:\,W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\,.

(5.12)

In the following we assume that
\mathrm{h}_{\alpha}
has at least one negative discrete spectrum point. It happens, for example, if
\int_{[0,d]}\alpha(x_{2})\mathrm{d}x_{2}\geq 0
, cf.
[
35
]
. Following the convention of the previous discussion, we will keep the notation
\{E_{\alpha;1}\leq E_{\alpha;2}\leq...\leq E_{\alpha;N_{\alpha}}\}
and
\{\phi_{\alpha;n}\}_{n\in{\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}}}
for the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
\mathrm{h}_{\alpha}
, see. (
2.5
).
On the other hand, the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
H_{0,\beta\mu}
will be denoted by

\{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;1}\leq\mathcal{E}_{\beta;2}\leq...\leq\mathcal{E}_{\beta;N_{\beta}}\}
and

\{\omega_{\beta;n}\}_{n\in{\mathcal{N}_{\beta}}}
, cf. (
2.21
). In the following we assume that there exists
n\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
and
j\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}
such that

\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}\in(E_{\alpha;j}\,,E_{\alpha;j+1})
.

To investigate the problem of resonances in the generalized potential system
we need to estimate

G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\beta R_{\alpha,0}(z)|_{\mu\mu}(\mathrm{I}-\beta R_{\alpha,0}(z)|_{\mu\mu})^{-1}R_{\alpha,0}(z)|_{\mu\mu}\,:\,L^{2}_{\mu}\to L^{2}_{\mu}\,,

(5.13)

which states an analogue of (
3.3
). Similarly as before, we start from the estimates of the embeddings of the free resolvent

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|R(z)|_{\mu 0}\chi_{\Sigma}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}|I_{\mu}K_{0}(k_{z}|x-y|)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}y\\
&\leq\sup_{x\in\Omega}\frac{|\mu|}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|I_{\mu}K_{0}(k_{z}|x-y|)|^{2}\mathrm{d}y\leq C\frac{|\mu|\ }{|z|^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{z}}\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2}\mathcal{T}_{z}\rho}}{\rho}\,,\end{split}

(5.14)

where
|\mu|=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}\mu
and the embedding
I_{\mu}
is applied to the kernel defined by
K_{0}
understood as the function of the variable
x
.
Consequently, taking into account the fact that
(\mathrm{I}-\beta R_{\alpha,0}(z)|_{\mu\mu})^{-1}
is bounded, the operator
G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\,:\,L^{2}_{\mu}\to L^{2}_{\mu}
can be estimated

\|G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\leq C_{1}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2}\mathcal{T}_{z}\rho}}{\rho}\,,

(5.15)

where
C_{1}
is a positive constant depending on
|\mu|
and
z
. Formula (
5.15
)
is a generalisation of (
3.11
).

Repeating the reasoning from Section
3.1

we construct the second sheet analytic continuation of
R_{\alpha,0}(z)
via the segment
(E_{\alpha;j}\,,E_{\alpha;j+1})
in the sense of (
3.12
). Using this result and relying on the same analysis as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
we can prove the that
R_{\alpha,\beta\mu}(z)
has the second sheet pole located at

z_{n}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{\beta,n}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2|\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}|}\rho}}{\rho}\Big)\,.

(5.16)

Moreover, the dominated term of its imaginary part satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma_{n}(\rho)=-\beta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}}&\Big[\Big|\int_{\Omega}I_{\mu}(\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\varphi_{k}(x,p_{1}))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\Big|^{2}_{p_{1}=(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k})^{1/2}}\\
&+\Big|\int_{\Omega}I_{\mu}(\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\varphi_{k}(x,p_{1}))\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\Big|^{2}_{p_{1}=-(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k})^{1/2}}\Big]\,.\end{split}

(5.17)

The above result states a generalization of Fermi’s golden role (
4.12
) for the Kato class measures.

5.1
A special case: the trap defined by a delta interaction

In this section we consider a special case of the Kato class measure, defined by the Dirac delta potential supported on a curve.
Let
\Omega
be a finite
C^{1}
planar curve (open arc or loop) with the length
l
.
Assume that
\Omega
is parameterized by the arc length
s\in I_{l}:=[0,l]
and is the graph of the function
s\mapsto\varpi(s)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Our aim is to analyze the resonances problem for the Hamiltonian which can be symbolically written

-\Delta-\alpha\chi_{\Sigma}(x)-\beta\delta(x-\Omega)\,,\quad\alpha<0\,,\,\,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(5.18)

Remark 5.1

Delta interaction: a particular case of Kato class measure.

In the analyzed case, the measure
\mu
corresponding to the delta interaction will be denoted as
\delta
. It is defined as follows: for any Borel set
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we determine

\delta(\mathcal{B})=\mathrm{lin}(\mathcal{B}\cap\Omega),

(5.19)

where
\mathrm{lin}(\cdot)
denotes the linear measure. Consequently,
\delta
belongs to the Kato class of measures, cf.
[
7
]
.

To give the expression (
5.18
) a mathematical meaning, we introduce the corresponding embedding

I_{\delta}\,:\,W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(I_{l})
acting as
I_{\delta}f=f(\varpi(\cdot))
.
Then, for this specific case,
the sesquilinear form (
5.2
) can be expressed as

\mathcalligra{E}_{\alpha,\beta\delta}[f]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\alpha\int_{\Sigma}|f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\beta\int_{I_{l}}|f(\varpi(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\,,\quad f\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,.

(5.20)

The special case of (
5.4
) takes the form

\int_{I_{l}}|f(\varpi(s))|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\leq\varepsilon\Big[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x-\alpha\int_{\Sigma}|f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\Big]+C_{1}(\varepsilon)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\,.

(5.21)

Consequently, the operator
H_{\alpha,\beta\delta}
associated to
\mathcalligra{E}_{\alpha,\beta\delta}
is self adjoint.

Remark 5.2

Negative eigenvalues of
H_{0,\beta\delta}
for the delta interaction trap.

It is worth mentioning that for any negative coupling constant
\beta
, the operator
H_{0,\beta\delta}
always admits at least one negative eigenvalue. However, it should be noted at the same time that in systems of dimensions three or higher, a sufficiently weak coupling constant supported on a finite curve does not generate a discrete spectrum,
cf.
[
7
, Thm 4.2(iii)]
.

The embeddings of
R(z)
to
L^{2}(I_{l})
defined by the Dirac delta are given by

R(z)|_{0\delta}f=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{0}(k_{z}|x-\varpi(s)|)f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,:\,L^{2}(I_{l})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,,

(5.22)

and

R(z)|_{\delta\delta}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{I_{l}}K_{0}(k_{z}|\varpi(\cdot)-\varpi(s)|)f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\,:\,L^{2}(I_{l})\to L^{2}(I_{l})\,,

(5.23)

moreover,

R(z)|_{\delta 0}=I_{\delta}R(z)\,:\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}(I_{l})
.
This allows to construct the resolvent
(H_{0,\beta\delta}-z)^{-1}
by

R_{0,\beta\delta}(z)=R(z)+\beta R(z)|_{0\delta}(\mathrm{I}-\beta R(z)|_{\delta\delta})^{-1}R(z)|_{\delta 0}\,.

(5.24)

Assume that
\beta>0
. Relying on Remark
5.2
we conclude that the set of negative eigenvalues of
H_{0,\beta\delta}
is not empty; the same holds for
H_{\alpha,0}
. This means that the assumption formulated for the general measure
\mu
are automatically satisfied. We keep the notation
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}
,
n\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha;k}
,
k\in\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}
.
For the delta interaction the inequalities (
5.14
)
take the forms

\displaystyle\|R(z)|_{\delta 0}\chi_{\Sigma}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\Omega}\frac{l}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|K_{0}(k_{z}|x-y|)|^{2}\mathrm{d}y\leq C\frac{l\ }{|z|^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{z}}\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2}\mathcal{T}_{z}\rho}}{\rho}\,.

(5.25)

Furthermore, a realization of (
5.17
) for the discussed special case is giveen by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma_{n}(\rho)=-\beta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}}&\Big[\Big|\int_{I_{l}}(\omega_{\beta;n}(\varpi(s))^{\ast}\varphi_{k}(\varpi(s)p_{1}))\mathrm{d}s\Big|^{2}_{p_{1}=(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k})^{1/2}}\\
&+\Big|\int_{I_{l}}(\omega_{\beta;n}(\varpi(s))^{\ast}\varphi_{k}(\varpi(s)),p_{1}))\mathrm{d}s\Big|^{2}_{p_{1}=-(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k})^{1/2}}\Big]\,.\end{split}

(5.26)

The above formula provides Fermi’s golden rule for the resonant pole that appears in a neighborhood of
\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}\in(E_{\alpha;j},E_{\alpha;j+1})
, in systems governed by
H_{\alpha,\beta\delta}
.

Remark 5.3

Waveguide with a point interaction.

Notably, Fermi’s golden rule does not hold in the form analogous to (
5.26
) for a trap defined by a more singular potential determined by a point interaction. This follows from the fact that a point potential in two dimensional system requires logarithmic regularization, cf.
[
3
]
, and consequently, the eigenfunctions
\omega_{\beta;n}
exhibit a corresponding singularity at this point. While we can expect resonances with an exponentially decaying imaginary component, Fermi’s golden rule requires modification. We defer the exploration of this topic, along with related concepts, to future research.

Appendix: The asymptotics of futher terms contributing to
\Im z_{n}(\rho)

First, we discuss the asymptotics of
(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)A_{n}(z)G_{\alpha,\beta}(z)w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, which is contributes to (
3.28
). Repeating the argument from the proof of Theorem
3.3
it suffices to consider this component for
z=\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}
. Furthermore, since we are interested Fermi’s golden rule
our aim is to analyze

\displaystyle\Im(w_{\beta;n}\,,G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})A_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

(5.27)

We use the decomposition
G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})=G_{\alpha,\beta}^{R}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})+iG_{\alpha,\beta}^{I}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})
, where
G_{\alpha,\beta}^{R}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})
and
G_{\alpha,\beta}^{I}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})
are self adjoint. In fact
G^{I}_{\alpha,\beta}
is determined by the operator employed in (
4.10
). Then (
5.27
) can be written

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Im(w_{\beta;n},G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})A_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})G_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&=(w_{\beta;n}\,,G^{I}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})A_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})G_{\alpha,\beta}^{R}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(w_{\beta;n}\,,G^{R}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})A_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})G_{\alpha,\beta}^{I}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})w_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.\end{split}

(5.28)

It suffices to estimate one of the above components. Using the expression from (
4.10
) we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\big|\Im(\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n}&,G^{I}_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})A_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})G_{\alpha,\beta}^{R}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\chi_{\Omega}\omega_{\beta;n})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big|\\
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^{j}\frac{\beta}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}\big|\cos(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}-E_{\alpha;k}}|x_{1}-y_{1}|)\phi_{\alpha;k}(x_{2})\phi_{\alpha;k}(y_{2})^{\ast}\omega_{\beta;n}(x)^{\ast}\\
&\quad\qquad\times(A_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})G_{\alpha,\beta}^{R}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\omega_{\beta;n})(y)\big|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&\leq\mathrm{const}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\sqrt{|E_{\alpha,j}|}\rho}\,|\Omega|^{2}\,\|\omega_{\beta;n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\|A_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})G_{\alpha,\beta}^{I}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\omega_{\beta;n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq\mathrm{const}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\sqrt{|E_{\alpha,j}|}\rho}\,|\Omega|^{2}\,\|\omega_{\beta;n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|A_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})G_{\alpha,\beta}^{I}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})\omega_{\beta;n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,,\\
&\leq\mathrm{const}\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2\sqrt{|E_{\alpha,j}|}\rho}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2|\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n}|}\rho}}{\rho}|\Omega|^{2}\,\|\omega_{\beta;n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,,\end{split}

(5.29)

where we used
\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq|\Omega|\,\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, in the last inequality we applied the norm bound for
G_{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot)
, (see (
3.11
)) and the boundness of
A_{n}(\mathcal{E}_{\beta;n})
; the constants contained in the symbol ’const’ depend on
j
and coupling constants
\alpha
and
\beta
. The resulting estimate shows that the asymptotics of (
5.27
) is stronger then (
4.19
) and, consequently, is does not contribute to the dominated term. The further terms contributing to (
3.28
) can be estimated analogously.
