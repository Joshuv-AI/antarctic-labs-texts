Differences in transcranial doppler parameters between pre-eclamptic and normotensive pregnancies: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jing Wang, Hong Luo, Tian Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPre-eclampsia (PE) is a prevalent pregnancy complication that poses significant risks to neonatal and maternal health. Timely detection of PE is essential for early prevention, monitoring, and intervention to enhance patient outcomes. Transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasound allows non-invasive assessment of cerebral blood flow velocity and vascular resistance. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize available evidence to determine whether there are differences in TCD parameters between women with pre-eclampsia and those without pre-eclampsia.MethodsA comprehensive literature search and meta-analysis were conducted to assess the differences in cerebral artery parameters, measured by TCD, between pregnant women with pre-eclampsia and normotensive controls. These parameters, encompassing resistance index (RI), pulsatility index (PI), as well as mean blood flow velocity (MBFV), were obtained from the posterior cerebral artery (PCA), middle cerebral artery (MCA), and anterior cerebral artery (ACA).ResultsTwenty-two studies involving 1,625 participants (643 PE patients and 982 controls) were included. Significant differences were found in key TCD parameters between the PE and control groups. For the MCA, MBFV increased (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 1.17; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.74–1.60; I² = 90.4%), whereas PI and RI decreased markedly (PI: SMD = -1.52; I² = 95.6%; RI: SMD = -1.86; I² = 94.7%). End-diastolic velocity, peak systolic velocity, and cerebral flow index showed no significant heterogeneity. For the ACA and PCA, MBFV also increased (ACA: SMD = 1.69; PCA: SMD = 1.38), while PI and RI decreased (all P < 0.001), despite substantial heterogeneity (I² > 92%).ConclusionsThe significant differences in TCD parameters between women with pre-eclampsia and normotensive controls underscore the association between pre-eclampsia and maternal cerebrovascular alterations.Clinic trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08583-z.

Background

Pre-eclampsia (PE), characterized by new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation along with end-organ dysfunction (including proteinuria, hepatic or renal impairment, and neurological symptoms), affects 5% to 7% of women during pregnancy and leads to over 500,000 fetal deaths and 70,000 maternal deaths globally each year [1]. The pathogenesis is not fully clarified. It is thought to arise from an ischemic placenta, which releases antiangiogenic factors into the maternal circulation and subsequently causes endothelial impairment and multi-organ failure among pregnant women [2]. Women with PE exhibit neuroinflammation and blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption [3]. Hemodynamic changes in cerebral circulation, as compensatory responses, may provide more direct additional indicative information. Transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasonography noninvasively measures hemodynamic parameters in transcranial arteries, providing sensitive detection of cerebrovascular autoregulation dysfunction. Clinically, TCD allows dynamic monitoring of cerebral vasospasm or hyperperfusion during pregnancy, with the resistance index (RI) and pulsatility index (PI) of the middle cerebral artery (MCA) demonstrating particular prognostic value for PE. However, limitations remain, including substantial heterogeneity across study populations, a lack of standardized examination protocols, and limited consensus on the interpretation of hemodynamic parameters of the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) and posterior cerebral artery (PCA). Moreover, findings across studies are inconsistent. For example, Zunker, Zatik, Riskin-Mashiah, et al. reported elevated mean blood flow velocity (MBFV) in the MCA of PE patients [4–6]. However, Williams, Vanveen, Janzarik, et al. found no substantial difference in MCA-MBFV between women with PE and healthy pregnant women [7–9]. By integrating multi-center data through a systematic review and meta-analysis (MA), this research aimed to estimate the relationship between TCD parameters and PE, thereby offering an evidence base for its precise clinical management. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist.

Methods

The present MA was carried out following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines [10], and was prospectively registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251074935). The reporting of outcomes adhered to the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines [11]. Since all data were obtained from published studies, informed consent and ethical approval were waived.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were observational studies (including retrospective or prospective cohort, cross-sectional, and case-control studies) that assessed any TCD parameters (peak systolic velocity [PSV], MBFV, end-diastolic velocity [EDV], cerebral flow index [CFI], PI, and RI) of MCA, ACA, and PCA in pregnant women with and without PE. Exclusion criteria included: (i) posters, conference abstracts, case reports, review papers, or animal studies; (ii) studies lacking a control group, full-text availability, complete data, or published in non-English; and (iii) studies involving women with PE complications, including hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count syndrome or eclampsia, were excluded to ensure sample homogeneity.

Literature search

Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane, and PubMed were comprehensively searched from inception to May 31, 2024. PubMed was used as an example to illustrate the search strategy, where Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms were combined with free-text keywords through Boolean operators (“AND,” “OR,” “NOT”). The search algorithm included both MeSH terms and keywords: ((Middle Cerebral Artery[Mesh] OR Anterior Cerebral Artery[Mesh] OR Posterior Cerebral Artery[Mesh]) OR (“Ultrasonography, Doppler, Transcranial” [Mesh]) AND (Pre-Eclampsia[MeSH Terms] OR “eclamptic toxaemia” OR “preclampsia”). No restrictions were applied to the publication year. Reference lists of eligible articles were also screened to identify additional relevant articles. The full search strategy is presented in the Supplemental files (Appendix 1).

Study selection and data collection

The titles, abstracts, and keywords of the retrieved literature were screened by two independent reviewers (Wang J and Tian T). The full texts of potentially relevant articles were then assessed to determine eligibility according to the predetermined eligibility criteria. Finally, reference lists of selected studies were carefully reviewed to include eligible articles. The two reviewers independently extracted the data from the selected studies using a pre-designed data extraction form. The collected information contained: country, study design, eligibility criteria, name of first author, timing of measurement, publication year, mean maternal age, ultrasound characteristics, gestational age at sampling, and number of patients. The primary author entered the data, which were then verified by the secondary author. Outcomes of interest comprised TCD parameters of the MCA, ACA, and PCA in pre-eclamptic and healthy pregnant women, including MBFV, cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), EDV, PSV, PI, RI, CFI, and resistance area product (RAP). Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by the corresponding author acting as a senior reviewer.

Quality assessment

The quality of included studies was appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for case-control and cohort studies, and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for cross-sectional studies. Case-control studies were evaluated for risk of bias (RoB) in the comparability of groups, ascertainment of exposure, selection of cases and controls, and non-response rate. Cohort studies were assessed for outcome assessment, selection and comparability of exposed and non-exposed cohorts, and adequacy of the follow-up period. Cross-sectional studies were assessed by the JBI checklist, considering non-response rate, sample representativeness, assessment of outcomes, sample size, comparability, ascertainment of exposure, as well as statistical test. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were addressed by the corresponding author through deliberation or discernment. NOS thresholds followed Wells et al. (2014): ≥ 7 stars = high quality; 5–6 = moderate; ≤ 4 = low. JBI items were recorded as “met” or “unmet.”

Statistical analysis

The MA was carried out using STATA version 15. Between-study heterogeneity was examined by the I² statistic, with I² > 50% suggesting significant heterogeneity. When I² > 50%, a random-effects model was utilized to pool effect sizes, whereas a fixed-effects model was adopted if I² < 50%. Standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to quantify the relative differences in intracranial vascular parameters between women with PE and healthy pregnant women. This approach accounted for variations in units and absolute values of the outcome indicators across the included studies [12, 13]. A positive SMD indicated higher Doppler indices in women with PE compared with normotensive women, and a negative SMD indicated the opposite. Confidence intervals (CIs) were set at 95%. Changes in the pooled results and the impact of individual studies on the MA outcomes were examined through sensitivity analyses via a leave-one-out approach. Publication bias was estimated qualitatively and quantitatively using funnel plots and Egger’s test, respectively [14]. Moreover, meta-regression and subgroup analysis were carried out to find potential sources of heterogeneity, which were categorized according to study type [15]. The p-value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Literature selection process

The study screening process is illustrated in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1). Overall, 3,382 records were initially identified, of which 3,355 were excluded after screening titles and abstracts. Full texts of the remaining 27 articles were assessed for eligibility, and five studies were subsequently excluded [16–19]. Ultimately, 22 studies were included, comprising 1,625 women. Among them, 643 were diagnosed with PE and 982 served as healthy pregnant controls.

Characteristics of the included studies

The characteristics (gestational age at assessment, country, examined parameters, ultrasound characteristics, and study design) of the included studies are provided in Appendix 2. Most studies were conducted in European countries (including Hungary, the United Kingdom, Germany, and the Netherlands), followed by North American countries (including Canada and the United States). The mean age of the included individuals ranged from 24 to 39 years, and publication years spanned 1997 to 2025. Most studies (63.6%) employed a case-control design, whereas four studies (18.2%) applied a cross-sectional design, and four studies (18.2%) were prospective cohort studies. Most studies evaluated TCD parameters during the 3rd trimester of pregnancy. 2 MHz probes were utilized to assess ultrasound indices.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was appraised using design-specific critical appraisal tools: the NOS for cohort and case-control studies, and the JBI checklist for analytical cross-sectional studies. All studies were considered low RoB based on the NOS and JBI assessments. Quality assessment results for the included studies are illustrated in Supplemental files (Appendix 3). Overall, the included studies were of high quality, with 19 studies (86.4%) judged to have a low RoB. Three studies (13.6%) (Demarin 1997, Ohno 1997, Willians 1998) were considered to have a moderate RoB due to primary limitations of inadequate adjustment for gestational age and comorbidities (comparability domain) and unclear blinding during TCD assessment (exposure ascertainment). No studies were classified as having a high RoB.

Pooled effect sizes on TCD parameters

The forest plots of all TCD parameters are presented in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9. Women with PE exhibited significantly higher MCA-MBFV (SMD = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.74 to 1.60, P < 0.001, I²= 90.4%), MCA-PSV (SMD = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.59, P = 0.038, I² = 0%), MCA-EDV (SMD = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.62 to 1.22, P = 0.038, I² = 16.7%), MCA-CPP (SMD = 1.82, 95% CI: 0.78 to 2.85, P = 0.001, I²= 94.6%), MCA-CFI (SMD = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.34 to 1.59, P = 0.002, I² =84.3%), and MCA-RAP (SMD = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.22 to 1.71, P = 0.011, I² = 89.8%) compared with controls. In contrast, MCA-PI and MCA-RI were significantly lower in women with PE compared with controls (PI: SMD = -1.52, 95% CI: -2.40 to -0.65, P = 0.001, I² =95.6%; RI: SMD = -1.86, 95% CI: -3.02 to -0.71, P = 0.002, I² = 94.7%).

ACA-MBFV and PCA-MBFV were significantly elevated in women with PE than controls (ACA: SMD = 1.69, 95% CI: 0.55 to 2.83, P = 0.004, I²= 95.5%; PCA: SMD = 1.38, 95% CI: 0.55 to 2.22, P = 0.001, I²= 92.7%). For the PI and RI of the PCA, the pooled SMDs were − 2.11 (95% CI: -3.91 to -0.31, P = 0.022, I²= 96.8%) and − 2.72 (95% CI: -5.15 to -0.28, P = 0.029, I²= 97%). Overall, PI and RI of the intracranial arteries were significantly lower in women with PE compared with controls.

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression

The influence of predefined factors on pooled estimates and the sources of heterogeneity were assessed by subgroup analyses and meta-regression. These factors encompassed study design (case-control, prospective cohort, and cross-sectional), geographical region (Europe, America, and Asia), maternal positioning during TCD examination (supine, left lateral, and sitting), as well as gestational age at assessment (second trimester and third trimester).

Regarding geographical differences, stronger MBFV alterations observed in European cohorts may reflect ethnic-specific cerebrovascular adaptations or technical discrepancies. For gestational dynamics, the pronounced MBFV elevation during the third trimester aligned with the theory of progressive hemodynamic compensation in PE. For maternal positioning, the enhanced sensitivity of PI in the left lateral position supported standardizing this posture in protocols. Concerning study design, lower heterogeneity in prospective cohort studies underscored their reliability for future validation. Subgroup analysis results of all TCD parameters are illustrated in Appendix 4.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

The sensitivity analysis via a leave-one-out approach demonstrated that the overall results for MBFV, PSV, EDV, PI, RI, and CFI of the MCA, ACA, and PCA remained stable, as the omission of any single study did not alter the statistical significance (Fig. 10). No significant publication bias for MCA-PSV (p = 0.128), MCA-EDV (p = 0.995), MCA-CPP (p = 0.100), MCA-CFI (p = 0.475), MCA-RAP (p = 0.535), ACA-MBFV (p = 0.098), ACA-PI (p = 0.296), ACA-RI (p = 0.209), PCA-MBFV (p = 0.123), PCA-PI (p = 0.092), and PCA-RI (p = 0.071) was found by Egger’s test and Funnel Plot (Fig. 11). Nevertheless, it should be noted that assessment of publication bias has inherent limitations, and undetected bias may still exist. Some evidence of publication bias was observed for MCA-MBFV (p = 0.031), MCA-PI (p = 0.002), and MCA-RI (p = 0.007), which may slightly overestimate the associations. However, after applying the trim-and-fill method, the results remained stable, indicating that the findings retain reference significance for clinical practice.

Discussion

PE, as a life-threatening pregnancy disorder found only in human beings, is a main cause of neonatal and maternal morbidity and mortality [20]. It is mainly initiated by abnormal placental development, leading to widespread maternal endothelial dysfunction [19–21]. This process impairs cerebral autoregulation, which is highly relevant to TCD findings of this MA [22].

Our results indicated significant elevations in MBFV and reductions in PI and RI of the intracranial arteries in pregnant women with PE. In normal pregnancy, cerebral blood flow is slightly decreased or remains unchanged, despite elevations in blood volume and cardiac output, reflecting the normal autoregulatory function of the cerebral arterioles [5, 23, 24]. Under the context of PE, vascular endothelial dysfunction occurs, and the balance between vasoconstrictive factors and vasodilatory factors is disrupted, leading to abnormal cerebral vasoconstriction [24, 25]. The changes in TCD parameters reflect impaired compensatory dilatory capacity of cerebral blood vessels and altered resistance status of the distal microvascular bed. This further confirms that the pathological process of PE not only affects the tone of large blood vessels but also affects microcirculation, collectively contributing to cerebrovascular dysregulation. Changes in these parameters provide a dynamic observation window for understanding cerebrovascular complications in PE.

Current evidence indicates that PE-related cerebrovascular dysregulation constitutes a progressive pathophysiological process rather than an abrupt occurrence at the onset of symptoms. These changes in TCD parameters may occur earlier than classic clinical manifestations, such as hypertension and proteinuria, offering a potential time window for early warning. A longitudinal study demonstrated that abnormally elevated MBFV in the MCA can be detected as early as mid-pregnancy in some women who ultimately developed PE. This latent hemodynamic change is synchronized with the progression of systemic endothelial dysfunction caused by placental-derived factors and may precede significant clinical signs in target organs, such as the kidneys and liver. Therefore, TCD-monitored changes in cerebral blood flow can be regarded as an early intermediate link between placental pathology with clinical outcomes in the central nervous system.

In current studies, the MCA is the preferred target for evaluating cerebrovascular function in pregnant women with PE. As the largest terminal branch of the internal carotid artery, the MCA supplies approximately 2/3 of the cerebral hemisphere, covering critical regions, such as the basal ganglia, internal capsule, and most of the cerebral cortex. This extensive perfusion area renders it extremely sensitive to hemodynamic changes. When PE induces systemic vasospasm and impairs cerebrovascular autoregulation, the MCA territory is most vulnerable, with the most significant hemodynamic alterations. In contrast, the ACA and PCA have smaller blood supply areas. Additionally, the main trunk of the MCA runs straight, and the temporal window is ideal for ultrasonic detection, allowing TCD to obtain stable and reproducible blood flow spectra with minimal angle error. In comparison, the ACA and PCA have more tortuous anatomical courses, larger detection angles, and greater difficulty in signal acquisition. Their measurement results are easily affected by the operator’s experience, leading to relatively poor consistency and reliability of the data. As a result, there are more studies on the MCA than on the ACA and PCA.

At present, there are many prediction models for PE. The most commonly used clinical prediction model is the Fetal Medicine Foundation first-trimester multi-factor combined model, which includes mean arterial blood pressure, ethnic background, uterine artery pulsatility index (UtA-PI) on ultrasonography, medical and obstetric history, maternal age, maternal circulating placental growth factor (PIGF) levels at 11–13 weeks of gestation, and weight and height to examine the individual risk of PE [26]. Second-trimester models, including the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio, may serve as complementary tools. TCD, as a functional hemodynamic assessment tool, is highly complementary with existing predictive markers. During early pregnancy, maternal factors and serum biomarkers can be used for initial screening. For high-risk populations, dynamic TCD monitoring from mid to late pregnancy may help more accurately identify extremely high-risk pregnancies, who present not only placental dysfunction but also compensatory cerebrovascular dysregulation, approaching clinical onset. This dual assessment strategy of “placental-maternal vascular response” holds promise for refining PE prediction from simple risk evaluation to dynamic prediction of pathophysiological progression and clinical onset timing, thereby providing critical evidence for individualized intervention timing.

In previous TCD studies targeting individuals with PE, Riskin-Mashiah et al. found that high MBFV, along with low PI and RI of the MCA, were associated with compensatory cerebral vasodilatation in women with PE before the onset of cerebral symptoms [6, 27]. Their results align with our study. Lee et al. demonstrated that PI and RI values were significantly decreased among individuals with PE (P < 0.05), and MBFV was significantly elevated only in the PCA of individuals with PE (P = 0.040 and 0.028, respectively) [28], which differs from our study. Their results revealed that PCA may be linked to cerebral symptoms in PE women. Martínez et al. indicated that there was no substantial difference in the RI values of intracranial arteries between individuals with PE and healthy pregnant women before delivery, with differences observed only in some arteries on days 7 and 30 during the postpartum follow-up. In the present study, MBFV of intracranial arteries in PE patients was significantly increased, while PI and RI were significantly decreased. These differences may stem from the heterogeneity among the included studies, variations in patient characteristics, differences in measurement techniques, or measurements conducted at different gestational weeks. A study involving 165 women during pregnancy reported cerebral hemodynamic alterations in the anterior circulation of PE patients, which resolved by one month postpartum following assessments at different time points during pregnancy and the puerperium [29]. Our study did not include TCD parameters of intracranial vessels in postpartum PE patients, as relevant research on the postpartum period was limited. Future studies could focus on this aspect to better understand the remission of PE.

The substantial heterogeneity (I² > 90%) observed in the parameters of cerebral arteries warrants caution. It can be attributed to variations in vascular calibration angles during measurement, differences in gestational age, and variations in study methods. A comprehensive approach was adopted for literature search and selection, data extraction, as well as quality assessment in the present study. Nevertheless, some limitations still exist. Firstly, as individual patient data from the included studies were unavailable, outcomes were aggregated at the study level. Secondly, insufficient data precluded analysis of the impact of cranial artery Doppler on estimating PE severity at different stages. Third, owing to the limited available covariates and the small number of studies within each potential subgroup, neither meta-regression nor subgroup analysis successfully identified the sources of heterogeneity. Fourth, although the pooled results may provide evidence for selecting TCD parameters in detecting PE, additional well-designed prospective diagnostic studies in the early stages of pregnancy are needed to estimate the specificity, sensitivity, and accuracy of these TCD parameters.

While our MA established TCD-derived intracranial parameters as promising biomarkers for cerebral compensation in PE, multicenter prospective diagnostic trials are required to validate their predictive utility as standalone measures. Further research is needed to examine the association between intracranial results and uterine artery Doppler to better identify patients at risk of PE. It is recommended that TCD parameters be incorporated into validated high-performance prediction models to capture complementary pathophysiological pathways.

Conclusion

In this study, MBFV, PI, and RI of the three cerebral arteries demonstrated significant differences between PE patients and healthy pregnant controls. Further research is warranted to examine the combined utility of these TCD parameters and their predictive performance, thereby offering stronger evidence supporting both clinical application and scientific exploration.
