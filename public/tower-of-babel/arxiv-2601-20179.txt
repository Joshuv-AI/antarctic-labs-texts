Asymptotic Betti bounds for hypersurfaces in a singular variety
Xuanyu Pan, Dingxin Zhang, Xiping Zhang

Abstract
. We show that for any degree d d hypersurface Y ⊆ X Y\subseteq X in a possibly singular projective variety X X , the total Betti number of Y Y is bounded by 3 ​ deg ⁡ ( X ) ⋅ d n + C ⋅ d n − 1 3\deg(X)\cdot d^{n}+C\cdot d^{n-1} for some explicit constant C > 0 C>0 independent of d d and Y Y . When X X is a local complete intersection, the bound improves to deg ⁡ ( X ) ⋅ d n + C ⋅ d n − 1 \deg(X)\cdot d^{n}+C\cdot d^{n-1} . In this case, the bound is asymptotically sharp. Similar bounds are also established for general constructible sheaves.

Introduction

1.1.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of arbitrary
characteristic. (Topologists may take

k=\mathbf{C}
.) Let
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{N}
be a possibly
singular projective variety over
k
.
The main aim of this paper is to establish a uniform upper bound for
the total Betti number

B(Y,\mathbf{F}_{\ell})=\sum_{i}\dim\mathrm{H}^{i}(Y;\mathbf{F}_{\ell}),

where

•

\ell
is a prime number invertible in
k
,

•

\mathbf{F}_{\ell}
is the finite field with
\ell
elements,

\mathrm{H}^{\ast}(-,\mathbf{F}_{\ell})
denotes étale
cohomology, and

•

Y\subseteq X
is the intersection of
X
with a
degree
d
hypersurface in
\mathbf{P}^{N}
.

We are particularly interested in the asymptotic behavior of

B_{X}(d)\coloneq\sup\left\{B(Y,\mathbf{F}_{\ell}):Y\text{ is a degree }d\text{ hypersurface in }X\right\}

as
d\to\infty
.

When
k=\mathbf{C}
, the étale cohomology groups above are
isomorphic to the singular cohomology of the corresponding complex
analytic space with coefficients in
\mathbf{F}_{\ell}
. In
particular,

B(Y,\mathbf{F}_{\ell})=\sum_{i}\dim_{\mathbf{F}_{\ell}}\mathrm{H}^{i}(Y^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{F}_{\ell})\geqslant\sum_{i}\dim_{\mathbf{Q}}\mathrm{H}^{i}(Y^{\mathrm{an}};\mathbf{Q}).

Thus, bounds for
B(Y,\mathbf{F}_{\ell})
truly
concern the topology of algebraic varieties.

Beyond its intrinsic topological interest, a high-quality uniform
upper bound has applications in the arithmetic of algebraic varieties
and in analytic number theory.

1.2.

When

, this problem was studied by Milnor
[
Mil64
]
and
Thom
[
Tho65
]
for complex varieties (for singular cohomology
with coefficients in

[

in general. Many others have contributed as well. In the context of random real

[
Anc23
]

More recently, Hu and Teyssier
[
HT25
]
studied the asymptotics of Betti numbers
from a different perspective, using ramification theory.

In

]

[

asymptotically optimal bounds were established. There exists a

Moreover, the constant

[

]

we also obtain asymptotically optimal bounds for the Betti numbers of
complete intersections in affine or projective space.

In this paper, we establish analogous bounds for hypersurfaces in an

arbitrary
projective variety

complete intersection, the quality of our upper bound matches that in

(
\Leftarrow

Suppose

\Leftarrow

Suppose

2.
How to obtain Betti bounds?

We briefly survey existing methods for bounding Betti numbers,
analyzing their strengths and limitations. We then explain what’s new
in our approach. Readers not interested in these technical
discussions may wish to proceed directly to

3.1.1

Method 2.1. Chain-level counting
[
Bom66
,
Bom78
,
AS87
]
.

In the Morse theory approach used by Milnor and Thom, the strategy is
to construct a Morse function on
X
and estimate Betti numbers by
counting the critical points, which can be bounded via Bézout’s
theorem. At first glance, this method appears too topological to
extend to varieties over fields of positive characteristic, and, as we
now know, the bounds given by Milnor and Thom are non-optimal even for
complex varieties. Nevertheless, the underlying “chain-level
counting” philosophy can be adapted to the framework of Dwork theory
and rigid cohomology.

By a standard reduction (“Cayley’s trick”), the problem of
estimating the Betti numbers of a variety defined by a regular
function can be reduced to estimating the Betti numbers of certain isocrystals associated to
certain exponential sums.

For exponential sums over an algebraic torus,
Bombieri
[
Bom78
]
and
Adolphson–Sperber
[
AS87
]

construct explicit chain complexes that compute the associated
cohomology, along with a detailed description of the Frobenius
action at the chain level. This explicitness allows them to estimate
the number of Frobenius eigenvalues with small
p
-adic slopes
directly from the chain complex. Because there are a priori upper bounds
on the slopes of Frobenius acting on cohomology, and since the
chain-level Frobenius is so well understood in this context, one obtains
sharp and effective upper bounds for the Betti numbers.

The primary limitation of this method is its dependence on detailed knowledge
of the infinite matrix representing the chain-level Frobenius, which is so
far achievable only for certain special F-isocrystals over tori or Shimura
curves of PEL type, where excellent Frobenius liftings are available.

Method 2.2. Euler characteristic trick
[
Kat01
,
Zha25
]
.

In
[
Kat01
]
, Katz used the weak Lefschetz theorem to develop
a method that reduces estimating Betti numbers to the problem of
bounding Euler characteristics. The essential idea is that if one has
a uniform upper bound for the Euler characteristic for
all

hypersurfaces, and the
n
-dimensional affine variety
X
is
sufficiently mild in its singularities so that the weak Lefschetz
theorem applies, then the difference between the Euler characteristics
of
X
and a hyperplane section
X\cap L
gives an upper bound
for the middle Betti number of
X
. The remaining Betti numbers can
then be controlled by induction using the weak Lefschetz theorem. (We
have re-implemented this strategy in Lemma
7.5
.)

A prerequisite for this method is the existence of a uniform,
effective upper bound for the Euler characteristic. In loc. cit.,
Katz obtained such bounds by using results of Bombieri and
Adolphson–Sperber, who employed Dwork theory (
p
-adic methods) to
estimate the Euler characteristic. Additionally, a certain perversity
condition is required to ensure that the weak Lefschetz theorem
applies; see Lemma
3.2.7
and
Lemma
3.2.8
.

Beside the applicability of weak Lefschetz, the above prerequisite on
Euler characteristic is also the main limitation of this approach. In
the cases Katz treated, such bounds were established via Dwork theory
for tori. In turn, this boils down to establishing a sharp lower
bound for the Newton polygon associated to the Fredholm determinant of
the chain-level Frobenius operator discussed in
Method
2.1
.

Method 2.3. Perverse degeneration
[
WZ25a
]
.

When uniform upper bounds for the Euler characteristic are
unavailable, the above method cannot be applied. However, if the weak
Lefschetz theorem remains valid, one can instead focus on estimating
the dimension of the cohomology that does not arise from hyperplane
sections. For example, if the variety has local complete intersection
singularities, the shifted constant sheaf is perverse, so the problem
reduces to bounding the middle-dimensional cohomology.

In this context, the so-called perverse degeneration lemma

[
Kat03
,
WZ25a
]

is very useful. This lemma allows us to control the middle-dimensional
cohomology of an affine hypersurface by relating it to that of a nearby
hypersurface. Once the middle-dimensional cohomology is bounded,
the weak Lefschetz theorem, together with perversity, allows us to
handle the cohomology in the remaining degrees by induction.

By combining the weak Lefschetz theorem with the perverse degeneration
lemma, one arrives at the following strategy for obtaining Betti number
bounds for hypersurfaces:

•

For a
general
hypersurface
Y
, the Euler characteristic
usually can be computed explicitly (see Lemma
5.5
).
The weak Lefschetz theorem then allows
one to determine the middle cohomology group precisely for such
Y
.

•

The perverse degeneration lemma provides uniform bounds for the
middle-dimensional cohomology of
arbitrary
hypersurfaces, not only
the general ones.

•

Finally, applying the weak Lefschetz theorem inductively (when possible)
enables control of the remaining (non-middle) cohomology groups, provided
that appropriate perversity conditions are satisfied.

Method
2.3
works well if the constant sheaf is a
perverse sheaf, but is insufficient for Theorem
1
. In
this case, the shifted constant sheaf is generally not perverse, nor
is its restriction to an arbitrary hypersurface. Consequently, there
may remain cohomological degrees that are not accessible
simultaneously via the weak Lefschetz theorem and the perverse
degeneration lemma.

2.4. New methods in this paper.

The approach in this paper is similar to
Method
2.3
, but introduces two new ingredients.

The first is more conceptual: rather than proving bounds for constant
sheaves, we work with general
perverse
sheaves, see
Theorem
5.3
. This shift in perspective allows us to
apply the perverse weak Lefschetz theorem to control most cohomology
groups. Bounds for constant sheaves are then deduced from the
perverse case via dévissage. See Theorem
6.2
.

The second new ingredient is more technical: the Proper Degeneration
Lemma
4.1
. When perversity conditions
hold, this lemma is as effective as the perverse degeneration lemma,
but it has the advantage of applying to cohomology in
all

degrees, not just the middle one. However, this greater generality
leads to less optimal bounds.

Except in §

3.2.
Perverse sheaves

In this subsection, we present some properties of perverse sheaves
that will be used throughout this paper.

3.2.1.

For basic facts about perverse sheaves, we refer the reader to

[
BBDG
]
and

[
KW01
]
. The

t
-structure on
D^{b}_{c}(X,\Lambda)
associated with the middle
perversity is denoted by

({}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\leqslant 0}_{c}(X,\Lambda),{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\geqslant 0}_{c}(X,\Lambda))
. We use the notations

{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{n}
,
{}^{\mathrm{p}}\tau_{\leqslant}
, and
similar conventions as in loc. cit. Constructible complexes in

{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\leqslant 0}_{c}(X,\Lambda)
are called
perverse
connective
, and those in
{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\geqslant 0}_{c}(X,\Lambda)

are called
perverse coconnective
. The duality functor

\mathbf{D}_{X}
interchanges the connective and coconnective parts
(see
[
BBDG
, 2.2 and 4.3.1]
).

To bound the total Betti numbers of a constructible complex,
we first make a straightforward reduction to the case of perverse
sheaves:

Lemma 3.2.2
.

Let
V
be a variety and
\iota\colon Z\to V
a closed
subvariety. Then for any
\mathcal{F}\in D^{b}_{c}(V,\Lambda)
,
we have

B(Z,\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{F})\leqslant\sum_{i\in\mathbf{Z}}B\bigl(Z,\iota^{\ast}({}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{i}(\mathcal{F}))\bigr).

Proof.

Given any constructible complex
\mathcal{F}
, there exist integers

a\leqslant b
such that

{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{i}(\mathcal{F})=0
unless
a\leqslant i\leqslant b
.
We proceed by induction on the integer
b-a
, which we refer to as the
length of
\mathcal{F}
.

If
b-a=0
, then, up to shift,
\mathcal{F}
itself is a perverse
sheaf. In this case, the result is immediate.

For the induction step, let
b
be the largest index such that

{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{b}(\mathcal{F})\neq 0
.
There is a distinguished triangle

{}^{\mathrm{p}}\tau_{\leqslant b-1}\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}\to{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{b}(\mathcal{F}),

which, after applying
\iota^{\ast}
, yields

\iota^{\ast}({}^{\mathrm{p}}\tau_{\leqslant b-1}\mathcal{F})\to\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\to\iota^{\ast}({}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{b}(\mathcal{F})).

Therefore,

B(Z,\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{F})\leqslant B(Z,\iota^{\ast}({}^{\mathrm{p}}\tau_{\leqslant b-1}\mathcal{F}))+B(Z,\iota^{\ast}({}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{b}(\mathcal{F}))).

The length of
{}^{\mathrm{p}}\tau_{\leqslant b-1}\mathcal{F}
is
smaller than that of
\mathcal{F}
, so we can apply the inductive
hypothesis to this term. Thus, by induction, the lemma is proved.
∎

Next, recall the following fundamental fact:

Theorem 3.2.3

(Artin’s vanishing theorem)
.

Let
\pi\colon X\to S
be an affine morphism of varieties.
Let
\mathcal{F}\in{}^{\mathrm{p}}D_{c}^{\geqslant 0}(X,\Lambda)
be perverse
coconnective on
X
. Then
R\pi_{!}\mathcal{F}
is perverse
coconnective on
S
.

Proof.

See
[
SGA“,4
, Exposé XIV, 3.1]
or

[
BBDG
, Théorème 4.1.1]
.
∎

We then prove some elementary properties about how perversity changes under
some special operations.

Lemma 3.2.4
.

Let
f\colon U\to V
be an étale and surjective morphism. Let

\mathcal{F}\in D^{b}_{c}(V,\Lambda)
. Suppose

f^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\in{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\leqslant 0}_{c}(U,\Lambda)

(resp. in
{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\geqslant 0}_{c}(U,\Lambda)
, or is perverse).
Then
\mathcal{F}
lies in
{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\leqslant 0}_{c}(V,\Lambda)

(resp. in
{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\geqslant 0}_{c}(V,\Lambda)
, or is perverse).

Proof.

We treat only the case of
{}^{\mathrm{p}}D_{c}^{\leqslant 0}
; the other
cases follow from Verdier duality and the fact that for an étale
morphism
f
, we have
f^{!}=f^{*}
.

Since
f
is surjective, for any generic point
\eta
of

\operatorname{Supp}\mathcal{H}^{i}(\mathcal{F})
, there exists a
point
\eta_{U}
lying above it. The support

\operatorname{Supp}\mathcal{H}^{i}(f^{*}\mathcal{F})
contains the
closure of
\eta_{U}
, which has the same dimension as the closure of

\eta
, by the étale property. Outside the closures of these

\eta_{U}
, the stalks of
\mathcal{H}^{i}(f^{*}\mathcal{F})
are zero.
Therefore,

\dim\operatorname{Supp}\mathcal{H}^{i}(f^{*}\mathcal{F})=\dim\operatorname{Supp}\mathcal{H}^{i}(\mathcal{F}).

Thus, if
\mathcal{F}
is connective in the perverse sense, so is

f^{*}\mathcal{F}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a perverse sheaf on a variety
V
. Let

\iota\colon D\to V
be the inclusion morphism of a Cartier
divisor. Then

{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{e}(\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{P}[-1])=0

for
e\neq 0,1
, and

{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{e}(i^{!}\mathcal{P}[1])=0
for

e\neq 0,-1
.

Proof.

The second statement follows from the first for

\mathbf{D}_{V}(\mathcal{P})
. So it suffices to prove the first
statement.

Let
j\colon U\to V
be the inclusion morphism of the complement of

D
. Then
j
is both affine and quasi-finite, hence
j_{!}
is
t-exact
(
[
KW01
, Corollary III 6.2]
).
Thus,
j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{P}
is a perverse sheaf on
V
.
Applying
{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{\ast}
to the excision
triangle

\mathcal{P}[-1]\to\iota_{\ast}\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{P}[-1]\to j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{P},

gives the following exact sequence

{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{e-1}(\mathcal{P})\to{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{e}(\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{P}[-1])\to{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{e}(j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{P}).

Since
\mathcal{P}
and
j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{P}
are perverse,
the result follows.
∎

Lemma 3.2.6
.

Let
V\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{N}
be a quasi-projective variety.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a constructible complex on
V
.
Then for a general hyperplane section
\iota\colon L\hookrightarrow V
,
we have

\iota^{\ast}({}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{i}(\mathcal{F})[-1])\simeq{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{i}(\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[-1]).

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the length of
\mathcal{F}
, as in the proof
of Lemma
3.2.2
. If the
length is zero, then after a shift, we can assume
\mathcal{F}
is a
perverse sheaf. By Lemma
3.2.5
,

\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[-1]
is perverse coconnective
and
R\iota^{!}\mathcal{F}[1]
is perverse connective.
Since
L
is general, the purity isomorphism holds; that is,
there is an isomorphism

\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[-1](-1)\xrightarrow{\sim}\iota^{!}\mathcal{F}[1].

See
[
WZ25
, Lemma 3.2]
. The claim then follows from
Lemma
3.2.5
.

For general
\mathcal{F}
, we first apply a shift and reduce to the
case
\mathcal{F}\in{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\leqslant 0}_{c}(V,\Lambda)
.
There is a distinguished triangle

{}^{\mathrm{p}}\tau_{\leqslant-1}\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}\to{}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{0}(\mathcal{F}).

Applying
\iota^{\ast}[-1]
yields

\iota^{\ast}({}^{\mathrm{p}}\tau_{\leqslant-1}\mathcal{F})[-1]\to\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[-1]\to\iota^{\ast}({}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{0}(\mathcal{F}))[-1].

For general
L
, the induction hypothesis shows that

\iota^{\ast}[-1]
commutes with the perverse cohomology sheaves of

{}^{\mathrm{p}}\tau_{\leqslant-1}(\mathcal{F})
, which are also the
perverse cohomology sheaves of
\mathcal{F}
. In particular,

\iota^{\ast}({}^{\mathrm{p}}\tau_{\leqslant-1}(\mathcal{F}))[-1]
still
lies in
{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\leqslant-1}_{c}(L,\Lambda)
. Therefore,

\iota^{\ast}({}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{0}(\mathcal{F}))[-1]={}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathcal{H}^{0}(\iota^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[-1]).

This completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we recapitulate some weak Lefschetz theorems for
perverse sheaves. The first one is just a consequence of Artin’s vanishing theorem.

Lemma 3.2.7
.

Let
X
be a projective variety and suppose

\mathcal{F}\in{}^{\mathrm{p}}D_{c}^{\geqslant 0}(X,\Lambda)
is perverse
coconnective. Then for any ample hypersurface
Y
in
X
, the
restriction map

\mathrm{H}^{m}(X;\mathcal{F})\to\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y;\mathcal{F}|_{Y})

is injective for
m=-1
, and bijective for
m\leqslant-2
.

Proof.

Since
X\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}Y
is affine, Artin’s vanishing theorem
(Theorem
3.2.3
) implies

\mathrm{H}^{m}_{c}(X\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}Y;\mathcal{F})=0
for

m\leqslant-1
. The lemma then follows from the standard long exact
sequence associated to the excision triangle.
∎

The second lemma is less straightforward. Ultimately, it relies on
Deligne’s generic base change theorem, which is used to apply
Artin’s vanishing theorem. As a result, it holds only for general
hyperplane sections.

Lemma 3.2.8

(Perverse weak Lefschetz theorem
[
WZ25
, Lemma 3.8]
)
.

Let
X
be an algebraic variety over an algebraically closed field

k
, and let
\iota\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{N}
be a quasi-finite
morphism. Suppose

\mathcal{F}\in{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\leqslant 0}_{c}(X,\Lambda)
is
perverse connective. Then, for a general hyperplane

B\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{N}
, the Gysin map

\mathrm{H}^{i-2}_{\mathrm{c}}\left(X\times_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}B;\mathcal{F}(-1)\right)\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{i}_{\mathrm{c}}(X;\mathcal{F})

is surjective for
i=1
and is an isomorphism for all
i\geqslant 2
.

Sketch of proof.

Set
\mathcal{G}=\mathbf{D}_{X}\mathcal{F}
. Then

\mathcal{G}\in{}^{\mathrm{p}}D^{\geqslant 0}_{c}(X,\Lambda)
.
By Deligne’s perverse weak Lefschetz theorem

[
Kat93
, Corollary A.5]
, for a
general hyperplane
B
, the restriction map

\mathrm{H}^{i}(X;\mathcal{G})\to\mathrm{H}^{i}(X\times_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}B;\mathcal{G}|_{B})

(3.2.8.1)

is injective if
i=-1
, and bijective if
i\leqslant-2
. Although
the statement in loc. cit. is for perverse sheaves, the proof only
requires that the complex is coconnective, which is sufficient in
order to apply Artin’s vanishing theorem.

Using Deligne’s generic base change
(
[
Del77
, Corollarie 2.9]
), one can show that for
general
B
, the morphism

\iota\colon X\times_{\mathbf{P}^{N}}B\to X
also satisfies

(see, for example,
[
WZ25
, Lemma 3.2]
for details).

The lemma then follows by applying Verdier duality to
(
3.2.8.1
), and then using (
3.2.8.2
).
∎

Theorem 3.3.3

(Index Formula)
.

Let
X
be a smooth and proper variety over
k
. Then for any
constructible complex
\mathcal{F}\in D^{b}_{c}(X,\Lambda)
, we have

\chi(X,\mathcal{F})=\int_{X}\mathrm{cc}_{X}(\mathcal{F})

where

\chi(X,\mathcal{F})=\sum(-1)^{i}\dim\mathrm{H}^{i}(X;\mathcal{F})

is the Euler characteristic of
\mathcal{F}
.

Remark 3.3.4
.

Over the field of complex numbers,
Schwartz
[
Sch65
,
Sch65a
]

and MacPherson
[
Mac74
]

introduced the Chern–Schwartz–MacPherson class for constructible
complexes, answering a conjecture of
Grothendieck
[
Gro22
, Note 87
1
]
.
This gives a group homomorphism

c_{X,\ast}(-)\colon K_{0}(D^{b}_{c}(X,\Lambda))\to\mathrm{H}^{-\ast}(X,Ra_{X}^{!}\Lambda),

where
a_{X}\colon X\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathbf{C}
is the
structural morphism. Following Sabbah’s conormal
construction
[

]
,
Kennedy
[

]
gives an algebraic
construction in characteristic

Chern–Schwartz–MacPherson class factors through the Chow group. In
the topological setting this was also done by

[

Takeshi Saito’s definition of characteristic classes in the étale
setting
[

]

(cf.
3.3.2
) follows the approach of
Ginzburg mentioned above. In characteristic zero, the characteristic class

\mathrm{cc}_{X}(\mathcal{F})
coincides with the “shadow”

\check{c}_{X}(\mathcal{F})
(a term coined by
Aluffi
[
Alu04
]
) of the characteristic cycle of

\mathcal{F}
, which differs from the Chern–Schwartz–MacPherson
class by a sign:

\mathrm{cc}_{X,i}(\mathcal{F})=\check{c}_{X,i}(\mathcal{F})=(-1)^{i}c_{X,i}(\mathcal{F}).

Remark 3.3.5
.

In characteristic zero, the Chern–Schwartz–MacPherson class
gives rise to a natural transformation from

to

for proper morphisms. In
positive characteristic, however, the characteristic class

\mathrm{cc}_{X}(\mathcal{F})
does not extend to a natural
transformation for all proper morphisms;
see
[

, Example 6.10]

For historical remarks about the index formula,
see
[

4.
The proper degeneration lemma

In this section, we prove the following Proper Degeneration
Lemma, which is a major technical engine of this paper.

Lemma 4.1

(Proper Degeneration Lemma)
.

Let
\mathbf{P}
be a proper variety over
k
. Let

{L}
be an invertible sheaf on
\mathbf{P}
. Let

\sigma_{0},\sigma_{\infty}
be two linearly independent elements
of
\mathrm{H}^{0}(\mathbf{P};{L})
. For

t=[u,v]\in\mathbf{P}^{1}
, let
Y_{t}\subseteq\mathbf{P}
be the divisor
defined by the section
u\sigma_{0}-v\sigma_{\infty}
. Let

B=Y_{0}\cap Y_{\infty}
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be an object of

D^{b}_{c}(\mathbf{P},\Lambda)
.

•

For a general
t\in\mathbf{P}^{1}
, and
any
m
, we have

\displaystyle\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{0};\mathcal{F})\leqslant\

\displaystyle\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{t};\mathcal{F})+\dim\mathrm{H}^{m-1}(Y_{t};\mathcal{F}(-1))+\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(\mathbf{P};\mathcal{F})

(4.1.1)

\displaystyle+\dim\operatorname{Coker}(\mathrm{H}^{m-2}(Y_{t};\mathcal{F}(-1))\to\mathrm{H}^{m-2}(B;\mathcal{F}(-1)))

\displaystyle+\dim\operatorname{Ker}(\mathrm{H}^{m-1}(Y_{t};\mathcal{F}(-1))\to\mathrm{H}^{m-1}(B;\mathcal{F}(-1)))

\displaystyle\leqslant\

\displaystyle\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{t};\mathcal{F})+2\dim\mathrm{H}^{m-1}(Y_{t};\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle+\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(\mathbf{P};\mathcal{F})+\dim\mathrm{H}^{m-2}(B;\mathcal{F}).

•

If we assume additionally that

\mathcal{F}\in{}^{\mathrm{p}}D_{c}^{\geqslant 0}(\mathbf{P},\Lambda)
, and

{L}
is ample, then for
m\leqslant-1
, and

t\in\mathbf{P}^{1}
sufficiently general, we have

\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{0};\mathcal{F})\leqslant\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{t};\mathcal{F})+\dim\mathrm{H}^{m-1}(Y_{t};\mathcal{F}(-1))+\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(\mathbf{P};\mathcal{F}).

(4.1.2)

Remark 4.2
.

The inequality (
4.1.2
) can be viewed as a proper
version of the perverse degeneration lemma for affine morphisms (see

[
Kat03
, Proposition 9]
,

[
WZ25a
, Lemma 3.1]
).

For the study of middle-dimensional cohomology, the perverse
degeneration lemma suffices; for example,
Theorem
5.3
(2) can be derived from it. However, since
we consider possibly highly singular varieties, it is necessary to
control cohomology groups beyond the middle dimension. In these
situations, standard arguments such as the weak Lefschetz theorem are
not sufficient. Instead, the more general (though weaker) estimate
given by (
4.1.1
) becomes essential.

Unlike the perverse degeneration lemma, where the total space of the
degeneration plays no role, the approach of
Lemma
4.1
links the cohomology of the
special (degenerating) hypersurface to both the cohomology of a
general fiber and that of the ambient variety.

Before proving the Proper Degeneration Lemma,
we first establish a few lemmas that will be used in making this
connection.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(S,s,\eta,\overline{\eta})
be the strict henselian trait with
residue field
k
, generic point
\eta
, and a fixed geometric

\overline{\eta}

\displaystyle\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{s};\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\leqslant\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{F})+\dim\mathrm{H}^{m-1}(Y_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{F}(-1))

\displaystyle\quad+\dim\operatorname{Im}(\mathrm{H}^{m}_{Y_{s}}(\mathcal{Y};\mathcal{F})\to\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{s};\mathcal{F})).

Proof.

By the proper base change theorem, we have

\mathrm{H}^{m}(\mathcal{Y};\mathcal{F})\simeq\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{s};\mathcal{F})
. The
relative exact sequence for the inclusions

\cdots\to\mathrm{H}^{m}_{Y_{s}}(\mathcal{Y};\mathcal{F})\to\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{s};\mathcal{F})\to\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{\eta};\mathcal{F})\to\cdots.

\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{s};\mathcal{F})\leqslant\dim\operatorname{Im}(\mathrm{H}^{m}_{Y_{s}}(\mathcal{Y};\mathcal{F})\to\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{s};\mathcal{F}))+\dim\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y_{\eta};\mathcal{F}).

(4.3.1)

Let

with respect to
\overline{\eta}

(4.3.2)

5.
Hypersurface restriction of perverse sheaves

5.1. Setup.

Throughout this section, let
\mathbf{P}
be a smooth projective
variety over
k
endowed with a very ample invertible sheaf

{L}
. For simplicity, we shall refer to the hypersurface
cut out by a section of
{L}^{\otimes d}
as a “degree

d
hypersurface” in
\mathbf{P}
. If

\mathcal{F}\in D^{b}_{c}(\mathbf{P},\Lambda)
, we write

\mathrm{cc}(\mathcal{F})\in\mathrm{CH}_{\ast}(\mathbf{P})

instead of
\mathrm{cc}_{\mathbf{P}}(\mathcal{F})

\mathrm{cc}_{j}(\mathcal{F})

where

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\in D^{b}_{c}(\mathbf{P},\Lambda)
be a
constructible complex of
\Lambda
-sheaves with support

S=\operatorname{Supp}(\mathcal{F})
, endowed with the reduced
structure. We associate three numbers to
\mathcal{F}

•

We define
n(\mathcal{F})
to be the dimension of
S
.

•

Let
S^{\prime}
be the union of irreducible components of

S
of dimension
n(\mathcal{F})
. We define

\delta(\mathcal{F})=\deg S^{\prime}
to be the degree of

S^{\prime}
with respect to
{L}
.

•

We define

r(\mathcal{F})=(-1)^{n(\mathcal{F})}\cdot\operatorname{rank}^{\circ}(\mathcal{F}|_{S})

to be the virtual generic rank of
\mathcal{F}|_{S}
times

(-1)^{n(\mathcal{F})}
.
(Definition
3.3.8
). Note that if

\mathcal{F}
is a nonzero perverse sheaf, then

.

The goal of this section is to give a proof of the following theorem.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let notation be as in
5.1
. Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a
perverse sheaf on
\mathbf{P}
. Then there exist constants

C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}>0
, independent of the choice of
Y
below, such
that

(1)

For any degree
d
hypersurface
Y\subseteq\mathbf{P}
,
we have

\dim\mathrm{H}^{-1}(Y;\mathcal{P}|_{Y})\leqslant r(\mathcal{P})\cdot\delta(\mathcal{P})\cdot d^{n(\mathcal{P})}+C_{1}\cdot d^{n(\mathcal{P})-1}.

(2)

For any degree
d
hypersurface
Y\subseteq\mathbf{P}
such
that
\mathcal{P}|_{Y}[-1]
is a perverse sheaf, we have

B(Y,\mathcal{P}|_{Y})\leqslant r(\mathcal{P})\cdot\delta(\mathcal{P})\cdot d^{n(\mathcal{P})}+C_{2}\cdot d^{n(\mathcal{P})-1}.

(3)

For any degree
d
hypersurface
Y\subseteq\mathbf{P}
whatsoever, we
have

B(Y,\mathcal{P}|_{Y})\leqslant 3\cdot r(\mathcal{P})\cdot\delta(\mathcal{P})\cdot d^{n(\mathcal{P})}+C_{3}\cdot d^{n(\mathcal{P})-1}.

.

As we will see, the constant

can be made quite explicit

), as it is only related to the
characteristic classes

of

\mathcal{P}
where

The constants

(see

), but they are ultimately determined by

The factor

where the second step uses

(2), and we can take the

to be

5.9. Proof of Theorem
5.3
(2).

Let us choose an auxiliary flag

of general linear subspaces in
\mathbf{P}
with

\dim A_{i}=\dim\mathbf{P}-i
. Here, by a linear subspace, we
mean an intersection of divisors in the linear system

\mathrm{H}^{0}(\mathbf{P};{L})
. Since the flag

is general

\mathcal{P}_{i}=\mathcal{P}|_{A_{i}}[-i]
is a perverse sheaf on

(Lemma

), and

. By

, we can find a constant

\mathcal{P}|_{Y}[-1]

is chosen in a generic fashion, the perverse weak Lefschetz theorem

By the traditional weak Lefschetz theorem,

, we have

\mathrm{H}^{m}(Y;\mathcal{P}|_{Y})\cong\mathrm{H}^{m}(\mathbf{P};\mathcal{P})

in the

(1) and the preceding

6.
Betti number estimates

In this section, we use Theorem
5.3
to deduce the
asymptotic of total Betti numbers of a degree
d
hypersurface in
any projective variety.

6.1. Setup.

Throughout this section, let
X
be a possibly singular projective
variety. Let
{L}
be a very ample invertible sheaf on
X
.
We shall simply refer to the zero locus of a nonzero section of

\mathrm{H}^{0}(X;{L}^{\otimes d})
as a degree
d

hypersurface.

Our goal is to prove two theorems regarding the asymptotic properties
of the total Betti numbers of hypersurfaces in
X
. The first one
is surprisingly general, regardless how singular the ambient variety

X
is.

Theorem 6.2
.

Suppose
\dim X=n
, and suppose
\mathcal{F}
is
a plain constructible sheaf on
X
. Then there exist a constant
C>0
such
that for any degree
d
hypersurface
Y\subseteq X
(
C
is
independent of
Y
), we have

B(Y;\mathcal{F}|_{Y})\leqslant 3\operatorname{rank}^{\circ}(\mathcal{F})\cdot\deg(X)\cdot d^{n}+C\cdot d^{n-1}

(for the definition of the generic rank

\operatorname{rank}^{\circ}
, see
Definition
3.3.8
).

Proof.

We proceed by an induction on the dimension of
X
. When the
dimension is zero, there is nothing to prove. Suppose for any polarized variety

(D,{L})
of dimension
\leqslant n-1
, and any constructible sheaf
\mathcal{F}

on
D
, we have found a constant
C_{{L}}(D,\mathcal{F})
which makes
the inequality holds.

Let

\iota\colon X\to\mathbf{P}=\operatorname{Proj}\mathrm{Sym}^{\bullet}\mathrm{H}^{0}(X;{L})^{\vee}

be the projective embedding induced by
{L}
. Let

j\colon X^{\circ}\to X
be an open immersion of satisfying the
following properties:

•

X^{\circ}
is smooth,

•

X^{\circ}
contains are the generic points of the

n
-dimensional irreducible components of
X
,

•

\mathcal{F}|_{X^{\circ}}
is lisse of rank equal to

\mathrm{rank}^{\circ}(\mathcal{F})
.

By shrinking
X^{\circ}
, we may further assume that

•

X\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X^{\circ}
is a Cartier divisor.

Let
i\colon D\to X
be the inclusion of
the complement
D=X\mathbin{\rule[1.99997pt]{6.69998pt}{1.19995pt}}X^{\circ}
of
X^{\circ}
. Then
we have a distinguished triangle

j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[n]\to\mathcal{F}[n]\to i_{\ast}i^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[n].

Since
D
is a Cartier divisor,
j
is quasi-finite and affine.
Therefore,
j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[n]
, hence
\iota_{\ast}(j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[n])
, is perverse.

Let
Y
be any degree
d
hypersurface in
X
.
Let
H\subseteq\mathbf{P}
be the degree
d
hypersurface in
\mathbf{P}
such that
Y=X\cap H
.
Then by
Theorem
5.3
(3) that we have

B(Y,j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[n]|_{Y})=B(H,\iota_{\ast}(j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[n])|_{H})\leqslant 3\operatorname{rank}^{\circ}(\mathcal{F})\cdot\deg(\overline{X}{}^{\circ})\cdot d^{n}+C_{3}\cdot d^{n-1}

for some constant
C_{3}
. Here,
\overline{X}{}^{\circ}
is the
closure of
X^{\circ}
. The result then follows from an induction
on dimension: because

B(Y,\mathcal{F}|_{Y})\leqslant B(Y,j_{!}j^{\ast}\mathcal{F}[n]|_{Y})+B(Y\cap D,i^{\ast}\mathcal{F}|_{Y\cap D}),

we can simply take

C=C_{{L}}(X,\mathcal{F})=C_{3}+\max\left\{3\operatorname{rank}^{\circ}(i^{\ast}\mathcal{F})\cdot\deg(D),C_{i^{\ast}{L}}(D,i^{\ast}\mathcal{F})\right\}.

This completes the proof.
∎

The unpleasant factor
3
can be saved if
X
is a set-theoretically
a local complete intersection.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
V
be a variety over
k
of pure dimension
n
. We say

V
is
set-theoretically a local complete intersection
if
there exists an open affine cover
V=\bigcup_{m\in I}V_{m}
and
with the following property: for each
m\in I
, there exists an integer
r\geqslant 0
, and polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n+r}]
such that

V_{m,\mathrm{red}}\cong\operatorname{Spec}\frac{k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n+r}]}{\sqrt{(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})}}.

Clearly, any local complete intersection variety is set-theoretically a local complete intersection.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
V
be a variety over
k
of pure dimension
n

and let
\mathcal{E}
be a lisse sheaf of
\Lambda
-modules.
If
V
is set-theoretically a local complete intersection,
then
\Lambda_{V}[n]
is a perverse sheaf.

Proof.

Since
V
and
V_{\mathrm{red}}
have equivalent étale topoi,
and since the assertion is local on
V
, we may suppose
V
is realized
as a complete intersection in
\mathbf{A}^{n+r}
defined by
r

polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in k[T_{1},\ldots,T_{n+r}]
. By
successively applying Lemma
3.2.5
for each
defining equation, we conclude that
\Lambda_{V}[n]
is perverse
coconnective. Moreover, as it is simply a shift of the constant sheaf
by
n
, perverse connectivity holds automatically. The result
follows.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
V
be a variety over
k
of pure dimension
n
, and suppose
that
\Lambda_{V}[n]
is perverse. Then, for any lisse sheaf

\mathcal{E}
of
\Lambda
-modules on
V
, the shifted sheaf

\mathcal{E}[n]
is also perverse.

Proof.

If
\Lambda
is infinite, we may select an integral lattice in

\mathcal{E}
and reduce to the finite case, so we may assume
without loss of generality that
\Lambda
is finite. Furthermore,
since the property in question depends only on the reduced subscheme
underlying
V
, we may assume that
V
is a local complete
intersection.

Given that
\mathcal{E}
is a lisse sheaf of
\Lambda
-modules
and
\Lambda
is finite, there exists a finite étale cover

\pi\colon V^{\prime}\to V
such that
\pi^{*}\mathcal{E}
is a
constant sheaf, i.e.,

\pi^{*}\mathcal{E}\simeq\Lambda_{V^{\prime}}^{\oplus m}
. The
morphism
\pi
being étale implies that
V^{\prime}
is itself a
local complete intersection and that the pullback functor
\pi^{*}
is
t-exact for the perverse t-structure. Thus,
\pi^{*}\mathcal{E}[n]
,
being a direct sum of
\Lambda_{V^{\prime}}[n]
, is perverse
coconnective. It follows from Lemma
3.2.4

that
\mathcal{E}[n]
is perverse coconnective on
V
. Finally,
as
\dim V=n
and
\Lambda_{V}[n]
is perverse connective by
assumption, the conclusion follows.
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
X
be an irreducible projective variety of pure dimension

n
. Let
{L}
be a very ample line invertible sheaf on

X
. Suppose
\Lambda_{X}[n]
is a perverse sheaf on
X
. (This
is the case, e.g., when
X
is set-theoretically a local complete
intersection, by Lemma
6.4
).

Then for any lisse sheaf
\mathcal{E}
on
X
, there is a constant
C
(independent of the coefficient field

\Lambda
), such that for any degree
d
hypersurface

Y\subseteq X
, we have

B(Y,\mathcal{E}|_{Y})\leqslant\deg(X)\cdot\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{E})\cdot d^{n}+C\cdot d^{n-1}.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.4
, we see
\mathcal{E}[n]
is
a perverse sheaf.

Let
\iota\colon X\to\mathbf{P}
be the projective embedding
defined by
{L}
. Let
Y
be a hypersurface of degree

d
. Since
X
is
n
-dimensional and irreducible,

\dim Y=n-1
, and, by the Bertini theorem,
unless
n=1
,
Y
remains irreducible.
This implies that
\mathcal{E}|_{Y}[n-1]
is necessarily a perverse
sheaf (Lemma
3.2.6
).
We win by applying Theorem
5.3
(2) to the
perverse sheaf
\iota_{\ast}\mathcal{E}[n]
.
∎

7.
Uniformity in
\ell

In this section, we show that if
V
can be defined over a finite
field, and the perverse sheaf
\mathcal{P}
is part of a compatible
system of perverse sheaves, then the constants in the upper bounds of
Theorem
5.3
can be made independent of
\ell
.

7.1. Conventions.

In this section, we work over a finite base field
\mathbf{F}_{q}
of characteristic
p
and
let
k
be an algebraic closure of
\mathbf{F}_{q}
. We use
the following notation system:
capital Latin letters with a subscript
0
, such as

X_{0},S_{0}
, denote separated schemes of finite type defined over

\mathbf{F}_{q}
. Removing the subscript (e.g.,
X,S
) indicates
the base change of these schemes to a fixed algebraic closure

\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}
. If

\mathcal{F}\in D^{b}_{c}(X_{0},\Lambda)
, we shall still denote by

\mathcal{F}
its inverse image by the morphism
X\to X_{0}
.
This will hardly cause any confusions.

Definition 7.2

(cf.
[
Fuj02
]
)
.

Let
E
be a field of characteristic
0
.
Let
I
be a subset of

\{(\ell,\iota):\ell\neq p\text{ is a prime},\iota\colon E\hookrightarrow\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell}\text{ is a field embedding}\}.

For
\alpha\in I
, denote by
\ell_{\alpha}
the first coordinate
of
\alpha
.

Let
X_{0}
be a separated scheme of finite type over

\mathbf{F}_{q}
. We say a system

(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in I}\in\prod_{\alpha\in I}D^{b}_{c}(X_{0},\overline{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell_{\alpha}})

of constructible complexes on a finite type
\mathbf{F}_{q}
-scheme

is
weakly
(E,I)
-compatible
, if

•

the local factor

\det(1-t\mathrm{Frob}_{x},\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})=\prod_{i\in\mathbf{Z}}\det(1-t\mathrm{Frob}_{x},\mathcal{H}^{i}(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}))^{(-1)^{i-1}}

lies in
1+tE[t]

for any closed point
x
of
X
, where
\mathrm{Frob}_{x}
is the geometric Frobenius automorphism of
\mathrm{Gal}(k/\kappa(x))
.

•

for any closed point
x
of
X
, the polynomials

\det(1-t\mathrm{Frob}_{x},\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})
are all equal
for all
\alpha\in I
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
X_{0}
be a separated scheme of finite type over

\mathbf{F}_{q}
. Let
(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})_{i\in I}
be a
weakly
(E,I)
-compatible system. Then

\chi(X,\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})
is independent of
\alpha\in I
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the Grothendieck trace formula. Indeed,
by the compatibility, the L-functions

L(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha},t)=\prod_{x\in|X|}\det(1-t\mathrm{Frob}_{x},\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})^{-1}

lies in
1+tE\llbracket{t}\rrbracket
independent of
\ell
. By
the trace formula,
L(\mathcal{F},t)
is a rational function in

E(t)
, and
\chi(X,\mathcal{F}_{\alpha})
equals the degree
of the denominator of
L(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha},t)
minus the degree
of the numerator of
L(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha},t)
, hence is also
independent of
\ell
.
∎

Lemma 7.5
.

Notation as in Construction
7.4
,
suppose

Let
(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in I}
be a weakly

for

Proof.

We adapt Katz’s argument based on the Euler

[

, originally established for local

h^{i}_{c}(-)=\dim\mathrm{H}^{i}_{c}(-)

By constructibility of direct images, there exists an open dense

with

h^{i}_{c}(X\cap A_{e};\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}|_{X\cap A_{e}}[-e])

are constant. By the smooth and proper base change theorems, these

h^{i}(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})
.

Now, for any
\alpha,\beta\in I

lies in

U(\alpha)\cap U(\beta)
,
Lemma
7.3
yields

\chi(X\cap A_{e};\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}|_{X\cap A_{e}}[-e])=\chi(X\cap A_{e};\mathcal{P}_{\beta}|_{X\cap A_{e}}[-e])\,,

which implies

\chi(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})=\chi(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\beta})\,.

(7.5.1)

In other words, the Euler characteristic of

\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha}
is independent of
\ell
.

By Artin’s vanishing theorem (Theorem
3.2.3
),
we have
h^{i}_{c}(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})=0
for all
i<0
,
so we only consider non-negative degrees below.

Next, the weak Lefschetz theorem (Lemma
3.2.8
)
ensures that for each
\alpha
and a general flag
A_{\bullet}
in
U(\alpha)
,

\mathrm{H}^{j}_{c}(X\cap A_{e+1};\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}|_{X\cap A_{e}}[-(e+1)](-1))\to\mathrm{H}^{j+1}_{c}(X\cap A_{e};\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}[-e])

is surjective for
j=0
and an isomorphism for
j\geqslant 1
.
Thus, by smooth and proper base change, for all
e\geqslant 0
,

h^{0}_{c}(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})\geqslant h^{1}_{c}(X^{(e-1)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e-1)}_{\alpha})\geqslant\cdots\geqslant h^{e}_{c}(X;\mathcal{P}_{\alpha})

(7.5.2)

and, moreover,

h^{j}_{c}(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})=h^{j+1}_{c}(X^{(e-1)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{(e-1)})=\cdots=h^{j+e}_{c}(X;\mathcal{P}_{\alpha})\quad\text{for }j\geqslant 1\,.

(7.5.3)

Applying (
7.5.2
) and (
7.5.3
) for any
e\geqslant 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\chi(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=h^{0}_{c}(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})-h^{1}_{c}(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})+h^{2}_{c}(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})-\cdots

\displaystyle\geqslant h^{0}_{c}(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})-h^{0}_{c}(X^{(e+1)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e+1)}_{\alpha})+h^{1}_{c}(X^{(e+1)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e+1)}_{\alpha})-\cdots

\displaystyle=h^{0}_{c}(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})-\chi(X^{(e+1)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e+1)}_{\alpha}).

Thus, using (
7.5.2
), we find

h^{e}_{c}(X;\mathcal{P}_{\alpha})\leqslant h^{0}_{c}(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})\leqslant\chi(X^{(e)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e)}_{\alpha})+\chi(X^{(e+1)}_{\overline{\eta}};\mathcal{P}^{(e+1)}_{\alpha})\,.

In view of (
7.5.1
),
this gives an
\ell
-independent upper bound for the dimension of
each cohomology group of
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
. The proof is
complete.
∎
