Bioinspired smart catalytic microneedle patch with structural color for infected wound management
Authors: Yuting Huang, Yuqi Wu, Peipei Xi, Nengjie Yang, Yongxin Xu, Hao Zhang, Yunan Wang, Xiaodong Chen, Yujuan Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Infected wounds remain a major clinical challenge due to persistent inflammation, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), and bacterial colonization. Here, we present a bioinspired catalytic microneedle (MN) patch that integrates antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and real-time monitoring functions into a single platform. The patch possesses a hierarchical structure with spatially distributed nanocomponents: gold-modified barium titanate (AuB) in the MN tips enables ultrasound-triggered piezoelectric antibacterial activity, while cerium dioxide nanozyme (CeNP) in the backing layer efficiently scavenges ROS and mitigates inflammation. Of note, a structural color hydrogel incorporated in the backing layer provides visual, colorimetric indication of wound exudate, allowing timely patch replacement. Based on these features, it is demonstrated that the hierarchically structured MN patch exhibits robust antibacterial efficacy as well as anti-inflammatory treatment. In vivo evaluations further demonstrate its enhanced healing outcomes, which are characterized by improved tissue regeneration and accelerated resolution of inflammation. Collectively, these results highlight the potential of this smart hierarchical MN platform as an intelligent and multifunctional therapeutic strategy for efficient management of infected wounds.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03953-3.

Introduction

Infected wounds remain a significant clinical challenge due to persistent inflammation, impaired tissue repair, and high recurrence rates. Beyond the intrinsic difficulties of wound healing, infection plays a pivotal role in prolonging recovery and predisposing patients to severe complications. Such infections are often associated with underlying conditions such as diabetes, immunosuppression and microbial resistance, which can further delay healing [1–3]. The consequences extend far beyond physical symptoms: chronic infected wounds markedly compromise patients’ quality of life and impose a significant economic and healthcare burden worldwide. The process of infected wound healing in the human body is an intricate, multiphase journey involving coagulation, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [4, 5], all of which require precise orchestration by a diverse array of biological enzymes, including matrix metalloproteinases, collagenases, lysozymes, and catalases [6, 7]. Given the crucial role that enzymes play in wound repair, enzyme-based active wound care has emerged as a highly promising therapeutic approach. Between 2016 and 2022, the field experienced a remarkable compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 20.6% [8]. These innovative approaches offer a multitude of benefits: selective debridement of necrotic tissue through collagenase and papain; antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory actions, such as lysozyme’s ability to lyse bacterial cell walls and modulate oxidative stress; and enhanced tissue regeneration via extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, fibroblast proliferation, and angiogenesis stimulation [9–11]. However, natural enzymes face significant clinical limitations. Their structural instability in the variable environments of wounds, narrow therapeutic spectrum that fails to address the complexity of chronic wounds, and prohibitively expensive production processes all severely restrict their practical utility in wound management [12, 13]. These inherent constraints underscore the urgent need to develop faster, more cost-effective, and more controllable solutions. In addition to these biochemical challenges, effective wound management must also address a range of clinical symptoms that have a profound impact on patient quality of life and outcomes. These symptoms include odor, bleeding, pruritus, excessive exudate and persistent pain [14–16]. The multifaceted nature of these limitations highlights the critical need for more comprehensive and holistic wound care solutions that can effectively address both the underlying biological complexities and the diverse clinical manifestations of infected wounds.

Inspired by enzyme activities during wound healing, we propose a bioinspired smart catalytic microneedle (MN) patch with structural color (Fig. 1). MNs are emerging as advanced transdermal delivery systems that combine minimal invasiveness with efficient drug transport. Their microscale architecture allows spatial and temporal organization of functional components, enabling versatile applications in drug therapy and tissue repair. This spatial-temporal construction facilitates the co-delivery and synergistic action of various nanomaterials, such as nanozymes and metal oxides, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes. Additionally, nanomaterials exhibit enzyme-like activities and can mimic the diverse functions of natural enzymes [17, 18]. For instance, nanoenzymes can catalyze the breakdown of necrotic tissue, replicating the actions of natural collagenases or lysozymes to facilitate wound debridement [19]. Similarly, metal oxides can emulate the antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory functions of natural enzymes, as well as regulate redox reactions, thereby helping to alleviate inflammation [20]. Besides, structural color, which arises from the periodic arrangement of micro- or nanostructures that interfere with light, endows materials with vivid and dynamic optical properties [21, 22]. Owing to these unique characteristics, structural color has been widely applied in fields such as sensing and smart displays. By leveraging this feature, structural color MN patches can exhibit color changes in response to variations in the wound microenvironment, thereby providing real-time, non-invasive visual feedback. By integrating metal oxide nanoparticles, and structural color hydrogels into a single MN platform, it is expected to provide a novel strategy for wound management.

In this work, we fabricated the desired catalytic MN patch with hierarchical structure to realize the spatial distribution and co-application of functional nanoparticles for treating infected wounds. Also, a photonic crystal-based structural color hydrogel was incorporated into the system to allow real-time visual monitoring of wound exudate. Specifically, piezoelectric nanoparticles (AuB) and cerium dioxide nanoparticles (CeNP) have been incorporated into the tips and backing layer of MN patch, respectively. AuB enables potent antibacterial activity under US stimulation, demonstrating particular efficacy for treating deep-layer skin infections. CeNP exhibits significant anti-inflammatory effects by scavenging reactive oxygen species ROS that are generated at infected sites. Notably, the back layer is functionalized with the structural color hydrogel, which is capable of absorbing exudates from the wound. Its color change serves as a visual indicator of excessive fluid accumulation, thereby signaling the need for MN replacement. This feature allows for the convenient and real-time monitoring of wound exudate. In vivo experiments, the MNs exhibited significant therapeutic effects in promoting the healing of infected full-thickness wounds in mouse models. The smart MNs incorporating AuB and CeNP have demonstrated remarkable antibacterial activity against both Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. Moreover, the ROS-scavenging properties of CeNP effectively mitigated the expression of inflammatory cytokines in vitro. Promising therapeutic outcomes were also observed in vivo, highlighting their potential for treating infected wounds. Collectively, these results underscore the potential of the present smart MNs as a multifunctional platform that can simultaneously address wound treatment and provide real-time monitoring, offering a comprehensive solution for managing infected wounds.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of CeNP and AuB

Before fabricating spatiotemporally structured Ce/AuB MNs, CeNP and AuB were synthesized and analyzed, respectively. CeNP were synthesized according to our previous works [23]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed uniformly sized CeNP with diameters of approximately 4–5 nm (Fig. 2a-b). A homogeneous spatial distribution of Ce and O elements was observed across the composite, indicating their thorough integration within the structure (Figure S1a). CeNP exhibited a negative zeta potential (-28.57 mV) (Fig. 2c), which has been reported to facilitate cellular endocytosis [24]. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images further revealed the (200) facets of CeNP, corresponding to lattice fringes of 0.271 nm (Fig. 2d). To demonstrate the potential of AuB’s outstanding antimicrobial properties for treating deep wound infections in skin tissues, we first characterized AuB. The particle size of AuB were predominantly 197 nm (Fig. 2e). TEM images showed that AuB nanoparticles exhibited a 3D tetragonal phase structure, with gold particles uniformly distributed on the tetragonal phase (Fig. 2f). Elemental distribution maps indicated that Au, Ba, Ti and O elements were homogeneously dispersed within the composite structure (Fig. 2g). HRTEM images of the nanoparticle revealed the (200) facets of Au, corresponding to 0.204 nm lattice fringes, and the (110) facets of BTO, corresponding 0.282 nm lattice fringes (Fig. 2h). Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) images further confirmed the crystal structure (Figure S1b).

Fabrication and properties of Ce/AuB MNs

Then the spatiotemporal structured MN patch was fabricated by replicating from a negative mold. The negative mold was specifically designed to feature an ordered array of conical cavities. Photo-crosslinked GelMA hydrogel was adopted as the material for MN substrate and tips, owing to its excellent biocompatibility, bioactivity, biodegradability and versatility. MNs were sharply shaped, with a height of 820 μm and a base diameter of 340 μm (Fig. 2i-j). Scanning electron microscope (SEM) showed the porous surface morphology of MNs, suggesting their intricate and large-pore structure (Fig. 2k). To realize the successful tissue puncture, the MNs were then optimized with favorable mechanical strength by tuning the concentration of GelMA solution. When the compression displacement was 800 μm, the forces of 30% GelMA MN reached ~21.43 N per needle (Fig. 2l), which allowed to micro-puncture the skin by these tiny needles based on the existing research [25, 26]. The porcine skin insertion test confirmed that the microneedles penetrated the stratum corneum without fracture. H&E staining showed uniformly distributed microchannels matching the array pattern, with depths consistent with needle length and reaching the epidermis and superficial dermis (Figure S2).

The Ce/AuB MNs were fabricated using a template-based method with a poly(dimethyl siloxane) (PDMS) mold through a two-step drop-casting process. Specifically, the predetermined volume of GelMA solution with AuB was uniformly injected into the mold to form the tips of the MNs. Subsequently, the GelMA solution with CeNP was introduced to cover the tips and solidified by UV exposure. To further investigate the acoustic properties of AuB MN, COMSOL Multiphysics was employed to simulate their behavior under 30 MHz US. As illustrated in Fig. 2m, the acoustic pressure field distribution revealed a high-pressure region concentrated near the tip of the MN. The color gradient of the pressure field, transitioning from blue (low pressure) to red (high pressure), clearly demonstrated the significant acoustic pressure concentration at the tip of AuB MN. Vector field analysis revealed a regular, periodic wave propagation pattern, with directional arrows representing the acoustic wave vectors (Fig. 2n). Furthermore, the frontal plane acoustic field distribution showed a gradual decrease in acoustic pressure from the tip of the MN towards its base (Fig. 2o). These results highlight the remarkable ability of the AuB MN to effectively concentrate acoustic energy at the needle tip. This feature enables precise energy delivery and targeted drug release to deeper skin layers, thereby enhancing the therapeutic efficacy for deep tissue treatments.

Antibacterial and antioxidative capabilities of Ce/AuB MNs

During the initial phase of wound healing, an imbalance between excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and inadequate antioxidant enzyme defense leads to oxidative stress, hampering tissue regeneration. Endothelial cells and fibroblasts further exacerbate this process by generating ROS through nicotinamideadenine-dinucleotide-dependent oxidases. To address this, CeNP and AuB nanoparticles were fabricated into the MN patch, in which the spatially distributed nanocomponents provide complementary ROS-scavenging and catalytic activities to reestablish redox homeostasis in the wound microenvironment (Fig. 3a). To evaluate the stability of CeNP, they were dispersed in various media, including H2O, PBS, DMEM medium and 0.9% NaCl, and maintained for 24 h. The results showed excellent dispersion stability under these different conditions (Figure S3). Their unique ability of CeNP to alternate between Ce³⁺ and Ce⁴⁺ states via oxygen vacancies enables a self-regenerating antioxidant mechanism [27, 28], efficiently reducing ROS and improving the wound microenvironment. Then the multiple enzymes-like catalytic capabilities of CeNP were assessed. The inhibition rates of SOD and ·OH− significantly increased with rising CeNP concentrations, thereby confirming their multienzyme-like activity (Fig. 3b-c). Additionally, macrophages were cultured to investigate changes in intracellular ROS levels following treatment. Compared with conventional antioxidants Trolox, CeNP exhibited superior ROS scavenging capacity (Fig. 3d). FACS analysis confirmed the ROS-scavenging capability of CeNP, as evidenced by a substantial decrease in ROS signals after their introduction to H₂O₂-stimulated conditions. Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of CeNP was evaluated using NIH-3T3 cells. Fluorescence assays showed that cells exposed to varying concentrations of CeNP maintained high viability (Figure S4a-b), indicating minimal cytotoxicity. In conclusion, CeNP exhibit remarkable antioxidant properties and excellent biocompatibility.

Moreover, wound healing is highly susceptible to skin infections. Integrating gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) with barium titanate (BTO) yields a synergistic platform in which AuNPs catalyze ultrasound-triggered ROS generation for antibacterial action, while BTO’s piezoelectricity modulates redox balance and supports tissue repair [29]. Firstly, the cytotoxicity of AuB was assessed using NIH-3T3 cells. Fluorescence assays revealed that cells exposed to varying concentrations of AuB maintained high viability (Figure S6a-b), indicating minimal cytotoxicity. To evaluate the antibacterial properties of AuB, the antibacterial activity was assessed co-culturing AuB with both Gram-negative (E. coli) and Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus). After 6 h of treatment with various concentrations of AuB, the result of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) test shows a slight reduction in bacterial count following AuB treatment (Figure S5a-b). AuB exhibits a synergistic antibacterial effect under US, leveraging the piezoelectric catalytic effect, surface plasmon resonance, charge separation and cavitation effect to promote ROS generation [30]. When bacterial suspensions were treated with 100 µg/mL AuB and subjected to US for 5–10 min, the turbidity of the bacterial suspensions significantly decreased, and bacterial counts were markedly reduced (Figure S7a-b). To further evaluate bacterial viability, bacteria from each group were stained with Calcein-AM/PI (Fig. 3e). For E. coli, viability was approximately 90.48% in control, and decreased to 70.79%, 47.70% after 5 and 10 min of US treatment, respectively (Fig. 3f). For S. aureus, viability was approximately 98.47% in control, and decreased to 61.30%, 34.72% after 5 and 10 min of US treatment, respectively. (Fig. 3g). SEM images showed that after 5 min of US treatment, the bacterial surface was severely damaged and the morphology was significantly altered, demonstrating the potent bactericidal effect of AuB nanocomposites (Fig. 3h). Further analysis confirmed the role of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in this process. Under US irradiation, AuB generated hydroxyl radicals (•OH), as detected by a time-dependent enhancement in fluorescence intensity using terephthalic acid as a probe (Figure S8a). Quantitatively, AuB yielded 4.10 times more •OH than BTO after 5 min, illustrating its markedly enhanced piezoelectric activity (Figure S8b). In addition, singlet oxygen (¹O₂) generation was verified by the progressive decrease in DPBF absorption at 411 nm under US exposure (Figure S8c). The DPBF decomposition rate observed with AuB was 4.37 times higher than that with BTO, further corroborating its superior ROS-generating performance (Figure S8d). These results demonstrate the antibacterial efficacy of AuB, highlighting its potential for preventing skin infections during wound healing while maintaining excellent biocompatibility.

Structural color-indicating functions of MN

To visualize the amount of wound tissue fluid, the water content of the GelMA-HPC hydrogel was precisely adjusted to 37 wt%, 39 wt%, 41 wt%, 43 wt%, and 45 wt%, resulting in distinct color variations. As the water content increased, the hydrogel underwent a noticeable color transition from blue to green and eventually to red (Fig. 4a). Correspondingly, the reflectance spectra exhibited a red shift in the reflectance peaks, demonstrating a water-content-dependent color coding of the flexible structure (Fig. 4b). To further examine the internal architecture, cross-sectional SEM was performed on these GelMA-HPC hydrogels (Fig. 4c). High-magnification SEM images revealed the characteristic Bouligand arches within the cholesteric hydrogel, highlighting its unique structural organization. As illustrated in Fig. 4d, the structured colored hydrogel of the microneedle backing absorbs tissue permeate and displays different color changes depending on the amount of exudate. The red color indicates excessive fluid accumulation and the need for timely replacement of the MN. Based on recent studies of in situ sensing and microfluidic patches, the exudate rate from full-thickness excision wounds on the mouse back is ~ 2 µL·cm⁻²·h⁻¹. Therefore, for commonly used 8 mm diameter wounds, the theoretical collectable volume in the early postoperative period is approximately ~ 14–24 µL/day/wound. We simulated wound exudate by quantitatively adding PBS and performed spectral acquisition of the color changes in the structural color hydrogel (Fig. 4e). The results indicate that when the water uptake is less than 10 µL, the color appears purple. As water uptake increases to between 10 and 20 µl, the color gradually shifts from blue to green. When water uptake exceeds 40 µL, the hydrogel predominantly displays red coloration. This indicates that when the structural color hydrogel absorbs the exudate and turns red, it signifies that the absorption capacity has reached its maximum. A new dressing should be applied to continue absorbing the exudate.

To evaluate the practical applicability of this structurally colored hydrogel in vivo, it was tested using a mouse wound model. As the hydrogel absorbed exudate from the dorsal wound, a noticeable color change was observed. Initially displaying a blue-green hue, the hydrogel gradually shifted to red with increasing exudate absorption (Fig. 4f). This visual transition was corroborated by the corresponding reflectance spectra (Fig. 4g), which exhibited a progressive red shift in the reflection peaks. These results indicate that the hydrogel effectively absorbs wound exudate and undergoes structural color changes in response to varying exudate levels, providing an intuitive and real-time assessment of wound fluid accumulation.

Therapeutic effect in infected wound

To further investigate the therapeutic potential of Ce/AuB MNs, an infected full-thickness skin defect model was established. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, an 8 mm diameter section of skin was excised from the dorsal region of BALB/c mice, followed by the injection of Gram-positive bacterial suspension into the wound to induce infection. The mice were randomly assigned to 9 groups: control, MN, US, Ce MN, AuB MN, AuB US, Ce/AuB, Ce/AuB US and Human Epidermal Growth Factor Gel (rhEGF). Wound progression was monitored and recorded on days 0, 5, 9,12 and 14. As shown in Fig. 5b, Ce/AuB US group exhibited a significantly higher wound closure rate compared to the control group, with a more pronounced healing trend (Fig. 5c). Compared with the rhEGF group, the rhEGF group demonstrated rapid initial healing, with wounds achieving near-complete epithelialization within 14 days (Figure S9). Notably, body weight fluctuations remained minimal across all groups (Fig. 5d), indicating no adverse systemic effects.

Infection is one of the main factors contributing to difficulties in wound healing; therefore, the development of wound dressings with antimicrobial properties is essential to promote wound healing. In in vitro experiments, we verified the antimicrobial effect of the designed MN patch. We evaluated the antibacterial properties of different groups, and the experimental results showed a significant reduction in the number of colonies in AuB US group compared with the control group (Fig. 5e-f, Figure S10). This demonstrated that AuB US exhibited excellent antibacterial properties. In conclusion, these results suggest that Ce/AuB US treatment significantly promotes wound healing, epidermal regeneration, and collagen deposition, highlighting its potential clinical value.

Furthermore, histological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, lungs, stomach, and kidneys, revealed no signs of pathological damage, confirming the safety of the treatments (Figure S11). H&E staining on days 5, 9, and 12 demonstrated that Ce/AuB US group developed a more intact epidermal structure by day 12 (Fig. 6a, d; Figure S11; Figure S13a). Additionally, Masson’s trichrome staining at these time points revealed substantial collagen deposition in Ce/AuB US group compared to the control group (Fig. 6b, e; Figure S12; Figure S13b; Figure S14). To gain further insights, we conducted immunohistochemical staining (IHC) of skin tissue. IL-6 plays a crucial regulatory role in the wound healing process, including inflammatory regulation, cell proliferation, angiogenesis and tissue remodeling [31]. We examined the expression of IL-6 and found that it was lower in Ce/AuB US group than in the control group. This reduction in IL-6 levels may be attributed to avoid persistent inflammation that could interfere with collagen remodeling and normal tissue repair (Fig. 6c, f; Figure S15). These data further validate the therapeutic efficacy of the MN patch in accelerating wound healing.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a bioinspired catalytic microneedle patch that combines wound therapy with real-time exudate monitoring. The patch uses CeNP and AuB as artificial enzyme mimics, replacing natural enzymes which can be easily deactivated by changes in temperature, pH or storage conditions. These nanozymes provide stable catalytic activity under diverse wound environments, ensuring sustained antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects. Furthermore, incorporating a GelMA–HPC structural color hydrogel enables the intuitive visualization of changes in wound exudate, supporting timely clinical assessment. Together, these features enable effective infection control, inflammation modulation and dynamic monitoring, providing a robust and intelligent platform for next-generation wound management.

Building on this foundation, we have successfully developed a Ce/AuB US-responsive microneedle patch that exhibits integrated antioxidant, antibacterial, and wound-healing properties. Although its therapeutic outcome does not exceed that of rhEGF therapy, which directly supplies growth signals, the patch’s distinctive capability to modulate the wound microenvironment, combined with its multifunctional design, offers a highly promising alternative for managing complex, hard-to-heal wounds in clinical settings. This system lays the groundwork for future smart wound dressings with advanced functionalities. Beyond demonstrated efficacy, the clinical translation of this platform will require further attention to scalability, storage stability, and regulatory compliance. The fabrication process of CeNP and AuB hydrogels is compatible with current microneedle molding and casting technologies, supporting large-scale production, although systematic studies on long-term stability are still needed. Importantly, the catalytic nature of CeNP and AuB provides advantages over natural enzymes, as these nanozymes maintain robust redox activity under physiological conditions with higher reproducibility. Recent studies have further validated the antimicrobial efficacy of nano-enabled hydrogels and microneedles, showing that bioactive nanoparticles can achieve sustained antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus via reactive species release [32–34], while catalytic microneedle systems suppress bacterial growth and biofilm formation through ROS generation. These findings align well with the proposed mechanisms of Ce/AuB nanozymes and underscore their ability to overcome limitations associated with conventional antibiotic release. Moreover, newly developed microneedle designs with tunable heights and diameters allow for simultaneous multi-layer targeting and controlled release, enabling synergistic deep-layer antimicrobial delivery and superficial anti-inflammatory treatment [35]. Such designs could further enhance efficacy, reduce dosing frequency, and improve patient comfort, collectively underscoring the promise of Ce/AuB-based microneedles as a next-generation strategy for wound healing.

Materials and methods

Materials

Gelatin, methacrylic anhydride (MA), 2-Hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (HMPP), citric acid, ammonium hydroxide and hydrogeneric auric acid tetrachloroaurate trihydrate (HAuCl·3H2O) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Hydroxypropyl cellulose (Hydroxypropyl Cellulose (3–6 mPa·s, 2% in Water at 20 °C, TCI) was acquired from TCI (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan). Nano Barium Titanate (Tetragonal phase) was obtained from Nanjing XFNANO Materials Tec Corporation. Cerium nitrate hexahydrate (Ce(NO₃)₃·6 H₂O was purchased form Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) were purchased from Haibo Biological Technology (Shandong, China). All other chemicals used were of analytical or pharmaceutical grade.

Synthesis of GelMA

10 g gelatin was dissolved in 100 mL DPBS and stirred at 50 °C for 3 h. Upon complete dissolution, 10 mM NaHCO3 was added to adjust the pH 7–8, followed by the gradual dropwise addition of 6 mL MA to the gelatin solution. After a 3-hour reaction, unreacted MA was removed by centrifugation. Then, add 200 mL of 40 °C pure water to the lower layer to dilute. Pour the diluted solution into 12–14 kDa dialysis membrane and dialysize it in pure water at 60 °C for 6–7 days to maintain the solution state and completely remove unreacted small molecules and by-products, thereby ensuring the biological safety of the material and the effectiveness of subsequent experiments. After dialysis, adjust the pH of the solution to 7.2 using NaHCO₃. Filter the solution through a 0.2 μm filter, then freeze it at -20 °C overnight. Finally, the GelMA prepolymer was obtained after lyophilization for 2–4 days and was stored at 4 °C before use.

Synthesis of GelMA-HPC hydrogel

Methacrylated hydrogel powder was dissolved in ultrapure water to prepare a 5% (w/v) aqueous solution. HPC was then added to the solution to formulate GelMA-HPC hydrogels with varying water contents (37 wt%, 39 wt%, 41 wt%, 43 wt%, 45 wt%).

Synthesis of CeNP

217 mg Ce(NO₃)₃·6 H₂O was dissolved in 4 mL of deionized water, and 200 mg citric acid was dissolved in 2 mL of deionized water. The two solutions were mixed and rapidly added to 100 mL of 0.4 M ammonium hydroxide. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h, followed by dialysis for 3 days to obtain cerium dioxide powder.

CeNP enzyme activity detection

The superoxide dismutase activity and hydroxyl radical enzyme activity of CeNP were detected using enzyme activity assay kits (Nanjing Jianchen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Synthesis of AuB NPs

0.5 g BaTiO3 (Xianfeng, China) was dispersed in 100 mL ddH2O, and 2 mL of NH3·H2O was added dropwise to the solution. Then, 5 mL of HAuCl·3H2O was introduced. The solution was stirred at room temperature for 1 h. The resulting precipitate was washed three times with ddH2O and calcined at 300 °C for 1 h to obtain the final AuB NPs.

Fabrication of MN and Ce/AuB MN

GelMA precursor solutions at concentrations of 15%, 20%, and 30% (w/v) were prepared in PBS at 45 °C with 1% (w/v) HMPP as the photoinitiator. The precursor solutions were pipetted into polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) microneedle molds (height 820 μm, base diameter 340 μm) and placed under vacuum (− 0.08 MPa) for 5–10 min to remove air bubbles and ensure complete filling of the mold cavities. After leveling excess solution, the molds were irradiated with 405 nm light (10–30 mW cm⁻²) for 60–120 s to achieve crosslinking, followed by secondary exposure of the backing layer for 30–60 s to enhance mechanical strength. The solidified patches were carefully demolded and either air-dried overnight or lyophilized at − 80 °C for 24 h before use.

The Ce/AuB MNs were fabricated using a template-based method with PDMS mold through a two-step drop-casting process. GelMA (30% w/v) was prepared in PBS at 50 °C with HMPP (1% w/v) as photoinitiator. For the needle tips, 1 mg/mL AuB were dispersed into the GelMA precursor and cast into the molds, followed by vacuum treatment (− 0.08 MPa) for 5–10 min and brief photocrosslinking (405 nm, 10–20 s). For the backing, CeNP were incorporated into GelMA at 0.3 wt%, cast onto the tip layer, and fully crosslinked under 405 nm light (10–30 mW cm⁻², 60–120 s). The cured MNs were demolded and UV-sterilized (30 min) prior to use.

Ex vivo skin insertion test

The mechanical strength of the microneedles was determined using a universal testing machine (Instron 5934, USA).The microneedle patches were positioned with the needle tips facing upwards and pressure was applied at a rate of 0.2 mm/min until deformation occurred. The maximum force recorded on the force-displacement curve was noted as the fracture force per needle.

Skin penetration capability was assessed using excised porcine skin. After shaving and cleansing the skin, the microneedles were pressed into it at a uniform pressure. The treated skin was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The microneedle penetration depth and channel morphology were then observed under an optical microscope.

Cell culture

NIH-3T3 and macrophages (obtained from Procell, Wuhan, China) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (high glucose DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and the culture medium was changed every other day until the cells were ready for use.

In vitro cell culture studies

To evaluate cytocompatibility and cell proliferation, experiments were conducted using NTH-3T3. All samples were sterilized before the experiments. Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells in 24-well plates and culture with different concentrations of CeNP and AuB. After incubation for 24 h, live/dead cell staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (C2015M, Beyotime, China). After incubation for 30 min, cell images were taken using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX71, UK) with green and red filters to analyze the cytocompatibility of the samples.

Intracellular ROS assay

Macrophages were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured overnight to allow cell adhesion. Except for the control group, the remaining groups were treated with 3–6% H₂O₂ followed by exposure to CeNP (10 µg/mL) and Trolox (10 µg/mL) for 4–6 h. After incubation, the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (final concentration 10 µM, Solarbio) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Cells were then washed with PBS to remove excess probe. Intracellular ROS levels were quantified by flow cytometry and data analysis was performed using FlowJo software.

To detect ROS generation of AuB under ultrasound stimuli, a 0.5 mM TA solution was prepared in 2 mM NaOH, into which 0.5 mM AuB was dispersed. Following ultrasound (center frequency 30 MHz, bandwidth 18–38 MHz) treatment, the fluorescence of 2-hydroxyterephthalic acid was measured with an SHIMADZU UV-VS spectrophotometer at 200–400 nm excitation. To assess singlet oxygen, 20 µM 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) in ethanol was combined with 0.1 mM AuB. After identical US exposure, absorption spectra were collected, and the DPBF degradation rate was calculated from changes in absorbance at 411 nm.

Antibacterial activity in vitro

The in vitro antibacterial activity of AuB was tested against E. coli (CMCC(B) 44102) and S. aureus (CMCC(B) 26003). The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined using the resazurin method. Briefly, 100 µL of bacterial suspension (2 × 10⁵ CFU mL⁻¹) was added to wells containing 100 µL of graded AuB concentrations (50–1000 µg mL⁻¹). After 8 h at 37 °C, resazurin (0.0675%) was added, and color development was recorded after 4 h.

100 µL of AuB solution (100 µg/mL) was added to 900 µL of LB broth containing 2 × 105 CFU mL− 1 of bacteria. Sonication-assisted co-cultivation was performed at different time points and bacterial turbidity was measured using a microplate reader. Subsequently, live and dead bacteria were labelled with SYTO9 and PI staining, respectively. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope to assess bacterial viability.

Cell viability

Cell proliferative activity was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for a specific period before stimulation, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and absorbance values were determined at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

In vivo wound healing assay

Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Centre of Nantong University. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Protection and Ethics Committee of Nantong University (Approval No. S20250317-005). Mice were randomly divided into nine groups (15 mice per group) and anaesthetized with isoflurane (RWD). Two circular wounds (8 mm in diameter) were created on the back of each mouse. Subsequently, the wounds were inoculated with 50 µL of S. aureus suspension (1 × 10⁷ CFU mL⁻¹) and covered with sterile bandages. The treatment groups included: control, MN, US, Ce MN, AuB MN, AuB US, Ce/AuB, Ce/AuB US with US and rhEGF. Mice in the control groups were treated with adhesive tape only and the remaining groups were administered the corresponding medication daily. The progress of wound healing was documented by photographing the wounds, and wound areas were measured on days 5, 9, 12 and 14. Wound tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with H&E. Tissue sections were imaged using a virtual section scanning microscope (Olympus VS200) and changes in wound area were quantified using ImageJ software.

Histological analysis

Freshly excised tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with H&E and Masson trichrome. The thickness of the epidermal layer of the skin and the collagen content in the dermis was measured using ImageJ software to evaluate tissue regeneration and repair.

Statistical analysis

In our experimental results, data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test after confirming normality and homogeneity of variances. When these assumptions were not met, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc correction was used and significance levels were indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.
