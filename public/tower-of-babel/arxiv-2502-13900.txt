Optimistically Optimistic Exploration for Provably Efficient Infinite-Horizon Reinforcement and Imitation Learning
Antoine Moulin, Gergely Neu, Luca Viano

Abstract
We study the problem of reinforcement learning in infinite-horizon discounted linear Markov decision processes (MDPs), and propose the first computationally efficient algorithm achieving rate-optimal regret guarantees in this setting. Our main idea is to combine two classic techniques for optimistic exploration: additive exploration bonuses applied to the reward function, and artificial transitions made to an absorbing state with maximal return. We show that, combined with a regularized approximate dynamic-programming scheme, the resulting algorithm achieves a regret of order 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d 3 ( 1 − γ ) − 7 / 2 T ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{d^{3}(1-\gamma)^{-7/2}T}) , where T T is the total number of sample transitions, γ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \gamma\in(0,1) is the discount factor, and d d is the feature dimensionality. The results continue to hold against adversarial reward sequences, enabling application of our method to the problem of imitation learning in linear MDPs, where we achieve state-of-the-art results.

Introduction

Since the breakthrough work of
Jin et al. (2019)
, the class of linear Markov decision processes (MDPs) has become a standard model for theoretical analysis of reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms under linear function approximation. This work demonstrated the possibility of constructing computationally and statistically efficient methods for large-scale RL, and pioneered an analysis technique that influenced the entire field of RL theory. Hundreds of follow-up papers have studied variations of this model, studying extensions such as learning with adversarial rewards
(
Neu and Olkhovskaya, 2021
;
Zhong and Zhang, 2024
;
Sherman et al., 2023b
;
Dai et al., 2023
;
Sherman et al., 2023a
;
Cassel and Rosenberg, 2024
;
Liu et al., 2023
)
, without rewards
(
Wang et al., 2020
;
Wagenmaker et al., 2022
;
Hu et al., 2022
)
, or with unknown features
Agarwal et al. (2020)
;
Uehara et al. (2021)
;
Zhang et al. (2022)
;
Mhammedi et al. (2024)
;
Modi et al. (2024)
. The linearity constraint itself has been relaxed in a variety of ways
Zanette et al. (2020)
;
Cai et al. (2020)
;
Du et al. (2021)
;
Weisz et al. (2024)
;
Golowich and Moitra (2024)
;
Wu et al. (2024b)
. However, practically all of these developments retained one major limitation of the original work of
Jin et al. (2019)
: it only applies to finite-horizon MDPs. Generalizations to the more challenging (and practically much more popular) infinite-horizon MDP models have so far remained very limited, yielding only highly impractical methods or suboptimal performance guarantees
(
Wei et al., 2021
)
. In this paper, we propose an efficient algorithm that successfully addresses this long-standing open problem.

We consider the problem of learning a nearly optimal policy in
\gamma
-discounted MDPs
(
Puterman, 1994
)
, under the linear MDP assumption first proposed by
Jin et al. (2019)
(see also
Yang and Wang, 2019
). We consider an interaction protocol where a learning agent interacts with the environment in a sequence of
K
episodes of geometrically distributed length, and aims to pick a sequence of policies such that its regret against the best fixed policy is as small as possible. Our algorithm achieves a regret bound of order
H\sqrt{d^{3}T}+H^{7/4}\sqrt{dT\log|\mathcal{A}|}
, where
d
is the feature dimensionality,
H=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}
is the effective horizon,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space, and
T
is the number of interactions. This implies a bound on the sample complexity of learning an
\varepsilon
-optimal policy of the order
\frac{H^{3}d^{3}+H^{7/2}d\log\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}{\varepsilon^{2}}
. The algorithm returns a single softmax policy that is fully described in terms of a
d
-dimensional parameter vector and a
d^{2}
-dimensional feature-covariance matrix. This constitutes the first sample-complexity result of the optimal order
1/\varepsilon^{2}
achieved by a computationally efficient algorithm. The regret guarantees are also shown to hold if the reward function changes adversarially over time, and we additionally provide an extension of our method for the setting of imitation learning.

On the technical side, our main contribution is the development of a new optimistic exploration mechanism that combines two classic ideas from two different eras of RL theory. First, following the recipe of
Jin et al. (2019)
, we make use of additive UCB-style exploration bonuses which have been successfuly used for several decades in both bandit problems
(
Lai and Robbins, 1985
;
Auer et al., 2002
;
Auer, 2002
;
Dani et al., 2008
;
Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011
)
and reinforcement learning
(
Kaelbling et al., 1996
;
Strehl and Littman, 2008
;
Jaksch et al., 2010
;
Azar et al., 2017
)
. Second (and more importantly), we adapt another classic (but apparently recently less well-known) idea underlying the
Rmax
\xspace
algorithm of
Brafman and Tennenholtz (2002)
(see also
Szita and Szepesvári, 2010
and Chapter 8 in
Kakade, 2003
). Roughly speaking, this technique amounts to replacing the standard empirical model estimate with a fixed optimistic estimate in state-action pairs that are very poorly explored. This addresses the notorious problem of empirical estimates in linear MDPs that they tend to have extremely high variance in under-explored states, which can only be offset with very large additive exploration bonuses. Our
Rmax
\xspace
-style scheme counteracts these large bonuses by effectively swapping out the possibly over-optimistic estimates obtained via additive bonuses with more reasonably sized estimates. Besides
Brafman and Tennenholtz (2002)
, our algorithm design and analysis is also strongly inspired by the recent work of
Cassel and Rosenberg (2024)
who proposed a slightly limited variant of the same exploration mechanism for finite-horizon MDPs.

Preliminaries

2.1
Markov decision processes

A Markov decision process (MDP) with reward function
r
is defined by the tuple
\mathcal{M}(r)=\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},r,P,\gamma,\nu_{0}\right)
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the (possibly infinite) state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the finite action space,
r:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\left[0,1\right]
is the reward function assigning rewards to each state-action-next-state transition,
P:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\Delta\left(\mathcal{X}\right)
is the transition kernel,
\gamma\in\left(0,1\right)
is the discount factor, and
\nu_{0}\in\Delta\left(\mathcal{X}\right)
is the initial-state distribution. For convenience, we will assume that
\mathcal{X}
is countable but note that this can be lifted at the expense of making the measure-theoretic notation much heavier.
The MDP
\mathcal{M}(r)
models a sequential decision-making problem between a decision-making
agent
and its
environment
. The interaction starts with the environment drawing the random initial state
X_{0}\sim\nu_{0}
, whereafter in each time step
t=0,1,2,\dots
, the following steps are repeated: the agent observes state
X_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
, takes action
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
, and consequently the environment generates the next state
X_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot|X_{t},A_{t})
, resulting in reward
R_{t}=r(X_{t},A_{t},X_{t+1})
. With a slight abuse of notation, we denote the mean reward of a state-action pair
\left(x,a\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
by
r\left(x,a\right)=\mathbb{E}_{X^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot|x,a)}\left[r(x,a,X^{\prime})\right]
.

A
stationary state-feedback policy
(or, in short,
policy
) is a randomized behavior rule
\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
that determines the action taken in each time step
t
as
A_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|X_{t})
. The
action-value function
of a policy
\pi
in
\mathcal{M}
is defined for any state-action pair
\left(x,a\right)
as

\displaystyle Q_{P,r}^{\pi}\left(x,a\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P,\pi}\left[\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{\tau}r\left(X_{\tau},A_{\tau}\right)\middle|(X_{0},A_{0})=(x,a)\right]\,,

where
\mathbb{E}_{P,\pi}
denotes the expectation with respect to the random sequence of states and actions generated by the transition kernel
P
and the policy
\pi
. The
value function
of
\pi
at state
x
is defined as
V_{P,r}^{\pi}\left(x\right)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim\pi\left(\cdot\middle|x\right)}\bigl[Q_{P,r}^{\pi}\left(x,A\right)\bigr]
. With some abuse of notation, we define the conditional expectation operator
P:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}
via its action
\bigl(Pf\bigr)\left(x,a\right)=\mathbb{E}_{X^{\prime}\sim P\left(\cdot\middle|x,a\right)}\left[f\left(X^{\prime}\right)\right]
for any function
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
and state-action pair
\left(x,a\right)
. Its adjoint
P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
is the operator that acts on distributions
\mu\in\Delta\left(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\right)
as
P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu=\mathbb{E}_{\left(X,A\right)\sim\mu}\left[P\left(\cdot\middle|X,A\right)\right]
. With this notation, the value functions can be shown to satisfy the
Bellman equations
written as

Q_{P,r}^{\pi}=r+\gamma PV_{P,r}^{\pi}\,.

For convenience, we also introduce the operator
E:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}
defined via
\bigl(Ef\bigr)\left(x,a\right)=f\left(x\right)
and whose adjoint acts on state-action distributions as
\bigl(E^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\bigr)\left(x\right)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left(x,a\right)
. When interacting with an MDP, any stationary policy
\pi
induces a unique
state-occupancy measure
denoted as
\nu\left(\pi\right)\in\Delta\left(\mathcal{X}\right)
and a state-action occupancy measure
\mu\left(\pi\right)\in\Delta\left(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\right)
defined (with an unusual but helpful abuse of notation) as

\nu\left(\pi,\cdot\right)=\left(1-\gamma\right)\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{\tau}\mathbb{P}_{P,\pi}\left[X_{\tau}\in\cdot\right]\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mu\left(\pi,\cdot\right)=\left(1-\gamma\right)\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{\tau}\mathbb{P}_{P,\pi}\left[(X_{\tau},A_{\tau})\in\cdot\right].

Optimistically augmented Markov decision processes

A key concept in our algorithm design is that of
optimistically augmented Markov decision processes
(OA-MDPs), inspired by the construction of
Brafman and Tennenholtz (2002)

\mathcal{M}(r)

\mathcal{X}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=\mathcal{X}\cup\left\{x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\}

x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}

heaven

ascension function

p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}:\mathcal{X}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow[0,1]

x,a
to
x^{\prime}

\displaystyle P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\!\!\left(\cdot\middle|x,a\right)=\bigl(1-p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\!\!\left(x,a\right)\bigr)P\left(\cdot\middle|x,a\right)+p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\!\!\left(x,a\right)\mathbb{I}_{\left\{x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\in\cdot\right\}}\,.

x,a,x^{\prime}

r(x,a,x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}})=R_{\max}

r^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(x,a\right)=\mathbb{E}_{X^{\prime}\sim P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\!\left(\cdot\middle|x,a\right)}\left[r\left(x,a,X^{\prime}\right)\right]=\bigl(1-p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\!\!\left(x,a\right)\bigr)r\left(x,a\right)+p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\!\!\left(x,a\right)R_{\max}\,.

x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}

x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}

2.3
Online learning in linear MDPs

We consider a variation of the MDP setup described above, incorporating two modifications:
i)
periodic resets of the state evolution to the initial-state distribution
\nu_{0}
, and
ii)
the ability of the environment to change the reward function adversarially after each reset. This is a natural adaptation
1
1

1

See Section
6
for a discussion of the role of resets and related online-learning settings.
of the well-explored setting of online learning in adversarial MDPs to the discounted-reward case we study in this work. More precisely, we consider the following sequential interaction process between the learning agent and its environment. The interaction proceeds through
T
time steps, organized into
K
episodes (of random length) as follows. The initial state drawn as
X_{0}\sim\nu_{0}
and then the following steps are repeated in every consecutive round
t=0,1,\dots,T
:

•

The agent observes the state
X_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
,

•

the agent chooses an action
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
,

•

the environment generates the next state
X_{t+1}^{\prime}\sim P\left(\cdot\middle|X_{t},A_{t}\right)
,

•

the environment selects a reward function
r_{t}
,

•

the agent receives a reward
r_{t}\left(X_{t},A_{t}\right)\in\left[0,1\right]
and observes the function
r_{t}
,

•

with probability
\gamma
, the process moves to the next state
X_{t+1}=X_{t+1}^{\prime}
, otherwise a new episode begins and the process is reset to the initial-state distribution as
X_{t+1}\sim\nu_{0}
.

Without significant loss of generality, we will restrict the environment to update the reward function only at the end of each episode, and use
r_{k}
to refer to the reward function within episode
k
. Other than this restriction, the environment is free to choose the rewards in an adaptive (and possibly adversarial) way. The objective for the agent is to select a sequence of policies
\pi_{k}
so as to minimize its
pseudo-regret
over
K
episodes with respect to an arbitrary comparator policy
\pi^{\star}:\mathcal{X}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\rightarrow\Delta\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
, given by

\mathfrak{R}_{K}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\nu_{0},V_{P,r_{k}}^{\pi^{\star}}-V_{P,r_{k}}^{\pi_{k}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\mu\left(\pi^{\star}\right)-\mu\left(\pi_{k}\right),r_{k}\right\rangle\,,

where the second equality follows from the definition of occupancy measures. Since the learning agent can only learn about the transition function via interaction with the environment, it needs to address the classic dilemma of exploration versus exploitation. Clearly, this setup generalizes the more standard problem formulation where
r_{k}=r
holds for all episodes
k
. In this case,
\frac{\mathfrak{R}_{K}}{K}
corresponds to the expected suboptimality of the average policy played by the agent.

In later sections, we will consider the following structural assumption on the transitions.

Assumption 1

(Linear MDP)
.

A discounted MDP
\mathcal{M}=\left(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},r,P,\gamma,\nu_{0}\right)
is a

\varphi:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an unknown map

We will also use the operators
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\real^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}
such that
\left(\Phi\theta\right)\left(x,a\right)=\left\langle\theta,\varphi(x,a)\right\rangle
holds for any
\theta\in\real^{d}
and
M:\real^{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left(Mf\right)_{i}=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}f(x^{\prime})m_{i}(x^{\prime})
. Thus, we can write the transition operator and the reward function as
P=\Phi M
and

. Moreover, we assume that
\left\|w\right\|\leq W_{\max}

3
Algorithm

Our algorithm implements the principle of
optimism in the face of uncertainty
(OFU), by combining two classic ideas for optimistic exploration in reinforcement learning. We refer to these two separate mechanisms as two
degrees of optimism
, with
first-degree optimism
defined using the idea of exploration bonuses added to the rewards, and
second-degree optimism
leveraging the notion of optimistically augmented MDPs defined in Section
2.2
. These two incentives for exploration are respectively inspired by the upper-confidence-bound (UCB) methods popularized by
Azar et al. (2017)
, and the classic
Rmax
\xspace
algorithm of
Brafman and Tennenholtz (2002)
. These two mechanisms are combined with the regularized approximate dynamic programming method of
Moulin and Neu (2023)
, called “regularized approximate value iteration with upper confidence bounds” (
RAVI-UCB
\xspace
).

3.1
Overview

We begin by describing each element of our solution in general terms, and provide the pseudocode of the resulting algorithm (specifically tailored to linear MDPs) as Algorithm
1
. In recognition of the influence of the two algorithms mentioned above, we refer to our method as
Rmax-RAVI-UCB
\xspace
.

Regularized dynamic programming.

If the transition kernel
P
were known, the learner could achieve low regret by deploying the following regularized value iteration (RVI) scheme:

\displaystyle Q_{k+1}=r_{k}+\gamma PV_{k},~~~V_{k+1}(x)=\max_{u\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}\left\{\left\langle{u},{Q_{k+1}(x,\cdot)}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{\eta}\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(u\middle\|\pi_{k}(\cdot|x)\right)\right\},

(1)

and update its policies as
\pi_{k+1}(x|a)\propto\pi_{k}(x|a)e^{\eta Q_{k+1}(x,a)}
for some positive learning-rate parameter
\eta>0
. As observed by
Moulin and Neu (2023)
, the regularization in the policy updates is helpful for controlling the difference between consecutive policies and occupancy measures, thus addressing a major challenge that one faces when analyzing approximate DP methods in infinite-horizon MDPs. Unfortunately,
P
is unknown and needs to be estimated. The estimation error introduced in this process is taken care of by the two degrees of optimistic adjustments we describe next.

First-degree optimism.

Our method will make use of a (possibly implicitly defined) sequence of estimates of the transition operator
\widehat{P}_{k}:\real^{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\real^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}
, and an associated sequence of
exploration bonuses

\CB_{k}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\real
. We say that the sequence of bonuses is
valid
if it satisfies
\left|\left(\bigl(\widehat{P}_{k}-P\bigr)V_{k}\right)(x,a)\right|\leq\CB_{k}(x,a)
holds for all value-function estimates
V_{k}
calculated by the algorithm, simultaneously for all
x,a
and
k
. Using this property, a key idea in our algorithm is to use
\widehat{P}_{k}V_{k}+\CB_{k}
as an upper confidence bound on
PV_{k}
, therefore providing an optimistic estimate of the ideal value-function updates (
1
).

Second-degree optimism.

Unfortunately, relying only on first-degree optimism as defined above may result in value estimates that grow without bounds, thus leading to unstable policy updates. In order to prevent this unbounded growth, we employ the idea of optimistically augmented MDPs (defined in Section
2.2
), with the ascension function defined as
p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}(x,a)=\sigma\left(\alpha\CB_{k}\left(x,a\right)-\omega\right)
, where
\sigma:z\mapsto\frac{e^{z}}{1+e^{z}}
is the sigmoid function and
\alpha>0
and
\omega>0
are positive hyperparameters. Technically, this is implemented by defining our action-value updates for each
x,a
as

Q_{k+1}\left(x,a\right)=\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\!\!\left(x,a\right)\right)\left(r_{k}\left(x,a\right)+\CB_{k}\left(x,a\right)+\gamma\widehat{P}_{k}V_{k}\left(x,a\right)\right)+p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\!\!\left(x,a\right)\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\,.

This adjustment makes sure that the value estimates remain bounded, thanks to the multiplicative effect of the ascension function that effectively trades off the possibly huge values of
\CB_{k}+\gamma\widehat{P}_{k}V_{k}
by the constant upper bound
R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
in highly uncertain state-action pairs. Specifically, the effective bonus
\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)\odot\CB_{k}
is bounded
independently
of the magnitude of the value estimates
V_{k}
, which is crucial for preventing the exponential growth of their magnitude with
k
. Additionally, supposing that
\CB_{k}
is a valid sequence of exploration bonuses, one can verify that the inequality
Q_{k+1}\geq r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}+\gamma P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{k}
holds elementwise—that is, the estimates
Q_{k}
provide upper bounds on the ideal action-value updates (
1
) defined in the optimistically augmented MDP.

Model estimation and bonus design.

For estimating the transition model and defining the exploration bonuses, we follow the classic approach of
Jin et al. (2019)
(see also
Neu and Pike-Burke, 2020
). Specifically, we define the least-squares model estimate
\widehat{P}_{k}=\Phi\widehat{M}_{k}
as the operator that maps a function
v\in\real^{\mathcal{X}}
to
\Phi\widehat{M_{k}v}\in\real^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}
. The vector
\widehat{M_{k}v}\in\real^{d}
is the solution to the least-squares regression problem with features
\left\{{\varphi(X_{t},A_{t})}\right\}^{T_{k}-1}_{t=1}
and targets

3.3
Discussion

Before moving to the the analysis, we highlight some important features of our method.

Computational and storage complexity.

Due to its design outlined above (and detailed in
Algorithm

1
),
Rmax-RAVI-UCB
\xspace
produces a sequence of policies that are simply parameterized by a
d
-dimensional vector and a
d^{2}
-dimensional covariance matrix. Specifically, this is made possible by keeping the bonus function fixed within each epoch and resetting the policy to uniform at the beginning of each new epoch. Therefore, storing the policies in memory and drawing actions in each new state can be both done efficiently. This should be contrasted with most other known algorithms making use of softmax policies, which crucially rely on clipped value-function estimates that cannot be stored or sampled from efficiently (as they require storing the entire history of parameter vectors and exploration bonuses). Examples of such methods include
Cai et al. (2020)
;
Zhong and Zhang (2024)
;
Sherman et al. (2023b)
;
Moulin and Neu (2023)
. This not only makes the implementation of these algorithms impractical, but also results in suboptimal regret guarantees due to the excessive complexity of the policy and value-function classes. This major improvement is made possible in our algorithm by the incorporation of second-degree optimism (inspired by both
Brafman and Tennenholtz, 2002
and
Cassel and Rosenberg, 2024
), which obviates the need for explicit clipping of the value estimates and keeps these bounded via alternative means. All other elements in our algorithm (such as estimating the value estimates via least-squares regression) are standard, and match the complexity of other efficient methods for online learning in linear MDPs
Jin et al. (2019)
;
Wang et al. (2021)
;
He et al. (2023)
.

Relation with existing algorithms.

Being a combination of
Rmax
\xspace
and
RAVI-UCB
\xspace
, our algorithm can recover these two extremes and several other known methods by an appropriate choice of hyperparameters. Setting
\CB_{k}=0
, we recover algorithms which leverage only
second-degree
optimism. For example, in the particular case of
\alpha=\infty
and tabular features, we recover
Rmax
\xspace
up to some very minor changes
(
Brafman and Tennenholtz, 2002
;
Szita and Szepesvári, 2010
)
. On the other hand, using the ascension function
p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}_{k}(x,a)=\mathds{1}\left\{{r_{k}\left(x,a\right)+\CB_{k}\left(x,a\right)+\gamma\widehat{P}_{k}V_{k}\left(x,a\right)\geq\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}}\right\}
essentially recovers the standard truncation rule applied by most related methods (under the condition that the exploration bonuses be valid). In particular, under this choice of
p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}_{k}
, our algorithm reduces to
RAVI-UCB
\xspace
. Setting the regularization parameter
\eta=\infty
in the resulting method recovers optimistic value iteration methods such as
Azar et al. (2017)
;
Jin et al. (2019)
.

4
Main Result and Analysis

The following theorem states our main result about the performance of
Rmax-RAVI-UCB
\xspace
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds, and that Algorithm
1
is executed with parameters specified in Appendix
B.7
for a fixed number
K
of episodes. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\mathfrak{R}_{K}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{d^{3}H^{3}K}+\sqrt{dH^{9/2}K\log\left(\left|\mathcal{A}\right|\right)}\right)\,.

For the commonly studied case where the reward function
r
is fixed, this result can be easily translated to a bound on the sample complexity of producing an
\varepsilon
-optimal policy as well, as stated below.

Corollary 2
.

Let
I
be drawn uniformly from
\left\{1,2,\dots,K\right\}
. Then, policy
\pi_{I}
produced by
Algorithm

1
(with the same parameter tuning as in Theorem
1
) satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{I}\left[V^{\pi^{*}}_{P,r}-V^{\pi_{I}}_{P,r}\right]\leq\varepsilon
, if the number of episodes satisfies

K=\Omega\left(\frac{H^{3}d^{3}+H^{9/2}d\log\left|\mathcal{A}\right|}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\,.

As the length of each episode is geometrically distributed with expectation
H
, the number of interaction steps satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[T\right]=HK
when the number of episodes
K
is fixed. Taking this into account, both results can be restated in terms of the number of sample transitions
T
. Likewise, similar results can be proved when treating the sample size
T
as fixed and letting
K
be the smallest (random) number of episodes covering the sample budget.
The sample complexity bound in Corollary
2
is rate-optimal in the sense that the dependence on
\varepsilon
cannot be improved in virtue of the
\Omega(H^{3}XA\epsilon^{-2})
lower bound in
Azar et al. (2012)
. Notice their lower bound is proven for the setting of generative model access to the environment which is strictly easier than the trajectory access model we study.

In the remaining part of this section, we describe the main steps constituting the proof of Theorem
1
. The analysis will make crucial use of the notion of optimistically augmented MDPs defined in Section
2.2
. Specifically, we define an augmented MDP for each episode
k
as
\mathcal{M}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}_{k}=\mathcal{M}^{+}(r_{k},p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}})
and use the shorthand
\mathcal{M}_{k}=\mathcal{M}(r_{k})
for the true MDP with reward function
r_{k}
. Letting
\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}(\pi)
denote the occupancy measure induced by policy
\pi
in
\mathcal{M}_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}
, the first step in our analysis is to rewrite the regret as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\mu\left(\pi^{\star}\right)-\mu\left(\pi_{k}\right),r_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\underbrace{\left\langle\mu\left(\pi^{\star}\right)-\mu\left(\pi_{k}\right),r_{k}-r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle}_{=\texttt{reward-bias}_{k}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\underbrace{\left\langle\mu\left(\pi^{\star}\right)-\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle}_{=-\texttt{model-bias}_{k}\left(\pi^{\star}\right)}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right)-\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle}_{=\regretKplus}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\underbrace{\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right)-\mu\left(\pi_{k}\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle}_{=\texttt{model-bias}_{k}\left(\pi_{t}\right)}\,.

Here,
\regretKplus
corresponds to the (normalized) regret of
RAVI-UCB
\xspace
in the sequence of OA-MDPs
\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)
, and the other terms account for the difference between this term and the regret in the original problem. Some of these are easy to handle by exploiting the optimistic nature of our augmentation technique, whereas others require a more careful tuning of the ascension functions and exploration bonuses.

A large part of the analysis will be based on the condition that the exploration bonuses
\left\{\CB_{k}\right\}_{k}
used for performing optimistic value iteration are
valid
estimates of the uncertainty we have on the model, in the sense that the following event holds

\mathcal{E}_{\text{valid}}=\left\{\forall\left(x,a\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A},\forall k\in\left[K\right],\left|\left(P-\widehat{P}_{k}\right)V_{k}\left(x,a\right)\right|\leq\CB_{k}\left(x,a\right)\right\}\,.

(2)

For the sake of clearly presenting the main ideas of our analysis, we will work under the condition that the event
\mathcal{E}_{\text{valid}}
holds, and we will show later in the context of linear MDPs that this is indeed true with high probability. Furthermore, we will work under the condition that for any
k\in\left[K\right]
, there exists
Q_{\max}>0
such that
\left\|Q_{k}\right\|_{\infty}\leq Q_{\max}
. We will show this is true under an appropriate choice of
p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}
, and give an expression for
Q_{\max}
. Finally, we assume the episode lengths
\left(L_{k}\right)
are all less than some
L_{\max}
and refer to this event as
\mathcal{E}_{L}
. We will show that this also holds with high probability.

4.1
Controlling the bias due to the augmented rewards

The first lemma controls the bias introduced by the second-degree optimism introduced into the reward function in terms of the ascension functions.

Lemma 3

For any choice of

\texttt{reward-bias}_{k}\leq R_{\max}\left\langle\mu\left(\pi_{k}\right),p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle

The proof essentially follows from the definition of

4.2
Controlling the bias due to the augmented transitions

Next, we provide a bound on the bias due to introducing second-degree optimism in the transition function. It is easy to see that playing in the augmented MDP

always yields higher discounted return due to the presence of the heaven state

. The following lemma formalizes this claim by providing a bound on
\texttt{model-bias}_{k}\left(\pi\right)=\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right)-\mu\left(\pi\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle

Let

be any policy and

-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\texttt{model-bias}_{k}\left(\pi^{\star}\right)\leq 0\,,\text{ and}\;\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\texttt{model-bias}_{k}\left(\pi_{k}\right)\leq\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\mu\left(\pi_{k}\right),p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle\,.

(3)

4.3
Regret analysis in the optimistically augmented MDP

To control the main term
\regretKplus

RAVI-UCB
\xspace
due to
Moulin and Neu (2023)
with some appropriate changes. The key idea is to define an estimate
\widehat{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}

\left(\widehat{P}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}_{k}v\right)\left(x,a\right)=\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(x,a\right)\right)\cdot\left(\widehat{P}_{k}v\right){\left(x,a\right)}+p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(x,a\right)\cdot\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\,.

Then, it is easy to verify that the validity of the exploration bonuses (Eq.
2

\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)\odot\CB_{k}
satisfy the following analogous validity condition in the augmented MDP:

\left|\left(P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}-\widehat{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)V_{k}\left(x,a\right)\right|\leq\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(x,a\right)\right)\CB_{k}\left(x,a\right)\,.

With these insights, our algorithm can be seen as an instantiation of
RAVI-UCB

on the sequence of optimistically augmented MDPs

Suppose that the bonuses
\left\{\CB_{k}\right\}
are valid in the sense of Equation

(4)

5
Application: Imitation Learning from features alone

In this section, we show an application of the results presented in Section
4
, making crucial use of
the fact that our main result in Theorem
1
allows adaptively chosen reward functions.

5.1
Setting and motivation

We consider a linear MDP with an unknown reward function
r_{\textup{true}}
defined in terms of the feature map

\varphi_{r}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\real^{d_{r}}
as
r_{\textup{true}}(x,a)=\left\langle{\varphi_{r}(x,a)},{w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle
or with the operator notation
r_{\textup{true}}=\Phi_{r}w_{\mathrm{true}}
. The transition
function is defined using the feature map
\varphi_{P}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\real^{d_{P}}
via
P(x^{\prime}|x,a)=\left\langle{\varphi_{P}(x,a)},{m(x^{\prime})}\right\rangle
. It is easy to see that this is a linear MDP in terms of the concatenated feature map of dimension
d=d_{r}+d_{P}
. We consider a learning problem that we call
imitation learning from features alone
, where a learner
receives as input a data set of feature vectors
\mathcal{D}_{\pi_{E}}=\left\{{\varphi_{r}(X^{i}_{E},A^{i}_{E})}\right\}^{\tau_{E}}_{i=1}

where
X^{i}_{E},A^{i}_{E}\sim\mu({\pi_{E}})
generated by an
expert policy

{\pi_{E}}
by interacting with the MDP

\mathcal{M}(r_{\textup{true}})
. The learner is tasked with producing an
\varepsilon
-suboptimal policy
\pi^{\mathrm{out}}
that
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl\langle\nu_{0},V_{r_{\textup{true}}}^{{\pi_{E}}}-V_{r_{\textup{true}}}^{\pi^{\mathrm{out}}}\bigr\rangle\bigr]\leq\varepsilon
. The learner has no knowledge of the reward function
r_{\textup{true}}
apart from knowing a function class including
it, but has access to the feature maps and can interact with the MDP.

This framework captures the important special case where the rewards depend only on the state but not on the action,
by choosing a feature map
\varphi_{r}
that only depends on the states. In this case, the data set taken as input is
significantly less informative than a full record of states and actions
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{x,a}}_{\pi_{E}}=\left\{{(X^{i}_{E},A^{i}_{E})}\right\}^{\tau_{E}}_{i=1}
. However, observing expert actions has been found restrictive in various practical
scenarios, which gives a strong motivation to study the setting described above.
For instance in robotics, a features only dataset describing a robotic manipulation task
can be collected easily
via cameras and sensors
(
Torabi et al., 2018
;
Zhu et al., 2020
;
Yang et al., 2019
;
Torabi et al., 2019
)
.
On the other hand, collecting actions on top of features is more challenging as it
requires knowledge of the internal dynamics of the observed robots.
Another example of imitation learning from features alone is learning to drive from a video
which does not show the driver’s actions but only the movements of the car induced by those actions.
Finally, notice that imitation learning from states only, studied for example in
Sun et al. (2019)
, is a particular
case of our setting for
\varphi_{r}(x,a)=\mathbf{e}_{x}
. In this case, the expert dataset consists of states sampled from
the expert state occupancy measure.

5.2
Algorithm and sample complexity guarantees

In the following, we propose a provably efficient algorithm for imitation learning from features alone.
Our algorithm design and analysis is driven by the following decomposition of the regret, defined as
\regretIL=\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{\nu_{0}},{V^{{\pi_{E}}}_{P,r_{\textup{true}}}-V^{\pi_{k}}_{P,r_{\textup{true}}}}\right\rangle
, in terms of an appropriately chosen sequence of reward functions
r_{1},\dots,r_{K}
:

(1-\gamma)\regretIL=\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{r_{k}},{\mu\left({{\pi_{E}}}\right)-\mu\left({\pi_{k}}\right)}\right\rangle+\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{\Phi_{r}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu(\pi_{k})-\Phi_{r}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu({\pi_{E}})},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle.

The first term in this decomposition corresponds to the regret of our online learning algorithm for adversarial MDPs, and can be controlled by invoking
Rmax-RAVI-UCB
\xspace
on the sequence of rewards
\left(r_{k}\right)
. The second term in the decomposition corresponds to the regret of another online learning algorithm picking a sequence of reward functions, aiming to minimize the sequence of linear loss functions
\Phi_{r}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu(\pi_{k})-\Phi_{r}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu({\pi_{E}})
(or at least do as well as the fixed comparator
w_{\mathrm{true}}
). This objective can be achieved by running a standard online learning method such as projected online gradient descent (OGD,
Zinkevich, 2003
), using a sequence of unbiased loss estimates that can be computed efficiently using the observed feature vectors.
The full algorithm is specified as
Algorithm

3
in Appendix
A
and it is shown to satisfy the following guarantees.

Theorem 9
.

Algorithm
3
, when run for
K=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left({d^{3}H^{9/2}\varepsilon^{-2}\log(\left|\mathcal{A}\right|)}\right)
iterations with an expert dataset of size
\tau_{E}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\left({W_{\max}^{2}H^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}}\right)
, outputs an
\varepsilon
-suboptimal policy.

Notably, the above is the first known bound for this setting that achieves
a scaling
\varepsilon^{-2}
with the precision parameter for both the number of MDP interactions
K
and expert samples
\tau_{E}
.
We provide a detailed comparison with existing imitation learning theory works in Appendix
C
,
whereas the complete technical details supporting the above theorem are provided in Appendix
D
.

5.3
Lower bounds

The upper bound of Theorem
9
depends both on the number of interaction steps
K
and the expert
samples
\tau_{E}
. It is natural to ask if these dependences can be improved in the setting we consider. We address both
of these questions in the negative in a set of lower bounds described below.

Lower bound on the number of MDP interactions
K
.

Theorem
19
in Appendix
E
proves
that for any imitation learning from features alone algorithm, even in the setting
\tau_{E}=\infty
,
there exists an MDP and an expert policy where at least
K=\Omega\left({\frac{dH^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}}\right)
interactions
are needed to output a
\varepsilon
-optimal policy.
This lower bound shows that the upper bound provided for
K
in Theorem
9

achieves the optimal scaling in
\varepsilon
and can be improved at most by a factor
d^{2}H^{5/2}
.
More importantly, this lower bound marks a clear separation between standard and features only imitation learning:
purely offline learning (
K=0
) is impossible in imitation learning from features alone
while it is possible in standard imitation learning
where the experts actions are observed (see, e.g.,
Foster et al., 2024
).

In the construction of the lower bound, we consider a two state MDP with a reward
function that depends only on the state variable and we set
\varphi_{r}(x,a)=\mathbf{e}_{x}
which prevents
observing expert actions.
In this MDP, for
\tau_{E}=\infty
, the learner observes the expert state occupancy
measure exactly and
therefore, the “good” state that achieves the maximum of the expert state occupancy measure.
However, since the learner does not know the MDP dynamics, interactions with the MDP
are needed to find out the action that allows to maximize the learner state occupancy measure
in the “good” state.
Following standard techniques in MDP and bandits lower bound, we can ensure that the amount
of MDP interactions is at least
\Omega\left({\varepsilon^{-2}}\right)
.

Lower bound on the number of expert samples
\tau_{E}
.

Theorem
20
in Appendix
E
proves
that for any algorithm for imitation learning from features alone, even for
K=\infty
,
there exists an MDP and an expert policy where learning an
\varepsilon
-optimal policy requires at least

\tau_{E}=\Omega\left({W_{\max}^{2}H^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}}\right)
expert samples.
This lower bound proves that Algorithm
3
scales optimally with all the problem parameters
and in the precision
\varepsilon
. Moreover, this lower bound highlights again a clear distinction with standard
imitation learning.
On the one hand, standard imitation learning can be reduced to a supervised
classification problem when the optimal policy
is deterministic and the actions are observed in the dataset. As a consequence,
the classic lower bound for supervised classification of order
\mathcal{O}\left({\varepsilon^{-1}}\right)

(
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, 2014
)

holds and it is matched by a purely offline behavioural cloning
(
Rajaraman et al., 2020
)
.
On the other hand, in our lower bound construction we choose again
\varphi_{r}(x,a)=\mathbf{e}_{x}

to make the expert actions unobservable for the learner and we can prove a larger lower bound of order
\mathcal{O}\left({\varepsilon^{-2}}\right)

which holds even if the expert policy is deterministic.

For the proof, our construction is again a two state MDP (a “good” state with high reward and a “bad” state with
lower reward). The expert policy is chosen to be the optimal one.
The transition dynamics and the initial distribution are chosen in a way that the expert
state occupancy meausure is only marginally higher in the “good” state. That is, the
expert occupancy measure equals roughly
1/2+\varepsilon
in the “good” state
and
1/2-\varepsilon
in the “bad” state.
By standard arguments, we then conclude that the learner needs at least
\Omega\left({\varepsilon^{-2}}\right)

samples from the expert to identify the “good” state.

6
Concluding remarks

We close by discussing a few open problems and potential improvements to our results.

On the objective function.

We have focused on a relatively under-studied objective function: the discounted return from a fixed initial-state distribution. This is different from the objectives studied by other works such as
Liu and Su (2020)
;
He et al. (2021)
;
Zhou et al. (2021)
, but arguably more natural when one is interested in learning algorithms that produce a single near-optimal policy at the end of an interactive learning period (which is the case in most practical applications one can think of). It is easy to see that resets to the initial state are absolutely necessary in this setting, unless one wants to make strong assumptions about the transition dynamics. A more exciting question is if our algorithm can be adapted to the significantly more challenging setting of undiscounted infinite-horizon reinforcement learning where existing methods
(
Wei et al., 2021
;
Hong et al., 2024
;
He et al., 2024
)
either obtain suboptimal regret bounds or leverage oracles whose computationally efficient implementation is unknown. So far, our attempts towards tackling this problem have remained unsuccessful. We believe that significant new ideas are necessary for solving this major open problem, but also that the techniques we introduce in this paper will be part of an eventual solution.

On second-degree optimism.

Our key technical contribution draws inspiration from two sources: the
Rmax
\xspace
algorithm of
Brafman and Tennenholtz (2002)
, and the very recent work of
Cassel and Rosenberg (2024)
. While many of our technical tools are directly imported from the latter work, the concept of optimistically augmented MDPs and the connection with
Rmax
\xspace
has arguably brought about a new level of understanding that can be potentially valuable for future work. It has certainly proved useful for our setting, where the notion of “contracted sub-MDP” used in the analysis of
Cassel and Rosenberg (2024)
cannot be meaningfully interpreted and used for analysis. We hope our work can bring some fresh attention to older (but apparently still powerful) ideas from the past of RL theory such as the
Rmax
\xspace
trick.

On the tightness of the bounds.

We find it very likely that our performance guarantees can be improved to some extent in terms of their dependence on
H
,
d
and
\log\left|\mathcal{A}\right|
. In fact, we have made no attempt to optimize the scaling with respect to these parameters, and actually believe that the
H^{9/4}
factor in the regret bound can be improved relatively easily. Specifically, we find it very likely that performing several value-function and policy updates at the end of each episode can reduce this factor—but we opted to keep the algorithm simple and the paper easy to read. We invite future researchers to verify this conjecture. Likewise, we believe that the dependence on the dimension
d
can be improved by using more sophisticated estimators and concentration inequalities (as done in the finite-horizon setting by
He et al., 2023
;
Agarwal et al., 2023
), but leave working out the (possibly non-trivial) details for another paper.

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right),Q_{P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}},r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}-Q_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}-\gamma P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}},r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}+\gamma P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}+\gamma P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}-Q_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}\right\rangle\,,

Plugging the definition of
P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}
and this time using Bellman’s equation for
Q_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\texttt{model-bias}_{k}\left(\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}+\gamma\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)\odot PV_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}+p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\odot\mathbf{e}_{x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}V_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}-Q_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right),p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\odot\left(\mathbf{e}_{x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}V_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}-\gamma PV_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right),p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\odot\left(\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\mathbf{1}-\gamma PV_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}\right)\right\rangle\,,

(6)

where the last equality is due to having
\left(\mathbf{e}_{x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}V_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}\right)\left(x,a\right)=V_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}\left(x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)=\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}
for any state-action pair
\left(x,a\right)
. The lower bound follows from noticing that
PV_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}\preceq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbf{1}\preceq\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\mathbf{1}
,

\displaystyle\texttt{model-bias}_{k}\left(\pi\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}-\frac{\gamma R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\right)\cdot\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right),p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=R_{\max}\cdot\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right),p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 0\,.

Moving to the upper bound, from Equation
6
and
PV_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}\succeq 0
, we get

\displaystyle\texttt{model-bias}_{k}\left(\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right),p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\odot\left(\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\mathbf{1}-\gamma PV_{P,r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}^{\pi}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right),p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\left\langle\mu\left(\pi\right),p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle\,,

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
24
.
∎

B.3
Proof of Lemma
5
(optimistic regret)

In order to prove Lemma
5
, we first need the following result that shows that the functions
Q_{k}
are optimistic estimates of an ideal sequence of dynamic-programming updates computed in the augmented MDPs. The statement is is an adaptation of Lemma 4.2 of
Moulin and Neu (2023)
, and its complete proof is provided below.

Lemma 10
.

Suppose that the bonuses
\CB_{k}
are valid for the MDP
\mathcal{M}_{k}
in the sense of Equation
2
. Then, for any
k
and any state-action pair
\left(x,a\right)\in\mathcal{X}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\times\mathcal{A}
, the iterates
Q_{k}
satisfy

r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}+\gamma P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{k}\leq Q_{k+1}\leq r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}+2\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)\odot\CB_{k}+\gamma P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{k}\,.

Proof.

For
x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}
and any action
a
, it is straightforward to check that both inequalities are equalities. Let
\left(x,a\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
. We have

\displaystyle r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(x,a\right)+\gamma P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{k}\left(x,a\right)

\displaystyle=r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(x,a\right)+\gamma\left(P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}-\widehat{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)V_{k}\left(x,a\right)+\gamma\widehat{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{k}\left(x,a\right)

\displaystyle\leq Q_{k+1}\left(x,a\right)

\displaystyle\leq r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(x,a\right)+2\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(x,a\right)\right)\CB_{k}\left(x,a\right)+\gamma P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{k}\left(x,a\right)\,,

where both inequalities use the fact that

\left|\left(P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}-\widehat{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)V_{k}\left(x,a\right)\right|\leq\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(x,a\right)\right)\CB_{k}\left(x,a\right)\,,

which is implied by the event
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{valid}}
in Equation
2
.
∎

See
5

Proof.

We decompose
\regretKplus
as follows

\displaystyle\regretKplus

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Bigl(\underbrace{\left(\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle-\left(1-\gamma\right)\left\langle\nu_{0},V_{k}\right\rangle\right)}_{=\Delta_{k}^{\star}}+\underbrace{\left(1-\gamma\right)\left\langle\nu_{0},V_{k}\right\rangle-\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle}_{=\Delta_{k}}\Bigr)\,,

where we defined
\Delta_{k}^{\star}
and
\Delta_{k}

\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle-\left\langle E^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right)-\gamma\left(P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right),V_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}+\gamma P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{k}-EV_{k+1}\right\rangle+\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right),EV_{k+1}-EV_{k}\right\rangle\,.

Using the lower bound on
Q_{k+1}

\Delta_{k}^{\star}\leq\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right),Q_{k+1}-EV_{k+1}\right\rangle+\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right),EV_{k+1}-EV_{k}\right\rangle\,,

where the term in

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{e}}V_{k+1}\left(x\right)

\pi_{k_{e}}=\pi_{\texttt{unif}}

The second term can be bounded with Hölder’s inequality,

\sum_{e=1}^{E\left(K\right)}\left\langle E^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu_{k_{e}}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right),V_{k_{e+1}}-V_{k_{e}}\right\rangle\leq\sum_{e=1}^{E\left(K\right)}\left\|\nu_{k_{e}}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi^{\star}\right)\right\|_{1}\left\|V_{k_{e+1}}-V_{k_{e}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 2E\left(K\right)Q_{\max}\,,

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\langle E^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right)-\gamma\left(P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right),V_{k}\right\rangle-\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right),EV_{k}\right\rangle-\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right),r_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}+\gamma P_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}V_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right),EV_{k}-Q_{k+1}\right\rangle+2\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right),\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)\odot\CB_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k}\right),EV_{k}-Q_{k+1}\right\rangle+2\left\langle\mu\left(\pi_{k}\right),\left(1-p_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)\odot\CB_{k}\right\rangle\,,

where the first inequality follows from the upper bound on
Q_{k+1}
in Lemma
10
and the term in
x=x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}
being equal to zero, and the second inequality is due to Lemma
24
. Next, noticing
\left\langle\mu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k+1}\right),Q_{k+1}\right\rangle=\left\langle\nu_{k}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi_{k+1}\right),V_{k+1}+\frac{1}{\eta}\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi_{k+1}\|\pi_{k}\right)\right\rangle

k

B.5
Proof of Lemma

.

, we need the following result.

.

\mathcal{E}_{L}
holds. Let
\left\{f_{k}\right\}_{k\in\left[K\right]}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}
be a sequence of functions with values in
\left[0,M\right]

\mathcal{F}_{k-1}

\sigma

\displaystyle\left\langle\mu\left(\pi_{k}\right),f_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(1-\gamma\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}f_{k}\left(X_{\tau},A_{\tau}\right)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\,.

Plugging it back in the previous display,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\mu\left(\pi_{k}\right),f_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(1-\gamma\right)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}f_{k}\left(X_{t},A_{t}\right)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\,.

almost surely. Using the concentration inequality from Lemma

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\mu\left(\pi_{k}\right),f_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(1-\gamma\right)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}f_{k}\left(X_{t},A_{t}\right)+4\left(1-\gamma\right)ML_{\max}\log\left(\frac{2K}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(1-\gamma\right)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}f_{k}\left(X_{t},A_{t}\right)+4M\log\left(\frac{2K}{\delta}\right)^{2}\,,

where we used that

Appendix C
Motivation for
Learning from Features Alone
and related works in imitation learning

Related works in theoretical imitation learning.

A special case of our setting is imitation learning from state-only expert trajectories, which is recovered when
\varphi_{r}(x,a)=\mathbf{e}_{x}
.
This setting was first studied in
Sun et al. (2019)
in the finite-horizon setting with general function approximation.
There are some notable differences between their work and ours, primarily that they focus on the finite-horizon setting and learn a non-stationary policy.
In principle, their algorithm could be applied to the infinite-horizon setting by truncating the trajectories after
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1-\gamma)^{-1}
steps.
However, this would still result in a non-stationary policy, whereas our approach outputs a stationary policy.
Their realizability assumption on the expert policy and expert state-value function is not required in our work which leverages, instead, the linear MDP assumption.
These assumptions are not directly comparable, even when the function classes in
Sun et al. (2019)
are assumed to be linear.
Indeed, the realizability assumption imposed in
Sun et al. (2019)
would imply having access to the values of the features
\sum_{a}{\pi_{E}}(a|x)\varphi(x,a)
for each state
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
In contrast, our approach does not require this additional knowledge about the expert.

Furthermore, the guarantees on the number of expert trajectories in
(
Sun et al., 2019
, Theorem 3.3)
adapted to the infinite-horizon setting,
would scale as
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}((1-\gamma)^{-4}\epsilon^{-2})
whereas we only require
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}((1-\gamma)^{-2}\epsilon^{-2})
state-only samples from the expert occupancy measure.

Similarly,
Arora et al. (2020)
develop a framework for imitation and representation learning from observation alone based on bilevel optimization but assume the realizability of the state-value function, which is not needed in our work.

The work of
Kidambi et al. (2021)
investigates the idea of exploration in state-only imitation learning.
Unlike our work, they focus on the finite-horizon setting and on different structural assumptions on the MDP.
Specifically,
Kidambi et al. (2021)
consider tabular MDPs, nonlinear kernel regulators, and MDPs with Gaussian transition kernels and bounded Eluder dimension, whereas our work focuses on infinite-horizon linear MDPs and observing only the feature directions visited by the expert, which is a weaker requirement than observing the states directly.
Moreover, our algorithm
FRA-IL
\xspace
is computationally efficient, whereas the model fitting step in
Kidambi et al. (2021)
cannot be implemented efficiently for various situations, including linear MDPs
(
Jin et al., 2019
)
and KNRs
(
Kakade et al., 2020
)
.

Wu et al. (2024a)
operate under a different set of assumptions, namely that the learner has access to a function class for the expert’s score function and that the expected state norm remains bounded during learning.
Under this setting, the authors are the first to achieve first- and second-order bounds for imitation learning, which lead to a faster rate in the case of low-variance expert policies and transitions. The authors do not quantify the MDP trajectory complexity, but it would scale suboptimally with
1/\epsilon
because they require an expensive
RL in the loop
routine that we avoid in our work.

Xu et al. (2022)
develop an analysis for horizon-free bounds on
\tau_{E}
for a special class of MDPs, where expert states can be visited only by visiting all preceding expert states.

The trajectory access to the MDP
\mathcal{M}\setminus r_{\textup{true}}
assumed in this work should not be confused with interactive/online imitation learning, where the expert can be queried during learning
(
Ross and Bagnell, 2010
;
Ross et al., 2011
;
Swamy et al., 2021
;
Li and Zhang, 2022
;
Lavington et al., 2022
;
Sekhari et al., 2024b
;
Sun et al., 2017
;
Sekhari et al., 2024a
)
. Furthermore, our trajectory access is a much weaker requirement compared to generative model access used in
(
Swamy et al., 2022
;
Kamoutsi et al., 2021
)
.

Moreover, it is important to note that we do not require any ergodicity or self-exploration properties of the dynamics, whereas such assumptions are needed in
(
Viano et al., 2022
;
Zeng et al., 2022b
)
. Additionally, uniformly good evaluation error, which is essentially possible only under generative model or ergodic dynamics assumptions, is required in
(
Wu et al., 2023
;
Zeng et al., 2022a
;
Zeng et al., 2023
)
.
Also, the use of exploration bonuses in imitation learning has also been useful for the related problem of finding the reward feasible set without using a generative model
(
Lazzati et al., 2024
;
Lindner et al., 2022
)
.

, we can express the regret as

\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{\pi}(\mu({\pi_{E}}))

\displaystyle\leq 10H(BW_{\max}+R_{\max})\sqrt{\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{K}}+24HW_{\max}B\sqrt{\frac{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}{\tau_{E}}}

D.2
Proof of Theorem
18
(regret bound for the reward player)

Theorem 18
.

Assume that
w_{\mathrm{true}}\in\mathcal{W}
for some non-empty closed convex set
\mathcal{W}
and that for any
w\in\mathcal{W}
,
\left\|w\right\|\leq W_{\max}
. Then, OGD with
\eta_{r}=\nicefrac{{W_{\max}}}{{B\sqrt{K}}}
ran for
K
iterations satisfies with probability at least
1-2\delta
that

\mathfrak{R}_{K}^{w}(w_{\mathrm{true}})\leq 10H(BW_{\max}+R_{\max})\sqrt{K\log 1/\delta}+24HW_{\max}KB\sqrt{\frac{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}{\tau_{E}}}\,.

Proof.

Given the definition of the feature expectation vector
\lambda(\pi)
, we can rewrite the regret for the reward player as follows

\left(1-\gamma\right)\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{w}(w_{\mathrm{true}})=\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{\lambda(\pi_{k})-\lambda({\pi_{E}})},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle\,.

Then, adding and subtracting the estimators for the occupancy measures, we get

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{w}(w_{\mathrm{true}})

\displaystyle=\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{\varphi_{r}(X_{k},A_{k})-\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{\lambda(\pi_{k})-\varphi_{r}(X_{k},A_{k})},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}-\lambda({\pi_{E}})},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle\,.

Now, using the regret bound for OGD
(
Zinkevich, 2003
)
, we can bound the first term in the decomposition above as

\displaystyle\sum^{K}_{k=1}

\displaystyle\left\langle{\varphi_{r}(X_{k},A_{k})-\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\max_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\left\|w_{\mathrm{true}}-w_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2\eta_{r}}+\frac{\eta_{r}}{2}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\|\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}-\varphi_{r}(X_{k},A_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2W_{\max}^{2}}{\eta_{r}}+2\eta_{r}B^{2}K\,,

Looking at the term
\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{\lambda(\pi_{k})-\varphi_{r}(X_{k},A_{k})},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle
, we notice that

\psi_{k}=\left\langle{\lambda(\pi_{k})-\varphi_{r}(X_{k},A_{k})},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle

is a martingale difference sequence such that

\left|\left\langle{\lambda(\pi_{k})-\varphi_{r}(X_{k},A_{k})},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle\right|\leq 4R_{\max}\,.

Applying Azuma-Hoeffding’s inequality, we have that with probability
1-\delta

\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{\lambda(\pi_{k})-\varphi_{r}(X_{k},A_{k})},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle\leq R_{\max}\sqrt{8K\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}.

Then, plugging in this bound in the regret decomposition we obtain

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{w}(w_{\mathrm{true}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2W_{\max}^{2}}{\eta_{r}}+2\eta_{r}B^{2}K+R_{\max}\sqrt{8K\log 1/\delta}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}-\lambda({\pi_{E}})},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle\,.

Then, we treat the last term using Cauchy-Schwartz’s inequality

\displaystyle\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\langle{\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}-\lambda({\pi_{E}})},{w_{k}-w_{\mathrm{true}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{K}_{k=1}\left\|w_{\mathrm{true}}-w^{k}\right\|_{2}\left\|\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}-\lambda({\pi_{E}})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2W_{\max}K\left\|\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}-\lambda({\pi_{E}})\right\|_{2}\,.

It remains to find a high probability (dimension-free) upper bound on
\left\|\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}-\lambda({\pi_{E}})\right\|_{2}
. First, notice that
\left\|\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}-\lambda({\pi_{E}})\right\|_{2}=\left\|(\tau_{E})^{-1}\left({\sum^{\tau_{E}}_{i=1}\varphi_{r}(X^{i}_{E},A^{i}_{E})-\lambda({\pi_{E}})}\right)\right\|_{2}
. Then, we use the notation
u_{x,a}=\varphi_{r}(x,a)-\lambda({\pi_{E}})
for all state action pairs
x,a
and using that for all
x,a\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,
\left\|\varphi_{r}(x,a)\right\|_{2}\leq B
, we have

\sum^{\tau_{E}}_{i=1}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|u_{X^{i}_{E},A^{i}_{E}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\leq\sum^{\tau_{E}}_{i=1}\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\|\varphi_{r}(X^{i}_{E},A^{i}_{E})-\lambda({\pi_{E}})\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\leq 4\tau_{E}B^{2}\,.

Moreover, for any
x,a\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,
\left\|u_{x,a}\right\|\leq 2B
and
\mathbb{E}\left[{u_{X^{i}_{E},A^{i}_{E}}}\right]=0
because of the distribution of the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\pi_{E}}
. Thus, by applying
(
Hsu et al., 2012
, Proposition 2)
, it holds that for all
t>0

\mathbb{P}\left[{\left\|\sum^{\tau_{E}}_{i=1}u_{X^{i}_{E},A^{i}_{E}}\right\|>\sqrt{4\tau_{E}}B+\sqrt{32\tau_{E}t}B+(8/3)2Bt}\right]\leq e^{-t}

Therefore, choosing
t=\log\frac{1}{\delta}
, we obtain that with probability
1-\delta

\displaystyle\left\|\sum^{\tau_{E}}_{i=1}\varphi_{r}(X^{i}_{E},A^{i}_{E})-\lambda({\pi_{E}})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{4\tau_{E}}B+\sqrt{32\tau_{E}\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}B+\frac{16B}{3}\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 6B\sqrt{\tau_{E}\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}+\frac{16B}{3}\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\,.

Then, dividing by
\tau_{E}
we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}-\lambda({\pi_{E}})\right\|_{2}\leq 6B\sqrt{\frac{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}{\tau_{E}}}+\frac{16B}{3\tau_{E}}\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\,.

Then, for
\tau_{E}\geq\frac{64}{18^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}
, we have that

6\sqrt{\frac{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}{\tau_{E}}}\geq\frac{8}{3\tau_{E}}\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\,,

and hence that with probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\lambda({\pi_{E}})}-\lambda({\pi_{E}})\right\|_{2}\leq 12B\sqrt{\frac{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}{\tau_{E}}}\,.

Thus, by a union bound and choosing
\eta_{r}=\nicefrac{{W_{\max}}}{{B\sqrt{K}}}
, we have that with probability
1-2\delta
,

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\mathfrak{R}_{T}^{w}(w_{\mathrm{true}})

\displaystyle=4BW_{\max}\sqrt{K}+R_{\max}\sqrt{8K\log\delta^{-1}}+24W_{\max}BK\sqrt{\frac{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}{\tau_{E}}}

\displaystyle\leq 10(BW_{\max}+R_{\max})\sqrt{K\log\delta^{-1}}+24W_{\max}KB\sqrt{\frac{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}{\tau_{E}}}\,.

∎

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1-\gamma+2\gamma\delta\right)\left(\left(-\gamma\alpha\epsilon\right)-\left(-\gamma\epsilon\right)\right)}{2\left(1-\gamma-\gamma\epsilon+2\gamma\delta\right)\left(1-\gamma-\gamma\alpha\epsilon+2\gamma\delta\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(1-\gamma+2\gamma\delta\right)\gamma\epsilon\left(1-\alpha\right)}{2\left(1-\gamma-\gamma\epsilon+2\gamma\delta\right)\left(1-\gamma-\gamma\alpha\epsilon+2\gamma\delta\right)}\,.

Setting
\delta=\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}
and noting
\epsilon\geq 0
,
\gamma\geq\frac{1}{2}
, we can lower bound the difference as follows

\displaystyle\nu\left(\pi_{E}^{0},x_{0}\right)-\nu\left(\tilde{\pi},x_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{3\left(1-\gamma\right)\gamma\epsilon\left(1-\alpha\right)}{2\left(3\left(1-\gamma\right)-\gamma\epsilon\right)\left(3\left(1-\gamma\right)-\gamma\alpha\epsilon\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon\left(1-\alpha\right)}{12\left(1-\gamma\right)}\,.

(15)

Likewise, the difference between
\nu\left(\pi_{E}^{1}\right)
and
\nu\left(\tilde{\pi}\right)
in state
x_{1}
is given by

\displaystyle\nu\left(\pi_{E}^{1},x_{1}\right)-\nu\left(\tilde{\pi},x_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1-\gamma-2\gamma\alpha\epsilon+2\gamma\delta}{2\left(1-\gamma-\gamma\alpha\epsilon+2\gamma\delta\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\alpha\epsilon}{2\left(1-\gamma-\gamma\alpha\epsilon+2\gamma\delta\right)}\,.

Using the definition of
\delta
, and again
\epsilon\geq 0
,
\gamma\geq\frac{1}{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\nu\left(\pi_{E}^{1},x_{1}\right)-\nu\left(\tilde{\pi},x_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\alpha\epsilon}{2\left(3\left(1-\gamma\right)-\gamma\alpha\epsilon\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon\alpha}{12\left(1-\gamma\right)}\,.

(16)

Plugging Inequalities (
15
) and (
16
) into Equation (
14
) with
\alpha=\bar{\pi}^{0}\left(a^{\star}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x_{0}\right)
and
\alpha=\bar{\pi}^{1}\left(a^{\star}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x_{0}\right)
respectively, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\left\{0,1\right\}}\left\langle\nu_{0},V_{i}^{\pi_{E}^{i}}-V_{i}^{\bar{\pi}^{i}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon W_{\max}}{24\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\left(1-\bar{\pi}^{0}\left(a^{\star}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x_{0}\right)+\bar{\pi}^{1}\left(a^{\star}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x_{0}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon W_{\max}}{24\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\left(\sum_{a\neq a^{\star}}\bar{\pi}^{0}\left(a\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x_{0}\right)+\bar{\pi}^{1}\left(a^{\star}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x_{0}\right)\right)\,.

Next, we can lower bound the right hand side using the Bretagnolle-Huber inequality (see
Bretagnolle and Huber, 1979
, and
Lattimore and Szepesvári, 2020
, Theorem 14.2
), which gives

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\left\{0,1\right\}}\left\langle\nu_{0},V_{i}^{\pi_{E}^{i}}-V_{i}^{\bar{\pi}^{i}}\right\rangle\geq\frac{\epsilon W_{\max}}{24\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\exp\left(-\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}^{0}\left(\cdot\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x_{0}\right)\|\bar{\pi}^{1}\left(\cdot\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x_{0}\right)\right)\right)\,.

(17)

Then, using the data processing inequality and using the fact that the learning algorithm produces
\bar{\pi}^{i}
as a deterministic function of the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\pi_{E}^{i}}
for
i=0,1
, we have that

\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\bar{\pi}^{0}\left(\cdot\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x_{0}\right)\|\bar{\pi}^{1}\left(\cdot\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x_{0}\right)\right)\leq\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\tau_{E}}\|\mathbb{P}_{1}^{\tau_{E}}\right)\,,

where, for
i\in\left\{0,1\right\}
, we denoted
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\tau_{E}}
the probability distribution over datasets of size
\tau_{E}
induced by the interaction between the expert
\pi_{E}^{i}
and the environment (analog to what is done in the proof of Theorem
19
). Next, we denote
\kl\left(p,q\right)
and
\chi^{2}\left(p,q\right)
the KL and chi-squared divergences between bernoulli distributions of means
p
and
p^{\prime}
,
i.e
.
\xspace

\displaystyle\kl\left(p,q\right)

\displaystyle=p\log\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)+\left(1-p\right)\log\left(\frac{1-p}{1-q}\right)

\displaystyle\chi^{2}\left(p,q\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(p-q\right)^{2}}{q\left(1-q\right)}\,.

By definition of the KL, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\tau_{E}}\|\mathbb{P}_{1}^{\tau_{E}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tau_{E}\cdot\kl\left(\frac{3\left(1-\gamma\right)}{2\left(3\left(1-\gamma\right)-\gamma\epsilon\right)},\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tau_{E}\cdot\chi^{2}\left(\frac{3\left(1-\gamma\right)}{2\left(3\left(1-\gamma\right)-\gamma\epsilon\right)},\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\tau_{E}\cdot\chi^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma\epsilon}{3\left(1-\gamma\right)-\gamma\epsilon},\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\tau_{E}\gamma^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{\left(3\left(1-\gamma\right)-\gamma\epsilon\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tau_{E}\gamma^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\,,

where the first inequality follows from the concavity of the logarithm function, and the second inequality uses the fact that
\epsilon\leq\delta=\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}
. Thus, plugging in this last inequality into Equation (
17
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\left\{0,1\right\}}\left\langle\nu_{0},V_{i}^{\pi_{E}^{i}}-V_{i}^{\bar{\pi}^{i}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon W_{\max}}{24\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tau_{E}\gamma^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon W_{\max}}{24\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tau_{E}\epsilon^{2}}{\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}}\right)\,,

where we used
\gamma<1
in the second inequality. Introducing
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon\left(1-\gamma\right)^{-1}
, we can rewrite the previous inequality as

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\left\{0,1\right\}}\left\langle\nu_{0},V_{i}^{\pi_{E}^{i}}-V_{i}^{\bar{\pi}^{i}}\right\rangle\geq\frac{W_{\max}\epsilon^{\prime}}{24\left(1-\gamma\right)}\exp\left(-\tau_{E}\left(\epsilon^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right)\,.

It remains to make the lower bound small enough. To bound the average suboptimality gap by
\frac{W_{\max}\epsilon^{\prime}}{24e\left(1-\gamma\right)}
and have
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\left\{0,1\right\}}\left\langle\nu_{0},V_{i}^{\pi_{E}^{i}}-V_{i}^{\bar{\pi}^{i}}\right\rangle\leq\frac{W_{\max}\epsilon^{\prime}}{24e\left(1-\gamma\right)}
, we need at least
\tau_{E}\geq\frac{1}{\left(\epsilon^{\prime}\right)^{2}}
expert transitions. Therefore, to achieve

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in\left\{0,1\right\}}\left\langle\nu_{0},V_{i}^{\pi_{E}^{i}}-V_{i}^{\bar{\pi}^{i}}\right\rangle\leq\varepsilon\,,

for some
\varepsilon>0
, we need to choose
\epsilon^{\prime}=\nicefrac{{24e\left(1-\gamma\right)\varepsilon}}{{W_{\max}}}
, which means that every algorithm needs at least
\tau_{E}\geq\frac{W_{\max}^{2}}{24^{2}e^{2}\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}
to guarantee a suboptimality gap of order
\varepsilon
.
∎

Proposition 21
.

The occupancy measure
\mu(\pi)
of any policy
\pi
satisfies the following system of equations:

E^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi\right)=\gamma P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi\right)+\left(1-\gamma\right)\nu_{0}\,.

(18)

Proof.

Define the transition kernel induced by policy
\pi
as
P_{\pi}
, with
P_{\pi}(\cdot|x)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\left[P(\cdot|x,A)\right]
. The proof follows from the following standard calculation:

\displaystyle E^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\left(\gamma P_{\pi}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\right)^{\tau}\nu_{0}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}\left(\gamma P_{\pi}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\right)^{\tau}\nu_{0}+(1-\gamma)\nu_{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma P_{\pi}(1-\gamma)\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\left(\gamma P_{\pi}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\right)^{\tau}\nu_{0}+(1-\gamma)\nu_{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma P_{\pi}E^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi\right)+(1-\gamma)\nu_{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma P\mu\left(\pi\right)+(1-\gamma)\nu_{0},

where the last step follows from the easily-checked fact that
P\mu\left(\pi\right)=P_{\pi}E^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi\right)
.
∎

Lemma 22
.

Let
\pi
be any policy,
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}}
be any function defined on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
, and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
be such that for any
x
,
V\left(x\right)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim\pi\left(\cdot\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x\right)}\left[Q\left(x,A\right)\right]
. Then

\left\langle\mu\left(\pi\right),EV\right\rangle=\left\langle\mu\left(\pi\right),Q\right\rangle\,.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\left\langle\mu\left(\pi\right),EV\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\nu\left(\pi,x\right)V\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nu\left(\pi,x\right)\pi\left(a\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}x\right)Q\left(x,a\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left(\pi,x,a\right)Q\left(x,a\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu\left(\pi\right),Q\right\rangle\,,

where the second equality follows from the definition of the function
V
and the first equality from the definition of the state-action occupancy measure.
∎

Lemma 23
.

Let
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
be two policies. Then,

\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\mu\left(\pi\right)\middle\|\mu\left(\pi^{\prime}\right)\right)\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\left\langle\nu\left(\pi\right),\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi\|\pi^{\prime}\right)\right\rangle\,.

Proof.

Using the chain rule of the relative entropy, we write

\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\mu\left(\pi\right)\middle\|\mu\left(\pi^{\prime}\right)\right)=\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\nu\left(\pi\right)\middle\|\nu\left(\pi^{\prime}\right)\right)+\left\langle\nu\left(\pi\right),\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi\middle\|\pi^{\prime}\right)\right\rangle\,.

By the flow constraints and the joint convexity of the relative entropy, we bound the first term as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\nu\left(\pi\right)\middle\|\nu\left(\pi^{\prime}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\gamma P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi\right)+\left(1-\gamma\right)\nu_{0}\middle\|\gamma P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi^{\prime}\right)+\left(1-\gamma\right)\nu_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\gamma\right)\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\nu_{0}\middle\|\nu_{0}\right)+\gamma\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi\right)\middle\|P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi^{\prime}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi\right)\middle\|P^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mu\left(\pi^{\prime}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\mu\left(\pi\right)\middle\|\mu\left(\pi^{\prime}\right)\right)\,,

where we also used the data-processing inequality in the last step. The proof is concluded by reordering the terms.
∎

Lemma 24
.

For any MDP
\mathcal{M}
, any ascension function
p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}
, and any policy
\pi
, we have for any state-action pair
\left(x,a\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
,

\mu^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi,x,a\right)\leq\mu\left(\pi,x,a\right)\,,

where
\mu^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi\right)
denotes the state-action occupancy of
\pi
in
\mathcal{M}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}
, the optimistically augmented MDP induced by
p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}
.

Proof.

Let us consider a process
\left(X_{\tau},A_{\tau}\right)_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by the policy
\pi
in the MDP
\mathcal{M}
, that is, such that
X_{0}\sim\nu_{0}
, and for any
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
,
A_{\tau}\sim\pi\left(\cdot\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}X_{\tau}\right)
, and
X_{\tau+1}\sim P\left(\cdot\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}X_{\tau},A_{\tau}\right)
. Additionally, we define a process
\left(X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}},A_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)_{\tau\in\mathbb{N}}
coupled to the process defined above as follows. At the first stage, we set
X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=X_{0}
. Then for any
\tau\geq 1

X_{\tau+1}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}},A_{\tau+1}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=\begin{cases}X_{\tau+1},A_{\tau+1}\quad&\text{w.p.}\quad 1-p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(X_{\tau},A_{\tau}\right)\quad\text{if}\quad X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}},A_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=X_{\tau},A_{\tau}\\
x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}},a\quad&\text{w.p.}\quad p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(X_{\tau},A_{\tau}\right)\quad\text{if}\quad X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}},A_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=X_{\tau},A_{\tau}\\
x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}},a\quad&\text{if}\quad X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}},A_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\neq X_{\tau},A_{\tau}\end{cases}\,.

It is straightforward to check that this process follows the dynamics of the optimistically augmented MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}(r,p^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}})
(since its transitions obey the kernel

). By definition, for any state-action pair
\left(x,a\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\mu^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\left(\pi,x,a\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\gamma\right)\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{\tau}\mathbb{P}\left[X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=x,A_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=a\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\gamma\right)\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{\tau}\left(\mathbb{P}\left[X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=x,A_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=a,X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\neq x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=x,A_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=a,X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\gamma\right)\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{\tau}\left(\mathbb{P}\left[X_{\tau}=x,A_{\tau}=a,X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\neq x^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right]+0\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\gamma\right)\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{\tau}\mathbb{P}\left[X_{\tau}=x,A_{\tau}=a\right]

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\pi,x,a\right)\,.

In the third equality, the second term within the sum is equal to zero because

, and in the other term we replaced
\left(X_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}},A_{\tau}^{\mathsf{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\right)
by
\left(X_{\tau},A_{\tau}\right)
because the two coincide long as

. This concludes the proof.

Lemma 25

Under the event
\mathcal{E}_{L}

\displaystyle E\left(K\right)

Lemma 26
.

For all
z\geq 0
,
\omega\geq 0
it holds that
\sigma\left(z-\omega\right)\leq 2\left(z^{2}+\exp\left(-\omega\right)\right)
.

Proof.

Let us consider the function
g:z\mapsto\sigma\left(z-\omega\right)-\left(z+\frac{1}{e^{\omega/2}}\right)^{2}
. Note that for any
z
, we have
\sigma^{\prime}\left(z\right)=\sigma\left(z\right)\sigma\left(-z\right)
. Thus, the first two derivatives of
g
are given by

\displaystyle g^{\prime}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(z-\omega\right)\sigma\left(\omega-z\right)-2\left(z+\frac{1}{e^{\omega/2}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(z-\omega\right)\sigma\left(\omega-z\right)^{2}-\sigma\left(z-\omega\right)^{2}\sigma\left(\omega-z\right)-2\,.

Since
\sigma\left(z\right)\in\left(0,1\right)
for any
z
, the second derivative of
g
is nonpositive,
g^{\prime\prime}\left(z\right)\leq 0
, and
g
is concave. By the first order condition, for any
z\geq 0
,

g\left(z\right)\leq g\left(0\right)+g^{\prime}\left(0\right)z\,.

Furthermore, note that

g\left(0\right)=\sigma\left(-\omega\right)-\frac{1}{e^{\omega}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{\omega}}-\frac{1}{e^{\omega}}\leq 0\,,

and

\displaystyle g^{\prime}\left(0\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+e^{\omega}}\frac{1}{1+e^{-\omega}}-\frac{2}{e^{\omega/2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{\omega}}-\frac{2}{e^{\omega/2}}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{e^{\omega/2}}

\displaystyle\leq 0\,,

where we first used that
e^{-\omega}\geq 0
for any
\omega\geq 0
and then that
x+1\geq\sqrt{x}
for any
x\geq 0
. Thus, it holds that
g\left(z\right)\leq 0
for all
z\geq 0
,
i.e
.
\xspace
\sigma\left(z-\omega\right)\leq\left(z+\frac{1}{e^{\omega/2}}\right)^{2}
. Using
\left(a+b\right)^{2}\leq 2\left(a^{2}+b^{2}\right)
, it holds that

\sigma\left(z-\omega\right)\leq 2\left(z^{2}+e^{-\omega}\right)\,.

∎

Lemma 27
.

For all
\omega\geq 2
, it holds that

\max_{z\geq 0}z\cdot\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\leq\frac{\omega}{\alpha}\,.

Proof.

Let
\alpha>0
,
\omega\geq 2
, and
g:z\geq 0\mapsto z\cdot\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)
. We recall that the derivative of the sigmoid function is given for any
z
by
\sigma^{\prime}\left(z\right)=\sigma\left(z\right)\sigma\left(-z\right)
, and that
\sigma\left(-z\right)=1-\sigma\left(z\right)
.
g
is twice differentiable. Its first derivative is given by

\displaystyle g^{\prime}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)-\alpha z\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\left[1-\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\left[1-\alpha z\left(1-\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\right)\right]\,.

We set the derivative to zero and solve the equation to find the critical points. We have

\displaystyle g^{\prime}\left(z\right)=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{iff}\quad\alpha z=\frac{1}{1-\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)}

(19)

\displaystyle\quad\text{iff}\quad\alpha z=1+e^{\omega-\alpha z}

\displaystyle\quad\text{iff}\quad\left(\alpha z-1\right)e^{\alpha z-1}=e^{\omega-1}\,.

For
x>0
, the equation
we^{w}=x
has exactly one positive solution

which increases with

and where
W
denotes the Lambert function. Thus,
g^{\prime}\left(z\right)=0
if and only if
\alpha z-1=W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)
,
i.e
.
\xspace
z^{\star}=\frac{W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)+1}{\alpha}
. We check that
z^{\star}
is a local maximum. The second derivative of

is given by

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=-2\alpha\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\left[1-\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\right]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\alpha^{2}z\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\left[1-\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\alpha^{2}z\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)^{2}\left[1-\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-2\alpha\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\left[1-\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\right]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\alpha^{2}z\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\left[1-\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\right]\left[1-2\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z\right)\right]\,.

We evaluate it at the critical point
z^{\star}
and simplify the expression using Equation

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}\left(z^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle=-2\alpha\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z^{\star}\right)\left[1-\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z^{\star}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\alpha\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z^{\star}\right)\left[1-2\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z^{\star}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\alpha\sigma\left(\omega-\alpha z^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle<0\,,

thus
z^{\star}>0
is a local maximum. Since
g\left(0\right)=0
,
\lim_{z\rightarrow+\infty}g\left(z\right)=0
,
g\left(z^{\star}\right)
and
z^{\star}
is the only positive critical point, this means
z^{\star}
is a global maximum. We evaluate
g
to get the maximum

\displaystyle g\left(z^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)+1}{\alpha}\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)\right)e^{1-\omega}}

\displaystyle=\frac{W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)+1}{\alpha}\frac{W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)}{W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)+W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)\exp\left(W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)\right)e^{1-\omega}}

\displaystyle=\frac{W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)}{\alpha}\,,

where we used
W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)\exp\left(W\left(e^{\omega-1}\right)\right)=e^{\omega-1}
in the third equality. We now upper bound the Lambert function. Taking the log of the equation that defines it, we have
W\left(x\right)=\log x-\log W\left(x\right)
for any
x>0
. Note that
W\left(e\right)=1
and that
W
is increasing, so for any
x>e
, we have
W\left(x\right)>1
and thus
W\left(x\right)<\log x
. Using it on
g\left(z^{\star}\right)
, we further have

g\left(z^{\star}\right)\leq\frac{\omega-1}{\alpha}\leq\frac{\omega}{\alpha}\,,

where we used
\omega\geq 2
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 28
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and define
\LSE:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the function defined for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\LSE\left(x\right)=\log\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{x_{i}}\,.

Then
\LSE
is 1-Lipschitz with respect to the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
, i.e.
\xspace
for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\left|\LSE\left(x\right)-\LSE\left(y\right)\right|\leq\left\|x-y\right\|_{\infty}\,.

Proof.

For any
i\in\left[n\right]
and any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the gradient of
\LSE
is given by

\nabla\LSE\left(x\right)=\frac{e^{x}}{\left\langle e^{x},\mathbf{1}\right\rangle}\,.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By the intermediate mean value theorem, there exists a
z
on the segment
\left[x,y\right]
such that

\displaystyle\left|\LSE\left(x\right)-\LSE\left(y\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle\nabla\LSE\left(z\right),x-y\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla\LSE\left(z\right)\right\|_{1}\left\|x-y\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|x-y\right\|_{\infty}\,,

where the inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality.
∎
