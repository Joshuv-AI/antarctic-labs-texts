Effects of Eimeria challenge on growth performance, intestine integrity, and cecal microbial diversity and composition of yellow broilers☆
Authors: Linjie Su, Shuping Huang, Yanling Huang, Xue Bai, Runhui Zhang, Yan Lei, Xi Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The invasion of Eimeria causes damage to the intestinal barrier, nutrient leakage, and microbial imbalance in poultry. We aimed to investigate the effects of Eimeria infection on growth performance, intestinal integrity, and cecal microbial diversity and composition of yellow broilers. A total of 180 male yellow broilers were randomly divided into an unchallenged control and an Eimeria challenge treatment group within 18 floor pens (10 chicks/pen, 9 replicate pens/group). On day 10, 90 chicks received a cocktail of E. maxima, E. acervulina, and E. tenella oocysts (105/chick) to induce coccidial infection, and the other 90 received an aliquot of PBS. The Eimeria challenge resulted in increased bird feed consumption and FCR from day 11 to 21 (all P < 0.01). Higher fecal Eimeria counts, duodenal, jejunal, and cecal lesions were observed in the challenge group on day 12, 15, 15, 18 respectively (all P < 0.05). Furthermore, the infected birds had larger livers and small intestines, deeper villus crypt, and decreased expression of Claudin-1 on day 21 (all P < 0.05). The 16S rRNA sequencing indicated that alpha diversity (Sobs, Shannon, Simpson, Ace, or Chao) of cecal microbials was not affected by Eimeria challenge (all P > 0.05). However, the PCoA and LEfSe analyses indicated that the Eimeria challenge altered microbial distribution by decreasing the abundance of Firmicutes and enriching the abundance of Proteobacteria at the phylum level. At the genus level, Clostridia vadin BB60 and Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group were reduced, while Escherichia-Shigella were enriched in the challenged yellow broilers (all P < 0.05). Correlation analyses demonstrated that the birds with higher Lachonospiraceae NK4A136 group and Clostridia vadin BB60, and lower Escherichia-Shigella in their cecal content gained more BW and reached a lower FCR from day 11 to 21 (all P < 0.05). In conclusion, Eimeria infection compromised feed efficiency of yellow broilers by damaging intestinal barrier and shifting cecal microbiota towards colonizers associated with poor performance. Restoring the dysbiotic microbiome could be a potential strategy for improving feed efficiency in yellow broilers under coccidial challenge.

Introduction

The Eimeria spp. are intracellular parasites that can penetrate the intestinal linings and cause coccidiosis in poultry. The pathogenicity of different Eimeria ranges from moderate to severe, leading to a decrease in nutrient absorption, intestinal swelling, epithelial loosening, destruction of villi, hemorrhage, or even death (Mesa-Pineda et al., 2021). Moderate coccidiosis is self-limiting, however it promotes the onset of necrotic enteritis by secondary infection with Clostridium perfringens, resulting in high mortality in broilers (Collier et al., 2008). The annual cost of coccidiosis in global poultry industry exceeds 12.96 billion dollars, mainly due to compromised growth performance of birds (Blake, 2020). After orally ingesting Eimeria-contaminated feces, the sporocysts in Eimeria oocyst are released from mechanical disruption and digestive enzymes in the chicken gastrointestinal tract, rapidly rupturing enterocytes (Lopez-Osorio et al., 2020). This invasion impairs nutrient digestion and absorption, leading to compromised feed efficiency and weight gain loss in broilers (Wang et al., 2019a; Castro et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2023a and b). Additionally, Eimeria infection damages the intestinal morphological structure by lowering villus height and increasing crypt depth (Teng et al., 2020; Alagbe et al., 2024; Noruzi et al., 2024).

Despite the destruction of the intestinal barrier, Eimeria infection can release cellular nutrients for commensal microbes and reshape the microbiota structure in white-feather broilers, increasing the proportion of potential pathogens, including Escherichia coli, Enterococcus, Clostridium, and Proteus (Campos et al., 2022). Furthermore, the abundance levels of short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria such as Lachnospiraceae (Campos et al., 2022) and Rumminococcus torques (Lin et al., 2023a), as well as beneficial bacteria like Bifidobacteriaceae (Zhou et al., 2020) and Lactobacillus (Chen et al., 2020), were reduced in white-feather broilers when challenged with Eimeria. The microbial diversity responded inconsistently to different Eimeria species (Wang et al., 2019b; Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2023 a and b). A decreased microbial diversity and richness in broiler cecal contents were reported in Eimeria cocktail challenge models (Wang et al., 2019b; Lin et al., 2023 a and b) and E. maxima challenge model (Bortoluzzi et al., 2019). However, higher cecal OTU was observed in cecal contents (Zhou et al., 2020) and cecal scrapings (Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2020) of E. tenella challenged broilers. When exploring the poultry microbial community, more attention should be paid to microbial composition, function, and its relationship to bird performance (Wang et al., 2019b).

Yellow broilers, also known as yellow-feathered chicken/broilers, refer to Chinese local poultry breeds with higher yellow pigmentation in feather, bone, skin, and enriched aroma profiles (Qi et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2023). They contribute primary chicken consumption and dominant live poultry market in Asia (Tang et al., 2021). In China, yellow broilers are mainly raised in cages or slatted floor systems with separation from feces. However, the Eimeria prevalence is still high. According to a survey of 137 yellow broiler farms, the Eimeria prevalence was 97.17 % by PCR confirmation (Geng et al., 2021). The susceptibility and recovery rates from Eimeria infection differ among various chicken species (Attree et al., 2021). Under Eimeria tenella challenge, yellow broilers exhibited more weight loss, sever lesions, and lower anticoccidial index than Arbor Acres broilers (Du et al., 2024). Although extensive studies have been conducted to reveal Eimeria’s impact on microbial changes in white-feather broilers, little is known about that in yellow broilers. Therefore, we studied the effects of an Eimeria cocktail challenge on growth performance, intestinal integrity, and cecal microbial diversity and composition of yellow broilers, as well as correlation relationships between host and bacterial commensals. Understanding the microbiota-host relationship is critical to develop potential strategies to correct microbial dysbiosis and mitigate growth depression in yellow broilers undergoing coccidiosis.

Materials and methods

Birds and challenge treatments

The study was conducted at an experimental farm in the College of Animal and Veterinary Sciences at Southwest Minzu University. All experimental protocols and animal handling procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee at Southwest Minzu University (SMU-202309007).

A total of 180 one-day male yellow feathered chickens were randomly assigned into 18-floor pens with 10 chicks per pen. Each floor pen was equipped with one nipple drinker and a feeder and top-dressed with wood shavings. A rubber divider was used to avoid potential cross-contamination. The chicks had free access to water and commercial crumble feed, meeting the nutrient requirements of yellow broilers recommended by the industry standard (Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, 2020).

On day 10, 90 chicks were subjected to the Eimeria challenge, while another 90 chicks received an aliquot of PBS. Each chick was inoculated with 0.5 ml of PBS containing 20 × dose of a commercial vaccine via crop gavage (Standard Biotechnology, Foshan, China). This commercial vaccine contained 2 × 106 unattenuated live oocysts/mL (E. acervulina, E. maxima, and E. tenella), chosen based on previous research (Zhang et al., 2023).

Growth performance

Group BW and feed consumption per pen on days 0, 10, and 21 were recorded. Mortality was recorded and applied for corrected feed intake. The BW gain, feed intake, and feed conversion ratio (FCR) from days 0 to 10, 11 to 21, and 0 to 21 were calculated.

Oocyst shedding, tissue sampling, and lesion scoring

Fecal oocyst counts were recorded daily through day 9 (the day before the challenge) until day 23. Oocyst shedding was measured by the saturated salt floatation method (Long et al., 1976). Fresh fecal material and cecal droppings were collected and mixed homogeneously every 24 hours. Two grams of feces were mixed with 60 mL of saturated salt solution (the volume of dilution might change for low oocyst count), and the fecal oocyst count was calculated as number per gram of feces using the McMaster chamber and a light microscope (Nikon E200, Beijing, China).

On day 21, two birds with median body weights were selected from each pen (18 chicks/treatment) and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Inner organs, including thymus, gizzard, spleen, bursa, liver, duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were weighed. The relative weight of each organ was calculated as a percentage of the bird's body weight. The lengths of three small intestine segments were also recorded. Gross lesion scores in the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and cecum were measured through the Johnson and Reid method (1970). One inch at middle site of each intestinal segment was excised for histological examination. The mucosal slurry of the duodenum and cecal contents from each bird were collected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for mRNA expression or microbiota tests, respectively.

Intestinal morphology

Small intestine tissues from nine birds per treatment were examined for morphology. Histological slides with alcian blue-periodic acid schiff (AB-PAS) staining were prepared by a commercial lab (Lilai Chengdu, Sichuan). Five intestinal villi and crypts per slide were observed under the optical microscope (Nikon E200, Beijing, China). The morphological measurements including villus height, width, crypt depth, muscle thickness, goblet cell size and density (count/area), and the ratio of villus height to crypt depth (VC) were taken and calculated according to Wang et al., 2015.

Microbial diversity and composition

Cecal contents from five birds per treatment were analyzed for microbial diversity and composition using 16S rRNA sequencing. Total genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Beijing, China). The V3-V4 regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using specifically primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA G-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). The PCR amplicons were sequenced at a commercial lab (Shanghai Majorbio Biomedical Technology Co, China) using the Illumina Miseq platform.

The optimized sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97 % similarity. A random rarefaction of sample reads according to the smallest value was performed. The OTU sequences were taxonomically classified in phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species using the Silva database. The number of reads in the OTU table was standardized using the total sum approach to generate relative abundance (proportion). Sobs, Shannon, Simpson, Ace, Chao, and coverage indexes were calculated for alpha diversity evaluation for cecal microbiota. The principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray Curtis dissimilarity metrics was conducted for beta diversity. The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was performed to show the magnitude of the effect of the signature species identified between groups. The LDA threshold was set to 3.5 and subsequently applied for the Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis.

mRNA expression in gut health

The duodenal slurry samples from the same birds tested for intestinal morphology were used to evaluate mRNA expression of genes related to intestinal health. Inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IFN-γ), heat stress proteins (HSP70, HSP90), and tight junction proteins (Occluding-1, ZO-1, and Claudin-1) in duodenum were assessed. Total RNA was extracted using TransZol RNA Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and the concentration and integrity were confirmed by a spectrophotometer (DeNovix, Wilmington, USA) and 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. The cDNA was reversely transcribed by TransScript All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen Biotech) and confirmed on 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis. The qPCR assay was performed using a TB Green Premix Ex Taq II kit (Takara Bio, Beijing, China) on a real-time PCR (Bio-Rad CFX96TM, California, USA). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as a housekeeping gene. The expression levels were calculated on a 2 × ΔΔCT and house gene basis. Statistical analysis

A completely randomized design was used to determine the effects of Eimeria infection on growth performance, inner organ weight, intestinal morphology, the size and density of different types of goblet cells, mRNA expression, and alpha microbial diversity of yellow feathered broiler. The Eimeria treatment was set as a fixed factor and the block was set as a random factor. The one-way ANOVA statistical model is followed: y = µ + Eimeria treatment + block + ε, where µ is the mean of the observation, Eimeria treatment is the fixed effect, block location is random effect, and ε is the random error term. The one-way ANOVA analysis was conducted using PROC GLM in the SAS 9.4 software (SAS, North Carolina, USA). Intestinal lesion scores and microbial compositions (percentages) were analyzed using the non-parametric Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test. The correlation relationships between dominant microbiota (differential genera from LEfSe analysis) and bird performance (Eimeria affected bird responses) were analyzed using Spearman correlation. When P ≤ 0.05, there was considered a significant difference.

Discussion

The growth retardation induced by coccidia has been widely documented in white-feather broilers including decreased feed intake, depressed weight gain, and increased FCR (Wang et al., 2019a; Castro et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2023a and b; Miska and Fetterer, 2018; Freitas et al., 2023). Our study revealed that the Eimeria-infected yellow broilers exhibited increased feed consumption, leading to compromised feed efficiency. Previous research has shown that E. tenella caused more server lesions in yellow broilers compared to white broilers or layers (Du et al., 2024). The increased feed consumption by yellow broilers might be a compensatory mechanism for the nutrient loss caused by Eimeria invasion. Despite the increased feed consumption, Eimeria invasion downregulates the gene expression levels of brush border enzymes and nutrient transporters, further impairing feed utilization (Miska and Fetterer, 2018).

A sharp increase of oocyst shedding was observed in feces from two days post-infection, with the oocyst concentration peaking and reaching a plateau (∼104 oocyst/g) on day five post-infection, consistent with previous findings (Huang et al., 2024). White broilers exhibited a rapid oocyst shedding at one day post-challenge (Souza et al., 2024), followed by a peak at 7 days post-challenge (Jordan et al., 2011), and higher oocyst output (Du et al., 2024). Different chicken species may have different susceptibilities to coccidial infection (Boulton et al., 2018; Du et al., 2024).

In yellow broilers, duodenal and jejunal lesions were observed before cecal lesions. Eimeria spp. is highly host- and site-specific (Burrell et al., 2020). In this study, a cocktail of E. acervulina (upper small intestine), E. maxima (mid-small intestine), and E. tenella (caeca) was administrated to yellow broilers to initiate infection. Different Eimeria spp. might result in different host-parasite interactions, leading to varying rate of destruction of intestinal cells in yellow broilers, particularly in upper and mid-intestinal lesions.

Furthermore, enlarged thymus and liver, and elongated small intestines were observed in Eimeria-infected yellow broilers. Similar findings have been reported in white-feather broilers (Sadeghi et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019a). Sporulated Eimeria could directly interact with enterocytes, potentially causing inflammation and promoting growth in the gastrointestinal tract. However, this increased inflammation and organ hypertrophy following an Eimeria challenge is not limited to the gastrointestinal tract and affects the spleen (Wang et al., 2019a; Gelinas et al., 2024) and bursa (Wang et al., 2018). While transfer of Eimeria to the liver via the epithelium of the bile duct has been mainly reported in rabbits (Katsui et al., 2022), there is no evidence to support the migration of Eimeria cells from the impaired intestine to other internal organs. The enlargement of other organs following Eimeria challenge in yellow broiler requires further investigation.

The Eimeria infection caused intestinal crypt of yellow broilers to deepen, reducing the ratio of villus height to crypt depth, which is an indicator of slow turnover rate of enterocytes. This is consistent with previous studies on Hy-line Brown layers (Zhou et al., 2020) and Ross 308 broilers (Rysman et al., 2023), where a deeper crypt and shorter villus were observed following Emeria challenge. The stem cells in the crypt would migrate and differentiate into functional enterocytes and goblet cells lining the villus. There were two potential explanations for this deepened crypt. One possibility is that the villus ruptures after Emeria invasion, causing the crypt to compensate for the villus loss (Zhou et al., 2020). Another possible explanation is that before E. acervulina, E. maxima, and E. tenella initiate endogenous development in villus epithelial, these species travel to the intestinal crypts through interaction with lymphocytes (Jeurissen et al., 1996; Shirley et al., 2005; Burrell et al., 2020). The specific mechanisms involved in this type of migration in yellow broilers are not fully understood, and traffic via the portal vein and the lymphatic system might be an initial hypothesis. Additionally, the mRNA expression level of Claudin-1 was downregulated, which supported the damage of Eimeria to intestinal integrity on a molecular basis.

Although the Eimeria challenged did not affect cecal microbial diversity, it influenced microbial composition by decreasing Firmicutes abundance and enriching Proteobacteria abundance. A similar microbiota shift from Firmicutes to Proteobacteria was reported in Arbor Acres broilers and Lohmann pink layers exposed to E. tenella (Du et al., 2024). Firmicutes are the predominant commensals in chicken cecum and play an important role in nutrient metabolism and energy production (Oakley et al., 2014). Some Firmicutes members, such as Lactobacillus and Lactococcus spp., can produce bacteriocins and regulate inflammation by inducing anti-inflammatory cytokines in the host (Yang et al., 2022). Proteobacteria are dominant in young chicks and thereafter decrease significantly in later growing periods (Aruwa et al., 2021). In addition, Proteobacteria include a wide variety of potential pathogens, such as Campylobacter and Escherichia coli. This notable microbial distribution change suggests a significant dysbiosis occurred following Eimeria challenge. Choi and Kim (2022) proposed that increased protein concentration of cecal contents by shedding host cell debris would be one of the factors to increase the abundance of the Proteobacteria in broilers infected with Eimeria tenella. Eimeria invasion interrupts intestinal integrity, increases permeability, and induces inflammation (Pham et al., 2021), thereby interacting with intestinal commensals (Liu et al., 2024). Coccidia-induced nutrient leakage and mucin accumulation would promote the growth of potential pathogens, such like Clostridium perfringens (Collier et al., 2008; Lu et al., 2021). Furthermore, Eimeria infections cause initiate oxidative stress (Sharma et al., 2024). Those reactive oxygen species can form NO3− as a terminal electron acceptor for Proteobacteria growth via denitrification (Singhal et al., 2020). This microbial disturbance would further exaggerate the negative impacts of Eimeria invasion on yellow broilers exhibiting impaired growth performance.

Eimeria infection typically reduced the colonization of the Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 and Clostridia vadin BB60 in yellow broilers. These two microbes were associated with weight gain and feed efficiency in this study. Lachnospiraceae are known for their beneficial roles in birds by nourishing enterocytes and providing host nutrients (Shi et al., 2020). A butyrate-producing Lachnospiraceae genus was positively correlated with improved feed efficiency of white-broilers (Torok et al., 2011). Additionally, Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group abundance was shown to be associated with reduced amino acid metabolism (Ye et al., 2023), and another undefined Lachnospiraceae genus was positively correlated with muscle amino acid content (Liu et al., 2020). Although the role of Clostridia vadin BB60 in metabolism and microbiome is uncharacterized, it is prevalent in poultry cecal microbiota (Richards et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2022; Liu et al. 2023). In a dexamethasone-induced inflammation model, Clostridia vadin BB60 was found to be negatively correlated with inflammatory parameters, including serum IL-18, CXCL1, and CXCL2 levels, and jejunal IL-12, IL-18, and CXCL2 levels (Liu et al., 2023). In our study, the Eimeria-induced intestinal inflammation might also reduce the colonization of these potentially beneficial colonizers.

Our study found that the cecal microbiota of yellow broilers was strongly associated with host performance including feed efficiency, feed intake, weight gain, cecal lesion, and small intestinal morphology with a correlation coefficient varying from -0.91 to 0.92. Microbiota associated with feed efficiency, such as Pediococcus, vadin BB60, Lachnospiraceae NK4A136, Staphylococcus, and Ruminococcus were also related to the improved intestinal structure, such as shorter crypt. Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcus are significant butyrate-producing bacteria, which could favor the growth of enterocytes (Amaz et al., 2024). In the meantime, Escherichia-Shigella of yellow broilers, as the microbial associated with feed consumption in this study, were also related to a lower CV ratio, indicating a low epithelial cell renewal rate. Escherichia-Shigella as opportunistic pathogen could disrupt the intestinal structure and induce pro-inflammatory activities propagating the virulence factors (Ma et al., 2018). While intestinal commensals are neutral to hosts under normal circumstance, detrimental strains and opportunistic pathogens are also present (Berg et al., 2020). The major challenge of the present research in poultry microbiology is to determine the direction of interactions of those commensals and strains with their hosts (Markova et al., 2024). Recently, a series of cecal microbial transplant studies have been conducted to verify the microbial function of the host birds (Fu et al., 2022 and 2023; Khalid et al., 2024). The cecal microbiota from different donor birds led to different growth responses and health statuses in recipient chickens (Fu et al., 2022). The slow growing recipients also exhibited aggressive behaviors and higher inflammation levels in gastrointestinal tract and serum (Fu et al., 2023). Khalid et al. (2024) transported cecal microbiota from healthy yellow broiler donors to neonatal chicks and observed improved weight gain and reduced inflammation levels. A balanced cecal microbiota might benefit the host bird by suppressing inflammation and improving growth to a certain degree. However, an invasively intestinal breakdown would induce inflammation-driven bacterial dysbiosis and barrier dysfunction in a bidirectional manner.

In conclusion, yellow broilers may exhibit increased feed intake and reduced feed conversion ability when faced with an Eimeria challenge. This compromised nutrient utilization might be linked to damage to intestinal integrity damage and cecal microbiota dysbiosis. Typically, Eimeria infection can shift the cecal microbiota of yellow broilers towards colonizers associated with poor growth performance. Maintaining a balanced cecal microbiota is a critical for improving the performance of yellow broilers under Eimeria infection. Using probiotic or prebiotic treatments in poultry aiming correcting microbiota imbalances could be a potential direction to mitigate the negative impact of coccidiosis.
