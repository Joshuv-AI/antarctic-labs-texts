Coupling theory of electromagnetic fields with mass transport in non-uniform fluids
Fei Wang, Britta Nestler

Abstract
Navier-Stokes and Maxwell equations have been invented for fluid dynamics and electromagnetic systems, respectively, for centuries. The development of Navier-Stokes and Maxwell equations for homogeneous materials seems to be mature. However, when there is a phase interface, a coupling theory for the mass transport with the propagation of electromagnetic fields remains an open question. In the current work, we present a fundamental theory for the thermodynamics and the kinetics for mass transport and electromagnetic wave propagation in non-uniform system when an interface is present. We will demonstrate that Maxwell-Ampere equation, Lorenz force, and Gauss’ law for magnetic field all have to be modified at the phase interface. We expect that the modified Lorenz force and Maxwell equations will shed light on high-temperature superconductivity, where the coupling of mass effect, such as thermal noise, with electromagnetic fields is necessary.



I
Introduction

In classic physics,
we have mass and massless systems.
Examples are gas, liquid, and solid in the former case
and electromagnetic wave in the latter case.
For the mass system consisting of fluid phases (liquid and gas),
Fick’s law of diffusion,
Euler equation, and Navier-Stokes equations
are the foundation
for the mass transport in fluid dynamics
Landau and Lifshitz 2013
:

\displaystyle\text{Fick's diffusion:}~\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot D\nabla c,

(1)

\displaystyle\text{Euler equation:}~\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla p,

(2)

\displaystyle\text{Navier-Stokes:}~\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\eta(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T})+\nabla\cdot\lambda(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})\mathbf{I},

(3)

where
c
,
t
,
D
,
\rho
,
\mathbf{u}
,
p
,
\eta
, and
\lambda

denotes the composition, time, diffusion coefficient, density, fluid velocity,
pressure, bulk viscosity, and shear viscosity, respectively.
The matrix
\mathbf{I}
is an identity tensor.
However, these classic equations
are mostly applied for homogeneous systems, where there is no interface.
For systems with an interface, like, water-air interface,
these classic transport equations in fluids have been further developed into the diffuse interface model,
sometimes also known
as phase-field model
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
;
Cahn 1977
;
Anderson et al. 1998
;
Chen and Doolen 1998
.
In the diffuse interface concept, the fluid-fluid interface has a finite thickness, for example,
the water-air interface has a thickness of several nanometres
Fellows et al. 2024
.
The composition and fluid velocity vary across the interface
and in the bulk with time as

\displaystyle\text{Cahn-Hilliard diffusion:}~\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot M\nabla\mu,\ \mu=\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}-\kappa\nabla^{2}c,

(4)

\displaystyle\text{Navier-Stokes:}~\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\eta(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T})+\nabla\cdot\lambda(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})\mathbf{I}-c\nabla\mu,

(5)

where
M
is the mobility,
\mu
denotes the chemical potential,
f

depicts the bulk free energy density, and
\kappa

represents the gradient energy coefficient
as introduced by van der Waals
Rowlinson 1979

and Cahn & Hilliard
Cahn and Hilliard 1958
.
In comparison with the conventional transport equation
for homogeneous systems,
an additional force term
-c\nabla\mu

has been added to the Navier-Stokes equation
to account for the surface tension force
at the fluid-fluid interface
Jacqmin 1999
.
Moreover, the chemical potential
defined by Gibbs for homogeneous systems,
\mu=\partial f/\partial c

has been modified by the second term in Eq. (
4
)
at the fluid-fluid interface.
The density throughout the diffuse interface is assumed to be a constant
according to the Boussinesq approximation.

For the motion of charged fluids,
the electromagnetic field should be considered, namely
the Maxwell equations, which build the
fundamentals for the evolution behaviours of electromagnetic fields.
The Maxwell equations in
vacuum and media read
Wang 2022

\displaystyle\text{vacuum}:\begin{cases}&\text{Gauss law:}~\nabla\cdot\mathbb{E}=0,\\
&\text{Gauss law for magnetism:}~\nabla\cdot\mathbb{B}=0,\\
&\text{Maxwell-Faraday:}~\nabla\times\mathbb{E}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{B}}{\mathrm{d}t},\\
&\text{Maxwell-Ampere:}~\nabla\times\mathbb{B}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{E}}{\mathrm{d}t},\end{cases}

(6)

and

\displaystyle\text{media}:\begin{cases}&\text{Gauss law:}~\nabla\cdot\mathbb{E}=\rho_{e},\\
&\text{Gauss law for magnetism:}~\nabla\cdot\mathbb{B}=0,\\
&\text{Maxwell-Faraday:}~\nabla\times\mathbb{E}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{B}}{\mathrm{d}t},\\
&\text{Maxwell-Ampere:}~\nabla\times\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{J}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{D}}{\mathrm{d}t},\end{cases}

(7)

respectively.
Here,
\mathbb{E}
,
\mathbb{B}
, and
\mathbb{J}

depict the electrical field, magnetic field,
and electrical current, respectively.
In vacuum, the charge density
\rho_{e}
is zero
and therefore
\mathbb{J}=\mathbb{0}
.
By considering polarization and magnetization in media,
the electrical displacement field
\mathbb{D}
and the magnetizing field
\mathbb{H}
are
related to
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{B}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}=\varepsilon\mathbb{E},

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}=\frac{1}{\chi}\mathbb{B}.

(9)

Here,
\varepsilon
and
\chi

represent the electrical permittivity
and magnetic permeability, respectively.
The classic Maxwell equations in media are mostly applied
to uniform system with constant
\varepsilon
and
\chi

Wang 2022
.
Note that
in inhomogeneous system, these two parameters
\varepsilon
and
\chi
have a dependence
on the composition, namely

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\varepsilon(c),

(10)

\displaystyle\chi=\chi(c).

(11)

When there is a mass transport for the fluids with composition
c
,
the electromagnetic field should be affected
due to the composition dependent electrical permittivity
and magnetic permeability.
As yet, there is no such a solid coupling theory
for the time evolution of mass transport
with the propagation of the electromagnetic field.
To develop the coupling theory,
two basic open questions, Q1 and Q2 are as follows:

[Q1

]: What force should be added in the momentum and mass transport equations
to account for the side effect of electromagnetic wave propagation?

[Q2

]: What force should be added in the Maxwell equations
to account for the impact of mass transport?

These two basic questions will be addressed in the current work fundamentally.

In literature,
different coupling theories between fluid dynamics
and Maxwell equations
have been developed.
For example, a Cahn-Hilliard-Navier-Stokes-Gauss model has been
derived in Ref.
Zhang et al. 2024
,
where the Maxwell stress has been directly added into the Navier-Stokes equation
to account for the mass momentum evolution.
Similar approaches have been recently adopted in Refs.
Lin et al. 2012
;
Liu et al. 2019
.
In contrast to previous works, in the current work,
we will show that the Maxwell stress tensor
should be responsible for the momentum evolution of the electromagnetic field
rather than for the momentum evolution of the mass system.
We will demonstrate that
the effect of electromagnetic wave propagation
on the mass transport comes into play due to the
modified chemical potential
and the hydrodynamic pressure energy.

Another controversial point is whether the mass transport velocity
should be identical to the velocity of electromagnetic wave.
Let us assume that the charged fluid has a convection velocity
\mathbf{u}

and that the electromagnetic field has a propagation velocity
\mathbf{v}
.
The change of the composition
\mathrm{d}c
of the fluid
at a fixed point in space consists
of two parts.
The first part is the local variation,
(\partial c/\partial t)\mathrm{d}t=\partial c
.
The second part is the convection,
\mathrm{d}x(\partial c/\partial x)+\mathrm{d}y(\partial c/\partial y)+\mathrm{d}z(\partial c/\partial z)=\mathrm{d}\mathbb{r}\cdot\nabla c
(
x
,
y
,
z
: Cartesian coordinates,
\mathbb{r}
: position vector),
so that

\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{r}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\nabla c=\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla c,

(12)

known as an advection term.
Similarly, the electrostatic potential
\varphi
has the following total time derivative

\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbb{r}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\nabla\varphi=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla c.

(13)

In the following derivation, we insist on that the propagation velocity of the electrostatic field
\mathbf{v}

should differ from the mass convection velocity
\mathbf{u}
.
The velocities
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{u}

are coupled by the unsolved forces proposed in the questions Q1 and Q2.

In this article,
we will develop a coupling theory
of the transport equation for charged fluids
with the Maxwell equations for the electromagnetic wave propagation.
Our consideration is based on the fundamental concept
that the total energy of a closed system must be non-increasing, without making additional artificial assumptions.
We will show that
conventional mass transport equations
and Maxwell equations should be modified
when considering the coupling movement of
mass and electrostatic field.
Specially, due to the dependence
of the electrical permittivity
and magnetic permeability on the composition,
we obtain

•

a modified chemical potential for the mass transport,

•

a modified Lorenz force,

•

a modified Maxwell-Ampere equations,

•

a modified Gauss’ law for magnetic field.

II
Fick’s diffusion equation: connection between the first law of thermodynamics
and the second law of thermodynamics

At a constant temperature
T
for a binary system with a composition
c
,
the free energy functional of the system
\mathcal{F}
is integrated over the free energy density
f(c)
as

\mathcal{F}(c)=\int_{\Omega}f(c)\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(14)

where
\Omega
is the domain occupied by the system.
For a closed system, the total free energy of the system should be non-increasing,
namely

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}\leq 0.

(15)

Substituting Eq. (
14
)
into Eq. (
15
), we obtain the following equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}c}\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0.

(16)

From the above equation, we see
a natural definition for the chemical potential as
\mu=\mathrm{d}f/\mathrm{d}c
.
For a homogeneous system,
the free energy density
f
has a single minimum (Fig.
1
a, b)
and may be expressed by a parabolic formulation as

f(c)=ac^{2}+bc+d,

(17)

where
a
,
b
, and
d
are temperature dependent coefficients.
To minimize the energy according to Eq. (
16
), we propose
the following evolution equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot M\nabla\mu,

(18)

where
M
is a positive constant, termed as mobility.
Substituting Eq. (
18
)
into Eq. (
16
), the total energy evolution with time is rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}c}\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(19)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}c}\nabla\cdot M\nabla\mu\mathrm{d}\Omega

(20)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\mu M\nabla\mu\cdot\mathbf{n}\mathrm{d}{\partial\Omega}-\int_{\Omega}M(\nabla\mu)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(21)

where the integration by parts has been applied from the second to the final step.
The vector
\mathbf{n}
denotes the pointing-out normal vector of the domain boundary

\partial\Omega
.
Using the no-flux boundary condition for a closed system,
we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\int_{\Omega}M(\nabla\mu)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0.

(22)

By using the calculus of chain rule,
\nabla\mu=(\partial\mu/\partial c)\nabla c
,
Eq. (
18
) is written as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot M(\partial\mu/\partial c)\nabla c

(23)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot M(\partial^{2}f/\partial c^{2})\nabla c

(24)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot D\nabla c.

(25)

Defining the diffusion coefficient as
D=M(\partial^{2}f/\partial c^{2})=2Ma
,
we obtain Fick’s diffusion equation.

For a multicomponent system with
K
components, we define a composition vector as

\mathbb{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{K})
.
The total time derivative of free energy functional
\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{c})
reads

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\partial f}{\partial c_{i}}\frac{\mathrm{d}c_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(26)

Eq. (
18
) is reformulated for the time evolution of the
i
-th composition as

\frac{\mathrm{d}c_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot M_{i}\nabla\mu_{i}.

(27)

With the Gibbs-Duhem constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{K}c_{i}\nabla\mu_{i}=0
,
which can be rearranged as
\nabla\mu_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}(\delta_{ij}-c_{j})\nabla\mu_{j}
(
\delta
-Kronecker delta)
Wang et al. 2019
,
the multicomponent diffusion equation is finally expressed as

\frac{\mathrm{d}c_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{K}L_{ij}\nabla\mu_{j},

(28)

where the mobility matrix
\mathbb{L}=(L_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq K,\ 1\leq j\leq K}
is defined as
L_{ij}=(\delta_{ij}-c_{j})M_{i}
.
Substituting the multicomponent diffusion equation, Eq. (
27
)
into Eq. (
26
), we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\sum_{i=1}^{K}M_{i}\nabla\mu_{i}\cdot\mathbf{n}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega-\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\nabla\bigg(\frac{\partial f}{\partial c_{i}}\bigg)M_{i}\nabla\bigg(\frac{\partial f}{\partial c_{i}}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(29)

The first term in Eq. (
29
)
is eliminated due to the non-flux boundary condition.
To fulfill the requirement of energy decreasing,
the matrix
\mathbb{M}=(M_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq K,\ 1\leq j\leq K}
has to be symmetric and positive semi-definite:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{a}^{T}\mathbb{M}\mathbb{a}\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0,

(30)

with
\mathbb{a}=\nabla\bm{\mu}=(\nabla\mu_{1},\nabla\mu_{2},\cdots,\nabla\mu_{K})
.
The symmetric and positive semi-definite property of the mobility is
consistent with the Onsager’s theorem
Balluffi et al. 2005
.

In summary, by taking the homogeneous system as a simple example,
we have demonstrated that the energy dissipation and mass transport are not independent.
The mass transport
equation intrinsically indicates an energy dissipation.
Suffice to say, the first law of thermodynamics is not independent from the second law of thermodynamics; both are correlated.
In the following sections, this kind of relation between dissipation and conservation
will be extended for non-uniform system with phase interfaces.

Illustration of a uniform (a) and non-uniform (c) binary system.
In the former case, the two species (orange and cyan balls)
are homogeneously mixed with a single minimum for the free energy density (b).
In the latter case, there is an interface as highlighted by the dashed rectangle.
Within the interface, the two species coexist and the free energy density (d) depends not only on the composition
c
but also on the composition gradient
\nabla c
.

III
Dissipation principle

Based on the derivation in Section
II
,
we state the following dissipation principle. For a closed system, the following energy dissipation law

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0

(31)

implies a time evolution equation for the conserved variable
c

\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot M\nabla\frac{\partial f}{\partial c},

(32)

and a time evolution equation for non-conserved variable
\hat{c}

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{c}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\tau\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{c}},

(33)

where
M
and
\tau
are positive materials dependent mobilities.

Proof.

Substituting Eq. (
32
)
into Eq. (
31
), applying integration by parts,
and using the no-flux boundary condition, we obtain the energy evolution with time as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}\bigg(\nabla\cdot M\nabla\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\Omega

(34)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}M\nabla\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}\cdot\mathbf{n}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega-\int_{\Omega}M\bigg(\nabla\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}\bigg)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(35)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}M\bigg(\nabla\frac{\partial f}{\partial c}\bigg)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0.

(36)

Substituting Eq. (
33
)
into Eq. (
31
), we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\int_{\Omega}\tau\bigg(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{c}}\bigg)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0.

(37)

∎

The above dissipation principle will be further applied for vector fields
\mathbf{u}
and
\bm{\theta}
.
For a closed system, the following energy dissipation law

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\theta}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(38)

implies a time evolution equation for the conserved variable
\mathbf{u}

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot\tau_{1}(\nabla\bm{\theta}+\nabla\bm{\theta}^{T})+\nabla\cdot\tau_{2}(\nabla\cdot\bm{\theta})\mathbf{I},

(39)

and a time evolution equation for non-conserved variable
\hat{\mathbf{u}}

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{u}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\tau_{0}\bm{\theta},

(40)

where
\tau_{0}
,
\tau_{1}
, and
\tau_{2}
are positive materials dependent mobilities.

Proof.

Substituting Eq. (
39
)
into Eq. (
38
), applying integration by parts,
and using the no-flux boundary condition, we obtain the energy dissipation as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\int_{\Omega}\tau_{1}\nabla\bm{\theta}:\nabla\bm{\theta}+\tau_{2}(\nabla\cdot\bm{\theta})^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0.

(41)

Substituting Eq. (
40
)
into Eq. (
38
), we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\int_{\Omega}\tau_{0}\bm{\theta}^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0.

(42)

∎

IV
Extended diffusion equation for non-uniform system

For system with an interface, the concentration within the interface is non-uniform
and may be expressed as

\tilde{c}=c\pm\bm{\ell}\cdot\nabla c,

(43)

where
\bm{\ell}
is a characteristic length vector
reflecting the
repulsive and attractive forces
of neighbouring atoms and molecules.
Substituting the non-local concentration,
\tilde{c}
,
into the Boltzmann mixing entropy,
\tilde{c}\ln\tilde{c}
, we see that the free energy density depends
not only on
c
but also on
\nabla c
(see details in Ref.
Cai et al. 2024
), namely (Fig.
1
c, d)

\mathcal{F}(c,\nabla c)=\int_{\Omega}g(c,\nabla c)\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(44)

Next, we evaluate the total time derivative of the free energy functional as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\nabla c}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(45)

By using the definition of the materials derivative and the interchange rule of derivative,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\nabla c}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial\nabla c}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\nabla c=\nabla\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\nabla c,

(46)

Eq. (
45
) is rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\cdot\nabla\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\cdot\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\nabla c\mathrm{d}\Omega

(47)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}-\bigg(\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\bigg)\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\cdot\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\nabla c\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\cdot\mathbf{n}\bigg)\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega

(48)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}-\bigg(\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla c\bigg)+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\cdot\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\nabla c\mathrm{d}\Omega

(49)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\bigg)\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}+\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla c+\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\cdot\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\nabla c\mathrm{d}\Omega

(50)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\bigg)\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}+\nabla\cdot\bigg(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\otimes\nabla c\bigg)\cdot\mathbf{u}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(51)

From the first step to the second step, the integration by parts has been applied for the second term (see Eq. (
174
)).
From the second step to the third step,
we have omitted the boundary term
due to the non-flux boundary condition, so that the last term in
Eq. (
48
) vanishes.
Further, we have used the definition of the total time derivative
\partial_{t}c=\mathrm{d}c/\mathrm{d}t-\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla c
.
From the third step to the fourth step, we have combined
the first and the second terms.
From the fourth step
to the last step, we have used the following vector calculus:

\nabla\cdot(\bm{a}\otimes\bm{b})=(\nabla\cdot\bm{a})\bm{b}+\bm{a}\cdot\nabla\bm{b},

(52)

with
\bm{a}=\partial_{\nabla c}g
and
\bm{b}=\nabla c
.

From the first term in Eq. (
51
), we see a natural definition
for the modified chemical potential as

\mu=\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c},

(53)

which is also called as functional derivative.
The above derivation provides a physical interpretation
for the functional derivative from the aspect of thermodynamics
based on the energy minimization principle.
Similar to Eq. (
18
), we propose the modified diffusion equation for binary systems as

\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot M\nabla\bigg(\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\bigg)=\nabla\cdot M\nabla\mu,

(54)

and for multicomponent system as

\frac{\mathrm{d}c_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{K}L_{ij}\nabla\bigg(\frac{\partial g}{\partial c_{j}}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c_{j}}\bigg)=\nabla\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{K}L_{ij}\nabla\mu_{j}.

(55)

Noteworthily, in Eq. (
51
), differing from the uniform system,
we obtain a new term responsible for the
exchange of the free energy with the kinetic energy via defining a force term

\mathbf{f}=\nabla\cdot\bigg(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\otimes\nabla c\bigg),

(56)

the new term
\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{u}
is nothing but the power
done by the force
\mathbf{f}
.
By using the van-der-Waals-Cahn-Hilliard free energy
Rowlinson 1979
;
Cahn and Hilliard 1958

g(c,\nabla c)=f_{0}(c)+\frac{1}{2}\kappa(\nabla c)^{2}

(57)

and
\partial g/\partial\nabla c=\kappa\nabla c
, we obtain the Korteweg stress tensor
as
Korteweg 1901
;
Nestler et al. 2000

\bm{\Theta}=\kappa\nabla c\otimes\nabla c.

(58)

V
Mass system of fluids

For immiscible fluids with an interface, we propose that the system energy
consists of the free energy, the pressure energy, the kinetic energy, and the wall free energy.
The total energy is expressed as

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{F}+\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{K}+\mathcal{W}.

(59)

The pressure energy is formulated as

\mathcal{P}=\int_{\Omega}p\mathrm{d}\Omega=-\int_{\Omega}(g-\mu c)\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(60)

The kinetic energy is written as

\mathcal{K}=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\zeta}\cdot\mathbf{u},

(61)

with the inertial force
\bm{\zeta}=\rho\mathbf{u}
.

The total time derivative of the total energy reads

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{K}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{W}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}+\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{u}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\zeta}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\mathbf{u}+\bm{\zeta}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}+\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{u}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\Omega}\nabla p\cdot\mathbf{u}\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\zeta}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\mathbf{u}+\bm{\zeta}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg[\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}+\bigg(\mathbf{f}+\nabla p+\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\zeta}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg)\cdot\mathbf{u}+\bm{\zeta}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg]\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega.

(62)

From the first step to the second step, we have used the results for the time derivative
of the free energy and the chain rule for the derivative
of the kinetic energy.
For the second step to the third step, we have applied
the chain rule for the time derivative of the pressure

\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla p\cdot\mathbf{u}.

(63)

In the last step, we have collected all the
\cdot\mathbf{u}
terms.

Applying the dissipation principle to the first term in Eq. (
62
),
we obtain the generalized diffusion equation as

\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot M\nabla\mu=\nabla\cdot M\bigg(\frac{\partial g}{\partial c}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\bigg).

(64)

The second term in Eq. (
62
)
leads to the momentum balance equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\zeta}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla p-\mathbf{f}=-\nabla p-\nabla\cdot\bigg(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\otimes\nabla c\bigg).

(65)

The rest two terms in Eq. (
62
)

\int_{\Omega}\bm{\zeta}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega

(66)

will be discussed in section
IX
and section
X
.

VI
Electromagnetic fields in vacuum

We propose that the energy of the system
consists of the free energy
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
,
the pressure energy
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
,
the kinetic energy
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
, and the wall free energy
\hat{\mathcal{W}}
, namely

\hat{\mathcal{E}}=\hat{\mathcal{F}}+\hat{\mathcal{P}}+\hat{\mathcal{K}}+\hat{\mathcal{W}}.

(67)

The free energy is obtained by integrating over the energy density
\hat{f}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{F}}=\int_{\Omega}\hat{f}\mathrm{d}\Omega

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\chi_{0}}\mathbf{B}^{2}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\Omega

(68)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}(\nabla\psi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2\chi_{0}}(\nabla\varphi)^{2}\bigg]\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(69)

The parameters
\varepsilon_{0}
and
\chi_{0}

depict the electrical permittivity
and the magnetic permeability in vacuum, respectively.
The variables
\psi

and
\varphi

denote the potentials of electrostatics and magnetostatics, respectively.
The derivation of the free energy functional is as follows.
The free energy density is composed of the electrostatic energy

\rho_{e}\psi
due to the free charge (in vacuum,
\rho_{e}=0
)
and the gradient term
-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}(\nabla\psi)^{2}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{e}\psi-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}(\nabla\psi)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(70)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-(\nabla\cdot\varepsilon_{0}\nabla\psi)\psi-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}(\nabla\psi)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(71)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\partial\Omega}\varepsilon_{0}\nabla\psi\cdot\mathbf{n}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega+\int_{\Omega}\varepsilon_{0}(\nabla\psi)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}(\nabla\psi)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(72)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}(\nabla\psi)^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(73)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(74)

From the first step to the second step,
we have used the Gauss’ law for electrostatics.
By the similar way, the free energy of magnetostatics is derived.

We define the pressure energy density as

\hat{p}=-(\hat{f}-\hat{\mu}c).

(75)

Since there is no mass in vacuum,
\hat{p}=-\hat{f}
,
namely

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{P}}=\int_{\Omega}\hat{p}\mathrm{d}\Omega,\ \hat{p}=-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\chi_{0}}\mathbf{B}^{2}.

(76)

The kinetic energy is expressed by the production of the
momentum vector with the propagation velocity of the
electromagnetic wave as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}}=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mathcal{P}}\cdot\mathbf{v}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(77)

The wall energy term
\hat{\mathcal{W}}

will be discussed in Section
X
.

We propose that the electromagnetic momentum is expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{P}}=\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}.

(78)

The thermodynamic principle requires
that the total energy should be non-increasing for a closed system, namely

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{E}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{F}}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{P}}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{K}}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{W}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\leq 0.

(79)

Next, we derive the total time derivative of the free energy as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{F}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\psi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\varphi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\psi}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\nabla\psi}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\varphi}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\nabla\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(80)

The further derivation of Eq. (
80
) is quite similar
to that for Eq. (
45
),
where we have a local term
d\psi/\mathrm{d}t

and a non-local term
d\nabla\psi/\mathrm{d}t
.
Substituting Eq. (
69
)
into Eq. (
80
) plus integration by parts
and using the no-flux boundary condition, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{F}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\big[\Phi_{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}t}+\Phi_{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{M}})\cdot\mathbf{v}\big]\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(81)

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}=\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\psi}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\psi},

(82)

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}=\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\varphi}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\varphi},

(83)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{M}}=\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\psi}\otimes\nabla\psi+\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\varphi}\otimes\nabla\varphi.

(84)

The role of potentials
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}

is the same as the chemical potential
\mu
.
Applying the dissipation principle in Section
III

leads to the time evolution for the
electrical and magnetic fields as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot\tau_{1}\nabla\Phi_{1}=\nabla\cdot\tau_{1}\nabla\bigg(\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\psi}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\psi}\bigg),

(85)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot\tau_{2}\nabla\Phi_{2}=\nabla\cdot\tau_{2}\nabla\bigg(\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\varphi}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\varphi}\bigg),

(86)

where
\tau_{1}

and
\tau_{2}
are positive constants.
These time evolution equations result in the energy dissipation as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{F}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\Phi_{1}\nabla\cdot\tau_{1}\nabla\Phi_{1}+\Phi_{2}\nabla\cdot\tau_{2}\nabla\Phi_{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(87)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\tau_{1}(\nabla\Phi_{1})^{2}+\tau_{2}(\nabla\Phi_{2})^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0,

(88)

where the integration by parts and no-flux boundary conditions have been used.

The thermodynamic equilibrium is defined as

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}=\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\psi}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\psi}=0,

(89)

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}=\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\varphi}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\varphi}=0,

(90)

which give rise to Gauss’ law for electrical and magnetic fields as

\displaystyle\rho_{e}-\nabla\cdot\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}=\rho_{e}-\nabla\cdot\varepsilon_{0}\nabla\psi=0;

(91)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=\nabla\cdot\frac{1}{\chi_{0}}\nabla\varphi=0.

(92)

where the following relation has been used

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\psi}=\rho_{e};\ \frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\psi}=\varepsilon_{0}\nabla\psi

(93)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\varphi}=0;\ \frac{\partial\hat{f}}{\partial\nabla\varphi}=\frac{1}{\chi_{0}}\nabla\varphi.

(94)

Noteworthily, similar to the stress tensor
\bm{\Theta}
,
a stress tensor,
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
contributed by
\nabla\psi

and
\nabla\varphi
occurs in the time evolution of the free energy,
as demonstrated in Eq. (
81
).
To see the effect of the stress tensor
\bm{\mathcal{M}}
,
we derive the time derivative for the pressure and kinetic energy as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{E}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\big[\Phi_{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}t}+\Phi_{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{M}})\cdot\mathbf{v}+\nabla\hat{p}\cdot\mathbf{v}+\dot{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}\cdot\mathbf{v}+\bm{\mathcal{P}}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{v}}\big]\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(95)

Collecting all the terms
\cdot\mathbf{v}
in Eq. (
95
),
we obtain the momentum balance equation
for electromagnetic wave in vacuum as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathcal{P}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{T}},\ \bm{\mathcal{T}}=-\hat{p}\mathbf{I}-\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}\otimes\mathbf{E}-\frac{1}{\chi_{0}}\mathbf{B}\otimes\mathbf{B},

(96)

where the Maxwell-stress tensor is expressed as

\bm{\mathcal{T}}=-\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}^{2}+\frac{1}{\chi_{0}}\mathbf{B}^{2})\mathbf{I}+\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}\otimes\mathbf{E}+\frac{1}{\chi_{0}}\mathbf{B}\otimes\mathbf{B}.

(97)

Next, we will demonstrate that the divergence of the Maxwell-stress tensor
leads to the Maxwell-Faraday and Maxwell-Ampere equations
in vacuum.
By using the vector calculus
\nabla\cdot(\mathbb{a}\otimes\mathbb{b})
, see Eq. (
52
),
the divergence of the Maxwell-stress tensor results in

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla(\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}^{2}+\frac{1}{\chi_{0}}\mathbf{B}^{2})+\varepsilon_{0}[(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})\mathbf{E}+(\mathbf{E}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{E}]+\frac{1}{\chi_{0}}[(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B})\mathbf{B}+(\mathbf{B}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{B}]

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}[(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})\mathbf{E}-\mathbf{E}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})]+\frac{1}{\chi_{0}}[(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B})\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})]

(98)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}[(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})\mathbf{E}-\mathbf{E}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})]-\frac{1}{\chi_{0}}[\mathbf{B}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})]+\mathbf{J}\times\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{J}.

(99)

In the second step, we have used the vector calculus, Eq. (
175
)
and Gauss’ law for the magnetic field
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0
.
In the third step, we have added a zero term

\mathbf{J}\times\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{J}=0
.

By using the definition of the proposed electromagnetic momentum

\bm{\mathcal{P}}=\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}
,
we derive
the time derivative of the momentum density as

\varepsilon_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B})}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{B}\times\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{E}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\varepsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}\times\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}}{\mathrm{d}t}.

(100)

Collecting the
\mathbf{E}\times\bullet
terms in Eq. (
100
)
and Eq. (
99
), we obtain the Maxwell-Faraday equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla\times\mathbf{E}.

(101)

Collecting the
\mathbf{B}\times\bullet
terms in Eq. (
100
)
and Eq. (
99
), we obtain the Maxwell-Ampere equation

\chi_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{E}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\chi_{0}\mathbf{J}=\nabla\times\mathbf{B}.

(102)

The rest term excluding
\mathbf{E}\times\bullet
and

\mathbf{B}\times\bullet
terms in Eq. (
99
) is

\mathbf{f}=\varepsilon_{0}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E})\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{J}\times\mathbf{B}=\rho_{e}\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{J}\times\mathbf{B}.

(103)

For the derivation in Eq. (
103
),
the Gauss’ law for electrical field
has been used.
We see that the force
in Eq. (
103
)
is nothing but the classic Lorenz force.
The first term is a force in the direction of the electric field
\mathbb{E}
,
which is proportional to the magnitude of the electric field and the quantity of the charge.
The second term is a force at right angles to both the magnetic field
\mathbb{B}
and the velocity of the charge.

VII
Speed of electromagnetism wave in vacuum

In vacuum, we have
\rho_{e}=0
and therefore
\mathbf{J}=0
.
By considering these two conditions, the Maxwell-Ampere equation
is modified as

\chi_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{E}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\times\mathbf{B}.

(104)

By taking
\nabla\times
on both sides of Eq. (
104
)
and using Eq. (
176
), we obtain

\chi_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{B}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}=\nabla^{2}\mathbf{B}.

(105)

By using the total derivative for a one-dimensional setup without loss of generality
in
x
-coordinate,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}x}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}+v\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}x},

(106)

we obtain the second derivative of the magnetic field as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}B}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigg(\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}+v\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}x}\bigg)+v\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\bigg(\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}+v\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}x}\bigg)

(107)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}x}+2v\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}x}+v\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}x}\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}x}+v^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}B}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}

(108)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}B}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}x}+2v\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}x}+v^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}B}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}.

(109)

For the steady state,
\partial X/\partial t=0
,
X=B,\ \mathrm{d}B/\mathrm{d}x

and assuming that there is no acceleration,
\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t=0
, we obtain

\mathrm{d}^{2}B/\mathrm{d}t^{2}=v^{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}B/\mathrm{d}x^{2}

and hence the speed of light in vacuum as

v^{2}=C^{2}=\frac{1}{\chi_{0}\epsilon_{0}},\quad\text{or}\quad C=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\chi_{0}\epsilon_{0}}}.

(110)

VIII
Maxwell stress tensor in media and its coupling with mass system

For non-vacuum systems consisting of different phases, we define the electrical field displacement
\mathbf{D}

and the magnetizing field
\mathbf{H}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}=\varepsilon(c)\mathbf{E},\ \mathbf{H}=\frac{1}{\chi(c)}\mathbf{B},

(111)

where
\varepsilon
and
\chi

are respectively the electrical permittivity and
the magnetic permeability in the media.
Note that
\varepsilon(c)
and
\chi(c)

are no more constants but depend on the composition at the interface in a non-uniform system.
We will demonstrate that this dependence will modify the Maxwell-Faraday
and Maxwell-Ampere equations as well as the Lorenz force.

The total energy of the system is written as

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{F}+\hat{\mathcal{F}}+\mathcal{P}+\hat{\mathcal{P}}+\mathcal{K}+\hat{\mathcal{K}}+\mathcal{W}+\hat{\mathcal{W}}.

(112)

The free energy
is modified as

\displaystyle E_{f}=\mathcal{F}+\hat{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}e_{f}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(113)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}[g(c,\nabla c)+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{B})]\mathrm{d}\Omega

(114)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[f_{0}(c)+\frac{1}{2}\kappa(\nabla c)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon(\nabla\psi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2\chi}(\nabla\varphi)^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(115)

where the van der Waals-Cahn-Hilliard free energy density has been applied, namely

g(c,\nabla c)=f_{0}(c)+\frac{1}{2}\kappa(\nabla c)^{2}
.

The pressure energy is modified as

\displaystyle E_{p}=\mathcal{P}+\hat{\mathcal{P}}=-\int_{\Omega}[f_{0}(c)+\frac{1}{2}\kappa(\nabla c)^{2}-\mu c+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon(\nabla\psi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2\chi}(\nabla\varphi)^{2}]\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(116)

The kinetic energy is expressed as

\displaystyle E_{k}=\mathcal{K}+\hat{\mathcal{K}}=\int_{\Omega}\big[\bm{\zeta}\cdot\mathbf{u}+\varepsilon(\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B})\cdot\mathbf{v}\big]\mathrm{d}\Omega

(117)

Note that here we have two velocities:

•

\mathbf{u}

depicting the velocity of the mass transport;

•

\mathbf{v}
describing the propagation velocity of the electromagnetic field.

Following the same methodology as in Sections
IV
-
VI
,
we obtain the total time derivative of the free energy as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{f}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\big[\Phi_{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}t}+\Phi_{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{M}})\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mu\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\nabla\cdot\bm{\Theta})\cdot\mathbf{u}\big]\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(118)

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}=\frac{\partial e_{f}}{\partial\psi}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial e_{f}}{\partial\nabla\psi},

(119)

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}=\frac{\partial e_{f}}{\partial\varphi}-\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial e_{f}}{\partial\nabla\varphi},

(120)

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial c}-\kappa\nabla^{2}c+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\prime}(c)\mathbb{E}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\chi^{2}}\chi^{\prime}(c)\mathbb{B}^{2},

(121)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{M}}=\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\otimes\mathbb{E}+\frac{1}{\chi}\mathbb{B}\otimes\mathbb{B}.

(122)

At thermodynamic equilibrium, we have
\Phi_{1}=0
and
\Phi_{2}=0
, which respectively lead to
Gauss’ law for electrical and magnetic fields

\displaystyle\rho_{e}=\nabla\cdot\varepsilon\nabla\psi=\nabla\cdot\mathbb{D},

(123)

\displaystyle 0=\nabla\cdot\frac{1}{\chi}\nabla\varphi=\nabla\cdot\mathbb{H}.

(124)

Note that the Gauss’ law for magnetic field in Eq. (
124
)
differs from existing theories
\nabla\cdot\mathbb{B}=0
.
For a homogeneous system or vacuum, the magnetic permeability is a constant value
and Eq. (
124
) reduces to
\nabla\cdot\mathbb{B}=0
.
However, at a phase-phase interface, Eq. (
124
) should be applied.

The total time derivative of the pressure energy reads

\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla p\cdot\mathbf{u}+\nabla\hat{p}\cdot\mathbf{v})\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(125)

where the pressure energy density are expressed as

\displaystyle-p

\displaystyle=f_{0}(c)+\frac{1}{2}\kappa(\nabla c)^{2}-\mu c,

(126)

\displaystyle-\hat{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon(\nabla\psi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2\chi}(\nabla\varphi)^{2}.

(127)

The total time derivative of the kinetic energy reads

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\Omega}\bigg[\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\zeta}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\mathbf{u}+\bm{\zeta}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B}\big)\cdot\mathbf{v}+\big(\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B}\big)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg]\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(128)

Collecting all the
\cdot\mathbf{u}
term, we obtain the momentum balance equation for the mass transport
as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\zeta}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}(\rho\mathbf{u})}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla p-\nabla\cdot\bm{\Theta}.

(129)

Collecting all the
\cdot\mathbf{v}
term, we obtain the momentum balance equation for the electromagnetic field as

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B}\big)=-\nabla\hat{p}-\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{M}}=-\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{T}}.

(130)

The right hand side is nothing but the Maxwell stress tensor in media

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=-\big(\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{D}\cdot\mathbf{E}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{B}\big)\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{D}\otimes\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{H}\otimes\mathbf{B}

(131)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\mathbb{E}^{2}\mathbf{I}+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\otimes\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{1}}\underbrace{-\frac{1}{2\chi}\mathbb{B}^{2}\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{\chi}\mathbb{B}\otimes\mathbb{B}}_{\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{2}},

(132)

which is composed of the electro-part
\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{1}

and the magneto-part
\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
.
The divergence of the Maxwell stress tensor leads to

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\nabla(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{D}\cdot\mathbb{E})+\nabla\cdot(\mathbb{D}\otimes\mathbb{E})

(133)

\displaystyle=-\nabla(\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\cdot\mathbb{E})+[\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\mathbb{E})]\mathbb{E}+(\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\cdot\nabla)\mathbb{E}

(134)

\displaystyle=-\nabla(\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\cdot\mathbb{E})+[\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\mathbb{E})]\mathbb{E}-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\times(\nabla\times\mathbb{E})+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\nabla\mathbb{E}^{2}

(135)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\prime}\mathbb{E}^{2}\nabla c+[\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\mathbb{E})]\mathbb{E}-\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\times(\nabla\times\mathbb{E}).

(136)

From the first to the second step, we have applied the vector calculus of Eq. (
52
).
From the second to the third step, we have employed the vector calculus of Eq. (
175
).
Note that unlike the derivation in vacuum,
the last term cannot be cancelled out with the first term in Eq. (
135
).
This reason is that the electrical permittivity has a dependence on the composition.
Similarly, we derive the divergence for the magnetic part of the Maxwell stress tensor as

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\nabla(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{H}\cdot\mathbb{B})+\nabla\cdot(\mathbb{H}\otimes\mathbb{B})

(137)

\displaystyle=-\nabla(\frac{1}{2\chi}\mathbb{B}\cdot\mathbb{B})+[\nabla\cdot\big(\frac{1}{\chi}\mathbb{B}\big)]\mathbb{B}+\big(\frac{1}{\chi}\mathbb{B}\cdot\nabla\big)\mathbb{B}

(138)

\displaystyle=-\nabla(\frac{1}{2\chi}\mathbb{B}\cdot\mathbb{B})+[\nabla\cdot\big(\frac{1}{\chi}\mathbb{B}\big)]\mathbb{B}-\frac{1}{\chi}\mathbb{B}\times(\nabla\times\mathbb{B})+\frac{1}{2\chi}\nabla\mathbb{B}^{2}

(139)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\chi^{2}}\chi^{\prime}\mathbb{B}^{2}\nabla c+[\nabla\cdot\big(\frac{1}{\chi}\mathbb{B}\big)]\mathbb{B}-\frac{1}{\chi}\mathbb{B}\times(\nabla\times\mathbb{B})

(140)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\chi^{2}}\chi^{\prime}\mathbb{B}^{2}\nabla c-\frac{1}{\chi}\mathbb{B}\times(\nabla\times\mathbb{B})+\mathbb{J}\times\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{J},

(141)

where in the last step, we have added a zero term,
\mathbb{J}\times\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{J}=0
.
As in Eq. (
135
), we obtain a composition dependent term
\sim\nabla c

due to the dependence of the magnetic permeability on the composition.

The time derivative of the momentum reads

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B})=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{D}}{\mathrm{d}t}\times\mathbb{B}+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\times\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{B}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathbb{B}\times\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{D}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\times\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{B}}{\mathrm{d}t}.

(142)

Collecting all the
\mathbb{B}\times
terms in Eq. (
142
) and Eq. (
141
), we obtain
a modified Maxwell-Ampere equation in media as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{D}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{\chi}(\nabla\times\mathbb{B})-\mathbf{J}.

(143)

Note that the
1/\chi
-term is not a constant for inhomogeneous materials.
For homogeneous material,
1/\chi
can be placed inside the gradient operator,
leading to the conventional Maxwell-Faraday equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{D}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\times\mathbb{H}-\mathbf{J}.

(144)

Collecting all the
\mathbb{E}\times
terms n Eq. (
142
) and Eq. (
136
), we obtain
the Maxwell-Faraday equation as

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{B}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla\times\mathbb{E}.

(145)

The rest terms in Eq. (
141
) and Eq. (
136
)
result in a modified Lorenz force

\displaystyle\mathbb{f}

\displaystyle=[\nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\mathbb{E})]\mathbb{E}+\mathbb{J}\times\mathbb{B}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\prime}\mathbb{E}^{2}\nabla c+\frac{1}{2\chi^{2}}\chi^{\prime}\mathbb{B}^{2}\nabla c

(146)

\displaystyle=\rho_{e}\mathbb{E}+\mathbb{J}\times\mathbb{B}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\prime}\mathbb{E}^{2}\nabla c+\frac{1}{2\chi^{2}}\chi^{\prime}\mathbb{B}^{2}\nabla c.

(147)

Noteworthily, the last two terms depend on the composition gradient
and do not occur in the
conventional Lorenz force.

IX
Dissipation of the vectors
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{u}

There are some terms which have not been discussed in the energy minimization derivation.
For the mass system, the not yet discussed term is

\int_{\Omega}\bm{\zeta}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(148)

Following the dissipation theorem, we obtain two types of dissipation for the velocity as

\displaystyle\text{Non-conservative:}~\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\tau_{0}\bm{\zeta},

(149)

\displaystyle\text{Conservative:}~\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot\tau_{1}(\bm{\zeta}+\bm{\zeta}^{T})+\nabla\cdot\tau_{2}(\nabla\bm{\zeta})\mathbf{I}.

(150)

The first dissipation equation is equivalent to the Langevin equation when defining
\tau_{0}=\eta/\rho
, leading to

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\eta\mathbf{u}.

(151)

The second dissipation equation is related to the Newton viscous stress tensor when defining

\tau_{1}=\eta/\rho^{2},\ \tau_{2}=\lambda/\rho^{2},

(152)

leading to

\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot\eta(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T})+\nabla\cdot\lambda(\nabla\mathbf{u})\mathbf{I}

(153)

for a constant density.

For the massless system, the remaining term is

\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon_{0}\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B})\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(154)

for vacuum
and

\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B})\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\Omega

(155)

in media.

Applying the dissipation theorem,
we obtain the dissipation equation for the
electromagnetic velocity
\mathbf{v}
as

\displaystyle\text{Non-conservative:}~\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\tau_{0}(\varepsilon_{0}\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B}),

(156)

\displaystyle\text{Conservative:}~\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\cdot\tau_{1}[(\varepsilon_{0}\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B})+(\varepsilon_{0}\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B})^{T}]+\nabla\cdot\tau_{2}[\nabla(\varepsilon_{0}\mathbb{E}\times\mathbb{B})]\mathbf{I}.

(157)

In vacuum, we assume there is no dissipation, i.e.,
\tau_{0}=0
,
\tau_{1}=0
,
\tau_{2}=0
.
In media,
\tau_{i}>0
.

We remark that the conservation formulation,
such as mass and momentum conservations, is unique,
but the dissipation formulation can be of diversity.
The dissipation equation does not necessarily follow
the conserved type
\nabla\cdot M\nabla\mu

or the non-conserved type
-\tau\mu
.
Any other non-linear types of dissipation relating to
\mu^{n}
or
(\nabla\mu)^{n}
are also possible, as long as

\int_{\Omega}\mu(dc/\mathrm{d}t)\mathrm{d}\Omega\leq 0
.

X
Wall effect: chemical and electrowetting

The only term left in the time evolution of the
total energy is the chemical wall free energy

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{W}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega=\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{w}}{dc}\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega

(158)

and the electrical wall free energy

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathcal{W}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{f_{w}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega

(159)

The combination
of Eq. (
158
)
with the boundary term coming from the integration by parts (see Eq. (
48
))

\int_{\partial\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\nabla c}\cdot\mathbf{n}\bigg)\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega

(160)

leads to the Young’s law for chemical wetting
Wang et al. 2023
.
The combination
of Eq. (
159
)
with the boundary term arising from the integration by parts in the derivative of the
electromagnetic free energy
leads to the Young-Lippmann’s equation for electrowetting.
These wall effects are out of the scope of the present work.

XI
Conclusion and outlook

In conclusion, we have proposed a coupling theory
for the mass transport in fluids
with the propagation electromagnetic wave
in media.
Our derivation is based on the framework of energy minimization.
Differing from previous works, the following key points are obtained in the
coupling theory of the
current work:

•

We obtain a modified Lorenz force in media

\displaystyle\mathbb{f}=\rho_{e}\mathbb{E}+\mathbb{J}\times\mathbb{B}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\prime}\mathbb{E}^{2}\nabla c+\frac{1}{2\chi^{2}}\chi^{\prime}\mathbb{B}^{2}\nabla c,

(161)

vs. existing theories for uniform system

\displaystyle\mathbb{f}

\displaystyle=\rho_{e}\mathbb{E}+\mathbb{J}\times\mathbb{B}.

(162)

The first two terms in Eq. (
161
)
are responsible for the transport of the electrons in an electromagnetic field.
The last two terms in Eq. (
161
)
account for the mass effect that has been overlooked in London equations
for the derivation of conventional low-temperature superconductivity.
When temperature becomes high,
the thermal-noise related mass terms could play a comparable role
with the electromagnetic terms of electron transport.
We expect that the modified Lorenz force
will shed light on high temperature superconductivity.
A comprehensive discussion
about the modified Lorenz force
on the high temperature superconductivity
will be presented elsewhere.

•

We obtain a modified Maxwell-Ampere equation in inhomogeneous system

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{D}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{\chi}(\nabla\times\mathbb{B})-\mathbf{J},

(163)

vs. homogeneous system

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{D}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\nabla\times\mathbb{H}-\mathbf{J}.

(164)

The modified Maxwell-Ampere equation can be used to
explain the mechanism of thermoelectric effect.
By using the chain rule
(1/\chi)\nabla\times\mathbb{B}=\nabla\times\mathbb{H}-\nabla(1/\chi)\times\mathbb{H}
and considering the equilibrium state

\mathrm{d}\mathbb{D}/\mathrm{d}t=0
,
we obtain

\mathbb{J}=\nabla\times\mathbb{H}+\frac{1}{\chi^{2}}\chi^{\prime}\nabla c\times\mathbb{H}.

(165)

Eq. (
165
)
indicates that a composition or temperature gradient
can induce an electrical current and the associated magnetic field.

•

We obtain a modified Gauss’ law for magnetic field in inhomogeneous media

\nabla\cdot\mathbb{H}=0,

(166)

vs. in homogeneous media

\nabla\cdot\mathbb{B}=0.

(167)

•

We obtain a new force term accounting for the time evolution of mass momentum due to the movement of the electromagnetic fields as

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle=-\nabla p-\nabla\cdot\bm{(}\kappa\nabla c\otimes\nabla c);

(168)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=f_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa(\nabla c)^{2}-\big(f_{0}^{\prime}-\kappa\nabla^{2}c+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\prime}\mathbb{E}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\chi}\chi^{\prime}\mathbb{B}^{2}\big)c.

(169)

Appendix A
Calculus

When defining two vectors
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
we have the following equality:

\nabla\cdot(\bm{a}\otimes\bm{b})=(\nabla\cdot\bm{a})\bm{b}+(\bm{a}\cdot\nabla)\bm{b}.

(170)

This equality is also hold for non-curl field. If

\nabla\times\bm{a}=0
,
\nabla\times\bm{b}=0
,
we have
\bm{a}=\nabla\phi
,
\bm{b}=\nabla\varphi
.
In this case, we have the following vector calculus

\nabla\cdot(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\varphi)=(\nabla\cdot\nabla\phi)\nabla\varphi+(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla)\nabla\phi.

(171)

The total time derivative of a scalar field
\psi(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}
and vector
\nabla\psi(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

is expressed as

\displaystyle\dot{\psi}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\psi

(172)

\displaystyle\dot{\nabla\psi}=\frac{\partial\nabla\psi}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\nabla\psi=\nabla\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\nabla\psi.

(173)

For a functional
e(\phi,\nabla\phi)
:
F\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(
\phi\in F
)
and a scalar field
\phi(x)
:
\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we have the following integration by parts

\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial e}{\partial\nabla\phi}\cdot\nabla\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\Omega=\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\partial e}{\partial\nabla\phi}\cdot\mathbf{n}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\partial\Omega-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\frac{\partial e}{\partial\nabla\phi}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\mathrm{d}\Omega.

(174)

Two additional vector calculus reads

\frac{1}{2}\nabla(\mathbf{A}^{2})=\mathbf{A}\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{A})+(\mathbf{A}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{A},

(175)

and

\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{A})=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A})-\nabla^{2}\mathbf{A}.

(176)
