Momentum and Matter Matter for Axion Dark Matter Matters on Earth
Abhishek Banerjee ID, Itay M. Bloch ID, Quentin Bonnefoy ID, Sebastian A. R. Ellis ID, Gilad Perez ID, Inbar Savoray ID, Konstantin Springmann ID, Yevgeny V. Stadnik ID

Abstract
We investigate the implications of matter effects to searches for axion Dark Matter on Earth. The finite momentum of axion Dark Matter is crucial to elucidating the effects of Earth on both the axion Dark Matter field value and its gradient. We find that experiments targeting axion couplings compatible with canonical solutions of the strong CP puzzle are likely not affected by Earth’s matter effects. However, experiments sensitive to lighter axions with stronger couplings can be significantly affected, with a significant part of the parameter space suffering from a reduced axion field value , and therefore decreased experimental sensitivity. In contrast, the spatial gradient of the axion field can be enhanced along Earth’s radial direction, with important implications for ongoing and planned experiments searching for axion Dark Matter.

III
Solutions with Finite Asymptotic Momentum

The origin of the matter effects lies in the non-derivative couplings of axions to nucleons. These couplings follow automatically from the defining coupling of the QCD axion to gluons (see, e.g.,
[
92
]
).
Assuming the axion is responsible for the local
DM
density, the amplitude of the field
a
is sufficiently small that these effects are dominated by the quadratic coupling of axions to nucleons,

\mathcal{L}=-\sigma_{N}\bar{N}N\sqrt{1-\beta\sin^{2}\left(\frac{a}{2f_{a}}\right)}\simeq\frac{1}{2}\frac{z}{(1+z)^{2}}\sigma_{N}\bar{N}N\frac{a^{2}}{f_{a}^{2}}\,,

(6)

where
N=(p,n)^{\operatorname{T}}
is the nucleon field,
\beta=4z/(1+z)^{2}
with
z=m_{u}/m_{d}
, and
\sigma_{N}\simeq 50~\text{MeV}
is the nucleon sigma term (see, e.g.,
[
93
]
and references therein).
As the axion is CP odd, it is evident that in the absence of CP violation, an axion coupling to a CP-even nucleon operator would naturally arise at the quadratic order.
This coupling corresponds to
d_{m_{N}}^{(2)}=-z\sigma_{N}M_{\rm Pl}^{2}/[(1+z)^{2}f_{a}^{2}]
in the notation of, e.g.,
[
41
]
, where
M_{\rm Pl}
is the Planck scale.
In particular, the interaction in Eq. (
6
) implies that the axion mass is
reduced
in a background of finite nucleon number density

\braket{\bar{N}N}\simeq n\neq 0
such as Earth.
We can write the effective in-medium axion mass-squared as

\left(m_{a}^{\text{eff}}\right)^{2}=m_{a}^{2}-{\delta m}^{2}=\frac{z}{(1+z)^{2}}\frac{1}{f_{a}^{2}}\left(\epsilon\,m_{\pi}^{2}f_{\pi}^{2}-\sigma_{N}\,n\right),

(7)

where
{\delta m}^{2}>0
is the shift in the mass-squared due to the finite density background.

The axion’s EOM in three dimensions in the presence of Earth, which we model by a perfect sphere of constant density
\rho_{\oplus}\simeq n\,m_{N}\simeq 5.5\,\text{g}/\text{cm}^{3}
and radius
R\simeq 6400\,\text{km}
, is now

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left(\Box+m_{a}^{2}\right)a(\boldsymbol{r},t)=0\,,&r>R\\
\left(\Box+m_{a}^{2}-{\delta m}^{2}\right)a(\boldsymbol{r},t)=0\,,&r<R\end{cases}\,.

(8)

We have introduced the quantity
r=|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{\oplus}|
to denote the distance in the radial direction in Earth-centric coordinates. We assume that the axion constitutes all of the
DM
and is virialized in the galaxy with a characteristic momentum
k\simeq 10^{-3}m_{a}
. The goal of this section is to calculate solutions to the EOM that asymptote towards the virialized axion
DM
background at spatial infinity.

Before we proceed with solving the EOM, it is important to mention that they are not valid for cases where the mass reduction dominates over the vacuum mass, i.e.
{\delta m}^{2}>m_{a}^{2}
.
This region of invalidity is depicted as the red region in Fig.
1
.
Here, the axion field can be sourced by compact objects, independently of constituting significant parts of the
DM
, which leads to interesting phenomenology
[
25
,
94
,
35
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
. To treat this region properly, one should consider fluctuations around the
r
-dependent axion vacuum expectation value and linearize the
EOM
around it. Given the stringent constraints that arise by sourcing the axion, we do not pursue this approach in this work.

Since we are interested in steady-state solutions, we can expand the axion field as a sum over plane-waves weighted by the appropriate velocity-dependent factor
f_{\omega}
, i.e.,
a(\boldsymbol{r},t)\simeq\sum_{\omega}f_{\omega}a_{\omega}(\boldsymbol{r})e^{i\omega t+\phi_{\omega}}+h.c.
. Inserting this decomposition into the axion EOM, we obtain equations for each frequency mode,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left(\omega^{2}-m_{a}^{2}+\nabla^{2}\right)a_{\omega}=0\,,&r>R\\
\left(\omega^{2}-m_{a}^{2}+\nabla^{2}+{\delta m}^{2}\right)a_{\omega}=0\,,&r<R\end{cases}\,.

(9)

The solutions of interest are those that, far from the massive body, converge to the standard
DM
solution

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{r},t)\Big|_{r\gg R}\longrightarrow\,2a_{0}\cos{\left(\omega_{a}t-\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}\right)}\,,

(10)

where
a_{0}=\sqrt{\rho_{\rm DM}/(2m_{a}^{2})}
(this definition is half that of the more common notation for
a_{0}
).
In what follows, we factor
e^{i\omega t}
out of the solution, because the axion energy remains unchanged across the matter-vacuum boundary. Hence the field value is normalized to
a_{0}e^{-i\left|\boldsymbol{k}\right|\left|\boldsymbol{r}\right|\cos(\theta)}
at
\left|\boldsymbol{r}\right|\to\infty
, where
\theta
is defined as the angle between
\boldsymbol{k}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
.
We omit the random phase at infinity for simplicity, see appendix
A
for the more complete treatment of observables for a non-monochromatic field.

General solutions to Eq. (
9

r>R
) and inside (
r<R
) the massive body, with the boundary conditions at
r=R
fixing the unknown coefficients.
The solution outside has to be composed of the incoming wave, i.e., the solution at
r\rightarrow\infty
, and outgoing scattered waves, which vanish at
r\rightarrow\infty
in order to satisfy the
DM

Given the spherical symmetry of the problem, angular momentum is conserved. Furthermore, given the azimuthal symmetry of the boundary conditions around the momentum of the incoming wave
\boldsymbol{k}
, we know that our solutions decompose to
l
-modes with
m=0
(dictated by the choice of the
DM

Following these considerations, the solution outside the spherical body may be expressed solely in terms of the

j_{\ell}
, and spherical Hankel functions of the first kind
h^{(1)}_{\ell}
for outgoing waves

\displaystyle a^{\rm out}_{\omega}\left(r,\theta\right)=\sum^{\infty}_{\ell=0}a^{\rm out}_{\omega,\ell}\left(r,\theta\right)=a_{0}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\,i^{\ell}\,j_{\ell}(kr)\,P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)+\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{\ell}^{\text{out}}\,h_{\ell}^{(1)}(kr)\,P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\,,

(11)

where

is the axion momentum outside the massive body, and

the Legendre polynomials of order
\ell
.
The first term in Eq. (

\propto c_{\ell}^{\text{out}}
describes the outgoing scattered wave (adding terms
\propto h_{\ell}^{(2)}

Matching the solution and its first derivative at
r=R_{\oplus}
to the solution inside the massive body fixes the coefficients
c_{\ell}^{\rm out}
.

The solution inside the sphere may also be expanded in terms of Legendre polynomials and spherical Bessel functions.
However, the solution must not diverge at any point inside the sphere, so only the
j_{\ell}
spherical Bessel functions can contribute. We therefore obtain

\displaystyle a^{\rm in}_{\omega}\left(r,\theta\right)=\sum^{\infty}_{\ell=0}a^{\rm in}_{\omega,\ell}\left(r,\theta\right)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{\ell}^{\text{in}}\,j_{\ell}(qr)\,P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\,.

(12)

Energy conservation, along with the fact that the oscillation frequency is unchanged in our steady-state solution, enforces that the momentum-squared
inside Earth is
q^{2}=k^{2}+{\delta m}^{2}>k^{2}
.
5
5

5

Here,
{\delta m}^{2}>0
following Eq. (
6
). If the coupling in Eq. (
6
) had the opposite sign, then
{\delta m}^{2}<0
and the solutions for the scalar field would exhibit pure screening
[
41
,
42
,
45
,
95
]
.
As we focus on light QCD axions in this work, we do not consider this case in detail.

Matching term by term at the surface of the sphere, one finds

\displaystyle a^{\rm out}_{\omega,\ell}\left(r,\theta\right)

\displaystyle=a_{0}\frac{i^{\ell}(2\ell+1)P_{\ell}\left(\cos\theta\right)}{Q_{\ell}}\left[y_{\ell}(kr)\text{Im}\left(Q_{\ell}\right)+j_{\ell}(kr)\text{Re}\left(Q_{\ell}\right)\right]\,,

(13)

\displaystyle a^{\rm in}_{\omega,\ell}\left(r,\theta\right)

\displaystyle=-a_{0}\frac{i^{\ell}(2\ell+1)P_{\ell}\left(\cos\theta\right)}{kR^{2}Q_{\ell}}j_{\ell}(qr)\,,

(14)

\displaystyle\text{Re}\left(Q_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=kj_{\ell}\left(qR\right)y_{\ell+1}\left(kR\right)-qj_{\ell+1}(qR)y_{\ell}\left(kR\right)\,,

(15)

\displaystyle\text{Im}\left(Q_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=qj_{\ell+1}(qR)j_{\ell}\left(kR\right)-kj_{\ell}\left(qR\right)j_{\ell+1}\left(kR\right)\,.

(16)

Here, we used spherical Bessel functions of the second kind
y_{\ell}
instead of the spherical Hankel function, and the two are related by
h_{\ell}^{(1)}(z)\equiv j_{\ell}(z)+iy_{\ell}(z)
.
One can also write
Q_{\ell}=i[qj_{\ell+1}(qR)h_{\ell}^{(1)}(kR)-kj_{\ell}(qR)h_{\ell+1}^{(1)}(kR)]
instead.

Note that the scattered waves, or the deviation from the free case (i.e., the incident plane-wave solution), is given by

\displaystyle a_{\omega,\ell}^{\rm scattered}=a_{0}i^{\ell}\left(2\ell+1\right)P_{\ell}\left(\cos\theta\right)\left[y_{\ell}\left(kr\right)-ij_{\ell}\left(kr\right)\right]\frac{{\rm Im}\left(Q_{\ell}\right)}{Q_{\ell}}\,.

(17)

When
k=q
, we obtain
\textrm{Im}(Q_{\ell})=0\,,\textrm{Re}\left(Q_{\ell}\right)=-1/\left(kR^{2}\right)
, and the waves inside and outside the sphere are simply the unperturbed
DM
solution, as expected.
On the other hand, we expect a strong effect from a mode
\ell
when the scattered wave introduces an order one modification over the free one.

In this work, to simplify our calculations, we often remove the angular variable by averaging upon it. Then, the averaged field-amplitude-squared is given by

\langle|a_{\omega}(R)|^{2}\rangle_{{}_{\theta}}=\left(\frac{a_{0}}{k^{2}R^{2}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\left|\frac{j_{\ell}(qR)}{h_{\ell+1}^{(1)}(kR)\,j_{\ell}(qR)-\left(\frac{q}{k}\right)h_{\ell}^{(1)}(kR)\,j_{\ell+1}(qR)}\right|^{2}\ ,

(18)

where we introduce the notation
\langle\cdot\rangle_{{}_{\theta}}

\theta

\langle|a_{\omega}|^{2}\rangle_{\theta}+\langle|a_{-\omega}|^{2}\rangle_{\theta}=2\langle|a_{\omega}|^{2}\rangle_{\theta}

IV
Monopole and Dipole Contributions

In this section, we focus on the monopole
\ell=0
and dipole
\ell=1
contributions.
In the case when
kR\gg 1
, we find that many
\ell
-modes contribute, and one must sum up to
\ell=kR+\mathcal{O}(1)(kR)^{1/3}
(as can be understood by using the asymptotic spherical Bessel functions formulae
[
96
]
) to estimate the non-resonant contributions.
For
kR\ll 1
, we can Taylor expand our solution and its derivative around
k\to 0
(away from resonances) to find,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to 0}a_{\omega,\ell}(R)=a_{0}\frac{(1+2\ell)\left(-\frac{ikR}{2}\right)^{\ell}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-\ell\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{j_{\ell}({\delta m}R)}{{\delta m}R\,j_{\ell-1}({\delta m}R)}P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\ ,

(20)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to 0}\partial_{r}a_{\omega,\ell}(R)=-a_{0}\frac{(1+2\ell)\left(-\frac{ikR}{2}\right)^{\ell}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-\ell\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\left[(1+\ell)j_{\ell}({\delta m}R)-{\delta m}Rj_{\ell-1}({\delta m}R)\right]}{{\delta m}\,R\,j_{\ell-1}({\delta m}R)}P_{\ell}(\cos\theta)\ .

Due to the
(kR)^{\ell}
scaling, one would expect that for
kR<1
one may focus on the low
\ell
modes. Hence, this section which focuses on
\ell=0,1
can be understood as describing the axion in the
{k}_{\rm vir}R\ll 1
regime. Interestingly, the
\ell=0,1
modes generically dominate the overall amplitude in Eq. (
18
), even when sampling
k
stochastically (see Appendix
A
).
While one may worry about the presence of resonances from high
\ell
modes, for
{k}_{\rm vir}R\ll 1
, this rarely occurs.
One may estimate the distance in
q
between two adjacent resonances for large
qR
to be roughly
1/(qR^{2})
.
Since the virial integral integrates over a region
{\delta m}\leq q\lesssim\sqrt{{\delta m}^{2}+{{k}_{\rm vir}^{2}}}
, one can see that the region being scanned is narrower than
1/(qR^{2})
for
{k}_{\rm vir}R<1<qR
.
Hence, one should not expect resonances to contribute.
For further discussion, see also Appendix
C
.

At the end of the section, we will also discuss more broadly the field value and gradient deep inside Earth, and far outside of it.

IV.1
Terrestrial
a
observables

Let us first discuss the field values of the
\ell=0,1
modes at
r=R
.
We begin with
\ell=0
. The field value for
\ell=0
at the boundary of the object is

a_{\omega,0}(R)=\frac{e^{-ikR}a_{0}}{-ikR+{q}R\cot(qR)}\,.

(21)

This is to be compared to the
\ell=0
component of the unperturbed axion wave, which is simply
a_{0}\sin(kR)/(kR)
, which is approximately
a_{0}
for
kR\ll 1
. As
qR
grows, the modified field value would drastically change, since
\cot(qR)
diverges when
\sin(qR)=0
.
In fact, unless
\cos(qR)=0
, for increasing values of
qR
, the
\ell=0
mode would be suppressed by
1/(qR)
compared to the expectation far from Earth.
This means that unless
\cos(qR)\approx 0
, where the field is enhanced by
1/(kR)
(which corresponds to the
qR=\pi n+\pi/2
resonances displayed in Fig.
2
), the
\ell=0
mode would be suppressed for
qR\gg 1
, i.e., for
f_{a}\ll(f_{a})_{\oplus}^{c}
[where the critical decay constant
(f_{a})^{c}_{\oplus}
is given by Eq. (
3
) for Earth].
Away from resonances, the axion field value in the large
qR
limit becomes approximately

\displaystyle a_{\omega,0}(R)\sim a_{0}\min\left[1,\frac{f_{a}}{(f_{a})_{\oplus}^{c}}\right]\ .

(22)

Now let us examine how the
\ell=1
mode changes the picture.
The field value at the surface of Earth is

a_{\omega,1}(R,\theta)=\frac{3a_{0}kRe^{-ikR}\cos(\theta)\left[qR\cos(qR)-\sin(qR)\right]}{\left[kR(qR)^{2}-iR^{2}(k^{2}-q^{2})-3kR+3\right]\sin(qR)-qR\left[3-kR(3-ikR)\right]\cos(qR)}\,.

(23)

For
kR\lesssim 1\lesssim qR
and away from a resonance or anti-resonance [i.e., when
j_{0,1}(qR)=0
respectively], this field is suppressed by the factor
\mathcal{O}(k/q)
compared to the amplitude of the
\ell=0
mode.
However, when the
\ell=0
mode is suppressed (i.e., when
\sin(qR)=0
), this mode becomes enhanced as
1/(kR)
(which is again evident in Fig.
2
).
This means that within the aforementioned region, for most choices of
qR
, this mode is negligible; however, for specific choices of
qR
, where the
\ell=0
mode is suppressed, this mode becomes enhanced.

IV.2
Terrestrial
\partial a
observables

Ignoring the effect of the massive body, the gradient is of order
ka_{0}
.
However, the existence of a matter-sourced potential can lead to a significant deviation from this expectation.
Since
P_{0}(x)=1
, the
\ell=0
mode has only a radial derivative, whose value at the surface of the body reads

\partial_{r}a_{\omega,0}(R)=\frac{e^{-ikR}a_{0}}{R}\frac{qR\cos(qR)-\sin(qR)}{qR\cos(qR)-ikR\sin(qR)}\,.

(24)

For
qR\gtrsim 1
, its magnitude is generically enhanced with respect to the
ka_{0}
expectation (i.e., with respect to the
{\delta m}=0
case) by a factor of
1/(kR)
, and even by
1/(kR)^{2}
on the
\ell=0
resonance, i.e., when
\cos(qR)=0
. We emphasize that this enhancement is compared to
ka_{0}=(ka)_{\rm no~Earth}
, so that even though the field values
a(R)
become smaller for increasing
qR
, the derivatives are enhanced in absolute terms.

We find that the typical value of the radial gradient away from any resonances, given in terms of the axion mass and coupling constant is

\displaystyle\partial_{r}a_{\omega}(R_{\oplus})\sim ka_{0}\left(1+\max\left[1,\frac{1}{kR_{\oplus}}\right]\,\min\left[1,\left(\frac{(f_{a})^{c}_{\oplus}}{f_{a}}\right)^{2}\right]\right)\ ,

(25)

with
(f_{a})^{c}_{\oplus}
defined for Earth in Eq. (
3
).
We have written this with an overall factor of
ka_{0}
, which is the naive solution when not accounting for matter effects, so as to highlight the enhancement in the small
kR_{\oplus}
limit.

The
\ell=1
term has a radial derivative and one in
\hat{\theta}
. Focusing again on
kR\lesssim 1\lesssim qR
, when
\sin(qR)=0
, both derivatives scale like
1/(kR^{2})
, while for other values of
qR
, the radial derivative scales like
k
, while the gradient in the angular direction scales like
k/(qR)
.

Interestingly, even for
qR\lesssim 1
,
\nabla a
can still have a significant enhancement compared to the vacuum case. For
kR<qR<1
,
\boldsymbol{\nabla}a\approx-a_{0}{\delta m}^{2}R{\boldsymbol{\hat{r}}}/3
, which can be far larger than
ka_{0}
, which is the vacuum prediction. This is different from the sourced soliton solution, which stops being important for
{\delta m}R<1
.

V.2.1
Experiments in the Magneto-quasistatic limit

Our findings indicate that axion amplitudes and gradients are only affected for small axions or large couplings (see Fig.

). Therefore, the experiments with DC background
EM

l

\omega
, such that
\omega l\ll 1
. We further assume they are operated with a background DC magnetic field
B_{0}
and no background electric field.

As long as the axion gradient in the radial direction dominates, the equations for the axion-induced
EM

\displaystyle\frac{1}{l^{2}}E_{1}

\displaystyle\sim g_{a\gamma\gamma}B_{0}\left(m_{a}^{2}a-\frac{1}{l}\partial_{r}a-\partial_{r}^{2}a\right)\ ,

(31)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{l^{2}}B_{1}

\displaystyle\sim-g_{a\gamma\gamma}B_{0}m_{a}\left(\frac{1}{l}a+\partial_{r}a\right)\ .

(32)

Neglecting matter effects,
\partial_{r}a\sim ka_{0}
, and we get the usual solution for
E_{1}\sim(m_{a}l)^{2}B_{0}a_{0}
, while
B_{1}\sim(m_{a}l)B_{0}a_{0}

[

\nabla a
is affected alone (green coloured region of Fig.
1
), or
a(R_{\oplus})
is also affected (blue coloured region of Fig.

). Therefore, the common assumption that the axion-induced electric field is parametrically smaller than the induced magnetic field can be false.
Using the results of Eqs. (
22
) and (

a(R_{\oplus})
and
\partial_{r}a(R_{\oplus})
respectively, we find

\displaystyle E_{1}(R_{\oplus})

\displaystyle\sim g_{a\gamma\gamma}B_{0}a_{0}\left((lm_{a})^{2}\min\left[1,\frac{f_{a}}{(f_{a})^{c}_{\oplus}}\right]-\left(\frac{l}{R_{\oplus}}-\frac{l^{2}}{R_{\oplus}^{2}}\right)\min\left[1,\left(\frac{(f_{a})^{c}_{\oplus}}{f_{a}}\right)^{2}\right]\right)\ ,

(33)

\displaystyle B_{1}(R_{\oplus})

\displaystyle\sim-g_{a\gamma\gamma}B_{0}m_{a}a_{0}\left(l\min\left[1,\frac{f_{a}}{(f_{a})^{c}_{\oplus}}\right]+\frac{l^{2}}{R_{\oplus}}\min\left[1,\left(\frac{(f_{a})^{c}_{\oplus}}{f_{a}}\right)^{2}\right]\right)\ .

(34)

We emphasize that these approximate relations are robust for
kR_{\oplus}\lesssim 1
, but might break down for
kR_{\oplus}\gtrsim 1
.

The result is that
E_{1}(R_{\oplus})>B_{1}(R_{\oplus})
for sufficiently small values of
m_{a},~f_{a}
, as shown in Fig.

. Comparing with Fig.

\nabla a(R_{\oplus})
is affected, as well as in part of the blue region, where only

B_{1}(R_{\oplus})
is sensitive to

V.2.2
Experiments with AC EM fields

These experiments are typically operated in the regime where
\omega_{0}l\gtrsim 1

, where
\omega_{0}
is a resonant frequency of the apparatus
. This allows us to solve for the background fields
\boldsymbol{E}_{0}^{\rm AC}\sim\boldsymbol{j}/\omega_{0},~\boldsymbol{B}_{0}^{\rm AC}\sim\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\times\boldsymbol{j}/\omega_{0}
, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
is a unit vector enforcing orthogonality of
\boldsymbol{E}_{0},~\boldsymbol{B}_{0}
.

In the regime where an AC
EM
field is present, we can solve Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
) in Fourier space, accounting for the fact that these experiments often operate in the resonant regime, so that we must include an Ohmic loss operator that depends on the device quality factor. The solution for
\boldsymbol{E}_{1},~\boldsymbol{B}_{1}
is obtained by decomposing each of these into a sum over eigenmodes, labeled by ‘
n
’. We find

\displaystyle\left(k_{n}^{2}-i\frac{k_{n}\omega}{Q_{n}}-\omega^{2}\right)\boldsymbol{E}_{1,n}\simeq-g_{a\gamma\gamma}(i\omega)E_{0}\left(\hat{E}_{0}\times\nabla a_{\omega-\omega_{0}}-im_{a}a_{\omega-\omega_{0}}(\hat{k}_{0}\times\hat{E}_{0})\right)\ ,

(36)

where
a_{\omega-\omega_{0}}
indicates that the Fourier transform of the axion field should be evaluated at the frequency
\omega-\omega_{0}
. Evidently this will have the most support if
\omega-\omega_{0}\sim m_{a}
for
DM
axions.
The quantity
\hat{E}_{0}
is the orientation of the background
E
-field to zeroth order in
g_{a\gamma\gamma}
,
\hat{k}_{0}
is the orientation of the background
E
-field propagation, and
Q_{n}
is the quality factor of a given mode. This result assumes that the background
EM
field only has support at an angular frequency
\omega=\omega_{0}
, which is generally not the case due to oscillator phase noise
[
114
,
116
]
.

From Eq. (
36
) we can see that the axion gradient contribution (first RHS term) will dominate over the usual time-derivative contribution (second RHS term) in the same region where
\boldsymbol{E}_{1}
dominates over
\boldsymbol{B}_{1}
for a DC MQS experiment. The reason is that heterodyne experiments operate in the radiation regime where
\boldsymbol{E}_{0,1}\sim\boldsymbol{B}_{0,1}
. Therefore, the comparison in the source terms for both
\boldsymbol{E}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{1}
is made between
\nabla a(R_{\oplus})
and
\partial_{t}a(R_{\oplus})
, as can be seen in Eqs. (
29
), (
30
). This is the same comparison we found for DC MQS experiments with zero background electric field, except with
\nabla a(R_{\oplus})
and
\partial_{t}a(R_{\oplus})
as sources for
\boldsymbol{E}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{1}
respectively.

There is another key difference between heterodyne and MQS experiments, which is the readout. Experiments in the MQS limit are usually
B
-field optimised, and are therefore not necessarily sensitive to the increased
E
-field due to the enhanced axion gradient. Heterodyne experiments operate in a regime where they can be equally sensitive to
E
or
B
fields. However, it should be noted that the axion gradient-enhanced sensitivity will only exist as long as the overlap between the signal mode
\boldsymbol{E}_{1,n}
and
\hat{E}_{0}\times\hat{r}_{\oplus}
is non-zero. The unit vector
\hat{r}_{\oplus}
points in the radial direction in a coordinate system centered on Earth, while
\hat{E}_{0}
is in an unspecified coordinate system.

Full evaluation of the possible enhancement of low-mass axion signals for heterodyne experiments is beyond the scope of this paper. As in the case of DC MQS experiments above,
E_{1}
for heterodyne experiments is proportional to the same combination as
B_{1}
in Eq. (
35
) above, so our statements regarding reinterpretation of
g_{a\gamma\gamma}^{0}
in terms of
C_{\gamma}^{\oplus}
hold if
\hat{E}_{0}\times\hat{r}_{\oplus}=0
. Meanwhile, if
\hat{E}_{0}\times\hat{r}_{\oplus}\neq 0
, the enhanced gradient contribution to
E_{1}
affects the expected sensitivity. We show some results in Appendix
F
.

Unfortunately, no existing AC
EM
field experiment targets this parameter space, so there are no datasets to be re-analysed in light of our findings. However, proposed AC
EM
field experiments can in principle target the relevant parameter space, and a dedicated analysis of the impact of our findings for each one should be conducted.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we consider a class of ultralight, sub-eV, spin-0
DM
models with quadratic couplings to matter. It consists of a number of well motivated models (see
[
42
]
for a recent discussion).
Particularly interesting is the case of the QCD axion, which has an unavoidable quadratic interaction with nuclear matter, arising from its defining coupling
(a/f_{a})G\tilde{G}
.
In this work, we studied the intricate effects that Earth’s presence has on the axion
DM
field. A key element of our analysis is the inclusion of finite momentum for the
DM
solution far away from the source. We have assumed that the axion
DM
is virialized, and thus its momentum follows the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution.
We have not included in our treatment the presence of gravitational interactions that could lead to focusing
[
122
]
or a formation of Solar/Earth halos
[
50
,
48
]
, which might significantly alter the kinematic distribution of
DM
.
We also de facto approximated the velocity distribution of axions to be spherically symmetric; however, as discussed in Appendix
E
, this may wash away some non-negligible effects.

Our main findings are that for axion masses roughly above
10^{-14}\,
eV (in the blue region of Fig.
6
), the amplitude of the axion field on Earth could be significantly suppressed relative to the canonical value of
\langle a(t)^{2}\rangle=\rho_{\text{DM}}/m_{a}^{2}
. We find that typically
a(R_{\oplus})\leq a_{0}
in the blue region of Fig.
6
, with important consequences for experiments conducted on Earth.
Furthermore, for masses below roughly
10^{-11}\,
eV (the pure green region of Fig.
6
), the radial component of the spatial gradient of the axion field can be greatly enhanced compared to its canonical value.

Our findings have important implications for theories where
DM
contains both quadratic and linear interactions. This is the case of the celebrated QCD axion, but also other axion-like theories with moderate CP violation, such as some relaxion-models
[
12
]
.
For instance, experiments searching for low-mass axions via the photon interaction in a DC background field (e.g.,
[
112
,
111
,
113
]
) may benefit from measuring the axion-induced electric field, which is enhanced. Finally, experiments operating with comparable background electric and magnetic fields, such as heterodyne detectors
[
114
,
116
,
117
]
, can also benefit from the enhancement of the axion DM gradient.
One particularly exciting possibility would be, for instance, in the case where a signal would appear both in magnetic-gradient-based experiments, sensitive to the linear coupling, as well as in nuclear clock comparisons, which are sensitive to the quadratic coupling. The comparison between the two would enable us to study directly the above matter effects.

Let us briefly discuss a few limitations of this work, and potential directions for future studies.
Within this work, we adopted a framework where Earth is spherically symmetric and perfectly uniform.
This simplification is valid when the axion’s momentum
k
and the Earth-induced axion mass reduction
{\delta m}
are small compared to
1/R_{\oplus}
, but unreliable beyond it.
Imperfections of Earth are likely to broaden, shift, mix, or otherwise modify some of the resonances found in this work. For example, for
kR_{\oplus}\ll 1,{\delta m}R_{\oplus}\gg 1
we ignore resonances due to their sparseness, but should resonances be broadened, it is not obvious their contribution is negligible. In addition, we did not account for the presence of other nearby celestial bodies (such as the Sun, Jupiter, or Moon).
We have also assumed that the system is static and focused on the steady-state solution. Thus, we completely ignored questions associated with what happens during the formation of Earth (or the Solar System) and how the
DM
field reaches its asymptotic value.

One highly relevant question which is not fully addressed by this work is whether or not canonical QCD axion models (e.g., the KSVZ
[
85
,
86
]
and DFSZ
[
87
,
88
]
models) are affected by this effect.
Within our calculations, we find that for such axions with a velocity close to
10^{-3}
, the correction to their momentum is much smaller than their vacuum-momentum. And yet, treating the potential perturbatively (see Appendix
E
) tells us the field phase is non-negligibly modified by the presence of Earth, and it is therefore conceivable that some effects are present (especially for an experiment located at the antipodal point of the
DM
average incoming velocity). Alternatively, should non-virially distributed
DM
be present, especially if the average momentum and its standard deviation are reduced, a larger fraction of the parameter space is affected.
We leave a dedicated exploration of this to future work.

Finally, while we have focused on terrestrial experiments and observables, it is interesting to consider other celestial bodies. For example, even for canonical QCD axion models, major changes in the field amplitude would be present in the vicinity of neutron stars, which may be important for bounds relying on the conversion of axions to photons in the magnetosphere of a neutron star
[
127
]
.
Whether or not this produces observable effects for white dwarfs is also an interesting question. As mentioned before, the presence of the Sun or Jupiter may also introduce interesting effects which may motivate sending highly accurate sensors to either be closer or further away from them.

In our analysis, we have given a careful assessment of matter effects on spin-0 wave-like dark matter, accounting for its momentum distribution in the process. Our results demonstrate that great care must be taken in such an analysis, as the results are sensitive to the EFT being used, the vacuum around which one expands, and the velocity distribution of dark matter. Nevertheless, we have demonstrated that matter and momentum both matter for correctly determining the optimal experimental strategy to search for dark matter on Earth.

Appendix A
Virial Averaging and the Signal Power Spectral Density

For a virialized
DM
halo, let us find the expectation value for the field’s power.
We find the virial averaged squared field amplitude is

\displaystyle\langle a^{2}\rangle=2\int d^{3}k\left|a_{\omega}\right|^{2}f_{k}\approx 2\left(\sqrt{\pi}{k}_{\rm vir}\right)^{-3}\int d^{3}k\left|a_{\omega}\right|^{2}e^{-\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{k_{\oplus}}\right|^{2}}{{k}_{\rm vir}^{2}}}\,,

(37)

with the normalization of
\int d^{3}kf_{k}=1
, and
k_{\oplus}\equiv m_{a}v_{\oplus}\approx 10^{-3}m_{a}
is the galactic velocity of the solar system.
The integral reads

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\langle a^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\left(\sqrt{\pi}{k}_{\rm vir}\right)^{-3}\int d^{3}k\left|a_{\omega}\right|^{2}e^{-\frac{\left(\left|\boldsymbol{k}\right|^{2}-2\left|k\right|\left|k_{\oplus}\right|\hat{k}\cdot\hat{k}_{\oplus}+\left|\boldsymbol{k_{\oplus}}\right|^{2}\right)}{{k}_{\rm vir}^{2}}}

\displaystyle=2\pi^{-1/2}{k}_{\rm vir}^{-3}\,\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\int\,dk\,k^{2}\,a_{\omega,\ell}a^{*}_{\omega,\ell^{\prime}}e^{-\frac{\left(k^{2}+k_{\oplus}^{2}\right)}{{k}_{\rm vir}^{2}}}\int\frac{d\Omega}{2\pi}\,P_{\ell}(\hat{r}\cdot\hat{k})\,P_{\ell^{\prime}}(\hat{r}\cdot\hat{k})\,e^{\frac{{2\left|k\right|\left|k_{\oplus}\right|\hat{k}\cdot\hat{k}_{\oplus}}}{{k}_{\rm vir}^{2}}}\,\,.

(38)

Looking at the angular part only, we get

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}dx\,P_{\ell}(x)P_{\ell^{\prime}}(x)e^{\frac{2kk_{\oplus}x\cos{\theta}}{{k}_{\rm vir}^{2}}}I_{0}\left(\frac{2kk_{\oplus}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sin{\theta}}{{k}_{\rm vir}^{2}}\right)\,\,,

(39)

where
x=\hat{r}\cdot\hat{k}
and
\sin\theta
is the angle associated to
\hat{k}_{\oplus}
.
We see that modes where
\ell
is far from
\ell^{\prime}
are suppressed when
k\sim k_{\rm vir}
.

For simplicity, in most of this work, we focus only on
\ell=\ell^{\prime}
terms, essentially taking
k_{\oplus}\to 0
. Another way to reach a similar result is averaging over
\hat{k}_{\oplus}\cdot{\hat{r}}
, essentially asking what is the average observable for a randomly distributed location on Earth. As demonstrated in appendix
E
, this simplification can lead to an artificial suppression of Earth’s effect.

Furthermore, should a measurement last longer than
1/|\omega_{k}-\omega_{k^{\prime}}|
, the measured power spectral density of the field would allow distinguishing
k
and
k^{\prime}
. We also neglect this effect, essentially treating measurements as if they have no frequency resolution and cannot determine the lineshape of the axion.

m=0
spherical harmonic as
a_{\omega}^{\rm in\,(out)}(r,\theta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a_{\omega,\ell}^{\rm in\,(out)}(r,\theta)
, where
a_{\omega,\ell}^{\rm in\,(out)}
are given in Eq. (
13
).
Notice
a_{\omega,\ell}
s are given by the ratios of the spherical Bessel Functions,
j_{\ell}
and/or
y_{\ell}
, of different coefficients, and can written in the form of
a_{\omega,\ell}\propto f(j_{\ell},y_{\ell})/g(j_{\ell},y_{\ell})
where
f
and
g
are some generic functions.
Thus, if
f(j_{\ell},y_{\ell})
does not diverge and
g(j_{\ell},y_{\ell})
has no zeros for any
\ell
, then
a_{\omega,\ell}
is finite.

First, let us discuss the numerators.
By construction, the solution inside the source is regular as
r\to 0
.
On the other hand, as
\{j_{\ell}(r),y_{\ell}(r)\}\to 1/r\times(\cos(r),\sin(r))
as
r\to\infty
, the numerator is regular for solution outside the source as well.
Note that, as
y_{\ell}(r)\propto r^{-(\ell+1)}
as
r\to 0
, there is an apparent divergence of
a^{\rm out}_{\omega}
.
However as
r\to 0
, one should use the interior solution, so this divergence is never relevant.
Therefore, all we now need to show is that the denominators for both the solutions are always non-zero for
k>0
.

First we notice that the denominators of the inside and the outside solutions differ by a multiplicative factor of
k
.
As we are considering the case of
k\neq 0
, the denominator of interest for both the solutions is
Q_{\ell}
, which we write explicitly as

Q_{\ell}=\Big(kj_{\ell}(qR)y_{\ell+1}(kR)-qj_{\ell+1}(qR)y_{\ell}(kR)\Big)+i\Big(qj_{\ell}(kR)j_{\ell+1}(qR)-kj_{\ell}(qR)j_{\ell+1}(kR)\Big)\,.

(40)

For
Q_{\ell}
to vanish, both its real and imaginary parts must simultaneously be zero.
We will start with the assumption that this condition is met, only to demonstrate that it leads to a contradiction.

Before we start the proof, let us mention a lemma
[
96
]
: if
J_{\nu}(x)=0
and
x>0
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, then
J_{\nu+m}(x)\neq 0
for all non-zero integers
m
. Since
j_{\ell}\propto J_{\ell+1/2},y_{m}\propto J_{-m-1/2}
for positive arguments and integer orders, all
y
s and
j
s have no common zeros with each other or themselves.

Now, as
\ell
is an integer, using the above lemma we obtain, for
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
to vanish if
y_{\ell+1}(kR)=0
, then
j_{\ell+1}(qR)
will have to vanish and vice-versa.
This leads to
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})=-kj_{\ell}(qR)j_{\ell+1}(kR)\neq 0
using the above lemma.
Thus when
Q_{\ell}=0
,
y_{\ell+1}(kR)\neq 0
.
For non-zero
y_{\ell+1}(kR)
, one can take
j_{\ell}(qR)=\tfrac{q}{k}j_{\ell+1}(qR)y_{\ell}(kR)/y_{\ell+1}(kR)
for
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
to vanish.
With that choice, we obtain
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})=-qj_{\ell+1}(qR)/(k^{2}R^{2}y_{\ell+1}(kR))

using the Bessel function identity of
j_{\ell}(x)y_{\ell+1}(x)-j_{\ell+1}(x)y_{\ell+1}(x)=-1/x^{2}
.
Thus,
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})=0
requires
j_{\ell+1}(qR)=0
which in turns contradicts the lemma as
j_{\ell+1}(qR)\propto j_{\ell}(qR)\neq 0
(obtained from
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})=0
).
Thus, we have reached a contradiction.
Therefore, no individual term,
a^{\rm in\,,out}_{\omega,\ell}(r,\theta)
, can be divergent, for
k>0
.

Let us now show the behavior for
k\to 0
.
Note that in that case
q=\sqrt{k^{2}+{\delta m}^{2}}\simeq{\delta m}
.
By expanding in small powers of
k
, we obtain

\!\!\!{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})=-\frac{(kR)^{-1-\ell}(2\ell)!/\ell!}{2^{\ell+1}(2\ell-1){\delta m}R^{2}}\Bigg[(4\ell-2)({\delta m}R)^{2}j_{\ell-1}({\delta m}R)-(1+2\ell)(kR)^{2}({\delta m}R)j_{\ell}({\delta m}R)\Bigg]+\mathcal{O}\Big((kR)^{3-\ell},(kR)^{1-\ell}\Big)\,,

(41)

and

{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})=-\frac{2^{\ell}(kR)^{\ell}j_{\ell}({\delta m}R)\,\ell!}{R\,(2\ell)!}+\mathcal{O}\Big((kR)^{\ell+2}\Big)\,.

(42)

Since
j_{\ell},j_{\ell-1}
do not share zeroes for finite arguments,
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
scales as
(kR)^{-1-\ell}
or
(kR)^{-\ell+1}
.
As,
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})\propto(kR)^{\ell}
,
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
dominates over the imaginary part of
Q_{\ell}
for
\ell\geq 2
in the
k\to 0
limit.
Thus,
a^{\rm in}_{\omega,\ell}(r,\theta)\propto 1/(kR\,Q_{\ell})
scales as either
(kR)^{\ell}
or
(kR)^{\ell-2}
, both of which are finite.

Note that,
a^{\rm out}_{\omega,\ell}
has terms in the form of
y_{\ell}(kr){\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})/Q_{\ell}
and
j_{\ell}(kr){\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})/Q_{\ell}
.
Generally, for
\ell\geq 2
,
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
dominates over
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})
, and
j_{\ell}
is converges to 0.
Therefore, in
a^{\rm out}_{\omega,\ell}
, the term proportional to
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})j_{\ell}(kR)/Q_{\ell}
is finite.
Similarly, as
y_{\ell}(kr)\propto(kr)^{-\ell-1}
, the term proportional to
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})y_{\ell}(kr)/Q_{\ell}
scales as
(kR)^{\ell}
or
(kR)^{\ell-2}
, leading to a finite contribution.
Conversely, either by applying the above scaling, or using the explicit expressions given in Eq. (
21
) and (
23
), we see that both
\ell=0,1
scale as
1/(kR)
on resonances.
This scaling is correct for the solution both inside and outside the dense object, as well as for the gradients.

B.2
Infinite series convergence

After proving the convergence of each
a^{\rm in\,,out}_{\omega,\ell}(r,\theta)
, we want to prove their infinite sum over
\ell
i.e.
a_{\omega}^{\rm in\,,out}(r,\theta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a_{\omega,\ell}^{\rm in\,,out}(r,\theta)
is convergent as well.
As none of the
\ell
terms are divergent for
k>0
, we may remove any finite number of them, and the new series converges if and only if the full series converges.

For a fixed
k,q,R,r
, let us show that the sum absolutely converges i.e.
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}|a_{\omega,\ell}^{\rm in\,,out}(r,\theta)|
is convergent.
An absolute convergence,
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}|a_{\omega,\ell}^{\rm in\,,out}(r,\theta)|
implies convergence of
\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a_{\omega,\ell}^{\rm in\,,out}(r,\theta)
in Banach space (see
e.g.

[
129
]
).
Let us assume we start our sum at some
\ell=L>0
, and as we examine the terms, we will find what
L
needs to be.
Therefore, we examine the following sum

\Sigma_{1}=\sum_{\ell=L}^{\infty}|P_{\ell}(\cos(\theta))c_{\ell}(k,q,r,R)|\,,

(43)

with
c_{\ell}(k,q,r,R)
is either
a^{\rm out}_{\omega,\ell}(r,\theta)/P_{\ell}(\cos(\theta))
or
a^{\rm in}_{\omega,\ell}(r,\theta)/P_{\ell}(\cos(\theta))
for
r\geq R
and
r<R
respectively.
Since
|P_{\ell}(\cos(\theta))|\leq 1
,
\Sigma_{1}
absolutely converges if

\Sigma_{2}=\sum_{\ell=L}^{\infty}|c_{\ell}(k,q,r,R)|

converges.

We will begin by proving convergence of
\Sigma_{2}
inside Earth i.e.
r<R
. For convenience, we define
{\tilde{j}_{\ell}},\,{\tilde{y}_{\ell}}
as,

j_{\ell}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\Gamma(3/2+\ell)}\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)^{\ell}\tilde{j}_{\ell}(x)\,,\,\,\,y_{\ell}(x)=-\frac{\Gamma(1/2+\ell)}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\tfrac{x}{2}\right)^{-\ell-1}\tilde{y}_{\ell}(x)\,.

(44)

Now we will proceed with the convergence of
\Sigma_{2}
proof with the assumption that asymptotically
\tilde{j}_{\ell},\,\tilde{y}_{\ell}
approach unity i.e. for
\ell\to\infty
,
\tilde{j}_{\ell},\,\tilde{y}_{\ell}\to 1
(
lemma (i)
).
We will prove
lemma (i)
later.

As
c_{\ell}=a_{\omega,\ell}/P_{\ell}(\cos(\theta))
, like earlier we can write it as the ratio of a numerator and a denominator.
For
a^{\rm in}_{\omega,\ell}
, the denominator is
kR^{2}\,Q_{\ell}
.
To show absolute convergence, we may always decrease from the denominator.
By removing the imaginary part of
Q_{\ell}
, we get,

|Q_{\ell}|\geq\left|{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})\right|=\tfrac{1}{kR^{2}}\left(\tfrac{q}{k}\right)^{\ell}\Big|\tfrac{(qR)^{2}}{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\tilde{j}_{\ell+1}(qR)\,\tilde{y}_{\ell}(kR)-\tilde{j}_{\ell}(qR)\,\tilde{y}_{\ell+1}(kR)\Big|\,.

(45)

We will later show in
lemma (i)
that both
\tilde{j}_{\ell},\tilde{y}_{\ell}
approach 1 for
\ell\to\infty
.

Assuming that, we take
L
such that, for any
\ell>L
,
1/2\leq\tilde{j}_{\ell},\tilde{y}_{\ell}\leq 2
.
With this choice of
L
, the second term in the absolute value in Eq. (
45
) is at least
1/4
.
Furthermore, by taking
L>4(qR)
, which is to make sure there is no accidental cancellation between the first and the second term, we get the first term would be at most
4/(4\cdot 4^{2})=1/16
.
Since
|a-b|\geq||a|-|b||\geq||a|_{\rm min}-|b|_{\rm max}|
for
|a|>|b|
, we obtain,

kR^{2}\,|Q_{\ell}|\geq\left(\tfrac{q}{k}\right)^{\ell}\left|\frac{1}{16}-\frac{1}{4}\right|=\tfrac{3}{16}\left(\tfrac{q}{k}\right)^{\ell}.

(46)

For the inside solution, the numerator is
|i^{\ell}(1+2\ell)j_{\ell}(qr)|
.
Combining
lemma (i)
, and the choice of
L
, we find that the term in the sum is

|c_{\ell}(k,q,r,R)|_{r<R}\leq\frac{2^{5-\ell}(kr)^{\ell}\sqrt{\pi}}{3\Gamma(1/2+\ell)},

(47)

which converges for all
k,r
. Note that we have implicitly assumed that
r>0
; though for
r=0
only
\ell=0
is non-zero, and thus
c_{\ell}
is regular.

We now need to show that the series converges also for the outside solution i.e. for
r\geq R
as well.
While we can use the same lower bound for
Q_{\ell}
, we need to calculate the upper bound for the numerator.
It includes the summation of different
\tilde{y}
s and
\tilde{j}
s, and some of them are with relative signs to each other.
However, using triangle inequality,
|a_{1}+...+a_{n}|\leq|a_{1}|+...+|a_{n}|
, we simply sum the absolute values of individual terms and find an upper of that.
Like before, we choose an appropriate
L
so that each
{\tilde{j}}_{\ell},{\tilde{y}}_{\ell}\leq 2
for any
\ell>L
.
This gives us the following upper bound

|c_{\ell}(k,q,r,R)|_{r\geq R}\leq\frac{2^{5-\ell}k^{l}r^{-\ell-1}\sqrt{\pi}\left((k^{2}+q^{2})R^{3+2\ell}+r^{1+2\ell}(3+4\ell(2+\ell)+(qR)^{2})\right)}{3\Gamma(5/2+\ell)}\,,

(48)

which once again has an infinite convergence radius, due to the
1/\Gamma(5/2+\ell)
in large
\ell
limit.

Now we are left with proving our
lemma (i)
which is to show that for
\ell\to\infty
,
\tilde{j}_{\ell},\,\tilde{y}_{\ell}\to 1
.
Let there be
\epsilon>0
. We need to show that there exists an
L
, for which for any
\ell>L
,
|\tilde{j}_{\ell}(x)-1|<\epsilon
and
|\tilde{y}_{\ell}(x)-1|<\epsilon
.
Note that, for us
x>0
, and it only asymptotically approaches to
0
.
Let us start by proving this for
\tilde{j}_{\ell}
.
Starting from the infinite series expansion for the Spherical Bessel functions of the first kind
j_{\ell}(x)
which has infinite convergence radius, and using the definition of
\tilde{j}_{\ell}
, we obtain,

|\tilde{j}_{\ell}(x)-1|=\Bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}+\ell)(-1)^{k}(\frac{x}{2})^{2k}}{k!\,\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}+\ell+k)}\Bigg|\leq\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}+\ell)}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}+\ell+1)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\frac{x}{2})^{2k}}{k!}=\frac{2\,e^{x^{2}/4}}{3+2\ell}<\frac{e^{x^{2}/4}}{L}\,.

(49)

Thus by choosing
L>e^{x^{2}/4}/\epsilon
, we find that indeed
|\tilde{j}_{\ell}(x)-1|<\epsilon
.
Note that in practice, already for
\ell\sim x
, the approximation (
\tilde{j}_{\ell}(x)\sim 1
) works, so usually one only needs to examine
\ell
s up to
qR
in relation to the formulas discussed in the main text.
Similarly, to prove
lemma (i)
for
\tilde{y}_{\ell}
, we use similar series expansion and obtain

|\tilde{y}_{\ell}(x)-1|=\Bigg|\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}-k+\ell)(\frac{x}{2})^{2k}}{k!\,\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+\ell)}\Bigg|\leq\frac{\Gamma(\ell-\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(\ell+\frac{1}{2})}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\frac{x}{2})^{2k}}{k!}=\frac{2\,e^{x^{2}/4}}{2\ell-1}<\frac{e^{x^{2}/4}}{L}\,.

(50)

Thus, by choosing
L>e^{x^{2}/4}/\epsilon
, we obtain
|\tilde{y}_{\ell}(x)-1|<\epsilon
.
Note that in the above proof we also that for any integer
n
,
(-1)^{n}\Gamma(-n+1/2)\Gamma(n+1/2)=\pi
.

B.3
Convergence of the virally averaged integral

In this subsection, we address the finite nature of the virial averaged integrals i.e.

\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,k^{2}|a_{\omega,\ell}|^{2}
is finite even for arbitrarily small and arbitrarily large
{k}_{\rm vir}
.
As the standard halo model of
DM
, does not have arbitrarily large momenta, we only discuss issues related to the
k\to 0
limit.

We have shown in previous two subsections that for a small enough
k
, all
\ell\geq 2
modes are bounded by non-negative powers of
k
.
Hence, their value for
k\to 0
is easily analytically continued, and they are within
L_{2}
space.
On the other hand, the
\ell=0,1
modes diverge at most as
k^{-1}
.
Thus the virial averaged integrals scale as

\propto\int d^{3}k\,\,1/k^{2}\propto k
, and gives a finite result.

Appendix C
Resonant Contributions

In this appendix, we clarify the intention behind the terminology of “resonances”, which correspond to the observed drastic enhancements of the axion field value within a narrow band in momentum space (see Fig.
2
for instance).

To begin with, it is useful to invoke the asymptotic behavior of spherical Bessel functions to understand where and how often resonances will occur.
In order to obtain a sharp resonance, it is necessary that
i)

{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
and
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})
take different typical values within some region of
k
values and that
ii)
, within that
k
-region, the hierarchy between
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
and
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})
is flipped in a small
k
-subregion (or at least in isolated small
k
-subregions).
The primary difference between
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
and
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})
is that the former depends on
y_{\ell}(kR),y_{\ell+1}(kR)
, while the latter depends on
j_{\ell}(kR),j_{\ell+1}(kR)
.
The region wherein these functions take different typical values is where
kR\lesssim\ell_{+}
, with
\ell_{+}\equiv\ell+1/2
, where generically
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
is much bigger than
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})
. A resonance is thus found in the (isolated) sub-regions of
kR\lesssim\ell_{+}
where the hierarchy between
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
and
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})
is flipped. Given its functional form, this really means that
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
vanishes somewhere in these subregions. That this can happen imposes a further constraint on
qR
. Indeed, when
qR\lesssim\ell_{+}
,
\left|j_{\ell}(qR)\right|
decrease with
\ell
, while the opposite holds for
\left|y_{\ell}(qR)\right|
. Furthermore,
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})=0\iff j_{\ell}(qR)y_{\ell+1}(kR)=j_{\ell+1}(qR)y_{\ell}(kR)
. Therefore,
kR,qR\lesssim\ell_{+}
would make the left-handside much larger than the right-hanside, and the equality could not be fulfilled. Putting all this together, we find that the resonances only occur for
kR\lesssim\ell_{+}\lesssim qR
(or possibly for the subregion of
kR\ll\ell_{+}\ll qR
).

We thus now define
k_{\rm res}
as a momentum for which
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})=0
.
In principle, there could be multiple such
k_{\rm res}
values, for a given
\ell
.
Furthermore, we define the associated
q_{\rm res}
as the momentum inside Earth for this
k_{\rm res}
value.
Our goal is to estimate the size of the field, for
k=k_{\rm res}+{{\delta k}}
, where
{{\delta k}}
is small enough to fulfill a condition that will be discussed later. Expanding
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})
and
{\rm Im}(Q_{\ell})
at leading order in
\delta k
in the region
kR\lesssim\ell_{+}\lesssim qR
, we find

Q_{\ell}\approx-\frac{k_{\rm res}R{{\delta m}}^{2}j_{\ell}(q_{\rm res}R)y_{\ell-1}(k_{\rm res}R)y_{\ell+1}(k_{\rm res}R)}{q_{\rm res}^{2}y_{\ell}(k_{\rm res}R)}{{\delta k}}-i\frac{j_{\ell}(q_{\rm res}R)}{y_{\ell}({k_{\rm res}R})k_{\rm res}R^{2}},

(51)

where we used several Bessel identities, and the fact that
{\rm Re}(Q_{\ell})|_{k=k_{\rm res}}=0
. From the above, we can identify the width of the resonance,
\frac{2q_{\rm res}^{2}}{k_{\rm res}^{2}R^{3}\delta m^{2}\left|y_{\ell-1}(k_{\rm res}R)y_{\ell+1}(k_{\rm res}R)\right|}
, which scales like
\ell^{-2\ell}\left(k_{\rm res}R\right)^{2\ell}
.

Using the above, we can estimate the contribution of a given resonance,

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d\Omega_{k}\int d{{\delta k}}\,|a_{\omega,\ell}(R,\theta)|^{2}=\frac{a_{0}^{2}}{R}\left|\frac{(q_{\rm res}R)^{2}(1+2\ell)\pi y_{\ell}(k_{\rm res}R)^{2}}{(k_{\rm res}R)^{2}({{\delta m}}R)^{2}y_{\ell-1}(k_{\rm res}R)y_{\ell+1}(k_{\rm res}R)}\right|\,,

(52)

where we took the integral over
d{{\delta k}}
to be from
-\infty
to
\infty
, since resonant contribution is dominant, and the regions far from it contribute negligibly in the lorentzian approximation.

Within the regime of interest, the ratio
\tfrac{y_{\ell}(kR)^{2}}{y_{\ell-1}(kR)y_{\ell+1}(kR)}

is roughly one.
Therefore, if we take the Boltzmann distribution of axion momenta to be roughly
\pi^{-3/2}k_{\rm vir}^{-3}e^{-(|\mathbf{k}|/k_{\rm vir})^{2}}
, we get

\left.\int d^{3}kf_{\rm MB}(k)|a(r,\theta)|^{2}\right|_{\rm resonances}=\sum_{\ell,k_{\rm res}}\frac{8\sqrt{\pi}a_{0}^{2}e^{-k_{\rm res}^{2}/k_{\rm vir}^{2}}q_{\rm res}^{2}(1+2\ell)}{(k_{\rm vir}R)^{3}{{\delta m}}^{2}}\ .

(53)

Interestingly, the above implies that for
k_{\rm vir}R\ll 1
, a single resonance can have a major effect. (We do note that within the perfectly uniform spherical Earth approximation, when
k_{\rm vir}R\ll 1
, a fine-tuning of
f_{a}
to other parameters with an accuracy of
({k}_{\rm vir}R)^{2}
is required for a resonance to exist within the range of axion momenta spanned by the momentum integral.) Conversely, if we take
k_{\rm vir}R\gg 1
, no single resonance can contribute an amount
\gg 1
, unless
{\delta m}R>({k}_{\rm vir}R)^{3}
. To see this, we note that
(1+2\ell)
is at most of the order of
2q_{\rm res}R
(since otherwise there are no resonances). Therefore, the result is at most of the order of
\sim e^{-k_{\rm res}^{2}/{k}_{\rm vir}^{2}}q_{\rm res}^{3}/({k}_{\rm vir}^{3}R^{2}{{\delta m}}^{2})
. For any
k_{\rm res}
, the expression
e^{-k_{\rm res}^{2}/{k}_{\rm vir}^{2}}q_{\rm res}^{3}=e^{-k_{\rm res}^{2}/{k}_{\rm vir}^{2}}(k_{\rm res}^{2}+{\delta m}^{2})^{3/2}
is at most of the order
({k}_{\rm vir}^{2}+{\delta m}^{2})^{3/2}
, so we can use that as a rough upper bound. Therefore, we remain with an expression that is at most of the order of
({k}_{\rm vir}^{2}+{\delta m}^{2})^{3/2}/({k}_{\rm vir}^{3}R^{2}{\delta m}^{2})
. When
{\delta m}\lesssim{k}_{\rm vir}
, that expression is roughly
1/({\delta m}R)^{2}
, which is a small number.
Conversely, when
{\delta m}\gtrsim{k}_{\rm vir}
, it is roughly
{\delta m}/({k}_{\rm vir}^{3}R^{2})
.
This leads to our original conclusion.

Finally, it may be tempting to estimate the total number of resonances. However, the narrow width of the resonances, and their extreme sharp dependence on
{\delta m}R
, implies our calculations are not robust against minuscule deviations from the uniform, static, spherically symmetric Earth approximation. Hence, despite being able to make some estimates using the asymptotic behaviors of spherical Bessel functions
[]
, we do not provide it here. We only note that for
{k}_{\rm vir}R\ll 1
, the distance between two resonances is on average far larger than the range of
q
scanned by the virial integral. Hence, one does not expect to see resonances there, unless
{\delta m}R
is fine tuned.

Appendix D
Near-Threshold Bound States and Sommerfeld Enhancement

For all modes, we expect a resonant enhancement when there’s a significant overlap with a bound state of the potential. When the momentum is zero, an overlap with the threshold bound state can be large. However, for a given
{{\delta m}}^{2}
, not all
\ell
-modes might have a corresponding bound state at all. In fact, the first bound state appears when
{{\delta m}}\,R\geq\pi/2
, and will correspond to the
\ell=0
mode at the threshold.
Since higher-order
\ell
terms have an additional centrifugal potential compared to the
\ell=0
case, a deeper potential well is needed to yield a bound state for them.

The bound states are described by expressions similar to the ones encountered for the scattered wave (cp. Eq. (
17
)). They have an energy
\omega
which satisfies
m_{a}^{2}-{\delta m}^{2}\leq\omega^{2}\leq m_{a}^{2}
, hence the momentum outside is imaginary,
k_{\rm bound}^{2}=(i\kappa)^{2}
for real
\kappa
, while
q^{2}{}_{\rm bound}={\delta m}^{2}-\kappa^{2}\geq 0
. Inside Earth, we still have

\displaystyle a^{\rm in}_{\omega,\ell}\left(r,\theta\right)=b_{\ell}i^{\ell}\left(2\ell+1\right)P_{\ell}\left(\cos\theta\right)j_{\ell}\left(q{}_{\rm bound}r\right)\,,

(54)

though for bound states, the full solution does not have a sum over
\ell
(in fact each
\ell
bound state has a
2\ell+1
degeneracy, with
P_{\ell}(\cos(\theta))
replaced by the spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta,\phi)
).
Outside, the field should decay exponentially, which means that, it should be proportional to a spherical Hankel function of the first or second kind, according to whether
\kappa
is positive or negative, respectively. Choosing the former, we therefore find

\displaystyle a^{\rm out}_{\omega,\ell}\left(r,\theta\right)=c_{\ell}i^{\ell}\left(2\ell+1\right)P_{\ell}\left(\cos\theta\right)h_{\ell}\left(i\kappa r\right)\,.

(55)

Matching the field and its first derivative at the surface, one finds
\frac{b_{\ell}}{c_{\ell}}=\frac{h_{\ell}(i\kappa R)}{j_{\ell}(q{}_{\rm bound}R)}
and the bound state condition for a generic
\ell
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{{{\delta m}}^{2}-\kappa^{2}}\frac{j^{\prime}_{\ell}\left(R\sqrt{{{\delta m}}^{2}-\kappa^{2}}\right)}{j_{\ell}\left(R\sqrt{{{\delta m}}^{2}-\kappa^{2}}\right)}=i\kappa\frac{h^{\prime}_{\ell}\left(iR\kappa\right)}{h_{\ell}\left(iR\kappa\right)}\ .~

(56)

Focusing on near-threshold bound states, which have energy
\approx m_{a}
and
\kappa R\ll 1
, we obtain

i\kappa\frac{h^{\prime}_{\ell}\left(iR\kappa\right)}{h_{\ell}\left(iR\kappa\right)}\rightarrow-\frac{\left(\ell+1\right)}{R}
, and the bound state exists whenever

Following the discussion in Section

In the presence of the axion-photon interaction, Maxwell’s equations are modified and read

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=\rho-g_{a\gamma\gamma}\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\nabla a\ ,

(64)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=0\ ,

(65)

\displaystyle=0\ ,

(66)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{j}-g_{a\gamma\gamma}\left(\boldsymbol{E}\times\nabla a-\partial_{t}a\,\boldsymbol{B}\right)\ .

(67)

It is useful to write these as second-order differential equations. Furthermore, we can begin with the zeroth order equations,

\displaystyle(\nabla^{2}-\partial_{t}^{2})\boldsymbol{E}_{0}
