Gastric duplication cyst mimicking an adrenal-region cyst: a diagnostic challenge
Authors: Tong Yang, Wei Huang, Yanming Wang, Xinsheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Gastric duplication cysts (GDCs) are rare congenital malformations that may mimic adrenal or retroperitoneal cysts. Misdiagnosis can delay proper management. We report a unique case of a GDC that was misdiagnosed as a left adrenal cyst, with reversible hyperreninemia and indirect hyperbilirubinemia.Presentation of case:A 28-year-old asymptomatic man was incidentally found to have a left adrenal cyst on ultrasound. CT revealed a 4.1 × 5.5 cm non-enhancing unilocular cyst with preserved adrenal structure. Laboratory tests showed elevated upright plasma renin concentration (63.3 pg/mL) and indirect bilirubin (57.6 μmol/L). During laparoscopic excision, a cyst arising from the gastric fundus was identified. Pathology confirmed a non-communicating GDC lined by gastric mucosa and smooth muscle. Three months postoperatively, renin and bilirubin levels normalized.Clinical discussion:Ectopic GDCs in the adrenal region are extremely rare and often misinterpreted radiologically. The postoperative normalization of renin and bilirubin raises a possible association, but the mechanisms remain speculative, and causality cannot be established from this single case. Intraoperative cyst-fluid analysis and multidisciplinary evaluation assist in accurate diagnosis. Complete resection provided definitive diagnosis and a favorable short-term outcome in the present case; however, longer follow-up is required to assess recurrence and sustained biochemical remission.Conclusion:GDC should be considered in adrenal-region cystic lesions with atypical features. Total excision provides both definitive diagnosis and a favorable short-term outcome, although longer surveillance is required.

Introduction

Gastric duplication (GD) is a rare congenital anomaly with an incidence of 17 cases per million population[1]. GD manifests as cystic or tubular structures that are anatomically connected to the gastric wall, whose cyst wall is fused to the stomach’s smooth muscle layer. Histologically, these lesions consist of a gastrointestinal mucosal epithelial lining, an outer smooth muscle layer, and a blood supply shared with the stomach. The exact cause of GDCs is not yet fully understood, but recent research indicates they may develop from abnormal separation of the foregut during embryonic development, involving similar molecular pathways (such as disrupted SHH signaling) as seen in intestinal duplication anomalies[3]. In children, GDCs typically present with symptoms like abdominal pain, vomiting, a noticeable abdominal mass, feeding difficulties, and poor weight gain[4]. Adults with GDCs may experience no symptoms at all or have vague complaints such as epigastric pain (which can sometimes radiate to the back), feeling full quickly, difficulty swallowing, and nausea[5]. A rare case of asymptomatic GDC with an isolated large gastric cyst was reported in a 21-year-old male patient[6]. In some cases, GDCs in adults can press on nearby organs like the pancreas or adrenal glands, leading to additional symptoms related to those structures[7]. Unlike most previously reported GDCs, which are located along the gastric wall or pancreas, the present case was uniquely situated in the adrenal region and initially misdiagnosed as an adrenal cyst. Furthermore, the concomitant elevation of plasma renin and indirect bilirubin has not been documented in prior reports. This rare presentation underscores the diagnostic complexity of ectopic GDCs and the importance of multidisciplinary evaluation in atypical cystic lesions near the adrenal gland.

Surgical removal is necessary for all symptomatic GDCs[8]. Prophylactic excision is recommended for asymptomatic cases with high-risk features (e.g., ectopic acid secretion, malignant potential)[9]. Treatment options include laparoscopic and endoscopic techniques, with recent studies highlighting the safety and effectiveness of robot-assisted laparoscopic surgery for pediatric GDCs[10]. According to current surgical standards, complete excision remains the treatment of choice for all symptomatic or potentially malignant GDCs, consistent with recent consensus reports and evidence-based medicine[11], which emphasize comprehensive preoperative evaluation, adherence to evidence-based case reporting, and multidisciplinary collaboration for optimal outcomes.

This case report aims to describe a rare and unusual presentation of a GDC located near the adrenal gland, in which elevated renin and indirect bilirubin levels were observed preoperatively and normalized during short-term postoperative follow-up. By integrating the existing literature, we cautiously explore possible mechanisms and discuss relevant diagnostic and therapeutic considerations, while interpreting postoperative biochemical normalization as a temporal association rather than definitive causality. This case report is reported in accordance with the SCARE checklist[12].

Case presentation

Patient profile

A 28-year-old previously healthy man was found to have an incidental left adrenal mass during a routine ultrasound examination, although he reported no abdominal pain or hormonal symptoms.

Clinical evaluation

Imaging: A five-phase adrenal CT scan revealed a unilocular cystic lesion measuring 4.1 × 5.5 cm (19 HU) in the left adrenal region, without septations or contrast enhancement (Fig. 1). Laboratory findings: The upright plasma renin level was elevated (63.313 pg/mL; reference range: 3.8–38.8), while the supine plasma renin level was within normal limits (22.583 pg/mL; reference range: 2.4–32.8). Adrenal-related hormones, including cortisol, aldosterone, and catecholamines, were normal. Total bilirubin was 60.23 μmol/L (reference range: 3.42–20.5), with indirect bilirubin elevated to 57.63 μmol/L (reference range: 0.0–20.5).

Surgical and pathological findings

The patient was placed in the supine position under general anesthesia, and a transperitoneal laparoscopic approach was performed. Intraoperatively, the cystic lesion was identified in the left adrenal region but was found to originate from the gastric fundus rather than the adrenal gland (Fig. 2). The adrenal gland was clearly identified and preserved. The operative time was approximately 190 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 20 mL. After specimen retrieval, incision of the cyst revealed thick milky-white, viscous fluid. The cyst measured approximately 4 cm in diameter, with an intact capsule and well-defined margins (Fig. 3A). Figure 3B displays histological features visualized by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Pathological examination demonstrated a cystic wall (4 × 3 × 1 cm, wall thickness of 0.3–0.4 cm) lined by gastric mucosa with underlying smooth muscle layers. Focal epithelial denudation with histiocytic and inflammatory infiltrates was observed, consistent with a non-communicating gastric duplication malformation. Postoperative follow-up

The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged on postoperative day 4. At the 3-month follow-up, upright plasma renin had decreased to 22.751 pg/mL and indirect bilirubin to 16.27 μmol/L, with both returning to within normal ranges, and there was no evidence of recurrence on follow-up CT imaging. The patient reported anxiety when the lesion was initially suspected to be adrenal in origin and later expressed relief and satisfaction after adrenal preservation and pathological confirmation of a benign GDC.

Differential diagnoses for adrenal-region cystic lesions include GDCs, cystic lymphangiomas, adrenal cysts, cystic gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), pancreatic pseudocysts, and retroperitoneal cysts. Radiologically, adrenal cysts typically lack continuity with the gastric wall, while GISTs may show enhancement or solid components. Pancreatic pseudocysts are often associated with a history of pancreatitis. Lymphangiomas tend to be multilocular. In this particular case, the diagnosis was confirmed through intraoperative findings of adhesion to the gastric fundus and postoperative pathology. Additional diagnostic tools such as intraoperative cyst-fluid pH testing and biochemical analysis may also prove useful[21]. Although intraoperative cyst-fluid pH testing may aid diagnosis, it was not performed in this case due to intraoperative decision-making prioritizing complete and safe excision.

Pathophysiological mechanisms

The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying hyperreninemia in this case remain uncertain. Although cyst-related compression of the renal vasculature has been proposed as a potential mechanism for activation of the renin–angiotensin system, no direct evidence of vascular compression was observed in the present case, based on imaging and intraoperative findings. Alternative explanations may include local inflammatory responses or neurohumoral activation induced by the cyst, which could indirectly influence renin secretion. Subtle local hemodynamic changes cannot be excluded, but were not demonstrable in this case.

Regarding hyperbilirubinemia, it may be related to inflammatory or metabolic effects associated with the cyst; however, the exact mechanism remains unclear. Although other causes of unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia, including Gilbert syndrome, should be considered, the normalization of bilirubin levels after surgical excision suggests that this case is less likely to be consistent with Gilbert syndrome.

Importantly, the normalization of both renin and bilirubin levels following surgical excision is consistent with a possible temporal association between the cyst and these biochemical abnormalities, although a causal relationship cannot be definitively established.

Treatment and prognosis

Complete surgical excision is generally considered the preferred treatment, with one reported case of malignant transformation in duplicated gastric cysts[26]. Intraoperative aspiration of cystic fluid is avoided due to the potential risk of tumor dissemination, particularly when malignant cystic lesions cannot be excluded. Therefore, en bloc resection is preferred to ensure both diagnostic accuracy and oncologic safety. The strengths of this case lie in its rare presentation of a GDC in the adrenal region, a site that frequently leads to diagnostic confusion with adrenal or retroperitoneal cysts. Additionally, the patient’s preoperative biochemical abnormalities returned to normal 3 months after cyst excision, representing a noteworthy temporal observation rather than evidence of causality. However, the causal relationship between these biochemical improvements and the operation remains uncertain. Postoperative normalization of renin and bilirubin levels may have been influenced by other physiological or metabolic factors unrelated to the surgery itself. Therefore, while the temporal association is noteworthy, further evidence is required to clarify whether the biochemical recovery was directly attributable to cyst removal. Additionally, the relatively short follow-up duration (3 months) limits the assessment of long-term outcomes and recurrence, warranting further evaluation in future studies.

In conclusion, GDCs presenting as adrenal cysts are rare and pose significant diagnostic challenges due to their overlapping clinical and radiological features with adrenal cysts or retroperitoneal cystic lesions. A definitive diagnosis typically requires intraoperative exploration and histopathological confirmation, which reveals characteristic findings such as a gastric mucosal lining and smooth muscle layers. Complete surgical resection, whether laparoscopic or open, remains the preferred treatment to prevent complications, with adrenal preservation being feasible unless severe adhesions are present. In more complex cases, endoscopic ultrasound-guided aspiration combined with sclerotherapy may offer a viable alternative[27]. Moving forward, efforts should focus on improving clinical recognition of GDCs by leveraging advanced diagnostic techniques such as endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and technetium-99 m scintigraphy for preoperative evaluation, as well as exploring minimally invasive endoscopic treatment options. This case highlights the critical need to consider GDCs in the differential diagnosis of adrenal cystic lesions, even when imaging findings are atypical, to reduce the risk of misdiagnosis and ensure optimal patient management. Long-term surveillance is required to assess recurrence and sustained biochemical remission.
