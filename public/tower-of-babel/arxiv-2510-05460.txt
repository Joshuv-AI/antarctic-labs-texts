Fair metric distortion for matching with preferences
Jabari Hastings, Prasanna Ramakrishnan

Abstract
We consider the matching problem in the metric distortion framework. There are n n agents and n n items occupying points in a shared metric space, and the goal is to design a matching mechanism that outputs a low-cost matching between the agents and items, using only agents’ ordinal rankings of the candidates by distance. A mechanism has distortion α \alpha if it always outputs a matching whose cost is within a factor of α \alpha of the optimum, in every instance regardless of the metric space. Typically, the cost of a matching is measured in terms of the total distance between matched agents and items, but this measure can incentivize unfair outcomes where a handful of agents bear the brunt of the cost. With this in mind, we consider how the metric distortion problem changes when the cost is instead measured in terms of the maximum cost of any agent. We show that while these two notions of distortion can in general differ by a factor of n n , the distortion of a variant of the state-of-the-art mechanism, RepMatch , actually improves from O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) under the sum objective to O ⁡ ( n 1.58 ) O(n^{1.58}) under the max objective. We also show that for any fairness objective defined by a monotone symmetric norm, this algorithm guarantees distortion O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) .

Introduction

Matching is a central problem in social choice theory that underpins a wide array of resource allocation scenarios. Just within education, centralized systems match students to schools, dorms, and courses; faculty and TAs to teaching assignments; and courses to classrooms. While the quality of an outcome is often evaluated in terms of the participants’
cardinal
utilities (or costs) in the sense of von Neumann and Morgenstern
[
VNM07
]
, it can often be difficult for agents to quantify their utility and express cardinal preferences. In light of this, a variety of widely adopted mechanisms (such as
Random Serial Dictatorship
, the
Boston mechanism

[
AS03
]
, and
Deferred Acceptance
) operate based on
ordinal
preferences, which only require agents to compare one option against another.

Within social choice, one particularly fruitful approach towards reconciling the disconnect between cardinal utilities and ordinal preferences is the
distortion
framework, introduced by the seminal work of Procaccia and Rosenschein
[
PR06
]
(see
[
AFSV21
]
for a detailed survey). Under the this framework, a mechanism is evaluated in terms of how well it approximates the optimal outcome, in the worst case over all possible ordinal preferences, and all cardinal utilities consistent with these preferences. Perhaps unsurprisingly, every mechanism can be arbitrarily worse than the optimal solution without any limitations on the utilities. However, by adding relatively mild constraints, it becomes tractable to find mechanisms with strong, meaningful guarantees.

Along these lines, substantial research has focused on the
metric distortion
problem, originally introduced by Anshelevich, Bhardwaj, and Postl
[
ABP15
]
in the context of voting.
In this model, we imagine that the various voters and candidates occupy points in an unknown metric space, and each voter’s cost of a candidate is the distance between them (i.e., voters prefer candidates that are close). The objective is to find mechanisms (
voting rules
) that, using only each voter’s ordinal ranking of candidates by distance, always choose candidates whose
total
cost is only worse than the best possible by a small factor (called the
distortion
).
Though the metric distortion model was originally motivated by well-studied spatial models of voting
[
EH84
,
EH90
,
MG99
,
ABC+20
]
, the power of the model comes not from a conviction that preferences are truly represented by some metric, but rather from the worst-case guarantee that a low-distortion mechanism must find a solution that is near-optimal simultaneously in every metric space. Despite the challenging nature of this task, a number of voting rules can achieve distortion guarantees that are small constants, and a long line of work managed to pin down the optimal distortion of deterministic rules at just 3
[
ABP15
,
GKM17
,
MW19
,
Kem20a
,
GHS20
,
KK22
,
KK23
]
. Through this work, the metric distortion model has proven to be a powerful lens not just to gain a better understanding of existing rules (as in
[
ABP15
]
), but also to motivate the design of natural new voting rules (as in
[
MW19
,
KK22
,
KK23
,
CRWW24
]
). Metric distortion has even played a role as a kind of sandbox to understand the power of randomization
[
AP17
,
FGMP19
,
Kem20b
,
CR22
,
CRWW24
]
, and entirely different paradigms of voting and preference elicitation
[
GAX17
,
CDK17
,
CDK18
,
CSV22
,
GS24
,
GGM25
]
.

Even though matching is arguably just as ubiquitous a social choice problem as voting, it is significantly less understood within the metric distortion problem. In this setting, instead of voters and candidates we have
n
agents and
n
items, and rather than choosing a single winning candidate, the goal is to match the agents to items. Once again, agents’ costs of items are derived from a metric space. The question is whether matching mechanisms can, with only the agent’s ordinal preferences over items, choose a matching whose total cost is close to the minimum possible cost.

The initial work of Caragiannis et al.
[
CFRF+16
]
focused on the metric distortion of
Serial Dictatorship
mechanisms, which iterate over the agents and match them to their favorite unmatched item. They showed that with a deterministically chosen order, this mechanism can have distortion
2^{n}-1
, but with a uniformly random chosen order (
Random Serial Dictatorship
), its distortion is at most
O(n)
in expectation. This was followed by the work of Anari, Charikar, and Ramakrishnan
[
ACR23
]
, who designed a simple and novel deterministic matching mechanism with distortion at most
O(n^{2})
called
RepMatch
. They also showed that any mechanism, even with randomization, must have worst-case distortion at least
\Omega(\log n)
, dashing hopes of constant distortion mechanisms like in the voting setting. Despite the exponential gap between the best lower and upper bounds, further progress on the problem has been elusive.
A recent work of Filos-Ratsikas et al.
[
FRGL+25
]
has instead studied the distortion in simple metric spaces like the real line, but our understanding of the distortion in general metric spaces is still fairly limited.

While it remains reasonable to hope that resolving the metric distortion problem for matching will continue to lead to the discovery of natural new matching mechanisms, a fair critique is that this work takes for granted that the “optimal” choice is the one that minimizes the total social cost. For example, if the goal is to design a school choice mechanism such that students are matched to nearby schools, it would be difficult to argue that a matching that reduces a dozen students’ commute by 5 minutes but increases one student’s commute by an hour is just as good. From a fairness perspective, it might be more natural to aim to minimize the
egalitarian
social cost, which is the maximum cost incurred by any agent rather than the
utilitarian
social cost, which is the sum.

As it turns out, in the voting setting this criticism is definitively addressed by
[
GKM17
,
GHK18
]
.
Goel, Hulett, and Krishnaswamy
[
GHK18
]
show that any candidate that has distortion
\alpha
with respect to the utilitarian objective (simply,
sum-distortion

\alpha
) must also have distortion at most
\alpha+2
with respect to the egalitarian objective (
max-distortion

\alpha+2
). In fact, such a candidate has distortion at most
\alpha+2
with respect to any objective which is a monotone symmetric norm of the costs (called the
fairness ratio
). (Here, the
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{\infty}
norms would correspond to the utilitarian and egalitarian objectives respectively.)
As a result, guaranteeing low distortion with respect to the sum objective automatically ensures low distortion across a broad family of fairness objectives, each differing in how much emphasis it places on the worst-off agents versus the group as a whole.

Our central question is whether the same holds true in the matching setting.

Question 1
.

Do matching mechanisms with low sum-distortion also have low max-distortion and fairness ratio?

Our Contributions

In this work, we make the first steps towards answering
1

does not
imply low max distortion (
Proposition

2
) or vice versa (

Following along these lines, we consider the

. We analyze the max-distortion and fairness ratio of RepMatch with a minor modification to its tie-breaking procedure (detailed in

Naively, the link between distortion and the fairness ratio (given by

4

Theorem 1
.

1
guarantees a max-distortion
O(n^{1.58})
and fairness ratio

.

That is, we show that with a careful analysis, the distortion of RepMatch actually

Qualitatively, we view
Theorem

1
in an optimistic light. While it is not possible to simply lift a sum-distortion guarantee to a max-distortion or fairness ratio guarantee, it seems plausible that these objectives are still aligned with each other in some sense. We also view max-distortion as another avenue for finding new preferential matching mechanisms, with the hope that they will also satisfy strong sum-distortion and fairness ratio guarantees.

Finally, we complement these results with an exploration of lower bounds. We show that

can have sum-distortion (and therefore fairness ratio) as large as

, and max-distortion as large as

(

3
). We also show that every matching mechanism has max-distortion at least

(

), using constructions similar to the binary tree metrics introduced by
[

. While

(

1.2
Related work

The problem of matching under incomplete information was introduced by
[
HZ79
]
and has since been studied in a variety of contexts. Distortion in ordinal matching was first studied by

[
FRFZ14
]
. Instead of considering costs in a metric space, they consider
unit-sum
and
unit-range
valuations. Note that by focusing on valuations rather than costs, their setting becomes one of maximization.
[
FRFZ14
]
showed that the best distortion attainable by a randomized mechanism in their setting is
O(\sqrt{n})
and this is achieved by the Random Serial Dictatorship.

[
ABFRV22
]
later showed that for unit-sum valuations, distortion
\Theta(n^{2})
is the best possible for deterministic mechanisms.

Another interesting setting permits unrestricted valuation functions, but gives the mechanism the power to query a small number of agent valuations.

[
ABFRV22
]
showed that with
O(\lambda\log n)
queries per agent, it is possible to get distortion
O(n^{1/(\lambda+1)})
. Even with just two queries per agent,
[
ABFRV24
]
showed that one can get distortion
O(\sqrt{n})
.
[
ES25
]
ultimately showed that with
\lambda
queries per agent, a distortion of
O(n^{1/\lambda})
is attainable, which is optimal.

Our work is the first in the metric matching setting to study the fairness ratio within arbitrary metric spaces. We remark that the recent work of
[
FRGL+25
]
gives a mechanism with fairness ratio of 3 for line metrics, but the guarantees do not to extend to more general spaces.

Metric assumptions have also been applied to maximum matchings, as opposed to our minimization setting. Here, the best distortion bounds are constant;
[
AS16b
,
AS16a
]
gave a mechanism with distortion
1.6
and a truthful one that with distortion
1.76
(both of which are randomized). We also note that
[
AZ21
]
study minimum matchings in a simpler setting, where the locations of the agents are unknown, but the locations of the items are known.
With this additional information, they show that deterministic mechanisms can attain optimal distortion of
3
.

Preliminaries

Notation and terminology.

Throughout the paper we let
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
denote the set of agents, and
B=\{b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\}
denote the set of items, where
n\geq 2
is some integer. An
ordinal matching problem instance
, denoted by
\sigma
, is given by
n

preference lists
, one provided by each agent. Each preference list is a permutation of the items, given in non-decreasing order of distance. We say that agent
a_{i}

prefers
item
b_{j}

over
item
b_{k}
if the item
b_{j}
appears before
b_{k}
in her preference list. Agent
a_{i}^{\prime}s

favorite
item in a set
S\subseteq B
is the item in
S
that appears the earliest in her preference list.
Similarly, agent
a_{i}
’s favorite
t
items are the first
t
items in her preference list.

Metric spaces.

The distances between agents and items are conveyed by an unknown
pseudometric

d:(A\cup B)\times(A\cup B)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
By definition, the function
d
satisfies the following properties.

(i)

Identity of indiscernibles:
d(x,y)=0
if
x=y
,

(ii)

Symmetry:
d(x,y)=d(y,x)
,

(iii)

Triangle inequality:
d(x,y)\leq d(x,z)+d(z,y)
.

In certain definitions of metric spaces, it is required that different elements have positive distance (i.e.
d(x,y)=0
if and only if
x=y
), but we do not require this. We allow different agents or items to “occupy” the same point in the metric space, in which case their distance is
0
; however, if they are located at different points, they do indeed have positive distance between each other. We note that we can easily replace distances of
0
with sufficiently small
\varepsilon
’s without changing any results.

We say that a metric
d
is
consistent
with an instance
\sigma
if for every agent
a_{i}
, whenever that agent
a_{i}
prefers an item
b_{j}
over item
b_{k}
, then
d(a_{i},b_{j})\leq d(a_{i},b_{k})
. We let
\rho(\sigma)
denote the set of all metrics consistent with the problem instance.

Matchings.

We will use injective functions
M:A\to A\cup B
to represent matchings. For each agent
a_{i}
, we can have
M(a_{i})\in B
when the agent
a_{i}
is assigned an item, and
M(a_{i})=a_{i}
otherwise.
When
M
is a bijection between
A
and
B
, we say that it is a
perfect matching
.
For any set of agents
S\subseteq A
and matching
M
, we will let
M|_{S}:S\to A\cup B
denote the
restriction
of the matching
M
to the set
S
. We assume that
M|_{S}
is the identity map on the set
A\setminus S
.

Cost of Matchings.

Given a
norm

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, the cost of a matching
M
under a metric
d
is

\mathrm{cost}_{f}(M,d)\coloneqq f\left(d(a_{1},M(a_{1})),d(a_{2},M(a_{2})),\dots,d(a_{n},M(a_{n}))\right).

When the context is clear, we will simplify our notation and write
\mathrm{cost}_{f}(M)
instead of
\mathrm{cost}_{f}(M,d)

With a slight abuse of notation, we also define the top-
k
cost of a matching
M
to be the sum of the
k
largest distances between agents and their assigned items,

\mathrm{cost}_{k}(M,d)\coloneqq\max_{S\subseteq A,|S|=k}\sum_{a_{i}\in S}d(a_{i},M(a_{i})).

Notice that the functions
\mathrm{cost}_{1}(M,d),\mathrm{cost}_{n}(M,d)
compute the maximum distance and sum of distances respectively.

Distortion.

Given a norm
f
and metric
d
, the
f
-distortion
of a perfect matching
M
is the ratio of that matching’s cost compared to that of the optimal matching,

\mathrm{distortion}_{f}(M,d):=\frac{\mathrm{cost}_{f}(M,d)}{\min_{M^{*}}\mathrm{cost}_{f}(M^{*},d)}.

We will refer to the top-
1
distortion top-
n
distortion as the max-distortion and sum-distortion respectively.
The worst-case
f
-distortion of a matching is given by

\mathrm{distortion}_{f}(M,\sigma):=\sup_{d\in\rho(\sigma)}\mathrm{distortion}_{f}(M,d).

With a slight abuse of notation, the top-
k
distortion is defined in a similar fashion.

Fairness Ratio.

Borrowing the terminology introduced by
[
GHK18
]
, we also define the
fairness ratio
of a perfect matching
M
to be maximum worst-case top-
k
-distortion

\mathrm{fairness}(M,d):=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\mathrm{distortion}_{k}(M,d).

The worst-case fairness ratio of a matching is given by

\mathrm{fairness}(M,\sigma):=\sup_{d\in\rho(\sigma)}\mathrm{fairness}(M,d).

One of the main reasons for studying the fairness ratio is that it gives an approximation result for the class of monotone symmetric norms.

Proposition 1

(
[
GKM17
]
, Theorem 2.3)
.

If a matching
M
is such that
\mathrm{fairness}(M,\sigma)\leq\alpha
for some problem instance
\sigma
and some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, then for every monotone symmetric norm
f
,
\mathrm{distortion}_{f}(M,\sigma)\leq\alpha
.

Max-Distortion vs Sum-Distortion

In the voting setting,
[
GHK18
]
showed that in any metric that is consistent with a given problem instance, a mechanism’s max-distortion cannot exceed its sum-distortion by more than an additive constant
1
1

1

Note that
[
GHK18
]
also go on to prove the stronger result that for any
instance
, the difference between worst-case fairness ratio and worst-case sum-distortion is bounded by a constant.
. This is no longer the case in the matching setting. In particular, we can show that there are metrics in which a matching’s max-distortion and sum-distortion can differ by as much as a multiplicative factor of
n
.

Proposition 2
.

There exists a metric
d
and a perfect matching
M
such that

(n/2)\cdot\mathrm{distortion}_{n}(M,d)\leq\mathrm{distortion}_{1}(M,d).

Proposition 3
.

There exists a metric
d
and a perfect matching
M
such that

n\cdot\mathrm{distortion}_{1}(M,d)\leq\mathrm{distortion}_{n}(M,d).

Taken together,
Propositions

2
and
3

suggest that a bound on the sum-distortion may not automatically translate to a similar bound (up to constants) on the max-distortion and vice versa; a more careful analysis of the
worst-case
sum-distortion and max-distortion is likely needed.
Nevertheless, both quantities are somewhat useful for bounding the fairness ratio, as we show below.

Proposition 4
.

For any metric
d
and any perfect matching
M
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{fairness}(M,d)\leq n\cdot\min(\mathrm{distortion}_{1}(M,d),\mathrm{distortion}_{n}(M,d)).

Furthermore, for any problem instance
\sigma
and any perfect matching
M
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{fairness}(M,\sigma)\leq n\cdot\min(\mathrm{distortion}_{1}(M,\sigma),\mathrm{distortion}_{n}(M,\sigma)).

In the absence of a bound on the worst-case top-
k
-distortion, it can therefore be worthwhile to consider both the worst-case max-distortion and sum-distortion when analyzing the worst-case fairness ratio. For proofs of the above results, see
Appendix

A
.

4
Distortion of RepMatch

The RepMatch mechanism, proposed by
[
ACR23
]
, is a simple procedure that relies on the following intuitive premise. If two agents have similar favorite items, then they are likely to be close to each other in some sense, and thus a good assignment for one agent is also likely to be a good assignment for the other. The mechanism iteratively clusters
agents whose distance to each other is small, and uses the preferences of a single designated agent within the cluster, called the
representative
, as a proxy for the others’ preferences. If the representatives of two clusters overlap in their favorite items, then we can merge the clusters, and promote one of the representatives to become the representative of the merged cluster.
Once the representatives’ favorite items do not overlap, the mechanism arbitrarily matches each agent to one of their representative’s favorite items.

One design choice in the mechanism is in deciding which representative gets promoted when merging two clusters.
[
ACR23
]
do this by associating each cluster
S
with a parameter
\lambda
, and promoting the representative corresponding to the larger
\lambda
. Their analysis shows that the total cost of matching the agents in
S
to the top
|S|
choices of the representative is at most
|S|\cdot 2^{\lambda}
times their cost in the optimal matching, and at the same time
2^{\lambda}\leq|S|
, giving a bound of
O(n^{2})
for the sum-distortion.

We take a more direct approach, by simply promoting the representative whose cluster is larger. We show that this modification still retains the sum-distortion guarantee, but also allows the analysis to give improved results for the max-distortion and more generally top-
k
-distortion.

Below, we present the formal description of the mechanism.

Algorithm 1
.

RepMatch (with size-based promotion)

Initially, there are
n
singleton sets
S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{n}
, with
S_{i}=\{a_{i}\}
,
r_{i}=a_{i}
.

While there exist two sets
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
such that some item is both one of
r_{i}
’s favorite
|S_{i}|
items and one of
r_{j}
’s favorite
|S_{j}|
items:

•

Replace
S_{i}
and
S_{j}
with
S_{i}\cup S_{j}
.

•

The representative for
S_{i}\cup S_{j}
is
r_{i}
if
|S_{i}|\geq|S_{j}|
and
r_{j}
otherwise.

Arbitrarily match the agents in each
S_{i}
to the favorite
|S_{i}|
choices of
r_{i}
.

Athough the RepMatch mechanism was designed with the metric distortion framework in mind, it is quite natural and its description makes no mention of metric spaces. It seems reasonable for a representative to act on the behalf of other individuals with similar preferences. When two representatives share similar interests, it might also make sense for them to form a larger coalition.

Since the worst-case sum-distortion of the RepMatch mechanism was shown by
[
ACR23
]
to be
O(n^{2})
, a naive application of
Proposition

4
would have suggested a fairness ratio of
O(n^{3})
. However, a careful analysis of
1
presented in the following sections provides an improved bound for the fairness ratio and new
f
-distortion bounds for several norms
f
.

4.1
Upper Bounds

We begin by providing an upper bound on the top-

-distortion of

Theorem 2
.

.

1

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Fix a consistent metric
d

be the perfect matching that minimizes the function

. For the sake of the analysis, we associate each set
S

. For the initial singletons

. Whenever the mechanism merges sets

and
S_{j}

is as follows,

w(S_{i}\cup S_{j})=\begin{cases}\max(2w(S_{j}),w(S_{i}))&|S_{i}|+|S_{j}|\leq k\\
2w(S_{j})+w(S_{i})&|S_{i}|+|S_{j}|>k\end{cases}.

(1)

We will show that the mechanism maintains the invariant that for each set
S_{i}
and each agent
a\in S_{i}
,

d(a,r_{i})\leq-d(a,M^{*}_{k}(a))+w(S_{i})\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{k}(M^{*}_{k}|_{S_{i}}).

(2)

Note that this implies that for each agent
a\in S_{i}
,

d(r_{i},M^{*}_{k}(a))\leq d(r_{i},a)+d(a,M^{*}_{k}(a))\leq w(S_{i})\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{k}(M^{*}_{k}|_{S_{i}}).

Moreover, it follows that there are at least
|S_{i}|
distinct items
b
such that

d(r_{j},b)\leq w(S_{i})\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{k}(M^{*}_{k}|_{S_{i}}).

(3)

In particular, this holds when
b
is one of
r_{i}
’s favorite
|S_{i}|
items. If an agent
a\in S_{i}
is matched by the mechanism to one of those favorite
|S_{i}|
items, then by (
2
) and (
3
) she will incur a cost at most

\displaystyle d(a,r_{i})+d(r_{i},b)\leq 2w(S_{i})\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{k}(M^{*}_{k}|_{S_{i}})\leq 2w(S_{i})\cdot\mathrm{cost}_{k}(M^{*}_{k}).

We will additionally show that for every set
S_{i}
maintained by the mechanism,
w(S_{i})\leq k(n/k)^{\log_{2}3}
. This, coupled with the above, implies that sum of the

, which in turn implies the stated distortion bound.

Establishing the invariant.

Clearly the invariant holds initially.
So, we consider what happens at the end of an iteration given that the invariant held at the start of that iteration. During an iteration, there will be some some set
S_{i}

S_{j}

All other sets remain unchanged, so we only need to show that the invariant holds for the union

We assume without loss of generality that

If
|S_{i}\cup S_{j}|>k

Max-Distortion Lower Bounds

Finally, we discuss max-distortion lower bounds for arbitrary deterministic mechanisms.
We are only able to exhibit problem instances that guarantee constant distortion.

Theorem 4
.

Every deterministic matching mechanism has worst-case max-distortion at least
7
.

Our proof of the above theorem can be found in
Appendix

C
. It is inspired by the approach of
[
ACR23
]
, which was used to give lower bound of
\Omega(\log n)
for the sum-distortion. At a high level, their approach consists of two key ingredients: a set of preferences for
n=2^{k}
agents given by a tree metric and a family
\mathcal{F}
of tree metrics consistent with those preferences. Every metric
d\in\mathcal{F}
is such that
d(a,b)\in\{0,1,2\}
for any agent
a
and item
b
.
Since we desire large max-distortion, we consider families of tree metrics with larger distances.

We now discuss in more detail the structure of the tree metrics that induce agent preferences. For
n\geq 4
, construct a balanced binary tree with the
n
agents and
n-1
items, such that the leaf nodes are the agents and the other nodes are items. Let
v
be the root of this binary tree and let
\ell
and
r
be the left and right child respectively.
Make the remaining item, denoted by
u
, be the parent of the root node
v
.
Note that the nodes
u,v,\ell,r
are all items. Finally, add non-negative weights to edges. We call any set of preferences that arises from this procedure a
tree instance
.

One can easily verify that tree instances in
[

]
and the proof of
Theorem

4
are such that for every agent
a
in the subtree with root

(i)

Agent
a
prefers each item in the same subtree, as well as item

, over item

.

(ii)

prefers each item in the same subtree over any item in the subtree with root in the set

.

Let

6
Discussion

Since a mechanism’s sum-distortion and max-distortion could differ by as much as a factor of
n
, we believe that it is worthwhile to study both. Note that significant improvements for one could have immediate consequences for the other, as well as the fairness ratio. Thus, a milestone on the path to answering open problems raised by
[
AFSV21
]
and
[
ACR23
]
is the following question.

Open Problem 1
.

What is the optimal max-distortion for deterministic mechanisms for metric matching with ordinal preferences?

Our analysis of
1
mechanism shows that the optimal max-distortion is at most
O(n^{1.58})
.
However, it seems plausible to us that sublinear max-distortion is attainable and we boldly conjecture that the optimal distortion is in fact a constant.

Conjecture 1
.

There is a deterministic matching mechanism that guarantees max-distortion
O(1)
.

As a first step towards resolving this conjecture, we suggest the following weaker problem. Suppose that we know that each item is distance at most
1
to
some
unique agent. For each item, can we find an agent whose distance is at most
O(1)
? If the answer is no, this would immediately imply a super-constant lower bound for max-distortion. If the answer is yes, then perhaps one can use this knowledge to construct better mechanisms.

Another interesting direction relates to truthfulness. The Serial Dictatorship has exponentially large max-distortion (see
Appendix

D
for more details), but are there other truthful deterministic mechanisms with better distortion guarantees?

Appendix D
Max-Distortion of Other Mechanisms

We now briefly discuss how other deterministic mechanisms fare under the max-distortion lens.
We note that the sum-distortion of these mechanisms has already been studied. It turns out that many of the examples used to show lower bounds for the sum-distortion also provide similar lower bounds for the max-distortion because most of the mechanism’s cost is incurred by a single agent. For completeness, we provide full descriptions below.

We begin with the Serial Dictatorship, a well-known truthful mechanism for assignment problems. In a fixed an order over the agents, it assigns each agent her favorite item that is not yet assigned. The sum-distortion of this mechanism was shown by
[
CFRF+16
]
to be exponential. We show that this is also the case for the max-distortion.

Proposition 12
.

The Serial Dictatorship has worst-case max-distortion
\Omega(2^{n})
.

Proof.

We revisit the instance used by
[
CFRF+16
]
to study the sum-distortion of the Serial Dictatorship. This instance consists of
n
agents and items configured along the real line, parametrized by a positive constant
\varepsilon<1
. Agents are located at the points

\{1\}\cup\{2^{t}:t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},t\leq n-1\}.

There is one agent at each of these
t
locations. Agents are assigned items in order of their distance to
0
(the closest goes first). Items are located at the points

\{-\varepsilon\}\cup\{2^{t}:t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},t\leq n-1\}.

there is one item at each of these
t
locations.

It is straightforward to verify that the optimal matching
M^{*}_{1}
for the max-objective satisfies
\mathrm{cost}_{1}(M^{*}_{1})=1+\varepsilon
. Note however, that the Serial Dictatorship will assign the agent at
2^{n-1}
to the item at
-\varepsilon
due to a domino effect, resulting in a matching
M
with
\mathrm{cost}_{1}(M)=2^{n-1}+\varepsilon
. This implies the stated distortion bound.
∎

Remark 2
.

The Serial Dictatorship belongs to a class of truthful algorithms that
[
ACR23
]
call
serializable
.
It is not hard to show that this class similarly has exponential max-distortion.

The Boston mechanism
[
AS03
]
is another well-known deterministic mechanism and is commonly used in the school choice literature. The mechanism takes place over a series of rounds. In the first round, each agent proposes to her favorite item. Then, each item that receives a proposal is irrevocably matched to one its proposers and rejects all others. In the second round, the agents that failed to get matched then propose to their second favorite items, and the mechanism continues in the same way until all agents are matched. For simplicity, we will say that choices are made according to a fixed priority order, but this can be extended to the other settings (e.g., arbitrary tie-breaking). The sum-distortion of this mechanism was shown by
[
ACR23
]
to be essentially exponential. We show that this remains the case for the max-distortion.

Proposition 13
.

The Boston mechanism has worst-case max-distortion
\Omega(2^{\sqrt{n}})
.

Proof.

We revisit the instance used by
[
ACR23
]
to study the sum-distortion of the Boston mechanism. The agent and items locations are the similar to those in the proof of

Like the Serial Dictatorship, the Boston mechanism suffers from a domino effect.
