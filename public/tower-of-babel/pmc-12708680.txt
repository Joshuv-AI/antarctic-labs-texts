A broadly neutralizing antibody confers cross-genus protection against alphaherpesviruses by inhibiting gB-mediated membrane fusion
Authors: Guosong Wang, Yu Li, Chongxin Wu, Tian Chen, Mengxuan Gui, Yue Zeng, Hui Sun, Kaiyun Chen, Xiangfeng Xi, Yanbo Yang, Yuchen Jiang, Yanan Jiang, Liqin Liu, Chengyu Yang, Jiarui Xin, Caihong Liu, Yiyi Li, Ningning Huo, Yang Huang, Lina Lin, Hai Yu, Chenghao Huang, Quan Yuan, Shaowei Li, Kegong Tian, Qingbing Zheng, Ningshao Xia, Yixin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global prevalence and disease burden of alphaherpesviruses infections, including human-infecting viruses such as HSV-1, HSV-2, and VZV, as well as animal-infecting viruses like PRV, BHV, CHV, and FHV, highlight the unmet need for more effective and universal antiviral strategies. However, there has been no significant progress in developing broad-spectrum interventions against herpesvirus. Here we report the identification of a broadly neutralizing antibody against alphaherpesviruses, 16F9, which targets the glycoprotein B (gB) of alphaherpesviruses and offers cross-protection against multiple viruses such as HSV-1, HSV-2, and PRV in mice. 16F9 demonstrated robust therapeutic efficacy in various female mouse models of herpesvirus diseases including PRV-induced viral encephalitis, HSV-1-induced viral encephalitis, viral keratitis, cutaneous herpes, and neonatal herpesvirus infections. High-resolution cryo-electron microscopy structures revealed that 16F9 binds a conserved site of vulnerability on Domain I of gB. The binding of 16F9 disrupts the interaction between pre-gB and gHgL complex, thereby preventing viral membrane fusion and blocking viral infection. This study provides a foundation for advancing antiviral strategies and underscores the potential of gB-targeted interventions for combating herpesvirus infections.Subject terms: Target identification, Antivirals, Cryoelectron microscopy, Herpes virus

Introduction

Members of the Herpesviridae family, which are double-stranded DNA viruses, present significant health threats globally, affecting billions of humans and animals. This family is categorized into three subfamilies: neurotropic alphaherpesviruses, broadly infective betaherpesviruses, and lymphotropic gammaherpesviruses1. Within the alphaherpesvirus subfamily, several viruses, including herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1), HSV-2, and Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV), are major human pathogens, while others, such as pseudorabies virus (PRV), herpes B virus (BV), Bovine herpesvirus (BHV), Canine herpesvirus (CHV), Caprine herpesvirus (CpHV), and Feline herpesvirus (FHV), mainly infect animals. First, HSV-1 affects ~3.7 billion people worldwide2, which establishes lifelong latency and often causes oral herpes and, in a minority of cases, potentially leading to conditions like viral keratitis and encephalitis that can result in blindness and death3,4. Additionally, HSV-1 infection has been associated with various diseases, including Bell’s palsy and Alzheimer’s disease5,6. Second, HSV-2, infecting around 490 million people worldwide2, is linked with recurrent episodes of genital herpetic ulcers and may elevate human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection risk7. Third, VZV causes chickenpox globally, with estimated annual cases reaching 140 million, leading to 4.2 million severe complications necessitating hospitalization and 4200 deaths8. Moreover, about one-third of those infected may experience VZV reactivation in later life, leading to herpes zoster (shingles), typically characterized by severe pain followed by postherpetic neuralgia, severely affecting the quality of life9,10. Last, Animal-specific alphaherpesviruses, like PRV, BHV, CHV, and FHV, are crucial in animal morbidity and mortality, and PRV and BV can occasionally cross species, causing severe outcomes in humans11–17. Consequently, alphaherpesviruses constitute major health challenges worldwide.

Herpesviruses present a significant public health challenge, underscoring the urgent need for effective vaccines. However, there are currently no effective vaccines available for most herpesviruses except for VZV18. Although the existing VZV vaccines, such as the live attenuated vaccine for chickenpox and the recombinant vaccine for shingles19,20, have proven effective and continue to be improved upon the live attenuated varicella21, new generation vaccines are urgently needed to extend the duration of protection and to be effective in broader age groups. Furthermore, the lack of a cure and effective vaccine contributes to the ongoing transmission and persistence of HSV infections. There are also no vaccines available to protect humans from zoonotic transmission of BV and PRV22,23, which can lead to high mortality rates in humans, as well as other potentially cross-species transmissible alphaherpesviruses. On the other hand, available herpesvirus therapeutic drugs, primarily nucleoside analogs such as acyclovir, are limited by their significant side effects and resistance24–26, resulting in scenarios where no effective drugs are available for the treatment of herpesvirus infections. Furthermore, these drugs can only inhibit viral replication and are incapable of blocking viral infection, providing opportunities for the virus to remain latent and reactivate. Thus, developing potent and broad-spectrum drugs and preventative universal vaccines that can effectively block herpesvirus infection are of paramount importance.

The entry of herpesvirus necessitates the coordinated efforts of at least four core glycoproteins: gB, glycoprotein D (gD), glycoprotein H (gH), and glycoprotein L (gL). The gB protein is believed to act as the membrane fusogen, while the gHgL complex appears to function as an entry activator, making them critical targets for herpesvirus vaccine research. The gB protein is crucial for mediating the fusion of the viral envelope with the cellular membrane to complete the virus infection process27. The absence of gB completely abolishes viral infectivity28,29, underscoring the central role of gB in virus infection. The gD protein functions as a receptor-binding protein during the infection process of most alphaherpesviruses, except for VZV, which lacks gD30. The interaction with the gHgL heterodimer subsequently facilitates the membrane fusion function of fusion protein gB31. Nevertheless, this process is not absolute, as demonstrated by PRV, which can gain the capability to infect cells in the absence of gD, gL, or the N-terminal domain of gH, through compensatory mutations in other envelope glycoproteins32,33.

Broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) have been demonstrated, offering promising insights for universal vaccine development. Research efforts have been directed towards herpesvirus gB and gHgL complexes, as these are critical for viral entry and are functionally conserved across different herpesvirus species. However, bnAbs against herpesviruses with cross-species neutralization are rarely reported. Due to its critical function and conserved nature, gB has emerged as a prime target for the development of broad-spectrum neutralizing antibodies (nAbs). Research on gB-targeted antibodies has shown promise, offering potential therapeutic strategies against a variety of herpesviruses, including HSV, VZV, human cytomegalovirus (HCMV), and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), confirming the potential of gB as a target for developing antiviral inventions that block viral infection34–37. However, currently reported nAbs targeting gB are species-specific without exception35,38,39. The epitopes recognized by these nAbs are primarily located in domains I, II, and IV of gB’s five domains. Despite the conserved conformation in the structure of regions within these epitopes, which implies the potential for broadly nAbs35,40, developing broad-spectrum nAbs against gB faces several challenges.

In this study, we report the identification of a bnAb, 16F9, which targets the gB protein of herpesviruses. This antibody demonstrates broadly binding and neutralizing activity across the Simplexvirus and Varicellovirus genera of alphaherpesviruses. In animal models, 16F9 provided robust protection in treating PRV-caused viral encephalitis, HSV-1-caused viral encephalitis, viral keratitis, and cutaneous herpes, and neonatal herpesvirus infections caused by HSV-1/2. Structural analysis revealed that 16F9 binds to a broad-spectrum alphaherpesvirus universal epitope located in Domain I of gB, inhibiting conformational changes crucial for viral entry, thus blocking interactions between gHgL and gB, and highlighting its potential for broad therapeutic applications. Collectively, this study suggests that a gB-based broad-spectrum therapeutics or universal vaccines may provide a promising strategy to fight herpesvirus infections.

Results

Identification of alphaherpesviruses bnAb 16F9

The Alphaherpesvirinae subfamily encompasses a variety of viruses with a broad host range. The diversity of the full-length gB protein amino acid sequences from four representative viruses of the Simplexvirus genus and seven representative viruses of the Varicellovirus genus is presented in a dendrogram (Fig. 1a). Among these, VZV shows lower gB protein sequence similarity with other viruses of the Varicellovirus genus as well as with those of the Simplexvirus genus. In order to develop bnAbs that can cross neutralize different alphaherpesviruses, we constructed the ectodomain (ECD) of gBs from representative alphaherpesviruses HSV-1 (Simplexvirus genus), PRV and VZV (Varicellovirus genus), and performed a procedure of sequential immunization by using both gB DNAs and proteins (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Utilizing mouse hybridoma technology, we screened for antibodies with broad binding and neutralizing activity against PRV, HSV-1 and VZV, and successfully obtained a bnAb named 16F9 which could potently neutralize both PRV and HSV-1 (Supplementary Fig. 1b–d).

To validate the broad-spectrum binding activity of 16F9, the ECDs of gBs from more alphaherpesviruses (BHV-5, CpHV-1, PRV, VZV, BV, HSV-1, HSV-2, and BHV-2) were prepared and employed in a surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay to assess the affinity of 16F9. 16F9 exhibited nanomolar affinities in binding to the gB proteins of PRV, HSV-1, BHV-5, BV, HSV-2, and CpHV-1 (Kd values 1.27–8.27 nM), and sub-nanomolar affinities to those of BHV-2 and VZV (Kd values 14–59 nM) (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 2a–h). Additionally, ELISA confirmed that 16F9 effectively bound to all above gB proteins, with the half maximal effective concentration (EC50) values being <1 × 103 ng/mL, except for the VZV gB, which with an EC50 of 1.72 × 104 ng/mL (Fig. 1c). An immunofluorescence assay was then used to assess the binding activity of 16F9 to native gBs in cells infected with viruses (BHV-1, PRV, FHV-1, CHV-1, VZV, HSV-1 and HSV-2) or transfected with gB genes (BHV-5, CpHV-1, BV and BHV-2). The results showed robust binding activity of 16F9 with all tested native gBs except for VZV gB (Fig. 1d).

To further assess the broad-spectrum neutralizing activity of 16F9, we conducted the neutralization assays of four varicelloviruses (BHV-1, FHV-1, PRV, and VZV) and two simplexviruses (HSV-1 and HSV-2), respectively. 16F9 effectively neutralized BHV-1, PRV, HSV-1, and HSV-2, with the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 7.73, 19.77, 8.02, and 23.45 μg/mL, respectively, while with a relatively weaker neutralization to FHV-1 with an IC50 of 120.50 μg/mL (Fig. 1e-j). However, even a high concentration of 16F9 could not inhibit the infection of VZV completely (Fig. 1h), likely due to the low binding affinity of 16F9 against VZV gB. These findings underscore the potential of 16F9 as a broad nAb for targeting a range of alphaherpesviruses, although its lower affinity for VZV gB suggests further optimization may be needed. Furthermore, ADCC (antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity) represents another crucial mechanism by which antibodies exert antiviral effects. Our findings indicate that 16F9, upon binding to PRV gB, fails to effectively activate murine Fc gamma receptor IV (FcγRIV), indicating that the murine 16F9 may not operate with ADCC activity in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Cross-genus protection of the 16F9 against diverse alphaherpesviruses in mice

We then performed a series of animal assays to evaluate whether 16F9 confers cross-protection against alphaherpesviruses infections in vivo. HSV-1 is a neurotropic virus that causes several diseases, including herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE), keratitis, oral or facial lesions (Herpes labialis), and skin lesions (Herpes gladiatorum)41. HSV-1 can spread to the trigeminal nerve and brain via nasal infection, leading to significant weight loss and mortality in mice26. To evaluate the therapeutic activity of 16F9 against HSV-1, mice were challenged with 3 × 104 plaque-forming units (pfu) HSV-1 via the intranasal route. Mice were administered 16F9 (intraperitoneally), the control antibody (intraperitoneally) at a dose of 20 mg/kg on days 1, 2, and 3 post-infection (Fig. 2a). The mice were then monitored for changes in body weight and survival rates for 14 days. Mice treated with the control antibody exhibited significant weight loss due to HSV-1 infection, with survival rates of 0% (Fig. 2b, c). In contrast, mice treated with 16F9 maintained body weights comparable to uninfected mice and showed a 100% survival rate (Fig. 2b, c), indicating that 16F9 possesses highly effective therapeutic activity against lethal HSV-1 infection.

HSV-1 infection in the cornea can cause herpetic stromal keratitis (HSK), which is the leading factor for infectious blindness42. Furthermore, corneal HSV-1 can spread to the brain and lead to ocular lesions, weight loss, and death in mice43. Based on these studies, we constructed an ocular infection mouse model for assessing the therapeutics of 16F9. Mice were challenged via the eye with eye drops of 6 × 103 pfu HSV-1, and treated with 20 mg/kg 16F9, the control antibody, respectively, on days 1–3 post-infection. In the control group, mice experienced a significant weight reduction post viral infection, with a survival rate of only 16.7%, and all mice developed significant ocular lesions (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c). Mice treated with 16F9 did not experience a significant weight reduction compared to uninfected mice and achieved a 100% survival rate (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c). Furthermore, 16F9-treated mice had relatively healthy eyes with no significant lesions observed (Supplementary Fig. 4c). The high viral load in the brains was determined inthe control group, while undetectable in 16F9-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 4d). These studies demonstrated that 16F9 treatment has shown significant clinical potential in reducing ocular lesions, preventing weight loss, and ensuring survival in an HSV-1 ocular infection model.

Skin lesions are the most common symptoms of herpes simplex virus primary and recurrent infections2. HSV-1 could also lead to zosteriform lesions via skin infection in mice44. To evaluate the therapeutic activity of 16F9 against skin lesions induced by HSV-1, Mice were challenged with 4 × 105 pfu HSV-1 via skin, then treated with 20 mg/kg 16F9, the control antibody, respectively, on days 1–3 post-infection. No significant changes in body weight or survival rate were observed in mice of all tested groups (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). However, in the control antibody-treated group, ~70% mice showed evident zosteriform lesions in skin, whereas 16F9 was able to completely prevent the occurrence of zosteriform lesions (Supplementary Fig. 4g–i). These studies demonstrated that 16F9 treatment has shown significant clinical potential in preventing skin lesions caused by HSV-1 infection.

Neonatal herpes simplex virus infection is an unignorable concern, resulting in the highest infant mortality rate among perinatal viral infections in the United States45,46. Despite aggressive antiviral treatment, mortality subsequent to disseminated disease remains high (30%), and central nervous system (CNS) disease is associated with ∼70% neurological morbidity and 25% mortality47. To explore the protective potency of 16F9 against neonatal herpes simplex virus infection, a neonatal mouse model was constructed. Pregnant mice were pre-treated with antibodies (intravenously) 2 days prior to delivery. 3 days after the pups were born, neonatal mice were challenged with 500 pfu HSV-1 and 250 pfu HSV-2 in 1 μL, respectively, via the intranasal route (Fig. 2d), followed by monitoring the survival of pups. After being challenged with HSV-1, the majority of neonatal mice from pregnant mice treated with the negative control antibody did not survive, resulting in a survival rate of 18.2%. In contrast, neonatal mice from those treated with 16F9 exhibited a significantly higher survival rate of 84.2% (Fig. 2e). As for HSV-2 infection, similar results were achieved as pups born to those administered 16F9 showed 100% survival after viral infection, while ~50% mortality rate achieved in the control antibody group (Fig. 2f). These results indicated that 16F9 can effectively provide cross-protection against neonatal herpes simplex virus infection caused by both HSV-1 and HSV-2.

To further verify the cross-protective activity of 16F9 against the infection of Varicellovirus genus viruses, PRV, a representative virus of this genus, was selected for animal protection evaluation due to its sensitivity to various mammals. It’s reported that, when administered via intraperitoneal injection, PRV can cause skin itching, weight loss, and mortality in mice48,49. To confirm the therapeutic activity of 16F9 in such a model, Mice were intraperitoneally challenged with 3 × 103 pfu PRV. Antibodies 20 mg/kg were administered to mice two hours after infection, as well as on days 1 and 2 post-infection (Fig. 2g). It was observed that mice treated with control antibody experienced significant weight loss, with all animals succumbing within 6 days post-infection (dpi) (Fig. 2h, i). In contrast, mice treated with 16F9 did not exhibit obvious weight loss and achieved 100% survival (Fig. 2h, i). These findings demonstrated that 16F9 provides robust protective activity against the lethal challenge of PRV.

To expand the potential clinical applications of 16F9, a chimeric 16F9, designated C16F9, was constructed incorporating the fragment of antigen-binding (Fab) region of 16F9 and the fragment crystallizable (Fc) region of human IgG1. This chimeric antibody was subsequently characterized in subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments. C16F9 exhibited comparable gB binding affinity and cross-neutralizing activities against PRV, HSV-1, and HSV-2 (Supplementary Fig. 5a–h, Supplementary Fig. 6a–d). A total of 8 therapeutic dosages (20, 10, 5, 2.50, 1.25, 0.63, 0.31, 0.16 mg/kg) were tested and C16F9 provided 100% protection against mortality even at doses as low as 1.25 mg/kg (Supplementary Fig. 7a–d), with a calculated protective EC50 of 0.63 mg/kg (Supplementary Fig. 7b). These results highlighted the potential clinical application value of C16F9 against alphaherpesviruses.

Structural basis for the broad-spectrum alphaherpesvirus neutralization of 16F9

To elucidate the cross-neutralization mechanisms of 16F9, we resolved the cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of gB proteins from multiple alphaherpesviruses in complex with 16F9 Fab. Immune complexes of 16F9 Fab with gBs from HSV-2, BV, PRV, and VZV were prepared, yielding structures at resolutions of 2.18, 2.18, 2.78, and 3.12 Å, respectively (Fig. 3a–d, Supplementary Figs. 8–11, Supplementary Table 1). The cryo-EM densities of HSV-2-gB:16F9, BV-gB:16F9, and PRV-gB:16F9, each displaying C3 symmetry, revealed the decorating of gB trimer with three bound 16F9 Fabs (Fig. 3a–c). In contrast, the VZV-gB:16F9 showed an unsaturated binding of 16F9, with only one 16F9 Fab bound to each gB trimer (Fig. 3d), probably due to the relatively lower affinity of 16F9 to VZV gB (Supplementary Fig. 2d).

According to the previous structural studies, herpesvirus gB mainly comprises five structural domains: I–V50 (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). The broadly neutralizing antibody 16F9 specifically targets a region near the membrane in the gB trimer, focusing on an epitope centrally located within Domain I (DI). For HSV-2 gB, 16F9 binds 12 amino acids (F189, Y298, R299, E300, G301, H303, A311, K315, Q316, D318, G319, Y321), with an interaction surface area of 867.5 Å² (Fig. 3e). In contrast, its interaction with BV gB encompasses 15 residues (F189, A198, Y298, R299, D300, G301, H303, A311, D312, R315, Q316, V317, D318, G319, Y321), covering a larger area of 967.9 Å² (Fig. 3f). Despite minor differences, such as three additional residues in BV gB (A198, D312, V317) and two variant residues (E300/D300 and K315/R315), the chemical properties of these sites remain conserved. This conservation is key to 16F9’s robust binding ability. However, significant sequence variability exists between Simplexvirus (e.g., HSV-2, BV) and Varicellovirus (e.g., PRV, VZV) as seen in the 16F9 epitopes of the latter. The PRV gB epitope encompasses 12 residues (R198, L298, R299, E300, G301, H303, P311, G312, Q315, Q316, E318, H319), spanning 925.2 Å² (Fig. 3g). For VZV gB, it includes 13 residues (K198, L298, R299, D300, G301, Y303, M311, D312, H315, Q316, F317, E318, R321), extending to 939.2 Å² (Fig. 3h).

The ability of 16F9 to broadly bind across these diverse viral genera raises intriguing questions. Despite differences, several residues like Y298, R299, E300/D300, H303/Y303, and Q316 remain conserved across these genera, likely playing a crucial role in interacting with gBs (Fig. 3i). This high degree of conservation indicates 16F9’s cross-neutralization potential, extending to various Simplexvirus and Varicellovirus genera (Supplementary Fig. 13a, b).

Structural conservation of the 16F9 binding site

We conducted a superimposition of the Domain I regions from gB proteins across HSV-2, BV, PRV, and VZV (Fig. 4a). This analysis revealed strong structural consistency within individual genera, particularly between HSV-2 and BV, as indicated by the minimal RMSD value of 0.239 Å. This close similarity contrasts with the larger RMSD values observed in inter-genus comparisons, reflecting greater structural differences. Specifically, the RMSD measurements were as follows: PRV/VZV at 0.614 Å, HSV-2/PRV at 0.622 Å, HSV-2/VZV at 0.615 Å, BV/PRV at 0.619 Å, and BV/VZV at 0.652 Å. These values underscore the conserved nature of epitopes within the Simplexvirus genus and highlight notable variability between Varicellovirus species. This analysis demonstrated a strong structural consistency of Domain I among the four gB proteins, indicating its largely conserved nature. These values underscore the conserved nature of the 16F9 epitope within the Simplexvirus genus.

Within the Simplexvirus genus, HSV-2 and BV demonstrated nearly identical epitope conformations with minimal sequence divergences. Ten pairs of conserved amino acids were identified (F189, Y298, R299, G301, H303, A311, Q316, D318, G319, Y321), alongside two with slight variations (E300/D300, K315/R315), indicating a high degree of structural conservation (Fig. 4b). Conversely, a comparison between PRV and VZV revealed greater sequence disparities, retaining only four conserved amino acid pairs (L298, R299, Q316, E318) (Fig. 4c). Substitutions in PRV and VZV often involved changes in side chain properties and orientations, such as H303 to Y303 and P311 to M311, affecting structural alignment. Inter-genus comparisons revealed a substitution of Y298 in HSV/BV with L298 in PRV/VZV, and a unique Y314 mutation in VZV, contrasting with histidine in the other viruses. Detailed interaction analysis showed that 16F9 forms nine and eight hydrogen bonds with HSV-2 and BV gBs, respectively, mediated by conserved residues (R299, Y298, E300, H303, Q316) (Fig. 4d, e). For PRV and VZV, interactions involved seven and six hydrogen bonds, respectively, due to the Y298 to L298 substitution. Key conserved residues (R299, E300/D300, Q316) are essential for hydrogen bond formation with 16F9 (Fig. 4f, g). The Y to L substitution while transitioning from Simplexvirus to Varicellovirus affects interactions, indicating weakened 16F9 affinity in the latter. Mutation from H303 to Y303 between PRV and VZV further impacts VZV gB’s binding ability due to steric hindrance from the larger side chain (Supplementary Fig. 14a–g). Despite this, the core epitope residues (Y, R, E/D, H, Q) in 16F9 remain largely conserved across most alphaherpesviruses (Supplementary Fig. 13), affirming its broad-spectrum neutralizing capability.

Glycoprotein B protomers are synthesized as metastable prefusion trimers (pre-gB), undergoing significant conformational changes for membrane fusion. We explored 16F9’s binding profiles with prefusion gB. Due to a lack of structured data for the prefusion gB forms studied, the HSV-2 pre-gB structural model was built by homology modeling with Swiss-Model using HSV-1 pre-gB (PDB: 6Z9M) as a template and compared it with the HSV-2 post-gB structure (Fig. 4h, i). Superimposing HSV-2 gB:16F9 with the HSV-2 pre-gB structure revealed high conformational similarity in Domain I, notably at the 16F9 binding site (RMSD 1.243 Å) (Fig. 4j). These results suggest that 16F9 can recognize both pre- and post-fusion gB forms. Previous studies indicated that a histidine-to-proline mutation at residue 516 in HSV-1 gB (HSV-1-gB-H516P) results in a prefusion conformation (Vollmer et al.51; Zhou et al.52). To assess the binding activity of 16F9 with the prefusion conformation, we introduced this mutation into HSV-1 gB and conducted transfections of HSV-1-gB-WT and HSV-1-gB-H516P into 293 F cells. Flow cytometry verified that 16F9 effectively binds both forms (Fig. 4k–n), validating 16F9’s recognition capability of pre-fusion gB.

Neutralizing mechanism of 16F9

Herpesviruses exhibit some of the most intricate cellular entry mechanisms, necessitating the coordinated efforts of at least 4 glycoproteins: gD, gH, gL, and gB. The current model suggests that the binding of gD to its receptor initiates a “cascade” of sequential pairwise interactions, first activating the gHgL complex, which subsequently activates gB30. In situ observations indicate an interaction between the gHgL complex and the prefusion gB, suggesting the receptor engagement of gHgL triggers pre-gB conformational rearrangements, transitioning from prefusion to postfusion states, and is accomplished with membrane fusion. Previous studies propose that the gH subunit of the gHgL complex interacts with gB’s DI domain (spanning residues 153–363)53, raising concerns about the neutralizing mechanisms of 16F9 in inhibiting gB-gH interaction and the gB-mediated membrane fusion. To investigate this, we constructed plasmids expressing PRV gD, gH, gL, and gB proteins, respectively. Using these plasmids, we performed co-transfection to assess the membrane fusion inhibition potency of 16F9, alongside a control antibody, 4D5. To visualize cell fusion in transfected cells, the mNeonGreen (mNG) and mRuby3 genes were fused downstream of the gH and gB genes, respectively. In both the mock and the 4D5-treated groups, significant cell fusion was observed in transfected cells (Supplementary Movies 1 and 2), indicating successful reproduction of herpesvirus membrane fusion resulting in syncytia formation. Specifically, the fluorescence of gH-mNG covered a larger area (Fig. 5a), and the cell membrane integrity indicator, β-catenin (Alexa Fluor 647), was lost in the fused cells (Fig. 5a), confirming cell fusion. A high-content imaging system was employed to acquire images and quantify the fluorescent area of syncytia induced by co-expression of gD, gH-mNG, gL, and gB-mRuby3 proteins. In contrast, 16F9 effectively inhibited membrane fusion, as evidenced by significantly smaller gH-mNG fluorescent areas compared to the Mock and 4D5-treated groups (P < 0.0001) and the presence of β-catenin between cells (Fig. 5a, b, Supplementary Movie 3), confirming that 16F9 significantly reduced cell fusion induced by gD, gH, gL, and gB proteins.

To further quantify the inhibition potency of 16F9 against cell fusion, we introduced a previously reported luciferase reporter system to indicate cell fusion54. Plasmids encoding gD, gH, gL, gB, and T7 RNA polymerase were co-transfected into 293FT cells, while the plasmid with the luciferase gene under the control of a T7 promoter was transfected into another 293FT cells. After mixing the two cell populations, cell fusion mediated by gD, gH, gL, and gB allows the T7 RNA polymerase to initiate luciferase expression. The fusion efficiency of the two cell types can be assessed by measuring luciferase activity. Such luciferase activity assays revealed higher cell fusion potency in the mock and 4D5-treated groups, while the addition of 16F9 significantly reduced luciferase activity (Fig. 5c), suggesting that 16F9 may exert a neutralizing effect by inhibiting the viral fusion process.

In situ structure of gHgL complex adjacent to pre-gB rather than post-gB53. Thus, the binding of 16F9 to pre-gB domain I may sterically hinder the interaction of gHgL with pre-gB. To further confirm this conclusion, we have used a bimolecular fluorescence complexity (BiFC) Assay to evaluate the co-localization between gH and gB55. The principle is to add aa. 1–172 of mVenus to the C-terminus of gB (PTT5-gB-VN173), and aa. 173–238 of mVenus to the C-terminus of gH (PTT5-gH-VC173). When gB interacts with gH, mVenus can emit fluorescence. Due to the fusion with the mVenus fragment, Western blot analysis confirmed a significant increase in the molecular weight of both gH-VC173 and gB-VN173 (Supplementary Fig. 15a, b). When plasmids expressing gH-VC173 and gB-VN173 were transfected individually, no BiFC signal was detected. Although co-transfection of both plasmids did not induce cell fusion, the BiFC signals were observed intracellularly. However, no BiFC signal was detected near the plasma membrane labeled with β-catenin (Supplementary Fig. 15c, Fig. 5d). After transfecting PTT5-gB VN173, PTT5-gH-VC173, PTT5-gD, and PTT5-gL into 293FT cells, and adding control antibody or 16F9, we found that there were obvious BiFC signals on the cell membrane labeled by anti-β-catenin antibody in the Mock group and control antibody group, implying that interactions between gHgL and pre-gB may occur on the cell surface (Fig. 5d). In contrast, after adding 16F9, the BiFC signals were all located inside the cell, and no significant BiFC fluorescence signal was found near the cell membrane. It is confirmed that 16F9 could effectively block the interaction between gHgL and pre-gB on the cell membrane (Fig. 5d). To further confirm whether 16F9 can block their interaction, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) revealed a significant interaction between gH and gB in cells pre-transfected with plasmids expressing gD, gH, gL, and gB, treated with mock or the control antibody. However, treatment with 16F9 effectively inhibited this interaction, resulting in the pre-gB binding to significantly less gHgL complex (Fig. 5e, f). These findings confirm that 16F9 exerts its virus-neutralizing function by blocking the interaction between gHgL and pre-gB (Fig. 5g).

In summary, the neutralizing mechanism of 16F9 against herpesviruses involves a crucial strategy targeting the viral entry process (Fig. 5g). Antibody 16F9 inhibits membrane fusion by blocking the interaction between the gHgL complex and gB, a key step required for transitioning gB from its prefusion to postfusion state, as demonstrated by co-localization studies, immunoprecipitation, and luciferase reporter assays (Fig. 5c–f). Structural analyses suggest that 16F9 binding to pre-gB may sterically hinder the interaction with gHgL, preventing the cascade needed for viral fusion. This approach highlights 16F9’s robust antiviral potential.

Discussion

Herpesviruses are among the most common viruses and pose significant threats to the health of both animals and humans. The Alphaherpesvirinae subfamily can be divided into the genera Simplexvirus, Varicellovirus, Mardivirus, Ilctoviruse, and Scutavirus with a wide range of hosts including avian species, reptiles, mammals, and humans. These viruses are highly pathogenic, capable of causing severe diseases and even death in their hosts. In humans, persistent infections with HSV-1, HSV-2, and VZV have been a substantial public health concern due to their significant morbidity. Additionally, cross-species transmission of alphaherpesviruses, such as PRV and BV, may exhibit heightened pathogenicity. Moreover, several animal-derived herpesviruses, like BHV-156, have the capability to infect human cells57,58, indicating that cross-species transmission of animal-derived herpesviruses should not be disregarded. Current antiviral drugs for herpesviruses, such as acyclovir, present drawbacks including drug resistance25, nephrotoxicity24, and the inability to prevent latent infection and reactivation of herpesviruses. These drawbacks limit the therapeutic options available for treating herpesvirus infections. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop highly effective, broad-spectrum antiviral drugs capable of blocking herpesvirus infections. However, the complexity of herpesvirus infection mechanisms further complicates the development of antiviral drugs and the selection of vaccine targets. The challenge lies in obtaining broad-spectrum antibodies against herpesviruses. Previous studies have shown that an initial DNA immunization followed by a protein boost can facilitate the induction of broad-spectrum antibodies59. Additionally, sequential immunization with different immunogens also promotes the generation of broad-spectrum antibodies60. In terms of immunogen selection, homology analysis of various gB proteins revealed that within the Varicellovirus genus, BHV-1, BHV-5, CpHV-1, PRV, FHV-1, and CHV-1 display high homology, whereas BV, HSV-1/2, and BHV-2 also exhibit significant homology. In contrast, VZV shows low homology with these two groups. Therefore, we selected the gB protein of PRV, HSV-1, and VZV as immunogens and employed a DNA-protein sequential immunization strategy. Based on this immunization strategy, we successfully identified broad-spectrum antibodies that binds to the gB proteins of various herpesviruses. However, we noticed that the 16F9 antibody has a robust affinity for PRV and HSV-1 gB proteins, but a lower affinity for VZV gB protein, and no significant neutralizing activity against VZV. The reason for this phenomenon may be related to low homology with other gB proteins or our chosen sequential immunization procedure, as we arranged the immunity of VZV protein last, which may be related to the original antigenic sin61. Therefore, we want to screen for the pan-alphaherpesvirus neutralizing antibodies, including against VZV. Using VZV gB as the antigen for initial immunization may be a better immunization strategy.

Currently, effective prophylactic herpesvirus vaccines are still under research, despite their significant market potential. The HSV vaccines are still in clinical stages. Meanwhile, the live-attenuated VZV vaccine has been widely deployed and has shown positive results in preventing varicella (chickenpox). However, this vaccine may potentially lead to the reactivation of the virus, causing herpes zoster and neurological complications like meningitis62. Notably, the vaccine developed against VZV-induced herpes zoster has achieved remarkable success, providing valuable insights for the development of HSV vaccines. For instance, the HSV vaccine using a combination of gD/gB proteins induced a robust antibody response, yet its clinical trial results were disappointing63. Understanding the role of antibodies in preventing diseases caused by HSV infection is a crucial aspect in current vaccine development. We discovered that the 16F9 antibody effectively prevents diseases caused by HSV-1 infection through nasal, ocular, and skin routes. Additionally, 16F9 can be maternally transferred to the pups, providing effective protection against both HSV-1 and HSV-2 infections. These data collectively underscore the potential of high neutralizing antibodies in preventing HSV-related diseases. Current vaccines tend to induce antibodies targeting domain IV of the gB protein39,64,65. Therefore, the ability to induce high titers of antibodies similar to 16F9 might be a key factor in the effectiveness of herpesvirus vaccines. In addition, the Fc function of antibodies has been proven to play a crucial role in resisting viral infections66–68. Non-neutralizing antibodies targeting HSV-1 gB Domain IV can effectively protect mice from death caused by HSV infection through antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) effect64, indicating that the ADCC effect of antibodies is also an important direction for the development of therapeutic drugs and the evaluation of vaccine effectiveness, which is helpful for the prevention and treatment of herpesvirus.

We resolved the high-resolution structures of the antibody in complex with HSV-2, BV, PRV, and VZV gB proteins by Cryo-EM, revealing that the antibody recognizes an epitope located in domain I of the gB protein. Notably, while the gB proteins from the first three viruses can bind three Fab fragments, VZV gB binds to only one Fab fragment. This difference may explain the lower affinity and neutralizing activity of 16F9 against VZV. Further analysis of the epitope recognized by 16F9 identified a key residue, Y314, in the VZV gB protein that likely affects antibody binding. The side chain of Y is longer compared to H, leading to a potential clash with the 16F9’s light chain and thereby reducing the binding affinity. Additionally, we observed a significant displacement of Y314 in VZV gB upon antibody binding compared to its unbound state. Optimizing the amino acid sequence of the 16F9’s light chain through mutations may enhance the binding and neutralizing activity of 16F9 against VZV.

Analysis of the amino acid residues recognized by 16F9 revealed five pivotal residues: Y/L, R, E/D, H, and Q. These residues are highly conserved across both the Simplexvirus and Varicellovirus genera. Beyond HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, PRV, BV, BHV-1, BHV-2, BHV-5, FHV-1, CpHV-1 and CHV-1 included in this study, similar conservation is observed in, as well as various primate alphaherpesviruses, Cervid alphaherpesviruses, Equine alphaherpesviruses, Macropodid alphaherpesviruses, Leporid alphaherpesviruses, and even Pteropodid and Phocid alphaherpesviruses (Supplementary Fig. 13). Therefore, 16F9 may possess binding and neutralizing activities against these herpesviruses, suggesting broader potential applications for 16F9.

In previous herpesvirus studies, the mechanism by which neutralizing antibodies mediate their neutralizing effects upon binding to gB remains unclear. During herpesvirus infection, the primary process involves gD binding to cellular receptors such as Nectin1, subsequently transmitting signals to the gHgL complex, which then relays the signals to pre-gB. Pre-gB facilitates the fusion of the viral and cellular membranes to complete the infection. Crucially, the gB protein undergoes conformational changes during the transition from prefusion to postfusion in the process of membrane fusion; however, Domain I is largely unchanged in pre-gB and post-gB. Consequently, the DI-specific mAbs, such as 1B03 and 1G269 against HCMV, have the potential to recognize gB in various conformational states, including pre-fusion, post-fusion, and intermediates (Supplementary Fig. 12c), contributing to high neutralizing efficacy. D4840 against HSV-1, SM5-170 against HCMV, and 3A337 against EBV bind extensively to the superficial surface of post-fusion Domain II of gB. DII-mAbs target both pre- and post-gB without preventing their conformation (Supplementary Fig. 12d). Domain IV-mAbs, such as 3A537 against EBV and 93K35 against VZV, attach to the front of DIV, whereas HSV010-1364 binds the side of DIV with its long axis approximately perpendicular to the 3-fold (Supplementary Fig. 12e). Most antibodies targeting gB Domain IV do not directly block PRV entry but rather depend on the complement effect39. This implies Domain IV might not be a receptor-binding site for PRV gB and retains the same fold before and after membrane fusion, with domain rearrangements mediating membrane fusion.

Domain I may be involved in the interaction with gH53. When antibody 16F9 binds to gB Domain I, it potentially inhibits the interaction with gH, thereby disrupting the signal transmission from gHgL to pre-gB, which could be the underlying reason for the 16F9’s neutralizing effect. Additionally, the epitope recognized by 16F9 is located near the fusion loop of the gB protein. Therefore, upon antibody binding, it may inhibit the interaction between the gB fusion loop and the host cell membrane, contributing to its neutralizing effect. The multifaceted neutralizing mechanisms might account for the robust neutralizing activity of 16F9 against herpesviruses.

In summary, by targeting the fusion protein gB and employing a sequential DNA and protein immunization strategy, we generated a broad-spectrum neutralizing antibody, 16F9, which demonstrated effective protection against lethal HSV-1, HSV-2, and PRV challenges in mice. The epitope of 16F9 is mapped to a highly conserved region within gB domain I. We also elucidate the molecular mechanisms of 16F9’s neutralizing effects by blocking the binding of pre-gB to gHgL, thereby preventing viral membrane fusion. These results highlight the potential of 16F9’s binding site as a promising target for the development of broad-spectrum antiviral drugs and vaccines against herpesviruses.

Methods

Cell lines

293FT cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R700-07) and Vero E6 cells (mingzhoubio, MZ-2236) were obtained. MDCK cells, MRC-5, and ARPE-19 cells were gifts from Prof. Honglin Chen of Hong Kong University and Dr. Wei Wang of Xiamen University. MDCK, F81, MDBK cells were provided by Pulike Biological Engineering, Inc. (Luoyang, China). All cell lines used in this study were routinely tested for mycoplasma and found to be mycoplasma-free.

Viruses

HSV-1 (Strain 17) was a gift from Prof. Quan Yuan of Xiamen University. It was grown in MDCK cells. HSV-2 (Strain G) was obtained from the ATCC. PRV (Fa, HB2000) was grown in MDCK cells. The viral titers of HSV-1, HSV-2, and PRV were determined by plaque assay. FHV-1 (069), BHV-1 (LY), and CHV-1 (D004) were provided by Pulike Biological Engineering, Inc. (Luoyang, China). Their viral titers were determined by median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50). VZV (Oka) cell-free viruses were provided by Beijing Wantai Biological Pharmacy Enterprise Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Briefly, VZV was maintained in MRC-5 cells. VZV-infected cells with 90% cytopathic effect (CPE) were scraped into the VZV protection buffer (9% sucrose, 25 mM histidine, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.35) and stored at −80 °C. Aliquots were thawed and centrifuged at low speed (400 × g, 15 min) to obtain VZV cell-free virus8,71. The viral titer was determined by plaque assay21.

Plasmids

Gene encoding the ectodomain of PRV gB protein (AEM63980.1, residues 1–752) with the ‘GSAS’ substitutions at the furin cleavage site (residues 502–505) and C-terminal 8×His Tag was synthesized and cloned into pTT5 vector (Youbio, VT2202). Gene encoding the ectodomain of HSV-1 gB protein (P06437.2, residues 1-730) with the ‘GSAS’ substitutions at the residues 481–484 and C-terminal 8×His Tag was synthesized and cloned into the pTT5 vector. Gene encoding the ectodomain of VZV gB protein (NP_040154.2, residues 1–742) with the ‘GSAS’ substitutions at residues 491–495 and C-terminal 10×His Tag was synthesized and cloned into the pTT5 vector. Gene encoding the ectodomain of HSV-2 gB protein (YP_009137179.1, residues 1–723), BV gB protein (NP_851887.1, residues 1–713), BHV-5 gB protein (ARM56007.1, residues 1–769), BHV-2 gB protein (YP_009664618.1, residues 1–748), CpHV-1 gB protein (AAD46114.2, residues 1–748), a linker ‘GGGGS’, with C-terminal 8×His Tag were synthesized and cloned into pTT5 vector. The full-length gene sequences of gD, gH, gL, and gB were PCR-amplified from the genome of PRV (GenBank: JF797217.1). The gH-mNeonGreen fusion gene was constructed and cloned into the pTT5 vector. The genes encoding gH with N-terminal 6×His Tag, C-terminal 6×His Tag, and C-terminal HA tag were constructed and cloned into the pTT5 vector. The gB-mRuby3 fusion gene was constructed and cloned into the pTT5 vector. The gene encoding gB with a C-terminal Flag tag was constructed and cloned into the pTT5 vector. The genes encoding gD and gL were cloned into the pTT5 vector, respectively. pCAGT7 plasmid for the expression of T7 DNA polymerase and pT7EMCLuc plasmid, which expresses the luciferase reporter gene under the control of the T7 promoter, were reported previously37.

The variable region sequence of the 16F9 heavy chain was cloned into the PTT5 vector using the Gibson assembly, with an IgG signal peptide at the N-terminus and human IgG1 and mouse IgG2b constant regions at the C-terminus. Similarly, the variable region of the light chain was cloned into the PTT5 vector, with the C-terminus connected to the constant region of human or mouse Kappa chains.

Mouse immunization and monoclonal antibody production

Six-week-old female BALB/c mice were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. Initially, the mice were sequentially injected via the tail vein with 100 μg PTT5 plasmids expressing PRV, HSV-1, and VZV gB protein, respectively. Subsequently, the mice were sequentially immunized with 50 μg PRV, VZV, and HSV-1 post-fusion gB protein expressed by Expi293F cells. Each immunization was performed at 10-day intervals. Ten days following the final sequential immunization, the mice were boosted with a mixture of the three gB proteins. Three days later, spleen cells from the immunized mice were collected and fused with the mouse myeloma Sp2/0 cells72. The binding activity of antibodies in hybridoma supernatants to the gB proteins of HSV-1, PRV, and VZV was assessed using ELISA. Only hybridoma clones exhibiting positive reactivity to all three gB proteins were retained. These positive clones underwent subcloning to establish monoclonal hybridoma cell lines. This subcloning process was repeated for at least three cycles to ensure the isolation of monoclonal hybridoma cell lines with stable binding activity. Ultimately, twelve monoclonal hybridoma cell lines reactive with all three gB proteins were obtained. Hybridoma cells were injected into the peritoneal cavities of mice to induce ascites formation. Antibodies were subsequently purified from the collected ascites fluid using Protein A affinity chromatography. The purified antibodies were then subjected to neutralization assays against HSV-1 (strain 17), PRV (HB2000), and VZV (Oka). Among the purified antibodies, 16F9 demonstrated the most potent cross-genus neutralizing activity against both HSV-1 and PRV.

Sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 107 hybridoma cells using a MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa Bio Inc.). The extracted RNA was subjected to a reverse transcription reaction with the following primers: 5’-CCGTTTGKATYTCCAGCTTGGTSCC-3’ for reverse transcription of the light chain variable region gene and 5’-CGGTGACCGWGGTBCCTTGRCCCCA-3’ for reverse transcription of the heavy chain variable region gene. The coding regions of the H- and L-chains of 16F9 were amplified by PCR with the following primers: 5’-ATGGACTCCAGGCTCAATTTAGTTTTCCT-3’ (H-chain-forward) and 5’-CGGTGACCGWGGTBCCTTGRCCCCA-3’ (H-chain-reverse); and 5’-ATGAAGTTGCCTGTTAGGCTGTTGGTGCT-3’ (L-chain-forward) and 5’-CCGTTTGKATYTCCAGCTTGGTSCC-3’ (L-chain-reverse). The PCR products were sequenced. The sequences of the antibody variable regions were verified using BLAST alignment, and the corresponding amino acid sequences were determined.

Protein expression and purification

Plasmids for the expression of HSV-1, HSV-2, BV, BHV-2, BHV-5, CpHV-1, PRV, and VZV gB proteins were transiently transfected into Expi293F cells using PEI. After an expression period of 4–5 days, the cell-free supernatant was collected for purification. Subsequently, the proteins were purified by Ni Sepharose 6 Fast Flow (Cytiva) column. The purified proteins were stored at 4 °C in PBS buffer.

The paired heavy and light chain expression plasmids were then transiently co-transfected into Expi293F cells with equal amounts of plasmids according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Life Technologies). The antibodies were purified from cell supernatants using Protein A affinity chromatography (GenScript).

The antigen-binding fragments (Fabs) of 16F9 were prepared by digesting the antibody with papain at a papain-to-antibody mass ratio of 1:400. The digestion was performed at 37 °C for 12 h, followed by Fabs purification using a Protein A column, where Fabs were collected from the flow-through.

Immunofluorescence assay

MDCK, F81, MDBK, Vero E6, and ARPE-19 Cells were seeded into 96-well plates. Infections were performed as follows: MDCK cells with PRV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01, HSV-1 (MOI = 0.001), and CHV-1 (MOI = 0.01); F81 cells with FHV-1 (MOI = 0.01); ARPE-19 cells with VZV (MOI = 0.03); Vero E6 cells with HSV-2 (MOI = 0.01); and MDBK cells with BHV-1 (MOI = 0.1). Plasmids containing the BHV-5, CPHV-1, BV, and BHV-2 gB gene were transfected into MDCK cells according to the transfection reagent instructions (L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Twenty-four hours after viral infection or plasmid transfection, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, BEYOTIME) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. They were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min at room temperature, followed by blocking with goat serum (001, Wantai) for 2 h at 37 °C. The viral gB protein was labeled with primary antibodies and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Cells were washed five times and then labeled with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (A-21202, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-human IgG(H + L) (A-11013, Invitrogen) and incubated for 45 min at 37 °C. After five additional washes, cells were stained with DAPI (D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark to visualize nuclei. Images were acquired using the high-content screening imaging system (Opera Phenix).

Surface plasmon resonance assays (SPR)

The Biacore 8K system (GE Healthcare) was used to determine the affinity of the 16F9 antibody for the gB proteins of 8 different viruses: BHV-5, CpHV-1, PRV, HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, BHV-2, and BV. At the beginning of each cycle, the NTA chip was supplemented with Ni²⁺ using a 0.5 mM NiSO₄ solution. gB proteins were captured by NTA Sensor at a concentration of 1 μg/mL and a flow rate of 10 μL/min for 60 s. Followed by the gradient diluted 16F9 flowed through the sensor at a flow rate of 30 μL/min for 120 s and dissociated for 600 s. The regeneration solution (350 mM EDTA) was used after each cycle. Biacore Insight Evaluation software was used to perform multiple kinetic model fitting analyses on the data.

Binding assay for nAb by indirect ELISA

The binding activity was evaluated based on the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50). 10 μg alphaherpesvirus gB protein (BHV-5, CpHV-1, PRV, VZV, HSV-1, HSV-2, BV, and BHV-2) was coated onto a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h or at 4 °C overnight. After incubation, the plate was washed once with PBST washing solution. The wells were then blocked with NRA blocking solution and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h or at 4 °C overnight. The antibody concentration was initiated at 0.1 mg/mL and serially diluted in 10-fold times. The diluted antibodies were added to the coated plates and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the plates were washed 5 times with PBST washing solution. Subsequently, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse or goat anti-human IgG were added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the plates were washed 5 times. Finally, the substrate solution was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C, and stopped by 50 μL of 2 M H2SO4. OD was determined at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 630 nm.

Neutralization assay

Neutralization activity was evaluated based on the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50). Cells corresponding to different viral strains were grown in 96-well plates. The antibody concentration started at 1 mg/ml and was serially diluted in 2-fold times. The diluted antibodies were incubated with 200 pfu of viruses (including PRV Fa, HSV-1 Strain 17, HSV-2 Strain G, and VZV OKa) at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the antibody–virus mixture was added to the MDCK, MDCK, Vero E6, and ARPE-19 cells, respectively, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Following infection, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, BEYOTIME) at room temperature, protected from light, for 15 min. Subsequently, cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 15 min, and then blocked at 37 °C for 2 h. The cells were then incubated with virus-specific enzyme-labeled antibodies at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by washing 5 times with PBST washing solution. Substrate solution was added to the wells. After the appearance of distinct blue spots, the substrate solution was removed, and the plates were photographed and quantitated using an Elispot reader. BHV-1 (LY), CHV (D004), and FHV (069) were incubated with diluted antibodies at a dose of 100 TCID50, and then infected MBCK, MDCK, and F81 cells, respectively, for 48 h. The cytopathic effects (CPE) were observed under a microscope. Four replicates were conducted for each antibody concentration. The presence or absence of CPE in the wells was used to assess the neutralizing activity of the antibodies. Finally, a neutralization curve was plotted based on the extent of CPE at various antibody concentrations.

Protective activity of nAb against alphaherpesviruses in mice

To explore the therapeutic activity of the drug against HSV-1 infection, 6–8-week-old female BALB/c mice were infected intranasally with HSV-1 (3 × 104 pfu). Antibodies were administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 20 mg/kg, was administered orally at a dose of 20 mg/kg, once daily for three consecutive days. The mice’s body weight and survival rate were monitored daily.

For evaluating the protective activity of the drug against HSV-1-induced herpes simplex keratitis, the corneas of 6–8-week-old female BALB/c mice were scarified in a 2 × 2 cross-hatch pattern. Mice were then inoculated with HSV-1 (6 × 103 pfu) in the right eyes. One day later, antibodies were administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 20 mg/kg and then orally at a dose of 20 mg/kg, once daily for three consecutive days. The mice’s body weight and survival rate were monitored daily. On the sixth day post-infection, mice's brains were collected to assess HSV-1 viral titers.

To examine the therapeutic activity of the drug against HSV-1-induced skin lesions, hair removal was performed on the lower right abdominal region of 6-week-old female BALB/c mice using a depilatory cream. Three days later, 4 × 105 pfu HSV-1 viruses were subcutaneously injected into the skin of the right hind limb of the mice, followed by continuous observation. For the subsequent three days, mice were administered drugs once daily (antibodies at a dose of 20 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection). The mice’s body weight, survival rate, and progression of skin lesions were monitored daily.

To investigate the therapeutic activity of the drug against PRV infection-related mortality, 6–8-week-old female BALB/c mice were challenged intraperitoneally with PRV (3 × 103 pfu). Mice were treated two hours after infection, as well as on days 1 and 2 post-infection (antibodies at a dose of 20 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection). The mice’s body weight and survival rate were monitored daily.

To investigate the prophylactic efficacy of the drug against neonatal herpesvirus, pregnant C57BL/6 mice were administered antibodies intravenously at a dose of 50 mg/kg at a dose of 50 mg/kg two days prior to delivery. On the third day after birth, pups were challenged intranasally with HSV-1 (500 pfu in 1 μL) or HSV-2 (250 pfu in 1 μL) viruses, and their survival rate was monitored.

To explore the therapeutic activity of the drug against HSV-1 infection, 6–8-week-old female BALB/c mice were infected intranasally with HSV-1 (3 × 104 pfu). Mice were treated with control antibody (Ctrl Ab) (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally), C16F9 (20, 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25, 0.63, 0.31, 0.16 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) at days 1–3 post-infection. The mice’s body weight and survival rate were monitored daily.

The guidelines for humane endpoints were strictly followed for all in vivo experiments; animals that lost more than 25% of their initial body weight were immediately euthanized and were recorded as nonsurvivors. After the death of the mice, their weight was recorded as 70% of their initial weight72.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

Aliquots (3 µL) of 3 mg/mL mixtures of either purified HSV-2, BV, PRV, and VZV gB proteins, respectively, in complex with 16F9 Fab fragments were prepared and loaded onto glow-discharged (80 s at 20 mA) holey carbon Quantifoil grids (AU 1.2/1.3, 300 mesh, Quantifoil Micro Tools) using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 100% humidity and 4 °C. Data were acquired using the EPU software on a Titan Krios G4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV. Images were recorded using a Gatan K3 detector in a 32-frame movie mode at a nominal ×130,000 magnification with a super-resolution pixel size of 0.325 Å, and the total electron dose was set to 48 e− Å−2, with an exposure time of 1.35 s.

Image processing and 3D reconstruction

Drift and beam-induced motion correction were performed with MotionCor273 to produce a micrograph from each movie. Contrast transfer function (CTF) fitting and phase-shift estimation were conducted with Gctf74. Micrographs with astigmatism, obvious drift, or contamination were discarded before reconstruction. The following reconstruction procedures were performed using Cryosparc V375. In brief, particles were automatically picked using the “Blob picker” or “Template picker.” Several rounds of reference-free 2D classifications were performed, and the selected “good” particles were then subjected to ab initio reconstruction, heterogeneous refinement, and final non-uniform refinement. The final map was generated by deepEMhancer76. Localized refinement focusing on the local areas was also performed if necessary. The resolutions of all density maps were determined by the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation curve, with a cutoff of 0.14377. Local resolutions of maps were estimated with ResMap78.

Atomic model building, refinement, and 3D visualization

The initial model of the 16F9 Fab and BV gB was generated from homology modeling by Acelrys Discovery Studio software (https://www.3dsbiovia.com). The structure of the HSV-2 gB was modeled from the structure of the HSV-1 gB in complex with D48 Fab (PDB accession code 8KFA40). The structure of the PRV gB was modeled from the structure of pseudorabies virus glycoprotein B (PDB accession code 6ESC79). The structure of the VZV gB was derived from the structure of the VZV gB-nAb Complex (PDB accession code 6VN180). The initial HSV-2 pre gB structural model was built by homology modeling with Swiss-Model using HSV-1 pre gB (PDB accession code 6Z9M) as a template. We initially fitted the models into the corresponding final cryo-EM maps using Chimera81, and further corrected and adjusted them manually by real-space refinement in Coot82. The resulting models were then refined with phenix.real_space_refine in PHENIX83. These operations were executed iteratively until the problematic regions, Ramachandran outliers, and poor rotamers were either eliminated or moved to favored regions. The final atomic models were validated with Molprobity84,85. All figures were generated with ChimeraX86,87.

Virus-free cell fusion assay

It has been established and employed in previous studies54. A 10 cm dish of 293FT cells was co-transfected with plasmids (pCAGGS-T7, PTT5-gD, PTT5-gH, PTT5-gL, and PTT5-gB)54. Another 10 cm dish of 293FT cells was only transfected with pT7EMCLuc. After 8 h, two kinds of cells were mixed and cultured in a certain proportion. At the same time, 20 μg antibodies were added. Two days later, the Luciferase activity in the cells was measured with One-LiteTM Luciferase assay system (Vazyme, Cat# DD1203).

Cellular staining

293FT cells were transfected with a four-plasmid system expressing PRV glycoproteins. To ensure uniform transfection efficiency, cells derived from a 10 cm culture dish were homogenously seeded into cell plates. Transfection complexes (prepared by thorough mixing of transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Cat#L3000015) and plasmids) were equitably distributed across all wells. 293FT cells transfected with plasmids that mediate cell fusion were washed 3 times with PBS and fixed for 15 min with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. Then, cells were permeabilized for 15 min in 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS and blocked with 3% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following, cells were stained with fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI. Images were captured using ZEISS LSM 900 with Airyscan 2 and analyzed using ZEISS ZEN 3.10. Antibodies: anti-gB (1:1000; 16F9), anti-β-catenin (1:200; BD, Cat# 610153), Alexa FluorTM 488 goat anti-human IgG(H + L) (1:200; Invitrogen, Cat# A-11013), Alexa FluorTM 488 goat anti-mouse IgG(H + L) (1:200; Invitrogen, Cat# A-21202), Alexa FluorTM 647 donkey anti-mouse IgG(H + L) (1:200; Invitrogen, Cat# A-31571).

Co-IP

293FT cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well in a six-well plate. After 12 h, plasmids (PTT5-gD, PTT5-gL, PTT5-gH-HA, PTT5-gB-Flag) were transfected into the cells. Concurrently, 16F9 and control antibody were added at a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. After 24 h of transfection, the culture supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. A lysis buffer containing PSMF (50 mM Tris-base, 0.3 M NaCl, 12.7 mM EDTA, 1 mM PSMF) was added, and the cells underwent three cycles of freeze-thawing using liquid nitrogen. The mixture was centrifuged at 400×g for 1 min at 4 °C. Remove the supernatant, add IP solution containing PSMF (BEYOTIME, P0013), perform ultrasonic fragmentation, centrifuge at 16,099 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and remove insoluble substances. The supernatant was divided into groups, and suitable amounts of anti-flag magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, HY-K0207A), along with corresponding 16F9 and control antibody at a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL were added to the IP group. This mixture was incubated on a shaker at 4 °C overnight. The magnetic beads were washed four times with IP buffer. An appropriate volume of 1× loading buffer was added, and the samples were boiled at 95 °C for 10 minutes. Protein was separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto an NC membrane. The membrane was blocked at room temperature for two hours with blocking solution 1 (5% non-fat milk), followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. The membrane was washed three times with the wash solution and incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 hour. The proteins were visualized with NcmECL Ultra (A + B) (NCM Biotech, P10300), and images were acquired by Fusion FX7 Edge.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays

The gene encoding the full-length gB protein of PRV with amino acids 1–172 of mVenus fluorescent protein was cloned into the PTT5 plasmid (PTT5-gB-VN173). The gene encoding the full-length gH protein of PRV with amino acids 173-238 of mVenus fluorescent protein was cloned into the PTT5 plasmid (PTT5-gH-VC173). When gB and gH interact, the two mVenus fluorescent residues located at the C-terminal will form a complete mVenus fluorescent protein, emitting a fluorescence signal55. 293FT cells were transfected with PTT5-gB-VN173, PTT5-gH-VC173, PTT5-gD, and PTT5-gL, added corresponding control antibody or 16F9 to different experimental groups, cultured in an incubator for 24 h, and then perform fluorescence staining and photography.

ADCC assay

It has been established and employed in previous studies88. Initially, gB proteins were immobilized on plates for 2 h, followed by PBS washes and blocking with RPMI 1640 containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Antibodies (0.1 mg/mL) were added and incubated for 2 h. After two PBS washes, 2B4-FcγRIV effector cells were introduced. This engineered stable cell line expresses murine Fcγ-receptor IV (FcγRIV), which triggers green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression upon receptor activation. The GFP fluorescence was detected using Opera Phenix system (PerkinElmer).

Flow cytometry

To assess the binding activity of 16F9 to HSV-1 gB and HSV-1 gB (H516P), plasmid encoding HSV-1 gB or HSV-1 gB (H516P) was transfected into HEK293F cells using polyethyleneimine (PEI, Yeasen). Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the cells were collected and incubated with APC (Abcam)-Conjugated 16F9 or a control IgG at 4 °C for 1 h. After three washes, the fluorescence signal intensities of APC were measured using the BD LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer. Flow data was analyzed using FlowJo software.

Statistics and reproducibility

GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1) was used for all statistical calculations. To establish significant differences for survival curves, we used the log-rank test in GraphPad Prism 8.0.1. The curve of mouse body weight was obtained using the AUC analysis, statistical analysis was performed by one-way ANOVA. For statistical difference analysis, P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. ns: no significant difference; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001. IC50 and EC50 values were calculated using four-parameter non-linear regression. All experiments were repeated independently with similar results for at least three times.
