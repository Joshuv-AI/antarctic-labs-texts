An 86-year-old woman presented to our emergency department with a 1-week history of worsening of chronic dyspnea and new confusion. She reported some dyspnea at baseline, which was under investigation, attributed in part to obstructive lung disease. Her medical history included paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, hypertension, asthma, obstructive sleep apnea, spinal stenosis, kyphosis leading to spinal shortening, osteoporosis, gastroesophageal reflux disease, dyslipidemia, prior ischemic cerebellar stroke, and hypothyroidism. She lived at home independently. She was referred to our general internal medicine service for hypoxic respiratory failure and delirium. Her home medications included apixaban, bisoprolol, ezetimibe, levothyroxine, budesonide–formoterol, and rosuvastatin. When assessed by the general internal medicine service, the patient was afebrile, peripheral oxygen saturation was 93% on 2-L nasal prongs, blood pressure was 149/107 mm Hg, heart rate was 88 beats/min, and respiratory rate was 22 breaths/min. There was good air entry at bases bilaterally, without adventitious sounds. A systolic murmur was heard at the left sternal border, with no peripheral edema or jugular venous distension.

Initial investigations included a white blood cell count of 19.3 (normal 4.0 to 10.0) × 109/L with a urinalysis positive for leukocytes and blood but negative for nitrites. Chest radiography was normal. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with intermittent premature ventricular contractions, left axis deviation, and left ventricular hypertrophy. Noncontrast computed tomography (CT) of the head did not show an acute intracranial process. We admitted the patient with a provisional diagnosis of a pulmonary infection contributing to delirium and started empiric antibiotics with ceftriaxone, 1 g intravenously once daily, and azithromycin, 500 mg orally once daily.

On day 3 of admission, CT pulmonary angiography was negative for pulmonary embolism but showed peribronchial thickening and peribronchovascular soft tissue stranding, with subpleural reticulations in the lower lobes. These findings were not present on CT of the thorax coincidentally done 8 days before the patient’s emergency department presentation, as ordered by her outpatient respirologist to investigate chronic dyspnea. We suspected an acute infectious or inflammatory process, and broadened her antibiotics to piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g intravenously every 6 hours. Blood cultures showed no growth after 2 days of incubation. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), ordered to assess for evidence of heart failure, showed hyperdynamic left ventricular function with mid-cavity systolic obliteration and associated 40 mm Hg mid-cavity gradient with no change with Valsalva manoeuvre.

Despite broad-spectrum antibiotics, completing appropriate treatment for community-acquired pneumonia, and resolution of her infectious symptoms, the patient’s oxygen requirements continued to worsen. She was placed on oxygen via high-flow nasal cannula, without improvement, and when the cannula fell off no change in her oxygen saturation was noted. The patient described improvement of her dyspnea when lying flat, consistent with platypnea. We then noted orthodeoxia on clinical examination, prompting us to order contrast-enhanced echocardiography with bubble study, injecting agitated saline into a vein under direct visualization of the cardiac chambers via echocardiography, to assess for ashunt leading to platypnea–orthodeoxia syndrome (POS). This diagnostic study was strongly positive, with saline contrast causing near complete opacification of the left ventricle within 1 to 2 cycles, in keeping with a large right-to-left shunt. The differential diagnosis of the intracardiac shunt included an anatomic defect, such as a patent foramen ovale (PFO) or atrial septal defect (ASD).

The consideration of a PFO leading to POS prompted further confirmatory investigation with a right heart catheterization to assess for shunt and determine its hemodynamic significance. Preprocedure transesophageal echocardiography showed a stretched PFO measuring 9 × 16 mm with bidirectional flow (Figure 1A), an aneurysmal interatrial septum, and lipomatous hypertrophy of the interatrial septum (Figure 1B). Catheterization showed a hemodynamically significant right-to-left shunt across the PFO with normal right atrial pressure of 3 mm Hg (Appendix 1, Supplementary Table 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251337/tab-related-content). The interventional cardiology team was able to close the PFO endovascularly with a 37-mm Gore Cardioform ASD Occluder on postadmission day 27. The patient’s hypoxia immediately resolved, and postprocedure testing confirmed no residual shunting. We started apixaban and clopidogrel, and bisoprolol because of the left ventricular outflow tract obstruction noted on TTE.

The patient unfortunately suffered a fall while still in hospital and on CT of her head was found to have an intracranial hemorrhage, determined to be a result of trauma rather than her anticoagulation. This required an interruption in her anticoagulation therapy. The patient’s resulting cognitive deficits complicated her functional improvement to the point that she could not continue to live independently. Six weeks after admission, the patient was transferred to the subacute medicine unit, from which she subsequently went to rehabilitation before going to long-term care.

A shunt should be considered when supplemental oxygen does not lead to a sufficient rise in oxygen saturation, as seen in our patient.1 Shunts are usually intracardiac (most commonly a PFO) or intrapulmonic.1–3 Pulmonary shunts occur when venous blood bypasses alveolar–arterial blood gas exchange.1 Intracardiac shunts result from mixing of blood from venous and arterial circulations because of an abnormal connection, as seen in complex congenital heart disease.1,2 Left-to-right shunting occurs when oxygenated blood from the pulmonary veins returns directly back to the lungs rather than entering the systemic circulation.2 Comparatively, right-to-left shunting enables deoxygenated venous blood to be delivered to the body without appropriate oxygenation via alveolar–arterial gas exchange, as in our patient.2 The degree of shunting usually determines its clinical importance.2

Platypnea–orthodeoxia syndrome is relatively rare, defined as positional dyspnea and arterial hypoxemia characterized by a drop in peripheral oxygen saturation by greater than 4 mm Hg or arterial oxygen saturation greater than 5% when moving from the supine to upright position.4 Associated symptoms include platypnea and are dictated by the underlying mechanism of POS, caused by either caval blood flow returning through the atrial defect or by a transient increase in pressure in the right atrium resulting in a right-to-left pressure gradient.3,4 Most cases describe 1 anatomic and 1 functional defect resulting in right-to-left shunting. 3 A high degree of clinical suspicion is required, with careful history-taking to deduce possible etiologies (Table 2).3 The differential diagnosis of POS is categorized into intracardiac, extracardiac (typically pulmonary), combined cardiopulmonary, and miscellaneous causes (Table 2).3 Intracardiac etiologies are further subdivided into those with normal and elevated right atrial pressure. Intracardiac right-to-left shunt with normal right atrial pressure requires an anatomic and functional defect. Anatomic defects are usually in the form of a PFO or ASD; however, they have also been described in the presence of a fenestrated atrial septal aneurysm.3,5

Transthoracic echocardiography bubble study can differentiate an intracardiac versus extracardiac shunt and is the initial imaging modality of choice to appropriately assess intracardiac shunting.6,7 Right-to-left shunting, as seen in our patient, is first identified with a TTE bubble study2,6 when contrast is found within the left atrium within 3 cardiac cycles following right atrial opacification.6 A definitive diagnosis is made via right heart catheterization showing a decrease in oxygen saturation between the pulmonary veins and aorta and to determine if the shunt is hemodynamically significant and requires closure.3 A right heart catheterization shunt study can quantify the ratio of total pulmonary blood flow to systemic blood flow, with a ratio less than 1 identifying a net right-to-left shunt.2 Transesophageal echocardiography can be used to provide further anatomical detail of the shunt lesion.8

Patent foramen ovales occur in approximately 25% of the general population and are typically asymptomatic.2 Precipitating factors, such as pneumonia in our patient, can acutely increase pulmonary pressures and result in horizontal distortion of the heart, which perpetuates preferential right-to-left shunting across the PFO, leading to POS.4 Kyphosis and spinal shortening have been linked to right-to-left shunting via a PFO owing to alterations of the orientation of the heart in the thorax.9 Our patient had an acute medical illness in combination with contributing comorbidities, leading to preferential shunting across the PFO, causing POS.

Complications related to PFO include embolic stroke, migraines, air embolism, decompression sickness, and POS.7 Management depends largely on associated complications.10 For patients with PFO-associated POS, defect closure is currently reccomended.10 Risks of closure include bleeding, infection, damage to surrounding structures of the heart, arrhythmia, and stroke.10 Pulmonary hypertension should be investigated before any intervention to prevent postoperative clinical deterioration and perpetuation of right-sided heart failure. Closure immediately stops right-to-left shunting at the level of the PFO, thereby curing intracardiac POS.

Our case highlights the mechanism of right-to-left shunting with normal right atrial pressure. An acute respiratory illness, along with predisposing factors of spinal shortening and kyphosis, contributed to altered blood flow leading to right-to-left shunting through the PFO, causing POS. Platypnea–orthodeoxia syndrome should be considered when a patient describes symptoms consistent with platypnea. It is important to consider intracardiac shunts in patients with nonresolving hypoxia, particularly when hypoxia is minimally responsive to supplemental oxygen.
