Prevalence and stable acquisition of biogenic amine-synthesizing genes in lactic acid bacteria across diverse niches: implications for food safety and human health
Authors: Aqib Javaid, Nazia Tabassum, Abirami Karthikeyan, Young-Mog Kim, Won-Kyo Jung, Fazlurrahman Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiogenic amines (BAs) are metabolites produced by lactic acid bacteria (LAB) with implications for food safety and human health. Despite extensive phenotypic studies on individual BA biosynthesis genes, the distribution, evolutionary dynamics, ecological associations, and mobility mechanisms underpinning BA biosynthesis across LAB strains is unclear. Here, we conducted the first comprehensive genomic survey of 4880 LAB genomes to elucidate the prevalence and mobility of key BA genes. Ornithine decarboxylase (odc) showed a broader taxonomic distribution, while tyrosine decarboxylase (tdc), although predominant, was confined to Enterococcus. All BA genes exhibited strong ecological associations, suggesting niche-specific adaptation. Phylogenomic analysis highlighted complex evolutionary trajectories involving ancestral gains and extensive horizontal gene transfer (HGT), particularly for odc and tdc genes. Gene synteny and compositional signatures further supported lineage-specific retention and recurrent HGT events among phylogenetically related taxa. For example, the arginine decarboxylase (adc) gene and agmatinase were potentially co-acquired as an operon-level unit. Codon usage suggested translational assimilation, reflecting the functional integration of BA genes in LAB. Insertion sequences and composite transposons were predicted as putative vectors mediating BA gene mobility. Collectively, this study provides a framework linking BA gene distribution to niche and mobility, with implications for risk assessment in foods and probiotics.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Evolution, Genetics, Microbiology

Introduction

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are a diverse group of Gram-positive, facultative anaerobic microorganisms that play essential roles in ecological and industrial processes1. They contribute significantly to food fermentation and probiotics through the production of organic acids, bacteriocins, and other bioactive compounds, thus enhancing food preservation, flavor, and functionality2,3. Their genetic diversity enables adaptation to various niches, including plants, dairy products, and the gastrointestinal tract4. Within the functional food industry, LAB are valued for their probiotic properties and their ability to produce health-promoting metabolites5. Recent research has focused on harnessing LAB in synthetic microbial consortia to address challenges in food security, environmental sustainability, and human health.

LAB are widely recognized as safe (GRAS/QPS) due to their long history of use in food fermentation and their recognized health benefits6. However, several recent studies have shown that some LAB strains can produce biogenic amines (BAs), low molecular weight compounds that are potentially toxic7,8. High concentrations of BAs, particularly histamine and tyramine, can cause adverse health effects, including migraines, hypertension, and gastrointestinal distress9,10.

The biosynthesis of BAs such as histamine, tyramine, agmatine, and putrescine is mediated by specific decarboxylase genes, often organized in operons11. Genes encoding tyrosine decarboxylase (tdc), histidine decarboxylase (hdc), ornithine decarboxylase (odc), arginine decarboxylase (adc), and agmatinase may be chromosomally encoded or located on mobile genetic elements (MGEs), facilitating horizontal gene transfer (HGT) among strains and species12. The expression of these genes is also influenced by environmental factors such as pH, with acid stress potentially enhancing BA production11.

Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing and genomics have significantly improved our understanding of LAB genetics and BA synthesis. However, challenges remain, particularly in BA detection and population-level genomic analysis across diverse environments13–15. Metagenomic studies have identified key BA-producing LAB genera in fermented foods such as sufu16, underscoring the need for genome-scale investigations to unravel the evolution of BA biosynthesis and its implications for food safety.

These advancements highlight the critical importance of genome-scale studies in addressing key questions related to the evolutionary dynamics of BA biosynthesis, such as: which LAB lineages are enriched in BA biosynthesis genes? Are these genes lineage-specific or acquired through HGT? Are they more prevalent in strains isolated from specific niches (e.g., clinical, dairy, meat, and gut)? What is the potential for these genes to mobilize and spread across LAB populations?

In this study, we address these questions through a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of BA biosynthesis genes in 4880 high-quality LAB genomes. We integrated comparative genomics, phylogenetics, gene synteny, compositional analysis, codon adaptation, and mobile element association to characterize the occurrence, distribution, ecological association, phylogeny, and mobility of major BA biosynthesis genes (tdc, hdc, odc, adc, and agmatinase) across a diverse LAB dataset. This system-level approach offers new insights into the evolutionary mechanisms driving BA gene dissemination and highlights the ecological and biosafety implications of aminobiogenic traits in LAB used in fermented foods and probiotic formulations.

Collectively, our study represents the most extensive and detailed survey of the BA biosynthetic potential of LAB to date. It provides a high-resolution map of BA biosynthesis gene distribution across the LAB phylogeny. Since BAs such as histamine, tyramine, and putrescine are recognized toxicants with both acute and chronic health risks, our findings have direct implications for food safety and human exposure. In particular, the widespread occurrence of BA genes in LAB raises concerns about the safety of starter cultures, probiotics, and spontaneously fermented foods, emphasizing their relevance as emerging biological hazards. By addressing a critical gap in the understanding of the global distribution, diversity, and mobility of BA-producing genes, this study lays the foundation for targeted hazard assessment, microbial risk monitoring, and the development of mitigation strategies. Ultimately, these insights contribute to safer management of BA contamination in food systems and provide guidance for the regulatory oversight of LAB applications in food and health.

Results

Construction of a high-quality genome dataset

Initially, we retrieved 14,828 genomes representing all major genera of LAB. A rigorous, multi-step filtering process yielded a final dataset of 4880 high-quality genomes for downstream analysis (Supplementary Data 1 and Fig. S1). The selected LAB genomes belong to the phylum Firmicutes, class Bacilli, and order Lactobacillales. Specifically, the dataset comprises genomes from 16 genera: Enterococcus (n = 2126), Lactiplantibacillus (n = 767), Companilactobacillus (n = 25), Fructilactobacillus (n = 20), Lacticaseibacillus (n = 596), Lactobacillus (n = 189), Latilactobacillus (n = 79), Lentilactobacillus (n = 60), Leuconostoc (n = 236), Ligilactobacillus (n = 90), Limosilactobacillus (n = 156), Liquorilactobacillus (n = 3), Oenococcus (n = 17), Pediococcus (n = 309), Secundilactobacillus (n = 4), and Weissella (n = 174).

Identification of BA genes across LAB genomes

Across the 4880 high-quality LAB genomes, we identified five distinct BA biosynthesis genes with highly uneven distributions. Genes such as tdc (n = 2449) and odc (n = 959) were the most prevalent, whereas hdc, adc, and agmatinase were detected only sporadically ( ≤ 40 genomes). Because KEGG orthogroups (K01581, K01582, and K01585) overlap among ldc, odc, and adc, we re-evaluated 998 ambiguous sequences corresponding to these orthogroups to refine their functional identity. This curation confirmed 39 bona fide adc genes, while the remaining 959 sequences clustered closely with odc homologs (Fig. 1aand Supplementary Data 2).

Gene occurrence was strongly genus-specific (Fig. 1b and Table S1). The tdc gene was significantly restricted to members of Enterococcus, with limited presence in a few other LAB species. In contrast, odc was taxonomically widespread across multiple genera, including Lacticaseibacillus, Liquorilactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, Companilactobacillus, and Lactobacillus. In contrast, hdc was detected exclusively in Fructilactobacillus, Lentilactobacillus, and Limosilactobacillus. The rarest BA biosynthesis genes in the dataset, adc and agmatinase, were identified in Leuconostoc, Enterococcus, and Lactiplantibacillus genomes.

Co-occurrence of BA biosynthesis genes was rare and observed only in three dual combinations: odc + tdc (n = 13), adc + agmatinase (n = 29), and odc + agmatinase (n = 4), each found in fewer than 1% of genomes (Fig. 1c). Notably, 18.3% of genomes carried only odc, 38.8% only tdc, and 41.1% lacked all BA genes. At the genus level, the distribution of these genes and their combinations revealed substantial variation. The greatest diversity of gene combinations was observed in Companilactobacillus, Enterococcus, Lacticaseibacillus, and Latilactobacillus (Fig. 1d). For instance, the odc + tdc combination was found in 3.4% of Companilactobacillus genomes, adc + agmatinase in 1.3% of Enterococcus genomes, and odc + agmatinase in 0.7% of Lacticaseibacillus genomes. In contrast, other genera were dominated by single-gene profiles. Together, these genus-specific patterns highlight strong, lineage-specific trends in BA synthesis potential.

Furthermore, analysis of the global prevalence and temporal distribution of BA genes (Fig. 2a–e) by country and isolation date revealed a broad geographic spread. The earliest recorded occurrences were: odc in 1901, tdc in 1906, adc and agmatinase in 1986, and hdc in 1990. Notably, the number of isolates harboring these genes increased significantly after 2000, indicating a recent expansion. Country-wise data on BA gene prevalence, including the number of BA-positive genomes, total genomes analyzed per country, prevalence (%), and 95% confidence intervals, are provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Metabolic completeness and co-occurrence of BA biosynthesis pathways across LAB

The metabolic routes for BA biosynthesis in bacteria are illustrated in Fig. 3a. Among LAB genomes, the tyramine biosynthesis pathway was the most prevalent, with complete tdc open reading frame (ORF) identified in 1888 genomes. This was followed by the putrescine_1 (n = 55), histamine (n = 40), and putrescine_3 (n = 28) pathways (Fig. 3b). An additional 10 genomes carried only the adc gene, indicating potential for agmatine production. Only one genome contained a combination of the tyramine and putrescine_1 pathway. No genomes possessed the complete putrescine_2 pathway.

Despite the widespread occurrence of BA biosynthesis genes across LAB, complete metabolic pathways were rarely present within individual genomes. Instead, our analysis revealed a scattered and discontinuous distribution of these genes across numerous LAB genera. This inconsistent distribution prompted further investigation into the evolutionary origins and mobility of these genes.

Distinct ecological niche preferences of BA genes

The niche-specific correlation analysis revealed significant variation in the distribution of BA biosynthetic genes across seven distinct isolation sources: animal (n = 227), clinical (n = 609), environmental (n = 575), feces (n = 921), food (n = 1952), gut (n = 448), and probiotics (n = 84) (Fig. 3c). The most prevalent genes, tdc (adjusted p = 5.1 × 10−226) and odc (adjusted p = 6.3 × 10−44), exhibited the strongest statistical associations with specific niches (Table S2). Although tdc was broadly distributed across all niches, its abundance was highest in fecal and clinical isolates. In contrast, odc showed pronounced enrichment in food-associated genomes and was much less represented in other sources. The less prevalent genes, adc, agmatinase, and hdc, showed more restricted distributions. The hdc gene was significantly associated with food samples, whereas adc and agmatinase, although infrequent overall, frequently co-occurred in clinical isolates. Figure 3d shows the genus-specific prevalence of each BA gene in statistically significant niches. Both adc and agmatinase were prevalent in Enterococcus isolates from clinical samples. hdc was associated with Lentilactobacillus, followed by Fructilactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus. In contrast, odc was significantly present in food-associated Lacticaseibacillus, followed by Lactobacillus species. tdc was found across diverse ecological niches, with Enterococcus being the dominant genus. Moreover, both odc and tdc were also found in probiotic strains (Fig. 3e), particularly odc, in members of Lactobacillus, Lacticaseibacillus, and Ligilactobacillus.

Reconstruction of a core genome-based phylogenetic framework using a de-replicated genome set

All 4,880 LAB genomes were clustered into a non-redundant dataset of 836 secondary clusters at 95% ANI to preserve strain-level variation (Fig. S2). The core genome phylogeny reconstructed from this dataset resolved all major clades consistent with established genus-level taxonomy, with strong branch support ( > 95%) at all major nodes (core-species phylogeny with node support values shown in Fig. S3). This robust and well-supported tree provided a reliable framework for tracing the evolutionary history of BA biosynthesis genes across LAB (Fig. 4a). The presence and absence of these genes, overlaid onto the core genome species tree, revealed substantial heterogeneity across LAB taxa, with distinct patterns for each gene (detailed gene presence/absence matrix in Supplementary Data 4). The adc and agmatinase genes were almost exclusively confined to Enterococcus species, while hdc was detected in only three distinct clades, one Lentilactobacillus, one Limosilactobacillus, and two Fructilactobacillus species. In contrast, odc was widely distributed across multiple genera, including Companilactobacillus, Latilactobacillus, Lactobacillus, Lacticaseibacillus, Ligilactobacillus, and Liquorilactobacillus. The tdc gene showed a predominantly Enterococcus-restricted distribution, with sporadic occurrences in other genera such as Limosilactobacillus, Latilactobacillus, Companilactobacillus, and Pediococcus.

Ancestral acquisition followed by vertical and horizontal transmission of BA genes

Both adc and agmatinase genes were absent at the root, with marginal probabilities of presence (MPP) of 0.02 and 0.28, respectively. These genes remained absent across all deep ancestral nodes, with presence inferred only at terminal branches (Fig. 4b and c). For both genes, a single high-confidence gain event was inferred on the branch leading to node n114114 (adc: MPP = 0.95; agmatinase: MPP = 0.83). Following this acquisition, both genes appeared to have been stably maintained in all descendant genomes of Enterococcus cecorum and Enterococcus saccharolyticus. No additional independent acquisition events for adc or agmatinases were detected (detailed ancestral state and MPP data in Supplementary Data 5 and 6).

The hdc gene showed multiple independent gains with MPP approaching 1.00 at terminal nodes, and absence at ancestral nodes such as n361, n305, n24524, and n220220 (Fig. 4d). No single ancestral node had strong support for a shared gain event (MPP = ~0.01). The odc gene exhibited a broader and more phylogenetically-coherent pattern than the other BA genes (Fig. 4e). It was predicted to be absent at the root (MPP = 0.21) and most deep ancestral nodes. Multiple high-confidence gains were inferred at nodes n221 (MPP = 0.98), n130 (MPP = 0.95), and n162126 (MPP = 0.92), followed by clade-specific inheritance. Moreover, Latilactobacillus exhibited two terminal odc gain events.

Similarly, ASR of tdc revealed a major gain event at the base of the Enterococcus lineage (node n2; MPP = 0.96), suggesting a single ancestral acquisition event (Fig. 4f). Following this gain, the gene was largely retained within Enterococcus species. However, two subclades comprising E. casseiflavus, E. innesii, E. entomosocium, E. galinarum, E. cecorum, E. saccharolyticus, and E. avium lacked the tdc gene. Notably, within one of these subclades, E. dispar showed presence of tdc (MPP = 0.98). Outside Enterococcus, tdc was also observed in terminal branches of Limosilactobacillus, Latilactobacillus, Pediococcus, and Companilactobacillus.

Gene tree-species tree discordance suggests horizontal transfers

The gene tree-species tree reconciliation across all identified BA genes revealed a mixture of speciation-driven vertical inheritance and HGT events, with patterns varying by gene family (Supplementary Data 7). The adc and agmatinase gene tree reconciliations revealed a heterogeneous evolutionary history, combining speciation and transfer events (Fig. 5a and b). Approximately 50% of nodes were consistently inferred as pure speciation events, while the remaining 50% were classified as transfer events. The overall reconciliation results showed 100% consistency in event types and > 60% consistency in mapping across 50 runs. In contrast, for hdc (Fig. 5c), all 4 gene trees were consistently assigned to transfer events across all replicates (100% consistency in event types). However, donor–recipient mapping was ambiguous, with 0% consistency, indicating uncertainty in identifying specific donor–recipient relationships.

However, odc (Fig. 5d) revealed a particularly large and complex evolutionary history, characterized by pervasive HGT and high topological uncertainty. Of 185 nodes, 91 were consistently inferred as transfer events across all 50 reconciliation runs. Overall, 92.4% of event classifications were fully consistent across replicates, though donor–recipient mapping achieved only 61.1% consistency. The tdc gene (Fig. 5e) exhibited a similar trend, with 123 transfer events among 161 nodes. Most transfer events occurred with maximal frequency (50 out of 50 runs). Event classification consistency was relatively high (87%), but mapping consistency was lower (about 43%), reflecting ambiguity in donor–recipient identifications. Reconciliation analyses for odc and tdc generated an extremely large number of optimal solutions (9.27 × 1013 and 7.78 × 1021, respectively), highlighting significant uncertainty in reconstructing transfer pathways within these large, reticulate gene families. These patterns were supported by nRF analyses, where adc and agmatinase showed moderate topological discordance, odc and tdc displayed strong topological incongruence, and hdc appeared fully congruent, likely due to limited taxon sampling (Table 1).

Gene synteny revealed lineage-specific retention and insertional variability

BA biosynthesis gene loci generally display conserved flanking gene arrangements within genera but also showed intra- and inter-genus variation. The BA genes shared > 70% sequence similarity across distinct LAB species (Figs. S4–S7). The synteny of adc (also known as speA) and agmatinase (speB) loci revealed multiple distinct genomic contexts across LAB genera (Fig. 6a). In Enterococcus and Leuconostoc, two distinct gene environments were observed among strains. In contrast, Lactiplantibacillus strains displayed a conserved synteny block unique to the genus but distinct from those of Leuconostoc and Enterococcus. Similar variability was observed in other BA biosynthetic genes. The hdc (hdcA) loci exhibited diverse flanking gene architectures across genera (Fig. 6b). The tdc loci had relatively conserved synteny within genera but showed divergence across broader phylogenetic clades (Fig. 6c). Notably, the odc (odcI) regions showed the highest degree of gene order disruption, with strain-specific synteny variation, even within the same genus, suggesting multiple independent gene acquisition events (Fig. 6d).

Compositional signature analysis revealed gene-specific HGT dynamics

The compositional signature analysis of BA synthesis genes revealed consistent compositional deviations relative to the host genome across LAB genomes (Supplementary Data 8). The GC content of adc, agmatinase, hdc, and odc was notably higher than that of their respective host genomes, and each gene displayed distinct GC skew profiles, suggesting acquisition from donors with different base compositions. Among these genes, adc and agmatinase exhibited similar trends, with GC content ranging from ~39% to 41%, exceeding the host genome GC content ( ~ 34%–37%) by a median difference of ~4%. These genes also showed a median GC skew difference of ~0.2, with values consistently above 0.17, even in genomes exhibiting near-zero global GC skew (Fig. 7a, b), suggesting co-acquisition of adc and agmatinase. The hdc gene had a median GC content of 40.96%, compared to a host genome median of 39.71%, indicating a modest but noticeable deviation (median ΔGC = 1.32%). The GC skew difference was more consistent across genomes: hdc genes showed a median skew of 0.133, whereas the background genomes exhibited a near-zero skew (median = 0.0046), leading to a median GC skew difference of 0.127 (Fig. 7c). Similarly, odc regions consistently showed elevated GC content (median = 49.88%) compared to genome-wide medians (45.84%), with a deviation of 4.04%. A more striking divergence was observed in GC skew: whereas the genomic background exhibited a narrow unimodal distribution centered near zero (median ~ −0.00015), odc genes formed two distinct clusters, one ranging from −0.05 to −0.2 and another from 0.05 to 0.2, with no values near zero. This suggests acquisition of odc genes from phylogenetically distinct donor lineages (Fig. 7d). In contrast, tdc genes exhibited host-like GC content ( ~ 37%) and a median GC skew of only 0.0446, consistent with long-term vertical maintenance within Enterococcus lineages (Fig. 7e).

The dinucleotide bias, measured using the dinucleotide deviation index (δ*), further supported gene-specific differences in evolutionary history. Figure 7f presents the baseline compositional bias for core genomic regions of LAB. Two thresholds were used to classify atypical genes: the GMM midpoint (green line), which separates primary (host-like) from secondary (atypical) distribution modes, and the 95th percentile (blue line), which marks the upper boundary of compositional variation expected in native LAB chromosomal sequences. These empirically derived thresholds provide a LAB-specific framework for identifying genes with anomalous compositional signatures. Overall, odc and tdc showed broad δ* distributions (median δ* = 0.081 and 0.105; standard deviations σ = 0.020 and 0.016, respectively), with several outliers. In contrast, adc, agmatinase, and hdc showed narrower δ* distributions (median δ* = 0.089, 0.114, and 0.125; σ = 0.012, 0.026, and 0.024, respectively), indicating more consistent compositional features across genomes (Fig. 7g and Table S3). Pairwise Wilcoxon tests confirmed significant compositional divergence among most BA gene pairs (Table S4). Individual δ* distribution curves for each reference genome used to derive thresholds are shown in Fig. S8.

Furthermore, comparisons of GC content and GC skew with δ* revealed gene-specific compositional dispersion patterns. Genes such as adc, agmatinase, and odc showed the broadest dispersion in both GC- and skew-δ* deviation plots (Fig. 7h, i), whereas tdc clustered tightly near the origin in both plots, again consistent with stable integration.

Finally, CAI values were used to assess translational adaptation of BA genes to LAB genomes. All BA genes exhibited CAI values ranging from 0.6 to 0.8. Most adc, agmatinase, hdc, and tdc genes clustered around 0.70 (Supplementary Data 9), suggesting moderate adaptation. In contrast, odc showed a broader CAI distribution, with most values centered around 0.6 and some as low as 0.3–0.4. Collectively, these compositional trends support distinct gene-specific HGT histories: odc, adc, and agmatinase appear to be the most mobile and recently acquired loci, tdc is the most stably integrated across LAB lineages.

Insertion sequences (ISs) and composite transposons as potential vehicles for BA gene mobility

Given the mixed evolutionary patterns, we identified a notable association between certain BA biosynthesis loci and various IS elements and putative composite transposons (Supplementary Data 10). Among the five principal BA genes, multiple distinct IS elements were predicted to be in proximity to or overlapping with odc loci across different LAB strains (Fig. 8). These included well-characterized ISs such as ISL2 and ISLhe4, among others. In addition to IS elements, composite transposons were identified in several odc loci, including cn_5547_ISLjo1, cn_4803_ISLjo1, and cn_3390_ISLhe13. Both ISs and composite transposons exhibited > 93% identity with near-complete sequence coverage. Similarly, tdc loci were found in close proximity to several IS elements, including ISLpl3 and ISLpl1. In one case, tdc was located near a composite transposon (cn_7000_IS1062) with high sequence identity and full-length coverage. In contrast, hdc loci appeared more conserved in terms of mobility. Most hdc genes were consistently flanked or overlapped by ISLre2 across multiple strains of Limosilactobacillus reuteri and, in one instance, by ISLhe30. In a few cases, BA genes were also found near phage remnants or plasmid-related elements.

Discussion

In this study, we present the first comprehensive genomic analysis of LAB genomes, offering a novel perspective on the prevalence, evolutionary dynamics, and mobility of BA biosynthesis genes. While several studies have reported the production of all major types of BAs in LAB, often confirmed through phenotypic assays17, our analysis provides detailed genomic evidence that complements and extends these findings. Our results showed that the most prevalent BA biosynthetic genes within LAB were tdc and odc, consistent with previous reports18. However, our data revealed a more nuanced distribution. The tdc gene was largely confined to Enterococcus, with only sporadic occurrences in other LAB genera. In contrast, odc showed a broader phylogenetic distribution, suggesting that the potential for putrescine biosynthesis is a more pervasive metabolic trait among LAB. Other key BA biosynthetic genes, including adc, agmatinase, and hdc, were detected at relatively low frequencies in our dataset, suggesting more recent, likely horizontal, acquisition events. Our temporal and geographic analyses revealed that BA genes are globally dispersed without clear regional specificity, suggesting widespread dissemination across LAB. Although early occurrences were limited, a marked increase in prevalence after 2000 suggests a recent expansion, possibly linked to changes in modern fermentation practices or niche-specific selective pressures. However, causality cannot be confirmed. Overall, our genotypic data reinforces earlier phenotypic findings and broaden the known taxonomic range of BA biosynthesis in LAB, with important implications for food-associated microbiomes, probiotics, and human exposure risk.

Our findings also indicated that ecological context strongly influences BA gene prevalence. Both adc and agmatinase co-occurred primarily in clinical isolates, while hdc and odc were predominantly associated with food sources, suggesting that selective pressures in protein-rich, fermentative environments may drive gene retention. These findings align with earlier reports documenting the accumulation of putrescine and histamine in fermented foods such as cheese and wine19,20. These patterns raise questions about the forces shaping BA gene maintenance. BA production may confer adaptive advantages, such as enhanced acid tolerance or modulation of microbial interactions. Alternatively, these genes may persist due to linkage with other fitness-related loci, including stress response genes or mobile genetic elements. Future studies dissecting the functional and genomic contexts of these loci will be essential to clarify the mechanisms underlying their maintenance. Although tdc prevalence was associated with certain niches, such as clinical and fecal isolates, it was also detected across LAB strains from diverse environments, indicating ecological plasticity. Furthermore, detection of odc and tdc in commercial probiotic strains, particularly odc, suggests a potential for BA production, warranting further functional studies to evaluate food safety implications.

Despite the widespread presence of BA biosynthesis genes across LAB, complete BA metabolic pathways were rarely found within individual genomes, especially for putrescine biosynthesis. However, this does not necessarily preclude putrescine production via alternative intermediates such as ornithine, agmatine, or citrulline. Previous studies have emphasized two major pathways for putrescine formation in LAB: the ADI pathway and the AgDI pathway. Both require three sequential enzymatic steps to convert arginine into putrescine via ornithine or agmatine, respectively17. Our findings suggest that the AgDI pathway is under-represented and may not be the dominant route for putrescine biosynthesis in LAB. The limited presence of adc, which catalyzes the conversion of arginine to agmatine, restricts the prevalence of the AgDI pathway. Instead, LAB genomes predominantly carried adi, otc, and odc, indicating that the ADI pathway may be a more feasible route. The apparent absence of the AgDI pathway in our dataset suggests it may be niche- or strain-specific, as previously reported in Lactobacillus hilgardii X1B21, rather than a general feature of LAB. Notably, some Enterococcus strains harbored both adc and agmatinase, supporting the presence of an alternative putrescine synthesis route via agmatine. Our findings provide consistent genomic evidence supporting this alternative pathway in LAB.

Contrary to the long-held view that BA biosynthesis is strain-specific22, our findings suggest a more complex evolutionary scenario. This revised hypothesis is supported by the sporadic distribution of adc, agmatinase, and hdc genes across genera, which is consistent with recurrent HGT events. ASR indicated lineage-level conservation, particularly for tdc and odc, with evidence of complex inheritance involving both ancestral acquisition and vertical retention. Reconciliation analyses further suggested multiple independent HGT events, reinforcing the role of BA biosynthesis genes as dynamic and mobile metabolic traits. However, the presence of multiple optimal solutions with low mapping consistency, particularly for odc and tdc, reflects the inherent uncertainty in inferring the exact direction of HGT events. The significant instances of vertical inheritance for odc and tdc underscore the stable maintenance of these genes within LAB. At the same time, extensive horizontal transfer signals suggest that adaptive gene acquisition may have facilitated the ecological success of specific LAB clades, especially in food-associated environments where BAs not only enhance microbial fitness but also influence food flavor, shelf life, and consumer health risk23. Similarly, the other BA genes, adc, agmatinase, and hdc, displayed patterns indicative of recurrent HGT, consistent with their sporadic, strain-specific presence in LAB, food spoilage organisms, and fermentative microbes24,25. Taken together, our analysis provides strong evidence that BA gene distributions in LAB are shaped by a combination of vertical inheritance and HGT.

Gene synteny patterns corroborated these evolutionary signals. Across LAB, the tdc and hdc biosynthesis genes maintained conserved syntenic contexts within species but showed divergent flanking regions between species, suggesting multiple ancestral acquisitions followed by lineage-specific integration. These patterns, in combination with gene tree reconciliations, imply frequent HGT events among phylogenetically related taxa, likely occupying shared ecological niches, followed by integration into lineage-specific genomic hotspots. In contrast, odc exhibited the highest degree of syntenic variability, even among closely related species, alongside instances of conserved synteny. This indicates a reticulate phylogenetic pattern shaped by frequent gene mobility and vertical descent. Another notable observation was the co-occurrence and conserved synteny of adc and agmatinase in a subset of LAB strains, such as Enterococcus, suggesting operon-level HGT and coordinated expression of the complete putrescine biosynthesis pathway. Given the role of putrescine in acid resistance and cellular stress responses26, this may confer adaptive advantages in stress-prone environments, such as the human gut or clinical settings.

The association of BA biosynthetic genes with insertion sequences (IS elements) and composite transposons offers compelling evidence for their potential mobilization via non-canonical mechanisms. Subtle indicators of prophage or plasmid-like elements near BA loci suggest that transposons may mediate gene mobility through such vectors (Fig. 4e). Specifically, hdc, odc, and tdc genes were frequently flanked by IS elements and adjacent tRNA genes, forming composite transposon-like structures that could facilitate mobilization. In some cases, only a single IS element was detected near the BA biosynthesis gene, suggesting insertion into mobile hotspots with limited mobilization capacity. Additionally, AlienHunter consistently flagged these regions as HGT hotspots, supporting the possibility of transposase-mediated gene capture and transfer27,28. The repeated presence of distinct IS families and composite transposons near odc, particularly across taxonomically diverse LAB, reinforces its classification as a highly mobile locus capable of integrating and disseminating efficiently within and between species, especially in food-associated ecological niches. In contrast, the absence of MGEs near adc and agmatinase may reflect either under-detection due to divergence from known MGE sequences or the use of alternative co-mechanisms, such as operon-level acquisition via integrative elements or genomic islands.

Compositional analyses revealed gene-specific evolutionary trajectories shaped by both vertical inheritance and HGT. The adc and agmatinase genes exhibited similar GC content and GC skew, suggesting a common origin, while their moderate CAI values indicate stable integration and adaptation to host translational machinery following recurrent HGT events. Conversely, odc exhibited the strongest compositional incongruence, elevated GC content, and a bimodal GC skew, suggesting recent and repeated acquisition from phylogenetically distinct donors. The tdc gene followed an intermediate trajectory, showing compositional similarity to host genomes but limited GC skew variation, consistent with ancestral retention and occasional transfer among closely related strains. The hdc gene, although under-sampled, showed strong HGT signals and no evidence of vertical inheritance. Notably, δ* values for all BA genes were generally below the genome-wide compositional divergence threshold (0.145), indicating extensive amelioration and/or transfer from closely related LAB, possibly co-existing within specific niches. Pairwise δ* comparisons among BA biosynthesis genes revealed significant differences between most gene families, further underscoring their distinct evolutionary origins. CAI values for most BA genes indicated a high degree of translational assimilation, reinforcing their stable genomic integration and adaptation to host translation systems29,30. This pattern aligns with previous findings on LAB adaptability and suggests the long-term retention and successful integration of these genes into host genomes31–33.

Taken together, our findings support a dual-mode model for BA gene dissemination: (1) ancient acquisition followed by vertical retention in major LAB lineages, and (2) recurrent MGE-mediated HGT events driven by ecological pressures and facilitated by genomic hotspots. This model is consistent with previous reports of mosaic evolutionary histories in LAB and other food-associated bacteria34,35.

Importantly, the stable integration and codon optimization of BA genes suggest they may enhance microbial fitness under niche-specific stresses, such as acid exposure or host colonization. The frequent genomic proximity of BA genes to stress-response-associated loci, such as gadC, glnR, walKR, and miaA, further supports this interpretation36–39. However, the presence and dissemination of these genes represent a potential food safety concern, given that BA accumulation in fermented products has been associated with adverse health effects40. Our findings imply that monitoring BA genes alone may be insufficient. Instead, tracking associated MGEs and insertional hotspots may offer a more robust strategy for managing the aminobiogenic potential of LAB strains used in food production.

In summary, this study provides the first comprehensive, genome-scale analysis of BA biosynthesis genes across diverse LAB. Our results reveal that BA biosynthesis, particularly for tyramine and putrescine, is shaped by a combination of lineage-specific retention and extensive HGT, potentially mediated by MGEs. Among the genes analyzed, odc showed broad taxonomic distribution and strong association with food-related niches, while tdc was largely confined to Enterococcus. Phylogenomic and compositional data support a complex, dual-mode dissemination model involving ancient gene acquisition and recurrent, niche-driven HGT among closely related taxa. The close proximity of MGEs to BA biosynthesis genes, along with gene synteny patterns, underscores transposase-mediated mobilization. Codon adaptation and apparent stable integration of these genes in probiotic and food-associated strains suggest microbial fitness benefits, but also highlight the genomic potential for biogenic amine accumulation. Given that BAs such as histamine, tyramine, and putrescine are recognized toxic metabolites, the presence of their genetic determinants in LAB raises potential safety concerns. Further functional validation studies are necessary to assess their relevance to food safety and human exposure. These findings underscore the need for integrated surveillance of BA biosynthesis genes and associated MGEs in LAB, with important implications for their dissemination among co-existing food-associated and environmental microbiomes. Such gene flow may impact microbial community structure and contribute to emerging risks in fermented food ecosystems. Overall, this study provides a robust framework for evolutionary insights, and genomic potential of LAB to produce BAs. It highlights the importance of monitoring both BA biosynthesis genes and their mobile genetic contexts to effectively anticipate and manage potential risks in food safety, hazard assessment, and broader environmental applications.

Methods

Selection and re-annotation of a high-quality LAB genome dataset

We retrieved 14,828 genome assemblies representing phylogenetically diverse LAB from NCBI GenBank and RefSeq databases (as of 14 April 2025). Genome quality control and selection were performed as previously described41, with slight modifications. We retained genomes of size between 1.2 Mb and 4 Mb. Proper taxonomic assignments were established using Gambit, and finally, genomes with N50 ≥ 100 kb, ≤200 contigs, <5% contamination, >94% completeness, <5% missing genes, <5% duplicated genes, and <5% gaps were included in the final dataset.

To maintain consistent sample identifiers, contig headers were annotated with Biosample IDs corresponding to each GCA/GCF assembly retrieved from NCBI. All genomes were re-annotated using Prokka v1.14.642 with default settings.

Gene mining and distribution analysis of BA-synthesizing genes in LAB

To investigate the distribution of biogenic amine (BA) synthesizing genes in LAB, we selected functionally validated protein sequences for BA genes—Arginine decarboxylase (adc or speA), agmatinase (also known as speB), Histidine decarboxylase (hdc or hdcA), Lysine decarboxylase (ldc), Ornithine decarboxylase (odc or odcI), and Tyrosine decarboxylase (tdc) from the UniProt database (Table S5). These sequences served as queries to identify homologs in the LAB genomes. Coding sequences (CDS) extracted from the Prokka re-annotated genomes (n = 4880) were compiled into a custom BLASTP database using makeblastdb v2.15.0+43. Query sequences were aligned against this database using BLASTP, retaining hits with e-values ≤ 1e-5. Candidate homologs were curated through multiple sequence alignments against the reference sequences. The partial and non-specific hits were removed, and the resultant sequences were further validated by KEGG Orthology (KO) assignments using eggNOG-mapper v2.1.344. For sequences lacking one-to-one correspondence by eggnog, we first identified and separated sequences based on direct alignment with their canonical reference. The remaining sequences were clustered using CD-HIT45 with a 95% identity threshold. Representative sequences from each cluster were aligned to references, and an all-vs-all distance matrix was generated using Clustal Omega46. A heatmap based on this matrix was then constructed to facilitate precise classification of the sequences.

Next, the presence/absence of each BA gene (adc, agmatinase, hdc, odc, and tdc) was binarized per genome (1 = present, 0 = absent) across the dataset, and the percentage of genomes harboring each gene within each genus was calculated as follows:

These distributions were visualized using grouped stacked bar plots, and co-occurrence patterns of BA genes were further illustrated with pie charts representing unique and multiple gene combinations per genus across the entire dataset (n = 4880).

Pathway completeness analysis of biogenic amine biosynthesis

To capture the full biosynthetic potential of LAB genomes for biogenic amine production, we extended our gene presence/absence matrix by including 4 additional genes coding for Agmatine deiminase (agdi), Arginine deiminase (adi), ornithine transcarbamylase (otc), and putrescine carbamoyltransferase (ptc) (Table S5). These genes were predicted using BLASTP, applying the same criteria as used for identifying the other 5 BA biosynthesis genes. Based on a total of 9 genes, we defined six distinct BA biosynthesis pathways: Tyramine (via tdc), Histamine (via hdc), Putrescine_1 (via adi, otc, and odc), Putrescine_2 (via adc, agdi, ptc), Putrescine_3 (via adc, agmatinase), and Agmatine (via adc alone)17,22. To assess the metabolic completeness of each pathway, we evaluated the genomes for the presence/absence of all these gene combinations. We compiled a separate matrix for pathway completeness (binary: 1 for complete, 0 for incomplete). The resultant pathway-completeness matrix was subsequently visualized using an UpSet plot to illustrate potential intersections and co-occurrences of complete BA synthetic pathways across the dataset.

Comparative genomic analysis with ecological metadata

Next, we categorized the dataset of 4880 LAB genomes into 7 distinct isolation-source categories: animal, clinical, environmental, feces, food, gut, and probiotics, based on published metadata retrieved from the NCBI BioSample database. Genomes with ambiguous or missing isolation source information were excluded to ensure data integrity. Using the gene presence/absence matrix (discussed above), we investigated the association between BA genes (adc, hdc, odc, tdc, and agmatinase) and these ecological niches. For each gene, we performed statistical tests to evaluate niche specificity. Fisher’s exact test was applied for 22 contingency tables, while chi-squared tests with Monte Carlo simulation were employed for larger or sparse contingency tables (in case of odc and tdc) using a custom R-script in R-studio47. Multiple hypothesis testing corrections were applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, with an adjusted p-value cutoff of 0.05 to identify significant associations. The associations were visualized as bar plots showing their presence across isolation sources, annotated with significance stars indicating the adjusted p-values.

Genome de-replication and representative selection

To minimize redundancy, genome assemblies were clustered using dRep v3.6.248. Initial (primary) clustering employed MASH49 similarity with a 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) threshold (--P_ani 0.95) to rapidly group highly similar genomes. Primary clustering was followed by secondary clustering within primary clusters using FastANI50 at a more stringent 99% ANI cutoff (--S_ani 0.99) to select representative genomes. This hierarchical clustering yielded a high-quality, non-redundant genome set suitable for downstream phylogenomic analyses.

Species phylogeny reconstruction using core genes

Next, to investigate the evolutionary history and potential horizontal acquisition of BA genes in LAB, we constructed a robust species phylogeny based on conserved core genes. We used SCARAP v1.0.051 in direct core mode with the following options: 837 seed genomes (836 LAB + 1 outgroup genome), a minimum presence in 95% of seed genomes (core-prefilter = 0.95), required presence in 95% of genomes after filtering (core-filter = 0.95), and a minimum of 32 genomes required to retain orthologous groups. Protein sequence files predicted by Prodigal from the 836 LAB representative genomes and an outgroup Bacillus subtilis genome were used as input, and 115 single-copy core gene families were extracted. Protein sequences of each core gene family were aligned using MUSCLE v5.152. All 115 alignments were concatenated using a custom Python script into a single supermatrix alignment. To improve alignment quality, columns with more than 10% gaps were removed using TrimAl v1.4.rev1553. The final trimmed super-matrix was used to construct a maximum likelihood species tree using IQTREE v2.1.4-beta54, employing the LG + G + F, selected by IQTREE’s built-in ModelFinder (MPF), which automatically evaluates multiple amino acid substitution models and chooses the best-fit model according to the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Branch support was evaluated with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (-bb 1000) and 1000 SH-aLRT replicates (-alrt 1000). The outgroup B. subtilis was used to root the tree, and the resulting tree was visualized with iTOL v7 web server55.

Presence/absence and phylogeny of BA genes in LAB representative genomes

For creating a binary BA gene presence/absence matrix in representative LAB genomes, 5 principal BA gene sequences (adc,hdc,odc,tdc, and agmatinase) were extracted from eggnog curated BA sequence clusters (discussed in Gene mining and distribution analysis), and an in-house Python script was used to construct the matrix. All BA sequences corresponding to these BA genes were aligned with MUSCLE, and individual gene trees were reconstructed with IQTREE using the MFP option to determine the best-fit substitution model. Branch support was evaluated with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (-bb 1000) and 1000 SH-aLRT replicates (-alrt 1000). The resulting trees were visualized and annotated using the ITOL web server.

Ancestral state reconstruction (ASR) and gene gain/loss analysis

Next, we performed ancestral state reconstruction (ASR) to infer the evolutionary dynamics and ancestral presence of all 5 BA genes (adc,hdc,odc,tdc, and agmatinase) using PastML v1.9.5156. The rooted species tree (described above) served as the input tree, and the binary presence/absence matrix for the principal BA genes was used as the trait input. ASR was carried out by using the MPPA (Marginal Posterior Probability Approximation) algorithm under the F81 model, which provides probabilistic predictions of ancestral states at each internal node. The resulting PastML-derived annotated species tree, containing predicted ancestral states, was subsequently visualized using the iTOL to infer the gene gain/loss events. The tree was annotated to show genus names and binary presence/absence dots for each BA gene.

HGT inference by gene tree-species tree reconciliation

To infer potential HGT events, we performed gene tree-species tree reconciliation using Ranger-DTL v2.057. For all 5 BA genes (adc,hdc,odc,tdc, and agmatinase), the rooted species tree and unrooted gene trees for each BA gene were prepared in order to match the Ranger-DTL input. The built-in OptRoot package of Ranger-DTL was employed to identify the optimal root position for the gene trees, using the species tree topology to root the gene trees in a manner that minimizes duplication, transfer, and loss (DTL) events. The rooted gene trees thus obtained were added to the input file using the -r flag of OptRoot. Finally, the Ranger-DTL program was executed for 50 independent reconciliation replicates, each initialized with a different random seed (1-50) to account for stochastic variability in event assignment. The reconciliations obtained from each seed were aggregated using the AggregateRanger package of RangerDTL to generate a consensus output and robust event estimates. The reconciliation cost parameters used were duplication = 2, transfer = 3, loss = 1. To assess robustness, alternative parameter sets (duplication = 1–3, transfer = 2–4, loss = 1–2) were evaluated, which yielded qualitatively similar reconciliation patterns for the BA genes. The aggregated output was subsequently visualized using the ranger_to_recXML.py (https://compbio.engr.uconn.edu/software/ranger-dtl/), to produce XML-based reconciliation files and trees annotated with transfer, duplication, and loss events for all 5 principal BA genes. Topological discordance between BA biosynthesis gene trees and the LAB core gene species tree was further quantified using the Robinson-Foulds (RF) and normalized RF (nRF) metrics using ETE3 v3.1.158. For each gene, the species tree was pruned to match the taxon set present in the corresponding gene tree. Pairwise comparisons were performed with the ete3 compare function under the unrooted tree setting. The RF distance, maximum possible RF (maxRF), and nRF (RF/maxRF) were recorded along with branch support proportions for both trees.

BA gene synteny and genomic context analysis

Next, to investigate the genomic context of BA genes (adc, hdc, odc, tdc, and agmatinase), we extracted ±10 kilobases (kb) upstream and downstream flanks of each target BA gene from all BA-positive genomes. Prodigal v2.6.359 was used for gene prediction within these loci, and functional annotation of all predicted CDSs was performed using eggNOG-mapper v2.1.3. The resulting GenBank (.gbk) files from Prodigal were updated to reflect functional assignments: where available, preferred gene names from eggNOG-mapper were used; otherwise, Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers were assigned. These annotated GenBank files were then used as input for Clinker v0.0.2460 to visualize gene neighborhood and synteny relationships. For BA genes with many homologous loci, such as odc and tdc, redundancy was reduced by clustering the extracted loci with CD-HIT v4.8.1 at a sequence identity threshold of 90% (–c 0.90) to retain unique sequence variants. In case of BA genes with relatively fewer hits (e.g., adc, agmatinase, and hdc), all available loci from the complete LAB genome set (n = 4880) were included in the synteny analysis.

Mobility element prediction associated with BA gene

For all 836 representative LAB genomes, plasmid sequences were identified using PlasmidFinder v264 with default settings to detect known plasmid replicons within them. Prophage regions were predicted with Phigaro65 employing its default parameters to locate integrated viral elements. Furthermore, the nucleotide regions spanning ±10 kb around each BA biosynthesis gene locus from representative LAB genomes were systematically screened for mobile genetic elements (MGEs) using MGEfinder v1.1.266 employing default configurations. BA gene loci containing at least one MGE were submitted to Proksee67 for visualization and annotated using the built-in MobileOG database. To strengthen our inference of HGT, we additionally applied AlienHunter68 to these loci, providing a complementary, composition-based validation and a clear visual output of atypical regions.
