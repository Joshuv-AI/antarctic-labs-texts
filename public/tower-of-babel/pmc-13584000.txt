Synthesis, properties, and forensic applications of titanium dioxide nanoparticles in latent fingerprint development
Authors: Mahipal Singh Sankhla, Apoorva Singh, Rohit Kumar Verma, Anuj Sharma, Varad Nagar, Kumud Kant Awasthi, Garima Awasthi, Lokesh Kumar Jangir, Kamlendra Awasthi, Harsh Pandey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fingerprints are major evidence that helps establish a link between a crime and its perpetrator. There are multiple techniques available for developing latent fingerprints, and recent studies show that nanoparticles are among the best methods due to their excellent properties and surface area. In this study, Titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2 NPs) were synthesized using the sol-gel technique. Characterization of the NPs was performed using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Fingerprints were developed on 10 different surfaces, including ceramic, wood, paper, plastic, cardboard, glass, fiber, aluminum foil, thermocol, and metal. The synthesized TiO2 nanopowder produced clearly identifiable prints on all substrates examined. Fingerprints on the glass surface had the highest clarity, whereas on the ceramic surface, they had the lowest. The clarity percentage ranged from 60% to 100%. The results showed an average clarity of 90.83% on non-porous surfaces, 70.00% on porous surfaces, and 95.00% on the semi-porous surface (paper). Clarity was scored independently by three fingerprint examiners against a predefined clarity index, and the reported values represent the mean of these assessments pooled across aging intervals of 1 h, 1 day, and 5 days. The efficiency of various nanoparticles used to develop fingerprints has also been compared with that of TiO2 nanoparticles. The particle morphology and size of the synthesized TiO2 NPs, together with substrate porosity, are proposed as the factors governing adhesion to fingerprint residues and the resulting quality of ridge-detail development, consistent with trends reported for other nanoparticle powders.

Introduction

Epidermal ridges deposit a unique and complex pattern on surfaces due to the presence of natural secretions from the eccrine gland. In trace level amounts, fatty acids, proteins, amino acids, and inorganic ions are present. Since the early 20th century, fingerprint analysis in forensic science has been a significant tool for apprehending offenders due to its distinctiveness [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Because of their uniqueness and permanence, they are commonly used to identify individuals in both civil and criminal investigations.

With advancements in forensic science, it has become increasingly feasible to develop fingerprints on a wide range of surfaces recovered from crime scenes. Fingerprints are among the most well-known and widely used forms of physical evidence for identifying a person, as they can establish a direct link to the suspect. The sweat residue present on the friction ridges of the skin forms the Latent Fingerprint (LFP), which is not visible to the naked eye. These prints are formed when papillary ridges of the palm come into contact with a surface [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. The traces deposited on the surface during contact are invisible, necessitating appropriate development methods [4,10].

Various methods are used to detect latent fingerprints on porous and non-porous surfaces, including chemical, optical, and physical techniques. Of all powder dusting methods, the dusting technique is the most effective and widely used, owing to its ability to work on different surfaces and printing conditions [9,11,12]. The particle size and shape can impact the adhesion properties of powder formulations, with finer particles exhibiting better adherence to fingerprint residues [11,13,14].

Over the past few years, there have been growing efforts to develop sustainable methods for visualizing latent fingerprints using nanoparticles. Attention has been paid to the use of economical materials as an effective alternative to traditional powders for latent fingerprint visualization, while maintaining the integrity of ridge characteristics. According to a comparative study by Lohar et al. [12], several waste materials were identified as potential candidates for developing latent fingerprints due to their low cost and effectiveness. Further support was provided by Rai et al. [15], who found that roasted gram flour (sattu powder) was effective in visualizing latent fingerprints. Progress in nanotechnology has expanded the range of substances used for fingerprint detection, owing to nanoparticles' greater surface area and reactivity with fingerprint deposits. For instance, Verma et al. [16] developed ZnO nanoparticles and demonstrated their effectiveness in latent fingerprint processing, highlighting the importance of nanoparticle features for enhanced ridge visualization. Nagar et al. [9] studied the effect of different liquid treatments on latent friction ridge analysis of fingerprints printed on porous substrates. They found that the features of the developing medium greatly impacted the outcome of ridge detection. Later, Sharma et al. [17] assessed the surface-dependent efficiency of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles in fingerprint development and found that not only were nanoparticles important, but the physicochemical properties of the surface itself also mattered.

With the increasing need for environmentally friendly forensic processes, there is a growing interest in exploring green nanotechnology. This has been illustrated by the thorough review conducted by Sharma et al. [18], which examined the use of environmentally friendly nanoparticle-based techniques in forensic science. The benefits of such methods include improved surface properties, enhanced interactions with fingerprint components, reduced environmental impact, and potential use in developing latent fingerprints. Collectively, these reports establish an evolving trend towards sustainable, nanomaterial-based fingerprint development techniques. The use of nanotechnology in forensics has increased tremendously over the years, prompting studies on TiO2-related materials for various forensic applications. In toxicology testing, Awasthi et al. [19] analyzed the biological interactions and toxic effects of TiO2 nanoparticles when introduced to bacteria. The findings emphasized the need to consider the safety implications of using nanoparticles in forensic investigations. Kumari et al. [20] investigated the morphology and structure of PS-TiO2 nanocomposite membranes, whereas Kumari et al. [21] explored the luminescence properties of Tb3+-doped TiO2 nanoparticles produced by the sol-gel approach. Alternative techniques for identifying latent fingerprints using readily available materials include using incense stick ash to visualize fingerprint patterns, according to Mahida et al. [22]. Xie et al. [23] reported the use of Eu3+-activated double perovskite phosphors that visualize latent fingerprints with luminescent properties. These reports indicate a continuing effort towards developing highly effective and sensitive methods for latent fingerprint visualization using appropriate materials.

TiO2-based nanoparticles (NPs) have been used in prior studies for the detection of LFPs, and significant results have been obtained with dried powdered TiO2 NPs. In this research, the synthesis and characterization of TiO2 NPs are carried out, and their application for detecting LFPs on different porous, semi-porous, and non-porous surfaces is systematically investigated [24,25]. Unlike traditional powders, this powder is comparatively low-cost and efficient in LFP detection, although the safety profile of TiO2 nanoparticles warrants consideration during handling [19]. Comparison of TiO2 nanoparticles with other nanoparticles used for fingerprint development.

Although TiO2 nanoparticles have previously been employed for latent fingerprint development, few studies have evaluated the surface-dependent performance of a single, well-characterized TiO2 nanopowder across a broad range of substrate porosities under uniform conditions. The present study addresses this gap by synthesizing TiO2 nanoparticles via the sol-gel method, characterizing their morphology, crystalline structure, and particle-size distribution, and evaluating their effectiveness for latent fingerprint development on porous, semi-porous, and non-porous substrates.

In the study by Mi Jung Choi, a fluorescent dye synthesized from oleylamine and a perylene dianhydride was characterized using fluorescence spectroscopy, 1H NMR, UV-Visible spectroscopy, and quantum yield measurements. This dye was used to develop TiO2 fingerprint powder, achieving a higher success rate than commercial powder [28]. Hui Yang used the electrospray method to homogeneously deposit TiO2 nanoparticles onto fingerprints, finding it non-destructive to fingerprint morphology and highly effective for MALDI MSI analysis of fingerprint components [37]. Yadav et al. synthesized carbon dots from pomegranate waste peels and, through the hydrothermal route, prepared C-dot@TiO2 core-shell nanostructures [39,40]. K.R. Venkatesha Babu et al. produced TiO2:Eu3+ nano powders via the facile one-pot hydrothermal method; when optimized to 5 mol% Eu3+, these powders served as effective fluorescence labeling agents for visualizing latent fingerprints on various surfaces with high sensitivity and selectivity [30,33,41]. Hinners et al. used TiO2 powder in a single-step matrix technique for chemically imaging latent fingerprints, demonstrating good MS imaging quality for both endogenous and exogenous latent fingerprint compounds [42].

Collectively, these studies establish that the particle morphology and size of TiO2 NPs are fundamental determinants of their performance in fingerprint development, motivating the systematic investigation presented in this work. The comparison presented in Table 1 is intended as a literature-based overview of the advantages and limitations reported for individual nanoparticle systems, with each entry supported by a cited source. It should not be read as evidence of the universal superiority of TiO2 over other nanomaterials, since the performance of any fingerprint powder depends strongly on substrate characteristics, deposition conditions, and the composition of the fingermark residue. The present results qualify the entries in Table 1 in one respect that merits explicit statement: the synthesized TiO2 nanopowder performed best on non-porous substrates and least well on porous substrates, so the porous-surface advantages attributed to TiO2 in the cited sources apply to the specific formulations and deposition conditions described in those studies and were not reproduced under the uniform conditions adopted here.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Chemicals used for TiO2 synthesis: Ethanol, Titanium (IV) isopropoxide (TTIP), deionized water, hydrogen peroxide, and 1-thioglycerol. Equipment used included: Centrifuge, Hot air oven, Muffle furnace, Nova Nano FE-SEM 450 (FEI) electron microscope, LAMBDA 750 (PerkinElmer) UV-Vis NIR Spectrophotometer, and Magnifying Glass. The nano powder was applied using an ostrich-hair brush [Technomaxx Pvt. Ltd. (India)] to develop latent fingerprints. A tripod-mounted Nikon D-3100 DSLR camera was used to photograph the developed prints.

Synthesis of TiO2 nanoparticles

The synthesis of pure TiO2 was carried out using the sol-gel technique. This approach involved mixing 10 mL of ethanol with 5 g of titanium (IV) isopropoxide (TTIP) and stirring for 10 min. Subsequently, 50 ml of deionized water and 0.25 ml of 1-thioglycerol were added, and the mixture was stirred continuously for 20 min. The surplus alcohol was removed by centrifugation, and the resulting white precipitate was collected after repeated washes with deionized water. The precipitate was treated with 10 ml of hydrogen peroxide, producing an orange-colored sol that gelled within a few minutes. The gel was crushed into a fine powder after drying for 12 h at 60 °C, then heated in a muffle furnace at approximately 600 °C for 1 h. Only undoped TiO2 was synthesized in this work; no doped or composite TiO2 variant was prepared, characterized, or applied to fingermark development, and the results reported below relate exclusively to this single undoped nanopowder.

Characterization

Surface morphology was examined using a Nova Nano FE-SEM 450 (FEI) electron microscope. The crystalline structure was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) recorded over a 2θ range of 20–70° using [Rigaku SmartLab; Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5406 Å], and the diffraction pattern was matched against standard reference data (reference code 00-004-0477). Functional groups were characterized by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy over the range 400–4000 cm−1 using JASCO FTIR-4X. Particle size was measured from FESEM images using ImageJ software.

Sample surface selection

For this work, 10 different surfaces of varying porosity were selected: ceramic, wood, paper, plastic, cardboard, glass, fiber, aluminum foil, thermocol, and metal. These surfaces are classified as non-porous (glass, plastic, fiber, aluminum foil, thermocol, metal), semi-porous (paper), and porous (ceramic, wood, cardboard), and are frequently encountered at crime scenes. The ceramic substrate was unglazed, and the fiber substrate was a woven synthetic fabric. Glazed ceramic behaves as an effectively non-porous surface, so the porous classification adopted here applies specifically to the unglazed material examined, and the ceramic results should not be extrapolated to glazed ceramic ware.

Fingerprint sampling and development

In this study, 120 donors (60 male and 60 female), aged 18 to 25 years, were asked to leave intentional fingerprints on the selected surfaces in accordance with established protocols. Before fingermark deposition, donors were asked to avoid handwashing for 30 min, and a few seconds before deposition, their hands were rubbed together between the fingertips to ensure a homogeneous distribution of secretions. A total of 120 fingerprints were made on the surfaces mentioned above (12 per surface; 4 developed at each of the three aging intervals) and divided into three groups, aged for 1 h, 1 day, and 5 days, respectively, in the same room before development. The temperature in the laboratory was stable, ranging from 21 to 24 °C. Each donor contributed impressions to the study only once, so that the twelve impressions collected on any given substrate originated from different donors and donor identity was not confounded with substrate type. Donor to donor variation in eccrine and sebaceous secretion is known to influence fingermark quality, and the present design distributes this variation across substrates rather than quantifying it. The donor panel was restricted to the 18 to 25 year age band, so the clarity values reported here characterize fingermark residue of young adult donors and are not extrapolated to pediatric or elderly donors, in whom the eccrine and sebaceous composition of the residue differs. A dedicated study using a smaller, demographically stratified donor panel with repeated depositions per donor would be required to isolate the contribution of donor variability across age and sex, and is identified as an important step for future validation.

The synthesized TiO2 nanopowder was applied using a fine commercial brush for latent fingerprint development. During development, each latent fingerprint was processed using the synthesized pure TiO2 nanopowder. The developed prints were photographed using a tripod-mounted Nikon D-3100 DSLR camera. The UC (University of Canberra) comparative scale was used to assess the quality of the developed prints. A single clarity value was therefore assigned to each impression, and the values reported in Table 3 and plotted in Fig. 5 derive from that single assessment. Three fingerprint examination experts independently evaluated each developed print, and the mean value was used as the final result. Each examiner independently assigned a clarity percentage to each developed impression using the predefined clarity index criteria in Table 2, and the arithmetic mean of the three independent scores was taken as the clarity value reported for that substrate. The examiners assessed the impressions separately and were not aware of one another's ratings. Formal inter-observer agreement statistics and inferential testing of the differences between substrates were not computed in the present study, because the clarity index is an ordinal descriptive scale, and the number of replicate impressions per substrate was small. The clarity values reported in Table 3 are, consequently, descriptive summaries of examiner consensus, and the differences recorded between substrates are interpreted throughout as observed trends rather than statistically tested effects. Results

Morphologies and structure

To evaluate the morphology and particle size of the TiO2 nanoparticles produced, field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was used. From Fig. 1(a) and (b), the nanoparticles were predominantly spherical, with a clear trend towards agglomeration. The tendency to agglomerate was due to the high surface energy and the van der Waals forces usually associated with nanoparticles prepared via the sol-gel method.Fig. 1(a & b) FESEM images of synthesized TiO2 nanoparticles at different magnifications (scale bars: 500 nm in (a), 2 μm in (b)), (c) particle size distribution curve for TiO2 nanoparticles.

(a & b) FESEM images of synthesized TiO2 nanoparticles at different magnifications (scale bars: 500 nm in (a), 2 μm in (b)), (c) particle size distribution curve for TiO2 nanoparticles.

ImageJ software was used to calculate the average particle size from the FESEM images. The particle size of the synthesized nanoparticles was found to range from 25 to 30 nm, as shown in Fig. 1c. The particle size distribution graph showed that most particles fell within the specified size range, indicating fairly uniform particle sizes. These particles were smaller than conventional fingerprint powders, which corresponds to a larger specific surface area available for contact with the fingerprint residue. The contribution of this dimensional difference to development performance was not quantified in the present work.

These attributes of particle shape and size are considered advantages in the development of latent fingerprints because particles in this size range are reported to adhere readily to the oily secretions of sebum and sweat found in fingerprint residues. Such adhesion is expected to favor the deposition of particles on the details of friction ridges, thereby producing a clearer print image. This mechanism is offered as a plausible explanation, supported by prior literature, rather than as a relationship demonstrated in the present work, because a controlled comparison of TiO2 powders with different particle sizes under identical conditions was not performed. Such phenomena regarding the effect of particle nanoscale attributes on fingerprint development have previously been documented in the literature on nanoparticle fingerprint powders. Given the morphology of the TiO2 nanoparticles obtained, they are promising candidates for forensic fingerprint visualization.

The structural properties of the synthesized nanoparticles were investigated using XRD and FTIR spectroscopy. The XRD pattern presented in Fig. 2a reveals the polycrystalline nature of the synthesized nanoparticles with a tetragonal crystal structure. The diffraction peaks observed at various 2θ positions correspond to different crystallographic planes, as indicated in Fig. 2a, and are in good agreement with the standard reference data (reference code: 00-004-0477). The average crystallite size, estimated using the Debye–Scherrer formula, was approximately 15 nm. This crystallite size is smaller than the 25–30 nm particle size measured from the FESEM images, which is expected because the Debye–Scherrer estimate reflects the coherently diffracting domain size, whereas FESEM captures the overall particle dimensions comprising one or more crystallites; the difference is consistent with the polycrystalline nature indicated by XRD and with the agglomeration observed in the micrographs. The FTIR spectrum of the TiO2 nanoparticles, presented in Fig. 2b, verifies the presence of the characteristic functional groups and vibrational modes of the synthesized material. The broad absorption region between 400 and 800 cm−1, encompassing the bands resolved at 520 and 734 cm−1, is assigned to the intrinsic lattice vibrations of Ti–O and Ti–O–Ti bonds within the TiO2 crystal structure. The absorption peak located at 1098 cm−1 is attributed to C–O stretching of residual alkoxide-derived species retained on the particle surface, and the band at 1366 cm−1 to C–H bending of the same residual organic fraction. The band at 1740 cm−1 is assigned to C=O stretching of residual carbonyl and carboxylate species, and that at 2922 cm−1 to C–H stretching, both originating from incomplete removal of the titanium (IV) isopropoxide and 1-thioglycerol precursors during calcination. The broad absorption centered at approximately 3440 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching of surface hydroxyl groups and adsorbed molecular water, indicating the presence of hydroxylated species on the TiO2 surface. Influence of particle morphology on development effectiveness

The efficiency of the synthesized TiO2 nanoparticles as a material for developing latent fingerprints was assessed on ten commonly used surfaces with varying porosity levels. The selected surfaces were ceramic, wood, paper, plastic, cardboard, glass, fiber, aluminum foil, thermocol, and metal. The clarity of developed fingerprints varied markedly across the selected surfaces, indicating that surface type can affect the appearance of ridge details. The clarity of fingerprints varied from 60% to 100%. Each clarity value reported below is the mean of the three independent examiner scores and represents pooled observations across the impressions developed at 1 h, 1 day, and 5 days on that substrate; the values are not resolved by aging interval, and an aging-wise analysis was not performed because the number of replicates within each interval was insufficient to support it. Of all the tested surfaces, fingerprints on glass had the highest clarity, at 100%. Glass is a hard, non-porous surface on which ridge residues are retained on the outer surface, providing maximum contact between ridge residues and TiO2 nanoparticles during powdering. Prints with high ridge flow and recognizable minutiae were produced. Representative images of the fingermarks developed with the synthesized TiO2 nanopowder on all ten substrates are presented in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Latent fingerprints developed with the synthesized TiO2 nanopowder on different substrates: a) ceramic, b) wooden, c) paper, d) plastic, e) cardboard, f) glass, g) fiber, h) aluminum foil, i) thermocol, and j) metallic.

Latent fingerprints developed with the synthesized TiO2 nanopowder on different substrates: a) ceramic, b) wooden, c) paper, d) plastic, e) cardboard, f) glass, g) fiber, h) aluminum foil, i) thermocol, and j) metallic.

A high percentage of fingerprint clarity was also observed on the surfaces of the fiber (95%) and the aluminum foil (95%). Metal and plastic materials yielded lower results, with clarity levels of 85% and 80%, respectively. These values indicate consistent performance of the synthesized TiO2 nanopowder across the non-porous substrate class. Clarity percentages of 95% and 90% were found for the paper and thermocol samples, respectively. It means that fingerprint residues on such substrates retained their ridge detail to a level suitable for analysis. Despite possible absorption by the sample substrates, the small size of the nanoparticles resulted in titanium dioxide being sufficiently reactive with components of fingerprint traces.

Ceramic and cardboard substrates had the lowest clarity percentages, at 60% and 70%, while wooden substrates produced fingerprint ridge details at 80% clarity. Possible reasons may include variations in roughness and surface absorption, which could affect the quantity and quality of residues. Background color and powder deposition may also influence the final result. The differences observed among the three substrate classes may relate to how each surface retains the fingermark deposit. On smooth, non-porous substrates such as glass, aluminum foil, and metal, sweat and sebaceous residue remain on the outer surface as a continuous film, so that essentially the entire deposit is available for contact with the nanopowder, and the ridge detail is reproduced faithfully. On the semi-porous paper substrate, the aqueous fraction of the deposit is partially absorbed into the fiber matrix.

In contrast, the lipid fraction remains near the surface, leaving sufficient material for powder adhesion and accounting for the high clarity recorded. On porous substrates such as cardboard, wood, and the rough ceramic used here, capillary absorption into the substrate, together with a comparatively high surface roughness, reduces the quantity of residue accessible at the surface and disrupts the continuity of the ridges. At the same time, the porous texture retains powder within the furrows and reduces the contrast between the ridge and the background. Substrate color and reflectance further affect the visual contrast of the white TiO2 deposit, which contributed to the lower clarity values recorded on the light-colored ceramic and cardboard surfaces.

The results establish that the synthesized nanosized titanium dioxide visualized ridge structures on all ten substrates examined. Enhanced contact between nanoscale particles and the constituents of fingerprint residue, compared with coarser powders, has been reported in the literature. It is offered here as a plausible interpretation, since powders of differing particle size were not compared under identical conditions in this work. The findings identify TiO2 nanopowder as a promising candidate for further evaluation as an alternative to conventional fingerprint powders.

Fingerprint pattern recognition and minutiae analysis

The developed latent fingerprints were assessed to evaluate the ability of the fingerprint patterns and ridge features to be observed after application of the TiO2 nanoparticles. For the purposes of identifying patterns, a magnifying lens was used, and photographic images of developed prints were used to examine minute characteristics. The purpose of the evaluation was the forensic value of the developed fingerprint impressions rather than their physical properties. TiO2 nanoparticles enabled visualization of various fingerprint patterns, such as loops, whorls, and double loops, on the tested surfaces. The loop patterns were the most common, but whorl and double-loop patterns could also be found on some surfaces. Successful detection of these pattern types confirms that the nanopowder preserved the entire ridge pattern required for fingerprint classification. Various minutiae characteristics could be observed from the developed fingerprints. These include ridge ending, bifurcation, hook, short ridge, enclosure, intersection, spur, core, dot ridge, trifurcation, ridge return, fragmenting ridge, scar, and change-over characteristics. Depending on the type of substrate and ridge characteristics on each one of them, the amount of observed minutiae could vary.

Fingerprint impressions with relatively high clarity ratings were found to have more ridge features discernible compared to those with low clarity ratings. Fingerprint impressions made on glass, fiber, aluminum foil, paper, and thermocol substrates exhibited clear ridge flow and visible minutiae suitable for comparison. In contrast, fingerprints developed on ceramic and cardboard substrates exhibited poor ridge continuity and localized smudging, thereby limiting the ability to visualize certain minutiae. Multiple identifiable minutiae in the developed fingerprints indicate that TiO2 nanoparticles are suitable as an efficient developing reagent for fingerprint detection. Given that fingerprint uniqueness relies on comparing ridge characteristics, the ability to discern these characteristics plays an important role. The results presented herein demonstrate the effectiveness of the nanopowder in producing fingerprints with identifiable minutiae.

Practical considerations and limitations

Although the synthesized TiO2 nanopowder performed well under the controlled conditions used in this study, several practical constraints must be considered before the method is applied to forensic casework. The powder is white and provides poor contrast on white or light-colored substrates, where background powder adhesion can obscure the developed ridges; a colored or luminescent formulation, or an alternative imaging and lifting strategy, would be required in such situations. Substrate roughness is a further constraint, since results on ceramic, cardboard, and wood show that powder retained in surface irregularities reduces ridge continuity and produces localized smudging. The quantity of powder applied and the brushing technique also remain operator-dependent variables that influence the final quality of the developed mark.

The fingermarks examined here were deposited under laboratory conditions and were aged for a maximum of five days at a stable temperature of 21 to 24 °C. Marks encountered in casework are frequently older and may have been exposed to elevated temperatures, high humidity, sunlight, rain, or air movement, all of which alter the residue by evaporating the aqueous fraction and oxidizing the lipid fraction, thereby generally reducing the efficiency of powder-based development. Contamination of the fingermark or the substrate with cosmetics, food residues, oils, dust, or cleaning agents may likewise promote nonspecific powder adhesion and reduce the contrast of the developed mark. The performance reported here should be regarded as an estimate obtained under favorable conditions rather than as an indication of expected casework performance.

The study's design also limits the interpretation of the results. Three aging intervals of 1 h, 1 day, and 5 days were included to test development on marks of varying freshness. Still, the objective of the work was to compare substrate types rather than to conduct a dedicated aging study. The clarity values reported for each substrate represent pooled observations across the three aging intervals. They are not resolved by aging time, as the number of replicates within each interval was insufficient to support an aging-specific analysis. A dedicated investigation using a larger replicate set and a wider range of aging intervals, a controlled comparison of TiO2 powders of differing particle size, and a side-by-side evaluation against established commercial fingerprint powders are identified as the principal directions for future work.

Conclusion

Nanoparticles of TiO2 were synthesized by the sol-gel route for the evaluation of their utility in developing latent fingerprints on substrates having various degrees of porosity. FESEM, XRD, and FTIR analyses confirmed that the synthesized TiO2 nanoparticles were predominantly spherical and crystalline (tetragonal anatase phase; crystallite size ∼15 nm), with an average particle size of 25-30 nm and characteristic Ti–O surface functional groups. These nanoparticles interacted effectively with latent fingerprint residues and reproduced ridge detail at a level suitable for comparison. TiO2 nanoparticles are useful for developing latent fingerprints on substrates such as ceramics, wood, paper, plastics, cardboard, glass, fiber, aluminum foil, thermocol, and metals. Clarity obtained ranged from 60% to 100%. Impressions obtained on glass surfaces were the best, with maximum clarity of 100%. Impressions obtained on ceramic surfaces were comparatively poor.

In contrast, impressions obtained from fiber, aluminum foil, paper, and thermocol had well-defined ridge lines and minutiae features. Glass presented a smooth non-porous surface on which the sweat and sebaceous deposit remained available at the surface for contact with the nanopowder, which accounts for the highest clarity value recorded among the ten substrates examined. Analysis of the created images enabled identification of ridge patterns and minutiae features, such as ridge endings and bifurcations, on the developed prints. The sol–gel-synthesized TiO2 nanopowder visualized latent fingerprints with identifiable ridge detail across substrates of differing porosity, making it a promising alternative to conventional fingerprint powders rather than an established replacement for them. This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting these findings: a single batch of pure TiO2 (one morphology and size) was evaluated, so morphology- and size-dependent effects could not be isolated; no conventional or commercial powder was tested side by side, so superiority over traditional powders was not established experimentally; the reported clarity values represent the mean of three expert assessments without quantified inter-rater reliability or per-surface dispersion; and although prints were aged for 1 h, 1 day, and 5 days, the effect of print age was not separately analyzed. Validation of the method for routine forensic use requires a direct benchmark against established commercial fingerprint powders under identical experimental conditions, systematic variation of particle morphology and size, quantitative treatment of inter-rater agreement, and evaluation of aged fingermarks and authentic crime-scene substrates.
