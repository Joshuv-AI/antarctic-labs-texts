A calibrated scale to measure heritage learning in digital environments. A network analysis approach
Authors: Olaia Fontal, Alex Ibañez-Etxeberria, Víctor B. Arias, Benito Arias
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
To date, there have been scarcely any published studies dedicated to developing instruments with sufficient guarantees of accuracy, validity, and reliability for the assessment of heritage learning in digital contexts. Furthermore, very few such studies on this subject have employed the network analysis methodology. The present study seeks to address these research gaps by applying network analysis methodology to the responses of 1323 participants concerning 49 items grouped into seven dimensions constituting the Heritage Learning Sequence (knowing, understanding, respecting, valuing, caring, enjoying, and transmitting). Network estimation was conducted using the Gaussian Graphical Model with regularization, ensuring both the accuracy of the network and the stability of centrality indices. The results indicate that satisfactory values have been achieved in both the network structure and in terms of predictability, replicability, and sensitivity. Finally, the invariance of the network structure among groups (male/female and random subsamples) has been demonstrated. These findings offer promising avenues for further research on heritage learning assessment using the network analysis methodology.

Introduction

Research problem and scientific literature review

Heritage learning has shifted from being preferentially located in the school and museum environments to entering other informal settings where people, users and, ultimately, potential heritage legatees have taken agency and protagonism [1]). Among these informal contexts, digital environments have been prioritized over the last two decades [2,3] as they integrate immersive technologies with a clear potential to help the general public experience cultural heritage [4].

This merging of heritage learning with digital platforms has opened up new avenues for academic research, in turn leading to a reassessment of the effectiveness of these digital tools in promoting engagement, understanding, and appreciation of diverse cultural heritages [5]. Some studies have focused on understanding the ways in which digital environments can achieve effective communication of cultural heritage in terms of user engagement, interface optimization, and targeted evaluation [6]. Others have addressed how current Virtual Reality (VR) environments can contribute to an improved communication of cultural heritage, providing an experience of the past that is acceptable to the new generations; all of which has underpinned the discussion as to how museums and cultural institutions should adopt and use these technologies [7].

Studies focused on effectiveness have explored the use of gamification technologies as a heritage learning strategy compared to traditional transmissive techniques, demonstrating superior efficacy in both learning (primary school students) and teaching-related aspects [8]. The examination of values related to historical objects has been conducted, considering the debates among human and non-human actors within social contexts [9]. Artificial intelligence has also been studied in relation to evaluating the significance of cultural heritage, showing that generative AI can identify, catalogue, and classify culturally significant objects [10].

In evaluating heritage-related learning within digital environments, a distinction has been drawn between virtual reality-based learning and traditional text-based methods. Research suggests that creating a VR learning environment within a cultural heritage museum may enhance intrinsic motivation but may not demonstrably improve students' learning competence [11]. The effects of face-to-face interaction and virtuality on key dimensions of learning (social, cognitive, emotional) have also been examined, including participatory strategies, critical analysis, three-dimensional reconstruction, and surveys using both traditional and advanced digital techniques [12]. The acceptance of the ubiquitous learning system has been assessed using questionnaires and t-tests, confirming its reliability and the students’ willingness to engage with it, demonstrating that the system positively impacts student motivation and academic performance [13].

Research exploring learning outcomes in virtual environments has identified key factors influencing user retention in mobile augmented reality (AR) applications, including usability, satisfaction, emotional attachment, focus of attention, and flow experience. A consistent finding is that user satisfaction and flow experience have a positive impact on user retention [14]. Mid-air gesture-based interaction technologies in cultural heritage learning have also been measured, showing that participants’ learning outcomes around cultural heritage differed significantly in three experimental conditions: video learning only; interactive experience first and then video learning; video learning first and then interactive experience [15].

The evaluation of heritage learning in digital contexts represents a multifaceted and complex field, as it encompasses not only the technological aspects of digital platforms, but also the sociocultural dynamics that influence the transmission and reception of heritage-related knowledge [16]. Most of the studies that focus on the evaluation of heritage learning in digital environments are descriptive and exploratory, and measure the outcomes of specific implementations or focus on particular cases, which places their results within a limited range in terms of impact [17]. As regards the dimensions assessed, attention, confidence and satisfaction in digital learning environments have been successfully measured [18] along with affective, cognitive and operational dimensions [19]. However, no instruments have been found that comprehensively measure the conceptual, procedural, attitudinal, and experiential dimensions.

Regarding the instrumentation employed in heritage learning studies, a tool has been designed to assess teachers’ perceptions of heritage teaching using structural equation modelling (SEM). However, the reliance on a relatively small sample size raises concerns about adherence to the methodological requirements of SEM [20]. Additionally, the effectiveness of technological approaches and their impact on key performance indicators (KPIs) has been evaluated [21]; despite its use in measuring the impact of virtual reality on learning through a multiple-choice questionnaire administered to students, no information is provided regarding the calibration process. Furthermore, a framework has been developed to gather qualitative data on the digitisation of cultural heritage within the field of identity studies. Following psychometric studies of reliability and content validity, high levels of reliability and content validity have been demonstrated, supporting its suitability for use in heritage conservation, education, and cultural management [22].

With the proliferation of digital media and virtual environments, there is an urgent need to critically evaluate the impact of these technologies on the dissemination and interpretation of cultural heritage [23]. Developing instruments with a robust structure and adequate psychometric properties is crucial to ensure the reliability and validity of the results obtained through their application. In accordance with these detected shortcomings, we propose the construction and calibration of a standardized scale that accurately measures heritage learning based on a set of verbs that identify the main actions in the Heritage Learning Sequence (HLS), i.e., knowing, understanding, respecting, valuing, caring, enjoying, transmitting [24]. These verbs have been identified following the analysis of texts, treaties, and international recommendations on heritage [25] as well as the scrutiny of the main verbs used in the conceptualization of heritage by users of digital environments [26].

This article aims to explore the evaluation of heritage learning in digital contexts through the lens of network analysis so as to unravel the underlying structures and dynamics of digital heritage learning. The purpose is not only to evaluate the effectiveness of digital platforms in disseminating heritage knowledge, but also to identify the determinants that influence the reception and interpretation of cultural heritage in digital spaces. Network analysis is a relatively recent methodology that has nevertheless experienced a huge development in recent years [27]. It has been proposed as a conceptual alternative to traditional systems, especially measurement models based on structural equation analysis, to analyze the indicators of a theoretical construct and the relationships between them. The network analysis approach opens up new avenues for exploring the relationships across observable variables (e.g., items in an assessment instrument), and enables us to (a) understand the connections between nodes by examining the networks's graphical representation, (b) identify key nodes both in the overall network and in the sub-networks corresponding to the different dimensions of the instrument, (c) measure the relative importance of nodes, (d) analyze the existence of possible subgroups and evaluate the internal cohesion of the network, (e) detect emerging patterns by analyzing the tendency of nodes to form clusters of homogeneous elements, or (f) detect key nodes that support the design of effective intervention strategies.

Research design and hypotheses

The present study adheres to the methodology of cross-sectional survey designs, the main purpose of which is to provide a quantitative description of the opinions, beliefs, or behaviors of participants as manifested through responses to structured questionnaires [29,30]. The exploratory study starts from the Heritage Learning Sequence (HLS), which identifies the seven main verbs in heritage learning detailed in the followin section. These verbs constitute the seven dimensions of the Heritage Process Model (HPM) [26]. Each of the latent variables is assessed by seven indicators. The methods of analysis are part of the current approach known as Network Analysis of multivariate data, which involves the study of connections between the variables (items) that make up the seven constructs or dimensions —such connections being statistical relationships between the items [31]. The process followed here has been adjusted to the recommendations proposed by Burger et al. [28] for cross-sectional studies based on network analysis.

The hypotheses we intend to test are the following.1.In the network representing the observable variables (items), most of the edges will be positive, reflecting the interconnections of the items measuring the Heritage Learning Sequence (HLS).2.The edges of the items belonging to each of the seven dimensions (intra-dimension connections) will be stronger than those linking them to the rest of the items (inter-dimension connections).3.A significant part of the variance of each node will be explained by the rest of the nodes with which it is connected.4.The proportion of variance of each node explained by the nodes belonging to its theoretical dimension will be greater than that explained by the rest of the nodes in the network.5.The structure of the network will reach a sufficient degree of replicability, i.e., it will be invariant between two randomly selected subsamples of the original sample.6.The network structure will remain invariant regarding the comparison between men and women.

Method

General analysis routine

Sample collection

The dataset consists of the responses given by a total of 1967 participants aged 19–63 years (M = 26.70, SD = 9.77) who answered any of the items of the Q-Herilearn scale. This initial sample had to be considerably reduced, since participants with more than 30 missing data points in the full set of 49 items were eliminated from the analyses (listwise deletion of cases). This left the original sample reduced to a useable sample of N = 1477 persons.

Participants were invited to collaborate in the research, being informed of its aims and the importance of their contribution. Recruitment was conducted through (a) training courses (e.g., MOOCs, university courses, conferences, seminars) and (b) social media appeals. All participants completed an online survey (https://oepe.es/escala-herilearn/) between June 2022 and June 2023. Prior to taking part in the study, participants were informed of its purpose and assured complete confidentiality of their data, in accordance with the UPV-EHU Ethics Committee (CEISH, Cod: M10_2021_31). Participants could discontinue, postpone or abandon the survey at any time (in the latter case, the data were automatically deleted). A total of 1389 responses were obtained with complete sociodemographic information, along with 65 responses with partial information. To enhance the truthfulness of responses, no field was formulated as a mandatory. Acceptance of informed consent was a prerequisite for participating in the survey.

Variable selection procedure

Data were collected by administering the Q-Herilearn scale. Q-Herilearn is a probabilistic summated rating scale that measures different aspects of the learning process in Heritage Education. The process of construction and calibration, as well as the metric qualities of the scores, have been the subject of other work [32]. It consists of seven factors.1.Knowing: Acquiring an understanding of the range of cultural assets that are part of the historical and cultural heritage of a society or community.2.Understanding: Comprehending the meaning of heritage, its historical, cultural and social context, as well as the relationships and connections between different heritage items.3.Respecting: Adopting an attitude of care, appreciation, commitment and responsibility towards heritage.4.Valuing: Appreciating the importance and significance of heritage, recognizing its valuable qualities for a community.5.Caring: Taking action to protect, conserve and preserve heritage for present and future generations.6.Enjoying: Actively experiencing and appreciating heritage for pleasure and personal enrichment.7.Transmitting: Effectively sharing and communicating the knowledge, values, traditions, stories and significance of heritage to present and future generations.

Each dimension is measured by seven indicators scored on a 4-point frequency response scale (1 = Never or almost never; 2 = Sometimes; 3 = Quite often; 4 = Always or almost always). The wording of the items can be found in Tables 1–7 (SM).

The scale has sufficient evidence of content validity. It has reached both pure (BN) and weighted (BwN) agreement coefficients [33] following the ratings by 40 independent judges on relevance or importance (BwN = 0.811), appropriateness or adequacy (BwN = 0.808), and clarity of item formulation (BwN = 0.772). As for the ascription of the content of the items to the seven dimensions, the agreement among the judges was also satisfactory (BN = 0.539). Following the estimation of an exploratory structural equation model (ESEM) [34,35] with oblique target rotation, the scale showed a consistent factor structure, with RMSEA values between 0.086 (RES scale) and 0.037 (ENJ scale), CFI values between 0.983 (RES scale) and 0.998 (ENJ scale), and SRMR between 0.029 (RES scale) and 0.011 (ENJ scale). Reliability coefficients were high, with McDonald's omega ranging from 0.83 to 0.90, Composite Reliability (CR) between 0.83 and 0.92, and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) between 0.48 and 0.64.

In order to investigate the possible topological overlap between the variables, we analyzed the polychoric correlations between the 49 items. Of the 1176 correlations (Tables 9 and SM), only 9 (0.77 %) exceeded the value of 0.60, so that we did not consider it necessary to combine individual items into new variables. Both the mean (M = 0.306) and the dispersion (SD = 0.117) of the correlations were adequate for the purposes of the study.

The range of item means was 1.38 (car58) to 3.35 (res36) (Tables 8 and SM). Consequently, no items were found with excessively high or low values (very close to 4 and 1, respectively) that could present very high or very low partial correlations with the rest of the items. Therefore, no floor/ceiling effects were found in the distributions of the variables.

The minimum and maximum skewness values were −0.90 (res30) and 1.78 (car58) respectively. The minimum value of kurtosis was 1.38 (car58) and the maximum value was 3.35 (res36). Figs. 2–8 (SM) graphically present the main statistics defining the 49 items. Since the skewness and kurtosis values are not extreme, it was not considered necessary to perform any transformation (e.g., nonparanormal transformation, see Ref. [36]) or to use other options such as dichotomizing the data and running an Ising Model.

Appropriate correlations among items

In social science research it is common to find ordinal and asymmetric data. This poses a problem when choosing a correlation coefficient that is appropriate to the nature of the input data in order to make an adequate estimation of the network. Among the various options available (i.e., dichotomizing the data, using ordinal Spearman coefficients, using polychoric correlations, transforming the data) we have chosen to use polychoric correlations for two reasons: first, we have not found large differences between polychoric correlations and Spearman correlations (the average difference between the two matrices was M = 0.066, SD = 0.024). Secondly, we have not found cells with too few observations in the cross-tables, which could give rise to spurious negative edges [37]. Polychoric correlations can be found in Table 9 and Spearman correlations in Table 10 (SM). Graphical representations of the items for each of the seven dimensions are also provided in Figs. 2–8 (SM). For each pair of items, the x-y pairwise scatter plot, the locally smoothed regression line, the ellipse around the mean with the axis length reflecting one standard deviation of the x and y variables, and the Spearman correlations with the corresponding associated significance level are included. For each variable the values of skewness and kurtosis are included, as well as the frequency histogram and the density curve of the distribution. Of the 49 items, only two presented relatively high values for negative skewness (res30, Skp = −0.90; res36, Skp = −0.87) and three for positive skewness (car58, Skp = 1.78; car60, Skp = 1.42; tra97, Skp = 1.35).

Estimation method

In this paper we have used network analysis as the main methodological approach. Network analysis uses the principles of graph theory to analyze the relationships between items. The method goes one step further than traditional factor analysis, as it takes into account the complex network of relationships between items rather than simply considering their individual contributions to the measurement of a construct.

A network is an abstract system consisting of nodes (observable variables, items) connected by edges representing conditional dependence relations between variables. In this study, such relations were regularized partial correlations. The relationships denote the strength of the connection between the nodes. This type of model is called Pairwise Markov Random Field (PMRF) [38]. In our case, given the ordinal nature of the input data, we used GGM (Gaussian Graphical Model, EBICglasso) and regularization as estimation method [39], and a tuning-parameter gamma set to 0.5 [40]. In these models the parameters represent unique associations between two variables, after conditioning all other variables in the network [28]. The EBICglasso procedure requires estimating the variance-covariance matrix and returns a matrix of partial correlation coefficients. Since we have a four-point ordinal response scale, we estimate the matrix of polychoric correlations.

Network analysis offers advantages such as identifying patterns and structures within the network; refining the assessment instrument by identifying redundant or poorly connected items; grouping items into meaningful clusters based on the strength of their relationships; and understanding the influence of different items on the overall measure. All of these can contribute to creating more robust and theoretically grounded instruments, ensuring that items are adequately connected and contribute substantively to the overall assessment.

Network accuracy

Non-parametric bootstrapping methods based on 1000 samples have been used to analyze the accuracy of the network parameters (edge weights) and the stability of the centrality indices. For this purpose, the following three strategies have been followed [38]: (a) bootstrapped confidence intervals have been calculated to estimate the accuracy of the edge weights; (b) the stability of the centrality indices (strength, closeness, and betweenness) has been determined after removing successive portions of the data; and (c) bootstrapped difference tests have been applied to both edge weights and centrality indices to test for differences.

The above analyses have been complemented with bootstrapped analyses of the items within each dimension (see Figs. 11–19 in SM).

Statistical packages

The analyses were performed with R, version 4.3.1 [41]. Network estimation was performed with the bootnet package, version 1.5.3 [42]. Network visualization was performed with the qgraph package, version 1.9.5 [43]. The R code used is included in the SM.

Analysis-specific routine

Group comparisons

In order to examine the invariance of the different networks, two kinds of comparisons were performed: (a) by gender and (b) by random subsamples. Groups were compared for overall difference in strength by means of the Network Comparison Test, version 2.2.2 (NCT; [44]) using 1000 iterations and with seed set to “123”. In addition to assessing through the Network Comparison Test whether the two networks differed in their overall level of connectivity, we correlated the weighted adjacency matrices of each pair of networks (male – female, random subsample 1 – random subsample 2) as an additional measure of similarity between the networks. In both cases the starting hypothesis consisted of the invariance of the network structures in the compared groups, due to two reasons: (a) no literature has been found that analyzes or suggests the existence of differences between groups and (b) there are no theoretical reasons to assume that the network structure is different in both groups.

Centrality indices

We used, among the numerous centrality metrics available, three such indices: strength, closeness and betweenness [45]. These indices provide information on the structural and functional characteristics of the network nodes. Strength equals the sum of the weights of the edges connected to each node in a weighted network. It quantifies the importance (i.e., total influence) of a node on the basis of the strength of its connections, and is useful for identifying nodes with direct connections or interactions within the network. Closeness indicates how quickly a node can reach the rest of the nodes in the network, and is used to identify well-connected and central nodes in terms of accessibility. Betweenness measures the relative number of shortest paths passing through a specific node. Nodes with high betweenness centrality often act as bridges or intermediaries in the network, thus facilitating the flow of information or influence across different parts of the network. Of particular interest in this study is Strength Centrality.

Differences between edges whithin one network

In order to determine whether some of the edges stood out from the rest in the network structure, we performed a bootstrap difference test using the differenceTest function of the bootnet package, version 1.5.3 [42].

Clustering

Clustering is defined as the tendency to form groups of nodes according to the connections between them. In order to determine the optimal number of dimensions, we used parallel analysis [46] and Minimum Average Partial MAP [47]. These analyses were complemented by the calculation of BIC and EBIC. In order to determine the existence of clusters, we used the Exploratory Graph Analysis (EGA) method [48]. Finally, we compared, following the outcomes of the previous analyses, the results of two models: (a) seven factors and (b) bifactor.

Sample characteristics.

Participants were predominantly under 30 years of age (86.1 %), female (70.6 %) and residents in Spain (87.8 %), having visited three or fewer countries (57.5 %), living in urban areas (79.0 %), native speakers of Spanish, (81.5 %), with a higher education background (85.7 %) and with at least one daily Internet connection (86.0 %).

Data collection and cleansing

Data were retrieved from the LimeSurvey platform, transferred to R, and cleaned using the following three strategies: selection of anomalous responses, multivariate outlier detection, and missing data processing.

Selection of anomalous responses

Anomalous response patterns (e.g., repetitive, invariant, random, or sloppy responses) can deeply alter the results of data analysis, even if they occur in very small proportions [49,50] To avoid this bias, the data were cleaned in two ways: first, cases that provided the same response to all 49 items (Straight Lining) were eliminated (N = 10 = 0.68 %, SD = 0.00), considering that this pattern is highly unlikely given the number of test items.

Multivariate outlier detection

As shown in Fig. 1, we plot the robust Mahalanobis squared ordered distances of the observations against the empirical distribution function of MD2i. Fig. 1 shows the maximum value curve of the MD2i distribution and the maximum values detected by the specified quantile (97.5 %). Multivariate outliers, i.e., observations outside the 97.5 quantile of the χ2 distribution (N = 40, 2.71 %) marked in red in Fig. 1(b) were removed (numbers correspond to observations in the original database). The first subfigure shows the peak value curve of the MD2i distribution, and the second subfigure shows the peak values detected by the specified adjusted quantile (97.5 %). In the second place, we estimated on that reduced sample the polytomous mode of the standardized likelihood ratio l pz [51,52] for each response vector. To avoid the inaccuracies associated with a purely visual assessment, Epskamp et al. [52] introduced a statistic known as the correlation-stability coefficient (CS-Coefficient). This statistic signifies the mean percentage of the sample that can be omitted to maintain a correlation of r = 0.7 between the observed sample centrality indices and those derived from the case-dropped bootstraps. The underlying idea is that if we can exclude a substantial number of cases from the original sample and still maintain a high correlation across centrality indices, we have strong evidence that these nodes are not influenced by sample characteristics (i.e., they are likely to be stable in the population). To calculate the CS coefficient (r = 0.7), we use the ‘corStability(.)’ function of the bootnet package. The correlation-stability coefficient achieved was CS = 0.75, indicating the average percentage of the empirical sample that can be excluded while still observing a correlation of at least r = 0.70 between the sample centrality indices and the bootstrap case-dropping centrality indices.

Predictability

The predictability of a node quantifies how well that particular node can be predicted by all remaining nodes [[56], [57], [58]]. Node predictability is important because of several reasons: first, the edges connected to an item X could be interpreted by considering how much variance of X is explained by the remaining items in the network to which it is connected; second, we can use the predictability measure of X to determine how well we can influence X by intervening on the items to which X is connected; third, item predictability informs whether the network is largely self-determined (i.e, there are strong mutual connections between nodes) or whether it is determined by factors external to the network [56]. In this study we have used R2 as a measure of predictability. Table 13 (Predictability of Nodes, SM) presents the R2 values. These values ranged from 0.361 (res29) to 0.614 (enj81), M = 0.494, SD = 0.068, and are represented by colored segments outside each node in the network graph (Fig. 6). Considering each of the dimensions, the highest R2 values corresponded to items kno6 (R2 = 0.575), und18 (R2 = 0.551), res30 (R2 = 0.547), val49 (R2 = 0.553), car59 (R2 = 0.572), enj81 (R2 = 0.614) and tra86 (R2 = 0.610). These items, therefore, are strongly influenced by the rest of the nodes in the network. In contrast, the nodes with lower R2 values were res29 (R2 = 0.361), val50 (R2 = 0.371) and und15 (R2 = 0.392), indicating that these nodes may be relatively independent within the network. Considering the presence of multiple strong mutual interactions among the items, we can conclude that the network has a high predictability, with a marked tendency towards self-determination. This seems especially true for the dimensions ENJ (MR2 = 0.554), CAR (MR2 = 0.531), TRA (MR2 = 0.529) and KNO (MR2 = 0.502).

In addition to analyzing the amount of variance of each item explained by the rest of the items of the network, we have analyzed the R2 values determined by the connections of each item with the rest of the items of its factor or dimension. The results, synthesized in Fig. 21 (SM), lead us to several conclusions. First, the proportion of variance of each item explained by the items of its factor is much larger than that explained by the rest of the items of the network. Second, the correlations between the R2 values explained by the items of each dimension and the R2 values explained by all the nodes of the network have all been positive and very high, ranging from r = 0.560 (ENJ factor) to r = 0.953 (VAL factor). Third, the high intra-dimension R2 values suggest the existence of strong connections of the set of items that form them. These results lend support to hypothesis 4.

Replicability

Replicability is identified with the ability to reproduce or replicate the results of the analysis when similar data and methods are used. This is an essential issue in research, which aims to ensure that findings are not based on chance or specific conditions, but can be reproduced in different contexts or by different researchers. In this sense, replicability helps to establish the robustness, consistency and validity of the results in network analysis.

To examine replicability, we divided the sample into two random subsamples (N1 = 661, N2 = 662) and calculated for each subsample the corresponding network, centrality indices and R2 values. As can be seen in Fig. 1 (SM) both networks present a high degree of similarity, reaching a correlation of r = 0.608. On the other hand, the correlations between the centrality indices of both networks have been positive and significant in all cases: Strength: r = 0.849; Closeness: r = 0.711; Betweenness: r = 0.446; Expected Influence: r = 0.891.

Table 14 (SM) shows the R2 values for each item in both subsamples. The correlation coefficient between the R2 values was very high (r = 0.781). These results were confirmed using the Network Comparison Test [44,59], with 1000 iterations in all analyses and set.seed(123).

In the replicability analysis we performed three comparisons: (a) we checked whether the structure of both networks is identical; (b) we examined whether the edges are similar in strength in the two samples, and (c) we analyzed whether the global connectivity is the same in the two samples.

The maximum difference between edges was 0.142. The difference in strength between both networks amounted to 0.107. On the other hand, the results showed that the networks of both subsamples are comparable: the overall strength of the first subsample was 23.766 and that of the second sample 23.874 (S = 0.107, p = 0.785). We finally analyzed the invariance of the network structure. The results (M = 0.142, p = 0.389) indicated that, globally considered, the structures of both networks are not significantly different. Possible differences between some individual edges were discarded and are not worth discussing, given the level of p. No significant differences were found between the two samples in any of the centrality indices. All comparisons were non-significant (adjusted p range between 0.506 and 0.996).

In view of these results, we can conclude that the degree of replicability achieved is highly satisfactory, and that the probability of committing a type I error is clearly low. The above results lend support to hypothesis 5, demonstrating the invariance of the edges in two random subsamples.

Discussion and conclusions

Network analysis is a robust methodology derived from the field of complex systems, which provides an ideal framework for understanding the intricate connections and interactions of heritage learning indicators in the context of digital environments. Its implementation in this article has provided a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underpinning cultural heritage learning. The analyses performed provide evidence of the ability of the Q-Herilearn scale to measure each of the seven dimensions of heritage learning with sufficient guarantees of validity, stability, predictability, replicability, and sensitivity.

The consistency of essential network characteristics (i.e., centrality, accuracy of edge weights, stability of centrality indices, predictability of nodes) has been tested. Moreover, the invariance of the network structures between groups provides strong guarantees of the correct functioning of the items considered individually, as well as of their relationships within the same dimension. Some pairs of items analyzed suggest the possibility —and the potential interest— of combining them in the deployment of heritage learning, identifying short sequences that can be profitably used to structure teaching. The results in terms of the predictability of the items —both considering the whole sample and the random subsamples as well as the subsamples of men and women— show that a considerable part of the variance of the nodes is explained by the rest of the nodes with which they are connected (in the general network and in the sub-networks), so that we can consider the network as self-determined. On the other hand, the nodes with the highest levels of predictability were identified within each of the seven dimensions. In all cases the R2 values exceeded 0.5, which indicates the strong cohesion between the nodes, a cohesion that is endorsed by the correlation coefficients achieved. No node can be considered independent, since no R2 values ≤ 0.35 were observed. In any case, the lower values could be explained by the complexity of the model (i.e., 49 items grouped into seven factors).

The reduced range of values reached by the centrality indices could lead to several relevant conclusions. First, this is a limited network in terms of the diversity of connections, in that no significant diversity of inter-node connections between nodes is observed. While it is true that some nodes are more relevant than others, most have a similar number of connections, as we discussed in the Network Visualization section and as can be inferred from examining the BootSample table. Secondly, we can qualify the network as homogeneous, which points at the significance of all the nodes that make it up. Thirdly, with the exceptions noted in the Network Visualization section regarding the most strongly connected nodes in each dimension, the existence of hubs or highly connected nodes is not apparent. Consequently, we consider that the low range of centrality values falls within what can be considered normal.

The main heritage learnings are collected in this scale, which is specifically developed for digital environments. The breadth of the sociodemographic data collected (i.e., age, gender, country, countries visited, area of residence, mother tongue, studies, occupation, technology use habits and connectivity, as well as different cultural leisure habits in people's free time) will enable us to know the differences in learning outcomes in several contexts, according to the level of knowledge or even according to previous heritage experiences or technological and cultural habits. In this sense, the scale has been created by incorporating items from people in diverse geo-political and cultural contexts, so that its scope is international. In addition, it has been translated and adapted into Spanish, English, French, Basque, Italian and Portuguese; this will make it possible to obtain responses from people from a wide range of backgrounds and, as a consequence, will allow the comparison of results between them.

On the other hand, the results of the regularized partial correlations between the items indicate that, in their application, learning patterns can be identified from clusters that show the tendency to form relationships between several dimensions, i.e., heritage learning models (e.g., know-enjoy-transmit; understand-value-care). Finally, the behaviour of the items within each of the seven dimensions (see Fig. 20-a to 20-g, SM), enable each dimension to be applied as a separate scale or even to carry out specific combinations of several of these dimensions.

Implications

The uses of the Q-Herilearn scale not only refer to the measurement of heritage learning outcomes that take place in digital environments (e.g., apps, websites, museum educational spaces, video games, augmented reality, virtual reality), since it is also possible to measure heritage learnings derived from on-site or face-to-face educational interventions, where part of the learning process occurs in such environments. This makes it possible to use the scale as an instrument for measuring or evaluating learning outcomes generated by the implementation of such actions. In this case, we should be talking about the evaluation of heritage learning originated in a digital setting. By incorporating items linked to people's behavior in relation to heritage, we can also measure the different behavioral profiles which, in turn, indicate the degree of knowledge, understanding, appreciation, care, enjoyment, and transmission of the heritage content with which we are working on in our educational actions.

Additionally, the scale is useful in terms of its own structure because it identifies the seven main verbs in heritage learning (HLS) and, therefore, determines the objectives of heritage-related teaching. The order in which the seven dimensions are structured (i.e., knowing, understanding, respecting, valuing, caring and enjoying) has been endorsed by a previous study (Fontal et al., 2023), which also provides the sequential order in which to use these verbs (e.g., we cannot care for those cultural goods that we do not value, nor can we value that which we do not understand; therefore, the degree of understanding depends on the type and degree of knowledge with which we approach heritage).

On the other hand, by analyzing the regularized partial correlations between items (and the original variance-covariance matrix), it is possible to identify learning profiles that exhibit a tendency to form relationships between several dimensions. These could be termed “heritage learning models'’ (e.g., know-enjoy-transmit; understand-value-care, etc.), as outlined in a prior study [64].

Finally, the items themselves comprise statements or sentences that can be used in the design of educational proposals as actions that pursue the objectives linked to each dimension (e.g., “I make use of digital geolocation tools (maps) to identify places of heritage interest”, “Virtual reality and augmented reality are means that help me to better understand cultural heritage”, “Digital environments specific to heritage contribute to perpetuate attitudes of respect for it”, etc.).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Perhaps the most important one has to do with the use of a non-probabilistic incidental sample. Although sufficient precision and statistical power have been guaranteed, the nature of the sample could affect the external validity of the results. This limitation could take the form of (a) the sample's shortcomings in accurately representing the population, with the consequent risk of bias in the results; (b) limitation of the sample's generalizability, to the extent that the results may not be fully applicable to a larger population; (c) certain groups may be over- or under-represented, given that an online survey has been used; or (d) potential increase of systematic error (bias), since certain groups of participants may be more likely to be included in the sample than others; or (e) as the majority of participants are residents of Spain, the study's findings primarily reflect their experiences, limiting generalizability to residents of other countries within the sample. We consider, however, that these limitations are not particularly serious, given that this is a first exploratory study of the problem. In any case, it would be advisable, in future research, to use probability sampling procedures on the different populations to which the evaluation instrument is applied.

Future avenues for research

Understanding the intricate interdependencies and relationships that occur in heritage learning is paramount to designing effective strategies that optimize the potential of digital technologies. In this way, a deeper understanding and appreciation of cultural heritage can be fostered among diverse audiences. Through the application of network analysis, this study aimed to lay the foundation for the development of innovative and sustainable approaches to heritage learning in digital environments.

Building on the results of this article, we can identify some immediate, natural lines of continuity that we contemplate undertaking in the short term:

Evaluating the seven measurement models (one for each dimension) according to the PLS-SEM empirical rules (i.e., reliability of the indicators, internal consistency, evidence of convergent and discriminant validity, loadings and cross-loadings).

Performing a systematic evaluation of the structural model by means of specific procedures, e.g., collinearity (VIF), relevance (β), statistical significance (t) of the structural model relationships, explanatory power (R2), predictive power (Q2), etc.

Complementing the calibration proposed in this article by assessing reliability and validity on the basis of content and internal structure using Exploratory Structural Equation Modeling ESEM and the Graded Response Model GRM.

Applying the scale on different heritage education implementations so as to measure its effects.

Applying the scale to measure specific educational programs in which one or more verbs of the HLP Sequence are used.

Given the significant regional heterogeneity observed in Spain, future studies might benefit from analysing the results in terms of this variability.
