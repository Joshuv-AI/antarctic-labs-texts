The predicament of rural middle-aged and older adults in preparing for active aging: a qualitative study
Authors: Meiling Yang, Jiachun Lu, Yingai Cui, Yadan Liu, Thomas Kwok Shing Wong, Yanli Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to explore the preparedness and barriers faced by rural middle-aged and older adults coping with active aging, to reveal the challenges of aging management and provide recommendations for promoting active aging in rural China.MethodsThis study used a qualitative design with one-on-one semi-structured interviews and purposive sampling to select middle-aged and older adults from rural areas in Tongren, Guizhou Province, China as the study participants. NVivo 14 software and content analysis method were employed to analyze the interview data and extract key themes.ResultsA total of 17 participants were included in the study. Four main themes were identified: individual-level determinants, family-level determinants, societal-level determinants, and environment-level determinants, along with twenty-five sub-themes.ConclusionThe preparedness of rural middle-aged and older adults for active aging is generally low, and they face a complex array of challenges. Multiple sectors of society must develop measures to enhance the awareness of active aging among rural residents, alleviate economic pressures, establish a sound social security system, and promote the high-quality development of active aging in rural areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25678-6.Keywords: Active aging, Rural, Qualitative study, Middle-aged and older adults

Introduction

Currently, the rapid aging of the population is the most important medical and social demographic problem globally [1]. China’s aging process has been particularly rapid. According to data from the National Bureau of Statistics [2], the proportion of the population aged 60 and above reached 18.70% in 2020, and by the end of 2023, it further increased to 21.1%. Aging has thus become a focal point of attention across various sectors of society. The degree and speed of aging in rural areas of China are much higher than in urban areas, presenting a phenomenon of “urban-rural reversal“ [3]. Rapid aging has resulted in the issue of “aging before preparation“ [4], with both awareness and practices related to aging preparation being insufficient in Chinese society [5]. This is particularly evident in rural areas, where the supply of older adults care services is unable to meet the demand. Addressing the impacts of aging on individuals, families, and society has become a pressing challenge that needs to be tackled [6].

The term “Active Aging” was adopted by the World Health Organization (WHO) in the late 1990s and originated from the concepts of “Successful Aging”, “Healthy Aging” and “Productive Aging“ [7]. Today, it is widely used defined as older adults fully utilizing their physical, mental, and social potential, engaging in society according to their needs, desires, and abilities to improve their quality of life, while also receiving adequate support and care when needed [8]. Health, participation, and security are thus the basic pillars of active ageing [9]. These elements are key to ensuring that older adults can maintain a high quality of life. “Health” refers to the condition of maintaining positive health in physical, mental, and spiritual aspects, including physical health, mental health, and good social adaptation; “Participation” means that the older adults participate in socio-economic, cultural, and spiritual activities according to their abilities, needs, and preferences; “Security” refers to supporting families and communities to take care of the older adults through various ways when they are unable to take care of themselves [10].

The preparing for active aging relies not only on support from social or family systems but also on individuals proactively preparing in the earlier stages of life to ensure that the needs of later life are met [11]. Late-life preparation was defined as “an effort to ensure that major problems will not develop at some point in the distant future“ [12]. Each stage of human development affects and is affected by the previous one, so the quality of life in old age is influenced by the lifestyle during adulthood, and preparation for old age should begin in adulthood [13]. Through early preparation, individuals can enhance their ability to cope with the challenges and changes of aging, while fully leveraging the opportunities that later life offers (such as autonomy and freedom from external obligations) [5]. This ultimately improves the quality of life and well-being of older adults [14]. A systematic review [15] of 42 studies by Mahnaz and colleagues categorized aging preparation into six key dimensions: health, finances, psychological well-being, housing, social engagement, and leisure activities. For instance, regular physical exercise in midlife can reduce the risk of functional limitations and disability in old age, laying a solid physical foundation for active aging [16]. A well-established social network during middle age ensures adequate social support in retirement and later years, reducing the risks of loneliness and depression [17]. Adequate financial preparation positively influences healthcare decisions, physical health, and psychological well-being during old age [18].

In summary, this study defines preparing for active aging as the proactive planning and actions undertaken by individuals, with the collaborative support of family and social networks, across key areas such as health, finances, psychological well-being, housing, social engagement, and leisure, it aims to help older adults cope with later-life challenges and opportunities, realize their potential, and actively participate in society, thus ultimately enhancing the quality of life in their future senior years.

Currently, research on active aging, both domestically and internationally, primarily focuses on the development of assessment tools, analysis of influencing factors, and exploration of intervention measures, with a predominant emphasis on community-dwelling older adults [8]. In recent years, domestic scholars have gradually shifted their research focus to rural areas. However, compared to international studies, this area is still in its early stages [19]. Multiple evaluations indicate that the level of active aging among older adults in rural China is generally at a moderate to low level [20–23]. These research findings indicate that active aging among older adults in rural areas requires urgent attention and improvement. The factors influencing the level of active aging in rural areas primarily include age, cultural factors, health conditions, marital status, economic situation, and living conditions [19]. Current studies are mostly cross-sectional quantitative surveys, which primarily assess the level of active aging and its influencing factors among rural older adults. However, there is a lack of qualitative research that delves into the barriers to preparation for active aging in rural areas. Adequate preparation for active aging is crucial for the successful transition of middle-aged and older adults in rural areas to active aging.

This study employs one-on-one, semi-structured interviews to explore the preparation of rural middle-aged and older adults in China for active aging and the barriers they face. The findings can inform targeted interventions to help individuals prepare materially, psychologically, and spiritually for later life, enabling them to actively respond to aging. The research aims to advance active aging practices in rural areas, contribute to China’s active aging development, and supplement the WHO’s theoretical model of active aging.

Methods

Research design

This study utilized a qualitative design with semi-structured interviews to examine the preparation of rural middle-aged and older adults to cope with active aging and their hindering factors, in order to reveal key issues of rural old-age care. In preparing the manuscript, we followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (CORE-Q) checklist [24].

Study sample

This study employs a purposive sampling method. The middle-aged and older adults living in rural areas of Tongren City, Guizhou Province were selected as subjects. The sample includes farmers whose primary income source is agricultural cultivation, migrant workers whose main economic source is working outside, and middle-aged and older adults who primarily care for their grandchildren. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 50 years; (2) currently living in the countryside; (3) voluntary participation in the study; (4) clear verbal or written communication skills.

Data collection

The study was initiated by a nursing PhD (female) with 9 years of experience in the field of geriatric care, who established initial contact with participants. The research was carried out by two nursing master’s students (female) who received systematic qualitative research training, with support from three additional nursing PhDs (one male, two female) to ensure the scientific rigor of the study. Before the formal interviews, the relevant literature on active aging from both domestic and international sources was reviewed, and the initial interview guidelines were discussed with two nursing experts. Based on the results of the pre-interviews and feedback from experts, the interview guide was refined (see Supplementary File 1).

This study adopts one-on-one semi-structured interviews. The principal researcher of this study is from Guizhou and is fluent in the local dialect. Quiet and private locations were selected to ensure the confidentiality of the interviews. The researcher first introduced themselves, explained the research objectives and duration of the interviews, and clarified the significance and main content of the interviews to the participants before each interview. Participants were informed that they could withdraw at any time without any consequences and would not bear any responsibility. After obtaining informed consent from the participants and having them sign the consent forms, the entire interview process was recorded. During the interviews, the researcher communicated in Mandarin (the official language of China) for participants who were proficient in it. For participants who were less familiar with Mandarin, the interviews were conducted in the local dialect. In cases where dialect expressions were unclear, the researcher promptly clarified them. Each participant underwent only one interview.

Data analysis

Initially, within 24 h after each interview, the researcher repeatedly played back the recorded interviews, promptly transcribed the recordings into text. During transcription, any dialect portions were replaced with standard Mandarin to ensure the accuracy of audio-to-text conversion. Ensure that the recording materials are promptly destroyed after the completion of the study to maintain anonymity. To protect the privacy of the participants, we anonymized the information and used codes from F1 to F17. This study employed content analysis [25] for data analysis. The two authors carefully reviewed the interview transcripts stored in NVivo 14 software to thoroughly familiarize themselves with the data. They then conducted a detailed, line-by-line reading of the text to extract meaningful semantic units for open coding. This process transformed the extensive textual data into smaller, more manageable segments. Next, the authors compared the codes based on their attributes and the relationships between them, identifying similarities and differences. Codes with common characteristics were grouped into subcategories, which were then consolidated into broader categories based on the similarities between the subcategories. Any discrepancies in the coding and inductive theme identification process were resolved through discussions within the research team to reach a consensus. To enhance the credibility of the study, all codes were verified through interaction with two domain experts. In cases of disagreement, multiple rounds of discussions were conducted until consensus was reached. If necessary, a third-party expert was consulted for arbitration. This process resulted in the final, refined coding system.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The research design and data collection in this study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. The research procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Medical University (approval number L202306002). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, who signed the informed consent form. Additionally, participants were anonymized to protect their privacy.

It was scheduled for over 30 min, with the actual effective interview time ranging from 20 to 40 min ( calculated as follows: the time for greetings, explaining the research objectives and precautions, and later confirming and correcting the interview content with the participants was excluded). Respondents’ general information (n = 17)

The preparedness of rural middle-aged and older adults for active aging was generally insufficient. Among the 17 participants, only one had made some economic and mental preparations, yet still fell short of his desired outcomes. The remaining 16 acknowledged the importance of active aging for quality of life in old age but faced barriers that hindered their efforts. Despite their willingness to prepare, they felt overwhelmed by challenges and unable to effectively plan for the practical realities of aging.

“What is there to prepare for? For us rural folks, we hardly have the energy to consider these things.” (F12)

“What kind of preparations can we make? We’re still under so much stress. It’s really unnecessary to think about retirement. Let’s just focus on living well in the present.” (F2)

Through the coding of the interview data, 4 themes and 25 sub-themes were ultimately identified, revealing the challenges faced by rural middle-aged and older adults in preparing for active aging.

Theme 1: individual-level determinants

Weak awareness of active aging

Low expectations for active aging

Participants generally had low expectations regarding active aging and lacked a positive outlook on older adulthood. Most believed that the goal of older adulthood was simply to meet basic survival needs, without attributing further meaning or quality to their lives.

“For us rural people, the general expectation is that life is manageable. As for me, maintaining good health is sufficient.” (F5)

Continuous labor: avoid burden and maintain autonomy

Participants place labor value above health value, yet neglect the essential role that health plays in active aging. They believed older adulthood should involve labor to ease economic pressure and achieve self-sufficiency, avoiding being a burden to their children. Despite poor health, many rural older adults continue to work excessively, leading to health exhaustion and preventing them from enjoying a fulfilling life in old age. This mindset reflects a lack of awareness of active aging, where labor becomes a necessity driven by the fear of burdening others.

“In my sixties, I was in good health and wanted to take advantage of it to do more farming and save some money… I didn’t want to increase the burden on my children.” (F15)

“I just want to work while I can and not burden my children.” (F10)

Familial duty: prioritizing grandchild care over self-health

Participants fully associate the value of aging with family sacrifice, thereby hindering the potential for personal self-development in active aging. They commonly perceive their contributions in old age as being centered on caregiving for grandchildren and household chores, with the motivation of supporting their children’s participation in the labor market to sustain the family livelihood.

“Once I turn 60 and can’t work outside anymore, I’ll just stay at home to take care of my grandchildren, allowing my children to go out to work without worry.” (F8)

The mindset of “taking one step at a time”

The passive mindset directly undermines the internal drive of participants to set goals, plan pathways, and take action for active aging. When facing aging, they generally adopt a “go with the flow” attitude, lacking long-term planning and proactive preparation, instead responding with a mindset of accepting things as they come. Many have neither clear goals for their later years nor corresponding plans, preferring to passively adapt to changes in their environment.

“I’m only in my fifties, but I can’t find any work outside anymore. The family burden is still heavy. I’m just taking it one day at a time now.” (F13)

Limited knowledge of active aging

The lack of in-depth understanding of health management among participants leads to insufficient health preparation for active aging. They generally believe that health is determined by fate, and therefore lack effective strategies to address aging-related health issues. While some participants mention health maintenance methods such as exercise, mindset, and diet, the health care knowledge they provide is simplistic, lacking both professionalism and depth.

“It’s hard to say and really depends on luck. Some people get cancer when they’re older, while others in their seventies or eighties are still quite robust. You just can’t predict how things will turn out in the future.” (F17)

Lack of awareness of life adjustment for active aging

This lack of awareness and knowledge about adjustments prevents participants from effectively planning for and responding to the challenges of aging. 41.2% of participants believed that in rural areas, everyone leads a similar life, and aging does not have distinct characteristics. As long as basic needs are met, they felt no need to adjust their lifestyle. Some participants noted that adjustments for aging are related to economic conditions and self-care ability, and once these are lost, adjustments seem meaningless. 58.8% of participants generally lacked knowledge of healthy lifestyles and were unsure how to effectively adjust to aging.

“For people in rural areas, what adjustments are needed? Life is the same. As long as we can eat and wear warm clothes, that’s enough.” (F1)

“I haven’t read much, so I really don’t know how to express this.” (F2)

Health conditions: the strain of chronic illness

The psychological and physiological stress caused by severe illness weakens participants’ confidence and motivation for active aging preparation. Participants report that diseases such as cancer and myocardial ischemia not only threaten their survival but also intensify fear of death and a crisis in safety needs. Prolonged suffering leads to psychological exhaustion, with many participants experiencing a persistent sense of helplessness. Older participants express deep regret for not managing their health effectively in their youth, and these factors significantly diminish their quality of life.

“I was diagnosed with myocardial ischemia after going to the hospital, and it has been sixteen years now. Several times at night, I had episodes where I couldn’t breathe and had to get up and stand at the door for a long time to catch my breath. There were several instances where I thought I wouldn’t survive.” (F16)

Behavioral constraints

The impact of educational disadvantage on learning ability

Low levels of education limit participants’ ability to acquire health literacy and knowledge, hindering their preparation for health aspects of active aging. The illiteracy rate among participants was 58.8%, with the highest level of education being at the secondary school level. The generally low educational background created barriers for participants in terms of knowledge transfer, preventing them from effectively accessing written health information. In addition, participants commonly perceived themselves as lacking learning ability and lacked the confidence to acquire new knowledge, making it difficult for them to effectively grasp new information.

“You see, we didn’t go to school when we were young, and now we don’t recognize many words. How can we learn anything new?” (F12)

Loss of workforce leading to the erosion of economic independence

The loss of economic independence prevents participants from having the necessary financial capacity to plan and save for active aging. Illness has destroyed their ability to work, forcing them to withdraw from productive sectors and become marginal participants in household tasks. They rely on their children’s support but are unwilling to increase the financial burden on them, leading to a lack of focus on preparing for and addressing their needs in old age.

“My son works outside, and every month he has to spend money to buy my medicine. The pocket money for me and my husband at home also comes from him.” (F2)

“Now my lumbar and cervical spine are not in good condition, so I can’t go out to work anymore. I just do some housework at home.” (F7)

Theme 2 family-level determinants

Financial burdens

Economic struggles: debt and lack of savings

Financial difficulties have limited the participants’ investment and planning in core areas of active aging, such as health and social participation. Due to family hardships, such as the construction of new houses, many households are burdened with heavy debt. Additionally, the reduction in income and increase in expenses make it difficult for them to repay debts on time. Meanwhile, responsibilities such as raising children, caring for spouses, and a lack of proper financial planning contribute to the absence of savings, leaving them unprepared financially for their later years.

" We borrowed a lot of money to build this house, and now we can only pay it back slowly.” (F9)

“In the past, the money we earned from working outside was spent each year, and we didn’t save anything. Now, we’re just taking it one step at a time.” (F6)

Reduced financial resources of family

The decline in household income forces participants to focus their energy and resources primarily on maintaining their livelihood and managing financial pressures within the family. Due to age and illness, participants cannot work in the city, drastically reducing household income. They rely on low-income activities like farming, raising livestock, and teapicking to meet basic needs, resulting in low average monthly family income.

“Our family is under a lot of economic pressure, but our income is unstable. Sometimes we make two or three thousand yuan in a month, and other times we have no income at all.” (F3)

Heavy medical burden squeezing resources for elderly care

Chronic diseases exacerbate the economic burden, diverting resources needed for active aging preparation. Many participants and their spouses suffer from chronic illnesses with long durations, resulting in heavy medical expenses. They report requiring multiple hospitalizations annually, and the cost of medications they purchase monthly exceeds insurance coverage, with most of the expenses being out-of-pocket. As a result, they hope to reduce medical costs to alleviate the financial pressure on their families.

“Moreover, my health isn’t good either; I have kidney disease and have to be hospitalized every month, with each hospitalization costing over a thousand yuan.” (F12)

" Ideally, I would like to reduce my annual expenses for medication; this is my greatest wish.” (F4)

Intergenerational caregiving responsibilities

Participants currently face significant family responsibilities, which compel them to allocate most of their resources to meeting family needs, making it difficult to focus on self-development and preparation for active aging. Encompassing three main responsibilities: raising children and supporting older parents, covering children’s marriage expenses due to traditional values, and financing education, including tuition and living costs.

“We have a son who has dementia, and although my son-in-law provides some living expenses for my three grandchildren, we still end up spending our own money to support all three of them.” (F3)

“My son is still not married, and I’m anxious about his future marriage. It’s tough these days to find the money for weddings, and I need to prepare despite the costs.” (F11)

“I have a daughter at university and two in high school; the combined tuition and living expenses are quite substantial. I mainly cover these costs through my business.” (F12)

Theme 3 societal-level determinants

Institutional factors

Impact of economic environment

The pressure of the broader economic environment made it difficult for participants to make necessary plans and savings for their future older adulthood. Participants and the primary labor force in their families were mostly engaged in physically demanding jobs, such as in the construction industry, with limited career options and difficulty transitioning to other fields. More critically, participants generally felt the worsening of the overall economic environment, which directly led to a significant reduction in available job opportunities, lower and unstable income levels.

“To be honest, this year the economic environment isn’t good. The people around here hardly find work on construction sites, and there’s basically not much income.” (F1)

Age-related employment barriers and forced transition to farming

Participants faced significant employment barriers due to aging. Even before reaching retirement age, they struggled to find work in non-agricultural sectors due to age discrimination and policy restrictions. As they aged, this exclusion intensified, and they were eventually forced to return to agriculture, earning limited income from crop cultivation or raising livestock. This income primarily covered basic living expenses and helped reduce the financial burden on their children. Participants expressed a strong need for employment assistance to secure stable income for themselves and their families.

“Once I turned sixty, no one wanted to hire me to work outside. I could only stay at home, maybe plant some crops and manage to take care of myself.” (F5)

Infrastructural factors

Digital divide: lack of learning channels

The lack of systematic learning pathways severely weakens participants’ preparedness in building knowledge reserves and adaptive capabilities for active aging. Living in rural areas, participants generally face a digital divide, with smartphones being their only tool for accessing external information. However, their low educational levels limit their proficiency in using smartphone functions, relying mainly on grandchildren to teach them basic apps like WeChat and Douyin, and lacking effective channels for self-improvement. Female participants, in particular, face heavier caregiving responsibilities, leaving them with little time or energy for independent learning.

“I have trouble even using my phone. I just casually check it sometimes and hardly have any time to look at it. My daily routine is all about doing housework, cooking meals for several people, and even during break time, I have to take care of my granddaughter.” (F10)

Inadequate healthcare infrastructure and access barriers

The structural shortage of quality medical resources fundamentally undermines participants’ ability to establish a health foundation for active aging. Due to the insufficient diagnostic capabilities of primary healthcare institutions and the lack of essential medications, participants are unable to receive effective treatment for their conditions, and even basic medications are hard to obtain. As a result, they are forced to seek healthcare outside their regions to access better medical resources. However, some participants, due to low educational levels and caregiving responsibilities, face limitations in their healthcare choices, ultimately becoming trapped in an inefficient medical system, leading to inadequate treatment and the persistence of their health issues.

“The township hospital can’t provide a proper diagnosis; even after several days of acupuncture, it doesn’t help. To go to a big hospital for tests, I can’t read the characters, and I have no one to look after my grandchildren when I go out.” (F9)

Policy-related challenges: the imbalance in urban and rural elderly care protection

Participants generally perceive a significant disparity in pension security between urban and rural areas, leading to a strong sense of relative deprivation. This diminishes their economic security and motivation to plan and save for the future. In rural areas, many older adults do not receive pension benefits, and their retirement primarily depends on social insurance and financial support from their children. However, the existing pension funds are generally insufficient to meet basic living needs, failing to provide adequate financial security for older adults.

“When we get old, we only receive a little over a hundred yuan in pension insurance each month, and there’s nothing else. “(F9)

Theme 4: environment-level determinants

Rural isolation: lack of entertainment

The lack of community participation hinders participants from building the social connections and adaptive capabilities necessary for active aging. In rural areas, the absence of spaces and leisure facilities for older adults leads to a widespread sense of emptiness and loneliness among participants. This deficiency not only limits their social connections but also significantly lowers their quality of life. Faced with this situation, many participants are forced to rely on farming activities to pass the time and cope with their emotional void. However, this approach falls far short of fulfilling their deeper needs for social interaction and personal development.

“But if I don’t work, the days feel unbearably dull. It’s hard to get through until nightfall with just me and my spouse, as there are hardly any other people around, and nothing to pass the time. So I feed pigs and farm to keep the days from being so boring.” (F15)

Unsafe living conditions: a barrier to independent aging and health

Unsafe living environments directly pose health risks, limiting participants’ independence and well-being. Rural areas generally lack age-friendly modifications, and the unsafe physical environment significantly increases the risk of accidents such as falls. Two participants or their family members had experienced fractures due to falls, which not only caused physical injuries but also led to the loss of their ability to maintain their livelihood.

“He (my spouse) had a stroke caused by high blood pressure two years ago. Then last September, he fell, resulting in severe fractures in his lower back and leg. I have to cook for him every day because he can’t manage on his own.” (F13)

Discussion

Schwartz [26] found that having positive expectations regarding one’s aging might encourage people to maintain a more engaged and socially productive lifestyle. The research indicates that the participants exhibit a low level of positive attitudes toward aging, similar to the findings of Liu et al. [27]. Their attitudes toward aging remain at the stage of “poverty, illness, and worthlessness”, with a primary focus on basic security and physical health in old age. This contrasts significantly with the concept of “active aging” which emphasizes “continuing participation in social, economic, cultural, spiritual, and civic affairs“ [28]. Many rural older adults strive to sustain their livelihood through agriculture and odd jobs as long as they remain physically capable [29]. The fear of becoming a burden stems from the deep influence of traditional Chinese family values on rural middle-aged and older adults [30]. This fear leads them to prioritize the value of labor over their own health, hoping to maintain personal dignity and family value through work. They in poor health continue to engage in labor despite their physical conditions, leading to further deterioration of their health and creating a vicious cycle. Middle-aged and older adults in rural areas often associate the value of aging with their contributions to the family, neglecting personal growth and development. This mindset hinders their ability to focus on self-actualization and improving quality of life, thereby conflicting with the core principle of “self-actualization” in active aging. The mindset of ‘taking one step at a time’ reflects the middle-aged and older adults’ sense of powerlessness in the face of uncertainty about the future, resulting in their focus on immediate survival pressures while overlooking long-term planning for aging life. Research [31–33] has shown that rural older adults face issues such as a lack of health education knowledge, generally low health literacy, and inadequate levels of active aging. The findings of this study are similar, as the generally low educational level of older adults in rural areas results in lower literacy rates and limited learning abilities [34]. This educational disadvantage makes it difficult for them to understand and grasp professional health information, thereby restricting their ability to effectively use modern technology and media to access health knowledge. Consequently, they lack health awareness [35], which not only limits their motivation for self-health management but also leads to the absence of effective coping strategies during the aging process. Physical health is fundamental to performing activities associated with active aging [36]. In rural areas, 83.69% of older adults suffer from at least one chronic disease [37]. The participants in this study are severely affected by illness. On one hand, the diseases directly deprive them of economic independence, making it impossible to discuss the financial reserves needed for active aging preparation when basic survival depends on others. On the other hand, the persistent pain and existential threat caused by chronic severe illnesses shift their focus entirely to “surviving” and “relieving immediate suffering.” Long-term health issues lead to psychological exhaustion, further impacting their quality of life and social participation, which contradicts the goals of active aging.

Keanjoom [38] found that economic factors play an important role in active aging; higher economic status correlates with improved health, participation, and security. This study found that economic determinants are also a significant barrier to rural middle-aged and older adults’ preparation for active aging. The immense economic burden forces them to focus their energy and resources primarily on maintaining their livelihood and managing the financial pressures of the family. This not only affects their quality of life but also hinders the possibility of engaging in active aging preparation. Active aging requires individuals to maintain independence, health, and social participation in later life, but economic pressures limit their ability to achieve these goals. Due to their limited economic resources, some rural middle-aged and older adults still view meeting basic survival needs as their primary life goal, lacking both the motivation and capacity to pursue higher-level social participation [39]. Participants often allocate their limited funds to address urgent livelihood issues rather than pursuing personal development or future planning, lacking the willingness and motivation to engage in higher-level social participation. The findings of YH [40] underscore the necessity for targeted policy interventions to mitigate socioeconomic disparities in later life, thereby promoting active aging. The responsibility of intergenerational care compels them to allocate most of their resources to meeting family needs, often placing their own well-being and personal needs as secondary. This pressure prevents them from focusing on their health, development, and aging preparation, resulting in the long-term neglect and unmet fulfillment of their personal needs.

In recent years, rural middle-aged and older adults have encountered substantial challenges in securing stable employment due to the economic downturn and the rapid acceleration of technological advancements [41]. Currently, rural middle-aged and older adults commonly experience age discrimination in employment [42], leading them to reluctantly turn to agriculture, relying on meager incomes to sustain their families. Active aging emphasizes “productive aging”, yet the harsh economic environment, personal career limitations, and employment discrimination block important pathways for them to extend their working years or transition to lighter jobs to supplement retirement savings. This further compresses the space and time available for rural middle-aged and older adults to accumulate financial resources for their elderly life. The precarious “living on the edge of subsistence” situation leads them to prioritize using limited resources to address immediate survival crises rather than long-term retirement planning. Economic difficulties often result in psychological issues such as anxiety and depression [43], which directly undermine their motivation to engage in active aging planning. Deng [44] found that, compared to urban residents, older adults living in rural areas have fewer opportunities to participate in educational or training programs, in agreement with this study. Urban residents often have access to senior universities, which have been shown to improve the psychological health and quality of life of the older adults, and also increase their social participation in the digital era by exposing them to emerging internet technologies [45]. Internet use can improve the health of middle-aged and older adults, with more pronounced effects observed in rural areas, low-income groups, and among those under 60 years old [46]. Rural residents face barriers to effective learning opportunities due to low internet penetration [47] and household responsibilities, limiting their ability to adapt to new technologies and participate in the digital era. The digital divide reduces the level of physical and mental health and social participation of middle-aged and older adults and inhibits the level of guarantee of older persons, thus impeding their preparation of active aging [48].

The distribution and development of health resources between urban and rural areas in China is both unequal and inefficient [49]. The lack of capacity within the primary healthcare system, combined with individual access barriers, creates a dual squeeze that severely hinders rural middle-aged and older adults from obtaining timely and effective medical services, greatly damaging their current health maintenance capabilities. The inability to effectively manage and reserve for health risks significantly limits their ability to prepare for potential health issues in later life. There is a significant imbalance in the urban-rural pension system in China [50]. Compared to urban residents, rural residents generally lack work-based pension security and rely solely on the lower-protection national pension. This disparity leaves them feeling powerless and may trigger pessimistic expectations for the future, thereby weakening their motivation to prepare for active aging. Furthermore, the lack of effective social security measures forces rural middle-aged and older adults to continue working in old age, especially in physically demanding sectors such as agriculture, compelling them to extend their working careers [51]. This survival pressure consumes their energy for active aging preparation, further exacerbating the difficulties they face in adapting to aging.

Chrysikou [52] stated that environmental factors such as urban planning and architectural design are closely entwined with active aging, and thus active and healthy aging can be promoted by improving basic living facilities. Older adults living in communities/villages with underdeveloped infrastructures are less likely to participate in social and leisure activities [53]. The lack of public activity spaces and facilities in rural areas may close off the remaining social windows, further exacerbating rural hollowing [54]. Active aging emphasizes setting and pursuing personal goals, such as learning new things, serving the community, and developing hobbies. However, the scarcity of physical environments deprives middle-aged and older adults of these possibilities, leading to a loss of sense of purpose and preventing them from engaging in activities that could promote their well-being in later life. Some rural middle-aged and older adults opt for physical labor, such as farming, to pass the time, which may worsen their health issues and increase the risk of falls. Additionally, houses in rural areas often lack modern safety features, such as adequate lighting, non-slip flooring, handrails, and elevators [55]. In compared to older adults with non-agricultural household, those with agricultural household experienced more falls (21.3% vs. 18.1%) and fall-related injuries (9.1% vs. 7.6%) [56]. This highlights the significant impact of the rural physical environment on fall risks among middle-aged and older adults. Unsafe environments not only cause serious physical harm but also force individuals to disrupt their existing health management plans and social participation activities. In some cases, they even lose the ability to maintain their livelihood, which directly contradicts the core goals of active aging.

Suggestions

Provide educational and training opportunities for middle-aged and older adults in rural areas, enhancing investment in elderly education resources. Establish learning centers offering literacy classes and basic skills training, such as courses on modern technology, to help them adapt to the information age. Promote internet access and usage, overcoming challenges to help them access information and participate in society. Raise awareness of active aging through lectures on national pension policies, fostering the adoption of correct retirement concepts.

Accelerate rural revitalization by creating more employment opportunities for middle-aged and older adults, providing stable jobs and increasing family income. Develop light labor jobs, such as handicrafts, to reduce the physical strain of heavy labor. Expand rural pension coverage, improving basic living security and reducing economic dependence on families. Increase the reimbursement rate for rural health insurance based on regional conditions to reduce medical expenses.

Offer regular health education and improve access to health information, raising health literacy. Promote the decentralization of healthcare resources to reduce the urban-rural healthcare gap. Strengthen the infrastructure of township clinics and village health stations, upgrading equipment and medications. Provide professional training for primary healthcare workers to improve service quality, enabling local access to quality, convenient medical care.

Accelerate the age-friendly renovation of rural infrastructure and housing, such as improving road conditions and adding barrier-free facilities. Increase recreational facilities and build multi-functional centers for social and cultural activities to engage middle-aged and older adults in rural areas.

Limitations

This study is a qualitative research with certain limitations that should be addressed. Firstly, the sample is drawn from rural areas in a single city in Guizhou Province. While rural Guizhou somewhat represents the views of the majority of rural China, it does not cover economically developed or more underdeveloped regions, thus limiting the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, the participants have relatively low educational levels and limited professional diversity, which may affect the exploration of deeper topics. Given the vast geographical area and significant cultural differences across China, the local culture’s influence on the active aging preparedness of middle-aged and older adults in rural areas cannot be ignored. This suggests the need for further research to explore and compare the differences in perspectives across various cultural contexts. Lastly, due to time constraints, this study did not fully incorporate document records or conduct long-term observations, although the researcher and participants share common life experiences within the same community. Additionally, the qualitative analysis may involve some degree of subjectivity, which could affect the objectivity of the results. Future studies should consider expanding the sample area, incorporating cross-sectional surveys, and examining the challenges faced by middle-aged and older adults in rural China in preparing for active aging across different cultural, professional, and educational backgrounds, thereby providing a more robust theoretical foundation for the development of active aging in rural China.

Conclusion

This study reveals that middle-aged and older adults in rural China face multifaceted challenges, hindering their preparedness for active aging. These can primarily be summarized in terms of personal, family, and societal factors, as well as physical environmental aspects. Studies on active aging in China have tended to focus predominantly on urban communities, and thus there is a need to shift attention toward the unique challenges faced in rural areas. Targeted interventions are needed to enhance rural residents’ awareness of active aging and promote proactive preparation. Such measures would better equip rural populations for active aging, supporting their high-quality development in these regions.
