Screening colonoscopy utilization among adults living with spina bifida
Authors: Hiren V Patel, Debbie Goldberg, Isabel Elaine Allen, Hillary L Copp, Lindsay A Hampson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSpina bifida (SB) is the most common neural tube defect, often accompanied by neurogenic bladder and bowel dysfunction, hydrocephalus, and orthopedic sequelae. As life expectancy increases for individuals with SB, understanding disparities in healthcare utilization becomes crucial. This study examined colorectal cancer (CRC) screening colonoscopy utilization rates among adults living with SB.MethodUsing the California Health Care Access and Information database (2005–2017), a matched control group was established to compare colonoscopy utilization. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of screening colonoscopy utilization.ResultsAmong individuals aged ≥50 years, 9.5% of those living with SB underwent screening colonoscopy compared to 13.0% of matched controls. After adjustment, SB was independently associated with lower odds of screening colonoscopy (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.76–0.93).ConclusionFindings underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve CRC screening accessibility for this vulnerable population. Addressing structural barriers and enhancing patient-centered approaches may improve preventative care delivery and long-term health outcomes in individuals with SB.Keywords: Spina bifida, Screening colonoscopy, Preventative Care, Access to Care, Health Disparities

Introduction

Spina bifida (SB) is the most common neural tube defect, and patients often have motor and sensory deficit below the level of the lesion, with high likelihood of hydrocephalus, neurogenic bladder and bowel, and orthopedic sequela.1,2 Prior to widespread treatment of hydrocephalus, survival into adulthood for individuals with SB was uncommon. 1 Development of the neurosurgical techniques, such as ventricular shunts, together with multidisciplinary care have led to markedly improved longevity, creating a growing population of adults living with SB.

Improvements in patient education, multidisciplinary programs, and longitudinal care pathways have led to better care patterns in pediatric populations, but transitioning individuals into adulthood has remained a challenge. 3 With fewer adult clinics offering multidisciplinary care, maintaining lifelong care can be difficult. Furthermore, comorbid conditions, such as neurogenic bowel dysfunction, obesity, and incontinence, are often dynamic clinical problems requiring constant surveillance and maintenance, which can require more frequent outpatient visits and coordination of care.

As life expectancy lengthens for these individuals, understanding how healthcare screening is utilized is essential. The current study examined colorectal cancer (CRC) screening among patients with SB. It was hypothesized that adults living with SB would be less likely to undergo screening colonoscopies compared to the general population.

Methods

The Department of Health Care Access and Information (HCAI) database captures all California-licensed hospitals’ emergency room, inpatient admission, and ambulatory surgery encounters. The database was queried from 2005-2017 to identify individuals with SB and a control group was created by randomly selecting individuals without SB using a 5:1 match by birth year. Patients included in the analysis were further restricted to being ≥50 years old and without any prior colonoscopy history. Hospital encounters were followed longitudinally using record linkage numbers. International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 and -10 and common procedural terminology (CPT) codes were utilized to determine use of screening colonoscopy. ICD-9 (741.00-741.03, 741.90-941.93, 756.17), ICD-10 (Q05.0-Q05.9, Q07.01, Q07.03, Q76.0) and CPT codes (63700, 63702, 63704, 63706) were used to identify individuals with SB. Screening colonoscopy was identified using ICD-9 codes V76.50 and V76.51 as well as ICD-10 codes Z12.10 and Z12.11.

Descriptive analysis compared characteristics of patients with and without SB. Multivariable logistic regression modeling was utilized to determine factors associated with receiving a screening colonoscopy, controlling for age, sex, race, ethnicity, insurance type, Diez-Roux quartile, healthcare professional shortage area, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Diez-Roux score is a composite score of six domains based on zip code and was used to ascertain socioeconomic status. Healthcare professional shortage area designations are created by the Health Resources and Services Administration to determine areas with a shortage of medical professionals. CCI is a standardized score for assessing the impact of comorbid conditions on patient outcomes. Disposition was categorized as home or non-home, which included home health services, skilled nursing, intermediate care, and other. All analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4.

Results

A total of 5,776 patients with SB and 26,249 controls had an encounter from 2005-2017 (Table 1). Only 9.5% (n=549) of SB patients received a screening colonoscopy compared to 13.0% (n=3,325) of controls.

Discussion

Adults with SB are less likely to receive a colonoscopy. Multiple racial and demographic disparities were associated with a reduced likelihood of receiving a colonoscopy among all patients and within the SB population itself. This study adds to work highlighting the need for better implementation of practices that can improve screening colonoscopy utilization among vulnerable populations. Multipronged efforts to improve decision aids, implement interventions to address system-level referral barriers, and build community partnerships have been successful.4–6 Implementation of similar strategies in the SB population may prove to be beneficial.

Screening colonoscopy is an important preventative method for the early detection of CRC. Strategies to increase screening and awareness have largely been successful, but have been underutilized in vulnerable subgroups such as those with lower socioeconomic status or physical and intellectual disabilities.7,8 Critically, it must be thoroughly acknowledged that it is not known if everyone within the SB population is at equal risk for CRC. Individual risk profiles may vary significantly based on clinical severity, genetic factors, or differences in chronic inflammation, which cannot be fully captured here. While this study represents an initial attempt to examine these disparities, the inherent limitations of the administrative database mean that these findings may not be totally representative of the broader population and cannot be widely generalized at this point. However, highlighting these gaps is an essential first step that will hopefully invite further research to better characterize risk and improve screening guidelines.

Colonoscopy in adults with SB may be challenging due various SB-specific issues, such as neurogenic bowel dysfunction, fecal incontinence, and mobility limitations, all of which may complicate bowel preparation and procedural logistics. In this study, patient disposition was used as practical surrogate for determining baseline functional dependency, overall medical frailty, and social support availability. Adults living with SB with a non-home disposition were less likely to have a screening colonoscopy, which potentially highlights profound structural and logistical barriers to elective screening, bowel preparation coordination, and specialized medical transportation. Furthermore, lifelong exposure to invasive medical care may also contribute to healthcare-related anxiety, further reducing uptake of elective screening procedures. 9

Resources aimed at providing adequate CRC screening and possibly other cancer screening are critical in this vulnerable population as more patients reach adulthood. These findings also show that socioeconomic environment of neighborhoods, as measured by Diez-Roux score, significantly impact receipt of screening colonoscopy, suggesting that strategies for improving colonoscopy use need to be both patient- and neighborhood-centered. 10

The limitation of this population-based analysis is absence of data from outpatient visits to help understand decision-making regarding screening. Additionally, inaccuracies in diagnostic and procedural codes can lead to incorrect estimation of screening rates. In this study, the SB cohort was younger than the control group, which may reflect diagnostic evaluation, particularly in younger individuals with gastrointestinal symptoms such as fecal incontinence or rectal pain. Alternate methods of screening for CRC such as fecal occult blood test and fecal immunochemical test are not captured in the HCAI database. In addition, the HCAI database is not linked to a cancer registry or death files; as a result, this analysis cannot account for competing risks such as death or colon cancers prior to screening colonoscopy. However, these inaccuracies are likely randomly distributed among the entire cohort.

Conclusions

In this population-based analysis using statewide administrative data, adults living with SB demonstrated lower utilization of screening colonoscopy compared with matched controls. Differences in socioeconomic context, insurance coverage, and healthcare access were also associated with colonoscopy use, suggesting that multiple intersecting factors may contribute to observed disparities. As increasing numbers of individuals living with SB reach ages at which preventive cancer screening is recommended, greater attention to tailored approaches for screening delivery may help support more equitable utilization of colonoscopy.
