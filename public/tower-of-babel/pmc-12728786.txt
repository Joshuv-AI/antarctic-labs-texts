Intravenous Regular Insulin for Treatment of Hyperkalemia in Overweight Patients
Authors: Denise Kelley, Sarah Piccuirro, Prakruti Gandhi, Hannah Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionIntravenous regular insulin is often used for the management of hyperkalemia due to its rapid onset of action and predictable potassium‐lowering effects. Various studies have been conducted to determine optimal insulin dosing strategies that reduce serum potassium levels without increasing hypoglycemia risk. As data shifts towards lower or fixed insulin doses, validating the appropriateness of these dosing regimens for the management of hyperkalemia in overweight patients is warranted. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the serum potassium‐lowering effects of 5 units versus 10 units of intravenous regular insulin in hyperkalemic patients with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2.MethodsA multicenter, retrospective study was performed in adult patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 who received 5 or 10 units of intravenous regular insulin for the treatment of hyperkalemia. The primary outcome was the potassium‐lowering effects of 5 units versus 10 units of intravenous regular insulin. Secondary outcomes include the incidence of hypoglycemic episodes within 6 h of insulin administration, hospital length of stay (LOS), and treatment failure.ResultsOf 699 patients screened, 81 patients received 5 units and 81 patients received 10 units. There was no difference in the serum potassium‐lowering effects of 5 units versus 10 units of intravenous regular insulin (0.5 (0.1–1.1) mmol/L vs. 0.5 (0.2–1) mmol/L; p = 0.65). No significant differences were observed for any secondary outcomes. Subgroup analyses revealed no significant differences for BMI; the number of concomitant acute potassium‐lowering therapies received; or the degree of renal impairment, aside from a significantly larger potassium‐lowering effect with 10 units of intravenous regular insulin observed in the subgroup receiving no concomitant acute potassium‐lowering therapies as well as the subgroup with a creatinine clearance of 30–60 mL/min.ConclusionIn this small, retrospective cohort study, treatment with 5 units of intravenous regular insulin did not compromise the serum potassium‐lowering effect when compared to 10 units in overweight patients with hyperkalemia. Further controlled studies are warranted to confirm these findings.Keywords: emergency medicine, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, obesity, regular insulin

Introduction

Hyperkalemia is a life‐threatening electrolyte disorder that accounts for up to 10% of admissions to the emergency department [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Pathogenesis often involves drugs or disorders inhibiting the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS) and/or impaired urinary potassium excretion due to acute kidney injury (AKI) or chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1, 2, 6, 7]. Hyperkalemia may result in metabolic, neuromuscular, and gastrointestinal abnormalities and can induce fatal cardiac abnormalities if left untreated [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

Management of hyperkalemia imposes a significant economic burden on the health care system and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality [1, 3]. Current treatment options focus on stabilizing the cardiac membrane, shifting extracellular potassium into the intracellular space, and enhancing excess potassium removal from the extracellular compartment [1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 11]. Intravenous regular insulin shifts potassium intracellulary and is commonly used during acute hyperkalemic emergencies due to its rapid onset of action and predictable potassium‐lowering effects [1, 2, 5, 6, 10]. The American Heart Association and the National Kidney Foundation recommend administering 10 units of intravenous regular insulin with 25–50 g of intravenous dextrose to reduce the risk and incidence of hypoglycemia [1, 8, 12]. However, recent literature has demonstrated improved safety and comparable efficacy with lower insulin doses (5 units or 0.1 units/kg) for the management of hyperkalemia [1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11, 13].

Previous studies primarily explored optimal insulin and dextrose dosing strategies focused on a general patient population without considering extremes of body weight [3]. There is an association of impaired insulin‐mediated potassium uptake in patients with obesity that merits additional evaluation in this demographic [14]. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, obesity affects more than 100 million (41.9%) adults in the United States and accounted for over $173 billion of medical expenditures in 2019 [15]. The National Institutes of Health uses body mass index (BMI) to define overweight (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2), obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2), and severe obesity (BMI > 40 kg/m2) [16]. In general, scarce and inconclusive information exists on the pharmacokinetics of medications in an overweight patient population. With more adipose tissue and less lean tissue per kilogram (kg) of total body weight, the volume of distribution is expected to increase for lipophilic drugs and decrease for hydrophilic drugs, such as insulin [17, 18]. Given these pharmacokinetic changes and the lack of data from previous studies, validating the appropriateness of insulin dosing regimens for the management of hyperkalemia in overweight patients is warranted. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the serum potassium‐lowering effects of 5 units versus 10 units of intravenous regular insulin in patients with hyperkalemia and a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study was performed across 10 hospitals (all with a shared electronic medical record) within the Ascension Seton network. It was approved by the local Institutional Review Board and the need for informed consent was waived. Patients were assessed for inclusion if they received either 5 or 10 units of intravenous regular insulin for hyperkalemia based on a drug utilization report between January 2017 and June 2022. A random number generator was utilized to determine which charts to review for eligibility from this original patient list. All data collection was conducted by a single study investigator, using a standardized data collection tool. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had a baseline serum potassium ≥ 5.5 mmol/L, were ≥ 18 years of age, weighed ≥ 100 kg (with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), and had serum potassium levels measured within 1–6 h post‐insulin administration. Patients were excluded if they received doses other than 5 units or 10 units of intravenous regular insulin, received any type of renal replacement therapy between insulin administration and follow‐up serum potassium, received concomitant continuous bicarbonate infusions, or recorded an erroneous pre‐insulin or post‐insulin administration serum potassium level due to lab error or outside the specified time period. Additionally, patients were screened for administration of any concomitant potassium‐lowering therapies administered within the timeframe being observed following the intravenous regular insulin dose, including nebulized albuterol, intravenous furosemide, sodium polystyrene sulfonate, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate, patiromer, intravenous sodium bicarbonate boluses, and subcutaneous insulin therapies. When evaluating the impact of concomitant agents, these therapies were categorized by having acute lowering potential (nebulized albuterol, intravenous furosemide, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate, patiromer), delayed lowering potential (sodium polystyrene sulfonate), and variable (intravenous sodium bicarbonate bolus) or likely negligible lowering potential (subcutaneous insulin therapies).

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was potassium‐lowering effects of 5 units versus 10 units of intravenous regular insulin. Serum potassium reduction was defined as the difference between the baseline serum potassium recorded prior to administration of insulin and the first follow‐up serum potassium recorded within 1–6 h following insulin administration. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of hypoglycemic episodes (blood glucose < 70 mg/dL) within 6 h of insulin administration, hospital length of stay (LOS), and treatment failure. Treatment failure was defined as the administration of additional intravenous regular insulin within 24 h of the first dose of either 5 or 10 units of intravenous regular insulin. A subgroup analysis was performed to determine whether BMI, the degree of renal impairment, or the number of concomitant potassium‐lowering therapies influenced the potassium‐lowering effects of 5 units versus 10 units of intravenous regular insulin. These subgroups were predefined to be BMI (kg/m2) < 30, ≥ 30 to < 40, or ≥ 40; creatinine clearance (CrCl) (mL/min) < 30, 30–60, or > 60; and zero, one, two, or three concomitant acute potassium‐lowering therapies received.

Statistical Analysis

We anticipated that 10 units of intravenous regular insulin would lower serum potassium by 0.9 mmol/L ± 0.68 mmol/L [1, 8]. Subsequently, a sample size of 162 patients was calculated to detect a 0.3 mmol/L difference with 80% power and two‐tailed significance with a p‐value of 0.05 between 5 and 10 units of intravenous regular insulin. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous data were analyzed using Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon rank sum for parametric or non‐parametric data, respectively. Categorical data were evaluated with either a Chi‐squared test or a Fisher's exact test if expected counts were less than five. All statistical analysis was completed using STATA software (College Station, TX) and SAS JMP Version 18 software (Cary, NC).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 699 patient charts were screened for inclusion, of which 162 patients met inclusion criteria (Figure 1). Of the 162 patients included, 81 patients received 5 units of intravenous regular insulin, and 81 patients received 10 units of intravenous regular insulin. Patients who received 10 units of intravenous regular insulin had significantly higher baseline blood glucose, weight, and BMI in comparison to those who received 5 units of intravenous regular insulin, and more patients receiving 5 units were treated in a non‐ICU location (Table 1). Otherwise, baseline characteristics were largely similar between both groups with a slightly higher incidence of diabetes mellitus and AKI in those receiving 10 units of intravenous regular insulin (Table 1).

Outcomes and Subgroup Analysis

There was no difference in the primary outcome of the serum potassium‐lowering effects of 5 units versus 10 units of intravenous regular insulin (Table 2). No significant differences were observed for any secondary outcomes, including the incidence of hypoglycemic episodes within 6 h of insulin administration, hospital LOS, or treatment failure (Table 2). Subgroup analyses of the primary outcome revealed no significant differences based on BMI. No difference was observed in patients who received one, two, or three concomitant acute potassium‐lowering therapies; however, in the subgroup where zero concomitant therapies were given, a significantly greater potassium‐lowering effect was observed in the group receiving 10 units of intravenous regular insulin (Table 3). A significant potassium‐lowering effect was also observed with 10 units of intravenous regular insulin in patients with a CrCl of 30–60 mL/min, but this was not seen in the CrCl < 30 mL/min or CrCl > 60 mL/min subgroups (Table 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this was the first study to evaluate optimal insulin dosing strategies for the management of hyperkalemia in overweight patients. Our data demonstrated that 5 units of intravenous regular insulin had comparable potassium‐lowering effects to 10 units of intravenous regular insulin without affecting the incidence of hypoglycemic episodes. It adds to the limited literature evaluating the potassium‐lowering effect of intravenous regular insulin dosing strategies as the primary outcome and serves as the only study to focus on potassium‐lowering effects in patients with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2.

Although 10–20 units of intravenous regular insulin have been previously evaluated in the management of hyperkalemia, recent studies have compared the effectiveness of 10 units of intravenous regular insulin with lower insulin doses in an effort to reduce the risk of hypoglycemia [1, 5, 8, 9, 11]. Dosing strategies of 5 units and 0.1 units per kg have demonstrated lower hypoglycemia risk, but the effects on potassium were conflicting. Since many of these studies were designed to assess the incidence of hypoglycemia as the primary outcome rather than potassium‐lowering effects, using 10 units of intravenous regular insulin is often still a common dosing practice for the treatment of hyperkalemia. Notably, none of these aforementioned studies focused on overweight patients. This has led to a gap in the literature and variability in clinical practice ranging from fixed dosing to weight‐based dosing with and without dose capping in overweight patients. As practice shifts to lower insulin doses, confirming the efficacy of lower doses in higher body weight patients is warranted.

A recent retrospective study also evaluated mean serum potassium reduction as the primary outcome in hospitalized adults treated with intravenous regular insulin for the management of hyperkalemia [8]. Similar to our study, they found no significant difference in post‐insulin potassium reduction between patients receiving 5 units and 10 units of intravenous regular insulin (0.81 ± 0.58 mmol/L vs. 0.9 ± 0.68 mmol/L; p = 0.221) [8]. However, a post hoc subgroup analysis found that treatment with 10 units reduced potassium to a significantly greater extent in patients with baseline serum potassium ≥ 6 mmol/L compared with patients having a baseline serum potassium < 6 mmol/L (1.08 mmol/L vs. 0.83 mmol/L; p = 0.018) [8]. With a median pretreatment potassium of 6.1 mmol/L, 69.8% of patients in our study had a baseline serum potassium ≥ 6 mmol/L and this finding was not observed in our analysis.

Additional studies, all with mean baseline potassium values ≥ 6 mmol/L, have evaluated serum potassium reduction as secondary efficacy measures [1, 5, 9, 11]. One study found that 5 units intravenous regular insulin reduced serum potassium to the same extent as 10 units in adult patients with hyperkalemia and renal insufficiency (1.0 ± 0.8 mEq/L vs. 1.0 ± 0.7 mEq/L) [1]. Another study revealed a similar reduction in potassium levels with 5 units and 10 units of intravenous regular insulin (1.08 ± 0.89 mEq/L vs. 1.10 ± 0.91 mEq/L; p = 0.89) in hospitalized patients with hyperkalemia who had low estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), defined as on dialysis or with an AKI [11]. A study evaluating a weight‐based dosing strategy demonstrated comparable potassium lowering with 0.1 units/kg when compared to 10 units of intravenous regular insulin (1.34 ± 0.94 mmol/L vs. 1.35 ± 0.97 mmol/L; p = 0.94) in a targeted study population weighing less than 95 kg [9]. In contrast, the most recent of these studies compared intravenous regular insulin doses of < 10 units (median dose 5 units) versus 10 units and observed a significantly lower potassium reduction in patients with hyperkalemia who received doses < 10 units (0.94 ± 0.71 mmol/L vs. 1.11 ± 0.8 mmol/L; p = 0.008) [5]. This difference was not impacted by the number of concomitant potassium lowering therapies received between groups; however, a multivariate analysis did not independently associate insulin dose with potassium reduction [5].

Overall, our study results were consistent with the serum potassium reduction reported by previous studies, but our median potassium reduction of 0.5 mmol/L in each group is much lower than that of the other studies, despite having similar baseline potassium values. It is unclear whether weight could have contributed to this lower numerical reduction, though it is worth noting the median weight of patients evaluated in prior studies ranged from 69 to 89 kg compared to all included patients in our study having a body weight exceeding 100 kg (median 112–120 kg). Although all of the patients in our study were considered overweight, all but five patients actually had a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 resulting in a predominantly obese study population. Interestingly, when evaluating our BMI subgroup analysis, the potassium amount reduction slowly declined as BMI increased with median reductions as high as 0.9–1.2 mmol/L in the few patients with a BMI < 30 kg/m2 and as low as 0.4–0.5 mmol/L in the BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 group. The highest potassium reduction lowering was seen in patients with BMI < 30 kg/m2 who received 5 units of intravenous regular insulin, though with only two patients in this group, it is difficult to draw substantial conclusions. Despite these observations across BMI thresholds, a subgroup analysis based on BMI did not reveal significant differences, though this could still pose clinical relevance depending on the pre‐treatment potassium value, if a decline by 0.4–0.5 mmol/L would not provide enough clinical value for the desired goal. With a numerically higher incidence of treatment failure in the 10‐unit arm, in conjunction with this arm having statistically higher BMI and body weight at baseline, this may serve as a hypothesis generating finding warranting additional research to evaluate optimal intravenous regular insulin dosing based on BMI.

Prior studies largely looked at hypoglycemia as the primary safety outcome to reduce hospital LOS and mortality. One study evaluated the incidence of hypoglycemia (blood glucose < 70 mg/dL) as the primary outcome and severe hypoglycemia (blood glucose < 40 mg/dL) as the secondary outcome [1]. Both hypoglycemia and severe hypoglycemia occurred more frequently with 10 units in comparison to 5 units (hypoglycemia: 28.6% vs. 19.5%; severe hypoglycemia: 6.8% vs. 3%, respectively) [1]. Similarly, another study observed a hypoglycemic frequency of 8.7% in 219 patients receiving 5–10 units of intravenous regular insulin for hyperkalemia treatment [7]. Of the total hypoglycemic events, 58% occurred in patients receiving the conventional regimen of 10 units of intravenous regular insulin with 25 g of intravenous dextrose. In patients with renal dysfunction, prolonged duration of action may lead to increased rates of hypoglycemia. One study that solely evaluated patients with low eGFR found an overall hypoglycemia rate of 18.1% in patients treated with intravenous regular insulin and dextrose for hyperkalemia [11]. Notably, the incidence of hypoglycemia was higher in patients with end‐stage renal disease. Our study defines hypoglycemia as blood glucose less than 70 mg/dL within 6 h after intravenous regular insulin administration. The majority of our patients received up to 25 g of intravenous dextrose. Unlike previous studies, we found no difference in the incidence of hypoglycemic events with 5 units and 10 units of intravenous regular insulin. This may be explained by having fewer patients with renal dysfunction and higher pretreatment blood glucose values. At baseline, our 10‐unit group had a higher pre‐treatment blood glucose than the 5‐unit group (170 mg/dL vs. 127 mg/dL; p = 0.0014). In comparison, the initial study discussed above had comparable pre‐treatment blood glucose between the 10‐unit and 5‐unit groups (137.5 ± 76.6 mg/dL vs. 124.1 ± 47.8 mg/dL) [1].

In addition to conducting a subgroup analysis on BMI, subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the number of concomitant acute potassium‐lowering therapies or degree of renal impairment influenced the primary outcome. The subgroup analysis revealed no significant differences based on the number of concomitant acute potassium‐lowering therapies regardless of one, two, or three agents received. Interestingly, the subgroup that did not receive any concomitant acute potassium‐lowering therapies showed statistically significant potassium lowering in those receiving 10 units of intravenous regular insulin. The authors do not have an explanation for this, other than possibly a coincidental finding observed due to the small sample size in this subgroup. A significantly larger potassium‐lowering effect was also observed with 10 units of intravenous regular insulin in patients with a CrCl of 30–60 mL/min. This finding may indicate that higher doses are potentially needed in patients with better renal function, although this finding was not corroborated in our subgroup with a CrCl > 60 mL/min. However, with this subgroup only including 13 patients (Table 3), it might be too small to result in similar findings. Additionally, since these subgroup analyses entail multiple comparisons, there is a risk for family‐wise errors and false positives, which further emphasizes the need to interpret these findings with caution. Although the focus of our study was on overweight adult patients, a large percentage of patients appeared to have renal insufficiency at baseline, though accurate estimation of renal function in overweight patients can be challenging. Larger controlled studies are needed to further validate the efficacy of 5 units of intravenous regular insulin in comparison to 10 units for the treatment of hyperkalemia in patients without renal impairment.

Our study did have limitations, most notably, the retrospective design which limits the study's ability to account for potential confounding variables. An attempt was made to minimize this risk by excluding patients who received doses other than 5 or 10 units of intravenous regular insulin and utilizing computerized randomization when selecting charts to review from the original patient list. Additionally, the indication of intravenous regular insulin was confirmed with the electronic medical record, laboratory values, and provider notes. Having a timeframe of up to 6 h for evaluating potassium lowering following a dose of intravenous regular insulin may have fallen outside of the window of insulin's effects. There is variation in the literature for optimal timing of potassium reassessment, and, although our timeframe did extend up to 6 h, we evaluated the first potassium available and standard practice at the study sites is to obtain a repeat potassium sooner than 6 h post intravenous regular insulin administration. Additionally, the definition used by this study to capture treatment failure (additional intravenous regular insulin within 24 h of the initial dose) may be too long of a timeframe to declare any retreatment as failure of the initial intravenous regular insulin dose, which could have inflated the incidence of treatment failure in this study. This study did meet the prespecified power calculation, though the assumed potassium lowering was lower than initially anticipated for both treatment arms which may have impacted the actual sample size needed to be adequately powered. Finally, all data collection in this study was performed by a single investigator using a standardized data collection sheet, though no formal mechanism was in place to ensure accuracy and quality of the collected data.

Conclusion

Treatment with 5 units of intravenous regular insulin did not compromise the serum potassium‐lowering effect when compared to 10 units in patients with hyperkalemia and a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. None of the secondary outcomes resulted in significant differences. Further research to confirm these findings is warranted.
