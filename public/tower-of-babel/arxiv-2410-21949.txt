Quantum entanglement as an ambiguity of classical dynamics
Piotr Dulian, Adam Sawicki

Abstract
We study consequences of applying formalism of symplectic geometry to quantum mechanics. We propose a physical interpretation of a symplectic indicator of entanglement, introduced in [ 1 ] , given by the degeneracy of the symplectic form on manifolds of locally unitary equivalent states. We show that this degeneracy can be understood as an ambiguity of some classical dynamic.

On finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{S}
the space of pure states -
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})
carries a natural symplectic structure defined via the inner product. For any quantum Hamiltonian
\hat{H}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
we introduce a function on
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})
that we call classical Hamiltonian function:

\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})\ni\ket{\psi}\mapsto\bra{\psi}\hat{H}\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

and show that the integral lines of the corresponding Hamiltonian vector field
V_{\mathcal{H}}

\hat{H}

In order to incorporate in this picture quantum correlation we consider a bipartite system
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{S}_{B}
and non-interacting Hamiltonians
\hat{H}
that we call

\hat{H}_{loc}
the corresponding Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}_{loc}(\psi)=\bra{\psi}\hat{H}_{loc}\ket{\psi}
does not define the Hamiltonian vector field uniquely but only up to so-called null vector fields. Notably among the orbits of
U(\mathcal{S}_{A})\times U(\mathcal{S}_{B})
there is exactly one which is symplectic - the orbit of separable states
[
1
]
. Therefore we interpret entanglement as an ambiguity of so defined classical dynamics corresponding to local quantum Hamiltonians. This gives an interpretation of previously introduced in
[
1

notion of degeneracy of symplectic form as an entanglement indicator.

2
Classical mechanics as a symplectic theory

In classical mechanics one considers a set
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
with coordinates that are traditionally denoted as

\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=(q^{1},...,q^{n},p_{1},...,p_{n}).

(2)

The dynamics is given by a Hamiltonian function:

\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

(3)

and Hamilton’s equations:

\dot{q}^{i}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial p_{i}},\quad\dot{p}_{i}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial q^{i}}.

(4)

It is well known that classical mechanics can be expressed more formally as a theory that lives on a smooth manifold
M=\mathbb{R}^{2n}
equipped with a bilinear map on Hamiltonian functions:

\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial p_{i}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial q^{i}}\right),

(5)

called Poisson bracket. For any Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}
we can construct a vector field
V_{\mathcal{H}}
:

V_{\mathcal{H}}=\{\cdot,\mathcal{H}\}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial q^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}\right).

(6)

All vector fields that arise in this way are called Hamiltonian vector fields. Of course the vector filed
V_{\mathcal{H}}
can be interpreted either as a differentiation or as a function from
M
to the tangent bundle -
TM
. Using the second interpretation Hamilton’s equations can be expressed as:

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=V_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbf{x}).

(7)

In other words, solutions of Hamilton’s equations are integral curves of vector field -
V_{\mathcal{H}}
, which can be concisely expressed using the notion of vector flow of this field -
\Phi_{V_{\mathcal{H}}}
:

\mathbf{x}(t)=\Phi_{V_{\mathcal{H}}}(t)\mathbf{x}_{0}.

(8)

To check whether a given vector field

V=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{i}}+B_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}},

(9)

is Hamiltonian it is useful to employ the symplectic form:

\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{n}dp_{i}\wedge dq^{i},

(10)

in the following way. Consider 1-form:

\alpha=\omega(V,\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(B_{i}dq^{i}-A^{i}dp_{i}\right).

(11)

Since
M
is contractible if
d\alpha=0
then there exists a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
such that

\alpha=-d\mathcal{H}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial q^{i}}dq^{i}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial p_{i}}dp_{i}\right).

(12)

Comparing (
11
) and (
12
) we obtain:

A^{i}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial p_{i}},\quad B_{i}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial q^{i}},

(13)

thus
V=\{\cdot,\mathcal{H}\}
. Analogously one can show that for Hamiltonian vector field
V_{\mathcal{H}}
we have
d(\omega(V_{\mathcal{H}},\cdot))=0
. Moreover, one can easily check that

\{\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\}=-\omega(V_{\mathcal{F}},V_{\mathcal{G}}).

(14)

Thus symplectic form
\omega
gives us the whole information about the relation between Hamiltionians and Hamiltonian vector fields and as such can be treated as a corner stone of classical mechanics theory. This leads to the introduction of abstract symplectic manifolds:

Definition 1
.

A symplectic manifold is a smooth manifold
M
equipped with differential 2-form
\omega
which is closed:

d\omega=0,

(15)

and nondegenerate:

\forall_{u}\;\omega(v,u)=0\;\Rightarrow\;v=0.

(16)

Such a form is then called symplectic form on
M
.

The closeness of
\omega
is needed for Poisson bracket to satisfy Jacobi identity and nondegeneration ensures that for any Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}
one can uniquely choose related vector field as the one that satisfies
-d\mathcal{H}=\omega(V,\cdot)
. Furthermore, those two conditions combined by Darboux theorem
[
33
]
allow for local choice of coordinates
(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})
on
M
such that
\omega
takes the form as in (
10
).

2.1
Presymplectic manifolds

It will be useful for our later discussion to analyze what happens when we allow
\omega
to be degenerated while keeping it closed. In that case manifold
M
is called presymplectic and its symplectic form no longer gives a one-to-one correspondence between
1
-forms and vector fields.

To see that there are multiple vector fields corresponding to
d\mathcal{H}
let us define the null tangent space at point
x\in M

D_{x}=\{v\in T_{x}M\,|\,\forall_{u}\omega(v,u)=0\},

(17)

and a null distribution on
M

D=\bigcup_{x\in M}\{x\}\times D_{x}\subset TM.

(18)

If
D
is regular, that is the dimension of
D_{x}
is the same for all
x\in M
, then there are smooth vector fields on
M
called null which at all points
x\in M
belong to
D_{x}
. In this case, using Frobenious theorem, one can also show that the distribution
D
is integrable
[
34
]
. Moreover, when
V_{\mathcal{H}}
is a Hamiltonian vector field of
\mathcal{H}
and
V_{0}
is null then
V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{H}}=V_{\mathcal{H}}+V_{0}
is also a Hamiltonian vector field of
\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle\omega(V_{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime},\cdot)=\omega(V_{\mathcal{H}},\cdot)+\omega(V_{0},\cdot)=

(19)

\displaystyle=\omega(V_{\mathcal{H}},\cdot)=-d\mathcal{H}.

(20)

This implies that integral curves of
V_{\mathcal{H}}
and
V_{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}
-
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
respectively with the same initial conditions (
\gamma(0)=\gamma^{\prime}(0)
) are two different trajectories given by the same dynamics
\mathcal{H}
. It follows that for all
t
:

\mathcal{H}(\gamma(t))=\mathcal{H}(\gamma^{\prime}(t)),

(21)

that is
\mathcal{H}
has to be constant along integral curves of null vector fields or shortly:

d\mathcal{H}(D)=0.

(22)

We will call Hamiltonian functions satisfying the above equation
admissible
. Since tangent and cotangent vector bundles are isomorphic condition (
22
) is both necessary and sufficient for an existence of a Hamiltonian vector field of
\mathcal{H}

[
34
]
. For all other Hamiltonian functions 1-form
d\mathcal{H}
lays outside of the image of

V\mapsto\omega(V,\cdot).

(23)

Therefore for a presymplectic
(M,\omega)
only admissible Hamiltonian functions give Hamiltonian dynamics. If a Hamiltonian function
\mathcal{H}
is not admissible the notions of a corresponding Hamiltonian vector field and Hamilton’s equations make no sense.

3
Symplectic structure in quantum mechanics

To see how classical equations of motion apply to quantum mechanics we need to phrase it in terms of symplectic geometry.
Consider quantum system described by Hilbert space
\mathcal{S}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We define an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{S}
such that:

\ket{\psi}\sim\ket{\phi}\Leftrightarrow\exists_{\lambda\neq 0}\;\ket{\psi}=\lambda\ket{\phi}.

(24)

Each equivalence class of this relation is a line in
\mathcal{S}
. The set of all equivalence classes is called complex projective space
M=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})
and it is a
(2d-2)
-dimensional real (
(d-1)
-dimensional complex) differential manifold. Quantum states are normalized vectors in
\mathcal{S}
and they are defined up to a phase factor thus we can identify
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}
with a line going through it:

\left[\ket{\psi}\right]=\{\lambda\ket{\psi}\;|\;\lambda\in\mathbb{C},\,\lambda\neq 0\},

(25)

which is an element of
M
. To abbreviate the notation we will denote elements of
M
by their representatives that is simply as
\ket{\psi}
or
\psi
depending which is more convenient.

Any smooth curve in
M
-
\ket{\psi(t)}
can be represented as an action of a smooth map called evolution operator:

\hat{U}:\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto\hat{U}(t)\in U(d),\;\hat{U}(0)=\text{1},

(26)

where
U(d)
is a unitary group, on some initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
. That is:

\ket{\psi(t)}=\hat{U}(t)\ket{\psi_{0}}.

(27)

On the other hand, for any unitary matrix
\hat{U}
there exists hermitian matrix
\hat{H}
such that:

\hat{U}=e^{-i\hat{H}}.

(28)

This leads to:

\ket{\dot{\psi}}:=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\ket{\psi(t)}=-i\hat{H}\ket{\psi_{0}},

(29)

thus to any curve in
M
we can assign at each point a Hermitian matrix that determines the tangent vector at the point. Note that this assignment is not unique. According to relation (
24
) states that are proportional to each other represent the same point in
M
. This implies that

1.

for any real
\phi
curves
\ket{\psi(t)}
and
e^{i\phi t}\ket{\psi(t)}
have the same tangent vector,

2.

the part of
\ket{\dot{\psi}}
along
\ket{\psi_{0}}
does not influence the tangent vector.

We can easily get rid of the first ambiguity by assuming that
\hat{H}
is traceless since unitaries generated by
\hat{H}
and

\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{H}+\phi\text{1},

(30)

differ only by a global phase
e^{i\phi}
. Thus we will identify the tangent space
T_{\psi}M
with the set of traceless hermitian matrices
\mathcal{O}_{0}(\mathcal{S})
keeping in mind that the second ambiguity implies

\left(\hat{F}-\hat{G}\right)\ket{\psi}\sim\ket{\psi}\Rightarrow V_{\hat{F}}=V_{\hat{G}},

(31)

for any
\hat{F}
,
\hat{G}
and related tangent vectors
V_{\hat{F}}
,
V_{\hat{G}}
.

Now, we can define symplectic form at
\psi
as

\omega_{\psi}\left(V_{\hat{F}},V_{\hat{G}}\right)=i\bra{\psi}[\hat{F},\hat{G}]\ket{\psi}=i\langle[\hat{F},\hat{G}]\rangle_{\psi}.

(32)

Closeness of
\omega
can be shown by straightforward computation (for example using Theorem 13 from
[
35
]
). On the other hand, non-degeneracy comes from the observation that if for all
\hat{G}

\omega_{\psi}\left(V_{\hat{F}},V_{\hat{G}}\right)=0,

(33)

then for
\rho_{\psi}=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
we have

\displaystyle 0=\omega_{\psi}\left(V_{\hat{F}},V_{\hat{G}}\right)=\braket{[\hat{F},\hat{G}]}_{\psi}=

(34)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{\psi}[\hat{F},\hat{G}]\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left([\rho_{\psi},\hat{F}]\hat{G}\right)

for all
\hat{G}
which implies
[\rho_{\psi},\hat{F}]=0
. This is possible only when
\ket{\psi}
is an eigenstate of
\hat{F}
thus by
31
we have
V_{\hat{F}}=V_{\hat{0}}=0
.

Let us now consider what is the form of classical Hamiltonian for
\hat{H}
. First, note that the vector field
\psi\mapsto V_{\hat{H}}
, which for simplicity we will denote by
V_{\hat{H}}
, is Hamiltonian:

which by Jacobi identity is zero. Hence, there exists function
\mathcal{H}
such that
d\mathcal{H}=-\omega(V_{\hat{H}},\cdot)
. One possible choice of
\mathcal{H}
is

\mathcal{H}(\psi)=\langle\hat{H}\rangle_{\psi}.

(35)

Indeed, for any
V_{\hat{F}}
we have

\displaystyle-d\mathcal{H}(V_{\hat{F}})=

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\bra{\psi}e^{i\hat{F}t}\hat{H}e^{-i\hat{F}t}\ket{\psi}=

\displaystyle=i\langle[\hat{H},\hat{F}]\rangle=\omega(V_{\hat{H}},V_{\hat{F}}),

and by the non-degeneracy of
\omega
this choice is unique up to an addition of a constant factor.

The above construction is more interesting when applied to composite systems and entangled states. Consider a bipartite system

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{S}_{B},

(36)

and Hamiltonian that acts on
\mathcal{S}_{A}
and
\mathcal{S}_{B}
separately

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{A}\otimes\text{1}+\text{1}\otimes\hat{H}_{B}.

(37)

Then note that for
\rho_{\psi}=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\psi)=\bra{\psi}\hat{H}\ket{\psi}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{\psi}\hat{H}\right)=

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho_{\psi}\left(\hat{H}_{A}\otimes\text{1}\right)\right]+\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho_{\psi}\left(\text{1}\otimes\hat{H}_{B}\right)\right]=

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{\psi}^{A}\hat{H}_{A}\right)+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{\psi}^{B}\hat{H}_{B}\right),

where
\rho_{\psi}^{A},\rho_{\psi}^{B}
are reduced density matrices:

\rho_{\psi}^{A}=\mathrm{Tr}_{B}\rho_{\psi},\quad\rho_{\psi}^{B}=\mathrm{Tr}_{A}\rho_{\psi},

(38)

and
\mathrm{Tr}_{i}
is a partial trace over the system
i
. It follows that
\mathcal{H}
takes the same value for all states with the same partial traces as
\rho_{\psi}
. For example, in case
\rho_{\psi}
is maximally entangled we have
\rho_{\psi}^{A}=\alpha\text{1},\rho_{\psi}^{B}=\beta\text{1}
and then

\mathcal{H}(\psi)=\alpha\mathrm{Tr}\hat{H}_{A}+\beta\mathrm{Tr}\hat{H}_{B}=0,

(39)

for all maximally entangled states
\psi
while in general
V_{\hat{H}}
is not a zero vector in
T_{\psi}M
. In the next section we will show that this happens because
\omega
is degenerated on entangled states.

4
Degeneracy and entanglement

Consider state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{S}_{B}
and the set of all states that can be obtained from
\ket{\psi}
using only local evolutions:

\displaystyle L_{\psi}=\left\{U\ket{\psi}\;|\;U\in U(\mathcal{S}_{A},\mathcal{S}_{B})\right\},

where
U(\mathcal{S}_{A},\mathcal{S}_{B})
is the set of all matrices of the form
U_{A}\otimes U_{B}
for
U(\mathcal{S}_{A}),U_{B}\in U(\mathcal{S}_{B})
.

L_{\psi}
is a submanifold of
M
and for any
\varphi\in L_{\psi}
the tangent space
T_{\varphi}L_{\psi}
is spanned by Hamiltonian operators that generate local evolutions:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{A}\otimes\text{1}+\text{1}\otimes\hat{H}_{B},

(40)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{A}\in\mathcal{O}_{0}(\mathcal{S}_{A}),\;\hat{H}_{B}\in\mathcal{O}_{0}(\mathcal{S}_{B}).

(41)

We will denote the set of all such Hamiltonian operators as
\mathcal{O}_{0}(\mathcal{S}_{A},\mathcal{S}_{B})
.

By construction of
L_{\psi}
, as long as we consider only local evolutions, the state
\ket{\psi(t)}
will stay in
L_{\psi}
for all
t
. Thus if we want to analyze what is the form of classical Hamiltonian of
\hat{H}
we should do so by considering
L_{\psi}
as a manifold with a
2
-form:

\omega^{\psi}=\omega\big|_{L_{\psi}}.

(42)

But this turns out to be impossible because
\omega^{\psi}
is (in general) degenerated. Notice that for
\hat{F},\hat{G}\in\mathcal{O}_{0}(\mathcal{S}_{A},\mathcal{S}_{B})
and
\ket{\varphi}=U_{A}\otimes U_{B}\ket{\psi}\in L_{\psi}
we have

\displaystyle\omega^{\psi}_{\varphi}(V_{\hat{F}},V_{\hat{G}})=\omega_{\varphi}(V_{\hat{F}},V_{\hat{G}})=

(43)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{\varphi}^{A}[\hat{F}_{A},\hat{G}_{A}]\right)+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\rho_{\varphi}^{B}[\hat{F}_{B},\hat{G}_{B}]\right)=

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left([\rho_{\varphi}^{A},\hat{F}_{A}]\hat{G}_{A}\right)+\mathrm{Tr}\left([\rho_{\varphi}^{B},\hat{F}_{B}]\hat{G}_{B}\right),

thus if

[\rho_{\varphi}^{A},\hat{F}_{A}]=[\rho_{\varphi}^{B},\hat{F}_{B}]=0

(44)

we have
\omega^{\psi}_{\varphi}(V_{\hat{F}},V)=0
for all
V\in T_{\varphi}L_{\psi}
, that is
V_{\hat{F}}
is a null vector field. Furthermore (
44
) does not imply
[\rho_{\varphi},\hat{F}]=0
thus (in contrast to the case considered in (
34
)) there exists
\hat{F}
satisfying (
44
) such that
V_{\hat{F}}
is non-zero.

Therefore
L_{\psi}
equipped with
\omega^{\psi}
is a presymplectic manifold. Moreover, for any null vector
V_{\hat{H}}\in D_{\varphi}
and
U\in U(\mathcal{S}_{A},\mathcal{S}_{B})
such that
\ket{\varphi}=U\ket{\psi}
we have that
V_{U^{\dagger}\hat{H}U}\in D_{\psi}
. Hence null distribution of
L_{\psi}
-
D
is regular. It follows that we can choose Hamiltonian vector fields only for those Hamiltonian functions that are constant in the directions given by vectors in
D
(
22
). Notice that for

\ket{\varphi(t)}=e^{-i\hat{H}t}\ket{\varphi},

(45)

and local
\hat{H}
the derivative of reduced density matrix is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\rho^{A}_{\varphi}(t)=i[\rho^{A}_{\varphi},\hat{H}_{A}],

(46)

therefore from (
44
) we have that admissible are only those Hamiltonian functions that depend solely on reduced density matrices:

\mathcal{H}(\varphi)=\mathcal{H}(\rho^{A}_{\varphi},\rho^{B}_{\varphi}).

(47)

On the other hand, even if
\mathcal{H}
is admissible then the existence of null vector fields implies that we cannot uniquely determine related to it dynamic
V_{\mathcal{H}}
, in particular the correct dynamic
V_{\hat{H}}
. In particular for maximally entangled states the space of admissible Hamiltonian functions consists of constant functions and all vector fields are null. Thus the ambiguity in choosing Hamiltonian vector field is the largest (see also section 4 of
[
36
]
).

4.1
Degeneracy as an entanglement indicator

To see how all of this is related to entanglement let us recall that from regularity of
D
the level of degeneracy (non-symplecticity):

E:M\ni\psi\mapsto\mathrm{dim}D_{\psi}\in\mathbb{N},

(48)

is invariant under local transformations. Next, notice that for maximally entangled
\ket{\psi}
reduced density matrices are proportional to identity thus (
44
) is satisfied for all elements of
\mathcal{O}_{0}(\mathcal{S}_{A},\mathcal{S}_{B})
and admissible (
47
) are only constant Hamiltonians. On the other hand, for separable
\ket{\psi}
reduced density matrices are pure therefore (
44
) is satisfied only when
V_{\hat{F}}=0
(see (
34
) and discussion below). Separable
\ket{\psi}
satisfies also:

\rho_{\psi}=\rho_{\psi}^{A}\otimes\rho_{\psi}^{B},

(49)

thus all smooth functions on
L_{\psi}
are admissible. In summary, the level of degeneracy
E(\psi)
is invariant under local transformations, attains its maximal value for
\ket{\psi}
maximally entangled and is zero if and only if
\ket{\psi}
is separable.

The value of
E
can be easily computed using the formula proved in
[
1
]
. Let
p_{1},...,p_{d}
be Schmidt coefficients of
\ket{\psi}
that is:

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{p_{i}}\ket{i}\otimes\ket{i},

(50)

in some basis
\ket{i}
. Additionally, let
\tilde{p}_{1},...\tilde{p}_{r}
be unique values of non-zero Schmidt coefficients and
m_{1},...,m_{r}
their respective numbers of occurrences. Then we have
[
1
]
:

E(\psi)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}^{2}-1.

(51)

Therefore,
E
is higher for states that have many repeating non-zero Schmidt coefficients which tend to be also more entangled. However, it is possible to make
E(\psi)
bigger by transforming
\psi
using only local operations and classical communications (LOCC) which can be easily proved using conditions from
[
37
]
. Thus
E
is not an entanglement measure and that is why we call it an entanglement indicator.

5
