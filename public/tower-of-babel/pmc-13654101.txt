Fully automatic airway segmentation in CT images via dual-stage neural network with topology-constrained centerline completion
Authors: Harmony Yuan, Yuan Cheng, Chengxi Yan, Jiangang Chen, Qian Ding, Ying Zhou, Minglei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrecise segmentation of airways from computed tomography (CT) images is critical for pre-procedural planning in navigational bronchoscopy; however, existing methods often suffer from distal branch fractures and morphological losses due to slender diameters, low contrast, and noise. To address these limitations and achieve accurate, topologically complete airway segmentation, this study proposed a novel dual-stage neural network with topology-constrained centerline completion.MethodsA two-stage centerline-guided network (TSC-Net) was developed that innovatively combines centerline structural similarity (CSS) loss and centerline overlap rate (COR) loss. CSS loss maintains the continuity of airway tree structures by strengthening centerline morphological constraints, while COR loss enhances global centerline overlap to ensure branch integrity. Additionally, a topology-difference-driven fracture-region completion mechanism was adopted to adaptively repair the fracturedand misclassified areas. The model employs a residual UNet (ResUNet) backbone integrated with an atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP) module to capture multi-scale contextual features and adopts a two-stage training strategy in which the encoder is frozen during stage 2 while the decoder is fine-tuned using dynamically adjusted loss weights. This strategy enables targeted optimization of discontinuous regions without compromising globally well-predicted areas.ResultsExperiments on the Airway Tree Modeling Challenge 2022 (ATM’22), Lung Image Database Consortium and Image Database Resource Initiative (LIDC-IDRI), Extraction of Airways from CT (EXACT’09), and private datasets showed that TSC-Net achieved a Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 93.2%±2.1%, significantly outperforming SwinUNETR (92.9%±3.6%, P<0.05), nnUNet (91.2%±1.2%, P<0.05), and UNet (75.6%±4.3%, P<0.05). Introduction

Background

Respiratory diseases are among the leading causes of death and disability worldwide. Their early diagnosis and precise treatment are heavily dependent on the accurate analysis of airway anatomical structures (1). In clinical practice, computed tomography (CT) has become the preferred imaging modality for assessing these structures due to its high spatial resolution and three-dimensional (3D) imaging capability. However, automated airway segmentation from CT images remains challenging, particularly for the precise segmentation of distal airway branches (2).

Distal airways exhibit extremely delicate geometric morphology. Indeed, eighth-generation bronchi may measure <2 mm in diameter and appear as only a few voxels in CT images, resulting in significantly reduced signal intensity and blurred anatomical boundaries. Additionally, the airway tree exhibits a highly branched topological structure, with distal branches exhibiting variable angles and marked length disparities. These characteristics make it difficult for traditional segmentation methods to simultaneously address insufficient local contrast and diverse global topologies. The overlap in CT values among adjacent anatomical structures further exacerbates segmentation difficulties. Finally, under pathological conditions, anatomical changes such as luminal stenosis caused by tumor invasion, wall thickening due to tuberculosis, and morphological collapse from foreign body obstruction further increase the complexity of automated airway segmentation.

Related work

Traditional segmentation methods

Traditional airway segmentation methods largely rely on classical image processing techniques and regularized modeling approaches. These methods extract airway structures through various algorithmic mechanisms. Hessian matrix-based enhancement methods (3) detect tubular structures by analyzing the eigenvalues of Hessian matrices. Multi-scale analysis and level set methods (4) integrate multi-scale filtering and energy function optimization to ensure boundary continuity. However, due to their high computational complexity, this category of algorithms cannot meet the real-time requirements of clinical applications.

Deep learning-based segmentation methods

Deep learning methods learn complex airway features through data-driven strategies, significantly improving segmentation accuracy and robustness (5-11). Charbonnier et al. (5) used convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in conjunction with region growing to detect and remove redundant voxels in airway leakage regions. Jin et al. (6) developed a semi-supervised model capable of achieving high-quality airway segmentation using training datasets with incomplete annotations. Wang et al. proposed the NaviAirway algorithm (7), which incorporates a bronchiole-sensitive loss function and an iterative training strategy. Hatamizadeh et al. (8) introduced an attention mechanism to enhance feature responses in fine structures, effectively improving the model’s ability to recognize and segment critical areas. In addition to CNNs, graph neural networks (GNNs) (9) have also been applied to such segmentation tasks. For example, Selvan et al. (10) improved the accuracy of edge-probability prediction by integrating GNNs with mean-field networks.

Limitations and challenges

Despite recent advances, existing techniques still face several limitations. Deep learning has enhanced feature extraction; however, fine-branch detection (fine-BD), spatial context fusion, and topological continuity remain challenging. Further, inadequate spatial modeling between the airways and surrounding tissues can lead to anatomical inaccuracies, particularly at complex junctions. Additionally, variations in CT protocols across institutions can induce significant data distribution shifts, thereby limiting model robustness in clinical settings.

Contributions

The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

First, we developed the two-stage centerline-guided network (TSC-Net), which adopts a two-stage training strategy specifically designed to enhance the continuity and completeness of distal airway branches, thereby improving overall airway segmentation accuracy.

Second, we designed a loss function sensitive to distal airway branches by combining topological constraints: centerline structural similarity (CSS) and centerline overlap rate (COR). These constraints explicitly enforce geometric continuity and spatial correspondence of airway centerlines, thereby mitigating discontinuities in distal branches.

Third, we employed a human-in-the-loop (HITL) mechanism to obtain accurate airway centerlines, overcoming the limitations of traditional methods that rely solely on direct model generation. High-quality centerline annotations offer precise guidance during model training, effectively facilitating model convergence.

Finally, we implemented a cross-domain multi-source database integration strategy to enhance model generalizability and reliability. Additionally, we established a standardized experimental framework to assess the algorithm from multiple perspectives, thereby validating the effectiveness of our model design and training strategy. We present this article in accordance with the CLEAR reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1097/rc).

Methods

Training strategy

The two-stage neural network training strategy specifically addresses challenges such as structural discontinuity and morphological loss in distal airway branches (6-11). In stage 1, the network obtains basic airway features and structural information. However, due to the complex characteristics of distal branches, feature coupling remains insufficient, requiring further improvement in segmentation accuracy. In stage 2, TSC-Net uses the topological differences between the airway centerlines predicted in stage 1 and the ground-truth centerlines. Based on these differences, a discontinuity-region completion mechanism is employed to restore and refine fragmented airway segments.

Figure 1 provides an overview of the proposed two-stage centerline-guided training strategy. Stage 1 employs TSC-Net with two-dimensional (2D) slice inputs, and the converged weights are saved for stage 2 initialization. During stage 2 training, the encoder parameters from stage 1 remain frozen, while only the decoder undergoes fine-tuning. A data augmentation strategy involving random erasure and contrast perturbation is specifically applied to the fractured-region mask, enhancing adaptability to complex morphologies. Additionally, training samples containing fractured regions are oversampled at a 2:1 ratio to increase learning frequency for critical regions. This strategy effectively addresses morphological distortion issues in traditional single-stage methods through dynamic loss collaboration, targeted parameter optimization, and data distribution adjustments.

Model architecture

As shown in Figure 2, TSC-Net employs a residual UNet (ResUNet) as the backbone and integrates the atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP) module to enhance multi-scale target perception by expanding the receptive field. These residual connections mitigate vanishing gradients in deep networks and enhance small-object segmentation accuracy by enabling cross-layer feature reuse. The ASPP module explicitly models multi-scale spatial correlations and addresses variations in target size and background interference through parallel atrous convolutions with diverse sampling rates. Specifically, the ASPP module applies four atrous convolutions (with rates of 3, 6, 12, and 18) to the input, capturing contextual information across scales. The resulting features are fused into a 256-channel representation via a 1×1 convolution, enabling comprehensive multi-scale context modeling.

Diagram of the TSC-Net. ASPP, atrous spatial pyramid pooling; TSC-Net, two-stage centerline-guided network.

The use of 2D axial slices as network inputs in TSC-Net is primarily motivated by practical engineering considerations. Specifically, it leverages the ImageNet-pretrained backbones to accelerate convergence, reduces graphics processing unit (GPU) memory consumption, and enables larger batch sizes and more extensive data augmentation. Although each slice is processed independently, no perceptible discontinuities across adjacent slices are introduced in practice, as the small inter-slice thickness (0.5 mm after resampling) and the gradual spatial variation of airway branches ensure that the 2D predictions produce coherent volumetric reconstructions after they are stacked.

Loss function for stage 1

LDice serves as the baseline segmentation metric and quantifies the voxel-wise overlap between the predicted airway Y^ and the ground-truth Y as defined in Eq. [2]. It ensures overall coverage but, by itself, lacks sufficient sensitivity to slender distal branches because of their negligible volumetric contribution. To address this limitation, LCSS is designed as a normalized pixel-wise discrepancy metric that enforces geometric continuity and spatial alignment between the predicted centerline C^ and the ground-truth C {Eq. [3]}. In each forward pass, C^ is obtained by applying a differentiable soft-skeletonization operation to the binarized network output Y^, which iteratively applies minimum-pooling and maximum-pooling as differentiable proxies for morphological erosion and dilation, respectively, producing a fully differentiable centerline representation that enables gradient flow through the entire loss computation pipeline.

The numerator of LCSS effectively counts the number of mismatched pixels between the two centerline maps, corresponding to the size of their symmetric difference, while the denominator normalizes this discrepancy by the union size. This normalization prevents errors in small distal branches from being overshadowed by the larger central airways. By construction, LCSS produces distinct loss responses tailored to different types of structural failures. A shifted centerlinsegmentation accuracy but also enhancede increases the loss according to the displacement distance, as mismatched pixels accumulate along the shift boundary. Fragmented or broken centerlines increase the loss in proportion to the length of the missing segment. Partially missing distal branches incur a moderate penalty, as the union size decreases simultaneously and partially offsets the increased numerator. Similarly, redundant branches increase the loss due to asymmetric mismatches.

Complementary to LCSS, LCOR measures the overlap ratio between the predicted and ground-truth centerlines as defined in Eq. [4], reinforcing spatial alignment of the centerline structure from a coverage perspective. LCSS emphasizes the location and extent of geometric deviations in the centerline, and LCOR directly penalizes insufficient centerline coverage, ensuring that the overall extent of the airway tree is adequately captured. The two terms work synergistically:LCOR ensures that the model does not miss large portions of the tree, while LCSS refines the positional accuracy and continuity of every detected branch, particularly at bifurcations and distal ends where misalignment is most problematic.

To expedite convergence and mitigate the risk of getting trapped in local optima, larger weights (α,β) are assigned to the segmentation-related loss terms, and a smaller weight (γ) is assigned to LCOR. For example, we adopted α=0.6, β=0.3, and γ=0.1 in our experiments. After a fixed number of training epochs, the weight coefficients are dynamically adjusted to balance the contributions of individual loss functions, for example, to α=0.3, β=0.3, and γ=0.4.

Loss function for stage 2

In stage 2, a new sub-loss function LGap is introduced based on the topological discrepancies between the airway centerlines predicted in stage 1 and the corresponding ground-truth centerlines. It promotes the completion of discontinuous and mis-segmented regions.

Specifically, LGap is defined as follows:

MGapis defined as the symmetric difference between the predicted and ground-truth centerlines. This difference can be decomposed into two complementary error types: voxels present in C but absent in C^ (corresponding to fractured or missing segments) and voxels present in C^ but absent in C (corresponding to redundant or spurious branches). Both types are collectively captured by MGap and serve as the target regions for repair. Within this mask, LGap computes a Dice-like overlap loss that penalizes segmentation errors exclusively within these localized areas. As a result, when a missing distal branch fails to cover the corresponding voxels in MGap, a high penalty results, driving the network to reconstruct the break. Conversely, a false-positive branch triggers an equally high penalty that suppresses the erroneous prediction. Meanwhile, correctly segmented regions, where C^ and C coincide, are excluded from MGap and remain unaffected by LGap. This targeted design ensures that the network’s learning capacity is focused precisely on topological defects, prioritizing the repair of structural discontinuities without compromising globally well-predicted areas.

In stage 2, the training strategy employs a multi-level optimization mechanism to enhance the repair effects on fractured and mis-segmented regions. To give precedence to the repair of critical regions, the fracture completion loss weight δis initialized to 0.5 during the early training phase. Meanwhile, the weights of LDice, LCSS, and LCOR are set to 0.3, 0.1, and 0.1, respectively. As the training progresses, the value of δis gradually decreased every k epochs, and the weights of other sub-loss functions are increased proportionally. Ultimately, the weights converge to α=0.4, β=0.3, γ=0.2, and δ=0.1, thereby achieving a smooth transition from local repair to global optimization.

Centerline generation strategy

This study developed a pipeline for acquiring ground-truth airway centerlines based on a HITL mechanism rather than traditional end-to-end generation strategies. The mechanism operates in a closed-loop mode of “algorithm pre-extraction and human-machine collaborative correction”. First, the Zhang-Suen thinning algorithm (12) is applied to obtain the initial centerline skeleton. Subsequently, three radiologists, each with more than 5 years of experience in medical image diagnosis, review the centerlines on a case-by-case basis, using a customized semi-automated annotation tool with an interactive visual interface. Leveraging their anatomical expertise and clinical experience, the radiologists specifically focus on defects such as distal branch connectivity issues and structural deviations. This enables precise optimization of the centerline topological structure. A schematic illustration of the centerline extraction results for a representative case is presented in Figure 3.

Implementation details

All CT volumes were resampled to an isotropic voxel spacing of 0.5×0.5×0.5 mm3 using trilinear interpolation. Intensity normalization was performed by clipping Hounsfield units (HU) to the range of −1,000 to 600 and rescaling the clipped values to [0, 1] using minimum-maximum normalization. During training, each axial slice was resized to 256×256 pixels and used as network inputs. During inference, each axial slice was processed independently, and the predictions were stacked to reconstruct the full 3D volume.

The model was implemented in PyTorch 1.12.0 and trained on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU using the Adam optimizer. The initial learning rate was set to 1×10−4 and reduced to 1×10−6 using cosine annealing over 300 epochs. The batch size was 32; stage 1 was trained for 300 epochs, followed by 150 epochs of fine-tuning in stage 2. Online data augmentation was applied with a probability of 0.5 for each operation, including random rotation (±15°), random scaling (0.85–1.15), random horizontal and vertical flipping, and random gamma correction (γ∈[0.8, 1.2]). As described in the “Training strategy” section, random erasing was specifically applied in the fractured-region masks of 10% of the training samples. Post-processing consisted of connected-component analysis to remove isolated clusters containing <50 voxels, followed by final thresholding at 0.5 after sigmoid activation.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively evaluate the proposed algorithm, both subjective visual assessment and quantitative analysis were employed. The subjective evaluation was conducted by three experienced thoracic radiologists using a 5-point Likert scale across four dimensions: structural integrity, boundary accuracy, noise robustness, and clinical utility. A total of 100 complex cases from the ATM’22, LIDC-IDRI, EXACT’09, and XALung25 datasets were independently scored, with consensus discussions for discrepancies ≥1 point. Representative cases underwent 3D visualization to demonstrate the model’s performance in terms of branch continuity and noise resistance. The quantitative analysis included the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), precision, recall, intersection over union (IoU), tree-length detection (TD) rate, and BD rate. Comparative experiments

A comparative analysis was conducted against five state-of-the-art baseline methods: 2D UNet (16), ResUnet (17), nnUNet (18), SwinUNETR (8), and NaviAirway (7). The evaluation included 523 cases from four datasets: 350 from ATM’22, 91 from LIDC-IDRI, 40 from EXACT’09, and 42 from XALung25. A stratified random sampling strategy was adopted based on data source, with approximately 80% of cases from each dataset allocated to the training set and the remaining 20% of cases allocated to the validation set. Specifically, 280 ATM’22 cases were used for training and 70 for validation, 73 LIDC-IDRI cases for training and 18 for validation, 32 EXACT’09 cases for training and eight for validation, and 38 XALung25 cases for training and four for validation. This resulted in a total of 423 training cases and 100 validation cases.

For nnUNet, the 3D full-resolution configuration was used with its default pipeline preserved without modification, as its adaptive configuration is optimized internally for each dataset. For SwinUNETR, the official implementation from the MONAI library was used with the standard hyperparameter settings. For UNet and ResUNet, standard 2D architectures with a base channel of 64 were implemented, with ResUNet incorporating residual blocks in place of standard convolutional blocks. For NaviAirway, the official implementation was used with the authors’ recommended configuration.

Except for nnUNet, all baseline methods were trained using the same 2D axial slice inputs resized to 256×256 pixels, the same data augmentation strategy, and the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1×10−4, consistent with our method. To ensure a fair comparison, extensive hyperparameter tuning was not performed for any baseline method; instead, the default or recommended settings were followed. For consistency, the same training epochs of 300 were applied. nnUNet was trained using its native 3D pipeline. All models were evaluated on the identical mixed-domain validation set containing cases from all four datasets using the same metrics.

The ablation results (Table 3) showed that the Base configuration provided moderate but suboptimal performance. After incorporating the LCSSmodule, segmentation accuracy improved significantly (DSC: 89.4%±2.8% → 91.2%±3.2%; precision: 90.8%±4.5% → 92.7%±4.1%) while recall remained relatively stable. The topological integrity metrics, the TD and BD rates, also increased to 93.4%±4.8% and 91.7%±5.1%, respectively. While DSC volatility showed a slight increase, the standard deviation of precision decreased (±4.1% vs. ±4.5%). Taken together, these results indicate that LCSS not only improved the segmentation accuracy but also enhanced the stability of key feature extraction.

Discussion

This study developed a method that synergistically improves airway segmentation accuracy and topological integrity in CT images through a two-stage training strategy and topology-constrained loss design. The experimental results demonstrated clear improvements over the baseline models. These statistically significant superiorities can be attributed to several key innovations.

The centerline-guided topological constraint mechanism plays a pivotal role in mitigating distal branch fragmentation. The combination of LCSS and LCOR loss functions explicitly constrains the geometric continuity and spatial correspondence between the predicted and ground-truth centerlines, enabling the model to learn topological priors of the airway tree. In stage 2, based on the topological differences between the airway centerlines predicted in the first stage and the corresponding ground-truth centerlines, LGap accurately identifies discontinuous and redundant regions through the MGap mask. It then directly penalizes segmentation errors within these regions, encouraging the network to prioritize the correction of topological discontinuities. Together, LGap, LCSS, and LCOR establish a progressive framework of “prior constraint, difference localization, and targeted repair”, addressing branch discontinuities from multiple dimensions and thereby improving segmentation accuracy.

The ablation experiments showed that introducing CSS led to a 4.9% increase in TD. Moreover, combining COR increased the BD rate to 92.1%, supporting the effectiveness of topological constraints in preserving branch continuity. In the expert subjective evaluations, the “structural integrity” score of the proposed method was significantly higher than that of UNet. As shown in the visualization results in Figure 4, our method achieved complete coverage of the ground-truth branches with high overlap, outperforming traditional methods such as UNet and ResUnet, which exhibited discontinuities and spatial misalignment.

In conclusion, this mechanism addresses the limitation of traditional loss functions, which only optimize voxel overlap and lack structural constraints. This is especially important for small-diameter distal airway branches, as it effectively avoids segmentation discontinuities caused by ambiguous local features.

The two-stage training strategy represents another key innovation for repairing discontinuous regions. Stage 1 establishes foundational segmentation through the optimization of centerline-guided loss functions, while stage 2 performs targeted refinement of discontinuous regions based on topological discrepancies. The quantitative evaluations demonstrated that compared with single-stage training, the two-stage approach improved DSC by 0.7% and precision by 1.1%. As shown in Figure 4, the single-stage methods (e.g., SwinUNETR) exhibited discontinuities in distal branches. Collectively, the dynamic completion mechanism addresses the limitations of traditional single-stage training by enabling the precise capture of fine-scale branch features and the effective repair of discontinuities. These improvements result in enhanced overall segmentation performance, particularly in terms of topological integrity and clinical practicality.

The multi-dimensional feature fusion architecture significantly enhances the feature extraction capability for delicate structures. The ASPP module expands the receptive field through multi-scale atrous convolutions, enabling the simultaneous capture of local tubular wall details and global tree-like topology. Compared with NaviAirway, our approach achieves dual suppression of false detections and missed detections by balancing precision and recall. This balance is more consistent with clinical requirements of “zero missed diagnosis” in airway segmentation tasks.

In addition, the HITL mechanism ensures the geometric continuity and spatial accuracy of the ground-truth centerlines while reducing the random errors associated with purely algorithmic extraction. Consequently, this strategy enhances the precision of the feedback provided by the two-stage loss functions and accelerates model convergence. As shown in Figure 5, the expert-refined centerlines exhibit significantly better adherence to both airway tree trunks and distal branches than the directly generated algorithmic centerlines. These refined centerlines provide more precise optimization targets for the topology-constrained loss functions, thereby facilitating effective learning of branch continuity from the annotated data.

Finally, the generalizability of the model and the reliability of the finding is enhanced through the integration of multi-source data and the development of a standardized evaluation framework. We integrate and supplement manual annotations across our four datasets to improve data diversity. All comparative methods adopt identical training and test set partitions and are trained and validated under uniform hardware environments. A multi-dimensional evaluation system combining subjective and objective assessments is employed. As shown in Table 1, our method led in most quantitative metrics. As shown in Table 2, our method also ranked first in all aspects of the subjective assessment. These findings demonstrate the adaptability of multi-source data training to complex clinical scenarios.

However, this study had several limitations. First, the segmentation of extremely slender branches remains challenging, as evidenced by the 3.1% difference between BD and TD. Second, model performance may be compromised in cases of severe pathology with substantial morphological alterations. Third, the private dataset originated from a single center, limiting multi-center validation. Fourth, the use of 2D inputs may not fully exploit volumetric context; future studies should thus explore 2.5-dimensional (2.5D) or 3D architectures.

Conclusions

This study developed a fully automatic airway segmentation algorithm for CT images that addresses key limitations of traditional methods in distal branch segmentation accuracy and topological integrity. The proposed multi-component framework integrates two-stage training with topology-constrained loss functions, ResUnet with ASPP, expert-refined centerline acquisition, and multi-source data integration with a standardized evaluation framework. The quantitative results showed superior DSC, TD, and BD performance compared with baseline methods. The proposed two-stage training method outperformed single-stage methods, supporting the effectiveness of the “prior constraint, difference localization, and targeted repair” mechanism. Visualizations further showed reduced distal branch fractures and maintained topological integrity. The method demonstrates state-of-the-art performance, and shows potential for direct application to pre-procedural planning for robotic-assisted bronchoscopy in clinical practice.
