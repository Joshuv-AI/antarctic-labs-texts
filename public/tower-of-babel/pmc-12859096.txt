Staphylococcus aureus fatty acid metabolism governs saeRS-mediated aggregation in joint infections
Authors: Jinlong Yu, Mingzhang Li, Changming Wang, Musha Hamushan, Yujie Hu, Yi Yang, Feng Jiang, Jireng Yan, Boyong Wang, Xiaohua Chen, Jin Tang, Geyong Guo, Pei Han, Hao Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Staphylococcus aureus can form aggregates within human synovial fluid, contributing to joint infections that are resistant to conventional therapies. However, the regulatory pathways underlying this process remain unclear. Here, we reveal that the S. aureus two-component system saeRS contributes to synovial aggregation by controlling the expression of fibrinectin-binding proteins A and B (FnbA/B), which bind to human fibrinogen and cause aggregation. We further demonstrate that the fatty acid kinase (fak) system is the primary regulator for SaeS activity under host conditions. Mechanistically, fak is epistatic to saeRS by scavenging exogenous fatty acids and altering the localization of SaeS into functional membrane microdomains (FMMs), which are essential for SaeS function. We also identify that undecanoic acid (UDA) is capable of inhibiting the fak pathway, demonstrating its efficacy and potential applicability in treating joint infections. Altogether, these findings highlight the fak-saeRS axis as a potential therapeutic target for joint infections and underscore UDA as a promising treatment for these infections.

Introduction

Joint infections, either native or prosthetic, represent a formidable clinical challenge in musculoskeletal medicine1. Left untreated, joint infections can lead to irreversible joint damage, chronic pain, functional impairment, and even amputation2,3. Therefore, accurate diagnosis and prompt intervention are urgently needed to mitigate the potentially devastating consequences associated with joint destruction and systemic spread of infection. Although substantial research efforts have advanced our understanding of joint infections1,4, persistent challenges in clinical management and evolving microbial resistance patterns underscore the need for continued refinement of therapeutic strategies and molecular-level understanding of host–pathogen interactions. Further research remains crucial to address critical knowledge gaps and translate mechanistic insights into improved patient outcomes.

Staphylococcus aureus is the primary causative pathogen of septic arthritis and periprosthetic joint infections5,6. Notably, S. aureus strongly tends to aggregate within the synovial fluid, which promotes persistent infection and treatment resistance7. Although closely related, synovial aggregation and biofilm formation are distinct phenomena with different phenotypic manifestations. Synovial aggregates form quickly and develop much larger amounts of biomass. The aggregation process is believed to involve a sophisticated interplay between host and bacterial factors. Host molecules such as fibrinogen and fibronectin serve as substrates for bacterial adhesion via microbial surface proteins such as ClfA/B and FnbA/B8. Despite significant research efforts, the key regulator of this aggregation process and its specific impact on infection outcomes remain incompletely understood, highlighting a critical gap in pathogenesis knowledge. Therefore, elucidating the precise mechanism underlying synovial aggregation is essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

S. aureus transcription is tightly controlled by two-component systems (TCSs). In particular, the saeRS TCS regulates the expression of more than 20 surface-associated adhesins and secreted virulence factors, such as hla (ɑ-haemolysin), coa (coagulase), nuc (thermonuclease), and several proteases9,10. The activity of saeRS is modulated by various factors: it is positively activated by fatty acid kinase (fak) and codY, as well as by the presence of human neutrophil peptides, calprotectin, or hydrogen peroxide. In contrast, free fatty acids inhibit saeRS activity11,12. Notably, fak dysfunction significantly downregulates saeRS, underscoring a close functional relationship13,14. fak is a two-component enzyme that utilizes exogenous fatty acids to generate acyl-phosphate for phospholipid synthesis and cell membrane construction15–17. The fak system consists of a kinase domain protein (FakA) that phosphorylates a fatty acid enveloped by a fatty acid binding protein (FakB1/2)18,19. Parsons et al. 15. proposed that fak might activate saeRS activity through phosphorylation of the SaeS protein. However, this was not true in a previous study20. Yet the mechanism by which fak affects saeRS activity has not been determined. The relationships among eFA, fak, and sae need to be thoroughly explored.

Here, the critical role of S. aureus saeRS in synovial aggregation, a key pathogenic mechanism in joint infections, is elucidated. We determined that saeRS activity is regulated by the fak system within the host environment. Mechanistically, fak regulates the localization of the SaeS histidine kinase within functional membrane microdomains (FMMs). Furthermore, our findings demonstrate the high adaptability of S. aureus to the synovial fluid environment to facilitate its pathogenesis. Finally, we identified undecanoic acid (UDA), a medium-chain fatty acid (C11:0) capable of inhibiting the Fak system. UDA effectively inhibits S. aureus aggregation and enhances the efficacy of anti-FASII antibiotics, suggesting its potential as a promising adjuvant therapy for joint infections.

Results

S. aureus synovial aggregation contributes to joint infections

Bacterial synovial aggregation is fundamentally distinct from biofilm, as it rapidly surpasses the phagocytosis size (>10 µm in diameter21) within one hour and matures within a few hours (Fig. 1A–C). Currently, no evidence directly demonstrates the contribution of synovial aggregation ability to the severity of septic arthritis. To address this gap, we analysed S. aureus strains from patients with musculoskeletal infections (Fig. 1D). The isolates were classified into joint-involved and joint-not-involved groups (n = 41 for JI; n = 111 for N-JI). By quantifying their aggregation and biofilm formation abilities, we found that compared with the N-JI group, the JI group presented greater aggregation ability, whereas no difference in biofilm formation ability was observed between the JI and N-JI groups (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Data 1 and 2, and Fig. S1A, B). To further verify this, we selected the five strains with the greatest aggregation ability (H1–H5) and the five with the lowest aggregation ability (L1–L5) to establish a knee joint infection mouse model. First, the growth of the representative strains did not differ when cultured in 20% synovial fluid (Fig. S1C). Each mouse was subsequently infected with a high-aggregating isolate at the right knee and a low-aggregating isolate at the left knee. The results revealed that mice infected with high-aggregating S. aureus exhibited more severe infections with greater cartilage impairment (Fig. 1G), limited joint movement (Fig. 1H–J), and greater bacterial burden (Fig. 1K). When cocultured with immune cells or treated with antibiotics, high-aggregating isolates showed significant increases in resistance to immune cell killing ( ~ twofold, Fig. 1L, M), phagocytosis ( ~ twofold, Fig. S1D, E), and antibiotic killing compared with their unaggregated state (100–1000-fold, Fig. S1F–H). The phenotypic difference between the aggregated and unaggregated states of the same strain indicates that synovial aggregation behaviour, rather than other genetic factors, helps the bacteria evade both the host immune response and antimicrobial therapies. This provides a strong link between aggregation and joint infections.

Directed evolution revealed that synovial aggregation depends on SaeRS activity

Our analysis revealed no significant correlation between biofilm formation and aggregation in S. aureus, suggesting that distinct mechanisms govern aggregation. This prompted us to investigate the genetic pathways underlying synovial aggregation. We performed directed evolution by selecting planktonic S. aureus populations within SF (synovial fluid) (Fig. 2A). S. aureus strain USA300 was initially exposed to SF for aggregation; unaggregated cells were then isolated from the planktonic fraction and grown overnight in tryptic soy broth (TSB) before they were reintroduced into SF for the next round of aggregation. The evolution was repeated until aggregation was no longer observable (Fig. 2B). As anticipated, the evolved population exhibited increased susceptibility to antibiotics and reduced resistance to immune cell killing (Figs. 2C and S2A, B). Notably, the evolved population also showed a progressive loss of haemolysis rings on sheep blood agar plates (Fig. 2D). We confirmed that the haemolysis-ring-negative colonies were all nonaggregating (Fig. 2D) and that the mutation was irreversible, even when the cells were cultured for several passages. The sae locus, encoding the saeRS TCS and auxiliary saePQ factors, is central to the activation of key virulence factors and adhesins, with a link to fak, as shown in previous studies14,15. We confirmed the roles of fakA and saeRS in aggregation by testing their mutants in SF; ∆saeRS and ∆fakA were nonaggregating but were restored upon complementation (Fig. 2F). Consistent with these findings, both saeRS and fakA mutants exhibited reduced resistance to phagocytosis and heightened sensitivity to antibiotics and immune cell-mediated killing in SF (Figs. 2G–H and S2D–F). We also demonstrated that bacterial growth was not impaired for the mutants when they were grown in 20% SF (Fig. S2G). We next investigated whether fakA influences aggregation via saeRS by complementing ΔfakA with saeRS (ΔfakA::psaeRS) and ΔsaeRS with fakA (ΔsaeRS::pfakA). Notably, aggregation was restored in only the ΔfakA::psaeRS strain but not in the ΔsaeRS::pfakA strain (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, fakA knockout significantly decreased saeRS transcription, suggesting that fakA is epistatic to saeRS (Fig. 2J, K). These results agree with those of previously published studies13–15. We subsequently repeated the directed evolution experiment with high-aggregating clinical strains (H1–H5). The results revealed an anticipated decrease in aggregation ability (Fig. S2H). Subsequent genome sequencing revealed DEL/SNPs in the fakA, fakB1, and saePQRS loci (Fig. S2I and Supplementary Data 3), which further supports our findings that the fak–sae axis contributes to synovial aggregation. Molecular docking analysis of FakA binding with FakB1/2 revealed that the SNPs led to changes in amino acids that were critical for binding function, possibly explaining the dysfunction of the fak system (FakA [R246, E311, R314, F422, E532, E548], FakB1 [202 ~ 209]; Fig. S3A–D and Supplementary Data 3).

FnbA/B-fibrinogen interaction is the key step in synovial aggregation

To further explore the mechanism of aggregation, we first confirmed that the aggregates were primarily composed of proteins. This conclusion was based on the observation that treatment with dispersin-B, DNase-I, RNase-H, and lipase did not inhibit aggregation and that even the nucleation step was not affected (Fig. S4A, B), whereas proteinase K significantly inhibited aggregation (Fig. S4A), suggesting that proteins play a critical role in synovial aggregation. SDS–PAGE of the aggregates revealed enrichment of proteins at 60 and 80 kDa (Fig. 3A, B). To determine which bacterial adhesins interact with these proteins, we first identified saeRS-regulated genes from a large S. aureus transcriptome dataset with a machine learning method (Fig. 3D)22,23. Among the 34 genes predicted to be downstream of saeR, 23 were verified to be downregulated in the ΔsaeRS RNA-seq data, and 8 were adhesins (Figs. 3E and S5B and Supplementary Data 5). Complementing ΔsaeRS with each of the eight adhesins and inducing overexpression with anhydrotetracycline revealed that only fnbA/B restored aggregation (Figs. 3F, G and S5A). Moreover, the fnbA or fnbB mutant did not completely abolish aggregation, whereas the double mutant eliminated aggregation, suggesting that both fnbA and fnbB are essential for aggregation (Fig. 3H, I).

FnbA/B are proteins that bind fibronectin and fibrinogen24. We then explored how fibronectin and fibrinogen contribute to aggregation. The incubation of S. aureus with fibrinogen, but not fibronectin, induced aggregation, which was abolished in the ΔfnbAB strain, indicating that fibrinogen mediates aggregation through binding with FnbA/B adhesins (Fig. 3J, K). We also tested the pathogenesis of fnbAB in a mouse model of joint ss. Immunofluorescence detection of S. aureus revealed that the bacterial aggregates around the joint were reduced in the ΔfnbAB and ΔsaeRS groups, although the difference in bacterial burden between the ΔfnbAB and WT groups was not as significant as that in the ΔsaeRS group (Fig. S5C, D). These findings suggest that although fnbA/B are essential for synovial aggregation, other saeRS-regulated genes also contribute to pathogenesis.

S. aureus fak activates saeRS and fnbA/B by scavenging exogenous fatty acids

We subsequently tested how saeRS-fnbA/B responds to the human environment. By analysing the meta-transcriptome data of specimens from patients with S. aureus musculoskeletal infections, we observed higher read counts mapping to the fnbA/B locus in the JI group than in the N-JI group (Fig. 4A). Similarly, when S. aureus was cultured in vitro with SF or serum, upregulation of saeRS-regulated adhesins was observed (Fig. 4B–D and Supplementary Data 6). We hypothesized that fak controls the saeRS-fnbA/B axis in SF. First, we detected no change in the transcription of fakA or fakB1/2 when the cells were incubated with SF (Fig. S5A). The transcription level of saeRS-fnbA/B in response to SF was subsequently compared between the WT and ΔfakA strains, and no difference was detected for the response of the ΔfakA strain to SF (Fig. 4E), suggesting that the upregulation of saeRS observed in synovial fluid is abrogated in the fak mutant. To further explore this phenomenon, we used C-13-labelled oleic acid and acetic acid to trace exogenous (fak) and endogenous fatty acid synthesis pathway (FASII) activity among the high- and low-aggregating strains, respectively; higher fak activity was detected among the high-aggregate strains (Fig. 4F–G). This result implies that differences in fak activity might lead to differences in aggregation ability among clinical strains. Upon comparative analysis of FakA/B1/B2 and SaePQRS protein sequences retrieved from 500 S. aureus strains deposited in public databases, we observed a high degree of polymorphism in FakA amino acid sequences, whereas the SaePQRS and FakB1/2 amino acid sequences were relatively conserved (Figs. 4H and S7). Furthermore, sequencing of our high/low-aggregating strains (H1–5 and L1–5) revealed amino acid polymorphisms with increased binding energy between FakA dimerization and FakA–FakB1 binding (Fig. 4I). Although the absolute binding energy provides little biological meaning, the relative values among strains are still helpful in elucidating the discrepancies in aggregation ability and Fak activity among clinical strains.

The biological function of fak is to exploit exogenous fatty acids (eFAs)15. To explore whether fak influences saeRS-fnbA/B through eFA, we treated S. aureus with common human fatty acids and found that eFA (oleic acid, palmitic acid, and myristic acid) inhibited saeRS-fnbAB transcription (Fig. S6B). We propose that fak, as a consumer of eFA, scavenges fatty acids, thereby alleviating their suppression of saeRS-fnbA/B. To further validate this model, we treated ΔfakA with fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (FA-free-BSA) to scavenge intracellular fatty acids. A significant increase in sae activity was observed upon treatment of ΔfakA with FA-free BSA. (Fig. 4K). However, the rescue of sae transcription by FA-free-BSA was only partial compared with that of the wild type. This implies that other regulatory mechanisms are involved in the control of saeRS-fnbAB by fak.

fak regulates saeS activity by maintaining FMMs integrity

The mechanism by which fak regulates saeRS activity remains to be fully determined. Pathogens alter their cell membrane composition through the fak system to adapt to the host environment16,25. As SaeS is a membrane protein, it is likely influenced by changes in the cell membrane. FMMs, recently identified as crucial membrane structures, have been demonstrated to significantly influence the functionality of membrane-associated proteins26. A review of published FMM proteomic data27,28 revealed enrichment of SaeS in FMMs. Subsequent molecular dynamics simulations indicated potential binding between SaeS and FloA (scaffold protein of FMMs) within the cell membrane (Fig. S8A–D). This finding was confirmed in a bacterial two-hybrid (B2H) assay in which proteins were cloned as prey or bait in Escherichia coli (BTH101). The results revealed FloA–SaeS interactions but not with SaeP/Q (Fig. 5A–C). Furthermore, we performed a Co-IP experiment to verify the results in S. aureus. We first coexpressed USA300 with FloA-6 × HIS and SaeS-3 × FLAG. Then, FloA was precipitated using anti-HIS beads, and the precipitate was detected with an anti-FLAG antibody for SaeS. Metabolite screening reveals undecanoic acid as an inhibitor of the Fak system

Because fak activity is closely linked with eFA, it is hypothesized that eFA or its derivatives may affect the activity of the fak pathway. We tested 124 fatty acid metabolites and derivatives for their ability to inhibit aggregation (Supplementary Data 7), aiming to identify specific Fak–SaeRS inhibitors. Six metabolites significantly inhibited synovial aggregation (Fig. 6A–C), including itaconic acid (ITA) and its derivatives. Itaconic acid has been previously reported to alter carbon flow, reduce fatty acid synthesis, and inhibit bacterial growth at high concentrations ( > 10–30 mM)29,30, potentially accounting for its aggregation-inhibiting mechanism (Fig. S11). Among the other metabolites, linoleic acid (LA) is a known FASII inhibitor (FabI)31, and UDA has been reported to inhibit fatty acid synthesis in fungi32, although the exact mechanism remains unclear. Studies of 3b-hydroxy-5-cholenoic acid (HCA) have not been reported. S. aureus treated with LA and HCA inhibited growth, demonstrating antimicrobial effects (Fig. 6D). To investigate the effects of these candidate compounds on S. aureus fatty acid metabolism, we used C-13-labelled oleic acid and acetic acid to trace exogenous and endogenous fatty acid pathways, respectively. Itaconic acid and its analogues inhibited endogenous FA synthesis but slightly increased the activity of the exogenous pathway. LA and UDA inhibited the endogenous and exogenous pathways, respectively. HCA inhibited endogenous fatty acid synthesis only at high concentrations (Fig. 6F, G).

UDA was the only identified metabolite that inhibited eFA incorporation in this study and exhibited a favourable IC50 (IC50 = 19.5 µM; Fig. 6H). Given the structural similarity of UDA to fatty acids, which are natural substrates for FakB1 and FakB2, we hypothesized that these proteins are the binding targets of UDA. Computational docking of FakB1/2 with UDA or fatty acids revealed favourable binding energy between FakB1/B2 and UDA; MD simulation revealed that UDA forms a stable binding complex with FakB1. Additionally, the RMSF results revealed low values for key residues (FakB1: ILE94, LEU160, and PHE263; FakB2: ILE118, THR259, ILE263, and GLY273), which were also predicted by molecular docking (Fig. S12B–D). Moreover, we adopted surface plasmon resonance (SPR) to investigate the direct binding of UDA with FakB1/2 (Fig. 6I). UDA bound FakB1/2 with an affinity similar to those of its natural ligands (myristic acid-FakB1, oleic acid-FakB2).

To evaluate its efficacy against clinical isolates, we assessed the effects of UDA on fak activity, sae and fnbAB transcription, and synovial aggregation among high-aggregating clinical strains. The results revealed significant inhibition across all the strains tested, even after continuous treatment for 14 days (Fig. S13A–C), underscoring its broad applicability to clinical strains.

The results revealed that UDA enhanced the performance of AFN1252 under both eFA and SF conditions, whereas the efficiency of vancomycin and gentamycin was enhanced only in SF (Fig. 7A–C). However, when UDA was introduced, the frequency decreased by 4.68–32.5-fold (Fig. 7D–F). We further investigated the efficacy of UDA for in vivo application. The initial assessment of biocompatibility revealed no significant impairment in macrophage or fibroblast viability (Fig. S14A, B). Intra-articular injection in a murine model did not result in liver or kidney damage, as confirmed by both histological and biochemical analyses (Fig. S14C–F). In a murine model of septic arthritis (Fig. 7H), the bacterial load was lower in the vancomycin group than in the AFN1252 group at 2 weeks post-treatment. When UDA was coadministered with AFN1252, the treatment efficiency was enhanced, and the treatment efficiency was comparable to that of vancomycin. Accordingly, the combination of UDA with AFN1252 resulted in favourable bacterial clearance, alleviation of local inflammation, removal of bacterial clumps, and recovery of joint function (Figs. 7I, J, L and S15A–C). We also tested the efficacy of UDA in treating the high-aggregating clinical isolates; the results revealed a significant reduction in bacterial load (Fig. 7K). Taken together, these data demonstrated that UDA could enhance the efficacy of AFN1252 in treating septic arthritis.

Discussion

S. aureus aggregates in synovial fluid were historically termed “floating biofilms” or “biofilm-like aggregation”36. However, a growing body of literature8,37–39, along with the data from our study, demonstrates that these aggregates are fundamentally distinct from canonical surface-attached biofilms in terms of composition, structure, and regulatory mechanisms. First, mature aggregates are substantially larger (1–10 mm in diameter) than typical biofilms (approximately 100 μm thick), differing by orders of magnitude. Second, in contrast to the 4–6 h required for S. aureus aggregate maturation, typical biofilms necessitate overnight culture to mature. Crucially, the underlying formation mechanisms are distinct, as evidenced by the fact that weak biofilm-forming strains can be robust aggregators36. Our analysis of 152 clinical isolates confirmed this finding, revealing no significant correlation between biofilm-forming capacity and aggregation propensity. Based on data from clinical strains and results from mouse models, our study revealed that aggregation ability is a critical risk factor for joint infections; however, the mechanism controlling synovial aggregation is unclear.

Previous studies have explored the mechanisms of synovial aggregation. For example, Staats et al. demonstrated that a clfA/B and fnbA/B quadruple mutant abolished aggregation8. Additionally, using antibody-targeting methods, Knott et al. identified fibrinogen, albumin, and fibronectin as the primary components of aggregates40. However, these studies were largely hypothesis-driven and lacked a systematic method for studying aggregation regulators. In contrast, we employed a directed evolution assay to dissect the regulators of synovial aggregation comprehensively. Among the 33 generated nonaggregating mutants, all the nucleotide mutations reside in either fak or sae, confirming that the fak–sae axis is a critical regulator of synovial aggregation. Other mutations that occurred in both the evolution and control groups may confer bacterial survival adaptation within the synovial fluid environment.

In their previous transposon mutant library screening work, Dastgheyb et al. identified transposon mutants (fnbA, fnbB, clfA, clfB, and rsbU) that exhibited reduced aggregation7. Our findings indicate that only the fnbAB double mutant was nonaggregating. This discrepancy may have arisen from the use of different mutant strains in the experiments. In their study, transposon mutants were employed. Because fnbA and fnbB are located nearby on the chromosome, transposon insertion in fnbA could affect the transcription of fnbB, or vice versa. Further confirmation with in-frame knockout strains is necessary. Additionally, other genes (clfA/B and rsbU) were not identified in our study because the evolution-based method can detect only genes with high-frequency mutations. These findings further support that the fak and sae loci undergo high-frequency mutations in nature, explaining the clinical variability in aggregation. Although rsbU was not identified in our study, we strongly support the notion that rsbU is another regulator of aggregation because it controls the synthesis of FMM lipids through the crtPQMN operon41. Moreover, the S. aureus strain RN4220, carrying mutations in rsbU and fakB1, exhibited a nonaggregating phenotype. We speculate that both mutations contribute to this phenotype, and further study is currently being conducted to verify this hypothesis.

FMMs, as crucial membrane structures, have been demonstrated to be crucial for S. aureus pathogenesis26. Many FMM inhibitors have been shown to attenuate S. aureus virulence and increase antibiotic sensitivity27,28,42. Although we found that fak regulates saeRS through FMMs, the anti-FMM compounds were only effective at high concentrations (500 μM). This was possibly caused by the physicochemical nature (e.g., extremely high viscosity) of the synovial fluid, lowering its bioavailability. Therefore, a compound with amphipathicity should be suitable for SF administration.

We selected fak as a therapeutic target for two reasons. First, the natural ligands of fatty acids are typically amphipathic and thus suitable for application in synovial fluid. Second, the fak system is also involved in the mechanism through which S. aureus evades anti-FASII antibiotic treatment. Therefore, inhibiting fak could prevent aggregation as well as the development of anti-FASII resistance. UDA was demonstrated to be an effective inhibitor of the fak pathway. Moreover, in a study systemically evaluating the biology of free fatty acids, UDA was demonstrated to have no lipotoxicity, low effects on ER stress, and few effects on cell viability for different types of cells (beta cells, microglia, and epithelial cells)43. Our data showed that the UDA levels in human synovial fluid were relatively low both in uninfected and infected states. All this information demonstrated that UDA supplementation is an effective and safe therapeutic strategy.

Despite the importance of fak–saeRS–fnbAB in controlling synovial aggregation, the mechanisms need to be elucidated in a more detailed manner. Although fnbAB knockout eliminates aggregation, other molecules downstream of saeRS (e.g., efb, ecb, sbi, spa) may also contribute to this process, although not necessarily. In addition, proteomic data for aggregates revealed that immunoglobulins are the primary components of the aggregates and are likely enriched by the immunoglobulin-binding adhesion molecules sbi and spa44. This raises the question of whether immunoglobulins contribute to the acceleration of synovial aggregation, a matter that requires further investigation.

Targeting the S. aureus fak system by UDA offers a promising therapeutic strategy for treating septic arthritis, particularly for combating antibiotic resistance (Fig. S17). UDA coadministered with traditional antibiotics (vancomycin and gentamicin) addresses limitations in treating joint infections; however, UDA administration with AFN1252 has broader implications. By countering fatty acid-mediated resistance, this combination could expand the utility of AFN1252 across diverse infections, resulting in greater translational impact. Future research should elucidate the precise mechanisms of UDA activity. In conclusion, our study revealed that the fak–sae axis is a key therapeutic target for septic arthritis and that UDA is an effective inhibitor of the fak pathway.

Methods

Study approval

All experiments with C57BL/6 mice were performed according to protocols approved by the Animal Care and Experiment Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital and following standard protocols (approval no: DWLL2025-0007). The collection of human specimens/information in this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital (approval no. 2024-KY-256K). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The clinical data for this study were systematically gathered through a previously described patient cohort, including patients with musculoskeletal infection, from January 2015 to January 202045. The database contained records of 235 patients who were diagnosed with musculoskeletal infections from whom S. aureus was isolated. We excluded repeated isolates from the same patient and isolates from patients with culture-positive polymicrobial infections, focusing on 152 unique patient cases with complete clinical profiles and follow-up data. The study population was stratified into those with joint involvement (n = 41) and those without (n = 111).

Bacterial strains, plasmids, and growth conditions

The USA300-ΔfnbAB, ΔfnbA, ΔfnbB were graciously provided by Professor Binh An Diep from the University of California, San Francisco, USA. The USA300-ΔsaeRS and USA300-ΔfakA strains were constructed in this study. Additional strains utilized were either maintained in our laboratory or obtained from clinical sources.

The pRMC2 plasmid, a generous gift from Tim Foster (Addgene #68940), was employed as the backbone for gene overexpression in S. aureus. The genes were regulated by a tetracycline-inducible promoter. Sequences of the target genes for overexpression were chemically synthesized and inserted downstream of the tetracycline-inducible promoter using the seamless cloning method. Vectors for the B2H assay were acquired from Euromedex (Cat. EUK001), genes of interest were chemically synthesized and inserted into the MCS site using the seamless cloning method. The pRN11 plasmid (Addgene #84455) was employed as a backbone for the construction of the SplitmCherry reporter system. The pKOR1 plasmid (Addgene #133446) was utilized for the construction of gene in-frame knockouts. The pCM29 plasmid46 was employed for green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression in S. aureus. Strains harboring the respective plasmids were maintained in TSB/Tryptic Soy Broth Agar (TSA) supplemented with 10 µg/mL of chloramphenicol. Before each experiment, strains were cultured on TSA plates overnight. Subsequently, a single colony was inoculated into TSB and incubated at 37 °C. Detailed information for plasmids and bacterial strains involved in this study is provided in Supplementary Data 8.

Construction of fakA/saeRS mutants

In-frame deletions of the fakA/saeRS genes in the USA300 strain of S. aureus were achieved through allelic replacement using the plasmid pKOR1. The 1000 bp upstream and downstream sequences flanking fakA or saeRS were subsequently cloned and inserted into pKOR1 using the Gibson assembly method, resulting in the generation of recombinant plasmids (pKOR1-ΔfakA and pKOR1-ΔsaeRS), which were subsequently transformed into USA300 via electroporation. Transformants were selected and maintained on medium supplemented with chloramphenicol (10 µg/mL). Mutants were constructed using temperature-sensitive allele replacement45. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into S. aureus USA300 using bacteriophage 11. S. aureus USA300 transformants were then subjected to in-frame allele replacement by growing in temperature shifting between 30 °C and 42 °C. The candidate mutant strains were selected using the counterselection method (sensitive to chloramphenicol) and then confirmed by Sanger sequencing to validate the desired in-frame deletions.

Biofilm formation and quantification

A suspension of S. aureus (approximately 106 CFU/mL) was inoculated into a 96-well plate containing biofilm medium and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. For proteinaceous biofilms, bacteria were grown in TSB supplemented with 1% glucose (w/v, TSBG); for polysaccharide biofilms, bacteria were grown in TSB supplemented with 4% NaCl (w/v, TSBN). The plate was gently washed with PBS to remove nonadherent bacteria, after which the biofilms were left behind for subsequent analysis. Biofilm formation was quantified using crystal violet staining. First, the wells were washed three times with 200 µL of PBS. Then, the plate was fixed with methanol, air-dried, and stained with 200 µL of 1.5% crystal violet. The crystal violet bound to the wells was dissolved in 200 µL of 33% acetic acid, and 100 µL aliquots from each well were transferred to a new flat-bottom 96-well plate. The optical absorbance at 590 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., United States) to determine the biofilm biomass.

Aggregation assay

All of the human synovial fluid was derived from patients with osteoarthritis knees who received lubrication injections or prosthesis replacement surgery in our hospital. Patients with gout arthritis, joint infections, rheumatoid arthritis, and Charcot’s neuroarthropathy were excluded because the overrepresentation of immunoglobulin and uric acid crystals might affect aggregation and measurement. The samples were pooled together, filtered through a 0.45 µm filter sterilizer before the aggregation assay.

S. aureus was grown in TSB at 37 °C, 150 rpm for 6 h to reach the exponential stage, then centrifuged at 3500 × g for 5 min and washed with an equal volume of PBS. Bacteria were stained with 1 µM SYTOX Green (Invitrogen, United States) for 10 min and washed again with PBS. A 200-µL bacterial suspension was mixed with 1.8 mL of 20% synovial fluid in a 12-well plate and incubated at 37 °C, 150 rpm for 8 h or the indicated time. Aggregation ability was quantified using a modified method47: 100 µL of planktonic cells were sampled to quantify the fluorescence, aggregates were gently resuspended, and total fluorescence was measured. Relative aggregation was calculated as total fluorescence divided by planktonic fluorescence (As illustrated in Fig. S15).

Directed evolution for nonaggregation mutants

To develop an aggregation-deficient mutant strain of S. aureus, overnight cultures of bacterial suspension were washed and resuspended in an equal volume of sterile PBS. A 200-µL aliquot of this bacterial suspension was mixed with 1.8 mL of 20% human synovial fluid in a 12-well plate and incubated for 8 h at 37 °C and 150 rpm. Following incubation, the unaggregated planktonic cells were carefully collected and transferred to fresh TSB (5 mL) overnight growth. The expanded culture was then reintroduced into 20% human synovial fluid to initiate another cycle of aggregation selection. This process was repeated until the evolved bacterial population no longer formed visible aggregates. The evolved population was spread onto sheep blood agar to isolate single colonies, which were cryopreserved and subjected to whole-genome sequencing to characterize the genetic changes associated with the evolved phenotype. Concurrently, a portion of the aggregated cells from each evolution passage was also expanded and cryopreserved to serve as a control group for comparative analysis.

Genomic DNA extraction, whole-genome sequencing, and comparative genomic analysis

Bacteria were collected, and the cell walls were physically disrupted with 0.2 mm silicon beads using a tissue lyser. S. aureus genomic DNA was extracted using a Mag-MK Bacterial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis at 200 V for 30 min, and the DNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 2000.

The draft genome of the S. aureus strain was assembled using the SPAdes assembler (v3.15.5)48 with default settings. Genome annotation was performed using Prokka (v1.14.5)49, a prokaryotic annotation program. Comparative genomic analysis was conducted with breseq (v0.38.0)50, employing default settings to identify variations and compare the evolved strain with relevant reference genomes. SNPs and large fragment deletions unique to the nonaggregation groups were considered mutations responsible for the loss of aggregation.

Immune cell killing susceptibility assays

Isolation of human PMN cells

Peripheral blood was collected from healthy donors with informed consent at Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Fresh peripheral blood from healthy donors was collected using EDTA as an anticoagulant. A 5-mL blood sample was carefully layered over 5 mL of Polymorphprep (Serumwerk Bernburg, Germany) in 15-mL tubes and centrifuged at 550 × g for 30 min to obtain a polymorphonuclear leukocyte (PMN)-rich cell band. The PMNs were then transferred into RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. The remaining red blood cells were lysed using a cell lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich), followed by centrifugation at 500 × g for 10 min. The purified PMNs were resuspended in serum-free RPMI 1640 and incubated on a flat tissue culture dish.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 cells (Catalog: SCSP-5036) were obtained from the Cell Bank of Shanghai Institutes of Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. RAW264.7 cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco), and human PNM were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute Medium 1640 (RPMI 1640, Gibco). Both media were free of fetal bovine serum to avoid bacterial aggregation, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in an atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Bacteria-immune cell co-culture

RAW264.7 cells and PMN cells were seeded in plates and cultured overnight before being co-cultured with different groups of S. aureus for 2 h (Multiplicity of Infection [MOI] = 10 for PMN and 100 for RAW264.7). Subsequently, cells were permeabilized using 0.1% Triton ×-100 to release intracellular bacteria. Finally, the total number of viable bacteria was determined using the traditional CFU counting method.

Antibiotic susceptibility test

Each strain was routinely assessed for its minimum inhibitory concentration towards vancomycin and gentamycin to ensure inherent susceptibility to these antibiotics. Following the aggregation procedure described above, S. aureus was treated with antibiotics at a concentration of 5 μg/mL. After incubation with antibiotics for 4 h, the bacteria were resuspended to disrupt the aggregates and were subsequently washed twice with PBS to eliminate antibiotics. The number of surviving bacteria was determined using the CFU counting method.

Anti-FASII antibiotic-resistant generation

To prepare TSB-eFA and TSB-SF. TSB with 5% fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin(FA-free-BSA) was supplemented with 500 µM eFA (167 µM for each component: oleic acid, palmitic acid, myristic acid) or 20%(w/w) synovial fluids. The corresponding TSA plates were prepared by supplementing 1.5% agar. For developing resistance in broth, USA300 wildtype strains were grown overnight at 37 °C in TSB, TSB-eFA, and TSB-SF for adaptation. Then diluted with the same volume of fresh medium. Triclosan (0.5 µg/mL) or AFN-1252 (5 ng/mL) was added with/without a combination of UDA (100 µM). Incubating at 37 °C/150 rpm for 6 h. Sample 100 µL of the bacteria suspension and calculate the whole bacteria count with the serial dilution method. Then, sample another 100 µL bacteria suspension, spread on plates (TSA, TSA-eFA, TSA-SF) containing the corresponding concentration of Triclosan (0.5 µg/mL), or AFN-1252 (5 ng/mL). Plates were incubated at 37 °C, and colonies were counted after 48 h. The resistance frequencies were calculated by dividing the resistant colonies number to the total bacterial counts.

For developing resistance on the plate, USA300 wildtype strains were grown overnight in TSB, TSB-eFA, and TSB-SF. A 100-µL part of bacterial suspension was plated on corresponding plates(TSA, TSA-eFA, TSA-SF) to obtain a lawn. 1-cm diameter diffusion disc with Triclosan (10 µg/5 µL), AFN-1252 (1 µg/5 µL) were placed on center of the plates. Plates were incubated at 37 °C and photographed after 48 h.

Macrophage phagocytosis assay

The macrophage line RAW264.7 was inoculated into a 6-well plate (approximately 1 × 106 cells/well) and allowed to grow overnight to achieve confluence. S. aureus labelled with GFP or stained with SYTOX green (1 μM, 10 min), either in an aggregated or planktonic state, was subsequently added to the wells and cocultured with RAW264.7 cells for 2 h for bacterial phagocytosis. The MOI was controlled at 100:1. Following the incubation period, the cells were fixed using a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 1 h. Each well was then stained with PE-Phalloidin for 30 min to visualize the cytoskeleton and with DAPI for 10 min to stain cellular nuclei. After staining, the samples were washed with fresh PBS to remove any unbound stain. Finally, the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, DMi8) to assess the extent of bacterial uptake by the macrophages. The rate of phagocytosis was quantified with ImageJ for each representative microscope field.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and Coomassie blue staining for aggregates and synovial fluids

First, the aggregates were collected by removing the supernatant and gently washing with sterile PBS to remove the synovial fluid components attached to their surface. Then, the aggregates were frozen with liquid nitrogen, and the resulting frozen powder was transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube.

The aggregate powder or synovial fluid was dissolved in PBS. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Samples were prepared by diluting them to a final concentration of 5 μg/10 μL. Loading buffer was added to the protein samples, which were then heated at 95 °C for 10 min and placed on ice. For electrophoretic separation, equal amounts of protein (5 μg per lane) were loaded onto a sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel for electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and run at a constant voltage to separate proteins based on their molecular weight. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained with Coomassie blue using a staining kit (Sangon, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 0.5–1 h, followed by decolorization according to the manufacturer’s instructions for 4 h.

LC-MS/MS and proteomics for aggregate protein

Proteins were extracted as described above. A total of 4 biological replicate samples for synovial aggregates were analyzed. For digestion, an equal amount of protein from each sample was reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol at 55 °C for 30 min and alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 15 min. Proteins were then precipitated using 6× volumes of acetone at −20 °C for 4 h. After centrifugation at 8000 × g for 10 min, the pellet was air-dried and reconstituted in 50 mM NH₄HCO₃. Digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C with 20 μg/mL trypsin (TPCK-treated). The reaction was quenched by acidification. The resulting peptides were desalted and purified using a SOLA™ SPE 96-well plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Prior to LC-MS/MS injection, an iRT kit standard (Biognosys) was spiked into each peptide sample at a 1:20 (v/v) ratio to serve as an internal reference. Chromatographic separation was performed on a reversed-phase C18 analytical column (15 cm × 75 μm ID, 1.6 μm particles, IonOpticks) at a constant flow rate of 400 nL/min. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (buffer A) and 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile (buffer B). The peptide separation was achieved using the following linear gradient: 5% to 22% B over 20 min, 22% to 37% B over 4 min, followed by a rapid increase to 80% B in 3 min and holding at 80% B for 3 min to wash the column. Finally, the column was re-equilibrated to 2% B for 5 min. The total run time was 35 min. The eluted peptides were analyzed using a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) operating in DIA mode with parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation. The source parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage, 1.4 kV; dry gas flow, 3.0 L/min; and dry temperature, 180 °C. MS1 surveys were performed over a mass range of 100–1700 m/z. For DIA-PASEF acquisition, ion mobility (1/K0) windows were set from 0.7 to 1.3 Vs cm⁻², with collision energies ramping from 20 to 59 eV across the mobility range.

The acquired DIA raw data were processed and analyzed using Spectronaut Pulsar version 18.4 (Biognosys). A direct DIA library-free search strategy was employed against the uniprot-Homo sapiens-9606-2024.2.1.fasta file. The key search parameters were configured as follows: trypsin/P was specified as the enzyme with up to two missed cleavages allowed. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while oxidation of methionine and N-terminal acetylation were set as variable modifications. For protein identification and quantification, the false discovery rate was controlled at both the precursor and protein levels using a Q-value cutoff of 0.01. Protein quantification was derived from the MS2-level signal intensities of the fragment ions.

RNA extraction for qPCR and RNA-seq

S. aureus RNA extraction

S. aureus cells at different treatments were pelleted by centrifugation at 3500 × g for 10 min. The bacterial cell walls were then mechanically disrupted using a tissue lyser and 0.2 mm silicon beads (MatrixB, MP biomedicals). Total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol for the EZ-press RNA Purification Kit (EZBioscience, China). The integrity and quality of the RNA were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA).

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Following the elimination of genomic DNA (gDNA) by the addition of DNase, 1 μg of purified RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA utilizing an RT-PCR kit (EZBioscience, China). The cDNA was then diluted with nuclease-free water (ddH2O) at a ratio of 1:5 and employed as the template for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), which was carried out using a 2× SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (EZBioscience, China). The thermal cycling for DNA amplification comprised an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s, all conducted on a Roche LightCycler 480 system (Roche, Switzerland). Detailed primer sequences in this study are delineated in Supplementary Table S1. The relative quantification of gene expression levels was accomplished using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with the expression of gyrB serving as an endogenous control for normalization.

RNA-seq and bioinformatic analysis for bacterial samples grown in vitro

For each in vitro cultured bacteria sample, after the removal of ribosomal RNA, 10 micrograms of RNA were segmented into 200 to 250-base pair fragments and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with the application of random hexamers. The resulting cDNA libraries underwent sequencing on the HiSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, USA). Raw sequencing data were aligned to a reference genome using the HISAT2 aligner, and the read counts for each gene were determined with the FeatureCounts tool. The gene expression matrix generated was then analyzed and visualized in R, utilizing ggplot2 and DESeq2 for graphical representation and statistical analysis. The raw sequence data were deposited in the NCIB-SRA database under Bioproject ID PRJNA1209395 and PRJNA1209687.

Bacterial two-hybrid (B2H) assay

A B2H assay was used to assess the interactions among the proteins FloA, SaeP, SaeQ, and SaeS. The coding sequences of floA, saeP, saeQ, and saeS were subsequently cloned and inserted into the B2H expression vectors pKT25 and pUT18C at the site between KpnI and EcoRI, yielding N-terminal fusions with the catalytic domains T25 and T18 of the Bordetella pertussis adenylate cyclase. These constructs enabled the generation of pairwise plasmid combinations, which were subsequently cotransformed into the E. coli BTH101 strain. This strain harbours a lacZ gene under the control of a cAMP-inducible promoter, facilitating the detection of protein–protein interactions via promoter activation. Interacting proteins bring the T25 and T18 catalytic domains into proximity, thereby reconstituting an active adenylate cyclase enzyme. This results in elevated cAMP levels, which in turn induce the expression of the lacZ reporter gene. The transformed E. coli cells were incubated under conditions conducive to interaction (48 h at 30 °C). pKT25-zip and pUT18C-zip were cotransferred into BTH101 as positive controls.

To assess β-galactosidase activity quantitatively, the transformants were cultured in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 µg/ml), kanamycin (50 µg/ml), and 0.5 mM IPTG at 30 °C for 48 h. The optical density at 600 nm was measured prior to cell permeabilization. Then, o-nitrophenyl-β-d-galactopyranoside (ONPG, 4 mg/ml) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 10 min at 30 °C before termination with 1 M Na2CO3. The absorbance readings were recorded at 420 nm and 550 nm. The results are expressed in Miller units using the formula 1000 × ((OD420 − 1.75 × OD550)/(t × v × OD600)), where t = reaction time and v = reaction volume42.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

The bacteria were physically lysed with silica beads and resuspended in IP buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktails, and the total protein concentration was determined with a BCA assay kit. The lysate supernatant was incubated with anti-His magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0209) overnight at 4 °C, after which the beads were magnetically separated. Then, the immunocomplexes were washed 3 times with IP lysis buffer, and the samples were boiled in SDS–PAGE loading buffer and subjected to subsequent immunoblotting analysis. For the immunoblotting procedure, 20 µg of total protein was separated by electrophoresis on a 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel (80 V, 1 h). Proteins were transferred from the gel to a PVDF membrane with a pore size of 0.45 µm (400 mA for 30 min). The PVDF membrane was then blocked with skim milk for 1 h and incubated with anti-FLAG (1:10000, cat: 66008-4-Ig, Proteintech, China) or anti-HIS (1:5000, cat: 10001-0-AP, Proteintech, China) antibodies to detect the presence of SaeS-3 × FLAG and FloA-6 × HIS. After the membrane was incubated with the anti-mouse IgG-HRP secondary antibody (1:5000, cat: SA00001-1, Proteintech, China), proteins were detected using a chemiluminescent substrate kit (Epizyme, China). Images were recorded with an illumination system 5200 (Tanon, China). The full scan images are available in the Source Data file.

Construction of plasmids for gene expression and splitmCherry reporter

Gene fragments were chemically synthesized and inserted into the pRMC2 vector at the EcoRI site. The recombinant plasmids were verified by restriction enzyme digestion and Sanger sequencing of the cloning site. Detailed information concerning the overexpression plasmids is provided in Supplementary Data 8. The sequences were deposited in the Mendeley database with the accession numbers listed in Supplementary Data 8.

For the construction of splitmCherry, a gram-positive, codon-modified sequence was acquired from pRN11 plasmids. The sequences were first designed in silico and chemically synthesized. For the in silico design of sequences, mCherry was split between 159D and 160 G. The N-terminus was ligated with FloA, and mCherry was ligated with SaeS at its C- or N-terminus as a negative control. The generated sequences were inserted into pRN11 to replace the original mCherry sequence. Complete plasmid sequences were deposited in the Mendeley database with the accession numbers provided in Supplementary Data 8.

High-resolution CLSM and quantification

S. aureus strains carrying the pSplitmCherry plasmid were cultured overnight in TSB at 37 °C. These strains were then diluted 1:1000 into either TSB or 20% synovial fluid and incubated at 37 °C and 150 rpm for 4–6 h. After incubation, the bacterial cultures were washed twice with PBS to remove any residual medium. Then, the cells were stained with SYTOX green for visualization of the bacterial cytosol. Subsequently, 5 µL of the washed bacterial suspension was applied to a glass slide for imaging. Fluorescence microscopy images were captured using a Leica STELLARIS-5 confocal microscope. The fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ 7.0.

Tracing fatty acid metabolism with isotope labeling methods

S. aureus in the exponential growth phase was diluted 1:1 with fresh TSB. C13-labeled oleic acid or acetate was added to the medium to achieve a final concentration of 25 μg/mL for 13Coleic acid and 250 μg/mL for 13Cacetate. After a 2-h incubation along with specified treatments, S. aureus was centrifuged and washed three times with deionized water to remove C13-labeled compounds. The supernatant was discarded by centrifugation, sonicated in an ice bath for 30 min. A 2 mL aliquot of chilled extraction solution (1:2 chloroform/methanol, v/v) was added and vortexed for 5 min. An additional 0.5 mL of water and chloroform were added to induce phase separation, followed by vortexing for 1 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min, after which the lower layer containing the lipids was collected for subsequent C13 quantification using Liquid Chromatography-Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometry (LC-IRMS, MAT-253, Thermo). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a reversed-phase C18 column. The mobile phase was comprised of two carbon-free eluents: (A) ultrapure water and (B) HPLC-grade methanol. A linear gradient was applied at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min as follows: 0–5 min, 80% B; 5–30 min, 80% to 100% B; 30–35 min, hold at 100% B; 35–36 min, 100% to 80% B; followed by a 14-min re-equilibration at 80% B. The effluent was directed into the LC Isolink Interface for detecting the C13/C12 ratio (Rsq). The δ13 CVPDB value is calculated to express the relative difference in the isotope ratio 13C/12 C between the sample and an international reference standard (Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite, VPDB) using the formula δ(‰) = (Rsq/RVPDB-1) × 1000.

Septic arthritis mice model

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital (approval no: DWLL2025-0007). The replicates for each experiment group are detailed in the corresponding figure legends. Mice were housed at a density of five per cage under standard lighting conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and maintained at an appropriate temperature. This study does not involve the effect of sex hormones on S. aureus infection, and animal gender does not affect the experimental results. Male mice were specifically chosen for this study based on their characteristically higher levels of spontaneous activity and locomotor activity, which helps assessment of joint function during gait analysis. Adult male C57BL/6 mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were injected intra-articularly in both knees with the corresponding type of S. aureus suspended in sterile saline at a concentration of approximately 106 CFU/10 μL. Before inoculum, S. aureus single colonies were inoculated into TSB and grown overnight with shaking. Cultures were then sub-cultured (1:100) into fresh TSB and grown for 6 h with shaking (150 rpm) to mid-log phase. These homogeneous cultures were serially diluted and plated for precise CFU enumeration. Aliquots based on these counts were prepared and frozen. On the day of infection, frozen aliquots were thawed and diluted in PBS to achieve the desired, normalized inoculum concentration for all groups, ensuring that any animal received an identical and accurately quantified number of bacteria.

For the models assessing the effects of UDA, AFN1252, and vancomycin treatments, AFN1252 (5 mg/kg) and vancomycin (100 mg/kg) were administered intraperitoneally on days 1, 3, and 10 after the appearance of infection signs (typically 2 days post-infection). UDA (200 µM) was administered with local injection at a concentration of 500 μM, 20 μL, with saline as the control. On Day 14, all mice were anesthetized and euthanized by cervical dislocation. The total knee joints were harvested and homogenized in 5 mL of sterile saline for subsequent bacterial burden quantification. The homogenized suspension was serially diluted by tenfold. A total of 100 μL of the diluted suspension was spread onto sheep blood agar plates. After incubation at 37 °C overnight, CFU counts were performed according to the Chinese National Standard GB/T4789.2 protocol. Bacterial loads were normalized to tissue weight and presented on a log10 scale.

Gait analysis experiment

A gait analysis experiment was conducted at 14 days post-treatment. The mice were placed on the runway of the gait analysis device (VisuGait-XR-FP101, Shanghai, China) and allowed to run from start to end. The average pressure on the runway and foot contact with the ground from every footprint were quantified, and the mean values were calculated for each knee using VisuGait software.

Histopathology and tissue immunofluorescence

Mice were euthanized at specified intervals post-infection, and their lower limbs, including the knee joints, were collected. The specimens were fixed in a 10% formalin solution for 24 h prior to decalcification. Subsequently, the samples were embedded in paraffin wax. The paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned and prepared for histological examination using Masson staining.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining was conducted to assess S. aureus aggregation in vivo. Tissue sections underwent deparaffinization and then rehydration through a series of ethanol washes (95%, 80%, 70%), and sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer using microwave treatment and endogenous peroxidase blocking with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% goat serum at 20 °C for 10 min. Sections were then incubated with the anti-S. aureus primary antibody (ab20920, Abcam, USA) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibody, with fluorescent dye conjugation according to the manufacturer’s instructions (abs50012, Absin, Shanghai, China). Once all procedures were completed, slides were washed in PBST, counterstained with DAPI, and mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching sealing medium. The stained tissue sections were examined under a Leica DMI8 microscope (Leica, Germany). All sections were imaged using either an optical microscope or a DMI6 microscope.

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

FakA/B1/B2 and SaeP/Q/R/S protein sequences from 500 S. aureus genomes were obtained from the PanX database51. The R packages ggmsa and ggtree52 were used to visualize the results of multisequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction.

Computational modulation of protein–protein interactions

AlphaFold3 was used to construct protein–protein interaction models53. The model with the highest ranking score was selected as the best binding model. The HawkDOCK SERVER54 was utilized to evaluate the binding free energy of the best binding model and to rank the free binding energies of amino acid residue sites to understand their contribution to the binding interaction. Finally, PyMOL 2.4 software was used to visualize the sites with the greatest contributions to the binding of the two proteins.

Computational modulation of protein–ligand docking

We utilized AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 software to perform molecular docking of myristic acid (PubChem CID: 11005), oleic acid (PubChem CID: 445639), palmitic acid (PubChem CID: 985), and UA (PubChem CID: 8180) with the proteins FakB1 (PDB ID: 6ALW) and FakB2 (PDB ID: 4X9X)55. For FakA docking with ADP, the structure of FakA was predicted with AlphaFold3, and AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 software was subsequently used to perform molecular docking. Protein preprocessing, including the removal of water molecules and extraneous ligands and the addition of hydrogen atoms, was accomplished using PyMOL 2.4. ChemDraw 20.0 was employed for the energy minimization of the compounds. AutoDock Tools 1.5.6 was used to generate PDBQT files for the docking simulations. The docking box was set to encompass the entire protein structure, with all other parameters retaining their default values. The docking results were configured to output nine binding poses. The pose with the lowest binding energy and the highest clustering frequency was considered the most likely binding mode between the ligand and the protein. Finally, the docking outcomes were visualized using PyMOL 2.4 and Discovery Studio 2019 software.

Molecular dynamic modulation for protein interaction

Proteins and DPPG lipid molecules were coarse-grained, and their force fields were constructed using the CHARMM-GUI. The coarse-graining of STX molecules was performed using the parameters available at (https://github.com/cgkmw-durham/cg_param_m3), with the full-atom to coarse-grained correspondence depicted in Supplementary Fig. S7. Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2021, employing the MARTINI 3.0 force field. The system was built using PACKMOL, where DPPG and STX were incorporated in an 8:2 ratio to form the membrane, followed by the placement of four FloA proteins and four SaeS proteins within the membrane as required. Water was added, and the system was neutralized by adding Na and Cl ions to balance the overall charge. The simulations were carried out at a temperature of 298.15 K and a pressure of 1 bar. Prior to the run, energy minimization, NVT equilibration, and NPT equilibration were performed to relax the system, followed by a 1000 ns simulation. The production simulation employed the leapfrog algorithm for integrating Newton’s equations of motion with a 20-fs time step. The V-rescale method was used for temperature coupling, and the Parrinello-Rahman method for pressure coupling. The Verlet method was used for neighbor searching, with a 1.1 nm cutoff for Coulomb and van der Waals interactions, and long-range electrostatics were treated using the reaction-field method. Long-range dispersion corrections were applied to the energy and pressure calculations.

Molecular Dynamics modulation for UDA binding with FakB1/2

For molecular dynamics simulations of UDA binding with FakB1/2, the pose with the highest docking score for each ligand–protein complex was selected from the docking results, and the ligand–bound protein systems were constructed in a 150 mM NaCl aqueous solution. To investigate the stability of the docked ligand–protein poses, 100 ns simulations were performed. All simulations used the program GROMACS 2021. The amber99sb force field was used for the protein, the GAFF2 force field was used for the ligand, and the tip3p model was used for water. The simulation trajectories were analysed for structural fluctuations using root-mean-square deviation and root-mean-square fluctuation calculations.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

The SPR technique was used to identify the interaction affinities between the recombinant FakB1/B2 protein and fatty acid compounds (UDA and myristic acid/oleic acid). The recombinant FakB1/B2 was directly coupled to the CM5 sensor chip (BR-1005-30, Cytiva) for determination. Following incubation, a gradient of fatty acids was introduced into the FakB1 (or FakB2) protein–CM5 system. Binding interactions were analysed using BIAcore T200 evaluation software (v.2.0; GE Healthcare, USA).

Dissecting SaeR-regulated genes from transcriptome datasets with a machine learning method

Genes regulated by SaeR were dissected according to the workflow developed in our previously published research23. Briefly, a total of 506 quality-controlled S. aureus RNA-seq datasets were obtained from publicly available databases. The gene expression data were comprehensively analysed using the independent component analysis algorithm; therefore, genes were divided into several independently regulated gene sets according to their expression mode. Within each independently regulated gene set, gene members were assigned a weight value indicating their association with the gene set. Genes with a weight value above the threshold were determined to have a strong relationship with the gene set and were therefore regarded as being regulated by the regulator of that gene set.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed with R (v.4.3.3). Specific details of the individual tests and replicate values are provided in the figure legends. Continuous data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. For two-group comparisons, normally distributed data were analysed using an unpaired t test, and nonnormally distributed data were analysed with the Mann–Whitney U test. For multigroup comparisons, normally distributed data were analysed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons and with Dunnett’s post hoc test versus control values. A significance threshold of P = < 0.05 was applied in all the cases. Detailed statistical report for each graph was provided in the Source Data file.
