Introduction

The strong coupling constant of QCD,
\alpha_{s}
, is a fundamental parameter of the Standard Model (SM) that governs the dynamics of the strong interaction over all energy scales
[
1
,
2
,
3
]

\alpha_{s}
plays a crucial role in a wide range of precision observables, and is therefore essential for accurate theoretical predictions at lepton and hadron colliders, as well as in other important contexts such as Higgs decays,
B
-physics, and quarkonium phenomenology. For example, it directly affects the extraction of the top-quark mass, width, and Yukawa couplings
[
4
]
, and it induces a dominant
2\!-\!4\%
theoretical uncertainty in key Higgs production channels
[
5
]
. Furthermore,
\alpha_{s}
constitutes the leading source of uncertainty in the prediction of hadronic
Z
-boson decay widths
[

\alpha_{s}(m_{Z}^{2})=0.1179(9)

0.8\%

[

The non-strange hadronic

\alpha_{s}

[

8

[
9
]
-
[

from the non-strange hadronic

The contributions of higher-dimensional operators in the OPE to the hadronic

The strong coupling determination from the hadronic tau decays is marred by a dominant theoretical uncertainty arising in the treatment of the perturbative series. The hadronic
\tau

This formulation requires a prescription for setting the renormalization scale. Two commonly used approaches are fixed-order perturbation theory (FOPT), which sets
\mu^{2}=|s|=M_{\tau}^{2}

38

]
-
[

A resolution of this discrepancy is limited by the absence of explicit higher-order perturbative coefficients. This motivates the use of various resummation and convergence-improvement techniques, for instance, Borel summation, renormalon-inspired models, conformal mappings, Euler-type transformations, renormalization-group-improved expansions, and Levin-type sequence transformations

In view of the FOPT–CIPT discrepancy and the anticipated future precision in the determination of the strong coupling at the level of

\tau

\tau

The Shanks transformation is one of the most useful transformations available to date

97

It is a classical nonlinear sequence transformation designed to accelerate the convergence of slowly convergent or even divergent series by systematically eliminating the leading asymptotic error terms. \varepsilon

\varepsilon

2
The hadronic decay of the
\tau
lepton

From an experimental point of view, the hadronic decay width can be
resolved into vector (
V
), axial-vector (
A
), and strange (
S
)
components, corresponding to final states induced by the non-strange

\bar{u}d
and strange
\bar{u}s
charged weak currents. Dividing these
partial widths by the purely leptonic decay width

\Gamma(\tau\to\nu_{\tau}e^{-}\bar{\nu}_{e})
defines the dimensionless
observables
R_{\tau,V}
,
R_{\tau,A}
, and
R_{\tau,S}
.

The most reliable extraction of
\alpha_{s}
is obtained from the
non-strange channel, since effects from light-quark masses are strongly
suppressed. We therefore restrict the following analysis to the vector
and axial-vector contributions.

The inclusive non-strange hadronic decay rate is given by

R_{\tau;V/A}=\frac{\Gamma(\tau\to\nu_{\tau}+\mathrm{hadrons}_{V/A})}{\Gamma(\tau\to\nu_{\tau}e^{-}\bar{\nu}_{e})}=\frac{N_{C}}{2}\,|V_{ud}|^{2}\,S_{EW}\left[1+\delta^{(0)}+\delta_{EW}^{\prime}+\delta^{(2,m_{q})}_{ud,V/A}+\sum_{D\geq 4}\delta^{(D)}\right],

(2.1)

where
S_{EW}=1.01907\pm 0.0003

[
39
]
accounts for electroweak radiative
effects
[
99
,
100
]
, and
\delta_{EW}^{\prime}=0.0010

[
101
]
denotes residual
non-logarithmic electroweak corrections. The dominant contribution to
the decay rate arises from the perturbative QCD correction

\delta^{(0)}
, which represents roughly
20\%
of the total. The quark-mass term
\delta^{(2,m_{q})}_{ud,V/A}
corresponding to dimension-
D=2
is negligible
for light quarks, whereas higher-dimensional contributions

\delta^{(D)}
contain nonperturbative corrections associated with the
operator product expansion (OPE) and potential violations of quark–hadron
duality
[
102
,
103
,
32
,
36
]
.

A theoretical description of the hadronic decay rate is conveniently
formulated in terms of the vacuum two-point correlation functions of the
charged weak currents,

\Pi_{\mu\nu,ij}^{V/A}(p)=i\int d^{4}x\,e^{ipx}\,\langle\Omega|\,T\left\{J_{\mu,ij}^{V/A}(x)\,J_{\nu,ij}^{V/A}(0)^{\dagger}\right\}|\Omega\rangle,

(2.2)

with
J_{\mu,ij}^{V/A}(x)=\bar{q}_{j}\gamma_{\mu}(\gamma_{5})q_{i}(x)
for

i,j=u,d
. Lorentz symmetry implies the decomposition

\Pi_{\mu\nu,ij}^{V/A}(p)=(p_{\mu}p_{\nu}-g_{\mu\nu}p^{2})\,\Pi^{V/A,(1)}_{ij}(p^{2})+p_{\mu}p_{\nu}\,\Pi^{V/A,(0)}_{ij}(p^{2}),

(2.3)

where
\Pi^{(1)}
and
\Pi^{(0)}
denote the transverse (
J=1
) and
longitudinal (
J=0
) components, respectively.

Making use of analyticity and Cauchy’s theorem, the inclusive decay rate
can be written as a contour integral in the complex
s
-plane along the
circle
|s|=M_{\tau}^{2}
. After integrating by parts, the perturbative
contribution to the decay rate assumes the form

\delta^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|s|=M_{\tau}^{2}}\frac{ds}{s}\left(1-\frac{s}{M_{\tau}^{2}}\right)^{3}\left(1+\frac{s}{M_{\tau}^{2}}\right)\widehat{D}_{\rm pert}(a,L).

(2.4)

Here the reduced Adler function is defined as

\widehat{D}(s)\equiv D^{(1+0)}(s)-1
, with

D^{(1+0)}(s)\equiv-s\frac{d\Pi^{(1+0)}(s)}{ds}.

(2.5)

The perturbative expansion of
\widehat{D}(s)
can be written as

\widehat{D}_{\rm pert}(a,L)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\,c_{n,k}\,L^{k-1},

(2.6)

where

a\equiv\frac{\alpha_{s}(\mu^{2})}{\pi},\qquad L\equiv\ln\left(-\frac{s}{\mu^{2}}\right).

(2.7)

The coefficients
c_{n,1}
are independent and require calculations up to

(n+1)
loops, whereas the coefficients with
k\geq 2
are determined by
renormalization-group invariance. For
n_{f}=3
, the known values are
[
104
,
8
]
,

\displaystyle c_{2,1}

\displaystyle=1.63982,

(2.8)

\displaystyle c_{3,1}

\displaystyle=6.37101,

(2.9)

\displaystyle c_{4,1}

\displaystyle=49.076.

(2.10)

We take the QCD
\beta
-function coefficients
\beta_{i}
from
Refs.
[
8
,
105
]
. For three active quark
flavors (
n_{f}=3
), they are given by

\displaystyle\beta_{0}

\displaystyle=2.75-0.166667n_{f}=2.25,

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=6.375-0.791667n_{f}=4,

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

\displaystyle=22.3203-4.36892n_{f}+0.0940394n_{f}^{2}=10.059896,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\beta_{3}

\displaystyle=114.23-27.1339n_{f}+1.58238n_{f}^{2}+0.0058567n_{f}^{3}=47.228040,

\displaystyle\beta_{4}

\displaystyle=524.56-181.8n_{f}+17.16n_{f}^{2}-0.22586n_{f}^{3}-0.0017993n_{f}^{4}=127.322\,.

For higher orders (
i>4
), the coefficients are set to zero, following
the convention adopted in earlier analyses of high-order perturbative
series
[
29
]
.

The Adler function in FOPT is obtained
by fixing the renormalization scale to
\mu^{2}=M_{\tau}^{2}
, leading to

\widehat{D}_{\rm FOPT}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\,c_{n,k}\left(\ln\frac{-s}{M_{\tau}^{2}}\right)^{k-1}.

(2.12)

By contrast, CIPT
[
38
,
37
]
resums running-coupling effects by choosing

\mu^{2}=-s
. With this choice, Eq. (
2.6
) reduces to the compact form

\widehat{D}_{\rm CIPT}\left(\tfrac{\alpha_{s}(-s)}{\pi},0\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n,1}\left(\tfrac{\alpha_{s}(-s)}{\pi}\right)^{n}.

(2.13)

We use the value of the strong coupling at the
Z
-boson mass as an input,

\alpha_{s}(M_{Z})=0.11873(56)

[
106
]
, and evolves it to the
\tau
-lepton mass scale with the

RunDec
package
[
107
,
108
]
, which performs renormalization-group running in the

\overline{\mathrm{MS}}
scheme and implements heavy-quark threshold matching to properly account for the decoupling of the bottom- and charm-quark flavours.
After employing the four-loop evolution together with
\overline{\mathrm{MS}}
decoupling, we obtain

\alpha_{s}(m_{\tau})=0.31959(445)\,.

(2.14)

The perturbative correction
\delta^{(0)}
is evaluated through contour
integrals of the generic form

I(q,k)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|s|=s_{0}}s^{q}\left(\ln\frac{-s}{\mu^{2}}\right)^{k}ds,

which can be computed analytically
[
29
,
109

and yield

I(q,k)=s_{0}^{q+1}\sum_{p=0}^{k}\sum_{l=0}^{k-p}\frac{1-(-1)^{p}}{2}\,(-1)^{\tfrac{p-1}{2}}\,\frac{k!}{p!\,l!}\,\frac{(-1)^{k-p-l}}{(q+1)^{k-p-l+1}}\pi^{p-1}\left(\ln\frac{s_{0}}{\mu^{2}}\right)^{l},\qquad q\neq-1,

together with

I(-1,k)=\sum_{p=0}^{k}\frac{1+(-1)^{p}}{2}(-1)^{p/2}\frac{\pi^{p}k!}{(k-p)!\,(p+1)!}\left(\ln\frac{s_{0}}{\mu^{2}}\right)^{k-p}.

Taking
\mu^{2}=s_{0}=M_{\tau}^{2}
, one obtains the fixed-order expansion

\displaystyle\delta^{(0)}

\displaystyle=a+5.20232\,a^{2}+26.3659\,a^{3}+(78.0029+c_{4,1})\,a^{4}

\displaystyle\quad+(-391.546+14.25\,c_{4,1}+c_{5,1})\,a^{5}

\displaystyle\quad+(-7860.51+45.1119\,c_{4,1}+17.8125\,c_{5,1}+c_{6,1})\,a^{6},

(2.15)

with
a=\alpha_{s}(M_{\tau})/\pi
.

3
Shanks transformation

As discussed earlier, the
Shanks transformation
is a classical nonlinear technique for accelerating the convergence of divergent and slowly convergent sequences
[
97
]
. In its simplest form, the Shanks transformation coincides algebraically with Aitken’s
\Delta^{2}
process
[
110
]
. It was Schmidt who originally derived this transformation for solving linear systems with iteration
[
111
]
. It was neglected for a long time, and rediscovered by Shank again who studied its properties in details. Owing to its effectiveness and broad applicability, the Shanks transformation and its variants have found widespread use in numerical analysis
[
112
,
113
,
114
]
.

For defining the nonlinear Shanks transformation, consider the sequence
[
114
]

\displaystyle s_{n}=s+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}c_{j}\Delta s_{n+j},\qquad n\in\text{N}_{0}.

(3.16)

Let us assume that sequence elements
s_{n}
provide partial sums of an infinite series,

\displaystyle s_{n}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{n}a_{\nu},

(3.17)

the the sequence
s_{n}
can also be represented by,

\displaystyle s_{n}=s+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}c_{j}a_{n+j+1},\qquad n\in\text{N}_{0}.

(3.18)

In essence, the limit
s
of the infinite series is represented by the partial sum
s_{n}
supplemented by a weighted sum of the subsequent
k
terms

a_{n+1},a_{n+2},\ldots,a_{n+k}
. The associated model sequence
s_{n}
depends on

k+1
unknown parameters, the limit (or antilimit)
s
and the
k
linear
coefficients
c_{0},\ldots,c_{k-1}
, each appearing linearly in the construction.

Consequently, by Cramer’s rule, the sequence transformation

e_{k}^{(n)}(s_{n})
, which is exact for the model sequence
3.16
,
is given by the following ratio of determinants:

e_{k}(s_{n})=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}s_{n}&s_{n+1}&\cdots&s_{n+k}\\
\Delta s_{n}&\Delta s_{n+1}&\cdots&\Delta s_{n+k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\Delta s_{n+k-1}&\Delta s_{n+k}&\cdots&\Delta s_{n+2k-1}\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
\Delta s_{n}&\Delta s_{n+1}&\cdots&\Delta s_{n+m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\Delta s_{n+k-1}&\Delta s_{n+k}&\cdots&\Delta s_{n+2k-1}\end{vmatrix}},

(3.19)

where

\Delta s_{n}=s_{n+1}-s_{n},\qquad\Delta^{2}s_{n}=s_{n+2}-2s_{n+1}+s_{n},

(3.20)

and higher differences defined recursively. The computation of the transform
e_{k}(s_{n})
requires the sequence elements

s_{n},\ldots,s_{n+2k}
. Hence,
e_{k}(s_{n})
is a transformation of order
2k
.

For the first nontrivial case
k=1
, Eq. (
3.19
) reduces to

e_{1}(s_{n})=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}s_{n}&s_{n+1}\\
\Delta s_{n}&\Delta s_{n+1}\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}1&1\\
\Delta s_{n}&\Delta s_{n+1}\end{vmatrix}}.

(3.21)

Evaluating the determinants explicitly yields

e_{1}(s_{n})=\frac{s_{n}\Delta s_{n+1}-s_{n+1}\Delta s_{n}}{\Delta s_{n+1}-\Delta s_{n}}=s_{n}-\frac{(\Delta s_{n})^{2}}{\Delta^{2}s_{n}},

(3.22)

Moreover, Shanks showed that the transformation in Eq.
3.19
is exact for model sequences of the form

\displaystyle s_{n}=s+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}c_{j}\lambda_{j}^{\,n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(3.23)

where the remainders are given by sums of exponential terms. The parameters
\lambda_{j}
are ordered such that

\displaystyle|\lambda_{0}|>|\lambda_{1}|>\cdots>|\lambda_{k-1}|.

(3.24)

The sequence in Eq. (
3.23
) converges if the condition
|\lambda_{0}|<1
is satisfied. By analogy with physical transients that decay at late times, Shanks referred to the exponential terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (
3.23
) as
mathematical transients
, since all contributions with
|\lambda_{j}|<1
vanish in the limit
n\to\infty
. This notion, however, should be understood in a broader sense, as the Shanks transformation
e_{k}(s_{n})
can also be applied to the summation of divergent sequences and series. In particular, the model sequence in Eq. (
3.23
) becomes divergent if at least one of the parameters
\lambda_{j}
satisfies
|\lambda_{j}|\geq 1
, because the corresponding term
\lambda_{j}^{\,n}
does not decay as
n\to\infty
. Furthermore, Shanks also demonstrated that the transformation
e_{k}(s_{n})
is closely related to Padé approximants
[
97
]
.

4
Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm

The direct evaluation of the Shanks transformation becomes increasingly cumbersome for large values of
k
, as it involves the computation of ratios of determinants, see Eq. (
3.19
). An efficient and numerically stable alternative is provided by Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm, which generates all Shanks transformations
e_{k}(s_{n})
recursively without explicit determinant evaluations
[
98
]
.

Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm is defined through the recursion relation

\varepsilon_{k+1}^{(n)}=\varepsilon_{k-1}^{(n+1)}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}^{(n+1)}-\varepsilon_{k}^{(n)}},\qquad k,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(4.25)

with the initial conditions

\varepsilon_{-1}^{(n)}=0,\qquad\varepsilon_{0}^{(n)}=s_{n},

(4.26)

where
s_{n}
denotes the sequence of partial sums.

A key result due to Wynn is that the even elements of the
\varepsilon
-table coincide with the Shanks transformations,

\varepsilon_{2k}^{(n)}=e_{k}(s_{n}),

(4.27)

while the odd elements,

\varepsilon_{2k+1}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{e_{k}(\Delta s_{n})},

(4.28)

play the role of auxiliary quantities and do not themselves represent extrapolated limits.

To illustrate this connection explicitly, we derive the first nontrivial Shanks transformation from the
\varepsilon
-algorithm. For
k=0
, Eq. (
4.25
) yields

\varepsilon_{1}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{s_{n+1}-s_{n}}=\frac{1}{\Delta s_{n}},

(4.29)

where
\Delta s_{n}=s_{n+1}-s_{n}
is the forward difference.

Applying the recursion once more for
k=1
, we obtain

\varepsilon_{2}^{(n)}=s_{n+1}+\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\Delta s_{n+1}}-\frac{1}{\Delta s_{n}}}=s_{n+1}-\frac{\Delta s_{n}\,\Delta s_{n+1}}{\Delta^{2}s_{n}},

(4.30)

where
\Delta^{2}s_{n}=s_{n+2}-2s_{n+1}+s_{n}
denotes the second forward difference.

Using
s_{n+1}=s_{n}+\Delta s_{n}
, this expression can be written in the compact form

e_{1}(s_{n})=\varepsilon_{2}^{(n)}=s_{n}-\frac{(\Delta s_{n})^{2}}{\Delta^{2}s_{n}}.

(4.31)

This is precisely the Shanks transformation, which coincides with Aitken’s
\Delta^{2}
process and represents the lowest-order nontrivial extrapolate produced by Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm.

5
Generalizations of the
\varepsilon
-algorithm

While Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm provides an efficient recursive implementation of the Shanks transformation, it may suffer from numerical instabilities when the difference
\varepsilon_{k}^{(n+1)}-\varepsilon_{k}^{(n)}
becomes small or vanishes
[
114
]
. Moreover, the standard
\varepsilon
-algorithm is known to be ineffective in accelerating logarithmically convergent sequences
[
114
]
. Several generalizations of the
\varepsilon
-algorithm have therefore been proposed to address these limitations
[
115
,
116
,
117
,
118
,
119
]
. In the following, we briefly review the relevant modifications and discuss their applicability to the determination of higher-order coefficients in the perturbative expansion of
\delta^{(0)}
. Furthermore, we propose a new modification to the Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm, and show that it improves higher order behaviour of the hadronic tau decays.

5.1
Vanden Broeck–Schwartz modification of Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm

Vanden Broeck and Schwartz (VBS)
[
115
]
proposed a modification of Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm aimed at improving the convergence of logarithmically convergent sequences. This is achieved by introducing an additional control parameter
\alpha_{k}^{(n)}
, which may in general be complex
[
115
]
. The VBS modification replaces the standard recursion relation (
4.25
) by

\varepsilon_{k+1}^{(n)}=\alpha_{k}^{(n)}\,\varepsilon_{k-1}^{(n+1)}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}^{(n+1)}-\varepsilon_{k}^{(n)}},\qquad k\geq 0,

(5.32)

with the initial conditions
\varepsilon_{-1}^{(n)}=0
and
\varepsilon_{0}^{(n)}=s_{n}
. The choice
\alpha_{k}^{(n)}=1
recovers the standard Wynn
\varepsilon
-algorithm.

Although the VBS modification is well suited for accelerating logarithmically convergent sequences, we find that, when applied to the perturbative expansion of
\delta^{(0)}
, it does not provide stable or reliable estimates for the higher-order coefficients in the perturbative expansion of
\delta^{(0)}
.

5.2
Sedogbo–Sablonnière modification of Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm

To improve the numerical stability of the extrapolation, Sedogbo and Sablonnière (SS)
[
118
,
119
]
proposed a modified version of Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm. In this approach, the standard recursion relation is generalized by introducing a phenomenological weight factor, leading to the modified recurrence

\varepsilon_{k+1}^{(n)}=\varepsilon_{k-1}^{(n+1)}+\beta_{k}^{(n)}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}^{(n+1)}-\varepsilon_{k}^{(n)}},

(5.33)

where
\beta_{k}^{(n)}
are adjustable parameters designed to control the convergence properties of the transformation. When the parameters are taken to be constant,
\beta_{k}^{(n)}=\beta
, the SS-modified algorithm reduces to the standard Shanks transformation.

The algorithm is initialized using the standard conditions

\varepsilon_{-1}^{(n)}=0,\qquad\varepsilon_{0}^{(n)}=s_{n},

(5.34)

with
s_{n}
denoting the sequence of partial sums.

At the lowest nontrivial level, the algorithm generates

\varepsilon_{1}^{(n)}=\beta_{0}^{(n)}\frac{1}{s_{n+1}-s_{n}},

(5.35)

while the next even element,
\varepsilon_{2}^{(n)}
, provides the first meaningful extrapolated estimate of the sequence limit. Explicitly, the first extrapolated element is given by

\varepsilon_{2}^{(n)}=s_{n+1}+\beta_{1}^{(n)}\frac{(s_{n+2}-s_{n+1})(s_{n+1}-s_{n})}{\beta_{0}^{(n+1)}(s_{n+1}-s_{n})-\beta_{0}^{(n)}(s_{n+2}-s_{n+1})}.

(5.36)

We observe that in general, allowing
\beta_{k}^{(n)}
to depend on the local behaviour of the sequence introduces additional flexibility and can significantly enhance numerical stability, particularly for slowly convergent or irregular series. For simplicity and consistency at the lowest order, we impose the normalization

\beta_{0}^{(n)}=\beta_{0}^{(n+1)}\equiv\beta_{0},

(5.37)

so that the overall scale
\beta_{0}
factors out of the expression for the first
extrapolated element.

In particular, the weight factors can be chosen as,

\frac{\beta_{1}^{(n)}}{\beta_{0}}=\frac{1}{1+\mu\,(s_{n+1}-s_{n})^{2}},

(5.38)

where
\mu>0
is a free parameter. This choice suppresses large fluctuations when successive partial sums differ significantly, thereby stabilizing the extrapolation. This stabilization mechanism plays a central role in the construction of the generalized
\varepsilon
-algorithm introduced in the following subsection.

5.3
A new modification of Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm

Motivated by the complementary features of the Vanden Broeck–Schwartz (VBS) and Sedogbo-Sablonnière (SS) modifications, we propose a combined generalization of Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm. While the VBS modification improves the treatment of logarithmically convergent sequences through the introduction of a control factor
\alpha_{k}^{(n)}
, the SS modification enhances numerical stability by incorporating a phenomenological weight factor
\beta_{k}^{(n)}
. Their combination therefore provides a more flexible framework for estimating higher-order coefficients in the perturbative expansion of
\delta^{(0)}
.

The generalized recursion relation is defined as

\varepsilon_{k+1}^{(n)}=\alpha_{k}^{(n)}\,\varepsilon_{k-1}^{(n+1)}+\beta_{k}^{(n)}\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}^{(n+1)}-\varepsilon_{k}^{(n)}},

(5.39)

with the standard initial conditions

\varepsilon_{-1}^{(n)}=0,\qquad\varepsilon_{0}^{(n)}=s_{n},

(5.40)

where
s_{n}
denotes the sequence of partial sums.

The first nontrivial extrapolated (even) element generated by the generalized
recursion is given by

\varepsilon_{2}^{(n)}=\alpha_{1}^{(n+1)}\,s_{n+1}+\beta_{1}^{(n)}\frac{(s_{n+2}-s_{n+1})(s_{n+1}-s_{n})}{\beta_{0}^{(n+1)}(s_{n+1}-s_{n})-\beta_{0}^{(n)}(s_{n+2}-s_{n+1})}.

(5.41)

In the present implementation, the control factor is chosen to reflect the local
behaviour of the sequence and is defined as

\alpha_{1}^{(n+1)}=\frac{s_{n+2}}{s_{n+1}}.

(5.42)

For consistency at the lowest order, we further impose the normalization

\beta_{0}^{(n)}=\beta_{0}^{(n+1)}\equiv\beta_{0},

(5.43)

so that the ratio of weight factors entering the first extrapolated element
satisfies

\frac{\beta_{1}^{(n)}}{\beta_{0}}=\frac{\Delta s_{n+1}}{\Delta s_{n}},\qquad\Delta s_{n}=s_{n+1}-s_{n}.

(5.44)

Substituting these choices for the control and stabilization factors into
Eq. (
5.41
), the first extrapolated element of the modified
Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm simplifies to the compact form

\varepsilon_{2}^{(n)}=s_{n+2}-\frac{(\Delta s_{n+1})^{2}}{\Delta^{2}s_{n}},

(5.45)

where
\Delta^{2}s_{n}=s_{n+2}-2s_{n+1}+s_{n}
denotes the second forward difference.

Although the modified
\varepsilon
-algorithm defined by
Eq. (
5.45
) provides a compact and efficient extrapolation
procedure, its practical implementation may still encounter numerical
instabilities when the second forward difference
\Delta^{2}s_{n}
becomes small.
Such situations can arise naturally in perturbative QCD series, where only a
few partial sums are available and accidental cancellations may occur.

To stabilize the transformation, we introduce a regularisation parameter
\eta

directly into the denominator of Eq. (
5.45
) and define the
regularised first extrapolated element as

\varepsilon_{2,\eta}^{(n)}=s_{n+2}-\frac{(\Delta s_{n+1})^{2}}{\Delta^{2}s_{n}+\eta}.

(5.46)

This modification shifts the pole associated with small values of

\Delta^{2}s_{n}
and suppresses unphysical enhancements of the extrapolated
result.
2
2

2

The use of such a regulator is inspired by standard
regularisation techniques employed in quantum field theory to control singular
behaviour.

A convenient and numerically stable choice for the regularisation parameter is

\eta=k\,\Delta^{2}s_{n},

(5.47)

with
k>0
a dimensionless constant. The introduction of a positive
\eta

improves the numerical stability of the modified Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm
without altering its functional structure or its sensitivity to the local
properties of the perturbative sequence. In the limit
\eta\to 0
, the
unregularised expression in Eq. (
5.45
) is smoothly recovered.

5.4
Brezinski’s
\theta
-algorithm

Brezinski modified the Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm by suggesting to use the following sequence defined by the initial conditions
[
116
,
117
,
114
]
,

\theta_{-1}^{(n)}=0,\qquad\theta_{0}^{(n)}=s_{n},

(5.48)

together with non-linear recursion

\theta_{2k+1}^{(n)}=\theta_{2k-1}^{(n+1)}+\frac{1}{\Delta\theta_{2k}^{(n)}}

(5.49)

\theta_{2k+2}^{(n)}=\theta_{2k}^{(n+1)}+\frac{\Delta\theta_{2k}^{(n+1)}\Delta\theta_{2k+1}^{(n+1)}}{\Delta^{2}\theta_{2k+1}^{(n)}}

(5.50)

As in Wynn’s epsilon algorithms, only the elements
\theta_{2k}
with even
indices yield approximations to the (generalized) limit of the sequence
\{s_{n}\}
to be
transformed. The elements
\theta_{2k+1}
with odd indices serve merely as auxiliary
quantities and diverge when the transformation converges.

Given a truncated series with four known partial sums

\{s_{0},s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\}
, an accelerated estimate of its (generalized) limit may be
constructed by forming the sequence
\{\theta_{k}^{(n)}\}
up to the lowest-order
nontrivial even-subscripted element. The construction begins by identifying the
zeroth-order elements of the transformation with the original partial sums,

\theta_{0}^{(0)}=s_{0},\qquad\theta_{0}^{(1)}=s_{1},\qquad\theta_{0}^{(2)}=s_{2},\qquad\theta_{0}^{(3)}=s_{3}.

(5.51)

The subsequent stage consists in the evaluation of the auxiliary quantities with
odd subscripts, which are defined through the inverse differences of successive
partial sums. In the present case, these quantities take the form

\theta_{1}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{s_{1}-s_{0}},\qquad\theta_{1}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{s_{2}-s_{1}},\qquad\theta_{1}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{s_{3}-s_{2}}.

(5.52)

As discussed earlier, these odd-subscripted elements play an auxiliary role in
the transformation and do not themselves provide approximations to the limit.

The first nontrivial accelerated estimate arises from the even-subscripted element

\theta_{2}^{(0)}
, which is constructed via the recursion relation involving the
previously defined quantities. Explicitly, one finds

\theta_{2}^{(0)}=\theta_{0}^{(1)}+\frac{(\theta_{0}^{(2)}-\theta_{0}^{(0)})\bigl(\theta_{1}^{(2)}-\theta_{1}^{(1)}\bigr)}{\theta_{1}^{(2)}-2\theta_{1}^{(1)}+\theta_{1}^{(0)}}.

Upon substituting (
5.51
),(
5.52
) and simplifying, the
resulting expression for the even-subscripted element
\theta_{2}^{(0)}
in terms
of the partial sums reads

\theta_{2}^{(0)}=s_{1}+\frac{(s_{1}-s_{0})(s_{2}-s_{1})(2s_{2}-s_{1}-s_{3})}{(s_{1}-s_{0})(s_{2}-s_{1})-2(s_{1}-s_{0})(s_{3}-s_{2})+(s_{2}-s_{1})(s_{3}-s_{2})}

(5.53)

6
Higher order corrections to hadronic tau decay width

The discrepancy between the predictions obtained using FOPT and CIPT, together with the anticipated future improvement in the precision of the strong coupling determination to the level of
\lesssim 0.2\%

[
4
,
5
]
, poses a significant challenge for accurately describing the QCD dynamics of hadronic
\tau
decays. Resolving this issue is therefore crucial for a reliable extraction of the strong coupling from hadronic
\tau
decay data.

In this section, we estimate the yet unknown higher-order QCD corrections to hadronic
\tau
decays using the Shanks transformation and its modified variants constructed through Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm. To assess the predictive performance of the sequence-transformation methods considered in this work, we first perform a test using only three of the four exactly known perturbative coefficients, namely
(c_{1,1}
–
c_{3,1})
, to predict the fourth-order coefficient
c_{4,1}
. The percentage deviation of the predicted value of
c_{4,1}
from its exact result is used as a quantitative measure of the intrinsic accuracy of each transformation. The same set of three known coefficients is then employed to estimate the higher-order coefficients
c_{5,1}
–
c_{12,1}
. This procedure allows us to identify the most reliable sequence transformation, defined as the one that yields the smallest deviation in the prediction of
c_{4,1}
when only
(c_{1,1}
–
c_{3,1})
are used as input. Finally, the analysis is repeated using all four known coefficients in order to examine the stability and convergence properties of the method.

The estimates of the higher-order coefficients presented in this section are obtained by expanding the rational expressions generated by the various sequence transformations. In practice, these transformations yield ratios of polynomials in the expansion parameter, from which the desired perturbative coefficients are extracted by performing a series expansion. This procedure is closely analogous to the standard method employed in Padé approximant techniques.

\theta

8
Summary and conclusion

Achieving a precise determination of the strong coupling constant is among the central goals for the coming years and plays a pivotal role in Higgs, collider, and flavour phenomenology. The persistent tension between predictions based on FOPT and CIPT, together with the expected improvement in the precision of the strong coupling determination to the level of
\lesssim 0.2\%

[
4
,
5
]
, represents a major challenge for an accurate description of the QCD dynamics in hadronic
\tau
decays. Addressing this issue is therefore essential for a robust and reliable extraction of the strong coupling from hadronic
\tau
decay measurements.

Since explicit calculations of higher-order perturbative contributions to hadronic
\tau
decays are not expected to become available in the near future, the development of reliable estimation techniques remains crucial for precision phenomenology. In this work, we investigate the use of nonlinear sequence-transformation methods to improve the convergence properties of the perturbative series relevant to hadronic
\tau
decays. In particular, we apply the Shanks transformation and its several generalisations constructed through the Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm, including the SS modification, a new modification of the
\varepsilon
-algorithm introduced in this work together with its regularised implementation, and Brezinski’s
\theta
algorithm, to the perturbative quantity
\delta^{(0)}
.

The perturbative series for
\delta^{(0)}
constitutes the dominant contribution to the QCD correction in hadronic
\tau
decays and is known to be slowly convergent and asymptotic in nature. The sequence transformations considered here are designed to accelerate the convergence of such series or to extract reliable information from their truncated forms. In our analysis, these methods are used to generate rational expressions, from which higher-order perturbative coefficients are obtained by series expansion, in close analogy with standard
Padé-based approaches.

Within this framework, we have estimated the higher-order coefficients
c_{5,1}
–
c_{12,1}
of the perturbative expansion. We find that the values obtained from the different sequence transformation methods are mutually consistent and in good agreement with existing estimates available in the literature. It is particularly noteworthy that the predictions for the higher-order coefficients
c_{5,1}
–
c_{12,1}
, obtained using completely independent mathematical techniques, such as the standard Borel–Padé methods employed in Refs.
[
70
,
11
]
, the Levin-type transformations used in Ref.
[
96
]
, and the Shanks sequence-transformation approach adopted in the present work, converge towards a common set of values. In particular, the results of this work, together with the Levin-type predictions of Ref.
[
96
]
, further strengthen the confidence in the standard estimates reported in Refs.
[
70
,
11
]
.

Overall, our results demonstrate that the Shanks transformation and the various generalisations of Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm considered in this work constitute effective and complementary tools for improving the behaviour of perturbative series in QCD applications. In particular, they provide a systematic and reliable framework for investigating higher-order contributions to the perturbative component of hadronic
\tau
decays.

A natural extension of the present analysis is a dedicated determination of the strong coupling constant
\alpha_{s}
within the sequence-transformation framework employed here. Such a study would require a global treatment of the perturbative series appearing in all FESRs relevant to hadronic
\tau
decays, with the perturbative contributions resummed using the Shanks transformation and the various generalisations of Wynn’s
\varepsilon
-algorithm discussed in this work.

Within this framework, one could perform a simultaneous fit of
\alpha_{s}
, as it enters the resummed perturbative expressions, together with the parameters describing nonperturbative contributions. This would enable a fully self-consistent extraction of
\alpha_{s}
based on sequence-transformation-resummed perturbation theory and allow for a direct comparison with existing determinations based on standard fixed-order, contour-improved, or Padé-based approaches.

More broadly, the sequence-transformation techniques explored in this work can be applied to other perturbative series in QCD that are divergent or slowly convergent, including those arising in Higgs decay rates, QCD sum rules, and other precision observables. In this way, they offer a general and systematic framework for enhancing the reliability of perturbative predictions across a wide range of phenomenological applications.
