Novel Mechanism for Production of Planck Relics in Third Order Effective Field Theory Preprint: APS/123-QED
Lorens F. Niehof, Sjors Heefer, Andrea Fuster, Federico Toschi

Abstract
We present a novel mechanism for the formation of black hole remnants arising from a third order effective field theory correction to general relativity. We first calculate the corresponding modifications to the Schwarzschild metric and the Hawking temperature and use these to derive a modified evaporation law that deviates significantly from the classical prediction for sufficiently small black holes. Notably, the rate of mass loss vanishes at a critical mass M c ​ r ​ i ​ t M_{crit} , while the Hawking temperature remains finite. This leads to a freeze-out in the evaporation process, naturally producing stable black hole remnants of Planck scale called Planck relics. We also identify a local maximum in the evaporation rate for a certain mass M ∗ > M c ​ r ​ i ​ t M_{*}>M_{crit} , and compute the corresponding heat capacities which differ from Hawking’s predictions. Our results show that higher-order curvature corrections in effective field theory can provide a physical mechanism for the production of black hole remnants, although the validity of such predictions near the Planck scale remains limited by the breakdown of semiclassical approximations.



I
Introduction

In his groundbreaking 1974 paper, Hawking predicted that black holes should radiate as if they were black bodies
Hawking 1974
. As a consequence, a black hole would lose energy and thus mass through Hawking radiation in the absence of matter it can accrete. When given enough time, Hawking predicted, a black hole would evaporate in its entirety leaving nothing behind. Hawking’s predictions initiated the modern understanding of black hole evaporation and have had profound implications for quantum gravity.

More recently, it has been proposed that full evaporation is not the final stage of a black hole
MacGibbon 1987
;
Bowick et al. 1988
;
Barrow et al. 1992
;
Banks et al. 1992
;
Whitt 1988
;
Alexeyev et al. 2002
;
Giddings 1992
;
Aharonov et al. 1987
;
Banks et al. 1993
;
Callan et al. 1989
;
Myers and Simon 1988
;
Chen et al. 2015
. This is usually concluded through the black hole information paradox
Aharonov et al. 1987
;
Banks et al. 1993
;
Callan et al. 1989
;
Myers and Simon 1988
;
Chen et al. 2015
: after a black hole is formed and then evaporates fully through Hawking radiation, its final state can be described only through the mass, electric charge and angular momentum of the initial state. This suggests that it is possible for distinct initial states (before the collapse into the black hole) to evolve into the same final state, leading to the conclusion that information about the initial state is lost — a paradox, since quantum mechanics forbids the destruction of information.

This problem can be circumvented by postulating the existence of a black hole remnant, usually of Planck scale size

Unlike previous works that postulate corrections to black hole thermodynamics, here we derive these corrections from a third order effective field theory, resulting in an evaporation law that naturally predicts stable remnants. This paper differs from the work by Barrow et al. in two ways. First, we explicitly derive how the Hawking temperature should receive a correction rather than postulate it. Second, in our work the horizon radius also receives a correction, which meaningfully impacts the evaporation law.

The structure of this paper is as follows. First, we introduce an effective field theory, after which we show that the modification of the Schwarzschild metric considered by
Calmet and Kuipers 2021
gives rise to a modified evaporation law. Next, we show that this leads to the prediction of black hole remnants, possibly of Planck scale. We end up by discussing the heat capacity of such remnants.

II
Effective Field Theory

In this work, we consider an effective field theory approach to gravity in which higher-curvature terms represent quantum corrections to the classical Einstein-Hilbert action. For an overview of gravity as an effective field theory, see e.g.
Donoghue 2012
;
Burgess 2004
;
Saraswat 2017
;
Ruhdorfer et al. 2020
.

Since no corrections to the Schwarzschild metric exist in to second order
Calmet and Kuipers 2021
;
Calmet and El-Menoufi 2017
;
Calmet 2018
, we focus on the lowest order that can yield nontrivial effects — third order terms. These provide the first possible deviations from general relativity within the effective field theory framework.

We now construct the relevant effective field theory action. We first present the local third-order terms that contribute in vacuum, followed by a brief discussion of nonlocal corrections. We then show how the local terms modify the Schwarzschild metric, laying the foundation for the black hole evaporation analysis.

II.1
The Effective Action

While there are many ways to go about this to third order (see e.g.
Burgess 2004
), we use the methods employed in
Calmet and Kuipers 2021
, where the following local action in vacuum (with
c=\hbar=1
) is considered:

\displaystyle S_{EFT,L}=

\displaystyle\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left(\frac{R}{16\pi G}\right.

(1)

\displaystyle+c_{1}R^{2}+c_{2}R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}+c_{3}R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\left.+c_{6}GR^{\mu\nu}{}_{\alpha\sigma}R^{\alpha\sigma}{}_{\delta\gamma}R^{\delta\gamma}{}_{\mu\nu}\right),

where
G
is Newton’s constant. The term
\frac{R}{16\pi G}
corresponds to the usual Einstein-Hilbert action which leads to the Einstein field equations. The terms with the Wilson coefficients
c_{1},~c_{2}
and
c_{3}
constitute what is usually called Quadratic Gravity: an action with all second order terms in curvature taken into account. The addition of the term with constant
c_{6}
corresponds to what may be called Cubic Gravity. In fact, this term is the only non-vanishing term in vacuum among all possible cubic-in-curvature contractions of the Riemann tensor. This is due to symmetry and trace identities as shown in
Goroff and Sagnotti 1986
. Indeed,
R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}R^{\alpha}{}_{\epsilon}{}^{\gamma}{}_{\zeta}R^{\beta\epsilon\delta\zeta}
can be written as
R^{\mu\nu}{}_{\alpha\sigma}R^{\alpha\sigma}{}_{\delta\gamma}R^{\delta\gamma}{}_{\mu\nu}
and other terms with the Ricci tensor and scalar that can be shown to reduce to total derivatives or vanish due to the Bianchi identities. While higher-order terms (quartic and beyond) can in principle be added, we restrict to third order, which already yields nontrivial effects.

The nonlocal action is given by

\displaystyle S_{EFT,NL}=-

\displaystyle\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left(\alpha R\ln{\left(\frac{\Box}{\mu^{2}}\right)}R\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\beta R_{\mu\nu}\ln{\left(\frac{\Box}{\mu^{2}}\right)}R^{\mu\nu}\right.

(2)

\displaystyle\left.+\gamma R_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\ln{\left(\frac{\Box}{\mu^{2}}\right)}R^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\delta R^{\mu\nu}{}_{\alpha\sigma}\log{\Box}R^{\alpha\sigma}{}_{\delta\gamma}R^{\delta\gamma}{}_{\mu\nu}\right),

where
\alpha,~\beta,~\gamma
and
\delta
are coupling constants,
\Box=g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}
and
\mu
is the renormalization scale associated with the matching of effective field theory coefficients.

Nonlocal terms, while essential for ensuring renormalization group invariance in the full effective field theory, are not considered here: we restrict our attention in this work to local third order terms due to their role in modifying the Schwarzschild metric in vacuum. As shown in
Calmet and Kuipers 2021
, only the local
c_{6}
term contributes to modifications of the Schwarzschild metric in vacuum at third order. Furthermore, our focus is on the late stages of black hole evaporation, where quantum gravitational corrections to classical geometry dominate over nonlocal infrared effects. Indeed, nonlocal terms in gravitational effective field theory arise from loops of massless particles and encode infrared effects. They modify long-range behavior, such as corrections to Newton’s law
Donoghue 1994
;
Burgess 2004
, but do not contribute meaningfully to near-horizon, high-curvature dynamics relevant for black hole evaporation
Donoghue and El-Menoufi 2014
;
Calmet and Kuipers 2021
.

Hence, for the purpose of studying black hole remnants and their thermodynamics, the local effective field theory action truncated at third order provides a physically motivated and mathematically consistent approximation.

We now turn our attention to the evaporation time. Starting from the Stefan-Boltzmann law (

\displaystyle-\frac{dM}{dt}

\displaystyle\tau_{BH}(M\rightarrow 0)=\frac{5120\pi G^{2}}{\hbar c^{4}}M^{3}.

\displaystyle\tau(M_{0}\rightarrow M_{crit}+\epsilon)

The results above explain what happens to the horizon area of the black hole at the critical mass. Indeed, while

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)=\left(5\pi c_{6}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}\approx 1.99~c_{6}^{\frac{1}{4}}.

IV
Discussion

In this paper, we have presented a novel mechanism for the formation of Planck-scale black hole remnants — so-called Planck relics — within the framework of a third order effective field theory. By deriving corrections to the Schwarzschild metric and Hawking temperature using the effective field theory framework, we have shown that black hole evaporation deviates significantly from Hawking’s classical prediction at small mass scales.

The modified evaporation law predicts a global minimum in the rate of mass loss at a critical mass
M_{crit}=M_{p}\left(5\pi c_{6}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}
, where the mass loss vanishes. Moreover, it predicts a local maximum at a mass
M_{*}
. Remarkably, the Hawking temperature does not vanish at
M_{crit}
; instead, it remains finite while the horizon area shrinks to zero. This counterintuitive result leads to an evaporation freeze-out mechanism where black holes asymptotically approach
M_{crit}
without ever fully evaporating. Consequently, the black holes become mass-stable and give rise to relics. These are of Planck-scale provided
M_{crit}=\mathcal{O}(M_{p})
, which occurs when the Wilson coefficient satisfies
\left(5\pi c_{6}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Unlike previous works that postulate modifications to the Hawking temperature, our results derive these corrections from a well-defined third order local effective field theory, also taking into account modifications to the horizon. This derivation provides a physical mechanism for remnant formation rather than an ad hoc assumption.

These Planck-scale relics offer a potential resolution to the black hole information paradox by providing a stable end state that could, in principle, store information. While our model is semiclassical, it suggests that effective field theoretical corrections might encode remnants of quantum information without invoking full quantum gravity.

Moreover, if Planck relics are stable and produced in sufficient abundance during the early universe, they could constitute a form of dark matter. While this idea lies beyond the scope of this paper, it underscores the relevance of remnant scenarios for cosmology.

We further analyzed the heat capacity of these corrected black holes and showed that the third order effective field theory predicts quantitative deviations from Hawking’s results, particularly near
M_{crit}
and at
M_{*}
. Although these deviations vanish at large masses, they become significant in the Planckian regime.

It is important to note that effective field theory approaches are expected to break down near the Planck scale, where full quantum gravity effects become dominant. As such, the predictions made here—while self-consistent within the effective field theory framework—should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive. The goal of this work is not to claim a complete quantum gravity description, but to demonstrate that higher-order corrections in a semiclassical theory can give rise to remnant-like behavior and offer a natural mechanism for halting black hole evaporation.

These findings lend theoretical support to the idea of Planck relics as end-states of black hole evaporation and provide a concrete mechanism that supplements previously postulated models. Future work could explore whether even higher-order curvature corrections alter or reinforce the results presented here.
