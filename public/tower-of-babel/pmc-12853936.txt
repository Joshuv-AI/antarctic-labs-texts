Development of a multi-skill, haptic-supported virtual reality simulation for first-year nursing students: a pilot study
Authors: Ramazan Bozkurt, Özlem Doğu, Gülüzar Çit, Kayhan Ayar, Cemil Öz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVirtual reality and haptic systems offer interactive learning opportunities by enabling students to apply knowledge in a safe and controlled environment. Haptic systems, consisting of wearable modules with tactile sensors, provide realistic touch feedback when worn on the hands and fingertips, thereby enhancing the practice of psychomotor skills. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a multi-skill (physical assessment, vital signs, blood sugar measurement, oxygen therapy, aspiration and nasogastric catheter application, and subcutaneous injection) and haptic-supported virtual reality simulation designed for nursing education.MethodsThe study was conducted as a three-phase methodological design focusing on the development and integration of a haptic-supported virtual reality simulation for nursing education. In phase one, scenarios were developed, a virtual reality training environment was created using the Unity Game Engine, and a haptic device was designed and integrated into the environment. In phase two, software was developed to link the virtual models with the sensor systems, and scenario steps were implemented using gamification. In phase three, the virtual reality simulation environment was evaluated with 20 nursing students using the Presence Questionnaire. Presence Questionnaire is the subjective measurement of the level of immersiveness in 3D virtual environments.ResultsThe participants had a mean age of 22.6 ± 7 years, 55% were female, and 70% were first-year students. The mean score of participants’ prior virtual reality experience was 3.65 ± 2.08, and the mean Presence Questionnaire score was 124 ± 13.54.ConclusionA haptic-assisted virtual reality simulation was successfully designed and developed for nursing education. The integration of haptic devices with force sensors on the thumb and index fingers enhanced the sense of realism in nursing skill practice. While participants’ prior virtual reality experience was moderate, the high Presence Questionnaire scores suggest that the developed simulation environment provides an effective and immersive learning experience.Keywords: Nursing, Virtual reality, Skills training, Clinical decision making, Haptics

Introduction

Nursing education includes cognitive, sensory, and psychomotor skills teaching. Psychomotor skills teaching is vital in transferring theoretical knowledge to practice and ensuring skill development [1]. Students can reinforce their basic psychomotor skills through practice and repetition in the laboratory environment. In addition to virtual simulation and virtual reality (VR) applications, the acquisition of fundamental nursing skills also requires feedback focused on the sense of touch. Haptics refers to technologies that create a sense of touch, allowing users to feel physical sensations in a virtual environment [2]. Traditional VR devices typically rely on handheld controllers that provide only basic vibration-based feedback, which represents the simplest and most limited form of haptics technology. Beyond these conventional VR controllers, more advanced haptic technologies—such as VR gloves — allow users to interact with the virtual environment without holding a controller, thereby enabling more natural and unrestricted hand movements [3].

Haptic-supported simulations offer alternative solutions for developing new teaching methods. One of the key advantages of haptic technologies in virtual environments is the ease of re-creating difficult situations during real applications [4, 5]. Hands-free haptic systems, in particular, enable users to feel virtual objects more realistically, supporting immersive learning and can be specifically designed to provide task-oriented tactile feedback tailored to the requirements of particular psychomotor skills [6].

Due to the disadvantages of VR gloves used in addition to VR devices — such as high cost, weight, and difficulty of use — there are studies in the literature that have developed sensor-based haptic devices providing touch sensation only to specific fingers. One of these studies is presented by Kim et al. (2017) [7], who developed a portable system architecture capable of responding to tactile stimuli through multiple sensors. The prototype was designed to be applied only to the thumb and index finger, and its performance was evaluated using a back-support force sensor attached to the fingers.

Moreover, studies in the literature show that simulations have a practical and essential role in teaching invasive and complex care skills with multiple scenarios, skill practices, and repetitive practices [8–10]. However, there is no haptic-supported study in which several skills are expected to be performed in the same scenario for a first-year nursing student, and a sense of skill is provided. In this context, this study was conducted to develop a multi-skill, haptic-supported hands-free VR simulation environment for nursing education and to test with a presence questionnaire.

Background

Virtual simulation refers to simulations in an online format or a computerized interaction environment. VR, on the other hand, is the creation of a three-dimensional world in which objects that do not physically exist using computer software have a sense of spatial presence [11]. VR-based education contributes to improving the decision-making process of students and then to realize care practices without harming the patient [12]. At the same time, VR helps to teach the correct technique by giving feedback to the students at the end of the application and contributes to the motivation and success of the students. This feature can also be used as an objective evaluation method. In the literature, it is also stated that game-based VR applications have a positive effect on nursing students’ knowledge, satisfaction, and critical level [1, 13–18]. Breitkreuz et al. (2021) [19] found that the VR game developed for nursing students’ urinary catheterization skills increased the average skill score and provided a pleasant educational environment. Chang et al. (2022) [20] concluded that student achievement, motivation, and critical thinking skills were better. Therefore, they were more successful in the study in which a VR application was applied in childbirth education. Similarly, in the systematic review conducted by Woon et al. (2021) [21] to evaluate the effectiveness of VR training in improving knowledge, 14 randomized controlled studies were assessed, and it was concluded that VR significantly contributed to enhancing the level of expertise among nursing students. Similar to our study, Berg and Steinsbekk (2020) [22] provided training to first-year nursing students in a VR environment.

However, tactile feedback is a less recognized and promising technology [23]. In this direction, it is essential to include skills related to tactile sensations in education, and virtual tactile feedback is a critical feature in VR simulation environments [24]. The cognitive foundation acquired through theoretical knowledge can be developed in the affective and psychomotor domains through clinical experience. While there are numerous educational methods for developing nursing skills acquired through experiential learning, the literature emphasises that the most realistic virtual environment and haptic system are the most effective [6, 25]. The addition of tactile technology to VR, one of the fundamental technologies of the Fourth Industrial Revolution, contributes to the concretisation of the abstract educational environment. The haptic system also offers an experience that enhances learning outcomes and improves the quality of health education [26, 27]. VR education incorporating haptic technology, which adds tactile sensations to visual perceptions, provides users with immediate feedback, and offers a more realistic simulation, thereby increasing students’ confidence and performance capabilities, will contribute to the education of first-year students in basic skills.

Haptic feedback alone creates a greater sense of presence than visual feedback, and virtual simulation technologies enhanced with haptics help students develop practical skills and make their learning processes more effective [28, 29]. Within the scenario, multi-skill training is provided in the VR environment to enable the user to maintain continuity, and haptic feedback is provided to enhance realism. We believe that the limitation of scenarios in the literature stems from their focus on a single skill. This project integrates a VR environment incorporating multi-skill training into nursing education. The majority of existing studies with haptic feedback focus on a single skill, and systematic evidence regarding the learning process of novice students acquiring multiple skills within the same session remains limited. In a study, an augmented reality haptic simulation was shown to help students learn the physical characteristics of diseases. In this study, students examined edema in the legs in a three-dimensional environment and could feel the leg virtually [23]. In another study, haptic systems were used in intravenous catheter insertion training, and students performed intravenous catheter insertion into a virtual vein by touching the virtual patient’s hand. The haptic system was reported to be effective for training [30]. Although haptic systems are being developed in current studies, integrating multiple nursing skills within the scenario and developing a haptic-supported system could open an innovative window in nursing education.

The primary aim of this study is to develop a haptic-supported VR simulation that will create a sense of skill in nursing education and to test it with a presence questionnaire.

Methods

Design

The study was designed as a product development and methodological study. It was planned to develop a multi-skill and haptic-supported VR simulation in nursing education.

Sample

After developing the haptic-supported VR simulation, a presence test was performed to check the system. Power analysis was performed using the G*Power (v3.1.7) program to determine the sample size for the pilot application. The power of the study is expressed as 1-β (β = probability of type II error), and in general, studies should have 80% power. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) being a volunteer, (2) being able to speak and read Turkish, and (3) being a nursing student. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) not being able to speak and read Turkish, and (3) studying in a department other than nursing. According to Cohen’s effect size coefficients, the pilot study included 20 participants. The participants were the majority (70%) first-year nursing students (Table 1).

Data collection

The data were collected by the face-to-face interview method during working hours. The research schedule was planned within the scope of the relevant course hours to avoid overlapping with other students’ classes, and informed consent forms were obtained. Data were collected using the Presence Questionnaire.

Presence Questionnaire: Presence is the subjective experience of being in one place or environment, even when one is physically situated in another. The presence questionnaire measures the level of presence subjectively by evaluating a state obtained as a result of an individual’s attention and mental potential in 3D virtual environments being consistently or meaningfully focused on relevant stimuli, activities or events [31]. The presence questionnaire is a tool developed to evaluate users’ experiences within a virtual environment. In systems that include haptic components, it can also be used to assess the effectiveness of haptic feedback. There are 30 questions in the test applied to the users in the study. In the original scale, the questions related to the hand controllers in the VR headset were removed because they were not used in this study (the system is hands-free). The scale was calculated on a five-point Likert scale [32], and the average was taken. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was obtained as 0.901.

To determine how experienced participants were with VR, the question “How experienced are you with VR?” was asked. Participants rated their experience on a scale of 1 to 7. A score of 1 indicated “inexperienced,” while a score of 7 indicated “expert.”

The study procedure

The research consists of three main stages. In the first phase, the researchers prepared the scenarios included in the application. The scenarios were developed using the standards of the International Nursing Association of Clinical and Simulation Learning (INASCL) [33]. According to the INASCL Healthcare Simulation Standards of Best Practice, certain criteria are included in simulation design. These criteria state that simulations should be scenario-based. Furthermore, simulation training should be designed in consultation with content experts and simulation specialists knowledgeable about pedagogy and best practices in the field. Furthermore, a needs assessment should be conducted to provide fundamental evidence of the necessity for simulation-based experience, measurable objectives should be established based on the student’s fundamental knowledge, various types of fidelity should be used to create the necessary sense of reality, a plan should be developed to evaluate the student and the simulation-based experience, and simulation-based experiences should be pilot tested prior to implementation [34]. In this scenario, firstly, the training plan regarding the purpose and learning outcomes of the application was created, goals and objectives were determined to evaluate cognitive level skills, and the steps to be achieved in this direction were listed.

The psychomotor level for the skill steps was determined, and the skill steps were defined in the VR application. The psychomotor skill steps in this scenario consist of physical assessment, taking vital signs, including interventions for respiratory system problems, aspiration, nasogastric catheter application, oxygen therapy, and subcutaneous injection application skill steps [35–37]. The prepared scenario and the skill steps to be performed were finalized by taking the opinions of ten experts in the field of nursing principles. Studies in the literature indicate that the number of expert opinions can be between 5 and 40 [38, 39]. The Davis Technique was used in the evaluation of expert opinions. The content validity index of the expert opinions was obtained as 0.90.

The integration of the basic skill steps acquired by nursing students into a case study provides a multi-skill environment. The student is expected to make the necessary diagnoses in a case study, plan the care, and implement it. The patient’s diagnosis was provided in an abstract environment, namely the virtual environment. The tactile sensation provided by haptic technology, which gives a sense of skill, contributes to the concretisation of different skills. In the integrated VR environment, multiple nursing skills are structured to include sequential skill steps through a single virtual patient. These skills consist of physical assessment, measurement of vital signs, blood sugar measurement, oxygen therapy, aspiration, nasogastric catheter application, and subcutaneous injection. All applications are designed as nursing interventions to be performed on the same virtual patient within a single module. The user can apply each of these skills step by step in the VR environment.

In the first part of the second phase, an embedded system that provides haptic feedback was developed to increase the sense of reality in the environment experienced with a VR headset. The scenarios were developed for a VR application in the first part of the second phase. The application’s software was created using the Unity game engine, and C# was used as the programming language. Figure 1 shows a sample scene from the haptic-supported VR simulation system environment and the user image using the simulation system.

The haptic-supported virtual reality environment and a user’s image

The 3D virtual training environment developed with Unity is planned to be experienced in an immersive and risk-free manner through a virtual reality headset. In this regard, VR usage supports psychomotor skill acquisition by creating a tactile and realistic perception through vibrations delivered to the fingertips.

Instead of the teleport technique, stage tracking space is used to navigate the virtual environment. In this mode, users can move around the virtual patient room using their movements in the real environment. Our aim here is to give users the impression that they are in a real patient room and to create the application’s level of immersiveness.

The developed system consists of a VR headset (Meta Quest 3) and a Leap Motion integrated into the VR headset (see Fig. 2), providing more sensitive hand gesture detection to the hands-free system. A three-dimensional box was designed for the Leap Motion to stand stably in front of the VR headset, then printed on a three-dimensional printer and integrated onto the headset.

Three dimentional box model for attaching the Leap Motion in front of the VR headset

In the second part of the second phase, an embedded system that provides haptic feedback was integrated into the VR application to increase the sense of reality experienced with the VR headset. The part of the embedded system responsible for providing force feedback was designed to be worn on the users’ thumbs and index fingers, as the virtual reality application requires the use of both fingers on each hand.

The primary goal of haptic applications is to stimulate the user’s sense of touch through artificial inputs, thereby providing feedback about the surrounding environment. Multiple ways exist to deliver this feedback to the user [40]. In the embedded system developed in this study, vibration motors were used to provide haptic feedback to the user, and a super mini development board was used to control these vibration motors (Fig. 3). The vibration motors are small and lightweight, so users do not feel weight while using the application. At the same time, different levels/resolutions of vibration can be generated for the user. In nursing education, the thumb and index finger are frequently used (e.g. for subcutaneous injections). Accordingly, vibration motors have been placed on the thumb and index finger. A single motor can simulate the sensation of holding an object in the hand. The perception of holding a real object can be achieved through vibration motors. Figure 4 shows the design stages of the embedded system that will provide haptic support to the developed VR application.

Basic components of the developed haptic-assisted virtual reality application

Development stages of the embedded system providing haptic feedback

The embedded system consists of a box worn on the wrist and two fingertip attachments that users wear on their fingertips. These three-dimensional models were designed and printed using a three-dimensional printer to make these devices wearable. The fingertip attachments contain a vibration motor that will provide haptic feedback to the users. The fingertip attachment includes a cavity in which the vibration motor can sit. In this way, the user does not feel the vibration motor unless it is active. Holes have been drilled in the design to make it more flexible and lighter (approximately 1gr). With the cable channel on it, it is aimed to prevent the vibration motor cable from affecting the user experience. The box worn on the wrists contains a development card that communicates between the VR application and the fingertip attachment, and a battery that provides the system’s energy supply. At the bottom of the box design, there is also a connection space for the fabric that will allow it to be fixed to the user’s wrist. The communication is in the form of transmitting the signals received from the VR application to the development card, which in turn sends them to the vibration motors, and this communication is provided by the Bluetooth 5 protocol, which is also supported by the VR headset.

Figure 5 shows the life cycle of the communication between the embedded system developed to provide haptic support to the users, the fingertip attachment, and the VR headset. The connection between the virtual reality application running on the Android-based Meta Quest 3 headset and the developed embedded system was established via the Bluetooth protocol. An application class enabling the Meta Quest 3 device to broadcast was coded in the Java programming language for this connection. With the embedded system following this broadcast, the characteristic values determined for each finger were read, and the desired vibration was transmitted to the user’s fingertips.

Life cycle of communication between the haptic-based embedded system and the virtual reality device

MetaQuest 3 provides the necessary information about the user’s hand movements. How the user will grab objects in the virtual environment is determined beforehand by capturing the position of the fingers and the orientation of all finger joints. For example, when a syringe needs to be held, the appropriate finger position for holding the syringe is determined during the development phase of the application. When the user extends their hand to hold the syringe in the virtual environment and positions their fingers in the desired grip form, the syringe is placed in the user’s virtual hand. As long as the user doesn’t significantly distort the form of the fingers, the syringe will move along with the user’s hand. But in reality, the user is empty. The user receives no real-world feedback in response to the holding action. Without this feedback, users struggle to determine whether they are holding the virtual objects. More precisely, they may not be able to determine whether the act of holding has begun or not. This problem causes a disconnect between the virtual and real environments. Therefore, vibration motors placed on the fingertips are activated when users interact with objects in the virtual environment. Vibration motors can give the user tactile feedback.

When the grabbing action occurs, the vibration motors attached to the fingertips are activated. The amount of vibration can be increased depending on the joint angles of the fingers after they are grasped (the degree to which the fingers are closed). For example, when we want to push the tip of a syringe with our thumb, different vibration frequencies are used so that users can distinguish between holding and pushing action in the real world (Fig. 6). Similarly, pressing the virtual button to raise the bed initially provides a low vibration. As the user pushes the button further down with their fingertip, the vibration frequencies will increase. The vibration stops when the objects are released. Thus, users perceive the difference between interacting with an object in the virtual environment and not interacting with it.

Data analysis

IBM SPSS Version 29 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) package program was used in the statistical analysis of the data obtained at the end of the research. A content validity index was made for the scenarios by taking the opinions of ten experts. Descriptive (frequency, minimum, maximum, mean) analyses were used to analyze the data of the presence questionnaire.

Ethical considerations

Permission was obtained from Sakarya University Education Research Ethics Committee and the institution to conduct the study (E-61923333-050.99-285112, 21.09.2023). Before starting the application, the students included in the study were informed about the research’s purpose and plan. Their written consent was obtained with the informed consent form, and a copy was given to the student. The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Results

This section presents socio-demographic data of the users and the results of the Presence Questionnaire. The mean VR experience score of the participants was 3.65 ± 2.08, and the mean Presence Questionnaire score was 124 ± 13.54 (Table 1). Discussion

This study aimed to develop a haptic-supported VR simulation environment for nursing education and evaluate it with a presence questionnaire. Nursing education with haptic-supported VR simulations or high reality simulations supports the control of knowledge and skills by the instructor and provides an efficient training environment [30, 41]. It was stated that the inclusion of tactile feedback significantly improves task performance [42].

Today, the industrial revolution and Industry 4.0 developments have also led to changes in the field of educational technology. Students’ adaptation to these developments and the fulfilment of their expectations make the use of educational technologies inevitable. In this regard, it has revealed the need to use haptic support in increasing reality. Choi et al. (2015) [43] state that haptic systems represent an effort to modernise clinical nursing education and offer advantages over traditional methods in teaching clinical practice. An embedded haptic feedback system was designed to test the developed virtual environment. The developed virtual environment has a patient room scene for practicing nursing skills. Psychomotor skills are realized in this patient’s room among the scenario objectives. In the literature, haptic systems are used in cardiopulmonary resuscitation [44], intravenous and urinary catheterization [45], edema diagnosis [23], aspiration [46, 47], and nasogastric catheter application [48]. However, the haptic system developed in this study differs from those in the literature. The designed haptic feedback embedded system is placed on the thumb and index fingers to support the skill steps with a realistic feeling, and more than one nursing skill can be practiced (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). This result constitutes the answer to the research question.

In the literature, Kim et al. (2017) [49] developed a portable haptic hand system that can be used in various VR applications and uses Leap Motion as a haptic interface. Similarly, in this study, an embedded system was designed to send haptic feedback to the index and thumb fingers.

The “sense of presence”, which is considered when evaluating the implementation process in VR-based learning, is defined as the main features of VR-based education [50]. Ensuring that participants have adequate control over the environment is an essential condition for effective learning and constitutes a key component of VR simulation design. Moreover, presence is not a standalone feature of the virtual environment; rather, it emerges from the fidelity of representation and the level of interaction and user control [51]. In developing the Presence Questionnaire, Witmer and Singer assumed that an increased sense of presence would lead to enhanced learning and performance [31].

The Sense of Presence Questionnaire was administered to evaluate this process, and relatively high scores were obtained from the test. This result constitutes the answer to the research question. This indicates that users perceive the virtual environment as realistic and optimistic. Similar to our study, in the study conducted by Kim et al. (2017) [49], the developed haptic system presence questionnaire was evaluated and obtained at a high level. In a VR-based training programme designed to develop medication administration skills in nursing, the presence test score was similarly high in our study [51].

The fact that designed VR environments offer a realistic experience and work integrated with the user in an interactive manner demonstrates that these technologies can be an effective tool for the assessment and application of psychomotor skills in nursing skills education. Haptic systems represent the most advanced level of computer-based educational technologies, enabling learners to acquire skills at a higher level [52–54]. Haptic feedback in virtual simulators is advantageous for efficient and personalized learning, although careful consideration of simulator validation is essential [55].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the sample size of this pilot study consisted of 20 participants, and the fact that first-year students were predominant (70%) constitutes a limitation. Furthermore, the use of descriptive statistics alone may be considered a limitation of this pilot study. Secondly, the outcome measure of this study is based solely on the Presence Questionnaire and does not directly assess educational or behavioural outcomes such as skill performance, knowledge acquisition or self-efficacy. Thirdly, the proposed system employs vibration-based haptic feedback, which offers advantages in terms of cost-effectiveness, accessibility and ease of implementation; however, this type of feedback cannot fully represent complex tactile sensations such as pressure, or material properties. As a result, a perceptual gap between virtual and real environments may still persist; however, vibration cues can support the perception of interaction events such as grasping or pressing. In addition, the study focuses on system development and usability evaluation rather than a controlled comparison with traditional training methods.

Implications and recommendations

The development of a haptic-assisted virtual reality simulation provides nursing students with a safe and immersive environment to practice skills, enhancing competence and confidence before direct patient contact. By improving the realism of training, such innovations can contribute to better preparation of future nurses, reduce clinical errors, and ultimately promote safer, higher-quality patient care. Moreover, this approach can be effectively utilized during crises or extraordinary circumstances that disrupt traditional education. In future research, it will be important to evaluate both educational outcomes and system usability to more clearly validate the pedagogical significance of the developed haptic VR system. Subsequent studies may incorporate validated skill performance checklists, knowledge assessments, and self-efficacy scales to more rigorously determine the educational effectiveness of the VR-based system.

Conclusions

The use of innovative technologies in nursing skill practices has increased rapidly today. Skill training using a haptic-assisted virtual reality simulation application appeals to multiple senses of students. The design of the virtual reality environment and the force sensors that can be placed on the thumb and index fingers integrated into this environment increase the sense of reality in nursing skill practices. The suitability of the environment, in terms of perceived realism and users’ immersive experience, was supported by the high scores obtained from the Presence Questionnaire. The haptic-supported virtual reality simulation application we developed can provide more than one nursing skill training. To determine the pedagogical significance of the haptic VR system developed as a result of this pilot study more rigorously, studies integrating validated skill checklists, knowledge assessments will be conducted.
