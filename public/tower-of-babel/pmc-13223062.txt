Allopurinol has no effect on calcitriol or other CKD-MBD biomarkers: a randomized double-blind study
Authors: Tiago E Costa, Julia C Lauar, Mariana L R Innecchi, Luiza Karla R P Araujo, Venceslau A Coelho, Luciene M dos Reis, Rosa M A Moysés, Rosilene M Elias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNew evidence has emerged linking uric acid levels to Chronic Kidney Disease and Mineral and Bone Disorder (CKD-MBD). However, interventional studies remain scarce. We evaluated the effect of allopurinol on CKD-MBD biomarkers.MethodsThis is a randomized, double-blind clinical trial involving adult patients with stage 3–5 CKD under conservative management. Participants received either allopurinol 300 mg or a placebo for at least 90 days. Clinical data and blood samples were collected at baseline and at the end of the follow-up period to assess changes in calcium, phosphate, 25-hydroxyvitamin-D, parathyroid hormone (PTH), 1,25-hydroxyvitamin D, fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), α Klotho, and bone alkaline phosphatase.ResultsForty-nine patients completed the study (25 in the allopurinol group and 24 in the placebo group). Most participants were women (53.1%), with a mean age of 71 ± 11 years and an average eGFR of 27.8 ± 11.0 mL/min/1.73 m2. Uric acid levels were significantly decreased in the allopurinol group (7.49 mg/dL initial vs 3.96 mg/dL final, 43%, P < .001). FGF23 levels increased over time (P = .037), with no difference between groups. 25-hydroxyvitamin-D levels increased in the allopurinol group and decreased in the placebo group, a result that lost significance after adjusting for cholecalciferol supplementation and race. No other CKD-MBD biomarkers were significantly affected by allopurinol treatment.ConclusionDespite its urate-lowering effect, allopurinol did not produce meaningful changes in the major CKD-MBD biomarkers over the study period. Our results strengthen existing recommendations to restrict uric acid-lowering treatment to symptomatic gout, as we did not identify any associated improvements in mineral metabolism.Keywords: uric acid, calcitriol, mineral and bone metabolism, chronic kidney disease

Hyperuricemia is a common clinical condition, affecting approximately 20% of the general population [1] and up to 60% of individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [2, 3]. This increased prevalence among patients with CKD is primarily due to reduced urinary excretion of uric acid [4], although other factors, such as genetic predisposition and diuretic use, also play a role. Diabetes, one of the leading causes of CKD, also contributes to elevated uric acid levels due to insulin resistance.

Hyperuricemia has been associated with cardiovascular events in both the general population [5] and in patients with CKD [6, 7]. However, recent randomized clinical trials have failed to demonstrate a benefit of treating hyperuricemia in slowing CKD progression [8, 9]. As recommended by the KDIGO guideline, we currently treat only symptomatic patients with hyperuricemia [10].

Besides CKD progression, new evidence has emerged linking uric acid levels with CKD-Mineral and Bone Disorder (CKD-MBD), a condition characterized by abnormalities in phosphorus, calcium, 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)-vitamin D], 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), parathyroid hormone (PTH), and fibroblast growth factor-23 (FGF-23) [11]. An association between hyperuricemia and hypovitaminosis D has been demonstrated in the general population [12]. Uric acid has been shown to inhibit the activity of 1-alpha hydroxylase [13], thereby reducing levels of 1,25-hydroxyvitamin D in an experimental study. This result is in agreement with a previous clinical study in which serum calcitriol levels significantly increased in patients with CKD after one week of treatment with allopurinol at a dose of 300 mg daily [14]. Hyperuricemia has also been associated with elevated PTH levels [15-17], although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. In an experimental study involving uremic rats with secondary hyperparathyroidism and hyperuricemia, reduced urate excretion via both the gastrointestinal tract and kidneys was attributed to decreased expression of a urate transporter. Interestingly, this effect was prevented by cinacalcet, a PTH-suppressing agent [18]. Additionally, experimental data have shown that uric acid may also influence serum concentrations of FGF-23 [19-21], α-Klotho [22, 23] and bone-specific alkaline phosphatase [24], through mechanisms that have yet to be fully elucidated.

Due to the predominantly observational design of existing studies, uncertainty remains regarding the causal relationship between uric acid levels and CKD-MBD markers. To address this gap, we conducted a randomized, double-blind clinical trial to evaluate the potential effects of uric acid reduction on these parameters. We hypothesized that treatment with allopurinol, aimed at lowering serum uric acid levels, could influence CKD-MBD markers, particularly by increasing levels of calcitriol.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This was a controlled, randomized, double-blind study involving patients with CKD, defined as glomerular filtration rate lower than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, as estimated by the CKD-EPI equation [25], registered in clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05601271).

Participants were recruited from the nephrology outpatient clinic at the Hospital das Clinicas (Universidade de São Paulo) between March 2021 and October 2023. Inclusion criteria were age over 18 years, CKD stage 3 to 5 not requiring dialysis, and regular follow-up in the nephrology outpatient clinic. The exclusion criteria were a prior diagnosis of gout, current use of allopurinol, or known hypersensitivity to the drug. Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either allopurinol or placebo.

Intervention and randomization

After patients accepted the invitation to participate in the study and signed the informed consent form, they were randomized using a specialized platform (www.studyrandomizer.com). Two randomization variables were pre-established: sex and estimated GFR, which is divided into ≥ or < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2. Patients were then allocated to one of two groups: A or S.

After randomization, patients received guidance on medication use. They were instructed to take half a tablet daily for the first 30 days, followed by a full tablet daily thereafter. This dosage escalation was intended to minimize the risk of adverse reactions. Patients were advised to continue taking the medication until their follow-up visit or until the end of the study period (90-180 days).

Allopurinol, 300 mg taken once a day, was used as the active drug, and the placebo was manufactured by the pharmacy of Hospital das Clinicas (Universidade de São Paulo). Both allopurinol and placebo were packaged identically, differentiated only by a coded label—either “A” or “S”. The pharmacy retained the coding information, which was disclosed to the research team only after the completion of patient follow-up.

At the follow-up visit, the patient was asked about their medication use and adherence. They were questioned about any difficulties in taking the medication, missed doses, the number of days without medication, and any adverse effects experienced.

All patients received cholecalciferol supplementation with the aim of achieving serum levels of at least 20 ng/mL.

All randomized participants received the allocated intervention as per protocol, and there were no crossovers or deviations between study arms. Therefore, the per-protocol and intention-to-treat populations are effectively identical.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the change in calcitriol levels, measured at study entry and after at least 90 days. Secondary outcomes included changes in other CKD-MBD markers, assessed over the same time frame.

Clinical, demographic, and laboratory data

The demographic and clinical data were age, sex, race, etiology of CKD, and the presence of comorbidities such as diabetes, arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, and stroke. These data were extracted from the patient's electronic medical chart on the day of recruitment. Additionally, the use of certain commonly prescribed medications was recorded, as their mechanisms of action may influence both uric acid and CKD-MBD markers. Medications with their respective dosages documented were furosemide (mg/day), hydrochlorothiazide (mg/day), chlorthalidone (mg/day), losartan (mg/day), sevelamer (mg/day), cinacalcet (mg/day), calcium carbonate (mg/day), calcitriol (mcg/week), cholecalciferol (IU/week) and sodium bicarbonate (g/day).

The following routine laboratory tests were analyzed: urea (reference range (RR) 19 - 49 mg/dL), creatinine (RR < 1.2 mg/dL), estimated GFR calculated using the CKD-EPI equation (mL/min/1.73 m2), uric acid (with hyperuricemia defined as serum concentration > 7.0 mg/dL in men and > 5.8 mg/dL in women), calcium (RR 8.4-10.2 mg/dL), ionized calcium (RR: 4.0-5.35 mg/dL)), phosphorus (RR 2.7-4.5 mg/dL), 25(OH)-vitamin D (RR 30-100 ng/mL), PTH (RR 11-65 pg/mL), albumin (RR: 3.4-4.8 g/dL), sodium bicarbonate (RR: 22-26 mmol/L) and hemoglobin (RR > 12 for women and >13 g/dL for men). In addition, 1.84 PTH and 1,25-hydroxyvitamin D were measured using LIAISON® analyzer (DiaSorin, USA, RR 5.7-47.8 pg/mL and 25.0 and 86.5 pg/mL, respectively), FGF-23 and bone alkaline phosphatase (ELISA, catalog #60-6100, RRID:AB_2891250, Quidel, San Diego, CA, USA), and α Klotho (ELISA, catalog #27998, RRID:AB_2750859, IBL- America, Minneapolis, MN, USA), were assessed at baseline and end of the study. These samples were collected at baseline and stored for subsequent analysis.

Sample size was calculated based on a previous study that enrolled 9 patients in which the concentration of calcitriol increased from 30.8 ± 2.7 to 38.2 ± 4.8 pg/mL following the treatment with 300 mg of allopurinol per day [14]. We calculated the required sample size for two groups (placebo and allopurinol), with pre and postmeasures. Assuming a standard deviation of 4 pg/mL and an average difference of 7 pg/mL in the allopurinol group and 3 pg/mL in the placebo group, with a power of 80% and a significance level (alpha) of 5%, the required sample size was 22 patients per group. To account for potential loss of follow-up, we aimed to recruit at least 25 patients per group.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using Pearson correlation, Student's t test, ANOVA, and linear regression. For variables that did not follow a normal distribution, data were expressed as median and interquartile range (25th and 75th percentiles) and analyzed using Spearman's rank correlation, the Mann–Whitney U test, Kruskall–Walli's test, or Friedman test, as appropriate.

Categorical variables were expressed as both relative and absolute frequencies and compared using the chi-square test, McNemar's test, and Fisher's exact test.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a mixed model was used to examine the data across groups (placebo and allopurinol) and over time (repeated measures), including the interaction between group and time. Sphericity was evaluated using the Mauchly test. The univariate correction tests Wilks' Lambda and Greenhouse–Geisser, were used to evaluate the effects if sphericity was violated. Non-parametric variables were log-transformed before analysis. Multivariable analyses were adjusted for cholecalciferol supplementation and race, as these variables are known to influence vitamin D metabolism and CKD-MBD biomarkers and could act as potential confounders.

The tabulation of data and statistical treatment were performed in IBM SSPS 22.0 Statistics software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Values of P < .05 are considered significant.

Results

Population

Sixty-four patients were recruited; one was excluded due to missing data on estimated GFR. A total of 49 patients completed the follow-up (N = 25 in the allopurinol group and N = 24 in the placebo group). Seven patients in each group discontinued the study: one due to death from a pulmonary complication unrelated to the study, six were lost to follow-up, three in the allopurinol group due to adverse reactions to medication (skin rash), and four due to non-adherence to the research drug protocol. Discussion

In this randomized clinical trial, we evaluated the effect of allopurinol on CKD-MBD markers. While allopurinol effectively reduced UA levels, it did not significantly affect CKD-MBD markers. FGF-23 levels increased over time, with no significant differences between groups.

Although hyperuricemia is generally more prevalent in men in the general population [1, 26], it is more common in women among patients with CKD [2, 27]. In addition, it appears that hyperuricemia increases with age [1, 28]. We also observed that uric acid concentrations tended to be higher in patients with lower eGFR, a finding consistent with previous studies demonstrating a higher prevalence of hyperuricemia with advancing CKD [2, 29].

Patients were randomized in a controlled manner, stratified by eGFR and sex. This approach was necessary because the prevalence of hyperuricemia increases significantly with more advanced stages of CKD, particularly among women. Allopurinol was effective in significantly lowering uric acid levels and was generally well tolerated, with adverse reactions reported in only 3 patients (9.37%) in the treatment group. These findings are consistent with data reported in the existing literature [30].

Allopurinol therapy did not significantly change serum calcitriol, which was the primary endpoint of our study. In a previous study involving patients with gout, calcitriol levels improved significantly after one year of treatment with allopurinol or benzobromarone [31]. A similar finding was obtained in a prior study in which therapy with allopurinol 300 mg/day demonstrated a significant increase in calcitriol levels in 8 of 9 patients with CKD [14]. Since then, the current hypothesis is that hyperuricemia inhibits 1α-hydroxylase activity, reducing the synthesis of calcitriol. The authors concluded that the increase in 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D was unlikely to result from a direct effect of allopurinol. Instead, it was probably due to the inhibition of xanthine oxidase. However, the referenced studies date to 1993 and 1998, and their measurement technique (radioimmunoassays) is presumably less accurate than the methods used today, including those applied in the current study [32].

Previous studies have demonstrated that hypovitaminosis D is associated with hyperuricemia and that elevated uric acid levels may reduce 25(OH)-vitamin D concentrations [12, 33-37]. However, it should be noted that most of these studies are cross-sectional in nature. One observational study involving 137 patients with CKD found that allopurinol was associated with higher 25(OH)-vitamin D levels [38]. In addition, in vitro and in vivo evidence support a potential association between allopurinol and increased serum 25(OH)-vitamin D in patients with stage 3 to 5 CKD [39]. Despite the consistency of these findings, no interventional studies have been conducted to date. Although an increase in 25(OH)-vitamin D levels was observed in the allopurinol group, caution is warranted when interpreting this result, as the change was relatively small and no increase was seen in active vitamin D levels. Thus, a clear physiological explanation for this finding remains lacking.

We found no effect of allopurinol on any other CKD-MBD marker. Previous studies have reported associations between uric acid levels and several CKD-MBD parameters, including PTH [15-17], Klotho [23, 40], and alkaline phosphatase [41]. FGF-23 levels increased in both groups over the course of the study. This rise can likely be attributed to the deterioration of renal function in both groups, as FGF-23 is known to increase progressively with declining renal function [42, 43]. We observed no effect of allopurinol on FGF-23 levels, despite previous studies reporting a positive association between hyperuricemia and FGF-23 [19, 21].

Our study should be interpreted considering certain limitations. The sample size was relatively small, biomarkers were measured at a single time point, there was an imbalance in vitamin D supplementation at baseline, and the follow-up period was short, although comparable to previous studies. A key strength of our study is its randomized double-blind design, which supports causal inference. The intervention produced the expected reduction in serum uric acid levels, confirming the biological effect of the drug.

Although hyperuricemia has been associated with several changes in CKD-MBD markers, most supporting studies are retrospective or cross-sectional in nature. In the present randomized trial, we found no effect of allopurinol on any CKD-MBD marker.
