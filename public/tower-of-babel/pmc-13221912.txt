Longitudinal aging research in East and Southeast Asia: representativeness or depth?
Authors: Liang-Kung Chen, Li-Ning Peng, Fei-Yuan Hsiao
Published: 2026

The systematic review by Malhotra and colleagues1 offers a comprehensive synthesis of community-based longitudinal aging studies across East and Southeast Asia (ESEA). Its timing is critical: with more than 30% of the world's older population residing in this region and aging accelerating faster than in most Western nations, the absence of robust longitudinal evidence is not merely a research gap but a policy failure in the making. Of the 30 eligible studies across 10 countries, 57% were concentrated in five high-income economies, while countries such as Indonesia, Cambodia, and Myanmar have none, a finding that reflects stark inequality in research capacity with direct implications for how widely ESEA-derived evidence can be safely generalized.

The measurement gaps are equally sobering. Among the 30 cohort studies, only 12 measured physical frailty, despite its established status as a cardinal predictor of adverse outcomes in older adults.2 Most ESEA longitudinal data therefore offer no frailty information, leaving clinicians without evidence most relevant to risk stratification and care planning. Equally concerning is the heterogeneity of measurement instruments. Ongoing regional efforts to develop Asia-specific consensus for muscle health and sarcopenia diagnosis3 represent important correctives, but cross-cohort harmonisation across geriatric assessment domains, such as psychosocial and cognitive health, remains an unfinished agenda. Standardised protocols in future cohort designs could substantially reduce this measurement fragmentation.

The most important methodological concern is a tension between two distinct scientific goals: external validity through national representativeness, and construct validity through deep phenotyping of the aging process. These are not the same goal,4 and conflating them distorts both the identified gaps and their implications. The inclusion of large surveillance cohorts, such as the Japan Public Health Center-based Prospective (JPHC) Study (n = 113,461) designed primarily around cancer epidemiology, or the Malaysian Cohort (n = 106,527) structured as a general biobank, inflated the apparent richness of the aging research landscape. A cancer surveillance cohort was not designed to measure frailty; its absence of such constructs reflected intent, not omission. The measurement deficiencies the review identified are real, but their interpretation requires caution. The review's inclusion framework introduced a framing limitation that shaped which gaps appeared and why.

The representativeness criterion further excluded scientifically influential cohorts. The Framingham Heart Study,5 launched in a single Massachusetts town without national representativeness, defined cardiovascular risk factors and shaped clinical practice globally for seven decades. The lesson is unambiguous: scientific depth, not sampling architecture, determines a cohort's impact. Japan's National Institute for Longevity Sciences—Longitudinal Study of Aging (NILS-LSA), ineligible under the review's multi-site criterion, has produced mechanistic insights across aging dimensions that representative surveys cannot replicate.6 Similarly, the I-Lan Longitudinal Aging Study (ILAS) in Taiwan, designed to examine interrelationships between sarcopenia, frailty, and cognitive decline, has generated mechanistically rich longitudinal evidence on frailty trajectories and cognitive aging that nationally representative surveys rarely achieve.7 A two-tier framework, one for surveillance cohorts and another for deeply phenotyped mechanistic cohorts, would more honestly represent the landscape and better guide future research investment.

Despite these concerns, the review's forward-looking roadmap remains its most actionable contribution. Recommendations regarding Common Data Models and multi-national study development modelled on the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe provide concrete architecture for regional coordination. The call for digital biomarker integration is well-supported by evidence that wearable-derived activity profiling can serve as a valid biomarker of aging trajectories.8 Japan, South Korea, Singapore, and Taiwan, among the more active contributors to digital health research for aging populations,9 are well-positioned to pilot such integration; Asia-specific models may in turn inform global longitudinal study design, reversing the historical direction of methodological influence.

Several critical questions remain open. The molecular and genetic determinants of healthy aging in Asian populations may diverge substantially from the Western cohorts that have dominated geroscience; without adequately powered multi-omic data from ESEA, precision geromedicine in this region remains aspirational.10 The near-universal neglect of informal caregivers is a structural blind spot in a region where family-based care provides the majority of long-term support for older adults, and the absence of any new longitudinal study launched after 2020 signals concerning stagnation. This review merits reading as both an inventory and a call to action. The data infrastructure exists but is inequitably distributed, and the review's inclusion framework inadvertently conflated surveillance cohorts with mechanistic ones, a distinction that matters for correctly identifying where investment is genuinely lacking. Frailty must become a core measurement domain,2 harmonisation toward Asia-specific frameworks3 must not sacrifice the deep phenotyping that gives purpose-built cohorts their irreplaceable scientific value, and equity in data ownership must be embedded from inception, not retrofitted, a principle that matters for both scientific integrity and the populations this research ultimately serves. As Framingham Study established seven decades ago, scientific depth is not conditional on national representativeness; that principle should anchor the design criteria and funding frameworks for the next generation of ESEA aging cohorts.
