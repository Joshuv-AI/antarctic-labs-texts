Abstract
We present an algebraic algorithm for quantum state tomography that leverages measurements of certain observables to estimate structured entries of the underlying density matrix. Under low-rank assumptions, the remaining entries can be obtained solely using standard numerical linear algebra operations. The proposed algebraic matrix completion framework applies to a broad class of generic, low-rank mixed quantum states and, compared with state-of-the-art methods, is computationally efficient while providing deterministic recovery guarantees.



I
Introduction

The state of a quantum system is fully described by its density matrix—a Hermitian, positive-semidefinite (PSD) operator with unit trace—that provides a unified representation of both pure and mixed quantum states (probabilistic mixtures of pure states). Its diagonal entries represent state populations (probabilities), while its off-diagonal entries encode quantum coherences between states
[
23
]
. Accurately determining this matrix is a key challenge in quantum science, as many tasks, such as benchmarking and verification of quantum hardware, and fidelity estimation, essentially involve its estimation. This matrix is estimated by a technique known as quantum state tomography (QST)
[
23
,
8
,
13
,
19
]
. QST works by performing an informationally complete set of measurements on a large ensemble of identically prepared quantum systems. The collected data is subsequently used to reconstruct the density matrix. However, a major obstacle is the exponential growth in parameters (and correspondingly in measurements and reconstruction complexity) as the system grows, an obstacle known as the “curse of dimensionality.”

Physically relevant states occupy only a tiny, structured subset of Hilbert space, constrained by locality and finite-complexity dynamics that limit correlations and entanglement
[
9
,
13
,
19
]
. This subset includes pure or low-entropy states, low-energy states of local Hamiltonians, product states, and structured states in which only a subset of entries carry valuable information. Exploiting these low-rank or structural constraints can tame the curse of dimensionality.

Related Work

When the density matrix is known to be low rank or close to a low-rank matrix,
low-rank
QST exploits the structure to reconstruct the state from fewer measurements. Rank information can be incorporated either implicitly, by promoting low rank through a rank-minimization objective, implemented via the nuclear norm as a convex surrogate, under measurement constraints
[
13
,
20
,
19
]
, or explicitly, by optimizing a fixed-rank factorized model, as in Burer–Monteiro–type formulations
[
5
,
16
]
. If the density matrix is instead viewed as a high-order tensor of low rank in the tensor sense, efficient and scalable tensor network representations—such as matrix product states (tensor trains)—can be used to estimate it
[
8
,
25
,
24
,
17
,
30
]
.

Another line of research avoids full QST and focuses on learning only specific aspects of the underlying density matrix. For example,
permutationally invariant
QST efficiently reconstructs the permutationally invariant part of the underlying quantum state, with the number of measurement settings scaling quadratically, and is well suited for states that are close to being permutationally symmetric, such as Dicke or spin-squeezed states
[
32
,
21
]
. As another example,
shadow tomography
constructs a compact classical representation known as “classical shadow”—a collection of randomized sketches of the density matrix—obtained via random unitary transformations, allowing many observables (or arbitrary linear functions) to be estimated from the compact representation with measurement complexity that scales logarithmically in the number of observables
[
1
,
14
]
.

Extending the idea of learning partial information,
selective
QST focuses on directly estimating only a chosen subset of density-matrix entries, enabling targeted estimation without reconstructing the full state
[
2
,
6
,
10
,
22
]
. This approach can exploit additional structure: for instance, if the selected entries follow a specific low-rank–exploitable pattern, they can uniquely determine the global low-rank density matrix. Furthermore, these entries can be obtained via shadow tomography, allowing scalable estimation even of the full state
[
34
]
.

Contributions

In this work, we further develop selective QST, where we focus on
characterizing
structured entries that enable unique and efficient recovery of the full density matrix using an algebraic method (based only on standard numerical linear algebra (NLA) operations), and provide deterministic recovery guarantees and subspace error bounds. We compare our method with state-of-the-art (SOTA) techniques and show that it is computationally efficient while achieving competitive accuracy in reconstructing mixed low-rank quantum states. Finally, we touch on how scalability can be improved by estimating structured entries via shadow tomography, combining low‑rank and shadow tomography.

II
Low-rank QST formulation

QST can be formulated as an inverse problem. For an
N
-qubit system with density matrix
\bm{\rho}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}
, where
D=2^{N}
, we measure a set of Hermitian matrices (observables)
\{{\mathbf{{E}}}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, obtaining the expected outcomes
y_{m}=\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\rho}{\mathbf{{E}}}_{m})
. The goal of QST is then to reconstruct
\bm{\rho}
from the collected data
\{{\mathbf{{E}}}_{m},y_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
. This recovery problem is challenging because (1)
\bm{\rho}
must satisfy the physicality constraints, i.e.,
\bm{\rho}\in\mathcal{S}
, and (2) the number of measurement settings required to ensure unique recovery of a full-rank density matrix scales as
\mathcal{O}(D^{2})
. However, in the rank-
R
case,
\mathcal{O}(RD\log^{2}D)
measurement settings suffice to uniquely recover it with high probability
[
13
,
20
,
19
]
.

As mentioned earlier, there are two main formulations in which the low-rank structure can be exploited.

Rank Minimization

In this approach, the low-rank constraint is used implicitly as an optimization objective to minimize, with measurement data and physicality as constraints to satisfy. Formally, we can write this as
[
13
]
:

\vskip-5.69054pt\min_{\hat{\bm{\rho}}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times D}}\|\hat{\bm{\rho}}\|_{\star}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{\mathcal{{M}}}(\hat{\bm{\rho}})\|_{2}\leq\epsilon,\text{ and }\hat{\bm{\rho}}\in\mathcal{S},

(1)

where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
are the measurement outcomes, and the measurement map
\mathcal{M}
is
(\mathcal{M}(\bm{\rho}))_{m}=\operatorname{Tr}({\mathbf{{E}}}_{m}\bm{\rho})
. Note that we minimize the nuclear norm (the tightest convex surrogate of the rank function) over a full matrix; hence, it is not a feasible option for mid- to large-scale systems.

Error Minimization

This approach enforces low-rank by parametrizing the density matrix with a fixed, low-rank Cholesky-like (Burer–Monteiro) factorization
[
5
]
. A differentiable loss is minimized over the low-rank factors, yielding an efficient approximate solution to QST. We can express this as:

\min_{{\mathbf{{A}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times R}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{\mathcal{{M}}}\left(\hat{\bm{\rho}}\right)\|_{2}^{2}~\text{ with }\hat{\bm{\rho}}={\mathbf{{A}}}{\mathbf{{A}}}^{\mathrm{H}},\text{ s.t. }\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\bm{\rho}})=1.

(2)

Note that the Hermitian and PSD constraints are taken into account implicitly, and the unit-trace constraint is often relaxed to
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{F}^{2}\leq 1\Leftrightarrow\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\bm{\rho}})\leq 1,
to improve scalability
[
3
,
16
]
.

Proposed Method

Our approach employs selective QST to directly estimate a structured subset of entries of the density matrix, which can then be leveraged algebraically under low-rank assumptions to reconstruct the full density matrix from this partial information.

As noted earlier, in selective QST, the observables
\mathbf{E}_{m}
are chosen so that their expectation values correspond to specific entries of the density matrix
[
2
,
6
,
10
,
22
,
34
]
. Let the Hermitian operators be
\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{Re}}_{rc}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{s}_{c}\mathbf{s}_{r}^{\top}+\mathbf{s}_{r}\mathbf{s}_{c}^{\top})
and
\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{Im}}_{rc}=\frac{1}{2i}(\mathbf{s}_{c}\mathbf{s}_{r}^{\top}-\mathbf{s}_{r}\mathbf{s}_{c}^{\top})
. Thus, we have
\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{rc})=\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\rho}\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{Re}}_{rc})
and
\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{rc})=\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\rho}\mathbf{E}^{\mathrm{Im}}_{rc})
, leading to
\rho_{rc}=\overline{\rho_{cr}}=\mathrm{Re}(\rho_{rc})+i\,\mathrm{Im}(\rho_{rc})
. The diagonal entries
\rho_{rr}
can be obtained directly from computational-basis measurements, given by
\rho_{rr}=\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{\rho}\,\mathbf{s}_{r}\mathbf{s}_{r}^{\top})
. More generally, any entry
\rho_{rc}
can be estimated by measuring at most four rank‑1 observables
\{\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{H}}\mid\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{B}\}
, where
\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbf{s}_{r},\,\mathbf{s}_{c},\,(\mathbf{s}_{r}+\mathbf{s}_{c})/\sqrt{2},\,(\mathbf{s}_{r}+i\,\mathbf{s}_{c})/\sqrt{2}\}
. While many other entrywise-probing measurement operators have been studied
[
2
,
6
,
10
,
22
,
34
]
, we use the standard operators above for theoretical simplicity. Our focus is on determining
which
entries to estimate, not on designing measurement operators.

III
Efficient Low-Rank QST via Structured Measurements

Recent work has shown that structured observation patterns can enable efficient algebraic reconstruction: a generic low‑rank matrix can be uniquely recovered from a certain set of fully observed submatrices, allowing completion via standard NLA operations with deterministic guarantees
[
4
,
15
,
26
,
29
,
31
,
28
]
. Structured patterns for Hermitian PSD completion have likewise been characterized via chordal graphs
[
12
,
27
]
, where PSD constraints on the observed principal submatrices are sufficient to ensure a PSD completion of the full state
[
33
]
.

In line with
[
31
,
28
]
, we identify a set of fully observed submatrices that is informationally complete, meaning that these submatrices uniquely specify the full state. Without loss of generality, we focus on characterizing principal submatrices of the density matrix, as, under mild conditions, they ensure global Hermitian structure and positivity
[
12
,
27
,
33
]
. Moreover, only the upper (or lower) triangular entries need to be estimated via selective QST.

III-B

Global Subspace from Local Constraints

Assume we are given fully observed isorank principal submatrices
\{\bm{\rho}_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(l)}={\mathbf{{S}}}_{\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}}^{\top}\bm{\rho}{\mathbf{{S}}}_{\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}}\}_{l=1}^{L}
of
\bm{\rho}
and aim to compute its
R
-dimensional subspace,
\text{col}\left(\bm{\rho}\right)
. The isorank property ensures that these submatrices impose informative constraints on
\text{col}\left(\bm{\rho}\right)
; see
[
31
,
28
]
for details. Each
\bm{\rho}_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(l)}
admits a compact EVD
\bm{\rho}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{obs}}={\mathbf{{U}}}^{(l)}{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}^{(l)}{\mathbf{{U}}}^{(l)\mathrm{H}},
where
{\mathbf{{U}}}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{C}^{|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}|\times R}
has full column rank
\big(|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}|\geq R\big),
and
{\mathbf{{\Sigma}}}^{(l)}
is diagonal with eigenvalues in descending order. Let
Q_{l}:=\operatorname{span}\left({\mathbf{{Q}}}^{(l)}\right)
be the subspace induced by the
l
th submatrix in the ambient (padded) space, with orthonormal basis in
{\mathbf{{Q}}}^{(l)}=\left[{\mathbf{{S}}}_{\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}}{\mathbf{{U}}}^{(l)}\quad{\mathbf{{S}}}_{\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}^{c}}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times(R+|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}^{c}|)},
where
\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}^{c}=[D]\setminus\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}
. Then, we have
\text{col}\left(\bm{\rho}\right)\subseteq\left(\bigcap_{l=1}^{L}Q_{l}\right)
. If the submatrices are chosen such that the intersection of the associated subspaces has minimal dimension,
\dim{\left(\bigcap_{l=1}^{L}Q_{l}\right)}=R,
then

\text{col}\left(\bm{\rho}\right)=\bigcap_{l=1}^{L}Q_{l}
, yielding unique identifiability. Generic conditions (i.e., conditions that hold with probability 1 when the matrix entries are drawn from a continuous distribution) for this property are given in
[
31
,
28
]
and will be discussed in Section
III-D
; we now show how to compute the intersection.

To obtain an orthonormal basis
{\mathbf{{U}}}
for
\text{col}\left(\bm{\rho}\right),
we estimate a common subspace
U
closest to
\{Q_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
:

U^{\star}=\argmin_{\dim(U)=R}\sum_{l=1}^{L}d_{c}^{2}(U,Q_{l})\Leftrightarrow\argmax_{{\mathbf{{U}}}^{\mathrm{H}}{\mathbf{{U}}}={\mathbf{{I}}}_{R}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left\|{\mathbf{{U}}}^{\mathrm{H}}\left({\mathbf{{Q}}}^{(l)}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}.

(3)

The solution of this problem admits a closed form:
U^{\star}
is spanned by the top-
R
eigenvectors of the aggregate projector

{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}={\mathbf{{Q}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}{\mathbf{{Q}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{\mathrm{H}}
,
equivalently by the top-
R
left singular vectors of the concatenated matrix

{\mathbf{{Q}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}=[{\mathbf{{Q}}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{{Q}}}^{(L)}]
.
Moreover,
{\mathbf{{U}}}
can be computed using a matrix–free eigensolver, avoiding the explicit construction of
{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\mathrm{tot}},
via its matrix–vector product action:

v\;\mapsto\;\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Big({\mathbf{{S}}}_{\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}}{\mathbf{{U}}}^{(l)}\big({\mathbf{{U}}}^{(l)\mathrm{H}}\,({\mathbf{{S}}}_{\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}}^{\top}v)\big)\;+\;{\mathbf{{S}}}_{\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}^{c}}{\mathbf{{S}}}_{\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}^{c}}^{\top}\,v\Big),

(4)

with per-iteration complexity

\mathbf{\mathcal{{O}}}\big(R\sum_{l}|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}|+\sum_{l}|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}^{c}|\big)
.

Error Analysis

Let
\mathbf{E}^{(l)}
denote the residual error between the best rank-
R
approximation of the noisy submatrix
\tilde{\bm{\rho}}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{obs}}
and the ground-truth
\bm{\rho}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{E}^{(l)}=\tilde{\bm{\rho}}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{obs},R}-\bm{\rho}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{obs}}.
Let
\tilde{U}_{l}
and
U_{l}
denote their respective
R
-dimensional column spaces. Assume (i) the global subspace is uniquely identifiable; (ii) residual errors satisfy
\|{\mathbf{{E}}}^{(l)}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon,\;\forall l
; and (iii) a positive spectral gap,
\lambda_{R}\big(\tilde{\bm{\rho}}^{(l)}_{\mathrm{obs}}\big)\geq\delta>\epsilon>0,\;\forall l
. According to the perturbation bound established by
[
35
,
18
]
:

\|{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\tilde{U}_{l}}-{\mathbf{{P}}}_{U_{l}}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{2}\,\min\!\big\{\|\tilde{\bm{\rho}}^{(l)\dagger}_{\mathrm{obs},R}\|_{2},\,\|\bm{\rho}^{(l)\dagger}_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}\big\}\,\|{\mathbf{{E}}}^{(l)}\|_{F},

(5)

and since
\min\{\|\tilde{\bm{\rho}}^{(l)\dagger}_{\mathrm{obs},R}\|_{2},\|\bm{\rho}^{(l)\dagger}_{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{2}\}\leq 1/\delta
and
\|{\mathbf{{E}}}^{(l)}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}|}\,\|{\mathbf{{E}}}^{(l)}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\sqrt{|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}|}
, we obtain

\big\|{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\tilde{U}_{l}}-{\mathbf{{P}}}_{U_{l}}\big\|_{F}\;\leq\;\frac{\epsilon\,\sqrt{2\,|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}|}}{\delta}.

(6)

Note that
\|{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\tilde{Q}_{l}}-{\mathbf{{P}}}_{Q_{l}}\|_{F}=\|{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\tilde{U}_{l}}-{\mathbf{{P}}}_{U_{l}}\|_{F}
, since the padded parts are identical, they cancel out in the difference. Now, suppose the solution is obtained by solving (
3
), i.e., as the top-
R
eigenvectors of
\tilde{{\mathbf{{P}}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}
. The error bound between
\tilde{{\mathbf{{P}}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}
and its noiseless counterpart
{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}
is:

\big\|\tilde{{\mathbf{{P}}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}-{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}\big\|_{F}^{2}\;=\;\sum_{l=1}^{L}\|{\mathbf{{P}}}_{\tilde{Q}_{l}}-{\mathbf{{P}}}_{Q_{l}}\|^{2}_{F}\;\leq\;\frac{2\epsilon^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}|.

(7)

Finally, by applying the results of
[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
, the chordal distance between the (global) true column space
U
and its estimate
\tilde{U}
can be bounded as follows:

d_{c}(\tilde{U},U)\;\leq\;\frac{\epsilon\,\sqrt{2\sum_{l=1}^{L}|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}|}}{\delta\,\sigma_{\min}(\tilde{{\mathbf{{P}}}}_{\mathrm{tot}})}.

(8)

The global subspace error is bounded by the local noise-to-signal ratio
\epsilon/\delta
multiplied by an accumulation factor. If each submatrix contributes
d
new coordinates (so
|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}|=R+d
), then one possible construction—such as the overlapping block diagonal pattern shown in Fig.
1
—must satisfy
\cup_{l=1}^{L}\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}=[D]
to ensure coverage of all
D
coordinates. The orthogonal complement of each isorank submatrix provides
d
vectors orthogonal to the true
R
-dimensional column space, imposing
2Rd
real constraints. Since an
R
-dimensional subspace in
\mathbb{C}^{D}
has
2R(D-R)
intrinsic degrees of freedom, a necessary condition is
Ld\geq(D-R).
When
d=1
, the scheme provides sufficient conditions; for
d>1
, each submatrix provides more constraints, so fewer are needed. Larger, non-redundant overlaps introduce additional independent constraints in the concatenated matrix
\tilde{{\mathbf{{Q}}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}
(resp.
\tilde{{\mathbf{{P}}}}_{\mathrm{tot}}

III-C

From Subspace Identification to Full State Reconstruction

After computing a basis matrix
{\mathbf{{U}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{D\times R}
, we can recover
\bm{\rho}
column-wise.
Each column
\bm{\rho}_{:c}
can be expressed as
\bm{\rho}_{:c}={\mathbf{{U}}}\mathbf{x}
. If at least
R
entries of this column are known, we can generically obtain a unique solution
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{C}^{R\times 1}
by solving
{\mathbf{{S}}}_{\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}}^{\top}\bm{\rho}_{:c}=\bigl({\mathbf{{S}}}_{\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}}^{\top}{\mathbf{{U}}}\bigr)\mathbf{x}
in a least-squares sense.
Consequently, the estimated column is
\hat{\bm{\rho}}_{:c}={\mathbf{{U}}}\hat{\mathbf{x}}
. Alternatively, each entry
\bm{\rho}_{rc}
can be written as
\bm{\rho}_{rc}=(\mathbf{u}_{r:}\ast\overline{\mathbf{u}_{c:}})\,\bm{\lambda}
, where
\bm{\lambda}
is the vector of unknown eigenvalues, which can be estimated in a least-squares sense from the observed entries
(r,c)\in\cup_{l=1}^{L}\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}\times\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}.
Consequently, the full density matrix can be reconstructed as
\bm{\rho}={\mathbf{{U\Lambda U}}}^{\mathrm{H}},
where
{\mathbf{{\Lambda}}}=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\lambda})
.

III-D

Summary of Recovery Conditions

In Section
III-B
, we showed that the matrix
{\mathbf{{U}}}
, which holds an orthonormal basis for
\text{col}\left(\bm{\rho}\right)
, can be obtained from the isorank submatrices provided the corresponding ambient space subspaces satisfy
\dim\left(\bigcap_{l=1}^{L}Q_{l}\right)=R
. This condition is generically satisfied when some submatrices share at least
R
overlapping rows, i.e.,
|\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l_{i}}\cap\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l_{j}}|\geq R,
and every row appears in at least one submatrix, i.e.,
\cup_{l=1}^{L}\mathbf{\mathtt{r}}_{l}=[D]
; see
[
26
,
31
,
28
]
. Consequently, to recover the full density matrix uniquely, every column (resp. row) must contain at least
R
known entries, as discussed in Section
III-C
. The structured pattern shown in Fig.
1
satisfies these conditions generically.

Measurement Complexity

A rank-
R

D\times D
density matrix has
2RD-R^{2}-1
real parameters, implying that at least this many measurement are necessary for QST. Under random Pauli measurement settings, the sample complexity scales as
\mathcal{O}(RD\log^{2}D)
instead of
\mathcal{O}(RD)
, due to the coupon-collector–like effect
[
13
,
7

In standard selective QST, each entry requires a separate measurement, precluding reuse of prior data. In contrast, shadow tomography constructs classical shadows from
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\log(M/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
random Clifford or (biased) mutually unbiased unitaries and reuses them to estimate

entries within additive error

V
Conclusion

An algebraic QST approach is introduced for reconstructing generic low-rank quantum states, combining structured matrix completion and selective QST, with deterministic recovery guarantees. Numerical experiments show that the method yields accurate, valid results and is computationally fast. The estimation of structured entries via shadow tomography is briefly noted; future work will provide more detailed numerical experiments, show shadow-based estimation, and discuss practical (circuit-level) implementations.
