Abstract
. Videos make exercise instruction widely available, but they rely on visual demonstrations that blind and low vision (BLV) learners cannot see. While audio descriptions (AD) can make videos accessible, describing movements remains challenging as the AD must convey what to do (mechanics, location, orientation) and how to do it (speed, fluidity, timing). Prior work thus used multimodal instruction to support BLV learners with individual simple movements. However, it is unclear how these approaches scale to dance instruction with unique, complex movements and precise timing constraints. To inform accessible remote dance instruction systems, we conducted three co-design workshops (N=28) with BLV dancers, instructors, and experts in sound, haptics, and AD. Participants designed 8 systems revealing common themes: staged learning to dissect routines, crafting vocabularies for movements, and selectively using modalities—narration for movement structure, sound for expression, and haptics for spatial cues. We conclude with design recommendations to make learning dance accessible.



1.
Introduction

Millions of people now participate in exercises such as dance, yoga, and fitness using online videos.
As exercise videos allow people to learn on their own time and in their own environment, their popularity expanded with the COVID-19 pandemic
(
Nyberg et al. 2024
;
Sui et al. 2022
;
Delabary et al. 2022
;
Schmid and and 2022
)
and the corresponding rise in the use of video platforms
(
Mitkina et al. 2023
;
Nuhn et al. 2025
;
Warburton 2024
;
Hong et al. 2020
)
.
Such videos also have the potential to be particularly useful for people with disabilities as remote learning reduces travel barriers associated with in-person physical instruction
(
Jaarsma et al. 2014
;
lee 2014
)
, and increased physical activity can improve life satisfaction
(
Łabudzki and Tasiemski 2013
)
. Yet, this revolution in accessible fitness has systematically excluded blind and low vision (BLV) learners, as instructors primarily rely on visual demonstrations
(
Bläsing et al. 2018
)
that BLV learners cannot see.

Audio descriptions (AD) can make videos accessible by narrating the visual content that is important to understand the video. While AD is now common for TV shows and movies
(
Jordan and Oppegaard 2018
)
, it remains virtually non-existant for user-generated videos on platforms such as YouTube
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
and TikTok
(
Van Daele et al. 2024
)
. Thus, prior work explored manual
(
YouDescribe [n. d.]
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
, semi-automated
(
Pavel et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
, and automated
(
Van Daele et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
approaches to adding audio descriptions to videos. But, complex movements that appear in exercise videos remain challenging to describe with verbal narration alone
(
De Silva et al. 2023
)
as the AD must convey both
what
to do (complex body positions, spatial location, and orientation) and
how
to do it (speed, fluidity, pacing, and expressiveness). Even if the
instructions
of the video are understandable, it remains challenging for BLV audience members who can not visually compare their own progress to gain feedback
(
Huh et al. 2025
)
.

aPrior work thus explored how to provide accessible
feedback
to BLV learners with multi-modal approaches — from verbal feedback to correct yoga pose positioning
(
Rector et al. 2013
)
to haptic and audio feedback to improve performance in tennis
(
Morelli et al. 2010b
)
and bowling
(
Morelli et al. 2010a
)
exergames.
Such tools provide
feedback loops
that can improve performance and confidence
(
Nikooyan and Ahmed 2015
;
Todorov et al. 1997
)
, and alternative modalities (sound, haptic, tactile) that can support a more complete understanding of movements when combined with verbal narrations
(
Bläsing and Zimmermann 2021
;
Seham and and 2015
;
and 2023
)
. Dance movements in particular can be challenging to convey and correct due to the diverse, complex positioning of the body, such that prior work explored how to make movement instruction more accessible for in-person Contemporary dance classes with multiple modalities
(
De Silva et al. 2025
)
. While this rich body of prior work demonstrates that communicating and correcting individual movements to BLV learners is possible, it is not clear how to scale such knowledge to long, multi-move routines of online videos without the support of co-located human instruction and feedback.

In this work, we use a
participatory design approach
to investigate how to make remote dance learning accessible to BLV learners by including BLV learners and dance instructors as equal designers rather than mere consumers of technology.
We select dance as our domain of interest due to the rich complexity in movement mechanics, timing, and multi-movement sequences that makes dance particularly challenging to learn.
To co-design systems for remote dance instruction, we conduct
co-design workshops
with participants that have a wide range of relevant expertise to draw from complementary perspectives that are relevant to learning, instructing, and communicating around dance: BLV dance learners, dance instructors, and technical experts (in AD, sound, and haptics), along with participants with multiple types of expertise (
e.g.
, BLV dance instructors). Our workshops aimed to answer the research question:
How should we design systems to support remote dance instruction for BLV learners?

Over the course of three co-design workshops with eight design groups, 28 total participants generated considerations for accessible remote dance instructions and created eight unique system designs. Our analysis revealed several common themes across system architectures: using staged learning to disentangle placement and timing concerns, breaking routines into small movements, crafting multimodal vocabularies for movements (
e.g.
, unique names, or sound cues), and selectively using multiple modalities across learning stages —
e.g.
, narration for movement mechanics, sound for conveying expression, and haptics for spatial cues.
We conclude with design guidelines for accessible learning systems to inform future system design and reserach.

2.
Related Work

Our work relates to prior work on video accessibility, accessible movement instruction, and multimodal feedback for accessibility.

2.1.
Video Accessibility

Videos are a powerful medium for learning new skills, enabling learners to engage on their own schedule, replay segments, and control the pace
(
Chang et al. 2021
;
Huh et al. 2025
)
.
There is a plethora of dance educational videos online, yet BLV learners find it challenging to utilize them with underdescribed visuals. For instance, narration and on-screen text rarely describe what bodies are doing in sufficient detail for learning
(
Liu et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Peng et al. 2021b
)
.
Existing practice relies on
audio description
authored by volunteers
(
YouDescribe [n. d.]
)
or professionals
(
Pavel et al. 2020
)
, but authoring is time-consuming and coverage is limited. While recent systems explore automatic descriptions by identifying scene changes
(
Huh et al. 2023
;
Chang et al. 2024
)
or providing interactive access
(
Van Daele et al. 2024
;
Ning et al. 2024
;
Huh et al. 2022
)
, these tools primarily target high-level understanding in entertainment or general video contexts rather than conveying fine-grained, continuous motion needed for learning dance.

Beyond high-level AD, recent work has explored how-to video systems for BLV learners that scaffold procedural tasks (
e.g.,
cooking, crafts) by segmenting actions into discrete steps, aligning guidance to natural pauses, and providing correctness checks or alerts
(
Huh et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
;
Ning et al. 2025
)
.
However, dance features fluid transitions, overlapping actions across multiple body parts, and values expressing intent beyond right–wrong verification
(
De Silva et al. 2023
;
Ravn 2017
)
.
In this work, we focus on the underexplored setting of dance learning videos and investigate multimodal approaches that complement AD to make dance instruction more intuitive and efficient for BLV learners.

2.2.
Accessible Movement Instruction

Prior work explored movement instruction to BLV learners in in-person classes and game-like training tools.
In-person movement learning has emphasized embodied, multi-sensory pedagogy: pairing verbal guidance with physical in one-to-one settings to convey orientation, timing, and movement qualities that verbal description alone underspecifies
(
Seham and and 2015
;
De Silva et al. 2023
;
Aggravi et al. 2016
)
. Workshops in contemporary dance further explore sound cues and haptics to support spatialization and improvisation in dance
(
De Silva et al. 2025
)
. These approaches are effective in the studio but typically presume co-located instruction with touch, limiting remote access.

Exergames, or video games that integrate physical exercises into engaging experiences, often combine modalities that can increase engagement without vision. For instance, audio+haptic tennis
(
Morelli et al. 2010b
)
and bowling
(
Morelli et al. 2010a
)
have been shown to improve guidance and enjoyment.
Prior works have also explored sensor-based systems for
home exercise

(
Malik et al. 2021
;
Rector et al. 2013
)
, yet they focus on guiding stance and alignment by evaluating
discrete
poses, rather than the continuous transitions or expression central to dance. Feedback is typically stepwise (
e.g.,
verbal prompts, success tones) and correctness is judged at end-states (
e.g.,
hit/hold/balance), not on how motions unfold or whether movement
qualities
align with intent.
Dance learning from video, by contrast, demands precise timing, nuanced transitions between shapes, and attention to expressive dynamics.
Our work addresses this gap by exploring accessible dance education in
remote
video settings, which present unique opportunities and considerations for multimodal solutions.

2.3.
Multi-Modal Feedback for Accessibility

Across and beyond accessibility domains, combining modalities has often been shown to provide advantages over single-channel feedback.
In accessibility research, multimodal feedback has been used to guide both visual media consumption (
e.g.,
videos
(
Ning et al. 2024
)
, presentations
(
Peng et al. 2021a
)
) and creation tasks (
e.g.,
artboards
(
Zhang and Wobbrock 2022
)
, video editing
(
Huh et al. 2023
)
, DIY assembly
(
He et al. 2023
)
) for BLV people.
In museum contexts, pairing verbal description with non-verbal audio effectively communicates both objective detail and affect
(
Rector et al. 2017
)
. For dance, this can support rich information delivery: conveying objective elements (
e.g.,
timing, sequencing, spatial targets) as well as expressing higher-level movement qualities (
e.g.,
fluidity, energy, lightness) that non-verbal audio can carry
(
Frid et al. 2018
;
Landry and Jeon 2020
)
. As Blasing et al.
(
Bläsing and Zimmermann 2021
)
highlights
“the more modalities through which an observer has experienced a movement, the more enjoyment the observer derives from watching that movement”.
However, most prior work emphasizes observational experiences. Our work focuses on education, exploring how multimodal cues can complement and strengthen each other to make dance learning more precise and expressive for BLV learners.

3.
Method

We aimed to answer the following research questions:

RQ1

What are the current needs and design considerations of BLV learners seeking to learn dance on their own?

RQ2

How can we design accessible systems for dance learning with verbal narration, non-verbal audio, and/or haptics?

To incorporate the deep expertise and lived experience of relevant experts, we selected to use
co-design workshops
— a participatory design approach that invites community members (
e.g.
, BLV learners, dance instructors) to be creators and originators of the technology that they use rather than only end consumers
(
Magnusson et al. 2018
)
.
Similar to prior co-design workshops
(
Magnusson et al. 2018
;
Aflatoony and Lee 2020
;
Valencia et al. 2021
)
, we aimed to incorporate perspectives of not only potential end consumers of the technology (BLV dance learners) but also relevant experts in dance instruction (BLV and sighted dance instructors), and experts in multimodal technologies (audio description, sound design, haptics) to build on their complementary expertise.

3.1.
Workshop Design

Our in-person co-design workshops featured five stages (Figure
2
): (1) a pre-workshop
survey
to surface relevant individual dance backgrounds and lived experiences, (2) a large group
introduction
to share survey findings and discuss modalities for dance instruction, (3) small groups
teaching
example dances using non-visual techniques, then (4)
designing
systems for non-visual dance instruction, and (5)
presenting
their systems to back to the large group for discussion. The four in-person workshop phases are shown in Figure
1
.
Each in-person workshop was three hours long, and we compensated participants $90 for their participation. This study was approved by our institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB).

3.1.2.
Workshop Stages

We designed the workshop stages to maximize contributions by all workshop members, including using accessible workshop best practices (
e.g.
, all materials accessible, ask for accommodations in advance, provide multiple opportunities to update access requests) and using a mix of individual, small group, and large group activities. We structured large group activities to encourage participation from every workshop member (
e.g.
, think-pair-share, explicit turn order) and we selected small groups to represent diverse expertise to foster complementary contributions. These stages are shown in Figure
2
.

Figure 2.
Our workshop followed a five-stage process: 1) pre-workshop survey, 2) co-design workshop introduction, 3) teaching, 4) designing, and 5) presenting

Pre-Session Survey.
We invited BLV participants (13 participants) and dance instructors (11 participants) to share prior experiences with dance instruction in a pre-session survey. We asked BLV participants about their prior experiences learning movement skills, approaches for accessing remote movement education, and any benefits and challenges of remote instruction. We asked dance instructors about their teaching techniques, changes for remote settings and BLV settings (if applicable), and any observed or anticipated challenges of non-visual instruction. BLV dance instructors were asked both sets of questions.

From the survey results and our review of prior literature
(
Bläsing and Zimmermann 2021
;
De Silva et al. 2023
;
De Silva et al. 2025
)
we distilled three main themes for accessible remote dance learning to address during the co-design workshop: (1)
clarity
, as visual demonstrations are often not fully supplemented by other sensory forms, (2)
feedback
, as remote learners are currently unable to have their movements corrected by visual comparison or touch, and (3)
temporal and emotional qualities
, as existing verbal descriptions insufficiently convey fluidity, weight, timing, emotions, and textures in movements that are important for learning dance.

Introduction: 40 minutes.
We started each workshop with an interactive presentation with group discussions to build participant familiarity and prime considerations for the following small-group design exercises.

We first asked all participants and research staff to take turns introducing themselves to the group and answering the question “How would you describe what makes dance meaningful to you?” to consider aspects of dance they wanted to preserve in their later design exercise. We then shared survey themes around the benefits and challenges for accessible remote movement education.

We then presented a “toolbox” of non-visual teaching modalities with discussions and examples. Participants first discussed “What are non-visual ways to teach dance?” in pairs, then shared with the larger group (i.e. think-pair-share
(
Kaddoura 2013
;
Apriyanti and Ayu 2020
)
). We then presented examples of verbal audio description for dance, then examples of onomatopoeia and movement sonification (where movement qualities create sound). Following the examples, we asked participants via think-pair-share to discuss what was helpful or unhelpful in the examples for conveying movement.
We then presented and discussed haptics as a medium for movement instruction, explaining existing applications such as virtual exergames
(
Morelli et al. 2010a
)
and precise hand navigation
(
Hong et al. 2017
)
.

Teaching & Designing: 85 minutes.
Following the introductory presentation, we split participants into groups of 3-5 people with at least one BLV participant and one dance instructor per group (Table
2
). We assigned each group a dance tutorial clip around 2.5 minutes long as a reference point for creating concrete designs of dance tutorial systems. We typically formed groups such that multiple participants shared experience in a common dance style, and we selected the tutorial clip to match the dance style experience of the group. We also ensured that selected clips were narrated and suitable for beginners.
Partcipants remained with only their groups for the
teaching
and
designing
stages.

We asked dance instructors to
teach
BLV participants the content from the given dance tutorial clip to establish common ground and explore concrete examples of non-visual instruction.
We told dance instructors to pay attention to how they narrate instructions, provide feedback on movements, use tactile methods, and/or use sound cues and onomatopoeia. We told learners to identify techniques that were helpful or unhelpful.

3.2.
Data Collection and Analysis

All workshops and group sessions were audio-video recorded. Survey results, transcripts, and videos from session recordings were coded using existing frameworks for qualitative thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
;
Khandkar 2009
)
. After thorough open-coding of transcripts and survey results by four members of the research team, resulting codes were collaboratively organized into themes addressing RQ1 and RQ2.

4.
Results

In this section, we share participants’ strategies and challenges of current dance instruction, their preferred approaches for using different modalities in remote instruction, and their concepts for future multimodal systems for end-to-end remote dance instruction.

4.1.
Current Strategies and Challenges

4.2.
Modalities for Dance Instruction

Participants used a diversity of strategies to convey motion in the small group sessions (teaching phase, designing phase): verbal descriptions, non-verbal audio, and haptic or tactile approaches.

4.2.1.
Verbal Descriptions

All groups emphasized the importance of clear, consistent, and well-paced language that could describe movement mechanics, orientation, expression, and timing. These descriptions were broken down into manageable pieces, outlined into distinct vocabularies, incorporated with metaphors, and adapted based on learner experience and preferences. A8 explained the importance of descriptiveness in helping BLV learners visualize movements:

“When you’re teaching somebody, you have to talk every minute, every step. Everything that you’re doing, you have to talk about it and say what you’re doing. Because that’s the only way a person is going to be able to do it.”

High-level context

G5, G6, G8 provided high-level, cross-movement context, either before or during instruction of the routine. For example, before teaching, A8 explained the cha-cha clip by saying,
“The dance is working in opposites […] so if you go forward, you will complement that by going backwards.”
B8a noted that descriptions should first explain the rhythm using verbal counts before moving into defining the steps. On the other hand, B5a and B5b described the rhythm after movement definitions. D6 noted how it was better to use semantic cues that reference movements that happen at a specific rhythm, such as
‘forward’
,
‘back’
, and
‘close’
, instead of counts.

Takeaway:
Provide high-level, cross-movement context on dance routine patterns and rhythm.

Breaking down dance routines

Dance instruction involves teaching
routines
, or a sequence of movements occurring one after another. Some dances have designated names for specific movements that can facilitate the instruction of movements. However, D6, a dance historian, explained that current terms are not consistent across different dance styles or routines:
“If I say the word
forward
, that could be a whole bunch of different things. […] That could mean five steps forward or one step forward.”

All groups approached teaching routines verbally by breaking down routines into distinct movements, and labeling each movement of a routine in a
movement vocabulary
, examples of which are shown in Table
3
. B7a and B8b compared this vocabulary to a
’table of contents’
and a
’set of line items for the dance’
, respectively.

G3, G4, G5, G6, G7, and G8 emphasized the importance of clearly defining the
start position
of a dance routine, including the starting positions as an item in their movement vocabularies such as shown in Table
3
. B5a explained:
“Many people who try to learn from the video can see what she’s doing, but it doesn’t work for us. So trying to establish what’s my neutral position. Where my feet are, where are my arms?”

G2, G6, and G8 used memorable

Breaking down individual movements

Participants expressed that extensive, detailed verbal descriptions can be overwhelming for listeners and detract from the fun of learning dance. When defining the terms in their movement vocabularies, G4, G5, G6, and G7 concluded that definitions should be as descriptive as possible, but broken down into further pieces that are more manageable for learners. These pieces included descriptions of overall body movements and positioning, individual body part movements, and the movement of different body parts in relation to one another.

Many dance tutorials involve multiple body parts moving at the same time, as D4a emphasized:
“Dances will rarely be in just one body part. It will be in your leg, arm, torso, head, and other places at the same time.”
All groups found it important to
local descriptions
on what each body part is doing, including parts that remain still. D3 added on that it’s important to include how to orient individual body parts during motions, and “describing where each body part is on the 360 degree wheel.” Similarly, G2 and G5 discussed individual orientations of specific body parts such as the palms and arms.

While creating movement descriptions, groups created their own terms. D3 said ‘

“When I’m saying plant, that means in conjunction to whatever body part is moving. Like whenever I say pick something up and then plant it, it is usually referred to the same body part. […] And then if I’m saying something is stagnant […] it’s just still and […] we’re not engaging it.”

Provide verbal feedback by offering incremental corrections and affirmations. Adapt verbal descriptions based on the learner’s correctness and understanding of current descriptions.

4.2.2.
Non-Verbal Audio

Participants used sound cues and music to indicate specific movements, tempo, pacing and transitions, and feedback. Groups discussed considerations for combining sound with existing music.

Sound-to-movement mappings

Six groups found non-verbal audio helpful in creating sound-to-movement mappings, where each movement from the movement vocabularies are mapped to a distinct sound cue. G2 and G7 noted that this association would improve memory of the movements and provide independence for learners. Sound mappings were both direct (based on matching motion qualities to sound qualities) and semantic (based on metaphorical meanings of movements that match certain sounds).

G2, G4, and G5 incorporated
semantic mappings
. G2 chose sounds that metaphorically matched the how movements feel, such as a water splash sound to convey the fluidity of an arm movement. Additionally, G4 used a wind blowing sound for a three-step turn, which participants felt was reminiscent of a person being blown in the wind. D5 noted that contemporary dance allows for more freedom in how dancers decide to move. As such, their system provided different options for textures that learners could exhibit, each with a different sound cue.

G2, G3, G4, G5, G7, and G8 used
direct mappings
. G2, G3, G4, and G7 chose sounds matching the speed and rhythm of movements. For example, G2 used a wind sound that had the same speed and abrupt stop as the movement it was mapped to. G4 mapped the slow raising of the arms to the side of the body to a soft
violin vibrato
sound of the same duration. On the other hand, the abrupt moving of the hands to the chest was mapped to a
quick drum
sound. G4 additionally matched pitch to height for some sound cues — for example, they used a
rising harp
sound to indicate the arms moving up, and a
descending harp
sound to indicate a lunge. G8 decided to have a cue for each step or weight shift to match the rhythm. However, they differentiated between different types of steps with the sound being played. For example, a
cha
sound was used for each part of a Triple Step, whereas a
tha
sound was used for each rock in a Rock Step. To indicate directions, G8 incorporated spatial audio into sound-to-movement mappings. For example,
“Triple Step”
heard from the right ear would signal to the user that they should do a Triple Step to the right.

Takeaway:
Map items in the movement vocabulary to distinct sound cue, either semantically through metaphorical sounds or with direct associations between physical and sound attribute.

Sound for rhythm, pacing, and transitions.

G1, G2, G4, G7, and G8 used sound to ground learners in a dance routine. For example, G1 used a rising pitch to indicate the start of the routine and a falling pitch to indicate the end of a routine. Similarly, G7 used a bell sound to indicate the start of a routine. B7a emphasized how sound cues could help learners establish
when
they should be doing certain movements:
”If you hear that sound, and you’re not squatting yet, you know that you’re behind.”
G8 described how a distinct sound could play to indicate the transition
between
steps, such as switching directions from left to right.

G2, G5, and G7 used sound cues to help maintain
rhythm
. G7 used tapping and clapping sounds in their instruction to establish rhythm. On the other hand, G2, G3, and G5 used a metronome sound in the background of their tutorial to establish the tempo of the dance.

Takeaway:
Use sounds cues to ground learners on when movements happen, transitions within a routine, and rhythm of the routine.

Sound for emotions.

G5 used background tracks
separate from the music of the dance
, called
‘Incidental music’
, to establish the energy and emotion in their routine. D5 noted:
“In contemporary dance, we will have music softly playing in the background that isn’t giving a strict idea of the tempo, but is giving some idea of how the movement should be done.”
He then played an example sound track and asked participants to describe where the music took them emotionally before beginning instruction.

Takeaway:
Incidental music playing in the background during instruction may help establish emotion and energy of a dance.

Sound cues with existing music.

G1, G2, G6, and G7 discussed ways to combine sound cues with existing dance music. G2 and G6 noted that these combined sounds may be confusing. However, D6 highlighted that music is typically not included in instruction until after the movements are learned. Thus, these groups envisioned systems where sound cues would be introduced to aid instruction on movements before incorporating the music of the dance.

Furthermore, B1 preferred overlaying sound cues with music rather than words, as they were easier to process and pinpoint during instruction. Similarly, G7 discussed how overlaying sound cues can be used to show learners when movements take place once they have learned the movements themselves. G1, G2, and G4 used elements such as volume, pitch, and timbre to distinguish sound cues from the existing music.

Takeaway:
Introduce sound cues before incorporating dance music into instruction. When overlaying sound cues on music, use distinguishing elements, such as volume, pitch, or timbre.

Sound as feedback

G3, G6, and G8 incorporated sound cues as feedback. After deliberating on whether to give sound cues for both correct and incorrect motions, G6 decided that sound cues should play only for
positive feedback
. This would reduce discouragement and be less overwhelming for learners, as learners would hear an occassional positive cue rather than a constant series of negative cues as they attempt the movement multiple times.

G1, G3, and G6 incorporated live sound feedback on learners’ movements. G1 envisioned spatial audio providing
live feedback
on a learners movements. For example, as they moved their arm to the right, the sound feedback would pan to the right. B3b outlined a system where height and radius of the arm would be mapped to pitch and vibration intensity, respectively. G6 noted that having a sound of varying pitch to indicate how far a body part is from the target may be useful for conveying exact hand positioning. However, they concluded that
simpler sonification algorithms
with fewer variable elements would be easier for students to understand. For example, G6 discussed that one-dimensional sound feedback (such as only pitch mapped to height) is better than two or three-dimensional feedback.

Takeaway:
Use sound cues to deliver
positive feedback
on movements. When providing live feedback, use simple sonification mappings to convey body part positioning.

4.2.3.
Haptic and Tactile Input

G1, G3, G4, G6, G7, and G8 outlined the use of wearable haptic devices, tactile floor markers and mats, and 3D models for instruction and feedback. G7 noted how it is important for BLV learners that have haptic devices going on different places of the body to be easily differentiable through size, shape, or texture.

On-body instruction.

G1, G3, G4, G7, and G8 explored the use of on-body haptics for movement instruction. G1 proposed having haptic bands around the learner’s ankles or knees to instruct
limb placement
. Ankle devices would vibrate in the direction of movement, with intensity of vibration increasing with the end position’s distance from the center. D1 compared this to placing an elastic band around the feet, where tension should increase the further your feet move from the starting point.

G8’s cha-cha system involved a haptic device on each of the learner’s hands and on the back. Back-worn haptics would be used to first show feet placements for each of the step types. This was reenacted with B8a putting her hands on B8b’s back, shown as
‘Triple Step’
in Table
3
Hand-worn haptic devices were used to indicate left and right directions. With both back-worn and hand-worn devices, the intensity of the vibration would indicate the weight distribution of the step. D8a explained,
“If you’re putting all of your weight on one foot, maybe the intensity could be bigger. And then a slightly lighter, softer vibration means a tap with the toe, like with the Bachata.”

G7 proposed haptic devices on specific moving body parts. For their dance clip, this was the right arm and the left knee. Both devices would vibrate at the same time and keep vibrating for the duration of the movement to convey speed and length. Similarly, G8 discussed having hand-worn haptic devices provide consistent vibrations for the duration of a step to differentiate between steps of different lengths.

Takeaway:
Haptics for remote learning can function as a parallel to touch feedback to instruct foot positioning, body weight distribution, and movement duration.

On-body feedback

Haptics served as both confirmation of correct
positioning
and
negative feedback
to indicate mistakes. B3a mentioned that she thought about haptics being useful in the ‘reverse’:
“I would want haptics to tell me not when to move, but when to stop moving. For example, if I’ve moved too far, it would vibrate.”

G1’s prototype included devices around the hips and on the lower back. When a dancer had to rotate, the vibration would start in the direction that you are facing and would move to the direction of the rotation. H1 compared this system to a compass, where the direction of vibration always indicates the direction that the dancer should face.

G4 and G6 discussed that for dances where hand positions matter, having haptics or sound indicate when you’ve hit the correct position would be helpful. G3 discussed a system where there would be haptic bracelets on each wrist and ankle providing live feedback on hand positioning by vibrating with more intensity as the hands moved away from the starting position. However, S6 mentioned his experience of not being able to feel on-body haptic devices while moving during live performances. This outlines the importance of calibrating haptic device intensity, as discussed by G8, to be at a comfortable and helpful level.

Takeaway:
Wearable haptics can be used to provide feedback on limb positioning and body orientation.

Off-body haptics

G2, G3, G4, and G7 used the provided 3D models to learn
body poses
, by either having an instructor configure the model into the pose or by positioning the model into poses themselves and receiving confirmation. G3 and G7 explored having an automated 3D model that would be pre-programmed with specific poses that learners could feel. G6 and G8 described a tactile mat with markers and vibrations. Both groups incorporated a camera to feedback on the learner’s motions while the vibrating mat indicates where to move. D6 used physical anchors for instruction, having participants stand with their elbow to a wall to learn arm movements and better embody the kinesthetic metaphor in Table
4
.

G6 and G8 discussed off-body haptic
feedback
. G6 incorporated a textured ‘dance pad’, where teachers may tell learners to put their feet on spots with certain textures during instruction. Similarly, G8’s system involved a textured mat with indicators for orientation in degrees. G8 proposed having the mat pre-programmed with cha-cha steps. Thus, as the user completes the routine, the mat is able to record the movements and provide feedback on positioning and weight distribution.

Takeaway:
Off-body haptics offer external anchors that allow pose description and provide feedback on location and orientation.

4.3.2.
Phases of dance instruction

All groups identified different
phases
for teaching dance, including
learning phases
(focused on teaching movement vocabularies, cue mappings, and learning routines) and
practice phases
(focused on repetition and building muscle memory).

Learning phase.

All groups emphasized having detailed verbal descriptions defining movement vocabularies during early parts of learning. B5a described:

”If you were going to put this into three different parts, one of them is going to be the physical movement and positioning of the song. The other one is going to be talking about the differences in texture as they relate to you individually. And then a tempo and music to kind of put them all together.”

G2, G3, G4, and G5 noted that it is important to have specificity on the structure of the movements when first learning and less emphasis on feelings and textures in these early stages. Later, emotions, textures, and
how
to move would be introduced with non-verbal audio once dancers have practiced
what
to do. As this occurs, verbal descriptions also become less detailed. For example, after B8a Rock Steps and Triple Steps in detail, she described the routine as:
“Da da, cha cha cha”
to establish the rhythm, where
da
represented rocks and
cha
represented parts of the Triple Step.

G1, G2, G6, and G8 described having “onboarding” during the learning phase, where the multimodal movement vocabulary is introduced and users are able to get used to sound and haptic cues. B8b noted,
“If [the vocabulary] is something we have practice with, then you can associate them. And I think that has to be the first part […] what do the cues mean?”

Practice phase.

G2, G4, G5, and G6 outlined how the dance music should be introduced last when learning a routine. Music is therefore introduced during the practicing phase. D6 explained:

“In any dance class, we divorce the music from the steps and we add in the music later. First, we teach people how to do the motions and what their body positions are. Then, we see how it interacts with music.”

G1, G2, G4 (Figure
6
), and G7 (Figure
7
, Group 7) had sound and/or haptic cues transposed over the dance music during practice stages. By this point, cues would have become more ’automatic’, and dancers would have internalized that certain sound cues or vibration patterns correspond to specific movement patterns. D4b described that this would help learners
“understand where the motions are in the song”
.

Customized phases.

G5 discussed how these phases can differ based on prior knowledge. B5b explained,
“I think it depends on your experience, because I’m coming from no experience. So the importance to me is to let me know how to move my body.”
However, more experienced dancers may prioritize expressiveness as they are already familiar with movement structure.

Takeaway:
Systems should present modalities at different phases of the learning process, with verbal descriptions prioritized in early phases and non-verbal cues and music important in later, practice-heavy phases.

4.3.3.
Tradeoffs Between Modalities

Verbal descriptions were noted by G2, G3, G4, G5, and G7 as versatile for communicating details of body mechanics and positioning, but several groups also highlighted drawbacks. G2, G3, G6, and G8 described how short instructions lacked enough information to reconstruct a movement, while lengthy descriptions were inefficient compared to other modalities. Groups emphasized the need for consistent vocabularies, since without defined terms, verbal language risked multiple interpretations. D6 pointed out that even detailed verbal instructions could still result in divergent movements, emphasizing the importance of consistency and feedback to avoid ambiguity.

Sound cues were viewed as faster and more efficient than verbal descriptions, especially when overlaid with dance music. G1, G2, G4, and G8 described how learners could
“tune out words”
(P1) but process sound cues automatically once mappings were learned. Participants expressed that sound made the experience feel more natural and engaging, with P4 explaining,
“It’s nicer to dance to music.”
However, G3, G6, and G7 cautioned that mapping too many motion parameters to sound increased cognitive load. G6 concluded that simple mappings, such as pitch to height, were preferable in instructional contexts, even if they sacrificed representational accuracy.

Haptics were described by G1, G3, G7, and G8 as efficient and immediate, with learners able to process cues such as left–right vibrations more quickly than directional words. Participants emphasized that haptic cues also build muscle memory, allowing movements to become more internalized over time (P8a). In partner dance contexts, D8b noted that tactile input is essential for coordination and connection, which G8 sought to replicate through haptic design. Still, groups identified limits: G2 considered haptics overwhelming for contemporary dance with too many degrees of freedom, while G4 described haptic guidance as trial-and-error for complex motions. G8 also stressed cognitive limits, noting that learners could process only a few simultaneous taps before input became confusing, motivating them to restrict the number of haptic devices in their design.

Takeaway:
While verbal descriptions are versatile in conveying body mechanics, they are inefficient compared to non-verbal modalities. However, sound and haptic cues are less immediately interpretable than verbal descriptions.

4.3.4.
Repetition and Practice

All groups emphasized that repetition is important in dance education. G4’s workflow (Figure
6
, Group 4) had dancers start from the beginning of the sequence after adding one new motion each time. B4 noted,
“It helps build muscle memory to keep doing the same movements.”
Their system introduced verbal descriptions and sound effects intermittently. Dancers would learn 1-2 motions with verbal descriptions and sound cues. After new motions are added, the choreography is repeated from the beginning for reinforcement.

Takeaway:
Repetition of steps is important for dance instruction systems.

4.3.5.
Customization

G4 noted that differences in skill levels of learners may lead to differences in ’correctness’ criteria for feedback systems. For instance, since different users may have differences in flexibility, they determined that the system may or may not check if the dancer’s hands touched the ground during the lunge step when providing feedback.

G5’s system (Figure
7
, Group 5) was described as a ‘Choose Your Own Adventure’ game where users had different settings options, including level of detail in verbal descriptions, speed of the dance, adding a metronome beat, when to include sound cues, and when to include music. For beginners, G5 concluded that it is better to have verbal descriptions only, and gradually add non-verbal elements as the user becomes more proficient. However, for advanced learners, they preferred to have non-verbal elements incorporated at the beginning to establish fluidity, expressiveness, and tempo. G5 additionally noted that people have different preferences between auditory and tactile input.

Takeaway:
Dance instruction systems should be customizable to specific users based on correctness criteria, level of detail, level of experience, and modality preferences.

5.1.1.
Breaking Down Instruction.

We observed that groups provided high-level context for routines, broke routines down into movements, and further deconstructed those movements into lower-level movement descriptions (DG1-DG4).
A common existing approach to dance instruction for all learners is breaking down routines into more manageable pieces, such as into eight-counts.
Our workshops revealed that BLV students required more fine-grained dissecting of dance routines into distinct portions for learning movement structure.
This is accomplished through
multi-modal movement vocabularies
, and further breaking down of individual movements with
local and overall body descriptions
and
the relationship between body parts
.
As BLV learners can not directly preview the video, providing
high-level context
to frame the dance is also valuable.

In our study, participants typically used their own vocabularies to name and describe movements, occassionally using an existing movement term (
e.g.
, triple-step). However, prior work has proposed structured frameworks to describe movements. For example, the Locus framework
(
Graham 2016
;
Sulzman 1978
)
maps reference points in a body-sized cube to numbers or letters of the alphabet that are consistent across performances. To reduce cognitive load, our participants described motions locally—such as starting with the legs, then progressing to the torso, arms, and head—and separately combining them into a full movement (DG2). Similarly, Laban Movement Analysis (LMA)
(
Groff 1995
)
explicitly categorizes movements based on what parts are moving, orientation and direction of these parts, overall body shape, and dynamics such as weight and flow.
To increase consistency between dances, instructors or future systems could use such frameworks for movement instruction.

Further, BLV participants raised common stategies from their lived experience for conveying orientation and spatial information such as using the clock to convey orientation
(
Tabb 2025
)
. Thus, future instructors or systems could tailor their descriptions using best practices from orientation and mobility training or BLV learners’ own preferred terminology.

5.1.2.
Instructional Phases.

Accessible remote dance instruction systems should teach in phases (DG5-DG7). Our results conclude that early instructional phases should prioritize detail in vocabulary definitions, verbal descriptions, and specific feedback as users internalize movement structure. Following this, users are able to build practice through repetition and the emphasis on non-verbal cues to establish timing and expressive qualities. Eventually, users are able to explore how the routine interacts with music by overlaying the music on non-verbal cues, before eventually removing all cues.
Repetition was identified as essential for BLV learners to develop body awareness and internalize choreography, making structured practice phases (DG7) crucial for dance instruction systems. Our results found that repeating individual steps while learning a full routine is important for building confidence. Systems should allow for navigable steps that users can revisit and practice while putting routines together (DG6).
These learning phases reflect the Fitts and Posner model for motor skill learning, where learners gradually increase autonomy in motor skills with practice
(
Fitts and Posner 1967
)
. Insights on modality usage taken from our work can be used to extend this model into accessibility contexts to provide BLV learners with greater confidence and the practice required for autonomy in movement.

5.1.3.
Feedback

Our results showed that BLV learners benefit from immediate feedback to confirm orientation, spatial positioning, and overall confidence in their movements. Thus, we derived multimodal feedback techniques (DG8-DG11).
Our findings resonate with Hattie and Timperley’s Feedback Model
(
Hattie and Timperley 2007
)
for learning which outlines three questions that effective feedback should ask:
Where am I going?
(goals for movement),
How am I going?
(current progress towards goals), and
Where to next?
(corrections needed to reach goals). Future accessible dance instruction systems can incorporate this model into its feedback technique by providing clear descriptions of movement goals (DG3), multimodal affirmations on movement correctness (DG9, DG11), and incremental feedback to correct positioning and orientation (DG8, DG10).

5.1.4.
Customization

As outlined by participants and prior work
(
Aflatoony and Lee 2020
;
Jones et al. 2024
)
, customizable technologies improve the technology experience of people with disabilities. Our results showed clear differences across learners: some valued detailed auditory explanations, while others preferred tactile or haptic cues that allowed them to physically establish orientation and weight. Users additionally have varied requirements in the level of detail required in descriptions. Thus, remote education systems should allow customizable experiences that acccount for prior knowledge of dance style and skill level (DG12) and enable learners to configure systems based on modality preferences (auditory vs. tactile) (DG13).

5.1.5.
Temporal and Expressive Qualities

Many dance styles contain
temporal
(speed-based) and
expressive
(feeling-based) qualities that are critical for dance instruction
(
Blom and Chaplin 2004
)
. Non-verbal sound cues are an efficient way to convey such aspects that are difficult to verbalize concisely. Cues can be mapped semantically (DG14) or directly (DG15) to motions. Tactile metaphors and music clips can convey expressive movement qualities (DG16), while non-verbal sound cues can be used to establish tempo (DG17).
Our results, aligning with prior work
(
De Silva et al. 2023
;
Bläsing and Zimmermann 2021
)
, highlights the importance of sound cues and metaphors to convey nuanced movement qualities to BLV individuals. LMA’s
Effort
category captures temporal and expressive qualities of movements, such as
Weight
(strong vs. light),
Flow
(bound vs. free),
Time
(sudden vs. sustained), and
Space
(direct vs. indirect). This provides a systematic vocabulary for such dimensions that may be useful for future systems.

5.1.6.
Differences Between Dance Styles

By examining contrasts between groups, our workshops revealed differences in modality preferences and system designs across dance styles. Tactile and non-verbal communication are critical aspects of partner dancing
(
Grammer et al. 1998
)
, aligning with our findings where haptics was favored by all groups addressing partner dance clips (DG18, DG19). Haptic feedback is less descriptive than sounds and narration, and therefore less suited for conveying complex movements in expressive dance instruction systems (e.g., for contemporary dance). In such cases, sound cues and tactile metaphors may be beneficial (DG20).
More broadly, future systems may be customized for individual dance styles rather than assume a single system will suit all dances.

5.2.
Limitations

Our workshop focused on multi-modal technologies for remote dance education, and the specific dance styles considered were dependent on the background of the dance teachers within each group. Although our study found significant differences in system preferences across different dance styles, we did not consider all dance styles and future work with a narrowed focus on a specific style would provide more narrowed considerations.
Our workshops are exploratory and thus our design guidelines, system designs, and modality considerations are preliminary. Future work may explore empirical validation of design guidelines through prototype development and testing.
We provided participants with low-fidelity prototyping materials (
e.g.
, a simple soundboard, tactile stickers to represent haptics), but more advanced haptic devices, spatial audio systems, and automated movement analysis may expand the scope of design possibilities and limitations considered by the participants.

5.3.
Future work and implications

5.3.1.
Generalizability.

Multimodal instruction can generalize across movement practices, abilities, and learning preferences. Although our work focuses on dance, the design guidelines we propose extend to other domains where movement is taught through routines composed of specific poses and transitions. For example, martial arts
(
Luft et al. 2023
;
Jennings et al. 2020
)
and aerobics
(
Sun 2021
)
rely on choreographed sequences that combine discrete positions with fluid movements.
Similar to dance, these practices require learners to internalize not just static poses but timing, rhythm, and expressiveness of transitions. Our findings suggest that multimodal vocabularies, layered feedback, and staged practice could support instruction in these contexts as well. For instance, using existing or system-specific named poses could form a multimodal vocabulary for martial arts routines, while haptic cues could guide stance corrections or foot placement.

Non-verbal sound mappings could convey qualities such as speed, impact, or balance, paralleling how they are used to express weight and fluidity in dance.
Extending to other populations also reveals new opportunities for future work. For DeafBlind learners, systems could limit reliance on auditory feedback while still supporting rhythmic and expressive qualities through haptics. Furthermore, the decomposition of routines and movements to reduce cognitive load may be beneficial for neurodivergent populations
(
Stavraki [n. d.]
;
Sharer et al. 2016
)
.

5.3.2.
Instructor-authored systems for remote instruction.

While future systems may be fully automated (
e.g.
, to convert a dance video to an instructional system, similar to Vid2Coach for cooking videos
(
Huh et al. 2025
)
), dance instructors revealed that they may be interested in making custom material for systems (
e.g.
, D6 mentioned they would want to re-make a dance video with useful descriptions for BLV audiences). In the future, we may explore authoring systems for instructors seeking to create accessible asynchronous instruction materials. For example, the instructors could define their own movement descriptions, movement names, tactile cues, and haptic cues that could be flexibly applied by the system as students learn.

5.3.3.
Customizable vs. adaptive movement learning systems.

While our findings clearly reveal that BLV learners want to be able to customize their system experiences, this requires BLV learners knowing what options they want to use up front. In the future, we could explore closed-loop approaches to automatically adjust the descriptiveness level, modality level, or feedback type (
e.g.
, positive vs. corrective) based on the current performance of the learner. In the best case, learners may be able to adjust their customization preferences, then let the system adapt within their preferences to achieve a better outcome compared to what learner-driven customization or system-driven adaptivity could do alone.

6.
Conclusion

Routine-based exercise video accessibility, along with accessible body movement education as a whole, is an underexplored area of research in HCI. We conducted three co-design workshops with BLV individuals, dance instructors, and domain-specific experts (i.e., sound, haptics, AD) to understand user needs and preferences for remote dance instruction systems. We identified the challenges BLV learners face when learning dance with videos, and we examined how alternative modalities—verbal, sound, haptic, and tactile—can be orchestrated across stages of learning to provide feedback-oriented, customized instruction. From these findings, we developed a set of design guidelines (DG1–DG20) that address instruction of movement structure, feedback, practice, personalization, rhythm, and expressiveness. From the insights gained in this work, we can inform the design of systems addressing body movement education for a diverse set of populations and activities involving sequences of movements, movement expression, and precise timing.
