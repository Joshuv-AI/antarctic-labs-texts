Euphorbia kansui for latent virus reactivation in people living with HIV on suppressive antiretroviral therapy: A phase 1b/2a clinical trial
Authors: Jingna Xun, Xinyi Yang, Jun Chen, Luling Wu, Jiangrong Wang, Zichen Song, Li Liu, Zhenyan Wang, Wei Song, Tangkai Qi, Yang Tang, Junyang Yang, Min Zhang, Wenkang Jin, Meiyan Sun, Chaoyu Chen, Yueming Shao, Zhihang Zheng, Yuqi Zhu, Xinyu Zhang, Renfang Zhang, Yinzhong Shen, Hongzhou Lu, Huanzhang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ‘Shock and Kill’ strategy is pivotal in the quest for an HIV/AIDS cure. Our study explores Euphorbia kansui (Kansui), a traditional Chinese medicine, for its potential to reverse HIV latency while rigorously assessing safety.MethodsWe conducted a phase 1b/2a clinical trial at the Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center (NCT04503928). People with HIV (PWH) on ART were assigned to three groups: 1 g qd (once per day) for 7 days, 1g bid (twice per day) for 7 days, or 1 g qd for 14 days. Blood was collected on Days 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 14, and 21. Primary en dpoints included changes from baseline in cell-associated (CA) HIV RNA and plasma HIV viral load, while secondary endpoints involved integrated HIV DNA levels and safety assessments.ResultsNine PWHs male participants (mean age: 38.78 ± 13.85 years old) with a median antiviral treatment duration of 49 months, were enrolled in the clinical study. HIV latency reversal was primarily observed within the first seven days following Kansui administration. A ≥2-fold increase in cell-associated (CA) HIV RNA was detected in four participants, three of whom received the 1 g qd dose. Additionally, two participants exhibited a transient increase in plasma HIV RNA to >50 copies/mL, with one case in the 1 g qd for 7 days group and the other in the 1 g qd for 14 days group. Integrated HIV DNA levels transiently increased during the intervention and then quickly returned to baseline levels without significant decline. Kansui demonstrated a favorable safety profile, with no serious adverse events reported and only mild adverse events observed.ConclusionKansui can reactivate latent HIV viral reservoirs at a low-level in vivo and is well tolerated by PWHs on antiretroviral therapy which warrant further investigation.Keywords: Kansui, HIV, Shock and kill, Clinical trials, Efficacy, Safety

Introduction

HIV continues to pose a significant global health burden. Over 30 antiretroviral drugs have been developed and used globally, which effectively reduced morbidity and mortality rates among people with HIV (PWHs).1,2 Despite these advances, a major obstacle to achieving a definitive cure remains the persistence of latently infected HIV reservoirs within host cells.3,4 Consequently, the development of novel therapeutic strategies to eradicate these HIV reservoirs has become a key focus in HIV cure research.

In recent years, various functional cure strategies aimed at eliminating or controlling the HIV latent reservoir have been proposed, such as gene editing, stem cell transplantation, immunotherapy, and immune cell reprogramming, among others.5,6 Among these, one of the most representative and extensively studied strategies is the ‘Shock and Kill’ strategy. The core concept of this strategy is to first use latency-reversing agents (LRAs) to ‘shock’ silenced HIV proviruses, inducing their transcription and the production of viral antigens; subsequently, relying on the host immune system (such as cytotoxic T lymphocytes, CTLs) or in combination with other clearance methods to ‘kill’ these infected cells that have resumed HIV expression, thereby reducing or even eliminating the latent reservoir with the aim of achieving a functional cure through viral suppression.7 Numerous research groups worldwide have screened various LRAs as part of this strategy to disrupt latency.8 Several FDA-approved drugs, including vorinostat, valproic acid (VPA), and disulfiram, have shown promise and are currently being tested in clinical trials for repurposing as agents to target HIV latent reservoirs.9

Traditional Chinese medicine is increasingly gaining recognition in modern medicine due to its wide variety, extensive clinical applications, mild therapeutic effects, and minimal side effects.10, 11, 12 Euphorbia kansui (Kansui) is a traditional Chinese medicinal herb listed in the Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China (2020 edition) and possesses diuretic, anti-edematous, and anti-inflammatory properties.13 Using screening techniques for anti-HIV latency drugs, my team developed a high-throughput screening method based on an HIV latent infection model and has determined that Kansui can effectively activate the HIV latent reservoir in vitro and in animal experiments.11 Further isolation and purification of the active components of Kansui yielded more than 20 individual compounds capable of effectively reactivating latent HIV in vitro and in vivo.14,15 Among these, EK-16A can induce high levels of latent HIV activation even at extremely low concentrations, with EC50 values for activating the latent HIV reservoir in multiple HIV-infected cells of 3.53 nM and 4.06 nM, respectively—200-fold more potent than Prostratin, an internationally recognized potent activator of the same class.11 A mouse model of HIV latently infected cell transplantation also confirmed that EK-16A can activate latent HIV in mice.15 Subsequently, based on synergy assays with other latency activators and drug inhibition experiments, it was preliminarily found that EK-16A activates latent HIV primarily through the NF-κB-mediated Protein Kinase C (PKC) pathway,11 however, the specific targets and underlying mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated.

Building upon these findings, the present study aims to further investigate the effect of Kansui on the HIV latent reservoir in PWHs. Specifically, we conducted a clinical trial examining the use of Kansui in combination with antiretroviral therapy (ART), representing a novel application of an existing herbal remedy in HIV treatment.

Materials and methods

Participants and clinical trial design

We enrolled PWHs aged ≥18 but ≤70 years who received ART for at least two years with viral suppression (<50 copies/mL) for at least 1.5 years. The baseline CD4+ T cell counts of participants were greater than 350 cells/μL. The exclusion criteria include acute HIV infection, receiving a blood transfusion or hematopoietic growth factor treatment within 3 months, receiving compounds containing other LRAs within one month, having any serious acute illness within the past 8 weeks, and having a history of active infection with hepatitis B or hepatitis C infection. This study was approved by the Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center (SHPHCC) Ethics Committee (2020-S119-02) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants have signed a written informed consent form. The study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04503928).

Preparation of Kansui paste pills

The processing procedure was conducted in accordance with ‘the 2018 Shanghai Guidelines for Processing Chinese Herbal Medicinal Materials’.16 Raw Kansui originating from Shaanxi Province was soaked in water for 3–5 days, with the soaking water replaced twice daily, until the inner core was no longer dry. The material was then thoroughly rinsed, transferred to a pot, and combined with water and tofu, for every 100 kg of raw Kansui, 50 kg of tofu was used to reduce toxicity. The mixture was boiled until the core was no longer white. The herbal material was then removed, the tofu discarded, and the product sun-dried or air-dried until the exterior was dry while the interior remained slightly moist. Subsequently, the material was cut into thick slices, fully dried, and sieved to remove impurities.

The processed slices were then combined with dextrin to prepare the Kansui Paste Pills: 1 g of Kansui slices and 0.4 g of dextrin were mixed to yield approximately 1.4 g of a soft mass. This mass was shaped using a pill-forming machine into 10 pills, each weighing approximately 0.14 g. Ten pills were packaged per sachet (1 g per sachet), resulting in the final Kansui Paste Pills formulation.

Procedures of Kansui administration to PWHs

Participants were stratified into three distinct treatment groups (1) 1 g of Kansui administered daily for 7 consecutive days (1 g qd, 7 days), (2) 1 g of Kansui twice daily for 7 consecutive days (1 g bid, 7 days), and (3)1 g of Kansui administered daily for 14 consecutive days (1 g qd, 14 days). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected at the following time points: Day 0 (baseline), Day 1, Day 3, Day 5, Day 7, Day 14, and Day 21. ART regimens remained unchanged and were continuously maintained throughout the duration of the Kansui intervention (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of Kansui trials. Two stages of trials were studied. The symbol '+' denotes the time points for peripheral blood collection.

The detection of cell-associated HIV RNA and plasma viral loads

Cellular RNA was extracted by HiPure Total RNA Plus Mini Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China), and then cell-associated (CA) HIV RNA was reverse transcribed. The cDNA was amplified and quantified using a CA HIV RNA quantitative detection kit (SUPBIO, Guangzhou, China). Plasma HIV RNA viral loads of all the participants were determined by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (Cobas Amplicor, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) at the medical test center in SHPHCC. The lower limits of detection for the above three indicators are 20 copies/mL.

Measurement of integrated HIV DNA

Integrated HIV DNA was measured by an integrated HIV DNA quantitative detection kit (SUPBIO, Guangzhou, China), following the manufacturer's protocol, in which the primers were designed to target the conserved Alu and HIV LTR sequences in the human genome. A two-step nested PCR approach was utilized for enhanced accuracy and sensitivity. The first PCR round amplified both the Alu sequence and integrated proviral HIV DNA, followed by specific amplification of integrated HIV DNA in the second round. In the quantification process, following the acquisition of quantitative integrated HIV DNA values (copies/μL) alongside quantitative cell counts (cells/μL), the calculation of integrated HIV DNA (copies per million PBMCs) was meticulously performed. The lower detection threshold of integrated HIV DNA was established at 10 copies/106 PBMCs, with a quantification range spanning from 10 to 106 copies/106 PBMCs.

Study endpoint

The primary endpoint of this study was the criteria for latent HIV activation as a more than 2-fold increase in CA HIV RNA levels and/or a viral load greater than 50 copies/mL (17,18). The secondary study endpoints were assessed the level of integrated HIV DNA for the measurement of HIV reservoir change and measured by the assessment of adverse events (AEs) to determine the incidence of adverse events. For each AE, we assess its relationship with Kansui treatment and grade its severity according to the Common Terminology Criteria for AE (version 5.0).

Flow cytometry

PBMCs were resuspended and then washed with in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), incubated with LIVE/DEAD fixable aqua stain (Invitrogen) for 30 min at room temperature, after washed by staining buffer (Gibco) and continued with surface staining with antibodies to CD3-APH-H7 (BD Pharmingen), CD4-FITC (BD Pharmingen), CD8-APC (BD Pharmingen), CD45RA-BV711 (BD Horizon), CD197 (CCR7)-PE (Miltenyi Biotech), CD69-BV605 (BD Horizon), CD38-PE-Cy7 (Biolegend) and HLA-DR-Percp/cy5.5 (BD Pharmingen) for 20 min on ice. All of the antibodies were used at a 1:100 dilution in the staining buffer. Cells were then acquired on a BD LSR-Fortessa and analyzed using FlowJo version 10 (Fig. S1).

Statistical analysis

If the data followed a normal distribution and if the sample size was small, a student's t-test was employed. If the data did not meet the assumptions of the t-test, non-parametric tests were resorted. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) and SPSS 23.0 statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants enrolled in the study

A total of 14 PWHs were screened, 9 participants were enrolled in this clinical study, of the five PWHs who failed to enroll, two had abnormal liver function, one was diagnosed with syphilis, and two were unable to complete scheduled follow-up. The baseline demographic characteristics of these 9 participants are summarized in Table 1. All participants were male, with a mean age of 38.78 ± 13.85 years. The median duration of ART was 49 months (range: 21.0 to 126.6 months). The mean baseline CD4 count was 707.4 ± 194.1 cells/μL, and the median baseline CD4/CD8 ratio was 0.75 (range: 0.50 to 1.07). Seventy-seven percent of participants were receiving a regimen consisting of two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) in combination with a non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI), with six out of seven participants receiving ART based on efavirenz (EFV), the EFV based approach is still widely adopted as a first-line ART option due to its proven antiviral efficacy, good accessibility, and cost-effectiveness. While 22.2% were receiving protease inhibitors (PIs) as part of their ART regimen. The ART regimens remained unchanged throughout the trial.

Spikes in viral transcription and plasma HIV RNA during Kansui treatment

Out of the nine participants, five received the 1 g qd-7 days intervention (KS001, KS002, KS003, KS005, KS006), two received the 1 g bid-7 days intervention (KS009, KS010), and two received the 1 g qd-14 days intervention (KS012, KS013). Among all participants, four (KS001, KS002, KS005, KS009) showed elevated CA HIV RNA levels during the first 7 days of Kansui treatment. Specifically, three participants (KS001, KS005, KS009) exhibited an average 4.22-fold increase in CA HIV RNA by Day 3. One participant (KS003) demonstrated a greater than 2-fold increase in latency reversal by Day 21. In plasma, participant KS006 experienced a viral load rebound (>50 copies/mL) on Days 1 and 14 following Kansui administration, while KS013 showed a significant plasma viremia rebound on Day 5, which returned to baseline levels by Day 7 (Fig. 2). Based on the defined criteria for latent HIV activation, 6 of 9 participants (66.7%) experienced low-level virologic rebound. These observations suggest that once a participant is sensitized to Kansui, viral rebound-either in CA HIV RNA or plasma viral load-typically occurs within the 1 g qd-7 day intervention period.

The impact of Kansui on the size of HIV reservoirs in PWHs on ART

To assess whether Kansui-mediated reversal of HIV latency affected the viral reservoir, we quantified integrated HIV DNA using double fluorescence quantitative PCR. During the early phases of the Kansui intervention, specifically on day 1 and day 3, we observed 2-fold increasing in integrated HIV DNA levels. However, following this transient increase in HIV proviral DNA, a discernible decline in integrated HIV DNA levels was observed, with levels stabilizing and returning to baseline by day 7 post-intervention. On day 14, a subsequent wave of proviral HIV DNA induction occurred in 5 participants, but by day 21, the proviral HIV DNA levels in these participants decreased again. The remaining participants maintained low levels of proviral HIV DNA, with the exception of participant KS002 (Fig. 3).

Change in integrated HIV DNA post-Kansui administration. The black dashed line indicates integrated HIV DNA levels less than a two-fold change.

Overall, these findings suggest that while the integrated HIV DNA levels transiently elevated following Kansui treatment, they ultimately stabilized without a significant long-term decrease.

Kansui intervention increased the proportion of effector memory T cells among CD8+ T cells

Subsequently, we evaluated the impact of Kansui intervention on T cell subsets.

Given the exploratory nature of this immunological analysis and the limited number of participants in each dosing cohort, we focused this analysis on participants (KS001, KS002, KS003, KS005, KS006) who had received the 1 g qd for 7 days regimen to provide a more consistent longitudinal assessment of treatment-associated immune changes, designating them as the primary study cohort. In CD4+ T cells, no significant changes were observed across the Naive T Cell (TN), Central Memory T Cell (TCM), Effector Memory T Cell (TEM), and Effector Memory Cells re-expressing CD45RA (TEMRA) subsets at any of the time points analyzed (Fig. 4A and 4B-E, left). In contrast, within the CD8+ T cell population, the TCM subset showed a significant decrease on Day 5 (P = 0.04), while the TEM subset exhibited a significant increase on Day 5 and Day 14 (P = 0.007 and P = 0.04, respectively). No significant changes were noted in the TN and TEMRA subsets (Fig. 4A and 4B-E, right).

We further explored the activation of TEM cells in CD8+ T cells. Compared to baseline levels, CD69 expression in CD8+ T cells significantly increased on Day 1 (P = 0.003) and Day 7 (P = 0.02), while the expression of HLADR+CD38+ in CD8+ T cells showed a downward trend on Day 5 (P = 0.02), returning to baseline levels and stabilizing at subsequent time points (Fig. 5A–B). Finally, we observed that peripheral blood CD4+ T cell counts, CD8+ T cell counts, and the CD4/CD8 ratio remained stable throughout the treatment and observation periods, with no significant changes (Fig. 6 and Fig. S2).

Kansui was safe and well tolerated, especially in the low-dose group

Throughout the study period, no severe AEs were reported. A total of 23 AEs were documented, of which 21 were deemed related to the Kansui intervention (Table 2). The most frequently reported AE was diarrhea, observed in 5 cases (21.7%), of which 2 were classified as grade 2. The remaining 16 AEs associated with the intervention were mild (grade 1) and resolved spontaneously within a few days. Notably, no grade 3 or higher serious AEs were reported in connection with the Kansui intervention.

Discussion

The ‘shock and kill’ strategy aims to reactivate latent HIV reservoirs using LRAs under ART, followed by immune-mediated viral clearance through cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), with the ultimate goal of achieving a functional cure for HIV. The findings of this clinical study demonstrate that Kansui can reactivate latent HIV viral reservoirs at a low-level in vivo. The safety profile of Kansui was favorable, with no serious AEs reported, and the AEs observed were generally mild.

Kansui is an herbal substance extracted from the root of the Euphorbia kansui plant, known for its medicinal properties. However, it also carries some degree of toxicity, particularly hepatotoxicity and gastrointestinal side effects, which have limited its clinical applications. To mitigate these toxic effects, Kansui was processed using tofu, a method outlined in the ‘Guidelines for the Processing of Traditional Chinese Herbal Beverages in Shanghai, 2018’ 16, which has been shown to reduce toxicity. During the clinical study, diarrhea emerged as the primary adverse effect of Kansui in PWH on ART. Five participants experienced diarrhea, all of which were less than grade 3 and did not require dose reduction or discontinuation. One participant experienced a grade 2 adverse event, which was managed with Montmorillonite Powder. Although latency reversal was observed in vivo, the optimal dose of Kansui for HIV transcriptional activation has yet to be determined. Therefore, clinical dosing of processed Kansui should be approached with caution. Further research is needed to explore methods to reduce Kansui's toxicity without compromising its efficacy.

We noted that 44.4% of participants (4/9) showed increased levels of CA-HIV RNA during the treatment period, including one participant in the high-dose group (1g bid). In addition, two participants (KS006 and KS013) experienced a low-level increase in plasma viral load (>50 copies/mL). Although the magnitude of HIV reactivation was modest, these findings provide preliminary evidence of in vivo HIV latency reversal following Kansui administration. The absence of detectable plasma viral rebound in the remaining participants may reflect interindividual differences in drug exposure, host factors, or HIV reservoir characteristics. Interestingly, two participants who experienced a low-level rebound in plasma viral load without a detectable increase in CA-HIV RNA. This apparent discrepancy may reflect the distinct kinetics of intracellular HIV transcription and viral particle production and release, which are not necessarily synchronized.19 In addition, host immune responses and individual viral dynamics may further influence the level of plasma viremia. Thus, changes in plasma viral load and CA-HIV RNA may capture different aspects of HIV reservoir activity and should be interpreted together.20 Future research will first build upon the current 1g qd dosing regimen for Kansui combining pharmacokinetic assessments with weight-based dose optimization, which may help elucidate the relationship between Kansui drug exposure and HIV reactivation. At the same time, it remains to be seen whether combining Kansui with interventions that enhance viral expression downstream and/or promote immune-mediated clearance of HIV-infected cells can improve the overall efficacy of reversing latency.

Kansui treatment did not show a significant impact on the size of the HIV reservoir, i.e. HIV proviral DNA. Previous investigations into LRAs, such as histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACis) and PKC agonists, have demonstrated minimal clearance of HIV reservoir in vivo due to cytotoxicity and CTL dysfunction21, 22, 23. A more recent study on noncanonical NF-κB agonist AZD5582 reported successful induction of high levels of HIV latency reversal in animal models of HIV latency in vivo, yet failed to detect reductions in total or replication-competent viral reservoirs unless an anti-SIV specific antibody was applied.24,25 The aforementioned studies on latent activators targeting the HIV reservoir emphasize that, without an effective mechanism to ‘kill’ reactivated cells from the reservoir, simply reversing the latent state is unlikely to reduce the size of the viral reservoir.

We observed transient elevations in proviral HIV DNA in some participants, who also experienced elevated CA-HIV RNA or plasma viral load during the Kansui intervention. This elevation may reflect new integration events associated with viral replication following Kansui-induced HIV reactivation, as no integrase inhibitors were added to the ART regimens during the intervention. However, given the small sample size and the lack of direct evidence of new integration events, this explanation remains speculative and requires further investigation. Intriguingly, proviral DNA was largely returned to the basal levels at day 21 except for participant KS002.

If enhanced immune responses occur during HIV reactivation, it would be beneficial for the host in controlling the virus. This increased immune activation could facilitate the recognition and clearance of reactivated HIV-infected cells, potentially leading to a reduction in viral reservoirs and contributing to viral suppression.26 Considering that Kansui increased the proportion of effector memory T cells among CD8+ T cells, we hypothesized that, over time, CD8+ T cells played a key role in Kansui-induced clearance of HIV-infected cells that reactivate HIV thereby gradually reducing the size of the reservoir. Further analysis of TEM immune activation in CD8+ T cells also reveals a sustained trend of elevated expression of the early activation marker CD69 after Kansui intervention. Unfortunately, due to the small sample size and insufficient PBMCs, we were not able to detect continuous changes in CD38+HLADR+ expression levels, nor were we able to further validate the functional effects of Kansui treatment on HIV-specific CD8+ T cells by HIV peptide libraries.5 Thus, there remains a lack of sufficient evidence for the effect of Kansui on CD8+ T cell function.

Our findings may lay the groundwork for future HIV eradication trials involving Kansui, however, it is crucial to acknowledge the limitations inherent in this study. Firstly, the restricted volume of blood samples necessitated the assessment of our repository in unsorted PBMCs, limiting our ability to specifically characterize the repository size of resting CD4+ T cells, meanwhile, this study used a PCR-based HIV DNA assay to estimate the size of the HIV reservoir rather than a method specifically designed to quantify intact proviruses. Although this approach has been widely used as a surrogate measure of the HIV reservoir, it may include defective proviruses and therefore cannot directly reflect the replication-competent reservoir. Future studies incorporating assays such as the intact proviral DNA assay (IPDA) may provide an estimate of the frequency of near-full-length HIV genomes,27 however, the capacity of these genetically intact proviruses to be reactivated remains to be further determined. Secondly, the intervention period was deliberately kept short, and the long-term efficacy of Kansui was only preliminarily explored. Third, due to the absence of available effective and referable standardized products for Kansui, the pharmacokinetics of Kansui was not assessed in vivo in this study. Fourth, the study did not include a control group, which limits our ability to draw definitive conclusions about the causal relationship between Kansui treatment and the observed outcomes. It is important to note that the enrollment for clinical trials exclusively included male participants. As a result, caution should be exercised in generalizing the study's conclusions to female PWHs. Lastly, due to a number of uncontrollable factors during the study period, only 2 participants were recruited in the high-dose group and 1g qd for 14 days group, failed to conduct in-depth statistical analysis.

Although this exploratory clinical study did not demonstrate a measurable reduction in the HIV reservoir, the observed induction of HIV latency reversal, together with an acceptable safety profile, provides preliminary support for further evaluation of Kansui-derived compounds as potential components of HIV cure strategies. Larger, controlled clinical studies incorporating optimized dosing regimens and comprehensive reservoir measurements will be required to define the biological and clinical impact of Kansui-based interventions. A major challenge of the ‘Shock and Kill’ approach is that latency reversal alone is unlikely to achieve substantial reservoir reduction, as the efficient clearance of reactivated HIV-infected cells remains a critical barrier. Future studies should therefore investigate combination strategies that integrate Kansui-derived latency-reversing agents with immune-based interventions, including therapeutic vaccination, broadly neutralizing antibodies, immune checkpoint modulation, or engineered immune cell therapies, to enhance immune-mediated elimination of infected cells.

As a natural product-derived intervention, Kansui and its bioactive constituents, including EK-16A, also face challenges inherent to botanical therapeutics, such as complex chemical composition, variability of active components, and batch-to-batch reproducibility. Addressing these challenges will require standardized manufacturing processes, comprehensive chemical profiling, rigorous quality control, and mechanistic studies to define the contribution of individual constituents and optimize therapeutic formulations. Collectively, these findings provide a rationale for continued investigation of Kansui-derived latency-reversing strategies, with future efforts focused on optimizing clinical dosing, evaluating rational combination approaches, and elucidating the molecular mechanisms and active constituents responsible for HIV latency reversal in vivo.
