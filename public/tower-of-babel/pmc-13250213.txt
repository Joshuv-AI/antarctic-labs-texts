Reflections on a global partnership for perinatal quality improvement in Rwanda
Authors: Lisine Tuyisenge, Jean-Petit Habonimana, Alexis Nsengiyumva, Francois Regis Cyiza, Emmanuel Manzi, Samson Desie, Alexandra Pledge, Sarah McMurtrie, Clemence Muzard-Banes, Marie Bontoux, Sebastian Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBetween 2017 and 2023, the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health and the Rwanda Pediatric Association (its peer institution in Rwanda) collaborated with UNICEF, the Rwandan Ministry of Health and the Rwanda Biomedical Center to design and deliver a perinatal programme to improve clinical care and outcomes across multiple hospitals and selected primary health centres. Over the period, the programme showed measurable positive impact on quality of care and clinical outcomes. However, the persistence of preventable or avoidable mortality, in particular among newborns in hospital and health centres, highlights the influence of deeper systemic constraints on care quality and outcomes within the network of clinical care facilities, as well as in wider community-level determinants of health. Quantitative results are neither the only nor, arguably, the most important aspect of the kind of programme presented here. An equal value lies in qualitative understanding of how a programme of ‘quality improvement’ interventions, delivered continuously over time in multiple facilities through a professional partnership between equitably authoritative international and Rwandan counterparts, can reveal underlying structural factors that mediate the translation of clinical interventions into effective care systems.Keywords: Child Health, Health Policy, Health services research, Low and Middle Income Countries, Neonatology

Background

Rwanda has achieved significant progress in the health of its population, including mothers, babies and children, over the last three decades. Much of this has been accomplished through effective, large-scale preventive and public health interventions targeting community and primary levels of care, making significant inroads into major childhood communicable diseases, including lower respiratory infections, diarrhoeal disease, measles and malaria.1 Gains have been underpinned by strategic leadership from the Ministry of Health and the Rwanda Biomedical Center (RBC), fostering a decentralised, evidence-based and accountable health system. Government has also worked to mitigate geographical and financial barriers to maternal and newborn care through the equitable expansion of health infrastructure and successful implementation of community-based health insurance (the Mutuelles).2 3

More recently, however, progress in particular in newborn mortality has decelerated, accompanied by a rising recognition that with increased newborn survival come new challenges in early childhood development. Recognising these issues, the Rwandan Ministry of Health and RBC have extended their strategic attention beyond considering numbers of people able to access healthcare, to improving the quality of clinical care for mothers, newborns and children through the national network of district and provincial hospitals (and including a formal process of facility accreditation). Supporting this strategic development, between 2017 and 2023, the Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health (RCPCH Global) worked in partnership with the Rwanda Paediatric Association (RPA), with support from UNICEF and overseen by the Rwandan Ministry of Health and RBC, to design and deliver a neonatal (and subsequently perinatal) care quality improvement (QI) programme across multiple hospital facilities.

Programme design and data

The programme described here focused on improving clinical care and outcomes for mothers and newborns. In the initial phase, we concentrated on newborn care in the neonatal units (NNUs) of 12 hospitals within and outside Kigali (the capital city, population 1.75 m). Preliminary adaptation and delivery of training sessions with locally recruited hospital Champions was followed by continuous, iterative practical mentorship by joint international (RCPCH) and national (RPA) mentors and senior clinicians, alongside cumulative quality improvement (QI) initiatives at multiple points in the continuum of care. The potential efficacy of QI methodologies and initiatives to engender positive changes in clinical care and outcomes (at least in the short term) is widely reported. However, it is increasingly recognised that QI—often using ‘imported’ evidence-based protocols developed through high-income country (HIC) research and policy—can fail to account for, engage with or affect deeper systemic factors which determine scalability and sustainability of change in the specific context of individual local and national health system structure and culture.4 5

Our approach aimed to integrate multiple tailored QI initiatives continuously over multiple years, focusing on specific clinical functions along the continuum of perinatal care delivery. The primary objective was to sharpen immediate aspects of clinical care quality and related outcomes. But it became clear that analysis of quality in clinical functions created a lens through which to explore and understand underlying systemic factors which shape how quality happens and what that does for patient outcomes. For example, QI methodologies necessitated enhanced generation and use of process and outcome data at ward/unit and facility level, shifting what may be viewed as a routine approach to hospital information as an administrative burden to be processed for extraction in the form of reporting to higher governmental levels, towards a more active, locally led feedback approach enabling collaborative analysis of performance, problem identification and problem-solving, fitted to each facility context and evolving over time.6 7

The programme’s overarching QI strategy was to work within and optimise existing resources, rather than introducing interventions which rely on new or externally provided inputs (although we operated in a changing environment, including significant support from UNICEF in provision of critical equipment such as incubators, CPAP machines, syringe pumps, saturation monitors, etc.).8 Regular review of implementation and progress, through individual facility performance data as well as regular ‘cluster’ meetings across hospitals in groups, provided feedback to assess and adapt intervention modalities. Oversight and leadership of programme progress by the Rwandan professional paediatric body created the opportunity to advocate for and incorporate experience of local initiatives into national policy and health sector strategy (including, eg, development of national guidelines on clinical mentorship as well as innovative approaches to accelerating core neonatal skills acquisition among relevant health professionals).

The programme was founded on iterative, open dialogue between international and Rwandan professional medical organisations. It was grounded in real-world conditions in participating hospitals, but simultaneously linking with national policy-setting bodies. As such, the programme was able to forge a pathway between ‘QI’ in clinical practice and ‘health system strengthening’ at a deeper level, shifting partnership working away from projectised interventions which all too easily falsify local conditions towards realist Rwandan understanding of systemic factors and a more catalytic role for global partnership and local change.9,12

Results

The programme reported a number of positive results for care quality and mortality among newborns admitted to NNUs (which are described in a separate paper). But the process of achieving those results, through specific clinical QI measures, also helped to illuminate deeper systemic dynamics through which QI interventions are—or are not—converted into effective care. For example:

Neonatal resuscitation: initial short-course training (using ‘Helping Babies Breathe’, HBB) is a well-known and popular technical training option—and one which helped establish the programme’s bona fides with hospital leadership and participating staff. But iterative in-situ training, refresher sessions and associated QI initiatives helped us, over time, to explore and understand deeper systemic features of hospital and NNU management, including procurement of essential resuscitation equipment (bag-and-valve masks, etc.). Informal accreditation, through HBB training, enabled hospital administrative and clinical leadership to recognise programme nurses as neonatally skilled, strengthening longer-run advocacy to stabilise and specialise teams of nurses dedicated to newborn care more permanently assigned to the NNU, reducing the problem of staff rotation widely prevalent in hospitals. Over time, HBB training created an opportunity to address and strengthen often weak or absent communication and planning between clinicians in Maternity and Neonatology departments, as well as improving interaction between hospital and primary clinics, improving first-hour care at PHC level and referral decision making.13

Newborn respiratory care: a focus on newborn respiratory care prompted attention to lack of availability of medicines and procurement policy, specifically regarding availability and cost of surfactant and caffeine for babies requiring respiratory support. It enhanced system-level thinking about the role and optimal use of CPAP, including consultation with hospital Director-Generals, Ministry of Health and RBC on centralised procurement and supply-chain policies to consolidate preferred CPAP ‘make’. This created a basis for simplifying and standardising training for nurses responsible for use, as well as opening wider engagement with central government on essential equipment inventory and strategies for improving biomedical/maintenance. Improving clinical practice for respiratory care strengthened advocacy in favour of infrastructural investment in piped oxygen as a more reliable and ultimately cheaper option for hospital construction (as well as extending availability of oxygen in the form of concentrators at in-referring primary care centres), all of which were further accelerated in response to the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. At the same time, greater availability and use of uncontrolled oxygen created the need for more rigorous training for nurses on risks of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), on calibration and monitoring of oxygen delivery to newborns to mitigate ROP risk and expanded emphasis on specialised clinical education and ophthalmic support—and (in principle) greater attention to the longer-term consequences of rising newborn survival and the challenges of early childhood development.

Neonatal hypothermia: teaching on importance and impact of hypothermia in newborns necessitated procurement of thermometers for NNUs, as well as spurring increased frequency of nurse-led newborn observation and documentation (for measuring temperature but also as basis for more rapid identification of wider deterioration/risk signs), which also prompted attention to and advocacy on appropriate nurse-to-cot ratios and thus workforce planning. The focus on hypothermia also inspired review of hospital infrastructure and layout, including modification of environmental factors and development of locally led ‘frugal technology’ solutions to newborn transfer, including locally adapted transport incubators.

Neonatal sepsis: clinical attention to risks and signs of neonatal sepsis, supported through classroom teaching and cot-side training, enabled a broader review of antibiotic practice in Rwanda’s hospitals—including the widespread precautionary practice of overprescribing (or excessive length of prescription) likely resulting from a high level of risk aversion among clinicians seeking to avoid blame for lack of antibiotic cover if a baby’s condition deteriorates. However, this is wasteful in a resource-scarce context and may exacerbate antimicrobial resistance.14 Understanding this enabled a wider appreciation of divergence between prescribing protocol and realist practice, particularly in a context where mortality reduction is an increasingly pressurised policy goal. The focus on sepsis also enabled wider attention to improving infection prevention and control practices.

Kangaroo mother care (KMC): introducing evidence, rationale and technique for parental KMC as a low-cost, high-benefit option for management of eligible newborns admitted to NNU/neonatal intensive care unit (impacting positively on thermoregulation, early initiation of breastfeeding and bonding, as well as reduced risk of infection and incidence of re-admission), created space to empower nurses in decision-making and specialist skills development as well as engagement with hospital leadership on designation of infrastructural resources (room layout, water access, etc.) to enhance quality of KMC and thus mothers' willingness to engage with it. Introduction and expansion of KMC uptake also created the basis for recognition of parents as potentially valuable (and under-utilised) resource in the care of newborns, and as foundation for wider growth in advocacy and support for ‘parent-led care’ as a key strategic component of Rwanda’s developing health system.15 16 KMC also provided an entry point for parental interaction and education supporting wider attention to newborn nutrition. Over the course of the programme, we addressed early initiation of breastfeeding (as well as information and education on long-term benefits) starting immediately after delivery through skin-to-skin maternal contact. We also supported other modes of parent-led nutrition including enteral feeding and breast milk expression and cup feeding.17

Discussion

Reporting quantitative results from programmes such as the one described here, framed predominantly in terms of improvement in clinical processes and patient outcomes, is perhaps the most popular and conventional approach to dissemination and publication. But it risks displacing or obscuring attention to less well-represented but critically important qualitative system-level changes which underpin those results. This programme was designed to integrate QI interventions with wider health system strengthening, reflecting a growing interest not only in the importance of care quality but also the limitations of QI methodologies—often focused rather narrowly on a defined area of practice and on factors shaping quality at the microlevel, but with lesser action on contextual barriers and environmental factors at the mesolevel and macrolevel of facility, sector and national policy—and in a more grounded approach to research methods, in the field of implementation science, which may support better the linkage between quality and system-level actions.18,22

As well as pointing to the possibility of significant improvement in newborn outcomes through relatively simple, cost-efficient support to basic clinical capabilities and care delivery functions, experiences gathered through this programme also illuminate the potential for more structural (thus potentially more scalable and/or sustainable) changes in hospital and health system function. These relate, for example, to the ongoing challenges of developing a skilled and motivated health workforce, furnished with appropriate opportunities for education, training, professional development and specialisation; of procuring and—critically—maintaining essential equipment, reflecting both clinical-efficiency and cost-efficiency; of building positive norms of documentation, communication and collaboration between clinical cadres; of creating a culture of data value, in which localised data generation and utilisation support localised circuits of learning and improvement; and of continuing to forge pathways which can better integrate clinical services and community settings.23

To give two examples: first, maternity outcomes in Rwanda remain strongly influenced by malnutrition among women and girls. Although stunting has fallen from 51% in 2005 to 33% in the latest measure, chronic malnutrition remains considerably above national targets.24 Improving household dietary security and antenatal care access and quality offer key opportunities to address poor nutritional status and widespread anaemia which shape prospects for high-risk pregnancy and premature birth at hospital and health centre. Second, as generalised newborn survival in Rwanda improves, a rising proportion will go home with potential for developmental delay and longer-term disability. This emphasises the need—and opportunity—to forge stronger linkages between clinical care and community welfare, in particular strengthening communication between medical and social welfare services, following up high-risk babies in primary care through simple, systematic developmental monitoring, strengthening confidence in parent-led care, and enhancing attention to the critical early child development period.

Conclusions

The programme described here is the product of a decade-long partnership between two professional medical organisations, in the UK and Rwanda. Our collaboration is grounded in shared understanding of and commitment to a global evidence base of effective maternal, neonatal and paediatric care—but subject to and led by local interpretation and adaptation. Long-term, continuous professional partnership of this kind, based on open dialogue and respectful debate, offers a unique opportunity to link together what is recommended in clinical guidelines, what is included in national strategy, and what is practicable at the frontline of care delivery in local clinics and hospitals.25 26 This kind of partnership, we suggest, is critical for supporting real-world application of concepts and protocols too often forged in or through HIC research.27

But institutional partnership offers considerably more than this—especially so at the current inflection point in the architecture of global health and development.28 As of early 2025, sharply changing global health and finance modalities—challenging what may be viewed as the cultural myopia and political condescension of the post-colonial, post-War development aid model—point to the value of more equitable and efficient institution-to-institution relationships between rich and developing nations, groups and individuals, based on shared professional objectives and technical expertise rather than cliff-drop differences in access to money and policy influence.

As conventional flows of overseas development assistance diminish, new, better and more properly equitable modes of international cooperation between countries may emerge. As the tide of development assistance for health recedes, a hitherto concealed (or undervalued) landscape of LMIC aspirations and capabilities comes into view. We should by no means romanticise this process. But we may anticipate a greater space within which for local voices, ideas and initiatives to be raised in the field of health, visible already, for example, in the ‘Accra Reset’.29 As a party to that space, HICs may well want to refashion their ‘offer’ on institutional partnerships, using technical knowledge as the currency of exchange, and locally led adaptation—fitting what is available to what is needed—as the currency of engagement.
