Medical licensing examination preparatory program: designing, implementing, and evaluation for Saudi medical interns
Authors: Sajida Agha, Ahmed Al Rumayyan, Najla Qazi, Syed Waqas Hussain Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe present study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and diagnostic utility of a structured Medical Licensing Examination (MLE) preparatory course designed for Saudi medical interns. The evaluation focused on assessing the program participants’ readiness by evaluating their knowledge, test-taking skills, and participant satisfaction.MethodsThis convergent mixed-methods evaluation design study was carried out at King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences (KSAU-HS), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The entire cohort of 103 interns was included. The preparatory program was organized into a three-phase process. The initial planning phase involved identifying student needs and designing the course. This was followed by the implementation phase, which focused on delivering the course through interactive learning sessions and practice examinations. The final evaluation phase was dedicated to assessing participant satisfaction, gathering feedback, and analyzing benefits. For data collection, the researchers developed a questionnaire to assess satisfaction and administered a post-course mock examination as formative tool to measure the interns’ knowledge and readiness for the licensing exam. Data analysis integrated descriptive statistics for numerical data with thematic analysis for open-ended responses.ResultsOut of 76 interns, 67 (88.15%) reported high levels of satisfaction with all the sessions. Most participants agreed that the course objectives were clearly defined (n = 59, 77.6%) and that participation and interaction were encouraged (n = 75, 98.7%). The mean test score was 21.71 (43.42%), which suggests a relatively low level of preparation for this type of examination. Qualitative feedback further supported these findings, highlighting strengths in content delivery and instructional strategies.ConclusionThe integration of the MLE preparation program and mock assessment within the medical curriculum offers a structured approach to familiarizing interns with the format and content of the licensure examination. Participant feedback suggests positive perceptions of the learning experience, while the mock assessment provided a useful diagnostic snapshot of performance at the time of administration. These findings describe the potential role of such interventions in supporting exam preparation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09376-4.

Implications for practice

The findings of this study highlight several important implications for MLE education and licensure preparation in Saudi Arabia. First, integrating review sessions, interactive teaching strategies, and mock assessments aligned with the MLE blueprint should be considered an essential component of internship training. Second, the results indicate that interns benefit from learning environments that promote active engagement, critical thinking, and repeated formative feedback. The satisfaction ratings and qualitative feedback suggest that interns respond positively to teaching approaches, which combine traditional lectures, audiovisual resources, and interactive problem-solving activities. These strategies may affect intrinsic motivation, confidence, and clinical performance. Finally, the integration of mock exam preparation plays an important role in medical graduates’ readiness for licensing exams.

Introduction

In medical education, passing high-stakes national licensure examinations is a globally recognized metric of professional competence and a key indicator of institutional quality. These examinations serve as benchmarks for evaluating curriculum effectiveness, assessment practices, and institutional accountability. Effectively using these examinations requires knowledge of institutional curriculum and instructional methods to align assessment with learning outcomes and to decide what and how to test the students [1].

In Saudi Arabia, the Saudi Medical Licensing Examination (SMLE) is the primary gateway to clinical practice, ensuring that all graduates— regardless of medical school— meet the minimum standards of competence required for safe and effective patient care [2–3]. The Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS) oversees certification for all healthcare professions and mandates the SMLE as a requirement for medical sciences graduates seeking to practice in the country. Administered by the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS), the SMLE assess a candidates’ ability to apply foundational medical knowledge and clinical skills [3–4].

As medical schools in the Kingdom expands, the SMLE is increasingly important for standardizing graduate competencies and ensuring quality assurance nationwide. Evidence from international contexts has demonstrated substantial performance across medical schools, reinforcing the need for unified, high-stakes national examinations to maintain professional standards and equity [5–6].

Preparing students for such high-stakes examinations requires structured and evidence-based approaches. As outlined by Roberts et al. [6], developing valid and reliable assessment systems involves systematic processes, including content blueprinting, appropriate test formatting, and rigorous standard-setting procedures. Blueprinting is particularly important, as it aligns examination content with curricular objectives, thereby ensuring content validity [7–8]. Consequently, medical programs globally increasingly utilize mock examinations, formative assessments, and structured review courses to increase exam readiness and clinical reasoning [9].

Recent studies in Saudi Arabia and other health professions indicates that targeted licensure preparation programs and mock examinations with personalized feedback significantly improve candidate performance. For example, Almaghaslah [9] and Sales et al. [10] demonstrated improved outcomes on the Saudi Pharmacist Licensure Examination (SPLE) following structured preparation [9–10], while Al-Sheikh et al. [11] reported that repeated SMLE mock examinations enhanced both confidence and scores among medical graduates [4, 11]. Furthermore, evidence from nursing education supports the implementation of interactive review strategies, early remediation, and formative testing contribute to better performance in licensure examinations [12–14].

Despite the growing use of the national licensing examination as an educational quality indicator, there is a lack of structured preparatory courses within Saudi medical curricula. Moreover, limited local or regional empirical evidence exists regarding the effectiveness of such structured programs within undergraduate or internship programs, rendering this investigation both timely and necessary.

Although no official report or dataset from the SCFHS or the College of Medicine (COM), King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences (KSAU-HS) publicly declare cohort-wide first-attempt pass rate, internal evaluations and personalized feedback highlight a significant gap in exam readiness. To address this challenge, and uphold institutional commitments to continuous quality improvement, the COM developed and implemented its first structured MLE preparation program for medical interns. This initiative utilized Kern’s six-step approach to curriculum development, a widely adopted and flexible framework effective for designing medical training programs and medical curricula [15]. This framework enabled systematic problem identification, needs assessment, objective setting, selection of instructional strategies, implementation, and evaluation.

This study investigates the influence of this structured preparatory course, which included practice examinations mirroring the official SMLE, on interns’ knowledge, skills, perceived readiness and participant satisfaction. By providing evidence-based data, this study offers medical educators insight into how preparatory interventions improve student outcomes.

Furthermore, this study document the design and implementation process to serve as a resource for other institutions seeking to establish similar initiatives, providing a model for transforming learner experience through the thoughtful design and implementation of licensure preparation [16].

Methods

The College of Medicine, King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences (KSAU-HS) developed and implemented a preparatory course titled “KSAU-HS Enhance Your Performance in Achieving Success in Saudi Medical Licensing Examination” (KEYPASS). The course designed to strengthen student readiness for the Saudi Medical Licensing Examination (SMLE) through three main objectives. First, it helped students learn and develop essential study and test-taking skills needed for success in the Medical Licensing Examination (MLE). Second, it emphasized key themes and high-yield topics within each specialty to optimize revision. Finally, familiarized students with the MLE blueprint and registration processes to ensure compliance with examination requirements and increase confidence.

Conceptual framework

The six-step approach to curriculum development proposed by Kern et al. was adopted as the guiding conceptual framework [17]. This framework is widely recognized for its efficacy in designing both longitudinal medical curricula and short-term training programs. This framework included: problem identification and needs assessment, formulation of educational goals and objectives, selection of instructional strategies, implementation of the program, evaluation and feedback for continuous improvement.

This initiative was taken in response to constant feedback from intern end-of-course and program evaluations, which showed a strong demand for a locally tailored MLE preparatory program. Without these programs, previously many interns relied on resources designed for the USMLE (United States) and PLAB (United Kingdom). However, these resources often lack alignment with the specific structure and core competencies assessed by the SMLE. In response, the College of Medicine at KSAU-HS initiated the development of the KEYPASS program as a leading institution in medical education in Saudi Arabia.

Design and implementation process

Under the directive of the Dean of the College, the Department of Medical Education (DME) was tasked with identifying students’ needs and designing an evidence-based course. The needs assessment component involved a comprehensive literature review and analysis of graduates’ end-of-program evaluations to identify expectations and perceived gaps in MLE preparation. To gain deeper insights into students’ needs, a set of structured interview questions was developed.

Individual think-aloud interviews were conducted with eleven medical interns in the DME meeting room between January and March 2022 (Supplementary file 1). Participants, who had recently passed MLE, were invited via email to discuss factors associated with examination success. Those who agreed joined a 40–50- minute interview in English. The sessions were recorded, transcribed, thematically coded and analyzed to shape the course design (Supplementary file 2).

Based on the interview findings, the DME recommended the development of a comprehensive preparatory program (KEYPASS) for medical interns. The resulting KEYPASS program was grounded in constructivist learning theory and principles of test-enhanced learning, emphasizing active knowledge construction, retrieval practice, and feedback. A multidisciplinary team was subsequently formed, comprising clinical educators experienced in the SMLE and representatives from the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS). The team reviewed interns’ feedback and current SCFHS policies, held several planning meetings, and developed the curriculum.

Instructional strategies were guided by the framework proposed by Davis et al. [18], incorporating seven key elements: needs assessment, focused educational initiatives, interactive instructional methods, delivery across multiple sessions, opportunities for practice and feedback, enabling social and organizational support, and reinforcement techniques.

The curriculum covered all clinical disciplines, including anesthesia/critical care, dermatology, community medicine, emergency medicine, ENT, general surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, pediatrics, ethics and research, family medicine, internal medicine, ophthalmology, and psychiatry. The sessions included expert-led topic reviews, test-taking strategies, stress and anxiety management, note-taking skills, tips for multiple-choice question answering, and discipline-specific blueprints. Course objectives, presentations, and practice materials were reviewed and approved by the Curriculum Committee prior to implementation.

KEYPASS was officially launched in September 2022 at the College of Medicine, KSAU-HS, Riyadh. All medical interns who were enrolled in their final year at the college were eligible and received email invitations to participate. A total of 103 medical interns, including both male and female participants, registered for the course, which was offered free of charge. The program was delivered over one week (Sunday to Thursday) as a half-day intensive course. Each 50–60-minute module included a review of 20–40 multiple-choice questions (MCQs) per specialty. The sessions were conducted in the auditorium at the College of Medicine’s Simulation Center. The facility accommodates up to 200 participants and is equipped with modern technology and advanced audiovisual facilities.

The sessions were delivered by clinical faculty members who were actively engaged with the MLE in the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS). Punctuality and active participation were emphasized, and daily feedback was collected from the interns to guide ongoing improvement. The program concluded with a mock examination (13:00–15:00 h), after which participants received individualized performance feedback from the offices of the Dean and the Associate Dean for Clinical Affairs.

A mock examination was conducted at the end of the MLE preparatory program (KEYPASS) to assess interns’ knowledge acquisition and readiness across major clinical disciplines. The exam was aligned with the SMLE blueprint, to ensure proportional representation of clinical disciplines. The distribution of questions is shown in Table 1. The test consisted of 50 multiple-choice questions (MCQs). Items were developed by faculty familiar with the exam format and reviewed by two content experts for accuracy, clarity, and appropriateness. Each question was analyzed for item difficulty, discrimination index (as measured by the point- biserial correlation), and response distribution. Item difficulty (p-value) was calculated as the proportion of students who answered each item correctly (p = number of correct responses divided by the total number of students), with values ranging from 0.00 to 1.00; higher values indicate easier items. Items with difficulty indices between 0.50 and 0.80 were considered acceptable, whereas items > 0.80 were classified as very easy and those < 0.30 as very difficult and flagged for review [19–20]. Item discrimination was assessed to determine each item’s ability to differentiate between high- and low-performing students. The discrimination index (DI) was calculated using the upper–lower 27% method (proportion correct in the top 27% minus proportion correct in the bottom 27%), and the point-biserial correlation (PBS) between item score and total test score was also computed. DI and PBS values range from − 1.00 to + 1.00; values ≥ 0.40 were considered excellent, 0.30–0.39 reasonably good, 0.20–0.29 marginal (requiring improvement), and ≤ 0.19 poor, while negative values indicated potentially flawed items [19–20]. Internal consistency reliability was estimated using the Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20) for dichotomously scored items, calculated as KR-20 = (k / (k − 1)) × [1 − (Σpq / σ²)], where k represents the total number of items, p the item difficulty, q = 1 − p, and σ² the variance of total test scores. KR-20 values of 0.70 or higher were considered acceptable indicators of reliability. Distractor analysis was performed to evaluate the performance of incorrect options; a distractor was considered functional if selected by at least 5% of examinees. Distractor efficiency (DE) was calculated as the proportion of functional distractors per item, with higher DE reflecting better item quality and improved discrimination [19–20].

Upon completion of the course, a self-developed survey was administered to evaluate intern satisfaction across various aspects of the preparatory program. The questionnaire consisted of 14 items rated on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). To capture qualitative insights, three open-ended questions addressed the participants’ preferred course elements, suggestions for improvement, and the perceived impact of the course on their future exam preparation were included (see attached Supplementary file 3). The instrument was evaluated for content validity by a panel of five institutional medical education experts, who assessed item relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness. Revisions were made based on their recommendations. To strengthen validity, these items were cognitively pretested with a sample of five interns to ensure interpretability. The internal-consistency of the scale was confirmed with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.73. Incomplete surveys were excluded from quantitative analysis if more than 20% of items were missing; otherwise, mean imputation was applied within domains.

An explanation and request for informed consent, along with the link to the survey, were distributed via email to interns who had completed the preparatory course. Participation was voluntary, and all the information remained confidential since there was no link between the survey responses and the individual interns who participated.

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 28. The quantitative data from the surveys were analyzed via descriptive statistics to summarize the interns’ responses. In addition, qualitative data such as open-ended survey responses were analyzed thematically. Two independent researchers reviewed the data to identify recurring patterns related to program feasibility and developed a coding framework. Themes were finalized through consensus. To enhance trustworthiness, findings were triangulated with observational notes from program faculty and transcripts from intern focus group discussions. Integration occurred during the planning phase, where qualitative findings directly shaped the course content, and during the evaluation phase, where qualitative themes were used to contextualize and explain the quantitative satisfaction and performance scores, providing a comprehensive understanding of the program’s impact. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The study was ethically approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC), King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences (KSAU-HS) (IRB approval number: 00000264225) prior to data collection. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Results

A total of 103 medical interns participated in this study. More than half (n = 63, 61.2%) reported attending a public medical college, and were male. Additionally, the vast majority (n = 87, 84.47%) reported taking the SMLE Part I during their internship, whereas the remaining interns reported taking it during their fifth year.

Mock examination performance

A mock examination was administered to evaluate interns’ initial readiness for the licensure exam. The assessment consisted of 50 graded items, with a total of 50 possible points. The performance indicators from the test are presented in Table 2. The mean score of 21.71 (43.42%) indicates that, on average, interns achieved less than half of the total possible points, reflecting the overall distribution of intern performance relative to the total possible score and suggests a relatively low level of preparation for this type of examination. The range of scores (1–31) demonstrates substantial variability in student performance. The Kuder-Richardson 20 reliability coefficient of 0.70 indicates acceptable internal consistency of the test items (see Table 2).

The item discrimination (point biserial correlation) values ranged from − 0.16 to 0.53, with the majority of items demonstrating positive discrimination, revealing that higher-performing students were more likely to answer these items correctly. Items with point biserial values above 0.30 were considered to have good discriminatory power, and approximately 40% of the items met this criterion.

When analyzed by discipline, the anesthesiology and critical care items presented an average difficulty of 65% and moderate discrimination (rpb = 0.35). The family medicine questions displayed greater variability in performance, with difficulty levels between 7% and 61%, reflecting possible differences in topic familiarity (rpb= -0.03 to 0.16). The internal medicine questions demonstrated consistent moderate difficulty (average 46%) with acceptable discrimination (rpb up to 0.47). The surgery items performed well, with mean correct responses of approximately 53% and several items showing strong discrimination (rpb up to 0.43). OBGYN questions were among the highest performing, with multiple items showing > 60% correct responses and good discrimination (rpb = 0.38–0.48). The pediatrics items varied in difficulty (23–77%), with multiple well-discriminating questions (rpb = 0.39–0.49). The ophthalmology items had relatively high difficulty levels (44–74%) and the strongest discrimination value (rpb = 0.53) among all questions. Overall, these findings show that the mock examination distinguished between higher-performing and lower-performing students and provided diagnostic feedback to guide further learning. The results also indicate that most test items were appropriately designed in terms of difficulty and discrimination, supporting the reliability of the assessment in relation to students’ MLE preparedness (see Table 3).

Distractor performance mirrored item discrimination patterns, supporting classical test theory expectations. Items with strong discrimination (r_pb ≥ 0.40) constituted 12 of 50 items (24%) and demonstrated balanced distractor utilization and clear upper–lower group separation, suggesting cognitively plausible alternatives. Conversely, eight items (16%) showed low or negative discrimination (r_pb ≤ 0.10), including two items (4%) with negative discrimination, and were characterized by nonfunctional distractors or disproportionate selection patterns, raising concerns regarding option plausibility and construct alignment. These findings highlight the association between distractor quality and item functioning and overall test reliability.

Discussion

This study represents an initiative in Saudi Arabia to systematically design, implement, and evaluate a structured preparation program for the MLE. Although several studies have investigated remediation, enrichment, and preparatory programs for licensure exam performance, most have focused on nursing and respiratory students, leaving a significant gap in MLE-related preparatory programs in Saudi Arabia [4, 9]. Our findings contribute to addressing this gap by highlighting the feasibility, acceptability, and diagnostic utility of a blueprint-aligned, interactive program.

A notable finding of this study was the high proportion of distractor selections in the mock examination. This trend was particularly evident in the family medicine and internal medicine questions, where incorrect options attracted up to 80–90% of responses. These patterns indicate that certain items were more challenging for participants and may reflect difficulties in applying knowledge to these question types. Questions requiring application of principles or differential diagnosis were more frequently answered incorrectly, suggesting potential gaps in performance on higher-order, application-based items. Similar trends have been reported in studies on high-stakes medical licensing exams, where students often demonstrate adequate factual knowledge but face difficulties with clinical reasoning and the integration of concepts [21]. The consistent selection of distractors may also relate to factors such as item structure, familiarity with vignette-based questions, or alignment between teaching and assessment formats. While these interpretations are supported by the pattern of responses, they should be viewed as hypotheses warranting further investigation rather than direct measures of cognitive or learning styles. Overall, the findings suggest that the mock examination approximated the complexity of MLE-style questions and highlight areas where targeted instructional support, particularly in clinical reasoning, may be beneficial.

Another possible explanation is that interns were unfamiliar with the question format and stem construction used in the MLE in Saudi Arabia, which could have led to misinterpretation of key phrases or distractor plausibility. Moreover, the presence of well-functioning distractors —those that closely resemble the correct answer—may have contributed to confusion among less confident test-takers. These findings are consistent with earlier research. Studies have shown that students frequently rely on memorization strategies, which are associated with shallow learning and difficulty discriminating between closely related options [21–22]. These findings point to the value of incorporating test-enhanced learning strategies, including practice with clinical vignettes, reasoning-based tutorials, and feedback-oriented mock exams. Future course iterations may benefit from helping students analyze distractors, understand why certain options are incorrect, and develop metacognitive skills to improve test performance and conceptual mastery.

According to the literature, readiness for professional examinations has been linked to limited exposure to test-enhanced learning strategies, which are associated with improved long-term retention and exam performance [23–24]. Preparatory programs that incorporate frequent low-stakes quizzes, structured feedback, and deliberate practice with clinical scenarios have been associated with reduced distractor susceptibility and enhanced exam performance [25]. The findings of our study support the inclusion of more formative assessments to address this identified need.

The findings from this study are similar with the broader literature on licensure exam performance. Evidence from previous educational research has shown that demographic variables such as gender, institutional type, or choice of study tools often have limited predictive value for outcomes on high-stakes professional exams. For example, previous U.S.-based studies on dental licensure examinations revealed no relationship between exam performance and institution type or between gender and achievement. Rather, these studies concluded that individual study behaviors and the intensity of preparation were more influential determinants of exam success [26]. Overall, participant feedback confirmed program acceptability and perceived value, particularly regarding the clarity of objectives, relevance of topics, faculty preparedness, and the supportive learning environment. Qualitative feedback further confirmed these findings, with interns praising the alignment of content with MLE requirements and providing constructive recommendations such as extending sessions on complex topics and incorporating more MLE-format practice questions. Participants also expressed appreciation for the interactive discussions, practical tips for answering questions, use of the exam blueprint, and inclusion of a mock exam. These findings echo those of previous studies emphasizing the importance of structured preparatory interventions to increase learners’ confidence and exam readiness in health profession licensure examinations [27–28].

Intern feedback pointed to several areas for improvement, such as inviting experts from the Saudi Commission, adding real MLE-style questions, offering the course at more convenient times, reducing class size (organizing smaller group sessions to facilitate more effective engagement), and limiting disruptions during sessions. Interns also requested more practice questions, advance access to materials, detailed handbooks with explanations and correct answers, extended course durations (as one week was considered too short), improved teaching methods from some tutors, recorded sessions, and an optional crash course [9–10]. These insights highlight the importance of aligning course design with students’ learning needs in terms of both course structure and frequency and assessment expectations to support improved performance in future iterations.

Research in clinical training environments has noted that anxiety management and supportive learning structures are important factors in high-pressure contexts [29–30]. The positive feedback regarding our program’s supportive environment suggests that such aspects may help reduce preparatory anxiety and foster resilience. Furthermore, studies on engagement and individual factors indicate that interactive, feedback-oriented programs can promote sustained preparation behaviors, an element often challenged under high-stakes conditions [29]. Finally, evidence on training during system stress underscores the value of structured institutional support in bolstering preparedness [14]. Our program, by offering organized review and dedicated study time, represents one form of such institutional support and may contribute to mitigating stressors commonly experienced during the licensure preparation period.

Additionally, the results underscore the importance of aligning preparatory content with the MLE blueprint. Studies have noted that familiarity with exam structure and competencies is associated with improved strategic test-taking skills and overall outcomes [31]. Interns reported that the course helped them understand exam expectations, supporting the notion that blueprint-aligned preparation strengthens exam readiness. The mock examination results also point to the value of designing and implementing targeted interventions within MLE preparation programs.

In summary, the findings indicate that a structured MLE preparatory program successfully influences intern outcomes across four key domains: knowledge acquisition, skill refinement, perceived readiness, and satisfaction. By utilizing practice examinations that mirror the official SMLE, the course offered interns a realistic diagnostic of their reasoning gaps. Ultimately, the program proved both acceptable to the cohort and effective in building the confidence and competence required for the national licensing exam.

Limitations

Despite the high level of satisfaction, this study has several limitations. The findings are specific to one cohort from a single institution, which constrains the generalizability of the results. Second, the sample size was relatively small, limiting the statistical power needed to detect significant differences in performance outcomes. Third, self-reported perceptions may be subject to response bias, which could influence the accuracy of the satisfaction and feedback data. Additionally, although participation was high, self-selection bias may still be a consideration, as interns with stronger study habits or motivation may have engaged more deeply with program content. Finally, the questionnaire response rate was 73.8%, meaning that approximately one quarter of participants did not complete the post-course evaluation. This introduces the possibility of nonresponse bias, particularly if those who were more satisfied were more likely to respond, which could inflate the reported satisfaction estimates. Future studies could include comparisons with previous cohorts that did not receive the intervention to examine program effects.

From a broader perspective, medical colleges should consider implementing a structured MLE preparation program that includes focused content review, interactive teaching, and mock exams aligned with the licensure blueprint. This approach may facilitate collaborative, multi-institutional studies and help assess the generalizability of these findings. Further research incorporating longitudinal performance data and validated measures of anxiety and engagement would be valuable in examining the relationship between such programs, final exam outcomes, and learner well-being. Moreover, multicenter comparative studies could help identify effective national practices, and ongoing faculty development—supported by updated question banks and modern learning resources—is likely important for sustaining program quality.

Institutions may also consider providing protected study time during internships to support consistent preparation. Expanding blended learning methods such as case-based sessions, multimedia resources, online tools, and question banks will further enhance engagement and retention. Additional attention to challenging subjects through targeted practice and supplementary review is also recommended. Continuous evaluation via alumni and employer feedback may support ongoing program improvement.

Conclusion

This study described the implementation of a preparatory course for interns at King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences. It successfully delivered well-organized, relevant content through highly- rated, well-prepared facilitators and responded to the alumni-identified need for guidance and training from the college and peers.

Overall, the findings indicate that while the preparatory course addressed several key learning needs, further refinement is needed to strengthen conceptual understanding and reasoning skills. Nevertheless, institutions may wish to adapt implementation based on available resources, faculty expertise, and curricular structures. Integrating more case-based discussions, interactive problem-solving sessions, and regular feedback cycles may reduce distractor selection and improve performance. To build on the program’s strengths and address the noted limitations, future iterations may include more frequent preparatory courses of longer duration or more flexible schedules to allow deeper coverage and better accommodate intern schedules, as well as more high-fidelity practice questions, potentially in mock exam or question bank formats. Additionally, studies with larger sample sizes and multicenter collaborations would be useful in evaluating the scalability and adaptability of the program across diverse educational settings. This innovative approach may serve as a model for other medical programs in Saudi Arabia and may inform the design of similar preparatory initiatives within undergraduate curricula.
