The MST4–14-3-3ζ complex promotes pancreatic cancer by activating YAP
Authors: Luyang Tian, Zhangting Zhao, Shisi Ding, Mingquan Li, Yi Han, Chunxiao Zhu, Yu Pan, Xiaowei Wang, Wei Kang, Liwei An, Zhaocai Zhou, Yang Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe mammalian STE20-like kinase 4 (MST4) has been implicated as a potential oncogene in various cancers, including pancreatic cancer (PC). However, the precise mechanisms through which MST4 promotes PC malignancy remain incompletely understood.MethodsA Biotin identification (BioID)-based proximity labeling approach was employed to identify key MST4-interacting molecules in human PANC-1 pancreatic cancer (PC) cells, followed by cell migration assay to validate the synergistic promotional effect of the MST4-interacting complex on PC cell migration. Direct binding between MST4 and its interacting partners was confirmed via microscale thermophoresis (MST) and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), with further validation through X-ray crystallographic structural analysis. The efficacy of rationally designed peptides targeting this interaction was evaluated in both in vitro cell models and in vivo xenograft mouse models.ResultsIn this study, we demonstrate that MST4 forms a phosphorylation-dependent complex with 14-3-3ζ, leading to Yes-associated protein (YAP) activation and synergistic enhancement of PC cell migration. We observed significant upregulation of both MST4 and 14-3-3ζ in PC patient samples, which correlated strongly with YAP activation and poor prognosis. Structural analysis revealed the detailed interface of the MST4–14-3-3ζ complex, facilitating the rational design of peptide inhibitors that disrupt this interaction. These peptides effectively suppressed YAP activation and exhibited potent anti-tumor effects both in vitro and in vivo.ConclusionsOur findings establish the MST4–14-3-3ζ complex as a critical regulator of YAP signaling that synergistically promotes PC cell migration. Furthermore, rationally designed peptides targeting this MST4–14-3-3ζ interaction represent a promising therapeutic strategy for pancreatic cancer and warrant further clinical exploration.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00784-6.

Background

Pancreatic cancer (PC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies, characterized by aggressive progression and high mortality rates. It is projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths in Western countries by 2030 [1–3]. The median survival time for patients is approximately 4 months, with a 5-year survival rate of only about 13% [4, 5]. This dismal prognosis is largely attributable to the aggressive biology of the tumor and its asymptomatic early stages, which often lead to late diagnosis and advanced disease at the time of detection [6]. Current treatment strategies for PC primarily include chemotherapy and surgery [7, 8]. However, only 15–20% of patients are eligible for surgical resection at diagnosis, while over half present with metastatic disease [9], underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic targets and treatment modalities.

The Hippo signaling pathway is an evolutionarily conserved regulator of organ size control, tissue homeostasis, and tumorigenesis [10]. In PC, dysregulation of the Hippo pathway [11–15] and hyperactivation of its effectors YAP/ Transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) are frequently observed [16–20]. For instance, in genetically engineered mouse models, YAP/TAZ activation has been shown to initiate acinar-to-ductal metaplasia, a pivotal early event in pancreatic carcinogenesis [21]. Mechanistically, YAP/TAZ promote pancreatic cancer growth and metastasis through both KRAS-dependent and KRAS-independent mechanisms [22, 23]. Additional components of the Hippo pathway are also aberrantly regulated in PC: The mammalian STE20-like kinase 1 (MST1) suppresses tumor progression via Reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced pyroptosis [24], whereas methylation-mediated activation of MST2 exerts an oncogenic effect [25]. Kinase activities of ‌Large tumor suppressor homolog 1/2 (LATS1/2) are negatively correlated with PC progression [26], and reduced MOB kinase activator 1 (MOB1) expression is linked to adverse pathological features [27]. Moreover, immune checkpoint protein PD-1 can bind MOB1 and inhibit its phosphorylation [28]. Thus, targeting the Hippo pathway in combination with existing PC therapies represents a promising strategy to enhance antitumor efficacy.

MST4, a member of the mammalian STE20-like (MST) kinase family, contains an N-terminal kinase domain, a C-terminal dimerization domain, and a central regulatory linker region [29]. MST4 has been implicated in various cancers, including gastric cancer (GC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), colorectal cancer (CRC), and PC [30–41]. Interestingly, its role appears context-dependent: in GC, MST4 acts as a tumor suppressor by restraining YAP activation via a non-canonical pathway [33], while in HCC, it inhibits epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasion, and metastasis by suppressing the PI3K–AKT–Snail1 axis [34, 42]. These divergent findings highlight the complex functionality of MST4 in cancer biology. Notably, our previous work demonstrated that the MST4–MOB4 complex activates YAP signaling and exerts an oncogenic role in PC [39], identifying MST4 as a potential therapeutic target. To further elucidate its mechanisms, we aimed to systematically identify MST4-interacting proteins, characterize their structural associations, and propose novel targeting strategies for PC treatment.

In this study, we employed proximity-dependent BioID to profile the MST4 interactome in pancreatic cancer cells, uncovering a previously unrecognized role of MST4 in promoting migration. We demonstrate that MST4 forms a phosphorylation-dependent complex with 14-3-3ζ, leading to YAP activation. Furthermore, we established a clinical correlation between elevated MST4/14-3-3ζ expression, YAP hyperactivation, and poor patient prognosis. Based on these insights, we designed peptide inhibitors that disrupt the MST4–14-3-3ζ interaction, thereby restoring Hippo pathway activity and suppressing YAP-driven oncogenic processes. Our findings offer a new therapeutic avenue for targeting Hippo signaling in PC.

Methods

Plasmids and antibodies

Full length MST4, 14-3-3σ/ζ were cloned into a modified pCDNA3.1 vector that include an N-terminal 3×Flag or Myc tag or fused with BirA, respectively. For prokaryotic expression, the coding region of MST4 and 14-3-3ζ was cloned into another modified pET-28a vector that includes an N-terminal maltose- binding protein (MBP) -TEVase-cleavage site tag. All MST4 mutants used in this paper were generated by site-directed mutagenesis. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing.

Antibody specific for human MST4 (catalog ab52491) was from Abcam. Antibody to FLAG (A8592) was from Sigma. Antibody to Myc (sc- 40) was from Santa Cruze Biotechnology. Antibody 14-3-3 was from Proteintech (catalog 14503-1-AP). Antibody to HA (catalog no. 3724), pYAP (catalog no. 13008), and β-actin (catalog no. 4967 S) was from Cell Signaling Technology. Antibody to YAP (sc-101199) was from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (catalog no. 31460) and goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (catalog no. 31430) were from ThermoFisher Scientific.

Cells

HEK293FT cells were obtained from Shanghai Life Academy of Sciences cell library (Shanghai, China), and PANC-1 and KPC cells were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc and Shanghai YuanChuang biotechnology Co.,Ltd (Shanghai, China), respectively. All cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 µg/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

Protein expression and purification

Prokaryotic constructs of MST4 and 14-3-3ζ were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) Codon-Plus cells by the induction of 0.25 mM isopropylβ-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) in Terrific Broth medium at 18 °C. For MBP-14-3-3ζ and His-tagged MST4 recombinant proteins, cells were harvested by centrifugation. Then, cells were resuspended with lysis buffer (20mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 1 mM DTT). The cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 18 000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C, and the soluble fraction was loaded onto pre-equilibrated Dextrin beads or nickel-Sepharose. After washing with lysis buffer twice, proteins were eluted with lysis buffer supplemented with 20 mM D-maltose and then digested by TEV protease to remove the MBP tag. The proteins were further purified by gel-filtration chromatography (HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 75, GE Healthcare) in buffer containing 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT.

Crystallization, structural determination, and refinement

The purified full-length 14-3-3ζ proteins was concentrated and then mixed with the phospho-MST4 peptide (pMST4, “RENNpTHPEWS”) at a molar ratio 1:3 to obtain the pMST4–14-3-3ζ complexes for further crystallization. Crystals were cultivated via the sitting-drop vapor diffusion method at 16 °C in a reservoir solution composed of buffer 0.2 M MgCl2, 0.1 M Tris pH 8.5, 25% Polyethylene Glycol (PEG) 3350, pH 8.5. Before data collection, the crystals were cryoprotected by immersion in reservoir solution supplemented with 30% glycerol, followed by rapid flash-cooling in liquid nitrogen. Diffraction data were collected at beamline BL19U1 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF), China and processed using HKL2000.

The structure was solved with Phaser-MR implemented in the program suite PHENIX using the crystal structure of the 14-3-3 phosphopeptide complexes (PDB: 1QJA) as an initial search model. Structure refinement was carried out with Phenix following the standard protocols. Model building was performed manually with the program COOT. All pictures were drawn using the program PyMol.

MBP pulldown assay

Purified MBP-MST4 was autophosphorylated at 30 °C for 30 min in the presence of 1 mM ATP, 5 mM MgCl2. To dephosphorylated MBP-MST4, purified MBP-MST4 was treated with λPPase with a mass ratio of 1:100 at 30 °C for 30 min in the presence of 10 mM MnCl2. The MBP-fused linker fragments of MST4 were phosphorylated by untagged full-length MST4 and repurified with amylase resin to remove untagged MST4.

For MBP pulldown assays, MBP-tagged fusion proteins immobilized on Dextrin resin were incubated with respective prey proteins in binding buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT) at 4 °C for 1 h. Following incubation, resin-bound complexes were washed three times with binding buffer. Bound proteins were eluted using binding buffer supplemented with 20 mM D-maltose. Input and eluted samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and visualized via Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) and immunostaining.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assay

ITC experiments were performed using a MicroCal iTC200 instrument (Malvern Panalytical) at 25 °C. For each measurement, the calorimeter cell was loaded with purified 14-3-3ζ protein at a concentration of 100 µM, while the injection syringe was filled with a synthetic pMST4 peptide at 1 mM. Both the protein and ligand solutions were prepared in identical buffer conditions: 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl. Prior to titration, all samples were degassed under vacuum to eliminate air bubbles.

The titration protocol consisted of an initial 0.4 µL injection, followed by 19 sequential injections of 2 µL each. A 300 s interval was maintained between injections to ensure thermal equilibrium, and the solution was continuously stirred at 1 000 rpm to promote rapid mixing. Raw calorimetric data were baseline-corrected and integrated using the Origin-based software provided by MicroCal, with binding isotherms fitted to a one-site binding model to determine the dissociation constant (Kd).

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) assay

Label purified 14-3-3ζ protein with a suitable fluorophore according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Remove unreacted dye using desalting column. Prepare a 16-point 2-fold serial dilution of the pMST4 peptides in assay buffer. Mix equal volumes of labeled 14-3-3ζ protein with each pMST4 peptide dilution. Incubate samples at room temperature for 30 min to allow binding equilibrium. Load each sample into standard-treated MST capillaries (Monolith NT.115 Premium Capillaries). Place capillaries into the Monolith NT.115 instrument. Set excitation power to 20–80%, and set MST power to medium (40–60%) for standard assays, respectively. Acquire data with 5 s pre-thermophoresis, 30 s thermophoresis, and 5 s post-thermophoresis recording. Export raw data to MO. Affinity Analysis software for analysis and calculate Kd.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and immunoblotting

For IP assays, HEK293FT cells were transfected or stimulated as indicated and harvested 48 h post-treatment. Cells were lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 12 000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected and incubated with protein A/G agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or fusion label beads overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed three times with ice-cold RIPA buffer and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS-PAGE loading buffer.

For immunoblotting analysis, immunoprecipitates or whole-cell extracts were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes using a semi-dry transfer system (Tanon, Shanghai). Membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat dry milk in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST) and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with PBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai).

Transwell migration assay

PANC-1 cells were first transduced with lentiviral particles. After 24 h, the transduction medium was replaced with fresh complete DMEM medium. For transwell migration assay, 2 × 10⁴ cells resuspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber of an 8-µm pore polycarbonate membrane transwell insert (Corning). The lower chamber contained medium supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. Following 24 h incubation at 37 °C, non-migrating cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, stained with 0.1% crystal violet and washed three times with PBS. Five random fields per insert were imaged at 100× magnification using a brightfield microscope (Olympus). Image acquisition was performed in a blinded manner. For quantification, crystal violet was solubilized with 50% ethanol/0.1% acetic acid for 10 min, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Wound healing assay

Approximately 5 × 104 cells/well were plated and incubated in a 6-well plate. After overnight incubation, wounds were created by scratching the monolayer surface using a sterile 10µL pipette tip. Cells were treated with indicated conditions. The closure and gaps were measured using Image J software and the plates were photographed using an inverted microscope.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using RNA Isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme Biotech). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed in triplicate using the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Reactions were incubated at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was performed to verify primer specificity. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct method, normalizing to ACTB (β-actin) as an internal control. Each sample was analyzed in technical triplicates, and biological replicates were performed as indicated. The primers used in this study are as follows:

hCTGF-F: 5’-AAAAGTGCATCCGTACTCCCA-3’

hCTGF-R: 5’-CCGTCGGTACATACTCCACAG-3’

hACTB-F: 5’-ATCATGAAGTGTGACGTGGA-3’

hACTB-R: 5’-CTCAGGAGGAGCAATGATCT-3’

where F is forward; R is reverse.

Immunofluorescence assay

Cells were seeded on coverslips and cultured to appropriate confluency. Following removal of culture medium, cells were washed once with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After three washes with PBS, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. After three times washes with PBS, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Coverslips were washed three times with PBS to remove excess dye for further analysis. Images were captured using an Olympus FV3000 laser-scanning confocal microscope.

Mice

C57BL/6 mice (male) used in this study were obtained from the Huachuang Sino (China). All animals (6 to 8 weeks old) were randomly assigned to treatment groups. Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages under group housing conditions whenever possible. They were maintained in a controlled environment with a 12-hr light/dark cycle and provided ad libitum access to water and standard rodent chow.

Randomization of mice into experimental groups was performed in strict accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). All animal procedures were approved by the IACUC of Tongji University (approval ID: SHDSYY-2023-P0011), ensuring compliance with ethical standards for animal research.

Xenograft tumor model

Healthy C57BL/6 mice were maintained in pathogen-free conditions, and allowed to acclimate for 1 week before being used. During the tumor formation assay, KPC cells (2 × 106) were injected into the flank of the mice. Once tumors were detected, the mice (n = 6) were intraperitoneal injected every other day with 5 mg/ml or 25 mg/ml MIP#1–3 peptides. The mice were sacrificed after 3 weeks, and tumor volumes were then measured.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 9.0. Data are presented as means ± S.D. Student’s t test was used for continuous variables. Pearson’s coefficient test was used for correlation analysis. For correlation, the Spearman rank correlation was used for continuous variables. Survival curves were calculated according to the Kaplan-Meier method; survival analysis was performed using the logrank test. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant difference.

Results

MST4 and 14-3-3ζ synergistically promote PC cell migration

To elucidate the mechanisms by which MST4 promotes PC progression, we employed a BioID-based proximity labeling approach to map the MST4 interactome in human PANC-1 PC cells (Fig. 1A, upper). A BirA-fused MST4 construct was generated and transfected into PANC-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Following biotin supplementation, biotinylated proteins were isolated and analyzed via quantitative tandem mass spectrometry. We identified 804 potential MST4-interacting proteins (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Table 1), including known partners such as STK25 and MOB4 [39]. Among the top 10 interactors (including STK25, TIPRL, TAGLN2, 14-3-3σ/ζ, TUBB4A, COPG2, and QARS; Fig. 1B), we focused on 14-3-3 proteins due to their established roles as key regulators of cancer-relevant cellular processes [43].

We first performed Transwell assays and found that knockdown of either MST4 or 14-3-3σ/ζ reduced PANC-1 cell migration, with combined knockdown yielding a stronger inhibitory effect (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Fig. 1B). Given the 70.2% amino acid similarity between 14-3-3σ and 14-3-3ζ (Supplementary Fig. 1C), we overexpressed 14-3-3ζ or MST4 and observed enhanced cell migration (Fig. 1D, E; Supplementary Fig. 1D, E). Importantly, 14-3-3ζ depletion reversed the pro-migratory effect of MST4 overexpression (Fig. 1D), while MST4 knockdown attenuated 14-3-3ζ-driven migration (Fig. 1E). These results indicate that MST4 and 14-3-3ζ synergistically promote PC cell migration.

MST4 directly binds to 14-3-3ζ in a phosphorylation-dependent manner

Given the functional synergy, we hypothesized that 14-3-3ζ is a direct binding partner of MST4. Immunofluorescence staining in PANC-1 cells overexpressing Flag-MST4 revealed significant colocalization with endogenous 14-3-3ζ (Fig. 2A, left). Quantitative colocalization analysis further verified this conclusion (Fig. 2A, right). Exogenous co-IP confirmed interaction between Flag-MST4 and 14-3-3ζ (Fig. 2B), which was also observed endogenously (Supplementary Fig. 2A).

To assess direct binding, we purified recombinant MST4 and 14-3-3ζ proteins from E. coli. Pull-down assays showed that MBP-14-3-3ζ, but not MBP alone, bound MST4 (Fig. 2C). Since MST4 undergoes autophosphorylation, we tested phosphorylation dependence: λPPase treatment reduced interaction, which was restored by ATP addition (Fig. 2C). MST assay determined a binding affinity of ~ 14 µM between MST4 and 14-3-3ζ (Fig. 2D).

Based on 14-3-3’s preference for phospho-serine/threonine motifs, we identified Thr320 in MST4 as a critical binding residue (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Fig. 2B). Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measured a Kd of 21 µM between phospho-MST4 peptide and 14-3-3ζ (Supplementary Fig. 2C). A point mutant, MST4T320A, showed markedly reduced binding to 14-3-3ζ in co-IP assays and MST assay (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Fig. 2D). These data demonstrate that phosphorylated MST4 directly binds 14-3-3ζ.

Dissection of the MST4–14-3-3ζ structural interface

We then solved the crystal structure of 14-3-3ζ (residues 1–232) in complex with a phospho-Thr320 MST4 peptide (residues 316–325) at 2.30 Å resolution (Fig. 3A; Table 1). The 14-3-3ζ structure comprises nine α-helices. The pMST4 peptide interacted not only with one 14-3-3ζ molecule but also with symmetric homodimer counterparts (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. 3A), burying a total interface of 12,397 Å² (Fig. 3B). Hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions between phosphate-pThr320 and Arg56/Lys120/Arg127 of 14-3-3ζ explained the phosphorylation dependence (Fig. 3C, right). Molecular dynamics simulations confirmed stable interactions, with average distances of 2.85 Å and 2.69 Å between pThr320 and Arg56/Arg127, respectively. The 14-3-3ζ dimer interface, involving residues Leu12, Ala16, Arg18 of one monomer and Ser58, Ile65, Glu89 of the other, was also resolved (Fig. 3C, left).

A kinase-dead mutant (MST4K53R) reduced migration, while a constitutively active mutant (MST4T178E) increased it (Fig. 3E; Supplementary Fig. 3C), confirming phosphorylation-dependent regulation. 14-3-3ζ cooperates with MST4 to activate YAP

Given the role of MST4 in the Hippo-YAP pathway, we investigated whether MST4 and 14-3-3ζ coregulate YAP. Knockdown of either protein increased YAP phosphorylation (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. 4A). Furthermore, we investigated the mechanism by which MST4-14-3-3ζ complex regulates the activation of YAP. Co-IP results revealed that MST4 knockdown enhanced the YAP-LATS1 interaction but reduced the YAP-TEAD4 interaction (Fig. 4B). Re-expression of wild-type MST4 in MST4-knockdown cells reversed effects: it decreased the YAP-LATS1 interaction and increased YAP-TEAD4 binding. In contrast, rescued with the T320A mutated MST4 failed to restore the interactions, yielding results comparable to those in MST4-knockdown cells (Fig. 4B). In addition, immunofluorescence observations revealed that knockdown of either MST4 or 14-3-3ζ resulted in a reduced nuclear proportion of YAP (Fig. 4C). Further YAP nucleocytoplasmic fractionation assays also showed that MST4 knockdown markedly reduced nuclear YAP levels. Similarly, overexpression of the MST4 T320A mutant recapitulated this phenotype. In contrast, re-expression of wildtype MST4 rescued this effect in MST4 knockdown cells (Fig. 4D). Subsequently, MST4 depletion reduced TEAD4-luciferase activity and expression of YAP targets CTGF and CYR61; these effects were rescued by wild-type MST4 but not MST4T320A (Fig. 4E, F; Supplementary Fig. 4B). Similarly, 14-3-3ζ knockdown suppressed CTGF expression, and abolished MST4-driven CTGF induction (Fig. 4G). Also, we detected increased YAP phosphorylation with MST4 and 14-3-3ζ knockdown in another KPC cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the MST4-14-3-3ζ complex suppresses the LATS1-YAP interaction and promotes YAP nuclear translocation in a phosphorylation-dependent manner, thereby enhancing the YAP-TEAD4 interaction.

Elevated MST4 and 14-3-3ζ correlate with YAP activation and poor prognosis in PC

Subsequent analysis of GEO dataset (GSE16515) revealed elevated mRNA levels of MST4, YWHAZ (encoding 14-3-3ζ), and SFN (14-3-3σ) in PC tissues (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. 5A). MST4 and YWHAZ expression positively correlated (Fig. 5B), and high co-expression predicted poorer survival (Fig. 5C). YAP and its target BCL2L1 were also upregulated in PC (Fig. 5D), and their levels correlated positively with MST4 and YWHAZ (Fig. 5E). High MST4 or YAP expression inversely correlated with patient survival (Supplementary Fig. 5B, C), supporting the clinical relevance of the MST4–14-3-3ζ–YAP axis. To verify our above analysis, we collected tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues from six PC patients, and detected the protein levels of MST4, 14-3-3ζ, YAP, and phosphorylated YAP (p-YAP) by Western blotting. The results showed that the protein levels of MST4, 14-3-3ζ, and YAP were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues, whereas the level of p-YAP was significantly downregulated in PC tumor tissues relative to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 5F). These data further confirm the results of our clinical correlation analysis, namely that MST4, 14-3-3ζ, and YAP are significantly increased in PC tumor tissues, while the phosphorylation level of YAP is significantly decreased.

Selective disrupting of the MST4–14-3-3ζ complex suppresses tumor growth

Finally, we designed peptides targeting the MST4–14-3-3ζ interface: MIP#1 and MIP#2 from 14-3-3ζ (residues 215–227 and 42–56), and MIP#3 from phospho-Thr320 MST4 (residues 316–325) (Fig. 6A). MBP pull-down assays confirmed all three peptides disrupted complex formation (Fig. 6B) in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). Transwell assays showed reduced migration in peptide-treated PANC-1 cells, with MIP#2 being most effective (Fig. 6C). Peptide treatment also decreased CTGF expression (Fig. 6D). In a xenograft model using KPC cells, MIP#1–3 treatment reduced tumor weight and growth, with MIP#1 and #2 showing significant efficacy (Fig. 6E–G). Beside that, the wound closure rate was significantly decreased after 24 h of MIP#1–3 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 6C). Among these peptides, MIP#2 exhibited the most prominent inhibitory effect, which was consistent with the observations from our mouse model. Notably, co-treatment with MIP#2 and first-line clinical chemotherapeutic drug gemcitabine exerted strong inhibitory effects on the metastasis of PANC-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6D), suggesting its promising clinical translational potential. Based on above, these results demonstrate that targeting the MST4–14-3-3ζ complex inhibits YAP signaling and exerts anti-tumor effects in PC.

Discussion

While previous research has extensively focused on the YAP-TEAD interaction [44–47], recent drug discovery efforts have shifted toward targeting upstream regulators of the Hippo pathway in cancer and other diseases. For example, Guan’s lab revealed the critical role of PITPα/β in modulating LATS1/2 and YAP [48]; Yu’s lab identified WWC proteins as mediators of LATS1/2 activation [49]; and we uncovered the oncogenic function of the STRN3–PP2A complex in inhibiting MST1/2 activity [50]. These studies collectively highlight therapeutic strategies aimed at reactivating Hippo kinase signaling. Consequently, several novel agents—such as SHAP (a STRN3-derived Hippo-activating peptide), microcolin B, and SuperHippo—have been developed to restore upstream kinase cascades involving MST1/2 and LATS1/2. Additionally, other regulators such as MAP4Ks act in parallel to MST1/2 and contribute redundantly to LATS1/2 and YAP/TAZ regulation [51]. Our earlier work demonstrated that MST4 kinase suppresses YAP activation in GC by directly phosphorylating YAP at Thr83 [33], further underscoring the Hippo pathway as a promising therapeutic target.

PC is known as the “king of cancers” with an extremely poor prognosis. As a core pathway governing organ size and cell fate, the aberrant activation of the Hippo signaling pathway - especially the nuclear translocation of YAP/TAZ - is a key mechanism driving proliferation, metastasis, therapeutic resistance, and immune escape in pancreatic cancer. As the core effector of this pathway, targeting the interaction between YAP/TAZ and TEAD or their transcriptional activity represents the mainstream current strategy. IAG933 can selectively disrupt the YAP-TEAD interaction and reduce Hippo-mediated transcriptional activity [52]. The development of PROTACs has made direct targeting of YAP feasible. Researchers have identified high-affinity, highly specific YAP nanobodies through screening of a nanobody phage library, which can specifically degrade YAP protein and exert potent antitumor effects in in vivo models [53, 54]. However, direct targeting strategies for the upstream regulatory molecules of the Hippo pathway in pancreatic cancer remain limited, most being indirect. For example, protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) catalyzes the dimethylation of MST2; this methylation blocks the homodimerization of MST2, thereby inhibiting its autophosphorylation and kinase activity, leading to inactivation of the Hippo pathway in pancreatic cancer. The PRMT5 inhibitor GSK3326595 can reactivate the dysregulated Hippo signaling pathway and suppress the growth of pancreatic cancer xenografts in mice [55]. Recently, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), TGF-β1 enhances PDCD10 and MST4 complex formation, thereby enabling PDCD10 to phosphorylate pRB to become inactive and ERK1/2 to become active [56]. Therefore, MST4 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of PC.

In the present study, we revisited MST4 and propose that it may serve as an upstream hub in Hippo signaling. These findings encourage further systematic investigation into the context-dependent roles of MST4 across different cancers. Using proximity-dependent BioID, we identified 14-3-3ζ as a novel binding partner of MST4 that promotes PC migration. Previous studies indicated that the MST4–MOB4 complex disrupts MST1–MOB1 assembly and exerts oncogenic functions in PC [39], whereas MST4 acts as a tumor suppressor in GC [33]. This contrast highlights the tissue-specific regulatory complexity of MST4. Our study suggests that binding partners may critically determine whether MST4 functions as an oncogene or tumor suppressor. Moreover, we established a generalizable screening strategy for identifying MST4 interactors, which can be applied to elucidate its roles in other cancer types.

We further resolved the structural basis of the MST4–14-3-3ζ complex, revealing a phosphorylation-dependent interaction mediated by MST4 at Thr320. This finding aligns with previously characterized 14-3-3 binding motifs, RSXpSXP and RXY/FXpS/TXP [57, 58], consistent with our structural and biochemical data. Leveraging this structural insight, we designed mechanistically informed peptides (MIPs) that disrupt the MST4–14-3-3ζ interface and exhibit potent anti-tumor effects. Although peptide therapeutics have advanced significantly in areas such as diabetes and anti-infective applications, their development in oncology remains challenging due to short half-life, protease sensitivity, and poor membrane permeability. Future efforts will focus on optimizing these peptides through cyclization and backbone modifications to enhance stability and bioavailability for clinical translation. Specifically, several optimization strategies can be applied for our designed peptides (MIPs). (i) Introduced a cyclic structure by linking the N‑ and C‑termini or side chains via chemical bonds to reduce peptide flexibility, enhance structural stability, and improve target binding affinity. (ii) Incorporated unnatural amino acids to replace natural residues, thereby reducing protease recognition and improving in vivo stability. (iii) Substituted natural peptide bonds (–CO–NH–) with non‑peptide bond linkages to prevent protease‑mediated hydrolysis and prolong the in vivo half‑life. Given that our antitumor peptides (MIPs) require long-term administration, we will further develop a delivery system using biodegradable materials such as PLGA and chitosan to encapsulate the peptides into microspheres. This formulation allows sustained in vivo release, reduces administration frequency, maintains effective peptide concentrations, and minimizes potential toxic side effects associated with frequent dosing.

14-3-3 proteins function as key regulatory scaffolds whose activity depends on their oligomeric state [59]. Phosphorylation of 14-3-3ζ at Ser58 markedly reduces its homodimerization affinity [60], and environmental factors such as temperature, ionic strength, and client protein binding further modulate its dimerization status. Our structural data indicate that MST4 engages symmetrically with the 14-3-3ζ homodimer. Given that MST4 is a kinase, we hypothesize that it may phosphorylate 14-3-3ζ at Ser58 to promote homodimer formation—a mechanism requiring further experimental validation. We also demonstrated that 14-3-3ζ homodimerization is essential for MST4 binding, suggesting that not only the MST4–14-3-3ζ interface but also the 14-3-3ζ dimer interface itself represents a promising target for therapeutic intervention. Accordingly, we are developing additional peptides aimed at disrupting 14-3-3ζ homodimerization and evaluating their anti-tumor efficacy.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identifies the MST4–14-3-3ζ complex as a phosphorylation-dependent activator of YAP signaling that synergistically enhances migration in pancreatic cancer. We elucidated the structural details of this complex and developed peptide inhibitors that effectively disrupt this interaction, suppress YAP activation, and inhibit tumor growth. Treatment with MIPs and first-line clinical chemotherapeutic drug gemcitabine exerted strong inhibitory effects on the metastasis in cellular level. Our findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting the MST4–14-3-3ζ axis in pancreatic cancer.
