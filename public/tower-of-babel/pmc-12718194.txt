Non-antibiotic nanoplatform based CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs for inhibition pneumonia caused by MRSA and its mechanism exploration
Authors: Qing Fan, Xinzhong Zhang, Sai Zhang, Menglin Cao, Lingyu Zhang, Lu Li, Wenting Li, Guimin Zhang, Wei Hong, Xiangjun Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a major threat to human health due to its multi-antibiotic resistance, either intrinsic or acquired. One of the acquired mechanisms lies in MRSA biofilms, which shield bacteria from antibiotic eradication, contributing to chronic infections. To address this challenge, we constructed the novel non-antibiotic nano-delivery platforms based on copper sulfide/gold nanocluster@liposomes nanoparticles (CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs). Upon the excitation of 1064 nm near-infrared light, these NPs exhibit excellent photothermal effect and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation ability, destroying the structure of bacterial membrane to kill MRSA and removing biofilm effectively. In vivo experiments demonstrated the target enrichment at infection sites, significant therapeutic efficacy in the subcutaneous cyst and pneumonia models caused by MRSA in mice, and a favorable biosafety profile. This study provides novel non-antibiotic strategies for treating drug-resistant bacterial infections that combine efficient antimicrobial properties with biosafety.Keywords: Pneumonia, MRSA, CuS nanoparticles, Non-antibiotic therapy

Introduction

Bacterial infections, especially those caused by drug-resistant bacteria such as methicillin-resistant staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), lead to severe clinic conditions, including wound infections, lung infections, sepsis, and endocarditis, resulting in high mortality rates and posing a serious threat to human life and health [[1], [2], [3]]. A major contributor to these prolonged infections is biofilm formation which protects the internal bacteria from host immune responses and limits antibiotic penetration [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Though biofilm formation is reported as a major cause for antibiotic treatment failure in bacterial infections [6], standardized clinical strategies for biofilm eradication remain lacking. Current treatment usually relies on mechanical removal or sustained high-dose antibiotic therapies [8]. However, the slow antibiotic penetration through biofilms not only diminishes therapeutic effect, but may also increases the risk of drug resistance [9]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new therapeutic strategies to treat biofilm-associated persistent bacterial infections.

In recent years, the photothermal therapy (PTT) based on near-infrared (NIR) light have gained great attention for their potential in biofilm removal [10,11]. Compared with conventional antibiotic treatment, PTT offer good antibacterial activity, minimal risk of resistance and fewer side effects [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. In the process of PTT, the photothermal agents cause localized hyperthermia under NIR irradiation to kill the bacteria [12,15,16]. A variety of photothermal materials such as some fluorescent dyes (IR 780, indocyanine green (ICG), boron dipyrrole methylene), gold nanorods, graphene nanomaterials, polydopamine, and copper sulfide (CuS) nanoparticles (NPs), have been developed for bacterial infection treatment [15,[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. However, while the body can withstand a short-term high temperature, normal surrounding cells may be destroyed [22]. The second NIR window (NIR-II, 1000–1350 nm) allows for deeper penetration but less risk of photothermal damage to normal tissue compared with NIR-I (750–1000 nm) [[23], [24], [25]]. Photosensitizers are irradiated with NIR light to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are effective in killing bacteria by disrupting cell membranes or causing oxidative damage [16]. CuS NPs are good candidates for PTT due to their degradability and excellent photothermal conversion efficiency in the NIR-II band [[24], [25], [26]], which can also act as photosensitizers, producing ROS in the presence of 1064 nm laser [[27], [28], [29]]. The combination of ROS-catalyzed therapy and PTT has been found to enhance ⋅OH production, thereby reducing thermal damage to normal tissues by PTT [23,30]. At the same time, the high temperatures generated during PTT enhance the drug delivery efficiency. This dual impact helps to improve the overall efficacy of various treatments, resulting in a synergistic therapeutic approach [27,31]. However, CuS NPs are prone to agglomeration, which may lead to localized overconcentration and consequently damage normal cells [32]. Currently, researchers have developed various CuS composites to mitigate their defects and have applied them in antimicrobial research. Yang et al. constructed a CuS-CaO2-Res@ZIF-8 composite nanomaterial, ultimately achieving targeted destruction of biofilms through the synergistic action of PTT and chemodynamic therapy (CDT) [33]. Jia et al. prepared CuS/NO hydrogels for antibacterial and tissue repair promotion. The development of composite nanoagents based on CuS NPs represents a novel strategy to combat bacterial resistance [34].

Gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) have attracted researchers interest in their biomedical applications due to their ultra-small nano-size, good biocompatibility and optical/catalytic properties [[35], [36], [37], [38]]. The ultra-small size of AuNCs enables them to internalise into the bacteria, where they induce ROS accumulation, disrupt the normal metabolism of the bacteria and ultimately cause bacterial death [[39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. Although AuNCs hold promise, they still suffer from insufficient ROS generation and limited near-infrared absorption. To overcome these challenges, researchers have explored additional methods to synergistically enhance the efficacy of ROS generation based on AuNCs [42]. For example, Liu et al. effectively countered Staphylococcus aureus infection by combining ICG with AuNCs to regulate ROS production [43]. Additionally, Zheng et al. constructed a synergistic antibacterial agent by assembling paramagnetic holmium ions and AuNCs onto graphene oxide nanosheets, ultimately leading to the death of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [45]. One of the challenges in anti-infective therapy is achieving lesion-targeted drug delivery while maintaining spatiotemporally controlled release during treatment [24]. Nanomedicine-targeted drug delivery systems or environmentally responsive modifications on nanocarriers, such as those sensitive to temperature, light, pH, or enzymes, can effectively deliver drugs to the intended site of action [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50]].

In this study, we constructed the novel non-antibiotic nano-delivery platform. As shown in Scheme 1, CuS NPs were first synthesized via chemical precipitation, and then the prepared AuNCs were loaded onto CuS NPs to synthesis the CuS/AuNCs NPs. They were subsequently encapsulated within liposomes (Lip) based on pH-responsive 1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DSPE)-lysine-2,3-dimethylmaleic anhydride (DMA). The obtained CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs exhibit a negative surface under physiological conditions, which effectively prolongs their circulation in the body. When it is injected through the vein, it reaches the site of infection, under weakly acidic microenvironmental conditions, the amide bond on 2,3-dimethylmaleic anhydride (DMA) was broken, thus exposing the positively charged amino group, making the NPs positively charged and facilitating electrostatic binding with negatively charged bacteria. Meanwhile, under 1064 nm laser irradiation and bacterial microenvironment, the Cu2+ are gradually released, a Fenton-like reaction is initiated, leading to the generation of Cu+ and ROS. In addition, the heat generated, accelerated the release of AuNCs, which internalized into bacteria, and even makes the bacterial membrane wrinkled and concave, destroying the bacterial membrane structure synergistically to exert antibacterial effects. In summary, the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs prepared in this study provides a new method of synergistic therapy for treating bacterial infections.

Methods

Synthesis of AuNCs

Under dark conditions, 250 μL of an aqueous solution of HAuCl4 (Chloroauric acid, 20 mM) and 2 mL of MHA (6-Mercaptohexanoic acid, 5 mM) were added to 2.4 mL of ultrapure water. Subsequently, 250 μL NaOH solution (1 M) and 100 μL NaBH4 solution (20 mM) were added sequentially. After 3 h of reaction protected from light, the prepared AuNCs were obtained.

Synthesis of CuS NPs

0.2 mM CuCl2·2H2O and 200 mg PVP (Polyvinylpyrrolidone) were added to 100 mL of DI water with continuous stirring for 5 min. Then, 4 mL of NaOH solution (0.2 M) was added dropwise. After continuing the reaction for 5 min, 4 mL of L-AA (L-Ascorbic acid) solution (0.1 M) was added to it. The reaction was continued at room temperature for 5 min, then 4 mL of aqueous Na2S (1 M) was added and the solution was observed to turn black immediately. Subsequently, the reaction was continued with stirring for 3 h at 60 °C in a water bath. At the end of the reaction, the solution was centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 10 min) and washed alternately with water and ethanol to remove unreacted impurities. Finally, the CuS NPs were obtained by re-dispersing with appropriate DI water.

Preparation of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs

The CuS NPs solution prepared above was mixed with the AuNCs solution and stirred for at least 12 h, then centrifuged and washed to obtain the CuS/AuNCs solution.

CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs were prepared by thin film dispersion method. Firstly, soy lecithin, cholesterol and 1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DSPE)-lysine-2,3-dimethylmaleic anhydride (DMA) were ultrasonically dispersed in a mixture of chloroform and methanol (v:v, 2:1) in a mass ratio (7:1:3), followed by the preparation of liposome films using a rotary evaporator. Subsequently, the liposome films were hydrated by adding the CuS/AuNCs NPs solution prepared above and then treated with ultrasound to obtain CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution.

Interaction between free MRSA USA300 and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs

MRSA USA300 and rhodamine-B (RHB) labled CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution were incubated at 37 °C under dark conditions, and samples were collected at different time points (0.5, 1, 2 and 4 h). The fluorescence signals of RHB were determined qualitatively and quantitatively using flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II) and fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, BX53F2), respectively.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The broth dilution method was used for MIC determination. Briefly, MRSA USA300 cultured to logarithmic stage was diluted with broth to a concentration of 105 CFU/mL. Free AuNCs, CuS NPs and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs were diluted using the two-fold dilution method, and then with the different preparation groups and bacteria were co-incubated for 12 h in 96-well plates. To ensure the reliability of the experiment, the groups containing only bacterial suspension and only broth medium were used as control groups. Six replicates were set up for the MIC experiment for each sample.

Live/dead bacteria staining

Free AuNCs (26 μg/mL), CuS NPs (80 μg/mL), CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs (80 μg/mL, equivalent to 26 μg/mL AuNCs) and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser (1 W/cm2, 5 min) were incubated overnight with MRSA USA300 suspension. Subsequently, the bacteria were incubated with a mixture of SYTO 9 and propidium iodide (1:1) for 30 min at room temperature protected from light. The residual dye was removed by centrifugal washing with PBS, followed by resuspension of the bacterial precipitate with an appropriate amount of PBS, and the fluorescence images were recorded using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, BX53F2).

Bacterial morphological observations

Changes in bacterial morphology and membrane integrity after treatment with different formulation groups were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The MRSA USA300 suspension was first co-incubated with the different preparation groups for 4 h at 37 °C. It was then fixed by 2.5 % glutaraldehyde fixative for 12 h. Gradient elution was performed with different concentrations of ethanol (50 %, 70 %, 80 %, 95 % and 100 %). Finally, the processed samples were dried, sprayed with gold, and the bacterial morphology was observed using SEM (Zeiss, EVO LS15).

Measurement of ROS in bacteria

DCFH-DA fluorescent probe was used to study ROS generation in bacteria. The MRSA suspension was incubated with PBS, AuNCs (26 μg/mL) and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs (80 μg/mL, equivalent to 26 μg/mL AuNCs) at 37 °C for 30 min, then irradiated with a 1064 nm laser (1 W/cm2) for 5 min. After another incubation for 2 h, it was then washed by PBS and resuspended by adding 1 mL of PBS. Then, 2 μL DCFH-DA (10 mM) probe was added. After 30 min incubation away from light, 20 μL Hoechest 33,258 (1 mg/mL) was added for bacterial staining. Finally, fluorescence imaging was observed using an Olympus BX53F2.

Cytoplasmic membrane potential (MP) measurement

Bacterial MP was determined using the BacLight Bacterial Membrane Potential Kit. Briefly, bacterial suspensions with an OD600 of 0.5 were co-incubated for 1 h with free AuNCs (26 μg/mL), CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs (80 μg/mL, equivalent to 26 μg/mL AuNCs) and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser (1 W/cm2, 5 min). Then, the incubation was continued for 30 min by adding 3,3′ diethoxycarbon blue (DiOC2(3)). Red/green fluorescent ground changes were observed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCanto II).

Biofilm penetration

A suspension of MRSA bacteria in logarithmic growth phase, concentrated 100-fold, was added to a glass-bottomed dish and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h to form a mature biofilm. The cleaned biofilm was then co-incubated with RHB-labled CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution for different time (1 h, 2 h and 4 h). Subsequently, the mixed solution was discarded and washed gently with PBS. Then, SYTO 9 dye was added and stained at 37 °C for 30 min protected from light before being washed twice with PBS. The fluorescence within the bacterial biofilm was observed using a Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (Zeiss, LSM880) to evaluate the penetration of CuS/AuNCs@Lip into the bacterial biofilm.

In vitro antibiofilm ability

Crystal violet staining was utilized to evaluate biofilm removal by CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs. Briefly, mature MRSA biofilms cultured in 96-well plates were co-incubated with different concentrations of AuNCs solution, CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser (1 W/cm2, 5 min) at 37 °C for 12 h. The incubation was carried out in the same way as the incubation in a 96-well plate. It was then fixed with methanol for 15 min, stained with 0.5 % crystal violet solution for 30 min, washed with PBS, dried at room temperature, and observed for the removal of the biofilm using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, BX53F2).

The in vitro biofilm resistance of AuNCs (52 μg/mL), CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs (160 μg/mL, equivalent to 52 μg/mL AuNCs) and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser (1 W/cm2, 5 min) was assessed by CLSM. The different preparations were co-cultured with mature MRSA biofilms for 12 h after. A mixture of SYTO 9 and propidium iodide (1:1) was added and incubated at room temperature away from light for 30 min. Fluorescence data were subsequently observed using a CLSM (Zeiss, LSM 880).

SEM was further used to assess the changes in the appearance and morphology of the different preparations after interaction with the biofilm. That is, mature biofilms were co-cultured with AuNCs (52 μg/mL), CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs (160 μg/mL, equivalent to 52 μg/mL AuNCs) and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser (1 W/cm2, 5 min) for 4 h. The residual biofilm was cured overnight using 2.5 % glutaraldehyde and dehydrated in gradients with different concentrations of ethanol (50 %, 70 %, 80 %, 95 % and 100 %). Finally, the treated samples were dried, sprayed with gold, and the biofilm morphology was observed using SEM (Zeiss, EVO LS15).

Treatment of subcutaneous cysts

A subcutaneous cyst model was first established in Balb/c nude mice. A 108 CFU/mL bacterial suspension of MRSA USA300 was injected into the dorsal subcutaneous region at 200 μL per mouse. After successful infection, 200 μL of RHB-labled CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution was injected through the tail vein. Subsequently, the fluorescence intensity and targeting at different time points (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 h) were observed by a in vivo imaging system (IVIS) (PerkinElmer, IVIS Spectrum), and the mice were euthanized after 24 h. The cyst site and other major organs were taken to observe the distribution of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs in different tissues and the fluorescence values were analyzed and recorded.

During the in vivo treatment of mice, successfully infected dorsal subcutaneous mice were randomly divided into four groups, and were injected with saline in the tail vein (group I), free AuNCs (group II), CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs (group III, 1.2 mg/kg), and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser (1 W/cm2, 5 min) (group IV) administered at a fixed time every other day, and at 8 h after the injections. The infected sites of mice in group IV were illuminated using a 1064 nm laser, and real-time temperature changes were recorded. During the treatment period, the weight of the mice was recorded daily. At the end of treatment, the mice were euthanized and the cysts were ground for colony counting. In addition, muscle tissue around the cyst was taken and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h. After treatment, the tissue surrounding the cyst was stained with H&E.

Treatment of pneumonia

A BALB/c nude mouse pneumonia model was established to further evaluate in vivo therapeutic effects. A 108 CFU/mL bacterial suspension of MRSA USA300 was dropped into the nasal cavity of mice to allow inhalation into the lungs, 10 μL at a time, for 5 consecutive days of infection. Upon successful infection, 200 μL of RHB-labled CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution was injected through the tail vein. The fluorescence intensity and targeting were observed at different time points (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 h) by IVIS, and the mice were euthanized after 24 h. Lung tissues and other major organs were removed, and the distribution of the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs in the body was observed and analyzed.

For in vivo pharmacodynamic study of mouse pneumonia, the green fluorescently labeled MRSA USA300 strain (sfGFP MRSA USA300) was used as the model strain to establish a pneumonia model in BALB/c nude mice. When infection was successful, the drug was administered according to the treatment of subcutaneous cysts. The weight of the mice was recorded daily during the treatment period. At the end of treatment, the mice were euthanized, and their lung tissues were taken to observe the fluorescence intensity of the lungs of different treatment groups, after which some of the lung tissues were removed to be ground and homogenized, coated, and colonies were counted to analyze the treatment effect. In addition, the remaining lung tissue and other major organs were taken for H&E staining to observe inflammatory cell infiltration.

In vivo acute toxicity study

The maximum tolerated dose (MTD) was used to evaluate the in vivo acute toxicity of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs (12 mg/kg). Twelve healthy mice (female and male) were randomly divided into two groups of six mice each. One group was injected with saline in the tail vein and the other group was injected with CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution. The body weights of the mice were recorded every two days. After 14 days, blood was taken from the mice and serum samples were collected for testing and analyzed for liver and kidney toxicity. The mice were euthanized and the major organs were taken for H&E staining, and the pathological changes of each organ were observed by light microscopy.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs

In this experiment, CuS NPs with uniform size and good dispersion were first prepared by chemical precipitation (Fig. 1A). The results of X-ray diffraction (XRD) showed that the CuS NPs were consistent with the standard card (PDF#06–0464) (Fig. 1B), which indicated the successful preparation of CuS NPs. Next, sulfhydryl-protected AuNCs were synthesized (Fig. 1C) and loaded onto CuS NPs by physical mixing to obtain CuS/AuNCs NPs (Fig. 1D). To further enhance the biocompatibility of the materials, CuS/AuNCs NPs were wrapped with pH-responsive phospholipids by thin-film hydration to obtain CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs. Phosphotungstic acid-negative stained TEM (Fig. 1E) clearly showed the structure of the lipid bilayer on the outside of CuS/AuNCs NPs, indicating the successful encapsulation of Lip. Elemental mapping analysis detected the presence of S, Cu, and Au, while Au elements showed a uniform distribution on the surface of the CuS NPs (Fig. 1F), indicating the successful loading of AuNCs. The loading ability of AuNCs was 33.69 μg/mL per 100 μg/mL of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs. The particle sizes of CuS, CuS/AuNCs, and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 1G). Their hydrated particle sizes were around 126 nm, 135 nm, and 145 nm, respectively. The increasing trend of the particle sizes corroborated the layer-by-layer modification process of the materials. The stability of the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs was evaluated by monitoring their particle sizes under various storage conditions and in different solvents. The results indicated that the prepared NPs displayed exceptional stability. (Figs. S1, S2, S3). The Zeta potential indicated (Fig. 1H) that the zeta potential of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs was −60 mV at pH 7.4, while the potential flipped to 10 mV at pH 5.6. The pH-responsive charge reversal effectively enhances the adsorption of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs onto the surfaces of bacteria and biofilms, significantly improving targeting efficiency and interaction with bacteria in the biofilm microenvironment. We also analyzed the elemental composition of CuS/AuNCs NPs using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. As shown in the XPS full spectrum of CuS/AuNCs NPs (Fig. 1I), the prepared NPs were detected to contain the elements of Cu, S, and Au, as shown in the corresponding high-resolution spectra in (Fig. 1J, K, L), the two peaks of Cu 2p at 932.38 eV and 952.28 eV corresponded to Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2, respectively, the two peaks of S 2p at 161.88 eV and 162.88 eV correspond to S 2p1/2 and S 2p2/3, respectively; and the two peaks of Au 4f at 84.78 eV and 88.48 eV correspond to Au 4f2/7 and Au 4f2/5, respectively, and the above results further proved that CuS/AuNCs NPs were successfully prepared.

The UV–Vis–NIR absorption spectra show (Fig. 2A) that CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs exhibits stronger absorption in the NIR-II window beyond 1000 nm, which provides for the study of the photothermal effect of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs in the NIR-II region. In order to investigate the photothermal properties of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs, the solution was irradiated with a 1064 nm NIR laser to observe the warming. As shown in Fig. 2B, the temperature rise of 100 μg/mL solution within 5 min showed significant power dependence under irradiation with power density of 0.2–0.5 W/cm2. When the power reaches 0.4 W/cm2, the temperature can rise to over 60 °C. In addition, as shown in Fig. 2C, the temperature change of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution was positively correlated with the concentration of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs at concentrations ranging from 25 to 300 μg/mL after irradiation by a 1064 nm NIR laser (0.4 W/cm2) for 5 min. The corresponding infrared thermography images are shown in Fig. 2D, and again a positive correlation of temperature with concentration can be observed for the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution. In addition, based on the cooling curve (Fig. 2E) and the photothermal conversion equation of the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution, the system heat transfer time constant τs was measured to be 181.8 s (Fig. S4). Cyclic stability tests (Fig. 2F) showed that after four cycles, the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution still maintains a good warming effect and exhibits good photothermal stability.

The DCFH-DA was used to detect ROS release from CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs under NIR laser. As depicted in Fig. 2G, the ROS levels of the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution increased following NIR laser irradiation, with the generation of ROS rising in correlation with the concentration (Fig. S5). In contrast, the ROS levels in the free AuNCs solution and the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs solution were lower than those in the CuS/AuNCs@Lip + Laser group. Consequently, the results indicate that the NIR laser can induce CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs to produce a substantial amount of ROS, which is advantageous for enhancing the antibacterial effect. The CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs were put in different pH and mimetic microenvironment, the Cu content was assessed using inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis, as shown in Table S1, the release amount of Cu can reach 50.89 μg mL−1 at pH 5.6+H2O2 group. Considering that the concentration of H2O2 in bacterial inflammatory microenvironment is significantly higher than that in normal cells, we hypothesize that the released Cu2+ ions can undergo a Fenton-like reaction with endogenous H2O2 to produce hydroxyl radicals (·OH), thereby exerting bactericidal effects. To validate this hypothesis, we employed 5, 5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) as the ·OH trapping agent. As shown in Fig. 2H, after 5 min of light exposure, a characteristic set of quadruplet peaks with an intensity ratio of 1:2:2:1 emerged in the electron spin resonance (ESR) spectrum of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + H2O2+laser, whereas such signals were not detected in PBS group. Additionally, terephthalic acid (TA), which reacts with ·OH to form the fluorescent product TAOH, was utilized to monitor the generation of ·OH (Fig. S6). Upon incubation with H2O2 and laser, CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs exhibited limited fluorescence of TAOH, and the fluorescence intensity was significantly enhanced with the increase of the concentration of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs. The concentration of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs was positively correlated with the fluorescence intensity. Collectively, these findings confirm that the photoinduced release of Cu2+ from CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs in the NIR-II region triggers a Fenton-like reaction, leading to the production of ·OH and enhancing the bactericidal efficacy.

In vitro antibacterial evaluation

RHB-labeled CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs were observed under fluorescence microscopy to assess their affinity for free MRSA USA300 strains (Fig. 3A) and quantitatively evaluated via flow cytometry (Fig. 3B). The results showed that the intensity of the detected fluorescence gradually increased with the prolongation of the co-incubation time of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs with free bacteria, reaching the maximum at 4 h, proving that CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs can be effectively adsorbed on the surface of free bacteria, thus serving to release the drug to produce an antibacterial effect. In order to examine the antimicrobial activity of the NPs, we determined the MIC values of free AuNCs, CuS NPs, CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser, respectively (Fig. 3C). The results showed that the concentration of free AuNCs at around 100 μg/mL had an inhibitory effect on the growth of MRSA. The MIC value of CuS NPs is greater than 250 μg/mL. CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs was not seen to exert a significant antimicrobial effect at the tested concentration of 160 μg/mL, whereas after laser irradiation, it was able to exert a significant antibacterial effect at a concentration of 40 μg/mL. The inhibitory effect may be attributed to the accelerated release of AuNCs after laser irradiation, while the heat generated by CuS after laser irradiation and ROS synergize to exert a bactericidal effect.

The in vitro antibacterial effect of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs was further evaluated by live/dead bacterial staining. As shown in Fig. 3D and Fig. S7, S8A, only a small number of bacteria in the Control, free AuNCs, CuS NPs, and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs groups were stained red, while almost all bacteria in the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser group were stained red. It shows that CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs has a good bactericidal effect by killing almost all bacteria under NIR laser irradiation. The ROS levels within the bacteria were assessed using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe after treatment with different formulation groups (Fig. 3E, Fig. S8B and Fig. S9). Upon irradiation with 1064 nm laser, the bacteria in the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs-treated group exhibited intense green fluorescence, suggesting significant ROS production. In contrast, only a little green fluorescence was observed in the other groups. Consequently, it is evident that the NIR laser can augment ROS generation from CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs, which can cause oxidative stress damage to cells, proteins, and DNA, resulting in irreversible oxidative damage to bacterial cell membranes. Therefore, in order to accurately characterize the degree of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs-mediated impairment of membrane function, we detected the changes in bacterial membrane potential by means of the Bacterial Membrane Potential Kit. As shown in Fig. 3F, the free AuNCs and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs groups exhibited weaker depolarizing fluorescence signals compared to the control group, indicating that the bacterial membranes maintained a normal polarization state. The CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs-treated group excited by 1064 nm laser showed a significant fluorescence right shift, suggesting a strong membrane depolarization effect, indicating cell membrane damage and increased membrane permeability. This is consistent with the results observed by SEM (Fig. 3G). Bacteria treated with free AuNCs and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs group had only individual bacterial membrane surface crumpling, while under NIR light irradiation, the morphology of bacteria treated with CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs group was significantly deformed. The surface of bacterial membranes was concavely broken (red arrows pointing to). This may be due to the oxidative stress damage to the bacteria caused by the generated ROS, while the heat generated by CuS NPs under 1064 nm laser caused some damage to the bacterial membrane structure.

In vitro antibiofilm studies

The formation of bacterial biofilm hinders drug penetration and reduces its the effect on the bacteria encapsulated in the biofilm substrate, greatly weakening the bactericidal effect. Effective drug penetration, therefore, is a critical step in the therapeutic effect. In this study, the permeation of RHB-labled CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs to MRSA biofilm was investigated by using CLSM. As shown in Fig. 4A and Fig. S10, the red fluorescence signal gradually enhanced with the co-incubation time after CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs was co-incubated with the biofilm. It is shown that the degree of penetration of NPs into the biofilm becomes stronger with time. The ability of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs to scavenge MRSA biofilms was examined by using crystal violet staining. As shown in Fig. 4B, free AuNCs and CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs were still present with intact biofilms at the highest concentrations tested. In contrast, the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser group could have a certain removal ability for the biofilm at 80 μg/mL, and when the concentration reached 160 μg/mL, the biofilm was almost completely removed. Therefore, this phenomenon production may be attributed to the ability of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs to effectively penetrate the biofilm, as well as the synergistic ROS and photo-thermal production under 1064 nm laser irradiation. Next, a specific concentration of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs (160 μg/mL) was used to further investigate its removal effect on MRSA biofilm by CLSM. As shown in Fig. 4C and Fig. S11, strong red fluorescence could be observed in the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs-treated MRSA biofilm under the irradiation of laser, indicating that the biofilm was severely damaged. SEM results (Fig. 4D) showed the same results, the biofilm in the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser group showed a large structural collapse, leaving only a fragmented matrix with sporadic surviving free-living bacteria, while the untreated group maintained the typical dense three-dimensional network structure. The above results suggest that CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs can not only efficiently penetrate the biofilm, but also deeply disintegrated biofilm structure through the synergistic photothermal-photodynamic effect triggered by NIR light, providing an innovative therapeutic strategy for solving the biofilm-associated recalcitrant infections.

Evaluation of subcutaneous cyst treatment

First, the RHB-labled CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs was used to evaluate its in vivo targeting and tissue distribution in mice. In vivo imaging system (IVIS) showed (Fig. 5A) that fluorescent signals could be detected in the lesion area after tail vein injection of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs, and the fluorescence intensity reached its maximum after 12 h post-injection and remained significant 24 h after injection (Fig. 5B). This indicates that CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs can effectively target the infection site and achieve prolonged enrichment at the infection site, which helps to achieve a precise antimicrobial therapy. We further characterized the photothermal effect of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs in mice. After 8 h of drug administration, the cyst site was illuminated. As shown in Fig. 5C and D, the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs group could rapidly increase to more than 50 °C within 5 min, and this temperature could effectively disrupt the structure of bacterial membranes and exert antibacterial effects. Notably, there was no significant damage on the laser irradiated normal tissue regions (Fig. S12), which was attributed to the deep tissue penetration property of CuS NPs in the NIR-II window (1000–1350 nm), highlighting the advantages of this system in selective photothermal therapy.

In addition, the weight changes of the mice were dynamically monitored during the treatment period (Fig. 5E), and the weights of the mice in different groups were slightly increased, suggesting that the obtained system was well biocompatible. At the end of treatment, treatment efficacy was assessed by quantitative colony counts (Fig. 5F). Compared with the control group, the number of bacteria at the cyst site in the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser group was significantly reduced, which was significantly better than that in the free AuNCs group and the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs group. Pathological analysis of the tissue surrounding subcutaneous cysts further validated the advantages of combined CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs and laser therapy. H&E staining revealed significant inflammatory infiltration in the control group, in contrast, the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + laser therapy group exhibited significantly reduced inflammatory infiltration with tissue structure approaching normalcy (Fig. 5G). Together, these multidimensional experimental results indicate that CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs can achieve efficient clearance of deep tissue infections, providing a novel nanomedicine strategy with translational potential for the treatment of clinically resistant bacterial infections.

Therapeutic evaluation of pneumonia in mice

Addressing the therapeutic challenges of lung infections, this study further validated the targeting and therapeutic efficacy of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs in a mouse model of pneumonia. The results were similar to those of the dorsal cysts. In the results of in vivo 3D imaging (Fig. 6A) as well as in vivo fluorescence imaging (Fig. 6B) showed that the fluorescence of the lungs gradually increased with time after the injection of RHB-labled CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs through the tail vein, and strong fluorescence was still present at the test time of 24 h. The results of the pneumonia targeting study were similar to those of the dorsal cysts. The fluorescence intensity values in mouse lungs (Fig. 6C) further indicated the good targeting and long retention time of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs.

Based on the deep penetration advantage of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs in the NIR-II window, in vivo photothermal experiments confirmed (Fig. 6D and E) that infected lung tissues, which were subjected to laser irradiation after 8 h of drug administration in mice, the temperature could be increased to about 45.8 °C within 2.5 min, whereas there was no significant change in the temperature of the saline group, suggesting that it could effectively kill the pathogenic bacteria while avoiding thermal damage to the surrounding normal lung tissues. The changes in the body weight of the mice during the treatment period are shown in Fig. 6F. All the mice in the treatment group showed a gradual increase in body weight compared with the control group. At the end of the treatment, mouse lung tissue homogenates were coated and colony counting was performed, and the results, as shown in Fig. 7A and B, showed that bacteria in the lungs of mice treated with CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser were significantly reduced. In contrast, the lung tissue of the CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs + Laser treatment group did not show significant pathological changes. H&E staining of major organs of the heart, liver, spleen, and kidneys revealed no pathological changes, confirming the systemic safety of the nanosystem at therapeutic doses. Comprehensive results of the above experiments show that under NIR laser irradiation, CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs can break through the tissue depth limitation and provide a safe and efficient therapeutic solution for deep organ infections.

Biosafety evaluation

The biosafety of nanomaterials has been a common concern for researchers and an important factor in the further application of nanomaterials in vivo. The biocompatibility of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs was examined using MTT assay. As shown in Fig. 8A, the survival rate of Vero cells was higher than 85 % in all tested concentrations. Then, the erythrocyte hemolysis test was used to evaluate blood safety (Fig. 8B). CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs showed a hemolysis rate of less than 5 % at all concentrations tested and remained above the in vivo administered concentration even at the highest tested concentration of 256 μg/mL. These results confirm that CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs have good hemocompatibility and low cytotoxicity for in vivo injectable drug delivery. In addition, we administered high doses of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs (12 mg/kg) to healthy BALB/c nude mice and examined its effects on body weight, major organs and biochemical parameters. The results showed that CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs did not affect body weight or biochemical parameters (Fig. 8C and D). Importantly, no significant changes were found in the H&E of vital tissues and organs such as heart, liver, spleen, lungs, lungs, and kidneys (Fig. 8E), demonstrating that CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs has little, even no side effects within a certain concentration range.

Conclusions

In the present work, we successfully designed and synthesized the novel non-antibiotic antimicrobial platform of CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs, under physiological conditions, the systems bear a negative charge, allowing for extended circulation within the body. Upon arrival at the infection site, the weakly acidic microenvironment triggers a charge reversal, which increases the system's affinity for bacteria and enhances its penetration through biofilms. Simultaneously, under irradiation with a 1064 nm laser and in the presence of the bacterial microenvironment, Cu2+ is gradually released, initiating a Fenton-like reaction that produces Cu+ and ROS. Moreover, the generated heat accelerates the release of AuNCs, promoting their uptake by bacteria and ultimately resulting in bacterial death. Experimental results indicate that CuS/AuNCs@Lip NPs can be specifically targeted and concentrated at the site of infection, effectively penetrating the biofilm barrier. Under the NIR laser, the obtained NPs could result in the depolarization of bacterial membrane potential, disruption of membrane integrity, and degradation of the biofilm matrix. In vivo therapeutic outcomes further validate its superior antimicrobial efficacy. This strategy, which employs the synergistic effects of photothermal therapy and ROS to clear biofilms, presents an innovative solution with the potential for clinical application in treating drug-resistant bacterial infections. Additionally, the use of 1064 nm laser excitation in this study allows for deeper tissue penetration, offering a novel method for addressing infections that are located deeper within the body.
