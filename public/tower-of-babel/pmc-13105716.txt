Comments to the Editor: opinions on “Associations between dietary intake and pancreatic disease: a Mendelian randomization study”
Authors: Weilong Wang, Xu Qiu, Zhenfeng Zhou
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in research and literature is increasing, but we still need to exercise moderation in its application[1]. Recently, I read a study; this study employs two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) techniques for the first time to explore the potential causal relationship between dietary intake and pancreatic disease, a topic of significant clinical relevance. This study ingeniously employed two-sample MR to systematically evaluate the causal relationships between 26 dietary factors and acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, and pancreatic cancer. The most significant finding was that genetically predicted dried fruit intake exhibited a strong, independent, and robust protective effect against both acute and chronic pancreatitis (OR = 0.289, P < 0.001, FDR < 0.001). These results were further validated through meta-analysis of the second outcome dataset, multivariate MR (with adjustments for zinc, calcium, vitamin C, and vitamin E), and comprehensive sensitivity analyses. The independent protective effect of lettuce/raw vegetables against chronic pancreatitis was equally compelling. This finding not only represent the strongest causal evidence linking diet to pancreatitis to date, but may also be one of the few actionable recommendations in nutritional epidemiology that can be advised to patients immediately[2]. Regular consumption of dried fruits (such as raisins, apricots, figs, and dates) could significantly reduce the risk of pancreatitis. The robustness of this discovery sets a benchmark in current meta-analysis studies and warrants inclusion in future guidelines for pancreatitis prevention and management. However, as with all high-quality research, this paper has several areas for further improvement, including the following points: The dietary exposure measurement is crude. UK Biobank’s dietary data only records “frequency of intake” (ranging from never to multiple times daily), lacking information on intake amounts and failing to differentiate specific dried fruit varieties (e.g., raisins vs apricots vs figs). This makes it impossible to conduct dose-response analysis or determine whether specific dried fruits (such as anthocyanin-rich raisins or magnesium-rich apricots) contributed to the primary protective effects[3]. In the future, dietary records or plasma biomarkers (e.g. serum magnesium, alpha-linolenic acid, procyanidin metabolites) should be used as exposures for MR to obtain more accurate causal estimates.The strength of some exposure instrumental variables was insufficient. For certain dietary exposures (e.g., red wine, beer, bacon, lamb), the authors relaxed the significance threshold to P < 1 × 10−5 for SNP selection. Although the F-statistics were all >10, some exposures only had single-digit SNPs, resulting in limited statistical power[4], The significant results for red wine, bread, pork, and oily fish were not replicated in the second dataset, with no False Discovery Rate (FDR) thresholds met. Thus, the final conclusion was limited to a “may” statement. Future studies should prioritize larger-sample dietary genome-wide association studies (GWAS), such as the forthcoming UKB + 23andMe joint analysis, or employ multi-effectivity correction methods (e.g., MR-PRESSO global test + MR-Lasso) to further refine instrumental variables.Population ancestry limitations: Exposure data comes from the European UK Biobank, while outcomes are from Finns. Although both are of European ancestry, there are still certain population structure differences. Future studies should incorporate East Asian populations (such as China Kadoorie Biobank, BJB) or African dietary GWAS for cross-ancestry validation, especially whether the protective effect of dried fruits also exists in China populations, which is worth paying attention to[5].The most probable mechanism of protective effect of dried fruits is that they are rich in magnesium, polyphenols, vitamin E, monounsaturated fatty acids and other antioxidant and anti-inflammatory components[6], However, the author only adjusted for zinc, calcium, vitamin C, and vitamin E in the MVMR without conducting further mediation MR or performing bidirectional MR and co-localization analyses with plasma magnesium and polyphenol metabolites GWAS, thereby missing the opportunity to elevate the “phenomenal discovery” to “mechanistic evidence”.With only 3139 pancreatic cancer cases recorded by FinnGen, the failure to replicate risk signals from oily fish was unsurprising. Overall, these limitations do not materially compromise the core conclusions of this study – the protective effect of dried fruits against pancreatitis is robust enough to warrant immediate clinical implementation. Through a rigorous meta-analysis, authors like Zhang Qi have delivered a simple, safe, and cost-effective dietary recommendation for pancreatitis patients, demonstrating the greatest strength of the Mendelian randomization method. We look forward to the author team’s follow-up research: (1) separate analysis of different types of dried fruits; (2) MR with plasma magnesium or polyphenol metabolites as exposure; (3) validation in China population. This would be a real game-changer for clinical guidelines.

The strength of some exposure instrumental variables was insufficient. For certain dietary exposures (e.g., red wine, beer, bacon, lamb), the authors relaxed the significance threshold to P < 1 × 10−5 for SNP selection. Although the F-statistics were all >10, some exposures only had single-digit SNPs, resulting in limited statistical power[4], The significant results for red wine, bread, pork, and oily fish were not replicated in the second dataset, with no False Discovery Rate (FDR) thresholds met. Thus, the final conclusion was limited to a “may” statement. Future studies should prioritize larger-sample dietary genome-wide association studies (GWAS), such as the forthcoming UKB + 23andMe joint analysis, or employ multi-effectivity correction methods (e.g., MR-PRESSO global test + MR-Lasso) to further refine instrumental variables.

Population ancestry limitations: Exposure data comes from the European UK Biobank, while outcomes are from Finns. Although both are of European ancestry, there are still certain population structure differences. Future studies should incorporate East Asian populations (such as China Kadoorie Biobank, BJB) or African dietary GWAS for cross-ancestry validation, especially whether the protective effect of dried fruits also exists in China populations, which is worth paying attention to[5].

The most probable mechanism of protective effect of dried fruits is that they are rich in magnesium, polyphenols, vitamin E, monounsaturated fatty acids and other antioxidant and anti-inflammatory components[6], However, the author only adjusted for zinc, calcium, vitamin C, and vitamin E in the MVMR without conducting further mediation MR or performing bidirectional MR and co-localization analyses with plasma magnesium and polyphenol metabolites GWAS, thereby missing the opportunity to elevate the “phenomenal discovery” to “mechanistic evidence”.

With only 3139 pancreatic cancer cases recorded by FinnGen, the failure to replicate risk signals from oily fish was unsurprising. Overall, these limitations do not materially compromise the core conclusions of this study – the protective effect of dried fruits against pancreatitis is robust enough to warrant immediate clinical implementation. Through a rigorous meta-analysis, authors like Zhang Qi have delivered a simple, safe, and cost-effective dietary recommendation for pancreatitis patients, demonstrating the greatest strength of the Mendelian randomization method. We look forward to the author team’s follow-up research: (1) separate analysis of different types of dried fruits; (2) MR with plasma magnesium or polyphenol metabolites as exposure; (3) validation in China population. This would be a real game-changer for clinical guidelines.
