Effects of eHealth interventions on depression, anxiety, quality of life, and post-traumatic stress symptoms among parents of children with cancer: A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Jianmei Zhu, Xiaofei Chen, Yaping Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo synthesize evidence from randomized controlled trials on the effects of eHealth interventions on depression, anxiety, quality of life, and post-traumatic stress symptoms in parents of children with cancer.MethodsPubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Web of Science, CNKI, VIP, and SinoMed were searched from inception to 31 January 2026. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data, assessed risk of bias using RoB 2 and rated certainty of evidence using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE). For family-randomized trials, standard errors were inflated using a design effect with a primary intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.50; sensitivity analyses used ICCs of 0.10, 0.25, and 0.75. Exploratory subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias assessments were conducted when appropriate.ResultsThirteen randomized controlled trials involving 1022 parents were included in the meta-analysis. eHealth interventions significantly reduced depression (standardized mean difference [SMD] = −0.56, 95% confidence interval [CI] −0.76 to −0.36) and anxiety (SMD = −0.66, 95% CI −1.12 to −0.20). The effect on quality of life was not statistically significant (SMD = 0.36, 95% CI −0.06 to 0.78). A significant reduction in post-traumatic stress symptoms was observed (SMD = −0.48, 95% CI −0.84 to −0.11), but this result was not stable in sensitivity analysis. For anxiety, excluding one influential study reduced the pooled effect to −0.45 (95% CI −0.62 to −0.27) and I2 from 80.5% to 28.5%. Effect directions, statistical significance, and certainty ratings were unchanged across ICC assumptions of 0.10–0.75. The certainty of evidence was moderate for depression and very low for anxiety, quality of life and post-traumatic stress symptoms.ConclusionseHealth interventions may help reduce depression in parents of children with cancer. Evidence for anxiety, quality of life and post-traumatic stress symptoms remains limited and should be interpreted with caution. More rigorous randomized controlled trials with clearer intervention reporting, longer follow-up, and better adherence data are needed.Systematic review registrationCRD420251236158.Keywords: eHealth, Parents, Pediatric oncology, Childhood cancer, Anxiety, Depression

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, an estimated 275,000 to 400,000 new cases of childhood cancer occur each year worldwide.1,2 In 2018, the World Health Organization launched the Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer, which aims to achieve at least 60% survival for children with cancer worldwide by 2030.3 During the long course of treatment and recovery, the family becomes the main site of care, and parents are usually the primary informal caregivers. Unlike caregivers of adults with cancer, parents of children with cancer not only undertake demanding care tasks, such as medication management and symptom monitoring,4 but also need to respond to their child's developmental needs and to conflicts across multiple family roles.5 These intensive caregiving demands often place parents under sustained stress.6

Prolonged physical and psychological strain places many parents of children with cancer at substantial risk of mental health problems. According to the Family Stress Model, financial toxicity related to the cancer diagnosis and disruption to everyday life are major sources of stress.7 For example, a recent study of parents of children with retinoblastoma reported clinically relevant anxiety and depression rates of 34% and 41%, respectively, and 68.1% of parents had dysfunctional fear of disease progression.8 In addition, the diagnosis of cancer and the invasive treatment process can be traumatic for parents, which may contribute to a high burden of post-traumatic stress symptoms in this group.9,10 These symptoms may include persistent hypervigilance and avoidance, which go beyond general caregiving burden.

The psychological health of parents matters not only for their own well-being, but also for the recovery and well-being of the child.7,11 A meta-analysis by Bakula et al. (2020) showed a significant negative association between parental distress and children's health-related quality of life. Parents with high levels of anxiety or depression may find it harder to maintain positive parenting practices and may have more difficulty with treatment adherence management.12 This may in turn worsen the child's physical and psychological distress and create a negative cycle. Therefore, psychological support for parents of children with cancer is not only a humane need, but also an important component of comprehensive pediatric oncology care.

However, parents of children with complex care needs often face major time and geographic constraints, and traditional face-to-face psychological support services may be difficult to access.13 eHealth interventions offer a promising alternative because they are flexible, relatively low cost, and less restricted by time and place.14 Although previous studies have examined the effectiveness of eHealth interventions in adult cancer caregivers,15 evidence for parents of children with cancer remains fragmented. For example, Zhang et al. focused mainly on family dynamics outcomes and included quasi-experimental studies,16 which limits causal inference. Yang et al. focused only on psychological outcomes and performed only basic subgroup analyses for highly heterogeneous outcomes such as anxiety and depression,17 without further exploring moderators that may influence intervention effects.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize evidence from randomized controlled trials and to evaluate the effectiveness of eHealth interventions in improving anxiety, depression, quality of life, and post-traumatic stress symptoms in parents of children with cancer, who were the primary participants in the included trials. The findings may help clarify the value of eHealth in pediatric oncology care and inform the development of targeted family support strategies.

Methods

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.18 The review was registered in PROSPERO (Registration No. CRD420251236158).

Literature search and study selection

We searched nine databases, including Web of Science, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, PubMed, CINAHL, PsycINFO, CNKI, SinoMed, and VIP, from inception to 31 January 2026, using a combination of subject headings and free-text terms. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for children and adolescents, cancer, parents and family caregivers, eHealth and other digital interventions, and randomized controlled trials. The full search strategy is presented in Supplementary Table S1 of the Supplementary Material. We also used snowballing and manual searches to maximize completeness. No language filters were applied during database searching; eligibility was restricted to reports published in English or Chinese.

Studies were selected according to the PICOS framework. Participants were family caregivers, defined as parents, guardians, or other close relatives of children with cancer aged 0–18 years; paid caregivers or health care staff were excluded. Interventions included eHealth approaches delivered through the internet, online social networks, telephone, video, mobile health applications, web-based self-help programs, or multimedia technology. Comparators included usual care, wait-list control, or non-digital health interventions. Eligible outcomes included anxiety, depression, quality of life, and post-traumatic stress symptoms. Only randomized controlled trials were included. Qualitative studies, conference abstracts, letters, reviews, comments, and study protocols were excluded. Consistent with previous reviews of eHealth interventions,15,19 we defined eHealth as the use of digital technologies to deliver health-related services, education, psychosocial support, coaching, or communication without strict limits of time or place. Interventions were included only if they delivered structured content or support to caregivers through digital technologies. These technologies included web portals, mobile applications, online social networks, WeChat or WhatsApp, video conferencing, web-based self-help programs, and multimedia platforms. Digital tools used solely for appointment reminders, administrative communication, passive data transmission, or outcome collection were not considered eligible.

We further classified eligible interventions by delivery format. Digital-only interventions were defined as interventions in which all core intervention content was delivered through digital or telecommunication platforms. Blended interventions were defined as interventions that combined a planned digital component with face-to-face or usual in-person support. Blended interventions were included only when the digital component was an intentional and core part of intervention delivery rather than a minor add-on. Traditional face-to-face interventions, paper-based materials without a digital component, and remote monitoring used only for data transfer without an explicit intervention purpose were excluded.

Two reviewers (JZ and XC) independently screened titles and abstracts using Zotero and then assessed the full texts and supplementary materials of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer experienced in systematic reviews (YD).

Data extraction

Two reviewers (JZ and XC) independently extracted data using a predesigned Excel form. Extracted information included first author, publication year, country, participant characteristics, intervention characteristics, and outcomes. For each study and each outcome domain, only one measure was extracted to avoid double-counting. When several measures were available for the same outcome domain, we selected the measure that most directly matched the target outcome. When several post-baseline time points were reported, we selected the post-intervention assessment or the time point closest to the end of the intervention. When a published model-based or intention-to-treat estimate matched the target outcome and time point, it was preferred. Otherwise, we used the outcome-specific analyzed sample that generated the reported mean and standard deviation. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (YD).

Risk of bias assessment

We evaluated the risk of bias in the included randomized controlled trials using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. RoB 2 is designed to assess bias for a specific result. Therefore, judgments were made at the outcome/result level for the outcomes included in the meta-analysis. This instrument covers five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each domain is judged as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. The overall risk of bias is derived from these individual judgments. A study receives a low overall risk rating when all domains are rated as low risk. A high overall risk rating is assigned if any domain is rated as high risk, or if multiple domains have some concerns that substantially reduce confidence in the result. When at least one domain has some concerns but no domain is rated as high risk, the overall judgment is some concerns. Two reviewers independently completed the assessment, and disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third senior reviewer.

Certainty of evidence

Two reviewers (JZ and XC) independently assessed the certainty of the evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.20 The certainty of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low. Any unresolved disagreement was adjudicated by a third reviewer (YD) with experience in GRADE assessment.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed only when at least three studies reported the same outcome domain.21 Effect sizes were pooled according to outcome type. For continuous outcomes, we used mean differences (MDs) when the same instrument was used across studies and standardized mean differences (SMDs) when different instruments were used. Post-intervention scores were used as the primary effect estimate. For studies with multiple post-baseline assessments, the assessment conducted immediately after the intervention, or the time point closest to the end of the intervention, was selected for the primary meta-analysis.

When standard errors (SEs) were reported, they were converted to standard deviations (SDs) using the formula SD = SE × √n.21 For studies reporting sufficient post-intervention means, standard deviations, and sample sizes, SMDs were calculated from post-intervention values and expressed as Hedges' (g), which corrects Cohen's (d) for small-sample bias: g=Jd,J=1−34(nI+nC−2)−1 where (I) denotes the intervention group and (C) denotes the control group.22,23 We harmonized effect directions within each outcome domain. For effect sizes reported as unsigned magnitudes, the sign was assigned according to the direction of the reported between-group contrast and the scoring direction of the instrument. Negative values favored the intervention for depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress symptoms, whereas positive values favored the intervention for quality of life. For one study, post-intervention means were extracted from a published figure and baseline standard deviations were used as proxies because post-intervention standard deviations were unavailable; the robustness of the result was examined by excluding this study.

When sufficient raw post-intervention data were not reported, we first contacted the corresponding authors. If no response was received within 14 calendar days, we extracted the effect size reported in the original article. For studies reporting Cohen's (d) and a 95% confidence interval (CI), the standard error was derived as: SE=CIupper−CIlower2×1.96.24 The reported Cohen's (d) and its standard error were then converted to Hedges' (g) using the same small-sample correction factor. Given the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity among eHealth interventions, study populations, outcome measures, and follow-up durations, random-effects models were used as the primary analytical approach.25 Between-study variance was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood method. Forest plots were used to present the effect estimates for individual studies and the pooled estimates. We calculated 95% prediction intervals for effects expected in future similar settings. For studies that randomized families and reported outcomes for both parents within each family without adjusting for within-family correlation, we retained each study-level Hedges' g and inflated its standard error using the design effect DE = 1 + (m − 1) × ICC, where m = 2 parents per family. Standard errors were multiplied by √DE, and the adjusted estimates were pooled using the generic inverse-variance method.21 In the primary analysis, an ICC of 0.50 was adopted as a conservative central assumption, with sensitivity analyses conducted at ICCs of 0.10, 0.25, and 0.75. Means, standard deviations, observed parent denominators, and study-level point estimates were otherwise unchanged. Study-specific data checks and analytical decisions are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we conducted exploratory subgroup analyses based on key study and intervention characteristics. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. When enough studies were available to support funnel plot assessment (k ≥ 10), we also examined possible publication bias26,27 and performed Egger's test to quantify funnel plot asymmetry.28 When Egger's test indicated funnel-plot asymmetry (P < 0.05), the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure was applied as an exploratory analysis.29 Post hoc sensitivity analyses examined unusually influential estimates or estimates that relied on proxy standard deviations identified during data verification.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).30 Meta-analyses were performed using the meta package. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1) shows the study selection process. A total of 4197 records were identified through database searches, and 22 additional records were identified through snowball searching. After duplicate removal and title and abstract screening, 96 articles were considered potentially eligible. Two articles could not be retrieved in full text, and 94 articles underwent full-text and supplementary material review. Eighty-one articles were then excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria. In total, 13 randomized controlled trials were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis.

Characteristics of participants

All trials included in the final synthesis enrolled parents of children with cancer. A total of 1022 parents were included across the 13 studies. The sample sizes ranged from 40 participants35 to 146 participants.36 The mean or median age of the caregivers generally ranged from their early 30s to early 40s. The majority of the participating caregivers were female, with one study specifically restricting inclusion to mothers only.39

The pediatric patients cared for by these participants had a mean or median age generally ranging from 4 to 13 years. The children were diagnosed with a variety of oncological conditions, including acute lymphoblastic leukemia, lymphoma, central nervous system and brain tumors, osteosarcoma, and other solid tumors. While most studies included mixed cancer diagnoses, a few studies focused on specific cancer populations, including acute lymphoblastic leukemia,36 lymphoma,34 and osteosarcoma.33 Additional study characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Characteristics of eHealth interventions

The interventions in the included studies primarily used eHealth and mHealth platforms, including mobile applications, web portals, and video conferencing. In the Chinese studies, the WeChat platform (public accounts and communication groups) was the predominant delivery mechanism. Other delivery formats included WhatsApp, specialized web portals, and WebEx video conferencing.

The duration of the interventions varied substantially, ranging from 2 weeks to 6 months, with most interventions lasting 8–12 weeks. The theoretical frameworks and content guiding these interventions were diverse.

Characteristics of controls

Participants in the control groups predominantly received routine or standard care, which consisted of standard information provided by hospital staff without a structured educational or psychological program. Four studies utilized a wait-list control design, where the control group eventually received access to the intervention after the study timeline concluded. Additionally, one study utilized an attention control that provided general, non-therapeutic information to match the engagement time of the intervention group. Additional details are outlined in Table 1.

Outcome measures

The included trials assessed the outcome domains at time points ranging from immediate post-intervention to 12-month follow-up. Anxiety and depression constituted the most frequently assessed domains, utilizing tools including the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21), the Self-rating Anxiety/Depression Scales (SAS/SDS), the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), the Beck Depression/Anxiety Inventories (BDI-II/BAI), and PROMIS measures. Post-traumatic stress symptoms were measured in three studies using the PTSD Checklist-Civilian Version (PCL-C) and the Impact of Events Scale-Revised (IES-R). Caregiver quality of life was evaluated using instruments such as the PedsQL Parent HRQL Scale, the Short Form 6-Dimension Health Survey (SF-6D), the Quality of Life-Family Version (QOL-Family). Study- and outcome-specific data selection, time points, effect-size inputs, and effect-direction handling are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Fig. 2 presents the risk of bias at the level of the primary outcomes for each study. Six studies showed a low risk of bias in the randomization process while seven raised some concerns. For deviations from intended interventions, six studies had a low risk and seven presented some concerns. Seven studies demonstrated a low risk for missing outcome data, five raised some concerns, and one study showed a high risk. All studies raised some concerns regarding the measurement of the outcome and the selection of the reported result. The overall judgment indicated a high risk of bias in eight studies and some concerns in five studies. No study achieved an overall low risk rating. The risk of bias assessment is detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

According to the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate for depression, very low for quality of life, anxiety and post-traumatic stress symptoms. More details are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Effects of eHealth interventions

Depression

Eleven studies assessed depression. The pooled analysis showed that eHealth interventions significantly reduced depression (SMD = −0.56, 95% CI −0.76 to −0.36, P < 0.001, Fig. 3a), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 47.1%). The 95% prediction interval ranged from −1.12 to 0.00. Across ICC assumptions of 0.10–0.75, pooled SMDs ranged from −0.57 to −0.56 and all confidence intervals excluded the null.

Anxiety

Ten studies assessed anxiety. The pooled analysis showed that eHealth interventions significantly reduced anxiety compared with control conditions (SMD = −0.66, 95% CI −1.12 to −0.20, P = 0.005, Fig. 3b), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 80.5%), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from −2.32 to 1.00. The pooled estimate remained −0.66 after rounding under all ICC assumptions from 0.10 to 0.75, and all confidence intervals excluded the null.

Exploratory subgroup analyses showed no statistically significant differences by intervention duration (P for subgroup difference = 0.291), delivery format (P = 0.861), or intervention content (P = 0.737; Table 2).

All leave-one-out estimates remained statistically significant (SMD range −0.72 to −0.45; Supplementary Table S5). In a post hoc analysis excluding Ozturk et al. (2024),39 which contributed an unusually large effect estimate, the pooled effect remained statistically significant (SMD = −0.45, 95% CI −0.62 to −0.27, P < 0.001), while heterogeneity decreased from 80.5% to 28.5%. Egger's test suggested possible funnel plot asymmetry (t = −2.55, df = 8, P = 0.034). Trim-and-fill analysis added no studies, and the adjusted pooled estimate was unchanged (Supplementary Table S6 and Fig. S1).

Quality of life

Five studies assessed quality of life. The pooled analysis did not show a statistically significant improvement in quality of life after eHealth interventions (SMD = 0.36, 95% CI −0.06 to 0.78, P = 0.095, Fig. 3c). Moderate to substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 68.5%), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from −0.89 to 1.60.

No significant subgroup difference was found by delivery format (P = 0.455). Other subgroup analyses could not be conducted due to the limitations in the number of studies. The pooled estimate became significant after omission of Jin et al. (2023) (Supplementary Table S5).

Post-traumatic stress symptoms

Three studies assessed post-traumatic stress symptoms. The pooled analysis showed that eHealth interventions significantly reduced post-traumatic stress symptoms (SMD = −0.48, 95% CI −0.84 to −0.11, P = 0.011, Fig. 3d). Moderate heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 44.1%), and the 95% prediction interval ranged from −1.70 to 0.75. Across ICC assumptions of 0.10–0.75, pooled SMDs ranged from −0.48 to −0.47 and all confidence intervals excluded the null. Because only three studies were available, subgroup analyses and publication bias assessments were not performed. The pooled effect became non-significant after omission of Wang et al.,34 indicating that the result was sensitive to the influence of an individual study (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

Main findings

This meta-analysis showed that eHealth interventions were associated with reduced depression and anxiety in parents of children with cancer. No clear improvement was found for quality of life. A reduction in post-traumatic stress symptoms was observed, but this finding was not stable in sensitivity analysis. The certainty of evidence was moderate for depression, very low for anxiety, quality of life and post-traumatic stress symptoms. Therefore, the findings support the potential value of eHealth interventions in pediatric oncology caregiving, especially for emotional distress, but they should be interpreted with caution because of heterogeneity, risk of bias, and the limited number of studies for some outcomes.

Methodological quality

The methodological quality of the included trials was limited. Although all studies were randomized controlled trials, no study had a low overall risk of bias. In the RoB 2 assessment, eight studies were rated as high risk and five as having some concerns. Six studies had low risk in the randomization process, and six had low risk for deviations from intended interventions. Missing outcome data were generally acceptable. All studies had some concerns in outcome measurement and selection of the reported result. This was mainly because outcomes were caregiver-reported, participants were usually aware of allocation, and protocols or statistical analysis plans were often unavailable. These issues may increase deviations from intended interventions, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result.

According to GRADE, the certainty of evidence was moderate for depression and very low for anxiety, quality of life and post-traumatic stress symptoms. The main reasons for downgrading were risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, and possible publication bias for anxiety. The included studies also differed in intervention content, delivery format, duration, follow-up time, and outcome instruments. Therefore, the pooled effects should be interpreted as average effects across heterogeneous eHealth support models, not as evidence for one specific intervention. The findings for quality of life and post-traumatic stress symptoms need particular caution because they were based on fewer studies and were sensitive to the omission of individual trials.

Effects of eHealth interventions

Depression

The pooled results showed that eHealth interventions reduced depressive symptoms in parents of children with cancer. This finding is clinically important because parental depression is often related to long-term caregiving demands, uncertainty about the child's illness, changes in family roles, and reduced time for self-care. However, the prediction interval crossed the null value, indicating that the effect may vary across future settings.

A likely explanation is that eHealth interventions make psychological support easier to access. They allow parents to receive information, coping guidance, and emotional support without the burden of travel or fixed clinic visits.7,11 Li et al. found a similar benefit in informal caregivers of patients with cancer and suggested that digital interventions may reduce depression by improving access to professional advice and emotional support.15 Kim et al. also reported a significant pooled effect of web-based interventions on depression in cancer caregivers.44

The exploratory subgroup analysis suggested a larger pooled effect for blended interventions than for digital-only interventions. However, this finding should not be interpreted as causal evidence that blended interventions are superior. The subgroup comparison was based on study-level characteristics and a limited number of trials. Differences in intervention intensity, professional contact, intervention content, and participant characteristics may have contributed to the observed subgroup difference. The finding also differs from evidence reported among adult patients with cancer.45 Direct head-to-head comparisons of blended and digital-only interventions are required to reach conclusions on the better delivery mode. These comparisons should also explore how the mode of interaction, perceived usefulness, and trustworthiness influence engagement with eHealth interventions.46,47

Anxiety

eHealth interventions were also associated with a significant reduction in anxiety. This finding is consistent with a previous study on eHealth interventions for caregivers of patients with chronic diseases.48 From a clinical perspective, eHealth may help reduce anxiety by improving access to information, strengthening coping skills, and offering timely communication without the travel and scheduling burden associated with face-to-face services.14,15

Nevertheless, this result should be interpreted with considerable caution. Substantial heterogeneity was observed, and the prediction interval crossed the null value, suggesting that effects may differ across settings. Although the pooled estimate remained statistically significant in the leave-one-out analyses, the certainty of evidence was very low. Egger's test suggested possible funnel plot asymmetry, although the trim-and-fill analysis did not add any studies or change the pooled estimate. Substantial heterogeneity has also been reported in previous evidence syntheses of eHealth interventions,15 highlighting variability across intervention models and settings. High-quality randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and more detailed reporting of intervention components are needed. The anxiety estimate was influenced by Ozturk et al.,39 which had an unusually large study-level effect. Although excluding this study substantially reduced heterogeneity and did not change the direction or statistical significance of the pooled effect, the magnitude of the overall anxiety effect remains uncertain.

Quality of life

Quality of life did not improve significantly after eHealth interventions. This finding is inconsistent with previous meta-analyses involving adult cancer caregivers.15 Quality of life is a multidimensional concept covering physical, emotional, social, and functional wellbeing. These domains are interconnected. Parents of children with cancer often face severe caregiving stress, financial burdens, disrupted daily routines, and uncertainty about their child's prognosis and treatment. These factors can negatively affect their quality of life.49 eHealth interventions provide psychoeducation, emotional support, and coping resources. However, they may not address the broader practical and environmental challenges parents face.

Only five studies were included in this analysis, and substantial heterogeneity was present. The studies differed in intervention content, intensity, duration, delivery method, and the instruments used to measure quality of life. The prediction interval crossed the null value, and the pooled estimate became statistically significant after the omission of Jin et al.35 These findings indicate that the result was not robust. Given the very low certainty of evidence, current evidence does not establish that eHealth interventions improve quality of life in parents of children with cancer.

Post-traumatic stress symptoms

Only three studies assessed post-traumatic stress symptoms. The pooled analysis suggested that eHealth interventions may reduce these symptoms. However, the prediction interval crossed the null value. Given the small evidence base and the instability observed in the sensitivity analyses, the certainty of evidence was very low. Therefore, this result should be considered preliminary.

This outcome still deserves attention because the diagnosis and treatment of childhood cancer can be experienced by parents as an ongoing traumatic stressor rather than a single adverse event.9 Psychosocial problems may persist even after treatment ends, especially when uncertainty remains.40 Digital interventions may be useful in this context because they can provide repeated access to psychoeducation, coping guidance, and emotional support during periods of uncertainty and hypervigilance.50 At the same time, the certainty of evidence was very low, and larger trials are needed before firm conclusions can be drawn.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths. It included studies identified from both English-language and Chinese-language databases. In addition to pooled analyses, we assessed risk of bias using RoB 2, evaluated the certainty of evidence using GRADE, calculated prediction intervals, and conducted subgroup, sensitivity, and publication bias analyses when the available number of studies allowed. These analyses provide a more complete assessment of the strength and stability of the available evidence.

Several limitations should be noted. First and most importantly, the number of included studies remains limited, particularly those focusing on post-traumatic stress symptoms and quality of life. This restricts our ability to further explore sources of heterogeneity and assess publication bias. We only conducted exploratory subgroup analyses and could not draw conclusions supported by higher quality evidence. Egger's test did not show clear evidence of funnel plot asymmetry for depression but suggested possible asymmetry for anxiety. However, these analyses included only 11 and 10 studies, respectively, and therefore had limited power to detect small-study effects. The absence of imputed studies in the trim-and-fill analysis does not exclude publication bias. Details regarding eHealth interventions, such as platform usage, login frequency, and completion rates, were missing in most studies. This prevented us from further evaluating intervention adherence and engagement, thereby limiting our ability to provide detailed recommendations for future eHealth interventions. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed for some outcomes. This likely reflects differences in intervention platforms, theoretical frameworks, cancer types, treatment stages, outcome assessment methods, and follow-up durations. A similar pattern of heterogeneity has been reported in another complex supportive-care context, although the population and intervention differed.15,51 Third, selection bias in the included trials may reduce the robustness and generalizability of our findings, limiting their broader application. Fourth, Duan et al. and Wang et al. enrolled both parents from each family but did not report cluster-adjusted analyses or within-family ICCs. We therefore inflated the sampling variances using prespecified ICC assumptions. Finally, for Park et al.,42 post-intervention means were digitized from a figure and baseline SDs were used as proxies because post-intervention SDs were unavailable. Excluding that study did not materially alter the depression result. These derivations may nevertheless have introduced additional uncertainty.

Implications for nursing practice and research

For clinical practice, these findings suggest that eHealth interventions may be considered as an adjunct to supportive care for families of children with cancer. Nurses and clinical practitioners are well positioned to use eHealth platforms to deliver structured education, monitor caregiver distress and reinforce coping strategies.15,52 Drawing on emerging oncology prediction-model research, future studies should develop and validate caregiver-specific risk prediction models to identify families who may require escalation to more intensive psychological support.53 However, eHealth interventions should not be considered a replacement for professional assessment or face-to-face psychological care when these services are clinically indicated. Given the very low certainty of evidence for anxiety, quality of life, and post-traumatic stress symptoms, routine implementation should be accompanied by careful monitoring of caregiver outcomes.

The larger effect observed for blended interventions in the depression subgroup analysis should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as a basis for selecting one delivery format over another. Future studies should directly compare digital-only, blended, and face-to-face interventions. The included studies varied in service content, intervention intensity, professional support, and offline communication formats. Consequently, the pooled effect estimates likely reflect the benefits of comprehensive psychosocial support or education delivered via digital platforms rather than the effects of the digital format alone. Future trials should clearly describe specific intervention components and report detailed implementation data such as platform usage, login frequency, completion rates, therapist or nurse contact, and attrition. Direct comparisons among digital-only, blended, and face-to-face service models are also necessary to clarify the specific contribution of digital delivery.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis suggests that eHealth interventions may reduce depressive symptoms in parents of children with cancer, with moderate certainty of evidence. The pooled analysis also showed a reduction in anxiety, but the certainty of evidence was very low. No clear improvement was found for quality of life. Although the pooled analysis suggested a reduction in post-traumatic stress symptoms, this finding was based on only three studies, had very low certainty, and was not robust in the sensitivity analyses. eHealth interventions may provide a flexible adjunct to supportive care in pediatric oncology, but the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future trials should use rigorous designs, longer follow-up, and clearer reporting of digital delivery, professional support, adherence, and completion rates.
