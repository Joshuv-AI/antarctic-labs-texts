Risk factors for nosocomial meningitis in patients with external ventricular drainages
Authors: Coline Raffenot, Vincent Doat-Sarfati, François Antonini, Thibault Florant, Sophie Baron, Geoffray Agard, Valérie Minetti, Nicolas Bruder, Salah Boussen, Arthur Malet, Sophie Cataldi, Audrey Le Saux, Marc Gainnier, Julien Carvelli, David Couret, Lionel Velly, Pierre Simeone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExternal ventricular drain (EVD)-associated infections are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality. Nosocomial meningitis (NM) poses diagnostic challenges, and its prognosis heavily relies on the timely initiation of treatment. The aim of this study was to investigate the epidemiology of NM and risk factors in ICU patients.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective single-center cohort study of adult patients who received an EVD in a French ICU between 2018 and 2022. Patients were classified into those with NM or without meningitis based on biological and microbiological criteria. We assessed risk factors related to the patient, the device, and the primary pathology, treatment regimens, length of stay, and survival.ResultsOf 275 patients with EVD, 32 (11.6%) developed NM. Patients with meningitis had longer ICU stays (median 42.5 vs. 29 days; p = 0.019), though in-hospital mortality was similar between groups (29.5% vs. 21.9%; p = 0.668). A pathogen was identified in 75% of episodes. Significant risk factors associated with NM included CSF leakage at the insertion site (OR 3.47; 95% CI, 1.53–7.86; p = 0.002), longer drainage duration (OR 1.07 per day; 95% CI, 1.04–1.12; p < 0.001), β-lactam allergy (OR 6.83; 95% CI, 1.73–26.92; p = 0.002), and a cytochemical profile consistent with infection (leukocytes > 100/mm3, CSF-to-serum glucose ratio < 0.5, and protein > 1 g/) in the cerebrospinal fluid (OR 3.87; 95% CI 1.67–8.97; p < 0.001). We proposed a predictive model derived from these identified factors with an AUC-ROC curve of 0.89 (95% CI, 0.83–0.94) with a negative predictive value of 96.8%.ConclusionKey risk factors included β-lactam allergy, CSF leakage, prolonged drainage, and cerebrospinal biological profile. This predictive model, derived from these factors, could be used for the early detection and treatment of NM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40560-025-00828-5.

Background

The depletion of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) using a temporary drainage system via external ventricular drainage (EVD) is currently one of the standard treatments for intracranial hypertension [1, 2]. The use of EVD has significantly improved the prognosis for patients with brain injuries [1]. However, the major complication associated with EVD, as an invasive device, remains infection. The incidence of nosocomial meningitis (NM) related to this technique is estimated to range from 2 to 25%, according to various studies [3–6]. The most frequently implicated pathogens are commensal bacteria, typically responsible for contiguous infections, with coagulase-negative staphylococci being predominant, although bacterial epidemiology exhibits significant local variation [7–10].

NM resulting from EVD catheter infections doubles the duration of drainage, extends hospital stays, and increases overall healthcare costs [8, 9, 11]. It is associated with a high mortality rate, ranging from 9.3% to 40%, with neurological outcomes often being unfavorable, spanning from mortality to a loss of autonomy in daily activities [8, 12].

Factors such as CSF leakage, prolonged drainage duration, and multiple EVD placements are known to elevate the risk of NM [13]. However, recent studies assessing the prevalence, long-term mortality, and risk factors of NM in patients with EVD are lacking. The diagnosis of NM is challenging due to non-specific clinical signs, such as fever, and the unreliability of standard inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) [8, 20]. Diagnosis primarily relies on CSF analysis, categorized into contamination, colonization, probable infection based on cytochemical abnormalities as per Lozier et al. [13], and proven infection (with positive culture). While direct microscopy and culture remain the gold standards, emerging diagnostic tools such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR), CSF lactate, and the CSF cellularity index show promise for earlier detection [8, 21–24]. As well, the role of imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced MRI, plays a crucial role in identifying complications such as ventriculitis and abscesses in suspected cases [13, 21].

Prompt and appropriate treatment is essential to reduce morbidity and mortality associated with this severe condition [3, 8]. It requires immediate initiation of empiric intravenous antibiotic therapy tailored to local antibiograms. Therapeutic drug monitoring and individualized dosing are crucial due to variations in blood–brain barrier permeability and CSF drug penetration [8, 25, 26]. In some cases, intrathecal antibiotic administration may be required for difficult-to-treat infections [21, 27].

Although many studies have been published, both the risk factors and optimal management strategies for NM remain subjects of debate.

The primary objective of this study was to describe the epidemiology and identify risk factors for NM in patients with EVD. Secondary objectives were to develop a predictive model for the diagnosis of NM based on these risk factors.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective, single-center, open-label observational study in intensive care unit of Timone University Hospital (Marseille) from December 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022. Eligible patients were identified by an independent investigator in the Medical Information Department (DIM) of APHM using the Common Classification of Medical Procedures (CCAM) code for intracranial pressure monitoring (ABQP001). From this list, we included all adults with an EVD; minors and patients with an intracranial pressure sensor without CSF depletion were excluded.

Patient data were collected and processed in compliance with the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). The Ethics Committee for Anesthesia and Intensive Care Research (CERAR) issued an advisory opinion confirming that the study met regulatory requirements (CERAR—IRB 00010254—2024-115).

General data

Data were extracted from electronic medical records using Apache Solr to query all variables of interest. Patient-related variables encompassed demographics; active smoking status; diabetes; immunosuppression (defined as long-term immunosuppressant use, ongoing chemotherapy, primary immunodeficiency, HIV infection, or lymphoid/plasma-cell malignancy); obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2) and malnutrition (BMI < 20 kg/m2); information on β-lactam allergy that was retrospectively extract from medical records; presence of pressure ulcers; prior ventriculo-peritoneal or ventriculo-atrial shunt; the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II) and Glasgow Coma Scale on ICU admission; occurrence of pulmonary, urinary, or skin infection; bacteremia or septic shock; and need for tracheostomy.

Therapy-related variables included the duration of CSF drainage and mechanical ventilation, corticosteroid administration during drainage, and placement of a permanent shunt (ventriculo-peritoneal or ventriculo-atrial).

Primary pathology variables comprised aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), SAH from arteriovenous malformation rupture, traumatic SAH, ischemic stroke, community-acquired meningitis, and other indications (e.g., cyst, tumor, chronic hydrocephalus).

Device-related variables captured total drainage duration; number of clamp trials and failures; number of CSF samplings; number of EVD catheters used per patient; CSF leak at the insertion site; catheter placement location (operating room vs. ICU); and use of intraventricular fibrinolysis (e.g., alteplase protocol). Standardized protocol for placement, monitoring and maintenance of EVD is available in the supplementary appendix.

For patients with probable or proven NM, neuroimaging reports were reviewed for evidence of ventriculitis or abscess formation. Therapeutic management data included all antibiotics administered for suspected or confirmed meningitis, treatment duration, and whether the implicated catheter was removed. Finally, lengths of stay were obtained from the hospital information system.

Biological data

Laboratory data were obtained from the centralized hospital laboratory database. Three time points were defined for analysis: ICU admission (Day 0), the day of NM onset (Day I), and—for patients without meningitis—the cohort’s median meningitis-onset day (Day 18).

Blood parameters collected at each time point included glucose; leukocyte, lymphocyte, and neutrophil counts; C-reactive protein; procalcitonin; hemoglobin; creatinine; lactate; prothrombin time; albumin; and fibrinogen.

CSF analysis comprised total cell count; leukocyte and neutrophil counts; glucose; protein; and the CSF-to-serum glucose ratio. The identification method of pathogen if detected was recorded (culture or PCR).

For antimicrobial therapy, we recorded whether antibiotic drug levels were measured and noted any instances of underdosing or overdosing.

Diagnosis

CSF samples were classified according to Lozier et al. [13] into three categories: no infection (sterile cultures, colonization, or contamination), probable infection, or proven infection.

Mortality

In-hospital mortality was obtained from the hospital information system. Out-of-hospital mortality was ascertained via the “CépiDc” database of the French National Institute of Health and Medical Research (INSERM), which records causes of death.

Statistical analysis

Data distributions were assessed for normality. Means and standard deviations were used for normally distributed continuous variables, while medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were used for non-normally distributed variables. Comparisons between continuous variables in two groups were assessed using an unpaired two-sample t-test for normally distributed data or a Wilcoxon-Mann–Whitney U test when no distribution assumptions were made. Differences between categorical variables were assessed using Fisher's exact test. Missing data were not imputed. Statistical significance was assumed at P values equal to or less than 0.05. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (R2) was used to assess relationships between continuous variables. A univariate analysis using a logistic regression model was conducted to determine the odds ratios (OR) for the different risk factors. All analyses were performed using JMP version 18.

Predictive model construction

Several predictive models were developed to identify factors associated with the occurrence of nosocomial meningitis. Variables were preselected based on their clinical relevance and their association in univariable analysis (p < 0.20). Models were constructed using logistic regression. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROCAUC), as well as sensitivity and specificity. Only models with an ROCAUC > 0.70 were retained.

Results

Population characteristics

Between December 2018 and December 2022, 378 patients underwent intracranial pressure monitoring. Of these, 103 were excluded because they received only an intracranial pressure sensor without CSF diversion. The remaining 275 patients—all of whom had an EVD placed in ICU were included in the study.

Overall, 32 patients (11.6%) developed NM: 8 (25.0%) classified as probable and 24 (75.0%) as proven infections. The NM and non-meningitis (NNM) groups were similar in age (median 59.0 years [IQR 48.8–67.7] vs. 61.0 years [47.6–67.7]; p = 0.999) and sex distribution (53.7% vs. 68.8% female; p = 0.155). Patients who developed meningitis were more likely to experience CSF leakage during hospitalization (34.1% vs. 13.1%; p = 0.004) and to have a history of β-lactam allergy (12.5% vs. 2.0%; p = 0.009). Longer drainage duration was associated with increased infection risk (median 19 days [IQR 13.7–33.2] vs. 15 days [8–20]; p = 0.002). Tracheostomy was more frequent in the NM group (25.0% vs. 8.6%; p = 0.011), and patients requiring multiple EVD had a higher infection rate (median 1.5 devices [IQR 1–2] vs. 1 device [IQR 1–1]; p = 0.003). Interestingly, corticosteroid use during CSF drainage appeared protective (12.5% vs. 31.6%; p = 0.043). No other examined risk factors differed significantly between groups (Table 1).

Diagnosis and microbiological analysis

Direct microscopy was positive in 50% of NM cases, while CSF culture and PCR identified pathogens in 62.5% and 37.5% of cases, respectively, yielding a pathogen in 75% of the 32 episodes (24 proven, 8 probable infections). NM were predominantly caused by Gram-negative bacilli (66,7%), with Gram-positive cocci also frequent (54.2%). Methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus was the most common isolate (25%), followed by Staphylococcus epidermidis (16.6%). Two episodes were polymicrobial: one involving Enterobacter cloacae and Acinetobacter pittii, and the other Acinetobacter baumannii, Citrobacter koseri and Leclercia massiliensis.

Treatments

Among patients with probable or proven NM, 43.7% had not received empirical antibiotics at the time of NM suspicion. Once a pathogen was identified, antibiotic regimens were modified after a median delay of 2 days (IQR 1–14). Empirical therapy most often combined a β-lactam antibiotic—typically meropenem or ceftazidime—with linezolid in nearly half of cases; only one patient received linezolid monotherapy. Therapeutic drug monitoring was performed in 56% of patients, revealing both overdosing and underdosing in 18.7% of cases each. Proven NM was treated for a median of 15 days (IQR 9–21), and definitive antibiotic selections are detailed in Appendix 3. The infected EVD was removed in only 31.2% of episodes. Neuroimaging to detect complications—primarily CT scans and, less frequently, MRI—was performed in 46.8% of patients and identified nine cases of ventriculitis and one abscess (Appendice 4).

Annual incidence

Between 2018 and 2022, the incidence of NM was 9.4%, rising significantly to 21.6% in 2022–2023 (p = 0.043). This increase coincided with a change in catheter inventory: thinner EVD catheters (1.5 / 3 mm) were introduced at a median rate of 12 per quarter (IQR 1–14), whereas the previously used thicker models (1.9 / 3.4 mm) were no longer stocked (median 0 per quarter; IQR 0–10; p = 0.023) (Appendix 5).

Risk factors

Additional analysis of variables that differed significantly between groups identified β-lactam allergy as a strong risk factor for NM (OR 6.83; 95% CI 1.73–26.92; p = 0.002), as did CSF leakage (OR 3.47; 95% CI 1.53–7.86; p = 0.002) and each additional day of drainage (OR 1.07 per day; 95% CI 1.04–1.12; p < 0.001). Among CSF parameters, a cytochemical profile consistent with infection—leukocytes > 100/mm3, CSF-to-serum glucose ratio < 0.5, and protein > 1 g/L—was also strongly associated with NM (OR 3.87; 95% CI 1.67–8.97; p < 0.001). Conversely, corticosteroid therapy was identified as a protective factor (OR 0.31; 95% CI 0.10–0.91; p = 0.026).

Discussion

This retrospective analysis of 275 ICU patients with EVD studied the epidemiology, risk factors, and outcomes of NM in this population. We observed an 11.6% incidence—consistent with reported rates of 5–25% [3, 4, 7] and identified several independent predictors of infection, including β-lactam allergy, CSF leakage at the catheter site, and longer drainage duration.

Notably, β-lactam allergy emerged as a novel risk factor. To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify and quantify this association, which will require confirmation in future investigations. One possible explanation is that patients without a β-lactam allergy often receive broad-spectrum β-lactam antibiotics empirically in the ICU—doses typically sufficient to penetrate the inflamed blood–brain barrier and sterilize cerebrospinal fluid—whereas allergic patients receive alternative agents that may achieve lower CSF concentrations. Current guidelines recommend initial empirical treatment of NM with meningeal-dose β-lactams [21], and it is plausible that administration of these agents for other suspected infections in non-allergic patients confers inadvertent protection against NM. By contrast, alternative empiric regimens in β-lactam–allergic patients may be suboptimal both in spectrum and in CNS penetration. Indeed, several studies have shown that β-lactam–allergic patients experience more infectious complications, likely owing to inappropriate empirical therapy [29–32]. Given the urgency of prompt empirical antibiotic therapy in NM, this hypothesis merits further exploration.

We identified CSF leakage at the EVD insertion site as a significant risk factor for NM, corroborating previous reports [3, 6, 13, 14]. Leakage reflects abnormal communication between the subarachnoid space and the external environment, facilitating pathogen entry—particularly commensal skin flora—via contiguous spread [10].

A marked increase in infection incidence was observed in 2022–2023 compared with the 2019–2022 period. This surge coincided with the introduction of a new catheter model featuring a larger external diameter, which may have facilitated retrograde microbial migration or provided a wider entry point at the insertion site. Although our retrospective design cannot definitively attribute causality, these device-related factors represent plausible mechanisms and underscore the critical importance of material safety monitoring. Future investigations should systematically evaluate how changes in catheter specifications influence infection risk and incorporate real-time surveillance to detect and mitigate device-associated hazards.

Drainage duration was significantly associated with infection risk. Although many studies have identified prolonged drainage as a risk factor—presumably because extended catheter dwell times entail more frequent manipulations and greater opportunities for contamination [8, 13, 15, 16, 19]—other investigators have found no such association, often in cohorts with relatively short drainage periods [4, 14, 33, 34]. Thus, the role of drainage duration remains contentious in literature.

We found that tracheostomy was associated with a higher risk of NM—a novel observation that may reflect longer drainage and ICU stays in tracheotomized patients; this hypothesis deserves confirmation in larger cohorts. Likewise, patients requiring multiple EVD exchanges were at increased risk, likely because catheter replacements often followed accidental dislodgement and involved nonsterile handling or inherent insertion‐related contamination. This aligns with Repplinger et al [6]. and Lo et al. [34], who suggest that infection may occur at catheter insertion rather than by retrograde colonization, and with data showing that prophylactic catheter changes actually increase infection risk [5, 35].

In contrast to Lozier et al. [13], corticosteroid therapy during ICU stay appeared protective (OR 0.31; p = 0.026). In our cohort, most courses were brief (median two days) and given to facilitate early extubation for suspected laryngeal edema, suggesting that steroid recipients may have been less severely ill with shorter drainage times. An anti-inflammatory effect on cytokine production might also play a role, although recent meta-analyses have not confirmed a benefit in NM [35, 36].

We did not replicate other reported risk factors—such as frequent CSF sampling—perhaps due to robust, standardized nursing protocols in our unit. Nor did we observe an association between systemic infection or septic shock and NM, supporting the concept that EVD-related infections arise by contiguous spread of skin flora rather than hematogenous seeding [10, 13, 35].

Among CSF parameters, cytochemical abnormalities (leukocytes > 100/mm3, CSF-to-serum glucose ratio < 0.5, and protein > 1 g/L) were strongly associated with NM (OR 3.87; 95% CI 1.67–8.97; p < 0.001). Given their usual role as diagnostic criteria, these CSF cytochemical parameters were considered in our analysis as early indicators associated with ventriculostomy-related meningitis, rather than as predisposing risk factors. However their sensitivity and specificity are imperfect, especially in the setting of intraventricular hemorrhage [21, 37, 38]. We also confirmed lymphopenia, neutrophilia, elevated CRP, and shortened prothrombin time on the day of infection; these markers may aid diagnosis once other infection sites are excluded [20, 21]. Lower serum creatinine at ICU admission was noted in future NM cases but lacked clinical relevance in predictive modeling. Finally, the CSF cellularity index did not perform well in our cohort, although missing data limit this conclusion.

Consistent with prior studies [3, 7, 8], Gram-negative bacilli predominated among isolates (58%), although Staphylococcus aureus (25%) and S. epidermidis (17%) were also common. Direct microscopy and culture sensitivities (50% and 62.5%, respectively) paralleled the literature, whereas PCR remained underutilized [21–24, 37, 39, 40]. The median treatment duration for proven cases was 15 days, but only 31% of infected catheters were removed—below guideline recommendations—and therapeutic drug monitoring revealed dosing outside target ranges in nearly 40% of tested patients.

Our multivariable model—including CSF leak, drainage duration, β-lactam allergy, and a CSF-to-serum glucose ratio < 0.5 with protein > 1 g/L—demonstrated excellent discrimination. Because it relies on routinely available clinical and laboratory data, this tool could enable earlier identification of at-risk patients and guide more aggressive monitoring and empirical therapy but needs a prospective study to be validated.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as a single-center, retrospective observational study, our findings are subject to selection bias—patients with incomplete records or atypical presentations may have been systematically excluded—and information bias arising from variable documentation quality, both of which could either overestimate or underestimate the incidence and severity of CSF infections by an uncertain, but likely moderate margin. Second, for the same reasons, external validity is also limited: differences in patient demographics, surgical techniques, and microbiological surveillance across institutions may impede direct extrapolation of our conclusions to other settings. Third, we were unable to collect functional data on long-term patient outcomes, preventing assessment of the functional prognosis associated with device-related meningitis. Fourth, due to the retrospective design, the temporal sequence of certain exposures (e.g., tracheostomy, corticosteroid use) in relation to NM onset could not always be established, and time-varying analyses were not feasible. Fifth, data on β-lactam allergy were retrospectively collected from medical records, and it was not possible to verify whether the reported allergy reflected a formally documented diagnosis or only patient self-report. Finally, while this study provides a pragmatic predictive model that could support the timely diagnosis and treatment of patients, prospective multicenter validation is required before these findings can be applied.

Conclusion

Our study highlighted a high rate of NM in EVD patients. NM were mainly caused by Gram-negative bacilli and by Gram-positive cocci. Our study investigated both the risk factors and clinical characteristics of NM in patients with EVD. CSF leakage at the drain site, longer drainage duration, β-lactam allergy and a cytochemical profile consistent with infection (leukocytes > 100/mm3, CSF-to-serum glucose ratio < 0.5, and protein > 1 g/) in the CSF all emerged as significant predictors. Furthermore, we developed a simple, clinically relevant predictive model to identify high-risk patients early. Further prospective, multicenter studies are needed to refine these risk estimates, validate our predictive tool, and assess the long-term functional outcomes of affected patients.
