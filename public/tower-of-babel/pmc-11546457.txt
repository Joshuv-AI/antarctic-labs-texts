Using break-even analysis to explore the cost and carbon reduction benefits of solar and wind energy integration in microgrids for convenience stores
Authors: I-Hua Chung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In response to the increasing demands for net-zero carbon emissions in Taiwan and globally, this study explores the feasibility of implementing microgrid technologies in convenience stores to reduce carbon emissions. The study utilizes the commercial simulation tool HomerPro to evaluate the performance of integrating solar and wind power into microgrid systems, aligning with Taiwan's current priorities in renewable energy development. The analysis incorporates carbon pricing scenarios, grid reliability considerations, and financial variables such as discount and inflation rates. We simulated 256 system configurations to identify the most economically viable solutions under various conditions. The results indicate that integrating solar and wind energy into microgrid systems can significantly lower energy costs and carbon emissions, especially in high carbon-price scenarios. These benefits provide strong incentives for businesses to adopt carbon-neutral strategies. These findings provide valuable insights for policymakers and businesses, highlighting the critical role of reducing green power purchase prices in improving economic feasibility. Future research explores the impacts of integrating other renewable energy sources, offering broader technical support for achieving carbon neutrality.

Introduction

With the increasing challenges brought by climate change, many countries worldwide have committed to achieving net-zero carbon emissions. Under the Paris Agreement, countries have set targets to reach net-zero emissions by 2050, aiming to limit global temperature rise to within 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels [1]. This global commitment has significantly accelerated the development of renewable energy technologies, particularly solar, wind, and hydrogen energy advancements, which have become crucial pathways for reducing carbon emissions. For example, life-cycle carbon-intensity analysis of hydrogen energy has demonstrated its potential as a low-carbon technology [2], and studies on PV-battery-hydrogen energy flexible communities have shown that these technologies can effectively facilitate carbon-neutral transitions [3]. Furthermore, applying hydrogen fuel cells and electrochemical batteries in energy-flexible buildings can enhance the thermo-electric-hydrogen conversion efficiency of energy systems [4]. Meanwhile, Zhou et al. (2023) proposed a cross-scale modeling and decarbonization quantification approach, exploring pathways to achieve carbon neutrality in China. Their study emphasized that adopting technological innovation and policy coordination at the local government level is crucial for the effectiveness of decarbonization strategies, providing strong support for the application of renewable energy and low-carbon technologies [5].

In Taiwan, as global emphasis on achieving net-zero carbon emissions increases, government agencies, guided by the National Development Council, have established a 2050 net-zero carbon roadmap and plan to gradually increase the proportion of renewable energy in the energy mix. By 2023, the target for renewable energy's share in Taiwan’s energy portfolio is 20 %. As the government continues implementing energy transition initiatives and carbon pricing policies, this proportion is expected to rise further. The advancement of renewable energy technologies contributes to reducing carbon emissions and fosters sustainable economic growth through technological innovation and policy incentives.

Carbon pricing policy is a crucial measure mentioned in a report by the Ministry of Environment, with an initial rate set at NT$300/tCO2e (US$10), which is expected to rise to NT$2940/tCO2e (US$98) by 2030 [6]. This policy compels businesses, mainly industrial and commercial users, to reassess their energy strategies. While facing the challenge of high initial costs, using green power and microgrid technologies enhances a company’s environmental, social, and governance (ESG) profile, reduces dependence on Taiwan’s electrical grid, and can decrease long-term carbon-fee expenses. However, establishing a microgrid system requires in-depth economic feasibility assessments.

In this regard, the software employed in this study offers an efficient solution, enabling rapid cost-benefit analyses and precise forecasts suitable for evaluating the economic and environmental impacts of renewable energy systems such as microgrids, hydrogen production, and seawater desalination [7]. For example, in China, a study on a Stackelberg game-based peer-to-peer energy trading market demonstrated that the design of energy management and pricing mechanisms critically impacts the economic efficiency of energy systems [8]. The flexible operation of renewable energy technologies, particularly in energy-flexible communities, can effectively enhance energy efficiency and reduce carbon emissions [3].

This study further explores how Taiwan can leverage microgrid technologies in convenience stores to achieve carbon reduction amidst the increasing global emphasis on net-zero carbon-emission targets. Government departments have formulated carbon-reduction plans that align with the 2050 carbon-neutrality goals, and adjustments in carbon pricing are seen as a critical factor in prompting businesses to change their energy strategies. Considering the rising costs of electricity and carbon fees, this strategy will contribute to long-term improvements in corporate ESG performance while reducing dependence on the Taiwan power company. The construction of microgrids involves initial economic assessments, which require practical tools for accurate analysis. These tools facilitate rapid cost-benefit analyses and precise forecasts related to terrains, making them essential for planning and evaluating microgrid implementations [7].

Evaluating the energy requirements and carbon emissions of renewable-energy systems for seawater desalination is an innovative application. Studies have shown that an optimized renewable-energy system can not only meet the energy demands of an extensive desalination plant processing 1,500 m³ per day but also reduce the cost per kilowatt-hour to US$0.164 and reduce carbon emissions by 18.5 % when combined with an energy-storage system [9,10]. Other studies have found that optimizing the existing electrical grid can lower operational costs and reduce carbon emissions [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. In China, simulations have been used to evaluate wind- and solar-power storage systems powering charging stations and their impact on vehicle numbers and power supply [19]. In battery technology applications, these tools have assessed the performance of various battery technologies on a small island in India, demonstrating the advantages of zinc-bromine flow batteries under certain conditions [20]. Ultimately, these systems' design and operational adjustments show that such simulations can effectively achieve optimal economic benefits and system stability [21,22].

The evaluation and optimization of renewable-energy systems require advanced software tools capable of detailed cost calculations and system designs. These tools extend their application to comprehensive evaluations of the economic feasibility and environmental impact of various renewable-energy systems, including microgrids, hydrogen production, and seawater desalination [9,10,[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. In particular, they effectively optimize costs and scale in constructing hydrogen-based microgrids. Through detailed technical and economic analyses, these tools facilitate the development of isolated and community microgrids powered by renewable energy sources [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Additionally, it has been used to assess low-carbon hydrogen supply paths in Shanghai and wind-powered hydrogen refueling stations in South Africa, thus demonstrating its effectiveness in reducing carbon emissions [28,29]. In microgrid systems, selecting energy-storage devices is crucial for economic efficiency and operational performance. Various battery technologies can be chosen based on specific applications and environmental conditions to ensure efficient and stable grid operation [20].

In summary, advanced simulation software tools effectively evaluate the economic feasibility of renewable energy systems while optimizing system designs to meet specific energy needs. Break-even analysis (BEA) is a commonly used financial analysis tool to determine the minimum sales volume or production level required for a project or business to cover its total costs. In the economic feasibility assessment of renewable energy systems and microgrids, BEA provides researchers with a simple and effective tool to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of different system configurations. Previous studies have shown that BEA is widely applied in the energy sector. Bhandari and Stadler used BEA to analyze the economic feasibility of a small wind power system, showing that optimizing system configurations can significantly reduce costs and shorten the payback period [30]. Similarly, Al-Smairan applied BEA to evaluate the economic benefits of an off-grid photovoltaic power system, exploring the cost-effectiveness under different solar resource scenarios [31].

Additionally, other studies have explored the application of different analytical methods in evaluating energy systems. For instance, Chauhan and Saini used cost-benefit analysis (CBA) to assess the economic viability of microgrid systems, finding that CBA can provide more detailed financial return predictions under certain conditions compared to BEA [32]. Similarly, Ullah et al. employed net present value (NPV) analysis to evaluate the feasibility of a hybrid energy system, offering more accurate investment return forecasts by considering the time value of money [33]. While different analytical methods have their respective advantages and disadvantages, BEA stands out in preliminary feasibility assessments of energy systems due to its simplicity and rapid calculation. This study selects BEA as the primary analytical tool to quickly evaluate the cost-effectiveness of different microgrid configurations and provide foundational data for future in-depth analyses.

To ensure the reproducibility and replicability of this study, we have meticulously recorded and disclosed all parameters used, including environmental data, equipment configurations, and financial conditions, provided in Chapters II and III. Additionally, the data sources used in the study are either publicly available or accessible databases, and the design and setup of the simulation models follow rigorous procedures to ensure that the results can be reproduced under the same conditions. The study also clearly specifies the software versions and operating environments, along with a detailed description of the simulation process. This approach guarantees the reproducibility of the methods and results, providing a robust reference for future related research. Through this study's analytical process and findings, we hope to offer valuable insights and references for implementing microgrid technologies in Taiwan and other regions worldwide.

The time-of-use (TOU) pricing structure of the Taiwan Power Company significantly impacts the economic feasibility of microgrid systems. According to the three-tiered TOU pricing announced by Taiwan Power Company, energy usage costs vary greatly depending on the system's different operational behaviors. For example, electricity prices are substantially higher during peak hours on summer weekdays than during off-peak hours on winter weekends. The analysis indicates that microgrid systems can optimize energy scheduling based on different operational behaviors to minimize costs. Specifically, the microgrid can charge energy storage systems during low-cost off-peak hours and discharge them during high-cost peak hours to avoid high electricity prices during peak times. Additionally, when renewable energy (such as wind or solar) generates more power than the current load demand, the excess energy can be used to charge the storage system rather than being fed back into the grid. By dynamically adjusting energy usage, microgrids can improve renewable energy utilization and reduce overall energy costs.

Theoretical model

The simulation tool employs a unique algorithm that integrates environmental data provided by the user, such as wind speed, GHI, and temperature, along with selected equipment specifications, to simulate the supply-demand dynamics of the system at each time step. It conducts system simulations based on user-input equipment costs, financial conditions, and load profiles, with Sections A–C representing energy-storage calculations, system cost calculations, and carbon-emission assessment models.

Section A mainly explains the evaluation models for solar-power generation, wind-power generation, the energy-storage system's charge/discharge power, and stochastic assessment of load variability. The study evaluates the microgrid's economic feasibility based on the relationship between carbon fees and levelized energy costs. The research indicates that economically feasible microgrid systems provide more significant incentives for developers. However, the study does not explicitly explore the penetration rate of renewable energy sources and the proportion of green energy in the system component mix. In Section B, system costs include annualized and levelized energy costs.

Additionally, the simulation method incorporates residual value calculations; these are linear, meaning the reset costs are linearly adjusted based on the remaining lifespan. However, this study argues that predicting equipment residual values over the next 20 years is unpredictable, and different types of equipment have different residual value characteristics, which are not linearly related. Therefore, such considerations are not included in the analysis, and negotiations regarding material value and equipment reusability would be required at the time of disposal. In Section C, carbon-emission costs are crucial for system evaluation. This study utilizes the breakeven point to assess the cost-effectiveness of carbon reduction efforts.

3)Profit-and-Loss Break-even Point Analysis

Profit-and-Loss Break-even Point Analysis

A profit-and-loss break-even point analysis determines the economic viability of different system configurations under different carbon-fee scenarios. The break-even point of the carbon fee, denoted as X, refers to the point at which the Levelized CoE for each system configuration equals the CoE of the grid (G). Four points are selected for each system configuration, and Origin software is used for linear curve fitting based on the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm (LMA) of nonlinear least-squares fitting to obtain linear fits [53]. Thus, this obtains the respective slopes (a for system configurations, A for the grid) and intercepts (b for system configurations, B for the grid) for each fitted curve. The intersection points of these fitted curves represent the profit-and-loss break-even points for each combination. Further explanations of the codes are provided in Section V.

In the example provided in Fig. 20, the CoE of the G combination, which relies entirely on electricity from a carbon-emitting grid, significantly increases with the increase in carbon fees, as reflected in its more significant slope. By contrast, the GPWL and GPWF combinations, which include carbon-free solar photovoltaic and wind-power generation, experience relatively more minor impacts from carbon prices.Fig. 20Illustration of break-even analysis.Fig. 20

When the carbon fee is zero, the intercept of the GPWF combination is the highest, indicating that its initial CoE is the highest, mainly due to its higher NPC. Analysis and discussion

Based on data from the Microgrids for the Retail Sector report, retail chains such as Buc-ee’s in Texas and Whole Foods have successfully deployed microgrid systems to address power outages and peak electricity prices. These microgrids not only significantly enhance energy reliability but also reduce demand charges and improve energy efficiency, substantially reducing operational costs [55]; furthermore, according to a report by Supermarket News, the Stop & Shop supermarket chain is converting the power supply at 40 stores to clean energy microgrids. These microgrids, which integrate solar power generation, energy storage systems, and the existing grid, significantly enhance energy efficiency and reduce carbon emissions. This application improves the stability of the energy supply and reduces the stores' energy consumption and cost burden during peak periods [56].

Fig. 21 depicts the basic architecture and component configuration of the microgrid system. The alternating current (AC) side (AC BUS) comprises the system grid (Grid), wind turbine generator (EO10), renewable power sources (RPS), and loads (Critical Load and Others). Within the system, critical loads such as emergency lighting or essential operational equipment are designated as Electric Load 1, ensuring priority power supply in case of a power outage. Other non-critical loads, which can be temporarily interrupted when necessary, are categorized as Electric Load 2. The direct current (DC) side (DC BUS) includes solar photovoltaic panels (D2KH7A) and two types of energy-storage systems—lithium-ion battery storage (Formosa Smart Energy ES134-16EMF-1) and vanadium flow battery storage (VFlowTech PowerCube 5–30). Components on the DC side are connected via a DC busbar to converters, which convert DC power to AC power for use on the AC side. The objective of this simulation is to ensure that critical loads always have sufficient power supply, even during grid failures. Moreover, this configuration allows for the integration of green power sources such as solar and wind, and it enables surplus energy to be stored for future use through energy-storage systems. This design allows the microgrid to achieve optimized energy utilization while enhancing resilience against power interruptions.Fig. 21Microgrid system structure.Fig. 21

Microgrid system structure.

When simulating the economics and benefits of green-power systems, considering the trends in electricity prices and the performance degradation of solar modules is a crucial step. Applying this degradation rate to the simulation model allows for predicting the actual output of future solar power systems, thereby accurately calculating the return on investment and the financial feasibility of the system.

On the other hand, Fig. 22 illustrates the electricity price escalation set in the simulation model. According to the data mentioned in Section III, the average annual escalation of electricity prices is 2.12 %. This rate is applied to simulate the electricity price in the grid, corresponding to adjustments each year. According to simulation results, when carbon pricing is high (e.g., NT$3000/tCO2e), the economic benefits of integrating renewable energy and energy storage systems increase significantly, especially for hybrid solar and wind power systems. These systems are vital in reducing peak demand and enhancing energy autonomy. In contrast, under lower carbon pricing scenarios (e.g., NT$1560/tCO2e), the economic feasibility of standalone renewable energy systems (such as those relying solely on solar or wind power) is diminished.

As green energy procurement prices fluctuate, a company's procurement strategy will directly impact the economic outcomes of the system. In Scenario 4, when the green energy price decreases to NT$4/kWh, most system combinations show economic viability regarding the breakeven point. However, when green energy prices are higher (e.g., NT$6/kWh), companies need to evaluate the economic benefits of renewable energy procurement more cautiously.

Furthermore, the environmental benefits of carbon reduction are critical to system design and energy structure selection. Research shows that hybrid energy systems improve economic performance and significantly reduce carbon emissions, particularly under high carbon pricing scenarios. By integrating the environmental benefits of carbon reduction with economic models, decision-makers can achieve both economic and environmental benefits, leading to a win-win scenario.

Conclusion

This study extensively utilized a specialized tool for rapid and precise analysis of the economic feasibility of a microgrid, showcasing significant advantages in terms of system costs, cost per kilowatt-hour, and carbon reduction potential, thereby significantly reducing the time required for preliminary assessments. Through carefully designed simulation studies, this research innovatively applied a specialized simulation tool to feasibility analysis for microgrid deployment in convenience stores. The study also introduced diverse variables (such as grid failure frequency, wind turbine tower height, green-power purchase prices, and different carbon fees) to simulate the economic impact of future carbon fees on deploying renewable-energy microgrids. The key findings are summarized below:(1).Green-power Assessment: The analysis results for the Changbin site demonstrate that both wind- and solar-power generation have costs lower than the average cost of electricity, strongly indicating that the Changbin area is highly suitable for the construction of renewable-energy projects. Detailed analysis of the Changbin site further confirms the advantages of wind- and solar energy in the region, with long-term monitoring of wind-speed data and solar irradiance demonstrating the stability and predictability of energy yields. These green-power projects offer significant environmental benefits, promote regional economic development, and create employment opportunities. Moreover, diversifying and enhancing the reliability of energy supply also contributes to strengthening the resilience of the regional grid.(2).The Impact of Carbon Fees on Economic Viability: When carbon fees are zero, any form of energy-storage system or green-energy purchasing lacks economic feasibility. However, as carbon fees increase to NT$1560/tCO2e, solutions combining solar and wind energy with energy-storage systems become suddenly economically viable. This finding provides valuable insights for policymakers and decision-makers in the energy sector. The study finds that, under lower carbon-fee standards, microgrid solutions integrating green power demonstrate economic feasibility, especially when considering long-term operational costs and potential changes in carbon-emission regulations. Furthermore, the increase in carbon fees effectively stimulates economic fluctuations, prompting businesses to adopt a higher proportion of green power, thereby accelerating the energy transition. Promoting this model will contribute to advancing the development of a low-carbon, green-energy system.(3).Carbon Fee and Green-Energy Purchasing Strategies: As carbon fees continue to increase, the study showed that, besides the strategy of purchasing green energy, all other combinations are economically superior to traditional grid combinations, indicating the strong potential of microgrid and green-energy purchasing strategies in high carbon-fee environments. With the implementation of green-energy purchasing strategies, the design of policy frameworks and market mechanisms becomes essential. The study found that when carbon fees rise to a certain threshold, investment in green power becomes highly attractive, especially for long-term investors. Therefore, establishing reasonable green-energy purchasing prices and carbon-fee systems will provide strong support for the future development of the green economy. Additionally, combining green-energy purchasing with other market incentives, such as tax breaks and subsidies, will further accelerate clean energy adoption and transform the energy system.

This study proposes two potential directions for future research: further investigation into the effects of terrain on wind turbine placement and the relationship between renewable-energy purchasing volume and costs. These directions will not only optimize the economic viability of microgrids but also provide concrete policy recommendations for developing and establishing renewable-energy microgrids in Taiwan. Lastly, the research results demonstrate not only the innovation and practical value of this study but also provide substantial evidence and clear direction for advancing the development and implementation of microgrid technology. By delving into various factors influencing the economic viability of microgrids, this study indicates how to effectively plan and implement microgrid projects under different environmental and policy conditions to maximize cost-effectiveness and minimize carbon emissions.

The discussion can be placed in the latter part of VI. Conclusion. In addition to the current carbon pricing strategies, future carbon pricing changes will significantly impact microgrids' economic feasibility. Under high carbon pricing policies (e.g., NT$3000/tCO2e), the economic benefits of renewable energy and energy storage systems are greatly enhanced, particularly in reducing carbon emissions, where they demonstrate significant cost advantages. However, with fluctuations in carbon pricing policies, companies must flexibly adjust their energy structures and green energy procurement strategies to adapt to changing carbon prices. This study strongly recommends that the government develop stable and forward-looking carbon pricing policies, combined with market incentives such as tax breaks and subsidies, to promote the widespread adoption of microgrid technology. Additionally, policymakers should consider the potential for cross-sector applications of microgrids, such as in office buildings and factories, which would further enhance the flexibility of energy management and the resilience of systems.

Although policy development is crucial for advancing microgrids, the limitations of simulation tools must also be considered. This study primarily relies on the HomerPro simulation tool for analysis, which has certain limitations, including uncertainties in model configurations and data reliability. Future research could consider using a more diverse range of simulation tools or real-world experimental data to validate this study's results further. Furthermore, this study primarily focuses on integrating solar and wind energy in microgrids. However, future research will expand to include other renewable energy sources, such as hydrogen and geothermal energy, to provide a more comprehensive analysis of different energy combinations. The proposed future research directions will further extend the knowledge boundaries of this field, which is crucial for promoting the broader application of green power.
