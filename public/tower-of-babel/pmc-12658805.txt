Salt-Responsive Wettability Alteration of Gold Nanocomposites: Experimental and Molecular Dynamics Studies
Authors: Yanxia Zhou, Hui Ma, Lihua Xiao, Xin Song, Wenbo Bao, Xinyue Cao, Guoli Liu, Xudong Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Wettability alteration is of significant importance and practicality in daily life, including waterproofing, self-cleaning, and reducing flow resistance. To investigate the mechanism of wettability transition at the interface, this study presented a salt-responsive intelligent nanofluid (G@(MO)­NPs), modified with gold nanoparticles (GNPs), capable of regulating wettability transformation through NaCl stimulation. Uniform-sized GNPs, with an average diameter of approximately 10 nm, were synthesized via the sodium citrate reduction method. A nanocomposite material exhibiting salt responsiveness was then constructed by grafting the random copolymer MEO2MA-co-OEGMA (RPMCO) onto the GNPs. The findings demonstrated that G@(MO)­NPs migrated from the aqueous phase to the oil phase (n-decane) due to salt-ion addition, notably weakening the hydrogen-bond interactions. This change resulted in wettability alteration and directional self-assembly of the nanocomposite at the oil–water interface. This intelligent nanofluid system offers a novel approach for the wettability alteration of the oil displacement agent and demonstrates significant industrial application potential. However, the practical application may be challenged by factors such as the cost of gold nanoparticles, long-term environmental impact, and performance under high-pressure–high-temperature (HPHT) conditions, which warrant further investigation.

Introduction

Wettability refers to the ability of a liquid to wet a solid surface, that is, the tendency of a liquid to spread on a solid surface. Some researchers are investigating wettability reversal, which refers to the change of a liquid’s wetting ability on solids due to the introduction of a third substance. This phenomenon involves the mutual conversion between hydrophilicity and oleophilicity of a solid surface under specific conditions. − In addition, a considerable number of studies explore the regulation of wettability through external stimuli. This regulation is often controlled artificially through factors, − such as temperature, ultraviolet light, ultrasound, pH, chemical composition, mechanical vibration, pressure, electric field, magnetic field, water vapor, or chemical gas. Salt-Responsive Materials

The distinct color of gold nanoparticles (GNPs) makes them easy to be observed; therefore, this study focused on preparing a stable, functionalized GNP-based nanocomposite for wettability transformation in response to salt stimulation. GNPs were synthesized using the sodium citrate reduction method, with HAuCl4·3H2O as the golden source. Copolymers were grafted onto their surface to create a nanocomposite capable of undergoing a wettability transformation under salt stimulation. MD simulation was employed to analyze the mechanism by which the gold-based nanocomposite transferred from the water phase to the oil phase. This approach, specifically designed for wettability alteration, can further expand the application of nanofluids and advance their industrial implementation. Therefore, understanding the migration process and mechanism of nanofluids at the oil–water interface before and after the wettability transformation is of great significance. It should be emphasized that this study focused on the feasibility mechanism study and the first step of our entire project research plan. The crude oil phase, economic nanoparticles, advanced salt-responsive materials, condition of oil reservoir, and performance evaluation in porous formation will be involved in the subsequent study.

Experimental Section

Materials and Equipment

Tetrachloroauric acid trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O, 99%) and trisodium citrate dihydrate (C6H5Na3O7·2H2O, ≥98%) were used to synthesize gold nanoparticles (GNPs). The copolymer of methyloxy-terminated poly­(ethylene glycol)-methacrylate (MEO2MA-co-OEGMA) was employed to modify the GNPs for wettability transformation upon salt stimulation. To observe the wettability alteration of initially hydrophilic nanofluids, the oil phase was substituted with n-decane (≥99%). All the chemicals were obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.

The morphology of the synthesized GNPs was examined using a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM, GEM-2100F) and an atomic force microscope (AFM, Agilent 5500). The fluorescence spectrum was measured using a steady-state/transient fluorescence spectrometer (Edinburgh FLS1000). Particle size and zeta potential were assessed by using dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis with a Zetasizer Lab system (Malvern, UK). Infrared spectroscopy was performed with a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Spectrum One, PerkinElmer). The contact angle was measured using a video optical contact angle measuring instrument (KINO SL250, Shanghai Solon Information Technology Co., Ltd.).

Synthesis of Gold Nanoparticles (GNPs)

GNPs were synthesized using the citrate reduction method. Prior to the synthesis, solutions of trisodium citrate dihydrate (TSCD) and tetrachloroauric acid trihydrate (TCAT) were prepared at concentrations of 50 and 0.15 mg/mL,respectively. A 100 mL volume of the TCAT solution was then added to a 500 mL round-bottom flask and heated to boiling with stirring. Subsequently, 1.2 mL of the TSCD solution was rapidly introduced into the boiling TCAT solution. After 15 min, GNPs were obtained and collected once the solution cooled to room temperature. Excess reagents, including TSCD and TCAT, were removed using ultrafiltration with a centrifugal filter (molecular weight cutoff, MWCO = 3 kDa) at 8000 rpm for three cycles.

Modification of GNPs with Random Copolymer MEO2MA-co-OEGMA (RPMCO)

A 50 mL aqueous suspension of GNPs (prepared as described in Section ) was transferred to a round-bottom flask and vigorously stirred magnetically. To this suspension, 1 mL of an aqueous RPMCO solution (50 mg/mL) was added dropwise. The mixture was stirred for 12 h at room temperature to form the copolymer-modified GNP nanocomposite (denoted as G@(MO)­NPs) through hydrogen bonding. To remove any unbound polymer, high-speed centrifugation was performed at 8000 rpm for 20 min, repeated three times. After each centrifugation, the nanocomposite was redispersed in deionized water.

Wettability Response Behavior of Nanocomposite G@(MO)­NPs with Varying NaCl Concentration

To examine the effect of NaCl concentration on the wettability of nanocomposite G@(MO)­NPs, four transparent 5 mL centrifuge tubes were prepared at room temperature. An aliquot of 1 mL of G@(MO)­NPs was added to each tube, followed by the addition of 1 mL of n-decane. Subsequently, 0, 0.15, 0.5, and 1.0 M NaCl solutions were sequentially added to each of the four tubes. After shaking the tubes for 4 min, the changes in the oil and water phases were observed in a few minutes.

Contact Angle Measurement

The contact angles of different solutions were measured at the glass surface coated with paraffin wax using the sessile drop method. Before testing, the contact angle measuring instrument was started and preheated for 15 min. After the lens focal length and light source intensity were calibrated to ensure clear imaging, the amount of liquid dripped from the needle tube was set to 5 μL on the computer, and the release button was clicked. The computer automatically captured the image of the contact angle and outputted the data of the contact angle.

Establishment of Models for MD Simulation

To investigate the microscopic mechanisms of oil–water phase transfer in GNP-based nanocomposite G@(MO)­NPs with copolymer modification in the presence of NaCl, MD simulation models were systematically conducted, as shown in Figure . The atomic-scale model (Figure A) was built as follows: spherical gold nanoclusters were generated by geometrically expanding a face-centered cubic (FCC, space group Fm3̅m) gold unit cell (lattice constant a = 4.07 Å). The brine water stimulation conditions were defined by the molar ratio of sodium and chloride ions to water molecules. The G@(MO)­NP nanocomposite molecular model represented copolymer-grafting on the GNP surface. The oil phase was modeled using a normal n-decane molecular model. The G@(MO)­NP aqueous system (Figure B) was constructed through the following steps: First, a spherical gold nanocluster model with a radius of 10 Å, consisting of 249 gold atoms, was generated. Next, RPMCO was constructed where the polymer monomers MEO2MA and OEGMA were connected in a head-to-tail manner, with a monomer ratio of 9:1, based on the component specifications from the pharmaceutical supplier. Then, a simulation box with dimensions 60 × 60 × 60 Å3 was created, placing the gold nanocluster at the center. Two polymer chains (MEO2MA and OEGMA) and 4250 water molecules were added, forming the G@(MO)­NP aqueous system.

In systems with varying salinity, appropriate numbers of Na+/Cl– ion pairs were introduced to achieve target concentrations, with 27 pairs for 0.5 M and 54 pairs for 1.0 M, while the oil phase used 410 n-decane molecules. Single GNPs (Figure B) and G@(MO)­NPs (Figure C) were introduced for comparison studies.

To examine the effect of nanosystem assembly on the oil–water interfacial properties, a self-assembly model of nanocomposites at the oil–water interface was constructed. Since the unmodified nanoparticles are entirely present in the aqueous phase, they do not form self-assembled structures at the interface. Therefore, only the assembly structure of modified nanoparticles at the interface was considered. The self-assembly model employed a symmetric structure commonly used in interfacial studies. The central aqueous phase contained 1887 water molecules, with dimensions of 55.0 Å × 40.0 Å × 30.0 Å. At the oil/water interface, self-assembly layers consisting of four G@(MO)­NPs (Figure D) were symmetrically arranged, with the G@(MO)­NP nanocomposite oriented perpendicular to the aqueous surface and the modified polymer chains directed toward the oil phase.

Results and Discussion

Design of GNP-based Nanocomposite with RPMCO Modification

The schematic of the designed nanocomposite system is illustrated in Figure . Initially, HAuCl4·3H2O (Figure A) was selected as the golden source to synthesize GNPs (Figure B) via the sodium citrate reduction method. Due to the salt-responsive characteristics of RPMCO, it was grafted onto the GNP surface to produce the nanocomposite G@(MO)­NPs (Figure C). Upon dissolution in brine, a gold-based nanofluid (Figure D) was obtained. The nanofluid was then applied to investigate wettability transformation upon salt stimulation in the oil–water two-phase mixture. The G@(MO)­NP nanocomposite migrated from the water phase to the oil phase under an appropriate salt concentration, and the mechanism of action was elucidated (Figure E).

Diagrams of the designed nanocomposite system. (A) HAuCl4·3H2O as the golden source. (B) Synthesized GNPs; (C) G@(MO)­NP nanocomposite. (D) Nanofluid with nanocomposites in brine. (E) G@(MO)­NPs transitioning into the oil phase from the water phase under salt stimulation.

With unique optical, electrical, and chemical properties, GNPs have become a significant focus of research in nanotechnology, showing broad application potential in various fields, including biomedicine, catalysis, electronics, and optics. , GNPs exhibit excellent local surface plasmon resonance (SPR) effects, which result in their aqueous solutions displaying vibrant colors such as orange-red, red, and magenta, depending on the particle size. These bright colors facilitate the observation of experimental phenomena with the naked eye. Furthermore, the surface chemical properties of GNPs are easily modifiable, which enhances their biocompatibility and extends their applications in various fields. These characteristics make GNPs widely used in academic research. Consequently, the GNP-based nanofluid was employed to achieve wettability transformation.

The GNPs synthesized through the sodium citrate reduction method have their particle size precisely controlled by adjusting the ratio of sodium citrate to chloroauric acid. In this study, the ratio of chloroauric acid to sodium citrate was approximately 0.25, and the resulting aqueous solution of synthesized GNPs appeared wine-red. These GNPs can freely disperse in water and exhibit good hydrophilic properties.

RPMCO is a comb-like graft copolymer with the combined properties of hydrophilic oligo (ethylene glycol) (PEG) chains and a hydrophobic polymethacrylate backbone. It exhibits excellent temperature sensitivity and good biocompatibility. , Literature indicates that the temperature affects the stretching and curling of polymer chains, making RPMCO responsive to temperature changes. Similarly, salinity influences the conformation of polymer molecular chains. , Although high salinity concentrations can decrease the polymer solubility through the “salting-out effect”, polymer chains remain responsive to salt concentrations within a specific range. Therefore, RPMCO was used to modify GNPs for wettability alteration under salt stimulation. Given that NaCl is a common component in formation water, it was selected as a permeability enhancer for wettability modulation in GNP-based nanofluids.

The nanocomposite was synthesized by mixing the GNPs with RPMCO for surface modification through hydrogen bonding. In the aqueous solution of G@(MO)­NPs, no phase transition occurred in the water–oil two-phase mixture at room temperature, and the system remained hydrophilic. After the nanocomposite dispersed in a NaCl solution, the G@(MO)­NP nanofluid was formed. The addition of NaCl disrupted the system’s equilibrium, increasing the ionic strength of the solution. This elevated ionic strength reduced hydrogen-bonding interactions between the ether oxygen segments of the copolymer and water molecules, leading to dehydration. Salt ions also weakened the hydrogen-bond formation between water molecules, diminishing the interactions with the hydrophilic groups of the copolymers. As a result, the hydrophobic groups dominated, causing the copolymer to adopt a collapsed spherical form in the solution.

During migration in formation pore throats, it is assumed that the initially hydrophilic nanofluid undergoes a wettability change, developing an affinity for crude oil upon encounter. The oil will be captured and gathered, forming oil walls and displacing the remaining oil, thereby increasing the crude oil recovery. This design offers a novel approach for oil reservoir development, particularly for low-permeability reservoirs that urgently require EOR technology.

The synthesized GNPs were analyzed using UV–vis spectroscopy, as shown in Figure F. In the UV–vis absorption spectrum, a clear SPR absorption peak appeared around 520 nm, which is characteristic of GNPs’ typical optical behavior. It is important to note that the position and shape of this peak are directly related to the size and dispersion of the nanoparticles. When the GNPs are nearly spherical and their size is within the range of 6–15 nm, the maximum absorption peak typically appears near 520 nm. However, as the particle size increases or aggregation occurs, the absorption peak shifts toward longer wavelengths (red shift) and the peak shape broadens. Overall, these results indicated that the synthesized GNPs exhibited good monodispersity and typical surface plasmon responses, providing important evidence for subsequent characterization.

Before the experiment, a two-layer mixture was prepared, with n-decane as the organic phase and a G@(MO)­NP solution as the aqueous phase, where the water phase was at the bottom and the oil phase was on top. The transfer behavior of G@(MO)­NPs between the water and oil phases upon NaCl solution at various concentrations was investigated, as shown in Figure . In the absence of salt, G@(MO)­NPs remained in the water phase with a wine-red color and did not transfer to the oil phase. However, when a small amount of salt solution was added, an unstable water-in-oil emulsion formed upon shaking. As the emulsion coalesced, G@(MO)­NPs were released into the n-decane phase, with the wine-red color transferring spontaneously into the oil phase. Self-Assembly Behavior of Copolymer-Modified G@(MO)­NPs in Aqueous Solution

The self-assembly behavior of copolymer-modified G@(MO)­NPs in aqueous solution was evaluated using the distance between the centroid of the copolymer and GNPs, as well as the nonbonded energy, as shown in Figure . To investigate the regularity of the distance between the copolymer centroid and GNPS, two RPMCO chains (designated as p1 and p2) were selected for the simulation. When analyzing the interaction mechanism between the polymer and gold nanocomposite systems, changes in system potential energy and nonbonded interaction energy serve as key parameters for characterizing the dynamic evolution of intermolecular forces. Specifically, the increase in the total system potential energy and the convergence of the nonbonded energy curve demonstrate that the polymer chain exerts a directional attraction on the surface of GNPs through physical adsorption.

(A) Centroid spacing evolution. (B) Free-energy landscape dynamics.

As shown in Figure A, the variations in the distance between the centroid of RPMCO chains and GNPs were analyzed under both the canonical ensemble (NVT) and isothermal–isobaric ensemble (NPT). In the NVT ensemble, once the system reaches relative equilibrium, the average distance between p1 and GNPs was 21.53 Å, while the distance between p2 and GNPs was 19.53 Å. The standard deviations were 2.38 Å for p1 and 2.50 Å for p2, indicating similar fluctuations in the distances of the two chains over time. Under the NPT ensemble, the distance between the polymer chains and GNPs fluctuated within a relatively stable range (19.0 ± 0.6 Å for p1 and 17.76 ± 0.4 Å for p2), indicating that the system had reached a stable state. The potential energy and nonbonded energy under the NVT ensemble are analyzed in Figure B. As the polymer chains approached the GNPs, the nonbonded energy increased due to van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, further confirming the enhanced interaction between the polymer chains and nanoparticles. This dynamic behavior reflected molecular diffusion and interactions between the polymer chains and nanoparticles. These findings suggest that both p1 and p2 undergo an adsorption process on the GNPs, evolving toward a stable configuration driven by energy release over time.

In an aqueous solution environment at room temperature (298 K), dynamic equilibrium calculations revealed the motion trajectory of the RPMCO–GNP aqueous system. It was found that RPMCO and GNPs exhibited interaction, with RPMCO gradually approaching and adsorbing onto the surface of GNPs. For better observation, water molecules and the periodic box of the RPMCO–GNP system were hidden, as shown in Figure . The relative positions of RPMCO and GNPs at 0, 300, and 600 ps under the NVT ensemble are shown in Figure A and those under the NPT ensemble in Figure B. Initially, at 0 ps, there was a certain distance between RPMCO and GNPs, as seen in Figure A. After 300 ps of dynamic equilibrium calculation, the RPMCO atoms were adsorbed onto the GNP surface. Over time, the number of RPMCO atoms adsorbed onto the GNP surface continued to increase. Under the NPT ensemble, the interaction force between the copolymer and the GNPs gradually stabilized, ensuring the regional stability of the nanocomposite system.

Effect of Salt Concentration on the Phase Transfer Rate of G@(MO)­NPs

The number of hydrogen bonds indicates the strength of the interaction between the polymer and water molecules, while the radius of gyration (Rg) of the molecular chain reflects the degree of conformational extension of the polymer chain in solution, as shown in Figure . Specifically, when the system exhibits a high degree of hydrogen bonding and the molecular chain adopts an extended conformation (with an increased Rg), the intermolecular polar interactions are significantly strengthened, leading to pronounced hydrophilicity in the system. Conversely, when the hydrogen-bond network is sparse and the molecular chain adopts a compact conformation, hydrophobicity becomes the predominant characteristic. The average number of hydrogen bonds between water molecules in an aqueous solution is 706, which decreased to 667 in a salt solution, as shown in Figure A. This indicated that the introduction of salt significantly disrupted the hydrogen-bonding network among water molecules. As illustrated in Figure B, the number of hydrogen bonds between water molecules and polymer chains was 48 in pure water but decreased to 40 in the presence of a salt. This reduction was attributed to the competitive binding of ions in the salt solution with either the polymer chains or water molecules, thereby diminishing the water–polymer hydrogen-bonding interactions. Figure C provides further insights into the structural changes of the polymer in different solvents by analyzing the probability distribution of the radius of gyration (Rg). The results revealed distinct conformational behaviors of the polymer in water versus that in salt solutions. In water, the polymer exhibited a broad Rg distribution (7.66–10.44 Å), with a higher probability (P = 1.38 Å–1, y-coordinate of Figure C curve (a)) occurring in the range of 9.0–10.44 Å. This suggested that the polymer adopted a more extended conformation in water, indicative of its hydrophilic nature and good solubility. In contrast, in the salt solution, the polymer’s Rg distribution shifts to a narrower range (7.42–8.88 Å) with a higher probability (P = 2.51 Å–1, y-coordinate of Figure C curve (b)) compared to water solution. This conformational change was primarily due to the ion-shielding effect in the salt solution, which weakened the hydrogen-bonding interactions between water molecules and polymer chains. Consequently, the polymer chains adopted a more compact conformation. The presence of salt also promoted the formation of aggregated or micellar structures, reducing the polymer’s contact with water. This behavior was characteristic of hydrophobic polymers in salt solutions. Additionally, the counterions (e.g., Na+/Cl–) in the salt solution neutralize the surface charges of the polymer chains, reducing the electrostatic repulsion between the chains and further stabilizing the more compact polymer conformation. This was reflected in the left-hand shift of the Rg peak in the salt solution.

Oil–Water Interface Assembly Behavior of Nanoparticles

This study investigated the regulation mechanism of nanoparticles on the oil–water interface structure by comparing the equilibrium configurations of the pure oil–water system and the G@(MO)­NPs/oil/water composite system (Figure ). The thickness of the interfacial layer indicates the degree of mixing between the oil and the water phases at the interface. Generally, a thicker interfacial layer corresponds to weaker repulsion between the two phases, reduced mutual solubility, and lower interfacial tension. , In the pure oil–water system, the polarity disparity between oil and water molecules resulted in a narrow interfacial region (upper image of Figure A). In contrast, the composite system exhibited significant interfacial restructuring (upper image of Figure B). Specifically, the G@(MO)­NPs form a stable interfacial monolayer by extending their surface polymer chains into the oil phase, causing the interfacial width to expand from 1.0 Å in the pure system (lower image in Figure A) to 21.3 Å in the composite system (lower image in Figure B). This substantial increase in interfacial width suggested the formation of a strong “anchoring” effect between the oil and water phases, which alleviated molecular arrangement imbalance at the interface and effectively reduced interfacial tension.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study successfully designed and fabricated a type of functionalized gold nanocomposite (G@(MO)­NPs) based on salt-responsive polymers. Through experiments and MD simulations, the salt-responsive behavior of copolymer-modified G@(MO)­NPs at the oil–water interface was elucidated, and the mechanism of wettability inversion (from oil-wet to water-wet) under salinity regulation was clarified. The results indicated that salt ions decreased the number of hydrogen bonds in the hydrophilic segments (PEG) of the copolymer through the salting-out effect, triggering a conformational transition dominated by hydrophobic units and promoting the interfacial transfer of the nanocomposite from the aqueous phase to the oil phase. The introduction of salt ions disrupted the hydrogen bonds between water molecules (from 706 to 667) and between water and the copolymer (from 48 to 40), causing the copolymer molecular chain to transition from an extended conformation to a coiled state, with a reduced radius of gyration. This conformational shift resulted in hydrophobic dominance in material properties, triggering a transition from hydrophilicity to hydrophobicity in nanocomposites in aqueous solution, ultimately driving the phase transfer behavior. By overcoming the bottleneck of crude oil capture in low-permeability reservoirs through the “hydrophilic–hydrophobic” intelligent switch, its salt-responsive property enables the direct utilization of formation water to achieve intelligent oil displacement. This work provides a new paradigm for the development of salinity-adaptive nano-oil-displacing agents and promotes the petroleum industry to move toward precise and efficient oil recovery technologies. It is worth noting that the practical deployment of this nanofluid faces several challenges, such as the cost of gold precursors, the potential long-term ecological effects of the released nanomaterials, and its adaptability to extreme HPHT environments, which are critical areas for further investigation.
