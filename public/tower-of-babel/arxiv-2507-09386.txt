Free-running vs. Synchronous: Single-Photon Lidar for High-flux 3D Imaging
Ruangrawee Kitichotkul † and Shashwath Bharadwaj † and Joshua Rapp ∗ and Yanting Ma ∗ and Alexander Mehta § and Vivek K Goyal † and † Boston University ∗ Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories § University of California, Berkeley

Abstract
Conventional wisdom suggests that single-photon lidar (SPL) should operate in low-light conditions to minimize dead-time effects. Many methods have been developed to mitigate these effects in synchronous SPL systems. However, solutions for free-running SPL remain limited despite the advantage of reduced histogram distortion from dead times. To improve the accuracy of free-running SPL, we propose a computationally efficient joint maximum likelihood estimator of the signal flux, the background flux, and the depth using only histograms, along with a complementary regularization framework that incorporates a learned point cloud score model as a prior. Simulations and experiments demonstrate that free-running SPL yields lower estimation errors than its synchronous counterpart under identical conditions, with our regularization further improving accuracy.



1
Introduction

Single-photon lidar (SPL) is a direct time-of-flight lidar approach that combines a picosecond laser, single-photon detectors
and timing electronics with picosecond resolution, enabling high-resolution ranging at longer distances than traditional time-of-flight and
frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW)
lidar systems.
These features make SPL particularly suitable for applications such as terrain mapping
Yu et al. 2020
;
Swatantran et al. 2016
, underwater exploration
Maccarone et al. 2023
;
Shangguan et al. 2023
;
Halimi et al. 2021
, and autonomous vehicles
Rapp et al. 2020b

where precision and efficiency are paramount. SPL’s unique advantages have positioned it as a cornerstone technology for next-generation sensing and precision mapping.

Single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs) are widely used in SPL.
One often overlooked aspect of SPAD modeling is
dead time
— the period after each photon detection during which the SPAD remains unarmed and cannot register additional photons.
A single photon arriving at an armed SPAD can trigger
a detectable avalanche of charge carriers, which must be quenched before the detector can be reactivated.
The dead time facilitates quenching and mitigates afterpulsing effects
Cova et al. 2013
.
Many SPL studies assume a low-flux regime, where long photon inter-arrival times render dead-time effects negligible.
The
5% rule
suggests keeping flux low to ensure fewer than five detections per 100 laser pulse repetitions
O’Connor and Phillips 1984
.
However, applying these methods to high-flux settings can lead to suboptimal performance due to unmodeled dead-time effects. In this paper, we define “dead time” as the minimum duration a SPAD must remain unarmed after detection. Many SPADs, however, can be configured with a “hold-off time,” a longer unarmed duration determined by the detector’s reactivation policy.

There are two major approaches to reactivating the SPAD, as illustrated in
Figure

1
.
In the
synchronous
(clock-driven) mode, the detector remains unarmed until the next laser pulse emission, even after a dead time has passed
Pellegrini et al. 2000
;
Pediredla et al. 2018
;
Pawlikowska et al. 2017
.
This approach results in statistically independent detections, simplifying the estimation of depth and reflectivity. However, synchronous measurements suffer from pile-up: a distortion of the histogram due to recording only the first detection in each laser cycle. The pile-up can bury the histogram’s peak, which informs the target’s distance.
Alternatively, in the
free-running
(event-driven or asynchronous) mode, the detector is reactivated immediately after the dead time ends, maximizing its armed duration
Antolovic et al. 2015
;
Antolovic et al. 2018
;
Rapp et al. 2019
.
While the free-running mode
has statistically dependent detection times and complex histogram statistics,
the distortion due to dead times is typically less severe than the pile-up effect in the synchronous mode.

In this paper, we directly compare these two modes and definitively show
that the free-running mode achieves better performance.
Crucially, we introduce a computationally efficient maximum likelihood (ML) estimator that lowers the barrier to using the free-running mode.
Since log-likelihoods are commonly used as objective functions in regularization frameworks, we further propose a depth regularization algorithm that integrates a trained point cloud score model to exploit spatial structures in 3D reconstruction.

In summary, our main contributions are:

1.

Novel, computationally efficient joint ML estimators of signal flux, background flux, and depth from free-running and synchronous histograms.

2.

The
Score-based SPL Depth Regularization
(SSDR) algorithm, which uses a point cloud score model as a prior.

Simulation and experimental results reveal two key insights.
First, the free-running mode outperforms the synchronous mode by providing more accurate estimates of signal flux, background flux, and depth across all flux levels, maintaining accuracy at any depth, and performing better even at long dead times.
Second, the optimal flux for SPL is much higher than the recommended 5% rule. Controlled attenuation for flux higher than the optimal level can enhance free-running accuracy, similar to synchronous mode
Gupta et al. 2019b
.

Related Work

2.1
High-flux Single-Photon Lidar

Synchronous mode:

Fixed time gating is applicable when the target’s depth is within a known range.
For example, in long-range imaging, it is used to suppress the background and extend the unambiguous depth range
McCarthy et al. 2009
;
McCarthy et al. 2013
;
Pawlikowska et al. 2017
.
If prior knowledge of the depth is not available, uniform gating
Gupta et al. 2019a
and adaptive gating
Po et al. 2022
can also mitigate the pile-up.
Coates’s correction
Coates 1968
, which is ML estimation of the discretized photon arrival intensity,
i.e.
, the
transient

Pediredla et al. 2018
;
Gupta et al. 2019b
;
Heide et al. 2018
, is a purely computational approach to pile-up mitigation.
Some hardware approaches include
Beer et al. 2018
;
Acconcia et al. 2018
.

Free-running mode:

Rapp et al. showed that the statistical dependence of detection times in free-running mode results in a Markov chain, so ranging and transient estimation can be performed by computing the stationary distribution
Rapp et al. 2019
;
Rapp et al. 2020a
.
Alternatively,
Zhang et al. 2024
proposed a parametric model of the histogram, circumventing the computationally expensive stationary distribution computation
Zhang et al. 2024
.
Free-running detectors are also used in passive imaging, where dead times constrain dynamic range
Ingle et al. 2019
;
Ingle et al. 2021
and complicate frequency estimation
Wei et al. 2023
.

2.2
Regularization for Single-Photon Lidar

Transient Estimation:

in transient estimation, the parameters to be estimated are the discretized photon arrival intensity.
Since the log likelihood is concave, many works proposed to use variational regularization strategies often used in inverse problems
Heide et al. 2018
;
O’Toole et al. 2017
, some specializing their methods for scenarios with complex transients, such as multidepth estimation
Shin et al. 2016
;
Halimi et al. 2017
, through obscurants
Halimi et al. 2021
;
Tobin et al. 2021
, and non-line-of-sight imaging
Heide et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Huang et al. 2023
.

Scene parameter estimation

In a single-depth setting, the estimation parameters are the signal flux, background flux, and the depth, or their subsets.
The log likelihood is nonconcave, so proposed methods are often sampling-based in the Bayesian framework, such as
Altmann et al. 2016
;
Tachella et al. 2019b
.
For dynamic scenes, spatiotemporal priors have been proposed
Altmann et al. 2020
;
Legros et al. 2021
.
Some of the other approaches include nonlocal correlation
Chen et al. 2019
and plug-and-play prior
Tachella et al. 2019a
.

Some regularization methods assume a uniform grid of scan locations, enabling priors like total variation that rely on neighboring pixels
Heide et al. 2018
;
O’Toole et al. 2017
. However, in scanning systems, transverse locations depend on depth, and scan angles can be arbitrarily chosen. As a result, neighboring pixels may have varying transverse distances, requiring spatially adaptive regularization.

While
Tachella et al. 2019a
was first to use a point cloud denoiser for regularizing SPL reconstructions,
t
o our knowledge, SSDR is the first SPL regularization method to apply a learned model directly to 3D point clouds, allowing it to handle arbitrary scan locations.

3
Pixel-wise Maximum Likelihood Estimation

We first describe a photon arrival model
and the log likelihood function for each detector mode. Then, we propose computationally efficient ML estimators.
The relevant derivations are deferred to the Supplement.

3.1
Photon Arrival Model

At each scan location (pixel), the laser emits
n_{r}
pulses with repetition period
t_{r}
.
The per-pixel acquisition time is therefore
n_{r}t_{r}
.
The pulse’s temporal profile is
f(t)
, which is normalized such that
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t){\rm d}{t}=1
.
For simulation and experiments, we assume that the pulse profile is Gaussian:
f(t)=\exp(-t^{2}/(2w^{2}))/\sqrt{2\pi}w
, where
w
is the pulse width.
The photon arrivals at the detector follows an inhomogeneous Poisson process with an intensity function

\lambda(t):={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\sum_{k=0}^{n_{r}-1}}}\widetilde{\lambda}(t-kt_{r}),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in[0,n_{r}t_{r})

(1)

where the single-period intensity is

\widetilde{\lambda}(t)=Sf(t-2z/c)+b,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in[0,t_{r}).

(2)

The signal flux
S
is the mean number of photons from the laser detected in one repetition period, absorbing the effects of laser power, reflectivity, range falloff, and detector efficiency.
The background intensity is
b=B/t_{r}
, where the background flux
B
is the mean number of detections due to ambient light and dark counts per repetition period.
The signal-to-background ratio (SBR) is defined as
S/B
.
We denote the total flux by
\Lambda:=S+B
.
The time-of-flight
\tau
is the round-trip travel time of a laser pulse and is related to the depth
z
by
\tau:=2z/c
, where
c
is the speed of light.
We assume that
z\in[0,z_{\max})
, where
z_{\max}=ct_{r}/2
, ensuring
\tau\in[0,t_{r})
. Exceeding this range causes distance aliasing.
Let
t_{d}
denote the detector’s dead time.
We assume that
t_{d}
is longer than the electronics dead time, which could otherwise further complicate the measurement model
Rapp et al. 2020a
.

3.2
Data Retention

Suppose there are
N
detections during the acquisition interval
[0,n_{r}t_{r})
.
Let
(T_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
denote the sequence of
absolute detection times
relative to
t=0
.
Each
T_{i}
has a corresponding
relative detection time

X_{i}=T_{i}\bmod t_{r}
, which is the time elapsed between
T_{i}
and the most recent pulse emission.
Though the timing electronics could be configured to record
(T_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
, typical SPL systems retain only the histogram of quantized relative detection times.
The theory in this paper neglects quantization effects.
The SPL system is assumed to retain an unordered set of relative detection times
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

3.3
Ideal Detector

A hypothetical detector with no dead time serves as our gold standard.
If the detector is always armed, the detection process is identical to the photon arrival process (which has included the detector’s efficiency).
The log likelihood of an ideal measurement
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
follows from the sample function density of an inhomogeneous Poisson process
Snyder and Miller 2012
:

{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{ideal}}=-n_{r}\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\widetilde{\lambda}(X_{i}).

(3)

We emphasize that
{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{ideal}}
and other log likelihoods in this section are functions of the scene parameters:
S
,
B
, and
z
.
Practical detectors, whether synchronous or free-running, behave similarly to an ideal detector when the total flux
\Lambda
is low, so the 5% rule,
i.e.
,
\Lambda<0.05
, is traditionally recommended
O’Connor and Phillips 1984
.
Although this photon detection model is simple, it fails to capture real SPL systems in high-flux regimes where dead-time effects are not negligible.

3.4
Synchronous Detector

In the synchronous mode, the detector is reactivated when the next laser pulse is emitted, following a hold-off time, which subsumes the dead time after each photon detection.
As demonstrated in
Figure

1
, a synchronous detector may be active for fewer repetition periods than
n_{r}
, because some hold-off times span across two (or more) periods.
Let
N_{r}^{\prime}
denote the number of armed repetition periods. Given a measurement
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the number of armed periods is
N_{r}^{\prime}=n_{r}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}_{[0,t_{r}-t_{d})}(X_{i})
, where
\bm{1}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(x)
is an indicator function taking the value 1 when
x\in{\mathcal{A}}
and 0 otherwise.

In the synchronous method, only the first photon detection after each laser emission is recorded, resulting in statistically independent detection times.
The log likelihood of a synchronous measurement conditioned on
N_{r}^{\prime}
is

{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{sync}}=-(N_{r}^{\prime}-N)\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log(\widetilde{\lambda}(X_{i}))-\widetilde{\Phi}(X_{i}),

(4)

where
\widetilde{\Phi}(x):=\int_{0}^{t}\widetilde{\lambda}(t^{\prime}){\rm d}{t^{\prime}}=SF(t-2z/c)+Bt
is the single-period cumulative flux,
and
F(t):=\int_{0}^{t}f(t^{\prime}){\rm d}{t^{\prime}}
is the cumulative pulse profile.
We will take this conditional log likelihood
{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{sync}}
as the objective for ML estimation.
We remark that
{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{sync}}
is the (unconditional) log likelihood if the number of active repetition periods is fixed.

3.5
Free-running Detector

In the free-running mode, the detector is reactivated immediately after a dead time. The absolute detection times follow a
self-exciting point process
, whose intensity is either
\lambda(t)
when the detector is armed or
0
when it is unarmed
Snyder and Miller 2012
.
An approximation of the log likelihood of
absolute
detection times can be expressed in terms of
relative
detection times:

{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{free}}\approx-n_{r}\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\lambda(T_{i})+\Phi(X_{i}+t_{d})-\Phi(X_{i}),

(5)

where
\Phi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(t^{\prime}){\rm d}{t^{\prime}}
is the cumulative flux.
The approximation error is relatively small for a large
N
.

Pixel-wise Maximum Likelihood Estimation

The joint ML estimator of the scene parameters

While joint ML estimation of

Following

The proposed ML estimators for the three detector types offer two key advantages over previous methods. First, they enable joint estimation of

Second, the proposed estimators’ ranging accuracy is independent of bin size. Although a practical matched filtering implementation requires histograms of quantized detection times, depth estimates can be refined via gradient-based optimization. This allows benchmarking in simulations with floating-point-resolution detection times, providing insights into performance across various lighting conditions, depths, and dead times, as demonstrated in

Moreover, there are other advantages, including improved accuracy for synchronous measurements and faster computation for free-running meausurements.

For background flux estimation, NRMSE increases as SBR decreases due to higher
B
, leading to more background photon detections.

4
Score-based SPL Depth Regularization

The ML estimators operate on single-pixel measurements. We can acquire multi-pixel data by raster scanning the laser or using an array detector. While pixel-wise estimation enables 3D reconstruction, regularization can further improve accuracy by leveraging spatial structures.
Since measurements from different pixels are statistically independent, the sum of log likelihoods described in
Section

3
across all pixels naturally serve as the data-fidelity term required in many regularization approaches.
Particularly, we aim to combine SPL log likelihoods with a learned prior.
Diffusion models for inverse problems
Song et al. 2022
;
Chung et al. 2023
;
Kawar et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2024
are well-suited for SPL regularization with nonconcave log likelihood, because randomness, to some extent, allows the algorithm to avoid local optima
Wu et al. 2024
. We introduce Score-based SPL Depth Regularization (SSDR), the first sampling-based SPL regularization method using a learned point cloud score model as the prior.
Our key insight is that 3D scores for a point cloud can be projected onto the detector’s line-of-sight to effectively compute depth scores.
We focus on regularizing the depth estimates and leave the regularization of signal and background flux estimates to future work.

We assume that the SPL system raster scans the laser onto the scene with galvos and detect photons with a single-element SPAD.
Suppose the detector and the laser are approximately co-located at coordinate
{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which can be set to
{\bm{0}}
without loss of generality.
There are
P
scan locations indexed by
\{1,\ldots,P\}
.
The scan angles are
\{(\theta_{p},\phi_{p})\}_{p=1}^{P}
, where
\theta_{p}\in[0,\pi]
is the polar angle and
\phi_{p}\in[0,\pi]
is the azimuthal angle.
Suppose
z_{p}
is the depth of point
p
. Then, the Cartesian coordinate of the point
{\bm{x}}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be computed from the spherical coordinate
(\theta_{p},\phi_{p},z_{p})
.
We remark that our regularization framework can be generalized to array detectors and other configurations as long as the detector’s view angles are known.

4.2
Median Smoothing for Initialization

ML depth estimates often exhibit outliers from random peaks due to background
Rapp and Goyal 2017
. When the largest histogram peak contains signal photons, errors are millimeter-scale, dictated by pulse width. However, if the peak arises from background noise, the estimate becomes uniformly distributed within the unambiguous range, leading to meter-scale errors. This effect is observed across different detector modes.
To speed up the regularization algorithm, we initialize the depths of outliers with median smoothing.

Future works

Future work may investigate the statistical properties of free-running measurements, including the distribution of detections

and the Cramér-Rao lower bound. For the synchronous mode, we derived the likelihood function conditioned on the number of active repetition periods, but its distribution warrants further study. Additionally, one could compare free-running measurements to the synchronous mode with fixed gating and other gating strategies
Gupta et al. 2019a
;
Po et al. 2022
.

Another direction is improving the SSDR algorithm by training the score model on a larger, more diverse dataset, modifying its architecture, or incorporating techniques from diffusion models

;
Chung et al. 2022
.

Appendix A
Derivation of Likelihood Functions

A.1
Ideal Detector

The log likelihood for an ideal detector was previously derived in
Bar-David 1969
. We include a derivation here for completeness.
Without dead time, the detector is always armed, and detection times follow an inhomogeneous Poisson process with intensity function
\lambda(t)
described in (
1
). Let
(T_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
denote the sequence of absolute detection times. Then, following
(
Snyder and Miller 2012
, eq. (2.33))
, the log likelihood function is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{ideal}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{n_{r}t_{r}}\lambda(t){\rm d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\lambda(T_{i})

(15)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}-n_{r}\int_{0}^{t_{r}}\widetilde{\lambda}(t){\rm d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\widetilde{\lambda}(X_{i})

(16)

\displaystyle=-n_{r}\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\widetilde{\lambda}(X_{i}),

(17)

where step (a) follows from
\lambda(t)=\widetilde{\lambda}(t\bmod t_{r})
, where
\widetilde{\lambda}(t)
is the single-period intensity (
2
) in the main paper.
We remark that the log likelihood
{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{ideal}}
is that of
absolute
detection times
(T_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
, but it can be expressed in terms of
relative
detection times
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

A.2
Synchronous Detector

A synchronous detector detects only the first arriving photon in a repetition period. According to
(
Snyder and Miller 2012
, eq. (2.20))
, the probability density of the first detection time
X\in[0,t_{r})
is

\displaystyle p_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\lambda}(x)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{x}\widetilde{\lambda}(t){\rm d}t\right)

(18)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\lambda}(x)\exp(-\widetilde{\Phi}(x)).

(19)

The probability that there is a photon detection in a given repetition period is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(0\leq X<t_{r})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{r}}p_{X}(x)=1-e^{-\Lambda}.

(20)

Hence, the conditional probability density of
X
given that there is a detection is

p_{X|N}(x|1)=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\Lambda}}\widetilde{\lambda}(x)\exp(-\widetilde{\Phi}(x)).

(21)

The synchronous detector will be inactive in some repetition periods when there are carried-over dead times from previous repetition periods.
Suppose there are
N_{r}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}_{[t_{r}-t_{d},t_{r})}(X_{i})
active repetition periods.
Since the probability of detecting a photon in a given repetition period is
1-e^{-\Lambda}
, the number of detections
N
follow a binomial distribution:

N\sim\text{Binomial}(N_{r}^{\prime},1-e^{-\Lambda}).

(22)

Since the detection times are independent and identically distributed, the probability density of all detection times is

\displaystyle p_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},N|N_{r}^{\prime}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},n|n_{r}^{\prime})

(23)

\displaystyle=p_{N|N_{r}^{\prime}}(n|n_{r}^{\prime})p_{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}|N,N_{r}^{\prime}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}|n,n_{r}^{\prime})

(24)

\displaystyle=p_{N|N_{r}^{\prime}}(n|n_{r}^{\prime})\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{X_{i}|N,N_{r}^{\prime}}(x_{i}|n,n_{r}^{\prime})

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\binom{N_{r}^{\prime}}{n}(1-e^{-\Lambda})^{n}e^{-(N_{r}^{\prime}-n)\Lambda}\\
&\qquad\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1-e^{-\Lambda}}\widetilde{\lambda}(x_{i})\exp(-\widetilde{\Phi}(x_{i}))\end{split}

(26)

\displaystyle=\binom{N_{r}^{\prime}}{n}e^{-(N_{r}^{\prime}-n)\Lambda}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{\lambda}(x_{i})\exp(-\widetilde{\Phi}(x_{i})).

(27)

The log likelihood conditioned on
N_{r}^{\prime}
is therefore

{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{sync}}=-(N_{r}^{\prime}-N)\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\widetilde{\lambda}(X_{i})-\widetilde{\Phi}(X_{i})+\text{ const}.

(28)

The constant does not depend on the parameters of interest (
S
,
B
, and
z
), so it is dropped for the sake of ML estimation.

A.3
Free-running Detector

The free-running detector is unarmed for
t_{d}
whenever it detects a photon. The absolute detection times follow a self-exciting point process (SEPP) whose intensity becomes zero during dead times
Snyder and Miller 2012
.
We denote the SEPP by
\{N(t),t\geq 0\}
, where
N(t)
is the number of detections up to time
t
.
The SEPP’s intensity, which itself is a stochastic process, is

\mu(t)=\begin{cases}\lambda(t),&\text{ if }t>T_{N(t)}+t_{d},\\
0,&\text{ if }T_{N(t)}<t\leq T_{N(t)}+t_{d},\end{cases}

(29)

where
T_{N(t)}
is the most recent detection time at time
t
.
According to
(
Snyder and Miller 2012
, Theorem 6.2.2)
,
the log likelihood of the
absolute
detection times
(T_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
over the acquisition period
[0,n_{r}t_{r})
is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{free}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{n_{r}t_{r}}\mu(t){\rm d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\lambda(T_{i})

(30)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{n_{r}t_{r}}\mu(t){\rm d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\widetilde{\lambda}(X_{i}).

(31)

We approximate the integral:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{n_{r}t_{r}}\mu(t){\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\lambda(t){\rm d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\int_{T_{i}+t_{d}}^{T_{i+1}}\lambda(t){\rm d}t\\
&\quad+\bm{1}_{[0,n_{r}t_{r})}(T_{N}+t_{d})\int_{T_{N}+t_{d}}^{n_{r}t_{r}}\lambda(t){\rm d}t\end{split}

(32)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad=\Phi(T_{1})+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\left(\Phi(T_{i+1})-\Phi(T_{i}+t_{d})\right)\\
&\quad+\bm{1}_{[0,n_{r}t_{r})}(T_{N}+t_{d})\left(\Phi(n_{r}t_{r})-\Phi(T_{N}+t_{d})\right)\end{split}

(33)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\approx}}\Phi(T_{1})+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\left(\Phi(T_{i+1})-\Phi(T_{i}+t_{d})\right)\\
&\hskip 43.20012pt+\left(\Phi(n_{r}t_{r})-\Phi(T_{N}+t_{d})\right)\end{split}

(34)

\displaystyle\quad=n_{r}\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi(T_{i})-\Phi(T_{i}+t_{d})

(35)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}n_{r}\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi(T_{i}\bmod t_{r})+\lfloor T_{i}/t_{r}\rfloor\Lambda\\
&\hskip 43.20012pt-\Phi(T_{i}\bmod t_{r}+t_{d})-\lfloor T_{i}/t_{r}\rfloor\Lambda\end{split}

(36)

\displaystyle\quad=n_{r}\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi(X_{i})-\Phi(X_{i}+t_{d}).

(37)

In step (a), we replace the indicator function
\bm{1}_{[0,n_{r}t_{r})}(T_{N}+t_{d})
with one. If the number of detections
N
is large, then the approximation will have a relatively small effect on the integral.
Moreover, if the detector is active at
t=n_{r}t_{r}
, the indicator function takes the value one, and the approximation becomes an equality.
Step (b) follows from
T_{i}=\lfloor T_{i}/t_{r}\rfloor t_{r}+T_{i}\bmod t_{r}
and
\Phi(nt_{r})=n\Lambda
for any
n\in\{0,\ldots,n_{r}\}
.
Substituting (
37
) into (
31
) completes the derivation:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm{free}}

\displaystyle\approx-n_{r}\Lambda+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\widetilde{\lambda}(X_{i})+\Phi(X_{i}+t_{d})-\Phi(X_{i}).

(38)

To write the log likelihood more explicitly in terms of the unknown parameters,
we remark that
\Phi(X_{i})=\widetilde{\Phi}(X_{i})
, where
\widetilde{\Phi}(t)=SF(t-2z/c)+bt
is the single-period cumulative flux defined in Section 3.4.
For the other term,
\Phi(X_{i}+t_{d})=\widetilde{\Phi}((X_{i}+t_{d})\bmod t_{r})+\bm{1}_{[t_{r},\infty)}(X_{i}+t_{d})\Lambda
, which can then be written in terms of
S
,
B
, and
z
.

In practical SPL systems, the detection times are quantized to a predefined temporal resolution, and the data are stored as a histogram.

Suppose the period

, where the bin size is
\Delta=t_{r}/M
.
Let us consider a set of relative detection times
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
Each detection time is assigned to a bin center:

X_{i}\in\left\{(m+1/2)\Delta\big|m\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}\right\}.

(56)

We can express
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
in terms of the histogram:

h_{\Delta}[m]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}_{\{(m+1/2)\Delta\}}(X_{i}).

(57)

Now, let us consider
h(t)
in the main paper defined as

h(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(t-X_{i}).

(58)

If the detection times are discretized, then

\displaystyle h(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}\delta(t-(m+1/2)\Delta)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}_{\{(m+1/2)\Delta\}}(X_{i})

(59)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}\delta(t-(m+1/2)\Delta)h_{\Delta}[m].

(60)

At time
t=(m^{\prime}+1/2)\Delta
for some
m^{\prime}\in\{0,\ldots,M\}
, which is the
m^{\prime}
-th bin center of the histogram
h_{\Delta}[m]
, we have

\displaystyle h(t)

\displaystyle=\delta(t-(m^{\prime}+1/2)\Delta)h_{\Delta}[m^{\prime}].

(61)

This shows that there is a one-to-one correspondence between
h(t)
and the histogram
h_{\Delta}[m]
when
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is quantized according to (
56
).

Even if
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is not quantized, we need to compute the quantized histogram
h_{\Delta}[m]
with a bin size
\Delta
in order to implement the depth estimators efficiently as discrete-time correlation.
For example, the ML estimator of
\tau
for an ideal detector is implemented as

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}

\displaystyle=(m^{*}+1/2)\Delta

(62)

\displaystyle\text{where }\ m^{*}

\displaystyle=\argmax h_{\Delta}[m]\oplus w_{\Delta}[m]

(63)

\displaystyle=\argmax\sum_{m^{\prime}=-\infty}^{\infty}h_{\Delta}[m^{\prime}]w_{\Delta}[m^{\prime}-m],

(64)

and
w_{\Delta}[m]=w((m+1/2)\Delta)
is the discrete-time filter.
In simulation where we have access to
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with floating-point precision, we may further improve
\tau
estimate by maximizing the log likelihood using gradient-based methods.

Appendix C
Initializing the ML Estimator

We initialize the alternating maximization algorithm (
1
) for ML estimation using the following estimators.
For free-running measurements, we use the estimators for ideal measurements described below in
Section

C.1
.

C.1
Ideal Detector

We use the traditional log matched filtering method
Snyder and Miller 2012
to obtain an initial time-of-flight estimate:

\widehat{\tau}_{\rm{init}}^{\rm{ideal}}=\argmax_{\tau}h(t)\oplus\log f(\tau).

(65)

Inspired by the Neyman-Pearson censoring estimator
Rapp and Goyal 2017
, we assume that the detection times in the window of size
t_{\rm{win}}
around
\widehat{\tau}_{\rm{init}}
are mostly due to the signal.
In this paper, we set
t_{\rm{win}}=4w
, where
w
is the pulse width.
Let
N_{\rm{cl}}
denote the number of detections in this window,
i.e.
,
N_{\rm{cl}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{1}_{[\widehat{\tau}_{\rm{init}}-t_{\rm{win}}/2,\widehat{\tau}_{\rm{init}}+t_{\rm{win}}/2]}(X_{i})
.
Then, the signal flux initial estimate is

\widehat{S}_{\rm{init}}^{\rm{ideal}}=\max\left(\frac{N_{\rm{cl}}}{n_{r}},\epsilon\right),

(66)

where we lower bound the signal flux estimate by a small positive number
\epsilon>0
to ensure that arguments of the log terms in the log likelilhoods are positive.
In our simulations and experiments, we use
\epsilon=10^{-5}
.
We estimate the background flux as

\widehat{B}_{\rm{init}}^{\rm{ideal}}=\max\left(\frac{N}{n_{r}}-\widehat{S}_{\rm{init}},\epsilon\right),

(67)

since
N/n_{r}
is an estimate of the total flux
\Lambda
, and
\Lambda=S+B
.

C.2
Synchronous Detector

We first use Coates’s correction to estimate the photon arrival intensity
Coates 1968
.
Suppose the detection times
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are quantized into a histogram
h_{\Delta}[m]
with bin size
\Delta
.
The Coates-corrected histogram, which is the ML estimate of the photon arrival intensity
Gupta et al. 2019b
, for the
m^{\rm{th}}
time bin is

\widehat{\lambda}[m]=\log\left(\frac{N-\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{m-1}h_{\Delta}[m^{\prime}]}{N-\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{m}h_{\Delta}[m^{\prime}]}\right).

(68)

Then, we apply the log-matched filter
Snyder and Miller 2012
to estimate the time-of-flight:

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{\rm{init}}^{\rm{sync}}

\displaystyle=(m^{*}+1/2)\Delta,

(69)

\displaystyle\text{where }\ m^{*}

\displaystyle=\argmax\widehat{\lambda}[m]\oplus s[m]

(70)

\displaystyle s[m]

\displaystyle=\log f((m+1/2)\Delta),

(71)

where
f(t)
is the pulse’s temporal profile.
Again, the signal and background flux estimates follow from censoring of the estimated photon arrival intensity:

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{\rm{init}}^{\rm{sync}}

\displaystyle=\max\left(\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{K}}_{\rm{win}}}\widehat{\lambda}[k],\epsilon\right),

(72)

\displaystyle\widehat{B}_{\rm{init}}^{\rm{sync}}

\displaystyle=\max\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\widehat{\lambda}[k]-\widehat{S}_{\rm{init}}^{\rm{sync}},\epsilon\right).

(73)

Appendix D
Depth Score from Point Cloud Score

In the SSDR algorithm, the depth score
\sigma_{p}\in\mathbb{R}
is obtained by projecting the point cloud score
{\bm{s}}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
onto the detector’s line of sight, which is determined by the scan angles
(\theta_{p},\phi_{p})
.
The trained model’s point cloud score approximates the Stein score, i.e., the gradient of the log density of the distribution of 3D points
Luo and Hu 2021
.
In this section, we show that
\sigma_{p}
relates to the Stein score of the depth distribution corresponding to the implicit prior on 3D points in the trained model.

D.1
From Cartesian to Spherical

Figure 8
:
Relationship between Cartesian coordinate
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
and spherical coordinate
\widetilde{{\bm{x}}}=(z,\theta,\phi)
.

We first describe the transformation between the Cartesian coordinate
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
and the spherical coordinate
\widetilde{{\bm{x}}}=(z,\theta,\phi)
, where
z
is the depth from the detector, assumed to be at the origin.
According to
Figure

8
,

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=z\sin\theta\cos\phi,

(74)

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=z\cos\theta,

(75)

\displaystyle x_{3}

\displaystyle=-z\sin\theta\sin\phi.

(76)

We denote this transformation by
T
,
i.e.
,
\widetilde{{\bm{x}}}=T({\bm{x}})
, and the inverse map by
T^{-1}
.

D.2
Spherical Coordinate Prior Distribution

Consider a prior distribution of a 3D point in the Cartesian coordinate with density
\pi({\bm{x}})
, representing the implicit prior in the trained point cloud score model for a single point conditioned on all others.
Since
\widetilde{{\bm{x}}}=T({\bm{x}})
and
T
is bijective, we can compute the density of the distribution of
\widetilde{{\bm{x}}}
, denoted by
\widetilde{\pi}(\widetilde{{\bm{x}}})
, using the change-of-variable formula:

\widetilde{\pi}(\widetilde{{\bm{x}}})=\pi(T^{-1}(\widetilde{{\bm{x}}}))|\det J_{T^{-1}}(\widetilde{{\bm{x}}})|,

(77)

where
J_{T^{-1}}
is the Jacobian of
T^{-1}
.
We now compute the Jacobian:

\displaystyle J_{T^{-1}}(\widetilde{{\bm{x}}})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial x_{1}}{\partial z}&\frac{\partial x_{1}}{\partial\theta}&\frac{\partial x_{1}}{\partial\phi}\\
\frac{\partial x_{2}}{\partial z}&\frac{\partial x_{2}}{\partial\theta}&\frac{\partial x_{2}}{\partial\phi}\\
\frac{\partial x_{3}}{\partial z}&\frac{\partial x_{3}}{\partial\theta}&\frac{\partial x_{3}}{\partial\phi}\end{bmatrix}

(78)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sin\theta\cos\phi&z\cos\theta\cos\phi&-z\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
\cos\theta&-z\sin\theta&0\\
-\sin\theta\sin\phi&-z\cos\theta\sin\phi&-z\sin\theta\cos\phi\end{bmatrix}.

(79)

The determinant is

\det J_{T^{-1}}(\widetilde{{\bm{x}}})=z^{2}\sin\theta.

(80)

Therefore, the density of the point in spherical coordinate is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}(z,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=\pi(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\cdot z^{2}\sin\theta.

(81)

D.3
Deriving the Depth Score

At pixel
p
, the detector’s line of sight is defined by the scan angles
(\theta_{p},\phi_{p})
.
Given a point cloud score in Cartesian coordinate
{\bm{s}}_{p}=[\partial\log\pi/\partial x_{1},\partial\log\pi/\partial x_{2},\partial\log\pi/\partial x_{3}]
, the Stein score of the depth
z
can be computed from the density (
81
) evaluated at these angles:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\log\widetilde{\pi}(z,\theta_{p},\phi_{p})=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\log(z^{2}\sin\theta_{p})+\frac{\partial\log\pi}{\partial z}

(82)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{z}+\frac{\partial\log\pi}{\partial x_{1}}\frac{\partial x_{1}}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial\log\pi}{\partial x_{2}}\frac{\partial x_{2}}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial\log\pi}{\partial x_{3}}\frac{\partial x_{3}}{\partial z}

(83)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{z}+\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\log\pi}{\partial x_{1}}\\
\frac{\partial\log\pi}{\partial x_{2}}\\
\frac{\partial\log\pi}{\partial x_{3}}\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\sin\theta_{p}\cos\phi_{p}\\
\cos\theta_{p}\\
-\sin\theta_{p}\sin\phi_{p}\end{bmatrix}

(84)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{z}+{\bm{s}}_{p}\cdot\widehat{{\bm{r}}}_{p}=\frac{2}{z}+\sigma_{p},

(85)

where
\widehat{{\bm{r}}}_{p}
is the unit vector pointing in the direction of the scan angles
(\theta_{p},\phi_{p})
.
In SSDR, we use only
\sigma_{p}={\bm{s}}_{p}\cdot\widehat{{\bm{r}}}_{p}
as the depth score, since we find that
2/z
often dominates the other term and does not provide good guidance.

Figure

15
shows additional 3D reconstructions from experiments.
We introduce another scene and include background flux estimates, along with statistics such as the average background flux estimate and the MAE of depth estimates. Reconstructions from low-flux measurements serve as the reference for computing signal flux and depth errors.

Consistent with simulation trends, free-running measurements yield more accurate signal flux and depth estimates. In contrast, synchronous mode fails to reconstruct the scene, as pile-up causes depth underestimation, resulting in a point cloud near the detector.
For the “Mannequin” scene, SSDR reduces MAE of depth estimates by 28%.
Background estimates for high-flux measurements remain uniform across pixels, as expected, while in low-flux measurements, they correlate with signal flux estimates due to laser multiple scattering.
In the “Dog” scene, low reflectivity in dark regions makes estimation difficult, even in low-flux conditions.
Both synchronous and free-running high-flux estimates remain unreliable in those regions.
For low-flux measurements, some estimated points on the Dog’s dark regions appear on the cardboard, because the laser spot has a finite size and it partially illuminates the more reflective cardboard.
