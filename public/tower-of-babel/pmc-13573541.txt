Continuing Professional Development in an Era of Physician Burnout
Authors: Robin Stevenson, Lisa Sullivan, Eugene Pozniak
Published: 2026

Concerns about physicians’ professional wellbeing, burnout, and clinical performance have never been greater. Yet physicians have never had greater access to medical knowledge or more opportunities to maintain their professional competence. Interactive continuing medical education (CME), targeted at important practice gaps, together with the internet and generative artificial intelligence (AI), has transformed physicians’ ability to keep up to date. Nevertheless, improvements in physician knowledge and competence have consistently translated into less evident gains in patient outcomes [1]. This disconnect has traditionally been attributed to barriers such as organisational constraints, time pressures, and patient factors, relevant to the broader concept of continuing professional development (CPD) [2]. But as healthcare systems become increasingly strained, an important question arises: have these barriers become so pervasive that the principal determinants of clinical performance are no longer physicians’ knowledge and competence, but the environments in which they practise?

The New Stressors

In healthcare, the explosion of investigative and treatment options has outstripped the ability of countries to pay for it. Today’s physicians must therefore cope with challenging technological advances, societal pressures, and unwieldy bureaucracies intent on demanding more for less. Emergency medicine and cancer care are prioritised and waiting times for elective surgery become impossibly protracted. Not all healthcare professionals can deal with this level of stress. Although some perform well in such circumstances, the well-being of many suffers [3]. It may be relevant that the trainee physicians in the UK, despite being awarded an increase in salary of greater than 20% in 2024, repeatedly took part in further strike action subsequently.

There is a spectrum of workplace stress from effects on occupational functioning with cognitive overload to the relatively new phenomenon known as burnout. This is defined as a “psychological syndrome arising in response to chronic emotional and interpersonal stressors on the job, with three key dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, and a decreased sense of personal accomplishment” [4]. More succinctly it is described as “breakdown in the relationship of people with their work” [5]. It is increasing dramatically and is associated with poor quality of care. The main causative factors are work overload, loss of autonomy, adverse working conditions, and lack of organisational support [6].

Does Current CME/CPD Need to Be Updated?

These observations prompt us to re-appraise contemporary CME/CPD. It requires the identification of professional practice gaps by a vast array of investigative procedures including electronic health records (EHR), quality improvement (QI) data, clinical audits, insurance claims and clinical registries. Gap data are often de-identified, aggregated or anonymised and planners design educational interventions on the assumption that the physicians’ knowledge and competence just need refreshing or updating.

The studies on barriers to performance, usually related to adverse working conditions, and the data on physician well-being challenge the validity of this assumption and suggest instead that the practice gap may be more related to physicians’ behaviour than to their knowledge and competence. This means that the needs which CME/CPD planners aim to assess should be revised to include not only deficiencies of knowledge and competence but also of performance and behaviour, the latter often reflecting workplace stress. In other words, the gaps addressed by traditional CME/CPD have usually been disease-specific, whereas the gaps now are often not disease-specific but rather may be due to aberrant physician behaviour.

If CME/CPD planners fail to attribute a practice gap to such behaviour, their education will be designed and delivered inappropriately and will not result in an improved outcome. It has therefore now become important for physician behaviour to be considered as a possible or indeed probable cause for a practice gap.

Most countries have monitoring systems for physician performance. In the US, Ongoing Professional Practice Evaluation (OPPE) uses metrics like complication rates, readmissions, and patient outcomes. In the UK, the NHS organises annual appraisal of performance and physicians are assessed for revalidation every 5 years. Although used variably, Multi-Source Feedback (MSF), also known as 360-degree feedback, is the most discriminating instrument for assessing physician behaviour. MSF is not a replacement for audit when clinical outcomes need to be assessed, but is more suited to assessing interpersonal relationships, communication skills, professionalism, and teamwork [7]. The main factors influencing how it can improve physician behaviour are facilitation of the feedback to physicians with narrative comments from sources believed to be credible by the physician [8].

Currently MSF is carried out quite separately from CME/CPD activities. It is used mainly by regulators as a tool in recertification and revalidation of physicians. It has not previously been considered as a means of identifying performance or behaviour gaps in the context of CME/CPD, although its potential for improving physicians’ performance has been appreciated as noted above. The Accreditation Council for CME (ACCME) in the US, the European Board for Accreditation of Continuing Education for Health Professionals (EBAC) and the European Accreditation Council for CME (EACCME) make no mention of MSF in advice to CME/CPD providers.

How to Implement Updated CME/CPD

Although less problematic, knowledge and competence gaps will not go away. Analysis may be carried out by interrogation of big data often by specialist societies either regionally or nationally. An example would be widespread failure of guideline adherence where physicians have simply fallen behind recent developments. Repair of these gaps is primarily educative and often supervised by the specialist societies. It is aptly described as CME. Physician performance and patient health outcomes are seldom assessed by the societies because it is expensive and notoriously difficult to do on a large scale. Such assessment is more feasible locally at hospital level, ideally by the CME/CPD department where one exists.

When knowledge and competence are considered adequate in relation to an identified practice gap, sub-optimal physician behaviour becomes probable and may be tested by MSF.

If confirmed, it is important to uncover the workplace environmental causes and to present the feedback data to physicians in such a way that they find themselves in “teachable moments” when they will be receptive to suggestions aimed at helping them to deal with the adverse environment. These remedial measures would not be purely educative and would be better described as CPD. In other words, in CME, physicians learn to treat their patients, whereas in their CPD, they discover how to treat themselves [9].

Given that behavioural problems in physicians usually arise from adverse working conditions, it makes no sense for CME/CPD planners to deal only with damaged physicians without trying to remedy the workplace problems. CME/CPD departments must therefore also design parallel corrective initiatives aimed at the governance systems of hospital management. Such interventions may fall outside conventional accreditation frameworks, but are nonetheless essential to improve physician performance, well-being, and patient care.

Not all practice gaps will be attributable to local adverse conditions in the workplace. Healthcare policies and priorities imposed by management bureaucracies at regional or national levels are often influenced by financial and societal considerations. Healthcare professionals are seldom involved in these processes and this loss of autonomy is recognised as a major contributing factor for burnout [6]. A strong case can be made for including senior physicians and nurses in discussions on the policies, philosophy and priorities of contemporary medical practice.

Conclusion

This paper seeks to re-define the roles of CME and CPD in a rapidly changing medical world dominated by technological advances, AI, financial constraints, workplace stresses and behavioural problems in healthcare professionals. The defining challenge for CPD in the coming decades will not just be to help physicians to learn more about medicine, but more importantly to work with healthcare systems to enable their physicians to practise better medicine.
