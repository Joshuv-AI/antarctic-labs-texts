HPV18 DNA detection and pathological laryngopharyngeal reflux in a young woman with glottic squamous cell carcinoma in situ: a brief report
Authors: Zhi Liu, Jinhong Zhang, Wenqing Chen, Jiaming Zheng, Thiansze Wong, Wenbin Lei, Kexing Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Laryngeal cancer predominantly affects elderly males with long-term smoking and alcohol histories, and its occurrence in young females is extremely rare. We report a 26-year-old woman with glottic squamous cell carcinoma in situ who presented with persistent hoarseness. She had no history of smoking, alcohol use, or radiation exposure. Upon evaluation, high-risk HPV18 DNA was detected in FFPE tumor tissue by Lcn PCR, and 24-hour MII-pH monitoring indicated pathological laryngopharyngeal reflux. Targeted sequencing of 535 genes showed no driver mutations, tumor mutation burden elevation, or microsatellite instability. The patient underwent transoral laser resection followed by postoperative anti-reflux management. At follow-up, she remained recurrence-free. This case suggests a possible contribution of HPV18 and laryngopharyngeal reflux to carcinogenesis in a young non-smoking woman with glottic carcinoma in situ. The findings support selective virologic evaluation and individualized reflux assessment in clinically atypical laryngeal squamous lesions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13027-026-00783-2.

Introduction

Laryngeal cancer is one of the most common malignancies of the head and neck, with marked variations by age and sex. Its incidence rises substantially after the age of 50 and peaks after 65 years. Long-term tobacco and alcohol use are major risk factors, particularly among elderly men [1], with reported male-to-female incidence ratios ranging from 6.4 to 24.2:1 [2, 3]. Notably, supraglottic carcinoma is more frequent in women, followed by glottic carcinoma [4], suggesting potential sex-related differences in disease pathogenesis. Hormonal factors, such as estrogen decline after menopause, have been proposed to contribute to female susceptibility [5]. Consequently, glottic carcinoma in young women without a history of tobacco or alcohol exposure remains exceptionally uncommon.

In recent years, human papillomavirus (HPV) infection has gained increasing attention in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, particularly the high-risk subtypes HPV16 and HPV18, which are associated with the onset and progression of laryngeal cancer [6]. The HPV E6 and E7 oncoproteins promote malignant transformation by inactivating tumor suppressor genes and inducing aberrant proteasome-mediated degradation [7]. In oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, p16 immunohistochemistry is widely used as a surrogate marker for transcriptionally active high-risk HPV infection; however, its clinical significance in laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma remains to be fully clarified [8, 9]. Beyond traditional risk factors, accumulating evidence indicates that laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) also contributes to laryngeal cancer development. Recurrent exposure of the laryngopharyngeal mucosa to gastric contents may induce epithelial hyperplasia and malignant transformation [10].

This report describes a young woman with glottic squamous cell carcinoma in situ, in whom HPV18 DNA was detected and pathological laryngopharyngeal reflux was demonstrated during evaluation. The interplay of these risk factors remains poorly understood. By analyzing this case, we aim to enhance understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms underlying atypical laryngeal cancer, support the development of individualized precision management strategies, and provide evidence-based insights for improving prognosis and reducing recurrence risk.

Case presentation

Basic patient information

A 26-year-old Han Chinese woman presented with a chief complaint of persistent hoarseness for more than four months. She was admitted to our hospital on April 22, 2025, and diagnosed with glottic laryngeal carcinoma. Her past medical history was unremarkable, with no hypertension, diabetes, or other chronic diseases. She denied previous HPV infection, smoking, alcohol consumption, or exposure to radiation or cytotoxic agents. There was no family history of head and neck malignancies.

Diagnosis and treatment

In January 2025, the patient developed persistent hoarseness of insidious onset, which progressively worsened. In March 2025, electronic laryngoscopy performed at an outside hospital revealed a mass on the left vocal cord. She subsequently underwent supported laryngoscopic resection of the lesion on April 1, and postoperative pathology indicated squamous cell carcinoma in situ. Her hoarseness improved after surgery; however, the local hospital recommended further management at our center to ensure negative surgical margins.

On April 10, electronic laryngoscopy at our outpatient clinic demonstrated mild congestion of both vocal folds, a leukomembranous attachment at the left anterior–mid vocal fold, preserved vocal fold mobility, and incomplete glottic closure (Fig. 1). Pathological review of the outside specimen showed squamous epithelial carcinoma in situ of the left vocal cord, characterized by severe dysplasia with papillary growth, enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei, and mitotic figures (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemistry showed mottled p16 positivity, wild-type p53 expression, EGFR 2+, and a Ki-67 labeling index of approximately 30%. Laryngeal MRI showed slight swelling of the left vocal cord without evidence of surrounding soft tissue invasion or regional lymphadenopathy (Fig. 3).

After exclusion of surgical contraindications, the patient underwent trans-supraglottic laryngoscopic laser-assisted anterior commissure partial laryngectomy under general anesthesia on April 27. Postoperative laryngoscopic review demonstrated well-preserved bilateral vocal fold mobility, pseudomembrane formation at the operative site of the left vocal fold, and absence of purulent secretions (Fig. 4).

Reflux assessment and examination

Electronic laryngoscopic images were independently evaluated by an experienced otolaryngologist. The Reflux Finding Score (RFS) was 10 (> 7, positive for LPR), self-reported Reflux Symptom Index was 15 (> 13, positive), and Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease Questionnaire (RDQ) score was 3 (< 12, negative). Psychological assessment

The patient’s self-reported mental status was generally stable before and after disease onset. Psychological assessment conducted after admission revealed mild depressive symptoms, with a Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) score of 5, and mild anxiety, with a Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) score of 5. The Esophageal Hypervigilance and Anxiety Scale (EHAS) score was 7. Detailed scores for each assessment are presented in Table 1.

HPV Lcn PCR genotyping

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples from the patient were submitted to Suzhou Precision Medicine Technology Co., Ltd. for analysis. HPV18 DNA was detected in FFPE tumor tissue using the Lcn PCR method.

Gene sequencing

Targeted sequenc ing of a 535-gene panel identified no actionable somatic alterations, no elevated tumor mutation burden, and no microsatellite instability, supporting the absence of an obvious alternative genomic driver in this young non-smoking patient.

Follow-up management

At present, the patient is eleven months postoperatively. She received standardized anti-reflux therapy and psychological support after surgery. No tumor recurrence was observed during routine follow-up, and her quality of life was reported as good. PHQ-9, GAD-7, and EHAS scores showed significant improvement at both one- and nine-month postoperative assessments.

Discussion

This case is distinctive in several aspects. The patient was a young woman without a history of smoking or alcohol consumption, representing a rare subgroup of laryngeal cancer. During the clinical course, both LPR and HPV18 infection were documented, suggesting the involvement of multiple nontraditional risk factors. Unlike the typical profile of middle-aged and elderly male patients, the pathogenesis of laryngeal cancer in young women may be more closely associated with endocrine hormone levels, viral infection, and chronic inflammatory stimulation [11].

High-risk HPV, particularly HPV16 and HPV18, has been implicated in a subset of laryngeal squamous lesions, although its etiologic role in the larynx remains less well established than in the oropharynx. Its oncogenic mechanisms involve genomic integration, resulting in host gene mutations, altered gene expression, and abnormal cellular metabolism, ultimately driving malignant proliferation [6]. HPV DNA can enter the nucleus of damaged laryngeal epithelial cells and integrate into the host genome, activating proto-oncogenes such as RAS, EGFR, and Myc. This promotes cell cycle progression from G1 to S phase, accelerating proliferation and facilitating tumorigenesis [12]. Additionally, the viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 inhibit tumor suppressor genes including p53 and Rb, disrupting cell cycle regulation, sustaining proliferation, and impairing apoptosis [13,14]. HPV infection can also modulate genes related to survivin, cyclin D1, and telomerase, further promoting cellular immortality and tumor progression [15,16]. In this case, HPV18 positivity suggests a potential role of this subtype in laryngeal cancer among young women without traditional risk factors such as long-term smoking or alcohol use. Combined detection of HPV DNA and E6/E7 expression may improve assessment of tumor biology, molecular subtype, and progression, thereby guiding precision therapy and prognostic evaluation. This case highlights the potential value of selective HPV-specific evaluation in clinically atypical laryngeal squamous lesions, particularly in young patients without conventional risk factors.

LPR is recognized as an important risk factor for laryngeal cancer [10]. Compared with the esophageal mucosa, the laryngeal and hypopharyngeal mucosa lack carbonic anhydrase and thus cannot generate sufficient bicarbonate for neutralization and protection, rendering them more susceptible to chemical injury from gastric acid and pepsin [17]. Pepsin remains enzymatically active at pH 2.0 and, although inactivated at pH 6.5, can be reactivated upon re-exposure to an acidic environment, thereby sustaining epithelial injury [18]. In this patient, 24-hour MII-pH monitoring documented two episodes of pharyngeal acid reflux, providing objective evidence of mucosal damage. Mechanistically, pepsin disrupts intercellular junctions, downregulates E-cadherin, and upregulates vimentin and β-catenin, thereby inducing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and promoting cell migration, invasion, and carcinogenesis [19]. In parallel, pepsin enhances the expression of inflammatory mediators such as IL-8, IL-1 A, and CXCL14, leading to chronic mucosal inflammation and microenvironmental remodeling, which accelerate malignant transformation of precancerous lesions [19]. Pepsin also modulates gene and microRNA expression, upregulating oncogenes (e.g., Ras) while suppressing tumor suppressor genes (e.g., BCL2-related proteins), thereby driving metabolic dysregulation and uncontrolled proliferation [18, 20]. Chronic reflux further induces histological metaplasia and compromises mucosal barrier function, creating conditions that facilitate exposure to carcinogens and viral infections.

Notably, LPR and HPV infection may act synergistically in laryngeal carcinogenesis. Chronic acidic reflux increases mucosal permeability and disrupts intercellular junctions, facilitating HPV invasion into epithelial and basal cells and promoting viral DNA integration into the host genome. Simultaneously, LPR-induced chronic inflammation and alterations in the local immune microenvironment may impair immune surveillance, further enhancing the oncogenic potential of HPV.

Psychological distress may have amplified reflux-related symptom perception and influenced postoperative management, but was not interpreted as a primary oncogenic factor in this case.

This report has several limitations. Although HPV18 DNA was detected in FFPE tumor tissue, the present work did not include E6/E7 mRNA testing or RNA in situ hybridization to establish transcriptionally active HPV-driven carcinogenesis. In addition, p16 staining was mottled rather than diffuse and block-positive. Therefore, our findings support a possible association between HPV18 and this unusual laryngeal lesion, but do not prove causality. Likewise, pathological laryngopharyngeal reflux may have acted as a local cofactor, although a causal role cannot be inferred from a single case.

Conclusion

This brief report describes a rare glottic squamous cell carcinoma in situ in a young non-smoking woman with HPV18 DNA detection and objectively documented laryngopharyngeal reflux. The case supports considering selective virologic testing and individualized, stepwise reflux assessment in clinically atypical laryngeal squamous lesions, while highlighting the need for further studies to clarify whether HPV and reflux act independently or cooperatively in laryngeal carcinogenesis.
