Abstract
. We consider the convergence problem in the setting of mean field control with common noise and degenerate idiosyncratic noise. Our main results establish a rate of convergence of the finite-dimensional value functions V N V^{N} towards the mean field value function U U . In the case that the idiosyncratic noise is constant (but possibly degenerate), we obtain the rate N − 1 / ( d + 7 ) N^{-1/(d+7)} , which is close to the conjectured optimal rate N − 1 / d N^{-1/d} , and improves on the existing literature even in the non-degenerate setting. In the case that the idiosyncratic noise can be both non-constant and degenerate, the argument is more complicated, and we instead find the rate N − 1 / ( 3 d + 19 ) N^{-1/(3d+19)} . Our proof strategy builds on the one initiated in [ DDJ24 ] in the case of non-degenerate idiosyncratic noise and zero common noise, which consists of approximating U U by more regular functions which are almost subsolutions of the infinite-dimensional Hamilton-Jacobi equation solved by U U . Because of the different noise structure, several new steps are necessary in order to produce an appropriate mollification scheme. In addition to our main convergence results, we investigate the case of zero idiosyncratic noise, and show that sharper results can be obtained there by purely control-theoretic arguments. We also provide examples to demonstrate that the value function is sensitive to the choice of admissible controls in the zero noise setting.

Introduction

1.1.
The
N
-particle and mean field control problems

Mean field control theory is concerned with the large population limits of certain optimal control problems. In the setting addressed in the present paper, we have, for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, a stochastic control problem set on the state space
(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}
, where
\mathbb{T}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is the
d
-dimensional flat torus. This problem is efficiently described by a value function
V^{N}:[0,T]\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is defined informally as follows:

(1.1)

\displaystyle V^{N}(t_{0},{\bm{x}}_{0})=\inf_{\bm{\alpha}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L(X_{t}^{i},\alpha_{t}^{i})+F(m_{\bm{X}_{t}}^{N})\Big)dt+G(m_{\bm{X}_{T}}^{N})\bigg],

where we write
{\bm{x}}_{0}=(x_{0}^{1},...,x_{0}^{N})
, and the
(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}
-valued state process
\bm{X}=(X^{1},...,X^{N})
is determined from the control
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha^{1},...,\alpha^{N})
via the dynamics

\displaystyle dX_{t}^{i}=\alpha_{t}^{i}dt+\sigma(X_{t}^{i})dW_{t}^{i}+\sigma^{0}dW_{t}^{0},\quad t_{0}\leq t\leq T,\quad X^{i}_{t_{0}}=x_{0}^{i}.

The problem is set on a filtered probability space hosting the independent
d
-dimensional Brownian motions
(W^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
, and the controls
\alpha^{i}
are adapted
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued processes satisfying appropriate integrability conditions. For
{\bm{x}}=(x^{1},...,x^{N})\in(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}
, we are using the notation
m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{i}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for the empirical measure associated to
{\bm{x}}
. The data for our problem consists of

(1.2)

\displaystyle L:\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\quad F,G:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R},\quad\sigma:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\quad\sigma^{0}\geq 0.

The Brownian motions
(W^{i})_{i=1,...,N}
are
individual
or
idiosyncratic
noises, while the Brownian motion
W^{0}
is a
common
noise which impacts all players. We can interpret this control problem by imagining that there is a team of
N
agents. For
i=1,...,N
, agent
i
has state process
X^{i}
, which they can control by choosing
\alpha^{i}
, and the players cooperate to minimize the cost function appearing in (
1.1
).

We recall (see
[
FS06
]
for instance) that under mild assumptions on the data,
V^{N}
is the unique viscosity solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation

(
\text{HJB}_{N}
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}V^{N}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{tr}\big(A(x^{i})D^{2}_{x^{i}x^{i}}V^{N}\big)-A_{0}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\operatorname{tr}(D^{2}_{x^{i}x^{j}}V^{N})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H(x^{i},ND_{x^{i}}V^{N})=F(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}),\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\hskip 256.0748pt(t,{\bm{x}})\in[0,T)\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N},\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle V^{N}(T,{\bm{x}})=G(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}),\quad{\bm{x}}\in(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N},\end{cases}

where we have defined

(1.3)

\displaystyle H(x,p)=\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(-L(x,a)-a\cdot p\Big),\quad A(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sigma(x)\sigma^{T}(x),\quad A_{0}=\frac{1}{2}|\sigma^{0}|^{2}.

As
N\to\infty
, we expect that these control problems should converge in some sense to the corresponding mean field control problem, which is described by a value function
U:[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
defined informally by

(1.4)

\displaystyle U(t_{0},m_{0})=\inf_{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\Big(L(X_{t},\alpha_{t})+F\big(\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|W^{0})\big)\Big)dt+G\big(\mathcal{L}(X_{T}|W^{0})\big)\bigg],

where the state process
X
is determined form the control
\alpha
via the dynamics

\displaystyle dX_{t}=\alpha_{t}dt+\sigma(X_{t})dW_{t}+\sigma^{0}dW_{t}^{0},\quad X_{t_{0}}\sim m_{0}.

Above, the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Brownian motions
W
and
W^{0}
are independent,
the initial condition
X_{t_{0}}\sim m_{0}
indicates that
X_{t_{0}}
has law
m_{0}
and is independent of
W^{0}
, and
\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|W^{0})
denotes the law of
X_{t}
conditional on the
\sigma

W^{0}
.

By the dynamic programming principle (see e.g.
[
DPT22b
]
), we expect that under appropriate conditions on the data, the function

solves the infinite-dimensional Hamilton-Jacobi equation

(

)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}U-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(A(x)D_{xm}U(t,m,x)\big)m(dx)-A_{0}\Delta_{\text{com}}U\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}H\big(x,D_{m}U(t,m,x)\big)m(dx)=F(m),\quad(t,m)\in[0,T)\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle U(T,m)=G(m),\quad m\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{com}}U(t,m)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(D_{xm}U(t,m,x)\big)m(dx)+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(D^{2}_{mm}U(t,m,x,y)\big)m(dx)m(dy),

and

are appropriate derivatives of

2

Without convexity conditions on

∞

,

. In the present paper, we shall not in fact use the uniqueness of viscosity solutions, but we will use the fact that

∞

).

1.2.
Existing results on the convergence problem

There are a number of ways to formalize the convergence of the
N
-particle control problems towards their mean field counterpart. For example, one can consider the convergence of the optimal controls or of the optimally controlled state processes, in the spirit of propagation of chaos. In the present paper, we work under conditions that do not guarantee uniqueness of optimizers for the limiting problem, which makes convergence of the optimal controls or states quite subtle. We thus focus on the convergence of the value functions, i.e. we aim to show that
V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})\approx U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})
when
N
is large.

The qualitative convergence of
V^{N}
to
U
was first demonstrated via probabilistic compactness methods in
[
BDF12
]
, for a class of models with purely quadratic Hamiltonians arising from the study of large deviations of weakly interacting particle systems. This result was greatly generalized by Lacker in
[
Lac17
]
, and then Lacker’s result was further generalized in
[
DPT22a
]
and
[
Dje22
]
to allow common noise and interaction through the controls, respectively. In
[
GMS21
,
MS23
]
, qualitative convergence of
V^{N}
to
U
is obtained for models with purely common noise via PDE methods, by leveraging a comparison principle for the limiting Hamilton-Jacobi equation (

HJB
∞

). We mention also
[
CLOS22
,
FLOS19
]
for a treatment of deterministic mean field control problems via
\Gamma
-convergence techniques,
[
TTZ22
]
for the convergence of the value functions of certain mean field optimal stopping problems, and
[
Dau23
]
for an extension of the probabilistic compactness methods from
[
Lac17
]
to problems with state constraints.

The references in the previous paragraph all rely in some way on compactness arguments, and so do not provide a rate of convergence of
V^{N}
to
U
. On the other hand, if
U
is smooth enough, then an explicit computation shows that
U^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}):=U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}):[0,T]\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
solves (

HJB
N

) but with an additional error term on the right-hand side of the form

(1.5)

\displaystyle E^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})=-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{tr}\big(A(x^{i})D^{2}_{mm}U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},x^{i},x^{i})\big).

In particular, by the comparison principle
|U-V^{N}|=O(1/N)
if
D^{2}_{mm}U
is bounded. This is in fact a simpler version of the argument introduced in
[
CDLL19
]
to show that a similar regularity estimate for the master equation implies convergence of the so-called Nash system, in the context of mean field games.

Unfortunately, smoothness of
U
is only expected under certain restrictive convexity conditions, and establishing a rate of convergence in the non-convex regime has proved much more subtle. In
[
Cec21
]
, a sharp rate was provided for mean field control problems set on a finite state space, in which case the limiting value function

is defined on a finite-dimensional simplex. Similar techniques were used in
[
BCC23
]
to obtain rates of convergence with a continuous state space, but with costs and dynamics which depend on
m
through finitely many of its moments. The first quantitative result for a general class of non-convex diffusion models came in
[
CDJS23
]
, where the estimate

(1.6)

\displaystyle|V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})-U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})|\leq CN^{-\beta_{d}}

was established

, with

[

is Lipschitz. In particular, it was shown in

On the other hand, it was shown in

1.3.
Our contribution

The results of
[
DDJ24
]
indicate that the optimal rate of convergence is somewhere between
N^{-1/d}
and
N^{-1/2}
, depending on the metric with respect to which
U
is Lipschitz. However, the results of
[
DDJ24
]
require a constant, non-degenerate idiosyncratic noise and zero common noise. Thus, in the present paper we aim to investigate the following question:

How is the rate of convergence impacted by the structure of the noise?

In particular, our goal is to obtain sharp estimates on
|V^{N}-U|
in the case that
\sigma(x)
is degenerate and
\sigma^{0}
is non-zero.

Let us first make an important point regarding the role of the idiosyncratic noise. As explained above, the rate
N^{-1/2}
cannot be expected unless
U
is Lipschitz with respect to a “sufficiently weak” metric, as in the
H^{-s}
-regular case. On the other hand, if
A_{0}=0
,
F=0
, and
G(m)=\int g(x)m(dx)
for some
g:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, then we have
U(t,m)=\int u(t,x)m(dx)
, where
u
solves

(1.9)

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}u-\operatorname{tr}\big(A(x)D^{2}u)+H(x,Du)=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},\quad u(T,x)=g(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}.

It is well known that if
\sigma
is degenerate (for example when
\sigma=0
), the solution
u
of (
1.9
) is at best Lipschitz, even when
g
,
H
and
A
are all smooth. This means that
U=\int u\,dm
is at best
\bm{d}_{1}
-Lipschitz, and so we cannot expect an anologue of
[
DDJ24
, Theorem 2.7]
when
\sigma
is degenerate, because in this case
U
does not “inherit”
H^{-s}
-Lipschitz regularity from the data. On the other hand, because we can still get
\bm{d}_{1}
-Lipschitz bounds for degenerate
\sigma
, we can hope for an analogue of Theorem 2.6 of
[
DDJ24
]
, i.e. a dimension-dependent rate which is close to
N^{-1/d}
. This is exactly what we obtain in Theorem
2.5
below, where we show that

(1.10)

\displaystyle|V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})-U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})|\leq CN^{-\gamma_{d}},

where

(1.11)

\displaystyle\gamma_{d}\coloneqq\begin{cases}1/(3d+19)&\text{if $d$ is even},\\
1/(3d+16)&\text{if $d$ is odd}.\end{cases}

under general conditions on the data, which in particular allow for both degenerate idiosyncratic noise and non-zero common noise. In fact, if either (i)
\sigma
is constant or (ii)
U
is globally Lipschitz with respect to the metric inherited from
W^{-2,\infty}
, then (
1.10
) can be improved to

(1.12)

\displaystyle|V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})-U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})|\leq CN^{-\gamma^{\prime}_{d}},

where

(1.13)

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}_{d}=\begin{cases}1/(d+7)&\text{if $d$ is even},\\
1/(d+6)&\text{if $d$ is odd},\end{cases}

which is very close to the conjectured optimal rate of

, and obviously sharper than the estimate (
1.8
). We note that a

-Lipschitz bound on

can be obtained when the data is smooth enough and
\sigma
is non-degenerate via the arguments in Section 3 of
[

]
. Thus while our main motivation is the case of degenerate

and non-zero

, even in the case
\sigma=\sqrt{2}I_{d\times d}
,
\sigma^{0}=0
, our main result improves on

and provides an almost optimal result in this setting, with the only possible improvement being to replace

(or

. We note that the improved rate in the case of constant

comes from an improvement in a key technical step of the subsolution bound for the regularized

(see Paragraph

In addition to the general results discussed above, we study in Section

\sigma=0
. The mean-field limit in this setting was already addressed in the literature in the presence of a pure common noise in
[

]
and without any noise in
[

,

. From a PDE point of view, this case is intriguing since the Hamilton-Jacobi equations describing the

∞

\displaystyle 0\leq V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})-U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\leq Cr_{N,d},

\displaystyle r_{N,d}=\begin{cases}N^{-1/d}&d\geq 3,\\

We then turn our attention to the special case of no noise (
\sigma=\sigma^{0}=0
) and purely quadratic Hamiltonian

). In this setting a Hopf-Lax formula for both

. Finally, we highlight two other interesting phenomena in the special case of zero noise and quadratic Hamiltonian. First, we show that in this case the value function

To the best of our knowledge, these results in the zero idiosyncratic noise regime are new and improve in some ways on the existing literature

. In particular, the observation that the value of the mean-field control problem is always lesser than the value of the

This being said, we rely there heavily on the control formulation, and, in some places, on an explicit Hopf-Lax formula, available for purely quadratic Hamiltonians. We also believe that, by contrast, these findings shed some light on the role of the idiosyncratic noise with respect to the comparison of the value functions.

To summarize, the main contribution of the present paper is to establish the bound (

) in a setting where
\sigma(x)

\sigma^{0}
can be non-zero. This result extends Theorem 2.6 of
[

]

\sigma

d\geq 3

1.4.
Strategy of the proof

The basic strategy of the proof is similar to that of Theorem 2.6 in
[
DDJ24
]
. In particular, as in
[
DDJ24
]
we obtain the bound
V^{N}\leq U+Cr_{N,d}
, with
r_{N,d}
as in (
1.15
), by a control-theoretic argument which has already appeared several times in the literature. For the more difficult inequality
U\leq V^{N}+CN^{-\gamma_{d}}
, we use a more analytical approach, and attempt to approximate
U
by a function which is almost a subsolution to (

HJB
∞

), and which is smooth enough that the “error term” appearing (
1.5
) can be controlled. In
[
DDJ24
]
, this approximation is achieved in three steps
U\rightarrow U^{\delta}\rightarrow U^{\delta,\epsilon}\rightarrow U^{\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}
,
defined successively by

\displaystyle U^{\delta}(t,m)=U(t,m*\rho_{\delta}),\quad U^{\delta,\epsilon}(t,m)=\sup_{m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\Big\{U^{\delta}(t,m^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\|m-m^{\prime}\|_{-s}^{2}\Big\},

\displaystyle U^{\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}(t,m)=U^{\delta,\epsilon}\big(t,(1-\lambda)m+\lambda\mathrm{Leb}\big),

where

is a standard smooth approximation to the identity,

is the Lebesgue measure on

, and

,

,

is to obtain quantitative error estimates which show that

\delta,\epsilon,\lambda\to 0
,

(2)

converges to

as
\delta,\epsilon,\lambda\to 0

(3)

is a subsolution of (

∞

) up to an error term which vanishes as

These bounds show that

N

) up to an error which can be estimated in terms of

There are various challenges we have to face when attempting to adapt this regularization scheme to the case of common noise and degenerate idiosyncratic noise. Here we explain the main issues caused by the the degenerate idiosyncratic noise and the common noise, respectively, as well as the techniques we use to overcome these challenges.

1.4.1.
The degenerate and non-constant idiosyncratic noise

Let us set
\sigma^{0}=0
for the moment, and discuss only the effect of idiosyncratic noise. In this case, the main difference with the case treated in
[
DDJ24
]
comes in the step
U\to U^{\delta}
. In particular, the proof that
U^{\delta}
is almost a subsolution of (

HJB
∞

) (see Lemma

) is quite involved when
\sigma
depends on
x
, and in order to conclude we have to establish a sort of commutator estimate, see Lemma
5.9
. Moreover, in order to complete the proof of Lemma
5.7
, we have to treat the “full Hamiltonian”

(1.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\ni(q,p,x)\mapsto\operatorname{tr}\big(A(x)q\big)+H(x,p)

as globally Lipschitz. Notice that while
H
is generally just locally Lipschitz, we can show that
U
is
\bm{d}_{1}
-Lipschitz, hence we have (at least formally) an a-priori estimate on
\|D_{m}U\|_{\infty}
, which allows us to treat the map

as Lipschitz. When

is constant, this is enough, but when

is non-constant, the full Hamiltonian in (

) will be Lipschitz only when

is bounded, i.e. when

is Lipschitz in

, which formally holds when

is

-Lipschitz. In other words, when

is non-constant we need an a-priori estimate on the

-Lipschitz constant of

in order to conclude that
U^{\delta}
is almost a subsolution of (

HJB
∞

), even formally. But as discussed above, when

In order to circumvent this first difficulty, we use a vanishing viscosity procedure, i.e. we begin by replacing

by

, where
U^{\eta}
is defined exactly like

but with an additional idiosyncratic noise of strength
\eta
in the dynamics of

. For technical reasons, we also mollify

and

in an appropriate way when defining

. We define

similarly, see Subsection

(1.18)

\displaystyle|V^{N}-V^{N,\eta}|\leq C\sqrt{\eta},\quad|U-U^{\eta}|\leq C\sqrt{\eta},\quad\text{Lip}(U^{\eta};W^{-2,\infty})\leq C/\eta,

where
\text{Lip}(U^{\eta};W^{-2,\infty})
denotes the Lipschitz constant of

in

with respect to the

-metric. The bounds in (

is

-Lipschitz, at least provided that we can track the dependence of the estimates on the

-Lipschitz constant. Indeed, in Proposition

, we obtain the bound

\displaystyle U\leq V^{N}+C\Big(1+C_{\text{Lip}}(U;W^{-2,\infty})\Big)^{1-1/(2s^{*}+1)}N^{-1/(2s^{*}+1)},

with

given by (
5.5
) below,
under the additional assumption that

is

-Lipschitz, which, when applied to each

, leads to

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\eta}+U^{\eta}\leq V^{N,\eta}+C\sqrt{\eta}+C\eta^{-1+1/(2s^{*}+1)}N^{-1/(2s^{*}+1)}

\displaystyle\leq V^{N}+C\sqrt{\eta}+C\eta^{-1+1/(2s^{*}+1)}N^{-1/(2s^{*}+1)},

and it is optimizing in

and using the definition of

which leads to the estimate (

). When

is constant or

is already known to be

-Lipschitz, this vanishing viscosity step turns out to be unnecessary, which explains why we get a sharper estimate in these cases.

We emphasize that while the estimate on

is standard, the estimate on

is not, because the natural control-theoretic technique for obtaining Lipschitz bounds on

[

]
, leads to bounds on

which have an exponential dependence on

, where

is defined like

but with an additional idiosyncratic noise of strength

[

, this is enough to conclude the desired bound on

.

1.4.2.
The common noise

The vanishing viscosity procedure discussed above allows us to deal with the degeneracy of the idiosyncratic noise, so we now assume that the noise is non-degenerate and
U
is
W^{-2,\infty}
-Lipschitz, so we can safely ignore the issues pointed out above. Nevertheless, we still have to incorporate the common noise into the regularization scheme. The issue here is not with the step
U\to U^{\delta}
(which works fine with common noise) but instead with the step
U^{\delta}\to U^{\delta,\epsilon}
. Indeed, when
\sigma^{0}>0
, we are unable to show that the function
U^{\delta,\epsilon}
defined in (
1.16
) is almost a subsolution to (

HJB
∞

). Roughly speaking, the problem seems to be that the common noise operator
\Delta_{\text{com}}
is simply not compatible with the sup-convolution operation appearing in the definition of
U^{\delta,\epsilon}
. We found a way around this issue by employing a change of variables which has recently been used in
[
BEZ
,
DJS23
]
to treat the comparison principle for equations like (

HJB
∞

). In particular, we first define
\widehat{U}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
and
\widehat{V}^{N}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\widehat{U}(t,z,m)=U\big(t,(\text{Id}+z)_{\#}m\big),\quad\widehat{V}^{N}(t,z,{\bm{x}})=\widehat{V}^{N}(t,z,x^{1},...,x^{N})=V^{N}(t,x^{1}+z,...,x^{N}+z).

It turns out that
\widehat{U}
solves (formally at least) the PDE

(1.19)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\widehat{U}(t,z,m)-A_{0}\Delta_{z}\widehat{U}(t,z,m)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(\widehat{A}(x,z)D_{xm}\widehat{U}(t,z,m,x)\big)m(dx)\\
\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\widehat{H}\big(x,z,D_{m}\widehat{U}(t,z,m,x)\big)=\widehat{F}(z,m),\quad(t,z,m)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle\widehat{U}(t,z,m)=\widehat{G}(z,m),\end{cases}

in a viscosity sense, where we have set

(1.20)

\displaystyle\widehat{A}(x,z)=A(x+z),\quad\widehat{H}(x,z,p)=H(x+z,p),\quad\widehat{F}(z,m)=F(m^{z}),\quad\widehat{G}(z,m)=G(m^{z}),

and we have used the notation
m^{z}=(\text{Id}+z)_{\#}m
for simplicity. For
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we are defining the sum
x+z
in the obvious way, i.e. if
x=[y]
, i.e.
x
is the equivalence class of

, then
x+z=[y+z]
. Obviously,
\widehat{U}
is periodic in
z
, so could instead be viewed as a function on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, but it is convenient for technical reasons to work with
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Similarly,
\widehat{V}^{N}
satisfies

(
\widehat{\text{HJB}}_{N}
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\widehat{V}^{N}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{tr}\big(\widehat{A}(x^{i},z)D^{2}_{x^{i}x^{i}}\widehat{V}^{N}\big)-A_{0}\Delta_{z}\widehat{V}^{N}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{H}(x^{i},z,ND_{x^{i}}\widehat{V}^{N})=\widehat{F}(z,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}),\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\hskip 256.0748pt(t,z,{\bm{x}})\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N},\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle\widehat{V}^{N}(T,{\bm{x}})=\widehat{G}(z,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}),\quad(z,{\bm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{N}.\end{cases}

In other words, this change of variables exchanges the common noise operator
\Delta_{\text{com}}
for a finite-dimensional Laplacian in the variable

, and so we focus instead on the equivalent problem of estimating
\widehat{U}-\widehat{V}^{N}
. Remarkably, if we define
\widehat{U}^{\delta}
,
\widehat{U}^{\delta,\epsilon}
,
\widehat{U}^{\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}
by

\displaystyle\widehat{U}^{\delta}(t,m)=\widehat{U}(t,z,m*\rho_{\delta}),

\displaystyle\widehat{U}^{\delta,\epsilon}(t,z,m)=\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}^{d},m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\Big\{\widehat{U}^{\delta}(t,z^{\prime},m^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\Big(\|m-m^{\prime}\|_{-s}^{2}+|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}\Big)\Big\},

\displaystyle\widehat{U}^{\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}(t,m)=\widehat{U}^{\delta,\epsilon}\big(t,(1-\lambda)m+\lambda\mathrm{Leb}\big),

then we can in fact get all the relevant error estimates, and so once again complete the proof by choosing
\delta,\epsilon,\lambda
as functions of
N
. To make this argument rigorous, we have to argue that
\widehat{U}^{\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}
is almost a subsolution to (
1.19
) and satisfies certain regularity estimates, and then infer that the function
(t,z,{\bm{x}})\mapsto\widehat{U}^{\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}(t,z,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})
is almost a subsolution to (

^
HJB

N

). In
[
DDJ24
]
, this point is less subtle because there are no second-order terms in the equation, so that the subsolution estimates can be interpreted in a pointwise fashion (see
[
DDJ24
, Lemma 5.13]
). Here, we instead use an argument based on Jensen’s lemma to verify that
(t,z,{\bm{x}})\mapsto\widehat{U}^{\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}(t,z,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})
satisfies the relevant estimate in a viscosity, rather than pointwise, sense; see the proof of Proposition

below.

1.4.3.
Summary of the regularization argument

We have discussed how to overcome the difficulties posed by the degenerate idiosyncratic noise and the non-zero common noise separately. In order to treat them at the same time, we have to put together (i) the vanishing viscosity procedure necessary to create
W^{-2,\infty}
-Lipschitz bounds because of the degenerate and non-constant idiosyncratic noise, (ii) the change of variables necessary to exchange the common noise operator with a finite-dimensional Laplacian, and (iii) the three transformations already used in
[
DDJ24
]
. This leads to the sequence of transformations

\displaystyle U\to U^{\eta}\to\widehat{U}^{\eta}\to\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta}\to\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta,\epsilon}\to\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}.

The function
U^{\eta}
is defined just like
U
but with an additional vanishing viscosity term (see (
3.3
) below), and then the remaining transformation are defined successively by

\displaystyle\widehat{U}^{\eta}(t,z,m)=U^{\eta}\big(t,(\text{Id}+z)_{\#}m\big),\quad\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta}(t,z,m)=U(t,z,m*\rho_{\delta}),

\displaystyle\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta,\epsilon}(t,z,m)=\sup_{z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}^{d},m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\Big\{\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta}(t,z^{\prime},m^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\Big(\|m-m^{\prime}\|_{-s}^{2}+|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}\Big)\Big\},

\displaystyle\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}(t,z,m)=\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}\big(t,z,(1-\lambda)m+\lambda\mathrm{Leb}\big).

As in
[
DDJ24
]
, the key technical step is then to obtain quantitative error bounds showing that

(1)

D^{2}_{mm}\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}
blows up at a certain rate as
\delta,\epsilon,\lambda\to 0
,

(2)

\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}
converges to
U
as
\delta,\epsilon,\lambda\to 0
,

(3)

\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}
is a subsolution of (

HJB
∞

) up to an error term which is vanishes as
\delta,\epsilon,\lambda\to 0
.

Finally, using these bounds we must argue that
(t,z,{\bm{x}})\mapsto\widehat{U}^{\eta,\delta,\epsilon,\lambda}(t,z,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})
is a subsolution of (

^
HJB

N

) up to some explicit error bounds, which leads to

\displaystyle U\leq V^{N}+C(\eta,\delta,\epsilon,\lambda,N)

for some explicit function
C
. Finally, we choose
\eta,\delta,\epsilon,\lambda
as functions of
N
to conclude.

1.5.
Possible extensions and limitations of the method

Our main assumptions (see Assumption
2.2
) are fairly minimal in terms of regularity. We require only that
F
and
G
are
\bm{d}_{1}
-Lipschitz and
TV
-semiconcave (explained below),
H
is
C^{2}
and satisfies natural convexity and local Lipschitz properties, and that
\sigma
is
C^{2}
. These represent a simple set of conditions under which we can verify that
U
is
\bm{d}_{1}
-Lipschitz and
TV
-semiconcave, which is the basic starting point of our regularization scheme.

Regarding the Hamiltonian, we could relax the
C^{2}
assumption at the expense of an additional mollification step, and also we could replace
H(x,p)-F(m)
with
\mathcal{H}(x,p,m)
, provided that
\mathcal{H}
satisfies appropriate regularity conditions. We choose to work with separable Hamiltonians, because there are several steps (e.g. when we mollify the data or when we differentiate
V^{N,\eta}
twice in order to prove that
|D^{2}_{x^{i}x^{i}}V^{N,\eta}|\leq C/(N\eta)
) where the separability of the Hamiltonian leads to shorter and cleaner arguments.

We could also relax somewhat the dynamics of the state process. For example, we could replace the linear drift control (
2.12
) with

\displaystyle dX_{t}=b(X_{t},\alpha_{t})dt+\sigma(X_{t})dW_{t}+\sigma^{0}dW_{t}^{0}

without much difficulty, at least provided that
H(x,p)=\sup_{a}\Big(-L(x,a)-b(x,a)\cdot p\Big)
satisfies appropriate regularity conditions. If we included
m
-dependent dynamics of the form

\displaystyle dX_{t}=b(X_{t},\alpha_{t},\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|W^{0}))dt+\sigma(X_{t},\mathcal{L}(X_{t}|W^{0}))dW_{t}+\sigma^{0}dW_{t}^{0},

most of the proofs go through but there is a technical problem with the
TV
-semiconcavity bound on
U
. In particular, it is not clear to us whether an analogue of Lemma
3.9
holds in this case, the issue being that linear Fokker-Planck equations always generate contractions in the total variation norm (even if they have irregular data), but the same is not true for non-linear Fokker-Planck equations we would get if we had
m
-dependent dynamics. But when
\sigma
is non-degnerate,
TV
-semiconcavity can be recovered by another argument, and so we expect in this case including
m
-dependent dynamics should not be a major problem. We do not pursue this direction because we prefer to focus on degenerate idiosyncratic noise.

Finally, we mention that we certainly cannot allow
\sigma^{0}
to depend on
x
(or
m
) with our current methods, because then we are unable to perform the same change of variables to handle the corresponding common noise operator. This is one of the primary limitations of our approach.

Spaces of functions and distributions on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
:
Given

, we define
\mathbb{T}^{d}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
to be the
d
-dimensional flat torus. We write
x=(x_{1},...,x_{d})
for the general element
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and abuse notation slightly by writing
|x-y|
for the distance between elements
x
and
y
of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. We endow
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with the usual Lebesgue measure. For
\phi:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write
D\phi=(D_{x_{1}}\phi,...,D_{x_{d}}\phi)
for the gradient of
\phi
and
D^{2}\phi=(D^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}\phi)_{i,j=1,...,d}
for the Hessian of
\phi
. For higher derivatives we use the following multi-index notation: for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{k})\in\{1,...,d\}^{k}
, we write
D^{\alpha}\phi=D_{x_{\alpha_{1}}}...D_{x_{\alpha_{k}}}\phi
. We write
|\alpha|=k
to indicate that
\alpha
is a multi-index of length
k
. We write
C^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for the space of functions
\phi:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
whose partial derivatives up to order
k
are continuous, and we endow
C^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with the norm

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{C^{k}}=\|\phi\|_{\infty}+\sum_{1\leq|\alpha|\leq k}\|D^{\alpha}\phi\|_{\infty},

with
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
being the usual
L^{\infty}
(essential supremum) norm.
Similarly, we write
W^{1,\infty}=W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
or
\text{Lip}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for the space of functions
\phi:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
which are Lipschitz continuous. Likewise, we define
W^{2,\infty}=W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
to be the space of function
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
with Lipschitz first derivatives, and endow these spaces with the norms

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{1,\infty}\coloneqq\|\phi\|_{\infty}+\|D\phi\|_{\infty},\quad\|\phi\|_{2,\infty}\coloneqq\|\phi\|_{\infty}+\|D\phi\|_{\infty}+\|D^{2}\phi\|_{\infty}.

We define
L^{2}=L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
to be the usual space of square-integrable functions on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, with the inner product

\displaystyle\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\phi\psi dx.

For
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
H^{s}=H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
to be the Hilbert space of functions whose derivatives up to order
s
are in
L^{2}
, and we endow
H^{s}
with the usual inner product

\displaystyle\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{s}=\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq s}\langle D^{\alpha}\phi,D^{\alpha}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}},

where if
|\alpha|=0
, we set
D^{\alpha}\phi=\phi
. We denote by
H^{-s}
the topological dual of
\phi
. We recall that
H^{-s}
inherits an inner product from
H^{s}
, which we denote
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{-s}
. For
\phi\in H^{s}
, we denote by
\phi^{*}
the dual element of
H^{-s}
, defined by the formula
\phi^{*}(\psi)=\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{s}
. Likewise, given
q\in H^{-s}
, we denote by
q^{*}
the dual element of
H^{s}
, which satisfies
\langle q^{*},\phi\rangle_{s}=q(\phi)
. We note that if
\psi
is smooth enough, then we can integrate by parts to get

\displaystyle\phi^{*}(\psi)=\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{s}=\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq s}\langle D^{\alpha}\phi,D^{\alpha}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq s}(-1)^{|\alpha|}\langle\phi,D^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}.

In particular, this means that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\phi^{*}=\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq s}(-1)^{|\alpha|}D^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}\phi

in a distributional sense, and in particular if
\phi\in C^{2s}\subset H^{s}
, then we have the bound

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|\phi^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq C\|\phi\|_{C^{2s}},\quad C=C(d,s).

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

We denote by

At times we will also endow

We say that a function
\Phi(m):\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
admits a linear derivative if there exists a continuous function
\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(m,x):\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Phi(m^{\prime})-\Phi(m)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}\big(tm+(1-t)m^{\prime},x\big)(m-m^{\prime})(dx)

for each
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, and

(2.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(m,x)dx=0,\quad m\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

We call
\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}
the linear derivative of
\Phi
. The linear derivative is uniquely defined when it exists, thanks to the normalization convention (
2.4
). If
\Phi
admits a linear derivative such that
x\mapsto\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(m,x)
is
C^{1}
for each fixed
x
, we define

\displaystyle D_{m}\Phi(m,x)=D_{x}\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(m,x).

We say that
\Phi
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
if
D_{m}\Phi:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
exists and is jointly continuous. Higher order derivatives can be defined by iteration, e.g.
D^{2}_{mm}\Phi(m,x,x^{\prime})=D_{m}\big[D_{m}\Phi(\cdot,x)\big](m,x^{\prime})
,
D_{xm}\Phi(m,x)=D_{x}\big[D_{m}\Phi(m,\cdot)\big](x)
. We say that
\Phi
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
if
D_{m}\Phi
,
D_{xm}\Phi,D^{2}_{mm}\Phi
exist and are continuous. We refer to
[
CD18
]
for more details about calculus on spaces of probability measures.

We note that, as in
[
DDJ24
]
, we will at time work with functions
\Phi:H^{-s}\to\mathbb{R}
, and we will make use of Lemma 2.11 and Proposition 2.12 of
[
DDJ24
]
, which point out some connections between analysis on
H^{-s}
and analysis on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. In particular, Lemma 2.11 therein states that if
s>d/2+1
and a function
\Phi:H^{-s}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
with respect to the Hilbertian structure on
H^{-s}
, then in fact the restriction of
\Phi
to
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
in the sense described above, and we have the formulas

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(m,x)=D_{-s}\Phi(m)(x)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}D_{-s}\Phi(m)(y)m(dy),

(2.6)

\displaystyle D_{m}\Phi(m,x)=D_{x}D_{-s}\Phi(m)(x)

for
m\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, where
D_{-s}\Phi:H^{-s}\to H^{s}
denotes the Frechet derivative of
\Phi
. The same arguments show that if
\Phi
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
with respect to
H^{-s}
for
s>d/2+2
, then in fact

\displaystyle(m,x)\ni\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(m,\cdot)\in C^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

is continuous, and in particular
D_{xm}\Phi(m,\cdot)
exists and

(2.7)

\displaystyle\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(H^{-s})\text{ for }s>d/2+2\implies\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\ni(m,x)\mapsto D_{xm}\Phi(m,x)\text{ is continuous.}

Proposition 2.12 of
[
DDJ24
]
, meanwhile, states that if
s>d/2+1
and
\Phi:H^{-s}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
with respect to
H^{-s}
, then there is a constant
C
depending only on
d
and
s
such that

(2.8)

\displaystyle|D_{m}\Phi(m,x)-D_{m}\Phi(m^{\prime},x)|\leq C\big[\Phi\big]_{C^{1,1}(H^{-s})}\bm{d}_{1}(m,m^{\prime})

for each
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and each
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, where
\big[\Phi\big]_{C^{1,1}(H^{-s})}
denotes the seminorm

\displaystyle\big[\Phi\big]_{C^{1,1}(H^{-s})}=\sup_{q,q^{\prime}\in H^{-s},q\neq q^{\prime}}\frac{|\nabla_{-s}\Phi(q)-\nabla_{-s}\Phi(q^{\prime})|}{\|q-q^{\prime}\|_{-s}},

and
\nabla_{-s}\Phi=(D_{-s}\Phi)^{*}
is the Hilbertian gradient of
\Phi
.

We close this section with a useful technical lemma. Its proof is an easy generalization of the argument leading to equation (5.29) in
[

]

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Phi,\Psi:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
, and assume that that
\Psi\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathcal{P}(T^{d}))
. Assume moreover that for some
m_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
satisfying
m_{0}\geq c\mathrm{Leb}
for some

, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(m_{0})-\Psi(m_{0})=\sup_{m\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\Big(\Phi(m)-\Psi(m)\Big).

Then,

(1)

If
\Phi
is Lipschitz with respect to
\bm{d}_{1}
, with Lipschitz constant
C_{\text{Lip}}(\Phi;\bm{d}_{1})
, then

\displaystyle\|D_{m}\Psi(m_{0})\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\text{Lip}}(\Phi;\bm{d}_{1}).

(2)

If

is Lipschitz with respect to

The aim of this subsection is to prove some regularity properties of

Proposition 3.1

Let Assumption

Proof.

We will write the proof in the case

Proof of (

\displaystyle|Y^{i}_{t_{0}}|^{2}

\displaystyle V^{N,\eta}(s,\bm{x})-V^{N,\eta}(t,\bm{x})=\mathbb{E}\big[V^{N,\eta}(s,\bm{X}_{s})\big]-V^{N,\eta}(t,\bm{x})+V^{N,\eta}(s,\bm{x})-\mathbb{E}\big[V^{N,\eta}(s,\bm{X}_{s})\big].

∎

3.4.
Some technical lemmas

We now give some technical lemmas about the control problems under consideration. We start with explaining how the bounds in Proposition

imply that we can restrict our attention to bounded controls for the limiting problem.

Lemma 3.2

Let Assumption
2.2

, any

\delta

Proof.

Let us fix

which take values in

like

, but with the infimum in (

\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{t_{0}}^{N}
with

taking values in

for each

remains uniformly bounded, independently of

for all

∎

[

Let Assumption

Proposition 3.6

Lemma 4.1
.

The function
\widehat{V}^{N,\eta}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1,2}
on

, and there is a constant

depending only on the data such that the following is satisfied in a classical sense:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\widehat{V}^{N,\eta}-\eta\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(D_{xm}\widehat{V}^{N,\eta}(t,m,x)\big)m(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(A(x)D_{xm}\widehat{V}^{N,\eta}(t,m,x)\big)m(dx)\vskip 2.84544pt\\
\displaystyle\quad-A_{0}\Delta_{\text{com}}\widehat{V}^{N,\eta}+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}H\big(x,D_{m}\widehat{V}^{N,\eta}(t,m,x)\big)m(dx)\leq F^{\eta}(m)+Cr_{N,d},\quad(t,m)\in[0,T)\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\vskip 2.84544pt\\

Proof.

The smoothness of

Lemma 5.5

First, assume that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},m\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\Big\{\Phi(z,m)-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\Big(|z-z_{0}|^{2}+\|m-m_{0}\|_{-s}^{2}\Big)\Big\}

satisfies the bound (
5.22
).
Because we are assuming that
U
is smooth, we have an explicit formula

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,\cdot)=\rho_{\delta}*\frac{\delta\widehat{U}}{\delta m}(t,z,m*\rho_{\delta},\cdot),

from which we can deduce the bound

(5.24)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,\cdot)\right\|_{C^{s}}\leq C(s)\delta^{s-1}

for each
s>1
. Now, we observe that by the semiconcavity bound on
\widehat{U}^{\delta}
from Lemma
5.6
, we can choose
C
large enough that if
\epsilon<\frac{1}{C}\delta^{2s}
, then the map

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\ni(z,m)\mapsto\Phi(z,m)-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\big(|z-z_{0}|^{2}+|m-m_{0}|^{2}\big)

is strictly concave (with respect to the Hilbertain structure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times H^{-s}
), which means that the optimization problem

(5.25)

\displaystyle\sup_{z,m}\Big\{\Phi(z,m)-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\big(|z-z_{0}|^{2}+\|m-m_{0}\|_{-s}^{2}\big)\Big\}

admits exactly one solution. Moreover, again by strict concavity, if the system of first-order conditions

(5.26)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}z-z_{0}=\epsilon D_{z}\Phi(z,m),\\
m-m_{0}=\epsilon\Big(\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,\cdot)\Big)^{*},\end{cases}

admits a solution
(z_{\epsilon},m_{\epsilon})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, then
(z_{\epsilon},m_{\epsilon})
must be the unique optimizer for the optimization problem (
5.25
) (see Step 3 in the proof of
[
DDJ24
, Proposition 4.4]
for more details). We note that here
\big(\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,\cdot)\big)^{*}\in H^{-s^{*}}
is the dual of
\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,\cdot)\in H^{s^{*}}
.

We now argue that if
m_{0}
satisfies the lower bound from the statement of the lemma, then we can in fact solve (
5.26
). For this, we define a map

\displaystyle\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times H^{-s},\quad\Psi(z,m)=\Big(z_{0}+\epsilon D_{z}\Phi(z,m),\,m_{0}+\epsilon\Big(\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,\cdot)\Big)^{*}\Big).

First, notice that by convention

\displaystyle\langle\Big(\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,\cdot)\Big)^{*},1\rangle_{-s,s}=\int\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,x)dx=0,

and moreover using (
5.24
) and (
2.2
), we have

(5.27)

\displaystyle\left\|\Big(\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,\cdot)\Big)^{*}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\left\|\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,\cdot)\right\|_{C^{2s^{*}}}\leq C\delta^{2s^{*}-1}.

Thus, taking
C
larger if necessary, we find that if
m_{0}\geq C\epsilon\delta^{-(2s^{*}-1)}
, then in fact
m_{0}+\epsilon\big(\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z,m,\cdot)\big)^{*}
is a positive measure with total mass 1, i.e. a probability measure, so that

\displaystyle\Psi(z,m)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad\text{for each $(z,m)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$}.

Because we have assumed that
U
, hence
\Phi
, is smooth, we see that
\Psi
is continuous, and so by Schauder’s fixed point theorem it has a fixed point
(z_{\epsilon},m_{\epsilon})
, which by (
5.27
) must satisfy

(5.28)

\displaystyle\|m_{\epsilon}-m_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\epsilon\delta^{-(2s^{*}-1)}.

Since by design
(z_{\epsilon},m_{\epsilon})
solves the system (
5.26
), we conclude by our earlier discussion that
(z_{\epsilon},m_{\epsilon})
is the unique optimizer for the problem (
5.25
). Moreover, we have the identity

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(z_{\epsilon},m_{\epsilon},\cdot)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}(m_{\epsilon}-m_{0})^{*},

from which we infer

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{\epsilon}(m_{\epsilon}-m_{0})^{*}\right\|_{1,\infty}\leq C_{\text{Lip}}(\Phi;W^{-1,\infty})\leq C_{\text{Lip}}(U;W^{-1,\infty}),

(5.29)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{\epsilon}(m_{\epsilon}-m_{0})^{*}\right\|_{2,\infty}\leq C_{\text{Lip}}(\Phi;W^{2,\infty})\leq C_{\text{Lip}}(U;W^{-2,\infty}),

which yields (
5.23
). This completes the proof.
∎

is a subsolution to the relevant PDE.

Lemma 5.12
.

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0})-A_{0}\Delta_{z}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(\widehat{A}(x_{0},z)D_{xm}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0},x)\big)m_{0}(dx)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\widehat{H}\big(x,z_{0},D_{m}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0},x)\big)m_{0}(dx)\leq\widehat{F}(z_{0},m_{0})+C(1+C_{\text{Lip}}(U;W^{-2,\infty}))\big(\delta+\epsilon\delta^{-(2s^{*}-1)}\big).

If
A
is constant, then the dependence on

can be removed, i.e. the right-hand side of (

C\big(\delta+\epsilon\delta^{-(2s^{*}-1)}\big)
.

Proof.

We let
C
be large enough that the conclusion of Lemma
5.11
holds, and we suppose that a
\Phi
touches
\widehat{U}^{\delta,\epsilon}
from above at a point

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},m\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\bigg\{\widehat{U}^{\delta}(t_{0},z,m)-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big(|z-z_{0}|^{2}+\|m-m_{0}\|_{-s^{*}}^{2}\big)\bigg\}

is achieved. Standard arguments from the theory of viscosity solution show that the function
\Psi(t,z,m)=\Phi(t,z_{0}-z_{\delta,\epsilon}+z,m_{0}-m_{\delta,\epsilon}+m)
(here we use that
\Phi
is defined on
H^{-s}
) touches
\widehat{U}^{\delta}
from above at
(t_{0},z_{\delta,\epsilon},m_{\delta,\epsilon})

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0})-A_{0}\Delta_{z}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(\widehat{A}(x,z_{\delta,\epsilon})D_{xm}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0}))m_{\delta,\epsilon}(dx)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\widehat{H}\Big(x,z_{\delta,\epsilon},D_{m}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0},x)\Big)m_{\delta,\epsilon}(dx)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}\Psi(t_{0},z_{\delta,\epsilon},m_{\delta,\epsilon})-A_{0}\Delta_{z}\Psi(t_{0},z_{\delta,\epsilon},m_{\delta,\epsilon})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(\widehat{A}(x,z_{\delta,\epsilon})D_{xm}\Psi(t_{0},z_{\delta,\epsilon},m_{\delta,\epsilon}))m_{\delta,\epsilon}(dx)

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0})-A_{0}\Delta_{z}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0})-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(\widehat{A}(x,z_{0})D_{xm}\Phi(t_{0},z_{0},m_{0}))m_{0}(dx)

\displaystyle\qquad+I+II+III,

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(\widehat{A}(x,z_{\delta,\epsilon})D_{xm}\Phi(t_{0},m_{0},z_{0},x)\big)m_{\delta,\epsilon}(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(\widehat{A}(x,z_{0})D_{xm}\Phi(t_{0},m_{0},z_{0},x)\big)m_{0}(dx),

, Lemmas
5.10
and
5.11
to get

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\leq C\|D_{xm}\Phi(t_{0},m_{0},z_{0},\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\big(|z_{\delta,\epsilon}-z_{0}|+\|m_{\delta,\epsilon}-m_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\big)\leq C\epsilon\delta^{-(2s^{*}-1)}(1+\text{Lip}(U;W^{-2,\infty})).

and the fact that

by Lemma

to find a constant

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon\delta^{-(2s^{*}-1)}.

5.10
and
5.11
easily give

By combining (

), (

), (
5.33
) and (

), we obtain (
5.30
). To see the improvement when

is constant, we use the representation of

from Lemma
5.10
to see that

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(AD^{2}_{xx}\frac{\delta\Phi}{\delta m}(t_{0},m_{0},z_{0},x)\big)(m_{\delta,\epsilon}-m_{0})(dx)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\operatorname{tr}\big(AD^{2}_{xx}(m_{\delta,\epsilon}-m_{0})^{*}\big)(m_{\delta,\epsilon}-m_{0})(dx)

6.2.

In this section we show how to sharpen the rates of Theorem

On the other hand, since we will not rely on the sup-convolution operation, there is no additional difficulty in taking the state space to be

(absolutely continuous curves with values in

\partial_{t}m_{t}+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{t}m_{t})=0,\quad\mbox{ in }(t_{0},T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad m(t_{0})=m_{0}.

We still call this set of controls

Lemma 6.2

Under Assumption

Proof.

Given a couple

(with

therein)

2

6.2.1.
Hopf-Lax formula and first convergence result.

We can express the value functions in terms of Hopf-Lax transform of the terminal cost.

Lemma 6.3
.

Assume that
G:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is bounded from below. For every
(t,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(6.4)

U(t,m)=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\bigl\{G(\nu)+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m,\nu)\bigr\},

where
\bm{d}_{2}
is the
2
-Wasserstein distance. Similarly, for every
(t,{\bm{x}})\in[0,T]\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
,

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})=\inf_{{\bm{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}\bigl\{G^{N}({\bm{y}})+\frac{1}{2(T-t)N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|x^{i}-y^{i}|^{2}\bigr\},

where
G^{N}({\bm{x}}):=G(m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}}).

Proof.

These results are well-known (see for instance
[
Lio
]
for the first statement) and we omit the proof, noting only that the first statement is a consequence of the Benamou-Brenier formulation of optimal transport with quadratic cost.
∎

For every
{\bm{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
we can permute the coordinates without changing the value of
G^{N}({\bm{y}})
. Therefore we immediately see that

(6.5)

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})=\inf_{{\bm{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}\bigl\{G^{N}({\bm{y}})+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N})\bigr\},

and we deduce the following result.

Proposition 6.4
.

Assume that
G:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is bounded from below. For every
(t,{\bm{x}})\in[0,T]\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
it holds

(6.6)

U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\leq V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}).

Assume as well that
G
satisfies, for some
C_{G}>0
,

(6.7)

\bigl|G(m^{2})-G(m^{1})\bigr|\leq C_{G}\bigl(1+M_{2}(m^{2})+M_{2}(m^{1})\bigr)\bm{d}_{2}(m^{1},m^{2}),\quad\forall m^{1},m^{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where we use the notation
M_{2}(\nu):=\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}d\nu(x)\Bigr)^{1/2}
. Then, for every
(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})\in[0,T]\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
such that
m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}^{N}}\rightarrow m
in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
it holds

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})=U(t,m).

Proof.

The first part of the statement follows directly from the representation formula (
6.4
) and (
6.5
). For the second part we fix
(t,m)\in[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For some
\epsilon>0
we let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that

U(t,m)\geq G(\nu)+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(\nu,m)-\epsilon.

Now we take a sequence
({\bm{y}}^{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
such that

\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}\bm{d}_{2}(m^{N}_{{\bm{y}}^{N}},\nu)=0.

Given the representation formula (
6.5
) for
V^{N}
we have

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})\leq G(m_{{\bm{y}}^{N}}^{N})+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{y}}^{N}}^{N},m_{{\bm{x}}^{N}}^{N}).

Therefore, by continuity of
G
we have

\limsup_{N\rightarrow+\infty}V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})\leq G(\nu)+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(\nu,m)\leq U(t,m)+\epsilon.

Since this is valid for any
\epsilon>0
we have

(6.8)

\limsup_{N\rightarrow+\infty}V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})\leq U(t,m).

Notice that this inequality only requires the continuity of
G
and not the quantitative regularity condition (
6.7
). On the other hand, condition (
6.7
) ensures that
U(t,\cdot)
is continuous over
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for every
t\in[0,T]
, see
[
Cal18
]
Lemma 8.5 for the relevant property of the inf-convolution. Since, for all
N\geq 1
we have

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})\geq U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}^{N}}^{N})

we can deduce from the continuity of
U(t,\cdot)
that

\liminf_{N\rightarrow+\infty}V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})\geq U(t,m)

which concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

6.2.2.
On the choice of admissible controls

In this setting with degenerate idiosyncratic noise, the set of admissible controls plays an important role. To wit, define
U^{f}
(
f
standing for feedback), the feedback value function by

U^{f}(t_{0},m_{0})=\inf_{\alpha\in L^{1}_{t}(Lip_{x})}\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\alpha_{t}(x)|^{2}dm_{t}(x)dt+G(m_{T}),

where the infimum is taken over the feedback controls in
L^{1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
which are Lipschitz in space and
(m_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solves

\partial_{t}m_{t}+\operatorname{div}(\alpha_{t}m_{t})=0,\mbox{ in }(t_{0},T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad m_{t_{0}}=m_{0}.

Notice that this is the type of controls considered in
[
FS14
]
. Define accordingly
V^{f,N}
to be the infimum of the
N
-particle problem, when the controls take the form

\alpha_{t}^{i}=\alpha^{i}(t,X_{t}^{1},\dots,X_{t}^{N})

for some Lipschitz maps
\alpha^{i}(t,\cdot).

Now take
{\bm{x}}_{0}=(x_{0}^{1},\dots,x_{0}^{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
and
\alpha_{t}(\cdot)
an admissible control for
U^{f}(t_{0},m_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}^{n})
with corresponding trajectory
(m_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
. Given the regularity of the control, we easily check that

m_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}},

where, for all
1\leq i\leq N
,
X_{t}^{i}
is the solution to the ODE

dX_{t}^{i}=\alpha_{t}(X_{t}^{i})dt,\quad X_{t_{0}}^{i}=x_{0}^{i}

and the corresponding cost is

\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\alpha_{t}(X_{t}^{i})|^{2}dt+G\bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{T}^{i}}\bigr).

We can give the same control
\alpha
to each particle in the
N
-particle control problem and, therefore, we have the inequality

(6.9)

V^{f,N}(t_{0},{\bm{x}}_{0})\leq U^{f}(t_{0},m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}),

giving the opposite inequality of Proposition
6.4
.

Notice that in general
U^{f}(t_{0},m_{0})\neq U(t_{0},m_{0})
, as shown in Corollary
6.9
.

6.2.3.
Convergence rate

Although one inequality is clear, we would like to find a bound for the other side, i.e. we look for a bound of the form

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})\leq U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{n})+\epsilon_{n}

for some
\epsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0.
For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define the following error

\epsilon_{N}(\nu):=\inf_{{\bm{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},\nu).

Lemma 6.5
.

Assume that
G
is
\bm{d}_{1}
-Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{G}
. Then, for all
(t,{\bm{x}})\in[0,T)\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
there is
\overline{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(6.10)

G(\overline{\nu})+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})=U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})=\min_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}G(\nu)+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},\nu),

and every such
\overline{\nu}
satisfies

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})\leq U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})+4L_{G}\epsilon_{N}(\overline{\nu}).

Proof of Lemma
6.5
.

Let us fix
(t,{\bm{x}})\in[t_{0},T)\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
for some
N\geq 1
. We first justify that there exists
\overline{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that (
6.10
) holds. Since
\nu\mapsto G(\nu)
is globally
\bm{d}_{1}
-Lipschitz continuous it has at most linear growth in
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|d\nu(x)
and therefore
\nu\mapsto G(\nu)+(2(T-t))^{-1}d_{2}^{2}(m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}},\nu)
is bounded from below. Let
(\nu^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a minimizing sequence. For the same reason, we easily have that
(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}d\nu^{n}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded.
As a consequence, the sequence admits a converging sub-sequence (still denoted
(\nu^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
) converging in
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to some
\overline{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Since
G
is
d_{1}
-Lipschitz continuous and
(\mu^{1},\mu^{2})\mapsto\bm{d}_{2}(\mu^{1},\mu^{2})
is weakly lower-semi-continuous (when
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is endowed with the usual topology of weak convergence of probability measures, i.e., convergence holds against bounded continuous functions) we deduce that
\overline{\nu}
is indeed a minimum of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni\nu\mapsto G(\nu)+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},\nu)
.

Let take such an element
\overline{\nu}
of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying (
6.10
). Using the Hopf-Lax formula for
V^{N}
and
U
we have, for every
{\bm{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N},

\displaystyle V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})-U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})

\displaystyle\leq G(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N})-G(\overline{\nu})+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}},m^{N}_{{\bm{y}}})-\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{n},\overline{\nu})

\displaystyle\leq L_{G}\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\left[\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{n},m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N})+\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})\right]\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})

\displaystyle\leq L_{G}\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\left[2\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})+\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})\right]\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},\overline{\nu}),

where we used the
\bm{d}_{2}
-Lipschitz regularity of
G
(which is clearly inherited by its
\bm{d}_{1}
-Lipschitz regularity) as well as the triangular inequality.
By definition of
\overline{\nu}
we have

G(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{n})\geq G(\overline{\nu})+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},\overline{\nu}),

which leads, in view of the Lipschitz regularity of
G
, to

(6.11)

\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})\leq G(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})-G(\overline{\nu})\leq L_{G}\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})

and therefore, it holds

(6.12)

\bm{d}_{2}(m_{x}^{N},\overline{\nu})\leq 2L_{G}(T-t).

In particular, this implies that
M_{2}(\overline{\nu})\leq M_{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})+2L_{G}(T-t).

As a consequence, we have

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})-U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\leq\Bigl(3L_{G}+\frac{\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})}{2(T-t)}\Bigr)\bm{d}_{2}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},\overline{\nu}).

Taking the infimum over
{\bm{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
we get, by definition of
\epsilon_{N}
,

(6.13)

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})-U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\leq\Bigl(3L_{G}+\frac{\epsilon_{N}(\overline{\nu})}{2(T-t)}\Bigr)\epsilon_{N}(\overline{\nu}).

On the other hand, using first the representation formula for
V^{N}
and then the definition of
\overline{\nu}
we have

\displaystyle V^{N}(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})-U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})

\displaystyle\leq G(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})-U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})

\displaystyle\leq G(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})-G(\overline{\nu}).

Recalling (
6.11
) and (
6.12
) this leads to

(6.14)

V^{N}(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\leq U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})+2L^{2}_{G}(T-t).

Now we distinguish between the values of
t\in[0,T]
. If
2L_{G}(T-t)\geq\epsilon_{N}(\overline{\nu})
we use estimate (
6.13
) and otherwise we use estimate (
6.14
). In both cases we find

V^{N}(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\leq U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})+4L_{G}\epsilon_{N}(\overline{\nu}).

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Assume that
G
is
\bm{d}_{1}
-Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
L_{G}
. Take
(t,{\bm{x}})\in[0,T]\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
and
\overline{\nu}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying (
6.10
). Then for any
p\geq 5
,

M_{p}(\overline{\nu})\leq L_{G}(T-t)+M_{p}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})

where
M_{p}(\nu):=\bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{p}d\nu(x)\bigr)^{1/p}
.

Proof.

Step 1.

We fix
(t,{\bm{x}})\in[0,T)\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
and
\overline{\nu}
and element of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying (
6.10
). Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be an atomless probability space and
(\overline{Y},X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})
a couple of random variables in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\mathcal{L}\bigl((\overline{Y},X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\bigr)
is an optimal transport plan between
\overline{\nu}
and
m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}
. Then, for any
Y\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have, denoting
\overline{G}
the lift of
G
to
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\overline{G}(Y)+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|X_{x}^{N}-Y|^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\geq G(\mathcal{L}(Y))+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},\mathcal{L}(Y))

\displaystyle\geq G(\overline{\nu})+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},\overline{\nu})

\displaystyle=\overline{G}(\overline{Y})+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}-\overline{Y}|^{2}\bigr]

where we first used the definition of
\bm{d}_{2}
as infimum over couplings, next the definition of
\overline{\nu}
as a minimum in (
6.10
) and finally the fact that
\mathcal{L}((X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N},Y))
is an optimal coupling between
m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}
and
\overline{\nu}
. This shows that
\overline{Y}
is a minimum, over
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
of

Y\mapsto\overline{G}(Y)+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}-Y|^{2}\bigr].

Step 2.
The map
\overline{G}
is obviously Lipschitz continuous with respect to the
L^{1}
-metric over
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
with constant
L_{G}
. We claim that
\overline{Y}-X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}
belongs to
L^{\infty}
and

(6.15)

\left\|\overline{Y}-X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq L_{G}(T-t).

Indeed, for any
Y\in L^{2}
we have, by minimaliy of
\overline{Y}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}-\overline{Y}|^{2}\bigr]-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}-Y|^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq(T-t)\bigl(\overline{G}(Y)-\overline{G}(\overline{Y}))

\displaystyle\leq L_{G}(T-t)\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Y-\overline{Y}|].

And therefore, for all
Y\in L^{2}
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\overline{Y}-X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\cdot(\overline{Y}-Y)\bigr]\leq L_{G}(T-t)\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Y-\overline{Y}|\bigr]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Y-\overline{Y}|^{2}\bigr].

For fixed
Y
and any
\epsilon>0

and dividing by
\epsilon

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\overline{Y}-X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\cdot(\overline{Y}-Y)\bigr]\leq L_{G}(T-t)\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Y-\overline{Y}|\bigr]+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|Y-\overline{Y}|^{2}\bigr].

Letting

This shows that

belongs to

and (

\displaystyle M_{p}(\overline{\nu})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\overline{Y}|^{p}\bigr]^{1/p}\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\overline{Y}-X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}|^{p}\bigr]^{1/p}+\mathbb{E}\bigl[|X_{{\bm{x}}}^{N}|^{p}\bigr]^{1/p}

which concludes the proof of the Lemma.

-Lipschitz with respect to

Now we exploit results on the optimal quantization of the Lebesgue measure over the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
to prove that the bound is optimal.

Proposition 6.8

(Optimality of the bound)
.

We take
G(m):=\bm{d}_{1}(m,\mathrm{Leb}|_{[0,1]^{d}})
. To simplify we take
t_{0}=T-1/2
. There exists
c_{d}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all

{\bm{x}}_{N}\in([0,1]^{d})^{N}
such that

V^{N}(t_{0},{\bm{x}}^{N})-U(t_{0},m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}^{N}})\geq c_{d}N^{-1/d}.

Proof.

The value functions read

(6.18)

U(t_{0},m)=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\bigl\{\bm{d}_{1}(\nu,\mathrm{Leb}|_{[0,1]^{d}})+\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m,\nu)\bigr\},

(6.19)

V^{N}(t_{0},{\bm{x}})=\inf_{{\bm{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}\bigl\{\bm{d}_{1}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},\mathrm{Leb}|_{[0,1]^{d}})+\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\bigr\}.

On the one hand, by (
6.18
) we have, for all
m\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

U(t_{0},m)\leq\bm{d}_{2}^{2}(m,\mathrm{Leb}|_{[0,1]^{d}}).

By partitioning the unit cube into
N
cubes of length roughly equal to

and putting a point at the center of each of this cube we can easily show that, for some
c_{1}>0
depending only on the dimension
d
,

\forall N\in\mathbb{N},\exists{\bm{x}}^{N}\in([0,1]^{d})^{N},\quad\bm{d}_{2}(m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}^{N}},\mathrm{Leb}|_{[0,1]^{d}})^{2}\leq c_{1}N^{-2d}.

And therefore, for every
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, for the same constant
c_{1}
and the same point
{\bm{x}}^{N}\in([0,1]^{d})^{N}
we have

(6.20)

U(t_{0},m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}^{N}})\leq c_{1}N^{-2/d}.

On the other hand, by (
6.19
), we have

(6.21)

V^{N}(t_{0},{\bm{x}})\geq\inf_{{\bm{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}\bm{d}_{1}(m_{{\bm{y}}}^{N},\mathrm{Leb}|_{[0,1]^{d}}),\quad\forall{\bm{x}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}.

Now, let us denote
v_{N}:=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{1,N}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}d_{1}(\mathrm{Leb}|_{[0,1]^{d}},\mu)
where the infimum is taken over the probability measures
\mu
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a support containing at most
N
points. It is obvious that

(6.22)

\inf_{{\bm{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}\bm{d}_{1}\bigl(m^{N}_{{\bm{y}}},\mathrm{Leb}|_{[0,1]^{d}})\geq v_{N}.

Now, combining Lemma 3.5 in
[
GL00
]
(that gives an equivalent definition of
v_{N}
) and Theorem 6.2 in the same reference we get

\inf_{N\geq 1}N^{1/d}v_{N}>0.

Together with (
6.22
) and (
6.21
) this implies that there is
c_{2}>0
such that, for every
N\geq 1
and every
{\bm{x}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}

(6.23)

V^{N}(t_{0},{\bm{x}})\geq c_{2}N^{-1/d}.

To conlcude the proof of the Proposition it is enough to combined the upper bound (
6.20
) and the lower bound (
6.23
).
∎

We note that the proof that
U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})\leq V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}})
holds equally well in the present setting, and so as a consequence of Proposition
6.8
we get the following.

Corollary 6.9
.

In the setting of the above Proposition, there exists
(t_{0},m_{0})\in[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
U(t_{0},m_{0})<U^{f}(t_{0},m_{0})
.

Proof.

Applying (
6.6
) and (
6.9
) and exploiting the inclusion of the sets of controls, we have for any
t_{0}
,
N
and
\bm{x}

U(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}})\leq V^{N}(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}})\leq V^{N,f}(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}})\leq U^{f}(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}}).

Thus if, by contradiction,
U^{f}(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}})\leq U(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}})
, then we would get
U(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}})=V^{N}(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}})
which is in contrast with Proposition
6.8
.
∎

Similarly, denoting by
\widetilde{U}(t_{0},m_{0})
the infimum over controls
\widehat{\alpha}(t,\xi)
that are measurable functions of time and the initial condition only, we have the following:

Corollary 6.10
.

In the setting of the above Proposition, there exists
(t_{0},m_{0})\in[0,T]\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
U(t_{0},m_{0})<\widetilde{U}(t_{0},m_{0})
.

Proof.

We show that

V^{N}(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}})\leq\widetilde{U}(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}})
for any
t_{0}\in[0,T]
and
\bm{x}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, so that the conclusion follows as in the previous corollary.
Consider a control for the mean field problem
\alpha_{t}=\widehat{\alpha}(t,\xi)
, with corresponding process
X
, for an initial condition

\xi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}1_{\xi=x_{i}}
with law
m^{N}_{\bm{x}}
. For the
N
players, starting at
\bm{X}_{t_{0}}=\bm{x}
, define the controls
\alpha^{i}_{t}=\widehat{\alpha}(t,x_{i})
. Thus we have
\alpha_{t}=\sum_{i}\alpha^{i}_{t}1_{\xi=x_{i}}
and then
X_{t}=\sum_{i}X^{i}_{t}1_{\xi=x^{i}}
, so that
\mathcal{L}(X_{t})=m^{N}_{\bm{X}_{t}}
and we conclude
V^{N}(t_{0},\bm{x})\leq\widetilde{U}(t_{0},m^{N}_{\bm{x}})
.
∎

We remark that in the case of finite dimensional control, without mean-field interaction in the cost, all the formulations are equivalent, basically because the cost becomes a linear functional of the law of the process:
U=U^{f}=\widetilde{U}
.

6.2.4.
Convex Case.

Under some conditions on
G
of convexity type we can actually prove equality of the value functions.

Proposition 6.11
.

Assume that
G:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is bounded from below, continuous and satisfies, for any probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
,

(6.24)

G\bigl(\mathcal{L}\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)\bigr)\leq G\bigl(\mathcal{L}(X)\bigr)\quad\forall X,Y\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Then, for all
t\in[0,T]
and all
{\bm{x}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
, we have
V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})=U(t,m_{{\bm{x}}}^{N})
.

Notice that the condition on
G
is satisfied, for instance, if the lift of
G
to any
L^{2}
space of random variables over an atomless probability space is convex, as a consequence of Jensen’s inequality, see Lemma
6.12
below.

Proof.

Thanks to Proposition
6.4
, it suffices to prove the inequality
V^{N}(t_{0},{\bm{x}}^{N})\leq U(t_{0},m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}})
. We are going to show that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and all
{\bm{x}}^{N}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
we have

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})\leq V^{nN}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N},\dots,{\bm{x}}^{N}),

where
{\bm{x}}^{N}
is repeated
n
times in the argument of
V^{nN}
. Let
(y_{i}^{j})_{1\leq i\leq N,1\leq j\leq n}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{nN}
.
On some probability space, we build two independent random variables
I
and
J
uniformly distributed respectively on
\{1,\dots,N\}
and
\{1,\dots,n\}
and we let
Y:=y^{I}_{J}
. We check that

\mathcal{L}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\Bigl[Y|I\Bigr]\Bigr)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{i}^{j}}

and so, by assumption on
G
,

G\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{i}^{j}}\Bigr)\leq G\Bigl(\frac{1}{nN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{y_{i}^{j}}\Bigr).

On the other hand, by convexity of
x\mapsto|x|^{2}
, we have

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{i}^{j}-x_{i}\bigr|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{nN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl|y_{i}^{j}-x_{i}\bigr|^{2}.

Combined together we get, for all
(y_{i}^{j})_{1\leq i\leq N,1\leq j\leq n}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{nN}
,

\displaystyle G\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{i}^{j}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{i}^{j}-x_{i}\bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq G\Bigl(\frac{1}{nN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{y_{i}^{j}}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\frac{1}{nN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl|y_{i}^{j}-x_{i}\bigr|^{2}.

Since the left-hand side is always greater than
V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})
we get, taking the infimum over
{\bm{y}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{nN}
,

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})\leq V^{nN}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N},\dots,{\bm{x}}^{N}).

Since
G
is continuous we have, see (
6.8
),

\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}V^{nN}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N},\dots,{\bm{x}}^{N})\leq U(t,m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}^{N}})

and we can deduce that, for all
N\geq 1
,

V^{N}(t,{\bm{x}}^{N})\leq U(t,m^{N}_{{\bm{x}}}),\quad\forall t\in[0,T],\forall{\bm{x}}^{N}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N},

which concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

We say that the map
G:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
L
-convex if, for any atomless probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
its lift
\overline{G}
, defined, for all
X\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
\overline{G}(X)=G\bigl(\mathcal{L}(X)\bigr)
, is convex in the usual sense.

Lemma 6.12
.

Assume that
G:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
L
-convex function, then it satisfies the condition (
6.24
) for any probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F};\mathbb{P})
.

Proof.

For some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, let
X,Y\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
be two random variables. By Corollary 3.22 in
[
GT19
]
we can find an element of the sub-differential of
\overline{G}
at
\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]
(with
\mathbb{E}
denoting the expectation under
\mathbb{P}
) of the form
\xi\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)
for some measurable map
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is square-integrable with respect to
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr])
. By definition of the sub-differential of a convex function we have

(6.25)

\overline{G}\bigl(X\bigr)-\overline{G}\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)\geq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\xi\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)\cdot\bigl(X-\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)\Bigr].

However, by properties of the conditional expectation we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\xi\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)\cdot\bigl(X-\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\xi\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)\cdot\bigl(X-\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)\Big|Y\Bigr]\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\xi\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)\cdot\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]-\mathbb{E}\bigl[X|Y\bigr]\bigr)\Bigr]\Bigr]=0.

Getting back to (
6.25
) and recalling the definition of
\overline{G}
concludes the proof of the statement.
∎
