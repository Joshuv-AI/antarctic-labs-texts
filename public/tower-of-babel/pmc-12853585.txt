Comprehensive species sampling reveals pervasive pseudogenization and recurrent complete loss of plastid ndh genes in the genus Cymbidium (Orchidaceae)
Authors: Hai-Yao Chen, Li-Qiong Chen, Xin Yao, Zhi-Rong Zhang, Ji-Dong Ya, Xiao-Hua Jin, De-Zhu Li, Jun-Bo Yang, Wen-Bin Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlastome evolution in plants with diverse nutritional strategies provides unique insights into genomic-level adaptive processes. The genus Cymbidium (Orchidaceae), characterized by its heterogeneous life forms and transitions toward mycoheterotrophy, serves as an exemplary natural system for investigating chronospatial dynamics between ecological adaptation and plastomic evolutionary trajectories.ResultsIn this study, plastomes and nrDNA datasets of 56 Cymbidium species (representing about 90% of all recognized species, encompassing 38 epiphytes and 18 terrestrials) were analyzed using phylogenomic, ancestral state reconstruction, and selection pressure tools (RELAX, aBSREL, BUSTED) to assess plastome structural variations, ndh pseudogenization, and evolutionary dynamics. Four inverted repeat (IR) boundary types were identified, with recurrent contractions linked to the ndhF gene loss, especially in terrestrial species (Type I/II). The ndh genes were functional in the ancestral state but underwent progressive degradation across both epiphytic (Clade II) and terrestrial (Clade III) lineages. The leafless Cymbidium macrorhizon exhibited delayed plastome degradation (retained 4 ndh genes) despite holomycoheterotrophy, reflecting recent divergence (~ 2.5 Ma). Evolutionary selection analyses indicated the relaxed selection on certain genes and intensified selection on others, particularly within the ndh gene group. Phylogenetic incongruence was found between the plastome and nrDNA datasets, yet ancestral reconstructions remained consistent robustness.ConclusionsBoth epiphytic and terrestrial species experienced independent ndh degradation, likely reflecting adaptive responses to different ecological environments, not life form alone. Temporal decoupling of morphological-genomic evolution highlights plastome degradation lag in recent heterotrophic transitions. Integrating incongruent phylogenies strengthens evolutionary inferences, revealing conserved degradation trajectories across lineages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07945-1.Keywords: Cymbidium, Mycoheterotrophy, NA(D)H dehydrogenase-like complex, Plastome evolution, Pseudogenization

Background

Chloroplasts are indispensable organelles for photosynthesis in autotrophic plants, and their genomes (plastomes) exhibit remarkable evolutionary conservatism in size, structural organization, and gene contents across photosynthetic lineages [1, 2]. Plastomes are characterized by a conserved quadripartite genomic architecture, comprising a large single-copy (LSC) region, a small single-copy (SSC) region, and two inverted repeat (IR) regions. A typical plastome encodes approximately 100–120 unique genes, including 70–88 protein-coding sequence (CDS) genes, and 30–35 structural RNA genes [1, 2]. In contrast, plastomes of heterotrophic plants often exhibit marked deviations from this conserved plastomic structure and gene contents, having undergone dramatic structural degradation and gene reduction following their nutritional transition from autotrophy to heterotrophy [3]. These changes are driven by relaxed selective pressures and manifest in various forms, including structural reconfiguration, IR boundary fluctuations, and in extreme cases, complete loss of specific genomic regions. This evolutionary trajectory has been well documented across numerous lineages [3–8], particularly in parasitic plants such as Cuscuta L. [2]. and Orobanchaceae [3, 4].

Orchidaceae, the second largest plant family, forms obligate and specialized symbiotic relationships with fungi. This critical interdependence originates from the initial phases of seed germination. Almost all extant orchid species (∼20,000 spp.) produce powdery, endosperm-lacking seeds that rely completely on fungal partners providing nutrition for germination [9], an adaptive strategy formally described as initial mycoheterotrophy [10]. Following germination, orchids have developed three distinct nutrition strategies of fungal dependency. Some species transition to full autotrophy while retaining rudimentary fungal associations. Others transition to mixotrophy, simultaneously acquiring carbon through both autotrophic and mycoheterotrophic pathways [10]. This dual nutritional strategy is now recognized to be widespread across the family [11]. The most extreme dependency is exemplified by holomycoheterotrophic orchids, which have completely lost their photosynthetic capability and rely entirely on fungi for carbon and nutrition. This kind of orchid is characterized by the absence of visible green tissue in general [10]. Current research suggests that holomycoheterotrophic orchids have evolved from mixotrophic ancestors, with mixotrophy serving as pre-adaptations for the evolution of holomycoheterotrophy [12, 13].

Comparative plastomics of mycoheterotrophic orchids has revealed convergent genome degradation trajectories paralleling those in parasitic plants [5]. The evolutionary transition from autotrophy to holomycoheterotrophy coincides with the reduction of both photosynthetic and housekeeping plastid genes [5, 14, 15]. This plastomic degradation generally initiates with pseudogenization and subsequent deletion of NA(D)H dehydrogenase genes [5, 7, 14, 15], which encode the NDH complex comprising eleven plastid-encoded subunits (ndhA-ndhK). As a thylakoid membrane-embedded apparatus, this complex is functional for photosynthetic electron transport, ATP generation and stress tolerance [16–18]. Furthermore, plastome structural variations frequently correlate with ndh gene reduction, with evidence in the co-occurrence of ndhF gene loss and structural reconfiguration at IR/SSC boundaries [19–21]. Within Orchidaceae, pseudogenization and loss of ndh genes are found in four of the five recognized subfamilies [19, 22], suggesting repeated evolutionary processes of plastome degeneration across the family [23]. Intriguingly, recent studies have revealed a temporal disconnect between morphological and genomic degradation in mycoheterotrophic orchids [14, 24, 25]. Some species, which exhibit clear morphological adaptations to heterotrophy, such as leaflessness, maintain relatively intact plastomes with degradation primarily limited to ndh genes [14, 24–26]. These findings highlight a critical knowledge gap in our understanding of the temporal dynamic between morphological adaptations and plastome degradation during the evolutionary transition to mycoheterotrophy.

The genus Cymbidium Sw. (Orchidaceae), comprising around 60 well-recognized species distributed across tropical and subtropical Asia to northeastern Australia [27], serves as an exemplary natural system for investigating the chronospatial dynamics between ecological adaptation and plastome evolution during the transition to mycoheterotrophy. This genus exhibits diverse nutritional strategies and life forms, following an evolutionary trajectory from epiphytic-leafy-autotrophic to terrestrial-leafless-holomycoheterotrophic forms, with terrestrial-leafy-mixotrophic species representing intermediate evolutionary stages [12, 13]. Recent studies have revealed that Cymbidium species are experiencing initial phases of plastome degradation [21, 25, 26]. Both genome size and gene content display significant reduction in terrestrial and mycoheterotrophic species compared to their epiphytic relatives [25]. Notably, the epiphytic Cymbidium mannii Rchb.f. exhibits extensive ndh gene degradation despite its foliar morphology [25]. The leafless species Cymbidium macrorhizon Lindl. presents a distinctive case: it is a holomycoheterotrophic species which maintains its plastome in early degradation phases without subsequent genomic reduction [25, 26]. Current evidence suggests that the common ancestor of Cymbidium species likely possessed a non-functional ndh complex, with subsequent lineage-specific accumulation of substitutions and indels attributable to relaxed selective constraints [21, 26]. Given the prevalent phylogenetic incongruence within Cymbidium and its complex evolutionary history [28, 29], establishing a robust phylogenetic framework is essential for comprehensively elucidating the evolutionary trajectories of plastomes across this genus.

In this study, we implement a comprehensive sampling of the genus Cymbidium, encompassing diverse epiphytic and terrestrial species including both leafy and leafless forms, then conduct phylogenetic analyses using both nuclear ribosomal DNA (nrDNA) sequence and plastome datasets. Based on the robust phylogenetic frameworks, we inferred the evolutionary dynamics of ndh gene loss in relation to life form transitions and nutritional strategies shifts. The main objectives of this study were: (1) to elucidate evolutionary trajectory of plastome degradation and variations in the genus Cymbidium, with emphasis on ndh genes; (2) to assess evolutionary selection of plastid genes between lineages exhibiting life form transitions and nutritional strategies changes; and (3) to evaluate whether nrDNA and plastome phylogenies have effect on evolutionary analyses.

Results

General features of the plastomes in Cymbidium

The average sequencing depth of plastomes across all samples was 283.11× (Table S1). As shown in Fig. 1 & Table S2, the plastome sizes ranged from 148,112 bp (Cymbidium defoliatum Y.S.Wu & S.C.Chen) to 167,808 bp (Cymbidium wenshanense Y.S.Wu & F.Y.Liu). The length of four regions varied as follows: 83,292–86,187 bp in the LSC region, 405–18,196 bp in the SSC region, and 24,990–41,013 bp in the IRa/b regions. The plastome sizes of five outgroup species ranged from 144,793 bp to 159,502 bp. The plastomes of Cymbidium species contain between 104 and 114 unique genes, consisting of 69 to 79 CDS, 30 tRNA, and four rRNA genes. Regarding gene degradation, the ycf15 gene was consistently identified as a pseudogene across all Cymbidium species. Furthermore, the ndh genes remained intact in 21 Cymbidium species, and showed pseudogenes or were completely lost in the remaining 35 Cymbidium species.

Phylogenomic analyses of the nrDNA dataset

The nrDNA dataset was 11,738 bp in length, containing 2,943 variable sites and 1,377 parsimony-informative sites, and exhibiting 45.92% missing data. Phylogenetic analyses strongly supported monophyly of Cymbidium using the nrDNA dataset (Maximum likelihood bootstrap (MLBS) = 100/Bayesian inference posterior probability (BIPP) = 1.00, see Fig. S1), resolving four major clades. The nrDNA phylogenetic framework was largely consistent with the plastome topology (Fig. 2), with exceptions that the clade sect. Floribundum I was excluded from the Clade III as a separate clade, and the Clade I (i.e., sect. Bigibbarium) was nested within the Clade IV. Divergent times estimation of the nrDNA dataset

As shown in Fig. S2, the stem and crown age of Cymbidium was estimated at 23.11 Ma (95% Highest Posterior Density (HPD): 12.55–30.23 Ma) and 13.55 Ma (95% HPD: 7.38–19.62 Ma), respectively. The crown age of the Clade II was estimated at 12.74 Ma (95% HPD: 6.90–18.64 Ma). The stem age of the remaining three major clades was estimated at 12.54 Ma (95% HPD: 6.82–18.25 Ma), while the crown ages of Clades IIIA and IIIB, and the Clade I + IV were estimated at 9.24 Ma (95% HPD: 4.81–14.41 Ma), 9.45 Ma (95% HPD:4.88–14.68 Ma), and 10.96 Ma (95% HPD: 6.93–16.16 Ma), respectively. Notably, the leafless C. macrorhizon diverged from its sister leafy species Cymbidium caulescens Ridl. approximately 2.57 Ma (95% HPD: 0.88–5.51 Ma) in the Clade IIIA.

Evolutionary history of the IR boundary type

The Cymbidium plastomes exhibited the typical quadripartite structure and structural conservatism in gene contents and genome organizations (Figs. 2 & S3), with exceptions including pseudogenization and loss of ndh/ycf15 genes and length variations in the IR regions. Using the plastome of C. devonianum as the reference, we identified four types of IR boundary based on gene composition and arrangement patterns (Fig. 2A). A significant IR expansion was observed in the ycf1-trnLUAG region exclusively in C. wenshanense and C. sichuanicum (Fig. 2). The LSC/IRa and IRb/LSC boundaries were consistently anchored by the rpl22 gene and between the truncated rpl22 and the psbA gene, respectively, showing minimal structural divergence. Structural variations occurred at IRa/SSC and IRb/SSC boundaries, with differences in gene composition across the four IR boundary types:

Type I is characterized by the IR contraction, displaying the IRa/SSC junction between the trnNGUU tRNA and the ycf1 gene, and the SSC/IRb boundary between the rpl32 gene and the trnNGUU tRNA. This type was found in three species. Type II is featured as the ndhF gene loss in the SSC region, with the SSC/IRb junction between the rpl32 gene and the truncated ycf1, while maintaining the intact ycf1 gene at the IRa/SSC junction. This type is found in 19 species and is predominant in terrestrial species. Type III is the reference type of C. devonianum and is similar to the Type II at the IRa/SSC junction, with differences in having the fragmented ndhF pseudogene (subtype 1, 8 species) or the intact ndhF gene (subtype 2, 25 species) in the SSC region. The Type IV is characterized by the elongation of the IRa region from the ycf1 gene to the trnLUAG tRNA, resulting in the SSC reduction to a single rpl32 gene. This type is unique to C. wenshanense and C. sichuanicum (Fig. 2A).

Ancestral state reconstruction revealed eight times (plastome phylogeny) to nine times (nrDNA phylogeny) contraction events in the IR region, with only one expansion event (Fig. 2B). Both nrDNA and plastome phylogenetic inferences supported the Type III as the ancestral IR boundary type in Cymbidium plastome (node 1 in Fig. 2B). Five independent transitions from the Type III to II by loss of the intact or fragmented ndhF gene at following key nodes: the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of Cymbidium suave R.Br. and Cymbidium madidum Lindl. (node 2 in Fig. 2B); an ancestor of C. mannii (node 3 in Fig. 2B), Cymbidium bicolor Lindl. (the node 4 in Fig. 2B) and C. tigrinum (node 7 in Fig. 2B); and the MRCA of sect. Jensoa and sect. Pachyrhizanthe (node 6 in Fig. 2B). Two or three independent transitions from the Type II to I through a contraction of IR region in the ycf1 gene at ancestors of Cymbidium cyperifolium Wall. ex Lindl. (node 8 in Fig. 2B), Cymbidium qiubeiense K.M.Feng & H.Li (node 9 in Fig. 2B), and C. caulescens (node 10 in Fig. 2B). Only a single transition from the Type III to IV by the elongation of IRa region to SSC region with loss of the ndhF gene, which occurred at the MRCA of C. sichuanicum and C. wenshanense (node 11 in Fig. 2B). In addition, one contraction event was found in the outgroup Thecostele alata (Roxb.) C.S.P.Parish & Rchb.f., while four other outgroups retained the ancestral Type III configuration.

Evolutionary analyses of pseudogenization and gene loss

Pseudogenization of the ycf15 gene was ubiquitous in plastomes of 56 Cymbidium species. Twenty-one Cymbidium plastomes have 79 functional CDS genes, the remaining 35 Cymbidium plastomes exhibited progressive degradation of the eleven ndh genes through premature stop codons, truncations, or complete deletions (Fig. 3 & Table S2). Comparative analysis revealed greater ndh gene retention in species from Clades I (C. devonianum) and IV compared to those in Clades II and III. Terrestrial Cymbidium species (18 species) predominantly showed ndhF and ndhH loss, ndhA truncation, and premature stop codons in ndhD (14 species) (Fig. 3). The plastome of leafless C. macrorhizon retained four intact ndh genes (ndhB, ndhC, ndhG, and ndhK), lost two ndh genes (ndhF and ndhG), and displayed pseudogenization in the remaining five ndh genes. For the outgroups, plastomes of Grammatophyllum scriptum (L.) Blume and Grammatophyllum speciosum Blume have eleven intact ndh genes, those of Thecostele alata and Thecopus maingayi (Hook.f.) Seidenf. displayed progressive degradation, and the plastome of Acriopsis javanica Reinw. ex Blume lost five ndh genes and had six pseudogenes.

Degradation of ndh genes has occurred recurrently throughout the evolutionary history of Cymbidium, concentrated in Clades II and III, with Clade IV exhibiting fewer events.

This clade exhibited the highest frequency of degradation events in the ndh genes, with 37 degradation events and two reversions identified. Notably, the largest terrestrial clade (node 6 in Fig. 3A and B) demonstrated congruent evidence of 24 degradation events across both plastome and nrDNA phylogenies.

Evolutionary selection on plastid genes

The results of RELAX analyses based on nrDNA and plastome phylogenies are presented in Fig. 4 & Table S4. When Cymbidium was set as the test clade, both analyses indicated that four genes (petD, rpl16, rpoB, and rps14) undergone significant relaxed selection (K < 1, P < 0.05), and the ndh gene group and five genes (ndhA, ndhF, ndhI, ndhK, and rps18) showed significantly intensified selection (K > 1, P < 0.05). When the terrestrial Cymbidium species was specified as the test clade, both analyses showed that the psa gene group and five genes (ndhB, ndhF, psaB, rbcL, and rps16) displayed significantly relaxed selection (K < 1, P < 0.05), and the ndh gene family and three genes (ndhA, ndhD, and ndhK) exhibited significantly intensified selection (K > 1, P < 0.01). When the leafless species C. macrorhizon was specified as the test clade, both analyses supported that the psa gene group and ndhG experienced significantly relaxed selection (K < 1, P < 0.05), and the atp gene group and ycf1 + ycf2 had significantly intensified selection (K > 1, P < 0.05). Additionally, the analysis based on the plastome phylogeny revealed significantly intensified selection in ycf1 and ndhD (K > 1, P < 0.05) and significantly relaxed selection in psaA (K < 1, P < 0.05).

The results of the aBSREL and BUSTED analyses based on nrDNA and plastome phylogenies are largely consistent (Fig. 4, Tables S5 & S6), with dN and dS values are shown in Fig. S5. In the genus Cymbidium, both analyses identified positive selection signals in ten ndh genes, with ndhJ solely detected by BUSTED based on the nrDNA phylogeny. Discussion

A large-scale pseudogenization and loss of ndh genes

In Cymbidium, except for ndh and ycf15 genes, other CDS genes in the plastomes remain functional. Typically, the plastomes degradation initiates with the pseudogenization of ndh genes [3, 5, 7, 14, 15]. Consistent with previous studies, we found that the plastomes of Cymbidium exhibit an early-stage degradation [19, 21, 25], characterized by a large-scale pseudogenization and loss events in ndh genes. Some earlier studies hypothesized that the MRCA of Cymbidium species likely lacked the functional ndh complex in the plastome [21, 26]. Regarding the topological incongruence between the plastome and nrDNA phylogenies, we chose to perform ancestral state reconstruction using both phylogenies to ensure robustness (Fig. 3). Based on comprehensive species samplings and the congruent results obtained from both plastome and nrDNA phylogenies, this study demonstrated that the ancestral state of the ndh gene family was functional in Cymbidium. Subsequently, the ndh genes underwent progressive pseudogenization and reduction in the evolutionary history of Cymbidium. In Orchidaceae, the ndh gene degradation exhibits complex patterns across at least four of five recognized subfamilies [19, 22]. Independent ndh pseudogenization and loss events have been documented across numerous lineages in the family [22], including tribe Neottieae [30], and genus Corallorhiza Gagnebin [14, 31], Pogonia Juss. and Vanilla Plum. ex Mill. [32], as well as Dipodium R.Br. [24]. In Cymbidium, the ndh degradation had been previously reported [21, 25, 26, 33], with an earlier study suggesting broader pseudogenization and gene loss of the ndh genes in terrestrial Cymbidium species [25]. By expanding sampling of epiphytic species, our findings reveal substantial ndh pseudogenization and gene loss occurring in both terrestrial (Clade III) and epiphytic species of Cymbidium (Clade II). Notably, the ndh genes remain relatively conserved in the Clade IV with epiphytic species (Clade IV). It has been hypothesized that epiphytic Cymbidium species, inhabiting sun-exposed canopies, retain conserved plastid genes, whereas terrestrial species in the shaded understories and more reliant on fungal nutrient acquisition may experience relaxed functional constraints on ndh genes due to ecological adaptations [25]. However, this study detected no significant relaxed selection in most ndh genes (except ndhB and ndhF) among terrestrial species. This discrepancy may arise from ndh pseudogenization and loss patterns not being restricted to the terrestrial clade, but also prevalent in epiphytic lineages, especially the Clade II (Fig. 3), indicating a more intricate evolutionary trajectory of ndh degradation.

The retention of intact ndh genes in epiphytic species, particularly those within the Clade IV, likely represents an adaptive evolutionary response to the sub-exposed environments characteristic of their arboreal niches. The ndh genes encode critical components of the NDH complex, which plays key roles in photosynthetic electron transport, ATP generation, and enhances stress resilience [16–18]. Crucially, the NDH complex is essential for cyclic electron flow (CEF) around the photosystem I, a mechanism vital for photoprotection under high-light stress and for maintaining the optimal ATP/NADPH ratio during photosynthesis [34]. These functions prove indispensable in epiphytic habitats, where plants routinely encounter environmental stressors such as solar radiation, desiccation, and thermal fluctuations. Comparative physiological investigations demonstrated that photosystems in the terrestrial species Cymbidium sinense (Andrews) Willd. (Clade III) exhibit greater susceptibility to photoinhibition under the high-light conditions than those in epiphytic Cymbidium tracyanum L.Castle (Clade IV). While C. tracyanum employs robust CEF activation as a primary photoprotective strategy under high-light conditions, the weak CEF capacity in C. sinense constrains its adaptive flexibility in such environments [35]. This functional dichotomy aligns with genomic evidence: the conservation of intact ndh genes in the Clade IV species may reflect an adaptive mechanism to exploit intermittent direct sunlight in canopy microhabitats, thereby optimizing light-energy utilization and enhancing photoprotective capabilities critical for epiphytic survival.

The Clade II exhibited a large-scale pseudogenization and gene loss within the ndh gene family. Zhe et al. [25] hypothesized that the ndh degradation in Cymbidium may correlate with the evolution of strong CAM (crassulacean acid metabolism) photosynthesis in Cymbidium. There are three species demonstrating strong CAM photosynthesis [36] within the Clade II showing the ndh degradation (Clade II in Fig. 3), i.e., Cymbidium finlaysonianum Lindl. (nonfunctional ndhA, D, F genes and loss of ndhH), C. bicolor (nonfunctional ndhA, B, C, D, H genes and loss of ndhF) and Cymbidium canaliculatum R.Br. (nonfunctional ndhA, B, D, E, F, J genes and loss of ndhH). Notably, the Clade II with epiphytes possesses a distinctive evolutionary trajectory during the middle Miocene, i.e., these species initiated to migration and radiation into Southeast Asia and underwent extensive adaptation to the tropical rainforest ecosystems [29]. It is speculated that the transition from weak to strong CAM photosynthesis in the MCRA of the extant Clade II species represents an adaptation to the water-limited tropical lowland environments [36]. In contrast, the Clade IV species predominantly utilize C3 or weak CAM photosynthetic pathways [36] and are distributed across humid habitats spanning the eastern Himalayas to Mountains of Southwest China [27, 37]. The association between CAM photosynthesis and ndh gene degradation may reflect reduced reliance on CEF in the CAM plants. The temporal decoupling of nocturnal CO2 fixation and diurnal Calvin cycle activity in the CAM species potentially diminishes the necessity for precise ATP/NADPH ratio modulation typically facilitated by NDH-dependent CEF [38]. Furthermore, the CAM plants employ alternative photoprotective mechanism, including enhanced non-photochemical quenching and specialized anatomical adaptations, that may reduce the dependence on NDH-mediated CEF [39, 40]. The ndh gene degradation is not unique to Cymbidium, but recurs consistently in diverse constitutive CAM lineages, including the genus Dendrobium Sw. [41, 42]. , the family Cactaceae [43–46], and Bromeliaceae subfamily Hechtioideae [47]. Several studies speculated that the ndh pseudogenization may be functionally associated with constitutive evolution of the CAM photosynthesis [8, 42, 46–48]. This trend is particularly evident in the genus Dendrobium, where constitutive CAM species exhibited reduced plastome sizes due to extensive ndh degradation [42]. Although most epiphytic Cymbidium species belong to Clades II and IV, the Clade IV retains almost intact ndh genes in all species compared to the severe degradation observed in the Clade II, suggesting divergent evolutionary trajectories shaped by distinct environmental pressures.

While the large-scale ndh gene degradation in the epiphytic Clade II may be correlated with the shift to strong CAM, the terrestrial Clade III also underwent substantial pseudogenization events. The observed degradation in the Clade III is likely driven by a reduction in photoprotective requirements and a subsequent decreased reliance on photosynthesis. These terrestrial species, which primarily inhabit the shaded forest understory [27], exhibit a reduced overall capacity for light protection compared with the high-exposure epiphytes of Clade IV [35]. Meanwhile, terrestrial orchids maintain a close association with symbiotic fungi. This mixotrophic strategy, which partial acquisition of carbon and nutrients from their fungal partners, effectively compensates for the lower photosynthetic output achievable in low-light conditions [12, 13]. This trophic strategy may thus reduce the selective pressure on the plastid photosynthetic capability, providing a window of evolutionary tolerance that permits the accumulation of degradation and gene loss within the ndh gene family [25]. This evolutionary trajectory reaches its extreme in C. macrorhizon, the only holomycoheterotrophic species in this genus, which remains entirely subterranean during its non-flowering period, deriving virtually all its energy from fungi.

In conclusion, the plastome degradation process in Cymbidium is complicated, involving in multiple interconnected factors, including photoprotective requirements, the evolutionary trajectory of photosynthetic types, and the degree of reliance on mycorrhizal partners. Further comprehensive investigations need to integrate large-scale genomic data with detailed physiological, morphological, and ecological data to fully elucidate the diversification history of Cymbidium and reveal the intricate relationship between adaptive evolutionary changes and the plastome evolution.

IR boundary variation patterns across Cymbidium life forms

Plastome structures typically exhibit high conservation at the genus level, characterized by a canonical quadripartite architecture comprising LSC and SSC regions, and two inverted repeat regions [49]. While the plastomes of Cymbidium and its outgroup species generally conform to this organization, several Cymbidium species display notable structural variations, particularly in the extent of IR boundary contractions and expansions (Fig. 2). Evolutionary analysis reveals predominance of contraction events over a single observed expansion in Cymbidium, with these contractions frequently associated with the ndhF pseudogenization or loss.

Cymbidium species exhibit diverse life forms, which may be associated with plastome structural variations. Over half of the investigated species (33 species) displayed the Type III boundary, where the SSC/IRb junction is located between ndhF and fragmental ycf1. The Type III boundary represents the ancestral type and was most prevalent found in the epiphytic species, as well as two terrestrial species, i.e. Cymbidium hartinahianum J.B.Comber & Nasution and Cymbidium borneense J.J.Wood (Fig. 2). In contrast, terrestrial species predominantly feature either the Type II boundary (i.e., SSC/IRb junction located between rpl22 and fragmental ycf1; 19 species) or the Type I boundary (i.e., SSC/IRb junction located between rpl22 and trnN; 3 species). The difference between Type II and III boundaries is in the absence of ndhF in the SSC region of the Type II with contraction of the IR region. Previous studies proposed ancestral ndhF loss in terrestrial Cymbidium species [25]. Through comprehensive species samplings, these results revealed that ndhF loss was highly coincident with an IR contraction event in terrestrial species at 8–10 Ma using plastome and nrDNA datasets (node 6 in Figs. 2 and 3, S4). The existence of ndhF could affect the IR boundary stability in Cymbidium [21]. This pattern parallels observations across the family Orchidaceae [19]. Generally, the SSC/IRb boundary is located between ndhF and fragmental ycf1, whereas ndhF truncation or loss correlates with IR boundary variations [19, 20].

In addition, a large IR expansion was reported in the plastomes of C. sichuanicum and C. wenshanense, where only the rpl32 gene and a small intergenic spacer remains in the SSC region (Fig. 2A). This unique structural configuration is exclusively observed in these two species of the Clade IV. However, the precise evolutionary mechanism driving this large IR expansion remains unknown and warrants further comprehensive investigation into the underlying factors of genomic instability in these two species.

Lagging degradation history of the plastomes of Cymbidium macrorhizon

Cymbidium macrorhizon exhibits a notable temporal decoupling between morphological transition to leafless form and plastome degradation, a phenomenon potentially attributable to its short evolutionary history. Consist with previous studies [21, 25, 26], our results confirmed that the plastome of the leafless C. macrorhizon was at an early stage of degradation, i.e. ndhF and ndhG genes were completely lost, and ndhB, ndhC, ndhG, and ndhK genes remained functional (Fig. 3). Notably, evolutionary analysis revealed no significant relaxation of purifying constraints on most CDS genes, with the exception of the ndhG gene and the psa gene group (Fig. 4).

The degradation of plastomes in heterotrophic plants typically progresses through five sequential stages of physical and functional reduction accompanied by relaxed selection pressure: (1) NA(D)H dehydrogenase complex (ndh genes), (2) photosynthesis-related genes (e.g., cemA, ccsA, pet, psa/b, rbcL and ycf3/4 genes), (3) plastid-encoded RNA polymerase genes (rpo genes), (4) plastid ATP synthase genes (atp genes), and (5) ‘housekeeping’ genes (e.g. matK and rpl genes) [3, 5, 7, 14, 15]. In many leafless orchids, plastomes displayed extreme degradation as observed in tribe Gastrodieae [50] and genus Hexalectris [51], indicating plastome degradation typically parallels morphological transition from leafy to leafless forms. However, C. macrorhizon presents a striking exception. Despite being a holomycoheterotrophic orchid without leaves and subterranean growth except during flowering [12, 13, 27], the plastome structure and gene content resemble to its leafy sister species, i.e. Cymbidium lancifolium Hook. and C. caulescens. In these three species, seven of eleven ndh genes are physically and functionally degraded. Some differences include functional retention of ndhG in C. macrorhizon alone and functional retention of ndhI in C. lancifolium and C. caulescens but its pseudogenization in C. macrorhizon. This pattern deviates from established models of plastome evolution in heterotrophic plants, with C. macrorhizon exemplifying delayed degradation dynamics. Our evolutionary analyses showed no significant relaxation of purifying constraints for most CDS genes in C. macrorhizon, except for the psaA gene (plastome phylogeny alone), the ndhG gene, and the psa gene family in both plastomes and nrDNA phylogenies. Moreover, evolutionary rate analyses revealed no accelerated substitution rates in C. macrorhizon compared to leafy congeners (Fig. S5), corroborating the hypothesis of delayed plastome degradation.

The delayed plastome degradation may reflect C. macrorhizon’s relatively recent transition to holomycoheterotrophy, estimated at approximately 2–4 Ma ago [26, 29]. Although C. macrorhizon has completely shifted to the leafless form, the short evolutionary time likely limited the accumulation of plastome degradation. Comparable early-stage plastome degradation patterns occur in other recently diverged leafless orchids, such as Dipodium sect. Dipodium (diverged at approximately 7.3 Ma), which exhibits analogous plastome conservation [24]. Beyond Orchidaceae, similar temporal decoupling between morphological and genomic evolution has been documented in the largest parasitic family Orobanchaceae. Such as Lathraea squamaria L. (toothwort), a holoparasitic species diverging at 13–14 Ma ago, retains a nearly intact plastome in size (150,504 bp), with physical retention of 72 unique CDS genes, and all tRNA and rRNA genes [52]. Furthermore, Cuscuta exaltata Lam. (Convolvulaceae), a holoparasitic species in the parasitic lineage over 53.0 Ma, maintains a large plastome (125,373 bp) retaining many functionally photosynthetic-related genes, further exemplifying delayed plastome degradation [2, 53]. Collectively, these examples suggest plastome degradation dynamics correlate with evolutionary duration during heterotrophic transition, exhibiting a temporal lag relative to morphological adaptations.

Effect of topological incongruence on plastome evolution analysis

A robust phylogeny is essential for accurately reconstructing the evolutionary history of a lineage. In this study, we integrated both nrDNA and plastome phylogenies by sampling all eleven sections to comprehensively investigate Cymbidium plastome evolution. Two phylogenies revealed that nine of the eleven sections maintained consistent positions, while topological incongruence was restricted to the position of the Clade I (C. devonianum) and the Clade IIIB (sect. Floribumdum I). The plastome phylogeny resolved the Clade I was sister to all other lineages, whereas the nrDNA phylogeny showed it nested within the Clade IV. In addition, the Clade IIIB shifted from the Clade III in the plastome phylogeny to sister clade with the Clade IV in the nrDNA phylogeny. This incongruence is found in previous studies based on nrITS and plastid datasets. For instance, nrITS phylogenies placed sect. Floribumdum I as sister to all other Cymbidium species [28, 54, 55], while plastid phylogenies supported it as sister to the Clade III (subgenus Jensoa) [54, 56, 57]. Meanwhile, C. devonianum (Clade I) exhibits discordant placements: nrITS phylogenies supported it as occupying the most divergent position within the Clade IV (subgenus Cyperorchis) [28, 54, 56] or sister to the Clade III in plastid phylogenies [54, 56, 57].

Despite these topological discrepancies between nrDNA and plastome phylogenies, the ancestral state reconstructions of IR boundary types and ndh genes remained largely congruent. The ancestral states of the genus and major clades were identical, being the Type III IR boundary (Fig. 2), and having intact and full functional of eleven ndh genes (Figs. 3 & S4). Furthermore, only three ndh genes (ndhB, ndhF, and ndhG genes) experienced significant relaxed selection in terrestrial species across both phylogenies (Fig. 4), while solely the ndhG gene exhibited significant relaxed selection in the holomycoheterotrophy C. macrorhizon. These results indicated that ndh degradation is not exclusive to terrestrial species and may experience delayed progression in the holomycoheterotrophic species like C. macrorhizon. Additionally, despite variable phylogenetic placements of Clade I (sect. Bigibbarium) and Clade IIIB (sect. Floribumdum I), the plastomes of these species remain structurally conserved. Only two species from Clade IIIB, C. hartinahianum (with nonfunctional ndhD, ndhF and ndhH) and Cymbidium floribundum Lindl. (with nonfunctional ndhA, ndhF, ndhG, ndhH, and ndhJ) exhibited plastome degradation in ndh genes. However, neither species had gene loss or IR boundary variation. Moreover, while Clade IIIB shifts between Clades III and IV, it occupies a sister position to the remaining members of either clade, which has less impact on ancestral state reconstruction.

The phylogenetic incongruences observed in Cymbidium may result from incomplete lineage sorting, hybridization, or reticulate evolutionary histories in some lineages [28, 57]. This study underscores the importance of comparative analyses using incongruent phylogenies in evolutionary inferences, providing a more comprehensive framework for interpreting plastome evolution in Cymbidium. Our findings demonstrated that integrating both plastome and nrDNA phylogenies yields a multidimensional perspective on evolutionary trajectories, even amid topological discordance. The consistency of these results across divergent phylogenetic hypotheses reinforces the robustness of our conclusions regarding the plastome evolution in Cymbidium.

Conclusions

In this study, we explored the plastome evolution of Cymbidium through integrative analyses of 56 plastomes and nrDNA datasets. Our findings demonstrated that Cymbidium plastomes exhibit typical quadripartite structures with size variations and consistent GC content distributions. We also identified four distinct types of IR boundaries in Cymbidium. Ancestral state reconstruction suggested Type III as the ancestral IR boundary type. Contraction events at IR boundaries are predominant and often coincide with the ndhF gene loss, particularly in terrestrial species. Comprehensive analyses revealed that the ndh genes were functional in the ancestral state and underwent progressive pseudogenization and reduction across multiple lineages. Both epiphytic (especially Clade II) and terrestrial species (Clade III) experienced independent ndh pseudogenization and loss. These degradation patterns likely represent adaptive responses to different ecological environments. In the Clade II, ndh degradation may be linked to the evolution of strong CAM photosynthesis of the epiphytic lifestyle. Conversely, in the Clade III, ndh degradation likely reflects adaptation to a terrestrial lifestyle in the low-light forest understory habitats. Notably, the holomycoheterotrophic C. macrorhizon retains an intact plastome despite its transition to a leafless form. This suggests a time lag between morphological and plastomic evolution due to its short evolutionary time (2–4 Ma). This temporal decoupling between morphological and plastomic evolution provides novel insights into how plastome degradation dynamics correlate with evolutionary duration during heterotrophic transition, exhibiting a temporal lag relative to morphological adaptations. Evolutionary selection analyses indicated relaxed selection on certain genes and intensified selection on others, particularly within the ndh gene group. Despite topological incongruence between nrDNA and plastome phylogenies, our conclusions regarding plastome evolution in Cymbidium remained robust. This study underscores the importance of integrating multiple phylogenetic perspectives to enhance our understanding of evolutionary trajectories in Cymbidium.

Methods

Plant sampling, DNA isolation and sequencing

Our sampling included 56 Cymbidium species, representing all eleven currently recognized sections of the genus [27]. Based on literature and our field observations [27, 37], these species were classified into two ecological groups: epiphytic (38 species) and terrestrial (18 species). Additionally, they were categorized by morphology: leafy (55 species) or leafless (1 species, which is the only holomycoheterotrophic species in the genus Cymbidium) based on the presence or absence of visible leaf tissue.

Leaf tissues for sequencing were collected from herbarium specimens, greenhouse-cultivated plants, and wild populations. All materials were collected with appropriate institutional and governmental permissions, and specimens were taxonomically identified by Jun-Bo Yang and Ji-Dong Ya. The purified DNA was subsequently fragmented to a target length of 350–500 bp. Sequencing library were constructed following standard protocols, utilizing the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. Finally, 150 bp pair-end reads were generated on an Illumina Hi-Seq 2500 Sequencing System.

For the nrDNA dataset, we newly sequenced 61 nrDNA sequences from the 56 Cymbidium species and five outgroup species from the subtribe Cymbidiinae. Both the whole plastome sequences and the nrDNA sequences used in this study originated from the same sequencing project. The whole plastome sequences used in this study were derived from our previous studies [29, 57]. The voucher information, collection details, and accession numbers for both the plastome and nrDNA sequences are provided in Table S1. Genomic DNA was isolated from dry leaf material using a modified CTAB method [58].

Plastome assembly and annotation

Clean raw reads were used for de novo assembly of both the whole plastomes and the nrDNA sequences using the GetOrganelle toolkit v1.7.7.1 [59]. Assembly graphs for both datasets were visualized using Bandage v0.9.0 [60] to confirm the completeness of the assembly. Plastome annotation were initially performed automatically using CPGAVAS2 [61] and PGA [62]. The results were subsequently manually verified and adjusted using Geneious v2021.1.1 [63]. Manual verification focused on confirming the start and stop codons of protein coding genes, as well as the identification of pseudogenes and indels.

During the manual annotation process, the functional states of genes were systematically categorized based on sequence length and structural completeness. The classification criteria were established as follows: (0) Functional: intact open reading frame (ORF) with complete sequence; (1) Pseudogenized: premature stop codon present while retaining > 2/3 functional reference sequence length; (2) Truncated: sequence length between 90 bp and 2/3 functional reference sequence length; and (3) Lost: the sequence fragment < 90 bp or complete loss. The categorized functional states were used for the downstream evolutionary analysis.

The final sequence coverage was calculated using the “evaluate_assembly_using_mapping.py” and “summary_get_organelle_output.py” scripts within the GetOrganelle toolkit v1.7.7.1 [59] to assess assembly quality. The average base coverages of plastomes were presented in Table S1. The sequence orientation and annotations of the nrDNA assemblies were adjusted using Geneious v2021.1.1 [63], with the nrDNA of Cymbidium macrorhizon serving as a reference annotation (Genbank accession: MK333261).

Plastome feature characteristics and structural analysis

The general plastome features, including genome size, gene content (CDS, tRNA, rRNA, and pseudogenes), the lengths of the LSC, SSC, and IR regions, and GC content across the entire plastomes and its regions, were characterized and quantified using Geneious v2021.1.1 [63].

To check structural variations of plastome sequences, all plastomes of Cymbidium were aligned using Mauve 2.4.0 [64] with the progressiveMauve algorithm, then the alignment was manually checked and visualized using Geneious v2021.1.1 [63]. The plastome of Cymbidium devonianum (GenBank accession: ON983906) generated in our previous study served as the structural reference, given its basal phylogenetic position and conserved plastome structure [29]. We specifically compared the boundaries between the IR/SC regions to identify and characterize IR expansion and contraction across the samples. Structural variations in the IR boundary were systematically classified based on comparative gene composition and arrangement patterns within the IR regions. The categorized IR boundary types were used for the downstream evolutionary analysis.

Phylogenetic analyses

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using two datasets: the whole plastome and the nrDNA matrix. For the plastome dataset, one IR region was excluded. The remaining plastome sequences were then aligned using MAFFT v7.505 [65] with default parameters. Subsequently, the resulting alignments were trimmed using trimAI v1.2 [66] with the parameters “-gt 0.9 -cons 60”. For the nrDNA dataset, the seven regions (5’ETS-18 S-ITS1-5.8 S-ITS2-28 S-3’ETS) were aligned separately using MAFFT v7.505 [65] with the default parameters. Subsequently, the seven alignments were trimmed with trimAI v1.2 [66] using the parameters “-gt 0.9 -cons 60”, and then were concatenated into a contiguous supermatrix dataset using the “concatenate_fasta.py” script (https://github.com/Kinggerm/PersonalUtilities). The ML analysis of both the plastome and concatenated nrDNA datasets was conducted using RAxML v8.2.10 [67] with the GTR + Γ + I model. Support values of each node were assessed through 1,000 bootstrap replicates, with MLBS values ≥ 75 were considered well supported. The Bayesian inference (BI) analysis was conducted using MrBayes v3.2.7 [68]. The best-fit substitution model was calculated using ModelTest-NG v0.1.7 [69]. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis runs for 10,000,000 generations, sampling every 100 generations. The initial 20% of iterations were discarded as burn-in. Convergence was assessed by examining the average standard deviation of split frequencies (< 0.01) and the potential scale reduction factor (approaching 1.0). BIPP values ≥ 0.95 were considered well supported. The phylogenetic trees were visualized using the online tool tvBOT (https://www.chiplot.online/tvbot.html) [70]. The ML phylogenetic trees of plastomes and nrDNA were used for the downstream analysis.

Estimation of divergence time of the nrDNA dataset

To estimate divergence time of the nrDNA dataset, we used MCMCTree program [71] implemented in PAML v4.10.5 [72]. The phylogenetic topology derived from the ML analysis was fixed and used as the input in MCMCTree program. The plastome chronogram used in this study was extracted from a larger Orchidaceae chronogram encompassing 170 species in our previous study [29]. This estimation of Orchidaceae incorporated four calibration constraints: (1) the crown age of the Orchidaceae (80–100 Ma), informed by previous comprehensive Orchidaceae studies [73, 74]; (2) the Goodyera clade crown age (15.0–50.0 Ma), based on the Miocene amber-encased Meliorchis caribea pollinarium [75]; and the crown ages of both the (3) Earina-sister and (4) the Australian Dendrobium clades (20.0–50.0 Ma), based on the fossil leaves of Miocene orchids (Dendrobium and Earina) [76]. Subsequently, the nrDNA dataset was subjected to a secondary calibration where calibration constraints for the Cymbidium crown and stem nodes were set to 7.00–20.00 Ma and 10.00–30.00 Ma ranges, respectively, derived directly from the plastome estimation.

Initial substitution rate estimation was conducted using BASEML program [71] in PAML v4.10.5 [72]. The MCMCTree analysis was then executed through two independent runs employing the approximate likelihood method, with each run consisting of 400,000 iterations. To ensure convergence, the first 40% of iterations were discarded as burn-in during the post-processing. Analytical parameters included implementation of the independent rates model (clock = 2) and the GTR + Γ model (model = 7) in the control file to accommodate lineage-specific rate variation and nucleotide substitution complexity. The resultant chronogram was visualized using the online tool tvBOT (https://www.chiplot.online/tvbot.html) [70] and subsequently employed for downstream evolutionary analyses.

Evolutionary analyses of IR structure, pseudogenization and gene loss

To comprehensively investigate the evolutionary history of the Cymbidium plastomes, ancestral state reconstruction was performed utilizing chronograms derived from both plastome and nrDNA datasets. Ancestral state estimation for IR boundary types and ndh genes was inferred using the ‘Equal Rates (ER)’ model [77] implemented in the R package ‘phytools’ [78].

Testing selection of plastid genes

Eighty unique CDS genes, including pseudogenes were extracted from annotated plastomes using the “get_annotated_regions_from_gb.py” script (https://github.com/Kinggerm/PersonalUtilities). For data preparation, the stop codon within each CDS was replaced with gaps using the “cln” command, then each CDS gene was aligned using a codon-based dynamic programming algorithm with the “caln” command in Hyphy v2.5 [79]. These CDS alignments were subsequently categorized into ten functional groups based on their shared functional roles within photosynthesis [3, 5, 14]. These ten groups include NAD(P)H dehydrogenase genes (ndh), ATP synthase genes (atp and pet), photosystem I and II genes (psa and psb), protein synthesis genes (rps and rpl), and plastid-encoded RNA polymerase genes (rpo), and others (ycf1 + 2 and ycf3 + 15). The complete list of all eighty CDS genes and ten groups were detailed in Table S4. Then the categorized genes were concatenated into supermatrixes using the “concatenate_fasta.py” script (https://github.com/Kinggerm/PersonalUtilities). The phylogenetic topologies required for the selection pressure analysis was derived from both the plastome and nrDNA ML trees. To ensure congruence between the sequence alignment and the input tree for each gene tested, we excluded species only when the gene was completely lost. The exclusion was executed by using the drop.tip function from the R package ape [80] to remove tips corresponding to the missing taxa. This procedure ensured that the gene alignment and its corresponding input tree always shared an identical and matched taxon set.

To detect whether 80 CDS genes and ten functional gene groups were experiencing relaxed or intensified selections, we conducted RELAX analyses [81] using HyPhy v2.5 [79]. Using default parameters, we calculate the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitution rates (ω = dN/dS). We incorporated both plastome and nrDNA phylogenies for the RELAX analysis to enhance the consistency of estimations. Comparative RELAX analyses were performed across three evolutionary contrasts: (1) other species of subtribe Cymbidiinae (outgroups) versus Cymbidium; (2) epiphytic versus terrestrial species in Cymbidium; and (3) leafy Cymbidium species versus leafless C. macrorhizon species. The relaxation coefficient (K) was used to quantify selection pressure differentials between test and background branches, with K > 1 indicating intensified selection, K < 1 reflecting relaxed selection, and K = 1 representing neutral selection.

Meanwhile, we implemented complementary selection detection approaches using aBSREL (adaptive branch-site random effects likelihood) [82] and BUSTED (branch-site unrestricted statistical test of episodic diversification) [83] with HyPhy v2.5 [79]. These methods were employed to detect episodic diversifying selection among the functional gene groups and the eleven ndh genes. The aBSREL test for lineage-specific selection patterns using the branch-site random effects model, and BUSTED evaluates gene-wide signatures of episodic diversifying selection. Relative values of dN and dS were calculated under the Muse-Gaut codon substitution model with GTR framework [84] using the FitMG94.bf scripts (https://github.com/veg/hyphy-analyses/tree/master/FitMG94) in Hyphy v2.5 [79]. The analysis inferred synonymous and non-synonymous substitution rates independently for each phylogenetic branch, maintaining separate rate estimations across the phylogenetic tree.
