Deep neural network approximation for high-dimensional parabolic partial integro-differential equations
Marcin Baranek

Abstract
In this article, we investigate the existence of a deep neural network (DNN) capable of approximating solutions to partial integro-differential equations while circumventing the curse of dimensionality. Using the Feynman-Kac theorem, we express the solution in terms of stochastic differential equations (SDEs). Based on several properties of classical estimators, we establish the existence of a DNN that satisfies the necessary assumptions. The results are theoretical and don’t have any numerical experiments yet.

Introduction

We investigate the existence of a deep neural network(DNN) approximating
the solution of the following Couchy problem

\begin{cases}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\mathcal{L}_{t}u(t,x)+b(t,x)\quad\forall{(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}},\\
u(T,x)=g(x)\quad\forall{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}.\end{cases}

(1)

Where
T>0
,
A
is a positive definite matrix.
By the symbol
\nabla
, we mean a gradient.
We discuss the dependency between the requested accuracy and number of network’s parameters.
The precise assumptions about the functions
u,\ b
, and
g
are present later in the paper.
The operator
\mathcal{L}
has the following form

\mathcal{L}_{t}u(x)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u(t,x)+\int_{\mathcal{Z}}u\left(t,x+c(t,x,z)\right)-u(t,x)-c(t,x,z)\circ\nabla u(t,x)\mathrm{d}\nu(z),

for
\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
\nu(\mathrm{d}z)
is finite Lévy measure on
\left(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z})\right)
.

We mainly focus on the high dimensional problems,
where due the dimension classic numerical methods such as finite difference or finite element methods are too expensive.
Roughly speaking, we sink in the curse of dimensionality.

To be precise, we search for a numerical method approximating the solution of (
1
)
with a certain accuracy not dependent exponentially on the dimension of the domain.
There is a method called sparse tensor discretizations applied to non integro differential equations,
but the logarithm of the
inversion of the accuracy is the base, and the dependence with respect to the dimension is still
exponential, see
[
21
]
.

Monte Carlo methods work pretty well.
However, there are restricted to a single evaluating point.
To obtain coverage in mean squared error sense, we can’t avoid the exponential growth of cost together with accuracy,
hence the approximation of solutions in high dimensions on the full domain remains with a room for improvement.

There has already been done plenty of research in the approximation of solutions to
PDEs in high dimensions using deep neural networks, see

[
2
,
3
,
4
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
15
,
16
,
18
]
.
Moreover, we can find articles applying deep neural networks in the field of finance

[
5
,
8
,
14
]
.
According to our best knowledge, the integral part included in the operator
\mathcal{L}
hasn’t been discussed yet.
Therefore, we apply already known approaches to integro-PDEs.

The key contribution of this article is
to show that there exists a deep neural network approximating the solution of the problem (
1
),
where the operator
\mathcal{L}
includes an integral part
and proposes asymptotic bounds on the number of network’s parameters.
The main result of this work is
theorem
1
, that proves
the existence of a network approximating the solution with error not greater than the desired accuracy
\delta\in(0,1)
.
The error is calculated in
L^{2}
norm with respect to any
"
well-behaved
"
distribution defined on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The idea of the proof is as follows.
We use the Feynman-Kac theorem to express the solution as a sum of two expected values.
Then we build a Monte Carlo estimators approximating those expected values.
Next,
we search for a bound on the mean error
coming from the Monte Carlo estimators,
and we replace the functions defined in the equation (
1
) by predefined DNNs.
Using gained bound and the mean value theorem, we achieve the existence of such approximating network.
The last step is to calculate the asymptotic number of parameters in the obtained network.
A more formal plan is found in the next section together with clarified assumptions.

Approximation algorithm

In this section we introduce our approach to the approximation of the solution using DNNs.

3.1
Basics of the algorithm

We establish several auxiliary lemmas related to stochastic differential equations.
