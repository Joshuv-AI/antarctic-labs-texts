Cerebrospinal fluid cell-free DNA sequencing in pediatric CNS tumors: towards liquid molecular neuropathology
Authors: William Gehin, Marie-Sophie Merlin, Alexandre Harle, Jean-Louis Merlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric central nervous system tumors remain a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in children, while their diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapeutic management increasingly depend on integrated molecular characterization. However, representative tumor tissue is often difficult to obtain because of tumor location, surgical risk, limited biopsy material, and the impracticality of repeated sampling during disease evolution. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) has therefore emerged as a particularly informative liquid biopsy compartment for many CNS malignancies, enriched in tumor-derived cell-free DNA and, for tumors in contact with the CSF spaces, more directly reflective of intracranial tumor biology than plasma; its yield nonetheless varies with tumor biology and anatomical proximity to CSF pathways. Here, we review the evidence supporting CSF cell-free DNA sequencing as an emerging extension of molecular neuropathology in pediatric CNS tumors. Targeted next-generation sequencing, low-pass whole-genome sequencing, methylation-based classifiers, and nanopore sequencing now enable complementary assessment of somatic mutations, copy number alterations, epigenetic tumor class, and longitudinal tumor burden from low-input pediatric CSF samples. Recent studies have moved the field beyond analytical proof of concept towards defined clinical scenarios, including molecular diagnosis when biopsy is infeasible, molecular staging of high-CSF-shedding tumors, minimal residual disease monitoring in medulloblastoma and other embryonal tumors, and clarification of ambiguous radiological progression. CSF-based sequencing does not replace tissue neuropathology, but provides a liquid molecular layer that can complement, extend, or in selected situations partially substitute tissue-based diagnosis. Introduction

Pediatric central nervous system (CNS) tumors are the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in children, with a global incidence estimated at 47,600 new cases per year (1.8 per 100,000) and approximately 23,500 deaths annually [34]. Unlike most pediatric malignancies, for which survival has improved dramatically over the past decades, outcomes for several CNS tumor subtypes (including diffuse midline gliomas, high-grade embryonal tumors, and recurrent medulloblastoma) remain poor, largely because of limited access to tumor tissue and an incomplete understanding of the molecular drivers of treatment resistance [33, 48].

The 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of CNS tumors has fundamentally shifted the diagnostic paradigm from histopathology towards integrated molecular characterization [33]. Accurate diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment selection now require the identification of specific genetic and epigenetic alterations, such as histone H3 K27 and G34 mutations in diffuse gliomas, molecular subgrouping in medulloblastoma, and C19MC amplification in embryonal tumors with multilayered rosettes [2, 33].

This molecular imperative places an increasing burden on tissue acquisition and quality. In this context, CSF cell-free DNA sequencing should no longer be viewed merely as a circulating biomarker assay, but as a potential liquid extension of molecular neuropathology. When tumor tissue is unavailable, insufficient, spatially unrepresentative, or temporally outdated, CSF-derived cfDNA can provide a partial molecular surrogate for tissue by capturing diagnostically relevant mutations, copy number alterations, and, increasingly, methylation-based tumor class. This does not imply that CSF-based testing should replace histopathological examination when tissue can be safely obtained; rather, it provides an additional molecular layer that may complement, extend, or in selected high-risk settings partially substitute tissue-based integrated diagnosis.

This concept is particularly relevant in pediatric neuro-oncology, where tissue sampling is frequently limited by tumor location, surgical risk, and the small volumes obtained, particularly for tumors arising in the brainstem, thalamus, or spinal cord, where stereotactic biopsy carries a non-negligible risk of neurological morbidity [24, 44]. Even when resection is performed, single-site sampling fails to capture the spatial and temporal heterogeneity that characterizes many pediatric brain tumors, and repeat biopsies at relapse are rarely feasible [40]. These constraints create a pressing clinical need for minimally invasive approaches that can provide molecular information at diagnosis and throughout the disease course.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) has emerged as a particularly informative biofluid for CNS tumors because of its direct anatomical contact with the tumor microenvironment. Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) shed into the CSF includes a tumor-derived fraction, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), which is present at substantially higher concentrations than in plasma, owing to the proximity of many pediatric brain tumors to the ventricular and subarachnoid spaces [40, 68]. As early as 2015, Wang et al. demonstrated the feasibility of detecting tumor-derived DNA in CSF from patients with primary brain tumors [68], and subsequent work by Miller et al. showed that serial CSF sampling could track clonal evolution in gliomas with greater fidelity than plasma-based approaches [40]. These foundational observations established CSF as a preferred liquid biopsy compartment for CNS malignancies, while indicating that this advantage is greatest for tumors in contact with CSF pathways and varies with tumor biology.

Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies (including low-pass whole-genome sequencing (LP-WGS), targeted hybrid-capture panels, and, more recently, methylation-based classifiers) now permit comprehensive genomic profiling from the minute quantities of cfDNA typically present in pediatric CSF samples [15, 20, 41]. These assays have demonstrated clinical utility across multiple scenarios: molecular diagnosis without tissue biopsy in brainstem tumors, staging and risk stratification in embryonal tumors, minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring during therapy, and early detection of relapse before radiographic progression [13, 16, 24, 65]. Importantly, in embryonal and other high-CSF-shedding tumors studied at staging, CSF-based NGS has consistently outperformed conventional CSF cytology, with detection rates of 80–100% versus 11–20% across three independent pediatric cohorts [13, 16, 65]; because these cohorts are enriched for high-shedding disease, this margin does not generalize to lower-shedding or deeply parenchymal entities, in which yield is considerably lower (§4.3).

Several narrative and systematic reviews published between 2020 and 2023 have summarized the early evidence for liquid biopsy in pediatric brain tumors [23, 39, 61, 67]. However, this field has evolved rapidly: since 2023, pivotal studies have expanded the evidence base for LP-WGS in embryonal tumors [16], demonstrated molecular diagnosis of brainstem gliomas through CSF cfDNA [24], introduced methylation-based tumor classification from CSF [31, 58], and reported nanopore sequencing of CSF for rapid diagnostics [1].

A comprehensive and updated synthesis is therefore needed. In this review, we argue that the central question is shifting from whether CSF-based NGS works as a molecular assay to when, how, and on what level of evidence it can be integrated into pediatric neuro-oncology practice. It is useful to distinguish three levels of clinical maturity: an assay may be clinically informative (it reliably provides accurate molecular information), clinically actionable (that information can guide a specific management decision), and outcome-improving (acting on it has been prospectively shown to improve patient outcomes). Building on the evidence accumulated since 2015, we contend that CSF-NGS is now clinically informative across four defined scenarios: molecular diagnosis when tissue biopsy is not feasible, initial molecular staging in high-CSF-shedding tumors, serial MRD monitoring, and discrimination of true progression from pseudoprogression. It is also clinically actionable in several of these settings, whereas its outcome-improving value remains to be demonstrated in prospective interventional studies. The principal barriers to broader adoption, in our view, are therefore standardization of workflow and the generation of prospective evidence that CSF-guided decisions improve outcomes, rather than analytical performance. The review is accordingly structured around three aims: to critically appraise the clinical evidence supporting these four indications, to propose a decision-making framework with graded evidence levels for defined diagnostic and monitoring scenarios, and to identify the pre-analytical, organizational, and research priorities that remain.

Molecular technologies for CSF cfDNA analysis

A range of molecular technologies can be applied to pediatric CSF cfDNA, each with distinct trade-offs between breadth of coverage, analytical sensitivity, input requirements, and turnaround. This section covers sequencing-based approaches, the primary focus of the review, alongside PCR-based methods that serve as complementary tools for targeted, high-sensitivity monitoring of predefined alterations. Targeted sequencing panels

Targeted NGS assays, based on either amplicon or hybridization-capture chemistry, enable deep sequencing of predefined gene sets from the minimal cfDNA quantities recoverable from pediatric CSF. Hybrid-capture approaches, such as MSK-IMPACT, provide broad coverage of clinically relevant genes and detect single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions/deletions, copy number variations (CNVs), and structural rearrangements at variant allele frequencies (VAFs) as low as 0.5% [45, 52]. Smaller panels such as UW-OncoPlex have demonstrated feasibility from as little as 200 µL of CSF, with detection of clinically actionable variants including gene fusions [53]. Amplicon-based methods offer an alternative when input DNA is extremely limited, achieving sequencing depths exceeding 50,000, but their scope is restricted to predefined targets [42]. The choice between approaches depends on the clinical question: broad panels are preferred for initial characterization, while focused assays may suffice for monitoring known mutations.

Low-pass whole-genome sequencing

Low-pass whole-genome sequencing (LP-WGS), also referred to as shallow (sWGS) or low-coverage WGS (lcWGS), provides genome-wide copy number profiling at shallow sequencing depth (typically 0.1–1 × coverage). Because many pediatric CNS tumors harbor characteristic chromosomal gains and losses (such as isodicentric chromosome 17q, monosomy 6, or MYCN amplification in embryonal tumors), LP-WGS can identify tumor-derived cfDNA through its copy number signature without prior knowledge of specific mutations [16, 65]. This makes it particularly well suited for minimal residual disease assessment, where the copy number profile serves as a quantitative surrogate for tumor burden.

Ultra-low-input protocols have extended this approach to picogram-level DNA quantities, achieving high technical success rates across multiple tumor types in real-world pediatric cohorts [20]. Combined workflows pairing LP-WGS for genome-wide CNV profiling with targeted panels for mutation detection are increasingly adopted as a pragmatic strategy that leverages the complementary strengths of both approaches [46, 47, 52].

Emerging approaches: methylation profiling and nanopore sequencing

DNA methylation-based classification has redefined the diagnostic neuropathology of CNS tumors over the past decade. The reference dataset assembled by Capper et al. [10] now underpins integrated diagnosis under the WHO 2021 framework [33], and several pediatric CNS tumor entities (including medulloblastoma molecular subgroups, ependymoma molecular groups, and embryonal tumors with multilayered rosettes) are now defined principally by their methylation signatures. Translating this paradigm from tumor tissue to CSF cfDNA represents one of the most consequential recent developments in pediatric neuro-oncology liquid biopsy, because tumor-derived cfDNA fragments retain the methylation patterns of their cell of origin and CSF preserves these signatures sufficiently for downstream classification despite the small fragment lengths and ultra-low quantities typical of pediatric specimens.

Several technical approaches have been applied to CSF cfDNA. Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing provided the first proof of concept: Li et al. accurately resolved medulloblastoma molecular subgroups directly from CSF cfDNA [31], demonstrating that the granularity required for clinically actionable classification is achievable from a liquid biopsy substrate. Reduced-representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) extends this to a broader pediatric tumor spectrum at lower sequencing cost, with Cornelli et al. demonstrating diagnostic-level classification across multiple entities [15]. Bisulfite chemistry, however, damages DNA in a manner that compounds with low input, a particular concern for pediatric CSF, which has prompted growing interest in immunoprecipitation-based and enzymatic alternatives. Cell-free methylated DNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (cfMeDIP-seq), introduced by Shen et al. [56], enables 5-methylcytosine profiling from nanogram-level cfDNA without bisulfite conversion and has been applied successfully across multiple cancer types; enzymatic methyl-sequencing similarly preserves fragment integrity and is becoming an attractive option for picogram-level pediatric CSF inputs.

Comparative studies have consistently positioned CSF as the substrate of choice. Iser et al. demonstrated that CSF substantially outperforms matched plasma for methylation-based glioma classification [27], confirming the compartment-specific advantage already established for mutation calling. In adult intra-axial tumors, Zuccato et al. showed that CSF methylome-based classification approaches the accuracy of standard-of-care tissue diagnosis and exceeds both plasma methylome and CSF genomic alteration-based approaches [69]. In the pediatric setting specifically, plasma-based methylation profiling has shown discriminative performance between tumor and non-neoplastic disease [3] but remains limited by the very low circulating tumor fractions characteristic of childhood CNS malignancies, reinforcing the rationale for CSF as the preferred biofluid.

A particularly important property of CSF methylation profiling is that a single sequencing run can yield three concurrent layers of clinically relevant information: tumor classification aligned with the WHO 2021 reference, genome-wide copy number profiles derived from coverage variation, and (with sufficient input) tumor microenvironment composition through cellular deconvolution. The M-PACT deep neural network recently introduced by Smith et al. implements this multi-task framework on pediatric CSF cfDNA [58], producing the closest CSF analogue yet of the integrated tissue diagnosis defined by the WHO 2021 framework [10, 33].

Two practical caveats remain. First, classifier calibration scores (the cfDNA analogue of the tissue methylation classifier confidence threshold) require CSF-specific validation, since reference cohorts are predominantly tissue-derived and tumor fraction in CSF is typically lower than in resected specimens; classifications below conservative confidence thresholds should be reported with explicit reservation. Second, methylation-based assays generally require higher cfDNA input than LP-WGS, and the ongoing optimization of ultra-low-input protocols [20] remains a prerequisite for routine clinical use in centers handling small pediatric CSF volumes.

Nanopore sequencing extends methylation-based classification into a real-time, single-run modality. Because long-read sequencers report base and methylation calls within milliseconds of a strand traversing the pore and require only minutes of library preparation, a single run can in principle yield genome-wide copy number variations, methylation-based tumor class, and point mutations together, with same-day or even intraoperative turnaround. On tissue, adaptive nanopore workflows such as Rapid-CNS2 [51] and ROBIN [18] deliver integrated methylation and copy number diagnoses within a day or during surgery, and rapid probabilistic classifiers have recently pushed live methylation-based classification to within roughly 15 min of sequencing [7]. Applied to liquid biopsy, Afflerbach et al. demonstrated nanopore-based classification of tumor-derived cfDNA directly from CSF [1], the same rapid-classifier framework has been extended to pediatric CSF cfDNA with accurate calls in samples meeting a minimum tumor fraction threshold [7], and pre-operative CSF classification by nanopore has also been reported [62]. CSF-specific validation nonetheless remains early, confined to small cohorts and dependent on sufficient cfDNA input and tumor fraction (Table 1).

PCR-based approaches as complements to NGS

Although not strictly sequencing techniques, digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) and reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) are integral to the CSF cfDNA analytical toolkit and are therefore covered here. They provide exceptional analytical sensitivity for predefined targets and serve as complementary tools alongside broader NGS assays, most commonly for longitudinal monitoring of a known driver alteration identified by prior sequencing. ddPCR enables absolute quantification of specific mutations at VAFs below 0.1%, making it particularly useful for tracking known driver alterations such as H3F3A K27M in diffuse midline gliomas [50, 60]. RT-PCR-based quantification of cerebrospinal fluid microRNAs has also shown diagnostic promise, particularly for germ cell tumors where specific miRNA panels achieve high sensitivity and specificity [35, 43]. Together, these PCR-based methods offer cost-effective, rapid, and highly sensitive monitoring tools that complement the broader genomic coverage of NGS platforms.

Pre-analytical and analytical considerations

CSF collection and transport

Practical pre-analytical and analytical recommendations for clinical CSF-NGS are summarized in Table 2 and discussed in detail below. The route of CSF acquisition (lumbar puncture (LP), ventricular reservoir (Ommaya), or external ventricular drain (EVD)) can influence ctDNA concentration and composition. Ventricular CSF may contain higher cfDNA levels for supratentorial tumors, whereas lumbar CSF may be more informative for infratentorial or disseminated disease [38, 52]. Consistent with this, Madlener et al. found in pediatric glioma that ddPCR detection of H3F3A K27M or BRAF V600E in CSF tracked the proximity of the sampling site to the tumor: detection reached 100% in intra-operatively obtained CSF, 93% in ventricular CSF (Ommaya reservoir or shunt), and 66% by lumbar puncture [36]. Where feasible, the CSF aspiration site should therefore be chosen to maximize proximity to the tumor and to any involved leptomeningeal compartment.

Blood contamination during collection introduces genomic DNA from leukocytes, which dilutes the tumor signal and complicates downstream analysis; the use of low-bind polypropylene tubes and careful needle placement to minimize traumatic taps is therefore essential [66]. CSF volumes as small as 200 µL have proven sufficient for targeted sequencing [53], although larger volumes (1–3 mL) improve cfDNA yield and analytical sensitivity for genome-wide approaches [20, 65].

Prompt processing is critical. cfDNA degrades rapidly through nuclease activity, and delays beyond 1 to 2 h allow leukocyte lysis, increasing background genomic DNA noise. When immediate centrifugation is not feasible, dedicated cfDNA-stabilization tubes (e.g., Streck or PAXgene) preserve sample integrity for up to 48–72 h at room temperature [12, 30]. A standardized double-spin protocol (an initial low-speed centrifugation to remove cells followed by a high-speed spin to clear residual debris) is recommended before cfDNA extraction or storage at − 80 °C [59, 66].

cfDNA extraction and library preparation

Pediatric CSF typically yields ultra-low cfDNA quantities, with reported median concentrations ranging from 54.6 pg/µL to 2.8 ng/mL depending on disease burden and tumor type [42, 65]. Extraction kits specifically validated for low-input cfDNA, such as column-based or bead-based protocols optimized for short DNA fragments (90–150 bp), are essential; standard genomic DNA kits designed for higher inputs perform poorly in this context [12, 64]. Elution volumes should be minimized (10–20 µL) to maintain detectable concentrations.

Library preparation must similarly accommodate low inputs. Unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) are strongly recommended, as they enable computational removal of PCR duplicates and sequencing artifacts, thereby enhancing the reliable detection of variants at low allele frequencies [46, 47, 52]. Input-adaptive library preparation protocols that adjust enzymatic conditions based on cfDNA quantity further improve success rates. Amplicon-based methods provide high depth from minimal input but are limited to predefined regions, whereas hybrid-capture approaches offer broader genomic coverage at the cost of higher input requirements [39].

Analytical validation and quality control

Reliable interpretation of CSF-NGS results requires rigorous quality control at every step. Key metrics include library complexity, duplication rate, on-target coverage uniformity, and noise thresholds for copy number segmentation [46, 47]. For LP-WGS, the estimation of tumor fraction (the proportion of sequenced cfDNA derived from tumor cells) is essential to distinguish copy number variations from noise, and several computational tools (ichorCNA, Wisecondor) are now routinely used for this purpose [16, 20]. The tumor fraction threshold for reliable CNV detection by LP-WGS is not fixed: it ranges from approximately 3% to 10% depending on sequencing depth, the size of the copy number segment considered, and the analytical method used, so a single universal cut-off should be avoided. Tumor fraction estimation is also not yet a standardized clinical metric: practices vary between laboratories, and because copy-number-based estimators assume clonal copy number changes, subclonal or sparse copy number variations can cause under- or mis-estimation, so estimates should be reported with the method specified and treated as approximate. For targeted panels, analytical sensitivity thresholds must be defined prospectively; hybrid-capture approaches typically achieve reliable variant detection at VAFs of 0.5–1%, while amplicon methods may detect variants at 0.4% but with higher false-positive rates [42, 45].

Importantly, a negative CSF-NGS result requires careful interpretation. True negatives (absence of ctDNA because the tumor does not shed into CSF) must be distinguished from technical failures (insufficient cfDNA for analysis). Tam et al. reported that 30 of 61 CSF samples were insufficient for LP-WGS analysis, reflecting both biological absence of tumor DNA and low DNA quantity [65]. Reporting standards should explicitly communicate sample adequacy, tumor fraction estimates, and the limits of detection achieved for each individual sample, so that clinicians can appropriately contextualize results.

Toward standardization

Despite the growing number of CSF-NGS studies in pediatric neuro-oncology, no international consensus currently exists for CSF collection, processing, or analytical workflows in this setting. Teunissen et al. proposed a general consensus protocol for CSF biobanking over a decade ago [66], but this predates modern cfDNA applications. More recently, Bagley et al. published minimum technical and pre-analytical data elements recommended for CSF cfDNA reporting in neuro-oncology [6], providing a framework that could be adopted by pediatric centers. The development of multi-center harmonization protocols and external quality assurance programs remains an urgent priority if CSF-NGS is to transition from single-institution research to a widely adopted clinical tool.

Embryonal tumors: medulloblastoma, ATRT, and ETMR

Embryonal tumors are the strongest CSF-NGS use case, owing to their propensity for leptomeningeal dissemination and to recurrent copy number alterations amenable to LP-WGS. Within this group, medulloblastoma is also the entity in which CSF-NGS engages most directly with the molecular taxonomy now embedded in WHO 2021. The international consensus framework recognizes four molecular subgroups (WNT, SHH, Group 3, Group 4) with distinct cellular origins, copy number landscapes, clinical behavior, and therapeutic implications [11], and contemporary risk-adapted protocols leverage these subgroups to calibrate treatment intensity: from de-escalation in WNT to intensification in metastatic Group 3 [55]. Subgroup assignment is therefore not merely descriptive but operational, which makes its non-invasive recovery from CSF a clinically meaningful endpoint.

At initial staging, LP-WGS detects tumor-derived CNVs in 80–100% of samples versus 11–20% by cytology across three independent cohorts [13, 16, 65], a consistent four- to ninefold advantage. Copy number profiles concurrently identify prognostically relevant alterations including isodicentric 17q, monosomy 6, and MYCN amplification [13, 16], and subgroup-defining cytogenetic patterns (notably the WNT-associated monosomy 6) can be inferred directly from the CSF copy number profile. Beyond CNVs, methylation-based classification on CSF cfDNA recapitulates subgroup assignment: Li et al. resolved WNT and SHH medulloblastoma subgroups directly from CSF [31] and Smith et al. extended this to a multi-task neural network framework [58]. Ronsley et al. demonstrated 100% concordance between CSF- and tissue-derived somatic mutations using a validated targeted panel, confirming that small-panel approaches complement LP-WGS in this setting [53]. Of note, MYCN amplification carries strong prognostic weight but remains undruggable at present; its detection by CSF LP-WGS therefore contributes to risk stratification rather than target identification in this context. Beyond genome-wide profiling, targeted ddPCR provides a focused route to individual copy number markers: Stepien et al. used ddPCR of MYC amplification to monitor group 3 medulloblastoma in CSF [63], a PCR-based complement to LP-WGS when a single recurrent amplification is the monitoring target.

Medulloblastoma is also the most validated indication for serial MRD monitoring. In the landmark prospective study by Liu et al. (476 samples, 123 patients), baseline MRD positivity reached 85% in metastatic and 54% in localized disease, persistent positivity predicted progression, and molecular detection preceded imaging recurrence by approximately 3 months in half of relapsed patients [32]. Tumor-associated CNVs remained detectable in 62% of cytology-negative samples. Escudero et al. and Kojic et al. provided independent validation in medulloblastoma and in broader pediatric cohorts using patient-specific mutation screening [19, 29]. For ATRT, diagnosis rests on demonstrating bi-allelic inactivation of SMARCB1 (or, in the rare SMARCB1-retained variant, SMARCA4); methylation profiling additionally resolves the three molecular subgroups (ATRT-TYR, ATRT-SHH, further divisible into SHH-1 and SHH-2, and ATRT-MYC) [25], which are biologically distinct and prognostically relevant [21], even though they do not yet carry direct therapeutic implications. These alterations and methylation classes provide entry points for both targeted-panel and methylome-based CSF profiling, although dedicated CSF-NGS cohort data remain modest. For ETMR, RT-PCR of miR-517a (a marker of the defining C19MC amplification) offers a complementary entity-specific biomarker supporting diagnosis and relapse detection [35].

Diffuse midline gliomas, brainstem gliomas, and related H3K27me3-loss tumors

DMG and brainstem tumors represent the highest biopsy-risk scenario, and CSF-NGS has had its most immediate diagnostic impact here. Hill et al. achieved molecular diagnosis in 60–64% of unbiopsied patients, identifying disease-defining alterations in up to 90% of biopsied cases tested in parallel [24]. This is built on foundational work establishing H3K27M detection in CSF by ddPCR [26, 50, 60] and by multi-gene targeted sequencing [8]. Several factors likely contribute to this incomplete yield. ctDNA shedding into CSF varies with tumor cellularity, contact with CSF pathways, and disease burden, so low-shedding tumors can fall below the assay limit of detection, while pre-analytical factors (sampling route, CSF volume, timing relative to radiotherapy, blood contamination) and the cfDNA quantity recovered modulate detection further. Mutation-based panels and ddPCR also capture H3K27M and other covered alterations but can miss epigenetically defined cases (such as EZHIP-overexpressing H3-wildtype DMG) that require methylation profiling; the higher parallel yield in biopsied cases (up to 90%) is consistent with both biological non-shedding and assay coverage limits in the unbiopsied group.

Beyond the canonical H3K27M mutation, the WHO 2021 entity diffuse midline glioma, H3K27-altered, explicitly recognizes EZHIP overexpression as an alternative molecular mechanism that converges on the same H3K27me3-loss phenotype through PRC2 inhibition [33]. This convergence extends across diagnostic boundaries: posterior fossa group A (PFA) ependymomas, although biologically and clinically distinct from DMG, share the same global loss of H3K27me3 and frequently overexpress EZHIP, such that EZHIP immunohistochemistry has been validated as a sensitive and specific tissue biomarker for both PFA-EPN and DMG H3-wildtype with EZHIP overexpression [4]. The clinical implication for liquid biopsy is direct: EZHIP overexpression cannot be detected by mutation-calling panels and is invisible to ddPCR-based H3K27M assays. CSF methylation profiling [27, 31, 58, 69], by contrast, can in principle capture both H3K27M-mutant DMG and EZHIP-driven H3-wildtype DMG through their shared epigenetic signature, and may extend to PFA-EPN classification by the same mechanism. Clinically, however, the molecular convergence with DMG matters less than the radiological context: DMG arises in the brainstem and is usually evident on imaging, so the diagnostic value of CSF-based classification for a posterior fossa PFA ependymoma lies chiefly in distinguishing it from other posterior fossa tumors, particularly embryonal tumors such as medulloblastoma. Dedicated CSF-NGS cohorts in PFA ependymoma remain limited at present, and the available data are dominated by mixed-entity series in which ependymomas are represented in small numbers [41]; this represents a clear and tractable evidence gap (further discussed in §6).

A second distinct indication in this group is discrimination of true progression from pseudoprogression. Serial H3K27M VAF tracking in CSF predicted response to ONC201 and resolved ambiguous imaging in defined cases [9, 28]. In a proof-of-concept application, Arthur et al. reported CSF-guided molecular targeted therapy in an otherwise inoperable brainstem neoplasm [5], illustrating the direct therapeutic potential when tissue is inaccessible.

High-grade and low-grade supratentorial gliomas

Supratentorial gliomas display marked heterogeneity in CSF-NGS performance, driven by tumor grade and by proximity to the ventricular system. Nobre et al. reported positivity of 84% in high-grade versus 50% in low-grade gliomas (overall 70% in active disease) [45], and Miller et al. found approximately 47% positivity across 12 histopathological subtypes with detection strongly associated with disseminated disease [41]. Iser et al. further demonstrated CSF-based glioma classification aligned with the WHO methylation framework [27]. This classification capability also helps resolve clinically ambiguous lesions: applying M-PACT to CSF cfDNA, Smith et al. could distinguish a true relapse from a secondary malignancy, for example a secondary glioblastoma arising after treatment for medulloblastoma [58].

Longitudinal CSF sampling uniquely captures clonal evolution under therapeutic pressure: Miller et al. first showed that serial CSF cfDNA tracks clonal dynamics and identifies emergent resistance mutations absent from the original resection [40]. Across grades, CSF-NGS supports identification of therapeutically actionable alterations (BRAF V600E, NTRK fusions, and FGFR alterations) when tissue is unavailable or unrepresentative [41, 45]. For several of these, detection now carries direct therapeutic consequences rather than only trial eligibility: BRAF V600E and NTRK fusions have approved targeted agents in pediatric tumors (BRAF/MEK and TRK inhibitors, respectively), whereas other alterations support enrollment in molecularly stratified trials such as AcSé-ESMART, in which eligibility is driven by pathway activation rather than by single mutations [22]. Beyond detection, such actionable drivers can be tracked serially by ddPCR: Madlener et al. showed that CSF BRAF V600E allele frequency falls under BRAF/MEK inhibition (trametinib and dabrafenib), providing the first ddPCR-based treatment-response readout for this alteration in glioma [36], complementing the CSF-guided targeted-therapy proof of concept reported in a brainstem tumor [5]

Comparative performance: CSF versus cytology and plasma

Two comparisons apply across tumor types. First, in staging cohorts dominated by embryonal and other high-CSF-shedding tumors, CSF-NGS outperforms cytology by a four- to ninefold margin (80–100% vs 11–20% across three independent pediatric cohorts [13, 16, 65]), and Liu et al. additionally recovered tumor-associated CNVs in 62% of cytology-negative samples [32]. Molecular assays also characterize disease that cytology cannot resolve at all, such as the MYC amplification tracked by ddPCR in group 3 medulloblastoma [63]. These figures are cohort-dependent: cytology yield itself varies with disease burden and sampling practice, and the CSF-NGS advantage narrows in lower-shedding tumor types (§4.3). Cytology retains value as a rapid screening tool, but its false-negative rate limits molecular characterization and MRD assessment.

Second, CSF outperforms plasma as a CNS liquid biopsy substrate. De Mattos-Arruda et al. first demonstrated higher genomic fidelity of CSF over plasma [17]; Nobre et al. reported 89% versus 4% detection in matched samples [45]; and Pagès et al. confirmed this in a tri-compartmental pediatric study of 564 specimens from 258 patients [49]. Plasma retains complementary value primarily in overt blood–brain barrier disruption. Madlener et al. add nuance to this comparison: in pediatric glioma, CSF outperformed plasma for H3F3A K27M (sensitivity 85% versus 74%; AUC 0.92 versus 0.70), whereas for BRAF V600E plasma was at least as informative as CSF (AUC 0.91 versus 0.87), indicating that the optimal compartment is partly marker-dependent [36]. This pattern also contrasts with adult neuro-oncology, where plasma liquid biopsy plays a comparatively larger role, although mainly for brain metastases and leptomeningeal disease from systemic cancers, in which plasma ctDNA reflects the extracranial primary. For primary CNS tumors, the blood–brain barrier limits plasma yield in adults as in children, so CSF remains the preferred compartment; the pediatric setting is skewed further towards CSF because primary childhood CNS tumors rarely provide a systemic source of circulating tumor DNA.

Integrating CSF-NGS into clinical practice

“Molecular technologies for CSF cfDNA analysis” to “Clinical applications” have established that CSF-NGS is analytically capable and clinically informative across multiple pediatric CNS tumor types, while the evidence supporting its clinical use remains largely observational and the field remains pre-standardization. We therefore frame CSF-NGS as an emerging approach that is already applicable in selected contexts: the evidence base is sufficient to define candidate indications and grade them by level of evidence, but clinical adoption remains heterogeneous and largely confined to specialist centers, and prospective demonstration that CSF-guided decisions improve outcomes is still lacking. Throughout, neuroimaging, in particular MRI, remains the clinical gold standard for diagnosis, staging, response assessment, and surveillance; CSF-NGS is positioned as a molecular complement to imaging rather than a replacement, adding entity-level molecular specificity, detecting measurable residual disease before imaging recurrence, and helping resolve situations that imaging alone cannot adjudicate. In this section, we propose a structured framework for integrating CSF-NGS into defined pediatric neuro-oncology diagnostic and monitoring pathways, built around three elements: a set of clinical indications graded by evidence level (Table 5) and mapped onto a decision algorithm that follows the clinical pathway from diagnosis to follow-up (Fig. 1); an operational workflow specifying the roles and decision moments of the multidisciplinary team; and a discussion of the regulatory, ethical, and economic considerations that condition broader adoption. Throughout this section, we use "CSF-NGS" as a practical shorthand for the CSF molecular-profiling workflow as a whole. Although sequencing is its principal engine, the indications and workflow proposed here also encompass PCR-based assays where these are the method of choice: ddPCR for absolute quantification of a known driver (for example H3K27M) in serial monitoring and in the discrimination of progression from pseudoprogression, and RT-PCR of microRNAs in germ cell tumors and ETMR.

Proposed clinical indications

Building on the tumor-type evidence reviewed above, we propose four indications for which the current evidence supports defined, evidence-graded clinical use, plus one transversal role (Fig. 1, Table 5). These are (1) molecular diagnosis when tissue biopsy is not feasible; (2) initial molecular staging in high-CSF-shedding tumors; (3) serial MRD monitoring and early relapse detection; and (4) discrimination of true progression from pseudoprogression. The associated tumor types, expected performance, and evidence level for each are summarized in Table 5. Transversally, CSF-NGS supports identification of therapeutically actionable alterations (BRAF V600E, NTRK fusions, FGFR alterations) across all entities when tissue is unavailable, enabling molecularly matched treatment, whether an approved targeted agent or enrollment in a molecularly stratified trial, throughout the clinical pathway.

Indications are graded in Table 5 using the Oxford CEBM 2011 framework. Indications (ii) and (iii) reach Level 2 on the basis of multiple consistent cohorts and large prospective studies; (1) and (4) sit at Level 3, pending prospective interventional validation. Figure 1 maps the four indications onto the diagnostic and follow-up phases of the clinical pathway, distinguishing proactive use (scheduled staging and MRD) from reactive use (pseudoprogression discrimination).

Two further considerations situate these indications within routine practice. First, molecular classification is a fast-moving field: AI-based methylation classifiers (“Emerging approaches: methylation profiling and nanopore sequencing”) increasingly enable entity-level diagnosis directly from CSF, including pre-operatively, where pre-surgical CSF classification has been shown with nanopore sequencing [62]; benchmarking such classifiers against the eventual tissue diagnosis is also the most direct way to characterize false negatives, which remain concentrated in low-shedding tumors and distal lumbar sampling. Second, beyond the four core indications, CSF-NGS can help adjudicate dilemmas that imaging resolves poorly: distinguishing true recurrence from a secondary malignancy, as when a methylation classifier separates a secondary glioblastoma from a medulloblastoma relapse (“High-grade and low-grade supratentorial gliomas”), and distinguishing tumor from treatment-related inflammation in diffuse glioma, where detection of a tumor-derived molecular signal supports tumor while a negative result must be weighed against the false-negative rate in low-shedding gliomas.

Multidisciplinary workflow

Operationalizing the framework proposed in Fig. 1 requires a multidisciplinary team with clearly delineated responsibilities at each decision point of the clinical pathway. At diagnosis, the treating neuro-oncologist, in concert with the neurosurgeon, determines whether tissue biopsy is feasible and, if not, initiates the request for CSF molecular profiling as part of the initial diagnostic workup, in coordination with the molecular pathology laboratory [24, 61]. At staging, CSF-NGS should be piggybacked onto the lumbar puncture already performed for conventional cytology and intrathecal chemotherapy, avoiding any additional invasive procedure for the child [13, 16]. During follow-up, MRD monitoring should be scheduled prospectively at treatment initiation, embedded in the treatment protocol rather than ordered reactively, with sampling timepoints aligned with existing imaging or intrathecal therapy visits. In contrast, CSF-NGS to discriminate true progression from pseudoprogression is a reactive indication, triggered at the imaging review meeting when radiographic findings are inconclusive [9, 28].

Rapid sample transport to the molecular laboratory, standardized pre-analytical processing within 1 to 2 h (or the use of cfDNA-stabilization tubes), and timely turnaround of results are essential for clinical utility (see Table 2) [6, 66]. We propose, as an aspirational target rather than a description of current typical practice, a turnaround of 5 to 7 working days for diagnostic and progression-related indications and of 2 to 3 weeks for scheduled MRD monitoring. Whether this target is attainable depends heavily on the assay, the sequencing platform, and the institution: rapid PCR-based methods and nanopore approaches can return results within a few days, whereas hybrid-capture panels, LP-WGS performed on standard short-read instruments (e.g., Illumina, Element Biosciences, or Thermo Fisher), and methylation-based classifiers more commonly require 1 to 3 weeks, depending on the laboratory (Table 1). Send-out testing to reference laboratories, sample batching, and local validation status add further variability, and real-world turnaround in pediatric centers has not yet been systematically benchmarked. Results should be reviewed at the molecular or neuro-oncology tumor board before any therapeutic decision is taken, with a written report signed out by the molecular pathologist that communicates not only detected alterations but also sample adequacy, tumor fraction estimates, and assay limits of detection, enabling clinicians to interpret both positive and negative results in their appropriate context [6]. Centers without on-site CSF-NGS capability should establish formal referral pathways to reference laboratories and, where available, participate in pan-European or national standardization initiatives [57] to ensure reproducibility and comparability across institutions.

Regulatory, infrastructural, and economic barriers

Beyond workflow standardization, the regulatory status of CSF-NGS is unsettled and jurisdiction-dependent. Most CSF cfDNA assays are currently offered as laboratory-developed tests (LDTs), designed, validated, and used within a single laboratory, rather than as commercially distributed in vitro diagnostic (IVD) kits; the two pathways carry very different validation and documentation requirements. In the United States, LDTs have historically been overseen under the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments rather than as FDA-regulated devices; a 2024 federal rule that would have brought LDTs under device regulation [14] was vacated by a federal court in 2025 and subsequently rescinded, leaving LDTs under existing laboratory oversight for now. In the European Union, in-house tests manufactured and used within a single health institution are exempt from most IVDR requirements (Article 5(5), [70]), but only if the laboratory is ISO 15189-accredited, operates an appropriate quality-management system, publicly declares conformity with the Annex I safety and performance requirements, and documents that no equivalent CE-marked device on the market meets the patient group’s needs; this last condition will progressively narrow the in-house route as commercial CSF-NGS assays reach the market. This evolving, jurisdiction-specific landscape is itself an obstacle to the multi-center harmonization on which broader adoption depends.

Adoption also depends on infrastructure and bioinformatics capacity that few centers currently possess: accredited handling of ultra-low-input cfDNA, suitable sequencing platforms and UMI-compatible chemistries, and validated, version-controlled pipelines for variant calling, tumor fraction estimation, and methylation classification, together with molecular pathologists and bioinformaticians for interpretation and sign-out. These requirements concentrate capability in specialist centers and reinforce the case for formal referral pathways (§5.2) rather than uneven local adoption. The same heterogeneity of platforms and pipelines underlies the inter-laboratory reproducibility gap and the need for external quality assurance noted in §3.4 and §6.1.2, while the absence of cost-effectiveness data and dedicated reimbursement pathways (§6.1.3) constrains equitable access even where the assay is technically feasible.

Limitations and future directions

Current limitations

Despite the compelling evidence summarized in this review, several important limitations must be acknowledged. We organize them into three categories (limitations of the evidence base, biological and technical ceilings, and ethical and implementation challenges) to distinguish problems that require more data, more technology, or more deliberation.

Limitations of the evidence base

First and most fundamentally, no prospective interventional trial has yet tested whether acting on a CSF-NGS result modifies clinical outcomes. The existing evidence, including the largest prospective cohorts [32], is observational: it demonstrates that CSF-NGS detects tumor DNA, tracks tumor burden, and anticipates relapse, but not that management guided by these findings improves survival, reduces toxicity, or enables more timely salvage. This is the distinction between a mature biomarker and a validated clinical intervention.

Second, published cohorts are affected by marked selection and publication biases. Patients with advanced, disseminated, or high-shedding tumors, particularly embryonal tumors, are overrepresented because they are those for whom CSF sampling is clinically justified and tumor signal is most readily detected, while cohorts yielding low detection rates or high rates of technical failure are underrepresented in the published record. Detection rates cited throughout this review therefore probably overestimate performance in routine pediatric practice, where a broader spectrum of disease severity is encountered. Third, inter-study heterogeneity in sampling route, assay platform, analytical pipeline, and reporting format precludes formal meta-analytic synthesis and complicates cross-cohort comparison [6].

Biological and technical limitations

Analytical sensitivity is bounded by tumor biology. For localized, low-grade, or deeply parenchymal tumors that shed minimally into the CSF, detection rates fall to approximately 50% [45], and a negative result does not exclude disease. This is a biological ceiling, not a technical failing, and it defines the boundaries of clinical applicability, a point that should be made explicit in both result reports and the multidisciplinary interpretation of negative findings.

At the opposite analytical extreme, increasingly sensitive assays capable of detecting variants at VAF ≤ 0.1% raise the complementary risk of ultra-sensitive false positives. Sequencing noise, clonal hematopoiesis-like phenomena detectable in cfDNA, age-related background variants, and technical artifacts at the limit of detection can generate signal that is analytically real but clinically meaningless. As detection thresholds are pushed lower in the pursuit of earlier relapse detection, specificity becomes as important as sensitivity, and orthogonal confirmation of low-VAF calls will be increasingly necessary.

Finally, the absence of harmonized pre-analytical workflows, sequencing platforms, bioinformatic pipelines, and reporting standards remains a technical limitation with direct clinical consequences: it prevents confident pooling of data across institutions, hinders external quality assurance, and slows the accumulation of the multi-center evidence on which future guidelines will depend.

Ethical and implementation limitations

The most pressing implementation question is not whether CSF-NGS can detect MRD but how persistent positivity should be acted upon. Clinically actionable MRD thresholds have not been established, and the risk of overinterpretation is real: low-level ctDNA positivity in the absence of clinical or radiographic progression may not always warrant therapeutic escalation, and premature intensification carries its own toxicity in children.

This is compounded by a diagnostic–therapeutic gap: for several pediatric CNS tumor types in which CSF-NGS can detect molecular relapse, no evidence-based salvage therapy currently alters outcomes once that relapse is documented. Detecting disease that cannot yet be acted upon creates a distinct ethical tension in pediatric oncology, and one that merits explicit discussion with families at the time of consent.

Finally, no formal cost-effectiveness analyses have been published for CSF-NGS in pediatric neuro-oncology [23]. Although plausible arguments can be made that early relapse detection and MRD-guided therapy modification reduce downstream healthcare burden, these arguments remain theoretical and constitute a major gap in the case for routine reimbursement and equitable access across healthcare systems.

Future directions

These limitations are not intractable, and several converging developments are likely to accelerate the clinical maturation of CSF-NGS. Multi-omic approaches integrating mutational profiling, methylation-based classification, and proteomic or extracellular vesicle analysis within a single CSF sample are moving towards comprehensive, single-platform diagnostics [38, 54, 58]. Artificial intelligence, exemplified by the M-PACT deep neural network for methylation-based tumor classification [58], will further enhance diagnostic accuracy and may enable predictive modeling that integrates longitudinal molecular data with imaging and clinical parameters. Beyond mutation calling and methylation profiling, fragmentomic analysis of cfDNA (leveraging fragment-length distributions, end-motif patterns, and tumor fraction estimates) has recently emerged as a complementary layer of information accessible from the same low-pass sequencing data. Markowitz et al. applied this approach to CSF cfDNA from the Liu et al. medulloblastoma cohort and achieved subgroup classification at an AUC of 0.94 [37], opening the prospect of pre-operative molecular subgrouping with direct implications for neurosurgical planning and risk-adapted therapy.

Nanopore sequencing holds particular promise for same-day molecular diagnosis from CSF, potentially transforming intraoperative and point-of-care decision-making [1, 18]. International consensus initiatives addressing pre-analytical standards, analytical validation requirements, and minimum reporting elements are urgently needed and are beginning to emerge [6, 57]. Most critically, prospective clinical trials incorporating CSF-NGS as a molecular eligibility criterion or MRD-guided treatment stratification endpoint are required to demonstrate that molecular monitoring translates into improved patient outcomes. Upcoming international pediatric medulloblastoma protocols are expected to incorporate scheduled CSF cfDNA monitoring as part of the standard treatment pathway, with predefined collection timepoints aligned to surgery, post-induction restaging, end of therapy, and surveillance MRI, an operational model that will generate the prospective, harmonized data currently missing from the field. Adaptive trial designs in which therapy intensity is modulated based on real-time CSF ctDNA kinetics represent a particularly promising avenue for medulloblastoma and other high-risk embryonal tumors [32].

Conclusion

CSF-based NGS has moved beyond analytical proof-of-concept and is now clinically informative in selected pediatric CNS tumor scenarios. Standardization of workflows and evidence of interventional benefit, rather than analytical feasibility alone, now set the pace of broader clinical adoption. We close with four recommendations ordered by implementation priority. First, pediatric neuro-oncology centers should consider integrating CSF-NGS into defined diagnostic and monitoring pathways for the four indications proposed in this review: molecular diagnosis when biopsy is infeasible, initial staging in high-CSF-shedding tumors, serial MRD monitoring, and discrimination of true progression from pseudoprogression. Centers without on-site capability should establish formal referral pathways rather than defer access. Second, multi-center standardization of pre-analytical and analytical workflows is the single most impactful short-term priority, and should include external quality assurance schemes adapted to the ultra-low-input cfDNA quantities typical of pediatric CSF. Third, the field needs published real-world performance benchmarks stratified by tumor type and clinically actionable MRD thresholds defining when persistent ctDNA positivity should trigger therapeutic escalation, both requiring coordinated multi-center registries rather than single-institution cohorts. Fourth, prospective interventional trials with adaptive designs modulating therapy intensity on real-time CSF ctDNA kinetics should be embedded into the next generation of international pediatric brain tumor consortium protocols.
