Medication use in elderly patients with hypertension: a cross-sectional study at a medical center in Vietnam, 2024–2025
Authors: Van Thi To Nguyen, Hung Van Nguyen, Tuyen Duy Pham, Giang Thanh Nguyen, Thuy Thi Thu Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension (HTN) is a leading cause of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications, especially in the elderly with multiple risk factors. In addition, low treatment adherence poses a significant challenge, requiring effective solutions to improve healthcare quality.MethodsA cross-sectional descriptive study based on prescriptions of patients aged ≥ 60 years diagnosed with hypertension in District 5, who met the inclusion criteria and did not violate the exclusion criteria from August 2024 to February 2025.ResultsThe study sample included 720 patients, with a male-to-female ratio of 1:1.26. The findings showed that prescriptions containing five or more drugs (37.8%) and those with three or more antihypertensive drugs (44.9%) were predominant. Among antihypertensive medications, angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) and bisoprolol were the most commonly prescribed (79.3% and 56.94%, respectively). The rate of potential drug–drug interactions was notably high at 72.4%, with 1.9% being severe interactions. Factors associated with drug–drug interactions included the use of calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, diuretics, and the presence of comorbidities such as dyslipidemia and gastritis.ConclusionThe study highlights the complexity of comorbidities and medication regimens, with ARBs being the most commonly used drug class. Given the high rate of potential drug–drug interactions, rational adjustments in accordance with treatment guidelines are necessary to achieve blood pressure control and reduce cardiovascular events in the elderly hypertensive population in District 5. Pharmacist-led medication reviews and deprescribing protocols should be considered to minimize inappropriate drug combinations and improve medication safety among older adults.

Introduction

Hypertension (HTN) is a chronic medical condition with steadily increasing global prevalence and is currently regarded as one of the leading contributors to morbidity and mortality worldwide. A cross-sectional descriptive study conducted among 326 elderly individuals residing in Nam Son commune, An Duong district, Hai Phong City during 2019–2020 revealed the hypertension prevalence of 35.3% within this population, with stage 2 hypertension representing the highest proportion (14.4%) [1].

In a separate investigation involving 250 hypertensive outpatients aged 60 years and older at a cardiology clinic, the overall treatment adherence rate was reported at 77.6% (mean adherence score: 55.23 ± 8.66). Specifically, medication adherence accounted for 81.4%, adherence to scheduled follow-up visits 75.2%, dietary compliance 68.4%, and adherence to weight management, psychological regulation, and home blood pressure monitoring 66%. The lowest adherence was observed in relation to physical exercise, at only 42% [2]. Despite a relatively high level of adherence, effective blood pressure control among elderly individuals in community settings remained suboptimal. It was estimated that approximately 70% of community-dwelling individuals aged ≥ 65 years were affected by hypertension, yet only half of them achieved adequate blood pressure control through treatment interventions [3].

These findings underscored the pivotal role of effective hypertension management in reducing disease burden and preventing adverse cardiovascular outcomes. However, the rational use of antihypertensive agents remains a considerable challenge, particularly among the elderly. Numerous studies have demonstrated that irrational prescribing practices may contribute to increased rates of hypertension-related complications and mortality. The complexity of antihypertensive treatment regimens is often compounded by multiple influencing factors, including advanced age, the presence of comorbidities, duration of illness, and variable levels of treatment adherence.

In the context of an aging population, District 5, Ho Chi Minh City is witnessing a notable rise in the number of elderly residents, which places substantial demands on healthcare services, including both physical and mental health care. Research on medication use in this population remains limited at the local level, particularly in District 5. Therefore, this study was conducted to evaluate patterns of antihypertensive drug utilization, identify commonly used treatment regimens, and assess the proportion of potential drug–drug interactions (pDDIs) among elderly hypertensive patients. The findings are expected to provide evidence-based insights to support safer and more effective medication management in this vulnerable group. This study aimed to (1) To evaluate patterns of antihypertensive drug utilization, identify commonly used treatment regimens. (2) To assess the proportion of pDDIs and factors associated with pDDIs among elderly hypertensive patients.

Methods

Design and setting of the study

A cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted from August 2024 to February 2025 at five commune health stations affiliated with the District 5 Medical Center, Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam including the Ward 1 Health Station, Ward 5 Health Station, Ward 7 Health Station, Ward 12 Health Station, and Ward 14 Health Station. Data were retrospectively collected from outpatient prescriptions, supplemented by patient interviews to verify medication use and relevant clinical information.

The minimum required sample size was calculated to be n = 320. A design effect of k = 2 was applied due to the use of cluster sampling. Considering an estimated 10% non-response rate, the final required sample size was 712 patients.

Sampling method

Five wards were randomly selected out of a total of 14 in District 5, Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam (based on administrative divisions prior to January 20, 2025) using a lottery method. The selected wards were Ward 1, Ward 5, Ward 7, Ward 12, and Ward 14.

The required minimum sample size was calculated as 712 patients and was proportionally allocated across the five selected wards, resulting in approximately 130–180 patients per ward, depending on the number of eligible patients available during the study period. Data collection was conducted from August 1, 2024, to February 28, 2025. Each working day, around 8–10 eligible patients’ prescriptions and interview forms were collected at each selected health station. After data collection, all records were reviewed for completeness, and those with missing or illegible information were excluded. Within each ward, all valid records were numbered and assigned random values using the RAND() function in Microsoft Excel 2010. The lists were sorted in ascending order of random numbers, and the first 130–180 records per ward (depending on the number of valid prescriptions available) were selected.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Patients aged 60 years and older with a confirmed diagnosis of hypertension were eligible for inclusion. Only individuals who had visited commune health stations in District 5, Ho Chi Minh City, and had been prescribed antihypertensive medications during the study period were considered. All participants were required to provide informed consent prior to data collection.

Patients were excluded if their prescriptions did not clearly state a diagnosis, or if the medication information was illegible or smudged, preventing accurate data extraction. Individuals who had been receiving antihypertensive therapy for less than one month were also excluded to ensure adequate treatment exposure for assessment.

Study variables

Demographic characteristics: Demographic characteristics included gender, ethnicity, age group, residential address, educational level, marital status, occupation, and cohabitation status. Clinical characteristics included duration of hypertension, family history of hypertension, comorbidities, and types of comorbid conditions.

The variables to be investigated included: Prescription-related variables included the total number of medications per prescription, the number of antihypertensive drugs in the treatment regimen, characteristics of antihypertensive drug classes, and specific antihypertensive agents used. The analysis also examined the proportion of prescriptions containing angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE), calcium channel blockers (CCBs), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), beta-blockers (BBs), and diuretics. Treatment regimens were classified into monotherapy, two-drug combinations, and regimens with three or more antihypertensive agents. The study also assessed the prevalence of clinically significant pDDIs, as well as the proportion of moderate and severe interaction pairs.

Statistical analysis

Potential drug–drug interactions (pDDIs) were identified using the Drugs.com interaction checker database.

Methods of data processing and analysis: Data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2010 and analyzed using Stata. Before performing regression analyses, bivariate comparisons were conducted using Chi-square tests for categorical variables to identify crude associations with the occurrence of pDDIs. Subsequently, univariate logistic regression was performed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and evaluate the association between each potential factor and pDDIs. Variables with p < 0.25 in the univariate analysis, together with clinically relevant covariates, were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model using the forward likelihood ratio method to identify independent predictors of pDDIs. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p > 0.05 indicating adequate fit), and multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF < 5 considered acceptable. All statistical analyses were conducted at a 95% confidence level.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee in Biomedical Research of Hong Bang International University under Decision No. 170/PCT–HĐĐĐ–SDH.

Discussion

This cross-sectional descriptive study examined the utilization of antihypertensive drugs and the prevalence of pDDIs among 720 elderly patients (≥ 60 years) diagnosed with hypertension in District 5, Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, during 2024–2025. The findings revealed that triple-drug combination regimens were the most common (39.7%), followed by two-drug combinations (33.5%) and monotherapy (21.7%), with four-drug regimens being least frequent (5.1%). ARBs were the most frequently prescribed class (79.3%), followed by CCBs (68.8%) and BBs (58.1%). Bisoprolol, amlodipine, losartan, and hydrochlorothiazide were the most common active ingredients prescribed. Notably, pDDIs were identified in 72.4% of prescriptions, with 1.9% classified as severe. Eleven clinically significant interaction pairs were documented, including combinations that increase the risk of hyperkalemia, hepatotoxicity, serotonin syndrome, and bleeding.

A study conducted on 720 elderly hypertensive patients recorded that the proportion of female patients with hypertension was 406 (56.4%), while the proportion of male patients was 314 (43.6%), showing a difference of 12.8% between the two groups. Lanza et al. (2024) reported a similar distribution, with female patients accounting for 56.13% and male patients for 43.87% [5], which is consistent with the findings of this study. The difference in hypertension prevalence between males and females can be explained by various factors, including physiological, hormonal, lifestyle, and psychological aspects. The comorbidity rate was notably high (94.6%), indicating the complexity of health issues among the study sample. Similarly, Ngoc Cam Quyen Nguyen et al. (2023) [6] reported a comorbidity rate of 94.4%, which aligns with this study’s findings. As age increases, the aging process leads to a decline in body functions, raising the risk of chronic diseases, including hypertension. In terms of treatment regimens, the study showed that combination therapy involving three or more antihypertensive drugs accounted for the majority (44.9%). Saqlain (2020) also reported similar findings, with monotherapy at 23.2%, dual therapy at 31.2%, and triple or more drug combinations at 45.6% [7]. The use of multiple antihypertensive agents is often necessary to achieve therapeutic targets, but it also requires close monitoring to ensure safety and effectiveness. Among the drug classes, ARBs were the most commonly prescribed (79.3%), followed by CCBs at 68.8%, and BBs at 58.1%. These findings are consistent with Rashikh (2023) [8] in Saudi Arabia, who also reported that ARBs/ACEi and CCBs were the most frequently used classes. Similarly, Trong Nhan Nguyen (2022) [9] documented usage rates of 48.42% for ARBs and 28% for CCBs. Several studies have shown that ARBs enhance osteoblastic activity, accelerate bone injury healing, and improve bone trabecular structure. CCBs do not cause disturbances in lipid metabolism, blood glucose, or electrolytes, which is particularly important in elderly hypertensive patients, as they are often at high risk of developing metabolic disorders such as dyslipidemia and diabetes (Ghosh, 2024) [10]. Bisoprolol was the most frequently prescribed drug across antihypertensive regimens for the elderly, with a usage rate of 98.1%. Bisoprolol reduces both systolic and diastolic blood pressure and is particularly effective in patients with tachycardia. It helps lower the risk of stroke, myocardial infarction, and heart failure, making it a safe option for elderly hypertensive patients.

pDDIs are a common cause of adverse symptoms in patients and contribute significantly to decreased treatment adherence. Factors such as diet, environment, and genetic predispositions also play a substantial role in the occurrence of pDDIs. Based on the results from the Drugs Interaction Checker tool, the frequency of pDDIs identified in this study was 72.4%. This rate is comparable to that reported by Nguyen Trong Nhan (2022), who observed a prevalence of 71.3% [9]. The study findings indicated that the prevalence of pDDIs among elderly patients with hypertension is relatively high. In univariate analysis, comorbidities, dyslipidemia, gastritis, and the use of CCBs, BBs, and diuretics were statistically significant with pDDIs. After adjustment for confounders in multivariate logistic regression, diabetes, dyslipidemia, gastritis, and the use of CCBs, BBs, and diuretics were statistically significant predictors. Patients with diabetes were approximately 2.4 times more likely to experience pDDIs (OR = 2.43; 95% CI: 1.40–4.19; p = 0.001). The coexistence of diabetes often necessitates multiple pharmacotherapies such as antihyperglycemic, antihypertensive, and lipid-lowering agents, which may increase the likelihood of metabolic and pharmacodynamic interactions. Dyslipidemia was also identified as an important risk factor (OR = 5.77; 95% CI: 3.24–10.31; p < 0.001). Patients with dyslipidemia are frequently prescribed statins or other lipid-lowering agents, which are prone to interactions with cardiovascular drugs due to metabolism via CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein. According to Gallo et al., the combination of atorvastatin with verapamil or diltiazem is among the most common pDDIs within this drug-metabolizing pathway [11]. Patients with concomitant gastritis were also at higher risk of pDDIs (OR = 4.14; 95% CI: 2.27–7.55; p < 0.001). This group is often prescribed proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), antacids (such as sucralfate or bile acid sequestrants), or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which may alter gastric pH and subsequently affect the absorption of co-administered drugs. For instance, sucralfate and bile acid sequestrants may bind to concomitant medications in the gastrointestinal tract, thereby reducing their absorption when taken simultaneously [12]. Regarding antihypertensive therapy, the use of CCBs was strongly associated with pDDIs (OR = 26.02; 95% CI: 15.45–43.82; p < 0.001), potentially due to the inhibitory effects of certain CCBs (particularly diltiazem and verapamil) on CYP3A4/P–gp, leading to increased plasma concentrations of drugs metabolized via these pathways. Gallo et al. also reported that common interactions involving CCBs include the co–administration of atorvastatin with verapamil or diltiazem [11]. Similarly, BBs use was significantly associated with pDDIs (OR = 3.19; 95% CI: 2.05–4.97; p < 0.001). Gallo et al. suggested that the combination of non-selective β-blockers with β₂-agonist bronchodilators could potentially cause respiratory compromise in patients with COPD or asthma. The use of diuretics was also identified as a contributing factor that increased the risk of pDDIs among elderly hypertensive patients (OR = 4.65; 95% CI: 2.48–8.72; p < 0.001). A study conducted in Italy (2021) reported that hypertensive patients treated with diuretics were at higher risk of serious pDDIs compared to those not receiving diuretics, particularly interactions leading to electrolyte disturbances [11]. These associations may also reflect the frequent co-prescription of CCBs, BBs, and diuretics with other cardiovascular or metabolic agents—such as statins, ACE inhibitors, or oral hypoglycemic drugs—which are commonly used in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities, thereby increasing the risk of potential pDDIs.

This study was conducted on a sample of 720 patients, ensuring representativeness of the elderly population in District 5. It provided valuable insights into the current status of hypertension care and treatment among the elderly, serving as a basis for timely and effective intervention strategies. However, due to its cross-sectional design, the study only reflects medication use at a specific point in time and cannot assess changes in treatment over time. Further in-depth studies are needed to comprehensively evaluate the impact of pDDIs on elderly patients with hypertension.

The findings of this study have important clinical and policy implications. For clinicians, the high prevalence of complex regimens and pDDIs underscores the need for careful medication review, especially when prescribing ARBs, CCBs, BBs, and diuretics in combination. Integrating pharmacist-led medication review and structured deprescribing strategies into primary care practice could further minimize pDDI risk and enhance medication safety in older adults. Regular use of drug interaction screening tools should be integrated into routine prescribing for elderly hypertensive patients to reduce adverse events. For healthcare administrators, the study highlights the importance of developing training programs on rational prescribing and polypharmacy management for primary care providers. At the policy level, these results can inform the development of local hypertension treatment guidelines that emphasize both therapeutic efficacy and safety.

Conclusion

The study highlighted the complexity of comorbid conditions in elderly patients with hypertension, with treatment regimens most commonly involving a combination of three or more antihypertensive drugs. The ARB drug class was the most frequently selected for treatment. With a relatively high rate of pDDIs (72.4%), including 1.9% classified as severe, appropriate adjustments in accordance with treatment guidelines are necessary to achieve optimal blood pressure control and reduce cardiovascular events among the elderly hypertensive population in District 5.
