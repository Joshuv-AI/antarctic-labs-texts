Use of ICT: What effect on youth access to employment in Cameroon?
Authors: Astride Claudel Njiepue Nouffeussie, Cosmas Bernard Meka'A, Romus Noufelie, Bruno Ndouyang Balguessam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to explore the impact of Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs) on job acquisition for young people aged 15 to 35 in Cameroon. The research uses data from the fourth Cameroon Household Survey (CHS-4) conducted by the National Institute of Statistics (NIS) in 2014. The study compares the results with those of senior employees to highlight the digital divide. The results suggest that internet use increases the probability of finding a job, regardless of age group, between 15 and 31 % for young people, between 0.31 and 1.214 % for senior citizens, and between 15 and 38 % for all workers. Young Internet users are 15 times more likely to find a job compared to senior employees. The results are consistent after specificity/sensitivity tests. The study contributes to the understanding of the digital divide and its impact on job acquisition.Keywords: ICTs, Employment, Young, Instrumental variable, Cameroon

Introduction

Since the middle of the 20th century, Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs) have experienced considerable growth, especially through the development of television, the computer, the telephone, and the Internet. This boom has considerably transformed the world, which has become a “global society” [1] Moreover, the development of ICTs in the most diverse areas of society has led some to predict the advent of a “new economy”. For the [2,3], ICTs integrate all the technologies that include computing, advanced multimedia, and telecommunications technical, which have enabled the emergence of more efficient means of communication and improved the processing, storage, dissemination, and exchange of information.

At the global level, the European Commission (EC) indicated that ICTs represented almost 5.2 % of the Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in 2015 ([4]); and [5] expected a contribution to GDP growth of 10 % in 2025. Nowadays, digital technology affects all sectors of activity and plays an important role in employment and growth. Moreover, their distribution involves a productivity improvement ([[6], [7], [8]]). According to [9]: " In the new economies, technology is the major driver of improved quality of life for the underdeveloped or developing people ". Thus, the ICTs used and adopted could enhance the populations well-being in developing countries.

In Cameroon, ICTs using and adopting should be a significant contribution to economic growth [10]. Indeed, ICTs have experienced a gradual diffusion in society since the reform of the telecommunications sector following Law 98/014 of July 14, 1998. The number of Internet subscribers (fixed and mobile) has literally increased, with the internet penetration rate rising from nearly 0.0 % to approximately 46 % over the period 2000 to 2021; and for fixed and mobile, these rates are going to 0 % in 2008 to reach a threshold of 41 % in 2020, the decrease till 38 %, probably due to the Corona virus 19 pandemic (Fig. 1). In addition, from 2005 to 2016, the penetration rate of mobile telephony also increased, respectively, from 12.26 % in 2005 to 43.96 % in 2010, and then to 82.87 % in 2016 (Mobile telephonic regulation agency, 2021). Depending on the age group, there is a differentiated use of ICTs. Indeed [11], points out that young people constitute the segment of the population that mostly uses these tools.1 Likewise, dissemination of these technologies is thus part of the 2020 digital development objectives2 set by the government. Getting a job is one of the major issues among the young sample in developing countries ([12]; [13]), and Cameroon is no exception. The diagnostic of Cameroonian's labor market reveals worrying situations either for those in activities or in unemployment [14]). Indeed, for youths aged 15 to 35, the unemployment rate rose from 8.9 % to 10.5 %3 between 2010 and 2016; and the unobserved underemployment rate from 70.6 % to 75.8 %.4 An ever-increasing number of young people exposed to unemployment or held in precarious, temporary, or generally poor-quality jobs could express our choice of this population sub-group. According to the National Institute of Statistics [15], young people aged 15–34 gather 65.37 % of the workforce, 59.43 % of the active population, and 37.53 % of the whole population. They are proportionally more numerous in urban areas (61.17 %) than in rural areas (38.82 %); and they are the most vulnerable.

However, according to [16], the dynamic of the labor market is correlated with those ICTs, since ICTs tools significantly contribute to economic growth and, above all, enhance information flows. On the one hand, the job search theory of [16,17], and the network spillover theory of [18] acknowledge that, information is an economic efficiency relevant factor and ICTs are an important tool for matching individual skills with the labor market's needs [19]. [20] added that, by increasing the individual social network, ICTs mitigate the holdbacks in access to the labor market. The literature on channels of access to employment placed greater emphasis on the traditional channels, namely: social networks, announcements, public-private mediators etc. ([[20], [21], [22], [23]]). However, this approach is costly for access to information's by job seekers, and their effectiveness to strive for unemployment is not sustained.

Hence, the contribution of the ICTs for getting jobs has been suggested by other authors. For instance Refs. [24,25], have found a negative effect of internet use on unemployment duration in France. A strand of literature supports this thesis in various other contexts, such as Engand and American ([26,27]; Ford, 2015). On the other hand, the authors such as [[28], [29], [30]], among others, discovered a positive ICTs-employment nexus, respectively in Italia, French and Germany. This conclusion has been validated more recently by [31] in England [32,33], in USA contexts. And thirdly [34], provided a neutral relation between using ICTs and getting a job.

Finally, empirical literature shows that getting a job and using ICTs nexus is not definitively established. The objective of this article is to analyse ICTs effect on young access to employment in Cameroon. Answering this question allows us to contribute to current literature fourfold. First, it allows to conciliate the empirical debate regarding employment access's determinants in general and the ICTs-employment relationship in particular. Secondly, in the Cameroonian case, to our best knowledge, there is not a similar study. On the one hand [14,35] deal with youth employment factors, ignoring the ICTs factor; and on the other hand [11,36,37], studied the determinants of internet access and use, but without evaluation of its contribution to getting a job. Third, some authors as [25] who used microeconomic data, employed a classical probit approach; however, the author does not take into account the potential endogeneity bias, although put forward in the previous studies. To address this concern, we retain an Instrumental Variable Probit model (Ivprobit), giving their advantages to find consistency estimators and draw up by [38,39]. Fourth, taking into account the intergenerational digital divide highlighted in the Cameroonian context by [40], our study analyzes their feedback effect on access to the labor market by distinguishing young workers, who are our baseline sample, from senior ones.

Thus, this study aims to fill this gap by applying data from the Cameroonian Household Survey (CHS-4) carried out by the National Institute of Statistics (NIS) in 2014 to an IVprobit model. The use of ICTs in this study refers to the use of Internet services by employed workers. Using the internet increases the chance of getting a job, and this is for all age groups considered; however, the magnitude of this effect is higher in the youth group sample than that of the of the senior.

In the Cameroonian context, existing studies on the issue of access to employment focus more on human capital ([14,41]) or social capital [42] as explanatory factors. In addition, those who consider ICTs as a factor have focused particularly on the quality of youth employment [43]. To our knowledge, analysis of the effect of ICT on young people's access to employment has been neglected. This study is therefore the first to analyse the effect of ICTs on access to employment for disabled people. Thus, this article is also interesting for at least three reasons: Cameroon is a ‘’youthfu” country with young people representing more than 35 % of its population, of which around (about 33.44 %) is not in employment [15]. Thus, this paper reminds us of the urgent need for developing countries, which have a very young population and public policies that are not always targeted, to take an interest in this section of the population, which represents a very large proportion of the workforce. This article focuses on the issue of decent jobs, which is one of the goals of sustainable development and at the heart of the African Union's Agenda 2063. Finally, this paper raises the issue of the digital divide, which is still very marked in developing countries such as Cameroon.

To the follow of this paper, sections 1, 2 expose the literature review and methodology, respectively; the presentation of results is at section 3, and at the end, section 4 concludes.

Literature review

In this section, present at (1.1) how the traditional approaches established the information-employment nexus; at (1.2), draw up the theoretical link between ICT and access to employment by insistent on relevant channels; and end a critical empirical review at (1.3).

Information and employment access: traditional approaches

The problem of access to employment emerged with the great crisis of 1929. It was [44] who drew attention to this problem, which was presenting itself with sharpness. He then spoke of involuntary unemployment and underemployment. Hence, Job search theory developed by [16] highlighted information as the main determinant of access to employment. It stemmed from a relaxation of the assumption of perfect information on potential jobs in a labor market as put forward by the traditional theory. In fact, the individual will devote not only time to job prospecting and research but also financial resources, which is generally reflected in terms of opportunity cost ([45,46]).

[47] in his reference work offers a fruitful reading grid to characterize the diversity of employment access by distinguishing between informal channels, which offer the best information available, and formal channels, characterized by a large quantity of information disseminated. Spence [48] added that by choosing a channel, the unemployed give a signal to employers about their productivity. [20], on the other hand, insists on the preponderance of networks (personal relations) as an effective means of access to employment. Indeed, these networks would be, among other arguments, a source of quality information for job seekers.

Nevertheless, mention that these various traditional channels suffer from many problems, in particular the high cost of information access, a long delay in accessing this information, and the inefficiency in ensuring transparency on the job offers available. In view of these various limitations, individuals are more likely to use the ICT channel. As a result, job search via the Internet (Internet job search) replaces traditional channels because it is less expensive and faster by combining the attraction of candidates and enterprises seeking to get in touch ([18,49]). The importance of the informational factor in the context of job search gives a broad echo to ICT. Indeed, their advent has revolutionized the circulation of information on a global scale.

ICT and access to employment: theoretical background

Despite the increasingly frequent use of ICT [50], notes that there is not absolute unanimity on the definition of this concept. The authors are particularly divided as to the elements to be taken into account in its content. Thus, according to [51], ICT refers to all instruments carrying immaterial messages (images, sounds, character strings). He then subdivides ICT into three categories: audiovisual (sound and image), computing, and telecommunications [52,53]. go further in this sense, also defining the term “ICTs” as a set of all technologies ensuring the communication, transmission, storage, creation, sharing, or exchange of information.

ICTs can thus profoundly modify the job search process [54]. Indeed, prospecting for a job has a cost, particularly the costs induced by the search for information on the availability of jobs as well as on salaries ([16,45,46]). Therefore, having a direct impact on the availability of information, ICT can allow job seekers to have access to information on the characteristics of job offers for which they wish to apply, at a lower cost ([16,24,55]). Recent analyses of the new microeconomics tell us more about the role that ICT can play in reducing job search time. Thus, through the Internet, a large amount of information with low cost is available to job seekers and fully justifies the migration of the labor market to the Internet ([56]).

ICTs are part of the register of formal employment access channels and more specifically as a component of advertisements. In the specification drawn up by [47], ICT figure among the formal channels of access to employment. Since the mid-1990s, ICT, in the internet toll case, it is played an increasing role in the coordination of the labor market [57]. In this way [58], argued that we could do without the public placement service. In addition, the use of the Internet would have become obvious for job seekers, as the gains made possible by this tool compared to other job search methods are perceptible [59]. In their study, these authors come to the conclusion that some say they prefer ICT (Internet) to other media, such as the press or networks. Thus, ICT constitutes for job seekers not only the main job search tool but also their main source of information on the labor market and an effective channel for access to employment; according to human capital theory ([60,61]).

ICT and access to employment: empirical literature review

The analysis of the ICT effect on access to employment has been the subject of numerous empirical studies in various contexts. Indeed, by exploring the expansion effect of the internet on getting a job, our study contributes to the (still limited) literature that focuses on the different job search channels, including research via ICT (internet).

Some investigations suggested a positive correlation between the ICT-employment nexus. In this way [29], reproduced [24] analysis, this time combining information with the Youth longitudinal survey. By comparing the relationship between internet use and unemployment durations in 1998–2000 and 2008–2009, authors found an insignificant effect with the first period, while for the second, internet use was associated with a reduction of near 25 % of unemployment duration. In Germany's labor market [30] suggested further that the introduction of high-speed internet increases the re-employment probability. This result joins those [28] in the Italy case who argued that Internet access reduces by 1.6 percent the probability for graduates to get a job.

However, Thomsen and Wittich [62] used the German socio-economic panel to demonstrate that Internet use does not significantly increase the probabilities of re-employment. To explain it [23], argued that traditional mail remains dominant in the reception of unsolicited applications (nearly 91 %), far ahead of the Internet (about 23 %). Moreover, [[58], [63]] investigated the Internet's effect on access to employment over the period from 1998 to 2000 in a French. Findings did not show a significant difference between users and non-internet users in terms of unemployment duration. Furthermore [25], performed logistic regressions with a job search indicator as the dependent variable and discovered a similar result.

Many other empirical studies claim that, although ICT use is relevant for access to employment, there exist inequalities in terms of their use at different levels of socio-demographics, notably among the age groups. Instant, several surveys, such as the Graphic, Visualization, and Usability Center's (GVU) carryout by Georgia's technology institute in 1994, confirmed that the primary user of the internet was relatively young (last 34- years -old). Moreover, following their survey [64], a similar conclusion has been supported. Furthermore, empirical investigations suggest also that among the individual features, age is an important factor determining the ICT using gap ([11,36,65]).

Indeed, if well used, SM is an important tool for youth who are underresourced and with low income, as it helps to build social capital among themselves, which supports the sharing of crucial information such as getting work, connecting to professional contacts, as well as gaining career mentorship [66]. Similarly [67,68], suggested that access to employment is higher impacted by experience level, skill, knowledge, positive development, or globally socio-economic ideas, which could improve through mastering use of social media and mobile phones. Finally [69], carried out a qualitative study in Tanzania to confirm these results. According to stylized facts, young groups are marginalized in the labor market, however, they can use ICT to increase their opportunities to get a job or for self-employment in several ways. Therefore, well-used ICTs significantly and positively impact the lives of users, since that contributes to reinforcing social capital established by social ties and therefore assisting youth to get jobs compared to non-users [70].

In contrast to the previous work [71], focus on the creation/destruction aspect of jobs imputed to ICT. Indeed, they explore the impact of ICT on job creation/destruction in the Turkish manufacturing industry using a labour demand estimation framework. To achieve this, the authors mobilise the GMM system model on data from the Turkish manufacturing industry, including all firms with 20 or more employees for the period 2003–2013. Their results suggest that ICT have positive effects on employment in the Turkish manufacturing industry. More specifically, ICT are a source of job creation and therefore facilitate.

In a similar idea [72], examines the impact of ICT access on employment in Botswana. To achieve this, he applies a probit model for the binary choice responses of being employed or not, using data from the 2014 Botswana Household Access and Individual Use of Information and Communication Technology Survey conducted by Statistics Botswana. The empirical results show that access to ICT has a positive impact on employment in Botswana. In other words, ICT facilitate access to employment for young people in the Botswana labour market.

In their study [73], analyses the impact of the ICT sector on employment in Romania in the context of the EU. The results of the comparative analysis at EU level show that there are significant negative gaps between Romania and the EU average, as well as between Romania and the Visegrad countries, in terms of the Digital Economy and Society Index (DESI), the ICT sector's contribution to employment and GDP, and ICT specialists employed (as a % of total employment). Similarly, Sagayaraj et al. (2020), in their study, analyse the role of information and communication technology (ICT) skills in improving employment opportunities. The authors show that 60 % of graduates are unemployed. There is a significant gap between the number of graduates who obtain their diploma and those who are actually inserted into jobs in industry. However [74], is more interested in gender equality in the labour market. More specifically, the author seeks to answer the question of whether ICT have an impact on gender equality in the labour market. Using an analysis of panel data for 156 countries over the period 1991–2014, the author argues that ICT contribute positively to the reduction of the gender gap in the labour market, mainly due to the increase in female labour force participation. However, the impact is weaker in developing countries, suggesting that ICT are not necessarily a quantum leap strategy as many hopes in terms of women's participation in the labour market and a more gender-equal labour market.

At the macroeconomic level, empirical consideration of the role of ICTs on access to employment is controversial. A large body of literature supports the thesis of a positive effect and argues that ICT can be exploited to correct the information asymmetry that is a major feature of the labour market and, in addition, help the quality of education by offering more flexible, interactive and expanded choices of learning resources inaccessible via traditional means of education ([[75], [76], [77]]). An equally relevant strand of the literature shows that improvements in physical and non-physical infrastructure promote economic growth by improving productivity, increasing accessibility to employment opportunities and supporting financial stability ([73,[78], [79], [80]]). Moreover, some studies conducted in the context of advanced economies support this thesis of the positive effects of ICTs on employment ([[81], [82], [83]]). On the other hand, there are also critical views on the negative consequences of deploying ICT as a solution to the problem of access to employment for young people ([[84], [85], [86], [87]]). Indeed, according to [87] the negative effects of ICT could worsen the youth unemployment situation, especially in less developed economies, which may be difficult to produce enough skills to keep up with the rapid deployment of technologies. On the other hand [88]also argues that an increased commitment to investment in such infrastructure may not show a positive impact in promoting access to employment in developing countries due to endemic corruption and institutional failure in these countries.

These different studies show that the importance of ICT as channels for access to employment has not yet been decided, at least not in all contexts. In the case of Sub-Saharan Africa in general and Cameroon in particular, very few studies have focused on the issue; hence the need for more empirical studies, particularly on the labor market.

Methodology

This part will be devoted to the presentation of data and sample selection; the econometric model and the estimation technique; as well as the descriptive statistics.

Data and survey design

For this study, we use data from the Fourth Cameroonian Household Survey (CHS 4) conducted by the National Institute of Statistics (NIS)5in 2014. To carry out this survey, NIS built a random sample, which was stratified at 2 levels: enumeration area or primary level (EA) and enumeration household or secondary level (EH). Particularly, at the household level, selection has been made through two stages: first, EAs are sorted and recoded according to administrative units, namely divisions and sub-divisions; and second, EH are drawn with equal probabilities according to their size, with size being the number of households located in the EA. To select secondary unities, after the primary units have been drawn and before the survey, households will be counted and the map updated in each EA selected. This step was followed by the selection of the fixed number of households in each EA. To facilitate the calculation of sampling weights, the sampling probabilities for each level of sampling are calculated by stratum and for each EA. The basic weights are adjusted for non-response at the household and individual level. The sampling weights will be used in all analyses related to CHS4 to ensure the representativeness of the sample at the national and domain level. In total, 12,847 households have been selected over 1024 areas, and in 12 regions (10 and Douala (economic capital) and Yaounde (political capital) considered both particular regions). In each region and in each stratum (urban, semi-urban, and rural), the survey lasted two and a half months. This survey provides additional information on the characteristics of households, their working conditions, and so forth, which makes possible the empirical analysis of the relation between ICTs and access to employment.

Indeed, contrary to the adoption, which does not inform us about how the ICTs tolls could be exploited for the young needs, an approach based on ICTs using allow does not fill this gap. Therefore, the aim of this paper is to analyse the effect ICTs are having on access to employment for young people in Cameroon. During the survey, in the following questions, did you obtain your job (or undertake this activity)? A question has been asked to the current workers: Through what channel did you obtain your job (or undertake this activity)? However, the Internet was the only ICTs item appearing among the channels proposed. Thus, our question could be addressed as follows: What is the internet using to affect youth getting jobs in Cameroon? In this study, young worker refers to a current worker whose age ranges from 15 to 35 years (EMPJ). This definition is that used by the National Institute of Statistics (NIS) during the survey and the Cameroonian government.

Discussion of results

Estimates show that the use of ICTs contributes significantly and positively to access to employment, regardless of the age group of the individual. More specifically, it turned out that the use of this job search channel improves the probability for a young person to get a job. Thus, ICTs constitute a factor of (positive) discrimination of professional mobility on the labor market [24,55]. who obtained the same result provide an explanation by indicating that this state of affairs is due to the improvement in the level of access and the reduction in the cost of information. In the same vein, a study carried out in the Cameroonian context by [11] points out the extent to which the digital divide is a Cameroonian reality, which plays an important role in socio-economic disparities. Moreover, contrary to the negative [34] or insignificant ([34]) or insignificant [25] effects of ICTs on access to employment, our result is similar to those of [29,56,57], [100] in England contexts; and [30] in German contexts. Furthermore, in the context of developing countries, our results corroborate those of [71] in the Turkish context [72], in Bostwana [83], in Romania or again.

Our result proves that ICTs positively and significantly influence the probability of getting a job, irrespective of the national level and sub-groups considered in our study. Our result is similar to their many authors, such ([85,101,102]). ICTs can help young people enter the job market by raising their skill levels. Indeed, education and training in today's world cannot be done without ICTs. The latter also makes it possible to offer more skills in line with the needs of the labor market, which has long been decried as a major cause of underemployment in developing countries. ICTs are also said to be a factor in network diversification, as they enable people to extend their social and professional networks ([20,103]), thereby increasing their chances of finding a job ([77,82,104,[105], [106]]). However, the gains occur mainly, or only, when individuals have access to this technology and make good use of it. Yet it is becoming increasingly clear that there are major discrepancies between population sub-groups in terms of Internet access and use, particularly between young and older people [[36], [107]].

Additionally, this link highlights a certain heterogeneity depending on the age group of individuals, given that the marginal contribution of ICTs varies between 15 and 31 % in young people, from 15 to 38 % in the sample of older workers over 15 years old, and 0.31–1.214 % among seniors. In this way, this result would be linked to the “digital divide” effect put forward in the Cameroonian context by Refs. [11,36], obtained a similar conclusion that put forward the higher effect of internet use on the employability of young, comparatively to elders. Furthermore, empirical investigations suggest also that among the individual features, age is an important factor determining the ICTs using gap ([11,36,65]). Thus, the amplitudes of the effects obtained between the first two categories of employed workers are almost identical, which could be associated with the fact that the labor force is essentially young, who also constitute the bulk of Internet users.

As a first explanation of this conclusion, it appears on the one hand that, the ability to use the internet of youths would be higher than that of elders; these are more than more access to several educational systems and are most available for the deep trained. And on the other hand, whereas their counterfactual seniors, who generally generally have family support, and this is a relevant feature of developing countries; consequently, they have the least time for training and then observed a relatively low contribution of ICTs using on their chance to be employed. These advantages occur through several mediating channels depicted in literature. Some authors discovered that mobile phones heap access to employment through improving experiences, skills, and knowledge ([67,68]) or online platform, as showed by [70].

Second, another strand of empirical literature putted forward the sharing role of information played by internet, which by reducing cost and time to access to the information on labor market, increase the probabilities of getting job. Additionaly [108], suggested that, mastering ICTs improve interactive and informative and maintain the link possibilities in society, and then create the employment opportunities for users. It is the observation have been made by [[69], [109]] who attested that by reducing this cost, government allowed to young graduate Tanzanian to become entrepreneurs. Author added that through the Social Medias, various population segments are aided to share positive ideas and gain new knowledge that is critical to help youth facilitate undertakings of income generation.

Third and end, social networks would be another relevant channel. Indeed, [66]) found that ICTs affect young employment throughout increasing their social capital among themselves, crucial for improving the possibilities to gain career mentorship. And as one might expect, seniors would find greater efficiency in other channels as a personal network than the Internet to access employment, giving that age increase with social personnel networks ([110]). Several empirical works carried out in various contexts support this thesis of Granovetter [111].

With regards to the other factors, place of residence and level of education have significant impacts on access to employment for each of these two categories of workers. In particular, regardless of the group considered, the influence of place of residence is significant and positive at the 1 % level. Indeed, urban active seniors have less than 0.0816 % chance of finding a job, compared to their rural counterparts. This proportion is between 0.0821 and 0.0611 % in the working and employed population as a whole. This result is due to urban employability difficulties, unlike in rural areas where almost all households have agricultural fields, which can be exploited by working people; all things that contribute positively to their employability.

Conclusion

This study aimed to assess the effect of ICTs on access to employment for young people in Cameroon. From the data provided by the National Institute of Statistics, which were collected from households during the fourth national survey (CHS4), we have, after having shown that the estimation by a simple probit tends to provide biased results, used a probit model with instrumental variables, in order to overcome this bias. In this study, ICTs is reduced to the use of the Internet by workers employed at the time of the survey, and who got their job via this channel; and a young person, is an individual aged between 15 and 35 years. In addition, the results obtained were compared with those of working people over the age of 15 overall, and also of working senior workers (over 35 years old). To track the endogeneity bias due to ICTs using, a robust instrumental variable probit model is preferred to standard probit one. The estimation suggests that using internet increase the probability to getting a job, irrespectively of age group considered; this increases it between 15 and 31 % for youth, 0.31–1.214 % for seniors and 15–38 % for whole worker. Furthermore, compared to senior employees, the young internet users are 25 times luckier to find a job. Moreover, the diagnostics analyse following the specificity/sensitivity tests show that the results are consistent.

Implication for Cameroon

On the basis of the results obtained and for young's employment concerns; policymakers should go beyond to strive unemployment, for invest in promoting the innovating channels to get a job. In particular, it would be relevant mitigate the obstacles linked regarding the costs and availability of the digitalization's tools. This purpose could by achieve by increasing the investing for digital infrastructures development; which allow to young, most economically vulnerable, to access and use it with a low cost, and consequently improve their getting job's opportunities. In addition, the government can encourage private companies to make job offers more flexible for people with digital skills, so that they can gain experience and hope for a job in the labor market, instance by reducing the tax charge for enterprise which engage this worker's categories, notably, the first-time job seekers in order to allow them to improve their practice skills.

Implication for other countries

First, countries with the same level of income as Cameroon should, as a matter of urgency, provide all the necessary facilities for training young people in the management of ICTss in order to offer job opportunities to unemployed young people in their countries. Secondly, since the use of ICTs has spread in all sectors of human activity and to all age groups, greater emphasis needs to be placed on the provision of ICTs facilities in higher education establishments in order to bridge the digital divide between age groups. Third developing nations should greatly imbibe maintenance culture in ICTs equipments such as the developed nations so that thousands of unemployed youths will gain employment in ICTs maintenance.

Ideas for future research

Future research can explore several avenues to further develop the literature. First, we would be the construction of an ICTs usage index, which would make it possible to compare the overall effect with the individual effects of the various ICTs tools. Second, we could analyse the effect of ICTs on the subjective aspect of employment, in particular job satisfaction. Taking this subjectivity into account will make it possible to analyse the job in terms of its dual impact on job satisfaction.
