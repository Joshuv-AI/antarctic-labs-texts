To document traditional ethnobotanical knowledge of medicinal plants used by indigenous and saint communities of Chitrakoot, India
Authors: Abhishek Kumar Pandey, Faiz Bux, Kamlesh Sonkar, Abhismita Roy, Harish Yadav
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTraditional medicinal systems are integral to the healthcare practices of indigenous and local communities, especially in remote regions where modern healthcare may be inaccessible. Ethnobotany and ethnomedicine play vital roles in these systems, providing effective treatments derived from local flora. This study investigates the wealth of ethnomedicinal plant knowledge among the indigenous and saint communities of Chitrakoot, India.MethodParticipatory rural appraisal (PRA) and rapid rural appraisal tools were used to assess ethnobotanical information. Ethnic data were collected from saints and indigenous communities through interviews, discussions, and semi-structured questionnaires. Quantitative analysis was conducted via relative frequency citation (RFC), the informant consensus factor (IFC), and use value (UV). In the present study, a total of 205 individuals (168 males and 37 females) participated in interviews and discussions on ethnomedicine The research was conducted from June 2021 to December 2024.ResultsThis study identified 119 ethnomedicinal plants, representing 107 genera and 50 families, used to treat various ailments categorized into 15 groups. Piper nigrum had the highest RFC value (1.00) and UV (1.20), followed by Allium sativum (0.84). The highest ICF value was found for the snake, scorpion, and insect bite categories (0.88), followed by the digestive system disorders category (0.84). The most common method of drug administration is the use of plant powder, with leaves being the most commonly used part of the plant.ConclusionChitrakoot saint and tribal communities hold rich ethnomedicinal knowledge rooted in their forests, underscoring the need to preserve this vital indigenous heritage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-026-00931-3.Keywords: Ethnobotanical study, Medicinal properties, Tribal uses, Conservation, Traditional medicine, Ayurveda

Introduction

Traditional medicinal systems have long been used to prevent, treat, and manage diseases in both humans and livestock, and their widespread use has been extensively documented in the literature, particularly in ethnobotanical and folkloristic records [1–5]. These systems primarily rely on plant-derived materials as the principal ingredients for therapeutic preparations [6, 7]. Currently, nearly half of all pharmaceutical drugs are derived directly or indirectly from ethnomedicinal knowledge and research conducted worldwide [8, 9]. Despite remarkable advances in modern healthcare, many remote regions in developing and underdeveloped countries continue to experience limited access to adequate medical services [10–12]. Consequently, a substantial proportion of the population relies on traditional medicinal systems to meet their primary healthcare needs [13, 14]. People living in remote and rural areas often depend almost entirely on these systems because they are affordable, readily accessible, culturally acceptable, and locally available [15–18].

It has been reported that the traditional medicinal systems of Ayurveda, Homeopathy, Siddha, Unani, and Tibetan medicine collectively utilize approximately 8,000 plant species [19–21]. Among these, Ayurveda remains one of the most widely practiced systems and continues to be embraced by diverse communities because of its cultural significance and therapeutic effectiveness [19, 22]. Ethnobotany and ethnomedicine, as integral components of traditional healthcare systems, play a central role in documenting and preserving indigenous knowledge, particularly in Ayurveda, where remedies are predominantly prepared from plants and other natural resources [23]. Ethnobotanical research has made significant contributions to medicine, agriculture, and industry [24, 25]. Such studies provide fundamental insights into the traditional knowledge, management, and utilization of plant resources by different cultures and communities [26]. They involve the systematic documentation and analysis of interactions between humans and plants, particularly among indigenous and local populations [27]. Previous ethnobotanical investigations in India and other parts of the world have primarily focused on medicinal plant use, ethnoveterinary practices, or wild edible plants within specific communities, thereby providing valuable but fragmented insights into traditional knowledge systems [28, 29].

Ethnomedicine, deeply rooted in the principles of Ayurveda, forms an essential part of the healthcare practices of indigenous and local communities [30–32]. This knowledge is generally transmitted orally and through experiential learning across generations, making it an integral component of community-based healthcare systems [33, 34].The Chitrakoot district of Uttar Pradesh, bordering Madhya Pradesh, India, possesses considerable cultural, historical, and religious significance. According to Indian mythology, Lord Rama spent thirteen years of his exile in this region, making Chitrakoot a revered sacred landscape [35]. Owing to its spiritual importance, people from diverse regions and communities often migrate to Chitrakoot during the later stages of their lives and adopt ascetic lifestyles. The saint community of Chitrakoot, many of whom reside in or near forested areas, possesses extensive knowledge of medicinal plants. Although several studies have investigated specific tribes or localized settings within Chitrakoot [36, 37], the region remains insufficiently explored from a comprehensive ethnobotanical perspective [32]. The local population can broadly be classified into two groups: (i) localized communities, including Brahmin, Kurmi, and Kuchwaha; and (ii) indigenous and tribal communities, including Saints, Kol, Bhil, Gond, and Mawasi. A considerable number of traditional healers from both groups continue to practice indigenous medicine. They utilize a wide variety of plant-derived materials to prepare traditional remedies for the treatment of common ailments and the maintenance of primary healthcare. Many patients report substantial relief after receiving herbal treatments from these healers, particularly when modern medical treatments are either ineffective or economically inaccessible [38, 39]. However, previous studies have rarely undertaken a comparative assessment of plant-use knowledge between localized communities (Brahmin, Kurmi, and Kuchwaha) and indigenous and tribal groups (Saints, Kol, Bhil, Gond, and Mawasi). Addressing this gap represents a key strength and novel contribution of the present study [40].

This traditional knowledge, deeply embedded within localized communities such as Brahmin, Kurmi, and Kuchwaha, as well as indigenous and tribal groups including Saints, Kol, Bhil, Gond, and Mawasi, forms the foundation of primary healthcare for many residents, particularly those living in remote and forested areas [41, 42]. During social interactions, community members actively exchange and transmit knowledge of medicinal plants, especially in response to health-related challenges, a pattern consistently documented in previous studies [43–45]. In the Chitrakoot region, this knowledge is predominantly transmitted orally across generations, with Vaidyas (traditional healers) playing a central role in preserving and disseminating ethnomedicinal practices [46]. Despite the presence of more than ten thousand saints and ascetics residing in the region for spiritual purposes, systematic ethnobotanical investigations focusing on this unique population remain scarce in the scientific literature [47–49]. The saint community is devoted to the worship of Hindu deities and dedicates its members’ lives to spiritual practice and religious service. As a mark of devotion, many saints traditionally append the suffixes “Das” or “Sharan” to their names [47, 48]. The present study is among the first to systematically document and scientifically contextualize the ethnomedicinal knowledge of localized communities (Brahmin, Kurmi, and Kuchwaha) together with indigenous and tribal groups (Saints, Kol, Bhil, Gond, and Mawasi), thereby addressing a major gap in the existing literature.

However, traditional healing practices in Chitrakoot are facing a serious threat. Most traditional healers belong to older generations, whereas younger people are increasingly reluctant to continue these practices because of limited economic opportunities and changing socio-economic aspirations. Consequently, this valuable knowledge is at imminent risk of being irreversibly lost unless it is systematically documented and preserved [11, 50, 51]. The erosion of indigenous knowledge systems has been widely reported across many regions of the world, particularly in areas undergoing rapid socio-economic development and cultural transformation [52, 53]. Although previous studies have examined specific tribes or localized aspects of plant use, there remains a pressing need for comprehensive research that documents traditional medicinal knowledge within a broader ethnomedicinal framework [54–56]. Unlike earlier fragmented studies, the present investigation integrates ecological, cultural, and utilitarian dimensions of plant use, thereby providing a more holistic and interdisciplinary perspective [57, 58]. The present study adopts a comprehensive and integrative approach that distinguishes it from previous investigations. Most earlier studies have focused on specific categories of plant use, such as marketable species, ethnoveterinary applications, or wild edible plants. In contrast, no study from the Chitrakoot region has systematically documented the full spectrum of plant uses—including food, fuel, fodder, furniture, fencing, and cultural or symbolic applications—within a unified framework. By incorporating both medicinal and non-medicinal uses, the present study provides a holistic understanding of the local biocultural system rather than examining isolated aspects of plant use. The urgency of such documentation is further emphasized by the rapid socio-economic transformations occurring in the Chitrakoot region. Increasing urbanization, infrastructure development, including highways and overbridges in ecologically sensitive areas, and expanding human settlements are placing considerable pressure on forest ecosystems [59, 60]. These changes are restricting access to natural habitats and reducing the availability of medicinal plant resources [42]. At the same time, declining economic returns and changing lifestyles are discouraging younger generations from preserving traditional knowledge [52]. Consequently, the intergenerational transmission of ethnobotanical knowledge is weakening, posing a significant threat to both intangible cultural heritage and biocultural diversity [61, 62]. By documenting this knowledge at a critical transitional stage, the present study provides a valuable baseline dataset to support future monitoring, conservation planning, and policy development [55].

In this context, the present study aims to address critical knowledge gaps through a systematic ethnobotanical survey of the Chitrakoot region. Specifically, the objectives are to: (1) comparatively assess the distribution and depth of ethnomedicinal knowledge among localized communities (Brahmin, Kurmi, and Kuchwaha) and indigenous and tribal groups (Saints, Kol, Bhil, Gond, and Mawasi); (2) document and prioritize ethnomedicinal plant species based on their frequency of use and cultural importance; (3) systematically record traditional knowledge, including vernacular names, plant parts used, methods of preparation and administration, dosage, and associated precautions; (4) quantitatively evaluate the cultural significance and consensus of plant use using standard ethnobotanical indices; and (5) identify similarities, differences, and unique patterns in ethnomedicinal practices by comparing the findings with those of previous studies conducted in similar phytogeographical regions.

The present study aimed to document the traditional knowledge of medicinal plants used by indigenous communities in the Chitrakoot region and to quantitatively evaluate their ethnobotanical importance using established indices, including Number of Use Reports (NUR), Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), and Use Value (UV). Furthermore, the study investigated the patterns of variation in medicinal plant utilization using multivariate analysis. We hypothesized that (H₁) medicinal plant species exhibit variation in their ethnobotanical importance, as reflected by quantitative ethnobotanical indices, and (H₂) this variation is structured by key ethnobotanical variables (RFC, UV, NUR, and the number of plant parts used), resulting in distinct utilization patterns among species. By integrating ecological, cultural, and socio-economic dimensions of plant use with quantitative analyses, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of ethnobotanical knowledge in the Chitrakoot region. The findings are expected to contribute to biodiversity conservation, preservation of traditional knowledge, sustainable utilization of medicinal plant resources, and evidence-based policy development.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The Chitrakoot region extends across the states of Madhya Pradesh (MP) and Uttar Pradesh (UP), India, and is often referred to as the “Wonder of the Hills.” It marks the beginning of the Vindhya Mountain Range and is renowned for its religious, cultural, and historical significance. The region is home to several sacred hills and pilgrimage sites, including Kamadgiri, Hanuman Dhara, Gupt Godawari, Laxman Pahadi, and Sati Anusuiya. The Uttar Pradesh portion covers an area of 3,214 km², whereas the Madhya Pradesh portion extends over 1,353 km², of which 1,262.68 km² is rural [63]. Geographically, the Chitrakoot region spans two Indian states and is characterized by distinct coordinate ranges. The Uttar Pradesh portion lies between 24°48′–25°12′ N latitude and 80°58′–81°34′ E longitude, while the Madhya Pradesh portion extends between 25°10′–25°12′ N latitude and 80°51′–80°52′ E longitude. The region is bounded by Kaushambi to the north, Satna to the south, Banda to the west, and Prayagraj (Allahabad) to the east. Figure 1A illustrates the location of the study area. Chitrakoot experiences a warm tropical climate that strongly influences its vegetation. Summer generally extends from mid-February to September, with June being the hottest month, during which temperatures may reach 46–47 °C. The monsoon season usually begins in late June or early July and continues until mid-September. The region receives approximately 1000 mm of annual rainfall, most of which occurs during the monsoon season. Winters, extending from November to January, are relatively mild, with minimum temperatures occasionally falling to 5 °C [163]. Administratively, the Chitrakoot region is shared between the states of Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh. According to the Census of India (2011), the Chitrakoot district of Madhya Pradesh has a population of 23,316, comprising 12,675 males and 10,641 females, with an overall literacy rate of 70.01%. In contrast, Chitrakoot Dham in Uttar Pradesh has a population of 57,402, including 30,421 males and 26,981 females, and a literacy rate of 78.50%. The elevation of the region ranges from 137 to 400 m above sea level, resulting in a diverse topography.

Chitrakoot supports a rich diversity of medicinal plant species; however, their abundance has declined considerably over the past decade due to increasing anthropogenic pressures. Unregulated urban expansion, extensive road construction, overgrazing, large-scale migration, and illegal as well as unsustainable harvesting have collectively contributed to the depletion of medicinal plant resources in the region, reflecting trends reported from many parts of the world [64, 65]. Consequently, traditional ethnobotanical knowledge associated with these resources is also gradually declining. A previous floristic survey documented approximately 750 flowering plant species belonging to 445 genera and 111 families in the Chitrakoot region [63]. The rich floral diversity, coupled with limited access to modern healthcare facilities in many rural areas, has encouraged local communities to continue relying on traditional medicinal practices for primary healthcare.

Surveys and questionnaire

Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA) and Rapid Rural Appraisal (RRA) methods were employed to actively engage local communities and facilitate the systematic collection of ethnobotanical information [54, 55]. These participatory approaches are widely recognized as effective tools for documenting indigenous knowledge systems and understanding community-based resource use patterns [55, 187]. A preliminary survey was conducted before the main field investigation to identify knowledgeable informants, including traditional healers (Vaidyas) and other individuals possessing extensive ethnomedicinal knowledge. This initial phase also helped establish rapport with participants and familiarize them with the objectives of the study. Extensive field surveys were conducted between 2020 and 2024 across different seasons to ensure comprehensive documentation of plant-use patterns and seasonal availability [66]. Seasonal surveys are considered essential in ethnobotanical research because they increase the likelihood of documenting species with different phenological stages and seasonal uses [56]. Each field visit lasted approximately 4–7 days.

A total of 205 informants (168 males and 37 females) participated in interviews and discussions. During the study period, the first author personally visited all selected locations and interviewed each respondent. Informants were selected using purposive and snowball sampling techniques, targeting individuals with extensive traditional knowledge [67, 68]. These non-probabilistic sampling methods are widely used in ethnobotanical research to identify key knowledge holders and facilitate access to specialized knowledge networks [66, 69]. The respondents represented diverse communities, including localized groups such as Brahmin, Kurmi, and Kushwaha, as well as indigenous and tribal communities including Kol, Mawasi, Gond, Bhil, and members of the local saint community. Demographic information of the participants

Data were collected primarily through individual semi-structured interviews supplemented with open-ended questions, following standard ethnobotanical survey protocols [177]. Group discussions were conducted where appropriate to validate and enrich the information obtained during individual interviews. In addition, participant observation and guided field (transect) walks were undertaken with key informants to identify plant species in their natural habitats and verify their traditional uses. Guided field walks are widely recognized as an effective validation method because they enable direct observation of plant species and their utilization in the local context [54]. Each informant was interviewed two to three times, and in some cases more frequently, to improve data reliability. Repeated interactions facilitated trust-building, cross-verification of information, and the collection of detailed ethnomedicinal knowledge. Such repeated verification and data triangulation are recommended to enhance the reliability and validity of ethnobotanical datasets [70–73]. All interviews were conducted in the local Bundelkhandi dialect or Hindi to ensure effective communication. Information recorded included local plant names, plant parts used, methods of preparation, routes of administration, dosage, precautions, reported side effects, harvesting locations, and conservation practices. To develop a comprehensive inventory of plant uses, informants were also asked about additional use categories, including food, fuel, fodder, furniture, fencing, and cultural or ritual applications. The information obtained was subsequently cross-verified through repeated interviews with multiple informants and validated during field observations, thereby enhancing the consistency and reliability of the recorded ethnobotanical knowledge [70–73].

Collection and identification of plant material

Plant materials used for ethnomedicinal purposes were collected from open forests, grasslands, and roadside vegetation throughout the Chitrakoot region. Traditional healers guided the researchers to the natural habitats of medicinal plants, enabling direct observation of their ecological distribution and traditional harvesting practices. Voucher specimens were collected during field surveys for taxonomic verification. The collected specimens were identified based on their morphological characteristics using standard taxonomic procedures and authenticated with the aid of published botanical literature [74–76]. Voucher specimens were assigned accession numbers ranging from CDKU1 to CDKU119 and deposited in the Herbarium of the Department of Botany, Kalinga University, for future reference. Taxonomic authentication was further supported by consulting regional floras, including Flora of Madhya Pradesh (Vols. I–III) and Flora of Uttar Pradesh (Vols. I–III) [75, 77]. Herbarium voucher documentation represents a fundamental standard in ethnobotanical research, ensuring taxonomic accuracy, reproducibility, and future verification of the reported plant species [188].

Preference ranking exercise

Following the methodology of previous researchers [84], a preference ranking exercise was conducted with ten key informants to evaluate the perceived efficacy of five medicinal plants traditionally used to treat asthma. Asthma is one of the common respiratory disorders in the Chitrakoot region, and its prevalence has reportedly increased in recent years, partly because of increasing environmental pollution. Initially, the key informants identified five medicinal plant species commonly used for asthma treatment. Through group discussions, each informant ranked the plants according to their perceived therapeutic effectiveness using a scale from 1 (least effective) to 5 (most effective). The final preference ranking for each species was obtained by summing the scores assigned by all informants, thereby identifying the most preferred medicinal plants for asthma management.

Direct matrix ranking exercise

The direct matrix ranking method described by Martin [55] and Cotton [54] was employed to evaluate the multipurpose uses of selected medicinal plant species. Species with diverse uses were selected for assessment. Key informants assigned scores ranging from 5 (highest importance) to 0 (not used) for each use category. The average scores for each species were calculated and used to determine their overall ranking. Additionally, a priority ranking exercise involving ten key informants was conducted to identify and rank the major threats affecting medicinal plant species in the study area. Each quantitative index used in this study assessed a different aspect of ethnomedicinal knowledge. RFC measured the relative popularity of plant species, UV reflected the diversity of reported uses, ICF quantified the level of agreement among informants, FL identified species with high therapeutic specificity, JI enabled comparisons with previous ethnobotanical studies and among indigenous communities, whereas preference and direct matrix ranking evaluated perceived therapeutic effectiveness and multipurpose utility. Collectively, these indices provided a comprehensive and multidimensional assessment of ethnomedicinal plant use in the study area.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to quantitatively evaluate the ethnobotanical importance and utilization patterns of medicinal plant species documented in the Chitrakoot region. Two complementary hypotheses were examined. Hypothesis 1 (H₁): Quantitative ethnobotanical indices, including the Number of Use Reports (NUR), Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), and Use Value (UV), exhibit significant variation among the documented medicinal plant species, reflecting differences in their cultural importance and utilization patterns. Hypothesis 2 (H₂): Variation in the ethnobotanical importance of medicinal plant species is structured by quantitative ethnobotanical indices (RFC, UV, NUR, and the number of plant parts used), resulting in distinct utilization patterns among species. To evaluate Hypothesis 1, descriptive statistical analyses were performed for NUR, RFC, and UV. Measures of central tendency and dispersion, including the mean, standard deviation, range, and 95% confidence intervals, were calculated to summarize the distribution of each ethnobotanical index and to characterize variation in the cultural importance and utilization of medicinal plant species. Statistical analyses were conducted at a significance level of α = 0.05 using appropriate methods based on the distributional characteristics and measurement scales of the respective variables. To evaluate Hypothesis 2, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using four quantitative ethnobotanical variables: Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Use Value (UV), Number of Use Reports (NUR), and the number of plant parts used. PCA is a widely used multivariate technique for reducing data dimensionality and identifying the principal variables responsible for species differentiation and utilization patterns [32, 164]. Prior to analysis, all variables were standardized to eliminate differences in measurement scales [164].

The PCA was performed in R software (version 4.1.2) using the PCA() function of the FactoMineR package, together with the factoextra, patchwork, and ggplot2 packages [165, 166]. Principal components were retained according to the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues > 1) [167], and the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which explained the greatest proportion of the total variance, were selected for interpretation.

The PCA results were visualized using the fviz_pca_biplot() function. Plant species were represented as points, while ethnobotanical variables were displayed as loading vectors. Species were colour-coded according to growth habit (herb, shrub, tree, and liana), and 95% confidence ellipses were included to illustrate clustering patterns among habit groups. The contribution matrix (res.pcacontrib) was used to identify the ten plant species contributing most strongly to PC1 and PC2, and these species were labelled in the PCA biplots to facilitate interpretation of the major sources of variation in ethnobotanical utilization patterns.

Results

Demographic information of the participants

Several field visits and repeated interviews with traditional healers were conducted to document ethnobotanical knowledge. A total of 205 participants representing diverse communities contributed to the study, including members of the saint community (38.98%), Kol (15.25%), Kurmi (13.55%), Brahmin (12.71%), Kuchwaha (11.86%), Bhil (4.32%), Gond (1.68%), Nai (0.84%), and Baghel (0.84%). Of the participants, 168 were male and 37 were female, with ages ranging from 30 to 82 years. The educational background of the participants varied: 12 had completed postgraduate education, 28 were graduates, 55 had completed matriculation, and 92 had attained only elementary education, while the remaining participants were illiterate. Most participants (57.07%) reported that they had inherited their ethnomedicinal knowledge from their forefathers, whereas 42.30% acquired their knowledge through self-experimentation. All participants belonged to the Hindu religion and identified Hindi as their primary language. Detailed demographic characteristics of the participants, including community affiliation, educational level, and occupation, as well as information on the study sites—including location, GPS coordinates, altitude, habitat, ethnicity, language, religion, population, and participant characteristics—are presented in Tables 1 and 2 and illustrated in Fig. 1B (Supplementary file).

Documented plant species

A total of 119 plant species belonging to 107 genera and 50 families were documented based on information provided by the participants. Of these, 90 species were collected from wild habitats, whereas 29 were cultivated. Among the recorded families, Fabaceae was the most dominant, comprising 14 species, followed by Malvaceae (8 species) and Apocynaceae (6 species). At the generic level, Ficus was the most represented genus with four species, followed by Terminalia (three species), and Piper and Solanum (two species each). The distribution of plant families is presented in Fig. 2. Based on growth habit, herbs constituted the largest proportion (39%), followed by trees (29%) and shrubs (29%), whereas lianas accounted for only 3% (Fig. 3). Leaves were the most frequently utilized plant part (36.11%), followed by roots (13.19%), seeds (11.80%), and whole plants (10%). Other plant parts included fruits (9%), bark (5%), rhizomes and flowers (4% each), and tubers, bulbs, and gums (1% each) (Fig. 4). Powder was the most common method of preparation (29%), followed by decoction (24%), paste (23%), and fresh plant parts or juice (9%), whereas oil-based preparations were the least common (1%). Most remedies were administered orally (72%), while the remaining 28% were applied topically (Fig. 5).

Quantitative analysis of ethnobotanical data

Relative frequency of citation (RFC)

The Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC) reflects the proportion of informants who cited a particular plant species relative to the total number of informants surveyed. In the present study, the highest RFC value was recorded for Piper nigrum (1.00), followed by Zingiber officinale (0.86), Allium sativum (0.84), and Curcuma longa (0.76). In contrast, the lowest RFC values were recorded for Balanites roxburghii (0.08), Sterculia urens (0.09), and Grewia hirsuta (0.11).

Informant consensus factor (ICF)

The Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) measures the degree of agreement among informants regarding the use of medicinal plants for treating specific disease categories. In the present study, the highest ICF value was recorded for the category of snake, scorpion, and insect bites (0.88), followed by digestive system disorders (0.84) and respiratory disorders (0.83). The lowest ICF value was recorded for dental disorders (0.64). The disease categories and their corresponding ICF values are presented in Table 4.

Jaccard index (JI)

The present study exhibited the highest similarity with ethnobotanical studies conducted in Central India, particularly in Madhya Pradesh and adjoining regions. The highest Jaccard Similarity Index (JI) was recorded for Amarkantak, Madhya Pradesh (22.80%) [170], followed by Pushprajgarh Block, Anuppur District (19.89%) [171], and northeastern Madhya Pradesh (14.29%) [169]. Similarly, studies from Chitrakoot, Madhya Pradesh (14.48%) [41] and Chitrakoot, Uttar Pradesh (14.75%) [89] showed moderate similarity. This relatively high overlap is likely attributable to comparable floristic composition, similar agro-climatic conditions, and long-standing cultural exchange among geographically adjacent tribal and rural communities. Furthermore, the presence of similar ethnic groups and traditional healers may have contributed to the observed similarity in ethnomedicinal knowledge.

Compared with studies from other regions of India, the similarity indices ranged from moderate to low. For example, studies from Chandauli District (17.74%) [93], the Palamalai region of the Eastern Ghats (17.33%) [95], and Sarguja District (10.34%) [121] exhibited moderate similarity, indicating partial overlap in medicinal plant use despite geographical separation. In contrast, lower JI values were reported for the Garhwal Himalaya (7.32%) [168], Madurai District, Tamil Nadu (7.19%) [127], and northern Bengal (3.20%) [162], reflecting regional differences in plant diversity and ethnomedicinal practices. These variations may be attributed to differences in ecological conditions, altitudinal gradients, and cultural traditions that influence the selection and use of medicinal plants.

Comparison with ethnobotanical studies conducted outside India revealed very low JI values, emphasizing the localized nature of traditional medicinal knowledge. The lowest similarity indices were recorded for Central Macedonia, Greece (1.48%) [126], Parbat District, Nepal (2.03%) [123], Woyde District, Ethiopia (2.33%) [122], and Khatling Valley, western Himalaya (3.31%) [94]. Similarly, studies from Ensaro District, Ethiopia (3.83%) [97], Muja District, Ethiopia (3.48%) [125], and Ramechhap District, Nepal (10.73%) [79] also demonstrated limited similarity in ethnomedicinal practices.

Overall, these findings indicate that ethnomedicinal knowledge is highly region-specific and is strongly influenced by local biodiversity, cultural traditions, and the independent evolution of traditional knowledge systems. The limited overlap among geographically distant regions underscores the importance of documenting indigenous ethnomedicinal knowledge before it is irreversibly lost (Table 6).

Direct matrix ranking

Direct matrix ranking revealed that local communities utilize several plant species for multiple purposes beyond their medicinal applications. These uses include fuelwood, fencing, house construction, food, farm implements, and furniture. The ten most important multipurpose plant species identified were Mangifera indica, Dalbergia sissoo, Madhuca longifolia, Azadirachta indica, Ficus religiosa, Ocimum sanctum, Diospyros ebenum, Ziziphus jujuba, Piper longum, and Zingiber officinale (Table 8). The cumulative ranking across seven use categories showed that the principal uses of these plant species, in descending order of importance, were fuelwood, fencing, farm implements, house construction, medicinal purposes, fodder, and food.

Most of the medicinal plants documented in the present study were collected from wild habitats, including open forests, roadside vegetation, and riverbanks. Some medicinal ingredients, particularly spices and seeds used in the preparation of herbal remedies, were purchased from local markets. Only 20.16% of the documented species were cultivated by traditional healers or obtained through commercial sources. The heavy reliance on wild-harvested plants (approximately 80%) with limited cultivation raises concerns about the long-term sustainability of traditional ethnomedicinal practices [128]. The distribution of all recorded species was documented, and their conservation status was evaluated using the IUCN Red List [129] (https://www.iucnredlist.org/). One-sample t-test analysis

The quantitative ethnobotanical indices, including Number of Use Reports (NUR), Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), and Use Value (UV), were analysed to characterize the ethnobotanical importance and utilization patterns of the 119 medicinal plant species documented in the Chitrakoot region (Tables 9 and 10; Fig. 6A–C). The mean NUR was 1.98 (95% CI: 1.79–2.18), indicating that the recorded medicinal plant species were associated with multiple reported therapeutic uses. The relatively high NUR values reflect the extensive utilization of medicinal plants and the diversity of traditional knowledge maintained by the local communities. The mean RFC was 0.362 (95% CI: 0.328–0.395), suggesting that individual medicinal plant species were cited by varying proportions of informants. This pattern indicates differences in community recognition and familiarity with medicinal plants, with some species being widely known while others were reported by relatively few informants. The mean UV was 0.470 (95% CI: 0.432–0.508), indicating moderate overall utilization of the documented medicinal plant species. The observed variation in UV reflects differences in the frequency and diversity of medicinal uses assigned to individual species by the respondents. Collectively, these quantitative ethnobotanical indices demonstrate substantial variation in the cultural importance and utilization of medicinal plant species in the Chitrakoot region. Higher NUR values highlight the broad therapeutic applications of several species, whereas the variability in RFC and UV indicates that ethnobotanical knowledge and patterns of plant use are not uniformly distributed among the documented flora. These findings provide quantitative evidence of the diversity and richness of traditional medicinal knowledge within the study area and complement the multivariate patterns revealed by the Principal Component Analysis.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

To test Hypothesis 2 (H₂), which proposed that variation in ethnobotanical importance among medicinal plant species is associated with Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Use Value (UV), Number of Use Reports (NUR), and the number of plant parts used, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using data from 119 medicinal plant species. The first two principal components explained 79.7% of the total variance, with PC1 accounting for 48.2% and PC2 accounting for 31.5% (Figs. 7 and 8; Table 10), indicating that these components effectively summarized the major patterns of ethnobotanical variation. The PCA biplot (Fig. 7) revealed two complementary dimensions of ethnobotanical importance. RFC and UV were strongly associated with the positive axis of PC1, indicating that this component primarily represents species recognition, citation frequency, and utilization intensity within the indigenous community. Consequently, species with high positive PC1 scores exhibited greater cultural importance and were more frequently utilized. In contrast, NUR and the number of plant parts used were strongly associated with PC2, representing the diversity of medicinal applications and versatility of plant-part utilization. Species with higher PC2 scores were characterized by broader therapeutic applications and greater flexibility in traditional medicinal practices. The ordination pattern showed considerable overlap among herbs, shrubs, trees, and lianas, suggesting that ethnobotanical importance was not strongly determined by growth habit. However, herbaceous species formed a relatively compact cluster, indicating more consistent ethnobotanical characteristics, whereas shrubs and trees exhibited greater dispersion, reflecting higher variability in medicinal uses and traditional knowledge. The contribution plots (Fig. 8A–B) identified the plant species contributing most strongly to the principal components. Species with the greatest contributions to PC1 included Piper nigrum L. (SP88), Zingiber officinale Roscoe (SP118), Grewia hirsuta Vahl. (SP57), Butea monosperma (Lam.) Taub. (SP21), Allium sativum L. (SP6), Sterculia urens Roxb. (SP101), Aristolochia indica L. (SP12), Balanites roxburghii Planch. (SP16), Punica granatum L. (SP94), and Terminalia bellirica (Gaertn.) Roxb. (SP105), reflecting their high community recognition and utilization. Species contributing most strongly to PC2 included Ficus racemosa L. (SP51), Madhuca longifolia (J.Koenig) J.F.Macbr. (SP70), Argemone mexicana L. (SP11), Tridax procumbens L. (SP110), Ziziphus jujuba Mill. (SP119), Morus alba L. (SP75), Boerhavia diffusa L. (SP19), Cassia fistula L. (SP24), Anacyclus pyrethrum (L.) (SP8), and Ocimum sanctum L. (SP81), indicating broader therapeutic applications and more diverse plant-part utilization. Overall, the PCA demonstrated that ethnobotanical importance in the Chitrakoot region is structured by two complementary dimensions: (i) community recognition and utilization intensity, represented by RFC and UV, and (ii) diversity of medicinal applications and plant-part utilization, represented by NUR and the number of plant parts used. Together, these variables explained a substantial proportion of the observed variation among medicinal plant species, thereby supporting Hypothesis 2 (Table 11).

Discussion

Socio-cultural patterns in knowledge distribution

The predominance of male participants observed in the present study reflects the socio-cultural organization of the region, where traditional medicinal knowledge is often inherited through patrilineal systems and associated with greater mobility in forest-based plant collection. Similar gender-based patterns of knowledge transmission have been reported in earlier ethnobotanical studies [85, 181]. However, women also play an important role in household-level healthcare practices, suggesting complementary knowledge systems within communities. The wide age range (30–82 years) among participants indicates that ethnomedicinal knowledge is concentrated largely among older individuals, highlighting the risk of knowledge erosion due to modernization, migration, and reduced interest among younger generations. Comparable age-related patterns have been documented in other indigenous communities [182, 183]. The predominance of family-based knowledge acquisition further emphasizes the oral and experiential nature of traditional healthcare systems [86–88]. Such orally transmitted knowledge is particularly vulnerable to decline if intergenerational transfer weakens. Overall, these findings show that ethnomedicinal knowledge in the study area remains deeply embedded in socio-cultural traditions shaped by gender roles, inheritance systems, and informal knowledge transmission.

Ethnomedicinal plant diversity and utilization patterns

The number of plant species documented in the present study was higher than that reported in previous ethnobotanical investigations conducted in the Chitrakoot region and adjoining districts [36, 37, 41, 89]. This difference may be attributed to the broader geographical coverage of the present study and the inclusion of multiple communities, which likely captured greater floristic and cultural diversity. Similar increases in medicinal plant richness associated with wider sampling coverage and community heterogeneity have been reported in other ethnobotanical studies [181]. The dominance of the Fabaceae family may be attributed to its ecological abundance, wide distribution, multipurpose utility, and rich phytochemical composition [90, 91]. Similar dominance of Fabaceae has been reported in several ethnobotanical studies [92, 93]. Families such as Malvaceae, Apocynaceae, and Solanaceae also contributed substantially to ethnomedicinal practices in the study area, likely because of their diverse secondary metabolites and broad therapeutic potential.

The predominance of herbaceous species probably reflects their abundance, easy accessibility, and rapid regeneration, making them suitable for regular medicinal use. Comparable findings have been reported from other regions of India [94–96]. Leaves were the most frequently used plant part, possibly because of their year-round availability, high phytochemical content, and relatively sustainable harvesting compared with roots and bark [79, 98, 184]. In contrast, the frequent use of roots, although therapeutically valuable [99], raises conservation concerns because root harvesting directly threatens plant survival [184]. Powdered formulations were the most common method of preparation, likely because they are easy to prepare, store, and administer, as also reported in previous studies [100]. The predominance of oral administration is consistent with the treatment of systemic ailments and the practical ease of remedy consumption [25, 87]. Likewise, the widespread use of polyherbal formulations reflects the traditional belief that combining multiple plant species enhances therapeutic efficacy while reducing potential toxicity [41, 70, 88]. The addition of honey, jaggery, and rock sugar not only improves the palatability of herbal preparations but may also enhance their therapeutic effectiveness [95, 101, 102, 104]. Furthermore, the incorporation of culinary spices into herbal remedies highlights the close relationship between food and medicine, a characteristic feature of indigenous healthcare systems [104–106].

Interpretation of RFC values and ethnomedicinal importance

RFC values provide an estimate of the popularity and cultural importance of medicinal plants based on the frequency of citation by informants [58, 78]. The high RFC values recorded for Piper nigrum, Zingiber officinale, Allium sativum, and Curcuma longa indicate their widespread use and strong cultural importance in the study area [13, 103]. Their prominence reflects not only their therapeutic effectiveness but also their widespread availability and integration into household healthcare practices. Similar observations have been reported in other ethnobotanical studies, where frequently cited species are often considered culturally preferred and pharmacologically validated medicinal plants [78]. Piper nigrum is widely recognized for its bioenhancing properties, primarily attributed to piperine, which enhances the bioavailability of other herbal compounds [107, 108]. Its frequent citation may therefore reflect its important role in polyherbal formulations. Zingiber officinale is extensively used in traditional medical systems, including Ayurveda, Traditional Chinese Medicine, and Unani, for the treatment of digestive, respiratory, and circulatory disorders [109, 110]. Its high RFC is likely associated with its broad therapeutic spectrum and routine dietary use. Allium sativum and Curcuma longa likewise occupy a central position in traditional healthcare because of their diverse pharmacological properties, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities [111–115]. Their high citation frequency further highlights the close relationship between food and medicine, whereby commonly consumed spices also serve as preventive and therapeutic remedies. The prominence of these species in the present study is consistent with their well-established ethnomedicinal importance across diverse traditional healthcare systems [185].

Interpretation of ICF and UV values

A high ICF value indicates strong informant agreement on medicinal plant use for specific ailments, reflecting the reliability of traditional knowledge and potential pharmacological significance [25, 80]. The high ICF values recorded for snakebite, scorpion sting, and insect bite categories indicate strong agreement among informants and suggest the existence of specialized, well-established treatment practices for emergency conditions [13, 103]. Such high consensus is often considered evidence of repeated empirical validation of traditional remedies over generations [116, 117]. Likewise, the high ICF values for digestive and respiratory disorders probably reflect both their frequent occurrence and the availability of effective traditional therapies. In contrast, the relatively low ICF value for dental disorders may indicate greater variation in treatment practices, lower specialization, or limited exchange of ethnomedicinal knowledge among traditional healers. During fieldwork, several healers were reluctant to disclose their medicinal knowledge, which may have further contributed to the lower consensus observed for certain disease categories. Similar patterns of knowledge secrecy, often associated with socio-economic insecurity and concerns about knowledge exploitation, have been reported in previous ethnobotanical studies [118, 119, 186].

Use Value (UV) quantifies the relative importance of a plant species based on citation frequency, with higher values indicating greater ethnobotanical significance and versatility [32].The Use Value (UV) analysis further highlights the relative cultural and therapeutic importance of medicinal plant species [13, 103]. Species with high UV values, such as Piper nigrum and Allium sativum, exhibited broad medicinal applicability and were used to treat multiple ailment categories, underscoring their central role in community healthcare. Their high UV values may also reflect their widespread availability and the strong confidence placed in them by traditional healers. In contrast, species with low UV values are likely to represent specialized remedies or medicinal plants used for relatively uncommon ailments. Comparable patterns of use values have been documented in previous ethnobotanical studies [3, 32, 120].

Significance of fidelity level in ethnomedicine

High Fidelity Level (FL) values indicate strong agreement among informants regarding the use of specific plant species for treating particular ailments, reflecting their perceived therapeutic effectiveness and cultural reliability [82]. Species such as Piper nigrum, Allium sativum, and Zingiber officinale exhibited high FL values, underscoring their prominent role in local healthcare practices and corroborating their well-established importance in traditional medicinal systems. Comparable findings have been reported in previous ethnobotanical investigations, where species with high FL values are generally regarded as culturally important and are frequently associated with specific medicinal applications [160, 186]. Owing to their consistent traditional use and high informant consensus, these species represent promising candidates for future phytochemical, pharmacological, and clinical investigations to validate their therapeutic potential.

Comparative ethnobotanical analysis using Jaccard Index

Jaccard Index measures similarity between two communities based on shared species, indicating floristic overlap and biodiversity relationships . The comparative analysis based on the Jaccard Index (JI) revealed considerable spatial heterogeneity in ethnomedicinal knowledge, shaped by ecological and socio-cultural factors. The generally low to moderate similarity values indicate that medicinal plant use is largely localized and community-specific, consistent with findings from previous comparative ethnobotanical studies [181, 184]. Higher similarity was observed with studies conducted in Central India, particularly in Amarkantak [170], Pushprajgarh Block [171], Northeastern Madhya Pradesh [169], and the Chitrakoot region [41, 89]. This overlap is likely attributable to geographical proximity, comparable vegetation, and shared ethnocultural traditions, which facilitate knowledge exchange and similar patterns of medicinal plant use. In contrast, similarity decreased with increasing geographical distance. Moderate overlap with Chandauli district [93], the Palamalai region of the Eastern Ghats [95], and Sarguja district [121] suggests partial convergence resulting from shared medicinal traditions and the occurrence of widely distributed plant species. Comparable regional patterns have also been documented elsewhere in India [182, 183]. Conversely, the much lower similarity observed with studies from the Garhwal Himalaya [168], Madurai district [127], and Northern Bengal [162] highlights the influence of climatic conditions, floristic composition, and cultural specialization in shaping distinct ethnomedicinal traditions.

The very low similarity recorded with studies conducted outside India, including Central Macedonia, Greece [126], Parbat District, Nepal [123], and Woyde District, Ethiopia [122], together with other reports from Ethiopia and Nepal [79, 97, 125], further emphasizes the highly localized nature of traditional medicinal knowledge. Such interregional variation has been widely attributed to ecological differences and the independent evolution of traditional healthcare systems [185]. Notably, the occurrence of common plant species does not necessarily correspond to similar therapeutic applications. The same species may be used to treat different ailments across communities, reflecting culturally adapted healing practices and locally accumulated empirical knowledge. Similar observations have been reported in previous comparative ethnomedicinal studies [186]. These findings underscore the importance of region-specific documentation for preserving indigenous knowledge and identifying novel medicinal resources.

Preference ranking identified Justicia adhatoda and Allium sativum as the most preferred species for the treatment of asthma, indicating strong community consensus regarding their perceived efficacy [55]. Their high ranking reflects both repeated practical use and confidence in their therapeutic effectiveness. This finding is supported by previous studies documenting the widespread use of Allium sativum for asthma, bronchitis, and cough because of its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties [172–174]. Likewise, Justicia adhatoda has been extensively reported for its bronchodilatory and anti-asthmatic activities, primarily attributed to alkaloids such as vasicine and vasicinone [175, 176]. The agreement between traditional knowledge and pharmacological evidence strengthens the credibility of these species as potential therapeutic resources.

Direct matrix ranking assesses the multipurpose value and exploitation pressure of plant species, supporting conservation and sustainable resource management [55]. Direct matrix ranking demonstrated the multipurpose importance of species such as Mangifera indica, Dalbergia sissoo, and Madhuca longifolia, which are widely utilized for food, fuelwood, construction, and other livelihood needs. The greater preference for fuelwood and fencing over medicinal use reflects the dependence of rural communities on plant resources for subsistence and household requirements. Similar patterns of multipurpose utilization have been documented in ethnobiological studies from India and other parts of the world [177–180]. Such intensive exploitation may increase harvesting pressure, particularly on woody species with slow regeneration rates [184].

The heavy reliance on wild-harvested medicinal plants (approximately 80%) raises important concerns regarding sustainability and the long-term availability of plant resources. Comparable dependence on wild medicinal flora has been reported in several ethnobotanical studies and is frequently associated with limited domestication efforts and easy access to forest resources [94, 182]. The relatively low level of cultivation observed in the present study further indicates inadequate domestication, thereby increasing pressure on natural populations. Although most documented species are currently classified as Least Concern, the presence of Vulnerable and Near Threatened taxa warrants immediate conservation attention. Furthermore, the large proportion of species that have not yet been evaluated highlights substantial gaps in conservation assessment. Similar conservation concerns have been emphasized in previous ethnobotanical investigations [183, 184]. Collectively, these findings underscore the urgent need to promote sustainable harvesting practices, encourage the cultivation of medicinal plants, and implement targeted conservation strategies to safeguard both plant diversity and the associated traditional knowledge systems.

Comparative analysis of the current findings with existing local and regional research

This study highlights the rich ethnobotanical knowledge preserved by the saint and tribal communities of the Chitrakoot region and makes a valuable contribution to ethnomedicinal research. The systematic documentation of 119 medicinal plant species, together with their therapeutic applications and preparation methods, enriches the existing ethnobotanical database of India and provides an important foundation for future pharmacological, phytochemical, and conservation research.

Comparative analysis showed that 163 medicinal plant species belonging to 69 families have been reported in previous ethnobotanical studies conducted in the Chitrakoot region [36, 37, 41, 63, 89, 130]. Of the ethnomedicinal uses documented in the present investigation, 47% were consistent with earlier reports, supporting the reliability and continuity of traditional knowledge systems. In contrast, the remaining 53% represented different or previously unreported therapeutic applications, highlighting the dynamic, adaptive, and localized nature of ethnomedicinal knowledge. These variations may reflect differences in healer expertise, disease perception, plant availability, and socio-cultural practices.

Furthermore, 78 of the documented species were also reported from adjoining districts of Chitrakoot, demonstrating strong regional continuity in medicinal plant use and knowledge transmission. At the same time, the identification of novel therapeutic applications enhances the scientific value of the present study and provides a basis for future phytochemical, pharmacological, and clinical investigations aimed at validating their medicinal potential.

Cultural value of recorded plant

Many medicinal plant species documented in the present study possess considerable cultural and religious significance among the indigenous communities of the Chitrakoot region [28]. The wood of Butea monosperma is traditionally used in wedding ceremonies [26]. According to Indian mythology, Phyllanthus emblica is regarded as the abode of Lord Vishnu, and local tribal communities perform religious rituals beneath this sacred tree [68]. Likewise, Ocimum sanctum is revered as the dwelling place of Goddess Lakshmi and is cultivated in the households of many traditional healers for both religious worship and medicinal use [69]. Rosa species are widely cultivated for their ornamental and aromatic value [131]. The leaves and fruits of Aegle marmelos (bael) are traditionally offered to Lord Shiva during religious ceremonies [23]. Garlands made from the leaves of Mangifera indica are commonly hung at the entrances of houses during auspicious occasions as symbols of prosperity and good fortune [38]. Similarly, Ficus religiosa, Ficus benghalensis, and Ficus virens occupy an important place in the spiritual and religious traditions of local communities [132]. Nyctanthes arbor-tristis is traditionally believed to be a celestial tree brought to Earth by Lord Krishna for his wife, Satyabhama [133]. The flowers and fruits of Madhuca longifolia are used in various folk ceremonies and cultural celebrations [133]. In addition, the rhizome powder of Curcuma longa is mixed with water to prepare a ceremonial paste that is applied to the bride and groom before marriage as part of a traditional ritual believed to enhance skin complexion and symbolize purity and prosperity [134, 135].

Identification of novel medicinal plants and uses

A review of twelve previous ethnobotanical studies conducted in Chitrakoot and its surrounding regions indicates that the present investigation provides the first ethnobotanical documentation of 39 plant species from this region. In addition, this study employs quantitative ethnobotanical indices, including Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Informant Consensus Factor (ICF), and Use Value (UV), providing a more rigorous analytical framework for evaluating the cultural and medicinal significance of plant species than has previously been reported in the Chitrakoot region.

The study also documented 81 previously unreported medicinal uses, highlighting the dynamic nature of indigenous healthcare knowledge. Novel therapeutic applications were recorded for several species, including Achyranthes aspera for asthma and scorpion bites, Agave americana for constipation, Argemone mexicana for leucoderma and respiratory disorders, Aristolochia indica for snakebite, Asparagus racemosus for urinogenital infections, Azadirachta indica for burning sensations and indigestion, Bauhinia variegata for goiter and stomach ulcers, Boerhavia diffusa for jaundice and asthma, Butea monosperma for erectile dysfunction and ulcers, and additional novel uses of Calotropis procera, Triticum aestivum, Aegle marmelos, Dalbergia sissoo, Rosa indica, Cordia macleodii, Croton tiglium, and Cinnamomum verum. These findings substantially expand the regional ethnobotanical database and identify promising candidates for future phytochemical and pharmacological investigations.

Traditional healthcare practices in Chitrakoot rely extensively on polyherbal formulations, consistent with Ayurvedic principles in which multiple plant ingredients are combined to enhance therapeutic efficacy through synergistic interactions while reducing adverse effects [136, 137]. Traditional healers also recommend dietary restrictions, particularly avoiding oily, spicy, and processed foods during treatment. Such practices correspond with Ayurvedic dietary principles aimed at maintaining dosha balance and improving digestive efficiency, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes [138–150]. A particularly noteworthy observation was the frequent inclusion of Piper nigrum in herbal formulations. This reflects its recognized role as a bioenhancer through piperine-mediated enhancement of the bioavailability of co-administered herbal compounds [140, 141]. The strong confidence expressed by Vaidyas in traditional medicine further highlights its socio-economic importance, as these remedies are considered effective, affordable, accessible, and associated with comparatively fewer side effects [142, 144].

The quantitative analyses further emphasized the ethnomedicinal importance of several medicinal species. Piper nigrum and Allium sativum exhibited the highest Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC) values, indicating widespread community acceptance and frequent therapeutic use. These species are also extensively described in classical Ayurvedic texts such as the Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita for their antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and digestive properties [144, 145]. Likewise, the highest Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) recorded for snakebite and digestive disorders indicates strong agreement among traditional healers regarding effective treatment practices. These disease categories are similarly emphasized in Ayurveda, where plant-based formulations are widely used for detoxification and digestive regulation [146, 147]. The high consensus observed in the present study further supports the ethnopharmacological relevance of these remedies and identifies them as promising candidates for future pharmacological validation.

The present investigation also provides valuable insights into the current status of medicinal plant diversity and the growing threats to phytodiversity in the Chitrakoot region. Anthropogenic activities, including overgrazing, road construction, infrastructure expansion, habitat degradation, and unsustainable harvesting, are contributing significantly to the decline of medicinal plant populations, consistent with observations reported from other regions of India and worldwide [34, 42]. Species-specific observations further illustrate these conservation challenges. For example, Bacopa monnieri has experienced habitat degradation due to embankment construction along the Mandakini River, a phenomenon similarly reported for riparian medicinal flora elsewhere [151]. Likewise, Alectra parasitica subsp. chitrakutensis is increasingly threatened by deforestation, fire, and human encroachment [152]. The decline of Physalis minima under pressures of overgrazing and climate variability is consistent with previous reports demonstrating the vulnerability of herbaceous medicinal plants to ecological disturbances [153]. Furthermore, excessive harvesting of high-demand species such as Pueraria tuberosa, Dioscorea bulbifera, Withania somnifera, Asparagus racemosus, and Hemidesmus indicus reflects a broader pattern of unsustainable exploitation documented across traditional medicinal systems [154, 155]. The decline of culturally important species such as Aegle marmelos further illustrates how biodiversity loss can directly affect indigenous healthcare systems and traditional cultural practices [156].

To address these challenges, conservation initiatives including ex situ cultivation of medicinal plant propagules in the Kalinga University botanical garden have been undertaken as an important step toward safeguarding threatened species. Similar integrated approaches combining ex situ and in situ conservation have been widely recognized as effective strategies for sustainable medicinal plant management [157]. Furthermore, stricter regulation of anthropogenic activities in ecologically sensitive areas such as Anasuya Forest and Kamadgiri is essential, as protected areas and sacred groves have consistently demonstrated their effectiveness in conserving medicinal plant diversity [158]. Community-based conservation, sustainable harvesting, domestication of economically important medicinal species, and the integration of indigenous knowledge with scientific research are therefore essential for preserving medicinal plant diversity while supporting local livelihoods and strengthening future pharmacological research [11, 59, 159].

Conclusion

The present study provides a comprehensive ethnobotanical assessment of medicinal plant utilization by saint and tribal communities in Chitrakoot, Madhya Pradesh, India, and offers several novel contributions to the field of ethnomedicine. Unlike earlier studies that primarily focused on species documentation, this research integrates quantitative ethnobotanical indices to identify culturally and therapeutically significant plant species, thereby prioritizing taxa with high use value and consensus for further pharmacological investigation.A key finding of this study is the identification of highly preferred medicinal plants with restricted distribution, indicating their vulnerability to overexploitation and habitat disturbance. The study also reveals a critical gap between traditional knowledge of plant use and the lack of cultivation practices, which poses a significant risk to the long-term sustainability of these resources.Furthermore, the research highlights the increasing impact of anthropogenic pressures, including habitat disturbance and unsustainable harvesting, on both plant diversity and the transmission of indigenous knowledge. These findings underscore the urgent need for integrated conservation strategies, such as the establishment of community-based medicinal plant gardens, promotion of sustainable harvesting practices, and documentation of traditional knowledge systems.Importantly, the study contributes to local healthcare systems by validating the continued reliance of indigenous communities on plant-based remedies, particularly in regions with limited access to modern medical facilities. The identification of key medicinal species and their therapeutic applications provides a valuable baseline for future pharmacological and phytochemical research, thereby supporting potential drug discovery initiatives.Overall, this work establishes a framework that links ethnobotanical knowledge, biodiversity conservation, and healthcare applications, offering a model for similar studies in other biodiverse and culturally rich regions. By emphasizing both scientific validation and conservation priorities, the study contributes significantly to the preservation of ethnobotanical heritage and sustainable utilization of medicinal plant resources.
