A critical role of the histone lysine demethylase JmjC1 in regulating severe malaria-associated virulence genes in Plasmodium falciparum
Authors: Ruoyu Tang, Jiayao Pang, Yifei Deng, Zhiwei Jiao, Ang Li, Qilong Li, Changhong Wang, Zimeng Wang, Jingxuan Wang, Yanna Li, Lianda Wang, Yizhuo Zhang, Le Wang, Yong Zhi, Jing Wu, Jia Jia, Xuan Chen, Ning Jiang, Qijun Chen, Yali Liu, Xiaomin Shang, Qingfeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral malaria involves the expression of a particular var subgroup, termed upsA, in Plasmodium falciparum, which encodes a subset of PfEMP1 variants that avidly bind to human receptors on endothelial cells, resulting in obstruction of microvasculature in the brain and severe pathogenesis. Despite the clinical importance of the upsA var genes, the mechanism of their low-frequency activation in asymptomatic or uncomplicated malaria cases remains poorly understood. Here, we identify a JmjC-domain–containing histone demethylase PfJmjC1 as a vital transcriptional silencer of upsA var genes, which may negatively regulate malaria pathogenesis. Genome-wide profiling reveals that PfJmjC1 localizes to heterochromatic loci including the entire var gene family, and interacts with canonical heterochromatin factors such as HP1 at the schizont stage. However, PfJmjC1 preferentially binds to the promoter regions of upsA var loci in ring-stage parasites in which non-upsA vars are activated. Genetic deletion or conditional knockdown of PfJmjC1 selectively derepresses upsA var and disrupts the var expression “counting” mechanism, accompanied by increased H3K4me3 and H3K9ac, as well as decreased H3K9me3 modification at upsA var loci. The altered PfEMP1 expression patterns and acquisition of multiple endothelial receptor–binding phenotypes are experimentally verified at the single-cell level. Additionally, Hi-C-seq and three-dimensional genome modeling show that PfJmjC1 deficiency reorganizes nuclear heterochromatin architecture, repositioning upsA var loci toward a euchromatic environment. Together, these findings not only uncover a mechanism controlling the transcriptional activity of severe malaria-associated virulence genes but also provide a potential therapeutic target against severe malaria.Subject terms: Malaria, Parasite immune evasion

Introduction

Plasmodium falciparum infections cause approximately 610,000 deaths each year1, predominantly in young children in sub-Saharan Africa. In the most severe cases, infections can lead to organ failure and death2. A central factor in immune evasion and the pathogenesis of severe malaria is antigenic variation, which is primarily mediated by the var multigene family3,4. This family encodes the variant surface antigen PfEMP1 (Plasmodium falciparum Erythrocyte Membrane Protein 1), which is expressed on the surface of infected erythrocytes and mediates cytoadherence to host endothelial receptors5. Each parasite genome contains approximately 60 var genes, but only one is actively transcribed at a time, while all others remain transcriptionally silent by default6,7. The mutually exclusive expression of var genes is governed by a complex network of epigenetic regulation and nuclear architectural mechanisms8.

Epigenetic regulation plays a pivotal role in establishing and maintaining var gene expression states. The histone methyltransferase PfSET2 catalyzes H3K36 trimethylation (H3K36me3), which is essential for maintaining var gene silencing9. Silenced var loci are also enriched in H3K9me3, a hallmark of heterochromatin, and bound by heterochromatin protein 1 (PfHP1), thereby reinforcing their transcriptional repression10–12. In contrast, active var genes are associated with enrichment of H3K9ac and H3K4me2/3 in the 5′ flanking region (5′UTR)13. Additionally, histone deacetylases, including PfHda2 and the sirtuins PfSir2a and PfSir2b14–16, further contribute to silencing by removing acetyl groups from histone tails. In contrast, activation of a single var gene is accompanied by euchromatic histone modifications, including histone H4 acetylation and H3K4 methylation catalyzed by the acetyltransferase PfMYST and the methyltransferase PfSET10 17,18, respectively. The histone variants PfH2A.Z and PfH2B.Z, together with H3K4me3 and histone acetylation, are enriched at the promoter of the active var gene, marking it as transcriptionally competent19,20. Due to the fact that the majority of var genes are maintained in a transcriptionally silent state by default, elucidating the mechanisms that preserve this silencing is essential for understanding the fundamental principles underlying antigenic variation.

Based on sequence characteristics of the coding and upstream regulatory regions, var genes are classified into five subtypes (A–E)21. Subtypes A and B are typically located in subtelomeric regions, while subtype C genes are found in internal chromosomal regions. The subtype A subgroup (upsA), rarely activated under in vitro culture conditions22, is strongly associated with severe malaria, e.g., cerebral malaria (CM)23,24, because the encoded PfEMP1 variants mediate the adhesion of infected RBCs to the endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR) and ICAM-1 in the brain25–28. Thus, elucidating how group A var genes are regulated is essential for understanding the pathogenesis of severe malaria. Previously, we uncovered a post-transcriptional regulatory pathway in which the RNA-degrading enzyme PfRNase II, a component of the RNA exosome complex, contributes to the selective repression of upsA var expression through degradation of nascent transcripts in situ29. However, the molecular mechanism that specifically controls the transcriptional activity of upsA var genes remains unclear, although several general epigenetic silencers contribute to silencing of var genes without significant subtype specificity have been identified, such as HP1, Sir2a/b, Hda2, PfSET2, AP2-P, and MORC9,12,14–16,30–32, etc.

Plasmodium JmjC1 (PF3D7_0809900) is a Jumonji C (JmjC) domain-containing protein that functions as a putative histone demethylase in P. falciparum33. As a histone demethylase, PfJmjC1 may act as a potential regulator of heterochromatic genes, particularly the var gene family. PfJmjC1 was initially predicted to demethylate H3K4, H3K9, and H3K3633,34. However, analyses of conserved sequences and structural similarity suggest that PfJmjC1 is most closely related to H3K4-specific demethylases (Fig. 1a)33,35. Furthermore, experimental evidence indicates that PfJmjC1 interacts with PfHP136, implying that it may act as an important heterochromatin-associated factor involved in var gene silencing, but its role in gene expression in P. falciparum remains unclear.

In this study, we demonstrate that the histone lysine demethylase PfJmjC1 functions as a transcriptional repressor involved in virulence gene silencing. Disruption of the PfJmjC1 gene results in derepression of heterochromatic genes, with pronounced activation of the subtype A var genes at both mRNA and protein levels. ChIP-seq analysis reveals that PfJmjC1 preferentially binds to the promoter regions of upsA var loci, and PfJmjC1 gene knockout leads to a global increase in H3K4me3 levels and a reduction in H3K9me3 at heterochromatic loci, including var genes. Hi-C experiments further indicate that PfJmjC1 is essential for maintaining heterochromatin architecture associated with var gene silencing. Notably, loss of PfJmjC1 specifically interferes with the spatial positioning of upsA var loci in the nucleus, highlighting its critical role in regulating the low-frequency activation frequency of this var gene subtype linked to the pathogenesis of severe malaria.

Results

PfJmjC1 is involved in the heterochromatin environment

The PfJmjC1 gene (PF3D7_0809900, PlasmoDB) encodes a 1259-amino-acid protein, with a predicted JmjC catalytic domain characteristic of demethylase activity, located between amino acid residues 660 and 973 (https://www.uniprot.org). To trace the evolutionary relationships of JmjC1 proteins in eukaryotes, including malaria parasite species, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using the full-length sequences of PfJmjC1 protein orthologs. The analysis revealed that PfJmjC1 is closely related to H3K4 demethylases in other eukaryotes (Fig. 1a). To examine the genome-wide occupancy, localization and interacting proteins of PfJmjC1, we attempted to fuse a 3×HA-GFP tag to the C-terminus of PfJmjC1 gene through CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing in the P. falciparum 3D7 strain (Fig. 1b), and correct integration was verified by PCR and Western blot (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1a). To identify the repertoire of PfJmjC1 interactors, we conducted immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (IP–MS) with an anti-HA antibody at the mature-stage of PfJmjC1-HA-GFP parasites, the P. falciparum 3D7 strain was used as a negative control. Relative protein abundance between wild-type 3D7 and PfJmjC1-HA-GFP parasites was assessed using mean-normalized MS spectral counts from three independent experiments. Enriched proteins were defined by a fold change ≥1.5 and a P < 0.05 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data 2). First, PfJmjC1 itself was robustly enriched, confirming the efficiency of the immunoprecipitation. Chromatin remodeling proteins were detected, such as SNF2L (PF3D7_1104200), BDP2 (PF3D7_1212900), ISWI (PF3D7_0624600), PHD1 (PF3D7_1008100), and CHD1 (PF3D7_1023900) (Fig. 1c). In addition, four ApiAP2 transcription factors—AP2-I (PF3D7_1007700), AP2-12 (PF3D7_1239200), AP2-06B (PF3D7_0613800), and SIP2 (PF3D7_0614100) were also identified (Fig. 1c). Importantly, PfHP1 was significantly enriched, further supporting an association between PfJmjC1 and heterochromatin environment. Moreover, among the PfJmjC1-interacting proteins identified, several factors have previously been implicated in heterochromatin formation and maintenance, including MORC, CHD1 (an HP1-interacting protein), SIP2, AP2-12 (PF3D7_1239200), and GCN531,36–40. Notably, multiple proteins identified here, including HP1, ISWI, CHD1, SNF2L, MORC, and SIP2, have also been reported in var gene loci-enriched proteomic analyses41. These findings indicate that PfJmjC1 interacts with heterochromatin. In addition, immunofluorescence assays (IFA) analysis confirmed the extensive colocalization between PfJmjC1 and the heterochromatin marker H3K9me3 at the nuclear periphery, further supporting the involvement of PfJmjC1 in a heterochromatin environment (Fig. 1d).

To determine the genome-wide occupancy of PfJmjC1, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation with the GFP- tagged PfJmjC1 protein followed by DNA sequencing (ChIP-seq). Genome-wide occupancy profiles of PfJmjC1 across all chromosomes at both the ring (R) and schizont (S) stages in P. falciparum, together with previously published PfHP1 datasets39, are shown (Fig. 1e). While PfJmjC1 ChIP signals were predominantly enriched at heterochromatic regions and overlapped extensively with PfHP1-enriched regions, PfJmjC1 occupancy was not constrained to heterochromatin, but also present at euchromatic loci at the ring stage (Fig. 1e). Immunoprecipitation followed by Western blotting showed that PfHP1 was detected in PfJmjC1-IP samples, and conversely, PfJmjC1 was detected in PfHP1-IP (Fig. 1f), indicating a reciprocal interaction between PfJmjC1 and PfHP1. PfJmjC1 ChIP–seq identified 297 peaks targeting 238 genes at the ring stage (16 h post-invasion, hpi) and 1028 peaks targeting 403 genes at the schizont stage (40 hpi), with 69 genes shared between the two stages (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Data 3). Only ~20% of PfJmjC1 target genes overlapped with HP1 at the ring stage, whereas this overlap increased to ~81% at the schizont stage (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 1b). At the schizont stage, PfJmjC1 target genes exhibited a heterochromatin occupancy proportion comparable to that of PfHP1; in contrast, at the ring stage, PfJmjC1 target genes displayed a greater proportion of euchromatin occupancy compared with PfHP1 (Fig. 1h, k). PfJmjC1 binding sites were distributed across promoters (42.1%), 5′ UTRs (5.4%), 3′ UTRs (4.4%), exons (44.0%), and intergenic regions (3.4%) at the ring stage, whereas the corresponding proportions were 36.3%, 2.2%, 2.1%, 45.7%, and 13.1% at the schizont stage, respectively (Fig. 1i, j). To determine the DNA-binding specificity of PfJmjC1 in euchromatin and heterochromatin at different developmental stages, de novo motif discovery was performed. The most significantly enriched motifs bound by PfJmjC1 at 16 hpi were TGACATATTA (P = 1 × 10−10; euchromatin) and AGGGTTAAGGAT (P = 1 × 10−22; heterochromatin), whereas at 40 hpi, the dominant motifs were GACATAACAC (P = 1 × 10−10; euchromatin) and ATAGTTTACAAC (P = 1 × 10−31; heterochromatin) (Fig. 1k). Detailed characterization of PfJmjC1 target genes revealed that, in addition to binding a subset of antigenic variation-associated gene families, such as var, rifin, stevor, and phists. PfJmjC1 also showed extensive occupancy at tRNA and rRNA gene regions at the ring stage (Fig. 1l). Strikingly, at the schizont stage, PfJmjC1 binding was overwhelmingly concentrated in heterochromatin, with more than 96% of heterochromatic target genes bound by PfJmjC1 (Fig. 1k); notably, almost all rifin (135 of 153), var (55 of 61), stevor (29 of 31) and pfmc-2tm (11 of 13) virulence genes were bound by PfJmjC1 (Fig. 1l). These findings suggest a potential role for PfJmjC1 in the regulation of heterochromatic genes, especially antigenic variation-associated genes.

PfJmjC1 acts as a transcriptional repressor for global gene silencing

To investigate the biological role of PfJmjC1, we disrupted the gene by inserting a GFP cassette containing a premature stop codon, thereby causing early termination of translation. After transfection and drug selection, we successfully obtained PfJmjC1-KO lines, and genotyping was confirmed by PCR and sequencing (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1c). PfJmjC1-KO parasites showed no significant difference in parasitemia and cell-cycle progression compared with the WT control, indicating that PfJmjC1 is dispensable for parasite growth and development during the intraerythrocytic developmental cycle (IDC) (Supplementary Figs. 1d and 2a–c). To determine the impact of PfJmjC1 knockout on genome-wide gene expression in P. falciparum, we then performed RNA-seq to examine global transcripts from the ring (10–20 hpi), trophozoite (30–35 hpi), and schizont (40–45 hpi) stages of the asexual cycle. Overall, transcriptomic changes in the PfJmjC1-KO parasites displayed a predominant upregulation trend, with the most pronounced effect observed at the ring stage, where ~856 genes were upregulated (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data 4). In contrast, ~200 genes were upregulated at the trophozoite stage (Supplementary Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 4), and only ~150 genes showed increased expression at the schizont stage (Supplementary Fig. 3b and Supplementary Data 4).

We next examined whether loss of PfJmjC1 altered local chromatin accessibility by assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq). PlotProfile analysis of ATAC-seq signals revealed increased accessibility around transcription start sites in PfJmjC1 knockout parasites compared with the wild-type 3D7 (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Data 5). To evaluate the transcriptional output of these accessible regions, we analyzed RNA-seq expression levels of genes associated with differential ATAC-seq peaks. These open state genes displayed significantly higher expression levels in the knockout strain (Fig. 2d), indicating that loss of PfJmjC1 results in a more transcriptionally permissive state of local chromatin. To investigate the association between PfJmjC1 binding and gene expression, we integrated PfJmjC1 ChIP-seq data (Fig. 1g, l; defining PfJmjC1-bound and unbound genes) with stage-matched RNA-seq data at ring and schizont stages, respectively. We found that PfJmjC1-bound genes, in both euchromatic and heterochromatic regions, exhibited lower expression levels at the ring and schizont stages in 3D7 wild-type parasites compared with PfJmjC1-unbound genes at the corresponding stages (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 3h).

We further compared transcriptional changes between PfJmjC1-bound and PfJmjC1-unbound genes upon PfJmjC1 depletion. At the ring stage, no significant difference in RNA-seq fold change (PfJmjC1-KO/WT) was observed between bound and unbound genes (Fig. 2f, left). In contrast, at the schizont stage, PfJmjC1-bound genes, in both euchromatic and heterochromatic regions, displayed significantly higher expression changes than unbound genes (Fig. 2f, right and Supplementary Fig. 3i). Next, we compared PfJmjC1 ChIP-seq enrichment among genes classified as upregulated, not significantly changed, or downregulated based on RNA-seq analysis at the schizont stage (KO versus WT). PfJmjC1 ChIP-seq signal (log2ChIP/Input) was significantly higher at upregulated genes than at downregulated or non-significantly changed (nosig) genes (Fig. 2g), with this difference being most pronounced for heterochromatic genes rather than euchromatic genes (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g).

To further distinguish potential direct and indirect effects of PfJmjC1 loss, we integrated differential expression analysis with PfJmjC1 occupancy. At the ring stage, 80 upregulated genes overlapped with PfJmjC1-bound genes and were therefore considered putative direct targets, whereas 776 upregulated genes lacked detectable PfJmjC1 occupancy and were considered likely indirect targets (Supplementary Fig. 4a). These putative direct targets included antigenic variation genes, tRNA/rRNA genes, and the sexual commitment regulator ap2-g (Supplementary Fig. 4a, d). At the schizont stage, upregulated genes showed greater overlap with PfJmjC1-bound genes, with 162 classified as putative direct targets and 66 as likely indirect targets (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Together, these data indicate that, under steady-state conditions, PfJmjC1 occupancy is associated with low expression levels of its bound genes at both the ring and schizont stages. Upon loss of PfJmjC1, PfJmjC1-targeted genes at the schizont stage undergo direct derepression, whereas most transcriptional upregulation at the ring stage appears to occur more likely through indirect mechanisms. Alternatively, the relatively lower sensitivity of ChIP-seq analysis at this early stage may omit the potential binding signals at these loci.

At the ring stage, differentially expressed genes in PfJmjC1-KO parasites were mainly enriched in virulence, DNA replication, gametocyte development, and metabolic process pathway according to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 2h). At the trophozoite stage, enriched pathways included virulence and exported protein pathway, while at the schizont stage, the relatively few differentially expressed genes were predominantly associated with virulence and exported protein pathways (Fig. 2h). Further analysis of heterochromatin-associated gene expression profiles upon PfJmjC1 deficiency revealed that transcripts of heterochromatin-associated genes were broadly upregulated across the ring (R), trophozoite (T), and schizont (S) stages (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Notably, members of the rifin, stevor, and PHISTa/b/c families exhibited pronounced upregulation during the T stage, whereas var genes, particularly those belonging to the upsA subgroup, were robustly upregulated at the R stage (Fig. 2i). With the exception of the surfin family, which displayed a decreasing trend, nearly all variant antigen gene families showed transcriptional activation upon PfJmjC1 loss (Fig. 2i). In addition, genes involved in invasion and egress did not show significant transcriptional changes at the schizont stage upon PfJmjC1 deletion (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e), which is consistent with the growth phenotype observed in PfJmjC1-KO line (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that PfJmjC1 functions as a key silencing factor responsible for transcriptional repression of heterochromatic genes, particularly those variant surface antigen gene families.

Loss of PfJmjC1 leads to predominantly expression of upsA var genes

To investigate the impact of PfJmjC1 knockout on the var gene family, we analyzed RNA-seq data from ring-stage parasites (10–20 hpi) (Fig. 2j). Compared with the control line, upsA var genes were markedly upregulated in the PfJmjC1 knockout parasites, with several genes showing particularly pronounced increases in expression, Notably, the upsA var gene, PF3D7_0400400, was upregulated by approximately 55-fold in the PfJmjC1 knockout, PF3D7_0100300 was upregulated by approximately 25-fold, with an FPKM value (~200) reaching the level of a dominant active var gene, such as upsC var PF3D7_0412700 (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 5a). These results are consistent with strand-specific RNA-seq data, which showed that active transcription of var genes coincides with the expression of their corresponding antisense long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) between 10- and 20-h post-invasion (Supplementary Fig. 5a).

To examine PfEMP1-mediated adhesion associated with differential var gene expression, we compared the binding of wild-type 3D7 and PfJmjC1-KO parasites to the endothelial receptors CD36, PECAM-1, EPCR, and ICAM-1 under non-physiological adhesion conditions. We found that, compared with the 3D7 parental line expressing a single dominant var gene (PF3D7_0412700), which bound exclusively to CD36, the PfJmjC1-KO parasites exhibited binding to multiple endothelial receptors, including CD36, PECAM-1, EPCR, and ICAM-1 (Fig. 2k, m, and Supplementary Fig. 6b). Further co-immunofluorescence adhesion assays with CD36 and EPCR revealed that, compared with wild-type 3D7, PfJmjC1-KO parasites simultaneously bound both CD36 and EPCR at the single-cell level (Fig. 2l and Supplementary Fig. 6a), suggesting that the loss of PfJmjC1 may disrupts the mutually exclusive expression pattern of the var gene family.

To further assess clonal reproducibility, we isolated and characterized multiple PfJmjC1-KO subclones, including A7, A8, 10E, 10F, and 10C. We also selected a cleaner 3D7 clone predominantly expressing the upsC var gene PF3D7_0412700 for comparison (Supplementary Fig. 7a). We then examined the var gene expression profiles of these individual clones by qPCR. Notably, nearly all PfJmjC1-KO subclones showed activation of upsA var genes (Supplementary Fig. 7b–f). Four independent PfJmjC1-KO subclones displayed prominent expression of the upsA var gene PF3D7_0400400/PFD0020c (Supplementary Fig. 7b–e). Consistently, in the initial RNA-seq comparison, PF3D7_0400400 showed the highest fold change among all upregulated var genes in PfJmjC1-KO parasites relative to the control (Fig. 2j). Other PfJmjC1-KO subclones expressed additional upsA var genes, such as PF3D7_1300300 (Supplementary Fig. 7f).

Finally, we examined receptor-binding phenotypes of selected 3D7 and PfJmjC1-KO clones (A7, 10F, and 10C) using EPCR, CD36, ICAM-1, and PECAM-1 binding assays (Supplementary Fig. 8a). The purified 3D7 clone expressing PF3D7_0412700 showed strong CD36-binding activity but little or no detectable EPCR, ICAM-1, or PECAM-1 binding (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). In contrast, PfJmjC1-KO clones with activation of upsA var genes showed altered receptor-binding patterns. For example, the A7/10F clone, which expressed the upsA var gene PF3D7_0400400/PFD0020c, showed increased EPCR-binding signals (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). However, another PfJmjC1-KO clone, 10C, which predominantly expressed the upsA var gene PF3D7_1300300, did not show detectable EPCR-binding activity (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). This finding also supports the preferential association of the upsA var gene PF3D7_0400400 with EPCR binding. Although all PfJmjC1-KO subclones showed detectable CD36 binding, they differed in binding to other endothelial receptors. A7 and 10F showed ICAM-1 binding but lacked detectable PECAM-1 binding, whereas 10C showed PECAM-1 binding but little or no ICAM-1 binding (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). In addition, PfJmjC1-KO and it’s subclones expressing var2csa showed detectable CSA-binding activity (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Together, these results indicate that PfJmjC1 loss promotes activation of upsA var genes, with some upsA-expressing iRBCs preferentially binding EPCR under non-physiological in vitro adhesion conditions.

PfJmjC1 gene knockout leads to a global increase in H3K4me3 levels and a decrease in H3K9me3 at heterochromatic genes

PfJmjC1 functions as a putative H3K4 demethylase in P. falciparum and is therefore likely to regulate gene transcription by modulating chromatin modifications. To further explore the chromatin landscape governed by PfJmjC1 during the ring stage, we performed ChIP-seq analysis to examine genome-wide changes in histone modification patterns associated with its deficiency. Genome-wide profiles of histone modification changes upon PfJmjC1 knockout are presented in Fig. 3a. Analysis revealed that PfJmjC1-KO parasites exhibited significantly elevated H3K4me3 enrichment at regions both upstream and downstream of gene loci (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 10a). Integration of PfJmjC1 occupancy with H3K4me3 modification alteration revealed a strong positional overlap between PfJmjC1-occupancy regions and sites of H3K4me3 gain upon PfJmjC1 loss (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b), suggesting that PfJmjC1 acting as a putative H3K4me3 eraser. H3K9ac showed a similar pattern and was highly correlated with H3K4me3 (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 10a, d). Both euchromatic and heterochromatic genes exhibited elevated H3K4me3 and H3K9ac levels upon PfJmjC1 loss (Fig. 3c). In contrast, H3K9me3 was specifically decreased over heterochromatic genes in the knockout strain, while remaining low over euchromatic regions (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 10c). Zoomed-in track views of specific loci, including subtelomeric regions on chromosome 4 harboring varA, varB, and rif genes, the internal varC cluster on chromosome 7, and the pfap2-g locus on chromosome 12, revealed similar patterns of histone modification changes (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, putative direct PfJmjC1 targets showed coordinated chromatin remodeling after PfJmjC1 loss, with increased H3K4me3 and H3K9ac but reduced H3K9me3 enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 4c). These results suggest that loss of PfJmjC1 establishes a more open chromatin environment conducive to transcriptional activation.

Next, integrated analysis of RNA-seq and ChIP-seq data revealed that in PfJmjC1-KO parasites, heterochromatic genes showing decreased H3K9me3 exhibited pronounced H3K4me3 upregulation, with ~50% of these genes showing increased transcription (Supplementary Fig. 10e, f). Western blot analysis of global histone modifications revealed that loss of PfJmjC1 resulted in a pronounced remodeling of chromatin states at both the R and S stages. Compared with wild-type parasites, PfJmjC1-KO parasites displayed a clear increase in the active histone marks H3K4me3 and H3K9ac, together with a reduction in the repressive mark H3K9me3 (Fig. 3d, e). In contrast, the levels of H3K36me3, H4K8ac, and total H4 acetylation (H4Kac) showed no significant differences between knockout and control parasites (Fig. 3d, f). These global alterations are consistent with the ChIP-seq results, which further support a specific role of PfJmjC1 in regulating the balance between active and repressive histone modifications. Mechanistically, these findings suggest that loss of PfJmjC1 not only promotes global chromatin accessibility but also compromises heterochromatin integrity, thereby driving widespread transcriptional activation.

Preferential occupancy of PfJmjC1 to upsA var genes promoters contributes to their deeply transcriptional silencing

Our earlier analyses revealed that PfJmjC1 extensively binds heterochromatic genes, including var genes (Fig. 1). Further stratification by var gene families showed that, at the ring stage, PfJmjC1 displayed a preferential occupancy at upsA var genes promoters. In contrast, PfJmjC1 binding was high across all var families at the schizont stage, possibly reflecting global silencing of var gene expression at this stage (Fig. 4a–c). These findings suggest that, similar to PfHP1, PfJmjC1 occupies most var gene regions at the schizont stage, consistent with global var gene silencing. Interestingly, unlike PfHP1, PfJmjC1 shows preferential occupancy at upsA var genes promoters at the ring stage, maintaining their silenced state while conferring upsB or upsC var genes a more accessible chromatin state for transcriptional activation (Fig. 4d).

To further explore the correlation between histone modifications and var gene expression in the PfJmjC1 knockout line, we performed an integrated analysis of RNA-seq and ChIP-seq data. The results revealed that, upon PfJmjC1 loss, H3K4me3 enrichment at var loci positively correlated with their transcriptional fold changes, whereas H3K9me3 enrichment showed a negative correlation (Fig. 4e). Specifically, PfJmjC1 knockout led to a pronounced increase in H3K4me3 around the promoters of upsA and upsC var genes, but only upsA var genes exhibited striking reductions in H3K9me3 across the entire gene body (Fig. 4f, g, Supplementary Fig. 11a, b, and Supplementary Data 3). Therefore, we integrated RNA-seq and ChIP-seq data together with var subtype classification to generate a four-quadrant plot (Fig. 4h). In this plot, the size of each dot represents the fold change of gene expression between PfJmjC1 knockout and control (RNA-seq), while the color indicates the ups family subgroup (red, upsA; blue, upsB; and green, upsC). The X-axis shows the changes in H3K4me3 enrichment, and the Y-axis shows the changes in H3K9me3 enrichment upon PfJmjC1 knockout. As shown in Fig. 4h, upsA var genes, which displayed the most pronounced transcriptional upregulation upon PfJmjC1 knockout, also exhibited the strongest increases in H3K4me3 enrichment and the most significant reductions in H3K9me3 enrichment. These findings indicate that deficiency of PfJmjC1 promotes the accumulation of the active mark H3K4me3 while disrupting the heterochromatic environment and reducing H3K9me3, which together contribute to the upregulation of var gene expression.

Conditional knockdown of PfJmjC1 also activates upsA var genes

To more precisely illustrate the dynamic changes in var gene expression following manipulation of PfJmjC1 gene expression, we therefore engineered a ligand-regulated FK506-binding protein (FKBP) destabilization domain (DD) fusion to achieve conditional knockdown of PfJmjC1 (Fig. 5a). The DD fusion protein is stabilized by the synthetic ligand Shield-1 (Shld1) and is rapidly degraded upon its withdrawal. Genotyping confirmed successful gene editing, and Western blot analysis showed that the expression of PfJmjC1 was significantly reduced within the first two intraerythrocytic cycles following Shld1 removal (Fig. 5a). Notably, PfJmjC1-off parasites exhibited no significant changes in parasitemia compared with PfJmjC1-on parasites (Supplementary Fig. 14e), consistent with the growth phenotype of the PfJmjC1 knockout (Supplementary Fig. 1c), further confirming that PfJmjC1 gene is not essential for parasite survival during IDC. We next examined the effects of conditional PfJmjC1 knockdown on global histone modification patterns at both the ring (10–20 hpi) and schizont (35–45 hpi) stages. Increased levels of H3K4me3 and H3K9ac were observed under PfJmjC1-off conditions at both stages, whereas H3K9me3 levels were reduced significantly at the ring stage (Fig. 5b, c). These histone modification changes were consistent with those observed in the PfJmjC1 knockout parasites (Fig. 3).

Next, we performed a time-course transcriptome analysis (RNA-seq) on synchronized ring-stage parasites under different PfJmjC1 knockdown conditions. For convenience, cultures at 4 days, 6 days, and 1 month after Shld1 removal are denoted as off 4, off 6, and off 30, respectively. To assess whether the change of gene expression is reversible, we employed the destabilization domain system to restore PfJmjC1 protein levels by reintroducing Shld1 to the 1-month Shld1-removed parasite culture (denoted as off 30-on). The time-course experiment revealed that the number of upregulated genes peaked at 774 in off 6, which largely overlapped with upregulated genes in off 30 (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b, g). Notably, the upregulated genes observed in off 6 also displayed substantial overlap with genes upregulated in PfJmjC1-KO parasites (Supplementary Fig. 14g, h). Importantly, rescue of PfJmjC1 expression in off 30-on restored gene silencing across all targets, demonstrating the reversibility of PfJmjC1-mediated epigenetic regulation (Supplementary Fig. 14c, d, f).

To validate the observed transcriptional changes in severe malaria-associated upsA var genes, we examined their expression in PfJmjC1-DD29 transgenic parasites in on, off 6, off 30, and off 30-on. A robust overall increase in upsA var transcript levels was observed upon PfJmjC1 knockdown (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 7a), which progressively increased with the duration of Shld1 removal (from on to off 6 and off 30). In contrast, most upsB and upsC var genes showed no significant changes (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 15a). Notably, the upsA var gene (PF3D7_0100300) was upregulated by approximately 3.7-fold in off 6 and 5-fold in off 30, with an FPKM value around 40 in off 6 and 60 in off 30, reaching levels to a dominant active var gene except var2csa (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 15a). Rescue of PfJmjC1 knockdown in the off 30-on condition restored silencing across the upsA family, particularly returning PF3D7_0100300 gene expression to its baseline levels and confirming the reversibility of PfJmjC1-dependent epigenetic control (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 15a). By examining the normalized expression dynamics of the upsA, upsB, and upsC gene families, we observed that upsB and upsC showed no significant changes, whereas varA exhibited a progressive increase in expression with prolonged PfJmjC1 knockdown (Fig. 5e, f, and Supplementary Fig. 15b–e). These data further demonstrate that PfJmjC1 knockdown leads to increased expression of the upsA var group through the inducible system in parallel experiments. Notably, upsA-subgroup var genes are rarely activated in cultured parasites.

To examine var gene-encoded PfEMP1 expression, we performed immunofluorescence assays detecting upsA (PF3D7_0100300) PfEMP1 under PfJmjC1-on or off. Under PfJmjC1-on conditions, upsA PfEMP1 signal was rarely detected, whereas PfJmjC1 depletion was associated with an increased proportion of upsA-positive parasites. Re-addition of Shld1 reduced upsA PfEMP1 detection to levels comparable to PfJmjC1-on conditions (Fig. 5g, i). We further assessed co-expression of different PfEMP1 variants by co-immunofluorescence analysis. While PfJmjC1-on parasites showed only upsC (PF3D7_0412700) PfEMP1 signal, PfJmjC1-off parasites displayed detectable expression of both upsA and upsC PfEMP1 within individual infected erythrocytes. Similarly, rescue of PfJmjC1 levels restored the silencing of upsA PfEMP1 and maintained the expression of a upsC PfEMP1 variant (Fig. 5h, i). Therefore, under normal conditions, PfJmjC1 may constrain switching of upsA var genes specifically, playing a crucial role in maintaining the deep silencing of this subgroup.

PfJmjC1 is essential for sustaining heterochromatin architecture associated with upsA var gene silencing

To investigate the relationship between epigenetic modifications and chromatin architecture, we performed Hi-C experiments on tightly synchronized ring-stage parasites. Hi-C libraries were prepared from three biological replicates for each sample (WT and PfJmjC1-KO) and sequenced to an average depth of 30 G reads per replicate. Following alignment, pairing, mapping, and quality filtering, we obtained approximately 30 million valid interaction pairs per sample, which were binned at 10 kb resolution. Given the strong correlation between biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. 16d), we merged replicates for each WT/PfJmjC1-KO to increase data resolution for downstream analysis (Supplementary Fig. 16a, b, and Supplementary Data 6). To account for differences in sequencing depth and total valid interaction pairs, random sampling was performed on the WT data. Differential interaction analysis (q < 0.05) revealed that interactions between telomeres and var gene-containing regions across all chromosomes, including internal var genes, were significantly reduced following PfJmjC1 knockout for both intrachromosomal and interchromosomal interactions (Fig. 6a). These decreased interactions primarily occurred in regions exhibiting H3K9me3 loss, indicating that PfJmjC1 knockout disrupts heterochromatic regions. Analysis of intrachromosomal interactions on chromosomes 7 and 12 further revealed that (Fig. 6b), within heterochromatic regions, interactions among telomeres themselves and between telomeres and internal var genes were reduced following PfJmjC1 knockout. AB compartment analysis showed that many loci shifted to the open (A) compartment in PfJmjC1-KO parasites, whereas these loci resided in the closed (B) compartment in the control parasites (Fig. 6b). This indicates chromatin restructuring, in which telomeres are repositioned away from each other and further dispersed into a more relaxed heterochromatic structure.

To gain a more comprehensive view of chromatin architecture and identify structural changes associated with PfJmjC1 deletion, we generated 3D genome models using Pastis (Fig. 6c). Our goal was to identify regions showing clear structural alterations indicative of chromatin remodeling. Compared with wild-type parasites, PfJmjC1-KO parasites exhibited increased distances between telomeres, as well as between var genes, whereas distances between centromeres remained largely unchanged, suggesting a more relaxed heterochromatic structure (Fig. 6c, e, and Supplementary Fig. 16c). Notably, the distances between centromeres and telomeres decreased, and var genes were repositioned away from telomeres toward centromeres, indicating that var genes are moving out of heterochromatic regions toward more transcriptionally active regions. Further analysis of upsA, upsB, and upsC var subgroups revealed that, following PfJmjC1 knockout, upsA var genes showed greater inter-gene distances compared with upsB and upsC, and were positioned further from telomeres (Fig. 6d, f). These observations suggest that upsA var genes adopt a more relaxed heterochromatic structure and are more prone to escape silencing. Taken together, these findings indicate that PfJmjC1 functions as a key silencing factor, maintaining chromosomal conformation and playing a critical role in preserving heterochromatin structure.

Collectively, these data support a model in which PfJmjC1 contributes to the maintenance of a compact perinuclear heterochromatin compartment that constrains telomeres and var loci into a repressive cluster (Fig. 6g). In wild-type parasites, this organization is associated with stable silencing of most var genes, including the deep repression of the upsA subgroup, while allowing a single upsB or upsC locus to access a more permissive chromatin environment for transcription (Fig. 6g, left). Upon loss of PfJmjC1, heterochromatin architecture becomes relaxed, accompanied with dispersed telomeric regions and var loci, and chromatin marks shift toward an active state (Fig. 6g, right). This structural and epigenetic reconfiguration is accompanied by preferential derepression of upsA var genes, increased chromatin accessibility, and elevated transcriptional output, indicating that PfJmjC1 is a determinant linking higher-order genome organization with the selective silencing of virulence gene families in P. falciparum.

Discussion

In this study, we identify PfJmjC1 as a key chromatin-associated factor required for the maintenance of heterochromatin in P. falciparum. Multiple lines of evidence support this conclusion. PfJmjC1 localizes predominantly to the nuclear periphery, exhibits extensive overlap with the heterochromatic mark H3K9me3, and physically associates with a set of chromatin remodelers and heterochromatin regulators. Among these are MORC, CHD1, an HP1-interacting chromatin remodeler, SIP2, the ApiAP2 transcription factor AP2-12 (PF3D7_1239200), and the histone acetyltransferase GCN531,36–40. Notably, several components identified in our PfJmjC1 interactome—including HP1, ISWI, CHD1, SNF2L, MORC, and SIP2—were also recovered in independent proteomic studies of var gene-enriched heterochromatin41, reinforcing the idea that PfJmjC1 operates within a conserved repressive chromatin network that governs antigenic variation. Consistent with a recent findings from a preprint by Jonas Gockel and colleagues42, which also highlighted a critical role of PfJmjC1 in heterochromatin, our genome-wide analyses provide a deeper mechanistic perspective. We show that PfJmjC1 is preferentially enriched at heterochromatic regions, particularly at the schizont stage, and that PfJmjC1-bound genes are maintained at very low expression levels under steady-state conditions. Upon PfJmjC1 depletion, this repressive landscape becomes destabilized: H3K9me3 levels are reduced, active chromatin marks, such as H3K4me3 and H3K9ac, accumulate, chromatin accessibility increases, and a broad set of normally silent loci becomes transcriptionally activated. Many of these genes belong to clonally variant multigene families associated with virulence, including var, rifin, stevor, and pfmc-2tm, indicating that PfJmjC1 contributes directly to the epigenetic silencing of major antigenic and host-interaction determinants. Interestingly, despite this extensive derepression, PfJmjC1 is not essential for parasite survival during asexual blood stages. This likely reflects its primary function as a transcriptional repressor rather than an activator of essential housekeeping genes. Loss of PfJmjC1 therefore relaxes heterochromatin without immediately compromising core cellular processes. By contrast, our findings raise the possibility that excessive PfJmjC1 activity could also be detrimental, as hyper-stabilization of heterochromatin might interfere with the expression of genes required for normal growth and developmental plasticity. Collectively, these observations indicate that PfJmjC1 is not merely a passive chromatin binder but an active organizer of heterochromatin architecture, reinforcing epigenetic identity and safeguarding transcriptional silencing programs during the asexual cycle of P. falciparum.

Previous work has shown that both chromosomal position and var gene subtypes are strongly associated with switching behavior22. Var genes located in central chromosomal regions (predominantly upsC) display relatively stable expression, whereas subtelomeric var genes (upsA and upsB) switch more readily to alternative var loci and are therefore maintained in a more deeply silenced state. As most upsA var genes reside within subtelomeric heterochromatin, these findings imply that the upsA subgroup is intrinsically biased toward strong and stable repression and is particularly difficult to activate under in vitro culture conditions. Additional evidence further supports this notion. In a DC-J-off BSD var switching model, disruption of mutually exclusive var gene expression revealed that subtelomeric upsA loci not only exhibit very low basal transcription but are also the most refractory to activation among all var subtypes6,22. Moreover, treatment with the actin polymerization inhibitor jasplakinolide (JAS), which perturbs nuclear architecture and var gene regulation, resulted in derepression of multiple var genes; nevertheless, upsA genes remained among the least frequently activated and exhibited the lowest switching rates43. Together, these findings indicate that upsA var genes are subject to an unusually robust and deeply embedded silencing mechanism, consistent with their stable heterochromatic localization and their resistance to activation. However, clinical studies have consistently shown that upsA-type PfEMP1 variants are upregulated in patients with severe malaria23,24,29,44–47. This subset of PfEMP1 proteins preferentially mediates adhesion to endothelial protein C receptor (EPCR) and ICAM-1, interactions that are central to cerebral sequestration and the pathogenesis of severe malaria25–28. Despite their clear clinical importance, the subtype-specific regulatory mechanisms governing upsA var genes activation and silencing remain poorly understood. Although we previously identified a post-transcriptional regulator, PfRNase II, that selectively modulates upsA var transcripts29, transcriptional and epigenetic factors that specifically enforce the deep silencing or controlled activation of upsA var genes have not been described to date. Our results show that PfJmjC1 binds preferentially to upsA var promoters at the ring stage and that loss of PfJmjC1 leads to robust and reversible activation of upsA transcription, accompanied by loss of H3K9me3, gain of H3K4me3, increased chromatin accessibility, and surface expression of upsA-type PfEMP1. In contrast, upsB and upsC loci are considerably less sensitive to PfJmjC1 depletion. These findings suggest that PfJmjC1 functions as a subtype-specific epigenetic lock that maintains the unusually stable silencing of upsA var genes, thereby restricting the expression of highly virulent adhesion phenotypes under standard culture conditions. Although our immunofluorescence assays revealed that individual PfJmjC1-deficient parasites can display signals consistent with the presence of more than one PfEMP1 variant, suggesting a disruption of var gene “counting” mechanism, recent single-cell RNA-seq analyses48 provide a powerful framework for directly assessing var transcriptional profiles at single-parasite resolution. Applying such approaches to PfJmjC1-deficient parasites in future studies would enable a quantitative evaluation of whether loss of PfJmjC1 increases the fraction of cells expressing transcripts from multiple var loci. In addition, while the preferential PfJmjC1 occupancy at upsA var promoters supports a putative direct role in upsA var regulation, we cannot exclude indirect contributions from other regulators after PfJmjC1 loss.

Another important issue is the behavior of var2csa in PfJmjC1-deficient parasites. var2csa encodes a conserved PfEMP1 variant that binds to chondroitin sulfate A (CSA) on placental syncytiotrophoblasts and mediates pregnancy-associated malaria49. In addition, recent studies suggest another role of var2csa in var gene regulation, acting as a switching hub or sink node that helps coordinate transcriptional switching among var genes50–52. In our PfJmjC1-KO parasites and derived subclones, var2csa expression was repeatedly detected. This observation may be interpreted in light of the recently proposed dual regulatory states of var2csa52. In one state, var2csa is actively transcribed and translated, resulting in canonical VAR2CSA PfEMP1 production and CSA-binding activity. In another state, var2csa undergoes transient transcription without productive translation, which may facilitate var switching without generating a stable VAR2CSA surface phenotype. Our data likely support the former state, because PfJmjC1-KO parasites showed functional VAR2CSA expression. In addition, recent work suggests that switching-associated transient var2csa transcription occurs mainly during the very early ring stage, around 4–7 hpi, whereas our var transcript analyses detected var2csa expression at approximately 15 hpi. This later persistence of var2csa transcripts, together with CSA-binding activity, supports the interpretation that PfJmjC1 loss promotes more stable var2csa activation rather than only transient, non-productive transcription. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that transient non-translated var2csa transcription and stable translated var2csa expression coexist within part of the PfJmjC1-KO population. The relationship between PfJmjC1 and var2csa regulation remains to be elucidated in future work.

Previous studies in P. falciparum have established a close relationship between three-dimensional genome organization and transcriptional regulation during both asexual development and transmission-stage progression53–55. This spatial organization is particularly important for virulence-associated gene families, such as var genes, whose interactions, nuclear positioning, and transcriptional activity are tightly linked to heterochromatin architecture. For example, depletion of PfHP1 disrupts interactions among virulence genes and weakens the repressive heterochromatin compartment55. Similarly, activation of var2csa has been associated with release from H3K9me3-mediated heterochromatic silencing and three-dimensional repositioning away from telomeric repressive clusters56. Other chromatin regulators have also been implicated in maintaining this nuclear architecture. PfMORC associates with subtelomeric regions and antigenic-variation genes and is required for heterochromatin organization and transcriptional repression31. More recently, high-resolution Micro-C analysis revealed subtelomeric fold structures and long-range interactions that connect heterochromatinized antigenic-variation genes with active stage-specific genes through MORC/ApiAP2-associated mechanisms32. In addition, PfSET2 knockout leads to declining H3K36me3 levels and interactions among heterochromatin interaction regions, thereby activating most var genes57. In our study, PfJmjC1 also influences higher-order genome organization. Upon PfJmjC1 loss, this structure becomes visibly relaxed, with telomeres dispersing, telomere–telomere and telomere–var interaction decreasing, and var genes repositioning away from the repressive compartment toward more euchromatic nuclear regions. However, unlike more global regulators, such as PfSET2, PfHP1, and PfMORC, PfJmjC1 exhibits a more selective effect, with a pronounced impact on the upsA var subgroup. Consistent with this specificity, upsA loci show a stronger spatial separation than upsB or upsC var genes following PfJmjC1 loss, paralleling their preferential transcriptional derepression.

Together, these findings not only reveal a previously unrecognized transcriptional and epigenetic mechanism contributing to the regulation of severe malaria-associated virulence genes, but also identify PfJmjC1 as a potential therapeutic target for modulating pathogenic PfEMP1 expression and ultimately reducing malaria morbidity and mortality.

Methods

Plasmid construction

To construct the PfJmjC1 knockout transgenic line, homologous sequence fragments flanking the PfJmjC1 gene together with an eGFP epitope tag were inserted between the Asc I and Afl II restriction sites of the pL6cs-hDHFR plasmid. A guide RNA sequence specific to PfJmjC1 was cloned into the same plasmid between the Pst I and Xho I sites. To generate the PfJmjC1-HA-DD29 and PfJmjC1-I-HA-GFP transgenic lines, the C-terminus of the target gene was flanked by sequences containing 3×HA, GFP, or DD29 tags, which were inserted into the Asc I and Afl II sites of the pL6cs-hDHFR plasmid. Corresponding guide RNA sequences specific to the PfJmjC1 gene were also cloned into the same plasmid. Sequences of the guide RNA oligonucleotides and primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplementary Data 1. All pL6cs plasmids were co-transfected with the pUF1-Cas9 plasmid.

Parasite culture and transfection

Parasites were cultured as described58. The parasites were maintained in human erythrocytes using culture medium containing RPMI-1640 (10.44 g/L), 25 mM HEPES, 10% (v/v) Albumax I, 0.1 mM hypoxanthine, and 20 μg/mL gentamicin, at 37 °C in a controlled atmosphere of 5% O2 and 5% CO2. Parasite synchronization at the ring stage was performed with 5% sorbitol, whereas late trophozoite and schizont stages were enriched by 40% and 70% Percoll gradients, respectively. For transfection, fresh erythrocytes were electroporated with 100 μg of plasmid DNA pL6cs-sgRNA and pUF1-Cas9 in cytomix buffer, following the established protocol. Drug pressure was maintained under 2.5 nM WR99210 and 2 μg/mL blasticidin, according to the resistance marker of each construct. The conditional knockdown line PfJmjC1-HA-DD29 was maintained in culture in vitro in the presence of 0.5 μM Shld-1 (MCE, HY-112210).

Limiting dilution cloning of transgenic parasite line

Transgenic parasite lines were cloned by limiting dilution. Briefly, synchronized trophozoite-stage parasites were serially diluted to obtain suspensions containing approximately 10 to 0.1 infected erythrocytes per mL. The diluted cultures were seeded into 96-well plates at 200 μL per well in the presence of fresh erythrocytes and maintained under standard culture conditions. Medium was replaced twice weekly, and fresh erythrocytes were supplemented weekly. After approximately 2 weeks, parasite-positive wells were identified by microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained blood smears and expanded for subsequent analyses.

Growth curve assays

PfJmjC1-knockdown parasites were synchronized to a 5 h developmental window and seeded at 0.1% parasitemia in 6-well plates containing 2% hematocrit. Cultures were maintained in the presence or absence of 0.5 μM Shld-1. Similarly, JmjC1-knockout and wild-type 3D7 parasites were synchronized to a 5 h window and plated under the same conditions (0.1% parasitemia, 2% hematocrit). Parasitemia was assessed over the following three replication cycles by enumerating parasites on Giemsa-stained thin blood smears.

Western blot analysis

RBC-free parasites were isolated with 0.15% saponin treatment and resuspended in 1× SDS loading buffer following heating under 100 °C for 7 min. Proteins were then separated by gel electrophoresis and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% milk and incubated with antibodies, which could generate a chemiluminescent signal. The proteins were visualized by exposure to an imaging device. The antibodies used in this study were rabbit anti-HA (Proteintech, 51064-2-AP) and mouse anti-GFP (Proteintech, 66002-1-Ig) antibodies.

For immunoprecipitation, PfJmjC1-HA-GFP and 3D7-G7 parasite cultures were harvested by centrifugation, and the resulting iRBCs and RBCs were treated with 0.15% saponin in PBS to remove hemoglobin. The parasite pellets were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with PMSF (Servicebio, G2008-1ML). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were then incubated with anti-HA magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0201) or with anti-PfHP1 antibody conjugated to Protein A/G magnetic beads, respectively. After incubation, the beads were washed five times with PBS. Bound proteins were eluted with 1× SDS loading buffer and analyzed by Western blotting using anti-HA (rabbit,1:10,000) and anti-HP1 (rabbit, 1:10,000) antibodies.

Mass spectrometry and data analysis

Mature-stage infected erythrocytes were harvested and lysed with 0.15% saponin in PBS to release parasites. After three washes with PBS, the parasite pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 0.05% NP-40, and protease inhibitor cocktail) and incubated on ice for 30 min. The lysates were subjected to probe sonication for 3 min (3 s on, 5 s off, 220 W, 30% amplitude). After centrifugation, the supernatants were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-HA magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0201). The beads were collected and washed with wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Tween-20). Proteins bound to the beads were digested with trypsin to generate peptides. The resulting peptides were reconstituted in solvent A and loaded onto a custom-packed reversed-phase analytical column (25 cm length, 100 μm inner diameter). Peptide separation was performed on an EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using solvent A (0.1% formic acid in 2% acetonitrile) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 90% acetonitrile). The gradient was: 6–22% solvent B over 0–22.5 min, 22–34% over 22.5–26.5 min, 34–80% over 26.5–28.5 min, followed by a 1.5 min hold at 80% solvent B, at a constant flow rate of 700 nL min−1. Eluted peptides were analyzed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer equipped with a nano-electrospray ion source. The spray voltage was set to 2.3 kV and the FAIMS compensation voltage to −45 V. Both precursor and fragment ions were detected in the Orbitrap. Full MS scans were acquired over an m/z range of 450–850 at a resolution of 30,000, and MS/MS spectra were collected at a resolution of 30,000 using higher-energy collisional dissociation with a normalized collision energy of 28%. The automatic gain control target was set to 3 × 106 with the maximum injection time set to automatic. The DIA mass spectrometry data were processed using DIA-NN (v1.8). Tandem mass spectra were searched against the Plasmodium_falciparum3D7_5833_PlasmoDB-68_20250115_seqkit. fasta database (5375 entries) concatenated with a reverse decoy database for false discovery rate estimation. Trypsin/P was specified as the proteolytic enzyme, allowing up to one missed cleavage. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, and N-terminal methionine excision was enabled. Peptide and protein identifications were filtered at a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 1% using the target–decoy strategy implemented in DIA-NN. For normalized MS/MS counts, statistical significance was assessed using two-sided unpaired Student’s t tests. Proteins were ranked based on log2 fold change (x-axis) and −log10 (P value) (y-axis) in volcano plot analyses. Three independent biological replicates were analyzed for each parasite line. Proteins were considered significantly enriched with a fold change ≥1.5 and P < 0.05. Data visualization and volcano plots were generated in R using ggplot2 v4.0.2. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium (https://proteomecentral.proteomexchange.org) via the iProX partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD08034759,60.

Immunofluorescence assays (IFA)

IFA were performed as described previously29. Briefly, RBC-free parasites were fixed in suspension with 4% paraformaldehyde and deposited onto glass slides. After fixation, samples were washed three to five times with 1× PBS and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 25 min at room temperature. Following additional PBS washes, slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C in blocking buffer consisting of 2 mg/mL BSA in PBS supplemented with 0.05% Tween-20. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer as follows: mouse anti-HA (1:400), rabbit anti-H3K9me3 (1:400). After washing, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Thermo, A32723); Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rat IgG (Thermo, A11006), each at a dilution of 1:500.

For upsA- and upsC-PfEMP1 IFA experiments, antisera against the upsA-PfEMP1 variants PF3D7_0100300 and PF3D7_0937600 were kindly provided by the QiJun Chen laboratory. The antisera were generated as previously described61, in which female Wistar rats were immunized with recombinant proteins corresponding to the N-terminal regions (~110 amino acids) of the two upsA-PfEMP1 variants. Antisera against the upsC-PfEMP1 variant PF3D7_0412700 were generated similarly. A peptide corresponding to amino acids 1464–1494 “KDNKITAEKIGNGVDSTVLDMRVIDDS ATGF” within the CIDR1 domain of PF3D7_0412700, based on the PlasmoDB annotation, was selected as the antigen. Female New Zealand rabbits were immunized with the synthesized peptide. After parasite fixation and blocking as described above, rat antisera against upsA-PfEMP1 variants (1:500) and rabbit antisera against upsC-PfEMP1 variants (1:500) diluted in blocking buffer were incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rat IgG (Thermo, A11006) and Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo, A11036), each at a dilution of 1:500. Slides were then washed and mounted in Vectashield Antifade Mounting Medium containing DAPI (Invitrogen). Fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM-900 microscope and processed with ImageJ.

Assessing binding phenotypes of ICAM1, EPCR, CD31, and CD36

iRBCs were washed with PBS and fixed as thin smears on glass slides using methanol. After fixation, slides were washed with PBS, blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA, B2064) for 1 h at 37 °C, and washed three times. The samples were then incubated with 8 nM recombinant His-tagged ICAM-1 (Novoprotein, CS74), His-tagged EPCR (Novoprotein, C340), His-tagged CD31 (PECAM-1; Novoprotein, C322), or His-tagged CD36 (MCE, HY-P78409) diluted in 3% BSA for 1 h at 37 °C. Slides were washed three times with PBS and incubated with a mouse anti-His antibody (Proteintech, 66005-1-Ig; 1:10,000 dilution), followed by three additional PBS washes and incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:1500) for 1 h at room temperature. For CD36 and EPCR co-staining, iRBC smears were incubated with 8 nM recombinant non-His-tagged CD36 (Novoprotein, CP94) together with His-tagged EPCR (Novoprotein, C340) as described above. After three washes with PBS, slides were incubated with mouse anti-His antibody (Proteintech, 66005-1-Ig; 1:400 dilution) and rabbit anti-CD36 antibody (Immunoway, PT0434R; 1:400 dilution). Following three additional PBS washes, slides were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo, A11034) and Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo, A11031), each diluted 1:1500, for 1 h at room temperature. After three final washes with PBS, nuclei were counterstained, and slides were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant containing DAPI (Invitrogen). Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica fluorescence microscope.

Chondroitin sulfate A (CSA) binding assay

CSA-binding assays were performed as previously described with minor modifications62. Briefly, 35 mm Falcon Petri dishes (Falcon 1008) were coated with CSA (10 μg/mL in PBS, Sigma C-8529) overnight at 4 °C and blocked with 20 mg/mL BSA in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Percoll-enriched late-stage infected erythrocytes were added to the coated dishes and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C under standard culture conditions. After washing three times to remove unbound cells, adherent cells were fixed with methanol, stained with Giemsa, and quantified by light microscopy. CSA-binding activity was determined by counting the number of adherent infected erythrocytes (IEs) per mm² under a 40× objective lens. Three independent experiments were performed.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry was used to determine the intraerythrocytic developmental stages of Plasmodium falciparum. Synchronized parasite cultures were collected and stained with Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, C1027, 1:100 dilution) at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. Cells were then washed with PBS and analyzed using a BD FACSymphony™ A5 SE flow cytometer. Initial gates were established using unstained, uninfected erythrocytes as negative controls. Single cells were selected based on SSC-A/FSC-A and FSC-A/FSC-H gating to exclude doublets and aggregates. Only erythrocytes containing a single intracellular parasite were included in subsequent analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.163.

Phylogenetic analysis of PfJmjC1 and homologous sequences

The full-length sequences of PfJmjC1 (PF3D7_0809900) and its homologs from P. falciparum, P. reichenowi, P. gaboni, P. ovale, P. knowlesi, P. vivax, P. cynomolgi, P. gallinaceum, P. relictum, Babesia caballi, Cardiosporidium cionae, Besnoitia besnoiti, Cryptosporidium suis, Toxoplasma gondii, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Drosophila melanogaster, Caenorhabditis elegans, Mus musculus, and Homo sapiens were retrieved from PlasmoDB and NCBI, and multiple sequence alignments were generated using MEGA X64.

RNA isolation, reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), RNA-seq, and data analysis

Total RNA was isolated from synchronized parasites using QIAzol reagent, followed by purification with the Direct-zol RNA MiniPrep Kit (ZYMO) including on-column DNase digestion (MN). RNA integrity was assessed by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels. For qRT-PCR analysis, RNA was isolated as described above. Reverse transcription was performed using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme) with random primer mixes. cDNA levels were quantified by quantitative PCR using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Established var primer sets were used to measure var gene transcription49. For each primer pair, ΔCt was calculated by subtracting the Ct value of the target var gene from the Ct value of the reference gene seryl-tRNA synthetase (PF3D7_0717700). Relative transcript abundance was then calculated as 2^ΔCt. For RNA-seq, poly(A)⁺ RNA was enriched with KAPA mRNA Capture Beads (KK8441) and fragmented to ~300–400 nt. Strand-specific libraries were generated with the KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (KK8401) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were processed with Trim Galore v0.6.10 to remove adapters and low-quality bases, and aligned to the P. falciparum 3D7 reference genome (Pf3D7 v64, PlasmoDB) using HISAT2 v2.2.1 with default settings. SAMtools v1.6 was employed to filter low-quality reads and convert SAM files to position-sorted BAM format. Read counts were assigned to genes using featureCounts v2.0.1 based on PlasmoDB GTF annotations. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 v1.46.0; gene filtering was performed to retain only genes with at least 10 reads in either the control or KO condition. Considering genes as significantly regulated when the |log2 fold change| ≥ 2 and the adjusted P-value (padj) ≤ 0.01.

Gene Ontology (GO) term enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed on the selected gene set using the GO enrichment module implemented in PlasmoDB (https://plasmodb.org/). Terms with a P ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly overrepresented. The enriched GO categories were subsequently visualized in R using the ggplot2 v4.0.2 package to enable graphical summarization and interpretation of the functional profiles associated with the analyzed genes.

ChIP-seq and data analysis

ChIP assays were performed as previously described with minor modifications65. Parasites were synchronized and harvested at either the ring or schizont stage. Cross-linking was induced by incubation with 1% formaldehyde (Sigma, F8775) for 10 min at 37 °C with gentle rotation, followed by quenching with 0.125 M glycine for 5 min on ice. Infected erythrocytes were lysed with 0.15% saponin for 10-15 min on ice and washed three times. Parasites were then resuspended in lysis buffer (10 mM HEPES pH 7.9, 10 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 0.1 mM EGTA pH 8.0, 1 mM DTT, 0.25% NP-40, and protease inhibitors) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Nuclei were released by Dounce homogenization and pelleted by centrifugation. The nuclear pellet was resuspended in 200 μl SDS lysis buffer (1% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0), and chromatin was sheared to ~200–500 bp fragments using a Covaris M220 sonicator. Chromatin samples were incubated overnight with 1 μg of antibody (H3K9me3, Abcam; H3K4me3, CST; H3K9ac, Millipore; anti-GFP, Abcam) and subsequently bound to protein A/G Dynabeads (Invitrogen) for 4 h. Immunocomplexes were washed, cross-links reversed, and DNA purified using the MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). ChIP and input DNA were processed for library preparation by end repair (Epicentre), adenylation (NEB), and adapter ligation (NEB). DNA was purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter), size-selected, and amplified using the KAPA HiFi PCR Kit (KAPA Biosystems) under the following cycling conditions: 98 °C for 1 min; 12 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s and 65 °C for 1 min; final extension at 65 °C for 5 min. Libraries were multiplexed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using 150 bp paired-end reads. Each ChIP-seq experiment was performed with a minimum of two biological replicates.

Raw ChIP-seq reads were first processed with Trim Galore v0.6.10 to remove adapters and low-quality bases66. Clean reads were aligned to the P. falciparum 3D7 reference genome using Bowtie2 v2.5.467, and alignment files were converted to BAM format, sorted, and indexed with SAMtools v1.668. For visualization, BAM files were converted to BigWig format using bamCoverage from the deepTools suite v3.5.469 and viewed in the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV, v2.19.1)70. Signal profiles and heatmaps were generated with deepTools, using plotProfile to calculate average enrichment across selected genomic regions and plotHeatmap to display genome-wide signal distributions. For quantification of ChIP-seq signals at individual genes, H3K4me3 and H3K9ac enrichment was calculated as the average signal within the promoter-proximal region spanning −2.5 kb to the transcription start site (TSS). In contrast, H3K9me3 enrichment was quantified over the entire gene body, including the region from −2.5 kb upstream of the TSS to +1 kb downstream of the transcription end site (TES). Peak calling was performed with MACS2 v2.1.071 using a P-value threshold of 0.05, with input DNA as a control. Identified peaks were annotated using the ChIPseeker Bioconductor package72 v1.26.2, and replicate peak sets were intersected to define consensus peaks. MEME-Chip v5.5.7 was used for Motif discovery analysis (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme-chip73).

ATAC-seq and data analysis

ATAC-seq were performed as described previously74. ATAC-seq was performed using approximately 1 × 106 schizont-stage parasites obtained after red blood cell lysis with saponin. Parasites were resuspended in lysis buffer to permeabilize cellular membranes, and nuclei were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the Chromatin Profile Kit (Novoprotein, N248). The isolated nuclei were immediately incubated with Tn5 transposase in the tagmentation reaction mix for 30 min at 37 °C. The reaction was terminated by adding 5 μL stop buffer and incubating at 55 °C for 10 min. Tagmented DNA fragments were purified and subjected to PCR amplification to generate sequencing libraries.

Raw ATAC-seq reads were processed in parallel with ChIP-seq data. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed using Trim Galore v0.6.10. Filtered reads were aligned to the P. falciparum reference genome with Bowtie2 v2.5.4, and alignments were converted to BAM format. To account for the offset introduced by Tn5 transposase, read start sites were shifted by +4 bp for reads mapped to the positive strand and −5 bp for reads mapped to the negative strand. The shifted BAM files were sorted and indexed, and PCR duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates with coordinate-based duplicate identification. Genome-wide accessibility profiles were generated using deepTools v3.5.4. Average ATAC-seq signal across selected genomic regions was calculated and visualized with the plotProfile function. Peak calling for JmjC1-knockout samples was performed using MACS2 v2.1.0 with the corresponding wild-type ATAC-seq data as a control and a P-value cutoff of 0.05. Identified peaks were annotated with the ChIPseeker package v1.26.2 in Bioconductor, and overlapping peaks between biological replicates were intersected to define a set of high-confidence consensus regions.

Hi-C library preparation

Hi-C experiment was performed as described75. Parasite chromatin was cross-linked with 1.25% formaldehyde in pre-warmed 1× PBS. Cells were lysed in buffer containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 10 mM NaCl, 2 mM AEBSF, 0.1% (v/v) Igepal CA-360, and protease inhibitors, followed by homogenization through a 26-gauge needle. Cross-linked DNA was digested with MboI (NEB), and 5’ overhangs were filled in using dNTPs supplemented with Biotin-14-dCTP (Invitrogen) and DNA Polymerase I, Large (Klenow) Fragment (NEB). Blunt ends were ligated with T4 DNA Ligase (4000 U), and cross-links were reversed in buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1% SDS, 1 mM EDTA, and 500 mM NaCl. Biotinylated DNA was sheared to 300–500 bp fragments using a Covaris S220 sonicator (10% duty factor, 140 peak incident power, 200 cycles per burst, 65 s). Fragments were captured with MyOne Streptavidin T1 beads (Invitrogen). End repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation were carried out in low-bind tubes using the KAPA Library Preparation Kit (KAPA Biosystems). Libraries were amplified with HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems) and universal forward plus barcoded reverse primers under the following PCR conditions: 98 °C for 45 s; 12 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 62 °C for 30 s; final extension at 62 °C for 5 min. Amplified products were purified by double-SPRI size selection using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) with a 0.5× right-side and 1.0× left-side selection. Library quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and a Bioanalyzer system (Agilent) before multiplexing and sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end reads.

Hi-C data analysis

To maximize statistical power, paired-end reads from three biological replicates were merged prior to analysis. Adapter trimming and removal of low-quality bases were performed with Cutadapt v5.1. The processed reads were then analyzed using HiC-Pro v3.1.0, which handles read alignment, duplicate removal, and detection of valid chromatin interactions. After quality filtering, approximately 31 million and 29 million valid read pairs were retained for the control and experimental groups (Supplementary Data 6), respectively, providing high-confidence interaction data for downstream analyses. Valid read pairs were normalized using the iterative correction and eigenvector decomposition (ICE) procedure implemented in HiC-Pro and subsequently converted into genome-wide contact matrices at ≤5 kb resolution using Juicebox Tools v2.17.00. The normalized contact matrices were visualized and analyzed using Juicebox v2.17.00 to examine chromatin spatial organization and identify differential chromatin interactions between conditions. Correlation among Hi-C biological replicates was evaluated using HiCRep v0.2.676. Three-dimensional chromatin structures were inferred from the normalized Hi-C contact matrices using Pastis v0.4.0 to generate 3D coordinate matrices, and the resulting models were visualized using ChimeraX v1.1054–56.

Three-dimensional genome reconstruction and spatial distance analysis

To evaluate the spatial proximity of different gene sets within the three-dimensional genome organization, 10-kb resolution ICE-normalized contact matrices generated by HiC-Pro were used for three-dimensional chromatin structure reconstruction. 3D genome modeling was performed using Pastis v0.4.0, which converts Hi-C contact frequencies into spatial coordinates representing chromatin conformation. Gene positions were then mapped to the corresponding 10-kb genomic bins based on the P. falciparum 3D7 genome annotation (PlasmoDB v64). After gene annotation of the reconstructed 3D model, three-dimensional coordinates of the target gene sets were extracted, and pairwise Euclidean distances between genes from different gene sets were calculated77.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software v10.4.1). Differences between two independent groups were evaluated using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test (ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; and ****P < 0.0001). For ChIP-seq, RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and Hi-C data, statistical significance was assessed using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All data are presented as mean ± SD. The numbers of independent experiments and biological or technical replicates are specified in the corresponding figure legends.
