Comparison of photon-counting CT angiography with energy-integrating CT angiography in coronary artery stenosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Iman Kiani, Alisa Mohebbi, Payam Jannatdoust, Kimia Darmiani, Saeed Mohammadzdeh, Afshin Mohammadi, Ali Gholamrezanezhead
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo perform CT-angiography of coronary arteries, energy-integrating detector computed tomography (EID-CT) is widely used but it has several limitations, especially in overestimation of size of calcium plaques. Photon-counting detector computed tomography (PCD-CT) promises better image quality and lower radiation. This study compares the efficacy, radiation dose, and image quality of EID-CT and PCD-CT for coronary artery disease (CAD) diagnosis.Materials and methodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted per PRISMA guidelines. Four databases were searched for studies directly comparing EID-CT and PCD-CT. Data on coronary artery calcium scores (CACS), radiation dose index (mGy), dose-length product (DLP), and image quality were extracted. Risk of bias was assessed using the QUADAS-C tool.ResultsTwelve studies with 213 patients were included. PCD-CT showed a significant reduction in dose index (standardized mean difference (SMD) = -0.62, 95% CI: -0.89 to -0.35, p < 0.001) and DLP (SMD = -0.66, 95% CI: -0.98 to -0.35, p < 0.001) compared to EID-CT. Quantitative data for image quality and artifacts was limited, but qualitative assessments indicated PCD-CT’s superior performance in reducing beam-hardening artifacts and providing clearer images of calcified plaques. No significant difference was found in CACS between the modalities (intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) = 0.992, 95% CI: 0.981 to 1.000) based on individual patient data. PCD-CT results in reduced percentage and grade of stenosis compared to EID-CT in the available data.ConclusionPCD-CT has been suggested to offer better image quality and lower radiation dose compared to EID-CT, especially in visualizing calcified plaques. There is some evidence of less overestimation with PCD-CT, which should be confirmed by further studies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-02038-9.

Introduction

Historically, invasive coronary angiography (ICA) has been the gold standard for evaluation of coronary artery plaques [1]. However, its invasiveness, need for contrast administration, and associated procedural risks have prompted the development of non-invasive alternatives. Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) has gained widespread use as a reliable, less invasive imaging modality that offers high diagnostic accuracy in evaluating coronary anatomy [2]. In recent years, its diagnostic accuracy for detecting obstructive coronary artery disease has been well validated, further reinforcing its role as a first-line imaging tool in the assessment of coronary pathology [3].

Energy-integrating detector (EID) technology has been the standard CT imaging technology for years, measuring the total energy of all incoming X-ray photons as a cumulative signal. One major consequence of this technology is the occurrence of blooming artifacts, which make calcified plaques appear larger than they truly are [4]. Photon-counting detector CT (PCD-CT ), on the other hand, is a novel technology that has received clinical approval from the FDA in 2021 [5] and is based on detectors that can identify and count every single photon as it strikes the detector [5]. This technology allows for precise energy discrimination, separating photons into different energy bins [5]. This superiority can be particularly useful in distinguishing between calcified and noncalcified plaques without needing contrast agents [6], and it can also enhance spatial resolution [7]. Additionally, PCD-CT offers these enhanced imaging capabilities while potentially reducing the radiation dose, thereby increasing patient safety [5].

Another advantage of PCD-CT lies in its intrinsic capability to perform spectral imaging without relying on techniques like kV switching. In conventional EID-CT systems, dual-energy imaging is typically achieved through techniques such as kV-switching, dual-layer detectors, or dual-source acquisition [8, 9]. Among these, dual-source CT, which uses two X-ray tubes and detector arrays operating at different energy levels, is one of the most widely used clinical approaches [10]. This setup allows simultaneous acquisition of low- and high-energy datasets, enabling spectral separation and improved tissue characterization. However, dual-source systems come with limitations, including increased system complexity, potential misregistration between datasets, and compromised temporal resolution [11]. On the other hand, PCD-CT offers inherent multi-energy imaging capabilities without the need for multiple X-ray sources or kV-switching. By directly counting individual photons and measuring their energy, PCD-CT can provide high-resolution spectral data in a single scan with improved signal-to-noise ratio and potentially greater diagnostic accuracy [12].

Despite these advancements, studies have yet to systematically compare the performance metrics and radiation dose between EID-CT systems and PCD-CT. The present systematic review and meta-analysis aims to systematically compare the radiation dose, coronary artery calcium scores (CACS), stenosis severity, artifacts and imaging quality between EID-CT systems and PCD-CT.

Method

Our research methodology and analysis procedures follow the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Diagnostic Test Accuracy. The reporting of this review adheres to the standards set by the Preferred Reporting Items for a Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Diagnostic Test Accuracy (PRISMA-DTA).

The search syntax was collaboratively developed by two reviewers who initially worked independently. Utilizing both EMTREE and MeSH terms, they crafted a comprehensive search strategy refined through mutual discussions. A third reviewer was consulted in cases where initial consensus was challenging. The search, conducted without language restrictions, was executed across four databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library on May 10, 2024, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Conference abstracts were also included, and a thorough examination of reference lists was conducted. MedRxiv was also searched for pre-print studies. EndNote software facilitated the initial screening of records for duplicates, which were then manually clarified.

Our review included study designs of case-control, cohort, cross-sectional, and clinical trials, each with a minimum of five participants. Only studies directly comparing PCD-CT and EID-CT were considered. Exclusions were made for preclinical research that solely involved cadaveric subjects, animals, phantoms, or anthropomorphic models. Also, due to the head-to-head nature of this study (i.e., direct comparison), only studies performing both EID-CT and PCD-CT on the same patients were included to control the present bias further. When multiple publications reported on the same dataset, preference was given to the publication that provided the most comprehensive results.

In the screening phase, two reviewers independently screened the search results, setting aside irrelevant studies. For persisting disagreements, the intervention of a third reviewer was sought. For quality assessment, two reviewers independently assessed bias risk using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-Comparative (QUADAS-C) tools. In instances of disagreement, the third reviewer provided the final decision. Data extraction tasks, too, were executed independently by two reviewers. All extracted data were entered into an Excel spreadsheet for verification and cross-checking. Unresolved issues were referred to the third reviewer.

Regarding synthesis of data, in this study, we compared the image quality of coronary artery stenosis and radiation dose exposure between PCD-CT and EID-CT based on the grading reported in the included studies as standardized mean difference (SMD). In studies where these values were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR), these data were converted to mean and standard deviation using the method proposed by Cai et al. [13]. The impact of each study on the results was analyzed using influence diagnostics assessments and Galbraith plots. We also compared the mean difference in CACS and coronary artery calcium volume (CACV) as measured by both PCD-CT and EID-CT. The agreement of these measures was calculated based on the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). For the quantitative assessment of radiation dose exposure, we focused on the volume CT dose index reported in mGy and dose-length product (DLP). The comparison between EID-CT and PCD-CT based on artifact presentations in examinations was also made.

If studies provided individual-level data (e.g., figures), we used that data for individual-participant data (IPD) analysis. IPD data from figures was precisely extracted using Digitizer [14]. For IPD evaluation, the ICC type 1 A analysis method was performed due to the cluster nature of the data. To evaluate the coronary artery calcium score trend between methods, Bland-Altman (BA) and scatter plots were also drawn for trend evaluation. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3), Stata version 17.0, and MedCalc version 20.0, employing a random-effects model. The bootstrap method was performed for IPD evaluation with 1000 replications. Publication bias assessments were performed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. High statistical heterogeneity was identified with an I^2 value ≥ 50%. A significance level of 5% was adopted for all statistical tests.

Results

Search results and study characteristics

Twelve studies with 213 patients were included [15–26]. PRISMA flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Initially, 316 studies were screened. 27 studies were assessed for eligibility, from which twelve studies were included. Study characteristics are shown in Table 1. The number of patients ranged from 8 to 28. Furthermore, the age range of participants spanned from 58 to 69 years. Notably, the participant pool predominantly featured a higher percentage of females. Additionally, the studies showed a range of BMI values, most of which were within the overweight category. Risk of bias assessment

Figure 2 represents the risk of bias assessment based on QUADAS-C. A notable observation was the absence of the reference test (i.e. ICA) in the included studies, leading to the inapplicability of flow and timing and reference test risk of bias assessments. The QUADAS assessment also identified some studies with a high risk of bias, particularly concerning patient selection [15, 18, 20, 26]. For instance, some studies included healthy volunteers, which may not precisely represent the target population typically assessed for coronary artery disease, thereby introducing selection bias [15]. Additionally, exclusions due to poor image quality or data outside standard measurement parameters were noted [18, 20]. In the included studies, the time gap between EID-CT and PCD-CT scans was negligible, which minimized the potential for variability due to physiological changes and allowed for a more direct comparison of image quality and diagnostic accuracy.

Image quality and artifacts

Due to limited data, analysis was not possible in studies assessing image quality. Noteworthy, quality assessment was conducted using subjective scales. Si-Mohamed et al. found a 57% (95% CI: 41 to 72) proportion of score improvement with PCD-CT images compared to EID-CT in five-point quality score [17]. Similarly, Pinos et al. and Greffier et al. found that subjective image quality with PCD-CT was significantly superior on a 1–5 Likert scale [17, 21, 24].

Assessing artifacts, Symons et al. reported no significant differences in motion artifact scores, with both EID-CT and PCD-CT averaging around 1.3 and 1.4 out of a maximum score of 3, with 0 indicating no motion artifacts 3 indicating severe motion artifacts [15]. Conversely, Boccalini et al. demonstrated superior image quality with PCD-CT, where 85% to 90% of PCD-CT images achieved high-quality scores, significantly outperforming EID-CT’s 28%, largely attributed to PCD-CT’s ability to reduce beam-hardening artifacts and enhance the visualization of stents [16]. Additionally, Si-Mohamed et al. noted a significant advantage with PCD-CT in reducing beam-hardening artifacts in calcified plaques, with a reduction of 36.4% compared to EID-CT’s 48.4% (P < 0.001) [17]. Also, four studies compared the noise level in EID-CT and PCD-CT in the Hounsfield unit, all of which found no significant difference [15–17, 27].

Radiation dose

Figure 3A shows the radiation exposure in mGy for PCD-CT compared to EID-CT, expressed as SMD. The forest plot showed that PCD-CT had a significantly less radiation exposure of -0.62 (95% CI = -0.89 to -0.35, I^2 = 0.38, p-value < 0.001). Due to moderate heterogeneity, influential statistics and Galbraith plot analysis were performed. The influence of diagnostic assessments and Galbraith analysis showed that the study conducted by McCollough et al. had a significant impact on the results (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2) [19]. This might be explained by the retrospective gating conducted in the study. After removing this study, pooling the results showed that PCD-CT had a significant reduction of -0.49 (95% CI = -0.70 to -0.28, I^2 = 0, p-value < 0.001), indicating that the first results had an overestimation of the effect (supplementary Fig. 3). Figure 3B represents the comparison after subgrouping based on the risk of bias. The subgroup difference was not significant (p-value = 0.74). Subgroup analysis and meta-regression were conducted based on age, BMI, beta-blocker administration, Nitroglycerine administration, and slice thickness ratio, none of which showed significant differences. The funnel plot for this analysis showed to be symmetrical with an Egger’s test p-value of 0.810 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Six studies were included in the comparison of DLP between EID-CT and PCD-CT. SMD analysis showed that PCD-CT had a significant reduction of -0.66 (95% CI = -0.98 to -0.35, I^2 = 0, p-value < 0.001) (Fig. 4). The funnel plot was symmetrical, indicating the absence of publication bias (Supplementary Fig. 5) (Egger’s test p-value: 0.144).

Coronary artery calcium score

When assessing CACS, initial aggregate data analysis showed an insignificant difference between the two methods, with an SMD of 0.09 (95% CI = -0.19 to 0.38, I^2 = 0, p-value = 0.524) (Fig. 5A). The funnel plot was symmetrical with a p-value of 0.810 (Supplementary Fig. 6) (Egger’s test p-value = 0.800).

IPD analysis data was extracted from four papers [4, 15, 20, 26], with reported values for both imaging modalities in 150 patients. The difference in EID-CT score from PCD-CT score, like aggregate data, was insignificant, with the EID-CT score being higher by 0.42 (95% CI = -39.68 to 40.52). The Bland-Altman plot and scatter plot are shown in Fig. 5B and C, respectively. The ICC between EID-CT and PCD-CT was calculated as a promising value of 0.992 (95% CI = 0.981 to 1.000), indicating that for CACS evaluation, EID-CT is replaceable with PCD-CT achieving almost identical results.

As of CACV, in the study conducted by Schwartz et al., the median calcium volume reported was 41 [IQR: 409] for EID-CT and 60 [IQR: 407] for PCD-CT, with an insignificant p-value of 0.22 [22]. However, in the study by McCollough et al., significant discrepancies in mean calcium volume both in 3-mm and 1-mm image slices were observed, with EID-CT showing 55.5 (SD = 63.4) and 50.0 (SD = 56.3) and PCD-CT displaying 61.5 (SD = 69.4) and 59.5 (SD = 63.9) respectively [20]. Furthermore, comparisons in a phantom study by Wolf et al. revealed that PCD-CT was significantly more accurate than EID-CT, with EID-CT overestimating CACV values in more cases [4].

Stenosis assessment

Regarding the percentage of stenosis, Koons et al. observed that PCD-CT significantly reduced the percentage of diameter stenosis by an average of 11% (SD = 11%) compared to EID-CT, with statistical significance (p < 0.001) [18]. McCollough et al. presented data from two readers with measurements of 37.7 (SD = 28.5) and 37.5 (SD = 36.3) for EID-CT and 32.6 (SD = 28.0) and 30.0 (SD = 34.9) for PCD-CT, which also showed a significantly lower stenosis percentage in PCD-CT [19]. This finding could in part be attributed to reduced blooming artifact.

Another key outcome examined in this review was the impact of PCD-CT on downstaging or upstaging of coronary artery disease (CAD). Data from four studies on this topic are summarized in Table 2 [18, 19, 22, 25]. For instance, in Koons et al. (2023), out of 34 lesions assessed using CAD-RADS, PCD-CT resulted in downstaging in 13 lesions, while only 1 case was upstaged. Similarly, McCallough et al. (2023) reported that PCD-CT led to downstaging in 6 cases out of 25 patients according to one reader, with minimal upstaging observed. Schwartz et al. (2023), however, did not observe any downstaging or upstaging using the Agatston score in their 10 patients. Finally, Zsarnoczay et al. (2023) noted that in 6 of 66 arteries, abnormalities identified on EID-CT were not considered hemodynamically significant when evaluated with PCD-CT, with no cases of increased severity.

Discussion

EID-CT imaging modality currently represents the most commonly employed method in coronary CT angiography, mainly due to its proven reliability and cost-effectiveness. However, it faces challenges with limited spatial resolution and soft-tissue contrast. Additionally, EID-CT is associated with the drawback of delivering relatively high doses of x-ray radiation [28]. Another shortcoming of EID-CT lies in its overestimation of calcified plaque. This issue may arise from the blending of different densities within one voxel, together with beam-hardening artifact resulted from high-density calcifications, which is especially prominent in those with a high calcium burden [29].

PCD-CT modality has been developed to help resolve these issues, using direct conversion of photons. Comparison of EID-CT and PCD-CT can take place in different aspects. Regarding image quality, a direct comparison of PCD-CT and EID-CT within the same patients was rarely performed, with many studies opting to conduct CT scans on different cohorts [30, 31]. This approach limits the direct comparability of the findings. Nonetheless, among the studies that did compare PCD-CT and EID-CT directly, a significant improvement in image quality with PCD-CT was consistently reported. This enhancement is particularly noticeable in the reduction of beam-hardening artifacts (Fig. 6). However, while PCD-CT shows marked improvements in the above-mentioned areas, it did not notably surpass EID-CT in reducing motion artifacts or noise levels.

Notably, we limited our review to in vivo human data because bench models cannot faithfully reproduce the complex, patient-specific determinants of coronary CT diagnostic accuracy, such as variable and irregular cardiac motion at different heart rates [32]. Current imaging phantoms are limited in their ability to replicate the complexity of human anatomy and physiology [33]. There is a clear need for their further improvement and development to better support the evaluation and optimization of emerging imaging technologies.

Regarding radiation, our study found a statistically significant difference in the radiation dose between PCD-CT and EID-CT, aligning with existing clinical evidence. A recent meta-analysis reported a 30.4% lower radiation exposure with PC-CT in lung nodule imaging [34]. Additionally, phantom studies on liver lesion detection showed that PC-CT combined with quantum iterative reconstruction (QIR) enabled dose reductions of up to 54% while maintaining or even improving diagnostic accuracy [35]. This finding is important due to long-established findings regarding radiation-induced carcinogenesis, especially in cardiothoracic imaging [36]. The PROTECTION VI study reports a median DLP of 195 mGy·cm for CCTA in current clinical practice [37]. In our analysis, radiation exposure was significantly lower with PCD-CT compared to EID-CT, with an SMD of -0.66 (95% CI: -0.98 to -0.35). This finding shows the potential of PCD-CT to meaningfully lower patient exposure relative not only to the EID-CT comparators but also to the benchmark values reported in PROTECTION VI. Importantly, the dose reduction observed in our analysis was achieved without evidence of compromised image quality, suggesting that PCD-CT may offer a dual benefit of enhanced safety and preserved diagnostic performance. Thus, when interpreted alongside PROTECTION VI, our results support the notion that transitioning to PCD-CT in routine CCTA practice could mitigate long-term cumulative radiation risks for patients while maintaining the diagnostic standards established with current EID-CT technology. Worthy of mention, PCD-CT utilizes direct conversion detectors, which offer improved dose efficiency and enhanced spatial resolution compared to conventional EID-CT [7]. This is achieved through more efficient detectors that directly convert X-ray photons into electrical signals, minimizing the loss of low-energy information and reducing noise. Because PCD-CT inherently measures photon energy, it enables multi-energy acquisitions without kV switching or dual-source hardware [38]. The detectors are also better at capturing contrast at lower doses and enable high-resolution imaging, which can later be smoothed to reduce image noise and increased contrast-to-noise (CNR) rate [39]. Unlike EID-CT, which relies on indirect conversion and suffers from electronic noise and signal overlap, PCD-CT uses direct conversion detectors that count individual X-ray photons and sort them into energy bins. This capability enables the generation of low-keV virtual monoenergetic images, which enhance iodine contrast, without increasing radiation dose [40, 41]. Additionally, PCD-CT benefits from reduced electronic noise, especially at lower photon energies. Iterative reconstruction algorithms such as QIR are specifically designed to take advantage of this improved raw data quality, enabling noise suppression while preserving spatial resolution and contrast. When paired with optimized protocols, including lower tube currents and reduced iodine contrast volumes, these features contribute synergistically to significant dose reduction while maintaining or even improving diagnostic performance [26].

To enhance clarity, it is important to distinguish between radiation dose from CCTA and from the overall CT protocol, which often includes CACS as a separate step [42]. While our pooled analysis presented aggregated dose metrics, CCTA accounts for the majority of radiation exposure, and the dose contribution from CACS is typically lower but still clinically relevant [42]. In line with this, separating CACS and CCTA values provides a clearer picture of where PCD-CT offers the greatest benefit.

Furthermore, CACS and CCTA protocols differ substantially. For CACS, several acquisition approaches exist (e.g., prospective ECG-triggered, non-contrast scans), each with different dose implications [43]. For CCTA, protocol diversity is even broader, encompassing retrospective versus prospective ECG-gating, high-pitch acquisitions, UHR mode, and QIR techniques [44]. Radiation dose naturally varies across these approaches, and part of the variability observed in the literature reflects these methodological differences. Although subgroup analyses were not feasible with available data, but the distinction is important when interpreting the overall magnitude of dose reduction achieved with PCD-CT. By addressing CACS and CCTA separately, and acknowledging protocol diversity, our findings show that PCD-CT has a dose advantage across acquisition techniques but should be interpreted in the context of varying baseline exposures inherent to each protocol.

As for stenosis, in clinical practice, precise measurement of coronary artery calcium scores is crucial for assessing the risk of CAD, as it stands as an independent predictor of cardiovascular events [45]. Despite the anticipated technological superiority of PCD-CT, our findings revealed no significant overall difference in the CACS when comparing PCD-CT with EID-CT. Beam-hardening artifacts commonly affect EID-CT, leading to exaggerated images due to intense X-ray absorption by high-density materials like calcium. In contrast, PCD-CT’s superior resolution and energy discrimination offer a more accurate depiction of calcifications. This results in variable CACS and CACV across studies; PCD-CT often detects finer details, leading to higher scores, while EID-CT may overestimate severity due to beam-hardening.

Another important variable of interest that we could not include in the meta-analysis was the percentage of stenosis. This variable was reported to be significantly lower in PCD-CT images compared to EID-CT images in two studies [18, 19]. Furthermore, although we could not perform a meta-analysis specifically on the effect of using PCD-CT in clinical decision making and grading scores, evidence from several reviewed studies revealed evidence suggesting common overestimation of calcified plaques severity in EID-CT compared to PDC-CT, suggesting promising future role of PCD-CT in avoiding unnecessary measures [18, 19, 22, 25]. Interestingly, McCollough et al. found that the lower stenosis percentage in visual assessments of PCD-CT occurred despite similar quantitative values calculated by both PCD-CT and EID-CT [19]. This finding could explain the evidence of lower stenosis percentages in the aforementioned studies while maintaining similar CACS values in our meta-analysis and align with the lower overestimation in PCD-CT observed in the study by Wolf et al. [4].

Another key challenge is assessing stent patency and in-stent stenosis in coronary CT angiography, which is caused by artifacts from metal stents [46]. PCD-CT, with its lower artifact levels and superior resolution, has shown promising results in improving stent visualization and accuracy, outperforming EID-CT in both in vitro and in vivo studies [16, 47]. The use of sharp kernels in PCD-CT imaging has been shown to reduce the difference in attenuation between the stent lumen and the upstream coronary lumen, which leads to a notable reduction in intra-stent artifacts [16]. PCD-CT can detect up to 28 line pairs per millimeter near the center of the scan, which corresponds to a pair thickness of 178 μm, which although still slightly larger than the stent struts themselves, represents a major improvement compared to EID-CT, and is close to what Intravascular ultrasound can achieve [16, 48]. Moreover, the promising diagnostic accuracy of PCD-CT was confirmed in a study that compared it to the ICA reference standard, revealing accuracy levels exceeding 90% [49]. However, there is need for more in-depth studies to establish PCD-CT’s advantages in assessing stent patency.

While our systematic review focused on direct comparisons between PCD-CT and EID-CT within the same patients, it is important to contextualize these findings within the broader evidence base of PCD-CT diagnostic accuracy against ICA as the reference standard. Two patient-level investigations have since evaluated the diagnostic performance of PCD-CT. Hagar et al. (2023) prospectively studied 68 patients severe aortic valve stenosis undergoing pre-transcatheter aortic valve replacement evaluation and demonstrated that ultrahigh-resolution PCD-CT achieved excellent diagnostic accuracy with 96% sensitivity, 84% specificity, and 88% overall accuracy for detecting coronary artery disease defined as stenosis ≥ 50% compared to ICA [50]. Moreover, Kotranias et al. (2025), which was published after the final search date of this review and therefore was not included in the quantitative synthesis, assessed performance of ultrahigh-resolution PCD-CT in 100 patients. They observed an excellent AUC of 0.99 (95% CI: 0.98-1.00) for identifying coronary arteries with ≥ 50% luminal stenosis, further supporting the high diagnostic accuracy of PCD-CT [51]. The results of both studies reinforce the clinical applicability of PCCT for coronary assessment.

Importantly, our study is subject to a few limitations that could influence the interpretation of the findings. Firstly, most studies were not able to fully blind evaluators to the CT technology used (PCD-CT vs. EID-CT) due to apparent differences in slice and increment thickness. This limitation might have introduced bias into the subjective assessments. Secondly, the variability in image acquisition protocols across different centers added another layer of complexity. Another critical point is the adaptation of radiologists to new technology. The transition from EID-CT to PCD-CT involves changes in image characteristics that may require a period of adjustment for radiologists. While some studies have been conducted on radiologists’ adaptation, more research is needed [52]. Additionally, in the performed studies, most of the participants have been females and mean age of participants ranged from 58 to 69 years. Since CAD is more prevalent in males with older ages [53], future studies should address this issue. Also, the CACS of patients in the included studies was lower than its level in high-risk patients [54], which hinders the generalizability of the findings. Lastly, the economic implications, cost-effectiveness and the long-term impact on patient management were not assessed. Future studies should consider these aspects and strive for a direct comparison with ICA, the gold standard, to better validate non-invasive imaging modalities’ accuracy and standardize PCD-CT protocols for CAD and most importantly for the assessment of in-stent stenosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present meta-analysis demonstrates that while PCD-CT and EID-CT provide comparable results in terms of CACS, PCD-CT offers significantly less radiation exposure and potentially superior image quality, mainly by reducing beam-hardening artifacts. Additional studies are needed to standardize the widespread clinical deployment of PCD-CT to assess CAD and in-stent stenosis.
