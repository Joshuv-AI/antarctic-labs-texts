Influence of HIV Status on Organ Procurement and Allocation: A Discrete Choice Experiment of Australian Transplant Providers
Authors: David W J Griffin, Warittha Tieosapjaroen, Karl Vaz, Gopal Basu, William R Mulley, Sakhee Kotecha, Kovi Levin, Angeline Leet, Greg Snell, James H McMahon, Jennifer F Hoy, Jason J Ong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople with HIV (PWH) experience disparate access to solid organ transplantation (SOT) but are eligible to be organ recipients and donors in Australia. Clinician decision-making about the allocation and utilization of organs is central to transplant access. This study aimed to determine the influence of HIV infection, and other key attributes, in decision-making about SOT recipients and donors.MethodsWe conducted 2 discrete choice experiment (DCE) surveys, using a D-efficient design, among Australian transplant providers. Clinicians were presented with 6 hypothetical SOT recipients and donor pairs and asked to nominate a preferred candidate based on 8 recipient and 6 donor attributes. The DCEs were hosted in Qualtrics and distributed via professional networks. Relative attribute importance was calculated as the percent contribution of each attribute by dividing its utility range by the total of all attribute utility ranges.ResultsNinety-five respondents completed the recipient, while 87 completed the donor DCE. Most respondents (83.5%) were kidney or liver organ specialists. Expected survival without a transplant was the most influential attribute in the selection (relative importance, 23.1%) of a recipient, while HIV was the least influential factor (0.4%). Conversely, HIV status was the second most important attribute in donor selection (26.6%), behind only organ quality (32.0%).ConclusionsHIV status had limited influence on the choice of transplant recipients but was the second most influential factor in donor selection. Our results suggest that clinicians' decisions are guided primarily by medical urgency, not HIV status, when choosing a recipient, but that residual concern remains about the use of donors with HIV.

People with HIV (PWH) are living longer, with a rising burden of age-associated noncommunicable diseases, due to effective antiretroviral therapy [1]. As such, there is rising demand and increasing interest in solid organ transplantation (SOT) for PWH with end-stage organ disease, who experience similar short- and long-term graft and survival outcomes as demographically matched controls [2, 3]. Additionally, evidence supports the use of organs from donors with HIV to appropriate recipients with HIV [4, 5].

Despite SOT being standard of care for PWH when indicated, they experience additional unique and intersectional barriers to SOT compared with those without HIV [6, 7]. This includes knowledge and perceptions among health care providers (HCPs), who play a central role in the selection of prospective recipients and donors, and the organ allocation process [6, 8].

Previous studies have demonstrated that some HCPs and organ transplant programs continue to believe that HIV contraindicates organ receipt, that there is heterogeneity within and between specialties and different transplant centers [9–11]. Additionally, misconceptions about the risk of occupational exposure to HIV has been highlighted as a potential barrier among surgeons [9]. A study in organ procurement staff in the United States showed that staff were less likely to pursue a donor with HIV than a donor with lesser organ potential, a donor with hepatitis C, or an unregistered donor [12]. This was likely multifactorial, including stigma, generalizations about the social situation of the donor, uncertainty about HIV disclosure requirements, and perceived additional financial and clinical risks when evaluating a prospective donor with HIV [13]. Nevertheless, local data are important due to the inherent differences between the Australian and US health care and organ procurement systems, whereby financial disincentives and other barriers may differ [6].

The published Australian experience of SOT in PWH is limited [14, 15]. Little is known about the barriers to SOT experienced by PWH in Australia, as existing databases do not capture HIV status and transplant assessments occur at the institutional, rather than national, level [16, 17]. In Australia, any person can record their intention to donate organs through the “opt-in” Australian Organ Donor Registry. While not binding, it provides a national, confidential record of a person's intention to donate. This process does not capture comorbidities, with all prospective donors undergoing universal screening for blood-borne viruses at the time of donation [16]. Ultimately, the decision to proceed with organ donation, if appropriate, is dependent on the relevant transplant teams and the provision of consent by the prospective donor's substitute medical decision-makers, usually their next-of-kin [18]. Registration to donate has been associated with increased likelihood of consent to donation in the Australian context, especially when the family has been aware of the prospective donor's wishes [18, 19]. Identifying and addressing existing gaps while ensuring that all citizens, including PWH, have equitable access to SOT is a priority area for Australia's national strategy for organ transplantation [20].

Discrete choice experiments (DCEs) are increasingly used in health sciences, including HIV and SOT research [21]. They can be used to understand how decision-makers prioritize attributes and make trade-offs between alternatives [22]. Specifically, DCEs can identify the preferred characteristics of organ recipients and donors among clinicians and estimate the relative utility (value) of each of these attributes in the SOT decision-making process.

This study aimed to use 2 DCEs to understand the influence of HIV infection, and other key attributes, on decision-making about the allocation of organs to recipients and the utilization of organs from prospective donors among Australian clinicians.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

We developed a survey consisting of 2 linked, sequential online cross-sectional DCEs: a recipient DCE and a donor DCE. Participants completed embedded electronic informed consent, followed by the recipient and then the donor DCE, with demographic data collected at the end. After each DCE, respondents were asked about their understanding of the task and ease of choice on a Likert scale. Participants included a convenience sample of Australian HCPs, who self-identified as registered medical practitioners involved in transplant-related care (eg, organ specialists, transplant physicians, transplant surgeons, and critical care clinicians). The DCEs were anonymous, hosted in Qualtrics, and available online through a link or quick-response (QR) code between August 2024 and April 2025. There were no mandatory questions, allowing respondents to skip questions but not move backwards through the survey. Denominators reflect the number of participants answering each question. Recruitment occurred through Australian professional networks, including professional society newsletters and mailing lists like the Transplant Society of Australia and New Zealand, Gastroenterology Society of Australia, Australia and New Zealand Society of Nephrology, and Cardiac Society of Australia and New Zealand. Participation was not incentivized.

Discrete Choice Experiment

A D-efficient experimental design was created in Ngene software (ChoiceMetrics), with constraints to exclude impossible combinations (eg, living cardiac or lung donor). The DCEs were developed in accordance with existing guidance [23] and in collaboration with a panel of transplant experts, with HIV community input. Experts included 2 nephrologists, 3 pulmonologists, 1 hepatologist, and 1 cardiologist with experience in transplantation. After a targeted literature review and consultation with the expert group, a consensus list of 8 recipient and 6 donor attributes was selected (Table 1). These were felt to be most important in the selection of recipients and donors. Age, frailty, life expectancy post-transplant, medication compliance, social support, pre-SOT quality of life (QOL), and expected survival without a transplant were included in the recipient DCE. Age, type of donor, organ potential, organ quality, and donor registration were included in the donor DCE. HIV infection was added by the research team and hypothesized to be negatively associated with the selection of recipients and donors.

The DCEs were pilot-tested with a small group of renal physicians (n = 13) to assess usability and ensure the exclusion of impossible combinations (eg, living donor of thoracic organs), as well as clarity and completion time, leading to only minor wording refinements in the choice set introductions without major modifications. The donor DCE involved the selection of a hypothetical donor with an “appropriate recipient,” which may include a person with HIV. The combined DCEs took ∼15 minutes to complete.

Statistical Analyses

Sociodemographic data were summarized using descriptive statistics. A multinomial logit model was used to calculate the relative utility (beta coefficient [β]) for each attribute level, where positive utility indicates preferred attribute levels and negative utility indicates undesirable levels. The attribute relative importance was calculated using the coefficient range (ie, the highest minus the lowest level coefficient for each attribute) of an attribute divided by the sum of all attribute ranges [24]. P values <.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted in STATA, version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

This study was approved by the Alfred Human Research Ethics Committee, project 60/24.

RESULTS

Ninety-five participants completed the recipient DCE, and 87 (91.5%) completed the donor DCE. Characteristics of the 79 (83.1%) participants who contributed demographics data are summarized in Table 2. Slightly more respondents were male than female, and from the eastern Australian states. Participants were predominantly kidney and liver organ specialists, working in tertiary hospitals. Most self-identified as being involved in the selection or referral of candidates for SOT, but less than half felt knowledgeable about HIV. Among recipient DCE respondents, 60/86 (69.7%) indicated that it was somewhat or extremely easy to understand the question, while 19/86 (22.1%) reported finding it somewhat or extremely easy to choose between hypothetical recipients. Similarly, 59/85 (69.4%) respondents to the donor survey indicated that it was somewhat or extremely easy to understand the question, while 29/85 (34.1%) reported finding it somewhat or extremely easy to select between hypothetical donors.

Recipient Discrete Choice Experiment

Figure 2A shows the attribute importance for the recipient DCE. HIV was the least important attribute (0.4%) in the selection of an SOT recipient. Expected survival without an SOT was the most important attribute (23.1%), while age was the second most important (17.4%). Life expectancy post-transplantation, medication compliance, and social support were of similar importance. Individual attribute-level (β) coefficients reflect part-worth utilities and are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Part-worth utilities are presented in Figure 3A and highlight the importance of individual attribute levels in the selection of a hypothetical recipient. Expected survival without a transplant of <3 months was strongly associated with the selection of a hypothetical recipient (β, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.02–1.63), while expected survival of >12 months was strongly negatively associated with the selection of an alternative (β, −0.97; 95% CI, −1.23 to −0.72). Respondents preferred hypothetical recipients who were younger, had an anticipated survival of 10 years post-SOT, and had high medication compliance (Figure 3A). Conversely, older age, anticipated survival of only 5 years, lower medication compliance, and expected survival without a transplant of >12 months were negatively associated with recipient selection. The ideal recipient candidate is outlined in Figure 4A.

Donor Discrete Choice Experiment

Attribute importance for the donor DCE is presented in Figure 2B. HIV was the second most important attribute (26.6%) when considering a hypothetical donor. HIV infection was strongly negatively associated with the selection of a donor (β, −1.23; 95% CI, −1.43 to −1.04) (Figure 3B; Supplementary Table 2). The most influential attribute in the selection of a donor was organ quality (32.0%), with high organ quality positively influencing donor selection (β, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.04–1.63) and poor organ quality being strongly negatively associated with the selection of a donor (β, −1.63; 95% CI, −1.90 to −1.36). The most preferred donor was aged 35, a living donor, with high organ quality. Donors who could offer 2 kidneys and liver were also favored. However, age 70, donation after brain death, and organ potential of 2 kidneys, liver, and lungs were negatively associated with donor selection (Figure 3B). Donor registration or a lack thereof had no significant influence on the selection of a donor (Figure 3B; Supplementary Table 2). Although biologically implausible, the ideal donor candidate is outlined in Figure 4B, reflecting a combination of the preferred attribute levels.

DISCUSSION

This study provides valuable insights into the preferences of Australian transplant clinicians about the importance of key factors used in decision-making about the selection of organ recipients and donors. Among key HCP stakeholders, medical urgency—reflected in the expected survival without a transplant—and age were the most important attributes used to choose a prospective recipient, while HIV infection was the least important attribute. In contrast, when selecting between prospective donors, HIV infection was the second most important factor, following organ quality, even when an appropriate recipient was available. This study adds to the contemporary health preference literature regarding organ transplantation. This is the largest study using a choice-based framework to examine how HIV infection affects donor selection, and the first to do so in the Australian context. While the limited importance of HIV identified in the recipient DCE is somewhat reassuring, the relatively large influence of HIV infection on donor selection highlights an opportunity for further research, education, and policy development.

Despite the enduring barriers to SOT for PWH [6], our findings suggest that clinicians' decisions are now guided primarily by medical urgency and recipient factors, not HIV status. The finding that HIV had a part-worth utility close to 0 suggests that HIV status does not significantly influence clinicians' decisions when allocating organs to recipients, reflecting the equity embedded within Australia's organ allocation system and aligning with international strategies that promote nondiscriminatory access to transplantation [16, 25]. Jaure recently defined equity in SOT as any person with end-stage organ disease who could benefit from SOT, having a fair and just opportunity to receive a transplant, and having access to the aftercare required for optimal health outcomes [26]. This is reflected in the observed increasing frequency of renal transplantation for PWH in Australia over time [15]. Several studies in the United States also indicate that the frequency of transplantation has increased among PWH across kidney [27], liver [28], lung [29], and cardiac transplantation [30]. This increase occurs in the setting of a substantial body of evidence demonstrating that PWH who receive contemporary antiretroviral therapy have similar graft and overall survival following SOT, compared with the general population [6]. Despite this, several studies in North America indicate that some clinicians across SOT programs consider HIV a relative or absolute contraindication to SOT [10, 11, 31]. While there are no published Australian data pertaining to this, Australian transplant guidelines have used cautionary language around SOT for PWH, particularly for cardiac transplantation [32].

In contrast, HIV infection was the second most influential attribute in the decision to accept or reject an organ donor, despite the availability of an “appropriate” recipient. This is striking, as it suggests that there remains some residual concern about or opposition to the use of donors with HIV. This is despite international data demonstrating that the use of organs from donors with HIV for PWH is not only safe [4, 5] but reduces the waitlist time for SOT and leads to the receipt of higher quality organs [33, 34]. This aversion may reflect HIV-related stigma, apprehension about the risk of occupational transmission, or misconceptions about HIV and organ donation among clinicians, described in a study of organ procurement organization (OPO) staff in the United States, who described “looking for ways to rule-out” potential donors with HIV [13]. Stigma in health care was reportedly experienced by recipients of organs from donors with HIV in 1 qualitative study and is well described in other settings for PWH [35–37]. Conversely, practical considerations may favor donors without HIV, including there being a more limited pool of potential recipients, given that organs from donors with HIV are typically only utilized for other PWH, or apprehension relating to potential selection biases in available outcomes data relating to the use of only the highest quality HIV D+ donors. A recent survey among Australian HCPs indicates that knowledge about the eligibility of PWH to donate organs in Australia remains low [38]. While there are no data on the number of PWH awaiting SOT in Australia, this number is likely to be low at any given time, given the small population of PWH by international standards [39], and supported by the small number of PWH who have undergone SOT in Australia to date [14, 15]. Hence, activating organ retrieval and transplantation teams may not be perceived as an appropriate use of resources, where the organ or recipient potential is small [12]. Although there are no legal barriers to transplanting organs from PWH to recipients without HIV in Australia, this is not standard practice and has not occurred intentionally here. However, local transplant guidelines suggest that it may be considered for a consenting recipient, where medical urgency outweighs the risks of potential HIV transmission [16].

The prioritization of medical urgency, recipient age, and organ quality by HCPs reflects a strong alignment with the ethical principle of utility that underpins organ allocation decisions [16, 40]. This aims to maximize the benefit derived from the scarce pool of organs donated [40] and is consistent with community priorities highlighted in previous DCEs. Here, post-transplant QOL and years of life gained by SOT were highly important, while poor medication compliance was negatively associated with the choice of a prospective recipient [41]. In a similar Australian DCE, younger age and life expectancy without a transplant (ie, medical need) were prioritized over post-SOT survival, in keeping with our findings [42]. The prioritization of younger donors with the highest quality organs and living donors are in keeping with the need to optimize benefit for recipients and with previous studies demonstrating the desire to transplant the best organ for the best survival among Australian health care professionals involved in SOT [43]. These results are congruent with a previous DCE exploring the preferences of OPO staff about the pursuit of a donor with HIV. While organ potential was the most important factor, younger age and donation after brain death were preferred [12]. However, their study did not include organ quality as an attribute or the influence of living donation.

The strengths of this study include the use of the DCE methodology, demonstrated to have high external validity to predict health-related preferences [21], and the inclusion of DCEs about both recipient and donor selection. The influence of HIV has not been assessed previously in the context of organ receipt. Conversely, limitations include the potential for selection bias, if the survey attracted respondents with a particular interest in HIV and SOT or SOT equity. Although larger than Predmore's DCE exploring the influence of HIV on organ procurement [12], the small sample size limited our ability to perform subgroup and latent class analyses to identify differences between clinician groups. The underrepresentation of thoracic transplant specialists limits the generalizability to this group and may have affected the value of donors with increased organ potential as the ability to donate thoracic organs would be less relevant to kidney and liver specialists, who contributed the most responses. Alternatively, the preference for kidney and liver donors may be pragmatic, given that renal and liver transplants constitute ∼80% of transplanted organs in Australia [44]. Stated preference data acquired from a DCE may not translate to practice due to the presence of “hypothetical bias' and the nature of DCEs simulating rather than revealing real-world choices [45]. DCEs assume decision-making is “rational,” which may not be the case in high-pressure situations like whether to proceed with a recipient or donor for SOT, given the consequences of both action and inaction [46]. Moreover, stated preference data from DCEs may be prone to framing bias, with responses influenced by the way in which choice tasks and attributes are presented [47]. However, DCEs are less susceptible to social desirability bias than traditional surveys as respondents are indirectly rather than directly asked about the factor in question [48]. To mitigate these factors, we pilot-tested the survey to ensure that the questions and attributes were appropriately framed and maintained respondent anonymity. Finally, the choices made by clinicians may not reflect what organ recipients or donors value, and this study did not explore the preferences of patients or their substitute medical decision-makers, who are key stakeholders in the decision-making process.

Future research should explore the motivations underpinning these preferences among HCPs, perhaps using qualitative or mixed methods, to better understand their perspectives about PWH donating their organs to appropriate recipients. Understanding the inequities in SOT experienced by PWH is needed to tailor education and identify research and policy gaps that may preclude PWH from participating in SOT to their full potential. This study highlighted that less than half of participating HCPs felt knowledgeable about HIV, illustrating an opportunity for continuing medical education about contemporary HIV, multidisciplinary meetings, and collaboration between HIV and non-HIV specialist clinicians and incorporation of relevant HIV-specific considerations in guidelines. Certainly, increasing the knowledge of PWH and their HCPs about their eligibility to receive and donate organs where appropriate will be important [38, 49], along with ensuring that transplant guidelines are based on contemporary data and avoid discriminatory language [50]. Understanding consumer preferences about SOT is essential to ensuring societal trust and engagement in organ donation and allocation systems, and for informed consent. Ideally, a centralized, confidential, and coded Australian database of prospective donors and recipients would enable the understanding and closure of gaps in the receipt and donation of organs by PWH and other groups who are underrepresented in the SOT processes [6, 7, 17]. This could also be leveraged to identify and coordinate transplantation between donors and recipients with HIV, should the opportunity arise, and aligns with the Australian national organ transplant strategy's prioritization of enhanced data collection systems [20].

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates that while HIV infection has minimal influence on the decision to select a hypothetical recipient among Australian transplant clinicians, hesitancy remains about the pursuit of donors with HIV, even where an appropriate recipient exists. Medical urgency and maximizing the benefit of SOT were favored in the selection of organ recipients, while organ quality was most important in donor selection. These findings highlight both progress and challenges toward SOT equity for PWH in Australia, with additional work needed to understand and overcome barriers to SOT for PWH.
