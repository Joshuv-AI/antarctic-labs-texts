Incorporating serial uterine POCUS into routine ICU care for postpartum patients
Authors: Armin Ahmed, Deen Bandhu, Sukanya Sampath
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Obstetric patients may require intensive care unit (ICU) admission for various reasons, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and related complications (severe preeclampsia, eclampsia, and HELLP syndrome), sepsis, obstetric haemorrhage, cardiomyopathy, or complications following caesarean delivery.[1] An often-overlooked aspect of postpartum physiology in critically ill obstetric patients is ‘lochia’, which is the normal vaginal discharge resulting from the shedding of the uterine lining as the uterus involutes. It typically lasts 6–12 weeks and progresses through three stages: rubra, serosa, and alba [Figure 1].[2] The drainage of lochia is influenced by maternal body positioning and is facilitated by upright positions in ambulatory women. However, critically ill ventilated patients often remain supine for prolonged periods, resulting in uterine retroversion and pooling of lochia. This is further compounded by coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia, leading to increased endometrial shedding and fluid accumulation [Figure 1]. Over time, the retained fluid may accumulate to a clinically significant volume from a small initial collection. In some patients, retained lochia may become secondarily infected, resulting in postpartum endometritis or endomyometritis (involvement of both endometrium and myometrium). While commonly caused by gram-positive organisms, ICU cases often involve drug-resistant pathogens such as Enterococcus faecium, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae.[3] Several risk factors predispose patients to endometritis, including prolonged labour with multiple per vaginal examinations, prolonged rupture of membranes, caesarean delivery, meconium-stained amniotic fluid, missed intrauterine foetal death, and poor hygiene.

In this context, point-of-care uterine ultrasound (POCUS) becomes a valuable, non-invasive tool for early detection of lochia retention. We routinely perform serial uterine POCUS (every 24–48 h) in postpartum ICU patients who show reduced lochia drainage. Notably, the uterus may appear empty on day one postpartum, but it accumulates fluid over subsequent days due to immobility. Hence, a single scan may not be sufficient, requiring serial monitoring for dynamic assessment. The scan is typically performed with a curvilinear (2–5 MHz) probe just above the pubic symphysis in sagittal and transverse planes [Figure 1]. A normally involuting uterus shows a thin echogenic endometrial lining. In contrast, retained lochia appears as anechoic or low-level echogenic fluid in the endometrial cavity. If infected, collections may appear heterogeneous on ultrasound and clinically present as foul-smelling pus discharge per vagina.

Management involves prompt drainage and targeted antibiotic therapy. Gentle cervical dilatation or the use of local prostaglandins often restores uterine drainage, while suction evacuation may be required for significant pooling of lochia. Early recognition and intervention can improve maternal outcomes in the ICU.

We propose that serial uterine POCUS be incorporated as a standard part of ICU care for postpartum patients, particularly those on prolonged mechanical ventilation. While POCUS is widely accepted in the ICU for cardiac, lung, and abdominal evaluation, its routine use for uterine monitoring remains underutilised. Integrating this practice could improve early detection of endometritis, optimise antimicrobial stewardship, and improve maternal outcomes.
