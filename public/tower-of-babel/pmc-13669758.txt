Unrestricted drinking before procedural sedation using fasting cards: a prospective observational quality improvement study
Authors: Anne Rüggeberg, Eike A Nickel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsInternational guidelines recommend clear-liquid fasting for 2 hours before procedural sedation—but in practice, this fasting time is usually exceeded, often significantly. We aimed to align actual liquid fasting times with these guidelines by implementing ‟unrestricted drinking using fasting cards” for patients undergoing procedural sedation.MethodsIn a prospective, observational, quality improvement study, we implemented ‟unrestricted drinking before procedural sedation using fasting cards” in 2 ‟Plan-Do-Study-Act” cycles. Our patient population included those scheduled for elective procedures with sedation in gastroenterology, pulmonary, and cardiology.ResultsThe introduction of ‟drink until called” and subsequent education, including fasting cards, increased the proportion of patients who were aware they could drink before sedation to 81%. The awareness about permission to drink before procedural sedation correlated with significantly shorter median (interquartile range) clear-liquid fasting times of 1.2 (0.7-2.5) hours than those who were not informed (11.3 [4.1-12.7] hours; P < .00001) and a greater median cumulative preoperative drinking volume on the day of sedation (300 [200-500] mL vs 50 [50-100] mL; P < .00001). In addition, patients informed about their drinking permission experienced less thirst (71.6% vs 42.1%; P < .05) and were more likely to take morning medication (72.8% vs 52.6%; P < .05).ConclusionsOur results demonstrate the importance of aligning clinical practice with established guidelines to improve patient safety and comfort. Future efforts should focus on overcoming institutional and cultural barriers to enable widespread adoption of such quality improvement measures.

Introduction

Many medical interventions or examinations require sedation to perform the procedure or to increase patient comfort. Because sedation might compromise airway reflexes, fasting before the procedure is recommended to avoid pulmonary aspiration of gastric content. For the required fasting period, most medical societies refer to the guidelines of the anesthesiology societies (American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] and European Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care). These guidelines recommend that adult patients should be encouraged to drink clear liquids for up to 2 hours before the procedure.1, 2, 3 These short liquid fasting times substantially reduce thirst, hunger, and patient discomfort, reduce gastric residual volume, and improve mucosal visibility scores in gastroenterological procedures.4, 5, 6 Unfortunately, in practice, the median liquid fasting time is usually more than 12 hours.7,8 Attempts to reduce liquid fasting times before procedural sedation to levels recommended by guidelines have failed.5,9

Furthermore, the consequences of prolonged liquid fasting, such as dehydration, insulin resistance, increased stress response, delirium, renal failure, and other postoperative adverse events, have long been ignored.9 In response, an increasing number of anesthetists allow their patients to consume clear liquids even within the final 2 hours before procedures, mostly with protocols that allow clear liquids until the patients are called from the ward or waiting area to their procedure. The best-known protocol is “Sip Til Send,” endorsed by both the Centre for Perioperative Care and the Government of New South Wales in Australia.10,11 Studies that allowed patients to drink clear liquids until they were called for their procedure demonstrated significantly reduced liquid fasting times.12,13 A recent consensus statement on fasting before procedural sedation recommends already unrestricted clear-liquid intake in patients with negligible or mild risk factors for aspiration.14 In addition, an international multidisciplinary consensus statement involving 68 stakeholders from more than 10 disciplines recommends either encouraging patients to drink clear liquids until 2 hours before the procedure or implementing institutional protocols that allow clear liquids within the last 2 hours before induction of anesthesia or sedation.15

In 2022, we published our institutional quality improvement experience reducing the median liquid fasting time before surgery from 12.0 (interquartile range [IQR] = 9.6-14.0) hours to 2.1 (1.2-3.8) hours (P < .0001) through the development and evaluation of the protocol of ‟unrestricted drinking until called for surgery with fasting cards.”13 This protocol is based on 2 key elements. First, almost all patients are allowed to drink clear liquids until they are called to the operating theater, including drinking within the final 2 hours before their operation. Second, the protocol is implemented using fasting cards. Fasting cards are a 3-level color-coded training tool (Fig. 1).13,16 They are a freely available, noncommercial, nontechnological, open-source protocol licensed under the Creative Commons License CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.16 The color of the fasting card is assigned to each patient according to the patient's pre-existing conditions and interventions. Patients with no significant pre-existing conditions or interventional specialties are given a green card. They are allowed to drink clear liquids according to their wishes and habits until they are called for surgery or procedural sedation. A yellow card is assigned to patients who require an individualized approach. A red card is reserved for critically ill emergency patients who must remain fasting. The color of the fasting card was assigned to each patient during the preanesthesia consultation.

Assignment of fasting cards to patients before procedural sedation.13,16NPO, Nil per os.

In the initial part of our project as described, fasting cards were assigned on the basis of the anesthetic record. The color of the fasting card is noted on the anesthetic record to inform staff which fasting card should be given to an individual patient. However, procedural sedation is often performed without an anesthetist and therefore, there is no anesthetic record to review when assigning fasting cards. Building on the existing structures, the aim of our study was to introduce ‟unrestricted drinking before procedural sedation” for patients who need to follow preprocedural fasting rules according to their pre-existing medical condition and the specifics of the surgery or procedure.

Methods

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Helios Emil von Behring, Berlin, Germany, November 26, 2021, and is in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study conduct, measurements, and end points

As part of our continuous quality improvement efforts, we aimed to implement ‟unrestricted drinking before procedural sedation” using the protocol of fasting cards, as mentioned. Patients undergoing procedural sedation do not have an anesthetic record that would have allowed us to document the color of the fasting card for each patient. Instead, we developed and evaluated an algorithm that allows nursing staff or clinicians to independently assign the color of a fasting card.

The algorithm was discussed in advance with nursing staff and clinicians performing procedural sedation. With the simple flowchart (Fig. 2), there are 3 decisions that need to be made as follows:

1.Based on the patient's previous medical history, patients with serious pre-existing conditions (usually ASA classification 3-4) are sedated by an anesthetist who notes the alignment of the fasting card at the anesthetic record.

2.Patients with severe lower esophageal sphincter dysfunction, with regurgitation in the supine position, are allowed to drink clear liquids up to 2 hours before the procedure. They receive a yellow card.

Patients with severe lower esophageal sphincter dysfunction, with regurgitation in the supine position, are allowed to drink clear liquids up to 2 hours before the procedure. They receive a yellow card.

3.Before gastroenterological procedures, patients receive a yellow fasting card. For each gastroenterological procedure, the specifications can be individualized, with longer solid fasting times required for a colonoscopy and no milk added to coffee or tea for a gastroscopy, for example. All other patients receive a green fasting card.

Algorithm for the allocation of fasting cards by nursing staff or clinicians.

Inpatients were given a laminated fasting card to hang by their bed the evening before their procedure. Outpatients and preadmission patients received a fasting card as a paper flyer to take home during the medical consultation for the procedure.

This algorithm allowed most patients scheduled for procedural sedation to drink clear liquids until they were called for the procedure. All hospital staff members were informed about the algorithm by e-mail. Several presentations were made at team meetings. A member of the staff (A.R.) was regularly present on the wards to help with any problems. A local standard operating procedure was written and signed by the entire hospital management: medical, nursing, and administration. In this way, the hospital management took responsibility for the ‟drink until called” protocol.

We conducted 2 iterative “Plan-Do-Study-Act” (PDSA) cycles. The PDSA cycle represents a pragmatic scientific method for problem-solving and continuous improvement in complex systems. The 4 phases reflect the scientific process of planning a change (‟Plan”), implementing that change (‟Do”), analyzing and interpreting the results (‟Study”), and drawing conclusions to inform the next cycle (‟Act”). Ineffective changes contribute to learning processes, which is a basic principle of the PDSA cycle.17

After evaluation of the data from our first PDSA cycle, further procedures were determined and implemented. This makes traditional reporting of methods and results less feasible. Therefore, all actions taken in each cycle are fully described in the results.

All data were collected by a medical specialist (A.R.), who was responsible for study conception and implementation. Just before induction of sedation, all patients were asked about the time and amount of their last liquid intake, and most of the other secondary end points outlined later.

The primary end point was the proportion of patients who knew that they were allowed to drink. Secondary end points for the second PDSA cycle were preoperative liquid fasting time, ingested liquid volume, thirst, dry mouth, hunger, receipt of fasting cards, intake of recommended morning medication, and aspiration. These end points were evaluated through self-reporting by the patients. We took a cup of tea or coffee as 200 mL, and for water, we used bottles containing 500 mL. We used a numerical rating scale from 0 (not present) to 10 (worst) for thirst, dry mouth, and hunger. Adverse events were collected from the sedation protocol.

Patient cohort and procedural sedation

Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older, able to answer questions, and scheduled for outpatient or inpatient gastroenterological examination, transesophageal echocardiography, or bronchoscopy performed with the patient under procedural sedation. For procedural sedation, intravenous access was established, and pulse oxygen saturation, systolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, and heart rate were continuously monitored. All patients received nurse-administered propofol sedation for induction and maintenance of procedural sedation.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using Excel 2019 (Microsoft, Redmond, Wash, USA) and statistical software SPSS V28 (IBM Corporation, Chicago, Ill, USA). The χ2 test was used to determine the association between 2 categorical variables. The t test was used for continuous variables. In the absence of a normal distribution, liquid fasting time and drinking volume were presented as median IQR values. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare fasting times and liquid intake between groups. No corrections were made for repeated tests.

Flowchart showing the sequence of the “Plan-Do-Study-Act” cycles to reduce preprocedural liquid fasting times.

PDSA cycle 1

Plan: The first step was to raise awareness of prolonged fasting among all staff involved in the care of patients undergoing procedural sedation. In addition, we developed an algorithm for the allocation of fasting cards by nursing staff or clinicians (see Methods).

Do: In early 2022, the algorithm was implemented within our hospital (see Methods).

Study: On 1 day each in November 2023 and March 2024, 43 patients were asked whether they knew they were allowed to drink and the time of their last drink.

The main result was that only 22 (51%) patients realized that they were allowed to drink before procedural sedation with significantly shorter median (IQR) clear-liquid fasting times of 1.96 (0.94-4.25) hours than patients who did not know they were allowed to drink (8.00 [2.58-10.00] hours; P < .0001).

We evaluated 2 reasons why the algorithm was only partially implemented in clinical practice:

1.Despite our local standard operating procedure, most wards still noted “fasting" in their daily lists of patients instead of the color of the fasting cards.

2.Many outpatients or preadmission patients were given the instruction to ‟keep fasting” as part of their medical information without any further explanation or a handout of fasting card flyers.

Many outpatients or preadmission patients were given the instruction to ‟keep fasting” as part of their medical information without any further explanation or a handout of fasting card flyers.

Act: Two key elements of how patients were informed that they were allowed to drink were not implemented: fasting card flyers were not given out to outpatients or preadmission patients, and for inpatients, the daily list for most wards did not indicate the color of the fasting card.

PDSA cycle 2

Plan: Appropriate actions were planned to ensure that all patients received a fasting card before procedural sedation.

Do: The word ‟fasting” was replaced with the color of the fasting card on most ward lists to ensure that fasting cards were handed out to inpatients. For recurring procedures, such as gastroscopy or colonoscopy in inpatients, we printed additional laminated cards in dark yellow so that the staff did not have to fill out a yellow card each time. Clinicians performing procedural sedation were asked to give their outpatients and preadmission patients a fasting card flyer or other written information about ‟drink until called.” In addition, training sessions were held for every employee who cared for patients before procedural sedation.

Study: A follow-up survey in the fourth calendar week of 2025 included all adult patients in gastroenterology, pneumology, and cardiology who had been scheduled for elective procedures with procedural sedation within 1 calendar week and who were able to answer questions. Of the 112 patients, 4 (3.6%) did not speak German or English, 1 (0.9%) had a neurologic condition, and 1 patient (0.9%) with a tracheostomy was unable to answer all questions. Six patients (5.4%) were given a red fasting card because of their underlying medical condition and were not allowed to eat or drink anything.

Therefore, 100 patients (89%) were included in the final analysis: 46 underwent gastroenterological examination, 29 underwent transesophageal echocardiography, and 25 underwent bronchoscopy. The majority (73%) of these were inpatients. All patients received nurse-administered propofol sedation for induction and maintenance of procedural sedation, with a mean dose of 172 (standard deviation [SD] = 101) mg and supplemental oxygen. The mean duration of the procedures was 33 (SD = 34) minutes. No patient required advanced airway management.

The main result was that 81 (81%) patients knew that they were allowed to drink before procedural sedation. Of those who knew they were allowed to drink, 51 (63%) received a fasting card, 8 (9.9%) were informed by the nursing staff, 10 (12.3%) by their clinician, 2 (2.5%) were already familiar with the protocol, 1 (1.2%) did not want to drink, and 9 (11.1%) could not recall who gave them the information. Of those who did not know they were allowed to drink, only 1 patient received a fasting card, but this patient was illiterate.

The median clear-liquid fasting times were significantly shorter in patients who knew that they were allowed to drink before procedural sedation (1.2 [0.7-2.5] hours) than in patients who did not know (11.30 [4.1-12.7] hours; P < .00001). In addition, the median preoperative self-reported cumulative drinking volume on the day of sedation was greater in these patients (300 [200-500] mL vs 50 [50-100] mL, respectively; P < .00001). Of the available drinks, 69 patients (who knew they were allowed to drink) chose water, 12 chose coffee, 12 chose tea, and 1 chose cola (multiple choices were possible). Patients who did not know they were allowed to drink were significantly more often outpatients or preadmission patients (74% vs 17% for patients who knew they were allowed to drink), and only 1 patient received a fasting card (more details are available in Table 1 and Fig. 4).

Act: The goal of patients knowing that they were allowed to drink was achieved in most patients. For outpatients and preadmission patients, it was clear that the medical information provided by the treating department needed to be improved and that patients should more often be given fasting card flyers or other written information about their fasting instructions.

Discussion

The aim of our study was to introduce ‟unrestricted drinking before procedural sedation” for patients who need to follow preprocedural fasting rules. Within the framework of a quality management tool with 2 iterative PDSA cycles, most patients knew that they were allowed to drink; as a direct result, the median liquid fasting time was reduced to 1.5 hours. The algorithm allowed the use of well-established fasting cards for patients before procedural sedation—even when no anesthetist was involved. Therefore, the color of the fasting card was not assigned by the anesthetic record.

For more than 25 years, international guidelines have recommended 2 hours of liquid fasting before anesthesia18; however, implementation is lacking with median liquid fasting times of 9 to 12 hours.7,19,20 Intensive, individualized fasting instructions can only reduce liquid fasting times from 10.5 to 5 hours, still far from the guideline recommendations.5 In addition, even today, studies define nil per os (NPO) from midnight as the usual care, with up to 70% of fasting orders effective from midnight.7,21,22 Keeping patients NPO ensures availability for the next open procedure slot. Despite our best efforts, we still found 11 NPO orders, clearly illustrating the depth of the NPO tradition in people's minds.

The fasting card protocol is an institutional protocol that can be applied to all patients in a hospital to reduce liquid fasting times. After implementing the protocol in patients before surgery, 9% of patients still did not drink on the day of surgery13,23; in our patients before procedural sedation, 19% did not drink. The majority of patients who knew they were allowed to drink were inpatients (73%)—most of these patients were given a fasting card. Because we have been running the project for patients before surgery for several years, the ward staff are well trained in the ‟drink until called” fasting card protocol. However, this is not the case for clinicians performing procedural sedation; they were only introduced to the protocol recently.

The majority (73%) of the patients who did not know that they were allowed to drink were outpatients or preadmission patients. For these patients, clinicians were not familiar with the idea of reducing liquid fasting times before procedural sedation before we started the project. Accordingly, the medical departments had only partially revised all written material, leaflets, flyers, and information, and only a few patients received fasting cards as paper flyers to take home. Yet the availability of written information to patients is a key element in the successful implementation of ‟drink until called.” Therefore, it is of the utmost importance to train all clinicians in this protocol and encourage them to provide their patients with written information.

In our clinic, the protocol of unrestricted drinking before surgery and procedural sedation will be regularly re-evaluated to ensure the intervention remains effective. To encourage wider adoption, the protocol has already been awarded the Butterfly Impact Award in Patient Safety (second prize) by the European Patient Safety Foundation and the Patient Safety Award by the German Coalition in Patient Safety24 and is supported by an international multidisciplinary consensus statement.15 For those interested in adopting the fasting card protocol, it is available in 15 languages free of charge.16

Our study had several limitations. The generalizability and external validity of the study results are limited by the single-center study design. To be able to interview all patients directly before their procedure, we only interviewed patients before gastroenterological, pulmonological, and cardiological procedures because these took place in close proximity to each other.

In conclusion, we adapted the protocol for ‟unrestricted drinking before surgery with fasting cards” to patients before procedural sedation without anesthetic care. The algorithm developed allowed nursing staff or any clinician to assign the fasting cards, which enable an individualized approach for each patient, with the aim of increasing patient safety.24
