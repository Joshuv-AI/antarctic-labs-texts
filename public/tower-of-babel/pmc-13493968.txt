Understanding farmers’ participation in agricultural marketing networks through an exploratory sequential mixed-methods study integrating qualitative barrier identification and structural equation modeling
Authors: Xingyu Liu, Nazanin Kianmehr, Zeinab Asadi, Hajar Zarei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Agricultural marketing networks such as cooperatives are widely promoted as mechanisms to improve farmers’ market access and income; however, adoption rates remain low in many developing contexts, including Iran. Despite extensive literature based on the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), existing studies often fail to fully capture context-specific institutional and social barriers, and rarely integrate qualitative insights into quantitative model development. This study addresses these gaps by employing an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design to develop and test an integrated behavioral model of farmers’ participation in agricultural marketing networks in Fars Province, Iran. In the qualitative phase, semi-structured interviews with 32 farmers were conducted to identify context-specific barriers and underlying mechanisms shaping participation decisions. Thematic analysis revealed four main categories of barriers: economic, institutional, social-informational, and psychological. These findings were systematically transformed into measurement constructs and used to develop a structured questionnaire for the quantitative phase. In the second phase, data from 332 randomly selected farmers were analyzed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM). The results show that attitude (β = 0.30, p < 0.001), perceived behavioral control (β = 0.18, p < 0.05), and social networks (β = 0.37, p < 0.001) significantly influence farmers’ intention to participate, while subjective norms (β = 0.07, p > 0.05) and knowledge (β = 0.01, p > 0.05) do not. Trust significantly shapes farmers’ attitudes (β = 0.60, p < 0.001) and also has a weaker direct effect on intention (β = 0.09, p < 0.05). Importantly, the composite perceived barriers construct exerts a significant negative effect on intention (β = − 0.06, p < 0.05), reflecting the structural constraints identified in the qualitative phase. The study contributes to the literature by demonstrating how qualitative insights can be systematically translated into measurable constructs within a TPB-based framework, thereby improving contextual validity and explanatory power. It further distinguishes between different forms of social influence, showing that behavioral effects are driven more by observed peer participation through social networks rather than perceived normative pressure. These findings provide important implications for designing trust-based, socially embedded agricultural policies that extend beyond information-driven interventions.

Introduction

The agricultural sector plays a vital role in food security, economic stability, and rural livelihoods worldwide1,2. In addition to production, efficient marketing systems are essential to ensure fair returns for farmers and reliable food access for consumers3–5. In developing countries such as Iran, smallholder farmers often remain disconnected from formal markets due to limited market information, inadequate infrastructure, and dependence on intermediaries who capture a large share of profits6–9. Consequently, inefficient marketing systems contribute to low farmer incomes alongside high consumer prices, ultimately constraining agricultural development and national food security10,11.

Agricultural marketing networks (MNs), including cooperatives, producer associations, and contract farming schemes, have emerged as important mechanisms for addressing inefficiencies in agricultural markets12,13. Grounded in Transaction Cost Economics and Social Capital Theory, MNs can reduce transaction costs, strengthen cooperation, and enhance farmers’ bargaining power through trust and social relationships. These networks may also improve price stability, access to inputs, market information, credit, and demand-oriented production opportunities for farmers14–16. In this sense, the theoretical advantages of MNs are rooted in their ability to simultaneously improve economic efficiency, strengthen collective bargaining power, and enhance coordination efficiency across fragmented agricultural value chains. While the theoretical advantages are significant, the practical implementation and adoption of MNs have been inconsistent, and their success relies on a complicated system of contextual factors, including institutional capacity, trust in organizations, farmers’ socio-psychological perceptions, market infrastructure, and the strength of social networks, which jointly shape adoption outcomes17. These contextual determinants include institutional conditions (such as governance quality, cooperative management efficiency, and policy enforcement), economic constraints (such as access to credit, input costs, and price volatility), infrastructural limitations (such as transport, storage, and market access facilities), and socio-psychological mechanisms (such as trust, perceived risk, collective efficacy, and social learning processes), all of which interact in shaping farmers’ willingness and ability to participate in MNs. In rural agricultural contexts, marketing networks refer to organized collective arrangements among farmers—such as cooperatives, producer associations, and contract farming systems—through which farmers jointly access markets, pool production, coordinate sales, and strengthen their bargaining position relative to intermediaries18. These networks function by reducing transaction costs, improving price negotiation power, enhancing access to market information and credit, and promoting more stable and equitable income distribution among smallholder farmers19.

Beyond economic and institutional considerations, recent studies increasingly emphasize the importance of environmental psychology in explaining agricultural decision-making and behavioral intention. Environmental psychology examines how psychological perceptions, beliefs, social cognition, and contextual factors shape individuals’ responses to environmental, agricultural, and resource-management challenges. Within agricultural systems, farmers’ behavioral choices are not determined solely by economic rationality; rather, they are strongly influenced by psychosocial mechanisms such as trust, perceived risk, social learning, attitudes, and behavioral control. Recent evidence from Iran and other developing-country contexts demonstrates the growing applicability of socio-psychological theories for explaining farmer behavior under complex institutional and environmental conditions. For example, Nasiri et al.20 applied the Health Belief Model to explain farmers’ intentions toward low-water-demand cropping patterns and found that psychological perceptions and contextual barriers significantly shaped behavioral intention. Similarly, Mashhadi et al.21 highlighted the importance of integrating environmental psychology with collective decision-making frameworks to better understand farmers’ responses to agricultural land preservation and agroecosystem sustainability challenges. In addition, Shahangian et al.22 demonstrated that psychosocial processes—including attitudes, social perceptions, and behavioral evaluations—play a central role in shaping conservation-oriented behaviors through integrated SEM-based approaches. These studies collectively suggest that agricultural participation behaviors emerge from the interaction between institutional realities and socio-psychological processes rather than purely economic considerations. Therefore, incorporating environmental psychology into the present research provides a stronger theoretical basis for understanding how farmers cognitively and socially evaluate participation in agricultural marketing networks.

These challenges are particularly evident in Iran, especially in Fars Province, one of the country’s major agricultural regions23. Despite producing strategic crops such as wheat, barley, fruits, and vegetables, the province continues to rely on fragmented traditional marketing systems characterized by long supply chains, multiple intermediaries, price asymmetries, and considerable post-harvest losses24,25. Although agricultural cooperatives have been promoted to address these inefficiencies, their outcomes have often been mixed due to governance problems, weak management, and politically driven structural changes that have limited their effectiveness and farmer empowerment26,27. Consequently, participation in many cooperatives remains limited and largely nominal, creating a gap between the theoretical objectives of cooperatives and their practical outcomes28,29.

The gap between the intended benefits of marketing networks and their actual performance in regions such as Fars Province highlights important research gaps that require systematic investigation. Previous studies have largely treated participation as a simple dichotomous variable, overlooking differences in the intensity and quality of engagement30,31. In addition, much of the existing research has focused on isolated determinants rather than using an integrated framework that simultaneously considers economic, socio-psychological, and institutional factors influencing participation behavior. From a theoretical perspective, this reflects a gap in the integration of Transaction Cost Economics, Social Capital Theory, and the Theory of Planned Behavior into a unified explanatory model for participation in agricultural marketing networks. Methodologically, most studies rely on limited or single-method approaches, which restrict the ability to capture the complex and layered nature of participation behavior. Empirically, there is still insufficient evidence from context-specific settings such as Iranian agriculture, particularly Fars Province, where structural and behavioral determinants may differ from those reported in other developing-country contexts. Contextually, the unique socio-political and institutional structure of Iranian agricultural systems remains underexplored, particularly in explaining how constraints such as trust deficits, weak institutional support, and limited access to information jointly influence participation decisions. Most importantly, the unique socio-political context of Iranian agriculture has not adequately been documented and analyzed in terms of barriers to farmer participation, so we do not even understand if a lack of access to, or constraints of any type involving economic, social, informational, or institutional factors, is the most limiting factor, and therefore, food system interventions will continue as has been done for years in a trial-and-error manner. More importantly, existing literature has not clearly explained how socio-psychological mechanisms (such as trust, norms, and perceived control) interact with structural constraints to shape participation decisions, leaving an important explanatory gap in understanding farmer behavior. Accordingly, this study advances the literature by providing an integrated model that simultaneously captures economic, institutional, and psychological determinants within a single explanatory framework, thereby moving beyond fragmented and single-theory approaches commonly used in prior research. Accordingly, this study is guided by the following central research questions: (1) What socioeconomic, institutional, psychological, and social factors influence farmers’ intention to participate in agricultural marketing networks in Fars Province? (2) How do trust, social networks, attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control interact to shape farmers’ participation intentions within the proposed integrated conceptual framework? and (3) What are the major context-specific barriers that hinder farmers’ participation in marketing networks, and how can these barriers be incorporated into a behavioral model explaining participation intention? Importantly, the identified socioeconomic and institutional factors are conceptually grounded in Transaction Cost Economics and represent structural and contextual constraints shaping participation conditions, while attitudinal and psychological factors are theoretically embedded within the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), particularly through constructs such as attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. Accordingly, the study ensures full alignment between its empirical objectives and the integrated conceptual framework, where all variables are systematically derived from and operationalized within the proposed theoretical model.

Therefore, to address these significant gaps in research, this study aims to comprehensively identify and analyze the determinants and barriers affecting farmers’ participation in marketing networks in Fars Province. Specifically, it seeks to empirically examine socioeconomic, institutional, and attitudinal determinants within an integrated theoretical framework, and to develop and test a conceptual model that explains participation behavior in a context-specific setting. By providing a nuanced understanding of participation dynamics, this study seeks to generate actionable insights for refining policies and development programs, ultimately helping to bridge the gap between the theoretical promise of collective marketing and its practical realization in improving farmer welfare and strengthening Iran’s agricultural sector.

This research purpose is to contribute to the academic literature and practical policy-making in multiple ways. The study derives and tests a new integrated conceptual model to frame the constructs of Transaction Cost Economics, Social Capital theory and the Theory of Planned behavior in a single analytical framework. Importantly, this study does not treat these theories as parallel or loosely connected perspectives; rather, it explicitly integrates Social Capital Theory with the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) into a unified explanatory framework. In this integration, Social Capital Theory provides the structural and relational foundations of behavior (e.g., trust and social networks), while TPB explains how these socially embedded factors are cognitively internalized into attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, which in turn shape behavioral intention. From an environmental psychology perspective, this integration is particularly important because it conceptualizes farmer participation behavior as a psychologically mediated response shaped by both environmental context and social interaction. In other words, farmers’ intentions toward marketing network participation are understood not only through economic reasoning but also through psychosocial evaluations, behavioral perceptions, and socially constructed experiences. The research is an improvement over reductive approaches that provide a singular explanation of participation behavior in marketing networks by pulling together economic, social, psychological and institutional variables into one framework. More specifically, trust (a core dimension of social capital) is theorized to influence attitude and intention by shaping farmers’ expectations of institutional reliability, while social networks (another key dimension of social capital) function both as direct behavioral drivers and as antecedents to subjective norms within the TPB structure. This creates a coherent causal chain in which social structures (social capital) influence cognitive mechanisms (TPB constructs), ultimately determining behavioral intention. This multi-theoretical approach represents an advancement over earlier studies that relied on single theoretical frameworks. In addition, this study is among the first to provide a comprehensive mixed-methods examination of marketing network participation in the strategically important but under-researched context of Fars Province, Iran. It offers a grounded understanding of the institutional and cultural conditions shaping agricultural behavior, thereby addressing a key gap in the regional literature. Furthermore, the findings have clear practical implications for stakeholders. Overall, the study highlights the importance of collective action and provides practical pathways for strengthening farmer participation in marketing networks, improving market access, and enhancing rural economic resilience, with broader implications for agricultural development and food security in Iran.

Theoretical framework

Theory of planned behavior

The primary theoretical framework for this study is the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), a widely affirmed social-psychological perspective of human behavior by Icek Ajzen32 that describes and predicts a person’s intention to exhibit a behavior. TPB states that the most immediate precursor to actual behavior, behavioral intention, is determined by attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control32,33. In relation to this study, the specific behavior relates to whether a farmer participates in agricultural marketing networks (such as cooperatives or producer associations34,35). Attitude reflects the extent to which a farmer views that participation positively or negatively. It is shaped by farmers’ beliefs regarding the expected outcomes and overall desirability of participating in agricultural marketing networks36,37. Subjective Norms measures the perceived social pressure related to other important referents such as family members, peer farmers, community leaders, or agricultural extension agents to join or stay out of these marketing networks. According to Ajzen32, subjective norms refer to an individual’s perceived social pressure from significant others regarding whether a particular behavior should or should not be performed38. In the context of the present study, subjective norms are conceptualized as farmers’ perceptions of social expectations and approval from influential actors within their social environment regarding participation in agricultural marketing networks. This framework acknowledges that decisions around farming typically occur in a social context, where the views and actions of others are among the most salient influences39. This framework acknowledges that decisions around farming typically occur in a social context, where the views and actions of others are among the most salient influences39. Finally, Perceived Behavioral Control (PBC) is the farmer’s perception of the ease or difficulty of taking action, including their evaluation of personal considerations (i.e., knowledge, skills, resources) as well as evaluation of social or institutional obstacles (i.e., cost, logistical issues, etc.)40. Within the TPB, a farmer should show stronger intention to take action if they hold a positive attitude, perceive strong social normative pressure to do so, and believe they have the capability and opportunity to take action successfully20,41.

While the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) is a strong foundational model, using it to understand complex behaviors within complicated institutional contexts—such as agricultural marketing in Iran—requires adding context-dependent variables to enhance its explanatory power. Therefore, this study proposes an extended TPB model that includes three additional constructs that were found to be important from the literature and from our qualitative findings (Fig. 1): knowledge, trust, and social networks. These constructs are included to better reflect the contextual realities of agricultural marketing systems, particularly in rural Iran, where informational access, institutional relationships, and interpersonal interactions are widely discussed as relevant aspects of farmers’ decision-making environments. Their inclusion allows the framework to more comprehensively capture the broader social, informational, and institutional setting in which participation in marketing networks occurs.Fig. 1Theoretical framework of the study (+ and − show the direction of the hypothesis).

Importantly, although some constructs appear conceptually related, they are operationalized in this study as distinct theoretical domains. Specifically, “social networks” refers to the structural and interactional characteristics of farmers’ interpersonal ties (e.g., density of connections and frequency of communication), whereas “subjective norms” capture the internalized perception of social pressure arising from those networks. Thus, social networks are treated as an exogenous structural antecedent, while subjective norms represent a cognitive evaluative outcome of that structure. This separation reduces conceptual redundancy and allows empirical testing of network-to-norm transmission mechanisms rather than overlapping constructs.

Knowledge about the functions, advantages, and processes of the marketing networks is viewed as an important precursor to forming realistic attitudes and a realistic sense of behavioral control; if a farmer does not have knowledge about an institution, they cannot form a positive attitude toward by it. Trust, trust in the management and operating integrity of the marketing networks, is expected to be a direct determinant of both attitude and PBC as well. High levels of trust lower perceived risks, leading to a more favorable attitude, while also reducing perceived barriers relating to opportunism and unfair treatment, and thereby reinforcing PBC. These constructs are included because they are frequently highlighted in prior studies and in the qualitative findings as relevant elements of farmers’ engagement with agricultural marketing systems. In particular, knowledge is commonly discussed in relation to farmers’ awareness of institutional processes, trust is associated with perceptions of reliability and fairness in organizational arrangements, and social networks are often described as the relational context through which information and experiences are shared in rural communities.

Although trust and attitude are related in that both involve evaluative judgments, they are conceptually distinct in this study. Attitude reflects a farmer’s overall behavioral evaluation of participation (benefits vs. drawbacks), whereas trust represents a relational belief regarding the integrity, reliability, and fairness of the institutions managing the marketing networks. Trust therefore operates as an antecedent belief that shapes attitude formation rather than duplicating it. This distinction follows prior work in institutional and collective action literature, where trust is treated as a structural-relational belief system rather than a general evaluative disposition. Additionally, the existing structure of the farmer’s Social Networks is a proposed antecedent of subjective norms, as it is a means by which normative beliefs and social pressures are communicated and reinforced. In this model, Social Networks is specified as an exogenous predictor of Subjective Norms (H8), reflecting the theoretical assumption that exposure to peers and influential actors shapes perceived social expectations. This specification avoids construct redundancy by distinguishing observable social structure (networks) from perceived normative pressure (subjective norms), thereby reducing shared conceptual variance and improving discriminant validity.

Although the empirical model is operationalized primarily as an extended Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), the overall conceptual framework is theoretically grounded in three complementary perspectives: TPB, Social Capital Theory, and Transaction Cost Economics (TCE). Specifically, TPB serves as the central behavioral prediction mechanism explaining how attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control shape intention. Social Capital Theory is incorporated through the constructs of trust and social networks, which represent relational resources embedded in farmers’ social structures and are modeled as key antecedents of TPB components (particularly attitude and subjective norms). Transaction Cost Economics (TCE) underpins the inclusion of socioeconomic and institutional constraints and perceived barriers, conceptualizing them as transaction-related frictions (e.g., information asymmetry, opportunism risk, and coordination costs) that reduce perceived behavioral control and increase perceived difficulty of participation. Accordingly, the proposed framework is not a purely TPB extension, but a theoretically integrated model in which TPB functions as the central explanatory mechanism, while Social Capital Theory and TCE provide the structural and institutional foundations that shape TPB constructs.

Hypothesis development

H1

Effect of attitude on intention to participation in agricultural marketing.

According to the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), the main precursor to the intention to carry out a behavior is a positive attitude towards that behavior42. An attitude is defined as a person’s overall positive or negative evaluation of performing a behavior that is based upon their beliefs about the likely consequences of performing that behavior43–45. Regarding agricultural marketing, a farmer with a strong positive attitude believes that participating in a marketing network will produce positive consequences such as higher and more stable prices, reduced power of intermediaries, and increased access to marketing information46,47. This appraisal of the likely consequences provides a motivation to carry out the behavior48. In addition, empirical research provides strong evidence for this relationship. Several studies (e.g46,49,50.) that assessed farmers’ intentions to use innovative marketing channels found that farmers’ positive attitudes were one of the primary predictors of their behavioral intention. Because of this, we hypothesize that farmers that have more positive attitudes towards marketing networks will have stronger intentions to engage with them.

H2

Effect of subjective norms on intention to participation in agricultural marketing.

Subjective norms, an essential concept of the classical TPB framework, are defined as the perceived social pressure and expectations from significant others to participate in, or refrain from, a specific behavior51. Consistent with the original TPB framework proposed by Ajzen32, the present study operationalizes subjective norms primarily through the injunctive dimension (perceived social approval), rather than the descriptive dimension (perceptions of others’ actual behavior). When considering farmer participation in marketing networks, subjective norms are particularly noteworthy in rural agriculture communities where decisions are often made amidst a web of social networks to which farm families are often intricately bound. In these situations, the beliefs of family members, close friends, community members, and extension agents are particularly impactful when a farmer decides to participate or not34,52. In the present study, subjective norms are conceptualized specifically through the injunctive dimension, referring to farmers’ perceptions that important referent groups—such as family members, peers, community leaders, and extension agents—approve or support participation in agricultural marketing networks. Therefore, farmers who perceive stronger social approval from significant others are expected to demonstrate stronger behavioral intentions toward participation53. Subjective norms take on even more significance to this study as networking is a group to movement away from more traditional, individual marketing. Subjective norms may also translate into behavioral intent for marketing networks, as seen in Xu et al.54 and Chang et al.55 studies focused on contract farming, and in Xu and Mu56 research on cooperative membership and subjective norms on farmers’ intent to adopt new marketing practices. Accordingly, the hypothesis for this study is stronger subjective norms will lead to greater intent to participate in agricultural marketing networks.

H3

Effect of perceived behavioral control on intention to participation in agricultural marketing.

Perceived behavioral control (PBC) refers to how an individual perceives the easiness and/or difficulty to enact the behavior based upon their assessment of internal capacities and external constraints. In this study, PBC is conceptualized as farmers’ perceived self-efficacy and controllability regarding participation in agricultural marketing networks, reflecting both their confidence in their abilities and their perception of available facilitating conditions. Within the framework of the theory of planned behavior (TPB) PBC is assumed to influence behavioral intention; Otherwise stated, when individuals perceive that they possess the resources, knowledge, and opportunities and can manage or overcome perceived obstacles, they may possess stronger intention to act57,58. For example, for a farmer they would assess their ability to meet quality and volume criteria of a network, manage membership, manage costs, and underwriting logistics59. In empirical studies, perceived behavioral control (which has been called self-efficacy in some studies has shown to play an important role in agricultural decision making60,61. For example, perceived control in the process of participation in formal markets has shown to be a salient explanation of intention and has mitigated perceived risk. Therefore, we hypothesize that greater perceived behavioral control will positively relate to farmer intention to participate in marketing networks.

H4

Effect of trust on intention to participation in agricultural marketing.

Trust is commonly defined as the belief that an institution or organization will act in a reliable, fair, and competent manner62,63. Within agricultural marketing systems, trust has been widely discussed as an important relational mechanism that reduces uncertainty and perceived risks associated with collective marketing arrangements and institutional cooperation. Prior studies in agricultural marketing and collective action contexts have emphasized that farmers’ trust in organizations, managers, and market actors is closely associated with willingness to engage in coordinated economic activities64. In the present study, trust is conceptualized as farmers’ perceptions regarding the integrity, reliability, transparency, and fairness of agricultural marketing networks and their management structures. Although trust was not initially part of the TPB framework, it is included in this research as a significant determinant of intent, especially within the context of relational contracts and collective institutions62. Trust can be defined as the expectation a farmer has about the integrity, honesty, and competence of the marketing network and those who manage it. The more trust a farmer has, the lower the perception of the potential risk posed by opportunism, which could be in the form of delays in payment, grading of quality that is not equitably or consistently applied, or even financial mismanagement. When farmers trust an institution, their intent to sign up to enjoy the promised benefits of participation is likely to increase. Research and evidence from Giampietri et al.64 and Canova et al.62 regarding marketing of organic foods suggests that trust is an important determinant of intent, sometimes over and above economic calculations. Therefore, we posit that greater trust in the marketing network will have a direct and positive effect on farmers intent to participate.

H5

Effect of social networks on intention to participation in agricultural marketing.

Social networks refer to the set of social relationships, interpersonal ties, and communication interactions through which individuals exchange information, experiences, and social support65. In this study, social networks are conceptually defined as the structural configuration of farmers’ interpersonal relationships, including the density of ties, frequency of interaction, and access to information-rich or influential actors within rural communities. In rural and agricultural settings, social networks are widely recognized as important channels for knowledge diffusion, peer learning, and normative influence. Previous studies have shown that farmers’ interactions with peers, local leaders, extension agents, and community members significantly shape perceptions and behavioral decisions related to innovation adoption and market participation66,67. In this study, social networks are conceptualized as the structural and interactional characteristics of farmers’ relationships, including frequency of interaction, connectedness, and access to influential actors within rural communities. Social networks, defined as the collection of social relationships and interactions that farmers possess, are expected to have a direct impact on intention. Networks that are denser and more interconnected allow information and experiences to spread quickly about marketing networks65. However, the influence of social networks on behavioral intention in this study is considered indirect, primarily operating through subjective norms rather than directly through imitation effects. Specifically, social networks shape farmers’ perceptions of social expectations and approval, which are captured in the subjective norms construct. Accordingly, the mechanism “watching others succeed directly incites intention” is not interpreted as a direct effect, but rather as a normative learning process reflected in descriptive beliefs that contribute to subjective norms formation. This distinction ensures conceptual alignment with the TPB framework and avoids conflating structural exposure with cognitive intention formation. Thus, farmers who observe peers benefiting from participation may experience stronger perceived social expectations and validation within their networks, rather than automatic behavioral intention formation68. Additionally, it has been shown that peer effects have an element of social influence and network embeddedness that influence behavioral intention of farmers (see66,67,69). Thus, it is hypothesized, that farmers with more dense and connected social networks will demonstrate a stronger increased intention to participate in agricultural marketing.

H6

Effect of knowledge on intention to participation in agricultural marketing.

Knowledge generally refers to an individual’s awareness, understanding, and familiarity with the characteristics, processes, and expected outcomes of a particular behavior or institution70. In agricultural research, knowledge has frequently been identified as a critical informational resource influencing farmers’ evaluations of new technologies, organizational arrangements, and market opportunities. Existing literature suggests that improved access to accurate and relevant information reduces uncertainty and enhances informed decision-making among farmers71,72. In the context of the present study, knowledge is conceptualized as farmers’ understanding of agricultural marketing networks, including their operational procedures, membership requirements, governance structures, benefits, and associated obligations. Knowledge is considered an important precursor to intention, operating on the idea that a person cannot intend to carry out an action that they do not comprehensively understand. In this case, knowledge means a farmer’s awareness and understanding of how marketing networks work, how they operate and are operated, the benefits for members, costs to members, and governance70,73. Good knowledge means that misinformation, inaccuracies, and misjudgments are minimized, allowing for a more reasonable assessment of the anticipated outcomes and requirements of the behavior. This clarity reaffirms the farmers intention since ambiguity and asymmetry of information are known impediments to decision-making in agricultural systems. Evidence is provided in studies by Tama et al.72, Sarma et al.71, and Dilleen et al.70, which demonstrate that interventions that focus on knowledge increased farmers intentions when cognitive based knowledge was provided to improve the foundations of informed choice. In sum, a higher level of knowledge about marketing networks is hypothesized to lead to an increased intention of participation.

H7

Effect of trust on attitude to participation in agricultural marketing.

This hypothesis outlines a pathway where trust indirectly effects intention, by affecting a farmer’s underlying attitude. Trust is an important element of the cognitive base of an attitude. If a farmer has distrust in the management of a marketing network, he/she is likely to have a negative attitude toward participation in the network, expecting negative outcomes of participation, such as being exploited or losing something of value7,74. On the other hand, high trust develops a positive attitude as it causes the farmer to expect fair treatment, transparency and mutual benefit. The farmer’s belief that the network is trustworthy and acting in their interest will make participation seem more favorable and less risky17. This fits with the TPB, specifically the behavioral beliefs. In this case, trust effects the assessment of likely outcomes. Trust is well established as an antecedent to positive attitudes in studies of consumer behavior and organizational psychology63,75. Therefore, it is hypothesized that trust will have significant positive effects on farms’ attitudes toward participation.

H8

Effect of social networks on social norms to participation in agricultural marketing.

This hypothesis directly connects a farmer’s structural social environment to their subjective norms perception of social pressure around marketing networks. Social networks are important pathways for communicating and reinforcing normative information. Within social networks, farmers see how their peers, family, and local leaders feel about marketing networks76. Social networks that are dense and active increase subjective norms because they make these social norms more pronounced, visible (and possibly internalized)77. When it is common in a farmers’ social network to engage in conversations around the advantages and disadvantages of joining a marketing network, there increased feelings of subjective norms will occur78. Social learning theory and empirical work in rural sociology suggest this connection, as normative perception largely arises and is maintained through social relations and networks in which persons are directly involved. Thus, it is hypothesized that the strength and proliferation (or type) of farmer social networks will have a substantive positive effect on their perception of subjective norms toward marketing network participation.

H9

The effect of perceived barriers to participation on intention.

In addition to the psychosocial aspects of the TPB, this study adds contextual and institutional barriers surfaced in qualitative research. Perceived barriers in this study are conceptually grounded in established behavioral theories, including the Health Belief Model (HBM), Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), and Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), which emphasize perceived obstacles, costs, and risks as key determinants of behavioral avoidance. The thematic analysis suggested a range of individual, yet interrelated, barriers to engagement that included things like costs involved, distrust in organizations, and social expectations. However, to ensure conceptual accuracy, these barriers are now reframed as perceived structural, institutional, and informational constraints rather than loosely defined thematic categories. It is hypothesized that these barriers together will inform a farmer’s overall awareness of barriers to engagement. Following the mixed-methods research design, the qualitative themes were transformed into a quantitative “Perceived Barriers” construct. Based on the theory of perceived behavioral control, it is hypothesized that a stronger cumulative perception of the barriers will lead to lower motivation to engage in the activity, as the weight of each barrier make participation less plausible and less desirable. This is to test the direct negative effect of the context-specific constraints on behavioral intention.

Methodology

Research design

The research utilized an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design, which is a two-phase research design where an initial qualitative data collection and analysis phase is followed by a quantitative phase. This design is particularly appropriate when the aim is to explore a phenomenon in-depth first and then build upon those insights to develop instruments and test relationships quantitatively. This study was designed in this manner and was especially suited to a research problem where initial engaging and in-depth exploration was needed to discover key variables and context specific experiences not represented in prior literature before exploring a larger number to engage more broadly and examine the relationships among the variables and their explanatory power in the quantitative phase. In line with the exploratory sequential approach, the qualitative phase preceded the quantitative phase and directly informed the development of the measurement instrument and conceptual model tested in the second phase. The first exploratory qualitative phase involved in-depth, semi-structured interviews with a purposeful group of farmers to elicit the specific lived experiences and barriers they perceived related to marketing network participation in Fars Province. The outcomes of this phase were the themes and the construction of barrier constructs which in turn informed the development of the quantitative instrument, including the closed-ended questionnaire, ensuring it was rooted in the local reality, and represented issues relevant to farmers. The second, quantitative phase was confirmatory in nature, involving the administration of a structured questionnaire to a large, randomly selected sample of farmers, with the primary objective of statistically testing the hypothesized model derived from an integrated theoretical framework and quantitatively assessing the distribution and intensity of the barriers identified in the qualitative phase, as well as estimating the structural relationships among constructs. Thus, the study follows a clear exploratory-to-confirmatory sequence, consistent with an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design rather than an explanatory design. Furthermore, to ensure transparency and methodological rigor, detailed descriptions of participant recruitment procedures for both qualitative and quantitative phases are provided in Sections “Qualitative phase: Instrument development and barrier identification” and “Quantitative phase: Questionnaire design and sampling”, including sampling strategies, selection criteria, and construction of the sampling frame. The qualitative phase was exclusively used for the development of the “perceived barriers” construct because barriers to participation in agricultural marketing networks are highly context-specific, institutionally embedded, and not fully captured by existing standardized scales derived from prior behavioral theories. Therefore, an inductive, data-driven approach was required to ensure that the measurement items reflected the lived realities, language, and structural constraints experienced by farmers in the study area, rather than imposing pre-established external constructs. This approach ensures content validity and contextual sensitivity of the barriers construct within the extended TPB framework.

This study is situated in Fars Province, Iran, which represents one of the country’s most important agricultural regions characterized by diverse cropping systems, smallholder-dominated farming structures, and increasing policy emphasis on collective marketing and cooperative-based agricultural development. The selection of this case study area is theoretically and practically justified because farmers in this region face persistent structural challenges such as fragmented supply chains, limited bargaining power, and uneven access to formal marketing institutions. At the same time, Fars Province provides a suitable empirical context for examining participation in agricultural marketing networks due to the coexistence of traditional informal marketing channels and emerging formal cooperative structures. Therefore, this setting offers a rich and context-sensitive environment for analyzing behavioral, social, and institutional determinants of farmers’ participation decisions, making it highly appropriate for testing the extended Theory of Planned Behavior model developed in this study.

Qualitative phase: instrument development and barrier identification

The first qualitative phase was geared towards a deep, contextual understanding of the barriers farmers face. A semi-structured interview protocol was created with open-ended questions and probes to elicit rich, narrative data on their experiences related to participation in agricultural marketing networks, with a specific focus on identifying perceived barriers. These prompts were used as exploratory entry points to surface barrier-related experiences rather than to develop separate constructs for analysis and the rationale for their decision to participate in or forgo participation in these networks. In addition to guiding data collection, this qualitative phase served as the primary basis for item generation. Specifically, the development of measurement items followed an inductive approach, whereby statements were directly derived from the themes and sub-themes identified during the coding process. Each construct and its corresponding items were grounded in verbatim expressions and recurrent patterns observed in the interview data, ensuring strong contextual relevance and theoretical alignment. Importantly, “knowledge of marketing networks” and “perceived benefits/disadvantages” were not treated as independent constructs in the qualitative analysis. Instead, they were used as contextual discussion domains to help participants articulate underlying barriers (e.g., informational constraints, perceived risks, and access limitations). This approach ensured that only barriers emerged as analytic categories, thereby avoiding conceptual overlap or redundancy between constructs and indicators.

Participant selection for this phase utilized a combination of purposive and snowball sampling to ensure a range of perspectives. Initially, purposive sampling was employed to identify key informants, including both members and non-members of various marketing networks (i.e., cooperatives, producer associations) from different counties in Fars Province that also represented variation in farm size, crop type, and socioeconomic status. Then, snowball sampling was used to ask initial participants to identify other farmers who could provide relevant data, in order to also reach hidden populations. Specifically, purposive sampling ensured inclusion of farmers with diverse levels of engagement in marketing networks (full members, partial members, and non-members), while snowball sampling enabled access to less visible or less formally connected farmers through peer referrals, thereby improving the diversity and depth of experiential perspectives captured. Data collection continued until theoretical saturation was reached (i.e., after 32 interviews) or the point at which the new data no longer provided new thematic insights or properties to the emerging barrier categories.

More specifically, saturation was assessed iteratively throughout data collection and analysis. After approximately the 27th interview, coding comparisons indicated that no new first-order codes or substantive categories were emerging. Subsequent interviews were conducted primarily to verify and confirm the stability of existing themes rather than to generate new ones. The decision that theoretical saturation had been achieved was based on a structured set of criteria: (1) code saturation, where no new codes emerged in successive interviews; (2) thematic redundancy, where responses consistently replicated previously identified patterns; and (3) analytical sufficiency, where the existing categories were sufficiently rich to explain variation in farmers’ experiences and decision-making contexts. In addition, constant comparison was used throughout the analysis process to ensure that emerging data were continuously compared with previously coded data to verify conceptual stability and completeness.

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using a systematic thematic analysis approach, which involved iterative cycles of open, axial, and selective coding to distill the raw data into coherent themes and sub-themes representing the fundamental barriers to participation. To enhance the rigor of the instrument development process, preliminary items generated from the qualitative analysis were subjected to an expert review process to establish content validity. A panel of five experts in agricultural extension, rural development, and marketing—including two academic researchers and three experienced practitioners—evaluated the relevance, clarity, and representativeness of each item. Based on their feedback, several items were reworded, refined, or eliminated to ensure comprehensive coverage of the constructs and alignment with the study objectives. To enhance the credibility and dependability of the qualitative analysis process, coding and theme development were conducted collaboratively among the research team. The emerging codes, categories, and themes were independently reviewed and compared across researchers, and any discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussion until full agreement was reached. This process of cross-checking and consensus-building ensured consistency in interpretation and strengthened the overall trustworthiness of the qualitative findings.

Quantitative phase: questionnaire design and sampling

To conduct the quantitative phase, a developed, close-ended questionnaire was used that had multiple sections (Table 1). The questionnaire was comprised of three sections: The first section consisted of socio-demographic and farm characteristics (i.e. age, education level, farming experience, farm size, land tenure, annual income, and primary crops); The second section measured the variables of the extended theory of planned behavior model—attitude, subjective norm, perceived behavioral control and intention, alongside the additional variables: Trust, Social Networks and Knowledge. Most of these items were measured on five-point Likert scales (“Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree”). Although several constructs were theoretically informed (e.g., Theory of Planned Behavior), the measurement items were carefully adapted and contextualized based on insights from the qualitative phase to ensure contextual validity. It is important to note that core TPB constructs (i.e., Attitude, Subjective Norms, Perceived Behavioral Control, and Intention) were not entirely developed from scratch; instead, they were primarily adapted from well-established and widely validated TPB scales in the literature, particularly Ajzen’s foundational work, and then carefully modified to fit the agricultural marketing network context of the present study. This ensured both theoretical consistency with prior TPB research and contextual relevance to the study setting. The barriers scale was the third section of the questionnaire, which was developed specifically based on the qualitative phase coded data, which included a number of barrier statements (i.e. “I do not trust the management of the cooperatives,” “The membership fees are excessive for me”, “there is no transparent information regarding payments”)—which the respondents rated based on how much they perceived those barriers were an impediment (1 = ”Not at all an impediment” to 5 = ”A very strong impediment”) (Table 1). Although some barrier items may appear related to the trust construct, they were conceptually operationalized as context-specific participation impediments rather than generalized institutional trust evaluations. In particular, the trust construct measured farmers’ overall confidence in the honesty, fairness, and reliability of marketing networks, whereas the barriers construct captured practical, financial, informational, and managerial obstacles perceived to hinder participation. Therefore, the two constructs were theoretically differentiated and retained separately within the analytical framework. Furthermore, discriminant validity analysis confirmed the empirical distinctiveness of these constructs, indicating the absence of problematic construct redundancy. Furthermore, face validity of the questionnaire was assessed through a pilot test with a small group of farmers (n = 30) from the study area who were not included in the final sample. Participants were asked to evaluate the clarity, wording, and comprehensibility of the items. Minor revisions were made based on their feedback to improve readability, eliminate ambiguity, and ensure that the items were easily understood within the local context. To further ensure methodological rigor, multiple procedures were employed to evaluate the validity and reliability of the questionnaire prior to full-scale data collection. Face validity was assessed through the pilot study involving 30 farmers from the study area, during which respondents evaluated the clarity, readability, wording, and comprehensibility of questionnaire items. Content validity was established through an expert evaluation process in which five specialists in agricultural extension, rural development, and agricultural marketing reviewed the questionnaire for relevance, representativeness, conceptual consistency, and contextual appropriateness of the measurement items. Based on their feedback, several items were refined, reworded, merged, or removed to improve conceptual clarity and alignment with the study objectives. In addition, reliability of the instrument was evaluated during the measurement model assessment stage using internal consistency indicators, including Composite Reliability (CR) and indicator loadings within the PLS-SEM framework. The survey’s target population included all active farmers residing in Fars Province. Based on official statistics obtained from the Agricultural Jihad Organization of Fars Province in 2024 the total population of active farmers was approximately 460,268 at the time of the study. The sampling frame was constructed using updated and comprehensive lists of registered farmers provided by local agricultural extension offices at the village level, which represent the most reliable and accessible database of farmers in the region. These lists were cross-validated with local extension officers to remove inactive or duplicate records, ensuring accuracy of the sampling frame. A multi-stage random sampling technique was used to obtain the population in a representative way: first, several counties of the province were randomly selected; second, villages within each of those selected counties were randomly selected; and finally, from lists of farmers within those selected villages (acquired from agricultural extension offices), 386 farmers were selected as the target sample using a simple random sampling method.

Specifically, the selected counties included Shiraz, Marvdasht, Fasa, Jahrom, and Kazerun, which together represent the major agricultural zones of Fars Province in terms of crop diversity and production intensity. Within these counties, a total of 18 villages were randomly selected as primary sampling clusters to ensure geographic and agro-ecological diversity. To further enhance methodological transparency, the participant recruitment process followed a structured multi-stage protocol. First, counties were randomly selected from the main agricultural regions of Fars Province. Second, villages were selected within each county using proportional random sampling to ensure balanced geographic representation. Third, farmer lists obtained from local agricultural extension offices were used to construct the final sampling frame at village level. Finally, individual farmers were selected using simple random sampling through a random number generator, ensuring that each farmer had an equal probability of selection. Regarding the qualitative phase, participants were drawn from multiple counties (primarily Shiraz, Jahrom, and Fasa) to capture variation in farming systems and marketing experiences. In both the qualitative and quantitative phases, the target population consisted exclusively of male and female farmers actively engaged in agricultural production, as farming in the study region is predominantly a household-based activity involving both genders in varying roles (e.g., land management, production, and marketing decisions). For the qualitative phase interviews (n = 32), both male and female farmers were included to ensure diversity of perspectives. For the quantitative phase, due to cultural norms in rural Fars Province where field enumeration is more appropriately conducted by same-gender interviewers, data collection was carried out by trained male enumerators when interviewing male farmers, while female enumerators assisted in cases where female respondents were interviewed, ensuring cultural sensitivity and respondent comfort during data collection. This sample size was deemed adequate, using Krejcie and Morgan’s79 sample size table for a finite population, along with a non-response rate. Overall, these procedures ensured that the researcher-developed items demonstrated adequate content and face validity prior to large-scale data collection, thereby strengthening the credibility and robustness of the measurement instrument. The data collection process for the crucial quantitative phase was carried out by a group of trained enumerators who were well-versed in the agricultural characteristics of the local context and research objectives. These enumerators individually collected, through a structured questionnaire, data from the selected farmers in their respective communities, a process designed for clarification, rapport-building, and to maximize response rates that could be used in the analysis of the data collected. This process led to the submission of 332 completed and usable questionnaires from an initial distribution of 386 completed questionnaires, thus achieving an extremely high response rate of approximately 86 percent which limits any concerns regarding non-response bias, while also supporting the generalizability of the quantitative findings within the specific sampling frame. To further assess potential non-response bias, a comparison was conducted between early and late respondents on key demographic variables (e.g., age, education level, and farm size) and principal study constructs. The results indicated no statistically significant differences between these groups, suggesting that non-response bias is unlikely to have affected the study results.

Analytical procedures

The quantitative data was analyzed in detail and in sequenced stages, utilizing SPSS (version 28) for preliminary descriptive analysis, followed by an advanced multivariate analysis using Smart-PLS (version 3) to test the complex theoretical model. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was selected as the principal analytical approach because the study sought to simultaneously examine multiple interrelated latent constructs and estimate both measurement and structural relationships within a single comprehensive framework. Compared with first-generation statistical techniques such as multiple regression analysis, SEM provides several methodological advantages, including the ability to account for measurement error, test complex causal relationships among latent variables, and evaluate direct and indirect relationships simultaneously. Furthermore, SEM enables the concurrent assessment of the reliability and validity of measurement instruments alongside the evaluation of the structural model, thereby providing a more rigorous and holistic analytical framework for theory testing. In the present study, Partial Least Squares SEM (PLS-SEM) was considered particularly appropriate because the research emphasized prediction and theory extension, incorporated multiple latent constructs, and included researcher-developed measures derived from the qualitative phase. Additionally, PLS-SEM is well suited for exploratory and predictive research contexts and does not require strict assumptions regarding multivariate normality. PLS-SEM was further preferred over covariance-based SEM (CB-SEM) due to its methodological suitability for the present study design. Specifically, the objective of this research is prediction-oriented rather than strict theory confirmation, making PLS-SEM more appropriate. Moreover, the model includes multiple complex relationships among latent constructs and incorporates both established and newly developed measurement scales derived from the qualitative phase, which aligns better with the composite-based nature of PLS-SEM. In addition, the data characteristics do not require strict multivariate normality assumptions, and PLS-SEM is more robust under such conditions. Finally, PLS-SEM offers higher statistical power for detecting relationships in complex predictive models, which is particularly important in examining behavioral and institutional determinants of farmers’ participation decisions. In line with methodological recommendations by80,81, the evaluation of the PLS-SEM results followed a systematic two-step procedure, including a comprehensive assessment of both the measurement model and the structural model. This ensures full methodological rigor and transparent reporting of model quality.

In relation to Ajzen et al.’82 TACT framework, the questionnaire operationalization ensures full alignment of Target, Action, Context, and Time. The Target refers to individual farmers in Fars Province, while the Context refers to agricultural marketing networks such as cooperatives and producer associations. The Time dimension reflects the present and near-future farming and marketing cycle, as captured through intention-based items. Importantly, the Action component is consistently defined as a single unified behavior, namely “participation in agricultural marketing networks (e.g., joining and engaging in cooperatives or producer associations).” To avoid conceptual ambiguity, related behaviors such as learning about marketing networks, meeting quality requirements, or selling through networks were not treated as separate actions,instead, they were modeled as antecedent beliefs or enabling factors within constructs such as knowledge and perceived behavioral control. This ensures strict conceptual consistency across all TPB measures and avoids mixing heterogeneous behavioral outcomes within the same construct. This clarification also directly addresses potential overlap between Social Networks (SON) and Subjective Norms (SN). Specifically, SON items were operationalized to capture structural and relational embeddedness within farmers’ interpersonal communication and information exchange networks (e.g., frequency of interaction and information sharing), whereas SN items reflect perceived social pressure and normative expectations regarding participation in marketing networks. Accordingly, although both constructs involve social context, SON represents network structure and interaction patterns, while SN captures normative influence, ensuring conceptual and empirical distinctiveness between the two constructs.

Analytical analysis included a multi-stage analytical process that began with descriptive statistics to characterize the socio-demographic and farm characteristics of the sample. However, the strategic enactment of the analysis was centered around the subsequent analysis of the statistical modeling of Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) through Smart-PLS 3. This SEM method based on variance was chosen due to its ability to be applied to models of predictive research, its capacity to manage very complex models with high numbers of latent constructs, and it is nonrestrictive with respect to data distribution assumptions. The PLS-SEM Process was conducted we conducted using two phases as recommended. First, the measurement model (outer model) was evaluated following Hair et al.80,81, including indicator reliability (factor loadings), internal consistency reliability (Composite Reliability and Cronbach’s Alpha), convergent validity (Average Variance Extracted, AVE), and discriminant validity using both Fornell-Larcker criterion and HTMT ratio. Additionally, collinearity diagnostics (VIF values) were examined to ensure absence of multicollinearity among indicators, as recommended in advanced PLS-SEM reporting guidelines. Next, the structural model (inner model) was evaluated. This included the coefficient of determination (R2) for endogenous constructs, effect size (f2) to assess the contribution of each predictor, and predictive relevance (Q2) using the blindfolding procedure. Furthermore, path coefficients (β values) were assessed alongside bootstrapping (5,00 resamples) to determine statistical significance, and model fit was additionally examined using SRMR as a global fit indicator, consistent with recent reporting standards in PLS-SEM literature.

Results

The socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents were generally consistent with previous agricultural studies conducted in Fars Province, suggesting that the sample reasonably reflects the broader farming population of the region. For example, the average age of respondents in this study was 47.6 years, which falls within the range reported in earlier studies on farmers in Fars Province (approximately 41–53 years)83. Similarly, around 68% of the respondents were small- and medium-scale farmers, which is broadly consistent with regional reports indicating that a large proportion of farmers in Fars Province are smallholders84. The average farming experience of respondents was 21.8 years, closely matching findings from prior studies reporting averages of approximately 22–23 years83. In terms of education, most respondents had primary to secondary education levels, which also aligns with the educational patterns reported in previous agricultural research in the province. Overall, these similarities indicate that the sample does not substantially differ from the target farming population, reducing concerns about sampling bias and supporting the representativeness of the study findings.

Qualitative phase: thematic analysis and instrument development

Data collected from the 32 in-depth interviews were analyzed using a systematic thematic analysis approach developed by Braun and Clarke85. The analysis entailed going through iterative cycles of open, axial, and selective coding to condense the rich, narrative data into an organized set of themes and sub-themes that represent the core barriers to farmer engagement in marketing networks. The results from that phase provided a critical framework for constructing the quantitative instrument that was contextually relevant to the local development and expanding farmers’ viewpoints from Fars Province.

Open coding: identifying specific barrier constructs

The first step of the analysis involved open coding, the process of breaking apart the qualitative data to determine concepts and their attributes (Table 2). This meant reading each line of the transcripts of the interviews to label separate moments in which farmers described a barrier to their involvement. This resulted in a full list of initial codes that centered the participants’ particular and often minute concerns. For example, the statement, “the cooperative leaders won’t show us the accounts,” was coded, “Lack of Financial Transparency”, while statements about “having to pay a large sum upfront that I don’t have” were coded, “High Membership Fees.” As a result, connections remained close between the concepts that emerged in the coding process to the lived experience of farmers (Table 2). From this, there were 18 different items of barriers identified, which included economic pressures, institutional distrust, social pressures, and psychological barriers. These items formed the foundation of the quantitative barrier scale, maintaining a close relationship between the rich qualitative narratives and the subsequent statistical analysis. Axial coding: organizing concepts into coherent categories

Following open coding, the analysis progressed to the axial coding stage, which involved reassembling the data in new ways by making connections between the initial codes (Table 3). The aim was to categorize the discrete open codes into larger, more abstract categories and subcategories of them based upon their common characteristics and underlying dimensions. This process illustrated the structural relationships between barriers identified. For instance, the open codes “High Membership Fees” and “Small Farm Size” were grouped together under the subcategory titled “Financial Constraints,” which was also included under the broader axial category “Economic Barriers.” Codes such as “Lack of Transparency,” “Distrust in Leaders,” and “Complex Bureaucracy”, were grouped similarly under the category of “Internal Governance & Trust,” and this was included under the overall theme of “Institutional Barriers.” This process of grouping barriers resulted in a transition from an abbreviated list of 18 problems to a much more sophisticated depiction of multi layers problems. Of note during this process, it was clear that farmers did not have barriers in isolation, but rather, they encountered evolving clusters of different challenges. The axial coding provided a frame of reference with four major categories—Economic, Institutional, Social & Informational, and Psychological Barriers—each with defining subcategories. Selective coding: establishing the core narrative

The last step of the qualitative analysis was selective coding, which focused on bringing together and refining the categories to determine one overarching phenomenon that best represented the complete data. The intent was to create a core narrative, or “storyline,” to accurately reflect the ability to rationally and entirely connect all axial categories. After systematically addressing the relationships of Economic Viability, Institutional Trust, Social Influence, and Information Asymmetry axial categories, a central theme of a core narrative emerged. The core story that explained farmer participation behavior was: “The decision to participate in marketing networks is fundamentally determined by a calculus of Perceived Institutional Trust and Economic Viability, mediated by negative past experiences, strong social connections to traditional markets, and a fundamental lack of actionable information.” The core story indicates that a positive cost–benefit analysis is necessary for participation, but not sufficient. The economic calculus is fundamentally mediated by the farmer’s perceived institutional trust. Any lack of transparency, governance, or past negative experiences erodes it to the point of reasonable consideration even for a profitable opportunity. This essential distrust is then sustained and reproduced in the farmer’s social environment, where the pressures from traditional traders and a dearth of observable stories of success among peers create a normative bias away from engagement, while at the same time, evidence-based decisions are constrained by a lack of available clear, trustworthy information. This particular selective coding outcome provides the ultimate theoretical narrative for the entire study, positioning Perceived Institutional Trust and its social-informational antecedents as the primary mechanism of explanation.

The convergent validity of a construct indicates the extent to which a construct converges in its indicators. In this study, it was evaluated through the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) and outer loadings. Several constructs, specifically Intention (AVE = 0.70), Knowledge (AVE = 0.75), Social Networks (AVE = 0.75), and Subjective Norms (AVE = 0.70), exceeded the 0.50 threshold for AVE, indicating that more than half of the indicators’ variance is explained by the construct. Similarly, nearly all indicator loadings were above the 0.70 threshold for convergent validity. This provides evidence of acceptable convergent validity for most constructs in the model. All indicators exhibit outer VIF values well below the conservative cutoff of 5 and mostly below 3, indicating collinearity among the indicators is not a concern, which is important for obtaining stable and meaningful parameter estimates in the next stage of analysis for the structural model. Thus, multicollinearity does not pose a methodological issue in the measurement model.

While the model as a whole is good, a more detailed analysis of the general relational strength between constructs illustrates certain strengths and some minor weaknesses in the measurement of certain constructs. The Knowledge, Social Networks, and Subjective Norms constructs were measured well—as evidenced by their high-reliability scores (CR > 0.90) and excellent convergent validity (AVE = 0.75). For instance, the very high loadings for KNW3 (0.92) and the consistent loadings for all Social Network items (ranging from 0.81 to 0.86) indicates that these scales are incredibly precise and reliable. However, two constructs fall slightly short of ideal statistical benchmarks, still remaining acceptable and reliable for exploratory study. The construct of Perceived Behavioral Control (PBC) has an AVE of 0.52, which exceeds the 0.50 threshold, but notably one of its indicators (PBC5) has a loading of only 0.57, which is considered below the stringent cut-off of 0.70. This indicates that this item (e.g., PBC5, stated as a reverse-coded item, “A lack of information is a major barrier”) may not be as good a reflection of the PBC construct as the other items, and consideration should be given to improving this item for future studies. Similar to the PBC construct, Attitude and Trust constructs have AVEs of 0.57 and 0.56 which are acceptable, but imply a relatively higher degree of variance is shared within the indicator that does not share variance with the construct. For example, the ATT1 and ATT3 indicators only have loadings of 0.68, and as such indicate they are acceptable and worthy of possible enhancement. Despite the previously observed issues of less than ideal AVE and loading scores, the satisfactory results of their respective composite reliability scores indicated that the set of items together reliably measured their intended concepts. Overall, these results indicate acceptable measurement quality with minor indicators below recommended thresholds.

A significant and well-considered aspect of this model is treating the “Barriers” construct as a formative measurement model, which makes sense based on the different types of barriers (Economic, Institutional, Psychological, Social-informational) are theorized to influence the overall perception of barriers rather than being influenced by this term. This conceptualization is consistent with prior PLS-SEM literature, which suggests that formative measurement is appropriate when indicators represent distinct dimensions that collectively form a broader construct rather than reflect a single underlying latent variable80,81,86,87. The model provides evidence that allows for a valid test. All four barrier indicators had adequate and significant loadings (0.73 to 0.77), which provide evidence that each barrier is a meaningful contributor to the formative overall. More importantly, the outer VIF values for these formative indicators are all low (well below 1.7), and indicate that there is not a critical multicollinearity between barriers. This means that each barrier dimension adds unique non-redundant information to the overall “Barriers” score, an important prerequisite for a formative measurement. Overall, our substantial assessment of the measurement model shows that the data are reliable and valid to continue on to testing the hypotheses about the structural model. The high reliability indices, generally strong indicator loadings, and acceptable AVE for most constructs implies the formative construct is valid. In short, the model shows a lot of reliability. For confidence in the path coefficients and hypothesis tests that will occur in the structural model, it is essential to engage in this rigorous validation to ensure that there will be a meaningful interpretation of the relationships between Attitude, Subjective Norms, PBC, Trust, Knowledge, Social Networks, Barriers, and Intention. These findings confirm that the measurement model satisfies the evaluation criteria prior to structural model estimation.

Structural model results and hypothesis testing

The outcomes of the structural model analysis provide strong support for the application of the extended Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) framework in explaining farmer intentions to join agricultural marketing networks. As seen in Table 7, Figs. 2, and 3 (the result of bootstrapping analysis), the model reveals the interplay of psychosocial factors being complex, while confirming strong evidence for multiple hypotheses. The relationship between Attitude and Intention (β = 0.30, p < 0.001) is positive and statistically significant, confirming hypothesis H1 and representation of a foundational aspect of TPB. This suggests that farmers who think more positively about marketing networks will be significantly more likely to form an intention to participate. We see that Perceived Behavioral Control (PBC) also positively influences Intention (β = 0.18, p < 0.05), which confirms H3, as well. This result emphasizes that the farmer’s belief in their ability and control over their ability to participate is important. The strongest positive predictor of Intention is social networks (β = 0.37, p < 0.001), supporting H5. This indicates that farmers’ intentions are significantly shaped by their embeddedness in social relationships and exposure to other farmers’ participation behaviors within their networks, rather than by normative pressure alone. The model also supports the extended hypothesized relationships: Trust has a strong and significant positive impact on Attitude (β = 0.60, p < 0.001), supporting H7 and demonstrating that trust is one of the key antecedents that influence a farmer’s overall evaluation. The path from Social Networks to Subjective Norms is also significant (β = 0.51, p < 0.001), supporting H8 and demonstrating that social networks provide the principal conduit for perceived social pressure to form. Conversely, the path from Subjective Norms to Intention (β = 0.07, p = 0.14) is not significant, producing a rejection of H2 (Table 7, Figs. 2 and 3) showing that social networks build normative beliefs, but this perceived pressure to act does not translate into a stronger intention to actively participate. Finally, the path from Knowledge to Intention (β = 0.01, p = 0.78) is also non-significant, leading to rejection of H6, implying awareness itself does not inspire action. The narrative around these outcomes complicates the decision-making calculus farmers in Fars Province face. The strong support for H1, H3 and H5 supports the validity of the TPB core where Social Networks appears to play an important contextual role in this collectivist culture. However, what is perhaps the main takeaway, H2 (Subjective Norms—> Intention), lends credence to the “attitude-behavior” gap with farmers appears to know what others think (Subjective Norms) but need social proof from their observers to act on it from their Social Networks (H5). In other words, less actionable pressure to act from one knowing that the farmer’s peers are engaged is more likely to garner positive behavior change. The lack of evidence in Knowledge (H6) further shows that once farmers have observed their peers engaged it doesn’t move the needle in action; rather these bi-products of social engagement, trust and perceived control are the game changers. The most powerful relationship in the whole model, between Trust and Attitude (H7), is very significant. It shows that farmers’ positive attitude is built nearly entirely on trust in the marketing institutions. Even if trust is said to be higher, a positive attitude will not develop if a farmer does not trust, regardless of perceived value of existing potential economic benefit. The small but significant positive direct effect of Barriers (H9) demonstrates that tangible barriers such as cost and bureaucracy directly dampen intention, even if they have a small beta suggesting their efficacy is often mediated through other constructs, such as PBC. These findings indicate that when encouraging participation, the most effective interventions should focus on building open and trustworthy institutions, helping facilitate peer-to-peer learning and showing demonstrations through existing networks, and increase farmers perceived control of the participation process, rather than relying solely on education or expected social pressure.

Although the R2 value for intention to participate is relatively high, several procedures were conducted to minimize the possibility of model overfitting. Discussions and policy implications

Comparison with previous literature

The study utilizes an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design to understand the factors influencing farmers’ intention to participate in agricultural marketing networks. The initial qualitative phase lay the groundwork to understand context-specific barriers which were then tested quantitatively with an expanded Theory of Planned Behavior framework. The synthesized results provide powerful insights into the complexity of farmers’ decision-making processes, such that the psychological aspect of decision-making is enmeshed with the normative value of trust in institutions and social relations. When placed in the context of wider literature, these findings confirm and extend existing knowledge and provide a further defined pathway for policy. A core finding of this study is the strong role of social networks in shaping farmers’ intentions, which reflects the collectivist nature of agricultural decision-making in this context. Importantly, social networks in this study are conceptualized as a mechanism of observational learning consistent with Social Cognitive Theory, where farmers learn by observing the experiences and outcomes of peers’ participation in marketing networks, rather than being influenced by perceived social pressure (subjective norms). In this sense, information and behavioral cues are transmitted through interpersonal observation and lived experience within farming communities. This is consistent with a body of research. For instance, studies of Albizua et al.66, Mutyebere et al.67, or Ahmed et al.69 have shown that peer effects as a form of social influence and network embeddedness affect farmers’ behavioural intention. In summary, these studies highlight that social learning through observation of peers’ successful practices is a key driver of behavioral adoption. Similarly, evidence from Iranian agricultural contexts (see88) further supports the importance of behavioral and social determinants in shaping farmers’ intentions. For example, prior work on organic farming adoption has shown that rational evaluations, moral considerations, and fear-based perceptions jointly influence farmers’ willingness to engage in sustainable agricultural practices. Nevertheless, the current study makes an important theoretical distinction. Social networks (observational learning) significantly influence intention, whereas subjective norms (perceived social pressure) are not statistically significant. This indicates that farmers are more influenced by observed behavioral evidence of peer success than by perceived expectations of approval or disapproval. The finding is in contrast to some applications of Theory of Planned Behavior (see30,59), but is more in keeping with Rogers’work on Diffusion of Innovations, which centers on observability. This also confirms Nolan et al.’s89 work on norms whereby descriptive norms (what others do) resulted in greater influence than injunctive norms (what others approve of).

The analysis showed trust to be the most important determinant of a favorable attitude toward marketing networks, suggesting that farmers’ positive evaluations are primarily shaped by confidence in the integrity and reliability of institutions. A large body of literature on agricultural cooperatives supports this observation. For example, Bijman et al.90 emphasize that member trust and transparent governance are essential for cooperative success. The direct effect of trust on intention was also significant but relatively smaller in magnitude, suggesting that trust primarily operates by shaping attitudinal evaluations rather than directly driving behavioral intention. This is particularly relevant in the Iranian context, consistent with Movahedi et al.91, who identified mistrust in cooperative governance as a key cause of institutional failure. This finding also aligns with Transaction Cost Economics, which suggests that lower trust increases perceived risk and transaction costs, thereby reducing participation likelihood. In a similar vein, TPB-based studies in agricultural settings highlight the importance of trust-related constructs. For instance, Yazdanpanah et al.92 demonstrate that attitudes, perceived behavioral control, and normative beliefs influence behavioral intentions, while institutional trust moderates these relationships. Likewise, Yazdanpanah and Forouzani93 confirm the robustness of TPB constructs across behavioral domains, particularly attitude and perceived behavioral control. The present findings therefore reinforce previous evidence from agricultural cooperative research while also extending the literature by demonstrating that trust operates not only as a direct predictor of intention but also indirectly through attitudinal evaluations within a broader institutional context63,75.

The results also confirm the relevance of core TPB constructs. A positive attitude significantly influences intention, indicating that farmers who perceive participation outcomes as beneficial are more likely to intend to join. Perceived behavioral control is also significant, indicating that farmers’ perceived ability and resource availability play a meaningful role in shaping intention. This aligns with a substantial body of TPB-based agricultural research94. The strong relationship between perceived behavioral control and social networks suggests that socially derived support enhances farmers’ perceived ability to act, reinforcing the importance of community-based resources in enabling participation. This finding is broadly consistent with prior agricultural and adoption studies57,58 showing that farmers are more likely to engage in collective or innovative practices when they perceive sufficient resources, knowledge, and institutional support to do so.

The discovery that knowledge did not lead to a significant direct effect on intention is particularly useful. It casts doubt on an information deficit model. Farmers may know of marketing networks but may not believe there is a benefit to using them or may not trust them. This is contrary to a common assumption among extension services. It also aligns with critiques arguing that information is a necessary but insufficient condition for behavior change. Importantly, this non-significant finding should not be interpreted as a lack of relevance of knowledge, but rather as evidence that knowledge alone does not translate into behavioral intention without complementary enabling conditions such as trust, social learning, and perceived control. This highlights a critical theoretical implication: knowledge may function more as an indirect or facilitating variable rather than a direct driver of intention. The lack of significance surrounding subjective norms suggest an interesting difference between social norms as passive social pressure rather than acting as social learning. For instance, this differs from some studies in the TPB literature (like61), but may be less surprising in our context where formal institutions or rules are considered weak and individuals are more likely to treat outcomes they directly observed in their immediate context as more salient than broader norms that may or may not be expected. This finding is particularly important as it distinguishes between different types of social influence, indicating that injunctive norms (what others think one should do) may be less influential than descriptive, experience-based signals (what others actually do). Compared with previous TPB-based agricultural studies34,54 that reported significant effects of subjective norms, the present findings suggest that social influence in rural farming communities may operate more strongly through observational and experience-based mechanisms rather than normative approval alone.

The considerable adverse influence of the perceived barriers construct on intention lends support to the inclusion of a context-specific barrier framework in the theoretical model. This supports the fact that the physical barriers identified by farmers, such as high costs, bureaucratic hurdles, and logistics, have direct counter-motivational effects. Existing literature on the adoption of agricultural practices similarly highlights the persistent influence of economic and institutional barriers. The key contribution of this study is to contextualize these barriers within local farmer narratives and demonstrate their cumulative impact within a behavioral framework. The relevance of barriers to perceived behavioral control is equally strong, as many of the barriers directly impact a farmer’s sense of control over participation. Consistent with prior studies on agricultural technology adoption and cooperative participation53, the present findings confirm that structural and institutional constraints remain major obstacles to collective action among farmers, particularly in developing-country contexts characterized by governance and market limitations.

Theoretical implications

This study contributes to the Theory of Planned Behavior literature by demonstrating that the relative importance of behavioral determinants is highly context-dependent, particularly in environments characterized by institutional uncertainty and reliance on informal social structures. One of the main theoretical contributions of the study is the distinction between observational social learning and subjective normative pressure. While subjective norms were not statistically significant, social networks exerted a strong influence on intention, suggesting that farmers are more strongly influenced by observable peer experiences than by abstract expectations of approval. This finding extends conventional TPB applications by showing that descriptive and experience-based social mechanisms may be more influential than injunctive norms in agricultural decision-making contexts.

The study also advances prior theoretical work by integrating context-specific barriers into the TPB framework through a mixed-methods approach. Rather than treating barriers as purely external conditions, the study demonstrates how economic, institutional, and logistical barriers directly shape behavioral intention and perceived behavioral control. Furthermore, the findings suggest that knowledge alone may not directly motivate participation behavior unless accompanied by trust, enabling resources, and observable success within farmers’ social environments. Overall, the study extends existing TPB research by integrating institutional trust, observational learning, and context-specific barriers into a more comprehensive explanation of farmers’ participation intentions in agricultural marketing networks.

Practical implications

The findings provide several actionable implications for agricultural policymakers, extension organizations, and cooperative managers. First, because trust emerged as a major determinant of participation intentions, interventions should prioritize institutional trust-building through transparent governance, regular financial reporting, participatory decision-making, and accountability mechanisms within agricultural cooperatives and marketing organizations. Strengthening institutional legitimacy is likely to improve farmers’ attitudes toward participation and reduce perceived risks associated with collective marketing arrangements. Second, the strong role of social networks suggests that extension programs should move beyond top-down awareness campaigns and instead emphasize peer-based learning approaches. Farmer-to-farmer training programs, demonstration farms, peer ambassador systems, and local success-story dissemination may be more effective in encouraging participation because they provide visible and credible evidence of successful adoption within similar farming communities. Third, the significant negative effect of barriers on intention highlights the need for structural interventions that reduce operational and institutional obstacles. Policies should therefore focus on lowering membership costs, simplifying administrative procedures, improving transportation and marketing infrastructure, expanding access to reliable market information, and reducing bureaucratic constraints facing farmer collectives. Similarly, programs aimed at improving perceived behavioral control should provide farmers with practical training, technical assistance, and resource support that increase their confidence and capacity to participate effectively in marketing networks. Overall, the findings indicate that successful agricultural marketing policies should not rely solely on information provision or economic incentives. Instead, effective interventions require an integrated strategy that simultaneously strengthens institutional trust, facilitates peer-based social learning, and reduces practical participation barriers within local agricultural systems.

Conclusion, limitations and future directions

In summary, this research offers a unique investigation into the factors that influence farmers’ intentions to engage in agricultural marketing networks in Fars Province, Iran, and integrates mixed methods to incorporate rich qualitative data with strong quantitative validation to develop a model that is contextually relevant and theoretically justified. Overall, the evidence indicates that while the foundations of the Theory of Planned Behavior, attitude and perceived behavioral control, are still applicable, both are strongly mediated by two powerful meanings that are context-dependent: institutional trust and social networks. The primary contribution of this research has been the specification of trust as the foundation of a favorable attitude and that social learning within a peer network environment is a more powerful pathway for capturing engaged intentions than passive social pressures. Furthermore, the direct negative relationship validated through qualitative inquiry with the barriers concepts provides further evidence that tangible barriers are also critical, measurable, and significant factors in participation. Overall, this evidence move beyond simplistic explanations to provide a more nuanced model of farmer decision-making that economic calculations are not based on existing relationships, but embedded in a bundled of complex institutional and social relationships.

There are both theoretical and practical implications of this study. Theoretically, it reinforces the value of enhancing established behavioral frameworks with context-specific variables in order to improve the explanatory power of the behavioral frameworks when applied to varying cultural and institutional contexts. Practically, the findings offer a clear evidence-based plan for decision-makers and development practitioners. The study finds that approach interventions without involving participants, such as purely providing information or enhancing economic incentives, are not likely to successful unless effective change in clear and accountable institutions building is decisive in building trust. At the same time, engaging power through social networks faciliting peer to peer learning and addressing the specific economic, institutional, and logistical constraints for farmers are also necessary strategies for fostering participatory engagement on a scale that’s meaningful. This integrated strategy is essential to unlocking the collective marketing possibilities to increase farmer livelihoods and resilience in the agricultural sector in Iran and other similar context.

Although the strong findings from this study are based on a well-designed study with multiple forms of data collection and findings from the semi-structured interviews providing more nuanced perspectives on trust as an enabler for farmer participation, the study has some significant limitations, which nevertheless highlight opportunities for future research. One limitation is that the study took place in the Fars Province of Iran, which despite being highly representative of the country’s broader agricultural sector, may not lend itself to direct generalizability to other contexts with dissimilar cultural, political, or agro-ecological contexts. Future research could apply the same integrated model into other provinces in Iran or in other countries, to assess the invariance of the relationships in different contexts, particularly the strong role of trust and social networks. The second limitation is that the study employed intention to participate as the ultimate dependent variable. Although intention is often an effective predictor of actual behavior, a longitudinal design that follows farmers from intention to actual behavior and ultimately the economic and non-economic outcomes of participation would provide even richer understanding of the impact pathway and potential gaps between intention and actual behavior.

Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the quantitative phase provides a one-time snapshot, capturing relationships that don’t demonstrate how they would change over time. For instance, the dynamics of trust will flex as institutions grow and/or farmers gain direct experience. Future research may augment the current study by completing a longitudinal, or panel study, that examines how these psychological factors and social dynamics shape over time. Lastly, while the formative barriers construct was a primary strength, future research should explore the relative importance of each measure category and explore possible interaction effects. For example, we may investigate the aspects in which institutional reforms that try to increase transparency impact farmers differently based on their social capital, or economic capital. These types of nuanced investigations would enable further refinement of policy recommendations and advance efforts to build agricultural marketing systems that function better, and inclusion for all farmers.
