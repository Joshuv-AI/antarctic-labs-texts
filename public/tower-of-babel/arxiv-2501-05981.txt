Hermit Kingdom Through the Lens of Multiple Perspectives: A Case Study of LLM Hallucination on North Korea
Eunjung ChoETH Zurich, Won Ik ChoSeoul National University, Soomin SeoSogang University

Abstract
Hallucination in large language models (LLMs) remains a significant challenge for their safe deployment, particularly due to its potential to spread misinformation. Most existing solutions address this challenge by focusing on aligning the models with credible sources or by improving how models communicate their confidence (or lack thereof) in their outputs. While these measures may be effective in most contexts, they may fall short in scenarios requiring more nuanced approaches, especially in situations where access to accurate data is limited or determining credible sources is challenging. In this study, we take North Korea - a country characterised by an extreme lack of reliable sources and the prevalence of sensationalist falsehoods - as a case study. We explore and evaluate how some of the best-performing multilingual LLMs and specific language-based models generate information about North Korea in three languages spoken in countries with significant geo-political interests: English (United States, United Kingdom), Korean (South Korea), and Mandarin Chinese (China). Our findings reveal significant differences, suggesting that the choice of model and language can lead to vastly different understandings of North Korea, which has important implications given the global security challenges the country poses.

Introduction

The rapid development of large language models (LLMs) in recent years has led to their increasing use as alternative sources of information search
Zhu et al. (2023)
;
Spatharioti et al. (2023)
;
Sharma et al. (2024b)
. This is despite the critical limitation of current LLMs that may undermine such use case: hallucination – where LLMs generate content that appears factual but is not grounded in truth
Yao et al. (2023)
;
Tonmoy et al. (2024)
;
Xu et al. (2024)
.

Since the release of ChatGPT in November 2022, various risks associated with LLM hallucination have been highlighted. One of the most significant risks is its potential to contribute to the spread of misinformation
Pan et al. (2023)
. Misinformation, by definition, refers to false or inaccurate information shared unintentionally, as opposed to disinformation, which is intentionally misleading, often for malicious purposes like propaganda or phishing.

With LLMs now capable of generating highly convincing content, laypeople may struggle to assess the accuracy of the information generated, potentially leading to the unintentional spread of misinformation by those who believe the hallucinated information to be true
Chen and Shu (2024b)
.

To address this challenge, several mitigation strategies have been proposed. These strategies often focus on improving the accuracy of the generated information by backing it with information from reliable external sources
Lewis et al. (2020)
;
Asai et al. (2024)
or aligning models to express uncertainty in their answers
Yang et al. (2023)
;
Feng et al. (2024)
;
Xiong et al. (2024)
.

While these solutions may be effective in many scenarios, they fall short in contexts where even otherwise reliable sources
2
2

2

By ‘reliable sources’, we refer to sources that are widely trusted to provide accurate information.
may not be fully trustworthy, or where the goal is to not reduce people’s incentive to engage in a topic. For example, if a highly competent model consistently expresses uncertainty for a certain topic, users may lose the incentive to explore and learn about the topic.

North Korea presents a compelling case that highlights these limitations. Even authoritative and typically reliable sources can produce inaccurate or unverified information about North Korea due to several factors, including sensational media coverage (which has emerged as a lucrative source of traffic for news companies worldwide), the inherent difficulties of fact-checking information about the country, and a general lack of incentive to verify or correct such information
Seo (2023)
. Moreover, given the scale of the global security challenges posed by North Korea, it may not be desirable for LLMs to express excessive uncertainty or refuse to answer questions about the country. Such behaviour could reinforce the perception that obtaining accurate information about North Korea is impossible, further isolating the country. Despite its isolation, North Korea occupies a crucial geopolitical position in Northeast Asia, with the United States, China, Russia, Japan, and South Korea all deeply involved in the region. Rising tensions on the Korean peninsula, coupled with the China-Taiwan straits crisis, have heightened concerns about the possibility of global conflict.

These dynamics highlight the need for accurate, balanced, and timely information about North Korea, a challenge that may be exacerbated as the public increasingly relies on LLMs for information.

Our research questions are as follows:

•

RQ1.
How do current LLMs generate information about topics on North Korea, given the scarcity of reliable sources?

•

RQ2.
Are there differences in how various LLMs generate information about North Korea across different languages?

To address these questions, we construct a dataset focusing on two categories of topics about North Korea: 1) widely circulated but false rumours about North Korea that have seen limited correction by credible sources, and 2) lesser-known information about North Korea. We evaluate some of the most widely used LLMs - ChatGPT-3.5, Gemini, Claude 3 Sonnet, Solar-Mini (only for Korean), Qwen-72B (only for Mandarin Chinese) - on the topics, in three languages: Korean, English, and Mandarin Chinese. For 13 topics with verifiable ground truth, we measure the accuracy, consistency, and refusal-to-answer (RtA) rate of the models. We also explore the implications and limitations of these findings.

Our study makes two main contributions: (1) We highlight critical nuances that are often overlooked in current methods for addressing LLM hallucinations and misinformation. (2) We emphasise the need for more rigorous scrutiny when using LLMs in multiple languages, particularly in sensitive geopolitical contexts where misinformation can have serious and far-reaching consequences.

2
Background

2.1
North Korea and misinformation

The dearth of communication between North Korea and the outside world - and the resulting scarcity of reliable information - has led to a long history of misinformation about the country. Research indicates that pre-existing attitudes significantly influence people’s reliance on (mis)information, with the primacy effect and confirmation bias being particularly strong when it comes to countries like North Korea
Ecker et al. (2014)
. Additionally, racist attitudes towards North Koreans - who are often perceived as irrational - have fuelled the growth of misinformation surrounding the country
Seo et al. (2023)
.

Following North Korea’s gradual opening to the outside world in the early 2000s and the establishment of diplomatic ties with European nations, the amount of available information about the country increased. Western outlets such as the Associated Press and AFP opened news bureaus in Pyongyang, leading to more coverage from within the country
Seo (2018)
.
However, this rise in quality information about North Korea was counterbalanced by sensationalist reporting from many Western media outlets. For example, UK tabloids found that North Korea-related stories were highly popular, leading to a surge in misleading reports
Seo et al. (2023)
. The spread of false information such as rumours about the deaths of prominent North Korean figures, can thus be attributed to not just national ideologies but also to a lack of professionalism among individual journalists. Moreover, journalistic standards on ethics and fact-checking are often not rigorously applied to North Korea-related stories
Seo (2023)
.

2.2
Large language models, hallucination, and misinformation

A significant challenge in deploying LLMs across various domains is their tendency to hallucinate - generating inaccurate or misleading outputs
Dahl et al. (2024a)
;
Huang et al. (2024b)
. As LLM performance continues to improve, there is growing evidence that people tend to trust the models’ outputs without critically assessing their accuracy. Even when LLM outputs may contain subtle inaccuracies, users may accept them as true unless the errors are blatantly obvious
Spatharioti et al. (2023)
;
Kazemitabaar et al. (2023)
. This uncritical acceptance can lead to the unintentional spread of misinformation when users share the outputs with others, contributing to the broader issue of misinformation - a major challenge for online safety and public trust
Pan et al. (2023)
;
Huang et al. (2024b)
.

The causes of LLM hallucination are complex and multi-faceted. Hallucinations may arise from the training data, the model’s architecture, or the strategies used during training and inference
Huang et al. (2024a)
. As noted in the Introduction, existing strategies typically involve augmenting LLM outputs with external knowledge sources
Lewis et al. (2020)
;
Asai et al. (2024)
;
Peng et al. (2023)
, or teaching models to express uncertainty or refrain from responding when unsure
Yang et al. (2023)
;
Feng et al. (2024)
. However, these approaches have limitations
Tonmoy et al. (2024)
;
Xu et al. (2024)
;
Magesh et al. (2024)
, and the variety of contexts in which LLMs are deployed necessitates more nuanced mitigation strategies. Thus, developing effective methods to manage hallucinations in diverse domains remains an active area of research
Huang et al. (2024a)
;
Ji et al. (2023)
, along with efforts to establish benchmarks for detecting and evaluating hallucinations
Lin et al. (2022)
;
Cheng et al. (2023)
;
Niu et al. (2024)
.

2.3
Two challenges combined: North Korea misinformation and LLM hallucination

Some argue that fears surrounding the spread of misinformation by LLMs are exaggerated, especially in well-functioning democracies with robust media ecosystems. In such contexts, misinformation is relatively rare due to the diligence of professionals — journalists, fact checkers, experts, and well-established institutions - who detect and correct false information before it can have a significant societal impact
Simon et al. (2023)
. However, in cases like North Korea, misinformation is not only difficult to detect and correct but is also compounded by a lack of political incentive to do so.

Efforts within the NLP community, such as the creation of hallucination leaderboards
Hong et al. (2024)
and misinformation benchmarks
Liu et al. (2024)
, aim to help users assess the reliability of models in terms of misinformation. However, these benchmarks often have limited coverage of North Korea-related topics, meaning that even models that perform well on such leaderboards may fare poorly when tasked with generating information about North Korea.

There is also optimism that LLM hallucinations will become a less pressing issue as future AI systems develop ”grounded intelligence” - learning through direct interaction and perception rather than relying solely on existing text, images, or videos, as current models do
LeCun (2022)
. While this may hold true for many contexts, North Korea presents a unique challenge. The country’s seclusion makes it nearly impossible for machines to gain direct access to accurate, up-to-date information. As a result, even as AI systems evolve, users will likely continue to depend on textual and visual data about North Korea that are difficult to verify. Consequently, LLM hallucination will remain a critical issue when it comes to generating information about North Korea.

Languages

To address
RQ2
, we selected three languages: English, Mandarin Chinese, and Korean. These languages were chosen based on the countries that have significant stakes in their relationship with North Korea - English (primarily representing the United States), Mandarin Chinese (China), Korean (South Korea

Perrault and Clark (2024)
.

3.2
Topic categories

Our dataset is constructed on two broad categories of topics:

1. False rumours
: These are widespread rumours about North Korea that were later debunked but received limited corrections from sources. Rumours, by definition, are unverified information passed from person to person and are a form of misinformation.
5
5

5

https://www.unhcr.org/innovation/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Using-Social-Media-in-CBP-Chapter-6-Rumours-and-Misinformation.pdf
Due to the lack of corrective information in many texts including in journalism, we hypothesise LLMs may struggle to make accurate inferences about these rumours, leading to higher rates of hallucination.

2. Lesser-known information

This category covers topics related to North Korea that are less frequently discussed or documented in textual sources, and thus less familiar to both LLMs and the general public. Media coverage of North Korea tends to focus heavily on its nuclear program or humanitarian crises, leaving other aspects of the country underrepresented. We hypothesise that LLMs may generate more inconsistent or accurate information when prompted about these marginalised topics.

4.3
Evaluation

We use both quantitative and qualitative approaches for our evaluation.

4.3.1
Quantitative

Let the number of trials per model be denoted as
n_{T}
(in our case,
n_{T}=5
) and each trial is labelled as one of the following: ‘RtA’ (refusal to answer), ‘true’, ‘false’, and ‘uncertain’.

Refusal to Answer Ratio (
R_{RtA}
)
: This metric calculates how often the model refuses to provide an answer. It is defined as the number of times the model refuses to answer divided by the total number of trials:
R_{RtA}=n_{RtA}/n_{T}
where
0\leq n_{RtA}\leq n_{T}
. A model is considered to have refused if it declines to provide any information or does not indicate its stance on the given topic candidate
Zhang et al. (2024)
;
Cheng et al. (2024)
.

Accuracy (Acc.)
: Accuracy measures whether the model’s response aligns with the ground truth. The possible truth values for each topic candidate are: true, false, or uncertain. If a model provides evidence for both true and false, we weigh the balance of evidence. If the model leans significantly towards one side (true or false), we label the answer accordingly. If the evidence is equally balanced and a layperson would find it hard to draw a conclusion, we label the answer as uncertain. The accuracy of a model for a given topic is calculated as the number of correct answers among the trials:
Acc.=n_{correct}/n_{T}
where
0\leq n_{correct}\leq n_{T}
denotes the number of accurate answers.

Consistency (Con.)
: Consistency evaluates how uniformly the model behaves across multiple trials. Unlike accuracy, which relies on the ground truth, consistency is an intrinsic model metric. We count the number of distinct answer types (i.e., true, false, uncertain) given across the trials. A model is considered more consistent if it provides fewer types of answers. Consistency is calculated as:
Con.=1/n_{types}
where
n_{types}\leq 4
represents the number of distinct answer types (true, false, uncertain, RtA). For example, if the trial yields
\{\text{uncertain},\text{uncertain},\text{RtA},\text{false},\text{false}\}
,
Con.=\frac{1}{3}
. The consistency metric captures the variety of model responses rather than frequency. For example, if the trials produce four
uncertain
answers and one
RtA
, consistency is still
Con.=\frac{1}{2}
, even though the
uncertain
answer dominates. We make this assumption because LLM end users are diverse, and even a single
RtA
response can significantly influence user perception of the model’s behaviour, regardless of the other responses.

When measuring accuracy and consistency, we focus on the model’s direct response to the prompt - specifically, whether it leans towards agreeing or disagreeing with the prompt. We do not evaluate the accuracy or consistency of the more granular details within the answer.

4.3.2
Qualitative

For topic candidates where we applied a more granular taxonomy (see
4.1.1
), we qualitatively evaluate the models’ responses. For instance, if the nuances in the answers seem to reinforce existing stereotypes about North Korea, this may influence how the outside world continues to perceive the country and North Korean defectors attempting to integrate into new societies. This qualitative analysis also contributes to the growing body of research on how LLMs may replicate and perpetuate existing stereotypes
Kotek et al. (2023)
;
Dev et al. (2023)
.

Since the focus was solely on determining whether the model’s overall answer to the prompt was ‘true’, ‘false’, ‘uncertain’, or ‘RtA’, there was minimal ambiguity in the process.

5.1
Refusal to Answer (RtA)

Gemini exhibited a notably high RtA ratio across all languages compared to the other LLMs. This is likely due to the model’s stringent safety guardrails, especially given the sensitive nature of topics related to North Korea. The high RtA ratio persisted even for less sensitive topics, such as those classified as
verified
(see
4.1.1
for the taxonomy of topic candidates). This contrasts with other models, all of which had an RtA ratio of zero for such topics (see Supplementary Material for a full evaluation by topic classification).

Qwen-72B consistently showed a zero RtA behaviour across all topics tested, while Solar Mini exhibited a near-zero RtA rate. In contrast, for ChatGPT-3.5, Gemini, and Claude 3 Sonnet, the highest RtA ratios were found in the

Although being conservative in answering sensitive topics, (i.e. a high RtA rate) may seem prudent, this approach could have negative implications in the context of North Korea, as discussed in the Introduction. From a user’s perspective, if a model refuses to answer certain topics but performs well in others, such a model behaviour might reinforce the existing stereotype that North Korea is impenetrable - suggesting that even highly competent models cannot provide reliable information about the country. While more empirical research is needed to confirm this, such outcomes could discourage users from learning more about North Korea, which would be detrimental given the global security challenges the country poses.

5.2
Accuracy

We observed accuracy differences across the three languages. For ChatGPT-3.5, answers in English and Chinese were more accurate on average than those in Korean. Similarly, for Gemini and Claude 3 Sonnet, English answers exhibited much higher accuracy than both Chinese and Korean answers. This discrepancy is likely due to the English-centric nature of training corpora. However, the language-specific models - Qwen-72B (Chinese) and Solar Mini (Korean) - exhibited higher accuracy in their respective languages compared to the average performance of the other three models. Notably, both Qwen-72B and Solar Mini achieved 100% accuracy for
verified
topics. Such results seem to support the recent global discourse on the need for ”AI sovereignty”, to reduce reliance on foreign AI technologies and bolster a nation’s ability to protect and advance its interests.

Interestingly, models generally performed worse on topics with truth values being false compared to those that are true. Many of those topics were related to widespread rumours, such as rumours that North Korea reported that they won the 2014 World Cup (they did not make such report). This outcome supports our hypothesis that models may have encountered fewer corrections for such false rumours during training (see
3.2
). Additionally, we found that erroneous stories about the supposed deaths of three prominent North Koreans - Kim Jong Un, Hyon Song Wol and Jang Song Thaek - persisted, despite these being some of the most prominent cases of misinformation. It is possible that the overwhelming volume of erroneous coverage contributed to this, even though stories correcting these rumours were available.

Although we tested only on topics with verifiable ground truths, we found that the expected size or disclosed capacity of a model does not always correlate with higher accuracy. For example, across all three languages, Claude 3 Sonnet exhibited the highest accuracy, followed by ChatGPT-3.5, and then Gemini. Gemini’s lower accuracy was also due to its high RtA frequency, which lowered its overall performance. Additionally, Claude 3 Sonnet’s strong performance may be attributed to its recent public release, which likely exposed it to more up-to-date information, including post-mortem corrections.

5.3
Consistency

While we observed high levels of consistency for Chinese (as a language) and Claude 3 Sonnet (as a model), no significant patterns emerged across different languages or models. However, we did find notable differences in consistency across topic taxonomies.

The two broad categories for our topic candidates are false rumours and lesser-known information. Our results show that models tend to display higher consistency when answering questions about lesser-known information compared to false rumours. One possible explanation is that
partial
or
verified
topics, which are generally less sensitive, may not trigger the safety guardrails of closed or open LLMs as much. In contrast, topics under false rumours, such as
alienation
and
gossip
, are inherently more sensitive and potentially controversial. When these topics trigger the model’s safety guardrails or its internal alignment processes, the model may provide responses it deems ‘safe’ rather than those aligned with the ground truth it has learned from its training data.

Overall, consistency did not show any strong correlation with the other two metrics (RtA and accuracy). However, it is important to note that the high degree of consistency can sometimes result from a high number of RtA responses. For example, in the case of Gemini, the consistency score was 0.92, but the RtA ratio was 40%, indicating that the high consistency was largely due to frequent refusal to answer than consistent engagement with the topic.

6
Conclusion

Our exploratory results reveal significant differences in how some of the current best-performing LLMs generate information on the same topics related to North Korea, as well as notable variations across languages. This is particularly concerning given that the topics we used in this study are relatively simple with verifiable truths, suggesting that the variations could be even greater for more complex topics requiring deeper contextual knowledge and information synthesis. Despite the critical need for an accurate understanding of the country, our findings reveal that both the choice of model and language can significantly shape users’ perceptions of North Korea.

Looking ahead, our findings suggest several avenues for addressing these challenges. First, for domains requiring greater nuance, such as North Korea, systematic design choices should be made when curating data sources for models. For example, for models employing retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) workflow, instead of relying on top web search APIs, workflows could be designed to only include expert-curated sources - such as those vetted by specialists in international relations who understand the geopolitical implications. While there are some efforts in this direction, such as OpenAI’s initiative to incorporate democratic inputs into AI development
10
10

10

https://openai.com/blog/democratic-inputs-to-ai-grant-program-update
, more research efforts are needed. At the same time, the growing body of research on knowledge conflict - where a model’s parametric knowledge conflicts with external information provided e.g., through RAG - presents an interesting avenue for future exploration. Given the likelihood of knowledge conflicts in topics such as North Korea, studying how models behave under these circumstances could provide valuable insights for improving LLM performance in complex, sensitive contexts.

Our findings also contribute to the broader discourse on the values and priorities guiding the alignment of LLMs. The isolation of North Korea and the resulting near-total lack of input from the ordinary North Koreans likely have resulted in a lack of incentives to carefully consider how models should approach topics related to the country and its people. While participatory AI - an active area of research focussed on incorporating feedback from diverse communities - is often suggested as one way of mitigating LLM hallucinations
Leiser et al. (2023)
, this is nearly impossible in the context of North Korea.

North Korea is not the only context where current hallucination mitigation strategies fall short, but failing to pay close attention to such edge cases could result in the unintended propagation of misinformation with significant societal implications in other countries with more closed information systems. We hope that our work motivates greater attention to these edge cases and encourages more rigorous scrutiny of how LLMs handle them.

Limitations and societal impact

In this work, we focussed on testing LLMs using a small set of topics related to North Korea with verifiable ground truth, primarily for ease of evaluation. While we acknowledge the limited size of the dataset, we observed meaningful differences in model responses across models and languages. Given that the primary goal of this study is not to rank model performance but to assess the user perspective - highlighting how different models and languages can provide vastly different information - we believe that once such differences are apparent, the size of the dataset becomes less critical.

Future research could explore how models respond to a broader range of topics about North Korea, particularly those requiring deeper contextual knowledge and information synthesis. Also, our work could be expanded to evaluate multimodal LLMs (MLLMs). Additionally, our evaluation was conducted at a broad level - assessing whether the model generally agreed or disagreed with the given topic - rather than analysing the finer details of each response. A more granular evaluation would also be valuable, as the greatest risk from hallucinations often comes not from obvious falsehoods but from subtle inaccuracies, oversimplifications, or biased responses that are presented confidently, potentially convincing users of their truthfulness.

Lastly, we want to briefly note our use of the term ‘hallucination’. We adopted it in our work because it is currently a widely used term both within and beyond the NLP community. However, we recognise that the connotations of the term may not necessarily reflect what LLMs are doing: while the term implies that it is something that can be cleared up, for LLMs, it is rather a built-in feature, that some suggest the term should instead be replaced with ‘bullshit’
Hicks et al. (2024)
. While an in-depth engagement in such discussions is beyond the scope of this work, moving forward, we believe a critical examination of the term’s broader implications for research and society is necessary.
