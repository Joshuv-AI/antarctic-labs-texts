SpiralEdge-IoV: secure and adaptive task offloading in internet of vehicles edge computing using logarithmic spiral defense
Authors: Shankar J, Nagaraja S R
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, the internet of vehicles (IoV) has become an important enabler of intelligent transportation systems, providing vehicle-edge computing for latency-sensitive and computation-intensive vehicular applications. In such environments, the efficient offloading of tasks is pivotal; yet existing approaches primarily focus on optimising performance, often under the assumption of benign operating conditions or by employing static, trust-based mechanisms. However, these methods fall short in practical IoV implementations due to high mobility, short-lived connectivity, and the adversarial nature of IoV, where misbehaviour and resource exhaustion can substantially compromise the system’s reliability and security. In response to these problems, we design SpiralEdge-IoV, a secure and adaptive task offloading framework that tightly integrates defence and optimisation. This framework embeds a logarithmic spiral defence (LSD) mechanism that models trust as a deepening, adaptive path over time, enabling online risk evaluation and incremental offensive action against suspicious parties. We integrate these risk scores into an in-built bio-inspired Addax-optimisation-based decision model (LSD-AddaxNet) to obtain security-aware multi-objective offloading decisions that not only minimise latency, energy consumption, and execution cost, but also maximise robustness. A combination of realistic vehicular edge-offloading traces and the VeReMi misbehaviour dataset is employed to conduct an extensive simulation-based evaluation, confirming the effectiveness of the proposed framework. Relative to representative optimisation- and learning-based baselines, SpiralEdge-IoV delivers up to 18% lower average task latency, reduces energy consumption by about 15%, and increases task success rates in adversarial settings by over 20%. In addition, the analyses on convergence and scalability demonstrate that the framework enables stable optimisation with acceptable runtime overhead in dense vehicular scenarios. SpiralEdge-IoV can be helpful for attack-resilient, low-latency IoV edge computing and is thus suitable for safety-critical vehicular applications and future intelligent transportation systems, as shown in the results.

Introduction

There is no such detailed overview of the existing architecture, the mentioned benefits of IoV, and how IoV has dramatically changed traditional transportation systems through significant data exchange among vehicles, roadside infrastructure, and cloud services. Examples of such vehicular applications that impose strict latency, reliability, and energy-efficiency guarantees include autonomous driving assistance, traffic management, collision avoidance, and infotainment. To satisfy these demands, vehicular edge computing (VEC) has been regarded as a significant paradigm that brings computation and storage closer to vehicles via roadside unit (RSU)–based edge servers1. Nonetheless, the highly dynamic vehicular scenario with high mobility, sporadic connectivity, and dynamic workloads poses a challenge for efficient and secure task offloading.

Task offloading in the Internet of Vehicles (IoV) has been widely studied using optimisation-based methods1, deep learning models2, and reinforcement learning methods2. The research approaches have mainly focused on latencies, energy consumption, and execution costs under different network conditions. In fast-moving scenarios, service continuity is further enhanced by mobility-aware, handover-enabled offloading strategies3. Recent work has introduced learning-based adaptivity and trust via blockchain to augment robustness and resiliency4. These advances notwithstanding, a key limitation of the state of the art is that most approaches assume benign settings or treat security as a static or weakly coupled entity, typically based on inflexible thresholds, post-decision filtering, or heavyweight trust mechanisms. These designs are susceptible to misbehaviour, false task injection, replay attacks, and resource exhaustion, all of which are becoming real threats in open vehicular networks5.

Although widely studied in the context of vehicular edge computing, current research frameworks only optimise performance metrics, such as latency, energy consumption, and execution cost, under the tacit assumption of benign operating conditions. In realistic IoV scenarios, vehicles can act maliciously or selfishly, for example, by abusing resources, conducting replay attacks, or injecting false tasks. Such malicious actions can poison the observed system state and result in substantial loss of the offloading decisions. Most existing solutions treat security as an external filtering step or a static trust constraint, and, in turn, find it difficult to adapt to dynamic vehicular environments. Hence, we require a closely coupled defence–optimisation architecture that directly incorporates security awareness into real-time offloading decisions.

While learning-based task offloading methods in vehicular edge computing have mostly focused on optimising latency, energy consumption, and execution efficiency, the latest state-of-the-art works systematically introduce privacy preservation and distributed multi-agent coordination during offloading6,7. As an example, protected task offloading for low-altitude vehicular fog computing has been addressed using privacy-preserving hierarchical reinforcement learning6, and many-to-many task competition between vehicles and fog resources has been modelled with multi-agent deep reinforcement learning7. However, these studies did not directly pair (1) continuous trust evolution, (2) deviation-driven risk estimation, and (3) attack-responsive containment with the optimisation loop. It remains an essential gap when considering secure and adaptive offloading in IoV environments exposed to adversaries.

Other recent work has investigated mechanism design for privacy-preserving reputation management for vehicular networks. Sun et al. PPDR8—a privacy-preserving dual reputation management scheme designed for vehicle platoons, in which leader and follower reputation scores are separated to avoid reputation manipulation, while user identity remains private. Liu et al. PPRU: a privacy-preserving reputation updating scheme for cloud-assisted vehicular networks9 enables reputation evaluation while preserving sensitive user information with encrypted feedback aggregation. Such methods primarily address secure reputation updating and preserve privacy using cryptographic techniques. On the other hand, the proposed SpiralEdge-IoV framework enhances the trust evolution process by focusing on behaviour-based trust evolution and providing a risk-aware trust signal to the task offloading optimisation process, thus enabling adaptive decision-making in IoV dynamic environments.

These gaps serve as the impetus for pursuing a new class of secure offloading frameworks, in which security awareness is integrated into the decision-making process rather than an afterthought. This means that adaptive learning is not enough; reliable, attack-adaptive signals must inform. In response, the current research fulfils this requirement by presenting SpiralEdge-IoV, a secure and adaptive task-offloading framework that seamlessly combines a logarithmic–spiral–based defence mechanism with bio-inspired optimisation. The main goal of this work is to design an offloading system that provides low latency and energy efficiency while being robust to adversarial behaviours in dynamic IoV environments.

There are several significant innovations in the proposed methodology. First, by developing a Logarithmic Spiral Defence (LSD) mechanism, this work models trust not as a single value but as a continuous trajectory over time, supporting gradual containment and risk assessment. Then, these risk scores are directly fed into a multi-objective Addax optimisation process, forming LSD-AddaxNet, which acts as a system for offloading decisions in a security-aware way. Third, by establishing a closed-loop interaction mechanism between defence and optimisation, both runtime feedback and adaptive policy refinement can be achieved as workload and threat conditions change10. Collectively, these features embody the opposite of traditional decoupled approaches.

This paper has four main contributions. It proposes a closed-loop integrated defence-optimisation framework as a secure offloading architecture for IoV edge computing. It develops new trust, risk, and fitness models that directly incorporate adversarial behaviour. It offers a comprehensive experimental evaluation using realistic offloading traces and benchmarked misbehaviour datasets, demonstrating superior performance and robustness to state-of-the-art solutions. Last but not least, it provides scalability, ablation, and convergence analyses to demonstrate its practical deployability.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. Background Sect. 2 reviews related work on IoV task offloading and security-aware optimisation. The theoretical preliminaries and system fundamentals are outlined in Sect. 3. Section 4 describes the SpiralEdge-IoV framework and its main building blocks. The experimental evaluation and comparison are demonstrated in Sect. 5. Section 6 provides limitations and practical implications, and Sect. 7 concludes the paper.

Related work

In this section, we contextualise our work and present a critical review of current approaches to tackle task offloading in Internet of Vehicles edge computing, focusing on the limitations of learning-based optimisation methods and the way mobility and security are typically handled, which motivate the proposed SpiralEdge-IoV framework.

Computation offloading foundations in vehicular edge computing

The original work on vehicular edge computing (VEC) captured the baseline principles of computation offloading, highlighting the goal of reducing execution latency and energy consumption under high vehicle mobility. Co-integrated task scheduling and offloading optimisation was formulated in Sun et al.1, and it was shown that coordinated decisions are superior to isolated offloading strategies. Similarly, Wang et al.11 and Yang et al.12 studied baseline MEC-enabled vehicular architectures, focusing on how vehicle mobility and network dynamics affect offloading reliability. The findings in Huang and Huang13 and Li et al.14 show that naive offloading (i.e., without mobility awareness) may break the agreement and cause service interruptions, as the same user may frequently regenerate its allocated resources and retake the service. These initial studies focused primarily on adaptive and intelligent offloading mechanisms and were primarily for benign network scenarios, with little or no explicit mention of secure computation. Zheng et al.15 discussed IV communication and cloud computing, two distinct paradigms that reflect the differences between bulky and small devices designed explicitly for MEC. Practical requirements and constraints of V2X-oriented edge computing were summarised in16.

Mobility-aware and handover-driven offloading strategies

Following that, with vehicular mobility being one of the most significant challenges, mobility-aware and handover-enabled offloading schemes have emerged17. Lv et al.4, Yuan et al.10. The vehicle trajectory prediction method can be combined with the service migration method to reduce connection interruptions during vehicle travel Maleki et al.3. In dense vehicular scenarios, minimise the task failure probability via handover-enabled dynamic offloading. Liang et al.18,19 dealt with multi-cell and multi-fog mobility management. Enhancing Task Continuity via Coordinated Migration. It is well known that coordinated service migration leads to task continuity. Surveys by Zaman et al.20 and Alkaabi et al.5. Following this line of research, they conducted a comprehensive review of mobility-aware offloading and MEC handover mechanisms. They identified gaps, including a lack of security, scalability, and adaptive decision-making under adversarial conditions. In addition, Nahar et al.15 showed that software-defined fog can effectively support vehicular environments, supplementing and enhancing handover-centric strategies by providing improved dynamic resource coordination under mobility.

Game-theoretic and optimisation-based offloading models

Many studies that focused on resource competition among vehicles used game-theoretic and optimisation-based approaches to balance resource allocation. Another contribution of Sun et al.21 was a bargaining-based task-offloading mechanism to achieve equilibrium between vehicles and edge servers. Wang et al.22 proposed cost-aware and Stackelberg game formulations enabling hierarchical decision-making for infrastructure and vehicles. Zhang and Debroy23 and Raza et al.24 further addressed optimisation-centric frameworks focusing on latency-energy trade-offs. These approaches, although mathematically sound, tend to operate under fixed assumptions and are not robust to rapid topology changes, large-scale vehicular deployments, and changing attack patterns. Zhang et al. highlighted that latency modelling and estimation are key for augmentation accuracy. Therefore, even mathematically optimal formulations can be badly misled by poor latency estimation, as we demonstrate in25,26.

Deep learning-based offloading in IoV environments

As the complexity of vehicular data increased, deep learning models were increasingly applied to offloading decisions. Elgendy et al.2 used a deep neural network for multistage vehicle edge-cloud offloading, achieving better adaptivity than rule-based offloading approaches. In addition to generic DNNs, Deep learning/RL variants can capture nonlinear vehicular dynamics better (Lin et al.27 and Hu and Huang28. Cooperative offloading is proposed in Cho et al.29 as a potential solution to improve vehicle energy efficiency through cooperation. Shang et al.30 and Zhang et al.31 proposed an approach that used deep learning models to improve energy efficiency and task execution accuracy. Building on these foundational concepts, Kazmi et al.32 presented a systematic approach to offloading for connected and autonomous vehicles, with deep learning performance enhancements showing gains in urban traffic cases. Nonetheless, these solutions primarily focus on performance enhancement, with insufficient components to counter adversarial behaviours such as bogus task requests or compromised edge nodes.

Reinforcement learning and DRL-based task offloading

RL has emerged as the prevailing approach to adaptive offloading in the context of VEC1. Ning et al.33 proposed a DRL-based vehicular offloading approach and demonstrated that, even in dynamic environments, an agent can learn optimal policies. Zhan et al.34 and Geng et al.35 built on existing DRL frameworks and applied them to distributed vehicular networks, achieving reduced latency and increased throughput. Farimani et al.36. proposed deadline-aware and energy-aware RL models36. and Elgendy et al.37, respectively. Surveys by Uddin et al.38 and Yang et al.39 systematically explored DRL architectures for VEC and highlighted important challenges in convergence speed, scalability, and resilience against malicious state manipulation. These limitations demand learning frameworks that are more secure and better at detecting and defending against attacks in a given system.

RL-based edge intelligence targeted toward 6G was investigated in Wei et al.40, and offloading/network selection was formulated as an MDP (Markov Decision Process) for energy saving in41. Raja et al.42 have also explored the problem of reward-driven vehicular offloading—an area where carefully designed reward structures play an evident role. Xue et al.43 proposed caching-aware offloading, which uses joint service caching and computation offloading based on DRL. The work in Wang et al.44 also developed DRL-based computation migration for heterogeneous vehicular networks. Cao et al.45 also introduced broader RL-based offloading methods.

Over the last few years, there has been an evolution in learning-based offloading research that goes beyond purely optimising performance. Wei et al. The work in6 formulated a privacy-preserving hierarchical reinforcement learning framework for low-altitude vehicular fog computing, embedding privacy-aware policy learning into hierarchical offloading decisions. In addition, Wei et al. Inspired by the competition among different vehicles and the competition for fog resources7, formulated a multi-agent deep reinforcement learning problem for many-to-many vehicular fog task offloading. Based on these studies, we demonstrate that it is feasible to protect privacy and enable multi-agent coordination through learning-based offloading directly. However, they do not emphasise the continuous evolution of the trust radius, online risk scoring, and progressive containment of suspicious nodes, which are the main elements of the proposed SpiralEdge-IoV framework.

Security-aware and blockchain-enabled offloading

Research on security in vehicular edge computing has recently attracted attention. Nazih et al.46 identified vulnerabilities in vehicular fog and edge infrastructures, prompting attention to trust-aware offloading. Moghaddasi et al.47 presented a blockchain-based secure offloading mechanism, which was employed with double DQN to be resilient against tampering. The SDN-aided MEC systems were introduced in Shah et al.48 for enhanced quality of service and mobility management, with no direct consideration of adaptive attack mitigation. Although blockchain and trust-based models enhance integrity, their computational overhead and response latency limit their ability to handle rapidly evolving attacks in IoV. discuss AI-defined wireless networking views in which intelligent and offloading/resource allocation are co-joined. Shah et al.49, increasing the motivation for security-conscious learning stacks.

Emerging architectures: federated, digital twin, and space–air–ground offloading

Several novel architectural paradigms to enhance scalability and intelligence have been studied in recent work. Wu et al.50 introduced the federated reduction framework for offloading large models, reducing privacy leakage among vehicles. Zhao et al.51 used partial offloading assisted by a digital twin to improve decision precision through simulation in a virtual environment. Shen et al.52 and Dai et al.53 generalised offloading to space–air–ground and UAV-aided vehicular networks to broaden exposure and enable more flexible coverage. While these architectures enhance adaptability, they introduce new vulnerabilities and lack built-in defences designed for IoV threat dynamics.

The UAV-empowered MEC offloading is also systematically modelled in28 to understand the system-wide performance benefits54. Moreover, the applicability of MEC to immersive/latency-constrained apps (e.g., mobile AR) underscores the tightness of real-time constraints, as noted in Siriwardhana et al.55. Recent RL-based multi-fog resource allocation works have also been conducted by Akbarzadeh et al.56, demonstrating continued interest in multi-domain coordination.

Surveys, taxonomies, and identified research gaps

Many wide-ranging surveys and traffic taxonomies summarise progress in vehicular edge offloading. Hou et al.57, Jin et al.58, and Haibeh et al.59 on MEC infrastructure, optimisation strategies, and learning-based approaches. Kumar et al.60 proposed a critical taxonomy of offloading for connected and autonomous vehicles, highlighting open problems in security and robustness. The increasing complexity of IoV ecosystems is further evident in recent works on GenAI-augmented MEC-NOMA17, regional computing61, and an integrated terrestrial–non-terrestrial network62. Currently available studies, however, treat security primarily as a secondary constraint in the design rather than a design feature, despite abundant research. Last, summarised AI/ML Opportunities for wireless systems and open RAN-aligned edge intelligence. Finally, this trend confirms a general movement towards an intelligent, programmable vehicular edge infrastructure63.

Motivation for SpiralEdge-IoV

Building upon the above literature, we identify three main areas of improvement that need addressing: (i) Geometrically inspired, automated multi-fusion point, reactive in nature defense mechanisms against coordinated attacks, (ii) Tight coupling between security behavior and the learning based offloading decisions which is done as multi-agent/nash multi-objective optimization problem, (iii) Greater robustness when manipulated to deviating random mobility and workload patterns under practical scenarios, which, in our opinion, can help facilitate the real world deployment of adaptive, learning based task offloading.

Preliminaries

In this section, we present the preliminary concepts and notations relevant to the SpiralEdge-IoV framework, i.e., the IoV edge computing model, task-offloading metrics, and security–optimisation fundamentals. The proposed methodology draws on the theory presented in these preliminaries, and the detailed design of the SpiralEdge-IoV framework is presented in Sect. 4.

Basics of internet of vehicles, edge computing, and task offloading

Building upon vehicular ad hoc networking, the Internet of Vehicles (IoV) enables safety and intelligent transportation applications by enabling vehicles to communicate with the roadside infrastructure, pedestrians, and cloud services. In Internet of Vehicles (IoV) communications, the vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V), vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I), and vehicle-to-network (V2N) links are often combined, where the wireless channel exhibits characteristics such as high mobility, substantial blockage, and rapid topology change. As a result, the data rate and link reliability are time-variant, and service continuity hinges on handovers between roadside units (RSUs) and cellular access points. The above characteristics render traditional cloud-centric processing ill-suited for delay-sensitive vehicular services, thus motivating computation at the edge (near the vehicle).

Vehicular edge computing (VEC) has emerged as a paradigm for inter-vehicular communications at the network edge, enabling low-latency, backhaul-independent task processing via compute and storage offloading, often deployed at RSUs or nearby micro–data centres. In our proposed SpiralEdge-IoV, RSU-based edge servers function as intermediate execution points, serving tasks locally when possible, whereas the cloud serves as a backup for heavy processing tasks or non-time-sensitive workloads. Security and optimization foundations for IoV edge systems

The open and ultra-dynamic nature of vehicular communications poses challenges in security for Internet of Vehicles edge computing environments. Vehicles would be malicious and selfish by injecting false task requests, replaying valid messages from past time, or flooding the relevant edge servers with far more computation requests than they can digest to deplete their resources. This misbehavior can corrupt (network and workload) state information experienced by the nodes and lead to poor/offloading decisions with suboptimal and/or unsafe operational impacts. As VEC systems setup RSU-based edge servers to serve multiple fast-moving vehicles simultaneously (i.e. each vehicle expects the number of services from RSU to be higher than one instantaneously), even a small fraction of malicious nodes can considerably spoil service quality and reliability making it hard to achieve quality of service if security mechanisms cannot be tightly coupled with offloading logic.

Existing defense schemes in IoV and mobile edge computing use static trust scores or fixed threshold-based rules to identify nodes as benign or malicious. These mechanisms are easy to implement but very ill-fitted for vehicular environments. Indeed, static thresholds do not reflect gradual behavioral changes and are of the low ability to detect stealthy/on–off attacks since adversaries can simply switch their behavior between benign and malicious. Furthermore, the existing approaches isolate nodes with a sharp cut after surpassing a fixed threshold that results in an unnecessary loss of service in case of actual or suspicious network anomalies, or measurement noise. This motivates the need for adaptive trust modeling techniques that are able to evolve over a period of time and can adapt in a smooth manner to any behavioral deviations.

In order to overcome these challenges, trust-aware and risk-aware decision-making frameworks can assign a continuously updated score (such as trust or risk score) to each vehicle based on their historical interactions, protocol compliance, and observed performance outcomes. In contrast to binary classification, these trust-aware systems treat security as a graded quantity, which is used to make a system decision. As such, in task offloading it suggests that security risk should influence where and whether a task is executed, rather than being treated as an isolated filtering phase. Then, let be the estimated security risk of vehicle. This allows us to conceptualize risk-aware offloading as a constrained decision process where one can either optimize a set of performance objectives subject to a constraint on acceptable levels of risk, or risk can be incorporated directly in to the decision objective as a penalty term. Such viewpoint lays the foundation for including security signals into the optimization framework we use in SpiralEdge-IoV.

Due to its multi-objective and time-varying nature as an offloading problem, the key to IoV offloading lies in optimization. Latency, energy consumption and execution cost as defined in section III-A, are often contradictory and their relevance changes quickly with mobility and workload situations. As such, bio-inspired metaheuristic optimization techniques have attracted some level of interest within the edge computing literature, due to their ability to search large and complex solution spaces efficiently and without the need for convexity or exact gradient information. These methods are proven especially successful in scenarios where the system states change rapidly, and precise mathematical models are not stable.

Inspired by the natural process, the metaheuristics use swarm intelligence4, evolutionary adaptation, and animal foraging behavior5 to take the advantage of exploration of the new solution space while maintaining the good solution property10. This tradeoff is especially important in IoV scenarios where mobility can induce changes in road and traffic states: too much exploitation may drive the optimizer to converge to solutions that are locally optimal, only to become non-optimal afterwards due to mobility; yet too much exploration may introduce unnecessary overhead and lack of stability. This trade-off is well suited to bio-inspired optimizers since their population-based search mechanisms evolve naturally in changing conditions.

However, the classical optimization-based offloading frameworks either view security as an outside constraint or verify security in a posterior decision step. That division also makes it difficult for them to respond, with sufficient velocity, when they do detect hostile activity. A more principled situation is to interleave the defenses into the optimization for making security analysis to influence which search path is truly desired. In this integrated design, the trust and risk evaluations will continuously impact on the candidate solutions forcing the optimizer to move away from dangerous areas of the decision space while still achieving performance efficiency.

Log spiral dynamics is a mathematically rigorous yet intuitive basis for adaptive trust model. Since logarithmic spirals grow or decay logarithmically, they are suitable to capture the process of gradual rather than sudden trust change and evolution. When mapped into a spiral path the trust boundaries can be expanded for successful benign interactions and limited in a big factor for suspicious deviations. This feature effectively avoids limitations of constant thresholds reflecting the gradualism in containment for vehicular networks.

Keeping in mind the adaptive movement strategies of Addax antelope in arid and changing landscapes, Addax Optimization is selected as the optimization backbone which shows a good balance between global exploration and local exploitation. Such dynamic adaptability makes it an appealing candidate for IoV edge systems, where resource availability and network environments change constantly. SpiralEdge-IoV offers a solid defense–optimizaton framework that co-evolves security awareness and performance optimization by embedding risk derived from spiral into the Addax optimization objective. This integration provides the groundwork for concepts and methods for the LSD-AddaxNet model that is proposed in the next section. Materials and methods

Building on the preliminaries introduced in Sect. 3, this section presents the proposed SpiralEdge-IoV methodology, including the system architecture, logarithmic spiral defense mechanism, and LSD-AddaxNet secure offloading strategy.

Overview of SpiralEdge-IoV framework

The SpiralEdge-IoV framework proposed in this paper is an integrated security–optimization pipeline for adaptive task offloading targeting Internet of Vehicles edge computing environments. Abstract: The framework functions in a continuous decision-making cycle, where vehicular tasks are generated, assessed for security, optimized, and offloaded in the presence of dynamic network, mobility, and adversarial conditions. Modeling the system as an event-driven process in which the arrival of a task or a significant change in state triggers an evaluation process to consider execution efficiency along with the related security risk before committing to an execution decision.

The workflow in our proposed work is demonstrated in Fig. 1, starting from the vehicular layer—vehicles create heterogeneous computation tasks adding context data such as mobility state and communication quality. These requests for tasks are then forwarded on to the edge layer which is where the SpiralEdge-IoV control plane resides. The incoming requests first enter through Logarithmic Spiral Defense (LSD) module, before any optimization is done. This security element dynamically updates trust trajectories for vehicles from previous encounters and KBA, and returns a continuous risk value to express an observed risk level (no accept–reject-switch). With LSD being the entry-point gate-keeper in the architecture, SpiralEdge-IoV ensures that security is a key consideration early in the decision pipeline.

After trust and risk evaluation, the LSD-AddaxNet decision model encodes the optimization core of the framework invoked by the system. LSD-AddaxNet aggregates task parameters, network and resources states and risk scores from the spiral to build a security-aware decision space. The candidate offloading strategies in local, edge, and cloud execution modes are then explored by the Addax Optimization engine, which strongly penalizes high-risk or infeasible options during the search. This close coupling enables optimization to tailor to changes in performance as well as changes in security.

Shown in the last tier of Fig. 1, the cloud layer offers augmentative computing capacity for operations that exceed the capabilities of or require a higher latency than the edge. Edge and cloud servers provide execution feedback (actual delay, energy usage, and success or failure) to the control plane. Such feedback not only updates the perceived trust trajectories kept by LSD but also the state information used by LSD-AddaxNet, transforming SpiralEdge-IoV in a closed-loop adaptive system.

In fact, the security and optimization components in SpiralEdge-IoV perform complementary functions. LSD: provides a real-time, behavior-based risk signal that reflects adversarial and misbehavior effects on driving performance, and LSD-AddaxNet: utilizes LSD to direct multi-objective offloading decisions toward desirable operating points that are safe and efficient. The proposed framework not only goes beyond sequential “secure-then-optimize” designs but also provides a holistic foundation for the resilient task offloading within IoV edge computing through an integrated architecture integrating the above components.

In summary, this system and network model reflects the basic features of IoV edge environments, including heterogeneous computation resources, time-varying wireless links and hierarchical execution choices. These assumptions create a realistic basis for combining the security-aware trust modeling and optimization-based decision making, described in the subsequent sections for Logarithmic Spiral Defense and LSD-AddaxNet offloading strategy.

To make the model analytically tractable and reproducible, we adopt several modeling assumptions which are widely used in the vehicular edge computing literature. Contention at the remote cloud layer is neglected first, because cloud infrastructures normally offer radically more computation capacity than roadside edge servers, meaning that cloud processing delay is driven mostly by transmission and backhaul latency as opposed to compute contention. Second, the backhaul delay between RSUs and the cloud has been modeled as constant or slowly varying because the connection between RSUs and the cloud usually relies on stable wired or fiber backbone links and the delay fluctuations in the backbone are orders of magnitude lower than the access link. Third, the queueing delay q_(i, j) at edge servers abstracts waiting and scheduling delay due to task contention. This delay in our model is the dynamic workloads based total service delay without assuming the type of the scheduling discipline although, it can be instantiated by common queueing models like M/M/1 or priority based schedulers based on deployment scenario. Lastly, it is assumed that decisions about offloading are valid during the execution period of the task. While some handovers might occur in the case of long execution times for a vehicle, such effects are implicitly modeled into the latency model as aggregate delays for transmission and potentially schedule delays of the vehicle. These assumptions allow us to formulate a tractable system that still incorporates some vital aspects of IoV edge environments.

Task and threat modeling

The computation tasks in Internet of Vehicles environments for vehicular applications are heterogeneous due to vast differences in workload characteristics, urgency, and security requirements. In SpiralEdge-IoV, every task generated by vehicle encapsulates an abstraction described by the formulation discussed earlier in Sect. 3.1 and thus reiterated here for clarity. Vehicle movement in the network flows dynamically with time and, hence, the task arrival process exhibits a stochastic nature to model realistic driving and application usages. At each decision epoch, a vehicle has zero ormore pending tasks, and each must be executed locally, offloaded to an edge server, forwarded to the cloud, or rejected when system constraints cannot be met.

Besides the amount of tasks and the deadlines the vehicular tasks have to obey to, these tasks have different levels of sensitivity according to the application context. Safety-critical functions that involve collision avoidance, cooperative perception, or emergency signalling demand both tight latency guarantees and high trust in the execution entities. On the other hand, non-safety tasks such as infotainment analytics and route optimization can afford higher latencies and lower security requirements. SpiralEdge-IoV employs a sensitivity-aware task classification to effectively capture this difference. Every task i has an associated sensitivity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{i}\in\:\{\mathrm{1,2},\dots\:,S\}$$\end{document}, where a larger number means it is more crucial. The level of sensitivity affects the admissible execution options and the degree of risk tolerated: tasks with high are possibly prevented from being offloaded over untrusted paths or to highly congested resources, even if those execution options would represent the optimal solution from a pure performance perspective. With this classification, security considerations can be directly tied to offloading decisions instead of treated as an external filter.

We model the threat such that there exists a number of misbehaving or malicious vehicles that will try to disrupt the operation of the system by trying to abuse the shared edge infrastructure. We assume that adversaries are computationally-bounded and do not corrupt cryptographic primitives or the main infrastructure (e.g., RSUs and cloud servers). Rather, they operate at the vehicle level putting shock waves to signaling and the manner of interaction for tasks. This assumption aligns with realistic IoV applications in that compromised vehicles are more likely to perpetrate insider attacks than direct attacks against well secured infrastructure.

We make several classes of misbehavior, based on patterns seen in the VeReMi dataset. They include false task injection, in which a vehicle creates false or inflated task requests to capture edge resources, replay behavior, in which valid task or status messages are replayed to mislead the state of the system, and resource exhaustion attacks, in which a vehicle continuously injects tasks with tight deadlines or oversized workloads to increase queuing delay for the other vehicles. Time-varying misbehavior is also considered, where a vehicle changes between benign and misbehavior to bypass static detection mechanisms. Failure to mitigate/control these behaviours will consequently impact observed system metrics (e.g., queue lengths, service times, perceived quality of the link etc.), thereby affecting offloading decisions.

To formalize adversarial action, we think of each vehicle as a phantom behavior state that can change over time. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b_i}\left( t \right) \in \left\{ {{\mathrm{benign}},{\mathrm{malicious}}} \right\}$$\end{document} be the latent behaviour at decision epoch t. The system does not observe \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${b_i}\left( t \right)~$$\end{document}directly, and instead infers risk from deltas between expected and observed behavior (e.g., too many tasks arriving at once, or seemingly random changes in performance levels). The discrepancies lead to the risk estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R_i}\left( t \right)~$$\end{document}from the security mechanism, which is then used for decision making. If we see how evolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R_i}\left( t \right)~$$\end{document}is not just based on instantaneous observations but on the interaction history, it allows the system to build up trust over time gradually and to get some trust recovered slowly.

To parameterize and evaluate these misbehavior patterns, we use the VeReMi dataset reference source. To emulate adversarial behavior in the vehicular offloading process, we map attack traces and labeledmisbehavior events from VeReMi onto vehicular task streams. Such mapping enables the proposed framework to simulate the impact of misinformation, replay attacks, and resource misuse on trust dynamics and system effectiveness in a realistic set of scenarios. SpiralEdge-IoV employs a realistic dataset of vehicular misbehavior to ground the threat model in reality, thus bypassing any synthetic or overly simplistic assumptions about attacks and allowing for a robust assessment of security-aware task offloading.

In summary, SpiralEdge-IoV unifies task and threat modeling to incorporate heterogeneity of applications and behavior of adversaries. Our classification of tasks based on sensitivity ensure critical applications are protected properly, while the threat model based on actual vehicular misbehavior data lays a realistic ground for both the security mechanisms and the optimization strategies proposed in later sections.

LSD mechanism (logarithmic spiral defense)

To this end, we propose a Logarithmic Spiral Defense (LSD) mechanism adaptable as well as continuous security layer for IoV edge computing, which takes into account gradual trust evolution of the IoV devices and evades adversarial efforts rather than interrupting the service abruptly. LSD differs from static or linear trust models, whose limited ability to adapt to dynamic situations is often addressed with a single linear trust formulation: LSD captures behavioral dynamics as a nonlinear geometric space that is capable of building up confidence at an accelerated or decelerated rate throughout time. This mechanism functions as an essential part of the closed-loop workflow depicted in Fig. 2, where trust value evaluation directly impacts optimization and decision making.

The Logarithmic Spiral Defense (LSD) model is introduced to overcome the limitations of traditional trust mechanisms in vehicular networks. In contrast, many other trust models use constant thresholds or a binary classification of nodes as trusted or malicious. However, these methods commonly fail to capture the dynamic environment of the IoV network in which vehicle behavior may differ due to mobility, network overload or temporary communication loss. Using the logarithmic spiral formulation allows for trusting to evolve over time, gradually increasing trust if behavior is benign and contracting trust if behavior is repeatedly deviating. This geometric form is experienced to adapt more smoothly in such dynamic behavior change condition and become more resilient to stealthy or on–off misbehavior patterns which have been shown to escape detection by static schemes.

Closed-loop LSD–Addax interaction mechanism

In SpiralEdge-IoV, this close interaction between the Logarithmic Spiral Defense (LSD) and LSD-AddaxNet optimization engine builds a secure and adaptive behavior through a closed-loop interaction mechanism. Instead of working as separate modules, defense and optimization tightly couple iteratively at every decision epoch, allowing the system to adapt continuously to changes in the workload, mobility, and adversarial behavior. The Figure 3 represents the core workflow with respect to the trust evolution, optimization and feedback as a closed control cycle 1 running this interaction loop.

The communication system at the start of each iteration state-aware for the connected vehicular, network and resource environment, along with the execution outcome related to the previous iteration. These observations are passed into the LSD module, which in turn updates the trust trajectory and risk score (described in Sect. 4.4) for each vehicle. The above process does not check for instantaneous anomalies, but instead collects evidence through behavior over time to create an updated risk value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{i}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}. When this risk value is calculated, it is passed to the optimization stage and based on safety signal it governs the search space that is explored during Addax Optimization.

This is part of the closed-loop design, with risk feedback injection being a key differentiator. The risk score generated by LSD is integrated in the form of fitness function (17) and therefore instead of acting as a hard constraint, security has been enforced soft18. At iteration t, the fitness of a candidate offloading decision \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i}\:$$\end{document}is computed based on the latest risk estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{i}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}, with the guarantee that solutions correlated with suspicious behavior are gradually punished. Supervised by LSD, the risk term in the fitness function evolves along with the trust trajectories, enabling the optimizer to move away from unsafe regions of the decision space while also exploring to find (feasible) alternatives.

Below is the summary of the iterative interaction between LSD and Addax Optimization. Initial, feedback from current state and execution provide the basis for calculating deviation indicators and for updating trust trajectories49. Then, composite trust and risk values derived from spiral are injected into calculating the fitness of candidate offloading strategies. Third, the Addax population is updated according to a set of exploration and exploitation rules leading to an improved set of candidate decisions. In the last step, the decision with the best performance is implemented and the observed outcome of this implementation is passed back to the LSD module. This cycle continues for any number of underlying subsequent tasks and decision epochs, allowing for rapid adaptation without restarting the optimization procedure.

Both modules facilitate the adaptation at runtime. For the defence side, the spiral trust boundary smoothly enlarges and shrinks as interactions are gained, allowing the system to absorb temporary disruptions but respond strongly to ongoing failure of behavior. From the optimization perspective, the population-based nature of Addax Optimization allows candidate solutions to adapt to evolving risk profiles and resource availability. The second approach provides real-time adaptation to the control logic, making SpiralEdge-IoV constantly responsive to rapidly changing IoV environments in which static policies become irrelevant in a short time.

Algorithmic representation

In this section, we provide a mathematical representation of the SpiralEdge-IoV framework proposed in Section IIIwhich formalizes the Logarithmic Spiral Defense, the LSD-AddaxNet secure offloading strategy, as well as their closed-loop interaction. The algorithms enable a compact and reproducible representation of the key components of trust modeling, security-aware optimization, and runtime adaptation, and consequently, facilitate the transparent implementation and systematic validation of the secure task offloading approach.

Algorithm 1Logarithmic Spiral Defense (LSD) for Trust and Risk Modeling

Logarithmic Spiral Defense (LSD) for Trust and Risk Modeling

In IoV edge environments, Algorithm 1 formalizes the Logarithmic Spiral Defense mechanism used to model trust evolution and the accompanying security risk. It uses logarithmic spiral based formulation to maintain smooth trust trajectory for each vehicular agent, enabling trust expansion or contraction over even sequential behaviours. At each decision epoch, execution outcomes and protocol compliance give rise to deviation indicators that are aggregated to update the spiral progression angle. To put it another way, this updates the spiral radius, which is its current trust boundary. Whether a trust score is normalized to a bounded value, the correspondent risk value is computed as its complement. It captures accumulated behavioral characteristic rather than binary (trusted/ust) behavior and thus does not suffer from sharp behaviour transition and is also robust to short term fluctuations or on–off attacks as opposed to threshold based mechanisms multiple external factors, such as environmental events, organizational changes and competition in the market. According to the calculated risk, the algorithm specifies a level of security action from providing normal service to leaving the access restricted or isolated. Instead, it implements a gradual containment strategy that keeps false positives low, whilst helps ensure corrections are made for persistent misbehaviour. And the outputs of Algorithm 1 are security inputs for the optimization process.

Algorithm 2LSD-AddaxNet secure offloading optimization

LSD-AddaxNet secure offloading optimization

The main secure offloading decision process implemented in LSD-AddaxNet is described in Algorithm 2. The algorithm starts with the construction of an exhaustive feature vector, which merges the corresponding task characteristics, network and resources states, mobility features, and trust signals extracted from the LSD mechanism. With this feature representation, we use Addax Optimization for search over the discrete decision space that is found by local, edge, cloud, or reject-based execution. Each Addax agent represents a candidate offloading action and its quality is evaluated using a multi-objective fitness function that takes into account latency, energy consumption, execution cost, and security risk as derived from the spiral. Unlike the state-of-the-art rarely method, the algorithm directly embeds risk in the fitness formulation, thus penalizing unsafe decisions in the optimization process instead of filtering them out propagating the optimization process. These are mostly feasibility checks that ensure that latency deadlines, resource capacity constraints, and minimum trust requirements are satisfied. The proposed algorithm iteratively explores and exploits the offloading decision space to reach a stable and secure offloading decision that provides an effective trade-off between the performance objectives and the security requirements of the dynamic vehicular mobile environment.

Algorithm 3Closed-Loop LSD–Addax interaction and policy adaptation

Closed-Loop LSD–Addax interaction and policy adaptation

In a similar way, the last major work thread of Algorithm 3 merges the defense and optimization parts to form one closed-loop control mechanism that supports runtime adaptation. The algorithm works in a loop, starting with observation of the current state of the system and feedback from tasks that have already been carried out. The feedback is then utilized to update trust trajectories and risk scores through Algorithm 1, which satisfy these behavioral variations. The updated risk values are then injected into the fitness evaluation of the Algorithm2 to ensure that the optimization process is conducted on the latest security information. An identified offloading decision once taken and executed results in observed outcomes that are fed back into the system in order to affect future trust updates and the optimization steps. This feedback-loop to feedback-loop mechanism allows the framework to respond to changes (workload, mobility, and adversarial behavior) without the need for refactoring the optimization process. We define convergence as no difference in policy or minor improvement in fitness, which prevents more computation overhead. So, SpiralEdge-IoV to co-evolve with security awareness and performance optimization with resilience and adaptation, is made possible through Algorithm 3.

Implementation details

We have implemented and evaluated the SpiralEdge-IoV frame work over a simulation driven environment that represents a realistic Internet of Vehicles edge computing environment. Discrete decision epochs are used to model vehicular mobility, task arrivals, and network dynamics whereby updates of the system state and decisions are triggered by the arrivals of new tasks or by the significant change of the system state. The simulation reflects practical deployment scenarios by incorporating movement patterns for vehicles, RSU coverage, wireless link dynamics, and edge server workload dynamics. All simulations are run in a workstation-class setup and repeated with independent runs with different random seeds to provide statistical validation of the results.

We choose argumented values for system parameters in line with common IoV and vehicular edge computing scenarios from literature. The vehicle CPU frequency are assumed to be within a bounded range which represents the amount of processing unit available onboard a vehicle whilst edge servers are provisioned with greater but still a finite amount of computation capacity to incorporate realistic contention after deployment. The uplink wireless data rates are characterized as time-varying rates due to mobility and channel conditions at the edge, while the backhaul latency of edge servers connecting the cloud is considered as a stable but non-negligible latency. We preprocess the primary VEC Edge Server Offloading dataset to obtain the task arrival rates and workload distributions, to ensure that the task characteristics used for the simulation input mimic the real-world scenario.

Within LSD-AddaxNet, the Addax Optimisation component adopts a population-based search strategy. The population size is set to allow sufficient exploration without being too computationally intensive, with standard sizes ranging from 20 to 40 candidate solutions per decision epoch. We set a reasonable upper limit on the number of optimisation iterations per epoch to allow each epoch to converge to the solution without taking too long, and we enable early stopping when convergence conditions are met. Exploration and exploitation control parameters are initialised according to recommended Addax Optimisation settings and can be dynamically adjusted at runtime, allowing them to respond to changes in network and security conditions.

Parameter selection for trust models of Logarithmic Spiral Defence enables smooth, interpretable trust evolution. The initial trust scale defines the initial trust value for newly encountered vehicles, and the spiral growth rate determines how quickly trust can grow or shrink based on behavioural evidence. To avoid excessive reaction to temporary abnormalities, the learning rate controlling the trust trajectory updates is assigned a small positive value. The model defines two risk thresholds: a caution threshold that activates restricted service actions, and an isolation threshold that activates task rejection or temporary removal from the platform. The parameters here are tuned empirically on a validation set to balance false positives versus misses.

In terms of implementation, they are based on realistic communication scenarios and maintain consistency with the reproducibility and fair assessment objectives of ours. The experimental setup provides a clear means of isolating the performance and security improvements achieved with our proposed SpiralEdge-IoV design model by defining simulation settings, optimisation parameters, and trust model configurations.

Experimental results

This section describes the experimental evaluation of the SpiralEdge-IoV framework as proposed by us. It evaluates both performance efficiency and security robustness using realistic vehicular offloading traces and misbehaviour data. It is split into sections that discuss: benefits of latency, energy, and cost; resilience to adversarial conditions; and comparison and ablation analyses that confirm the validity of the logarithmic spiral defence and LSD-AddaxNet optimisation. In addition to evaluating latency and energy performance, the experiments analyze the behavior of the LSD trust mechanism by observing trust trajectory stability and the system’s ability to reduce the impact of malicious task requests injected from the VeReMi dataset.

Experimental setup and evaluation protocol

A simulation-driven setup that emulates a realistic Internet of Vehicles edge computing environment is used to evaluate SpiralEdge-IoV. It includes vehicular mobility, wireless communication, and computation offloading to vehicles, RSU-based edge servers, and a remote cloud. System parameters, including the operational scale of vehicles’ CPUs, edge server computation capacity, wireless data rates, and backhaul latency, are configured to model common IoV configurations. During each decision epoch in the simulations, tasks arrive, the status of each device is observed, trust levels are updated, and decisions about local or external offloading are made. The results are obtained by averaging the multiple independent runs with different random seeds, ensuring statistical robustness.

It is modeled based on the VEC Edge Server Offloading dataset64, which is employed as the main source for modeling the properties of tasks, execution delays and the patterns of resource utilization. The second dataset is divided into training and test sets in a common manner, where the train set is used to compute the parameters related to optimization and trust-based analysis parameters and the test set is only used for performance evaluation. The VeReMi dataset is only used for testing to create adversarial behavior65, no VeReMi data is used to train or tune parameters. It eliminates information leakage and thus enables fair evaluation of security generalization on unseen misbehavior patterns.

We choose the baseline methods as common offloading and security strategies for IoV and edge computing. A local-only execution scheme, an edge-only offloading scheme, a latency-aware but security-unaware offloading strategy, and a trust-based offloading approach with static thresholds are among them. To isolate the contribution of LSD, we also include an optimization-based baseline that does not include the spiral defense mechanism. To ensure fair comparison, all baselines are implemented under the same simulation environment,

The evaluation process evaluates both performance efficiency and security robustness together. Transportation system performance is measured by average task latency, energy consumption, execution cost, and task success rate under different vehicle densities and workload intensities. This includes both inter-domain and intra-domain trust evolution t, risk score dynamics s, and robustness of the system for misbehavior injected from VeReMi65. Security and robustness analysis under adversarial conditions

In this subsection, the evaluation of the security resilience of SpiralEdge-IoV is conducted when the IoV edge environment is subjected to adversarial and malicious conditions extracted from the VeReMi dataset65. We are using VeReMi to inject realistic vehicular misbehaviour patterns into the execution loop (at the testing stage). In contrast, performance learning and parameter calibration (at the training stage) target the VEC offloading traces64. Our goal is to demonstrate that the developed LSD and LSD-AddaxNet can effectively maintain the correct offloading decisions against attempts made by adversarial vehicles to alter task admission, reduce trust or deplete resources.

Misbehaviour cases are set up according to four common attack types corresponding to the nature and behaviour of VeReMi labels: false task injection (malicious vehicles diffuses over-claimed or irrelevant tasks), replay behaviour (replay of past-vlaid messages), resource exhaustion (high-rate and tight-deadline task submissions to saturate edge queues), and on-off misbehaviour (benign vs. malicious actions to escape detection based on thresholds). For these scenarios with different attacker ratios (e.g., 10%, 20%, 30% of malicious vehicles), the system response is evaluated based on trust trajectories, risk score dynamics, and service outcomes.

The trust evolution in SpiralEdge-IoV experiences as gradual and continuous transformation as the logarithmic spiral generates it. In benign behaviour situations, trust grows slowly and stabilises, resulting in low, stable risk scores. In misbehaviour, deviations lead to a spiral contraction that raises risk step by step rather than nondifferentiably. This allows for gradual containment: vehicles that are progressively detected as more suspicious are moved to restricted modes of operation (e.g., local-execution preference or limited edge access), and isolation occurs only when the risk exceeds an isolation threshold (d). On the other hand, the static-trust baselines often demonstrate unstable behaviour, with trust oscillating markedly due to temporary anomalies, and fail to detect attackers that hover near the threshold while attacking.

The effect of adversarial behaviour is quantified as the task success rate under attack, which is the ratio of tasks completed within the deadline and that comply with security policy feasibility constraints. For base (a), SpiralEdge-IoV sustains higher success rates as the risky offloading decisions are punished during optimisation, which discourages DDWL patterns from maliciously occupying edge resources. In our simulation results with the non-secure baselines, queue saturation under resource depletion of on–off attackers leads to dramatic performance degradations, and static-trust methods are prone to frequently misclassifying on-off attackers and fail in providing repeated service interruption.

Discussion

The explosive growth of the Internet of Vehicles (IoV) and vehicular edge computing has further increased the need for intelligent task offloading solutions that meet ultra-high-reliability latency requirements and withstand adversarial behaviours. Related work has provided a solid basis for performance-driven offloading with optimisation, deep learning, and reinforcement learning. However, most of the literature has implicitly assumed benign settings or used static trust thresholds and post hoc security analyses. These assumptions become weak in dynamic IoV environments, where mobility, disconnection, and malicious behaviour (e.g., false task insertion, replay attacks/denial of service) are prevalent.

A key gap in the state of the art is the lack of close integration between security and the offloading decision process. The flexibility of deep learning and DRL-based methods comes at the cost of remaining susceptible to manipulative attacks and adversarial inputs when security is decoupled from learning. Likewise, integrity is enhanced by several blockchain-based trust solutions, including those that mitigate excessive latency and computational overhead, thereby reducing their susceptibility to rapidly evolving attacks. These shortcomings call for learning-driven frameworks that can integrate security indicators to enable intelligent, real-time decision-making.

We attempt to close the gap with a novel approach, SpiralEdge-IoV, which incorporates a geometrically inspired Logarithmic Spiral Defence (LSD) closely coupled with a bio-inspired Addax optimisation engine, LSD-AddaxNet. In contrast with static approaches to trust establishment, the spiral-based defence views trust as a dynamic path. It allows for a gradual, risk-aware adjustment rather than simple acceptance or rejection. A major novelty is that we include the generated risk scores directly in the optimisation fitness, so that security considerations participate in the offloading decision process rather than simply being an add-on.

The tests are further quantified to confirm the construction’s performance. Performance evaluations indicate that SpiralEdge-IoV experiences lower latency, consumes less energy, and incurs lower execution cost than its counterparts, even under higher vehicle density or greater workload demands. More significantly, security and robustness tests against VeReMi-based adversaries demonstrate the persistence of task success rate and trust growth, verifying the advantage of closed-loop correlation between defence and optimisation. Ablation and convergence studies also demonstrate that these gains result from the joint effect of LSD, Addax optimisation, and risk-aware fitness integration.

While our proposed LSD-AddaxNet framework is indeed adapted for dynamic IoV environments to support executive decision-making in day-to-day situations, its computational overhead remains a major concern in extreme real-time scenarios. Most of the significant overhead is due to the iterative Addax optimisation process and the continuous trust–risk updates conducted by the LSD module. However, this overhead is mitigated in practice by running the decision engine at the edge control layer rather than on resource-constrained vehicles, thereby allowing moderate population sizes and iteration counts for optimisation. However, as situational awareness becomes more volatile amid high-density traffic and rapid workload changes, decision latency could increase, warranting restrictions during deployment.

Lastly, the provided framework should be aligned with the state of the art in emerging distributed offloading paradigms. Alternative offloading frameworks include federated learning-based schemes that enhance privacy and decentralised adaptation by training models collaboratively across multiple vehicles or edge nodes, and digital twin-assisted offloading frameworks that enhance decision accuracy by leveraging virtual representations of network and mobility states. By contrast, SpiralEdge-IoV tightly couples behaviour-driven trust evolution with optimisation-based task offloading in adversarial settings. Hence, it focuses mainly on decision-making at all security-aware, attack-responsive nodes, and its main benefit lies here. In contrast, lower federated and digital twin approaches are primarily differentiated by distributed intelligence, privacy, or predictive system modelling. Hybridisation of these paradigms with SpiralEdge-IoV in the future will be explored to enhance scalability and real-time within this paradigm.

Although the framework performs effectively, a comprehensive exploration of its limitations and potential avenues for future work is deliberately postponed to Sect. 6.1, where the scope of the present work and avenues for further development are carefully delineated.

Limitations of the study

While SpiralEdge-IoV shows significant performance and security advantages, this paper identifies several limitations. To begin with, the assessment is primarily based on simulations and uses public datasets to model task offloading and misbehaviour. Although the datasets are widely used and well-suited to controlled experimentation, we argue that they will not sufficiently characterise the complexity, heterogeneity, and inherent unpredictability of a real-world IoV deployment. Real-world environments can affect the evolution of trust and optimisation behaviour (e.g., through imperfect sensing, hardware variability, and network congestion).

Second, the adversarial model used in this work is based on known misbehaviours identified in the VeReMi dataset. While this provides a standardised and realistic basis for evaluating security, it does not cover additional attack vectors, such as adaptive or coordinated adversaries that evolve in response to the defence strategy. This leaves open the question of how well the logarithmic spiral defence generalises against completely novel or mutating attack patterns, an open question for further research.

The third is the sense that the current implementation presumes centralised or quasi-centralised decision-making associated with RSU-based edge controllers. This design choice allows for easier coordination and optimisation, but may not scale or be resilient in cases with an extremely large number of items or when infrastructure is constrained. Future work could investigate the fully distributed or hierarchical variants of the proposed framework to increase fault tolerance and deployment flexibility.

However, while performance is evaluated on realistic public datasets and simulation-based environments, real IoV deployments might introduce additional complexity that is hard to capture in simulation. In particular, non-ideal sensing scenarios, heterogeneous on-board hardware capabilities, random wireless interference, and abrupt traffic stalls degrade communication and computation performance. The datasets used feature representative metrics for vehicular workloads and misbehaviour patterns; however, practical deployments may introduce additional variability. Hence, in the future, we will explore prototype implementations and real testbed validation to evaluate the robustness and scalability of the SpiralEdge-IoV framework across a wide range of operational parameters.

The adversarial sample generation for this special issue is grounded in misbehaviour patterns from the VeReMi dataset, capturing typical vehicular attack scenarios such as external falsified information and internal malicious message injection. Although these datasets offer realistic and widely used benchmarks for assessing vehicular security mechanisms, they do not necessarily capture adaptive or coordinated attack strategies that evolve in response to the applied defence mechanisms. However, in real-world IoV settings, adversaries can dynamically change their behaviour to evade detection systems. Thus, future work will apply more complex threat models, such as adaptive adversary strategies and coordinated multi-node attacks, to assess and improve the resilience of the SpiralEdge-IoV framework.

Conclusion and future scope

In this paper, we present SpiralEdge-IoV, a secure, adaptive task offloading framework for Internet of Vehicles edge computing that couples logarithmic-spiral-based trust modelling with bio-inspired Addax optimisation. To address this motivation, the proposed framework establishes a tightly coupled security and optimisation loop with an interaction mechanism, thereby overcoming limitations of state-of-the-art static-trust and performance-centric approaches. SpiralEdge-IoV represents trust not as a static scalar value but as a dynamic trajectory spiralling through a trust space, and embeds risk awareness directly into the offloading decision-making process, enabling attack-resilient, latency-efficient, and energy-aware execution of tasks in high-dynamic vehicular environments. Extensive experimental evaluation using realistic offloading traces and adversarial behaviours from the VeReMIDataset reveals that SpiralEdge-IoV consistently outperforms the representative baseline methods. Experiments validate our theoretical analysis, showing reduced task latency, lower energy and execution costs, and a very significant improvement in the malicious task success rate. Ablation, convergence, and scalability analyses also confirm that the gains stem from a synergistic combination of the logarithmic spiral defence and Addax optimisation, rather than from isolated ingredients. These results are significant for the design of future IoV edge systems and serve to validate the effectiveness of security-aware optimisation as an embodiment of a foundational design principle. Based on these contributions, we outline several exciting avenues for future work below—deploying SpiralEdge-IoV in real vehicular testbeds or hardware-in-the-loop scenarios is a first step towards validating it under realistic network dynamics and operational constraints. Secondly, the robustness of the defence methods could be enhanced by generalising the adversary model to adaptive, coordinated, or learning-based attack methods. IOV, which has one or more physical networks, may improve scalability and fault tolerance with decentralised or hierarchical variants of the framework to distribute higher workloads or risks. Lastly, deploying lightweight deep learning predictors to predict workload spikes or to rate the probability of an event being an attack can make decisions more proactive. Collectively, these extensions provide a path to building high-performance, intelligent, and trustworthy vehicular edge computing systems.
