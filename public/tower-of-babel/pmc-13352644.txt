SLAMF8 promotes tumor growth of gastric cancer by enhancing M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages
Authors: Yi Wu, Xudong Zhang, Zixiang Tang, Fangjian Li, Lin Wu, He Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe signaling lymphocytic activation molecule family member 8 (SLAMF8) is predominantly expressed on the surface of macrophages and participates in modulating tumor immune microenvironment. However, the role of SLAMF8 in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in gastric cancer (GC) remains unclear.MethodsSLAMF8 expression and its association with patient prognosis were analyzed using various online databases. CCK-8 and EdU assays were used to assess GC cell proliferation. A xenograft tumor model was used to assess the in vivo functional role of SLAMF8.ResultsSLAMF8 expression was markedly increased and correlated with an unfavorable prognosis in GC. Additionally, SLAMF8 was primarily expressed in macrophages in GC tissues and increased in in vitro–generated-TAMs. SLAMF8 knockdown suppressed M2 polarization, whereas SLAMF8 overexpression promoted M2 polarization in in vitro–generated TAMs. SLAMF8 facilitates TAM-like polarization by coordinately inhibiting NF-κB activation and activating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Furthermore, SLAMF8-overexpressing TAMs enhanced the growth of MKN-45 cells both in vitro and in vivo and impaired CD8 + T cell function.ConclusionsSLAMF8 promotes macrophage M2 polarization and accelerates CD8 + T cell dysfunction, thereby facilitating the progression of GC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08130-0.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Macrophage polarization, Immune microenvironment, SLAMF8

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common cancer and the fourth major contributor to cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. This disease often develops quietly, with symptoms that are either vague or absent in its early stages. As a result, many patients are not diagnosed until the cancer has advanced, frequently spreading to lymph nodes or distant organs. At this stage, surgical options become limited, and the prognosis tends to be unfavorable [2, 3]. Apart from surgical intervention, chemotherapy remains the primary therapeutic strategy for advanced gastric cancer, often combined with immunotherapy, targeted therapies, and various multimodal approaches [4]. Nevertheless, the availability of non-surgical therapeutic approaches for advanced gastric cancer is frequently constrained by the tumor heterogeneity and the complex biological nature. Thus, identifying novel targeted therapies to improve patient outcomes remains a critical priority in the ongoing fight against gastric cancer.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) constitute a highly diverse and heterogeneous population within the tumor microenvironment (TME), sometimes comprising up to 50% of certain solid tumors. They play pivotal roles in tumor growth, immune modulation, metastatic spread, and chemoresistance [5–7]. Upon activation, macrophages can differentiate into two primary phenotypes: M1 (characterized by CD11b and CD86 expression) and M2 (marked by CD206 and CD163 expression) [8]. Typically, M1 macrophages elicit inflammatory responses targeting pathogens and cancer cells by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and CXCL10. Conversely, M2 macrophages produce anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10, IL-4, and TGF-β, which suppress immune activity while facilitating tissue regeneration and tumor development. In the GC microenvironment, TAMs predominantly exhibit an M2-polarized phenotype associated with tumor promotion and significantly contributing to disease progression [9]. Inhibiting M2 polarization of TAMs may hinder GC progression [10–12]. Subtypes of TAMs that warrant further investigation require more detailed exploration and analysis. Overall, TAMs display high plasticity, and understanding their phenotypic reprogramming may open new avenues for developing novel and effective tumor immunotherapies.

The signaling lymphocytic activation molecule family member 8 (SLAMF8) is the eighth receptor in the SLAMF co-stimulatory family and plays a critical role in mediating inflammation. Members of this family are essential for modulating the growth and activity of T lymphocytes, B cells, and macrophages [13, 14]. SLAMF8 expression is significantly elevated in multiple autoimmune inflammatory diseases and various types of tumors [15–17], highlighting its essential involvement in immune-driven inflammatory processes and the development of certain malignancies. Moreover, SLAMF8 is primarily localized on the cell membrane of macrophages and has been suggested as a promising biomarker for predicting the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint therapy in treating gastrointestinal malignancies [18]. However, the precise function of SLAMF8 in TAMs in GC remains unclear.

This study aimed to explore the function of SLAMF8 in macrophage polarization and the immunosuppressive microenvironment in GC. We observed a significantly elevated expression of SLAMF8 in in vitro–generated TAMs and SLAMF8 overexpression was found to promote M2 polarization of TAMs, leading to CD8 + T cell dysfunction and enhanced malignant growth of GC cells. Our findings highlight the biological significance of SLAMF8 and clarify its role in shaping the tumor microenvironment.

Materials and methods

SLAMF8 expression analysis

The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) is a publicly accessible, NIH-curated repository for functional genomics data submitted by the global research community [19]. RNA expression data from the GSE146996, GSE184336, GSE179252, and GSE192468 datasets, which include both normal and tumor tissue samples, were downloaded. SLAMF8 expression values were extracted from these datasets and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 7.0.

Analysis of clinicopathologic features

MEXPRESS (https://mexpress.be/) is an interactive web tool for the integrated visualization of gene expression, DNA methylation, and clinical data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [20, 21]. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the association between SLAMF8 expression levels and either copy number alterations or CpG-site DNA methylation.

Survival analysis of SLAMF8

The Kaplan–Meier Plotter (http://kmplot.com/) enables survival analysis across more than 40,000 tumor samples spanning 21 cancer types [22]. This database was used to investigate the association between SLAMF8 expression and clinical outcomes in gastric cancer. Using the median expression as the cutoff, patients were divided into high- and low-expression groups, and survival analyses—including overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and post-progression survival (PPS)—were performed. Statistical significance was assessed using the log-rank test, with p-values reported accordingly. Six GEO datasets, including GSE14210 (n = 145), GSE15459 (n = 200), GSE22377 (n = 43), GSE29272 (n = 268), GSE51105 (n = 94), and GSE62254 (n = 300), were selected for survival analysis.

Tumor immune estimation resource (TIMER) database

The TIMER database (https://compbio.cn/timer3/) is a widely used web-based platform that enables systematic evaluation of tumor-infiltrating immune cell composition across diverse cancer types [23]. We used the Exploration module to compare SLAMF8 expression levels in tumor tissues with those in adjacent normal tissues. Using the Immune module, we investigated the association between SLAMF8 expression and macrophage infiltration levels. TIDE (Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion) is a computational framework designed to quantify two major immune evasion mechanisms in the tumor microenvironment—T-cell dysfunction and T-cell exclusion—and to predict patient responses to immune checkpoint blockade [24, 25]. Specifically, the TIDE dysfunction score reflects the degree of functional impairment in tumor-infiltrating T cells. Given that TIDE scores are publicly available through the TIMER database, we investigated the association between SLAMF8 expression and the TIDE dysfunction score in STAD.

The Human Protein Atlas

The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) integrates proteomics, transcriptomics, and systems biology approaches to comprehensively map the spatial expression of human proteins across cells, tissues, and organs [26]. The HPA platform was used to examine SLAMF8 protein expression in normal stomach tissues (n = 6) and stomach adenocarcinoma tissues (n = 10) based on immunohistochemical data. Additionally, the Single Cell tool was used to assess the cell-type-specific enrichment of SLAMF8 across multiple normal tissues.

Single-cell sequencing analysis

The Tumor Immune Single Cell Hub 2 (TISCH2) (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/) is a single-cell RNA-seq resource for human and mouse tumors that enables gene expression profiling of the tumor microenvironment [27]. In this study, TISCH2 was used to explore SLAMF8 expression profiles across various cell populations in the STAD GSE167297 dataset.

Cell lines and in vitro culture conditions

Human peripheral blood monocytes (PBMCs, CD14 positivity > 90%) and the gastric cancer cell line MKN-45 were purchased from Pricella (Wuhan, China). MKN-45 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. PBMCs were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 25 ng/mL M-CSF for 7 days to promote differentiation into macrophages (M0). The culture medium was refreshed every 3 days to remove non-adherent cells. Subsequently, M0 macrophages and MKN-45 cells were co-cultured using a transwell system to induce TAMs (in vitro–generated TAMs). In these co-culture assays, MKN-45 cells were seeded in the upper chamber of the transwell insert (Corning), while M0 macrophages were placed in the lower compartment. The authenticity of MKN-45 cells was confirmed by short tandem repeat analysis prior to experimentation. All cell lines employed in this study were tested for mycoplasma contamination using a commercial kit (40612ES60, Yeasen).

SLAMF8 knockdown and overexpression

Small hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting SLAMF8 (sh-SLAMF8-1: GCAACTTCTCCGTGTTGAT; sh-SLAMF8-2: GAAGGCCTCCTACAAAGAT) and a scrambled negative control (shNC) were synthesized by GenePharma (Suzhou, China). The shRNAs were inserted downstream of the U6 promoter into the pLKO.1 plasmid. The recombinant lentiviruses were produced by transiently transfecting pLKO.1/sh-SLAMF8-1, pLKO.1/sh-SLAMF8-2, or pLKO.1/sh-NC, into HEK293T cells along with the packaging plasmid psPAX2 and the envelope plasmid pMD2.G, using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Lentiviral particles were harvested from the culture medium 48 h post-transfection and subsequently concentrated via ultracentrifugation.

The coding sequence of human SLAMF8 (GenBank GI: 115344337) was amplified by PCR from a human PBMC cDNA library, cloned into the lentiviral expression vector pCDH-EF1-MCS-T2A-Puro (Shanghai Gaining Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and validated as the recombinant plasmid pCDH-SLAMF8 by Sanger sequencing. The pCDH-SLAMF8 plasmid or empty vector was transfected into HEK293T cells together with pPACKH1 Packaging Plasmid Mix (System Biosciences, California, USA) to produce viral particles using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). After 48 h, lentiviral particles were collected and concentrated from the supernatant by ultracentrifugation. All lentiviral vectors (including the shRNA vectors targeting SLAMF8 and the SLAMF8 overexpression vector) were prepared by GenePharma.

To generate SLAMF8-overexpressing cells, TAMs were transduced with lentiviral particles carrying SLAMF8 overexpression vectors for 48 h. The transduced cells were then selected with 4 µg/mL puromycin (Gibco, A1113802, USA) for 3 days to eliminate uninfected cells. Surviving cells were maintained in culture medium containing 2 µg/mL puromycin throughout subsequent experiments. The lentiviral vector achieved long-term and stable overexpression of SLAMF8 by integrating it into the host genome. Similarly, for SLAMF8 knockdown, TAMs were transduced with lentiviral particles carrying shRNAs targeting SLAMF8 for 48 h, followed by puromycin selection. Transduction efficiency was assessed 48 h after lentiviral transduction using RT-qPCR and western blotting. SLAMF8 overexpression increased mRNA levels by ~ 21-fold and protein levels by ~ 2.8-fold compared to controls (Figure S1A–B). Conversely, knockdown of SLAMF8 using two independent shRNAs resulted in ~ 80% reduction in SLAMF8 mRNA levels and ~ 60% reduction in SLAMF8 protein levels ( Figure S1C–D).

Reverse Transcription quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription using the BeyoRT™ III First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Beyotime). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the BeyoFast™ SYBR Green qPCR Mix (2×) (Beyotime). The primers were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Suzhou, China), with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene for normalization. Primers used for qPCR were as follows: SLAMF8 forward-TCTCTGCCTGGCACTGGT, reverse‐TGTCTCTGGACCCACTCTGT; CD11b forward‐AGAACACTGGCATGTGGGAG, reverse‐GGCACACGGACACCTGG; CD68 forward‐GCTACTTTGCTGCCATCCTT, reverse‐GAACCGTGGCATTTCCATGAC; iNOS-forward‐TCTTGGTCAAAGCTGTGCTC, reverse‐CATTGCCAAACGTACTGGTC; CD86-forward‐GGAAGAAGAAGAAGCGGCCT, reverse‐AAACACGCTGGGCTTCATCA; CD206-forward‐ACCTGCGACAGTAAACGAGG, reverse‐TGTCTCCGCTTCATGCCATT; CD163-forward‐GCGGGAGAGTGGAAGTGAAA, reverse‐ACCTGCACTGGAATTAGCCC; ARG1-forward‐GGAAGTGAACCCATCCCTGG, reverse‐CGAGCAAGTCCGAAACAAGC;

PDCD1-forward-CCAGGATGGTTCTTAGACTCCC, reverse‐TTTAGCACGAAGCTCTCCGAT; TIGIT-forward‐TCTGCATCTATCACACCTACCC, reverse‐CCACCACGATGACTGCTGT; HAVCR2-forward‐TCCAAGGATGCTTACCACCAG, reverse‐GCCAATGTGGATATTTGTGTTAGATT; LAG3-forward‐GCGGGGACTTCTCGCTATG, reverse‐GGCTCTGAGAGATCCTGGGG; β-actin-forward‐GCATGGGTCAGAAGGATTCCT, reverse‐TCGTCCCAGTTGGTGACGAT.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed, and protein concentrations were measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of total protein from each sample were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies against SLAMF8 (6A4E2C4, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), p65 (ab16502, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), NF-κB p65 (phospho S536, ab86299, Abcam), JAK2 (ab108596, Abcam), STAT3 (10253-2-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), STAT3 (phospho Tyr705, ab76315, Abcam), JAK2 (phospho Tyr1007/1008, 44-426G, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and β-actin (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech), followed by appropriate HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (ab6721, Abcam) or goat anti-mouse (ab205719, Abcam) secondary antibodies. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence detection.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining of tumor tissue sections was carried out as previously described [28]. For cyto-immunofluorescence analysis, TAMs were cultured in six-well plates containing pre-positioned 24 mm glass coverslips. Non-specific binding was blocked using goat serum (ab7481, Abcam) and 0.3% hydrogen peroxide solution. The tissue sections and coverslips were then incubated with primary antibodies targeting SLAMF8 (6A4E2C4, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and CD206 (ab300621, Abcam). After extensive washing, the GC tissues and in vitro–generated TAMs were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies: goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (ab150113, Abcam) for SLAMF8 detection and goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 647) (ab150079, Abcam) for CD206 detection. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime).

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Human IFN-γ and TNF-α ELISA kits (E-EL-H0108 and E-EL-H0109, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) were used to determine the levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α. Additionally, the concentrations of TGF-β1, IL-10, CXCL10, and IL-6 released by in vitro–generated TAMs were assessed using human TGF-β1 ELISA kit (RAB0460, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), human IL-10 ELISA kit (RAB0244, Sigma-Aldrich), human CXCL10 ELISA kit (RAB0119, Sigma-Aldrich), and human IL-6 ELISA kit (EZIL-6, Sigma-Aldrich).

Flow cytometry

For macrophage surface marker detection, the cells were incubated with mouse monoclonal CD11b antibody conjugated to PE/Cy7® (ab2269334, Abcam) or mouse monoclonal CD206 antibody conjugated to PE/Cy7® (ab270682, Abcam) for 30 min in the dark, followed by two washes with cold PBS. Mouse IgG1 isotype control conjugated to PE/Cy7® (ab239464, Abcam) was used. Flow cytometry (Agilent, NovoCyte Advanteon Dx VBR, San Jose, CA, USA) was used to quantify the different macrophage subpopulations.

Cell viability

A total of 5,000 MKN-45 cells were uniformly distributed into the wells of 96-well culture plates and subsequently treated with conditioned medium derived from TAMs. Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime) after incubation for 2 h. Cell viability was examined at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h post-seeding, according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) proliferation assay

The proliferation capacity was examined using the BeyoClick™ EdU-594 Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (C0078S, Beyotime). Following a 48-hour exposure to conditioned medium from TAMs, MKN-45 cells were cultured with 10 µM EdU (Beyotime) for 2 h. Cells were then fixed with Immunol Staining Fix Solution (Beyotime) for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and subsequently incubated with Click-iT® reaction mixture for 30 min in the dark. Finally, after nuclear staining with DAPI, fluorescence images were captured utilizing a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Animal experiments

Female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, weighing approximately 18–22 g) were purchased from PhenoTek Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, constant temperature (22 ± 2 °C), and humidity (50 ± 10%), with free access to sterilized food and water. Mice were randomly allocated to two groups (n = 5 per group). A total of 5106 MKN-45 cells, alone or combined with 2105 SLAMF8-overexpressing TAMs, were suspended in 100 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor development was assessed every 3 days and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width²)/2. After 4 weeks, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The tumors were excised, weighed, and processed for further analysis. Immunohistochemical staining was performed to assess Ki-67 expression levels in the xenograft tumors. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College (No. NSMC[2025]032) and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines and the international ethical standards for laboratory animal care and use.

In vitro cytotoxic T-cell killing assay

Naïve CD8 + T cells were subsequently purified using an EasySep Human CD8 + T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada). Human CD8 + T cells were activated by incubation with T-activator CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the presence of human IL-2 at a concentration of 10 ng/mL. LDH release was quantified using an LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, C0016). Activated CD8 + T cells were co-cultured with TAMs for 48 h using a Transwell system, with TAMs in the lower compartment and CD8 + T cells in the upper chamber. Subsequently, the co-cultured CD8 + T cells were collected and then co-incubated with MKN-45 cells for 24 h. The supernatant from MKN-45 cells was collected for LDH concentration analysis. Maximum LDH release was induced by treating the cells with 1% Triton X-100, and the percentage of LDH release was calculated.

Statistical analysis

All data were systematically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 7.0. To assess the significance of differences between two groups, the parametric Student’s t-test was conducted for pairwise comparisons, while one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for analyses involving more than two groups. Data are presented as mean values ± standard deviation (SD), and P < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Results

SLAMF8 is upregulated in GC

To evaluate the expression profile of SLAMF8 in cancer tissues, we used the TIMER database for validation. The analysis revealed a marked elevation in SLAMF8 expression across multiple cancer types, including BRCA, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KIRC, KIRP, PRAD, STAD, and UCEC, whereas a decrease was observed in COAD and LUSC (Fig. 1A). Additionally, we retrieved four datasets (GSE146996, GSE184336, GSE179252, and GSE192468) from the GEO database to further investigate SLAMF8 levels in GC and surrounding non-cancerous tissues (Fig. 1B-E). The findings confirmed that SLAMF8 levels were modestly elevated in GC tissues. Subsequently, we employed the MEXPRESS database to explore the clinical significance of SLAMF8 in TCGA-STAD. Our findings indicated that SLAMF8 expression is associated with several clinical parameters, including anatomic neoplasm subdivision, Barrett’s esophagus, neoplasm histologic grade, pathologic T stage, family history of stomach cancer, primary therapy outcome success, H. pylori infection, residual tumor, race, tumor stage, histological type, and sample type (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, SLAMF8 expression was inversely correlated with its copy number. Notably, five CpG islands were detected in the promoter region of the SLAMF8 gene. Methylation levels at these CpG islands were negatively correlated with SLAMF8 expression, suggesting that SLAMF8 expression may be regulated by promoter methylation. Collectively, these results confirm the increased expression of SLAMF8 in GC.

Prognostic analysis of SLAMF8 in GC

To explore the prognostic value of SLAMF8 in GC patients, overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and post-progression survival (PPS) were examined using the KM plotter platform. Elevated SLAMF8 expression in GC patients was consistently associated with poorer OS outcomes (Fig. 2A). Likewise, higher SLAMF8 mRNA levels were significantly correlated with decreased PFS (Fig. 2B) and shortened PPS (Fig. 2C). Thus, our results show that increased SLAMF8 expression may act as an indicator of unfavorable prognosis in GC patients.

SLAMF8 is primary expressed in macrophages

We examined SLAMF8 protein expression in normal stomach and stomach adenocarcinoma tissues using immunohistochemistry data from the Human Protein Atlas database. A notable increase in the number of SLAMF8-positive cells was detected in GC tissues (Fig. 3A). Given that SLAMF8 is known to be expressed in myeloid-derived cells, particularly macrophages [29, 30], and SLAMF8 is predominantly expressed on macrophages in colorectal cancer [31], we investigated SLAMF8 expression in macrophages within various tissues. We leveraged single-cell RNA sequencing data available in the Human Protein Atlas to investigate SLAMF8 expression across diverse cell types in different normal tissues and found that SLAMF8 was predominantly expressed in macrophages across several normal tissues, including subcutaneous adipose, visceral adipose, adrenal gland, heart muscle, lung, minor salivary glands, and stomach (Fig. 3B). In contrast, SLAMF8 was primarily detected in fibroblasts within skeletal muscle. Analysis of stomach tissue cell types demonstrated that SLAMF8 was predominantly localized in macrophages (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the STAD-GSE167297 dataset obtained from the TISCH2 database were utilized to examine SLAMF8 expression in TME-related cells at the single-cell level. The highest levels of SLAMF8 were detected in monocytes/macrophages and dendritic cells (Fig. 3D). To definitively identify the cell lineage of these SLAMF8-positive cells, we performed immunofluorescence co-staining with the TAM marker CD206. As shown in Fig. 3E, most SLAMF8 signals colocalized with CD206, confirming that TAMs are the primary cell type expressing SLAMF8 in the GC microenvironment. Collectively, these findings indicate that SLAMF8 is preferentially expressed in TAMs within GC tissues.

SLAMF8 knockdown inhibits M2 polarization of in vitro–generated TAMs

Analysis of the TIMER database demonstrated a notable positive correlation between SLAMF8 expression and macrophage infiltration in GC (Fig. 4A). To further investigate the functional relationship between SLAMF8 and TAMs, we established an in vitro TAM model. Human PBMC-derived M0 macrophages were generated via standardized 7-day stimulation with recombinant human M-CSF—a well-validated protocol for obtaining unpolarized, functionally quiescent macrophages. To simulate TAM differentiation in vitro, M0 cells were co-cultured with MKN-45 cells using a Transwell system. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed that, relative to PBMCs, M0 and TAMs showed robust upregulation of pan-macrophage markers CD11b and CD68 (Figure S2A), while the expression of canonical TAM-associated genes (CD206, CD163, and ARG1) remained low, indicative of their unactivated baseline state (Figure S2B). Consistently, ELISA quantification demonstrated minimal secretion of the immunosuppressive mediators TGF-β1 and IL-10 by both PBMCs and M0 macrophages (Figure S2C). Flow cytometry further confirmed strong CD68 surface expression in M0 cells, with only marginal CD206 expression (Figure S2D). Upon co-culture with MKN-45 cells, macrophages exhibited significant transcriptional upregulation of TAM-associated genes, markedly increased secretion of TGF-β1 and IL-10, and pronounced surface enrichment of CD206 (Figure S2E). Collectively, these coordinated changes confirmed successful in vitro polarization of M0 macrophages into TAM-like cells.

SLAMF8 mRNA and protein expression were markedly increased in in vitro–generated TAMs compared to M0 macrophages (Fig. 4B-C). To explore the involvement of SLAMF8 in in vitro–generated TAM polarization, we utilized a lentiviral vector expressing shRNAs targeting SLAMF8 to decrease its expression (Fig. 4D-E). Notably, the reduction of SLAMF8 expression resulted in decreased CD206 protein levels in TAMs. Furthermore, the downregulation of SLAMF8 decreased the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β1 (Fig. 4F-G) and reduced CD206, CD163, and ARG1 expression (Fig. 4H-J), suggesting that SLAMF8 knockdown inhibited M2-type macrophage polarization. Moreover, SLAMF8 knockdown increased the secretion of CXCL10 and IL-6 (Fig. 4K-L) and upregulated CD86 and iNOS expression (Fig. 4M-N), suggesting that SLAMF8 knockdown promoted M1 polarization of in vitro–generated TAMs. Collectively, our findings indicate that SLAMF8 inhibition efficiently inhibits M2-type macrophage polarization.

SLAMF8 overexpression promotes M2 macrophage differentiation in TAMs

To investigate the effect of SLAMF8 overexpression on TAM polarization, we transduced TAMs with a lentiviral expression vector carrying the SLAMF8 coding sequence, which resulted in the upregulation of CD206 (Fig. 5A-B). SLAMF8-overexpressing TAMs increased the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β1 (Fig. 5C-D) and upregulated mRNA expression of CD206, CD163, and ARG1 (Fig. 5E-G), indicating that SLAMF8 overexpression promoted M2-type macrophage polarization. Moreover, SLAMF8 overexpression reduced the secretion of CXCL10 and IL-6 (Fig. 5H-I) and inhibited CD86 and iNOS mRNA expression (Fig. 5J-K), indicating that SLAMF8 overexpression inhibited M1-type macrophage polarization. Thus, these results suggest that SLAMF8 is a key regulator of TAM polarization.

SLAMF8 affects TAM polarization by NF-κB and JAK2/STAT3 signaling axis

Several studies have shown that the NF-κB and JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathways play important roles in the regulation of macrophage polarization. Activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway promotes M2-type polarization [32, 33], whereas NF-kB p65 primarily drives M1-type polarization [34, 35]. We observed that SLAMF8 overexpression significantly decreased the phosphorylation of p65 without affecting its total protein levels (Fig. 6A). Conversely, SLAMF8 knockdown increased p65 phosphorylation, suggesting that SLAMF8 inhibits the NF-κB signaling pathway. Moreover, SLAMF8 overexpression markedly increased the phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3, with no changes in their overall protein expression (Fig. 6B). This indicates that SLAMF8 activates the JAK2/STAT3 signaling axis. Taken together, these findings suggest that SLAMF8 influences TAM polarization by inhibiting NF-κB activation while simultaneously enhancing the JAK2/STAT3 pathway.

(B) Expression levels of phosphorylated JAK2, phosphorylated STAT3, total JAK2, and total STAT3 proteins, in in vitro–generated TAMs with SLAMF8 overexpression or knockdown were detected by Western blotting. SLAMF8 promotes the proliferation of GC cells

To examine the involvement of SLAMF8 in GC progression, we collected conditioned medium (CM) from TAMs with SLAMF8 either upregulated or silenced. CM derived from SLAMF8-overexpressing TAMs significantly enhanced the viability of MKN-45 cells (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, EdU staining confirmed that CM from SLAMF8-overexpressing TAMs enhanced the proliferation of MKN-45 cells (Fig. 7B). Conversely, CM from SLAMF8-knockdown TAMs markedly restrained the viability and proliferation of MKN-45 cells. To further elucidate whether SLAMF8 influences GC cell growth in vivo through the regulation of macrophage polarization, we subcutaneously co-injected MKN-45 cells with SLAMF8-overexpressing TAMs into nude mice. A marked elevation in both tumor volume and weight was observed in the SLAMF8-overexpressing group compared to the control group (Fig. 7C-D). Ki-67 immunohistochemical analysis revealed a higher density of Ki-67 positive cells in the SLAMF8-overexpressing group (Fig. 7E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SLAMF8-overexpressing TAMs promote GC growth, highlighting the crucial involvement of SLAMF8 in GC progression.

SLAMF8 is associated with CD8 + T cell dysfunction

TAMs are essential for the formation and sustenance of the immunosuppressive TME and can significantly modulate T cell functionality [36–38]. TIDE is used to assess how tumors evade immune surveillance by measuring T cell dysfunction and exclusion within the tumor microenvironment [24, 25]. In this framework, the dysfunction score quantifies the extent to which intratumoral T cells lose their effector functions. To investigate the association between SLAMF8 and T-cell dysfunction in GC, we performed a correlation analysis between SLAMF8 expression levels and the TIDE-derived dysfunction score using the TIMER database. As shown in Fig. 8A, SLAMF8 expression in STAD was significantly positively correlated with the dysfunction score, suggesting that SLAMF8 may play a role in the development of T cell dysfunction. To elucidate the interaction between SLAMF8 and the cytotoxicity of CD8 + T cells, we co-cultured CD8 + T cells with TAMs. Overexpression of SLAMF8 in TAMs led to increased mRNA levels of HAVCR2, LAG3, TIGIT, and PDCD1 in CD8 + T cells (Fig. 8B-D), while concurrently inhibiting the secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α (Fig. 8E-F). Furthermore, we performed a cell-killing assay involving co-cultured CD8 + T cells. Activated CD8 + T cells were first co-cultured with TAMs in a transwell system, and then these CD8 + T cells were harvested and co-cultured with MKN-45 cells to quantify their cytolytic function. SLAMF8 overexpression in TAMs decreased the LDH release by MKN-45 cells induced by CD8 + T cells, indicating that SLAMF8 overexpression in TAMs could impair the cytotoxicity of CD8 + T cells (Fig. 8G). Conversely, SLAMF8 knockdown in TAMs had the opposite effect. Collectively, these results suggest that SLAMF8 may promote CD8 + T cell dysfunction in the TME of GC.

Discussion

This study confirmed an increase in SLAMF8 expression in GC. Patients with higher SLAMF8 expression had a poorer prognosis. SLAMF8 is predominantly expressed in monocyte-macrophage populations. This finding prompted us to investigate the potential role of SLAMF8 in macrophage biology. Our study confirmed that SLAMF8 serves as a key factor in promoting M2 polarization of in vitro–generated TAMs by modulating the NF-κB and JAK2/STAT3 signaling axes, thereby inducing CD8 + T cell dysfunction and enhancing the growth of GC cells. These SLAMF8-mediated changes in TAM-driven immune regulation are important contributors to reshaping immunosuppression in cancer.

M-CSF is a critical growth factor that is indispensable for macrophage survival and differentiation. In vitro differentiation of human PBMCs into unpolarized M0 macrophages is routinely achieved by sustained stimulation with recombinant human M-CSF. This well-established protocol yields highly consistent, phenotypically stable M0 macrophages and is widely adopted as a reference standard in immunological and translational studies [39–42]. Upon binding to its cognate receptor, M-CSF induces receptor dimerization and subsequent autophosphorylation of intracellular tyrosine residues, thereby initiating multiple downstream signaling pathways. These pathways exert distinct functional outcomes: certain branches promote monocyte and macrophage survival, whereas others drive terminal differentiation of monocytes into mature macrophages [43]. In this study, M-CSF stimulation significantly upregulated the expression of pan-macrophage markers CD11b and CD68, confirming the successful generation of M0 macrophages. In ex vivo settings, M0 macrophages must undergo a polarization process to acquire their full functions, whether pro- or anti-inflammatory [44]. Ye et al. elucidated that cancer-cell–derived lactate induces M2 polarization of TAMs [45]. Dong et al. indicated that tumor–derived MMP28 promotes TAM recruitment and induces M2 TAM polarization [46]. The cancer-cell–secreted chemokine CCL20 is a major secretory chemokine for TAM polarization [47]. It is widely documented that tumor-derived cytokines can “educate” resident macrophages toward an immunosuppressive TAM phenotype. Co-culturing tumor cells with M0 macrophages induces phenotypic reprogramming toward a TAM–like state [48–51]. In this study, TAMs were generated in vitro by co-culturing human monocyte–derived M0 macrophages with MKN-45 cells using a Transwell system. Upon co-culture, the phenotypic conversion of M0 macrophages to the TAM–like state was rigorously confirmed. Compared to untreated M0 controls, the co-cultured macrophages exhibited a significant M2–polarized phenotype, with upregulation of M2 markers CD163 and CD206 and increased expression of immunosuppressive mediators TGF-β1 and IL-10. These phenotypic changes were accompanied by the functional acquisition of TAM characteristics.

The dysregulated expression of SLAMF8 has been linked to disease progression and prognosis in various tumor types [17]. Moreover, SLAMF8 serves as a potential immune checkpoint molecule in gastrointestinal malignancies [18], colorectal cancer [52], and non-small-cell lung cancer [53]. Additionally, downregulating SLAMF8 expression suppresses the growth of neoplastic mast cells and promotes apoptosis in these cells [54]. Similarly, our results revealed that SLAMF8 facilitated the growth of MKN-45 cells both in vitro and in vivo by indirectly promoting M2 polarization of TAMs.

Macrophages are critical immune cells within the TME, and their polarization status, whether M1 (anti-tumor) or M2 (pro-tumor), significantly impacts GC progression. Clinical studies have shown that a higher density of M2-type TAMs in gastric cancer tissues is associated with reduced overall survival [55, 56]. Treatment strategies targeting TAMs aim to either inhibit M2 polarization (e.g., via CSF1R inhibitors) or promote the M1 phenotype (e.g., using TLR agonists). CSF1R inhibitors can reduce the number of M2-type TAMs and enhance chemotherapy efficacy [57, 58]. Romero-Pinedo et al. demonstrated that SLAMF8 knockdown promoted the activation of iNOS, increased nitric oxide (NO) production, and upregulated IL-6 expression in RAW264.7 cells [59]. Consistently, we found that SLAMF8 knockdown also reduced IL-6 and iNOS levels in TAMs, whereas its overexpression exerted the opposite effect. These data demonstrate that SLAMF8 promotes M2 polarization while suppressing M1 polarization of TAMs, thereby promoting GC cell growth both in vitro and in vivo, highlighting the essential role of SLAMF8 in macrophage polarization in GC.

In specific contexts, TAM infiltration can promote immunosuppression by modulating the immune microenvironment, particularly by suppressing cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses [60]. T cell exhaustion is a widespread issue in cancer, where exhausted T cells typically display diminished effector capabilities, increased expression of inhibitory receptors, including PD-1, LAG-3, and HAVCR2, and alterations in metabolic and epigenetic regulation. Although advancements in PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy have partially reversed T cell exhaustion, its clinical efficacy remains limited [61]. Gao et al. demonstrated that higher SLAMF8 expression was positively correlated with improved response to anti-PD-1 therapy in gastrointestinal cancers [18]. In our study, we observed that SLAMF8 accelerated T-cell exhaustion and dysfunction by increasing the expression of LAG3, TIGIT, PDCD1, and HAVCR2 in CD8 + T cells. These results underscore the immunosuppressive role of SLAMF8 in GC.

The p65 subunit serves as the primary transcriptional activator of the NF-κB signaling cascade. When Toll-like receptors, such as TLR4, are activated, the inhibitory protein IκB is degraded, leading to the release and nuclear translocation of p65. Once in the nucleus, p65 initiates the expression of M1-associated genes—such as iNOS, TNF-α, and IL-6—thereby promoting macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype [62, 63]. Notably, a study has shown that Polyporus polysaccharides can activate the NF-κB pathway, leading to M1 polarization of TAMs [64]. Similarly, the aqueous extract of Cimicifuga dahurica stimulates TLR4, enhancing NF-κB signaling and boosting the antitumor activity of M1 macrophages [65]. Additionally, miR-155–loaded LDH nanoparticles have been demonstrated to have the ability to suppress the STAT3 pathway while simultaneously activating NF-κB, shifting TAMs toward the M1 phenotype, and alleviating immunosuppression within the TME [66]. In the present study, we observed that SLAMF8 markedly inhibited p65 phosphorylation, suggesting that SLAMF8 may inhibit macrophage polarization toward the M1 state by inactivating the NF-κB pathway.

Cytokines, such as IL-4, initiate signaling by binding to their membrane receptors, leading to the activation of JAK2 and subsequent phosphorylation of STAT3. Once phosphorylated, STAT3 dimerizes and translocates to the nucleus, where it functions as a transcription factor to drive the expression of M2-associated genes—including ARG1 and IL-10—facilitating macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype [67]. IL-6 has also been shown to promote the differentiation of macrophages into the tumor-promoting M2 subtype through STAT3 activation, which in turn enhances GC cell proliferation and migration [68]. Increasing evidence suggests that pharmacological agents or targeted molecular interventions that modulate this pathway can help regulate M2 polarization, offering potential therapeutic benefits in various diseases [69–71]. In our study, we found that SLAMF8 markedly enhanced the phosphorylation of both JAK2 and STAT3, indicating its role in promoting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway in TAMs and facilitating their polarization toward the M2 phenotype.

Macrophages are renowned for their remarkable phenotypic plasticity, which is dynamically regulated by signals from the microenvironment [72–74]. In our study, we demonstrated that upon co-culture with cancer cells, macrophages polarized toward an M2-like phenotype, driven by tumor-derived factors. Regarding the potential for reversion, although our current study did not experimentally address the “washout” or “removal” condition, the existing literature strongly suggests that macrophage phenotypes are not fixed and are highly dependent on continuous environmental cues. Plasticity implies that if the driving stimulus (i.e., cancer cells and their secreted factors) is removed, macrophages have the potential to be re-educated and may shift away from the tumor-promoting phenotype. For instance, Yang et al. demonstrated that M1 and M2 phenotypes could be maintained during co-culture with cancer cells, implying that the phenotype is an active response to the continued presence of these signals [75]. Conversely, TAM-targeted therapy is based on the premise that reprogramming M2-like TAMs back to an M1-like, antitumor state is possible by altering signals in tumor microenvironment [76, 77]. Therefore, it is highly plausible that in the absence of cancer-cell–derived polarizing signals, the macrophages in our system would undergo a phenotypic shift. They may revert to a resting state (M0) or, depending on the presence of other environmental factors, repolarize toward a different functional phenotype, potentially including the M1 type. This dynamic equilibrium is a hallmark of their biology. We acknowledge that the absence of rescue or reverse-functional experiments represents a limitation of the present study in fully characterizing the dynamic plasticity of macrophages—a key direction for future investigation. A clearer understanding of the kinetics and stability of the macrophage phenotype upon withdrawal of tumor-derived stimuli would provide deeper insight into the regulation of the tumor-immune microenvironment.

Conclusion

In summary, the current study suggests that M2 polarization of TAMs in the TME is potentially regulated by SLAMF8. SLAMF8 promotes M2 polarization of in vitro–generated TAMs, thereby amplifying their pro-tumorigenic functions and impairing cytotoxic CD8 + T cells, which collectively fosters an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment conducive to GC progression.
