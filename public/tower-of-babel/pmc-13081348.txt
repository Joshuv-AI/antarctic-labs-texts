Grief modular group therapy: theoretical bases and pilot study of a new bereavement intervention programme in China
Authors: Ningning Zhou, Suqin Tang, Jiaqi Zhou, Liping Guo, Lang Junlian, Bowen Situ, Xinxian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Bereavement is a major life stressor, with millions of Chinese individuals experiencing grief each year. Prolonged grief disorder (PGD) and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are prevalent among bereaved people in China, yet culturally appropriate interventions are scarce. Existing Western-based protocols often showed limited cross-cultural applicability when implemented in the Chinese context, highlighting the need for evidence-based, culturally adapted interventions.Objective: This study aimed to introduce and evaluate the effectiveness of a newly developed structured Grief Modular Group Therapy (GMGT), designed under the framework of the Dual Process Model and integrating multiple therapeutic elements and culturally relevant practices.Method: A non-randomized controlled trial was conducted. Trained therapists delivered a 10-session GMGT protocol to bereaved individuals with prolonged grief symptoms (N = 69). Their outcomes were compared with those of a blank (non-intervention) control group (N = 64). Symptoms of PGD and PTSD were measured at baseline, post-treatment, and 3-month follow-up. GMGT showed high acceptability, with 79.8% of participants completing the programme.Result: Compared with the control group, the intervention group demonstrated significant reductions in PGD symptom severity to a clinically significant extent (Cohen’s d = 0.85). Improvements were maintained at follow-up (Cohen’s d = 0.86). GMGT also reduced posttraumatic stress symptoms, though with smaller effect sizes.Conclusion: These findings suggest that GMGT is a promising, culturally sensitive, structured, and scalable intervention that could be remotely delivered for bereaved individuals in China, with potential applicability in other cultural contexts facing similar resource limitations in mental health care. Further randomized controlled trials are warranted.KEYWORDS: Prolonged grief disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder, dual process model, group therapy, grief modular group therapy, China

Introduction

The death of a loved one is one of life’s most stressful experiences (Shear & Shair, 2004). In 2025, China recorded approximately 11.3 million deaths (National Bureau of Statistics, 2026), leaving behind millions of families bereaved. Assuming that each death affects, on average, three bereaved people (Buur et al., 2024), nearly 33 million people may experience grief each year. In recent years, prolonged grief disorder (PGD), a diagnostic construct originally developed within Western psychiatric frameworks (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; World Health Organization, 2020), has increasingly been examined in the Chinese context (He et al., 2014; Killikelly et al., 2020; Li & Prigerson, 2016). Meta-analytic evidence suggests that the pooled prevalence of PGD and its symptoms in China is 8.9% and 32.4%, respectively (Yuan et al., 2024). Notably, even higher levels of distress have been reported among trauma-bereaved populations; for example, among parents who had lost their only child, the prevalence reaches 20.9% for PGD and 75.0% for PGD symptoms (Yuan et al., 2022). While these rates appear higher than the 3–5% reported in Western bereaved populations (Rosner, 2021; Shevlin et al., 2023; Treml et al., 2024) – a discrepancy that may stem from differences in sample representativeness and diagnostic criteria (Killikelly et al., 2025) – existing evidence suggests that the construct of PGD and its corresponding measurements possess acceptable cross-cultural applicability in China (Killikelly et al., 2020, 2023; Stelzer et al., 2020; Yi et al., 2016), provided that appropriate cultural adaptations are implemented (Killikelly et al., 2020, 2023; Stelzer et al., 2020).

In addition to PGD, symptoms of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) have also been observed among bereaved families (Wen et al., 2023), with a pooled prevalence of PTSD as high as 46.8% among Chinese Shidu parents (Wang et al., 2021b). These data highlight a strong demand for effective grief therapy services in China, particularly for individuals who have experienced traumatic loss.

However, China has only about 8 mental health professionals per 100,000 people, and only a small proportion have received systematic training in grief therapy and counselling (Tang et al., 2024). As a result, there is a significant gap between the demand for grief therapy services and the available counselling or therapy resources.

Over the past few decades, various grief interventions have been developed in Western countries, but the protocols and empirical evidence for the prevention and treatment of prolonged grief disorder remain limited (Johannsen et al., 2019; Simon & Shear, 2024). Our prior clinical experience suggests that directly applying existing intervention models – which often prioritize early loss-disclosure and psychoeducation – to Chinese bereaved individuals yielded suboptimal results. This is partly due to the profound conflict between Western therapeutic openness and Chinese cultural norms. For instance, the traditional expectation of ‘restraining grief’ (节哀) encourages suppressive coping (Hsu et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2021a). When participants are suddenly asked to confront the painful reality of the death and loss without prior preparation, it often triggers uncontrollable emotional flooding. Without early-stage emotion regulation training, such intense distress can overwhelm participants and lead to premature dropout. Therefore, the current intervention project incorporates relaxation techniques from the very beginning to build emotional resilience before addressing deeper trauma.

Additionally, grief experiences vary considerably across cultures in terms of mourning rituals, customary practices, and the beliefs and meanings attached to death (Rosenblatt, 2008). Consequently, several studies have emphasized the need for culturally sensitive interventions (Aaron et al., 2025; Aeschlimann et al., 2024). Therefore, it is crucial to develop intervention methods that, while based on successful evidence-based practices from Western countries, are also suitable for the Chinese cultural context and the current realities in China. Over the past several years, through iterative development and refinement, Grief Modular Group Therapy (GMGT) has emerged as a promising intervention for bereaved individuals in China. Our initial small-scale trials yielded encouraging results, prompting a more extensive open trial conducted in 2024 (Yu et al., 2024). The results demonstrated marked reductions in both the prevalence and symptom severity of PGD and PTSD. The following sections review the theoretical foundations, intervention settings, procedures of GMGT, and the results from this non-randomized controlled trial.

Theoretical foundations

GMGT is grounded in cutting-edge theoretical and empirical development, including the Dual Process Model (DPM) as the framework, the integration of evidence-based intervention elements, and cultural considerations.

Dual-process model framework

Stroebe and Schut introduced the Dual Process Model of coping with bereavement (Stroebe & Schut, 1999; Stroebe & Schut, 2010; Stroebe & Schut, 2021). The Dual Process Model posits that bereaved individuals face two kinds of stressors after experiencing a loss: loss-oriented (LO) stressors and restoration-oriented (RO) stressors. Loss-oriented stressors involve focusing on the grief and emotions related to the loss, while restoration-oriented stressors involve adapting to new life changes and roles. The DPM suggests that a healthy coping process involves a dynamic and flexible oscillation between confronting LO stressors and RO stressors. In contrast, rigid or inflexible oscillation, such as becoming overly focused on loss-oriented stressors or excessively avoiding them, has been associated with increased risk of prolonged grief symptoms (Fiore, 2021; Stroebe & Schut, 2010; Van Essen, 2022).

This oscillatory mechanism is conceptually consistent with the Chinese philosophical emphasis on dynamic balance, particularly Yin–Yang thinking, which views opposing forces as interdependent and mutually transforming within an ongoing process of change (Jiang, 2013). Moreover, Chinese dialectical thinking highlights contradiction, change, and holistic integration, supporting the coexistence and integration of seemingly opposing experiences (Spencer-Rodgers et al., 2010). These cultural perspectives provide additional theoretical grounding for the applicability of the DPM in the Chinese context.

The DPM not only theoretically reveals the characteristics of grief but also offers flexibility and integrative potential in interventions. When designing a grief intervention model within the framework of the Dual-Process Model, we carefully considered and integrated both loss-oriented and restoration-oriented stressors, the facilitation of oscillation, and culturally appropriate methods.. This approach helps to avoid overemphasizing a single type of stressor or neglecting the influence of cultural factors (Caserta et al., 2016; Chow et al., 2018; Lund et al., 2010).

Since its introduction, the DPM has been continuously refined and successfully applied in various grief interventions (Fiore, 2021), including Prolonged Grief Treatment (PGT), a leading evidence-based treatment for grief-related complications, which was explicitly based on the DPM to determine therapeutic tasks (Bottomley et al., 2025; Willis et al., 2024). The DPM has also been used as the framework for several interventions that effectively integrated Chinese cultural elements and achieved successful clinical results in China (Chow et al., 2018; Liu & Tang, 2024). Therefore, the Dual-Process Model is used as the framework for GMGT.

In addition, previous research indicates that in regions where grief education is not widespread, restoration-oriented themes are generally more readily accepted than loss-oriented themes (Caserta et al., 2014; Lundorff et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2024). Considering that grief education is very limited in China, GMGT emphasizes restoration-oriented themes in therapy practice. Nevertheless, both loss and restoration elements are necessary in GMGT.

Integration of intervention elements

Under the DPM framework, various evidence-based intervention elements are integrated into GMGT, including emotion regulation (e.g. mindfulness), Meaning-Centered Grief Therapy, resilience, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), continuing bonds, exposure, and grief and trauma psychoeducation.

Emotion regulation

Emotion regulation is a critical task in grief therapy, particularly in group settings where participants share distressing experiences of loss. In these situations, strong feelings of grief are inevitably triggered, which can have a ripple effect on other group members during therapy sessions (Dyregrov et al., 2014). Without adequate emotion regulation skills, some participants may find these intense emotions overwhelming, potentially leading to their withdrawal from therapy. To address this challenge, mindfulness has emerged as an effective approach. Mindfulness is defined as a ‘non-elaborative, nonjudgmental, present-centered awareness in which each thought, feeling, or sensation that arises in the attentional field is acknowledged and accepted as it is’ (Bishop et al., 2004). Research has demonstrated the efficacy of mindfulness in treating grief, anxiety, depression, and other psychological issues by enhancing emotion regulation (Grecucci et al., 2015; Liu & Tang, 2024). Specifically, mindfulness practices such as meditation and breathing exercises have been shown to effectively help in regulating emotions (Dolan et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2019). By incorporating these techniques into grief therapy, participants can develop valuable tools to navigate the intense emotional landscape they encounter, thereby improving their ability to engage with and benefit from the therapeutic process.

Mindfulness, rooted in Buddhism, is also an integral part of traditional Chinese culture and is widely known and practiced by Chinese people. This cultural familiarity makes it particularly suitable for implementation in grief therapy within the Chinese context. GMGT introduces effective mindfulness practices, including meditation and breathing exercises, at the beginning of the therapy process to help participants reduce painful emotional reactions during and after sessions.

Meaning-centered grief therapy

‘Meaning-making and meaning-reconstruction are frequently central processes in bereavement’ (Neimeyer & Hooghe, 2017). The lack of meaning in life and confusion about identity among bereaved individuals are directly linked to the development of PGD. Research indicates that approximately 92% of bereaved parents in China who have lost their only child find it challenging to find meaning in their lives (Lu, 2018). Given the prevalence of PGD symptoms in this population (ranging from 20.9% to 75%) (Yuan et al., 2022), reestablishing and reinforcing a positive sense of meaning in life is essential for both preventing and treating PGD.

Meaning-Centered Grief Therapy (MCGT), an evidence-based therapeutic approach derived from Viktor Frankl's logotherapy, helps bereaved individuals reflect on and seek positive meanings and life roles from various perspectives after significant loss. The process of discovering sources of meaning can provide emotional comfort and renewed direction in life (Lichtenthal et al., 2017; Lichtenthal et al., 2019). In our previous work with bereaved parents who lost their only child, the themes of Meaning-Centered Grief Therapy were well-received.

Resilience

Resilience is a positive psychological construct that has been studied for decades. Although there is no single agreed-upon definition, resilience is commonly described as the ability to recover from adversity, thrive in the face of challenges, and demonstrate positive responses or outcomes (Vella & Pai, 2019). Even when exposed to the death of a close person, many people continue to have positive emotional experiences and show only minor and transient disruptions in their ability to function (Bonanno, 2004). Resilience is not just an individual quality; it arises from access to and utilization of resources necessary for supporting mental health and well-being. Both culture and context influence the nature of resilience (Ungar & Theron, 2020). A recent meta-study lends support to the overall efficacy of resilience-building strategies in various interventions (Liu et al., 2020).

Over the past decades, different strategies have been developed to build resilience in coping with grief (Hone, 2017; Hooghe & Neimeyer, 2013). These strategies include maintaining a positive attitude grounded in realism, cultivating cognitive flexibility, adhering to moral principles, finding resilience in role models, confronting fears, establishing or seeking a supportive social network, prioritizing physical health, fostering a sense of gratitude, maintaining curiosity, experiencing fulfilment through helping others, and recognizing, utilizing, and nurturing unique personal strengths. These resilience strategies can help bereaved individuals navigate life's adversities and uncover personal strengths.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) for grief therapy is effective in helping individuals process and cope with their loss by addressing and modifying unhelpful thoughts, emotions, and behaviours (Boelen et al., 2007; Komischke-Konnerup et al., 2024; Zuelke et al., 2021). Integrated into GMGT, CBT elements involve identifying negative thought patterns and beliefs related to the loss, and reframing them into more constructive ones. In addition to the cognitions about meaning-of-life issues, which are heavily addressed, GMGT places particular focus on two cognitive issues – self-blame and feelings of inferiority, which are frequently present in bereaved individuals in China based on our past experiences (Xu et al., 2024). By effectively learning and applying CBT techniques to address these two common cognitive issues, participants can develop skills to more positively manage other loss-related cognitive challenges, such as anger.

Continuing bonds

Building healthy continuing bonds plays a crucial role in effective grief therapy (Root & Exline, 2014). Meaning and meaning-making in bereavement have always had an important place in the continuing bonds model (Klass, 2014). Different styles of continuing bonds can lead to either positive adaptation or maladaptation to the loss (Field & Filanosky, 2010). A positive continuing bond encourages individuals to keep comforting memories, values, and relationships with the deceased alive in their daily lives. These bonds offer a sense of ongoing presence and emotional support, helping individuals integrate their loss while honouring the deceased. Bereaved individuals often find comfort and a renewed sense of purpose through maintaining positive continuing bonds with the deceased, which facilitates a more adaptive grieving process. Various effective strategies for building positive continuing bonds are introduced and discussed. These include psychoeducation, narrative work, reminiscing about cherished moments with the deceased, engaging in Chinese culturally appropriate mourning rituals, and the ‘Six Expressions’ to the deceased: expressing love, expressing gratitude, expressing apologies (voicing guilt), expressing assurances (committing to live a meaningful life), expressing confusion, doubts, or complaints (if needed), and expressing farewells.

Exposure

Exposure is a crucial component of grief interventions, helping bereaved individuals confront and process painful memories, emotions, and reminders associated with their loss (Bryant et al., 2014; Shear, 2010). In fact, group therapy itself serves as a form of exposure, as participants inevitably share their painful experiences and emotions during discussions, thereby confronting distressing memories together. To maximize the benefits of exposure, it is essential to implement it strategically. The approach is most effective when guided by skilled therapists, supplemented with mindfulness techniques, and carefully paced to consider the appropriate ‘dose’ of exposure.

In both PGT and grief-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (GF-CBT), exposure is often applied in the early stages of therapy (Komischke-Konnerup et al., 2025; Simon & Shear, 2024). According to the cognitive behavioural conceptualization model of grief (Boelen et al., 2006), PGD is maintained by avoidance of loss-related memories and emotions, negative cognitions about the self or the loss, and disruptions in daily functioning and social engagement. Exposure targets these maintaining mechanisms by reducing avoidance, facilitating emotional processing, and enabling cognitive restructuring through repeated, safe confrontation with the loss (Boelen et al., 2007; Shear, 2010).

However, within the Chinese cultural context, death and grief are considered taboo topics (Chow et al., 2007), and bereaved individuals may feel apprehensive or fearful about expressing intense emotions (Hsu et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2021a), which may increase the risk of dropout if exposure is introduced too early. Our previous clinical experience similarly indicates that early exposure can provoke resistance or even lead to premature termination. Therefore, in this intervention, exposure is implemented during the later stages, after addressing less painful topics and ensuring participants have developed a solid understanding and adequate coping skills for grief and trauma.

For more extensive exposure, GMGT employs the narrative method developed by the International Association of Trauma Professionals (IATP), which is an evidence-based trauma treatment approach (Dubi et al., 2017). This method involves creating a timeline of trauma events and narrating experiences based on that timeline. Additionally, therapists reinforce the effects of exposure during one-on-one sessions by providing recursive narration based on the original grief narrative. In our past practice, this approach was well-received by participants (Yu, et al., 2024).

Grief and trauma psychoeducation

Individuals who experience significant loss may have comorbid PGD and PTSD. As Parkes (2001) aptly noted, ‘All bereavements are traumatic, but some are more traumatic than others; we all enter a strange and unpredictable world when we suffer a major loss, but some worlds are more hazardous and unpredictable than others’. This observation underscores the importance of grief and trauma psychoeducation in therapeutic interventions. Grief and trauma psychoeducation offers essential insights into the grieving and trauma processes, helping individuals understand and normalize their experiences (Nam, 2016). By educating participants about the nature of grief and trauma, it alleviates feelings of confusion and isolation. Psychoeducation also provides some practical coping strategies and promotes healthy grieving practices.

Unlike many other grief therapy approaches, GMGT incorporates grief and trauma psychoeducation throughout the intervention process across multiple sessions, rather than confining it to one or two sessions at the beginning of therapy. This approach facilitates deeper understanding as the topics presented vary depending on the specific themes addressed in each session.

The gradual introduction of psychoeducational content is particularly important in the Chinese cultural context, where open discussion of grief is not widely accepted. In our past practice, we observed that certain keywords associated with psychoeducation could trigger uncontrollable, intense pain for bereaved individuals, particularly those who have experienced traumatic loss. This sensitivity may lead to dropout. By distributing psychoeducation across multiple sessions, GMGT aims to mitigate this risk and enhance participant retention and engagement.

Cultural considerations

Cultural elements significantly influence the grieving process for bereaved individuals, as mourning practices vary across different cultural contexts (Rosenblatt, 2019). In addition to culturally adapting established grief-focused intervention elements, such as prioritizing restoration-oriented components in the early stages and deferring exposure to the later phases, GMGT places a strong emphasis on Chinese cultural elements. First, in procedure design, the first session explicitly addressed healthy lifestyle practices during grief, reflecting the strong cultural emphasis on ‘yangsheng’ (养生), or health preservation, in Chinese society. Participants were guided to discuss sleep regulation, dietary balance, and mindfulness practices as culturally familiar pathways to stabilize distress and restore daily functioning. This integration acknowledges that maintaining physical harmony is often perceived in Chinese culture as foundational to emotional regulation (Fogaça et al., 2021). Second, in the training, group facilitators were instructed to use culturally respectful language when referring to the deceased. For example, instead of using the direct term ‘dead,’ facilitators were encouraged to say ‘passed away,’ which aligns with common linguistic conventions in Chinese culture that demonstrate reverence for the deceased and reduce perceived harshness in grief communication. Third, participants were encouraged to engage in Qingming tomb-sweeping and other commemorative rituals as culturally meaningful ways to maintain continuing bonds with the deceased, providing structured opportunities for remembrance and emotional expression within socially sanctioned traditions (Chow et al., 2007).

From a public health perspective, intervention protocols should be relatively structured, easy to disseminate, and adaptable for internet-based delivery. The flexibility of the internet enables scalable and accessible support across regions where trained grief professionals may be scarce, particularly in China. Moreover, internet-based grief interventions have been increasingly developed in recent years (Eisma et al., 2021; Johannsen et al., 2019; Kersting et al., 2013; Litz et al., 2014).

The present study

Overall, the present study conducted a non-randomized controlled trial with a two-arm design, including an intervention group (IG) and a control group (CG). The present study aimed to (1) introduce the protocol and implementation of GMGT among Chinese individuals bereaved by the loss of a parent, spouse, or child; (2) examine the feasibility of GMGT by assessing programme completion and dropout rate; and (3) evaluate its effectiveness by comparing changes in PGD and PTSD symptom severity and prevalence between the IG and CG.

We proposed the following three hypotheses: (1) the GMGT could be implemented among different types of bereaved populations in China (Hypothesis 1); (2) the dropout rate of the GMGT would be comparable to those reported in previous grief intervention studies (Hypothesis 2); and (3) the GMGT would be effective, as evidenced by greater improvements in symptom severity and prevalence of PGD and PTSD in the IG than the CG (Hypothesis 3).

Method

Recruiting and training therapists

In this study, eight therapists were recruited from a pool of qualified professionals, including psychotherapists (n = 3), clinical psychologists (n = 3), and social workers (n = 2), all of whom had 10 years of experience in supporting bereaved individuals and demonstrated strong communication skills. Additionally, eight assistants were recruited from graduate students majoring in psychology to help facilitate the group therapy sessions. To ensure therapist competence and maintain consistency in each group, Liu (corresponding author, a Certified Grief Counselor), who developed the GMGT by integrating Western theoretical frameworks with Chinese cultural practices and years of effective clinical experience in grief intervention (Liu & Tang, 2024; Yu et al., 2024), provided training, ongoing supervision, continuous monitoring, and standardized session materials. All therapists followed the same protocol and used the same materials throughout the intervention.

Participants and procedure

A priori sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to determine the required sample size. Based on a large effect size (d = 0.8; informed by Bryant et al., 2024), a significance level of α = .05, and a power of 0.90, a minimum of 70 participants (35 per group) was required for the primary analysis. Considering the longitudinal nature of the study and an expected 50% retention rate, consistent with previous web-based grief interventions (Wagner, 2020), the recruitment target was set at 140 participants. This ensures that the final analyzed sample remains sufficiently powered despite anticipated attrition.

This study used a non-randomized controlled design. Participants in the IG and CG were recruited at different time points, and individuals in the CG were selected based on their relationship with the deceased to match the IG.

Recruitment was primarily conducted through online media. Potential participants were initially screened during a telephone intake by psychologists and evaluated using the Prolonged Grief 13 Revision (PG-13-R) (Prigerson et al., 2021). Participants were eligible for the study if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged between 20 and 75 years; (2) experienced the loss of a significant person (i.e. child, parent, partner) at least four months prior to recruitment; and (3) exhibited clinically significant prolonged grief symptoms, defined as a score above 24 on the PG-13-R. Individuals were excluded if they: (1) displayed current suicidal tendencies or ideation; (2) reported severe comorbid physical or mental illnesses that would impede participation; or (3) were currently undergoing other forms of psychological treatment.

In the IG, a total of 171 individuals initially registered. After preliminary screening, 69 participants were qualified and assigned to eight groups: two groups consisted of bereaved parents who had lost their only child, one group included parents who had lost a child but still had surviving children or grandchildren, two groups were composed of individuals who had lost a spouse, and three groups included individuals who had lost one or both parents. Among them, 24 had lost a child, 16 had lost a partner, and 29 had lost a parent. During the group therapy process, 14 participants withdrew (a dropout rate of 20.2%). Two completed one session, two completed two sessions, two completed three sessions, four completed four sessions, and four completed five sessions. Fifty-five participants were considered completers. On average, treatment completers received 9.27 sessions (SD = 0.95). Among the 55 completers, 6 participants did not complete the post-treatment assessment, and 7 participants did not complete the 3-month follow-up assessment, leaving 48 completers who completed assessments at all three waves. Some participants who withdrew also completed post-treatment and 3-month follow-up assessments. Therefore, among the 69, 55 participants completed post-treatment assessments, and 56 participants completed 3-month follow-up assessments.

The control group received minimal support, including access to an online social media group and self-help resources. Participants for the CG were recruited after the intervention was completed. A total of 262 individuals initially registered for the study. After preliminary screening, 129 participants were deemed eligible. Among them, participants who had lost a parent were overrepresented (parent loss = 92, partner loss = 25, child loss = 12). To balance the distribution of loss types across groups and ensure comparability between IG and CG (in IG, the ratio of partner/child loss vs. parent loss was 40/29), a subset of these participants (n = 27) was randomly selected. As a result, 64 participants remained (parent loss = 27, partner loss = 25, child loss = 12). Among them, 48 completed the post-treatment assessments and 45 completed the 3-month follow-up assessments. After the 3-month follow-up assessments were completed, participants in the CG were provided with the 10-week grief-focused modular group intervention if needed. Internet technology

In GMGT, the entire implementation process is supported by internet-based technology, primarily using Tencent Meeting (equivalent to Zoom) and WeChat (similar to Whatsapp). Trainers/supervisors use Tencent Meeting to deliver GMGT training, monitor each group’s progress, provide feedback, and conduct Q&A sessions with therapists during supervision meetings after each therapy session. Tencent Meeting is also used for each session, as it allows participants to host or join from anywhere with Wi-Fi – a feature that is very popular in China. WeChat serves as a secondary communication platform. Each group has its own WeChat group where participants actively share personal updates and offer mutual support. WeChat is also used to distribute self-study materials and collect assigned homework.

Group homogeneity

Although all participants have experienced loss, the nature of their relationships with the deceased can lead to different grieving experiences and challenges. When organizing grief group therapy, it is essential to consider factors of homogeneity. This approach creates a safer environment and fosters mutual understanding and empathy, allowing for better support, improved group cohesiveness, and better outcomes in group therapy (Bernard et al., 2008; Sugarman et al., 2021). Therefore, GMGT assigns participants with similar loss experiences to the same group.

Combining group and individual therapy

Some participants may find it challenging to express their inner thoughts and needs in a group setting, particularly when dealing with stigmatized loss events (such as suicide) or unresolved complex feelings toward the deceased. Incorporating individual and group therapy could yield more significant positive effects (Bryant et al., 2014; Maass et al., 2022). GMGT combines 9 group sessions with 1 individual session, which was specifically designed as an in-depth narrative session.

Homework assignment

Assigning and completing homework in grief therapy can enhance the effectiveness of interventions (Kazantzis et al., 2016; Ryum et al., 2023). The GMGT provides homework that involves various materials, such as handouts for the sessions, books, articles, videos, and writing exercises. Writing has been proven to be an effective method in grief interventions and is sometimes referred to as ‘writing therapy’ (Thatcher, 2024). It can help individuals express and process complex emotions, clarify their thoughts, and enhance self-awareness, assisting them in integrating their loss into their ongoing life narrative. Writing exercises are optional and may serve as a guide for subsequent discussion. Additionally, before starting a new theme in each session, there is a group discussion of the assigned homework from the previous session. This not only reinforces understanding of the prior theme but also encourages participants who may have missed the last session to engage with the homework and materials, effectively allowing them to ‘catch up.’

Outcome measures

The Prolonged Grief 13 Revised (PG-13-R) was used as the assessment tool of prolonged grief symptoms (Prigerson et al., 2021). This questionnaire is based on the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) for evaluating Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD). It consists of 13 items, of which 10 are rated on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much), indicating the frequency of the symptoms. Higher scores reflect more severe grief symptoms. A cutoff score of 30 is used to indicate potential PGD. At baseline, the PG-13-R had an internal consistency of Cronbach α = 0.70.

Trauma symptoms were assessed using the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ) (Cloitre et al., 2018). The ITQ consists of 9 items: the first 6 items measure symptoms across three dimensions – re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal – with each dimension assessed by 2 items; the remaining 3 items assess functional impairment caused by these symptoms. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, and the total score is calculated as the sum of the first 6 items, with higher scores indicating more severe trauma symptoms. A PTSD diagnosis requires that at least one item from each dimension (re-experiencing, avoidance, hyperarousal, and functional impairment) be scored 3 or above. At baseline, the ITQ demonstrated an internal consistency of Cronbach’s α = 0.69.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 23.0 (IBM).

Descriptive statistics were reported as means (SDs) for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Baseline differences between treatment groups and between completers and dropouts were examined using two-tailed t tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

Preplanned hypotheses were examined under an intent-to-treat framework (Gupta, 2011). To estimate intervention effects, we fitted linear mixed-effects models (LMM), which can accommodate varying numbers of observations across participants and account for incomplete data using maximum likelihood procedures (Little & Rubin, 2019). Missing data were assumed to be Missing at Random (MAR), as participants retained at follow-up did not significantly differ from those lost to follow-up on baseline characteristics (see Supplementary file eTable 1). The models specified assessment point, treatment group, and their interaction as fixed factors with an unstructured covariance matrix. Outcomes were evaluated across baseline, post-treatment, and 3-month follow-up. Fixed effects were tested with Wald statistics, with two-sided P values < .05 considered significant, and 95% confidence intervals reported. According to the criteria established by Cohen (1988) and Rice and Harris (2005), effect sizes were calculated as Cohen's d (between) and η2, with the latter representing the percentage of variance explained by the model. Effect sizes were considered small if Cohen's d < 0.5 (η2 < .06), moderate if 0.5 ≤ Cohen's d < 0.8 (0.06 ≤ η2 < 0.14), and large if Cohen's d ≥ 0.8 (η2 ≥ 0.14).

To determine the robustness of this approach, we repeated the analyses focusing only on intervention completers who completed the intervention and the 3-month assessment.

The clinical significance of the change was evaluated using two metrics: (1) Reliable Change Indices (RCIs) were calculated to determine whether participants experienced clinically meaningful changes beyond measurement error (Jacobson & Truax, 1991). RCI is a widely recognized empirical standard for assessing individual clinical change. A Reliable Change Index (RCI) is computed by dividing the difference between baseline and post-treatment scores by the standard error of the difference between the two scores. The Cronbach's alpha value from baseline was used. If the RCI is greater than 1.96, then the difference is reliable; a change of that magnitude would not be expected due to the unreliability of the measure. Conversely, if the RCI score is 1.96 or less, then the change is not considered to be reliable; it could have occurred just due to the unreliability of the measure. In the current study, RCIs were calculated from baseline to post-treatment (baseline–post-treatment) to assess immediate treatment effects, and from baseline to 3-month follow-up (baseline–3-month follow-up) to evaluate the maintenance of therapeutic gains over time. (2) The change in the probable diagnosis of PGD and PTSD was used to provide an additional approximation of clinical significance. The Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) model was used (Ziegler & Vens, 2010). The binary PGD outcome (0 = not meeting criteria, 1 = meeting criteria) was specified with a binomial distribution and logit link function. Group (intervention vs. control), time (Baseline, Post-treatment, 3-month follow-up), and their interaction were included as fixed factors. An exchangeable working correlation structure was used to account for within-subject dependencies.

Participant flow is depicted in Figure 1. Participants were on average 40.24 (SD = 11.98) years old, and 82.7% (110/133) were female. Posttraumatic stress disorder

After adjusting for age and sex, the linear mixed models demonstrated that a significant group × time interaction was observed for posttraumatic stress symptoms (F(2, 128.45) = 5.45, η2 = 0.08, p = .005). Between-group differences were small at post-treatment (Cohen’s d = 0.35) and increased to a medium magnitude by the 3-month follow-up (Cohen’s d = 0.52) (Table 3 and Figure 2). Analyses limited to completers produced results consistent with the intention-to-treat models, showing the same pattern of effect sizes across assessment points.

Regarding the Reliable Change Indices (RCIs), from baseline to post-treatment, 14 bereaved individuals (25.5%) in the IG demonstrated significant improvement, compared with 3 individuals (6.3%) in the CG. In addition, 1 participant (1.8%) in the IG and 1 participant (2.1%) in the CG showed symptom deterioration. This difference is statistically significant (χ1032 = 6.864, p = .032). From baseline to 3-month follow-up, 16 bereaved individuals (29.1%) in the IG improved, whereas 5 participants (11.1%) in the CG improved, and one individual (1.0%) in the CG experienced significant symptom deterioration. This difference approached significance (χ1002 = 5.820, p = .054). These findings suggest a potentially protective and stabilizing effect of the intervention in reducing trauma-related symptoms over time.

In terms of PTSD prevalence, 26.8% (15/55) of participants in the IG and 12.5% (6/48) in the CG no longer met the criteria for probable PTSD from pre- to post-assessment (χ1042 = 7.330, p = .026). By the 3-month follow-up, 29.1% (16/55) of the IG and 15.6% (7/45) of the CG showed a transition from meeting to not meeting the probable PTSD; however, this difference was not statistically significant (χ1002 = 3.235, p = .198).

Discussion

In response to the unmet mental health needs of bereaved individuals in China, we developed and evaluated a novel bereavement intervention programme, Grief Modular Group Therapy (GMGT), targeting prolonged grief and posttraumatic stress symptoms. The programme was specifically designed to alleviate challenges encountered during the grieving process. This study showed the feasibility and effectiveness of the GMGT in reducing symptoms of PGD and PTSD to a clinically significant extent.

First, the successful implementation of GMGT across diverse bereaved populations – specifically those grieving the loss of a parent, spouse, or a child – confirmed Hypothesis 1 regarding the feasibility of the intervention. The high completion rate (mean attendance of 9.27 out of 10 sessions) suggests that the modular structure of the intervention possesses sufficient clinical flexibility to address the distinct psychological trajectories associated with different kinship losses. By culturally adapting universal grief elements, such as emotion regulation, meaning-reconstruction, cognitive behavioural techniques, and continuing bonds, GMGT demonstrated broad applicability across different types of kinship loss within the Chinese context.

The feasibility of the GMGT was confirmed by a dropout rate of 20.2% (i.e. 14 of the 69 participants withdrew). This dropout rate is comparable to, and in some cases lower than, those reported in previous web-based bereavement intervention studies (ranging from 10% to 58%; Wagner, 2020), suggesting that the structured yet flexible modular design is acceptable to the Chinese bereaved. The low dropout rate supported Hypothesis 2.

The significant group × time nteraction effect, with the intervention group showing a steeper decline in PGD symptoms than the control, demonstrated the effectiveness of the GMGT in reducing prolonged grief symptoms. The results are consistent with Hypothesis 3. Notably, the intervention yielded large effects from baseline to post-treatment (Cohen’s d = 0.85) and to the 3-month follow-up (Cohen’s d = 0.86), resulting in clinically meaningful improvement. These outcomes align with prior meta-analyses, which consistently report that psychotherapies outperform waitlist controls, with effect sizes in the medium-to-large range (Hao et al., 2024; Johannsen et al., 2019). The observed effect size was close to those reported for GF-CBT intervention, which has been identified as one of the most effective intervention approaches (Hao et al., 2024; Komischke-Konnerup et al., 2024; Srivastava et al., 2025). Compared to findings from previous studies on bereavement group interventions which showed that bereavement groups were marginally more effective than the control groups post-treatment but not at follow-up (Maass et al., 2022), the current structured group intervention is quite promising. As an intervention with integrated theoretical bases, the GMGT incorporated components such as emotion regulation, psychoeducation, cognitive restructuring, meaning-making, social support, and narrative exposure – elements that align with those identified as most effective in systematic reviews of grief interventions (Pleshka et al., 2025).

Similarly, the significant group × time interaction effect showed the effectiveness of the GMGT in alleviating PTSD symptoms three months after the intervention, further supporting Hypothesis 3. Unlike prolonged grief symptoms, the effect sizes of PTSD symptoms were small from baseline to post-treatment (Cohen’s d = 0.35) and approached medium at 3 months (Cohen’s d = 0.52). The IG demonstrated clinically meaningful improvement compared with the CG, suggesting that the intervention not only alleviates PGD symptoms but also gradually ameliorates associated posttraumatic stress symptoms. These findings align with previous grief-focused psychotherapies, which reported small-to-medium effects on PTSD symptoms (Kaiser et al., 2022; Komischke-Konnerup et al., 2024). The emotion regulation module helps participants manage emotions evoked by traumatic memories, while the narrative exposure module facilitates the reprocessing of distressing memories, thereby contributing to reductions in posttraumatic stress symptoms (Kaiser et al., 2022).

The current intervention was characterized by the cultural adaptation of the GMGT framework, thereby adapting and extending traditional Western grief interventions such as PGT and GF-CBT (Hao et al., 2024; Komischke-Konnerup et al., 2024; Simon & Shear, 2024; Srivastava et al., 2025). While PGT and GF-CBT protocols typically introduce trauma-disclosure or exposure early in treatment, GMGT prioritized a ‘stabilization-first’ approach tailored to Chinese cultural sensibilities. In the early sessions (Sessions 1 and 2), we integrated mindfulness-based emotion regulation and the concept of yangsheng (health preservation), practices that are culturally familiar and provided participants with a safe psychological foundation. This allowed participants to stabilize their affect before confronting the reality of the loss through narrative exposure sessions. The pacing of GMGT further emphasized restoration-oriented elements – such as meaning-reconstruction and resilience – during the initial phase, aligning with cultural contexts that often discourage the overt expression of intense negative emotions (Hsu et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2021a). The integration of meaning-reconstruction distinguishes GMGT from PGT and GF-CBT, as these established interventions do not incorporate explicit meaning-reconstruction elements (Komischke-Konnerup et al., 2024; Simon & Shear, 2024). Resilience-oriented strategies have received increasing attention in bereavement interventions (Hone, 2017). In GMGT, such strategies are explicitly incorporated as a dedicated module, whereas they are not systematically or explicitly addressed in PGT or GF-CBT. In addition, different from PGT, cultural context is a key element of GMGT, shaping the content, modes of expression, homework, and session sequencing, etc. For example, PGT integrates interpersonal therapy elements (Simon & Shear, 2024). Both individual and group-based PGT typically invite a significant other of the bereaved person to participate in treatment (Shear et al., 2014; Supiano & Luptak, 2014), a component that was not adopted in the present study. This decision was made because bereaved individuals in Chinese cultural contexts are generally less willing to share their grief with people close to them (Zhou et al., 2023), yet are more comfortable disclosing grief with those who have had similar loss experiences. Furthermore, we incorporated culturally meaningful rituals, including participation in Qingming tomb-sweeping and other traditional commemorations, and used culturally respectful language when referring to the deceased. These adaptations likely enhanced participants’ sense of emotional safety and cultural resonance, reducing therapy-related fear and preventing the ‘emotional flooding’ that can occur in non-adapted protocols. Together, these strategies supported sustained engagement and clinically meaningful improvements in the Chinese bereaved population.

Even established GF-CBT requires flexibility to accommodate individual participants’ characteristics and clinical needs. For example, Maccallum et al. (2025) reported that patients with significant avoidance of the death event presenting a major challenge, highlighting the need for therapists to adjust the timing, emphasis, and delivery of core components based on individual patterns of avoidance, guilt, and relational dynamics. From this perspective, the cultural adaptations embedded in GMGT can be understood as structured extensions of the flexibility already inherent in grief-focused interventions – particularly in contexts where direct emotional expression and self-focused processing may be less normative (Hsu et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2021a).

Overall, the present findings suggest that culturally adapted GMGT is well-suited to the Chinese sociocultural context. As previously discussed, within this context, death and grief are often negatively perceived (Hsu et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2021a; Yu, 2007), which can lead individuals to experience repression, avoidance, and fear when facing the reality of their loss. GMGT addressed these challenges through a gradual, phased structure, prioritizing emotion regulation and restoration-oriented themes in the early stages. Grief- and trauma-focused exposure is introduced only after participants have acquired sufficient coping skills. Incorporating such cultural considerations into group counselling can reduce psychological resistance and increase participants’ engagement in the therapeutic process. Furthermore, this approach promotes authentic emotional expression and peer support, thereby enhancing the overall effectiveness of the intervention within this population.

Although this study did not directly compare culturally adapted GMGT with PGT and GF-CBT, the acceptable dropout rate and clinically meaningful symptom reductions observed in the intervention group suggest that cultural adaptation is not merely an ancillary modification, but a potentially important factor in optimizing grief interventions for Chinese bereaved populations. Future randomized controlled trials comparing culturally adapted and non-adapted grief-focused interventions are warranted to clarify the specific contribution of cultural tailoring to treatment outcomes.

Limitations and future directions

While our study successfully implemented the GMGT among Chinese bereaved people and showed satisfactory feasibility and effectiveness, several limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. First, we did not apply randomization when allocating participants to the intervention group and control group. Furthermore, the control group was a minimal support control group (having access to an online social media group and self-help resources) rather than an active control. Ideally, a randomized controlled trial with a care-as-usual control group (i.e. either a peer-support group or interpersonal group therapy) should be conducted to achieve the most rigorous results when demonstrating the effectiveness of the GMGT.

Second, although our study conducted 3-month follow-up assessments and found that the effects of GMGT were maintained over three months, it will be important to evaluate the long-term sustainability of these outcomes beyond this period. Assessing whether continued intervention is necessary to prevent relapse into higher distress levels or PTSD symptoms is a vital next step.

Third, the study's sample may have had selection bias, as participants were recruited online and consisted primarily of those with higher levels of digital literacy and motivation to seek help, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader bereaved population in China.

Beyond improvements in study design, future research should further explore the mechanisms underlying the efficacy of GMGT. Specifically, the role of cultural factors – such as the traditional Chinese emphasis on emotional restraint and filial piety – warrants deeper investigation to determine how they influence the cognitive appraisal of loss and the group sharing process. Additionally, future studies should examine the impact of individual grief experiences, including the quality of pre-existing social support and the bereaved’s interpersonal environment, to understand how these variables moderate the therapeutic outcomes. Identifying these underlying mediators and moderators will facilitate the development of more personalized and culturally responsive interventions for the Chinese bereaved population.

Conclusions

Prolonged grief has significant ramifications for individuals and society. In China, mental health services are limited, and specialized intervention options are still scarce. An acceptable and effective online group intervention may address the mental health care needs of the bereaved suffering from prolonged grief.

Our study extends previous findings by providing evidence for the efficacy of a structured group intervention that was specifically developed for Chinese bereaved people. It proved effective in decreasing the symptoms of PGD and PTSD to a clinically significant extent. Therefore, GMGT represents a promising approach for reducing the treatment gap among Chinese bereaved individuals experiencing prolonged grief symptoms.
