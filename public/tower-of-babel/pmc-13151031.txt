ABSTRACT
AbstractHospital discharge is a pivotal stage in the continuum of care, marking the transition from the acute clinical episode to subsequent therapeutic management. Rather than being a purely administrative procedure, discharge is a multifaceted process that requires structured planning, clear communication, and the coordinated participation of all healthcare stakeholders, with the patient playing a central role. Inadequate discharge processes may adversely affect clinical outcomes and increase hospital readmission rates, resulting in sub-optimal utilization of healthcare resources. This issue is particularly relevant in the context of an ageing and increasingly frail population, characterized by a high burden of comorbidities and complex clinical needs. In this setting, discharge planning should be treated as an integral component of the care pathway, embedded within a comprehensive, coordinated model of care. This document proposes an updated, practical framework for hospital discharge management, grounded in current scientific evidence and multidisciplinary expertise. Its primary objective is to enhance continuity of care, support individualized treatment strategies, and ultimately improve patient outcomes. Specifically, this paper aims to provide clinical cardiologists with a structured reference tool for discharge management, including actionable recommendations to strengthen physician–patient communication, optimize therapeutic regimens, and promote better health outcomes.

Introduction

Hospital discharge represents a crucial component within the continuum of care and a fundamental element of the hospital–community pathway. An appropriately structured discharge process enables patients to receive the information necessary to return home, gain awareness and reassurance, and allows primary care physicians to manage ongoing care safely and effectively. This contributes to improved therapeutic appropriateness and adherence, more rational use of healthcare resources, avoidance of unnecessary testing, and the implementation of pharmacological and non-pharmacological strategies consistent with the patient’s individual risk profile and recommended clinical targets.

Discharge should not be regarded as a single moment limited to the delivery of the discharge letter. Rather, it is a process that develops throughout hospitalization and relies on effective communication. Information should be provided in a language, with timing and depth of explanation tailored to each patient's characteristics, needs, and level of understanding.

The discharge letter is the primary document through which information is conveyed regarding the clinical course, the potential consequences of the acute event on daily life, and the therapeutic plan. This includes the initiation and long-term continuation of pharmacological treatment—some of which may be lifelong—together with clear recommendations on lifestyle modification.

At present, no specific guidelines strictly regulate the structure of discharge letters; available recommendations mainly outline the essential elements to include. In the development of this document, consideration was also given to the areas of competence of other healthcare professionals—such as nurses, physiotherapists, psychologists, and dietitians—who, depending on the organization and resources of individual centres, contribute to the care pathway. This broader perspective allows physicians receiving the discharge letter to obtain a comprehensive, multidimensional overview, reinforcing its role as a continuity-of-care tool and a bridge between the acute phase and long-term management. Building on previous evidence,1 an update was considered appropriate in relation to the recent data concerning pharmacological therapies and post-discharge management strategies. To improve practical adoption and use, the document incorporates flowcharts and checklists. Particular attention is paid to communication strategies and the expectations and needs of both patients and general practitioners, which are key elements in constructing an effective and clinically appropriate discharge pathway.

Communication strategies for ameliorating outcomes

An inaccurate discharge should be regarded as a communication failure. However, this failure cannot be limited to the final moment of discharge, as it usually results from the accumulation of unresolved communication gaps throughout the hospital stay.

Discharge may be viewed as a narrowing point in the care pathway: both the amount and the quality of information that successfully passes through must be carefully managed, so that what ultimately reaches the patient—and leaves with them—is sufficient, relevant, and meaningful.2 Many of these pieces of information must subsequently be translated into actions by the patient and therefore need to be practically applicable and enabling.

During hospitalization, communication exchanges should be approached with a forward-looking perspective, as a sequence of interdependent steps. This allows progressive adjustment to the patient’s communicative abilities, which is essential for identifying and sharing priorities and for reaching discharge with a concise set of key messages and clear instructions on symptom management, medication use, risk factors, and strategies for their control (Figure 1).

The phases of communication from the beginning of hospitalization to discharge. The sequence is determined by the progressive relevance of the objectives to be achieved, with a gradual narrowing of the focus on the patient and with a selection of information that takes into account the patient’s priorities and not only the disease. For each phase, the main objective and the operational sub-objectives are indicated; in addition, the communication skills that the physician must possess in order to achieve them are reported. Achieving the objectives of one phase is a pre-requisite for moving on to the subsequent phase and thus for arriving at good communication at discharge.

Today, more than in the past, physician–patient communication can be challenging, and a stable, effective connection is not always achieved or maintained. Shorter hospital stays reduce the time available for dialogue, increasing the likelihood of information gaps and misunderstandings. These, in turn, may amplify organizational barriers to an effective discharge, such as the absence of standardized procedures and insufficient coordination among healthcare professionals.

There are no specific categories of ‘critical’ patients; actually, there are recurrent situations which, if not adequately addressed from a communication standpoint, may weaken the physician–patient relationship and consequently affect the quality not only of discharge but of the entire care pathway (Table 1).

Effective communication requires an appropriate setting. When a dedicated space is unavailable, the environment should be managed to minimize interference. If discharge communication occurs in a crowded corridor, the physician’s posture and positioning remain relevant: standing with the back to the wall and facing the patient helps convey availability and attention rather than haste. Eye contact should be established and maintained, particularly in contexts where multiple healthcare professionals are present and engaged in parallel activities.

The internal structure of verbal messages is also important. Key information should be presented at the beginning of the sentence, reiterated at the end, and reinforced through consistent non-verbal cues, including tone of voice and gestures, to enhance clarity and retention.

The ability to deliver bad news is a core communication competence. Clinicians must identify what constitutes bad news from the patient’s perspective and use an appropriate approach to avoid communication that appears abrupt, detached, or uncomfortable. Information becomes bad news when it negatively alters an individual’s expectations regarding present or future life conditions.3 For patients, the perceived loss of opportunities and the discrepancy between expectations and reality often weigh more than objective clinical severity in defining the information as distressing.4

There is no universal formula for communicating bad news, given the number of variables involved. However, specific communication strategies can increase effectiveness and reduce common errors, which are frequently associated with distraction, time pressure, and inadequate exploration of patient expectations (Table 2).

Assessment of informational needs should take into account both age and gender. Young individuals without a prior history of disease may leave the hospital without fully understanding their diagnosis or the importance of preventive strategies, while frail, elderly adults may risk being discharged with therapeutic plans that are difficult to sustain, complex, or challenging to remember and implement.5

Women should receive information on gender-specific cardiovascular risk factors, which vary across different age groups, as well as on psychosocial stressors related to gender roles. The need for clarifications is often greater in women than in men. Young women frequently lack caregiver support and may be required to manage multiple roles simultaneously,6 whereas elderly women are often less autonomous. In both situations, specific lifestyle recommendations are necessary, and pharmacological treatment planning should consider the higher likelihood of sub-optimal adherence observed in women, which may require targeted motivational support.

Consistency in communication among all healthcare professionals involved in the care pathway should be ensured. Nurses should be actively involved in discharge preparation, as their closer and more frequent contact with patients and family members may provide valuable insights, including an assessment of whether the patient and caregiver feel ready for discharge.

Hospital discharge as a process: a systematic approach

Discharge planning during hospitalization

Effective hospital discharge management requires that discharge planning be initiated at the time of admission. Early assessment of the clinical complexity underlying hospitalization, together with evaluation of the patient’s overall condition and degree of frailty, allows the development of an individualized plan that supports appropriate length of stay and suitable post-discharge pathways.

Discharge should therefore be regarded as a process that begins at admission and involves the patient and, when appropriate, the caregiver. When adequately informed about the clinical situation, patients and caregivers can actively contribute to defining the different management steps, up to the transition from hospital to home or to another healthcare or social-care facility.

International guideline recommendations7 support the use of structured discharge approaches, as these are associated with reduced 1-year mortality. These documents outline key steps for discharge after an acute event, specifying objectives, responsible professionals, and operational tools.8,9

Conversely, when discharge is managed in a non-standardized manner and considered secondary to in-hospital treatment, the benefits achieved during the acute phase may be rapidly lost, with an increased risk of early hospital readmission.

A proper discharge process requires an integrated, multidisciplinary team (MDT) approach. Physicians and nurses play a central role and are responsible for overseeing all phases, including the key transitions that connect the patient, caregivers, primary care physicians, and any intermediate services potentially involved after discharge, such as inpatient or outpatient cardiac rehabilitation (CR), cardiology clinics for secondary prevention, and services dedicated to continuity of care.

Discharge planning is based on an appropriate assessment phase, which should be performed at admission and repeated during hospitalization as clinical conditions evolve. The definition of post-hospital care pathways should be completed before the planned discharge date. Only coordinated involvement of all stakeholders—sharing objectives, tools, implementation strategies, and roles—ensures an effective process (Table 3).

Multidimensional assessment of patient’s needs and individualized discharge planning

The epidemiological transition has led to a substantial increase in the number of elderly patients with chronic cardiovascular disease, frequently characterized by multi-morbidity and frailty. These conditions are associated with a higher risk of complications related both to the underlying disease and to diagnostic and therapeutic interventions—as well as increased mortality, rehospitalization, disability (defined as limitations in performing basic or instrumental activities of daily living), reduced quality of life, and greater healthcare resource utilization.10

Frailty is a multifactorial syndrome characterized by progressive biological decline, particularly affecting cognitive function, muscle strength, gait speed, endurance, asthenia, and unintentional weight loss, resulting in increased vulnerability to stressors.11 Although age is not included in formal definitions of frailty, advancing age is strongly associated with a higher likelihood of this condition.

Consequently, cardiologists are frequently faced with hospitalized elderly patients who present multi-morbidity, disability, and frailty, representing a population that differs substantially from those enrolled in clinical trials. This scenario requires integrated and holistic approaches at discharge, shifting the focus from the single disease to the overall complexity of the individual. One possible response, both at the individual clinician level and within healthcare systems, is the adoption of multidimensional assessment (MDA) processes aimed at systematically defining the patient’s clinical and care profile.

Multidimensional assessment can be defined as an inter-disciplinary diagnostic process that complements routine clinical evaluation, with the objective of quantifying medical, psychosocial, and functional problems. Its purpose is to develop a comprehensive plan for treatment, care, and short- and long-term follow-up, while also supporting safe and appropriate discharge planning.12

Multidimensional assessment enables an integrated and objective evaluation by using validated scales and tools exploring multiple health domains, including physical and functional status, psychological well-being, cognitive performance, and socio-economic factors (Table 4).

Several studies have shown that MDA is associated with improved survival, better functional and psychological outcomes, and reduced rehospitalization.

Recent national legislation has formally recognized the importance of MDA as a tool to facilitate access to services for elderly and frail individuals, and its role has been further emphasized within community-based access models.

The multidisciplinary and inter-professional team in discharge planning

Discharge planning is one of the most common activities in hospital settings, where the inter-professional team plays a central role in supporting the transition to home or to another care facility.15 The proportion of patients readmitted to hospital within the first year after an episode of heart failure has been estimated at ∼60.9%.16 For this reason, the development of a structured and individualized discharge plan aimed at addressing both healthcare and social needs is strongly recommended to reduce avoidable readmissions.

The Institute of Medicine identifies planned, timely, efficient, and safe hospital discharge as an indicator of high-quality care.17 Length of hospital stay is also considered a key performance indicator and a target of healthcare planning. However, hospitals frequently encounter difficulties in consistently meeting discharge targets and organizational benchmarks.18

Unstructured collaboration and communication among members of multidisciplinary and multi-professional teams are recognized sources of information duplication, role overlap, and fragmentation of responsibilities, potentially leading to inter-professional conflict.19 Multidisciplinary teams operate across both hospital and community settings; nevertheless, in highly fragmented organizational contexts, communication at discharge may be ineffective, creating barriers to continuity of care and compromising appropriate handover to other professionals.20

Effective discharge planning has positive implications not only for patients and their families or caregivers but also for healthcare organizations. It is associated with reduced hospital readmission rates, shorter hospital stays, increased patient satisfaction, and improved overall management of hospitalization.21

To ensure effective discharge planning within a MDT, coordination among professionals must be structured, collaboration among team members must be active, and each professional’s roles and responsibilities must be clearly defined and understood by the entire team (Figure 2).

Management of the discharge process by the multidisciplinary assessment team.

Among the actors involved in the discharge process, the patient must be considered an active participant in care decisions.22 Evidence indicates that active patient engagement during hospitalization and at discharge improves understanding of the clinical condition and reduces hospital readmissions.23

Across acute, rehabilitative, and community or home-care settings, effective inter-professional care models are required to support patients and families. These models should include strategies to strengthen coordination and collaboration within and between MDTs. Several studies show that MDT interventions improve clinical outcomes and patient satisfaction; however, evidence specifically addressing the effectiveness of teamwork during the discharge process remains limited.24

Useful information for the nurse

Hospital discharge is a key stage in the care pathway, as it represents the transition from a monitored clinical setting to an environment in which the patient is required to manage treatment and follow-up independently or with caregiver support. A structured discharge process is therefore necessary to ensure continuity of care and to limit complications and unplanned readmissions. In this context, nurses have a central role because of their continuous contact with patients and families.1,25

During the initial assessment, nurses evaluate clinical stability, the patient’s ability to self-manage, and existing care needs.26 This phase includes a review of medication therapy and an assessment of available social and family resources, enabling early identification of support requirements.27 Discharge planning is then carried out in collaboration with the MDT—such as cardiologists, physiotherapists, nutrition specialists, or other professionals according to clinical needs—allowing the definition of realistic goals and the organization of follow-up visits or home care services.

Education of patients and caregivers is a core component of the process. Nurses provide clear, tailored information on therapies, diet, physical activity, and recognizing warning signs of clinical instability. Verification of understanding is essential and can be supported through teach-back strategies, written materials, or practical demonstrations.1,26 Programmes that combine discharge planning with post-discharge support are associated with better outcomes.28 Nurses also facilitate access to community nursing services, specialist consultations, and social or volunteer networks, helping to maintain support after discharge.28

The final step involves planning appropriate post-discharge follow-up. When required, nurses may arrange early telephone contacts or short-term visits to assess adherence and clinical stability and to detect early signs of deterioration.29 These measures contribute to reducing rehospitalizations and improving patients’ sense of security. Overall, hospital discharge requires coordinated management in which nurses, through organizational and relational competencies, support a safe transition and promote the achievement of health objectives and patient well-being.30

Key dietary components and nutritional recommendations in cardiovascular prevention

The World Health Organization recognizes nutritional literacy as a central element in the prevention and control of non-communicable diseases.35 The acquisition of basic nutritional knowledge is fundamental for empowering patients and enabling informed dietary decisions.33,36-41 Moreover, multi-morbidity—with or without age-related frailty—is frequently associated with nutritional issues that may coexist with metabolic disturbances. This may necessitate the use of energy-dense foods, texture-modified diets and fluids, or, in selected cases, artificial nutrition. Early identification of nutritional risk is therefore crucial, and clinical guidelines recommend routine screening in elderly adults.36

Treatment goals are established based on the nutritional diagnosis,37 a process that identifies and describes nutrition-related problems that can be resolved or improved through dietetic intervention. Close collaboration among members of the cardiology care team is essential to achieve nutritional targets and optimize patient outcomes (Table 2).

Physiotherapy contributions to discharge planning

Resumption of physical activity after hospital discharge in cardiology patients must be individualized according to clinical status, in-hospital course, length of stay, and additional factors such as comorbidities, severity of the acute event, and age.42 Careful evaluation of functional status is required, as patients may range from physically fit individuals to those with severe functional limitations. This assessment is essential for defining a safe and effective rehabilitation pathway.

The main objectives of post-discharge physical activity include improving cardiac function and quality of life, preventing recurrence, and promoting patient autonomy. Appropriate determination of exercise intensity is a key element in cardiovascular rehabilitation, directly affecting both effectiveness and safety.43

During the first weeks after discharge, high-intensity physical activity should be avoided. Exercise intensity can be guided by target heart rate (typically 50–85% of age-predicted maximum heart rate), perceived exertion on the Borg scale, and the Talk Test, which indicates the patient should be able to speak without difficulty during activity. Exercise modalities should be adapted to the patient profile: sedentary individuals may begin with short walks and stretching; patients with better baseline fitness may perform light aerobic exercise; and elderly individuals should perform exercises to improve balance and muscle strength.

Continuous monitoring of the cardiovascular response during physical activity is necessary, and symptoms should be reported promptly. Guidelines recommend low-impact aerobic activities that enhance cardiovascular endurance, such as cycling on a low-resistance stationary bicycle with a gradual progression to higher workloads or outdoor cycling. Resistance training with light weights and low loads is also recommended, favouring sessions with more repetitions. Circuit training, combining resistance and aerobic components, may improve overall physical condition. Functional activities such as brisk walking and stair climbing should be integrated into recovery programmes.

When patients are referred to structured CR programmes, individualized pathways including supervised exercise, psychological support, and nutritional counselling are associated with reduced complication risk and improved functional capacity.

At discharge, the cardiologist should provide clear guidance on gradually increasing physical activity. Patients should be encouraged to walk, even at a slow pace, both indoors and outdoors, use stairs when possible, and participate in group activities that promote social engagement, such as low-intensity exercise classes or walking groups, while appropriately monitoring exercise intensity. The long-term goal is to achieve 30–60 min of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity on at least 5 days per week.44 Even lower levels of activity provide clinically relevant benefits, including improved mobility, autonomy, mood, and social participation, particularly in elderly adults (Table 6).45

Management of pharmacological and non-pharmacological therapy

Lifestyle recommendations

Lifestyle exerts a substantial influence on prognosis after hospitalization for an acute event. Hence the importance of intervening on all those lifestyle habits that, if modified, allow improvement in the outcome after discharge. Smoking cessation is a major priority among lifestyle interventions. Patients should be informed about the risks associated with tobacco use and strongly encouraged to achieve permanent abstinence. Strategies to support smoking cessation include structured counselling, psychological support, and, when indicated, pharmacological therapy.

Other important components of lifestyle management after hospitalization include physical activity and nutrition, which are discussed in dedicated sections of this document.

Recommendations to improve treatment adherence

Adherence to prescribed therapies is a key determinant of clinical outcomes; therefore, all feasible measures to support medication adherence should be implemented.46 One effective strategy is to initiate treatment during hospitalization or within a structured CR programme, where patients can receive clear explanations about the purpose of each therapy, possible adverse effects, and practical guidance on their management.

The use of fixed-dose combination therapies has been shown to enhance adherence, contribute to improved prognosis,47 and offer advantages in terms of cost-effectiveness.48

Medication reconciliation

At discharge, it is essential to implement medication reconciliation by reviewing the patient's previous therapy and, for each drug, providing clear instructions on continuation, possible dosage adjustments, or discontinuation. Careful attention to this process helps reduce errors due to incomplete information. Medication reconciliation may also represent an opportunity for deprescribing drugs that no longer provide benefit in a clinical context modified after the acute event, with a favourable impact on treatment adherence as well.46

Release of therapeutic plan cards

The prescription and reimbursement of several medications require the compilation of therapeutic plan cards. Medication supply at discharge

At discharge, in addition to detailed therapeutic prescriptions specifying administration route, dosage, and timing, it is advisable to provide the patient with all prescribed medications in quantities sufficient to cover the period between hospital discharge, reassessment by the general practitioner (GP) with issuance of prescriptions covered by the National Health Service (when applicable), and drug procurement from community pharmacies. This practice aims to ensure continuity of therapy and is particularly important when a patient is prescribed new medications.

Post-acute pathway and secondary prevention

Cardiac rehabilitation pathway

At discharge after an acute cardiovascular event, direct referral to a CR programme represents the most cost-effective strategy to achieve multiple care objectives, including further clinical stabilization, reduction of disability related to the underlying heart disease, favourable lifestyle modification (smoking cessation, diet, and physical activity), and the provision of appropriate psychosocial support.42 For a detailed description of the components and professional figures involved in the rehabilitation pathway, refer to the dedicated documents.49

Virtually, every major cardiovascular event is appropriate for referral to CR; however, in the current national context, patients with coronary disease or heart failure represent the main target population, not only due to their epidemiological relevance but also because of the stronger evidence supporting the effectiveness of rehabilitation interventions, particularly in terms of event reduction and lowering of residual risk.50,51

Cardiac rehabilitation programmes are delivered after discharge in either inpatient or outpatient settings, depending on patient characteristics and local availability. Inpatient rehabilitation is provided in structured hospital units, recently surveyed as 116 nationwide, with a total of 2075 beds,52 and is mostly classified under the ministerial code 56, which identifies intensive rehabilitation. Inpatient facilities may be located within the same hospital as the acute cardiology ward, in separate facilities, or within different healthcare institutions.

Inpatient CR activities have recently undergone regulatory revision (Ministerial Decree of 5 August 2021, ‘Criteria for appropriateness of access to hospital rehabilitation admissions,’ and further updates), which established appropriateness criteria based on combined evaluation of the hospital discharge report from both the acute and rehabilitation units, timing of patient transfer, and levels of intervention complexity. Outpatient pathways may be delivered in day-hospital, day-service, or complex outpatient settings, provided that an adequate quantitative and qualitative level of rehabilitation intervention is ensured to achieve the predefined clinical and prognostic goals. The system scenario described above, together with the well-known shortage of rehabilitation facilities relative to potential demand, significantly affects the referral process to CR at discharge.

The current CR referral rate reported by Agenzia Nazionale per i Servizi Sanitari Regionali, below 10% after acute coronary syndrome at a national level,53 highlights a marked misalignment with recommendations from major international guidelines, which almost unanimously assign a Class I, Level A recommendation to CR interventions. This situation—given its potential implications in terms of physicians’ professional responsibility—unmet obligations of care by hospital institutions, and, above all, population health loss cannot be simplistically accepted as being due to inevitable reasons.

Within the individualized follow-up programme, the frequency of subsequent visits—typically at 3–6 months and 12 months after the acute event—is modulated according to the individual risk and the underlying disease (myocardial infarction, heart failure, valvular disease, comorbidities, etc.). Thereafter, once the patient is stabilized and therapy optimized, visits may be spaced further apart, becoming annual and subsequently biennial.

In this context, the outpatient cardiologist (whether hospital-based or working in community care facilities) serves as the specialist reference point, managing drug titration, scheduling follow-up investigations [echocardiography, exercise testing, and Holter electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring], and indicating invasive or rehabilitation procedures when appropriate. A fundamental pre-requisite for community cardiology is the presence of an organizational structure (structural or functional, included within accredited private facilities) based on integrated specialist teams, ideally multidisciplinary (including diabetologists, nephrologists, pulmonologists, etc.), multi-professional, and equipped with the necessary diagnostic tools for at least first-level non-invasive patient assessment.59

Within this organization, the nursing outpatient clinic represents a crucial node for close clinical monitoring, MDA, and delivery of personalized health education interventions aimed at self-care and self-monitoring.26 The GP collaborates with the cardiologist to monitor the patient’s clinical course and manage associated conditions.

Finally, protected hospital discharge represents an opportunity to promote multidisciplinary and multi-professional integration processes and tools, as well as territorial organizational-care models, in order to harmonize healthcare and social-care responses to the person’s health needs along the hospital–community continuity of care axis. Integration should be implemented at the level of the territorial operations centre, where available, which performs a coordinating function in patient care management and in linking services and professionals involved across different care settings.59

Telemedicine pathway

Heart failure represents a clinical condition in which telemedicine has particularly relevant and paradigmatic applications, given the complexity of its management and the high burden of hospital utilization. The challenges associated with heart failure are well recognized and relate to the large number of affected patients and the high rate of hospital admissions.

Although the literature consistently shows that structured post-discharge follow-up after hospitalization for heart failure is essential to reduce mortality and rehospitalization, only a limited proportion of patients are enrolled in dedicated outpatient follow-up programmes at discharge. The 2023 ESC Heart Failure Guidelines recommend the use of four foundational pharmacological classes—angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI), beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and sodium–glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors—which have been shown to reduce mortality and heart failure hospitalizations by ∼20%.60 The benefit of these therapies depends not only on initiation but also on early implementation and achievement of target dosages. The STRONG-HF trial demonstrated that an intensive post-discharge follow-up programme led by physicians and nurses significantly reduced mortality and rehospitalization at 180 days; however, such intensive models are difficult to implement in routine clinical practice.60

The post-discharge period (transitional care) following an acute event or heart failure exacerbation is associated with a high risk of mortality and rehospitalization.61 International guidelines, including ESC recommendations, support integrated home-based care pathways aimed at improving patient self-management, enhancing treatment adherence, enabling early detection of clinical instability, and reducing rehospitalizations (Figure 4).60

Telemedicine services provide a potential solution by enabling patient follow-up within an integrated, multi-professional care pathway. Tele-assistance and tele-monitoring have demonstrated benefit in this setting, with meta-analyses reporting reductions in mortality and rehospitalization.62 Given the recognized role of CR as a key component of optimal heart failure management, tele-rehabilitation also represents an important strategy, both as an alternative setting to increase referral rates and as an option for long-term maintenance programmes (Figure 4).63

Risk-stratified tele-monitoring strategies may further support multidisciplinary management, reduce the need for in-person outpatient visits, and facilitate optimization and titration of guideline-directed medical therapy. Shared cloud-based data platforms can enhance communication among healthcare providers. Remote monitoring of clinical parameters (heart rate, blood pressure, body weight), instrumental data (oxygen saturation, ECG), and laboratory values (sodium, potassium, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, creatinine, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide, and complete blood count) allows early identification of clinical deterioration and supports therapy adjustment. The patient’s perspective

That being a physician is a job, is not in question. A job like any other one with a routine and a thousand habits, with hectic moments and others of calm, phases of difficulty, and moments of tranquility. During the day, it may happen that colleagues exchange a few jokes, that there is gaiety, or friction, perhaps an argument. That being a physician is a job with its everyday different features and aspects. To be carried out with conscientiousness and professionalism, but one in which thoughts and concerns from personal life inevitably intrude, just as thoughts and concerns from the working day end up going home. A job, in short.

And yet this job has particular characteristics that make it somewhat different from all the others. Because it has to do with people’s lives, who, when they come into contact with these professionals, find themselves at a moment of immense difficulty and enormous importance. In many cases, at a true crossroad.

The people who are the object of this profession are often facing a radical change in their existence. Nothing will ever be the same again, in any case.

This is why, at the time of discharge, when one is about to leave behind this terrible phase of life, a patient must in no way feel like part of a routine. There is no comfort in being pigeonholed into an ordinary case history; a shared misfortune is no consolation at all, not even halfway.

It is at the moment of discharge that a person needs their doctor. On that particular occasion, fear is greater. One is no longer monitored, no longer observed in the course of the illness, no longer the object of professionals’ attention. One will not be able to rely on immediate, reassuring urgent interventions; there is no bell to call for help, at night there will be no attentive nurses ready to support any need. One is left alone, and the main difficulty will be to observe oneself and understand how one actually feels. The fear is not understanding the signals of one’s own body—whether they are the usual ones, the ordinary ones that were there before, or whether they are alarm bells, warnings from the body of a new crisis, perhaps lethal, perhaps unmanageable. One finds oneself facing a new, unknown fragility. With the awareness that it will not be as before and that one must therefore come to terms with a new condition.

The boundary between reassurance and minimization, we realize, is very thin. On one hand one would like to return to a normal life and that is what one is often told, but one also knows that it was precisely the previous life, often excessive ways and wrong behaviours, that brought them where they are; therefore no, one cannot be exactly as one was, one must change, but without feeling disabled, without carrying fears and indecision along.

An explanation of the therapies will certainly be useful; medications are a new fact, the patient trusts them and they are a protective factor; knowing what they are for, what function they have, helps a great deal. And behavioural instructions—what must be paid attention to and to which signals—will be an important comfort, a constant reference.

More than anything else, however, the smile will matter. There is no other moment in which one has more need of friendship, of closeness, even of affection. Nothing fake, nothing imposed. More than professionalism, more than competence will be necessary to feel that the doctor who puts you back into the world is on your side. Even if they remain in that place of suffering and yet reassuring, that smile will be the element patients carry home and remember when they find themselves alone, facing the visible and invisible wounds.

A smile. That’s all.

Models of discharge letters with follow-up timing according to disease

The proposed documents are concise templates that include the main recommendations for continuation of care and secondary prevention measures, based on the contribution of multiple healthcare professionals involved in patient management, care, and treatment.

The Panel considered it appropriate to focus on the cardiovascular diseases of greatest relevance in terms of prevalence, mortality, and hospitalization rates: ischaemic heart disease and heart failure, as well as hospital admissions related to cardiac pacing and electrophysiological procedures.

The principal clinical characteristics, the determinants influencing recurrences/exacerbations and rehospitalizations, and the therapeutic targets are briefly described, with the aim of optimizing follow-up, improving continuity-of-care pathways, and positively influencing prognosis.

Acute coronary syndrome

Predictors of new coronary events64-66 have been identified in multi-vessel coronary artery disease, female gender, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, advanced age, and obstructive sleep apnoea syndrome. Achieving therapeutic targets represents the main objective to reduce ‘residual cardiovascular risk’.65

Residual risk and main therapeutic targets

Arterial hypertension

Hypertension induces endothelial dysfunction, dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system, and impaired vasoreactivity.67 European guidelines recommend, in secondary prevention, blood pressure values <130/80 mmHg.

Dyslipidaemia

LDL levels <55 mg/dL are recommended in patients with ischaemic heart disease (<40 mg/dL in very-high-risk categories and in case of recurrent ischaemic events), with initiation of high-intensity lipid-lowering therapy already during hospitalization, followed by the addition of non-statin lipid-lowering agents (ezetimibe, bempedoic acid, or PCSK9 inhibitors) if the target is not achieved.68 Cases of hypertriglyceridaemia should be reported, with possible indication for treatment.

Diabetes mellitus

Diabetic patients often present multi-vessel disease and multiple coronary lesions, a higher prevalence of unstable atherosclerotic plaques, and altered microvascular reactivity.69 The therapeutic goal is to maintain glycated haemoglobin <7%, with adjustments according to patient age.

Smoking

Smoking cessation reduces the risk of recurrent myocardial infarction or cardiovascular death by 30–50% over the subsequent 3–7 years.70

Chronic kidney disease

These patients have a higher burden of comorbidities, endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory and pro-thrombotic states, and anaemia,71 as well as incomplete revascularization and sub-optimal therapeutic optimization.72

Main recommendations for the discharge letter

Include the main pre-existing risk factors (including unfavourable psychosocial conditions and/or stress), those not at target, and ‘residual’ risk factors (in-hospital complications, residual coronary stenosis, reduced ejection fraction, and prescription contraindications), as well as corrective interventions proposed by the MDT.

Pharmacological therapy should be clearly and comprehensibly described. It is suggested to list medications in chronological order of intake (including the active ingredient name) to improve adherence and reduce medication errors.

Patients should be referred to follow-up programmes and CR when indicated (Supplementary figure 1).

Patient with heart failure

Several factors contribute to the high mortality, hospitalization, and 30-day rehospitalization rates characterizing heart failure.73-75

Demographic and socio-economic characteristics

Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is more prevalent in men, whereas heart failure with preserved ejection fraction is more frequent in women.76 Low socio-economic status is associated with reduced quality of care, limited access to community services, and consequently lower treatment adherence and medication titration.77

Comorbidities

Diabetes mellitus is present in ∼40% of patients and is an independent predictor of new hospitalizations.78,79

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,80 present in about 30% of heart failure patients, contributes to right ventricular overload and dysfunction.

Renal failure promotes oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, volume overload, electrolyte imbalance, release of uraemic toxins, and anaemia.81,82

Anaemia increases hospitalization risk,83,84 and treatment—particularly of iron deficiency anaemia—improves functional capacity.84

Main recommendations for the discharge letter

Highlight precipitating factors and risk factors that may predispose to clinical destabilization; assign the patient to a nurse case manager where available; enrol in structured follow-up programmes specifying the optimal timing for the first control visit; and refer to CR pathways when indicated.

Provide a clear list of prescribed medications, preferably in chronological order of intake, and schedule—or indicate—titration in terms of timing, dosage, and need for clinical reassessment (Supplementary figure 2).

Patient undergoing electrostimulation/electrophysiology procedures

Provide the patient, the GP, and other specialists with a description of the procedures performed and their indications regarding the underlying disease, the clinical course, and any complications. The models of implanted devices and the related electronic parameters should be specified.

Clearly state the therapeutic indications and recommendations the patient must follow for recovery, follow-up, and ongoing care, including pre-arranged appointments and visits (Supplementary figure 3).

Cognitive decline, dementia, frailty, and multi-morbidity represent negative prognostic determinants, influencing therapeutic adherence, continuity of care, regularity of follow-up, and the burden on family members/caregivers.

The discharge letter should report episodes of disorientation, aggression, or delirium occurring during hospitalization, the specific treatments administered, and the planning of specialist follow-up to be conducted remotely (telephone or virtual) for less complex cases, reserving direct in-person care for more complex situations.

The increasing proportion of elderly patients is associated with frailty, multi-morbidity, cognitive impairment, polypharmacy, and disability; therefore, a multidisciplinary intervention is necessary and more effective.86

Frailty87 complicates disease progression, with higher rates of delirium, prolonged hospitalization, post-operative complications, and need for institutionalization after discharge. Conclusions

An updated discharge model characterized by practical operational references, grounded in the most recent scientific evidence and supported by the multidisciplinary expertise that works alongside the clinical cardiologist, is essential to ensure that discharge represents a central element in improving the care pathway. This pathway begins at the time of hospital admission and is therefore an integral part of the patient’s care process.

The discharge letter must include all essential information for the GP in a clear and accessible format, so as to be useful not only to the physician but also to the patient and family members or caregivers. It thus becomes a fundamental tool for the transmission of information concerning the entire hospitalization. Table 17 summarizes the essential contents of the discharge letter.
