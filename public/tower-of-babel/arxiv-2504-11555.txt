Sub-optimality of the Separation Principle for Quadratic Control from Bilinear Observations
Yahya Sattar, Sunmook Choi, Yassir Jedra, Maryam Fazel, Sarah Dean

Abstract
We consider the problem of controlling a linear dynamical system from bilinear observations with minimal quadratic cost. Despite the similarity of this problem to standard linear quadratic Gaussian (LQG) control, we show that when the observation model is bilinear, neither does the Separation Principle hold, nor is the optimal controller affine in the estimated state. Moreover, the cost-to-go is non-convex in the control input. Hence, finding an analytical expression for the optimal feedback controller is difficult in general. Under certain settings, we show that the standard LQG controller locally maximizes the cost instead of minimizing it. Furthermore, the optimal controllers (derived analytically) are not unique and are nonlinear in the estimated state. We also introduce a notion of input-dependent observability and derive conditions under which the Kalman filter covariance remains bounded. We illustrate our theoretical results through numerical experiments in multiple synthetic settings.



1
Introduction

In many engineering settings, measurements result from the interaction between a measurement device and an unknown quantity.
Traditionally, the unknown quantity is assumed to be independent of the measurement process;
however, in general it may be influenced by being measured.
This “observer effect” is present in examples ranging from electronic circuits, where measurement devices can alter resistance or impedance
[
14
]
, to robotics, where active perception requires interaction
[
12
]
, and quantum systems, where measurement induces wavefunction collapse
[
17
]
.
Perhaps the simplest model which captures such interaction is a
bilinear
observation model,
in which the output is a bilinear function of the control input and the unknown state. Recent works
[
14
,
19
]
propose a dynamical system model with linear transitions and bilinear measurements, and study the system identification problem.

In such settings, control inputs both change the state and affect observation of it.
How should controllers be designed to account for these dual roles?
In this paper, we address this question for optimal control of a linear dynamical system with bilinear observations and quadratic costs.
The bilinearity is a small departure from the standard partially observed linear quadratic problem
[
4
]
(LQG),
in which the optimal controller is known to obey the
Separation Principle
: it suffices to independently consider state estimation and a state feedback controller.
Separation principle has also been shown to holds in several variants of LQG, e.g., when measurement and control are performed over a packet-dropping link
[
8
]
.
Separating estimation from control is practically convenient, and it is a commonly used paradigm in practice, even when it is not guaranteed to be optimal.

We seek to develop a fundamental understanding of the trade-offs which arise when applying the separation principle to nonlinear and partially observed settings.
We show that in the presence of bilinear observations, the optimal control law does not obey the separation principle, nor is it linear.
Our results highlight
the potential for tension between control and measurement, especially when the observations are less noisy than the state evolution.
In particular, we show that the minimum of the LQG cost may maximize the state estimation error by causing a loss of observability.
Such input-dependent observability is a key challenge which arises in nonlinear and partially observed settings.

Classic optimal control problems for linear dynamics and measurements are well studied.
Perhaps the most well known is the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
criterion, which results in a linear controller that satisfies the separation principle
[
2
,
22
]
.
The robust
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
criterion also leads to a linear controller, and it asymptotically follows the separation principle
[
6
]
.
Linear controllers are optimal in other settings, such as the state feedback min/max problem proposed by
[
10
]
.
Even when not optimal, linear controllers are widely used.
Modern approaches based on
online learning
[
11
,
18
]
and system level synthesis
[
1
]

restrict controllers to be linear, mainly out of convenience, but do not enforce or utilize the separation principle.

Bilinear optimal control is generally intractable
[
17
]
, thus a natural focus for control synthesis is stabilization. To achieve stabilization, performing state estimation is a key step. For fixed input sequences, optimal state estimation for bilinear systems
follows the same principles as linear time varying systems.
As a result, when the noise is Gaussian, Kalman filtering is statistically optimal
[
13
,
4
]
.
However, due to the input-dependent dynamics matrices, the Kalman filter is
nonlinear
in the input and output variables.
In fact, unlike for linear systems, different choices of inputs may lead to better or worse filter performance.
The problem of selecting inputs to minimize estimation error is traditionally studied in active learning or experiment design
[
9
,
16
,
5
]
; however these settings treat the underlying state as static.
Considering how best to measure the state of a dynamical systems is also studied in sensor design
[
20
]
.
However, work in this area often focuses on discrete and combinatorial decision spaces, and do not model the “observer effect” of measurement on the state.

The relationship between estimation and control also plays an important role in optimal decentralized control. The famous Witsenhausen counterexample
[
21
]
constructed a simple two-stage LQG system where two (scalar valued) control decisions are made given decentralized information, and showed that for this system, linear controllers are not optimal. This simple counterexample has played a crucial role in understanding the challenges of decentralized control.

Contributions:
We make the following contributions towards addressing the problem of optimal control from bilinear observations:
(i) we show that the separation principle does not hold when the observation model is bilinear, and (ii) provide a negative result, stating that the optimal controller is not affine in the estimated state.
(iii) Under certain settings, we show that the optimal LQG controller can result in significantly worse performance, as it locally maximizes the cost function instead of minimizing.
(iv) We then derive an analytical expression for the optimal nonlinear controller in these settings. (v) We also introduce a notion of input dependent observability and derive conditions under which the Kalman filtering covariance remains bounded.

Notations:
We use boldface uppercase (lowercase) letters to denote matrices (vectors).
We use normal letters (uppercase & lowercase) to denote scalars.
For a vector
{\bm{u}}
,
({\bm{u}})_{i}

-th element.
For a matrix
{\bm{X}}
,

\textup{{tr}}({\bm{X}})

{\bm{X}}

2.1
Bilinear Observation Optimal Control Problem

In this paper, we consider the problem of optimally controlling a partially observed linear dynamical system (LDS) from bilinear observations (BO), with the objective of minimizing quadratic costs in states and actions. Specifically, we consider the following state-space representation: for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{t+1}

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}_{t}+{\bm{B}}{\bm{u}}_{t}+{\bm{w}}_{t},

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\big({\bm{C}}_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{p}({\bm{u}}_{t})_{k}{\bm{C}}_{k}\big){\bm{x}}_{t}+{\bm{z}}_{t},

where
{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state,
{\bm{u}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the input,
{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the output,
{\bm{w}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the process noise, and
{\bm{z}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the measurement noise at time
t
. The matrices
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and
{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
govern the state evolution, whereas,
{\bm{C}}_{0},{\bm{C}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{C}}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
govern the bilinear observation model. For notational convenience, we define the input dependent observation matrix
{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{t}):={\bm{C}}_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{p}({\bm{u}}_{t})_{k}{\bm{C}}_{k}
. In the remainder of the paper, we use LDS-BO
({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}},{\bm{C}}_{0},{\bm{C}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{C}}_{p})
to refer to the state-space representation (
2.1
). With these definitions, we want to analyze the following finite-horizon optimal control problem:

\displaystyle\inf_{{\bm{u}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{u}}_{T-1}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\bm{x}}_{T}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}_{T}{\bm{x}}_{T}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left({\bm{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}{\bm{x}}_{t}+{\bm{u}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{R}}{\bm{u}}_{t}\right)\right],

(2.2)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\text{LDS-BO}({\bm{A}},{\bm{B}},{\bm{C}}_{0},{\bm{C}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{C}}_{p}),

where
{\bm{Q}}_{T},{\bm{Q}},{\bm{R}}
are the positive semi-definite cost matrices which are assumed to be known. In order to solve the optimal control problem (
2.2
), we assume that the dynamic matrices
{\bm{A}},{\bm{B}},{\bm{C}}_{0},{\bm{C}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{C}}_{p}
are also known a priori. Moreover, the initial state, noise processes and cost matrices satisfy the following properties:

Assumption 1
.

We have: (i) Gaussian noise processes,
\{{\bm{w}}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{\Sigma}}_{w})
,
\{{\bm{z}}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{\Sigma}}_{z})
, (ii) initial state distributed as
{\bm{x}}_{0}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0})
, and (iii) the positive definite cost matrices, i.e.,
{\bm{Q}}_{T},{\bm{Q}},{\bm{R}}\succ 0
.

Note that Assumption
1
is standard in the Linear Quadratic Gaussian (LQG) control problem
[
3
,
15
]
, which is a special case of (
2.2
) with
\{{\bm{C}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{p}=\mathbf{0}_{mn}
.
Since we don’t have access to the state vector
{\bm{x}}_{t}
at any time
t
, our control policy will be a function of all the information available at time
t
.

Definition 1

(Admissible Policy)
.

Starting from
\mathcal{I}_{0}=\phi
, let
\mathcal{I}_{t}:=\{{\bm{u}}_{0},{\bm{u}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{u}}_{t-1},{\bm{y}}_{0},{\bm{y}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{y}}_{t-1}\}
be the information available at time
t
with
\mathcal{I}_{t+1}=\{\mathcal{I}_{t},{\bm{u}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{t}\}
. Then, a policy
\mu_{k}
is admissible if
{\bm{u}}_{t}=\mu_{t}(\mathcal{I}_{t})
.

Note that, unlike the standard LQG case, the information vector
\mathcal{I}_{t}
does not include
{\bm{y}}_{t}
. This is because in (
2.1
) one must take the action
{\bm{u}}_{t}
to observe the output
{\bm{y}}_{t}
. In the next section, we will show how this effects the state estimation
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{x}}_{t}|\mathcal{I}_{t}]
at time
t
. Moreover, we will also show that the state estimation
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{x}}_{t}|\mathcal{I}_{t}]
, and the estimation error covariance
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[({\bm{x}}_{t}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{x}}_{t}|\mathcal{I}_{t}])({\bm{x}}_{t}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{x}}_{t}|\mathcal{I}_{t}])^{\top}\big|\mathcal{I}_{t}]
are the sufficient statistics to design the optimal feedback control policy.

2.2
Input-Dependent State Estimation

In this section, we will show the optimality of Kalman Filtering Algorithm
[
13
]
for predicting the state of the dynamical system (
2.1
). For the sake of completeness, we first present a variant of Kalman Filtering (KF) Algorithm here:

Bilinear Observation Kalman Filtering:
Let
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t|t-1}:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{x}}_{t}|\mathcal{I}_{t}]
be the estimated state at time
t
, and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t|t-1}:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[({\bm{x}}_{t}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{x}}_{t}|\mathcal{I}_{t}])({\bm{x}}_{t}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{x}}_{t}|\mathcal{I}_{t}])^{\top}\right]
be the estimation error covariance. Then, starting from
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0|-1}=\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}
, and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{0|-1}={\bm{\Sigma}}_{0}
, the estimated state, and the error covariance follows the recursion:

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t+1|t}

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t|t-1}+{\bm{B}}{\bm{u}}_{t}-{\bm{L}}({\bm{u}}_{t})\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{t})\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t|t-1}\right),

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t+1|t}

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t|t-1}{\bm{A}}^{\top}+{\bm{L}}({\bm{u}}_{t}){\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{t}){\bm{\Sigma}}_{t|t-1}{\bm{A}}^{\top}+{\bm{\Sigma}}_{w},

\displaystyle\text{where,}\quad{\bm{L}}({\bm{u}}_{t})

\displaystyle:=-{\bm{A}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t|t-1}{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{t})^{\top}\left({\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{t}){\bm{\Sigma}}_{t|t-1}{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{t})^{\top}+{\bm{\Sigma}}_{z}\right)^{-1}

is the input-dependent Kalman gain at time
t
.

Please refer to
[
4
, Eqs. (E.39) – (E.42)]
for the derivation of Kalman filtering equations above.
Importantly, unlike Kalman filtering from linear measurements, both the Kalman gain
{\bm{L}}({\bm{u}}_{t})
, and the error covariance
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t+1|t}
depend on the control inputs up to time
t
.
Hence, the separation principle (Def.
2
) is not valid when controlling the partially observed LDS from bilinear observations (
2.1
).
However, it is easy to show that, the Kalman filtering gives the optimal state estimation of (
2.1
), under Assumption
1
.

Lemma 1

(Optimality of KF)
.

Under Assumption
1
(i),(ii), the Kalman filtering algorithm gives:

(
a
)

optimal state estimation, that is,
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t|t-1}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{x}}_{t}|\mathcal{I}_{t}]
.

(
b
)

posterior distribution of the state
{\bm{x}}_{t}
given the information vector
\mathcal{I}_{t}
. Specifically, it gives
{\bm{x}}_{t}|\mathcal{I}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t|t-1},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t|t-1})
.

Lemma
1
readily follows from
[
13
]
by replacing
{\bm{C}}_{t}
with
{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{t})
which is a deterministic quantity conditioned on
{\bm{u}}_{t}
. Moreover, since the posterior distribution of the state
{\bm{x}}_{t}
is Gaussian, using similar argument as
[
4
, Section 4.3.1]
, we note that the values
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t|t-1},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t|t-1}
computed by the Kalman filter are sufficient statistics for any policy at time
t
.

3
Main Results

In this section we present our main results on the sub-optimality of the separation principle and linear controllers, and then derive the optimal nonlinear controller in a simple setting. First we present a formal definition of the separation principle, adapted from
[
3
]
.

Definition 2

(Separation principle)
.

The optimal control from partial observations can be generated by first using Kalman filtering to find the optimal state estimate, then using linear quadratic regulator (LQR) to find the optimal state feedback controller based on the optimal state estimate.

For the sake of completeness, in the following we present the sub-optimal LQG controller
[
4
]
for the system (
2.1
): at every time
0\leq t\leq T-1
, the LQG controller is given by the sub-optimal policy:

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{t}^{\rm LQG}

\displaystyle={\bm{L}}_{t}\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t|t-1}\quad\text{where,}\quad{\bm{L}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-({\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{K}}_{t+1}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{K}}_{t+1}{\bm{A}},

(3.1)

where the matrices
{\bm{K}}_{t}
, starting from
{\bm{K}}_{T}={\bm{Q}}_{T}
, are given recursively by the Riccati equation,

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}_{t}

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{K}}_{t+1}{\bm{B}}({\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{K}}_{t+1}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{R}})^{-1}{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{K}}_{t+1}{\bm{A}},

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\bm{K}}_{t}

\displaystyle={\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{K}}_{t+1}{\bm{A}}-{\bm{P}}_{t}+{\bm{Q}}.

We are now ready to present our main results.

3.1
Sub-optimality of the Separation Principle

In this section, we will analyze the dynamic programming (DP) algorithm to solve the optimal control problem (
2.2
) as follows: Using the same proof technique as
[
4
, Section 4.2]
, the optimal policy for the last stage is given by
{\bm{u}}_{T-1}^{\star}={\bm{u}}_{T-1}^{\rm LQG}
, which is the LQG controller given by (
3.1
). However, the LQG controller is no longer optimal for
t\leq T-2
. To show this, we use the DP algorithm
[
4
, Eq. (4.4)]
along-with the Woodbury matrix identity
[
23
]
to obtain

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{T-2}^{\star}=\arg\min_{{\bm{u}}_{T-2}}f({\bm{u}}_{T-2})

\displaystyle:=f_{\rm LQG}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})+g({\bm{u}}_{T-2}),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad f_{\rm LQG}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})

\displaystyle:={\bm{u}}_{T-2}^{\top}\mathcal{A}{\bm{u}}_{T-2}+2\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{T-2|T-3}^{\top}\mathcal{B}^{\top}{\bm{u}}_{T-2},

\displaystyle\text{and}\;\;\quad\quad\quad g({\bm{u}}_{T-2})

\displaystyle:=\textup{{tr}}\big(\mathcal{G}\big({\bm{\Sigma}}_{T-2|T-3}^{-1}+{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{z}^{-1}{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})\big)^{-1}\big),

where we define
\mathcal{A}:={\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{K}}_{T-1}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{R}}
,
\mathcal{B}:={\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{K}}_{T-1}{\bm{A}}
, and
\mathcal{G}:={\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{T-1}{\bm{A}}
for notational convenience.
Note that the second term
g({\bm{u}}_{T-2})
depends on control input
{\bm{u}}_{T-2}
via the input dependent observation matrix
{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})
, and it corresponds to the estimation error covariance at time
T-1
. Hence, the control input at time
T-2
directly affects the estimation error covariance at time
T-1
, violating the separation principle.
Our next result shows that, because of the additional term
g({\bm{u}}_{T-2})
, the optimal control policy is not affine in the estimated state.

Theorem 1

(Counter example)
.

Consider the optimal control problem (
2.2
). Suppose Assumption
1
holds, and
T\geq 2
. Let
\{u^{\star}_{k}=\mu_{k}^{\star}(\mathcal{I}_{k})\}_{k=0}^{T-1}
be the optimal control policy obtained by minimizing (
2.2
). Then, for
k\leq T-2
, the optimal control policy
\mu_{k}^{\star}(\mathcal{I}_{k})
is not affine in the estimated state
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[x_{k}|\mathcal{I}_{k}]
in general.

The proof of Theorem
1
is presented in Section
4.1
. Theorem
1
suggests that the optimal controller can be nonlinear under bilinear observations. However, finding an analytical expression for the optimal controller is challenging in general because of: (i) non-convexity, (ii) nonlinearity, and (iii) the existence of multiple maxima, minima, or saddle points. In the simple case of
T=2
, we find the optimal nonlinear controllers as follows.

Theorem 2

(Nonlinear controller)
.

Consider the optimal control problem (
2.2
).
Suppose,
n=m=p=1
, and
T=2
. Let
P_{1}={A^{2}B^{2}Q_{T}^{2}}/(B^{2}Q_{T}+R)
and
K_{1}={A^{2}RQ_{T}}/(B^{2}Q_{T}+R)+Q
.
Suppose Assumption
1
holds, and
\Sigma_{z}\leq C_{1}^{2}{A^{2}\Sigma_{0}^{2}P_{T-1}}/{(B^{2}K_{T-1}+R)}
.
Let
u^{\star}_{0}
, and
u^{\star}_{1}
be the optimal control actions obtained by minimizing (
2.2
). Let
u_{0}^{\rm LQG}
be the optimal LQG control (obtained when
C_{1}=0
). Suppose
C_{0}=-u_{0}^{\rm LQG}\times C_{1}
. Then:

(
a
)

u_{0}^{\rm LQG}
locally maximizes the cost in (
2.2
).

(
b
)

the optimal control action
u_{0}^{\star}
is nonlinear in
\hat{x}_{0}
and is not unique, that is,

\displaystyle u_{0}^{\star}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta\hat{x}_{0}}{\alpha}\left(1\pm\frac{1}{C_{0}}\sqrt{-\kappa+\frac{\alpha C_{0}}{\beta\hat{x}_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\alpha}}}\right),

(3.4)

\displaystyle u_{1}^{\star}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{AQ_{T}B}{B^{2}Q_{T}+R}\right)\hat{x}_{1|0},

(3.5)

where
\alpha:=B^{2}K_{1}+R
,
\beta:=BK_{1}A
,
\gamma:=\Sigma_{z}A^{2}P_{1}
, and
\kappa:=\Sigma_{z}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}
.

The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Section
4.2
.
The setting of Theorem
2
(supposing
C_{0}=-u_{0}^{\rm LQG}\times C_{1}
) means that the LQG control input exactly cancels the state observation in (
2.1
), and thus maximizes the estimation error covariance.
This suggests that the landscape of the cost-to-go function depends on the distance between the LQG controller and the maximizer of the estimation error covariance.
We investigate this further numerically (Figure
1
),
and see that
when this distance is large, the LQG controller is also the optimal controller. However, when this distance is small, the LQG controller becomes sub-optimal, and the optimal controllers are nonlinear is the estimated state.

3.2
Sufficient Conditions for Uniformly Bounded Cost

In this section, we derive conditions under which the estimation error covariance stays bounded. Since our observation matrices are input dependent, we first introduce the notion of uniform observability for bilinear observation systems
2.1
.

Definition 3

(Complete observability)
.

The system (
2.1
) is
uniformly completely observable
for input sequence
\{{\bm{u}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
if there exists a
\delta>0
such that, for all
\ell\geq 0
,

{\bm{O}}_{\ell}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}({\bm{A}}^{k})^{\top}{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{\ell+k})^{\top}{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{\ell+k}){\bm{A}}^{k}\succ\delta{\bm{I}}_{n}.

We call
{\bm{O}}_{\ell}
observability grammian related to (
2.1
). Note that unlike linear observation case, here
{\bm{O}}_{\ell}
is time varying because of the input dependent observation matrix. Our next result (adapted from
[
7
]
) establishes a useful connection between the KF error covariance and the observability condition introduced in Definition
3
.

Lemma 2

(Bounded error covariance)
.

Suppose that the system (
2.1
) is uniformly completely observable for input sequence
\{{\bm{u}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Then the Kalman filtering error covariance matrix remains bounded for all time, i.e. there exists a constant
c
such that
\|{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t|t-1}\|\leq c
for all
t\geq 0
.

Lemma
2
follows readily from Lemma 3.2 in
[
7
]
, noting that the positive definite condition in Definition
3
implies the nonzero determinant condition in Definition 3.1 of
[
7
]
.

The separation principle can lead to degenerate policies which fail to adequately estimate the state.
Consider, for example, settings with
{\bm{C}}_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{mn}
.
When
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{x}}_{0}]=0
, the system (
2.1
) will be non-observable, and it is impossible to find a stabilizing control policy. This is because when
{\bm{C}}_{0}=\mathbf{0}_{mn}
,
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{0}=0
implies
{\bm{u}}_{0}=0
, and
{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{p}({\bm{u}}_{0})_{k}{\bm{C}}_{k}=\mathbf{0}_{mn}
. This will result in the Kalman gain
{\bm{L}}({\bm{u}}_{t})
being zero for all
t\geq 0
. Hence, the estimated state is
\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{t}=0
for all
t\geq 0
.
Such failures may be preventable, e.g. by randomizing the initial state estimate.
While it is difficult to rule out this problem in general, for certain classes of observation models it is avoidable.
The following proposition (proved in Section
4.3
) provides sufficient conditions for ensuring observability regardless of the control policy.

Proposition 1

(Sufficient condition)
.

Let
{\bm{C}}_{0}^{\perp}
be the projection of
{\bm{C}}_{0}
onto the orthogonal complement of the span of
{\bm{C}}_{1},...,{\bm{C}}_{p}
. Then if
({\bm{A}},{\bm{C}}_{0}^{\perp})
is observable,
for any choice of control inputs
\{{\bm{u}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, the Kalman filtering error covariance matrix remains bounded for all
t{\geq 0}
.

Proofs of Main Results

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Our proof is based on the analysis of the dynamic programming (DP) algorithm to solve (
2.2
). Fix
n=m=p=1
, and consider the following optimization problem,

\displaystyle\inf_{u_{0},\dots,u_{T-1}}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[Q_{T}x_{T}^{2}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(Qx_{t}^{2}+Ru_{t}^{2}\right)\right],

(4.1)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\text{LDS-BO}(A,B,C_{0},C_{1}).

To begin, we use
[
4
, Eq. (4.4)]
to find the optimal controller
u_{T-1}^{\star}=\mu_{T-1}^{\star}(\mathcal{I}_{T-1})
as follows,

\displaystyle u^{\star}_{T-1}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{AQ_{T}B}{B^{2}Q_{T}+R}\right)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[x_{T-1}|\mathcal{I}_{T-1}\right],

(4.2)

and the corresponding optimal cost for the last two time steps is given by

\displaystyle J_{T-1}^{\star}(\mathcal{I}_{T-1})

\displaystyle=K_{T-1}{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}}\left[x_{T-1}^{2}|\mathcal{I}_{T-1}\right]+Q_{T}\Sigma_{w}+P_{T-1}{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}}\left[\left(x_{T-1}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[x_{T-1}|\mathcal{I}_{T-1}\right]\right)^{2}|\mathcal{I}_{T-1}\right],

4.3
Proof of Proposition
1

Proof.

Consider Frobenius inner product between matrices:
\langle{\bm{U}},{\bm{V}}\rangle=\textup{{tr}}({\bm{U}}^{\top}{\bm{V}})
.
By the fundamental theorem of linear algebra, we can decompose

{\bm{C}}_{0}={\bm{C}}_{0}^{\perp}+\bar{\bm{C}}_{0}
where
\bar{\bm{C}}_{0}
is in the span of
{\bm{C}}_{1},...,{\bm{C}}_{p}
.
Define
\bar{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{t})={\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{t})-{\bm{C}}_{0}^{\perp}
, then we have
\textup{{tr}}\big(({\bm{C}}_{0}^{\perp})^{\top}\bar{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{t})\big)=0
.
Then the observability Gramian
{\bm{O}}_{\ell}
for any
\ell
can be decomposed into
{\bm{O}}={\bm{O}}_{1}+{\bm{O}}_{2}+{\bm{O}}_{3}
where

\displaystyle{\bm{O}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}({\bm{A}}^{i})^{\top}({\bm{C}}_{0}^{\perp})^{\top}{\bm{C}}_{0}^{\perp}{\bm{A}}^{i}

\displaystyle{\bm{O}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}({\bm{A}}^{i})^{\top}\left[({\bm{C}}_{0}^{\perp})^{\top}\bar{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{\ell+i})+\bar{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{\ell+i})^{\top}{\bm{C}}_{0}^{\perp}\right]{\bm{A}}^{i}

\displaystyle{\bm{O}}_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}({\bm{A}}^{i})^{\top}\bar{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{\ell+i})^{\top}\bar{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{\ell+i}){\bm{A}}^{i}

(4.21)

The first term
{\bm{O}}_{1}
is the time invariant observability Grammian for
({\bm{A}},{\bm{C}}_{0}^{\perp})
, which is positive definite by assumption.
For
{\bm{O}}_{2}
, observe that
{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{O}}_{2}{\bm{v}}=0
for all
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
because the following inequality holds for any
{\bm{A}},{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle|{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{B}}{\bm{v}}|

\displaystyle=|\textup{{tr}}\left({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{B}}{\bm{v}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}\right)|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\textup{{tr}}\left({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{B}}{\bm{v}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}\right)^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\textup{{tr}}({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{B}})\,\textup{{tr}}({\bm{v}}{\bm{v}}^{\top})}

(4.22)

Lastly, the matrix
{\bm{O}}_{3}
is clearly positive semidefinite.
This shows that
{\bm{O}}
is positive definite, and thus the system is uniformly completly observable for any choice of inputs.
The conclusion follows by Lemma
2
.

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left({\bm{x}}_{T-1}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\bm{x}}_{T-1}|\mathcal{I}_{T-1}\right]\right)^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{T-1}\left({\bm{x}}_{T-1}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\bm{x}}_{T-1}|\mathcal{I}_{T-1}\right]\right)\big|\mathcal{I}_{T-1}\right]\bigg|\mathcal{I}_{T-2},{\bm{u}}_{T-2}\right]

\displaystyle=\textup{{tr}}\left({\bm{P}}_{T-1}{\bm{A}}\left({\bm{\Sigma}}_{T-2|T-3}^{-1}+{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{z}^{-1}{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})\right)^{-1}{\bm{A}}^{\top}\right)+\textup{{tr}}\left({\bm{P}}_{T-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{w}\right),

\displaystyle=\textup{{tr}}\left({\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{T-1}{\bm{A}}\left({\bm{\Sigma}}_{T-2|T-3}^{-1}+{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{z}^{-1}{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})\right)^{-1}\right)+\textup{{tr}}\left({\bm{P}}_{T-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{w}\right),

(4.29)

Finally, using (
4.29
) in (
4.26
), and after some algebraic manipulations, the optimal control input at time
T-2
is given by the following non-convex minimization problem,

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{T-2}^{\star}=\arg\min_{{\bm{u}}_{T-2}}\bigg\{

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{T-2}^{\top}{\cal{A}}{\bm{u}}_{T-2}+2\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{T-2|T-3}^{\top}\mathcal{B}^{\top}{\bm{u}}_{T-2}

\displaystyle+\textup{{tr}}\left({\cal{G}}\left({\bm{\Sigma}}_{T-2|T-3}^{-1}+{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{z}^{-1}{\bm{C}}({\bm{u}}_{T-2})\right)^{-1}\right)\bigg\}

(4.30)

where we set
\mathcal{A}:={\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{K}}_{T-1}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{R}}
,
\mathcal{B}:={\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{K}}_{T-1}{\bm{A}}
, and
\mathcal{G}:={\bm{A}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{T-1}{\bm{A}}
for notational convenience. This completes the proof.
∎

6
Conclusion and Discussion

We study the problem of minimizing a quadratic cost of controlling linear dynamical systems from bilinear observations.
Our results show that the separation principle does not hold in general for these problems.
Moreover, the optimal controller is not affine in the estimated state, although the estimation problem itself remains straightforward, that is, the KF still gives the optimal state estimates, and the posterior distribution of the state.
We find that the optimal control problem has a cost-to-go which is generally nonconvex, and the control inputs affect the estimated state and the estimation error covariance in non-trivial ways.
We derive analytical expression for the optimal nonlinear control policy in a simple setting and find that the optimal controller is indeed nonlinear in the estimated state.
We also derive conditions which guarantee uniform observability in the case of bilinear observations, and verify it through numerical experiments.

There are several open questions leading to interesting directions for future work. First, deriving an optimal nonlinear control law for (
2.2
) when
T>2
is still an open problem. An alternate approach to this will be direct policy optimization for (
2.2
). Second, when the dynamics matrices are unknown, it will be challenging to design an adaptive control scheme for the system (
2.1
). Lastly, extending this problem to study the optimal control of partially observed bilinear dynamical systems is also an important future direction.
