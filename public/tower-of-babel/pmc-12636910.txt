Prediction of adverse events after acute myocardial infarction: derivation and external validation of an extended CHA2DS2-VASc score model
Authors: Lina Carlsson, Jerzy Leppert, Jonas Selmeryd, Christina Christersson, Pär Hedberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe CHA2DS2-VASc score predicts poor prognosis in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI), with or without atrial fibrillation. In this observational study, we aimed to evaluate the CHA2DS2-VASc score by itself and extended with clinical data to predict adverse events in patients after AMI.MethodsIn this longitudinal observational study, we used a cohort of 955 patients hospitalised for AMI at Västmanland County Hospital, Västerås, Sweden, to derive prediction models. The CHA2DS2-VASc score alone and combined with clinical data (systolic blood pressure, creatinine level, ST-segment elevation and diuretic use at discharge) was analysed using Cox regression to evaluate the risk of major adverse events (MAE), defined as all-cause death or hospitalisation due to recurrent MI, heart failure or ischaemic stroke. Discriminatory performance was presented as the time-dependent area under the curve (tdAUC). The prediction models were validated in 416 patients with AMI hospitalised at Uppsala University Hospital, Uppsala, Sweden.ResultsDuring a median of 2.5 years, 287 (30.1%) patients experienced MAE. CHA2DS2-VASc scores of 2, 4 and 6 were associated with fourfold, ninefold and 18-fold increases in the relative risk of MAE, respectively, with a tdAUC of 0.76 at a 2-year follow-up. Extending the CHA2DS2-VASc score with clinical data significantly improved the prediction model (p<0.001), yielding a tdAUC of 0.81. The models performed well in the validation cohort, with satisfactory calibration and tdAUC values of 0.70–0.78.ConclusionThe addition of clinical data to the CHA2DS2-VASc score was superior to a model with CHA2DS2-VASc alone in predicting adverse events in patients after AMI, and the model performed well in external validation.

Introduction

Ischaemic heart disease is a major cause of morbidity and mortality globally and is essential to healthcare costs.1 2 Patients who have suffered from acute myocardial infarction (AMI) are at an increased risk of future cardiovascular (CV) events and death.

The CHA2DS2-VASc score is an established score for predicting stroke risk in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF).3 The CHA2DS2-VASc score is robust and easy to use and can easily be applied in clinical practice to help medical decision-making. Recently, this score has been shown to strongly predict a dismal prognosis in patients with AMI (with or without AF).47 Most of these studies have focused on Asian ethnicities, warranting the evaluation of other ethnicities. Furthermore, the score does not include other factors that may influence the outcome of patients with AMI, such as blood pressure and renal function.8 Therefore, it may be possible to enhance the predictive value of the CHA2DS2-VASc score by adding other variables to the existing score, which could lead to a more accurate and personalised assessment of prognosis in patients with AMI.

Our objective was to evaluate the CHA2DS2-VASc score and to extend it with clinical data to predict future adverse events after AMI. We also aimed to validate these prediction models in a patient population independent of the original cohort.

Methods

Derivation cohort

The prediction models were designed in a derivation cohort and externally validated in a validation cohort. The derivation cohort included patients from the Västmanland Myocardial Infarction Study (VaMIS; Clinical Trials.gov number NCT01452178).9 In the VaMIS observational study, consecutive patients (white Europeans) hospitalised in the Västmanland County Hospital, Västerås, Sweden, from November 2005 to May 2011 were screened for inclusion (n=1459). AMI was diagnosed using electrocardiogram (ECG) and biomarker according to criteria from the European Society of Cardiology at the time of study start: a dynamic rise in serum concentration of troponin-I to ≥0.4 mg/L, combined with (1) ischaemic symptoms; (2) development of Q waves on the ECG; (3) ECG changes indicative of ischaemia (ST-segment elevation or depression) or (4) coronary artery intervention.10 ST-elevation MI (STEMI) required a new or presumed new ST-segment elevation at the J point in two or more contiguous leads with the cut-off points 0.2 mV in leads V1, V2 or V3 and 0.1 mV in other leads.

A total of 401 patients were excluded because of language difficulties (n=57), dementia or confusion (n=81), other severe diseases (n=62), logistical problems (n=106) or other reasons (n=95). Furthermore, 50 patients declined to participate.

From the original VaMIS cohort (n=1008), 10 patients were excluded because of death within 5 days of admission, and 43 (4%) patients were excluded because of missing data on at least one of the variables included (diuretic at discharge, systolic blood pressure, creatinine level and ECG rhythm were missing in 24, 12, 5 and 3 patients, respectively), leaving 955 (641 men) patients for further analyses.

Validation cohort

The validation cohort consisted of patients from the observational REBUS (RElevance of Biomarkers for future risk of thromboembolic events in UnSelected postmyocardial infarction patients) study.11 From April 2010 to August 2012, 858 consecutive patients with AMI hospitalised in the Department of Cardiology, Uppsala University Hospital, were prospectively screened for inclusion. The inclusion criteria were MI diagnosed as a dynamic rise of troponin together with typical symptoms of MI or development of a significant Q-wave on ECG.

Patients who died within 5 days of admission, lived outside the catchment area of Uppsala University Hospital, or could not attend the scheduled study visits were excluded (n=256). Further, 27 patients were excluded for logistic reasons, and 154 declined participation. Five patients were excluded from the final REBUS cohort (n=421) because of missing data on creatinine level (n=4) and blood pressure (n=1), leaving 416 patients for validation analyses.

This study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board of Uppsala, Sweden (Dnr 2005/169, 2005/169/5 and 2019–02204).

Variables in the prediction models

The CHA2DS2-VASc score was calculated as the sum of points, where one point each was assigned for the presence of female sex, heart failure, hypertension, age 65–74 years, diabetes and previous vascular disease (claudication or MI) and two points each for age ≥75 years and history of stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA).

Clinical data to be combined with the CHA2DS2-VASc score in an extended prediction model were based on risk markers in the GRACE 2.0 (systolic blood pressure, creatinine level, ST-segment elevation and diuretic at discharge), excluding age (as it was included in the CHA2DS2-VASc score), heart rate and cardiac arrest at admission (as this information was missing in the derivation or validation cohorts). Additionally, AF was included in the extended model.

Outcomes

The endpoint was major adverse events (MAE), defined as a composite of all-cause death or hospitalisation due to MI (International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10): I21), heart failure (ICD-10: I50, I25.5 or I11.0) or ischaemic stroke (ICD-10: I63). In the derivation cohort, follow-up information was based on the linkage to the Swedish Population and National In-patient registries. Patients were followed up from the index examination until the first endpoint event or 31 December 2013, whichever came first. The follow-up time was truncated at 2.5 years (the maximum follow-up time for the validation cohort).

The follow-up data of the validation cohort were based on regular study visits (3, 12 and 24 months after the index event) and medical records. After study completion, clinical monitors from the Uppsala Clinical Research Center scrutinised the medical records of all the patients to identify potentially missed outcomes.

None of the patients in the derivation and validation cohorts was lost to follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean±SD, median (25th, 75th percentiles) or number (percentage). In the baseline analyses, variables were compared between cohorts using student’s t-test, the Kruskal-Wallis test or Fisher’s exact test. Variable distributions were assessed visually using frequency histograms and quantitatively by examining skewness and kurtosis. Formal normality tests were not performed, as these can be overly sensitive in large samples. The choice between parametric and non-parametric tests was based on these observed distribution characteristics.

The incidence rates of the two cohorts were compared using the Fisher’s exact test.

The derivation cohort was stratified into four approximately equally sized groups based on quartiles of the CHA2DS2-VASc score. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate survival curves, and comparisons between groups were performed using the log-rank test.

The CHA2DS2-VASc score alone or in combination with clinical data (STEMI, systolic blood pressure, creatinine, AF and diuretic treatment at discharge) was assessed using Cox proportional hazard regression analyses to evaluate the association with outcome during follow-up. In these analyses, the CHA2DS2-VASc score was included as a continuous predictor. All continuous predictors were assessed by restricted cubic splines with three knots located at the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles to relax the assumption of linear association with the outcome. All predictors were retained in the models regardless of their statistical significance. The goodness-of-fit between two nested Cox models was compared using the likelihood ratio test. Goodness of fit between two non-nested Cox models was compared using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values (where lower values suggest better fit). The proportional hazard assumption was visually verified using scaled Schoenfeld residuals.

The discriminatory performance of the models (ie, the ability of the model to differentiate between high- and low-risk patients) was assessed by the time-dependent area under the receiver operator characteristics curve (tdAUC).12 Calibration (ie, the agreement between the predicted and observed risks of MAE) was evaluated visually by plotting the observed risk calculated from the Kaplan-Meier estimator against the predicted risk. We internally validated the predictive performance of the models by calculating the optimism-corrected estimates of discrimination and calibration with 500 bootstrap replications.13

The generalisability of the prediction models was evaluated in the validation cohort, that is, in a new set of patients, independent of the derivation cohort. Patients in the validation cohort were divided into three risk groups based on their prognostic index (PI) tertiles. PI corresponded to the linear predictor calculated from the beta coefficients of the Cox regression models derived from the derivation cohort. The discriminatory performance of a model was assessed by tdAUC. An estimate of the baseline survival function is required to evaluate the calibration of a Cox model in an external dataset. In a fractional polynomial regression, we obtained the following approximation of the log cumulative hazard function in the derivation cohort: log(H0(t))=log⁡(t)+log(t)2 and calculated the baseline survival function as S0(t)=e−elog(H0(t)).14 The predicted survival function in the validation cohort was calculated as S(t|PIi)=S0(t)e(PIi), where PIi is the individual PI for a patient. The survival curves were averaged over all patients in each of the three risk groups to obtain the expected survival in each group. The expected survival curves and observed survival (obtained from the Kaplan-Meier estimation) were superimposed on a graph.

We performed some sensitivity analyses on the derivation cohort. First, we added smoking to the extended CHA2DS2-VASc Cox regression model. Second, we evaluated the extended CHA2DS2-VASc Cox regression model by removing sex from the CHA2DS2-VASc score (as suggested by the 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for the management of AF).15

Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Statistical analyses were performed in R V.4.1.0 with the packages survival, rms, boot and timeROC.16

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Compared with the derivation cohort, patients in the validation cohort were younger and more often men (table 1). Apart from the lower prevalence of peripheral artery disease history in the validation cohort (2.6% vs 6.5%, p=0.005), there were no significant differences in previous CV disease or diabetes. Lower CHA2DS2-VASc scores dominated the validation cohort than the derivation cohort (p<0.001). The index myocardial infarction (MI) was more often a STEMI in the validation cohort (46% vs 32%, p<0.001).

External validation

The external validation was based on 712 person-years and 74 events in the validation cohort. The incidence rate in the validation cohort was significantly lower than that in the derivation cohort (10.4 vs 15.2 per 100 person-years, χ2 7.57, p=0.006). The distribution of PIs in the derivation and validation cohorts for the model with CHA2DS2-VASc only and the model extended with clinical data is presented in online supplemental figures S2 and S3, respectively.

The CHA2DS2-VASC score with and without clinical variables performed well in the validation cohort, showing tdAUC values of 0.70–0.78 (table 3). Similarly, the models yielded satisfactory calibration (figure 2).

Discussion

This research showed that the CHA2DS2-VASc score is a powerful indicator of poor long-term outcomes in patients with AMI and that adding clinical data could enhance its predictive ability. The CHA2DS2-VASc score, alone and in combination with clinical data, was a robust prediction model in external validations.

This study aimed to develop a simple and accurate model to predict the risk of MAE in patients with AMI. The CHA2DS2-VASc score is an established score used to predict stroke risk in patients with AF.17 However, it has also been demonstrated that this scoring model and its predecessor, CHADS2, also have the potential to be a strong predictor of MAE in patients with AMI, with or without AF.46 1822 In a systematic review and meta-analysis, Zhou et al evaluated the impact of the CHADS2 score in predicting ischaemic stroke, TIA and death in eight cohort studies enrolling more than 30 000 patients with acute or stable coronary artery disease.23 They found that the pooled relative risk for incident mortality or stroke/TIA was more than doubled for a CHADS2 score ≥2 versus <2. These findings are unsurprising, as many risk factors for acute coronary syndrome are similar to those for stroke in patients with AF and covered in the algorithm (eg, age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus and previous CV disease).24

Chua et al compared the CHADS2 and CHA2DS2-VASc scores as predictors of MAE within 1 year of admission in 3000 patients with acute coronary syndrome.4 They found that the CHA2DS2-VASc score had better discriminative performance than CHADS2 and could further predict the risk of MAE, even in patients with CHADS2 scores of 0 or 1. Previous studies that evaluated CHA2DS2-VASc score as a risk stratification tool in patients with ACS were performed mainly in Asian populations, making it uncertain how well the score would perform in other cohorts. We extend these findings to a cohort of European ethnicity. Several differences were observed between the derivation and validation cohorts. Patients in the derivation cohort were older, more often women, and had a higher prevalence of PAD than those in the validation cohort. These differences were also reflected in the derivation cohort’s significantly higher CHA2DS2-VASc scores and outcome incidence rates. A plausible reason for these differences is that the recommended diagnostic limit of troponin I was lowered during the derivation cohort recruitment period. However, we continued to use the original diagnostic limit in the derivation cohort to maintain consistency in the study population. This may have resulted in a lower sensitivity for MI diagnosis in the derivation cohort than in the validation cohort. Another reason might be the socioeconomic differences between the populations. Despite the differences between the derivation and validation cohorts, the extended CHA2DS2-VASc score performed well in the validation cohort.

It is crucial to determine the appropriate level of patient care and follow-up for patients with MI. Thus, risk assessment is an essential component of clinical decision-making. Refined estimation of prognosis and prediction of the risk of adverse events enables an individualised approach to follow-up and, possibly, to a certain extent, even individualised adjustments in treatment. Extending the time between follow-up encounters might be feasible in low-risk patients. On the other hand, a patient with a higher risk might benefit from more frequent follow-ups, such as closer monitoring of blood pressure and creatinine levels. More personalised and cost-effective healthcare may be attainable in the long term.

Our model was based on the CHA2DS2-VASc, an established, easy-to-use and widely spread score. Furthermore, the variables included in the score are most often available in clinical practice, as are the variables added to the extended model. In our study, the CHA2DS2-VASc score extended with clinical data showed good calibration and discriminatory performance, and the results were consistent when validated externally. Hence, this model shows promising applicability and potential for guiding clinical decision-making by healthcare professionals.

The strengths of this study include prospective data from real-life clinical cohorts and external validation of the prediction model in an independent cohort. Furthermore, the variables included in the models are available for most hospitals, making the developed model widely applicable.

This study had several limitations. The cohorts comprised patients of European ethnicity, which may limit the generalisability to other populations. Compared with the European Society of Cardiology’s EuroHeart cohort (approximately 40 000 patients with AMI from multiple European countries),25 our data demonstrate very similar characteristics in terms of age, sex distribution, prior stroke, prior MI and prevalent AF. However, our cohort exhibited a slightly lower prevalence of hypertension and diabetes than what was reported in the EuroHeart registry.

Some variables known to be significant predictors of outcomes in patients with AMI were missing in the study cohorts (eg, heart rate and cardiac arrest at admission). In our analyses, sporadically missing predictors were handled by complete case analysis. This reduces sample size and may lower precision. In addition, in complete case analyses, variables that are not missing at random (MAR) might introduce selection bias. However, given the modest proportion of missingness (<4% per variable) in the current study, the risk of bias from excluding patients is probably limited. Heart rate at admission (not measured in the validation cohort) and cardiac arrest at admission (not registered in any cohort) represent a structural absence rather than data missing at random. Thus, the MAR assumption is not directly relevant in these cases.

The scope of this study did not include formal clinical validation. As a result, we could not assess physician compliance using this score or how it affected the outcome.

In conclusion, we developed a model based on the CHA2DS2-VASc score for risk prediction of MAE in patients with ACS. The model was superior to the CHA2DS2-VASc score alone and performed well in an external cohort. A nomogram was developed to facilitate individual prognosis prediction and clinical decision-making.
