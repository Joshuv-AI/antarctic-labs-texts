Incentive affects physicians’ willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortia: a comparative study of county and provincial hospitals in Central China
Authors: Bowen Zhang, Liang Ma, Wenjie Ma, Dingding Kang, Yiqing Mao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe World Health Organization asserts that incentives significantly impact physicians’ participation in healthcare initiatives. China introduced incentive programs to boost involvement in urban–rural medical consortia. However, research is limited on: (a) the effectiveness of these incentives on physicians’ willingness to continue participating, (b) disparities in incentive levels between county and provincial hospitals, and (c) the varying impact of incentives on physicians’ continued participation in county versus provincial hospitals. This study aims to address these issues and suggest appropriate strategies.MethodsAn online survey in Henan Province, Central China, was conducted among 1,837 physicians, comprising 1,088 respondents from county hospitals and 749 respondents from provincial hospitals. Participants reported their demographic characteristics, altruism, collaborative projects, incentive levels and willingness to continue participating. Chi-square and multinomial logistic regressions were used to determine the incentive factors of participants’ willingness.Results(a) For all participants, the level of economic returns, position and professional title promotion, skills improvement were positively associated with willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortia. (b) County-level hospitals had lower level of various incentives for urban–rural medical consortium projects than provincial hospitals. (c) County-level physicians who had a high level of position and title promotion, professional skills improvement and provincial physicians who had a high level of economic returns and rewards had a high level of willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortia.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that the current financial incentives in provincial hospitals and non-financial incentives in county hospitals are efficacious in enhancing physicians’ willingness to continue participating. This information can assist policymakers in hospitals at different levels in developing or improving incentive mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12080-y.Keywords: Incentive, Willingness to continue participating, Urban–rural Medical Consortia, Provincial and county level physicians

Introduction

Compared to urban areas, rural high-quality medical human resources shortages have persisted for decades, negatively impacting the health outcomes of over 500 million Chinese people residing in rural areas [1]. To alleviate this problem, the National Health Commission of China proposed the implementation of an urban–rural medical consortium project in 2017 [2]. The urban–rural medical consortium is one of the various medical consortium models implemented in China. On the basis of established long-term stable paired support relationships, provincial-level hospitals can dispatch management teams and expert teams to county-level hospitals. The goal of the urban–rural medical consortium project in China is to sink high-quality medical human resources and improve the medical service capacity in rural areas by organizing a vertically integration of county- and provincial-level hospitals [3]. These collaborative contents include that case discussions, surgical guidance, academic lectures, continuing education, remote consultation, regular outpatient service, two-way referral, scientific research cooperation and medical management. Participation of the urban–rural medical consortium is determined by participants’ willingness and the incentives they are provided [4]. It is well known that incentive mechanisms play an important role in changing the willingness of personnel to participate [5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) proposed a series of policies in 2010, including the implementation of incentive measures, to enhance the retention rate of rural regional health personnel, and posited that incentives may exert significant influence on the decision-making processes of health workers [6]. Therefore, many incentive mechanisms in the urban–rural medical consortium have been introduced to ensure the willingness to continue participating of physicians, sustainability of the project and support rural health care development over the past years.

Every type of incentive can produce a different impact on physician group settings [7]. A literature review revealed that over half of the studies reported positive impacts of performance-based incentives on improving physicians performance [5]. However, a recent study showed that financial incentives did not significantly affect the quality of health care but did affect the motivations and attitudes of physicians [8]. The research thought that financial incentives may only play a limited role in improving health care quality and should not be the only solution [9]. In addition to financial incentives, non-financial incentives are also important to motivate healthcare professionals such as opportunities for career development [10], professional title improvement [11], ranking of performance [12], reputational incentives [13], sufficient training [14], public recognition [15], social status [16]. Previous studies have shown that personal characteristics can affect the impact of incentives on individual behavior, such as gender, age, income, education background and altruism [11, 17]. There is considerable heterogeneity between studies on incentive effects on physicians [18, 19].

We surveyed different urban–rural medical consortia and summarized the main content of the incentive measures according to the financial and non-financial incentives mentioned above. Figure 1 shows the incentive content. The organizers of the urban–rural medical consortium designed these incentive mechanisms to increase physicians’ willingness of county-level and provincial-level hospitals to participate in the project to ensure the operational performance and sustainability of the project. However, little is known about whether diverse and appealing incentives are offered at different hospital levels and how these incentives are effective in improving physicians’ willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortia. Therefore, this study addresses the following research questions:

Incentive types of urban–rural medical consortia

Do incentive measures effectively affect physicians’ willingness to continue participate in an urban–rural medical consortium?

Do incentive measures effectively affect physicians’ willingness to continue participate in an urban–rural medical consortium?

What are the differences in the level of incentives implemented by county and provincial hospitals?

This article extends our understanding of incentives offered to improve physicians’ enthusiasm for participating and provides an overview of the differences in incentives that may influence physicians’ decisions to pursue an appointments in an urban–rural medical consortium. This information can help inform program leadership and policymakers interested in developing or improving rural medical services.

Methods

Sample population

This cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2022 to June 2023 using the Wen Juan Xing platform, a famous online survey platform in China. A web-based survey was created for physicians who participated in the urban–rural medical consortium in Henan Province, which was selected based on the central region and middle economic and social development status. The participants received the questionnaire via WeChat, and they responded using the “Quick Response code” on their mobile phones.

The composition of the urban–rural medical consortium included provincial and county hospitals. Hence, the sample population in this study included physicians from provincial and county hospitals. First, according to the geographical distribution of various counties in Henan Province, four county hospitals in the central region and three county hospitals in the east, south, west, and north regions were selected (i.e., 4 ×1+3×4=16 county-hospitals). Second, four provincial hospitals in the central region and two provincial-hospitals in the northern region of Henan Province were selected based on a large number of participants and implement situation. A total of 1,837 physicians comprising 1,088 respondents from county hospitals and 749 respondents from provincial hospitals who participated in the urban–rural medical consortium completed the survey. The study protocol was approved by the Life Science Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of the Zhengzhou University (approval number: 2021-KY-0038).

Data measures

This study identified incentive factors and collaborative projects through interviewing with managers and physicians of urban–rural medical consortia. The self-made interview outline showed in Appendix 1. The incentive factors of the urban–rural medical consortium included the following: (1) economic returns, (2) position promotion, (3) professional title promotion, (4) reward, (5) expanding fame, (6) obtaining respect, and (7) improving professional skills from participating in the urban–rural medical consortium. The participants indicated their perception level from “low,” “medium” and “high.” The collaborative projects of the urban–rural medical consortium included the following: (1) case discussions, (2) surgical guidance, (3) academic lectures, (4) continuing education, (5) remote consultation, (6) regular outpatient service, (7) two-way referral, (8) scientific research cooperation, and (9) medical management. Willing to participate level in urban–rural medical consortia was measured using one item, “I am willing to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortium projects in the future.” The participants indicated their perception level from “low,” “medium” and “high.” The control variables included those that might affect the dependent variable on the basis of previous research, namely, individual characteristics (i.e., gender, age, title, education, and income), altruism, and collaborative projects. Among them, altruism refers to voluntary actions to help others without expecting anything in return [20]. Therefore, altruism level was measured using one item, “Even if there are no benefits, I am willing to participate in the urban–rural medical consortium.” The participants indicated their perception level from “low,” “medium” and “high.” The questionnaire used in the current study was self-designed and showed in Appendix 2.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. All variables are presented as frequencies and distributions and percentages. The chi-square test was employed to investigate the corresponding associations between incentive factors and willingness to continue participating in the independent and control variables. Only the variables with statistically significant differences were included in the logistic regression model. Since this study has not passed the proportional odds assumption, multinomial logistic regression was used instead of ordered logistic regression model. The model equation was Y=j|X=expβ0j+β1jX1+⋯+βpjXp/(1+∑expβ0i+β1iX1+⋯+βpiXp. Variables and models with p < 0.05 (two-tailed) were considered statistically significant. All models were passed the collinearity diagnosis (VIF < 5).

Difference between county and provincial level physicians among socio-demographic, collaborative contents and incentive types

The characteristics of the respondents who came from county and provincial hospitals are provided in Table 1. Among the participants of county hospitals, the majority of the participants were female (65.0%) and had a bachelor’s degree (75.8%). Nearly half of the participants were 30–39 years (43.1%), had work experience more than ten years (49.2%), and had a primary professional title (49.3%). The major specialties of physicians were internal medicine (39.0%), followed by surgical medicine (29.2%), and medical technology (21.9%). More than half of the participants had less than 5000 RMB income in every month (64.2%) and had a high level of altruism (60.4%). Among the participants of provincial hospitals, there were more male (52.6%) than female (47.4%). More than half of the participants were 30–39 years (69.3%), had a middle professional title (72.2%), and had a master’s degree (73.7%). The major specialty of physicians was surgical medicine (45.4%). The majority of participants in provincial hospitals had more than 1,0000 RMB income per month (87.9%) and had a high level of altruism (84.5%).

Table 1 lists the significant difference between the characteristics of the respondents who came from county and provincial hospitals. A higher proportion among participants of county hospitals who were female (χ2 = 56.245, p < 0.001), were 18–29 years (χ2 = 234.166, p < 0.001), employed less than five years (χ2 = 129.566, p < 0.001), had primary professional title (χ2 = 295.686, p < 0.001), majored in internal medicine (χ2 = 72.041, p < 0.001) and had low and middle level of altruism (χ2 = 134.748, p < 0.001) than participants of provincial hospitals. Contrary, a higher proportion among participants of provincial hospitals who had master’s and PhD’s degree (χ2 = 776.851, p < 0.001), also had higher level of monthly income (χ2 = 1297.529, p < 0.001) than physicians of county hospitals.

Incentive of urban–rural medical consortia

Table 1 lists the level distribution of the seven incentive types of the urban–rural medical consortium. More than half of the participants had a high level of professional title promotion (56.3%) and improved professional skills (67.0%) by participating in an urban–rural medical consortium. Nearly half of the participants had a high level of economic returns (45.0%) and position promotion (47.6%) through participation in urban–rural medical consortium projects. In addition, the majority of the participants expressed a low and middle level of reward (58.9%), expanded fame (62.5%), and more respect (68.0%).

Nearly half of the participants in county hospitals expressed a low level of economic returns (48.8%). The majority of the participants had a low and middle level of position promotion (60.1%) and professional title promotion (64.5%). Nearly half of the participants expressed a moderate level of reward (44.6%), expanded fame (44.2%), and more respect (47.0%). More than half of the participants had a high level of improved professional skills (61.2) from participating in urban–rural medical consortium projects. Provincial hospital participants commonly expressed a high level of economic returns (67.3%), position promotion (58.7%), professional title promotion (86.6%), reward (52.5%), expanded fame (50.7%), more respect (54.6%), and skills (75.3%).

Table 1 lists the significant difference between the incentive levels of the respondents who came from county and provincial hospitals. Compared with incentive levels of participants among county hospitals, the participants who came from provincial hospitals seemed to have a higher level of economic returns (χ2 = 297.065, p < 0.001), position promotion (χ2 = 92.754, p < 0.001), professional title promotion (χ2 = 472.727, p < 0.001), reward (χ2 = 74.640, p < 0.001), expanded fame (χ2 = 126.505, p < 0.001), more respect (χ2 = 125.127, p < 0.001) and skills (χ2 = 54.221, p < 0.001) by participating in urban–rural medical consortium.

Willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortia

In terms of collaborative projects in the urban–rural medical consortium, the majority of the respondents participated in case discussions (73.2%) and continuing education (67.6%). Half of the physicians participated in academic lectures (58.9%) and in regular outpatient service (51.3%). A low proportions of physicians participated in surgical guidance (33.4%), remote consultation (38.5%), scientific research cooperation (12.4%) and medical management (28.9%) (Table 1).

Among the 1,837 respondents, 945 expressed a high level of willingness to continue participating in the urban–rural medical consortium (51.4%), whereas 196 (10.7%) and 696 (37.9%) expressed low and middle levels of willingness to continue participating, respectively (Fig. 2). As shown in Table 2, a significant difference was found between the levels of willingness to continue participating on the various variables. Among the characteristics of the participants, the physicians who came from county hospitals (χ2 = 207.067, p < 0.001), were older (χ2 = 60.352, p < 0.001), had longer work years (χ2 = 28.663, p < 0.001), had a primary professional title (χ2 = 60.088, p < 0.001), had a lower education degree (χ2 = 72.557, p < 0.001), majored in traditional Chinese medicine (χ2 = 21.344, p = 0.002), had lower income (χ2 = 175.068, p < 0.001), had a high level of altruism (χ2 = 598.949, p < 0.001) seemed to have a high level of willingness to participant in the urban–rural medical consortium. Among the collaborative projects, physicians who participated in surgical guidance (χ2 = 23.718, p < 0.001), academic lectures (χ2 = 9.076, p = 0.011), remote consultation (χ2 = 66.561, p < 0.001), scientific research cooperation (χ2 = 8.114, p = 0.017), and medical management (χ2 = 11.992, p = 0.002) had a high level of willingness to participant in the urban–rural medical consortium. However, respondents who participated in regular outpatient services had a low level of willingness to participant in the future (χ2 = 12.091, p = 0.002). Among the incentive types, the participates who expressed a high level of economic returns (χ2 = 2424.701, p < 0.001), reward (χ2 = 187.965, p < 0.001), position promotion (χ2 = 387.118, p < 0.001), title promotion (χ2 = 232.721, p < 0.001), expanded fame (χ2 = 114.661, p < 0.001), more respect (χ2 = 160.380, p < 0.001) and improved professional skills (χ2 = 642.864, p < 0.001) by participating in urban–rural medical consortia seemed to have a high level of willingness to continue participating in the future.

Correlation between county and provincial level physicians’ willingness to continue participating, and demographic, collaborative contents, incentive types

For participants of provincial hospitals, half of participants were a middle level of willingness to continue participating in an urban–rural medical consortium (54.2%), while 109 (14.6%) and 234 (31.2%) expressed low and high levels of willingness to continue participating, respectively (Fig. 2). As shown in Table 3, a significant difference was found between the levels of willingness to continue participating on the various variables, including altruism (χ2 = 52.842, p < 0.001), case discussion (χ2 = 13.816, p = 0.001), surgical guidance (χ2 = 24.746, p < 0.001), academic lectures (χ2 = 10.444, p = 0.005), remote consultation (χ2 = 26.322, p < 0.001), two-way referral (χ2 = 6.498, p = 0.039), scientific research cooperation (χ2 = 36.507, p < 0.001), medical management (χ2 = 31.320, p < 0.001), economic returns (χ2 = 87.551, p < 0.001), rewards (χ2 = 27.477, p < 0.001) and skills (χ2 = 28.614, p < 0.001).

Predictors affecting the influence of incentive on willingness to continue participating

For all participants, after discovering that hospital levels, age, work year, title, education degree, occupation category, monthly income, altruism, surgical guidance, academic lectures, remote consultation, regular outpatient, scientific research cooperation, and medical management were significantly associated with willingness to continue participating, which were controlled using multinomial logistic regression analysis to examine the incentive factors of willingness to continue participating in the urban–rural medical consortium. Physicians who had a high level of economic returns by participating in urban–rural medical consortia apparently had a higher level of willingness to continue participating than those with middle (β = 0.710, 95% CI = 1.288 to 3.213, P = 0.002) levels of economic returns. Compared with those who had a middle level of position (β = 0.924, 95% CI = 1.620 to 3.914, P < 0.001) and professional title (β = 0.590, 95% CI = 1.043 to 3.122, P = 0.035) promotion, respondents who had a high level of position and title promotion were more willing to participate in urban–rural medical consortia. Meanwhile, physicians who had a high level of professional skills improved by participating in urban–rural medical consortia apparently had a higher level of willingness to continue participating than those who had a low (β = 0.989, 95% CI = 1.049 to 6.892, P = 0.039) and middle (β = 1.234, 95% CI = 2.203 to 5.350, P < 0.001) improved level of skills. Discussion

This study indicated that more than half of the physicians who participated in the urban–rural medical consortium reported a high level of willingness to continue participating in Central China. Among them, physicians who came from county hospitals (65.3%) expressed a higher level of willingness to continue participating than those from provincial hospitals (31.2%). This result is related to the goal of the medical consortium to enhance the service capabilities of doctors in county-level hospitals [2]. Compared to physicians from provincial hospitals, physicians from county hospitals are the larger beneficiaries of the urban–rural medical consortium project, hence, they have a higher willingness to continue participating than others. It should be noted that as the main members of provincial-level hospital physicians providing high-quality medical services, their low willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortium may affect the performance and sustainability of the projects. Therefore, to truly understand which incentive interventions are successful in their mission to increase physicians’ willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortia, standardized program evaluations reporting across years are necessary [21]. From the perspective of collaboration contents, physicians of urban–rural medical consortia have a high proportion of participation in case discussions (73.2%), academic lectures (58.9%), and continuing education (67.6%), while they have less chance to participate in surgical guidance (33.4%), remote consultation (38.5%), scientific research cooperation (12.4%) and medical management (28.9%). This result may be related to professional limitations. This study revealed that physicians who participated in regular outpatient service seemed to be lowly willing to participate in the future. This result suggests that the current regular outpatient service may not be running smoothly, affecting physicians’ willingness to participate.

This study confirmed the positive impact of incentives on physicians’ willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortium projects. In terms of financial incentives, physicians who had a high level of economic returns from participating in the urban–rural medical consortium apparently had a high level of willingness to continue participating in the future. The impact of financial incentives on physicians’ behavioral reactions has become a common consensus [22]. Previous study showed that the implementation of a financial incentives could increase the motivations and attitudes of physicians [8] or improve medical service behavior [23]. These findings confirmed this conclusion. In terms of non-financial incentives, participants with a high level of professional title and position improved seemed to have a high level of willingness to continue participating in the urban–rural medical consortium. This result is related to the large number of young participants. More than half of the participants were less than 39 years old (69.9%), hence, they have more demand for professional title and position promotion than older physicians. In addition, this result showed that physicians who had a high level of professional skills improvement by participating in the urban–rural medical consortium had a high level of willingness to continue participating in the future. Therefore, for the urban–rural medical consortium program, economic returns, position and title promotions played a positive role in increasing the motivation of the participants and guaranteeing their willingness to continue participating in the program, positively guiding the behavior of the participants, which could indicate that the incentives currently implemented in the urban–rural medical consortium program are somewhat effective.

This study found that there were differences in the level of incentives implemented by county and provincial hospitals. Compared with incentive levels of participants among county hospitals, the participants who came from provincial hospitals seemed to have a high level of economic returns (67.3%), position promotion (58.7%), professional title promotion (86.6%), reward (52.5%), expanded fame (50.7%), more respect (54.6%) and skills improvement (75.3%) by participating in urban–rural medical consortium. As program providers, participation in the urban–rural medical consortium program increases the workload of provincial physicians, so provincial hospital administrators often need more robust incentives to increase the motivation of provincial physicians to participate, which includes both financial and non-financial incentives. County hospitals, as recipients of programs, are themselves the beneficiaries of the program, so the incentives implemented by county hospitals are often inadequate. This may result in county-level doctors have a low perception of overall motivation and missing out on opportunities to improve their personal competencies [24], thus losing the significance of the implementation of the medical consortium program. Mangers of county hospitals should pay equal attention to the design of various incentives to guarantee the willingness of county doctors to participate.

This study showed that the incentive factors that influenced on the willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortia differed among physicians in county and provincial hospitals. For participants of county hospitals, physicians who had a high level of position, title and skills promotion by participating in the urban–rural medical consortium apparently had a higher level of willingness to continue participating than those who had a low and middle improvement level of position, title and skills. In our interviews, most county-level physicians expressed that participating in medical consortia can indeed learn many new clinical diagnosis and treatment technologies and establish cooperative relationships with doctors from provincial hospitals who can provide timely assistance. Hence, the majority of county-level participants thought that they had a high level of professional skills improvement (61.2%). The improvement of individual’ skills means that they may have more opportunities for title and position advancement, which plays a positive circular role in promoting their willingness to participate in urban–rural medical consortia. However, county-level physicians rarely receive direct incentives from participating in medical consortium projects. Physicians of county hospitals could be treating more patients with the help of provincial doctors, thus gaining benefits such as economic returns. Therefore, county-level physicians were not sensitive to changes in incentives due to the lag in incentives and have a low perception of incentive for urban–rural medical consortium projects. Therefore, managers of urban–rural medical consortium projects should consider establishing comprehensive incentive mechanisms for county-level doctors to help them have sufficient personal abilities to ensure the health status of rural residents [21].

For participants of provincial hospitals, physicians who had a high level of economic returns and rewards by participating in the urban–rural medical consortium apparently had a higher level of willingness to continue participating than those who had a middle level of economic returns and rewards. Compared with county hospitals, supervisors at provincial hospitals seems more likely to use financial incentives to increase the enthusiasm of physicians to participate in medical consortium projects, including economic returns and rewards. The economic returns and rewards obtained by physicians in provincial hospitals are related to the number of times they participate in the medical consortium. A manager of a provincial hospital said, “According to regulations, doctors will receive a cash subsidy of 1000 RMB after each participation in the medical consortium project. By the end of the year, we will provide additional bonuses to the doctors who have more participation this year.” This result proves that organizers of provincial hospitals mainly use financial incentives to increase the enthusiasm of physicians to participate in urban–rural medical consortium projects, which are relatively successful in a short period of time. Although physicians in provincial hospitals have a significantly higher level of income than doctors in county hospitals, physicians in provincial hospitals face higher living costs due to living in urban areas, which means that their demand for financial resources is also higher than physicians at the county level. Therefore, financial incentives effectively increase physicians’ willingness to continue participating in projects. Financial incentive rules should be continuously improved to make them more effective in the future [25]. In terms of non-financial incentives, priority should be given to physicians who have participated in urban–rural medical consortium projects when promoting professional titles and positions, which is also a commonly used non-financial incentive measure for provincial hospital managers. However, a provincial physician said, "The path to promote position through participating in urban–rural medical consortium projects is not clear." This suggests that, similar to other researches [26, 27], non-financial incentives are difficult to standard implement in hospitals, explaining why non-financial incentives did not exert a positive impact on provincial physicians’ willingness to participate in this study. Previous research has shown that individuals with higher altruism levels have a greater preference for non-financial incentives [14]. Provincial participants in this study had a high level of altruism (84.5%), hence, these non-financial incentive measures meet expectations, should play a positive role through normative implementation [28].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, data were collected via online questionnaires using the convenience sampling approach instead of probability sampling, limiting the representativeness of the entire population. Second, this study focused on the impact of incentives on participant willingness rather than on the performance of the medical consortium, due to the lack of scientific performance measurement methods for the urban–rural medical consortium. Meanwhile, incentive levels are based on self-reports, therefore, the results represent the participant’s subjective assessments of some variables. Future studies should consider to evaluate the effectiveness of incentives through using objective data.

Conclusion

This study addresses the following research questions previously raised: (a) In the context of the urban–rural medical consortium program, economic returns, position and title promotions served as positive factors in willingness to continue participating of physicians. This result demonstrates the value of incentive mechanisms in guiding physician engagement behavior. (b) There were differences in the level of incentives implemented by county and provincial hospitals. There was a lower perception of various incentive levels for urban–rural medical consortium projects among county-level physicians compared to provincial physicians. Administrators of county hospitals should allocate equal attention to the design of diverse incentives to ensure the willingness of county physicians to participate. (c) Incentive factors that influenced willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortia differed among physicians of county and provincial hospitals. County-level physicians who had a high level of position and titile promotion, professional skills improvement, and provincial physicians who had a high level of economic returns and rewards had a high level of willingness to continue participating in urban–rural medical consortia. Financial incentive regulations should be continuously refined, and non-financial incentives should be systematically implemented to enhance their efficacy in the future.
