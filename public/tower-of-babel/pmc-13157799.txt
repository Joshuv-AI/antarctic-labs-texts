Healthcare utilisation patterns among frail individuals aged 50+ years: results from the Lolland-Falster Population Study (LOFUS)
Authors: Katja Kemp Jacobsen, Mathilde Glud Christensen, Charlotte Nilsson, Randi Jepsen, Lau Thygesen, Charlotte Suetta, Ellen Astrid Holm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesFrailty is a clinical syndrome characterised by impaired homeostatic mechanisms and reduced physiological reserve. Hospital admissions for ambulatory care sensitive conditions (ACSCs) are commonly used as indicators of quality in primary healthcare. We aimed to examine the association between frailty and the use of healthcare resources, including unplanned hospital visits due to ACSCs and non-ACSCs and visits to general practitioners (GPs) and medical specialists (MSs) in primary care. We hypothesised that frail individuals would have similar odds of hospital visits due to ACSCs and non-ACSCs.DesignRegistry-based epidemiological study.SettingData from the Danish Lolland-Falster Health Study and national health registers. Data were collected in a rural region of Denmark between February 2016 and February 2020.Participants10 154 randomly selected individuals aged ≥50 years participating in the Lolland-Falster Health Study with valid frailty measurements.Outcome measuresHospital visits due to any diagnosis, hospital visits due to ACSCs and non-ACSCs, and visits to GPs and MSs in the primary care sector.ResultsAfter adjustment for age, sex, comorbidity and socioeconomic factors, frail participants had higher odds of hospital visits due to any diagnosis (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.57; p=0.03). The odds of hospital visits due to ACSCs (OR 1.42, 95% CI 0.97 to 2.08; p=0.07) and non-ACSCs (OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.47; p=0.22) were not significantly different. Frail individuals had higher odds of visiting their GP (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.46; p=0.047) but not a medical specialist (OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.62 to 1.07; p=0.15).ConclusionsAmong frail individuals, the distinction between unplanned hospital visits due to ACSCs and non-ACSCs is not meaningful. This finding is consistent with the understanding of frailty as a state of reduced physiological reserve, in which minor stressors may lead to hospital care regardless of diagnostic category.

Introduction

Frailty syndrome

During ageing, physiological systems in the body gradually lose functional reserves. This process finally results in a state where several organ systems have no reserve capacity left and cannot maintain homeostasis when challenged. In frail individuals, even minor stressor events may precipitate multiorgan dysfunction, contributing to adverse outcomes such as falls, infections, increased morbidity and mortality, and higher rates of hospitalisation and institutionalisation.13

Prevalence of frailty among community-dwelling older adults is 7–10% increasing to over 25% among those over 85 years.148 Thus, in an ageing society frailty has a very large clinical and public relevance.

There is no universal consensus on how to measure frailty. However, the physical frailty phenotype has been used in many scientific studies since it was described by Linda Fried in 2001. The physical frailty phenotype includes five criteria for frailty: unintentional weight loss; self-reported exhaustion (fatigue); low energy expenditure (sedentary lifestyle); slow gait speed and weak grip strength.1

Several socioeconomic factors such as low educational attainment, low wealth and low income have consistently been shown to be associated with frailty.7 9 Living alone, lack of social network and lack of access to help from family and friends have also been shown to be associated with frailty.7

The ageing human organism can be understood as a complex system.1012 An important property of a complex system is that it responds in a nonlinear manner, meaning that a small perturbation to the system can result in a huge response. An example of this in frailty is demonstrated through the ‘domino effect’, wherein a minor disturbance initiates a cascade of system failures. In complex systems, resilience refers to the capacity to return to equilibrium following a disturbance; in humans, frailty and resilience are inversely related.13 14

Hospital admission and ambulatory care sensitive conditions

Many studies have demonstrated associations between risk of hospitalisation and frailty. In an effort to reduce hospital admissions, the concepts of ambulatory care sensitive conditions (ACSCs) and avoidable or preventable admissions have been introduced. However, it is an open question whether these concepts can be applied to people with frailty.

In 1993, Billings et al defined ACSCs as “diagnoses for which timely and effective outpatient care can help to reduce the risk of hospitalization by either preventing the onset of an illness or condition, controlling an acute episodic illness or condition, or managing a chronic disease or condition”.15 Since then, the concept has been used by health authorities in many countries as a quality indicator of primary care performance, assuming that higher quality in the primary sector (accessibility to general practitioners, social support, etc.) may prevent these admissions.1621 Thus, the concept of ACSCs is closely related to the concept of potentially avoidable/preventable admissions. Using data from zip code areas in New York City, Billing et al demonstrated that the frequency of admissions with ACSCs was strongly associated with area income levels. The association was strongest for the age group 24–45 years and weakest for the age group 75+.15 In a recent study, Loyd et al published national norms for hospitalisations due to ACSCs among adults in the USA, showing that the frequency of ACSC admissions increased with age from 7.0% among those below 45 years old to 16.2% of all admissions among the ≥90 years old. Black people had higher frequency of hospitalisations due to ACSCs than white, and this effect weakened after the age of 75 and even reversed in the 85+-year-olds.22 Given that the disease prevalence and the socioeconomic and cultural characteristics of populations and health systems vary between countries, there is no consensus on a definitive list of ACSCs, but diagnoses frequently seen in older patients such as dehydration, gastroenteritis, constipation, urinary tract infection, pneumonia, fragility fractures, anaemia, pressure ulcers and hospitalisation due to social causes are often included.20 23 24

Population-level factors,for example, demographics, socioeconomic deprivation and rurality, as well as system-level factors such as continuity of primary care and access to primary care have been demonstrated to be drivers for acute admissions.212527

Aims

In the present study, we aim to assess the use of different resources in the healthcare system according to frailty status. Frailty is associated with high mortality risk28 29; we therefore chose to assess the pattern of healthcare utilisation in a period leading up to frailty assessment, thereby eliminating the competing risk due to death. Due to the character of frailty being a gradually developing syndrome moving from robust over prefrailty to frailty, we found this method justified. During 1 year prior to frailty assessment, we assessed ORs for unplanned hospital visits due to any diagnosis, ACSCs and non-ACSCs according to frailty status. Furthermore, we assessed ORs for the use of ambulatory healthcare resources, that is, visits to general practitioners (GP) and to medical specialists (MS) in the primary healthcare sector according to frailty status. Based on the pathophysiological understanding of frailty, we hypothesised that (1) frailty is associated with unplanned hospital visits independent of classification in ACSCs or non-ACSCs and (2) frail elderly individuals use ambulatory services in the primary sector less than could be expected from their disease burden. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between frailty and admissions due to ACSCs.

Methods

Setting

The national healthcare system in Denmark is a universal, tax-financed system. All inhabitants are registered with a GP. The GP is gatekeeper for hospital referrals as well as for referrals to visits to an MS in the primary sector, for example, experts in cardiology, gastroenterology etc.

Population

The study population are participants in the Lolland-Falster Health Study (LOFUS). Lolland-Falster is a rural area with approximately 100 000 residents. It is a socially deprived area with lower income and higher mortality and morbidity rates compared with other areas in Denmark. The aim of LOFUS was to gain knowledge of health determinants in this area. The study is a household-based prospective cohort study including people of all ages. Participants were randomly selected through the Danish Civil Registration System.30 Overall, the response rate in LOFUS was 36%. However, among participants in the age group 50–79 the participation rate was 44% and among those over 80 years participation rate was 23%. Data collection included self-administered questionnaires on social, mental and physical health, lifestyle factors and a frailty measurement. The data collection started in February 2016 and ended in February 2020. Participants aged 50 years and above were included in the present study. Questionnaires used in the present study are provided in the online supplemental material.

Frailty status

Frailty status was assessed using items almost identical to the Share-Frailty Instrument developed from the SHARE study.8 It is based on five criteria: unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, slowness and low physical activity. An individual is considered frail if 3 criteria are fulfilled and prefrail if 1–2 are fulfilled.6 7 The frailty measurement used in LOFUS has been described in detail elsewhere.6

Use of healthcare services

Use of healthcare services and hospital visits were collected from national health registers for the year prior to the frailty assessment.31 Unplanned hospital visits were defined as any acute, that is, unscheduled visit to a hospital whether it was to an emergency department or to any other department and whether the visit included a stay overnight or not. Visits to GPs and MSs were categorised as above or below the median number of visits for all participants during the relevant time period.

ACSC diagnoses

We used the classification made by the Danish health authorities classifying ACSCs as the following International Classification of Diseases, tenth revision (ICD-10) diagnoses: dehydration (E86.9), constipation (K59.0), lower respiratory tract disease (J12–J15, J18, J20–J22, J40–J47), urinary tract infection (N30, except N30.3 and N30.4), gastroenteritis (A09), fragility fractures (S02, S12, S22, S32, S42, S52, S62, S72, S82, S92), nutritional anaemia (D50–D53), social conditions (Z59, Z74, Z75) and pressure sore (L89).

Covariates

Variables concerning education, social network (frequency of seeing one’s children and possibility to get help when needed) and self-assessed health were all collected from the LOFUS questionnaire. Economic status was assessed using a variable from the LOFUS questionnaire (difficulty in making ends meet) supplemented with data from a national registry containing information on wealth including value of owned house.31 32 All the above-mentioned variables were included as categorical variables. These variables were chosen due to their known association with frailty.33

Comorbidity

Chronic diseases were self-reported (individual diseases collected from the LOFUS questionnaire) and included myocardial infarction, angina, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, kidney disease, diabetes, arthritis, depression, anxiety, migraine and cancer.

Statistics

Statistics were done using R V.4.4.1. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the population according to frailty, socioeconomic situation (SES) and different healthcare resource use. Logistic regression models were used to assess ORs of using different healthcare services according to frailty status. Frailty is a gradually developing syndrome and prefrailty carries some of the same characteristics and risks as frailty. However, prefrailty is heterogeneous, and combining it with frailty may dilute the results. We therefore chose to use two explanatory statistical models. In model A, frail participants were compared with all others (non-frail+prefrail), in model B frail+prefrail were compared with non-frail. The fully adjusted models were adjusted for age, sex, comorbidities and SES factors. All covariates were entered simultaneously into the fully adjusted models. No model selection procedures were applied. Multicollinearity among covariates included in the fully adjusted models was assessed using generalised variance inflation factors (GVIF), reported as adjusted GVIF∧(1/ (2×Df)).

Data on the use of healthcare resources were collected for a historical period of 365, 180 and 30 days preceding the frailty assessment.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

LOFUS enrolled 18 949 individuals, of which 10 985 individuals were 50 years or above. Of these, 10 163 completed frailty assessment. Nine individuals were excluded due to three or more missing frailty components, leaving 10 154 individuals for analysis. Frequency of visits to hospital for any diagnosis as well as for ACSCs increased with higher age, frailty, poorer self-assessed health, being female and economic disadvantage (lower wealth and difficulties in making ends meet). Among frail individuals, 26.3% had at least one unplanned hospital visit for any diagnosis and 7.5% had at least one due to an ACSC (online supplemental table A).

Most SES variables implying disadvantage were associated with more frequent visits to a GP as well as to an MS in primary healthcare (higher age; female sex; being divorced or widowed; difficulty in making ends meet; lower wealth and lower frequency of seeing one’s children). However, higher degrees of frailty and lower educational attainment were associated with higher frequency of visits to a GP but not with visits to an MS (online supplemental table B).

Discussion

In this study, we have assessed the association between frailty status and hospital visits due to ACSCs and non-ACSCs as well as the use of GP and MS in the primary healthcare sector. Within a year prior to the frailty assessment frail participants had higher ORs for seeing a GP but not for seeing an MS.

When comparing frail participants to all others in a fully adjusted model, there were no significant differences between ORs for ACSC and non-ACSC-related hospital visits (table 2 and figure 2). The same pattern was seen when comparing the group of frail+prefrail to non-frail (table 3)

Use of outpatient healthcare resources in the primary sector

In the present study, frailty as well as advancing age and negative SES factors were associated with an increased use of GP but not with an increased use of MS. One possible explanation for the lower probability of frail people to see a specialist physician is accessibility, which may be compromised by geographical distance to a specialist physician, combined with mobility issues among frail people and insufficient public transportation in rural areas.3436 Another accessibility issue is the existence of appropriate specialist practices. In Denmark, there is a high degree of medical specialisation. Some specialties—among these geriatric medicine—have no specialist physicians employed in the primary healthcare sector, partly due to a shortage of geriatricians. Very old and frail patients may not be considered fit for referral to organ-specific specialist physicians and will therefore be taken care of solely by their GP. Older adults are generally less educated compared with younger individuals,37 and due to long-standing relationships and the development of trust and familiarity with their GP, some may feel more confident in and prefer to consult their GP rather than seeking a referral.38

There is an abundance of literature demonstrating that SES factors are associated with many diseases and with premature ageing and frailty.7 37 39 40 Studies investigating healthcare resource utilisation in relation to SES find a similar pattern concerning the utilisation of outpatient services.38 41 42

Lueckmann et al recently reviewed the literature concerning socioeconomic factors associated with the utilisation of primary care and MS in high-income countries finding that, according to SES, the most disadvantaged groups had more frequent visits to primary care physicians while often having lower probabilities of visiting specialist physicians.43 Referral practices among GPs have been shown to correlate with socioeconomic position. A recent Australian study indicated that patients from the lowest socioeconomic quartile had the lowest OR for being referred,44 while a Danish study found that missed or delayed diagnoses in dementia, due to lack of referrals to dementia clinics, was associated with neighbourhood socioeconomic deprivation.36

Unplanned hospital visits

Frail participants had higher ORs for unplanned hospital visits whether due to ACSCs or non-ACSCs. This is in accordance with the pathophysiological understanding of frailty as being characterised by minimal functional reserve capacity in several organ systems. Even a minor stressor event may lead to a massive breakdown. In this situation, an unplanned hospital visit may be the only rational solution, and it probably does not matter whether the stressor event is a diagnosis classified as ambulatory care sensitive or not.18

Difference between OR for ACSC admissions and non-ACSC admissions among frail individuals was diminished when the analysis was adjusted for SES variables. This supports the findings from other studies indicating that potentially avoidable admissions are partly driven by socioeconomic factors.27

It is difficult to compare studies concerning ACSCs due to the different lists of ACSCs used. A literature review identified 36 conditions each covering several ICD-10 codes included in various lists used by health authorities and organisations.20 However, most studies report increasing admissions rates for ACSCs with increasing age which is in accordance with the findings in our study.

In our study, educational attainment and wealth were not associated with unplanned admissions due to ACSCs. Billing et al found a strong association between area income and admissions due to ACSCs. However, the correlation between area income and admissions due to ACSCs peaked in the age group 25–44 and was substantially smaller among elderly participants.15 Likewise, Loyd et al showed that the effect of ethnicity was stronger among the younger age groups.22 These findings indicate that traditional socioeconomic factors may influence older people less than younger, possibly due to the stronger effect of ageing organ systems and frailty.

The combined findings of a less-than-expected use of specialist physicians in primary care and a high OR for unplanned hospital visits among frail people raise the question whether easier access to relevant specialist physicians might have prevented some of the unplanned visits. Comprehensive geriatric assessment is the gold standard for management of frailty.45 46 However, the number of geriatricians is far too low to cover the needs of all prefrail and frail elderly residents. A possible way forward includes training of other medical specialists and other health professions such as nurses and therapists, as well as setting up models for geriatric assessment through collaboration with intermediate care institutions and nursing homes.47 48

Strengths and limitations

The study included a large population-based sample and used validated methods for measuring frailty, including SHARE-FI, a modified version of Fried’s phenotype. The Danish national registries are known to be of high reliability and validity.49 Approximately 41% of those 50 years or above invited to LOFUS chose to participate, which aligns with similar research.33 However, non-participation was higher for older people and for those in more disadvantaged socioeconomic situations, and participants in the LOFUS study had lower mortality than non-participants.50 51 Thus, our study probably underestimates frailty prevalence as well as prevalence of socioeconomic disadvantage. This may bias the assessment of associations between frailty and SES.

The frailty assessment was based on self-reported data+objective measurement of hand grip strength. SES parameters were based on self-reported+national registry data. We cannot exclude recall bias which could have led to inaccurate classification of frailty status and thereby to underestimation of the differences between frail and non-frail.

We recorded healthcare resource use during the year preceding the frailty assessment. Morbidity in this period may therefore have contributed to the development of frailty. Consequently, we cannot exclude the possibility that some participants classified as prefrail or frail at the time of assessment were robust during the preceding year. As a result, differences in healthcare use between robust and prefrail/frail individuals may be underestimated. However, we assessed use of healthcare resources during time periods 30 days, 180 days and 365 days preceding the frailty assessment, and as shown in tables 2 and 3 our findings showed the same patterns at 180 days and 365 days (at 30 days there were too few cases to report). Furthermore, the frailty prevalence in our study is similar to other studies thus indicating that previous hospital visits have not led to increased frailty prevalence. Unplanned hospital visits due to non-ACSCs represent a very wide diagnostic range (all diagnoses not included in the list of ACSCs). The present study does not contribute to clarifying whether admission for specific diagnoses (eg, injuries, cardiac events) differs significantly according to frailty status.

Our findings concerning hospital visits due to ACSCs cover the diagnoses used by Danish healthcare authorities, and extrapolation to other healthcare systems must be done with caution.

Conclusion

In frail as well as for the combined group of frail+prefrail individuals, the distinction between unplanned hospital visits due to ACSCs or non-ACSCs is not meaningful. Older people with prefrailty and frailty have higher ORs for seeing a GP but not for seeing a specialist physician in primary healthcare. This raises the question if easier access to relevant specialist physicians could have prevented some of the unplanned hospital contacts; however, the present study cannot answer this question. Our findings underline the need of quality improvement attempts such as the nationwide cross sectoral quality database DANFRAIL, which has recently been established in Denmark.52
