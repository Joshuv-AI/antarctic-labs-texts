Loneliness predicts worse Parkinsonism: a longitudinal, community-based, clinical-pathological study
Authors: David Andrés González, Robert S. Wilson, Jessica Hemm, Christopher G. Goetz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Loneliness is increasingly acknowledged as important in parkinsonism-related disorders. However, little is known about reciprocal longitudinal relationships between loneliness and parkinsonism. In this study, 3,099 community-based individuals (Mage=78.09, 75.2% women) completed measures of loneliness, isolation, depression and were rated on parkinsonism severity annually with 846 having available postmortem data. Controlling for demographic and social-emotional covariates, mixed multilevel modeling evaluated longitudinal impacts of parkinsonism on loneliness and loneliness on parkinsonism. The loneliness→parkinsonism models explained greater variance, with loneliness associated with worse parkinsonism and greater rates of motor decline. Whereas parkinsonism→loneliness models revealed worse parkinsonism associated with worse loneliness, parkinsonism did not predict rate of loneliness change. Subjects with Lewy Bodies at autopsy had worse loneliness proximate to death and a more pronounced time×loneliness interaction compared to those without. There are bidirectional relationships between loneliness and parkinsonism, with loneliness being the more potent longitudinal predictor.

Introduction

Loneliness has been identified as a risk factor for mortality and chronic health conditions, with increasing prevalence across several age groups globally1–4. This has resulted in several health organizations, including the World Health Organization and United States Surgeon General, identifying loneliness as a condition of concern5,6. Concurrently, Parkinson’s disease (PD)—including its core parkinsonism features—has been increasing in prevalence over time7, and a link between parkinsonism and loneliness is being established8. Several cross-sectional studies have identified a potential link between parkinsonism and loneliness, whether demonstrating high rates of loneliness in people with PD or showing relationships to several non-motor features, subjective outcomes, and quality of life9–11. The literature is not unequivocal, however, with other studies suggesting no difference in loneliness levels between PD and non-PD12, or suggesting that loneliness levels do not relate to objective measures of parkinsonism severity11.

Longitudinal studies can help clarify our understanding, but only two such studies have been carried out to date. One found that individuals who affirmatively responded to a single loneliness question at baseline had a 37% increased risk of PD when followed over 15 years13. Another found that individuals with a self-reported PD diagnosis at first visit had relatively higher levels of loneliness at that visit relative to others without PD. Additionally, those individuals also had increased levels of loneliness at follow-up10. While provocative, it remains unknown whether objective measurement of parkinsonism relates to loneliness, and whether pertinent social-emotional factors, such as depression or social network size, were contributing to the loneliness change. Second, these studies separately suggest that loneliness can longitudinally predict parkinsonism, but also that parkinsonism can precede worsening loneliness. Understanding whether loneliness or parkinsonism longitudinally predicts the other can inform the prioritization of intervention targets.

The current study aimed to model loneliness and parkinsonism over time and tested the hypotheses that: (a), higher levels of loneliness are associated with higher levels and more rapid progression of parkinsonism; and (b), higher levels of parkinsonism are associated with higher levels and more rapid increase in loneliness. These hypotheses were tested in: 1) a community sample of older adults, 2) a subset of those with meaningful parkinsonism, and 3) a subset of those with relevant pathologies at autopsy.

Results

Descriptives

See Table 1 for fully tabulated descriptive statistics, and supplementary figures for distribution of loneliness and parkinsonism scores over time. The community sample was mostly older, White (33.5% Black American, 3.7% Hispanic/Latine), women with some college education. These individuals had minimal parkinsonism at baseline, with gait concern being the most prominent facet. Of the 3,099 at baseline, they were followed for 4.98 years on average (SD = 4.05), with 1480 individuals developing meaningful parkinsonism (i.e., 2+ definite signs). Pathologic examination was available for 846 individuals after being followed for 5.27 years on average (SD = 4.12). The postmortem sample had a higher proportion of men and less ethnic-racial diversity (9.9% Black American, 1.9% Hispanic/Latine). These individuals had slightly higher loneliness scores, slightly smaller social networks, and greater parkinsonism, with gait and bradykinesia being the most prominent signs. In the community sample, 77 (1.6%) had a probable clinical diagnosis of PD at baseline. In the postmortem sample, 23 (3.1%) had a probable clinical diagnosis of PD proximate to death and 169 (20%) had Lewy bodies (LBs) on pathologic exam, with relatively few (7.7% of those with LBs) being of nigral-predominant distribution. Predicting parkinsonism over time

Model statistics (loneliness + demographics + social-emotional covariates) are delineated in Table 2. Overall models had large variance explained across community, parkinsonism, and postmortem samples. Time and age were the largest predictors in these models. In the community sample, loneliness and its time interaction were both statistically significant; this indicates worse Parkinsonism at each time point and greater rate of worsening for those that are lonelier (Fig. 1 & supplementary Fig. 1). The interactive effect was not statistically significant in those with parkinsonism, but emerged as meaningful in the postmortem sample. In those with LBs, although loneliness did not predict worse parkinsonism initially, there were marked differences in the rate of parkinsonism decline, with lonelier individuals having steeper rates of decline (Fig. 2 & supplementary Fig. 2). Across samples, adding loneliness & loneliness×time predictors (to a demographics and time model) resulted in marginal ΔR2 of 0.02 to 0.03, and social-emotional covariates did not contribute meaningfully to model variance. Predicting loneliness over time

Across the community, parkinsonism, and postmortem samples, total explained model variance was always lower relative to the predicting parkinsonism (demographics only) models, with these effects being considered medium overall. This was partially related to time being less potent for predicting longitudinal loneliness, relative to its import in parkinsonism; a small effect overall. Furthermore, none of the interactions were significant across subsamples (Figs. 3–4 & supplementary Figs. 3–4). The direct effect of parkinsonism on loneliness was relatively consistent, being small and similar in size to the effect of loneliness on parkinsonism models. This indicates that individuals with worse parkinsonism were lonelier, but this loneliness did not disproportionately change over time. Across samples, adding the parkinsonism & parkinsonism×time predictors did not meaningfully improve predictive performance of demographics + time models (marginal ΔR2 < 0.01). In contrast to the parkinsonism models, adding social-emotional covariates—as expected—resulted in significant increases in explained loneliness variance. Loneliness proximate to death

We further explored the relationship between PD pathology and loneliness, and found that those with LBs were lonelier (t = 2.99, p = 0.003, d = 0.25). We additionally ran ANCOVAs to see if there were differences in loneliness across particular LB distributions, controlling for demographics, co-pathology, and social-emotional covariates; LBs were a significant predictor of loneliness (F = 7.27, p < 0.001), whereas AD (F = 2.52, p = 0.11) and vascular (Fs = 0.06 to 2.82, ps = 0.09 to 0.80) pathology were not. Posthoc comparison revealed that differences were accounted for by those with diffuse neocortical-type LBs; loneliness was statistically higher for this cortical LB group relative to those without LBs and those with nigral-predominant LB inclusions. See supplementary Tables 5-7 for analysis details.

Sensitivity and posthoc analyses

Analyses were subsequently corrected for the false discovery rate (corrected p-values flagged in supplementary Table 1) and robustly re-run with normalized variables (supplementary Table 2). Although most predictors remained statistically significant, the time×loneliness effect became marginally significant (p < 0.10) in two models: community and postmortem. The robust models’ total explained variance tended to increase meaningfully across models. In the predicting parkinsonism models, age generally became a stronger predictor, and the time×loneliness effect was also attenuated here. However, the primary effect of loneliness became stronger in the postmortem sample. In the predicting loneliness models, social network became a stronger negative predictor across all three samples, and Parkinsonism had a stronger direct effect in the postmortem sample. Across all models and samples, depression became a less important predictor.

Given that the impact of loneliness may vary by sex or age, we ran posthoc analyses to see if either impacted time×loneliness interactions. Sex did not impact model findings. However, three-way interactions with age were statistically significant in the total sample. Specifically, for individuals that were older, loneliness resulted in the highest rate of parkinsonism progression. These results are presented in supplementary Tables 3−4. Additionally, although we controlled for the number of individuals in social networks, certain relationships may be more protective than others (e.g., having close family relationships may be more beneficial than the same number of neighbors). Although we did not have coded information on relationship type, we did have information regarding the perceived support quality in extant relationships, which we included as a covariate in supplemental models. Although the primary findings were mostly unchanged, there are two noteworthy trends: the time×loneliness effects became more prominent across all three samples. Additionally, the time×parkinsonism effect also became more prominent in the postmortem sample (albeit just borderline significant), suggesting that when also considering the supportiveness of social networks, more parkinsonism could result in worsening loneliness for those with Lewy Bodies.

Discussion

Given the provocative literature on a parkinsonism-loneliness relationship without extensive longitudinal data, we sought to understand whether parkinsonism or loneliness was the stronger prospective predictor of the other. Of the two, we found that loneliness was a potent predictor. Not only did loneliness predict worse parkinsonism across time, it also predicted worsening parkinsonism. This time interaction—worse rate of parkinsonism decline for those that are lonelier—was even more prominent in those with LB-validated PD pathology. This suggests that social connection may positively buffer against the impact of disease on parkinsonian motor symptoms, such that those who are lonelier may have less reserve and be more likely to demonstrate clinical signs. There may be unmeasured variables, such as inflammatory levels, that may underlie these findings as well. Of note, the modest change in explained model variance by adding the interaction, and fact that it was attenuated in corrected robust models, suggests that the independent effect is small overall.

In contrast, although people with more parkinsonism may be lonelier on average, this remains relatively stable over time. The fact that this was observed despite parkinsonism itself getting worse over time argues against a hypothesis that those with worsening motor symptoms may socially withdraw because of motoric disability and potentiate a self-reinforcing cycle of worsening loneliness. Although both parkinsonism and loneliness had similar main effects, only loneliness showed a time interaction, indicating loneliness has more predictive weight.

We also found a link between LB pathology and loneliness, not found with co-pathologies, even when controlling for demographic and social-emotional differences. This was particularly prominent for the neocortical-type LB distribution, suggesting that this deposition may have a uniquely detrimental relationship with loneliness. Of the different LB distributions, neocortical type is most likely to have dementia, and recent research has shown that loneliness can be both a risk for and symptom of, dementia. However, the fact that the link was found only with LB, and not AD nor vascular, pathology suggests that there is some unique relationship between LB pathology, its phenotypic manifestation, and loneliness that should be explored in future research.

Differences in significance patterns across subsamples could reflect differences in case mix, outcome variability, and statistical power. In the parkinsonism subgroup, the selection criteria may have reduced between-person variability in longitudinal parkinsonism slopes which attenuated the interaction. In contrast, among Lewy body–positive cases, loneliness may be more closely linked to the rate of symptom progression, yielding a significant loneliness × time interaction even when average differences are smaller due to everyone having the same underlying pathology. These subgroup analyses should be considered with caution, given the differences in selection and reduced sample size.

Depression is an important variable worthy of further discussion. First, a subjective sense of isolation can cause sadness and other aversive emotions, which can precipitate social approach behaviors14. Second, depression can also lead to negative evaluations of, and withdrawal from, others in a way that results in more loneliness. Additionally, depression can be found in parkinsonism prodromes, and the functional impact of parkinsonism can result in depression. Given these potentially confounding relationships, we wanted to consider whether our results held even when controlling for depression. Although depression attenuated the strength of loneliness’ unique and interactive predictive power, it did not nullify it, adding to the body of evidence indicating the unique importance of loneliness.

The three-way interaction with age in the total sample is intriguing and suggests that loneliness is most pernicious in potentiating parkinsonism for the oldest old. Some potential hypotheses are that there is the least amount of brain reserve in this group, or that there is a greater confluence of health and environmental presses in this group, such that greater loneliness is more likely to result in health deterioration, such as parkinsonism. However, once health has already been impacted by other conditions (e.g., those with Lewy Bodies), age no longer potentiates the deleterious effect of loneliness.

This study has several strengths, including its longitudinal objective data on loneliness, parkinsonism, pertinent social-emotional covariates, and pathology. These findings suggest that loneliness interventions should not just be emphasized at the community level5, but also that loneliness should be assessed at initial visits in individuals presenting with parkinsonism, since individuals with more loneliness may have a more malignant course of parkinsonism.

Our data point to several lines of inquiry to be pursued in future studies. For example, understanding potential mediators and moderators of the relationship between loneliness and parkinsonism will be important. Some potential moderators include physical activity and cognitive abilities, which may drive or influence how much loneliness leads to Parkinsonism. For example, loneliness is also known to precede cognitive decline, and those who develop cognitive decline may be less able to troubleshoot loneliness-related concerns, which could exacerbate loneliness’ impact on health and parkinsonism. Other potential moderators include behavioral-psychological symptoms beyond depression, like apathy and impulse-control, can that co-occur with parkinsonism. Additionally, it may be that the presence of a critical amount of a condition can change the relationship among variables, and future studies should use advanced statistics to better elaborate whether loneliness results in differing risk (e.g., time-to-event for significant parkinsonism or cognitive impairment) and whether these critical saturations change the trajectory of loneliness-parkinsonism (e.g., a steeper decline after developing cognitive impairment). Although we cannot establish causality with this data, future studies may consider marginal structural models or related statistical methods to estimate causal model analogs. Furthermore, certain loneliness subtypes, such as social and emotional loneliness, may be more or less relevant for predicting health, and these should be explored in future studies. Future research may also consider in vivo biomarkers to help clarify their impacts on loneliness and parkinsonism over time. It could be, for example, that inflammatory markers drive both manifestation of loneliness and parkinsonism, or that Lewy Bodies precede increases in loneliness that drive observed effects.

There are several constraints on generality that should be considered. The primary one is that this study came from a community cohort with low PD prevalence and relatively mild parkinsonism as well. It bears emphasizing that this study focused on all-cause parkinsonism, which can result from a number of conditions other than PD, such as vascular health, drug effects, and aging. Although we created subgroups of those with relevant symptoms and pathologies to be a better analog of PD, future research should specifically investigate those with PD, as there may be differences in motor, behavioral-psychological, and cognitive signs that have a unique relationship with loneliness over time. Understanding the import of loneliness on all-cause parkinsonism still sets the stage for understanding its broad impact on movement disorders, regardless of pathology. There was also less ethnic-racial diversity for the postmortem sample, such that it is unclear if or how these findings could generalize to minoritized groups with a different portfolio of risk and resilience factors. Another limitation is that this deidentified dataset did not have date available, so we could not consider the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on observed effects, which have been demonstrated to have impacted loneliness levels. It also remains to be seen whether interventions aimed at addressing loneliness have similar ameliorative impacts on those with PD and parkinsonism, which should also be explored in future research.

Methods

Sample

Participants came from two clinical-pathologic cohort studies: the Rush Memory and Aging Project (MAP) and Minority Aging Research Study (MARS). Since 1997, MAP enrolls consenting individuals in Northeastern Illinois willing to participate in annual exams and organ donation who are at least 50 years old and without a dementia diagnosis at baseline15. Since 2004, MARS has focused on enrollment strategies tailored for Black Americans in the same region, consenting individuals with similar inclusion criteria to MAP excepting required organ donation16. We selected individuals for whom we had complete measures of loneliness and parkinsonism, and limited analyses to the first 15 years of follow-up. All participants consented to involvement in these studies, as approved by the Rush University Medical Center IRB. This study was conducted in accordance with the American Psychological Association Ethics Code.

Measurement

Loneliness

Loneliness was assessed with a modified short form of the self-report De Jong Gierveld Loneliness Scale (dJGLS; average of five, 5-point-rated items)17–19. Scores range from 1-5, with higher scores indicating more loneliness. In contrast with the original dJGLS, which has social and emotional subscales, the implementation of the dJGLS in the MAP and MARS cohorts was abbreviated to focus on emotional loneliness items17.

Social network

Network was assessed with a structured interview that inquired on number of people seen within a month across several network types (e.g., friends, family). Scores are unbounded, with higher numbers indicating a larger social network.

Depression

Depression was assessed with a modified short form of the self-rated Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (sum of ten, yes-no rated items)20–22. Scores range from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater depression symptomatology.

Parkinsonism

Parkinsonism was assessed with a modified 26-item version of the clinician-administered Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS)23,24. There are four parkinsonism domains: bradykinesia, gait, rigidity, and tremor. Each domain is scaled to a range of 0−100, with higher scores indicating more severe parkinsonism, and a global parkinsonism score is created by averaging all four domains. “Meaningful Parkinsonism” is classified by a clinician if 2 or more definite signs of parkinsonism are present in the individual.

Lewy Bodies and additional pathology

Seven regions were sampled for Lewy Bodies (LBs) postmortem, including: substantia nigra, two limbic regions, and four neocortical regions. Procedures have been previously detailed25,26, and Lewy Bodies are rated binarily, as present-absent, and ordinally: absent, nigral predominant, limbic type, or neocortical type. In this ordinal classification, those with neocortical type LBs typically had LBs in limbic and nigral regions. Neuritic amyloid plaques, diffuse amyloid plaques, and neurofibrillary tangles were sampled from five regions, averaged, and scaled to create a global Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology score. A dichotomous rating of one or more gross infarcts and microinfarcts (sampled from nine regions) was also used. AD and vascular pathology measures were used to covary with LBs in postmortem subsample analyses to see if loneliness relationships were unique to LBs as opposed to general pathologic change.

Analytic approach

Descriptives statistics were used to understand sample characteristics at baseline, as well as characteristics at death for the postmortem subsample. Longitudinal effects were analyzed with mixed multilevel modeling. Models were participant-grouped (accounting for autocorrelation among participants across time), with random intercepts and random slopes, using restricted maximum likelihood and Satterthwaite estimation, with emphasis on marginal R2 as total model effect size. Models were first run with parkinsonism as an outcome, and loneliness (is more loneliness associated with worse parkinsonism at each time point) and loneliness×time (is loneliness associated with steeper decline in parkinsonism over time) as predictors. Models were then run with loneliness as an outcome, and parkinsonism (is more parkinsonism associated with worse loneliness at each time point) and parkinsonism×time (is parkinsonism associated with steeper decline in loneliness over time) as predictors. Demographics (age, sex, education) were entered as covariates. Depression and social network were entered as additional covariates to see if any longitudinal trends were specific to loneliness, and not attributable to depression or network size, as these often covary with loneliness.

To understand the relationship of LBs to loneliness, a t-test was first run comparing loneliness proximate to death in those with and without LBs. This was followed by an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to see if LB distribution (absent, nigral, limbic, and neocortical) was associated with loneliness levels proximate to death, after accounting for pathological (AD, vascular), demographic (age, sex, education), and social-emotional (depression, social network) covariates.

As sensitivity analyses, models were re-run with corrections for false discovery rate (implemented with the Benjamini-Yekutieli (BY) method) and normalization of all continuous variables (implemented with modified negative-inclusive Box-Cox power transformations). Analyses were conducted in the R environment (version 4.4.3), supplemented with lme4 (1.1-37), parameters (0.26), performance (0.14), Hmisc (5.2-3), car (3.1-3), and psych (2.5.3) packages.
