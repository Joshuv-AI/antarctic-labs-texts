Exact Hull Reformulation for Quadratically Constrained Generalized Disjunctive Programs
Sergey Gusev <sgusev@purdue.edu>, David E. Bernal Neira <dbernaln@purdue.edu>

Abstract
Generalized Disjunctive Programming (GDP) is a framework for optimization problems involving discrete decisions and nonlinear constraints. The widely used Hull Reformulation transforms GDPs into Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Programming (MINLP) problems with tighter continuous relaxations. However, it typically employs an ε \varepsilon -approximation of the closure of the perspective function for nonlinear constraints, which can lead to numerical instability and a weaker continuous relaxation. We present an exact Hull Reformulation for GDPs with quadratic constraints, preserving the original quadratic structure and avoiding relaxation weakening. The method applies to both convex and non-convex constraints and extends earlier approaches developed for second-order cone representable functions. We prove equivalence to the conventional exact Hull Reformulation for quadratic constraints and demonstrate improved computational performance and numerical stability in extensive computational experiments. Benchmarks include random GDP instances, Continuously Stirred Tank Reactor network optimization, k k -means clustering, and constrained layout optimization problems. The computational results demonstrate improved solution times and numerical stability of the proposed exact Hull Reformulation, making it a preferable approach for GDP problems with quadratic constraints.

Introduction

Problems involving continuous and discrete variables, including those that require making discrete decisions, can generally be represented in the form of Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Programs (MINLPs).
However, formulating problems involving a large number of discrete decisions using traditional MINLP frameworks can become overly complex and counterintuitive.
For instance, common tasks in Process Systems Engineering (PSE), such as selecting process units or determining process configurations, often result in cumbersome algebraic constraints and numerous binary variables.
When modeled in the classical form of MINLP, such problems can be challenging for practitioners to implement and interpret
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.

To address these limitations, Generalized Disjunctive Programming (GDP) has emerged as a higher-level modeling paradigm that more naturally integrates logical decision-making with algebraic constraints.
Introduced by
[
4
]
, GDP extends Balas’s disjunctive programming framework
[
5
]
to include nonlinear constraints and logical relations
[
6
,
1
,
2
]
.
In GDP, discrete alternatives, such as choosing among different equipment configurations or operational modes, are explicitly represented through Boolean variables and logical disjunctions (OR conditions) rather than through algebraically encoded binary variables.
This approach enables clearer and more intuitive formulations compared to conventional MINLP models, making it much easier to formulate the model and apply the framework to practical challenges in real-world scenarios
[
7
]
.

A GDP problem can be formally expressed as follows
[
4
,
6
]
:

\begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y}}\quad&f(\mathbf{x})\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&g(\mathbf{x})\leq 0,\\
&\bigvee_{i\in D_{k}}\begin{bmatrix}Y_{ik}\\
h_{ik}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0\end{bmatrix},\quad\forall\,k\in K,\\
&\underset{i\in D_{k}}{\underline{\bigvee}}Y_{ik},\quad\forall\,k\in K,\\
&\Omega(\mathbf{Y})=\text{True},\\
&\mathbf{x}^{\ell}\leq\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u},\\
&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
&Y_{ik}\in\{\text{False},\text{True}\},\quad\forall\,k\in K,\;i\in D_{k}.\end{aligned}\qquad\text{(GDP)}

(1)

In this GDP formulation,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the continuous decision variables and

\mathbf{Y}
the collection of Boolean variables.
The objective function is given by
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Global constraints are represented by
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
where
p
is the number of global inequalities.
The condition
g(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
is understood componentwise, i.e.,

g_{j}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
for all
j=1,\dots,p
.
Each disjunct
i
within disjunction
k
is associated with

h_{ik}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{q_{ik}}
,
where
q_{ik}
is the number of inequalities in that disjunct.
The condition
h_{ik}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
is likewise understood
componentwise, i.e.,
h_{ik,\ell}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
for all
\ell=1,\dots,q_{ik}
.
The set
K
indexes all disjunctions, and
D_{k}
denotes the set of disjuncts within disjunction
k
.
The exclusive OR (XOR) condition,

\underset{i\in D_{k}}{\underline{\bigvee}}Y_{ik},

ensures exactly one Boolean variable
Y_{ik}
per disjunction
k
is
True
.
Possible logical relationships among Boolean variables are represented by
\Omega(\mathbf{Y})=\text{True}
.
These logical conditions can be systematically translated into algebraic constraints, enabling their integration
into conventional optimization frameworks
[
2
]
.

Although GDPs can be solved directly using specialized logic-based solvers, the more common practice is to reformulate GDPs into MINLP problems and leverage the maturity and broad availability of MINLP solvers

,

.
Among the various reformulation strategies, the Hull Reformulation is particularly notable due to its tight continuous relaxation properties, which could enhance solver performance by providing strong lower bounds
[

]
.

One of the major challenges in applying Hull Reformulation to nonlinear constraints in GDP problems arises from numerical instabilities associated with existing methods to reformulate constraints, such as the widely used
\varepsilon
-approximation of the closure of the perspective function

Although computationally practical, the
\varepsilon

Other works have addressed quadratic constraints through second-order cone representations

, but it can be extended only to convex quadratic constraints.
This motivated the present work, which extends this reformulation approach to any quadratic constraint to form an exact Hull Reformulation.
The proposed reformulation retains the quadratic functional form of the original constraints and provides an exact description of the Hull Reformulation for disjunctive constraints without relying on approximations.
We provide theoretical proofs of equivalence between the proposed exact reformulation, which maintains the functional structure, and the original exact Hull Reformulation for quadratic constraints.

Extensive computational experiments conducted in this work illustrate that the exact Hull Reformulation outperforms the

2
GDP to MINLP Reformulation Strategies

2.1
MINLP Reformulation Basics

GDP problems are commonly solved by reformulating them into MINLP problems.
Although several reformulation approaches exist, all typically share a common structure.
All MINLP reformulations introduce binary variables to represent Boolean conditions and convert propositional logic into linear constraints, but they differ in how they reformulate the constraints within the disjunctions.
The disjunction-independent part of the MINLP reformulation can be presented as follows:

\begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\quad&f(\mathbf{x})\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&g(\mathbf{x})\leq 0,\\
&\sum_{i\in D_{k}}y_{ik}=1\quad\forall\,k\in K,\\
&\mathbf{E}\mathbf{y}\geq\mathbf{e},\\
&\mathbf{x}^{\ell}\leq\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u},\\
&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
&y_{ik}\in\{0,1\}\quad\forall\,k\in K,\,i\in D_{k}.\end{aligned}\qquad\text{(MINLP without Disjunctions)}

(2)

In this formulation, the Boolean variables
Y_{ik}
from the original GDP are converted to binary variables
y_{ik}\in\{0,1\}
, which activate or deactivate the corresponding disjunctive constraints.
The constraint
\sum_{i\in D_{k}}y_{ik}=1
represents the exclusive OR (XOR) condition
\underset{i\in D_{k}}{\underline{\bigvee}}Y_{ik}
, ensuring that exactly one disjunct from each disjunction set
D_{k}
is selected.
All logical relationships between disjuncts, originally denoted by
\Omega(\mathbf{Y})=\text{True}
are translated into linear constraints
\mathbf{E}\mathbf{y}\geq\mathbf{e}
using established methods
[
2
]
.

However, the presented MINLP reformulation does not yet specify how the disjunctive constraints
h_{ik}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
should be handled.
Each MINLP reformulation strategy differs mainly in how it reformulates these constraints.
The following subsections address how this challenge is handled by all reformulations used in this paper.

2.2
Big-M Reformulation

In the Big-M reformulation, constraints within each disjunct are activated conditionally using binary variables and large constants (“Big-M”)
[
14
]
. Specifically, disjunctive constraints are reformulated as:

\begin{aligned} h_{ik}(\mathbf{x})\leq M_{ik}(1-y_{ik})\quad\forall\,k\in K,\,i\in D_{k},\\
\end{aligned}\qquad\text{(Big-M Reformulation)}

(3)

where
M_{ik}
are sufficiently large constants chosen such that when
y_{ik}=0
the constraint is always evaluated to values less than
M_{ik}
in the domain of the problem, and thus the constraint becomes trivially satisfied regardless of the value of
\mathbf{x}
.
On the other hand, when
y_{ik}=1
, the constraint reverts exactly to its original form
h_{ik}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
, activating the corresponding disjunct.

The quality of continuous relaxation in the case of Big-M reformulation strongly depends on the choice of
M_{ik}
.
Excessively large values of
M_{ik}
lead to weaker continuous relaxations and poorer lower bounds, thus increasing computational effort.
Conversely, selecting tighter
M_{ik}
values improves relaxation quality and solver performance but requires careful, problem-specific tuning.
Despite yielding potentially weaker relaxation compared to the Hull Reformulation
[
11
]
, the Big-M reformulation remains an attractive and widely used approach due to its simplicity, ease of implementation, and the fact that it does not increase the number of variables or constraints compared to the original GDP
[
15
]
.

2.3
Hull Reformulation

The Hull Reformulation offers tighter continuous relaxations and stronger lower bounds for GDP problems compared to other common approaches, such as Big-M.
The Hull Reformulation ensures that the continuous relaxation of each disjunction in the resulting MINLP corresponds exactly to the convex hull of all disjuncts in a disjunction, provided the constraints within disjunctions are convex
[
11
,
13
,
15
]
.

2.3.1
Reformulation of Disjunctions

The Hull Reformulation of disjunctions can be expressed as follows:

\begin{aligned} &\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i\in D_{k}}\mathbf{v}_{ik},\quad k\in K\\
&\left(\text{cl}\,\widetilde{h}_{ik}\right)(\mathbf{v}_{ik},y_{ik})\leq 0,\quad k\in K,\,i\in D_{k}\\
&\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y_{ik}\leq\mathbf{v}_{ik}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y_{ik}\\
&\mathbf{v}_{ik}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad k\in K,\,i\in D_{k}\\
\end{aligned}\qquad\text{(Hull Reformulation)}

(4)

The reformulation introduces disaggregated variables
\mathbf{v}_{ik}
as separate copies of the original variables
\mathbf{x}
, each associated with disjunct
ik
in disjunction
D_{k}
. Constraints
\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y_{ik}\leq\mathbf{v}_{ik}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y_{ik}
ensure that
\mathbf{v}_{ik}=0
when the corresponding binary variable
y_{ik}=0
.
The functions in disjunctions
h_{ik}(\mathbf{x})
are replaced by the closure of the perspective function

\left(\text{cl}\,\widetilde{h}_{ik}\right)(\mathbf{v}_{ik},y_{ik})

[
16
]
,
which in the domain of Hull Reformulation, restricted to
\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y_{ik}\leq\mathbf{v}_{ik}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y_{ik}
, can be expressed in the following way, provided
h(\mathbf{v})
is defined and finite in the domain of reformulation
[
17
,
11
,
18
,
12
]
:

\left(\text{cl}\,\widetilde{h}\right)(\mathbf{v},y)=\begin{cases}yh\left(\frac{\mathbf{v}}{y}\right)&\text{if }y>0\\[5.0pt]
0&\text{if }y=0\\[1.0pt]
\end{cases}

(5)

Clearly, when
y_{ik}=1
, the original constraint
h_{ik}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
is recovered precisely.
On the other hand, when
y_{ik}=0
, the reformulated constraint simplifies to
0\leq 0
, making the constraint trivially satisfied.

2.3.2
Numerical Challenges and
\varepsilon
-approximation

Equation (
4
) outlines the general strategy for handling constraints in Hull Reformulation.
In the case of linear constraints of the form
A\mathbf{x}\leq b
, instead of using the definition of closure of the perspective function, the reformulation of linear constraints can be expressed simply as
A\mathbf{v}\leq by

[
5
]
, which preserves linearity and exactly analytically describes the convex hull of the disjunction.

However, nonlinear constraints present a numerical challenge since the closure of the perspective function is not defined analytically at
y=0
.
To overcome this issue,
\varepsilon
-approximation method (where
\varepsilon
is a small number, e.g.
10^{-4}
) proposed by
[
12
]
is typically employed:

\left(\text{cl}\,\widetilde{h}\right)(\mathbf{x},y)\approx\left((1-\varepsilon)y+\varepsilon\right)h\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}}{(1-\varepsilon)y+\varepsilon}\right)-\varepsilon\,h(0)(1-y)

(6)

Although computationally practical,
\varepsilon
-approximation results in exact Hull Reformulation only when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, and however small, any values
\varepsilon>0
enlarge the feasible region of the continuous relaxation
[
12
]
, potentially weakening solver effectiveness.
Additionally, application of
\varepsilon
-approximation of the closure of the perspective function creates a more complex functional structure compared to the original disjunctive constraints, introducing numerical instabilities, further motivating the development of an exact reformulation approach presented later in this work.

2.3.3
Convex and Non-Convex Constraints

When dealing with convex GDPs, where each disjunctive constraint is convex, the continuous relaxation of Hull Reformulation creates a convex hull of disjuncts in a disjunction
[
11
]
, which is the tightest possible convex relaxation for the original disjunctive structure.
This property is the key theoretical motivation behind the Hull Reformulation and is what makes it particularly attractive.

However, the behavior of the closure of the perspective function at binary values, described above, and thus the validity of reformulation, is independent of convexity, making it a valid reformulation to apply to non-convex GDPs (Appendix
A
).
Applying Hull Reformulation directly to non-convex GDPs results in non-convex MINLP problems that can be solved by global optimization solvers.

Figure
1
illustrates the feasible region of continuous relaxation obtained from applying Hull Reformulation to a non-convex GDP, providing intuitive insight into the relaxation’s structure and its enlargement when using approximations.
However, it should be noted that, since in this case the relaxation is not convex, the solver might need to apply its own techniques to obtain convex relaxations during the solution process to compute lower bounds, meaning that the convex region explored by the solver can be larger compared to the non-convex one obtained after applying the Hull Reformulation.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of the effect of the
\varepsilon
-approximation on the feasible region of the continuous relaxation resulting from the Hull Reformulation applied to a non-convex GDP. The figure compares the exact feasible region with the enlarged region obtained using the
\varepsilon
-approximation, highlighting how the approximation leads to a relaxation that is larger than the exact one and weakens as
\varepsilon
increases. The Big-M relaxation is not shown, as its feasible region would significantly exceed the plotted area, taking up most of the figure at this scale and axis limits.

2.3.4
Trade-offs and Practical Considerations

The Hull Reformulation significantly improves the quality of the continuous relaxation, leading to tighter lower bounds.
However, this comes at the cost of increased model complexity due to the introduction of additional variables and constraints
[
7
]
.
This trade-off between stronger relaxations and higher dimensionality makes it difficult to determine in general whether the hull reformulation will outperform the Big-M reformulation in terms of solution time, and the results vary on a case-by-case basis
[
15
,
3
]
.

2.4
Binary Multiplication Reformulation

One of the simplest and most straightforward methods to transform the GDP problem into a MINLP formulation is the binary multiplication approach.
This method involves directly multiplying each constraint within a disjunct by the binary variable associated with the disjunct, which activates or deactivates the corresponding constraint.
Binary multiplication reformulation of disjunctions can be expressed as:

\begin{aligned} &y_{ik}h_{ik}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0\quad\forall\,k\in K,\,i\in D_{k},\\
\end{aligned}\qquad\text{(Binary Multiplication Reformulation)}

(7)

In this formulation, when the binary variable
y_{ik}=0
, the corresponding constraint becomes
0\leq 0
, which is trivially satisfied. When
y_{ik}=1
, the original constraint
h_{ik}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
is recovered.
Therefore, the disjunctive logic of the original GDP formulation is correctly enforced, and the reformulation is valid.

However, the main drawback of this approach is that the continuous relaxation of the reformulation is often non-convex.
In fact, the continuous relaxation has the same feasible region as the original MINLP with binary values, when projected to
\mathbf{x}
, providing no additional convex relaxation benefit to the solver.
As a result, MINLP solvers must rely on internal convexification techniques to navigate the non-convex feasible space efficiently during the branch-and-bound procedure.

Despite its lack of favorable relaxation properties, the binary multiplication reformulation remains valid and easy to implement.
It can be directly applied to GDP problems and passed to a general MINLP solver, which can handle non-convex problems.

3
Quadratically constrained GDP

In this work, we focus on the hull reformulation of GDPs with disjunctive quadratic constraints.
Disjunctions of quadratically constrained GDP can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i\in D_{k}}\begin{bmatrix}Y_{ik}\\
\mathbf{x}^{\top}Q_{jik}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}_{jik}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+d_{jik}\leq 0,\,j\in J_{ik}\end{bmatrix},\quad k\in K

(8)

where
J_{ik}
is a set of quadratic constraints in a disjunct,
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},d\in\mathbb{R}
.

Application of Hull Reformulation to the quadratically constrained disjunctions, using
\varepsilon
-approximation of the closure of the perspective function shown in Equation (
6
), after algebraic simplification yields:

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{cl}\,\widetilde{h}\right)(\mathbf{v},y)\approx\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q_{jik}\mathbf{v}}{(1-\varepsilon)y+\varepsilon}+\mathbf{c}_{jik}^{\top}\mathbf{v}+d_{jik}y\leq 0,\quad j\in J_{ik},\,k\in K,\,i\in D_{k}

(9)

Thus, it is clear that application of
\varepsilon
-approximation to the quadratically constrained problem results in an MINLP with a more complicated functional structure as the quadratic term is replaced by a fractional term of the form
\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q_{jik}\mathbf{v}}{(1-\varepsilon)y+\varepsilon}
.

4
Proposed Hull Reformulation

Consider a GDP in which the disjunctions contain quadratic constraints of the form

h(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}+d\leq 0,

with continuous relaxation obtained through the standard exact Hull Reformulation.
In lifted space, the feasible set of the continuous relaxation associated with a single constraint is

\displaystyle S_{1}\;=\;\Bigl\{(\mathbf{v},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\mid y\in[0,1],\;\operatorname{cl}\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{v},y)\leq 0,\;\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y\Bigr\},

(10)

where the closure of the perspective function simplifies to

\operatorname{cl}\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{v},y)\;=\;\begin{cases}\dfrac{\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{v}}{y}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{v}+dy,&y>0,\\[6.0pt]
0,&y=0.\end{cases}

(11)

Multiplying the function
\dfrac{\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{v}}{y}+c^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{v}+dy
by
y
motivates the alternative
set:

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\mathbf{v},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\mid y\in[0,1],\;\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{v})y+dy^{2}\leq 0,\;\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y\right\}

(12)

We demonstrate the equivalence of these two sets in the following proposition:

Proposition 1
.

For a quadratic constraint
h(\mathbf{v})\;=\;\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}Q\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{v}+d\leq 0,
define the sets

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\mathbf{v},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\,\middle|\,y\in[0,1],\;\operatorname{cl}\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{v},y)\leq 0,\;\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y\right\}

where

\displaystyle\left(\text{cl}\,\widetilde{h}\right)(\mathbf{v},y)=\begin{cases}\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}}{y}+\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{v}+dy&\text{if }y>0\\[5.0pt]
0&\text{if }y=0\\[1.0pt]
\end{cases}

and

S_{2}=\left\{(\mathbf{v},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\mid y\in[0,1],\;\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{v})y+dy^{2}\leq 0,\;\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y\right\}

Then, the two sets coincide:
S_{1}=S_{2}

Proof.

Case
y>0
:

Since
y
is positive, the constraint
\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}}{y}+\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{v}+dy\leq 0
can be multiplied by
y
to yield a constraint with the same feasible region:

\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}}{y}+\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{v}+dy\leq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{v})y+dy^{2}\leq 0,\quad\text{when }y>0.

Case
y=0
:

The constraints
\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y
force

\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
at
y=0
.
Substituting
(\mathbf{0},0)
into the

\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{v})y+dy^{2}
yields 0, identical to

\operatorname{cl}\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{0},0)
.
Thus, the feasible points with

y=0
are the same in both sets.

Since the feasible regions coincide in both subcases, the equivalence
S_{1}=S_{2}
is established.
∎

Hence, the Hull Reformulation for quadratically constrained GDP problems can be exactly expressed using set
S_{2}
for each quadratic constraint, avoiding the need for approximations and maintaining quadratic functional structure in the reformulation.
Consequently, when applying Hull Reformulation it is proposed to use
S_{2}
for any disjunctive quadratic constraints. In this case, the exact Hull Reformulation of quadratically constrained disjunctions shown in Equation (
8
) can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i\in D_{k}}\mathbf{v}_{ik},\quad k\in K

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{ik}^{\top}Q_{jik}\mathbf{v}_{ik}+\mathbf{c}^{\top}_{jik}\mathbf{v}_{ik}y_{ik}+d_{jik}y_{ik}^{2}\leq 0,\quad j\in J_{ik},\,k\in K,\,i\in D_{k}

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y_{ik}\leq\mathbf{v}_{ik}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y_{ik}

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{ik}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad k\in K,\,i\in D_{k}

The proposed exact reformulation provides two major advantages over
\varepsilon
-approximation approaches.
First, since it is exact, it yields a tighter continuous relaxation, improving solver performance by delivering stronger lower bounds.
Additionally, it preserves the quadratic structure of the problem, thereby enhancing computational efficiency and eliminating numerical instabilities associated with the
\varepsilon
-approximation.
This structural preservation enables the use of advancements in Mixed-Integer Quadratically Constrained Programming (MIQCP) solvers when the original problem is quadratically constrained, which is not the case when
\varepsilon
-approximation is used, which alters the problem structure.
Thus, we believe that the exact Hull Reformulation presented herein is preferable for any GDP problems involving quadratic constraints.

The proposed reformulation generalizes earlier results for specific quadratic constraints, such as
y\geq x^{2}

[
18
]
, and for second-order cone–representable quadratic functions
[
13
]
, applicable to convex quadratic function, and extends the approach to arbitrary quadratic functions, including non-convex ones.

Additionally, we note that this approach can be further generalized to polynomial constraints of arbitrary degree, enabling the construction of a Hull Reformulation in which the resulting expressions retain the same polynomial degree (Appendix
B
).
Alternatively, polynomial constraints can be reformulated into an equivalent problem with quadratic constraints using established techniques
[
19
]
, to which the proposed exact Hull Reformulation for quadratic constraints can be applied.
Thus, using either strategy, the proposed exact Hull Reformulation approach can be extended to polynomial constraints of any degree.

Convex Quadratically Constrained GDP

Convex GDP usually refers to a problem in which the objective function and all constraints in the original GDP formulation are convex.
Specifically, for quadratically constrained GDPs, convexity implies that the matrices
Q
associated with quadratic constraints are positive semi-definite (
Q\succeq 0
).

If
Q\succeq 0
then
S_{1}
is convex and therefore
S_{2}
is also convex set by Proposition
1
, even though
\mathbf{v}^{\top}Qv+(c^{\top}\mathbf{v})y+d\,y^{2}
may not be a convex function in
(\mathbf{v},y)
.
Some solvers detect convexity via the eigenvalue analysis of each quadratic expression and thus they may misclassify
S_{2}
as non-convex, depending on the values of
\mathbf{c}
and
d
.

If for convex quadratically constrained GDP it is desired to obtain a reformulation, that contains only convex functions, easily verified by eigenvalue analysis, it is possible to apply known convexification techniques to do so.
Consider the following alternative convex set
S_{3}
, derived from set
S_{2}
by relaxing the bilinear term
\mathbf{v}y
through Glover’s linearization
[
20
]
and by substitution of
dy^{2}
with
dy
, which is equivalent at binary
y
, to avoid non-convex function for
d<0
:

\displaystyle S_{3}=\Big\{(\mathbf{v},y,\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\Big|\;y\in[0,1],\;\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{z}+dy\leq 0,\;\mathbf{z}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y,\;\mathbf{z}\geq\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y,\;

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}\leq\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{x}^{u}(1-y),\;\mathbf{z}\geq\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{x}^{\ell}(1-y),\;\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y\Big\}

The transformed formulation utilizing
S_{3}
maintains exact equivalence at binary values of
y
, ensures convexity of the functions, and can be verified by convexity detection methods.
However, this explicitly convex reformulation may compromise solution efficiency because of the increased number of variables and constraints, as well as a potentially weaker continuous relaxation.

The set
S_{3}
is included primarily to demonstrate that explicitly convex formulations are achievable and to illustrate one possible method by which solvers might internally manage the non-convex constraint
\mathbf{v}^{\top}Q\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{v})y+dy^{2}\leq 0
, when
Q\succeq 0
.

However, this approach can be less efficient because it weakens the continuous relaxation and increases computational overhead.
Ideally, convexity detection techniques used by solvers should be applied directly to the original constraints in the GDP formulation, and the knowledge that certain constraints in the reformulation or the entire problem are convex should be retained and passed to a solver.
This could eliminate unnecessary convexification steps or redundant relaxations that a solver may perform, thereby improving its computational performance.
Unfortunately, most current solvers lack the capability to flag individual constraints or entire models as convex in this way, highlighting an area for improvement in solver design.

We believe that if solvers could recognize or be informed of the convexity of constraints mentioned above, or at least accept information indicating that the entire MINLP is convex when all disjunctive constraints are convex, the computational efficiency of the proposed exact Hull Reformulation for convex GDPs could be additionally improved compared to the results presented in this study.

6
Computational experiments

The primary objective of the computational experiments presented in this paper is to evaluate the performance of the proposed exact Hull Reformulation in comparison to
\varepsilon
-approximation method, which is usually used for any nonlinear constraints.
Although the theoretical foundations of the Hull Reformulation have been developed for convex GDPs
[
16
,
11
]
, and most existing literature concentrates on convex GDP formulations!
[
13
,
11
,
7
]
, as highlighted above, Hull Reformulation is a valid approach to apply to both convex and non-convex problems.
Consequently, this study investigates both convex and non-convex GDPs to provide a comprehensive comparison.

The existing literature on non-convex GDPs proposes convexifying the constraints within each disjunct prior to applying techniques such as Hull Reformulation or Big-M to obtain a convex MINLP
[
21
]
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, no prior work has conducted a comparative analysis between the direct application of the Hull Reformulation and initial constraint convexification.
This represents a compelling direction for future research, although it lies beyond the scope of this paper.
Similarly, a thorough discussion of the advantages and disadvantages of alternative reformulation methods, such as the Big-M and Binary Multiplication approaches, compared to the Hull Reformulation, especially for non-convex problems, is reserved for future studies.
However, the performance results are presented here to provide a comprehensive context.

In this section, we present results of computational experiments for various quadratically constrained GDPs, which were solved using various state-of-the-art general MINLP solvers after applying different reformulation approaches, such as
\varepsilon
-approximation Hull Reformulation, proposed exact Hull Reformulation, Big-M, and Binary Multiplication.
All test problems were initially formulated as GDP and reformulated to MINLP using
Pyomo
(version 6.9.2)
[

]
.
As part of this work, we implemented exact Hull Reformulation for quadratic constraints within

[

All computational experiments used the default
Pyomo

\varepsilon=10^{-4}
for
\varepsilon
-approximation approach.

The resulting MINLP formulations were subsequently solved using

(version 49.1.0)

Random Instances

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed Hull Reformulation comprehensively, we conducted computational experiments using randomly generated instances of quadratically constrained GDPs, with quadratic disjunctive constraints, no global constraints, and a quadratic objective function.
These problems featured dense matrices and vectors of coefficients, meaning all coefficients were populated with nonzero values.
Coefficients were randomly generated from a uniform distribution over the interval

For the convex problem instances, eigenvalues of the generated random matrices

, and the number of explicitly guaranteed feasible points.
In the experiments carried out, each instance included 10 randomly generated points explicitly ensured to be feasible by adjusting the constant

A total of 240 convex instances were generated with parameters in the following ranges:

3\leq|K|\leq 10,\;10\leq|D_{k}|\leq 15,\;|J_{ik}|=10,\;3\leq n\leq 9
.

In summary, computational experiments on randomly generated quadratically constrained GDPs demonstrate that the proposed exact Hull Reformulation offers not only improved computational efficiency but also enhanced numerical stability, resulting in fewer solver failures and fewer incorrect solutions compared to the

6.2
Continuously Stirred Tank Reactor network problem

Consider the following superstructure optimization problem involving a network of Continuously Stirred Tank Reactors (CSTRs).
The objective is to minimize the total volume of CSTRs in series, in which the autocatalytic reaction
A+B\rightarrow 2B
takes place.
The superstructure allows a recycle stream originating from the last reactor to be returned to any preceding reactor within the series.
An illustration of this problem is provided in Figure
6
.
The details and formulations of the problem were previously described in the literature
[
28
,
9
]
and are summarized in Appendix
C
.

Originally, the constraints defining this optimization problem were linear or cubic.
Since any polynomial constraint can be systematically represented using quadratic constraints, for the purposes of this work, cubic constraints are reformulated into equivalent quadratic forms, using quadratic equality constraints.
Consequently, due to the presence of quadratic equality constraints, the resulting GDP formulation is inherently non-convex.
Therefore, this problem allows for testing reformulations in an application, where constraints are linear and non-convex quadratic.
This structure makes the problem particularly suitable for evaluating reformulation strategies in applications where constraints are both linear and non-convex quadratic.

Figure 6
:
Superstructure representation for the continuously stirred tank reactor (CSTR) network problem.
The configuration includes
N_{T}
reactor stages arranged in series, where each stage can either be a reactor or a bypass.
The flow proceeds from left to right, and a recycle stream from the last reactor is allowed to return to any upstream stage.
This flexible structure enables the optimizer to choose the optimal number of reactors and location of recycle to minimize the total reactor volume.
[
28
,
9
]
.

Computational experiments were conducted to evaluate solution times for the CSTR network optimization problem under various reformulation strategies.
In these experiments, the maximum permissible number of reactors in the network was varied, keeping all other model inputs unchanged.

summarizes the computational performance obtained in this work.
The clearest difference between reformulations was seen when problems were solved using
BARON
.
As shown in Figure

, the exact Hull Reformulation performed similarly or notably better for all problems in terms of solution time compared to the
\varepsilon
-approximation when solved using
BARON
.
In several instances, the proposed exact formulation provided faster solutions compared to all other methods tested.

6.3
k
-Means Clustering

The
k
-means clustering problem is a classic unsupervised learning task in which
|N|
data points in
|D|
dimensions are partitioned into
|K|
clusters in a way that minimizes the total squared Euclidean distance from each point to its assigned cluster center.
Although it is typically solved using heuristics, the problem can also be formulated as a convex GDP with linear and quadratic constraints
[
29
]
and solved to global optimality with established reformulation and optimization techniques.
This makes it a suitable benchmark problem within the GDP framework
[
13
,
15
]
.

The GDP formulation of the
k
-means clustering problem can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d},Y}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}d_{i}

(15)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle c_{k-1,1}\leq c_{k,1},\quad k\in\{2,\ldots,|K|\}

\displaystyle\bigvee_{k\in K}\left[Y_{ik}\land\left(d_{i}\geq\sum_{j\in D}(p_{ij}-c_{kj})^{2}\right)\right],\quad i\in N

\displaystyle\underset{k\in K}{\underline{\bigvee}}Y_{ik},\quad i\in N

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{|N|}

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{|K|\times|D|}

\displaystyle Y_{ik}\in\{\text{False},\text{True}\},\quad i\in N,k\in K

Here,
p_{ij}
is the coordinate of point
i
in dimension
j
,
c_{kj}
is the coordinate of cluster center
k
in dimension
j
, and
d_{i}
is the squared distance from point
i
to its assigned center.
The constraint
c_{k-1,1}\leq c_{k,1}
is a symmetry-breaking condition that ensures consistent cluster ordering.

We generated 96 instances of this problem with parameters in the following ranges:
3\leq|K|\leq 5
,
10\leq|N|\leq 17
, and
2\leq|D|\leq 5
.
The coordinates of the points were generated randomly by sampling a uniform distribution in the interval
[-1,1]
.

Figure
8
presents the cumulative number of instances solved as a function of wall‑clock time for each reformulation across the three solvers.
In many instances, particularly those solved with
SCIP
, the time limit was reached, so the profiles are supplemented with cumulative plots showing the absolute optimality gap (the difference between the upper and lower bounds) for these cases. Figure
9
provides a direct comparison of solution times for the same instances across reformulations.
A summary of solver outcomes is provided in Table

.

Across all solvers, the proposed exact Hull Reformulation consistently showed improved numerical stability and outperformed the
\varepsilon
-approximation in terms of the number of instances solved correctly within the time limit.
For
SCIP
and
Gurobi
, most instances were solved faster with the proposed approach.
However, for
BARON
, the
\varepsilon
-approximation achieved faster solve times for the subset of instances where both approaches converged to the correct solution (Figure

).
However, only 38 out of 96 instances solved using the

-approximation yielded correct solutions within the time limit, compared to 64 for the proposed reformulation.

We hypothesize that the improved performance of the

-approximation for this particular problem in

may be due to internal convexity detection or solver-specific optimizations tailored to the conventional reformulation and this problem type. Nonetheless, even for
BARON
from an overall standpoint, the proposed reformulation produced fewer incorrect solutions, and thus more problems solved in the given time limit, affirming its robustness and superior practical value.

These results further demonstrate the advantages of the proposed method for convex GDP formulations involving quadratic constraints such as those arising in

-means clustering.

6.4
Constrained layout problem

The constrained layout involves placing a set of non-overlapping rectangular components within a bounded area to minimize a given objective, the total cost of connections, while satisfying geometric and containment constraints.
This problem can be modeled as a convex GDP with both linear and quadratic constraints and is frequently used as a benchmark in GDP literature
[
30
,
12
,
13
]
.

In the version of the problem considered here, rectangular 2D components must be positioned within one of several enclosing circular regions, such that the rectangles do not overlap and the total pairwise connection cost is minimized.
The objective function employs either an
\ell_{1}
or
\ell_{2}
norm to penalize distances between rectangles.

The GDP formulation can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\delta_{x},\delta_{y},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j\in N,\,i<j}c_{ij}\cdot\|(\delta_{x_{ij}},\;\delta_{y_{ij}})\|_{p}

s.t.

\displaystyle\delta_{x_{ij}}\geq x_{i}-x_{j}\quad

\displaystyle i,j\in N,\,i<j

\displaystyle\delta_{x_{ij}}\geq x_{j}-x_{i}\quad

\displaystyle i,j\in N,\,i<j

\displaystyle\delta_{y_{ij}}\geq y_{i}-y_{j}\quad

\displaystyle i,j\in N,\,i<j

\displaystyle\delta_{y_{ij}}\geq y_{j}-y_{i}\quad

\displaystyle i,j\in N,\,i<j

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Y_{ij}^{1}\\
x_{i}+L_{i}/2\leq x_{j}-L_{j}/2\end{array}\right]\vee\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Y_{ij}^{2}\\
x_{j}+L_{j}/2\leq x_{i}-L_{i}/2\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\vee\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Y_{ij}^{3}\\
y_{i}+H_{i}/2\leq y_{j}-H_{j}/2\end{array}\right]\vee\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Y_{ij}^{4}\\
y_{j}+H_{j}/2\leq y_{i}-H_{i}/2\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle i,j\in N,\,i<j

\displaystyle\bigvee_{t\in T}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}W_{it}\\
(x_{i}+L_{i}/2-x_{ct})^{2}+(y_{i}+H_{i}/2-y_{ct})^{2}\leq r_{t}^{2}\\
(x_{i}+L_{i}/2-x_{ct})^{2}+(y_{i}-H_{i}/2-y_{ct})^{2}\leq r_{t}^{2}\\
(x_{i}-L_{i}/2-x_{ct})^{2}+(y_{i}+H_{i}/2-y_{ct})^{2}\leq r_{t}^{2}\\
(x_{i}-L_{i}/2-x_{ct})^{2}+(y_{i}-H_{i}/2-y_{ct})^{2}\leq r_{t}^{2}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle i\in N

\displaystyle Y_{ij}^{1}\underline{\vee}Y_{ij}^{2}\underline{\vee}Y_{ij}^{3}\underline{\vee}Y_{ij}^{4}\quad

\displaystyle i,j\in N,\,i<j

\displaystyle\underset{t\in T}{\underline{\bigvee}}W_{it},\quad

\displaystyle i\in N

\displaystyle x_{i}^{l}\leq x_{i}\leq x_{i}^{u}\quad

\displaystyle i\in N

\displaystyle y_{i}^{l}\leq y_{i}\leq y_{i}^{u}\quad

\displaystyle i\in N

\displaystyle\delta_{x_{ij}},\delta_{y_{ij}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\quad

\displaystyle i,j\in N,\,i<j

\displaystyle x_{i},y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\quad

\displaystyle i\in N

\displaystyle Y_{ij}^{1},Y_{ij}^{2},Y_{ij}^{3},Y_{ij}^{4}\in\{\text{False},\text{True}\}\quad

\displaystyle i,j\in N,\,i<j

\displaystyle W_{it}\in\{\text{False},\text{True}\}\quad

\displaystyle i\in N,\,t\in T

Here,
N
is the set of rectangles. The variables
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
denote the coordinates of the center of rectangle
i
, while
\delta_{x_{ij}}
and
\delta_{y_{ij}}
represent the horizontal and vertical distances between rectangles
i
and
j
.
The parameters
L_{i}
and
H_{i}
denote the width and height of rectangle
i
, respectively.

The set
T
contains all enclosing circles, each with center coordinates
(x_{ct},y_{ct})
and radius
r_{t}
. The binary variables
Y_{ij}^{t}
encode the relative position of rectangles
i
and
j
to enforce non-overlap, while
W_{it}
indicates whether rectangle
i
is assigned to circle
t
.
The coefficient
c_{ij}
is the cost of the distance between connected rectangle pairs in the objective.

The objective function minimizes the total pairwise connection cost based on the
\ell_{p}
-norm of the distance between rectangle centers, where
p\in\{1,2\}
. Therefore, depending on the value of
p
, the objective function takes the form:

\min\sum_{i,j\in N,\,i<j}c_{ij}\cdot\begin{cases}\delta_{x_{ij}}+\delta_{y_{ij}}&\text{if }p=1\\
\sqrt{\delta_{x_{ij}}^{2}+\delta_{y_{ij}}^{2}}&\text{if }p=2\end{cases}

To assess reformulation performance, we tested six instances of this problem implemented in the
Pyomo.GDP
example library
3
3

https://github.com/Pyomo/pyomo/tree/main/examples/gdp/constrained_layout
using both
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
norm.

CLay0305

\ell_{2}

CLay0304
using
\ell_{1}

.

For

In contrast, for

Conclusions

This paper proposed a strategy to form an exact Hull Reformulation for any quadratic constraints in GDP problems, effectively addressing the drawbacks associated with the traditional
\varepsilon
-approximation of the closure of the perspective function method.
By preserving the quadratic functional structure of constraints and providing an exact reformulation without approximations, the proposed approach improves solver performance, numerical stability, and reliability.

Theoretical results establish that the proposed reformulation is equivalent to the original exact Hull Reformulation, yielding an optimization problem with an identical feasible region.
While the conventional exact Hull Reformulation is not expressed in a closed analytical form, our approach provides an analytically defined reformulation that is exact for quadratic functions and therefore directly implementable in practice.

Extensive computational experiments demonstrated that the proposed exact Hull Reformulation approach outperformed the
\varepsilon
-approximation approach in various problem types, including random GDP instances, CSTR network optimization,
k
-means clustering, and constrained layout problems.
The results highlighted not only a generally improved solution time, but also a reduction in numerical instabilities and solver failures to provide the correct result.

Furthermore, it is expected that the results obtained could be further improved if the convexity detection methods of the solvers could recognize certain reformulations of the quadratically constrained GDPs as convex, or if the solvers could directly utilize the convexity information obtained before the reformulation.
This suggests an area for future development to improve the solvers’ performance.

Several future research directions emerge from this work.
First, the performance of the extension of the exact Hull Reformulation to polynomial constraints of higher degree could be thoroughly evaluated, as we did for quadratic constraints in this work.
Second, a systematic evaluation could be conducted to compare the direct application of Hull Reformulation to non-convex GDPs against strategies that first convexify the constraints.
Third, it would be valuable to compare the performance of the Hull Reformulation with alternative reformulation methods, such as Big-M or Binary Multiplication, when these are applied directly to non-convex GDPs.

Overall, the findings of this study strongly support the adoption of the exact Hull Reformulation as the standard approach for GDP optimization problems involving quadratic constraints.

Appendix A
From Binary Multiplication to the Hull Reformulation

We outline how the Hull Reformulation for a single disjunction can be systematically derived from the simpler Binary Multiplication reformulation by taking a convex combination of disjuncts.
This derivation does not rely on convexity assumptions, demonstrating that the Hull Reformulation is applicable to both convex and non-convex GDPs.
It also offers additional insight into the interpretation of the reformulation when applied to non-convex problems.
This appendix is adapted from Appendix A of
[
31
]
.

Consider one disjunction

\displaystyle\bigvee_{D}\begin{bmatrix}Y_{i}\\
h_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\underset{i\in D}{\underline{\bigvee}}Y_{i}

where every
h_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is proper (defined and finite on its domain) and continuously
extended at the boundary of the feasible region, and where continuous variables are bounded:

\mathbf{x}^{\ell}\leq\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}
.

Binary Multiplication reformulation

The Binary Multiplication reformulation of the disjunction, shown above, activates the constraints by multiplying it with the corresponding binary
y_{i}
:

\displaystyle y_{i}\,h_{i}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\leq 0

\displaystyle\forall\;i\in D,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in D}y_{i}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle\in\{0,1\}\quad\forall\;i\in D.

Because
y_{i}=1
restores
h_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
and
y_{i}=0
makes the constraint trivially satisfied, the reformulation is exact for binary
y
, and therefore valid.
However, its continuous relaxation (
y_{i}\in[0,1]
) provides the same feasible region as the MINLP problem, when projected to
\mathbf{x}
, hence it offers no relaxation advantages and the continuous relaxation problem is equivalent to the original.

Taking a convex combination

Let
\mathbf{x}_{i}
be a copy of the original variable associated with the feasible region of disjunct
i
. We now form a convex combination of the disjuncts, which yields the convex hull of the disjunction if all
h_{i}(\mathbf{x})
are convex.
Note, however, that convex combinations can also be taken for non-convex sets, but the resulting set will not, in general, be convex, which is illustrated in Figure
1
.
Thus, these steps remain valid for non-convex problems, although they no longer guarantee the convex hull of the disjunction.
The convex combination of disjuncts can then be defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i\in D}y_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i},

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in D}y_{i}=1,

\displaystyle y_{i}\,h_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\leq 0

\displaystyle\forall\;i\in D,

\displaystyle y_{i}\in[0,1]

Hull Reformulation

Let
\mathbf{v}_{i}=y_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a disaggregated copy, scaled by
y_{i}
. Because
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is bounded by
\mathbf{x}^{\ell}\leq\mathbf{x}_{i}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}
,
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is bounded by
\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y_{i}\leq\mathbf{v}_{i}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y_{i}
.

The constraint function
y_{i}\,h_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
that defines the feasible region of each disjunct reduces to zero when
y_{i}=0
, since
h_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
is assumed to be finite. When
y_{i}>0
, we can apply the change of variables
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{v}_{i}}{y_{i}}
. Also it should be noted that
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i\in D}y_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\sum_{i\in D}\mathbf{v}_{i}

Collecting all constraints yields the Hull Reformulation of the disjunction:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i\in D_{k}}\mathbf{v}_{i},

\displaystyle\left(\text{cl}\,\widetilde{h}_{i}\right)(\mathbf{v}_{i},y_{i})\leq 0,,i\in D

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y_{i}\leq\mathbf{v}_{i}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y_{i}

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},i\in D

where in the domain of reformulation

\left(\text{cl}\,\widetilde{h}\right)(\mathbf{v},y)=\begin{cases}yh\left(\frac{\mathbf{v}}{y}\right)&\text{if }y>0\\[5.0pt]
0&\text{if }y=0\\[1.0pt]
\end{cases}

Thus, Hull Reformulation can be systematically obtained from Binary Multiplication by taking a convex combination of disjuncts in lifted space.
For non-convex
h_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, the construction remains valid, but the continuous relaxation is no longer guaranteed to be convex.

Appendix B
Extension of the Exact Hull Reformulation to Polynomial Constraints of Degree
d

Consider a polynomial disjunctive constraint
h(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
, such that:

h(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}(\mathbf{x}),

where a scalar polynomial of total degree
d\geq 2
, expressed as a sum of its homogeneous components (parts of the polynomial consisting of all monomials of the same total degree)

p_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}
of degree
k
(
p_{0}
is the constant term).

The continuous relaxation of the standard Hull Reformulation for this polynomial constraint introduces the following set in the lifted space:

S_{d}^{(1)}\;=\;\Bigl\{(\mathbf{v},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\times\![0,1]\;\bigm|\;\operatorname{cl}\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{v},y)\leq 0,\;\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y\Bigr\},

where the closure of the perspective function in the domain of the reformulation can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\bigl(\operatorname{cl}\widetilde{h}\bigr)(\mathbf{v},y)\;=\;\begin{cases}y\,h\!\bigl(\mathbf{v}/y\bigr),&y>0,\\[2.0pt]
0,&y=0.\end{cases}

which, in the case of polynomial constraints, simplifies to:

\displaystyle\bigl(\operatorname{cl}\widetilde{h}\bigr)(\mathbf{v},y)\;=\;\begin{cases}\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}(\mathbf{v})\,y^{\,1-k},&y>0,\\[2.0pt]
0,&y=0.\end{cases}

Multiplying
\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}(\mathbf{v})\,y^{\,1-k}
by
y^{\,d-1}
motivates the alternative set

S_{d}^{(2)}\;=\;\Bigl\{(\mathbf{v},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\times\![0,1]\;\bigm|\;\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}(\mathbf{v})\,y^{\,d-k}\leq 0,\;\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y\Bigr\}.

We demonstrate the equivalence of these two sets in the following proposition:

Proposition 2
.

For any polynomial constraint
h(\mathbf{v})\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}(\mathbf{v})\leq 0
of degree
d\!\geq\!2
, define the sets

S_{d}^{(1)}\;=\;\Bigl\{(\mathbf{v},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\times\![0,1]\;\bigm|\;\operatorname{cl}\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{v},y)\leq 0,\;\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y\Bigr\},

where

\displaystyle\bigl(\operatorname{cl}\widetilde{h}\bigr)(\mathbf{v},y)\;=\;\begin{cases}\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}(\mathbf{v})\,y^{\,1-k},&y>0,\\[2.0pt]
0,&y=0.\end{cases}

and

S_{d}^{(2)}\;=\;\Bigl\{(\mathbf{v},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\times\![0,1]\;\bigm|\;\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}(\mathbf{v})\,y^{\,d-k}\leq 0,\;\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y\Bigr\}.

Then,
the two sets coincide:
\displaystyle S_{d}^{(1)}=S_{d}^{(2)}
.

Proof.

Case 1,
y>0
:

Because
y^{d-1}>0
, multiplying the defining inequality of

S_{d}^{(1)}
by
y^{d-1}
preserves the feasible region and produces the
polynomial inequality that defines
S_{d}^{(2)}
.
Hence
S_{d}^{(1)}\cap\{y>0\}=S_{d}^{(2)}\cap\{y>0\}
.

Case 2:
y=0
:

From the constraints
\mathbf{x}^{\ell}y\leq\mathbf{v}\leq\mathbf{x}^{u}y

it follows that
y=0
implies
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
.
Substituting
(\mathbf{v},y)=(\mathbf{0},0)
into the polynomial expression
gives
\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}(\mathbf{0})\,0^{d-k}=0
, matching

\operatorname{cl}\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{0},0)=0
.
Therefore, the two sets also coincide on
\{y=0\}
.

Since both cases produce identical feasible regions, the sets are equal.
∎

Therefore, when applying Hull Reformulation, any polynomial disjunctive constraint

\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}(\mathbf{x})\leq 0
, can be reformulated in a way that forms exact hull and preserves polynomial structure as
\sum_{k=0}^{d}p_{k}(\mathbf{v})\,y^{\,d-k}\leq 0
(sum of monomials of degree
d
), instead of using the closure of the perspective function or its
\varepsilon
‐approximation.

As with the quadratic case (
d=2
), addressed in the paper, the reformulation of polynomials for
d\geq 2

(i)
is exact, guaranteeing a strictly tighter continuous relaxation than
\varepsilon
‐approximation, when
\varepsilon>0

(ii)
retains the original polynomial degree, preserving
algebraic structure of the constraints, and

(iii)
avoids potential numerical instabilities that arise from dividing by small values in
\varepsilon
‐approximations of the closure of the perspective function.
