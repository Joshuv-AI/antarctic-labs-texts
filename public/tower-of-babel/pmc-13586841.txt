Introduction

Commotio cordis is a rare, potentially fatal arrhythmic event precipitated by blunt trauma to the chest, typically during sports, in the absence of structural heart disease. The term, derived from Latin meaning ‘agitation of the heart’, reflects the mechanism in which a precisely timed impact during the vulnerable phase of ventricular repolarization induces ventricular fibrillation. While most reported cases occur in paediatric or adolescent populations, adult presentations remain less common. We report a case of commotio cordis in a 25-year-old competitive hurling player, highlighting the clinical presentation, comprehensive evaluation, and favourable long-term outcome.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old male athlete with no past medical history or known cardiac conditions, and no family history of sudden cardiac death or other relevant cardiac disorders, collapsed during an intermediate hurling championship match after a high-speed collision in which an opponent’s knee struck his anterior chest (panel A). Immediate assessment revealed unresponsiveness and absence of a pulse. Bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was initiated, and an automated external defibrillator (AED) was applied. The AED detected ventricular fibrillation (VF), and spontaneous circulation was restored after 1× shock within 4 minutes.

After return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), the patient was transferred to the nearest hospital, where a 12-lead ECG revealed sinus rhythm with normal conduction and a normal QTc interval. Serial troponin T levels were mildly elevated (66, 80, 42, and 45 ng/L), consistent with a degree of myocardial injury, likely secondary to VF rather than ischaemia.

Extensive evaluation excluded structural or inherited cardiac disease. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed normal left ventricular systolic function without evidence of cardiomyopathy. Computed tomography coronary angiography demonstrated normal coronary arteries. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging showed high-normal left ventricular volumes with preserved systolic function, consistent with athletic remodelling, and no late gadolinium enhancement, excluding infarction, fibrosis, or myocarditis. STIR imaging demonstrated a focal area of increased signal in the basal inferior wall, likely representing oedema secondary to VF. Invasive electrophysiological studies, including programmed ventricular stimulation and ajmaline challenge, were normal. Continuous telemetry for 10 days showed persistent sinus rhythm without arrhythmias.

At 2-year follow-up, the patient remains asymptomatic, with serial echocardiograms demonstrating normal findings, and a repeat CMR demonstrated resolution of focal area of increased signal in the basal inferior wall. He has resumed competitive hurling without restrictions.

Discussion

Commotio cordis is a rare cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes, alongside other conditions. It is most frequently described in paediatric populations, but adult presentations, particularly in high-impact sports, are increasingly recognized. It is hypothesized that young children are more susceptible due to the pliability of their chest wall.2

This event occurs when blunt chest trauma, precisely timed during the upslope of the T-wave, induces ventricular fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia. It is theorized that trauma to the chest wall causes premature ventricular depolarization during this narrow interval of the cardiac cycle, through activation of the stretch sensitive K + ATP channels, leading to ventricular arrhythmias2 (Panel B and C).

Classical commotio cordis is defined as near immediate cardiac arrest resulting from a witnessed blow to the precordium, with ventricular fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia recorded at the time of arrest. As it is a diagnosis of exclusion, other causes for cardiac arrest have to be excluded and the absence of underlying structural heart disease has to be confirmed. Key management principles include immediate recognition, standard cardiac arrest guidelines and early defibrillation. After return of spontaneous circulation patients should be transferred to hospital for comprehensive post-arrest evaluation to exclude alternative aetiologies, particularly in adults, where underlying cardiomyopathies or channelopathies are more prevalent. As there is no evidence of commotio cordis increasing future arrhythmic risk, ICD implantation is not recommended.2 Return to sport is assessed on a case-by-case basis.

Our case demonstrates classic features: A previously healthy adult with no personal or family history of cardiac disease, cardiac arrest temporally related to a precordial impact, and rapid restoration of circulation via AED. Comprehensive evaluation excluded structural, ischaemic, or inherited arrhythmogenic causes. Mild troponin elevation likely reflected secondary myocardial injury. Long-term follow-up over 2 years confirmed preserved cardiac function and safe return to competitive sport.

In light of the confirmed diagnosis of true commotio cordis in our patient, the assured reversibility of the condition, the isolated nature of the event in a long-term competitive athlete, and the patient's adult age with a less pliable chest wall, a shared decision was reached between the medical team and the patient to allow return to play, with ongoing regular medical follow-up.

Literature review of commotio cordis cases revealed a handful number of cases reported as ‘commotio cordis’, with some of them showing lack of evidence and/or classical presentation. Of note, commotion cordis might act as a trigger in patients with underlying cardiac conditions.

A case of commotio cordis in a Norwegian premier football league player was published in 2011. He had an impact to the chest wall from another athlete’s knee while jumping for the ball, with him becoming pulseless soon after. No defibrillator was used at the time and no arrhythmia was recorded, but ROSC was obtained after two precordial thumps during CPR. He was discharged from hospital the following day after a normal biochemistry, ECGs and echo in hospital.3 In this case, the absence of an identifiable rhythm, as well as lack of detailed CMR assessment to investigate structural abnormalities might challenge the diagnosis of commotio cordis.

In a case from Sweden published in 2017, a 25-year old had an impact by a tennis ball to the chest and went into cardiac arrest. While ROSC was obtained after two defibrillation attempts, subsequent ECGs showed atrial flutter requiring cardioversion, and a trifascicular block requiring pacemaker insertion. The fact that the arrhythmias persisted after ROSC makes the diagnosis uncertain.4

In another case from Ireland, a 13-year old went into cardiac arrest after precordial impact from a hurling ball. ROSC was obtained after one defibrillation from an AED. However, the patient developed multiple episodes of non-sustained ventricular tachycardia that required esmolol for termination while in hospital.5 This case also included persistent arrhythmia, which is not usually seen in true commotio cordis.

A case from Austria was published in 2023, where a 32-year-old man suffered a cardiac arrest after being hit by a soccer ball to the thorax. Ventricular tachycardia was identified and ROSC was obtained after three defibrillation shocks. Despite no abnormalities on cardiac work up, an ICD was implanted. On different occasions, a few years after the device insertion, episodes of ventricular tachycardia were recorded. Sixteen years after the initial presentation, successful VT epicardial ablation was performed.6 Also, in this case, the typical diagnostic criteria of commotio cordis were not met as multiple VT events took place with no relation to any traumatic events.

A few cases were reported in Forensics Journals where commotio cordis was identified as the cause of death; however, all the cases were diagnosed during post mortem autopsies.7,8

To our knowledge, only two published cases fulfil all the criteria for commotio cordis. One being a 25-year-old Australian man that went into cardiac arrest shortly after being hit with a cricket ball to the precordium. On arrival, he was found to be in ventricular fibrillation. ROSC was obtained after one shock. Echo, stress test, and cardiac MRI were all normal in hospital. The patient returned to work as an engineer 2 weeks later and no further events were recorded.9 Another one being a 11-year-old girl who went into cardiac arrest after being punched to the chest by her brother. ROSC was obtained after one shock and she made a full recovery.10

In conclusion, our case highlights commotio cordis as a reversible cause of sudden cardiac arrest in adult athletes, emphasizes the importance of rapid defibrillation, and demonstrates that affected athletes may safely return to competitive sports following thorough evaluation and monitoring.

A well-known example highlighting the critical importance of early CPR and defibrillation, albeit in a different clinical context, occurred during UEFA Euro 2020, when Christian Eriksen suffered a cardiac arrest on the football pitch. Following prompt resuscitation, he experienced no long-term neurological sequelae and was able to safely return to professional football.

Our case occurred during a hurling game that is a traditional Irish field sport characterized by high-speed play and physical contact using wooden sticks (hurleys) and a small ball (sliotar). Although predominantly played in Ireland, the sport is also played competitively in Irish communities and Gaelic Athletic Association clubs across Europe, North America, Australasia, and other regions.

To our knowledge, our case is the first case of an adult case of commotio cordis in Europe. As experience in sports cardiology continues to expand, pre-participation screening remains a cornerstone of athlete evaluation. While it could neither have detected nor prevented this particular episode of commotio cordis, it remains essential for identifying cardiovascular conditions associated with an increased risk of life-threatening or fatal events.
