IκBζ target genes in chronic lymphocytic leukemia
Authors: Ilenia Sana, Miriam Meloni, Francesca Genova, Maria Elena Mantione, Anna Sofia Tascini, Ettore Zapparoli, Greta Carbone Faccin, Dimitra Paschaloudi, Martina Del Nero, Chiara Lenzi, Pamela Ranghetti, Eleonora Perotta, Lydia Scarfò, Paolo Ghia, Marta Muzio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIκBζ, an atypical IκB protein induced downstream of Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, is pivotal in orchestrating inflammatory responses and modulating inflammation-associated B cell malignancies. However, the transcriptional and epigenetic programs regulated by IκBζ in both normal and malignant B cells remain poorly described. Here, we analyzed chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) cells by epigenomic profiling to dissect the molecular function of IκBζ in leukemia. In primary human B cells and CLL samples, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) and cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) analyses delineated a comprehensive genome-wide landscape of IκBζ binding sites. Key genes, including NFKB2, BATF, and FOXP1, gained IκBζ binding after TLR stimulation in both CLL and B cells, whereas CXCR5 was mainly bound in CLL cells. Pharmacological or genetic inhibition of IκBζ signaling disrupted the induction of these molecules, highlighting its potential role as a central transcriptional tuner that integrates TLR-mediated inflammatory pathways with B cell receptor (BCR) signaling and migration.Keywords: Toll-like receptors, TLR, NFKBIZ, IκBζ, MAIL, INAP, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, CLL

Introduction

NF-κB signaling represents a key hub regulating inflammatory responses, immune cell function, and cell survival. Among the proteins that shape this transcriptional program, the NFKBIZ gene product IκBζ has emerged as an atypical member of the IκB family, acting as a nuclear transcriptional regulator rather than a classical cytoplasmic NF-κB inhibitor. IκBζ is induced within the first inflammatory response at the transcriptional level and is localized into the nucleus, where it modulates gene expression of the second inflammatory response in cooperation with NF-κB subunits p65 and p50.1,2,3 In addition, IκBζ has recently been shown to act as a coactivator of the POU family of lineage transcription factors through direct interactions mediated by two distinct domains, expanding its role beyond canonical NF-κB signaling.4,5 Furthermore, studies in melanoma have demonstrated that IκBζ can enhance STAT3-dependent transcription at selected loci while repressing other targets through the recruitment of chromatin-modifying enzymes such as EZH2 and HDAC3, highlighting its context-dependent regulatory functions.6

NFKBIZ was originally identified as an Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)- or interleukin 1-inducible gene and is transcriptionally upregulated following stimulation of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and members of the interleukin 1 receptor family.7,8,9 In this context, IκBζ acts as a nuclear modulator of NF-κB dependent transcription and plays a key role in inflammatory responses.3,10 Consistent with this function, increased IκBζ expression has been implicated in the pathogenesis of inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, including psoriasis and arthritis.11,12,13,14

Aberrant upregulation of IκBζ has also been reported in B cell malignancies, including diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), where inflammation/antigenic stimulation seems to play a key role in their pathogenesis. In a subset of activated B cell-like (ABC) DLBCL, mutations affecting the 3′ untranslated region of NFKBIZ result in increased mRNA stability and elevated protein levels.15 In line with its oncogenic relevance, NFKBIZ has emerged as a selective dependency gene in both B and T cell lymphomas across large-scale functional genomic screens, reinforcing its importance in the context of lymphoid malignancies (https://depmap.org/portal).16

In CLL, the same 3′ UTR mutation described in lymphoma has been identified, although not frequently,17,18 and additional missense mutations have been reported in a small number of cases.19 More recently, post-transcriptional mechanisms have been implicated in IκBζ upregulation in CLL. In particular, in vitro stimulation of leukemic cells with TLR ligands, mimicking microenvironmental signals occurring in vivo, results in a marked induction of NFKBIZ mRNA and an even more pronounced increase in IκBζ protein levels. Notably, higher IκBζ expression is observed in a subset of CLL samples characterized by negative prognostic markers20; targeting this signaling axis with pharmacological inhibitors has shown significant therapeutic activity in preclinical models, highlighting a novel signaling vulnerability.21,22 However, the genome-wide direct target genes regulated by IκBζ in B cell neoplasia remain largely undefined. To address this gap, we sought to fully characterize the IκBζ binding landscape in primary human normal and malignant B cells. We applied different techniques (i.e., chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing [ChIP-seq] and CUT&Tag) and data analyses (consensus peaks and differential binding analysis) to B lymphocytes isolated from the peripheral blood (PB) of healthy donors or patients with CLL. Unstimulated cells were used as an internal control, as low to undetectable levels of IκBζ are present; on the contrary, CpG-stimulated cells induce high levels of IκBζ with concomitant activation of NF-κB,20 thus representing an interesting model for molecular characterization.

Results

IκBζ binding undergoes a platform-dependent but convergent expansion upon CpG stimulation

To characterize the genome-wide binding landscape of IκBζ in CLL patients in response to CpG stimulation, we compared consensus peak sets obtained by ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag in unstimulated (CT) and CpG-stimulated (CpG) conditions (Tables S1 and S2). Both technologies showed a marked increase in the number of IκBζ binding sites following CpG stimulation, consistent with the low to undetectable IκBζ protein levels observed in unstimulated cells, although the magnitude of this increase differed substantially between platforms (∼55-fold for ChIP-seq versus ∼3-fold for CUT&Tag). This difference likely reflects the experimental differences of the protocols: the double crosslink ChIP-seq exhibits enhanced robustness, effectively capturing both indirect and unstable bindings while mediating the signal of several million cells; the CUT&Tag approach excels at identifying the strongest and most direct binding events, even in just a few hundred nuclei under native conditions.

ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag were performed in two independent small patient cohorts (n = 2 for ChIP and n = 4 for CUT&Tag). No overlap was observed between the two platforms in the unstimulated condition, indicating that basal IκBζ binding at low protein levels is largely patient-specific and not reproducible across independent cohorts, consistent with a more variable occupancy pattern at rest. To identify genomic loci bound by IκBζ with high confidence in CpG-stimulated conditions, we intersected the ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag consensus peak sets at the level of genomic coordinates, requiring a minimum reciprocal overlap of 100 bp. This analysis identified 421 peaks (22.6% of CUT&Tag CpG peaks; Table S3) that were present at concordant genomic positions in both platforms and both patient cohorts, independently of whether these sites were already occupied in the unstimulated condition. These 421 loci therefore represent regions where IκBζ binding in CpG-stimulated cells is reproducible across technologies and across independent patient samples and constitutes our highest-confidence set of IκBζ binding sites.

Genomic annotation of these peak sets revealed a consistent shift toward promoter-proximal binding upon stimulation (Table S4). In CUT&Tag, the fraction of peaks annotated to promoters increased from 60.8% (CT) to 69.0% (CpG), while in ChIP-seq the increase was even more pronounced, from 9.3% to 33.1%. Concomitantly, the fraction of intronic and intergenic peaks decreased on both platforms. The ChIP ∩ CUT&Tag overlap set showed an intermediate distribution (44.4% promoters), consistent with its composition of high-confidence sites detected independently by both approaches. Together, these data indicate that CpG-mediated IκBζ expression favors binding toward gene promoters, supporting a direct role in transcriptional regulation. To better characterize genes whose association with IκBζ binding is specifically induced by CpG stimulation, we performed a gene-level comparison independently within each platform: genes associated with CpG consensus peaks but not CT consensus peaks in ChIP-seq, and genes associated with CpG consensus peaks but not CT consensus peaks in CUT&Tag. The intersection of these two gene lists identified 654 genes gained by IκBζ in both ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag datasets, representing robust candidate targets of CpG-induced IκBζ recruitment (Table S5). This gene-level approach is therefore conceptually distinct from the genomic-coordinate overlap described above: the 654 genes capture what IκBζ newly targets upon stimulation in both platforms, while the 421 peaks identify where IκBζ binds with spatial reproducibility across platforms in the CpG condition regardless of the CT baseline. Only a single gene (HUNK, hormonally upregulated Neu-associated kinase) was lost from both datasets upon stimulation, indicating that the transcriptional response to CpG is dominated by gain rather than loss of IκBζ occupancy, in accordance with low to undetectable protein levels of IκBζ in unstimulated cells.

CpG-induced IκBζ target genes converge on NF-κB signaling and B cell activation

Among the 421 high-confidence ChIP ∩ CUT&Tag overlap peaks (Figure 1A; Table S3), annotation identified 394 unique gene symbols, which were subjected to pathway enrichment analysis using seven curated databases (Table S6; Figure 1B). Enriched pathways converged on eight non-redundant biological concepts supported across multiple databases. The strongest and most broadly supported signal was B cell receptor (BCR) signaling (BioPlanet adj-p = 1 × 10−6, confirmed in three independent databases), followed by TNF-α/NF-κB signaling (Hallmark adj-p = 5 × 10−6, confirmed in five databases), IL-2/STAT5 signaling (Hallmark adj-p = 2.4 × 10−4, four databases), and chemokine signaling (WikiPathways adj-p = 4.7 × 10−4, three databases). Apoptosis-related terms were represented at two levels of resolution: a broad apoptosis program (Hallmark) and, more specifically, BCL-2-regulated apoptosis through BH3-only protein interaction (Reactome combined score = 441), the latter consistent with the identification of BCL2 among the IκBζ target genes. Finally, ATM-dependent DNA damage response was significantly enriched (WikiPathways adj-p = 6 × 10−5, two databases), pointing to an unexpected connection between IκBζ-regulated chromatin and genomic stress response programs in CLL. The major pathways identified in the high-confidence set are not restricted to this stringent gene list, as confirmed by the extended analysis performed with multiple gene sets as reported in the Figures S1A and S1B. Together, these enrichment results indicate that IκBζ binding sites in CpG-stimulated CLL cells are functionally associated with the core signaling pathways governing CLL cell survival, activation, and microenvironmental interactions.

Differential binding analysis confirms a core set of high-confidence IκBζ targets

To statistically validate these consensus-based observations, differential binding analysis (CpG vs. CT) was performed independently for ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag datasets (Table S7). The two platforms showed markedly different statistical power: while both showed a strong skew toward gained binding (ChIP-seq, CLL patients: 515/544 regions, 95%; CUT&Tag, CLL patients: 86/90 regions, 96%), only a single ChIP-seq region reached a false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05, compared with 65 of 90 regions in CUT&Tag patients. This suggests that CUT&Tag provides robust differential analysis, consistent with its low background noise; ChIP-seq differential regions were therefore considered nominally differential and cross-validated through CUT&Tag and consensus overlap.

Genes fulfilling all three criteria simultaneously, present among the 654 CpG-gained consensus genes in both platforms, gained in the ChIP-seq nominally differential analysis, and significantly gained in the CUT&Tag differential analysis (FDR < 0.05), represent the highest-confidence candidate target genes of CpG-induced IκBζ recruitment in CLL. Thirty-nine genes met all three criteria (Table S8), spanning functional categories including B cell transcriptional regulators (FOXP1, BATF, TCF4, TCF7, and POU2F2), NF-κB pathway regulators (TNIP1, TNFSF4, and CD40LG), and chromatin-modifying factors (NCOR2 and SMARCE1).

Conversely, differential analysis of ChIP-seq data identified 29 genes showing loss of IκBζ binding upon CpG stimulation, including DLEU1 (located at the 13q14 locus, the most frequently deleted region in CLL23,24,25) and DUSP22 (a negative regulator of ERK/JNK signaling previously studied in CLL26).

To functionally validate selected targets emerging from the differential binding analysis, we arbitrarily selected four genes on the basis of prior evidence of involvement in B cell pathobiology and distinct epigenomic characteristics: NFKB2, a bona fide promoter-specific IκBζ target previously characterized in a B cell lymphoma cell line4; BATF, FOXP1, and CXCR5, which showed IκBζ peaks in both intragenic and intergenic regions (Figure 1C).

We first analyzed primary CLL cells isolated from the PB of patients and treated with CpG for 4 h in the presence or absence of dimethyl fumarate (DMF), a drug previously demonstrated to block the TLR/IκBζ axis,22 though not strictly specific for IκBζ alone. As shown in Figure S2, CpG induced the expression of all three genes, while DMF-mediated inhibition of IκBζ decreased their levels. To functionally validate the specific role of IκBζ in regulating these candidate targets, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout (KO) was next performed on seven fresh primary CLL samples. As expected, heterogeneity in IκBζ protein induction was observed across samples following CpG stimulation; nevertheless, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated KO resulted in a clear decrease in IκBζ protein levels in virtually all samples (Figure S3). Real-time PCR analysis demonstrated that NFKB2 and CXCR5 were no longer induced in the absence of IκBζ, while BATF showed a trend toward induction only in control samples (Figure 1D). FOXP1 induction was lower under these experimental conditions, and no clear effect was observed after IκBζ deletion (Figure S4). Overall, these data demonstrate that IκBζ is required for strong NFKB2 and CXCR5 upregulation in primary leukemic cells after TLR stimulation, while FOXP1 transcription regulation may require additional factors.

CpG stimulation is associated with IκBζ redistribution to transcriptionally active chromatin

To determine whether the gain of IκBζ binding was accompanied by changes in local chromatin state, we took advantage of the combined analysis of the same samples by CUT&Tag. We profiled the signal of three histone marks over IκBζ consensus peak sets in CT and CpG conditions, in CLL patient samples (n = 4) and B cells isolated from healthy donors (n = 3) separately. The selected histone modifications were H3K27ac (active enhancers and promoters), H3K27me3 (polycomb-mediated repression), and H3K4me3 (active promoters) and are shown in Figure 2.

In both experimental systems, IκBζ binding sites in unstimulated cells were characterized by a heterogeneous chromatin environment. In CLL patients, CT binding sites (n = 635) displayed detectable H3K4me3 signal alongside a subset of sites showing co-enrichment of H3K27me3, a hallmark of bivalent chromatin associated with transcriptionally poised genes (Figure 2A).

Following CpG stimulation, the chromatin state at IκBζ binding sites underwent a pronounced shift toward transcriptional activation in both systems. In CLL patients, H3K27ac signal increased markedly at the CpG consensus peak set (n = 1859), with sharper and more focused enrichment at peak centers compared to CT, while H3K27me3 signal was substantially reduced, consistent with resolution of bivalency and loss of polycomb-mediated repression. H3K4me3 signal also increased, consistent with activation of promoter-proximal regulatory regions. A concordant directional change was observed in B cells, where H3K27ac enrichment at IκBζ peaks was higher in the CpG condition relative to CT.

To directly compare CT and CpG chromatin states at the same genomic loci on a shared scale, we focused on the 86 IκBζ peaks showing statistically significant differential gain upon CpG stimulation (FDR < 0.05, DiffBind/edgeR) and profiled both conditions simultaneously over this fixed reference set (Figure 2B). At these differentially gained loci, IκBζ signal was nearly absent in CT and acquired a clear, focused peak in CpG, confirming their identity as bona fide newly recruited binding sites. Notably, H3K27ac signal was already detectable at these loci in CT despite the absence of IκBζ binding and increased substantially in the CpG condition, while H3K27me3 remained at background levels in both conditions. This pattern indicates that IκBζ is preferentially recruited to loci carrying pre-existing partial chromatin activity rather than to fully repressed regions, and that its recruitment coincides with or may contribute to a further gain of active chromatin marks.

Together, these chromatin-level data indicate that CpG stimulation drives a coordinated epigenetic transition at IκBζ binding sites, from a heterogeneous or transcriptionally poised state to an active chromatin configuration marked by H3K27ac gain and H3K27me3 loss. This chromatin remodeling is consistent with the large-scale redistribution of IκBζ binding toward gene promoters described previously and with the identification of chromatin-modifying complexes, including HDACs and SWI/SNF-associated factors, among the 654 highest-confidence consensus target genes (Table S5), pointing to a model in which IκBζ participates in the coordinated epigenetic and transcriptional response to CpG stimulation in CLL.

Motif analysis reveals NF-κB as a universal signature and identifies POU family transcription factors as a CpG-specific co-regulatory signal

To identify transcription factor motifs associated with IκBζ binding and their relationship to CpG stimulation, motif enrichment (HOMER; Figures 3A and 3B) and central motif positioning (CentriMo; Figure 3C) analyses were performed on five complementary peak sets: consensus IκBζ peaks (using CpG peaks as target and CT peaks as background) in B cells from healthy donors (n = 621; Table S2) and CLL patients (n = 1859; Table S2), CpG-gained differential peaks in both systems (n = 44 and n = 90, respectively; Table S7; see next paragraph for details on B cells), and the highest-confidence ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag IκBζ peak set in patients (n = 421; Table S3).

Across all five datasets, NF-κB family motifs (p65/RelA, p52/NFKB2, and REL, RELB) were consistently among the top enriched and, where statistical power allowed, centrally positioned motifs (NFKB2 CentriMo adj-p = 4.8 × 10−15 in the highest-confidence IκBζ peak set; HOMER p = 1 × 10−99 in B cell consensus; Table S9). This finding, observed across both technologies and all peak sets analyzed, is consistent with the established role of IκBζ as an atypical IκB family co-regulator of NF-κB and provides an internal validation of binding specificity, directly supporting the NF-κB pathway gene signature identified in the differential gene analysis (BCL2, BIRC3, NFKB2, TNFAIP3, and MAP3K14).

In contrast, POU-domain transcription factor motifs (Oct2/POU2F2, Oct1/POU2F1, Oct4/POU5F1, Brn1/POU3F2, and Oct6/POU3F1) emerged as a CpG-specific signature considering the analysis of the differential peaks. These motifs were not among the top enriched hits in the B cell consensus peak set but became prominent among CpG-gained differential peaks in both normal and leukemic B cells and constituted the single most significantly enriched and centrally positioned motif family in the ChIP-seq/CUT&Tag overlap-validated peak set (Table S9). CentriMo analysis of this high-confidence set revealed that POU2F2/MA0507.3 was the most significantly centrally enriched motif overall (rank 1; adjusted p = 1.8 × 10−18), with NFKB2_DBD ranking second (adjusted p = 2.7 × 10−17) and a second POU2F2 matrix also showing central enrichment (Table S10; Figure 3). The sharp, summit-proximate positional enrichment of both motifs supports direct binding at these elements rather than indirect co-occupancy. Given the established role of OCT2 and its coactivator OBF-1/BOB1 in driving immunoglobulin gene transcription and B cell differentiation, these results suggest that CpG stimulation directs IκBζ toward regulatory elements co-occupied by OCT2, representing a CpG-induced rather than constitutive co-regulatory relationship.

Additional motifs identified in the overlap-validated peak set include AP-1/bZIP family motifs (BATF, Fos, Fra1, and Atf3) and ETS-family B lymphoid factors (PU.1/SPI1 and SpiB), which were significantly enriched (HOMER p < 1 × 10−23; full results in Table S9; SpiB also shown in Figure 3). Notably, BATF itself was independently identified among the 39 highest-confidence IκBζ target genes, with concordant CpG-induced gain of binding in both ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag differential analyses (Figure 1C, genome browser tracks). In the full consensus peak sets, CentriMo additionally identified strong central enrichment of SP3/SP4/KLF family in CLL patients (Table S10), likely reflecting the predominantly promoter-proximal localization of the bulk IκBζ consensus peak set and is presented as chromatin context rather than a candidate cofactor.

Of note, statistical power for CentriMo positional testing was limited in smaller differential peak sets (e.g., the patient CpG-gain set, where only one motif met the testing threshold); in these cases, HOMER enrichment analysis nonetheless recovered the same NF-κB and POU family signatures observed in larger datasets, indicating that the absence of CentriMo significance reflects reduced statistical power on small peak numbers rather than the absence of biological signal.

IκBζ binding in healthy B cells recapitulates the CpG-induced program and reveals cell-type-specific features

To determine whether the CpG-dependent IκBζ binding program described in patients with CLL is a disease-specific phenomenon or reflects a more general B cell response to TLR9 stimulation, we performed parallel CUT&Tag analysis of IκBζ in 3 B cell samples purified from the PB of healthy donors.

In unstimulated healthy B cells, IκBζ binding was restricted to 195 consensus peaks, predominantly localized to intergenic regions (86.2%), with only 10.3% of peaks at promoters. Following CpG stimulation, healthy B cells showed a substantial expansion of IκBζ binding to 621 consensus peaks (∼3.2-fold increase), accompanied by a major redistribution toward promoter-proximal regions (30.8% in CpG vs. 10.3% in CT). This parallels the directional pattern observed in CLL patients, though the final promoter fraction remained considerably lower than in stimulated CLL cells (30.8% vs. 69.0%). Together, these data suggest that CpG stimulation drives a conserved, TLR9-dependent IκBζ redistribution program in B cells irrespective of disease status, but that CLL cells mount a quantitatively amplified version of this response, particularly at promoter-proximal regulatory elements.

Differential binding analysis in healthy B cells identified 44 regions with significantly increased IκBζ occupancy upon CpG stimulation (44/44 gain; 43/44 FDR < 0.05; log Fold Change (FC) range 1.6–7.8), with no regions showing loss of binding: a pattern of essentially exclusive gain consistent with that observed in CLL patients. 18 of these 44 differentially gained regions were shared with CLL patients, including the most consistently and robustly regulated loci across both systems: FOXP1 (the highest-confidence target in the entire dataset, differentially gained in both systems with FDR <10−6), BATF, NFKB2, TCF7, TNFSF4, BMF, and the NFKB1-related locus REL-DT. The consistent differential gain at these loci across two independent cellular systems provides strong cross-system validation of this core IκBζ target gene set and further reinforces their identity as robust, functionally relevant targets of the TLR9-IκBζ axis in B cell biology.

Beyond this shared core, healthy B cells showed several differentially gained loci not detected in CLL patients, most notably PIM1 (logFC = 7.8, FDR = 8.8 × 10−7) and PIK3C2B (logFC = 6.6) (Table S7).

At the consensus level, CpG-stimulated healthy B cells shared 247 genes with CpG-stimulated CLL patients (Table S2), among which several genes with established roles in CLL were identified: BCL2, BIRC3, NFKB2, TNFAIP3, FOXP1, CXCR5, LYN, BCL11A, DLEU1, AKT2, BACH2, and POU2F2. The presence of these genes in both healthy B cell and CLL patient datasets indicates that IκBζ binding at these loci is not CLL specific but rather reflects a conserved component of the TLR9-activated B cell transcriptional program. The greater statistical robustness of the differential signal in CLL cells relative to healthy B cells (65/90 vs. 43/44 regions reaching FDR <0.05) further suggests that leukemic B cells mount a quantitatively exaggerated version of this normal response, with stronger and more reproducible IκBζ recruitment to a broader set of target loci.

IκBζ-dependent gene expression profile in mice confirms BATF as a positively regulated target gene

To investigate the functional role of IκBζ in the regulation of B cell-specific transcriptional programs, we took advantage of a genetically modified mouse model; we analyzed Mb1Cre+/Nfkbiz-flx/flx mice in which Nfkbiz is selectively deleted in the B cell lineage27 (Figure S5B). Flow cytometric analysis of PB cells showed robust induction of IκBζ protein in CD19+ B cells from wild type (WT) mice following LPS stimulation, while no protein expression was detected in Nfkbiz-KO mice (Figure S5A).

To assess the transcriptional consequences of IκBζ loss, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on splenic CD19+ B cells isolated from WT (n = 4) and Nfkbiz-KO (n = 4) mice under unstimulated conditions or after CpG stimulation. Comparison of CpG-treated versus unstimulated samples revealed that several genes were transcriptionally perturbed in both genotypes (Figures 4A and S6; Table S11). IL-6, a previously described murine IκBζ target,28 displayed a significant induction after CpG in WT but less in the Nfkbiz-KO B cells, as validated by quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) (Figure S5C).

To establish a functional link between IκBζ chromatin occupancy and transcriptional output, we integrated the CUT&Tag differential binding data (CpG vs. CT) with two complementary RNA-seq comparisons from Nfkbiz-KO and WT splenic B cells: the CpG-induced transcriptional response in WT mice (WT CpG vs. CT) and the transcriptional consequences of IκBζ loss under CpG stimulation (KO vs. WT both after CpG). Because this mouse model was generated on a healthy rather than leukemic background, comparisons were performed in the context of the healthy B cell epigenomic dataset. Genes at the intersection of these datasets were visualized as scatter plots relating epigenomic binding gain (x axis, logFC − CUT&Tag) to transcriptional changes (y axis, logFC RNA-seq), with orange indicating gene activation and blue indicating repression in the respective RNA-seq comparison (Figure 4C).

Cross-referencing the 44 CpG-gained IκBζ peaks with genes transcriptionally induced by CpG in WT mice (Figure 4C) identified 13 genes showing concordant epigenomic and transcriptional regulation. The seven highest-confidence genes, including FOXP1, BATF, and NFKB2 in the activated quadrant (positive on both axes), indicate that their CpG-induced transcriptional upregulation is accompanied by concurrent IκBζ recruitment to their regulatory regions in WT cells. PIK3C2B, ECE1, INPP5D, SLC43A2, ARHGAP25, and LENG9 showed gained IκBζ binding but reduced expression upon CpG stimulation, suggesting that IκBζ occupancy at these loci may be associated with transcriptional repression or may reflect a secondary chromatin event not directly linked to gene activation.

Direct assessment of IκBζ functional requirement, obtained by comparing KO and WT B cells both under CpG stimulation (Figure 4C), revealed a more heterogeneous picture consistent with the multifunctional nature of IκBζ. Among genes with CpG-gained IκBζ binding, BATF was significantly downregulated in KO relative to WT B cells, identifying it as the gene for which IκBζ is functionally required for CpG-induced expression.

By contrast, ECE1 and PIK3C2B showed higher expression in KO than in WT B cells under CpG stimulation, indicating that their CpG-induced transcriptional repression is dependent on IκBζ. FOXP1 showed a discordant behavior across the two comparisons, being highly induced by CpG in WT, yet not downregulated when IκBζ is not expressed (KO). This is consistent with the complex, multi-input regulation of this locus previously reported in B cell malignancies, where IκBζ represents one among multiple transcriptional activators converging on FOXP1.

Discussion

The datasets of IκBζ genome-wide binding targets generated in this study represent the first comprehensive map obtained in primary human normal and malignant B cells. This is particularly relevant given that IκBζ is known to interact with multiple endogenous transcription factors, thereby fine-tuning gene expression in a context-dependent manner. Consistent with this notion, our epigenetic analyses revealed that, in addition to canonical NF-κB consensus motifs, OCT/POU binding motifs were highly enriched at IκBζ binding regions in leukemic cells, suggesting a functional interaction between these transcription factors; indeed, both NF-κB and OCT/POU family members are expressed in CLL cells.29,30,31 This observation is in line with recent reports obtained in B cell lymphoma cell lines where IκBζ could bind to NF-κB and POU through distinct interaction domains.4 Beyond NF-κB and POU family members, we identified significant enrichment of motifs recognized by ETS family member SpiB, further highlighting the versatility of IκBζ as a transcriptional co-regulator.

A recent study in melanoma cells identified NF-κB and STAT3 as the predominant IκBζ cofactors,6 suggesting that IκBζ can modulate distinct transcriptional programs depending on the cellular context, while retaining its role as a bona fide NF-κB cofactor across diverse biological systems. Notably, several of the transcription factors whose motifs were enriched at IκBζ binding sites in our system, including Oct2 (POU2F2) and SpiB, are B lineage-specific regulators of differentiation, reinforcing the importance of studying endogenous levels of transcription factors within physiological cellular models.

Utilizing the CUT&Tag technology, we were able to profile IκBζ chromatin binding in primary circulating B cells from healthy donors and directly compare these profiles with those obtained from CLL cells. Importantly, integration of IκBζ binding with histone modification profiles within the same samples enabled the identification of a set of high-confidence IκBζ target genes associated with promoter and enhancer activation. Among these, FOXP1, NFKB2, and BATF emerged as prominent shared targets in both healthy and leukemic B cells, supporting a model in which IκBζ contributes to the regulation of key transcription factors.

FOXP1 was previously associated with poor prognosis in different hematologic malignancies, including DLBCL, in which it cooperates with NF-κB to promote cell survival.32,33 In CLL, FOXP1 expression is heterogeneous, and high levels are associated with adverse prognosis and increased BCR signaling, a hallmark of leukemia pathobiology. Distinct microRNAs were shown to restrain FOXP1 mRNA levels by post-transcriptional regulation34,35; however, it is unclear which transcription factors modulate its expression. Our data suggest that IκBζ contributes to the transcriptional regulation of FOXP1, as several IκBζ binding regions were identified across the FOXP1 locus. However, FOXP1 mRNA does not appear to be exclusively regulated by IκBζ as assessed by RNA-seq analysis of IκBζ-deficient cells.

NF-κB family includes NF-κB1 and NF-κB2; NF-κB1 (p105/p50) is cleaved and translocated into the nucleus as an active dimer (p50/RelA) and transmits a canonical signaling downstream of several inflammatory cytokine receptors. The NF-κB2 (p100/p52) is cleaved to p52, which forms dimers with RelB (p52/RelB) and mediates non-canonical signaling downstream of CD40 family members.29 Notably, NF-κB1 and NF-κB2 are constitutively overexpressed and activated in CLL cells, and their levels correlate with disease progression, predicting a worse prognosis.29 NF-κB2 can be positively autoregulated at the transcriptional level as NF-κB family members bind to its own promoter,36 but the mechanisms regulating its overexpression in CLL are not completely understood. Our data show that TLR-mediated IκBζ activation can directly control the transcription of NFKB2 mRNA, further implicating IκBζ in a feedforward loop of non-canonical NF-κB2 sustainment in leukemic cells. However, elevated NF-κB2 levels could result in both p52/RelB activation and potentially inhibitory p100/RelB complexes, making the functional outcome difficult to predict from NF-κB2 upregulation alone.

The concurrent enrichment of BATF motifs at IκBζ binding sites and IκBζ occupancy at the BATF locus suggest a positive regulatory feedback loop, in which IκBζ recruits and cooperates with BATF/AP-1 at activated regulatory elements while simultaneously driving BATF transcription: a self-reinforcing mechanism that may amplify the B cell response to TLR9 stimulation. BATF showed the most consistent pattern across both comparisons (induced by CpG in WT and reduced upon IκBζ loss), making it the single most robustly validated direct transcriptional target of IκBζ in the CpG response.

Recent data demonstrated that TLR9 stimulation can induce CXCR4-mediated migration of CLL cells.37,38 Although CXCR4 is the main regulator of CLL cell migration and homing to the lymph nodes, CXCR5 may also play a role. In CLL, CXCR5 is overexpressed as compared to normal circulating B cells.39 Higher levels of IκBζ in leukemic versus normal B cells may explain this observation.

RNA-seq of purified splenic B splenocytes isolated from WT or Nfkbiz-deficient animals and treated in vitro with CpG showed different transcriptional profiles. Epigenomic and transcriptomic integration identifies BATF as a direct functional target of the CpG/IκBζ axis and reveals multi-input regulation of FOXP1. Collectively, these data confirm that CpG-induced IκBζ binding is functionally heterogeneous: at a core set of loci, most prominently BATF and genes of the NF-κB pathway, IκBζ occupancy is tightly coupled to transcriptional activation and is required for the full CpG response; at other loci, IκBζ binding occurs in the context of broader chromatin remodeling without constituting the primary transcriptional driver.

Collectively, our data place IκBζ as a central transcriptional regulator that may couple TLR9-driven inflammation, BCR signaling, lineage-specific transcriptional programs, and migratory behavior in CLL.

Limitations of the study

Despite the limited number of independent primary samples analyzed for IκBζ binding (n = 6 for CLL and n = 3 for B cells), the study identified robust IκBζ targets, which could be further validated in a larger cohort of patients. At the same time, CUT&Tag revealed a limited number of peaks as compared to ChIP-seq, confirming that it may be suboptimal for the analysis of transcription factors, yet essential for combined chromatin state characterization when the amount of primary samples is limited.

For data analysis, in the absence of chromosome-conformation data (Hi-C/Capture-C), distal/intergenic peaks cannot be definitively assigned to a specific target.

Regarding cell-type specific IκBζ targets, it is possible that different levels of IκBζ observed in normal vs. leukemic cells may have impacted the overall number of positive hits.

Finally, although our motif analysis suggests a cooperation between IκBζ and other transcription factors known to be expressed in B cell types, only an additional biochemical analysis will confirm that this interaction actually occurs.

Method details

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and sequencing (ChIP-seq), data processing and analysis

ChIP-seq was performed in two primary CLL cases after TLR9 stimulation with CpG-ODN 2006 2,5 μg/ml for 4 h and the same two primary CLL cases with no treatment, used as controls. Forty million cells for each condition were pelleted, washed twice with PBS, resuspended in 5 mL PBS and prefixed with 2 mM DSG for 45min in gentle shaking. Cells were washed in PBS and fixed with formaldehyde 1% final concentration for 15 min in gentle shaking. The reaction was blocked with Glycine at a final concentration of 125 mM. Cells were washed twice with cold PBS and lysed. The nuclei were pelleted at 3000 rpm for 5 min at 4°, resuspended and sonicated in Diagenode Bioruptor Pico. Debris were eliminated by centrifugation, and the samples were diluted with 9 volumes of buffer and 60 μL of chromatin were kept as inputs. Lysates were precleared with beads, and then primary antibody was added over night at 4° in rotation. Protein G beads were washed 3 times and then added to the chromatin for 2h at 4° in rotation. 5 washes were performed (NaCl washing buffer + PMSF), and then 3 more washes with TE. Elution Buffer was added for 30 min at 65°. Magnetic beads were removed, and the elutes were left over night at 65°. DNA extraction was performed with QIAGEN kit in 40 μL of water. Anti- IkBz antibody from Cell Signaling cat.9244 was used 1:50.

The DNA samples were processed with the “TruSeq ChIP” library protocol in order to obtain 20.0M clusters of fragments of - 1x75nt of length through NextSeq 500 High 75.

Raw reads were quality-trimmed using Cutadapt v4.9 to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases. Trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using BWA-MEM2 v2.2.1. Duplicate reads were marked with Samblaster v0.1.26 and BAM files were sorted and indexed with Sambamba v1.0.1. Per-sample BAM files were merged using Picard v3.3.0. Merged BAM files were filtered to remove multimapped reads (MAPQ <15), mitochondrial reads, and reads overlapping ENCODE blacklisted regions. Peak calling was performed with MACS2 v2.2.7.1 in narrow peak mode. To account for background chromatin accessibility and sequencing artifacts, each ChIP sample was paired with the matched input control during peak calling. Input control libraries underwent the same preprocessing steps as ChIP samples and the resulting filtered BAM files were provided to MACS2 as background control via the --control parameter. Signal tracks were generated as bigWig files using deepTools bamCoverage v3.5.4 with library-size normalization.

Cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag): data processing and differential binding analysis

Four primary leukemic samples from patients with CLL, and 3 primary B-cell samples from healthy donors were used for the CUT&Tag analyses. CUT&Tag was performed considering IkBz together with three different histone marks (H3K27ac, H3K27me3 and H3K4me3) to account for the chromatin context of IkBz binding sites. After treatment of the cells, the nuclei were isolated and either resuspended in Wash Buffer (20 mM HEPES pH7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine) or lightly fixed with 0.1% formaldehyde for 2 min. Concanavalin A beads were resuspended in binding buffer (1M HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 1M KCl,1M CaCl2 and 1M MnCl2, and dH2O) and added to nuclei to have 100000 fixed nuclei for each TF analysis, and 50000 nuclei for each histone modification analysis. The following primary antibodies were added and incubated over night at 4°C: H3K4me3 1:50 Cell Signaling CS9751; H3K27ac 1:100 Cell Signaling CS8173; H3K27me3 1:50 Cell Signaling CS9733; IgG control 1:50 Cell Signaling CS2729; IkBz 1:50 Cell Signaling CS9244. Secondary guinea pig anti-rabbit antibody was incubated in rotation at RT for 2 h. After washing, pAG-Tn5 pre-loaded adapter complex was added and incubated 1h in rotation at RT. After 2 washes, samples were resuspended in tagmentation solution and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After a step of wash with TAPS wash solution (dH2O, 10 mM TAPS pH 8.5, 0.2 mM EDTA) samples were incubated 1h at 58°C with 0.1% SDS Release solution (10% SDS and 1M TAPS pH 8.5, dH2O). To neutralize SDS, 0.67% Triton neutralization solution (10% Triton X-100, dH2O) was added to samples.

To amplify libraries, PCR was performed with a combination of barcoded i5 primer and barcoded i7 primers, to allow the discrimination of different samples during sequencing. Purification of libraries was performed as manufacturer instructions with SPRI Ampure XP beads. Libraries obtained from the CUT&Tag protocol were checked and quantified with Agilent TapeStation and Qubit fluorometer.

DNA was sequenced with the “only_sequencing” library protocol in order to obtain 5.0M clusters of fragments of 2 x 200 nt of length through NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (200 cycles).

FASTQ files were trimmed using Cutadapt vs. 4.9 and aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using Bowtie2 vs. 2.5.4. Peak calling was performed with MACS2 vs. 2.1.0, considering narrow peaks.

Consensus peaks and differential binding analysis

For both CUT&Tag and ChIP-seq data, consensus peak sets were defined independently for each experimental group using DiffBind vs. 3.4.11 within the R environment 4.3.2. For each group, a DiffBind object was initialized from individual sample peak files, retaining only genomic regions present in all replicates (minOverlap = 1.0). Read counts were computed over consensus regions, duplicates were removed and reads with mapping quality below 15 were excluded. Library sizes were normalized using the TMM method with EdgeR as the analysis framework. For visualization, signal profiles were generated using deepTools (v3.5.6). For each experimental group (CLL patients and healthy B-cells), replicate bigWig files were averaged per condition (CT and CpG) and per chromatin feature (IκBζ, H3K27ac, H3K27me3, H3K4me3) using bigwigAverage and yelding one averaged bigWig file per group/condition/mark combination.

Figure 2A was generated using condition-specific consensus peak sets as genomic reference regions (--regionsFileName) and condition-matched averaged bigWig files as signal input (--scoreFileName). Specifically, CT consensus peaks were used as the genomic reference for CT panels, and CpG consensus peaks for CpG panels; in both cases, signal tracks from the corresponding condition were overlaid for all four chromatin features simultaneously. This design displays the chromatin context at the loci where IκBζ is present under each condition, using internally consistent reference regions and signal tracks. Color scales were fixed within each cell type (CLL patients: zMin = 0, zMax = 6; B cell lines: zMin = 0, zMax = 12). Because CT and CpG consensus peak sets are largely non-overlapping and differ in size (CLL patients: CT n = 635, CpG n = 1859; B cell lines: CT n = 195, CpG n = 621), the two panels within each cell type display different genomic loci and should not be interpreted as a direct locus-by-locus comparison; they instead characterize the chromatin landscape at condition-specific IκBζ binding sites.

Figure 2B was generated using the 86 statistically significant differentially gained IκBζ peaks (FDR <0.05, DiffBind/edgeR, CpG vs. CT, CLL patients) as a fixed genomic reference common to both conditions (--regionsFileName). CT and CpG averaged bigWig files for all four chromatin features were supplied together as signal input (--scoreFileName), resulting in eight adjacent columns (IκBζ CT, IκBζ CpG, H3K27ac CT, H3K27ac CpG, H3K27me3 CT, H3K27me3 CpG, H3K4me3 CT, H3K4me3 CpG). A single shared color scale (zMin = 0, zMax = 6) was applied to all columns, enabling direct quantitative comparison of chromatin state between CT and CpG at the same 86 loci. Rows were sorted by mean IκBζ signal in the CpG condition (--sortUsingSamples 2).

For both panels, signal matrices were computed with computeMatrix reference-point, centering on the midpoint of each peak (±3 kb, bin size 50 bp), with missing dataset to zero (--missingDataAsZero) and regions with zero signal across all samples excluded (--skipZeros). Heatmaps and profile plots were generated with plotHeatmap and plotProfile respectively.

Differential binding affinity analysis was conducted on CUT&Tag and ChIP-seq separately, applying the edgeR method implemented in DiffBind. Genomic regions exhibiting significant gains or losses in binding affinity were identified using a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Peaks were annotated using the ChIPseeker Bioconductor package (v1.32.0), with TxDb.Hsapiens.UCSC.hg38.knownGene and org.Hs.e.g., .db as reference transcript and gene annotation databases.

Differential binding analysis in CUT&Tag was performed independently for IκBζ and for each of four chromatin features (IκBζ (IK), H3K27ac, H3K27me3, and H3K4me3) across the following pairwise comparisons: CpG-stimulated versus untreated (CT) CLL samples; CpG-stimulated versus untreated (CT) healthy B-cells. Paired analysis was applied to account for sample identity.

Concerning the ChIP-seq dataset, the two primary CLL cases after CpG stimulation were compared to the two primary CLL cases with no treatment.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Genes associated with the 421 highest-confidence IκBζ peaks shared between ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag in CLL patient samples were used as input for pathway enrichment analysis. Peak annotation by ChIPseeker identified 394 unique gene symbols after removing duplicated gene assignments arising from multiple peaks associated with the same gene. Enrichment analysis was performed using Enrichr across seven pathway libraries: GO Biological Process, Reactome, BioPlanet, WikiPathways, KEGG, NCI-Nature, and MSigDB Hallmark. Terms were considered significant at an adjusted p-value <0.05. Significant results were manually reviewed to identify and remove redundant terms arising from shared gene sets across databases. Related pathways were grouped into broader biological concepts based on similarity of annotation and biological meaning. When the same concept was supported by multiple databases, one representative term was selected for visualization by prioritizing statistical significance, pathway specificity, biological relevance to the experimental context, and clarity of the pathway label.

Overlap between CUT&Tag and ChIP-seq

Overlap between ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag consensus peak sets was computed using the findOverlaps function from the GenomicRanges R package, requiring a minimum overlap of 100bp. Given the comparable mean peak width across datasets (ChIP-seq: ∼401bp, CUT&Tag: ∼404bp), this threshold corresponds to approximately 25% reciprocal overlap.

Transcription factors analysis

Known transcription factor motifs enriched within IκBζ peak sets were identified using HOMER v5.1, while positional motif enrichment around peak centers was assessed using CentriMo v5.5.9. Input regions were converted to BED format and resized to 500-bp windows centered on the peak midpoint based on the GRCh38 genome assembly.

For the consensus peak analysis, CpG-induced IκBζ consensus peaks were used as target regions and the corresponding CT IκBζ consensus peaks as background regions. Analyses were performed separately for healthy B-cell samples and patient samples. For differential peak analysis, CpG versus CT IκBζ regions identified by DiffBind were stratified according to the direction of logFC, and regions showing a gain in IKBZ binding were used as the target set for motif enrichment analysis, assessed against HOMER’s default genomically-matched background sequences. The subset of CpG-induced IκBζ peaks overlapping both ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag datasets was analyzed as an additional validated peak set.

Known motif enrichment analysis was performed using HOMER (findMotifsGenome.pl) with the parameter `-size given`, allowing the predefined 500-bp centered regions to be used directly as input. FASTA sequences were generated from the same BED coordinates using bedtools getfasta. Prior to CentriMo analysis, sequence identifiers were shortened, and the resulting sequences were used to evaluate positional motif enrichment relative to the peak center.

ChIP-seq and CUT&Tag signals of CLL samples were visualized at selected loci on the GRCh38 genome assembly using bigWig files. Loci of interest were selected from regions overlapping CpG-induced IκBζ ChIP-seq peaks and CUT&Tag regulatory regions, with priority given to validated target genes. Gene annotations were obtained from GENCODE human release 49 (GRCh38). Genome browser-style tracks were generated using pyGenomeTracks v3.9 with bigWig signal files, peak and overlap BED files, and a gene annotation track.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated NFKBIZ knockout in CLL primary cells

Purified B-cells isolated from patients were subjected to CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing targeting NFKBIZ. For each experimental condition, 12 × 10ˆ6 cells were electroporated with ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes assembled using synthetic guide crRNA with a tracrRNA_ATTO550 (cat. 1075928) forming gRNA. A non-targeting crRNA (cat. 1072544) was used as a negative control, while an NFKBIZ-specific crRNA was used for gene disruption. RNP complexes were generated by combining the gRNAs+with Alt-R Cas9 Nuclease V3 (cat. 1081058). Alt-R Cas9 Electroporation Enhancer (cat. 1075916) was added before nucleofection. All reagents described above were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). Cells were resuspended in P3 Nucleofector Solution (cat. V4XP-3032, Lonza) and electroporated using the Amaxa 4D-Nucleofector System (Lonza) with the DN-100 nucleofection program.

Following electroporation, cells were transferred to RPMI with 20% FBS medium without antibiotics and incubated for 24 h at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. After the recovery period, Pen-Strept was added to the culture medium. 48 h after nucleofection, the cells were either left untreated or stimulated with CpG ODN 2006 (InvivoGen) at a final concentration of 2.5 μg/mL for 4 h prior to downstream analyses.

Nucleofection efficiency was evaluated by flow cytometry based on ATTO fluorescence and consistently exceeded 80% of viable cells. To verify successful NFKBIZ disruption, cells were stimulated with CpG and IκBζ protein expression was assessed by Western blot using an anti-IκBζ antibody (1:1000 Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. #9244), with β-actin serving as the loading control.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from CLL cells subjected to CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing and stimulated with CpG 2,5 for 4 h (ODN-2006 from Invivogen) and from CLL cells treated or not with Dimethyl Fumarate (DMF from Selleckchem), using Mini kit protocol following manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). cDNA was synthesized using 0.5 μg total RNA with Iscrip kit (Biorad). To detect mRNA levels of target genes expression quantitative RT-PCR was performed using SsoAdvanced Universal Probes Supermix or SsoAdvanced Universal Green Supermix with the following commercial probes FAM or HEX dye labeled (cat.12001950 and cat.10025636, Bio-Rad): human FOXP1 (qHsaCEP0027768); human NFKB2 (qHsaCEP0049657); human BATF (qHsaCIP0026035), human β-Actin (qHsaCEP0036280); human CXCR5 (qHsaCID020761). Total RNA was extracted from purified B-cells were obtained from splenocytes as mentioned above, and the following commercial probe (cat. 12001950) were used: Mmu GAPDH (MMuCEP0039581), Mmu IL-6 (MmuCEP0054186). Real-time PCR was performed using CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-rad) and relative mRNA expression was calculated with CFX Maestro software as indicated in the figure legends.

RNA-sequencing

B-cells were isolated from the spleens of four wild type (WT) and four NFKBIZ-knockout (NFKBIZ-KO) mice. Purified B-cells were obtained from splenocytes after purification by immunomagnetic negative selection with EasySep Mouse B Cell Isolation Kit (cat #19854A, Stem cell technologies) following the manufacturer instructions. Purified B splenocytes were either left untreated or stimulated in vitro with CpG-ODN1826 for 4 h. RNA was extracted by column purification and quantified; up to 0.5 μg were used for library preparation and sequencing at Personal Genomics.

Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA kit (New England Biolabs) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads.

Read alignment to the GRCm39 Mus musculus reference genome and transcript quantification were performed using DRAGEN vs. 3.9.5. Raw read counts were imported into the R environment for differential expression analysis using DESeq2 vs. 1.40.2.

Differential expression analyses were conducted by comparing both Nfkbiz-KO and WT mice between untreated and CpG-treated conditions. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they met an adjusted p-value <0.05 and a Log2(FC) > 2 or < -2 (Volcano plot).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Relative RNA expression data are reported in Figures 1D, S2, S4, and S5C. Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism version 11 (GraphPad Software USA); all the statistical details can be found in the Figure legends.

Asterisks were used to indicate p values: ∗ indicates p Value < 0.05; ∗∗ indicates p Value < 0.01; ∗∗∗∗ indicates p Value < 0.0001.
