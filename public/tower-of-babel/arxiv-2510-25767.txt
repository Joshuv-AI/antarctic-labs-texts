Superconductivity in overdoped cuprates can be understood from a BCS perspective!
B. J. Ramshaw, Steven A. Kivelson

Abstract
We summarize key experimental studies of the low energy properties of overdoped cuprate high temperature superconductors and conclude that a theoretical understanding of the “essential physics” is achievable in terms of a conventional Fermi-liquid treatment of the normal state, and a BCS mean-field treatment of the ( d d -wave) superconducting state. For this perspective to be consistent, it is necessary to posit that there is a crossover from a strongly correlated underdoped regime (where a different theoretical perspective is necessary) to the more weakly correlated overdoped regime. It is also necessary to argue that the various observed features of the overdoped materials that are inconsistent with this perspective can be attributed to the expected effects of the intrinsic disorder associated with most of the materials being solid state solutions (alloys). As a test of this idea, we make a series of falsifiable predictions concerning the expected behavior of “ideal” (disorder free) overdoped cuprates.



I
Introduction

Background:

The search for a theoretical understanding of the experimentally documented properties of the cuprate high temperature superconductors has played a central role in condensed matter physics for almost four decades, in large part because these materials have been more intensively and incisively studied than any other correlated electronic material. Influential and broadly relevant ideas that germinated from such studies include the physics of quantum antiferromangets (including possible spin-liquids) and doped antiferromagnets, novel forms of charge, spin, current, and superconducting density wave orders, electronic analogues of various liquid crystalline phases, ways in which usual notions of metallic physics can break-down (non-Fermi liquid behaviors), bold new approaches to obtaining controlled (numerical) solutions of model problems - such as the Hubbard model - and a host of more phenomenological ideas focused on various experimental observations. Indeed, the observation of multiple novel behaviors in these materials justifies this exuberant exploration of various interesting observed phenomena.

One notable feature of this field is the enormous effort that has been devoted to obtaining exquisite control of the materials involved. Results in the field are now highly reproducible. However, one key feature we will stress is that this does not mean that the effects of “disorder” can be neglected. Most of the materials involved are alloys, meaning that even in “perfect” materials, there is inherent disorder associated with the random configuration of dopant atoms. While we are certainly interested in the measured properties of the actual materials, it is also useful to ask what the features of a putative “ideal” (i.e. disorder free) cuprate would be.

A central set of questions in the field concern the nature of the superconducting state itself: Why is
T_{c}
so high? What limits
T_{c}
? Why do the phase diagrams of many families of cuprates exhibit a relatively similar superconducting “dome?” What do we learn from the cuprates about unconventional superconductivity in other material systems?

II
What is a BCS superconductor?

Using the words “BCS” and “cuprate” in the same sentence is obviously provocative. As elaborated below, we apply the term ‘BCS superconductor’ to a situation in which the superconducting state emerges as a weak-coupling instability from a Fermi liquid normal state, independent of whether phonons play a role in the mechanism or not.

In what sense are “conventional” superconductors described by BCS theory?

The properties of crystalline (“clean”) conventional superconductors can be derived from the electron-phonon problem using the Migdal-Eliashberg (ME) approximation. Unlike the original BCS theory, which abstracts away the pairing mechanism with a generic attractive potential, ME explicitly keeps both the electrons and the “bosonic glue,” i.e. phonons.

At temperatures
T>\hbar\omega_{D}
(where
\omega_{D}
a characteristic phonon frequency - e.g. the Debye frequency), the quasi-particle lifetime (in both theory and experiment) is
\hbar/\tau=2\pi\lambda_{0}T
, where
\lambda_{0}
is the dimensionless (unrenormalized) electron-phonon coupling constant. It is not immediately obvious that Fermi liquid theory applies at these temperatures, where quasiparticles (in the Landau sense) are at best marginally well defined
Prange and Kadanoff 1964
. However, if one is only interested in treating the problem at temperatures,
T
, and energies,
\hbar\omega
, small compared to
\hbar\omega_{D}
, then it is possible to introduce (as was done from the first by BCS) a simpler low-energy description of the system in terms of well defined Landau quasi-particles with a weak residual interaction proportional to the dimensionless parameter,
\lambda-\mu^{\star}
. Here,
\lambda\sim\lambda_{0}/(1+\lambda_{0})
, and
\mu^{*}
are renormalized versions of the dimensionless electron-phonon and electron-electron interactions. Formally, this “effective field theory” can be derived
Polchinski 1992
;
Shankar 1994
;
Carlson et al. 2002
from the original problem by integrating out degrees of freedom with energies larger than
\sim\hbar\omega_{D}
, so that
\hbar\omega_{D}
appears only as the high energy cuttoff of the theory.

There are a few subtleties concerning the BCS perscription for integrating out the phonons that are worth mentioning: i) As there are always acoustic phonons, it is not literally the case that all the phonons get integrated out in this process. They can thus be neglected. ii) Even at low

It is interesting to note that an essential piece of the evidence that the “mechanism” of pairing is a phonon-induced attraction between electrons comes from analyzing the energy dependence of the gap function,

More detailed information about the pairing mechanism is somewhat hidden in this BCS approach. In principle, the attractive interaction could come from something other than phonons. Indeed, this is
the sense in which we are suggesting that overdoped cuprates can be described by BCS theory:
it is clear that the pairing interaction in the cuprates

What would a BCS approach to the
cuprates entail?

BCS approach to the overdoped cuprates

Because the cuprates are strongly correlated, the theoretical route from the microscopic physics of copper and oxygen orbitals to an appropriate low energy effective model is difficult and will not be addressed here. Instead, we assume that there exists an intermediate energy scale that is large compared to
T_{c}
—possibly of order the exchange energy in the parent antiferromagnetic insulator,
J\approx 1500
K
J. Birgeneau et al. 2006

The quasi-particle properties may be strongly renormalized, and the residual interactions can encode the effects of important high energy physics, especially the existence of local moments on the copper sites. Importantly, however, because the effective theory is obtained by integrating out high energy degrees of freedom, the residual interactions can be taken to be short-ranged, and both the interactions and the quasi-particle renormalizations must be smooth functions of microscopic parameters, such as the doping concentration,
p

IV
Experiments on overdoped cuprates

Here, we review a body of experimental evidence that contains useful information concerning the applicability of the BCS perspective in overdoped cuprates. We summarize sets of experimental observations that most clearly support the proposed perspective and then those that are inconsistent with or apparently inconsistent with it. In each case, we present arguments that features that are inconsistent with the BCS perspective can be plausibly understood as fluctuation effects arising from the sort of inhomogeneous electronic structure expected for a disordered
d

Non-mean-field behavior of the gap

The persistence of superconducting gaps above

Absence of intertwined orders

A central feature of the cuprate phase diagram is the presence of multiple intertwined phases that all occur with comparable energy scales—antiferromagnetism, spin glass, charge density wave,
and d-wave superconductivity all appear at the few-tens-of-kelvin to a couple hundred kelvin scale

Chakravarty et al. 2001

;
Zhang 1997

Absence of intertwined orders in overdoped cuprates

The situation is greatly simplified in the overdoped regime. There, the various non-superconducting orders clearly weaken and plausibly disappear beyond a doping that depends on the particular order under consideration, as well as on the particular cuprate. For example, charge density wave order (with correlation lengths

Evidence of intertwined orders in overdoped cuprates

While there is no doubt the various forms of SDW and CDW stripe order seen in LSCO are strongest in underdoped LSCO, with increasingly careful studies it has become clear that weak correlations - but with much the same stripe structure - persist deep into the overdoped range of

VI
Falsifiable Consequences

While it is not possible to have a disorder-free material, it is possible to identify circumstances in which the amount of disorder is significantly suppressed. The principal empirical test of the perspective presented here is that deviations from the predictions of Fermi liquid theory for the low energy properties of the normal state, and of BCS mean-field theory for the superconducting state, should tend to be decreasingly pronounced in overdoped cuprates the weaker the disorder. Below, we list some of the experimental properties that could serve as particularly good indicators of such trends, and where possible, we mention some existing data that constitutes encouraging evidence of the expected tendencies.

•

T_{c}\sim(p_{max}-p)
