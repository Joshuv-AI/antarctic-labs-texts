Visual Acuity: Comparison of ETDRS Charts Using Latin Letters, Tumbling E, or Landolt C Optotypes
Authors: Michael Kalloniatis, Henrietta Wang, Kimberly Meier, Nimesh B. Patel, Jack Phu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeVisual acuity (VA) is a key outcome measure in clinical practice, epidemiological studies, and in clinical trials. However, letter-based charts may disadvantage individuals who are illiterate or are unfamiliar with Latin text, thereby limiting subject participation and complicating comparisons across studies. We compared VA using Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) charts for Latin letters, Landolt-C, or Tumbling-E optotypes to determine their association and measurement variability.MethodsSeventy-eight patients from an eye clinic underwent VA testing using the various optotypes on an ETDRS logMAR chart. VA was measured with a pinhole (PH), uncorrected, and corrected for the three optotypes, and compared with results in severe VA loss. Regression slopes, coefficients of determination, and limits of agreement (LoA) were determined.ResultsAll three optotype VAs showed a similar response to PH use, with an average improvement of approximately three logMAR lines. For uncorrected VA, Tumbling-E and Landolt-C showed slopes near 1.0 relative to letter VA across a broad range with R2 > 0.93. For corrected VA, slopes were 0.73 (Tumbling-E) and 0.58 (Landolt-C), with R2 ≈0.70. Combining our data with prior studies extended the range to 20/2000 for Landolt C and letter VA, yielding slopes near 1.0 and R2 > 0.94. LoA across comparisons ranged from logMAR ±0.12 to ±0.23.ConclusionsNon-letter optotypes provide clinically useful and broadly comparable measures of VA at the population level supporting their use in clinical practice or population/clinical studies. However, the wide variability means they cannot be considered interchangeable for individual subjects.

In clinical practice, accurate and reliable measurement of visual acuity (VA) is an essential component of a comprehensive eye examination; it is also an essential outcome measure in epidemiological studies and clinical trials.1–6 The procedure provides valuable information about the performance of the visual system, although the measurement reports on central, predominantly foveal function, and displays considerable variability.

The primary method used for clinical VA measurement is the use of eye charts, often based on the Bailey-Lovie logMAR principle.7,8 Such charts are also predominantly used in clinical trials.2,3 The Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) chart, based on logMAR design principles, provides more accurate VA measurements than traditional Snellen charts, especially when acuity is reduced due to ocular disease.9 It is also useful for comparisons against VA measured using other clinical techniques.10 The logMAR design standardizes acuity measurements by presenting the same number of letters on each line, typically 5, with each letter corresponding to 0.02 logMAR units. Because lines are scaled in equal 0.1 log-unit increments, the viewing distance may be adjusted without changing the relative resolution demand of the task.

VA is commonly measured with letter-based charts, but Latin-letter optotypes are not universally appropriate. Participants who are illiterate or whose native language does not use the Latin script may be unable to complete standard letter recognition tasks, creating a need for alternative optotypes. Some charts address this issue by substituting non-universal Latin characters with letters common to the Latin, Greek, and Cyrillic alphabets, consistent with logMAR chart-design principles.11 Landolt C and Tumbling E (sometimes called Illiterate E) provide additional alternatives because they reduce dependence on letter knowledge, instead requiring observers to report the orientation of a gap or optotype.

However, substituting optotypes also changes the psychophysical task. Letter charts typically require recognition among 10 Sloan letter alternatives, whereas Landolt C and Tumbling E charts are four-alternative forced-choice tasks with greater probability of correct guessing.7,8 In addition, Landolt C and Tumbling E should not necessarily be treated as equivalent: Tumbling E identification may be supported by the orientation of its bar structure, whereas Landolt C requires localization of a gap in circular contour. Thus, even when charts use a common logMAR or ETDRS format, standardization of size progression, spacing, and scoring does not ensure that the measured visual demand is identical across optotypes.

Prior studies suggest that VA measurements obtained with different optotypes may show acceptable mean agreement in some settings, but with substantial variability. A systematic review12 reported no systematic bias in distance VA measurements (mean difference of 0.03 logMAR, or 1.5 letters), whereas the 95% limit of agreement was approximately ±0.20 logMAR units, corresponding to approximately 4 acuity chart lines. Schulze-Bonsel et al.13 compared VA measured with ETDRS letters and Landolt C optotypes (using the FrACT test) over a range of approximately 0.5 to 2.0 logMAR (20/60 to 20/2000 Snellen) and found generally good agreement; however, there is insufficient data within the clinically common range of 0 to 1 logMAR (20/20 to 20/200 Snellen) for establishing associations among optotypes across the range from normal to severe visual impairment and to assess the variability of VA measurements.

This uncertainty limits interpretation across clinical trials and epidemiological studies that rely on or compare VA outcomes obtained with different optotypes.5 Non-Latin optotypes are used in many countries around the world, whereas those who are illiterate can detect the direction of the gap in the “C” or “E” optotype. Clinical trials that exclude non-English-speaking or illiterate subjects introduce potential biases by excluding these cohorts from the evaluation of new treatments. We aimed to compare VA measurements using Latin (letter), Tumbling E, and Landolt C optotypes, all within an ETDRS format, with and without correction of refractive error, and evaluate the impact of pinhole (PH) VA measurement.

We therefore addressed several enduring questions related to VA measurements, including VA variability. What is the impact of PH on VA measurements for the various optotypes? The results will provide guidance on a suitable cutoff point to identify the usefulness of this technique. What is the association between other optotypes (Landolt C or Tumbling E) and letter acuity (gold standard) when using uncorrected and corrected VA? The results will provide conversion factors, variability, and distribution limits for comparing non-letter optotypes with letter VA optotypes. How comparable are VA measures across a range of visual acuities, from normal to extreme vision loss, using different optotypes? This analysis will assess the usefulness of VA optotypes across a wide range of VA levels. Thus, the results from this study will guide the use of non-letter optotypes in clinical practice, including low vision care, population studies, and clinical trials, allowing the inclusion of a diverse range of subjects and not excluding those who are illiterate or unfamiliar with Latin-letter text.

Methods

All participants were recruited from patients presenting for visual assessment at the Centre for Eye Health (CFEH).14 The CFEH was a referral-only clinic in Sydney, Australia, providing diagnostic imaging and treatment services for patients suspected of having eye-related diseases. The patients present early in the disease process with a range of ocular conditions.14 The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethics approval was provided by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of New South Wales, Australia.

Seventy-eight subjects were recruited, with an average age and SD of 59.8 ± 14.6 years. The mean refractive error in spherical equivalent and SD was −0.89 ± 3.4 diopters. Inclusion criteria were: 18 years or older and able to read Latin letters. In some of the various manipulations, the total number of usable data points was fewer than 78.

The Medmont AT20P chart (Medmont International, Melbourne Australia) was used for all measurements using the ETDRS format with “Latin letters,” “Tumbling E,” and “Landolt C” optotypes. All three charts had five optotypes per line, using ETDRS scaling, with letter or optotype orientation randomly changing after each presentation. The average illuminance on a vertical plane at the patients’ location was 170 lux. The luminance of the monitor used to present the test chart was 57 cd/m2, yielding a retinal illuminance of approximately 1300 Trolands for a 5-mm diameter pupil, well within the photopic range of vision.6

Each participant underwent an assessment that began with a random allocation to one of the three optotype charts, followed by PH acuity testing for each chart. Uncorrected VA was measured without habitual correction (spectacles or contact lenses) using a descending method of limits. The test was stopped when errors were made on a single line. For example, if a subject made 2 errors on the 0.5 logMAR line, their VA score would be 0.54 logMAR, because each letter was assigned a logMAR score of 0.02. In effect, the approach is a variation of the “line assignment” and “letter-by-letter or ETDRS” approach.15 After determining the VA, the PH VA was determined for that chart using the same endpoint criterion. Testing continued with the second and, finally, the third VA chart. If the subject could not read the topmost line of the chart, the chart distance was halved from 4 meters to 2 meters or even closer if the top line could not be read at 2 meters. The measurements were repeated for the three optotypes when using the subject's habitual refractive correction to determine the relationship between corrected VA for the three optotypes.

To compare VAs at moderate to severe vision loss, data extraction (ETDRS versus Landolt C) from figure 3 in the study by Schulze-Bonsel et al.13 was performed using DataThief.16 The VA data were converted from Snellen fraction to logMAR notation and plotted against the empirically determined Landolt C data from the current study, which ranged from approximately logMAR 0 to 1.6. By combining the 2 studies, we provide a total range from 0 to 2.0 logMAR (20/20 to 20/2000).

Statistical analyses were carried out in R software (version 4.5.2) with the car package (version 3.1.3)17,18 and GraphPad Prism version 10 (La Jolla, CA, USA). For each task (letter, Tumbling E, and Landolt C), PH VA was modeled as a linear function of uncorrected VA using ordinary least squares regression, with separate models fit for each task. The 95% prediction intervals for individual observations were computed from the fitted models using the residual standard error (RMSE) and the leverage of the predictor. For the uncorrected and corrected analyses, to assess correspondence and potential systematic differences between tasks, ordinary least squares (OLS) regression models were fitted to predict letter acuity from Tumbling E and Landolt C acuity, separately. Intercepts were tested against 0 to assess evidence for constant group-level differences (bias) between tasks, and slopes were tested against 1 to assess evidence for systematic scaling differences (compression or expansion) between tasks. The R2 or adjusted R2 is reported, along with P values and F-test results for various conditions. A P value less than 0.05 was considered significant. All graphs were generated on GraphPad Prism.

We used the 95% prediction intervals to determine the upper limit and to assess PH and the associations between the various optotypes and uncorrected and corrected VA, providing estimates of variability at VA levels from logMAR 0 to 1.6. As an estimate of clinical variability in VA measurements, we can use the test-retest criteria reported by Vanden Bosch and Wall.15 We chose a criterion of one line (5 letters) as the significant cutoff, as Vanden Bosch and Wall15 found that the 95% confidence interval for test-retest variability was 3.5 letters for the letter-by-letter approach and 5 letters for the line assignment method. In addition, the one-line criterion was shown by Elliot and Sheridan19 to be a statistically significant VA change.

Correlation and regression results are inappropriate for comparing agreement between methods.20 We therefore also undertook the agreement between the methods using Bland-Altman analysis.20 We report on the mean (systematic bias) and SD of the difference, and the range of random error expressed as the 95% limits of agreement (LoA).

Results

PH VA shows a clear, clinically significant improvement over uncorrected VA, from 2.4 to 2.9 lines, and can be used as a screening tool to infer the refractive contribution. The range of acuity chart lines, as determined by regression and Bland-Altman analysis, is shown in the middle and lower panels of Table 1. As expected, there is substantial variation between the two approaches, as outlined in several papers by Bland and Altman.21,22 Thus, the use of the 95% prediction intervals is a more suitable way to describe the variability across the range of uncorrected VA rather than LoA. Supplementary Table S1 shows the mean and 95% upper and lower prediction intervals for all 3 optotypes. For all optotypes, an uncorrected logMAR value of 0.40 (20/50) will lead to an improvement after PH in 97.5% of cases (see Supplementary Table S1). At uncorrected VA better than logMAR 0.4, PH VA will simply reflect the variability expected by this relationship and is of questionable clinical significance.

The similar behavior of the three optotypes under PH conditions when measuring uncorrected vision suggests that their measurement outcomes provide comparable estimates; this will be investigated further in the following sections.

Association of Letter VA With Landolt C or Tumbling E Optotypes

Linear models predicting uncorrected letter acuity from uncorrected Tumbling E or Landolt C acuity are shown in Figures 2A and 2B and Table 2. All fits were excellent (adjusted R2 ≥ 0.94). The regression slopes did not significantly differ from 1 (t ≤ 1.29, P ≥ 0.202), indicating no evidence of systematic compression or expansion of VA values. Intercepts were small in magnitude and did not differ from zero (t ≤ 1.39, P ≥ 0.168), indicating minimal bias in mean estimates between tasks. The slopes for both were close to unity and highly significant (P < 0.0001), implying that uncorrected letter VA is predictable from either Tumbling E or Landolt C measurements at the population level. The functional implication is that, at the population level, uncorrected VA, either Tumbling E or Landolt C logMAR charts can be used and directly compared to letter acuity within the visual range of this study of logMAR 0 to approximately 1.6 (20/20 to 20/800). The equations in Table 2 can be used to calculate mean equivalent and 95% prediction intervals for letter VA from Tumbling E or Landolt C results (see Supplementary Table S2). The supplementary table provides easily accessible information useful to clinicians. The Bland-Altman analysis indicates minimal systematic bias between the two measurements (0.027 and −0.044 logMAR mean difference) and the LoA is approximately four lines for both, indicating reasonable agreement. Consequently, the logMAR range predicted by regression and Bland-Altman analysis largely coincides for uncorrected VA measurements (middle and lower panels in Table 2, see Supplementary Table S2).

We conclude that comparing VA data measured using refractively corrected Landolt C or Tumbling E optotypes and deriving letter acuity data requires a correction factor, as per the equations noted in Table 3. From the 95% prediction intervals, a corrected Tumbling E or Landolt C of logMAR 0 (20/20) will fall within approximately logMAR 0.1 (20/25) and thus within 5 letters (within test-retest variability of VA measurements15,19: see Supplementary Table S3). Therefore, any corrected VA measured with Tumbling E or Landolt C above logMAR 0 will require the correction factor (×0.71 for Tumbling E and ×0.58 for Landolt C) to derive corrected logMAR letter VA (as per Table 3, see Supplementary Table S3). However, for population studies, uncorrected VAs are directly comparable across the three optotypes, as noted above (see Table 2 for details), with prediction intervals allowing the range at any Tumbling E or Landolt C to be calculated. The Bland-Altman analysis indicates minimal systematic bias between the two measurements (−0.037 logMAR mean difference for both), and the LoA is approximately 2.4 lines for Tumbling E and 3.1 lines for Landolt C, indicating reasonable agreement (see also Supplementary Table S2). Consequently, the logMAR range predicted by regression and Bland-Altman analysis largely coincides for corrected VA measurements (see the middle and lower panels in Table 3). We also undertook linear regression analysis and Bland-Altman analysis of uncorrected and corrected VA for Tumbling E versus Landolt C optotypes (Supplementary Fig. S1; Supplementary Table S4). For both uncorrected and corrected VA, the linear regression slope was close to 1 and the LoA showing similar ranges to those of the letter versus non-letter optotypes.

We then wanted to determine the association over a broader VA range by combining the VA results from this study using Landolt Cs with published data on VAs from subjects with moderate-to-severe visual impairment.13

Combining the Landolt C Uncorrected VA Data With Data From Schulze-Bonsel et al.13 to Extend the Range to logMAR 2.0 (20/2000)

To confirm the association between Landolt C and letter acuity, we extracted the VA data from the Schulze-Bonsel et al.13 study and plotted all data on a single graph (Fig. 3). The FrACT method uses Landolt C and this VA measure was correlated to letter acuity by Schulze-Bonsel et al.13 VA in that study13 was measured in 100 eyes from 100 subjects after refractive correction and included patients with a range of eye diseases, resulting in visual acuities from approximately logMAR 0.5 to 2.0 (20/40 to 20/2000). Although the Schulze-Bonsel et al.13 data display a slope of 0.88, when combined with the current data, the overall slope is m = 0.98 (uncorrected VA from this study), and m = 1.01 for corrected (this study: see Figs. 3A, 3B) covering visual acuities from normal, logMAR 0 to approximately logMAR 2.0 (20/20 to 20/2000). Given the y-intercept is close to zero, we can conclude that when using Landolt C optotypes, VAs can be extrapolated to letter optotype acuity using a one-to-one transform, for population level VA comparisons. Further, we can infer that the same applies if Tumbling E optotype is used given the close to one-to-one relationship between letter VA and Tumbling E optotypes for uncorrected VA.

Letter VA versus Landolt C derived from the current study (using the corrected VA values, panel A, and corrected VA panel B) and the data from Schulze-Bonsel et al.13

The Bland-Altman analysis did not show a systematic bias between the two approaches (the mean difference and SD were −0.014 ± 0.117 and −0.011 ± 0.108 logMAR for the uncorrected and corrected analyses, respectively). The 95% lower and upper LoAs were (−0.24 and 0.22: uncorrected) and (−0.22 and 0.20: corrected), respectively, yielding logMAR unit ranges of 4.6 and 4.2, respectively, indicating reasonable agreement.

Discussion

VA measurement with various optotypes allows testing of this visual function in ways that align with the subject's linguistic or cognitive abilities. Latin letter VA measurement is a recognition task, typically using 10 letters (guessing correct rate of 1 in 10), whereas Tumbling E and Landolt C are 4-alternative spatial discrimination tasks, involving orientation judgments and gap localization, respectively.6 We found that the various optotypes behaved similarly (PH experiment) or showed an association well described by a slope of 1 (uncorrected letter VA with Tumbling E or Landolt C). The association between corrected letter VA and Tumbling E or Landolt C displayed a flatter slope, meaning that to predict corrected letter VA from either of the two optotypes requires a correction factor. We also established a slope-of-unity relationship between letter acuity and Landolt C from normal vision to approximately 20/2000 by incorporating the work of Schulze-Bonsel et al.,13 using either the corrected or uncorrected Landolt C results from this study.

VA Levels at Which Pinhole Acuity Provides Meaningful Information

The use of a PH increases the depth of focus of the eye, reducing the blur circle on the retina and thus improving VA, and it is useful for screening of refractive errors.23 In a large study of 1286 patients (2572 presenting to an eye department among eyes with VA of 20/40 or worse), 2104 (82%) showed improved VA with PH testing.24 This corresponds to approximately 96% of eyes responding to improved PH VA (2104 eyes) compared with eyes responding to refraction (2194 eyes). PH acuity also demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for identifying refractive error in a population-based study.25 In cases of media opacities, a PH may reduce light scatter, facilitating the prediction of VA post-cataract surgery.26 A nationwide survey27 found 3.1% of their 5937 participants had VA of logMAR 0.3 or worse, and PH acuity in approximately 60% of these improved more than would be expected by a 1:1 association. Although they did not report the correlation between uncorrected VA and PH VA, the distribution of their data appears qualitatively consistent with our findings.

We found that the relationships predicting PH VA did not differ significantly in their slopes or intercepts across all three optotypes. Thus, by averaging the slope and intercept for the three optotypes from Table 1, a simple equation of y = 0.584x–0.047 may provide a reasonable overall approximation, where “x” is the logMAR VA using the various optotypes and “y” is the PH logMAR VA. This equation may provide a useful population-level approximation of PH VA across optotypes, with wide LoA indicating that individual-level estimates should be interpreted cautiously. The Bland-Altman analysis indicated an almost three-line improvement across the different optotypes, although the LoA values were large and of limited use due to the flat slope and bias. Using the predictive intervals, all optotypes at logMAR 0.4 or 20/50 will return a PH VA at or at an improved level. At uncorrected VA better than 20/50, PH VA will operate within expected variability and is unlikely to provide useful clinical information.

Letter VA Prediction Using Tumbling E or Landolt C Optotypes

Landolt C optotypes are considered to provide equal difficulty to the 10 Sloan letters, and both optotypes are recommended for use rather than the Tumbling E optotype in children or illiterate adults.28 We found that both Landolt C and Tumbling E optotypes showed strong population-level associations with uncorrected letter VA, with slopes close to one (see Figs. 2A, 2B; Table 2). Consequently, we do not find evidence that the two optotypes (Tumbling E or Landolt C) are different in predicting letter VA when using uncorrected VA measurements. However, the size of the prediction intervals and the LoAs from the Bland-Altman analyses are in line with previous findings and indicate sufficient individual-level variability that direct substitution should be made cautiously.

Our results display a systematic departure from a slope of unity for predicted letter VA from either corrected Tumbling E VA or Landolt C VA (see Figs. 2C, 2D; Table 3). The near-unity slope for uncorrected acuity likely reflects a broad shared effect of refractive blur across optotype charts. After optical correction, acuity values are compressed into a narrower range near ceiling performance, which in turn reduces between-subject variability. This residual variability may depend more strongly on fine spatial processing, task-specific feature extraction, and measurement error. This may explain the shallower regression slopes observed in the corrected acuity relationships. Because relatively few participants had corrected VA worse than 0.3 logMAR, the resulting correction factors should be interpreted as best supported within the sampled corrected-VA range and should be validated in a larger cohort with ocular pathology and greater corrected acuity loss.

We note that conversion factors should not be viewed as fixed properties of the optotypes themselves. Although logMAR design principles standardize chart geometry, different optotypes place different demands on feature extraction and decision making. Thus, the relationship among letter, Tumbling E, and Landolt C acuity may depend on whether performance is limited primarily by optical blur, local resolution, orientation discrimination, gap localization, contrast sensitivity, or other disease-specific visual losses. This relationship may further be influenced by age and disease status, particularly because reductions in contrast sensitivity, less reliable sensory encoding, or impaired spatial integration may interact differently with the visual cues available in each optotype stimulus. Different optotypes also differ in cue redundancy and error structure: letters may be recognized from multiple partial cues, Tumbling E may be supported by global orientation information, and Landolt C requires more precise gap localization within a circular contour. Additional psychometric factors may also influence cross-chart relationships, including the number of response alternatives and chance guessing rate. The correction factors reported here should therefore be interpreted as empirical approximations for the tested population and testing conditions, rather than as evidence of complete psychophysical equivalence across optotypes.

Treacy et al.29 studied corrected VA in patients with and without ocular pathology, with 49% of eyes classified as healthy. They found the greatest discrepancy between Landolt C and letter acuity (0.10 logMAR), concluding that Tumbling E acuity was broadly comparable with ETDRS letter acuity and suitable for clinical research use in populations unfamiliar with the Latin alphabet, while cautioning against extrapolation to patients with poor VA. Our corrected VA findings confirm and extend this general conclusion, but add that conversion factors may be useful (see Table 3) for estimating corrected letter acuity from corrected Tumbling E or Landolt C. In contrast, for uncorrected VA, the near-unity slopes observed in our study suggest that non-letter and letter optotypes are comparable across the acuity range studied within population research, although agreement limits should still be considered for individual-level comparisons.

In children, non-letter optotypes may be required in early development. However, young children often have left-right confusion, leading to difficulties with optotypes requiring such discrimination.28 Picture charts have been used as an alternative for screening pre-literate children, but have been shown to overestimate letter acuity by as much as two to three lines.30 Similarly, Lai et al.31 reported improved VA with Tumbling E compared with Landolt C in children younger than 10 years, although the difference was small (logMAR 0.05 if Tumbling E was tested first and logMAR 0.03 if tested second). Similar results were reported by Lai et al.32 for 3 to 4-year-old children, but the difference in optotypes was not present in the 5 to 6-year-old group.

Bourne et al.5 state that, when combining VA data across different optotypes, the methods used must be clearly delineated. However, there is no mention of whether the various approaches using different optotypes are suitable for combining. Our study suggests that when logMAR design principles are used, VA data obtained with different optotypes are reasonably comparable to letter VA from the following studies: when using Landolt C33; presumably uncorrected with those worse than 3/30 examined by an ophthalmologist or Tumbling E,34 without, then with autorefraction correction35,36; with or without correction depending upon participant presentation37; uncorrected, habitual refractive correction and autorefraction). However, our results for corrected letter VA from Tumbling E or Landolt C VAs indicate that VAs at or worse than 20/20 require the application of the correction factor (see Table 3; Supplementary Table S3).

When we combined our results with those of Schulze-Bonsel et al.,13 we showed a slope of unity for VAs from normal to 20/2000 using the Landolt C optotype (see Fig. 3). We acknowledge that the comparison does not use raw data, our study and that of Schulze-Bonsel et al. used different methodology and our subjects had near normal corrected VA. Although combining our data with those of Schulze-Bonsel et al.13 extends the overall comparison across a broader acuity range, this does not fully substitute for validation of corrected-acuity conversion factors in a pathological cohort. The analysis adds to the usefulness of optotype comparison but requires further validation within a larger study with subjects with vision loss.

For all Bland-Altman analyses for corrected, uncorrected, and expanded VA ranges, there was no systematic bias, with LoA ranging from ±1.2 to ±2.3 lines, within the range reported by van der Zee et al.12 Because the observed cross-optotype differences are often similar in magnitude to typical VA measurement variability, small average biases should be interpreted cautiously. Although we also show high inherent variability between VA measurements using the different optotypes, the corrected VA for letters versus Tumbling E returned a variability of 2.4 lines, within the level suggested by clinical consensus of 3 lines.38 Consequently, we conclude that it should be possible for population-level comparison of Landolt C to letter VA across the range of VAs encountered in clinical practice and epidemiological studies, tempered with the knowledge of the variability of approximately 4.2 to 4.6 lines (Bland-Altman analysis using LoA).

Limitations of our study include the use of habitual pupils to measure VA, meaning that some of the variation in VA may be attributable to this variable, given that pupil size affects VA.39 Future studies would measure habitual pupil size and incorporate a pupil size cluster analysis to determine the impact of this variable on VA measurements, as well as different contrast levels. The number of subjects with corrected VA worse than 0.3 logMAR was small; therefore, the corrected-acuity conversion factors should be considered preliminary outside the sampled range. In addition, this study was not designed to determine how cross-optotype relationships vary as a function of specific ocular pathology, contrast sensitivity, sensory noise, spatial integration, age, or other testing conditions that may influence which visual features limit performance.

In summary, PH testing appeared to provide clinically meaningful information primarily when uncorrected VA was at logMAR 0.4 (20/50) or worse, with predicted improvement exceeding expected variability across optotypes. Uncorrected VA measurements were comparable at the group level across optotypes, and PH acuity showed similar relationships across optotypes. Corrected letter VA can be estimated from either Tumbling E or Landolt C optotypes using the appropriate correction factors, although individual-level prediction remains limited by the observed variability. By combining Landolt C measures from this study with those of Schulze-Bonsel et al.13 we also showed concordance with a slope of one across a broader acuity range. This finding supports the possible use in population-level comparison of letter acuity and Landolt C acuity over a range of logMAR 0 to 2 (20/20 to 20/2000). Across optotype comparisons, LoAs ranged from ±1.2 to ±2.3 lines, consistent with previous studies,12,38 reinforcing that VA should be interpreted in the context of the specific optotype, correction status, and testing conditions used.
