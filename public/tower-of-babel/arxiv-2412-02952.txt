Persistence/extinction scenarios in an almost periodic metapopulation with competition and habitat destruction
Pablo Amster, Gonzalo Robledo, Daniel Sepúlveda

Abstract
. We study an almost periodic version of a metapopulation model developed by Tilman et.al and Nee et.al in the nineties, which generalizes the classical Levins approach by considering several species in competition affected by habitat destruction. The novelty is to assume that the colonization and extinction rates are positive almost periodic functions whereas our main results show that the predominance of either colonization or extinction forces of a specific species is equivalent to the property of exponential dichotomy of a scalar linear differential equation. By using well known results of exponential dichotomy theory, we carry out a recursive and exhaustive description of persistence/extinction scenarios. In addition, we start a preliminary discussion describing a more elusive behavior when the colonization and extinction forces are similar in average.

1.
Introduction

The loss of habitat has multiple causes: expansion of agricultural land, exploitation of natural resources (wood, minerals, fisheries, aquifers), overgrazing, bad management of toxic wastes, uncontrolled wildfires, unregulated real estate expansion, desertification, coral reef degradation, etc,
which have a dramatic impact on the biodiversity declining. In fact, the loss of habitat is considered as the major threat to biodiversity

[
9
,
21
]
. Nevertheless, a detailed description of these effects remains to be done as well as the
modeling of these issues has been addressed from several perspectives. Within this context, the metapopulation theory has provided a fruitful framework for the study of the persistence or extinction of diverse populations affected by progressive habitat degradation.
In this article, we study a metapopulation model introduced by S. Nee
et.al

[
32
]

and D. Tilman
et.al

[
40
]
which retains the main features of the classical model introduced by R. Levins.

The theory of metapopulations
[
19
,
20
]
assumes the existence of fragmented habitats where
any habitable portion is called a
patch
. Now, all the species inhabiting a specific patch conform a
local population
and –roughly speaking– a
metapopulation
is a collection of local populations in a region.

According to P. Chesson
[
13
]
, a classical metapopulation has the following properties:

\bullet

The patches are partially isolated between them and are able of autosustaining for several generations in absence of colonization for other local populations.

\bullet

The local population extinction occurs in a time scale considering many generations.

\bullet

The migration between local populations leads to reestablishment of local populations following local extinction.

The metapopulation modeling has two time scales: a short scale only assumes that any patch is either occupied or unoccupied, and the internal demographic dynamics (births and deaths) of the patches is neglected. On the other hand, the dynamical balance between the processes of colonization and extinction takes place in a time scale encompassing several generations of demographical growth.

1.1.
The Levins model with competition and habitat destruction

The first generation of metapopulation models started with the seminal work of the mathematical ecologist Richard Levins
[
25
,
26
]
,
who coined the concept of metapopulation and described it by the ordinary differential equation:

(1.1)

p^{\prime}=cp(1-p)-mp,

where
p\in[0,1]
is the fraction of occupied patches by a single species. In addition
c>0
is the colonization rate and
m>0
is the extinction rate. If
p(0)\in(0,1]
the solutions of (
1.1
) have the following
behavior, which is determined by the dominance of either colonization or extinction forces:

\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}p(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\displaystyle 0&\textnormal{if}&c\leq m\\
\displaystyle p^{*}:=1-(m/c)&\textnormal{if}&m<c.\end{array}\right.

We point out that if
c\neq m
, the above equilibria
0
and
p^{*}
are hyperbolic and the convergence is exponential. On the other hand, a bifurcation occurs when
c=m
, namely, these equilibria collapse at zero and there is neither hyperbolicity nor exponential convergence.

The generalization of the Levins model has been carried out by a myriad of approaches and we refer the reader to
[
16
]
,
[
20
, Ch.4]
,
[
30
, Table 1]

for a detailed discussion and bibliography. In this article we will be focused on a specific extension
which considers a metapopulation encompassing
n
species in competition. For example, the following
model is considered in
[
12
]
:

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}p_{1}^{\prime}&=&c_{1}p_{1}(1-p_{1})-m_{1}p_{1}\\
p_{2}^{\prime}&=&c_{2}p_{2}(1-p_{1}-p_{2})-m_{2}p_{2}-c_{1}p_{1}p_{2},\end{array}\right.

where
p_{i}\in[0,1]
describe the fraction of the occupied patches by the

i
–th species. As before,
c_{i}
and
m_{i}
, respectively, are the colonization and extinction rates of each species. The system (
1.2
) deserves some remarks:

a)

The abundance of the species 1 is described by an equation similar to (
1.1
)
since it is not affected by the other one.

b)

The species 2 is unable to migrate a patch occupied by the species 1. Moreover,
if the species 1 invades a patch occupied by the species 2, this last species is immediately driven to extinction.

c)

By gathering the above statements, the species 1 and 2 are respectively called
superior

and
inferior
competitors.

d)

It is assumed that the colonization abilities of the species 2 are better than those of the species 1, that is
c_{1}<c_{2}
. For this reason, the species 2 is also called a
fugitive species
.

A generalization of (
1.2
) carried out in
[
32
]
considered habitat destruction, that is,
the existence of a fraction
h\in(0,1)
of habitable patches, which leads to

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}p_{1}^{\prime}&=&c_{1}p_{1}(h-p_{1})-m_{1}p_{1}\\
p_{2}^{\prime}&=&c_{2}p_{2}(h-p_{1}-p_{2})-m_{2}p_{2}-c_{1}p_{1}p_{2},\end{array}\right.

and a generalization for
n
species was carried out in
[
40
]
:

(1.4)

p_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}p_{i}\left(h-\sum_{j=1}^{i}p_{j}\right)-m_{i}p_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}c_{j}p_{i}p_{j},

where
p_{i}
represents the proportion of patches occupied by the
i
–th species with
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
c_{i}
and
m_{i}
are respectively the species-specific colonization and extinction rates. Similarly as in
[
12
]
and
[
32
]
, a fundamental assumption in
[
40
]

is the
competition–colonization trade–off
:

(1.5)

c_{1}<c_{2}<\ldots<c_{n},

that is, the colonization rate of the species tends to increase as its competitiveness declines. This leads to the fact that
the best competitor is the worst colonizer and vice versa. It is important to notice that the hierarchical structure of (
1.4
) allows
an easy identification of all its equilibria. In fact, by following an idea of
[
31
]
, we can rewrite (
1.4
)
as the generalized Lotka–Volterra system:

(1.6)

p_{i}^{\prime}=p_{i}\left(r_{i}(h)-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}p_{j}\right)\quad\textnormal{with $i=1,\ldots,n$},

where

r_{i}(h)=hc_{i}-m_{i}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad A_{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}c_{i}+c_{j}&\textnormal{if}&j<i\\
c_{i}&\textnormal{if}&i=j\\
0&\textnormal{if}&j>i.\end{array}\right.

By defining
A=\{A_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
and
r(h)=(r_{1}(h),\ldots,r_{n}(h))^{T}
,
a global study of (
1.6
) can be addressed for some configurations of the parameters space that give rise to
2^{n}
possible hyperbolic equilibria which describe scenarios ranging from to persistence of all species –the equilibrium
p_{1}^{\ast}=A^{-1}r(h)
– to the total extinction of the species, namely, the equilibrium
p_{2^{n}}^{\ast}=(0,\ldots,0)^{T}
. On other hand, the intermediate persistence/extinction scenarios are described by the positive/null components of the asymptotically stable equilibria
p_{i}^{\ast}=(p_{i1},\ldots,p_{in})^{T}
with
i\in\{2,3,\ldots,2^{n}-1\}
.

Furthermore, for restricted configurations of the space of parameters, a group of the above equilibria collapses into a non–hyperbolic equilibrium; this happens when the colonization forces of a specific competing species are equal to the extinction ones.

1.2.
The Levins model with almost periodic colonization and extinction rates

Among the so many generalizations of the Levins model, the assumption that -from a time scale encompassing several generations- the colonization and extinction rates can be considered constant has been weakened by using
a stochastic approach. In fact, Levins assumes in
[
25
]
that the extinction rate depends
randomly on the frequency of a gene in the population
whose average is
\overline{m}
and its variance is
\sigma_{m}^{2}
. When
c>\overline{m}+\sigma_{m}^{2}
it is proved the existence of
a modal distribution with a peak at
p=1-(\overline{m}+\sigma_{m}^{2})/c
. Nevertheless,
to the best of our knowledge, there are no generalizations from a deterministic point of view.

In this context, we will consider the Tilman’s model (
1.4
) with time varying colonization and extinction rates:

(1.7)

p_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}(t)p_{i}\left(h-\sum_{j=1}^{i}p_{j}\right)-m_{i}(t)p_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}c_{j}(t)p_{i}p_{j},\quad\textnormal{for $i=1,\ldots,n$}.

A crucial assumption of this work is that the colonization and extinction rates will be supposed to be positive almost periodic functions
c_{i},m_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\to(0,+\infty)
satisfying the additional condition:

(1.8)

\inf\limits_{t\in(-\infty,+\infty)}c_{i}(t)=c_{i}^{-}>0.

Let us recall that a continuous function

v\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is almost periodic in the sense of H. Bohr if, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a number

\ell_{\varepsilon}>0
such that any interval
of length
\ell_{\varepsilon}
contains a number
\tau
such that
|v(t+\tau)-v(t)|<\varepsilon
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and we refer the reader to
[
7
,
8
,
17
]
for a deeper treatment. For our purposes it will be enough to recall that almost periodic functions are bounded, uniformly continuous and have noticeable statistic properties. For example, its
average

is well defined by the following limit:

(1.9)

\mathcal{M}[v]:=\lim\limits_{L\to+\infty}\frac{1}{L}\int_{c}^{L+c}v(s)\,ds\quad\textnormal{uniformly for any $c\in\mathbb{R}$},

and we refer to
[
8
, pp. 39–44]
for details.

Furthermore, it will be assumed that the
competition–colonization trade–off
is satisfied from an average point of view:

(1.10)

0<\mathcal{M}[c_{1}]<\mathcal{M}[c_{2}]<\cdots<\mathcal{M}[c_{n}],

that is, the average of the colonization rate of the species tends to increase as its competitiveness declines.

It is worth to recall that, as the continuous
T
–periodic functions are a particular case of almost periodic functions,
it follows that if
c_{i}(\cdot)
is continuous and
T_{i}
–periodic, the above average takes a simpler form:

(1.11)

\mathcal{M}[c_{i}]:=\lim\limits_{L\to+\infty}\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}c_{i}(s)\,ds=\frac{1}{T_{i}}\int_{0}^{T_{i}}c_{i}(s)\,ds.

In addition, in case that the colonization rates
c_{i}(\cdot)
are
T_{i}
–periodic, the almost periodic framework will be
useful when
T_{k}/T_{j}\notin\mathbb{Q}
for some couple
k,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

The almost periodic system (
1.7
) deserves additional comments from a modeling, ecological and mathematical perspectives:

\bullet
From an ecological point of view, the system is
competitive
, that is, the growth of a species has a negative impact on the growth of any other species. From a mathematical point of view, this corresponds to the fact that all the off diagonal entries of the corresponding Jacobian matrix are negative or zero.

\bullet
From an ecological point of view, the system is
hierarchical
, that is, the species are ranked from
i=1

(best) to
i=n
(worst) according to its competitive ability. Notice that the dynamics of the
j
–th species is not affected by the
k
–th species with
k>j
.
From a mathematical point of view, the system is triangular, that is, its corresponding Jacobian matrix is lower triangular.

\bullet
From a modeling point of view, the autonomous system (
1.4
) has a strong assumption: the colonization and extinction rates are constant in big time scales. A more realistic assumption could be
to assume either deterministic or randomly time varying rates. In this modeling context, we studied the thought provoking works of M. Allais
[
1
]
and J. Bass
[
6
]
, in which it is explained how the almost periodic and another functions whose averages are well defined can be used in the simulation of random behavior. Furthermore, it is also important to emphasize that the almost periodic functions have not been considered in depth
for theoretical ecologists, despite of that, we think that they also provide interesting tools for describing cyclical behaviors
beyond the periodic one.

\bullet
From an ecological point of view, as in the autonomous case, the inferior competitors cannot colonize the patches occupied by the supperior competitors. This arises the following invariance result:

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
t\mapsto p(t)=(p_{1}(t),\ldots,p_{n}(n))
be a solution of the system (
1.7
). If

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(t_{0})\in(0,h)
then
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(t)\in(0,h)
for any
t\geq t_{0}
.

Proof.

See Appendix A.
∎

An essential difference with the autonomous case is that a qualitative study of a nonautonomous system as (
1.7
) cannot be addressed from an equilibria based approach since the persistence of some species could be not achieved under the form of an attractive equilibrium. This motivates to introduce the following definition:

Definition 1
.

A
non autonomous equilibrium
of the system (
1.7
) is a solution
\phi^{\ast}=(\phi_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\phi_{n}^{\ast})\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\sup\limits_{t\in(-\infty,+\infty)}|\phi_{1}^{\ast}(t)|+\ldots+|\phi_{n}^{\ast}(t)|<+\infty.

We stress that recent advances in the theory of non autonomous dynamical systems
[
24
]
have pointed out
that, instead to focus in the classical
static
equilibria, a global theory of non autonomous systems
can be carried out on the basis of the non autonomous equilibria, also called
globally bounded solutions
. In particular,
our main results will classify persistence/extinction scenarios described in terms of globally attractive non autonomous equilibria.

Until now, our description of metapopulation models has used intuitive notions of persistence and extinction. In order
to study the asymptotic behavior of (
1.7
) we will work with the following formal definitions:

Definition 2
.

The
i
–th species described in (
1.7
) is:

a)
Strongly uniformly persistent if there exists
\delta_{i}>0
such that the fraction of patches inhabited by the
i
–th species verifies

\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}p_{i}(t)\geq\delta_{i}\mbox{ for any non null initial condition.}

b)
Weakly persistent if the fraction of patches inhabited by the
i
–th species verifies

\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}p_{i}(t)>0\mbox{ for any non null initial condition.}

c)
Strongly driven to the extinction if the fraction of patches inhabited by the
i
–th species verifies

\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}p_{i}(t)=0\mbox{ for any non null initial condition.}

d)
Weakly driven to the extinction if the fraction of patches inhabited by the
i
–th species verifies

\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}p_{i}(t)=0\mbox{ for all non null initial condition.}

We point out that statements a) and b) from Definition
2
are inspired in
[
39
, pp.175–176]

and
[
41
, Def.3.4.2]
. On the other hand, the statements c) and d) are not standard in the literature but they allow highlighting that a species can be weakly persistent and
at the same time weakly driven to the extinction.

We will prove that the strong uniform persistence/extinction scenarios of (
1.7
) will be achieved under the form of a globally attractive positive almost periodic equilibria. In order to describe this convergence, we will use the little–o Landau’s notation
f(t)=\textit{o}(1)
to
say that
f(t)\to 0
when
t\to+\infty
. In addition, we will say that

f(t)
converges exponentially to zero if
f(t)=\textit{o}(1)
and there exist
\gamma>0
and a continuous map

\mathcal{K}\colon[0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
such that:

(1.12)

|f(t)|\leq\mathcal{K}(t_{0})e^{-\gamma(t-t_{0})}|f(t_{0})|\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geq t_{0}$}.

We stress that the convergence described by (
1.12
) depends on the elapsed time between

t
and
t_{0}
but also on
the initial time
t_{0}
. This type of convergence
is called
nonuniform exponential convergence
in the literature since the decay is exponential but it is not uniform with respect to the initial time
t_{0}
.

1.3.
Structure of the article

This article is on the intersection of theoretical ecology and the qualitative theory of nonautonomous differential equations. Moreover, the proof of our main results use methods and ideas which are current tools in nonautonomous dynamics but are far to be well known in theoretical ecology. In order to alleviate this problem, we believe useful to write a thorough Section 2 focused in providing a common basis for both research communities: the property of exponential dichotomy for the scalar case.

The sections 3 and 4 study the model (
1.7
) with
n=1
and
n=2
respectively and describes the possible scenarios of strong persistence/ strong extinction for the metapopulation. The case
n=2
illustrates a recursive procedure to determine necessary and sufficient conditions for strong persistence/extinction, which is briefly described in the section 5. It is important to emphasize that the above mentioned conditions
will be characterized in terms of the exponential dichotomy of a scalar linear equation combined
with
h\in(0,1)
as a bifurcation parameter.

The section 6 is devoted to explore some limit cases for
n=1
and
n=2
where the strong persistence/extinction scenarios are no longer verified. The resulting behaviors show that the weak extinction is achieved while the weak persistence could be achieved for some cases.

2.
Exponential dichotomy for scalar equations

From now on it will be very useful to emphasize that the first equation of (
1.7
) is of Bernoulli type, this implies that the change of variables
y=1/p_{1}
transforms it in a non-homogeneous equation of type:

(2.1)

y^{\prime}=\mathfrak{a}(t)y+\mathfrak{v}(t),

whose linear part is the equation

(2.2)

x^{\prime}=\mathfrak{a}(t)x.

The above mentioned change of variables combined with the hierarchical structure of the system (
1.7
) will allow us to carry out a recursive study of the qualitative properties of all and each one of the equations of system (
1.7
). Notice that
the behavior of the
i
–th species is determined by that of the competitively superior species, namely, the
j
–th species (with
j=1,\ldots,i-1
)
combined with the qualitative properties of the
i
–th equation of (
1.7
), which can be transformed into an equation of type (
2.1
).

A crucial observation will be that, at every stage, the balance between colonization and extinction forces is described by linear equations of type (
2.2
) and the predominance of one of these forces will be equivalent to the property of
exponential dichotomy
of (
2.2
).

There exists few applications of the exponential dichotomy theory in life sciences and we refer to
[
18
,
24
]
for seminal examples. Additional applications have been carried out for the study of Lotka–Volterra systems in
[
18
]
and for sensitivity analysis and total effects in trophic chains in
[
34
]
. This article can bee seen on this trend.

2.1.
Definition and properties

The exponential dichotomy
[
2
,
14
,
23
,
28
]
is a property of linear time varying ODE systems
which emulates the hyperbolicity condition of the autonomous case.
Surprisingly, there are few descriptions for the scalar case, which can be treated in a simple and self-contained manner. This is the goal of this section.

Definition 3
.

The equation (
2.2
) has an exponential dichotomy on the unbounded interval
J
if and only if there exist constants
C\geq 0
and
\alpha>0
such that

(2.3)

\left|\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr\right|\geq\alpha(t-s)-C\qquad\textnormal{for any $t\geq s$ with $t,s\in J$}.

By redefining the constant
C
if necessary, it is clear that Definition
3
always corresponds to one of the following situations:

a)

There exist constants
C\geq 0
and
\alpha>0
such that

(2.4)

\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr\leq C-\alpha(t-s)\qquad\textnormal{for any $t\geq s$ with $t,s\in J$}.

b)

There exist constants
C\geq 0
and
\alpha>0
such that

(2.5)

\int_{t}^{s}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr\geq\alpha(s-t)-C\qquad\textnormal{for any $s\geq t$ with $t,s\in J$}.

The above properties are equivalent to the standard definition of exponential dichotomy:

a’)

There exist constants
K\geq 1
and
\alpha>0
such that

(2.6)

e^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr}\leq Ke^{-\alpha(t-s)}\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geq s$ with $t,s\in J$}.

b’)

There exist constants
K\geq 1
and
\alpha>0
such that

(2.7)

e^{-\int_{t}^{s}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr}\leq Ke^{-\alpha(s-t)}\quad\textnormal{for any $s\geq t$ with $t,s\in J$}.

In fact, inspired in the standard definition, the case a) corresponds to the exponential dichotomy with the
identity projector
while the case b) corresponds to
the exponential dichotomy with the
null projector
.

In general, the intervals usually considered in the literature are

J=(-\infty,0]
,
J=[0,+\infty)
and
J=(-\infty,+\infty)
.

\bullet

If (
2.2
) has an exponential dichotomy on
J=[0,+\infty)
, the properties (
2.6
)–(
2.7
)
says that any solution either converges exponentially to zero (identity projector) or diverges exponentially (null projector) as
t\to+\infty
, since (
2.7
) is equivalent to
K^{-1}e^{\alpha(t-t_{0})}\leq e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr}
with
t\geq t_{0}\geq 0
.

\bullet

Similarly, if (
2.2
) has an exponential dichotomy on
J=(-\infty,0]
, the properties (
2.6
)–(
2.7
)
says that any solution either converges exponentially to zero (null projector) or diverges exponentially (identity projector) as
t\to-\infty
, since (
2.6
) is equivalent to
K^{-1}e^{\alpha(t_{0}-t)}\leq e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(r)dr}
with
t\leq t_{0}\leq 0
.

\bullet

If (
2.2
) has an exponential dichotomy on
J=(-\infty,+\infty)
then it also has an exponential dichotomy on
(-\infty,0]

and
[0,+\infty)
and inherits the above asymptotic behaviors at
\pm\infty
.

As we can see, if (
2.2
) has an exponential dichotomy on
J
, its solutions have
a
dichotomic
asymptotic behavior at
t\to+\infty
or
t\to-\infty
: they are either convergent or divergent at exponential rate. This qualitative asymptotic behavior is behind the name of exponential dichotomy.

Remark 1
.

As a consequence of the previous discussion we can see that, if the equation (
2.2
) has an exponential dichotomy on
(-\infty,+\infty)
then
the unique solution bounded on
(-\infty,+\infty)
is the trivial one. In fact, any other solution will be divergent
at
+\infty
or
-\infty
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The equation (
2.2
) has an exponential dichotomy on
[T,+\infty)
(resp.
(-\infty,-T]
) with
T>0
if and only
if it has an exponential dichotomy on
[0,+\infty)
(resp.
(-\infty,0]
).

Proof.

See Appendix A.
∎

A noteworthy fact is that a linear equation (
2.2
) can have an exponential dichotomy simultaneously on
(-\infty,0]

and
[0,+\infty)
but does not necessarily has an exponential dichotomy on
(-\infty,+\infty)
. For example, consider
\mathfrak{a}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\mathfrak{a}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}1&\textnormal{if}&t\leq-T\\
\phi(t)&\textnormal{if}&t\in[-T,T]\\
-1&\textnormal{if}&t\geq T,\end{array}\right.

where
T>0
,
\phi\colon[-T,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous such that
\phi(-T)=1
and
\phi(T)=-1
.
In fact, it is easy to see that (
2.6
) is verified on
[T,+\infty)

with
K=1
and
\alpha=1
, while (
2.7
) is verified on
(-\infty,-T]
with the same constants. Now, by Lemma
2.1

we have that
x^{\prime}=a(t)x
has an exponential dichotomy on
(-\infty,0]
and
[0,+\infty)
.
Nevertheless, by using Remark
1
we have that this equation has not an exponential dichotomy on
(-\infty,+\infty)
because any solution is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
. Finally, we stress that (
2.2
) has an exponential dichotomy on
(-\infty,+\infty)

if and only if it also has an exponential dichotomy on
(-\infty,0]

and
[0,+\infty)
with the same projector.

2.2.
Roughness, admissibility and spectra

The next result is known as the
roughness property
and shows that the set of bounded continuous functions such that an exponential dichotomy exists is open. More precisely,

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that (
2.2
) has an exponential
dichotomy on
[0,+\infty)
with constants
C
,
\alpha

and let

\mathfrak{e}\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function such that

\delta_{0}:=\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}|\mathfrak{e}(t)|<{\alpha}.

Then the perturbed equation

\dot{x}=[\mathfrak{a}(t)+\mathfrak{e}(t)]x

also has an exponential dichotomy on
[0,+\infty)
with constants
\tilde{C},\alpha_{0}
where

\tilde{C}>C
and
\alpha_{0}
is any constant satisfying the inequality

\alpha_{0}+\delta_{0}<\alpha
.

Proof.

See Appendix A.
∎

Now, let
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{D})
be a pair of function spaces,
this pair is said
to be
admissible
if for any
v\in\mathcal{D}

3.1.
Preliminiaries and main result

In this section, we will see that the strong persistence and/or strong extinction of the single species metapopulation described by (
3.1
) is determined by the properties of the linear part of (
3.2
), namely, the differential equation

(3.4)

v^{\prime}=a_{11}(t)v.

From an ecological point of view, the almost periodic equation (
3.4
) describes the balance between the opposing forces of colonization and extinction. The predominance of one of these forces is determined by the sign of the average
\mathcal{M}[a_{11}]
:

\bullet

The property
\mathcal{M}[a_{11}]<0
means that
h\mathcal{M}[c_{1}]>\mathcal{M}[m_{1}]
, that is, the colonization term
t\mapsto hc_{1}(t)
is dominant –in average– over the extinction rate
m_{1}(t)
. Hence, the strong persistence of the metapopulation is expected.

\bullet

The property
\mathcal{M}[a_{11}]>0
means that
h\mathcal{M}[c_{1}]<\mathcal{M}[m_{1}]
, that is, the extinction rate dominates –in average– over the colonization
t\mapsto hc_{1}(t)
and the strong extinction of the metapopulation is expected.

The above results also can be interpreted from an habitat destruction perspective by considering
the
habitability threshold
:

(3.5)

\mathcal{R}_{0}=\frac{\mathcal{M}[m_{1}]}{\mathcal{M}[c_{1}]},

which has been used by R.S. Etienne in
[
16
]
and P. Marquet in
[
30
]
. We will see that
\mathcal{R}_{0}
provides a lower bound
of habitable patches ensuring the persistence
of the metapopulation. In fact, it is straightforward to verify that:

(3.6)

\mathcal{M}[a_{11}]<0\iff h\mathcal{M}[c_{1}]>\mathcal{M}[m_{1}]\iff\mathcal{R}_{0}<h,

and

(3.7)

\mathcal{M}[a_{11}]>0\iff h\mathcal{M}[c_{1}]<\mathcal{M}[m_{1}]\iff h<\mathcal{R}_{0}.

The equation (
3.6
) means that the predominance of colonization over the extinction forces is equivalent to a fraction
h
of habitable patches larger than the habitability threshold
\mathcal{R}_{0}
. Analogously, (
3.7
) means that subordination of the colonization forces towards the extinction ones is equivalent to a fraction
h
of habitable patches inferior than the habitability threshold.

The next result will state that the strong persistence of the metapopulation is described by the existence of an almost periodic non autonomous equilibrium
t\mapsto p_{1,1}^{\ast}(t)
which is exponentially attractive while the strong extinction
is described by the exponential stability of the trivial solution
p(t)=0
. In addition, both possible scenarios are determined by the habitability threshold

\mathcal{R}_{0}
. The proof is based on the fact that the above mentioned equivalences (
3.6
)–(
3.7
) are also equivalent to the exponential dichotomy of the linear equation (
3.4
).

Theorem 3.1
.

If the colonization and extinction rates are such that
\mathcal{R}_{0}<h
,
then the fraction of occupied patches by the species 1 at time
t
, namely
t\mapsto p_{1}(t)
,
is strongly persistent and verifies an exponential convergence:

(3.8)

\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}|p_{1}(t)-p_{1,1}^{*}(t)|=0,

where

t\mapsto p_{1,1}^{*}(t)
is the almost periodic function:

(3.9)

p_{1,1}^{*}(t)=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{t}c_{1}(s)e^{\int_{s}^{t}a_{11}(r)\,dr}\,ds\right)^{-1}.

On the other hand, if the colonization and extinction rates are such that

h<\mathcal{R}_{0}
,
then the fraction of occupied patches decays exponentially towards zero when
t\to+\infty
.

Proof.

As we know by (
3.6
) that
\mathcal{R}_{0}<h
is equivalent to
\mathcal{M}[a_{11}]<0
, the statement a) of Proposition

2.4
says that the linear equation (
3.4
) has an exponential dichotomy on the real line
with the identity projector and constants
K\geq 1
and
\alpha>0
. Moreover, the statement a) of Proposition

2.5
says that the inhomogenous equation (
3.2
) has a unique almost periodic
solution
t\mapsto u^{*}(t)
described by

u^{*}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}c_{1}(s)e^{\int_{s}^{t}a_{11}(r)\,dr}\,ds,

while the Corollary
2.1
says that any other solution
t\mapsto u(t)
of (
3.2
) verifies

|u(t)-u^{*}(t)|\leq Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}|u(t_{0})-u^{*}(t_{0})|\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geq t_{0}$}.

By using (
1.8
) and the positiveness of
m_{1}(\cdot)
we can prove that

\inf\limits_{-\infty<t<+\infty}u^{*}(t)\geq\frac{c_{1}^{-}}{h||c_{1}||_{\infty}},

and by using Theorem 7 from
[
7
]
it follows that
t\mapsto p_{1,1}^{*}(t)=1/u^{*}(t)

is almost periodic. Moreover, as
p_{1}(t)=1/u(t)\leq h
, this implies that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|p_{1}(t)-p_{1,1}^{*}(t)|&=&\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{u(t)}-\frac{1}{u^{*}(t)}\right|\\
\\
&=&\displaystyle p_{1}(t)p_{1,1}^{*}(t)|u(t)-u^{*}(t)|\\
\\
&\leq&\displaystyle Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}|u(t_{0})-u^{*}(t_{0})|,\end{array}

then we can deduce

|p_{1}(t)-p_{1,1}^{*}(t)|\leq\frac{K}{p_{1}(t_{0})p_{1,1}^{*}(t_{0})}e^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}|p_{1}(t_{0})-p_{1,1}^{*}(t_{0})|

and the exponential convergence follows.

We point out that
(\ref{sol-periodique})
does not provide information
about whether or not
p_{1,1}^{*}(0)\in(0,h)
is satisfied. Nevertheless, let us recall that any non-constant almost periodic function has infinite growth and decay intervals since, otherwise, the function will have a limit, which is not possible. By using this fact combined with the differentiability of
p_{1,1}^{*}(\cdot)

t_{0}

which allows us to deduce that

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now, by (
3.7
) we know that that the strict inequality
h<\mathcal{R}_{0}

is equivalent to
\mathcal{M}[a_{11}]>0
. The statement b) of the Proposition

2.4
says that the linear equation
(
3.4
) has an exponential dichotomy on the real line with null projector, that is, there exist
K\geq 1
and
\alpha>0

such that

e^{\int_{s}^{r}a_{11}(\tau)\,d\tau}\leq Ke^{-\alpha(s-r)}\quad\textnormal{for any $r<s$},

then any solution
t\mapsto v(t)
of (

K^{-1}e^{\alpha(t-t_{0})}|v(t_{0})|\leq|v(t)|=e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}a_{1}(\tau)\,d\tau}|v(t_{0})|\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geq t_{0}$}.

The statement 2.- from Proposition
2.2
says that
the equation (
3.2
) has a unique solution bounded on
[0,+\infty)
. A careful reading of the proof, combined with the positiveness of

shows that this bounded solution
has a negative initial condition. In consequence, the solutions of (

are unbounded. We will explore in depth this fact:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}u(t)&=&\displaystyle u_{0}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}a_{11}(s)ds}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}c_{1}(s)e^{\int_{s}^{t}a_{11}(r)\,dr}\,ds\\

\\

now, as
p_{1}(t)=1/u(t)
, we can deduce that
0\leq p(t)\leq Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}

and the extinction of the metapopulation at exponential rate follows.

∎

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

be almost periodic with

and consider two bounded continuous functions
\mathfrak{b}\colon[0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
and
\psi\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
where
\psi(t)=\textit{o}(1)

there exists
\theta>0
such that any
solution of

(4.3)

verifies
\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}|u(t)|\leq\theta
.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]<0
, Proposition
2.4
says that the differential equation
x^{\prime}=\mathfrak{a}(t)x

has an exponential dichotomy on
\mathbb{R}
with the identity projector. Since this last equation also has an exponential dichotomy on
[0,+\infty)
with the same projector and

\psi(t)=\textit{o}(1)
, Proposition
2.1
says that
z^{\prime}=[\mathfrak{a}(t)+\psi(t)]z
also has an exponential dicothomy on
[0,+\infty)
with the identity projector, that is, there exist
K\geq 1
and
\alpha>0
such that

e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}[\mathfrak{a}(s)+\psi(s)]\,ds}\leq Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geq t_{0}\geq 0$}.

Let
\mathfrak{b}_{\infty}:=\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}\mathfrak{b}(t)
and note that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists

t_{0}
large enough such that:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|u(t)|&\leq&Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}|u(t_{0})|+K(||\mathfrak{b}||_{\infty}+\varepsilon)\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{-\alpha(t-s)}\,ds\\
\\
&\leq&Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}|u(t_{0})|+\frac{K}{\alpha}(||\mathfrak{b}||_{\infty}+\varepsilon)(1-e^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}).\end{array}

Finally, the result follows with
\theta:=(K||\mathfrak{b}||_{\infty}+\varepsilon)/\alpha
by taking the upper limit.

∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let us consider the equation

(4.4)

v^{\prime}=\mathfrak{a}(t)v+\mathfrak{b}(t)\quad\textnormal{with $t\geq 0$},

where
\mathfrak{a}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is almost periodic with
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]<0
and

\mathfrak{b}\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function. If
\mathfrak{b}(t)=\textit{o}(1)
,
then
v(t)=\textit{o}(1)
. Moreover, if
\mathfrak{b}(\cdot)
converges exponentially, then
v(\cdot)
also converges exponentially.

Proof.

As
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]<0
, Proposition
2.4
says that the differential equation
x^{\prime}=\mathfrak{a}(t)x

has an exponential dichotomy on
\mathbb{R}
and
[0,+\infty)
with the identity projector, that is, there exists
K\geq 1
and
\alpha>0
such that

e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(s)\,ds}\leq Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geq t_{0}\geq 0$}.

Firstly, assume that
\mathfrak{b}(\cdot)
decays exponentially, that is, there exists
\mathcal{K}\colon[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
and
\gamma>0
such that:

|\mathfrak{b}(t)|\leq\mathcal{K}(t_{0})e^{-\gamma(t-t_{0})}\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geq t_{0}\geq 0$},

and, without loss of generality, it can be assumed that
\alpha\neq\gamma
.

Now, let us note that any solution of (
4.4
) verifies

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|v(t)|&\leq&Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}|v(t_{0})|+K\mathcal{K}(t_{0})\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{-\alpha(t-s)}e^{-\gamma(s-t_{0})}\,ds\\
\\
&\leq&Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}|v(t_{0})|+K\mathcal{K}(t_{0})e^{-\alpha t}e^{\gamma t_{0}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{(\alpha-\gamma)s}\,ds\\
\\
&\leq&Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}|v(t_{0})|+\frac{K\mathcal{K}(t_{0})}{\alpha-\gamma}e^{-\alpha t}e^{\gamma t_{0}}\left\{e^{(\alpha-\gamma)t}-e^{(\alpha-\gamma)t_{0}}\right\}\\
\\
&\leq&Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}|v(t_{0})|+\frac{K\mathcal{K}(t_{0})}{|\alpha-\gamma|}\left(e^{-\gamma(t-t_{0})}-e^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}\right).\end{array}

Finally, we will prove the general case
b(t)=\textit{o}(1)
: As
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]<0
, it follows by Proposition
2.4

that the inequality (
2.4
) is verified with constants

and
\alpha>0
. Then by choosing

large enough, for any

\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr\leq C-\alpha(t-s)\leq\frac{C}{T}(t-s)-\alpha(t-s),

and there exists

such that
\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\tau)\,d\tau<-c(t-s)<0
whenever

. Next, given

we may fix

such that

for

, with

to be specified. Note that any solution of (
4.4
) can be written as follows

v(t)=v(t_{0})e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\tau)\,d\tau}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathfrak{b}(s)e^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\tau)\,d\tau}\,ds,

and we can deduce that

\left|v(t)-v(t_{0})e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\tau)\,d\tau}\right|\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\tau)\,d\tau}ds.

Next, for
t
large enough we may write

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\tau)\,d\tau}\,ds&=&\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t-T}e^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\tau)\,d\tau}\,ds+\int_{t-T}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\tau)\,d\tau}ds\\
\\
&\leq&\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t-T}e^{-c(t-s)}ds+\int_{t-T}^{t}e^{\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{\infty}T}ds\\
\\
&\leq&\frac{1}{c}+Te^{\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{\infty}T}.\end{array}

Setting
\tilde{\varepsilon}
such that

\tilde{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1}{c}+Te^{\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{\infty}T}\right)\leq\varepsilon

and passing to the limit and recalling that
e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\tau)\,d\tau}=\textit{o}(1)
, it is seen that

\limsup\limits_{t\to\infty}|v(t)|\leq\varepsilon
and the Lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be almost periodic with
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]>0
and consider two bounded continuous functions
\mathfrak{c}\colon[0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
and
\phi\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
where
\phi(t)=\textit{o}(1)
, then
there exists constants
K>0
and
\alpha_{0}>0
such that any
solution
t\mapsto v(t)
of

(4.5)

v^{\prime}=[\mathfrak{a}(t)+\phi(t)]v+\mathfrak{c}(t)\quad\textnormal{with $t\geq 0$}

with
v(t_{0})\geq 1
verifies
v(t)\geq K^{-1}e^{\alpha_{0}(t-t_{0})}
.

Proof.

As
\mathfrak{a}(\cdot)
is almost periodic and
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]>0
, the statement b) of Proposition
2.4
allows us to deduce
the existence of
C\geq 1
and
\alpha>0
such that

e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\tau)\,d\tau}\geq C^{-1}e^{\alpha(t-t_{0})}\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geq t_{0}$}.

In addition, as
\phi(t)=\textit{o}(1)
, the Proposition
2.1
allow us to deduce the existence of

K\geq 1
and
\alpha_{0}\in(0,\alpha)
such that

e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}[\mathfrak{a}(s)+\phi(s)]}\geq K^{-1}e^{\alpha_{0}(t-t_{0})}\quad\textnormal{for any $t>t_{0}\geq 0$}.

The above estimation, combined with the positiveness of
\mathfrak{c}(\cdot)
implies that, for any
t>t_{0}\geq 0
, the solutions of (
4.5
) with
v(t_{0})=v_{0}\geq 1
verify:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}v(t)&=&\displaystyle v_{0}e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}[\mathfrak{a}(s)+\phi(s)]\,ds}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathfrak{c}(s)e^{\int_{s}^{t}[\mathfrak{a}(r)+\phi(r)]\,dr}\,ds\\
\\
&\geq&\displaystyle K^{-1}e^{\alpha_{0}(t-t_{0})}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathfrak{c}(s)e^{\int_{s}^{t}[\mathfrak{a}(r)+\phi(r)]\,dr}\,ds\\
\\
&\geq&\displaystyle K^{-1}e^{\alpha_{0}(t-t_{0})},\end{array}

and the Lemma follows.
∎

4.2.
Strong persistence/extinction scenarios when

.

In order to

) will be studied with the help of the almost periodic equation

\dot{z}=a_{21}(t)z\quad\textnormal{with}\quad a_{21}(t)=-[hc_{2}(t)-m_{2}(t)-\{c_{1}(t)+c_{2}(t)\}p_{1,1}^{*}(t)],

hc_{2}(t)
and

m_{2}(t)
with an additive positive perturbation

is given by (
3.9
). Moreover, by (

) and (
4.6
)

\mathcal{M}[c_{2}]

(4.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{M}[a_{21}]<0&\iff&h\mathcal{M}[c_{2}]-\mathcal{M}[m_{2}]-\mathcal{M}\left[(c_{1}+c_{2})p_{1,1}^{*}\right]>0,\\

&\iff&\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1,1}^{\ast}:=\mathcal{R}_{1}+\underbrace{\frac{\mathcal{M}[(c_{1}+c_{2})p_{1,1}^{\ast}]}{\mathcal{M}[c_{2}]}}_{:=\ell_{1,1}}<h,\end{array}\right.

and

(4.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{M}[a_{21}]>0&\iff&h\mathcal{M}[c_{2}]-\mathcal{M}[m_{2}]-\mathcal{M}\left[(c_{1}+c_{2})p_{1,1}^{*}\right]<0,\\
\\
&\iff&\displaystyle\mathcal{M}[c_{2}]\left(h-\mathcal{R}_{1}-\frac{\mathcal{M}[(c_{1}+c_{2})p_{1,1}^{\ast}]}{\mathcal{M}[c_{2}]}\right)<0,\\
\\
&\iff&\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1,1}^{\ast}:=\mathcal{R}_{1}+\underbrace{\frac{\mathcal{M}[(c_{1}+c_{2})p_{1,1}^{\ast}]}{\mathcal{M}[c_{2}]}}_{:=\ell_{1,1}}>h.\end{array}\right.

Theorem 4.1

(Global persistence)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}[c_{1}]<\mathcal{M}[c_{2}]
if verified. If the fraction
h
of habitable patches together with the colonization and extinction rates are such that the inequality

\max\left\{\mathcal{R}_{0},\mathcal{R}_{1,1}^{\ast}\right\}<h,

is also verified, then the metapopulation (
4.1
) has a unique positive almost periodic solution
t\mapsto(p_{1,1}^{*}(t),p_{2,1}^{*}(t))
, where
t\mapsto p_{2,1}^{*}(t)
is defined by:

(4.9)

p_{2,1}^{*}(t)=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}a_{21}(r)\,dr}c_{2}(s)\,ds\right)^{-1}.

Furthermore, for every solution
t\mapsto(p_{1}(t),p_{2}(t))
of (
4.1
)–(
4.2
) it follows that

\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}|p_{1}(t)-p_{1,1}^{*}(t)|+|p_{2}(t)-p_{2,1}^{*}(t)|=0,

and the convergence is exponential.

Proof.

The proof will be divided into several steps.

Step 1: Existence of an almost periodic solution.
As

\mathcal{R}_{0}<h
, by Theorem
3.1
we have that
t\mapsto p_{1,1}^{*}(t)
is the unique positive almost periodic solution of the first equation of (
4.1
). When replacing

t\mapsto p_{1}(t)
by
t\mapsto p_{1,1}^{*}(t)
in the second equation, we obtain:

(4.10)

p_{2}^{\prime}=\displaystyle p_{2}\left\{c_{2}(t)\left(h-p_{2}-p_{1,1}^{*}(t)\right)-m_{2}(t)-c_{1}(t)p_{1,1}^{*}(t)\right\}.

The existence of a positive and exponentially attractive almost periodic solution of (
4.10
)
can be deduced analogously as in the proof of the Theorem
3.1
by the transformation
u=1/p_{2}
, which leads to

(4.11)

u^{\prime}=a_{21}(t)u+c_{2}(t).

Indeed, we know by (
4.7
) that
\mathcal{R}_{0}<h
is equivalent to
\mathcal{M}[a_{21}]<0
and Proposition
2.4
implies that the equation (
4.6
) has an exponential dichotomy on
\mathbb{R}
with the identity projector. That is,
there exist
K\geq 1
and
\alpha>0
such that

(4.12)

e^{\int_{t_{0}}^{t}a_{21}(s)\,ds}\leq Ke^{-\alpha(t-t_{0})}\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geq t_{0}$},

and, by Proposition
2.5
, there exists
a unique positive almost periodic solution
t\mapsto u^{*}(t)
of (
4.11
). This implies that

t\mapsto p_{2,1}^{*}(t)=1/u^{*}(t)
is an almost periodic solution of (
4.10
) and thus we conclude that
t\mapsto(p_{1,1}^{*}(t),p_{2,1}^{*}(t))
is an almost periodic solution of (
4.1
).

Step 2: Uniform persistence of the species 2.
The second equation of (
4.1
) reads:

p_{2}^{\prime}=p_{2}\left\{c_{2}(t)\left(h-p_{2}\right)-m_{2}(t)-[c_{1}(t)+c_{2}(t)]p_{1}(t)\right\},

which, by using
u=1/p_{2}
, can be transformed into

(4.13)

\dot{u}=b_{21}(t)u+c_{2}(t)\,\,\textnormal{where}\,\,b_{21}(t)=a_{21}(t)+[c_{1}(t)+c_{2}(t)]\{p_{1}(t)-p_{1,1}^{*}(t)\}.

Now, let us define
\phi(t):=[c_{1}(t)+c_{2}(t)]\{p_{1}(t)-p_{1,1}^{*}(h,t)\}
and note that the linear part of (
4.13
) is:

\dot{z}=b_{21}(t)z\quad\textnormal{or}\quad\dot{z}=[a_{21}(t)+\phi(t)]z,

where
\phi(t)=\textit{o}(1)
since
p_{1}(t)-p_{1,1}^{*}(t)=\textit{o}(1)
and
the colonization rates are bounded functions. This fact combined with
\mathcal{M}[a_{21}]<0

and Lemma
4.1
implies the existence
\theta>0
such that any solution
t\mapsto u(t)
of (
4.13
) verifies

\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}u(t)\leq\theta\quad\textnormal{or equivalently}\quad\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}p_{2}(t)\geq\theta^{-1},

and the strong uniform persistence of
p_{2}
follows.

Step 3: End of proof.

Let
u^{*}(t)=1/p_{2,1}^{*}(t)
and
u(t)=1/p_{2}(t)
. We define the auxiliary function
v(t):=u(t)-u^{*}(t)
and note that:

v^{\prime}=a_{21}(t)v+\phi(t)u(t),

where the function
u(\cdot)\phi(\cdot)=\textit{o}(1)
defined above also converges exponentially because

p_{1}(\cdot)-p_{1,1}^{*}(\cdot)=\textit{o}(1)
converges exponentially and
t\mapsto u(t),c_{1}(t),c_{2}(t)
are bounded.
By using this fact combined with
\mathcal{M}[a_{21}]<0
and Lemma
4.2
we can deduce that
u(t)-u^{*}(t)=\textit{o}(1)
and the convergence is exponential. Finally, note that

|p_{2}(t)-p_{2,1}^{\ast}(t)|=\frac{|u(t)-u^{\ast}(t)|}{u(t)u^{\ast}(t)}\leq h^{2}|u(t)-u^{\ast}(t)|,

which concludes the proof.
∎

The above result deserves some remarks:

Firstly, under the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
, a simple computation arising from (
4.6
) implies that the
averages of
t\mapsto p_{1,1}^{\ast}(t)
and
t\mapsto p_{2,1}^{\ast}(t)
verify the trade off condition:

(4.14)

\mathcal{M}[(c_{1}+c_{2})p_{1,1}^{\ast}]+\mathcal{M}[c_{2}p_{2,1}^{\ast}]=\mathcal{M}[c_{2}](h-\mathcal{R}_{1}),

then we can see that an increasing of the fraction of patches occupied by species 1 implies a declining of those occupied by the species 2 and vice versa. We point out that this fact is consistent with the competitive structure of the system (
4.1
).

Secondly, we know that the natural habitability threshold condition
\mathcal{R}_{1}<h

ensures the strong persistence of the second species in absence of competition. Nevertheless, this condition is not enough to guarantee the uniform strong persistence under the presence of a superior competitor in some cases. Indeed, Theorem
4.1
states that if the patches occupied by the superior competitor converges to a positive almost periodic fraction, then a necessary condition to ensure the strong persistence of the species 2 is given by
\mathcal{R}_{1,1}^{\ast}=\mathcal{R}_{1}+\ell_{1,1}<h
. In this context, and emulating the literature of the autonomous framework
[
10
,
22
,
31
]
, the interval
(\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{1}+\ell_{1,1})
will be called as the
exclusion interval
or
niche shadow
. The length of this is interval, namely
\ell_{1,1}
, is a constant
dependent of
h
and
p_{1,1}^{\ast}(\cdot)
and allow to see the threshold condition (
4.14
) from another perspective.

Finally, the niche shadow condition
h\in(\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{R}_{1}+\ell_{1,1})
must imply the extinction
of the second species, which is discussed in the following result:

Theorem 4.2

(Best competitor persists)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}[c_{1}]<\mathcal{M}[c_{2}]
is verified. If the fraction of habitable patches together with the colonization and extinction rates are such that the inequalities

4.1
)–(
4.2
), namely

\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}|p_{1}(t)-p_{1,1}^{*}(t)|+|p_{2}(t)|=0.

As
\mathcal{R}_{0}<h
, we know by Theorem
3.1
that
t\mapsto p_{1,1}^{*}(t)
is the unique non-trivial almost periodic solution of the first equation of (
4.1
) which attracts any solution

p_{1}(t)
at exponential rate. Now, the proof follows by proving that any component
p_{2}(t)
converges exponentially to
zero.

We know that the second equation of (
4.1
) can be transformed
via
v=1/p_{2}
in the inhomogeneous equation

(4.15)

v^{\prime}=[a_{21}(t)+\phi(t)]v+c_{2}(t),

where
t\mapsto a_{21}(t)
is defined by (
4.6
) and

\phi(t)=[c_{1}(t)+c_{2}(t)]\{p_{1}(t)-p_{1,1}^{*}(t)\}
, which is convergent to zero since

Moreover, as

4.8
) we have that
\mathcal{M}[a_{21}]>0
. In
addition, as
a_{21}(\cdot)

4.3

implies that

now, as

∎

5.1.
The general case

We will describe a formal procedure in several steps:

Step 1:

If
n=k-1
, let us assume that the value of
h\in(0,1)
implies that for any solution
t\mapsto p(t)=(p_{1}(t),\ldots,p_{k-1}(t))
of the system

(5.1)

p_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}(t)p_{i}\left(h-\sum_{j=1}^{i}p_{j}\right)-m_{i}(t)p_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}c_{j}(t)p_{i}p_{j},\quad\textnormal{with $i=1,\ldots,k-1$},

with
p_{1}(0)+\ldots+p_{k-1}(0)\in(0,h)
, there exists a unique
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,2^{k-1}\}

and
k-1
nonnegative almost periodic functions
t\mapsto p_{j,\ell}^{\ast}(t)
with
j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
such that

\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}|p_{1}(t)-p_{1,\ell}^{\ast}(t)|+\ldots+|p_{k-1}(t)-p_{k-1,\ell}^{\ast}(t)|=0,

and the convergence is exponential.

In other words, we are assuming the existence of
2^{k-1}
scenarios of strong persistence/extinction described by the almost periodic functions
t\mapsto p_{\ell}^{\ast}(t)
:

p_{\ell}^{*}(t)=(p_{1,\ell}^{*}(t),p_{2,\ell}^{*}(t),\ldots,p_{k-1,\ell}^{*}(t))\quad\textnormal{where $\ell\in\{1,\ldots,2^{k-1}\}$},

and any component
t\mapsto p_{j,\ell}^{\ast}(t)
is either
zero or a positive almost periodic function.

Step 2:

If
n=k
, the previous step ensures that the behavior of the first
k-1
components of the system

(5.2)

p_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}(t)p_{i}\left(h-\sum_{j=1}^{i}p_{j}\right)-m_{i}(t)p_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}c_{j}(t)p_{i}p_{j},\quad\textnormal{with $i=1,\ldots,k$},

is well known, while the dynamics of the last equation

(5.3)

p_{k}^{\prime}=c_{k}(t)p_{k}\left(h-p_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}p_{j}\right)-m_{k}(t)p_{k}-p_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}c_{j}(t)p_{j},

remains to be studied. In order to do that, we know by the previous step that for any solution

p(t)=(p_{1}(t),\ldots,p_{k-1}(t),p_{k}(t))
of (
5.2
), there exists a unique

\ell\in\{1,\ldots,2^{k-1}\}
such that

p_{j}(t)-p_{j,\ell}^{\ast}(t)=\textit{o}(1)\quad\textnormal{where $j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}$},

and the convergence is exponential. This allows us to rewrite the equation (
5.3
) as follows:

(5.4)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}p_{k}^{\prime}&=&\displaystyle c_{k}(t)p_{k}\left(h-p_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}[p_{j,\ell}^{\ast}(t)+\textit{o}(1)]\right)-m_{k}(t)p_{k}\\
&&\displaystyle-p_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}c_{j}(t)[p_{j,\ell}^{\ast}(t)+\textit{o}(1)],\end{array}

and we conclude that (
5.3
) is asymptotically
equivalent to the almost periodic scalar equation:

(5.5)

p_{k}^{\prime}=c_{k}(t)p_{k}\left(h-p_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}p_{j,\ell}^{\ast}(t)\right)-m_{k}(t)p_{k}-p_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}c_{j}(t)p_{j,\ell}^{\ast}(t).

Step 3:
We know that the change of variables

u_{k}=1/p_{k}
transforms (
5.5
) into the almost periodic inhomogeneous equation:

(5.6)

u_{k}^{\prime}=\underbrace{-\left[hc_{k}(t)-m_{k}(t)-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\{c_{k}(t)+c_{j}(t)\}p_{j,\ell}^{\ast}(t)\right]}_{:=a_{k\ell}(t)}u_{k}+c_{k}(t).

By Proposition
2.4
we know that the linear part of the above equation has an exponential dichotomy on
\mathbb{R}
if and only if

\mathcal{M}[a_{k\ell}]<0\iff h>\mathcal{R}_{k-1,\ell}^{\ast}\quad\textnormal{or}\quad\mathcal{M}[a_{k\ell}]>0\iff h<\mathcal{R}_{k-1,\ell}^{\ast},

where the habitability threshold
\mathcal{R}_{k,\ell}^{\ast}
is defined by

\mathcal{R}_{k-1,\ell}^{\ast}:=\mathcal{R}_{k-1}+\ell_{k-1,\ell}\quad\textnormal{with}\quad\ell_{k-1,\ell}=\frac{\mathcal{M}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\{c_{k}(t)+c_{j}(t)\}p_{j,\ell}^{\ast}(t)\right]}{\mathcal{M}[c_{k}]}.

By Proposition
2.5
and Corollary
2.1
we know that if
\mathcal{M}[a_{k\ell}]<0
then (
5.6
) has a unique almost periodic solution described by

(5.7)

u_{k,\ell}^{\ast}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\int_{s}^{t}a_{k\ell}(\tau)\,d\tau}c_{k}(s)\,ds,

while any other solution
t\mapsto u_{k}(t)
verifies
|u_{k}-u_{k}^{\ast}|=\textit{o}(1)
and the convergence
is exponential.

Step 4:
Similarly, we know that the change of variables

v_{k}=1/p_{k}
transforms (
5.4
) into the inhomogeneous asymptotically almost periodic equation:

(5.8)

v_{k}^{\prime}=-[a_{k\ell}(t)+\psi(t)]v_{k}+c_{k}(t),

where the function
\psi(t)=\textit{o}(1)
converges exponentially.

Case A:

\mathcal{M}[a_{k\ell}]<0
, the Lemma
4.1
implies the existence
\theta>0
such that any solution
t\mapsto v_{k}(t)
of (
5.8
) verifies

\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}v_{k}(t)\leq\theta\quad\textnormal{or equivalently}\quad\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}p_{k}(t)\geq\theta^{-1},

and the strong uniform persistence of
p_{k}
follows. Now, as
u_{k,\ell}^{*}(t)=1/p_{k,\ell}^{*}(t)
and
v(t)=1/p_{k}(t)
, we define the auxiliary function
w(t):=v_{k}(t)-u_{k,\ell}^{*}(t)
and note that:

w^{\prime}=-a_{k\ell}(t)w+\psi(t)v_{k}(t).

As
t\mapsto v_{k}(t)
is bounded on
[0,+\infty)
we have that
t\mapsto\psi(t)v_{k}(t)=\textit{o}(1)
and the

\psi(t)
convergence is exponential. By using this fact combined with
\mathcal{M}[a_{k\ell}]>0
, the Lemma
4.2

implies that
v_{k}(t)-u_{k,\ell}^{*}(t)=\textit{o}(1)
and the convergence is exponential. Finally, it can be deduced that

|p_{k}(t)-p_{k,\ell}^{\ast}(t)|=p_{k}(t)p_{k,\ell}^{*}(t)|v_{k}(t)-u_{k,\ell}^{*}(t)|<|v_{k}(t)-u_{k,\ell}^{*}(t)|=\textit{o}(1),

then we have that any solution of (
5.3
) converges exponentially to
p_{k,\ell}^{\ast}(\cdot)
and the
strong persistence of the
k
–th species in ensured.

Case B:

\mathcal{M}[a_{k\ell}]>0
, the Lemma
4.3
implies the existence
K>0

and
\alpha_{0}>0

such that any solution
t\mapsto v_{k}(t)
of (
5.8
) verifies
v_{k}(t)>K^{-1}e^{\alpha(t-t_{0})}
, which implies that
any solution
t\mapsto p_{k}(t)
of (
5.3
) verifies
p_{k}(t)\leq Ke^{-\alpha_{0}(t-t_{0})}
and the
strong persistence of the
k
–th species in ensured.

Final Step:
In the above steps, we have proved that for any fixed
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,2^{k-1}\}
. There
exists to possible scenarios of strong persistence/extinction of (
5.3
) provided that
h>\mathcal{R}_{k,\ell}^{\ast}

or
h<\mathcal{R}_{k,\ell}^{\ast}
. In consequence, there exist
2^{k}
scenarios of strong persistence/extinction for the metapopulation (
5.2
).

6.
Some Scenarios with weak extinction at some level

As we have seen, the strong persistence/extinction results of the previous sections for
n=1,2,3
were based
on the study of non homogeneous linear equations where the linear
part is an almost periodic function having nonzero average. In this section, we will drop
this assumption for some averages,
so the corresponding linear equation has not an exponential dichotomy
and, consequently, the results from section 2 cannot be used to study the metapopulation model at some step.

6.1.
Technical results

The following results are related to a linear scalar equation
and its nonhomogenous perturbation in absence of exponential dichotomy.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let us consider an almost periodic function
\mathfrak{a}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and a continuous function

\psi\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
. If
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]=0
and

\psi(t)=\textit{o}(1)
, then the perturbed equation

(6.1)

\dot{x}=[\mathfrak{a}(t)+\psi(t)]x

does not has an exponential dichotomy on
[0,+\infty)
.

Proof.

In order to conclude the result, we will prove a stronger fact, namely,
\Sigma_{[0,+\infty)}(\mathfrak{a}+\psi)=\{0\}

which implies, according to Definition
4
, that (
6.1
) has not an exponential dichotomy
on
[0,+\infty)
.

By Proposition
2.3
, we know that
\Sigma_{[0,+\infty)}(\mathfrak{a}+\psi)=[\beta^{-}(\mathfrak{a}+\psi),\beta^{+}(\mathfrak{a}+\psi)]
. Now, by using the
properties of lower and upper limits, we can deduce that:

\beta^{-}(\mathfrak{a})+\beta^{-}(\psi)\leq\beta^{-}(\mathfrak{a}+\psi)\leq\beta^{+}(\mathfrak{a}+\psi)\leq\beta^{+}(\mathfrak{a})+\beta^{+}(\psi).

Moreover, as
\mathfrak{a}(\cdot)
is almost periodic, we know that the Bohl exponents coincide with the average
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]
which implies that
\beta^{+}(\mathfrak{a})=\beta^{-}(\mathfrak{a})=0
and we only need to verify that
\beta^{+}(\psi)=\beta^{-}(\psi)=0
. Indeed, for
\varepsilon>0
fix
s_{0}
such that
|\psi(s)|<\varepsilon
for
s>s_{0}
, then for arbitrary
t>s>s_{0}
we obtain

\left|\frac{1}{t-s}\int_{s}^{t}\psi(r)\,dr\right|\leq\varepsilon

and the result holds trivially.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be almost periodic with
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]=0
and consider two bounded continuous functions: a positive almost periodic function
\mathfrak{b}\colon\mathbb{R}\to(0,+\infty)
with

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\mathfrak{b}(t)=\mathfrak{b}_{0}>0

and
\psi\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
where

\psi(t)=\textit{o}(1)
, then any solution of the perturbed equation

(6.2)

\dot{u}=[\mathfrak{a}(t)+\psi(t)]u+\mathfrak{b}(t)

with positive initial condition at
t=0
is upper unbounded on
[0,+\infty)
.

Proof.

The proof will be made by contradiction. Indeed, if we assume the existence of an upper bounded solution
t\mapsto\hat{u}(t)
of (
6.2
) with positive
initial condition at
t=0
, it follows that

0<\hat{u}(t)\leq M_{0}
for any
t\geq 0
because any solution of (
6.2
) with positive initial condition at
t=0
remains positive for any
t\geq 0
.

By using the boundedness of
\hat{u}(\cdot)
combined with

\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}\mathfrak{b}(t)=\mathfrak{b}_{0}>0
, the statement 4 from Proposition
2.2
implies that the linear equation
\dot{u}=[\mathfrak{a}(t)+\psi(t)]u
has an exponential dichotomy on
[0,+\infty)
, obtaining a contradiction with Lemma
6.1
.
∎

The above result only proves that the solutions
t\mapsto u(t)
of (
6.2
) verify

\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}u(t)=+\infty,

which arises the following question: Can we ensure that
\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}u(t)=+\infty
?. The next result provides a partial answer

Lemma 6.3
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
6.2
restricted to
T
–periodic functions
\mathfrak{a}(\cdot)
and
\mathfrak{b}(\cdot)
if follows that
\lim\inf\limits_{t\to+\infty}u(t)=+\infty
.

Proof.

The proof will be made by contradiction by assuming the existence of
M>0
such that
\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}u(t)=M>0
. Then, by the fluctuation Lemma
[
38
, Lemma A.1]
there exists
a divergent sequence
t_{n}
such that
u(t_{n})\to M
.

Moreover, the assumption
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]=0
implies
\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\xi)\,d\xi\geq-\int_{0}^{T}|\mathfrak{a}(\xi)|\,d\xi
for all
s\leq t
. Thus, if
u(\cdot)
is a solution of (
6.2
) with nonnegative initial condition, then
u(t)>0
for all
t>0
and, for arbitrary
\tau_{0}\geq 0
,

u(t)\geq\int_{\tau_{0}}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}[\mathfrak{a}(\xi)+\psi(\xi)]\,d\xi}\mathfrak{b}(s)\,ds\geq c\int_{\tau_{0}}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}\psi(\xi)\,d\xi}\,ds

for some positive constant
c
depending only on
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
. Next, fix
\tau_{0}
such that
|\psi(t)|<\varepsilon
for
t>\tau_{0}
with
0<\varepsilon<\frac{c}{M}
.
Taking
t=t_{n}
, it is seen that

u(t_{n})\geq c\int_{\tau_{0}}^{t_{n}}e^{-\varepsilon(t_{n}-s)}\,ds=\frac{c}{\varepsilon}\left(1-e^{-\varepsilon(t_{n}-\tau_{0})}\right),

which is a contradiction when
n
is sufficiently large.
∎

Remark 2
.

The above result cannot be extended to the almost periodic case since, as is well known, the assumption
\mathcal{M}[\mathfrak{a}]=0
does not imply that
the integral
\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(\xi)\,d\xi
is bounded from below for arbitrary
t>s

Remark 3

6.3.
An open question: existence of weakly persistent solutions

The above results only provide sufficient conditions ensuring weak extinction of a species and this is due
to Lemma
6.2
, which ensures the unboundedness of the solutions
t\mapsto u(t)
of (
6.2
). Nevertheless,
it does not ensure that
\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}u(t)=+\infty
since, as stated by Remark
2
, the case
\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}u(t)=\ell>1

is theoretically possible and, as
p(t)=1/u(t)
it implies that a species

will verify

\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}p(t)=0\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}p(t)=1/\ell>0,

that is, a species could be weakly persistent and driven weakly to the extinction at the same time.

The above comment combined with Remark
2
raise a natural question: in absence of exponential dichotomy, can a species be weakly persistent?
This is equivalent to determine additional conditions to those stated in Lemma

ensuring that some solutions of (

\mathfrak{w}(t)=-\mathfrak{v}(t)
, leading to
u^{\prime}=\mathfrak{a}(t)u+\mathfrak{w}(t)
, which also has a bounded solution
and the above proof can be carried out.

Proof of
3)\Rightarrow 1)
: Let
t\mapsto z(t)
be a solution
of
z^{\prime}=\mathfrak{a}(t)z+1
which is bounded on
[0,+\infty)
. Firstly, a noteworthy
property of the above equation is that: if
z(0)\geq 0
, the solution remains positive
for any
t>0
while, if
z(0)<0
the solution either remains negative or has a unique change of sign.

Secondly, let us verify that

(A.2)

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}|z(t)|>0.

In fact, this property is immediately verified when
z(\cdot)
is positive
after some value since
t\mapsto z(t)
is increasing at any value of
t
such that
|z(t)|\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{\infty}<1
. Now, if
z(t)<0
for any
t\geq 0
, we have to consider two cases:

Case a)

\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}z(t)<0
, which is equivalent to (
A.2
).

Case b)

\limsup\limits_{t\to+\infty}z(t)=0
, which cannot be possible, because
z(\cdot)
is increasing after some finite time and, consequently,
z(t)=\textit{o}(1)
, which leads to
\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}z^{\prime}(t)>0
, obtaining a contradiction with the boundedness of
t\mapsto z(t)
and (
A.2
) follows.

In consequence, we can assume that
0<c\leq|z(t)|\leq M
for any
t\geq T
. In the case that
z(\cdot)
is positive for
t>s\geq T
it follows that:

C\geq\ln\left(\frac{z(t)}{z(s)}\right)=\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr+\int_{s}^{t}\frac{1}{z(r)}\,dr,

which leads to:

\int_{s}^{t}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr\leq C-\frac{1}{M}(t-s).

Similarly, if
z(\cdot)
is negative for any
t\leq s
we will have that

\int_{t}^{s}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr\geq-C+\frac{1}{M}(s-t),

then the equation (
2.2
) has an exponential dichotomy on
[T,+\infty)

and the exponential dichotomy is verified on
[0,+\infty)
as a consequence of Lemma
2.1
.

A.5.
Proof of Proposition
2.3

We will prove an equivalent result, namely:

\mathbb{R}\setminus\Sigma_{[0,+\infty)]}(\mathfrak{a})=(-\infty,\beta^{-}(\mathfrak{a}))\cup(\beta^{+}(\mathfrak{a}),+\infty).

If
\lambda\notin\Sigma_{[0,+\infty)}(\mathfrak{a})

it follows from Definition
4
that
x^{\prime}=[\mathfrak{a}(t)-\lambda]x

has an exponential dichotomy on
[0,+\infty)
, that is either (
2.4
) or (
2.5
) holds for
\mathfrak{a}(\cdot)-\lambda
.

The proof will be a consequence of the following statements:

i)

The property (
2.5
) holds for
\mathfrak{a}(\cdot)-\lambda

if and only if
\lambda\in(-\infty,\beta^{-}(\mathfrak{a}))
,

ii)

The property (
2.4
) holds for
\mathfrak{a}(\cdot)-\lambda

if and only if
\lambda\in(\beta^{+}(\mathfrak{a}),+\infty)
.

Assume for example that (
2.5
) holds for
\mathfrak{a}(\cdot)-\lambda
, then for arbitrary
s>t
we have

\frac{1}{s-t}\int_{t}^{s}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr\geq\lambda+\alpha-\frac{C}{s-t}

for some
\alpha>0
. Taking lower
limit for
t\to+\infty
and
L:=s-t\to+\infty
, we deduce that
\beta^{-}(\mathfrak{a})\geq\lambda+\alpha>\lambda
. Conversely, for
\beta^{-}(\mathfrak{a})>\lambda
we may fix
\alpha\in(0,\beta^{-}(\mathfrak{a})-\lambda)

and
s_{0},L_{0}>0
such that

\frac{1}{s-t}\int_{t}^{s}\mathfrak{a}(r)\,dr>\lambda+\alpha\quad\textnormal{with}\quad t>t_{0}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad s-t>L_{0},

that is

(A.3)

\int_{t}^{s}[\mathfrak{a}(r)-\lambda]\,dr>\alpha(s-t)\quad\textnormal{with}\quad s-t>L_{0}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad t>t_{0}

We have to study the cases where the conditions
s-t>L_{0}
and
t>t_{0}
are not satisfied, namely: a)
0\leq s-t\leq L_{0}
, b)
t_{0}\geq s-L_{0}>t
and c)
s-L_{0}>t_{0}\geq t
.

Firstly, the cases a) and b) imply that
0\leq s-t\leq L_{0}
and
t\leq s\leq L_{0}+t_{0}
. Then if a) or b) are
satisfied we have that:

\int_{t}^{s}[\mathfrak{a}(r)-\lambda]\,dr\geq-\|\mathfrak{a}-\lambda\|_{\infty}(L_{0}+s_{0})\geq\alpha(s-t)-(\|\mathfrak{a}-\lambda\|_{\infty}+\alpha)(L_{0}+t_{0}).

Finally, if
t\leq t_{0}<s-L_{0}
, then by using (
A.3
) we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\int_{t}^{s}[\mathfrak{a}(r)-\lambda]\,dr&=&\int_{t_{0}}^{s}[\mathfrak{a}(r)-\lambda]\,dr+\int_{t}^{t_{0}}[\mathfrak{a}(r)-\lambda]\,dr\\
&>&\displaystyle\alpha(s-t_{0})-\|\mathfrak{a}-\lambda\|_{\infty}t_{0}\\
&\geq&\displaystyle\alpha(s-t)-(\|\mathfrak{a}-\lambda\|_{\infty}+\alpha)t_{0}.\end{array}

By gathering the above cases we have that (
2.5
) is verified with

\alpha>0
and
C=(\|\mathfrak{a}-\lambda\|_{\infty}+\alpha)t_{0}
, then the statement i) is verified.
The statement ii) can be proved in an analogous way.
