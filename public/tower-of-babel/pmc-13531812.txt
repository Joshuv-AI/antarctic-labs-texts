From patient to partner: reflections on being a patient leader in the COMFORT Nursing Programme
Authors: Malene Deele, Jeanette Finderup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMeaningful patient and public involvement (PPI) is increasingly recognised as an important component of health research. However, relatively few publications describe patient involvement from the first-person perspective of a patient partner or patient leader. This commentary reflects on my experience as a patient leader in the COMFORT Nursing Programme, a research programme developing person-centred approaches to symptom management for people living with advanced kidney disease and their informal caregivers.Main bodyI reflect on my experiences as a person living with kidney failure, an informal caregiver, and Chair of the Danish Kidney Association while working alongside researchers in the COMFORT Nursing Programme. I describe what motivated my involvement, how an equal partnership was developed, and how my lived experience, patient-leadership role and wider contact with patients and informal caregivers contributed to the research. My involvement included reviewing study materials and language, contributing to research discussions and priorities, supporting recruitment of patient partners, and participating in dissemination. I also reflect on the time and commitment required for intensive patient involvement, the challenges posed by fluctuating health, and the need for research teams to accommodate different preferences and capacities for participation.ConclusionMy experience illustrates that patient leadership can extend beyond sharing personal experiences to contributing to research priorities, methods, communication and dissemination. Patients and informal caregivers can bring forms of expertise that complement clinical and research knowledge. Meaningful involvement should therefore provide genuine opportunities to influence research while remaining flexible enough to accommodate individual needs, health circumstances and preferred levels of participation.

Main body

Targets and values for patient, informal caregiver and patient involvement

I accepted the role as patient leader in the COMFORT Nursing Programme [1] because it represented genuine partnership and meaningful involvement. My motivation to become involved in health research grew from my experiences both as a person living with kidney failure and as an informal caregiver for my husband, who is also receiving dialysis. Through these experiences, and through my role as Chair of the Danish Kidney Association, I have seen how strongly kidney disease affects not only patients but also families and everyday lives. I wanted to contribute to research that addresses the issues that matter to patients and informal caregivers and to help ensure that their perspectives are considered when research priorities and interventions are developed, tested and implemented.

My motivation to become involved in research developed before the COMFORT Nursing Programme. One of my first meaningful experiences was through Kidney Connect [2], an initiative developed with Novo Nordisk (Danish pharmaceutical company) to explore patient preferences in relation to different health conditions. For me, this was the first time I felt that patients and informal caregivers were genuinely invited to consider and articulate what they needed and valued. Exploring people’s preferences, with the hope that these perspectives might eventually influence treatment and care, was particularly meaningful to me.

My subsequent experiences with PPI were more mixed. I participated in several PPI groups where I did not always feel that patient perspectives were genuinely heard or able to influence decisions. At times, involvement felt more like a requirement to be fulfilled than a meaningful partnership. In some projects, I felt that the direction had already largely been decided before patients were invited to contribute. On other occasions, I was invited to a single meeting but never heard from the project again. These experiences left me frustrated because I felt that neither my competencies nor the potential contribution of patients were being fully recognised or used.

These earlier experiences shaped what I came to value in patient involvement. For me, meaningful PPI requires more than simply having patients present. Patients and informal caregivers need opportunities to influence decisions, contribute their different forms of expertise, and remain involved throughout the research process. This was an important reason why my experience in the COMFORT Nursing Programme felt different. The COMFORT Nursing Programme is a research programme focused on developing and evaluating a tailored, person-centred approach to symptom management for adults with advanced kidney disease and their informal caregivers [1]. The programme brings together researchers, nurses, patients and informal caregivers with the aim of developing approaches that reflect the needs and experiences of the people who will ultimately use them. From the outset, I was treated as an equal partner within the research team, which gave me confidence in my role as both a patient leader and co-researcher. This early inclusion established a foundation of trust and mutual respect that characterised the collaboration throughout the programme.

Equal person-centred partnership

Equality does not imply identical knowledge and expertise; rather, it means recognising that each perspective contributes unique and equally valuable insights to the research process [3]. For me, equality meant being listened to, included in conversations, and invited to contribute fully. I was included in email correspondence, invited to meetings and encouraged by the research team to participate actively.

Importantly, I did not enter the collaboration with lived experiences alone. My role as Chair of the Danish Kidney Association has given me experience of representing and communicating with a wider community of patients and informal caregivers. It has also given me experience of advocacy, communication, organisational work and translating individual experiences into broader patient perspectives. This helped me contribute not only my own experiences but also questions and concerns that I had encountered through contact with other people affected by kidney disease.

In addition to my lived experience and my role as Chair of the Danish Kidney Association, I bring a professional and academic background to the collaboration. I am trained in law, although I do not practise as a lawyer. I believe that my academic training has influenced the way I approach research: I am accustomed to reading complex texts, considering arguments critically and analytically, and engaging with new knowledge in a structured way. These skills have been particularly useful when reviewing research materials, protocols and other written documents within the COMFORT Nursing Programme.

Combined with my experience in patient advocacy and organisational leadership, this background has enabled me to contribute perspectives that extend beyond my own experience as a patient and informal caregiver. I therefore see my contribution as a combination of lived experience, patient leadership and skills developed through my academic and organisational background. From an early stage, I experienced a collaborative culture built on mutual respect and a shared commitment to supporting one another. Each member of the team contributed different expertise, creating an environment in which everyone could learn from one another. Everyone brought their best knowledge to the table, and together we built a stronger project. Personally, this was deeply meaningful, not only for my own sense of contribution but also because I could help others facing similar challenges.

My leadership role gave me a broader perspective. Through the Danish Kidney Association, I have contact with patients and informal caregivers whose circumstances, priorities and experiences may differ from my own. This helped me remain aware that my personal experience should not be assumed to represent the experiences of all people with kidney disease.

These complementary roles provided me with a broad understanding of symptom burden while also giving access to networks of patients and informal caregivers. They enabled me to contribute experiential knowledge, patient leadership and practical insight throughout the research process. They also provided direct pathways to people who wished to contribute to PPI group [4]. I found it particularly meaningful that nurses, patients and informal caregivers participated together in the PPI group [5].

How I was involved and my contributions

Assignments and early involvement

The Danish Kidney Association supported the COMFORT Nursing Programme application to the Novo Nordisk Foundation with a letter of recommendation. Although it is impossible to know whether this letter directly influenced the funding decision, meaningful patient involvement is increasingly recognised as an important element of health research and research funding across Denmark and Europe [6]. This underlines the importance of involving patients and informal caregivers early rather than adding their involvement after the research priorities and methods have already been determined.

Internationally, expectations regarding PPI vary between research systems. In some settings, including the UK, meaningful public involvement is now embedded within major health research funding structures, with expectations that involvement is appropriately planned, supported and resourced. This development is important because involvement should not become a tokenistic exercise undertaken merely to fulfil a funding requirement. Its value depends on patients and informal caregivers having genuine opportunities to influence research.

I also co-authored an article showing how patients can act as a driving force in research and education, highlighting the importance of symptom burden and self-management in a Danish patient population [2].

Concrete influence on the research

Being involved throughout the programme meant that patient and informal caregiver perspectives could influence practical aspects of the research rather than simply provide comments after decisions had been made.

One concrete example of how PPI influenced the programme concerned the language used to describe the experiences of informal caregivers. The term “caregiver burden” was initially used, but patient and informal caregiver partners reacted negatively to this wording. They felt that the word “burden” framed the caregiver experience as inherently negative and did not adequately reflect the complexity of their experiences. Following these discussions, more neutral language focusing on the “caregiver experience” was adopted. For me, this was a small but important example of how involving people with lived experience can change the language of research and make it more respectful and recognisable to the people it describes.

We also identified written materials in which the language was too complex from a patient perspective. These materials were subsequently substantially revised to improve clarity and accessibility. This reinforced for me that health literacy is not simply about providing information but about ensuring that patients and informal caregivers can understand and relate to the language used in research. For me, examples such as this demonstrate the difference between being consulted and being a partner. The value of involvement becomes visible when lived experience leads to a change in how research is designed, communicated or conducted.

Findings and dissemination

I contributed to dissemination in several ways. In 2024, I gave an oral presentation at the EDTNA/ERCA (European Dialysis Transplant Nursing Association / European Renal Caring Association) conference about the experiences of informal caregiving, using Maslow’s hierarchy of needs as an analytic framework. This presentation later developed into an editorial in Journal of Renal Care [7]. The editorial further highlighted the importance of studying the burden experienced by informal caregivers and developing interventions that support them.

I also participated in the ERA (European Renal Association) podcast, A Pinch of Salt (ERA) [8], discussing living with kidney disease and pursuing a full life. I was invited to participate through the COMFORT research group. Through the Danish Kidney Association, I also helped recruit participants to our PPI group using social media. My position as Chair enabled the invitation to reach a broad network of potential participants.

I did not receive formal training specifically to become a patient partner in research. However, I received informal support and guidance from members of the COMFORT research team as I became involved in research activities, scientific writing and dissemination. I also brought considerable previous experience in communication and teaching. Through my work with the Danish Kidney Association, I have presented on a wide range of topics, from organisational activities to self-management and living with kidney disease. I have also taught at the Kidney School at Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen University Hospital and contributed to the education of nursing students on patient involvement, values and communication with patients.

Learning to write for a scientific audience was more self-directed. I read nursing and health research articles to understand how scientific papers were structured and how arguments were presented. My academic background in law has probably influenced this approach: when I encounter an area that I do not fully understand, my instinct is to read, explore and continue learning. Combined with the support of the COMFORT research team, this helped me develop the confidence and skills needed to contribute to scientific publications and presentations.

Language and accessibility

Promoting health literacy in kidney care has been one of my highest priorities throughout the research programme. I worked actively on reviewing protocols, refining interview guides, and translating surveys to ensure that patients and informal caregivers could understand them as intended.

This is one area in which lived experience can reveal problems that may not be obvious to researchers. Words that are technically correct may nevertheless feel unfamiliar, confusing or stigmatising to patients. I therefore saw part of my role as helping the research team consider not only whether information was scientifically accurate, but also whether patients and informal caregivers could recognise themselves and their experiences in the language being used.

Being included in all communications and meetings enabled me to contribute continuously throughout the research process rather than only at predefined stages. Although this required substantial reading, preparation and participation in professional discussions, I found it very enriching. My patient and informal caregiver perspectives, combined with what I have learned from others, helped refine the programme in ways that could not necessarily have been foreseen at the outset.

Recognising different capacities for involvement

My own experience of extensive involvement has been positive, but I recognise that the same level of involvement will not be appropriate or desirable for every patient partner. Being included in many meetings, emails and research activities requires considerable time and energy. For someone living with chronic illness, this can sometimes be difficult, particularly when symptoms and treatment demands fluctuate.

This is especially relevant in kidney failure. Patients and informal caregivers may want to contribute but worry that their health will prevent them from attending a meeting, meeting a deadline or fulfilling a commitment. In our PPI activities, the unpredictable health of participants was one reason we sought to be inclusive and flexible.

Meaningful involvement should therefore not be defined by the number of meetings a person attends or the amount of work they undertake. Research teams should discuss with individual patient partners how they wish to contribute and recognise that preferences and capacity may change over time. Flexibility, clear expectations and reassurance that illness-related absence is understood can help prevent involvement from becoming an additional burden.

For me, being highly involved was meaningful and suited my interests and circumstances. However, equality also means respecting people who want a different level or form of involvement.

Recognition and support

Patient involvement requires time, knowledge and emotional and practical effort. Recognition of these contributions is therefore important. Recognition can take different forms, including acknowledgement, authorship when authorship criteria are met, opportunities for learning and development, reimbursement of expenses and, where appropriate, payment for time and expertise.

Recognition of patient partners can take different forms. For me, one of the most important forms of recognition has been the opportunity to develop my knowledge and skills within research and to receive feedback and acknowledgement for my contributions. My travel expenses for meetings and conferences are covered, which enables me to participate without personally bearing these costs.

I do not view my research involvement as separate from my role as Chair of the Danish Kidney Association. I consider it part of my broader work within the Association, for which I receive a salary. The relationship is also reciprocal: the knowledge and experience I gain through research can be brought back to the Association and used, for example, in activities and courses for patients and informal caregivers.

I also see involvement in research as an opportunity to develop a stronger evidence base for patient advocacy. The knowledge I gain through research can inform discussions about how healthcare might develop towards greater patient involvement. In this sense, recognition is not only financial; for me, it also comes from being able to develop professionally, contribute meaningfully to research, and use what I learn to benefit the wider kidney community. For me, an important form of recognition has been being treated as a genuine member of the research collaboration and being given opportunities to contribute to publications and dissemination. However, sustainable PPI should not rely only on the willingness of individual patients to volunteer substantial amounts of time. Research programmes should consider from the outset how patient partners will be practically supported and appropriately recognised.

Final reflections on patient and informal caregiver involvement

People living with chronic illness, and those supporting them, can carry a substantial symptom and treatment burden that affects the whole family [6]. Research should therefore consider not only patients as individuals but also family life and relational contexts [9].

My experience also has limitations. I am one patient leader reflecting on my own experience within one Danish kidney research programme. I am highly engaged in patient advocacy and have been able and willing to participate extensively in research activities. My experience should therefore not be interpreted as representative of all patients or informal caregivers. Other patient partners may have different expectations, skills, health circumstances, resources and preferences for involvement. Furthermore, many of the benefits I describe are based on my reflections and experiences rather than a formal evaluation of the impact of PPI within the COMFORT programme.

Nevertheless, my experience has shown me that involving patients and informal caregivers as research partners can bring perspectives that would otherwise be difficult to access [10, 11]. The challenge is not simply to involve patients, but to create conditions in which different forms and levels of involvement are possible and valued.

Conclusion

Living with kidney failure while also being an informal caregiver has taught me that research can have greater impact when it is informed by the voices of those living with the condition every day. My experience in the COMFORT Nursing project shows that patient leadership is not only about sharing stories, but can also involve shaping priorities, methods, language and dissemination.

True partnership is founded on mutual respect, trust, and recognition that patients, informal caregivers, and researchers bring distinct forms of expertise. At the same time, there is no single model of meaningful involvement. The intensive level of participation that suited me may not suit every patient partner, and research teams should create flexible opportunities that recognise differences in health, capacity, experience and preferences.

For me, being a patient leader has been both personally meaningful and a way to help others. I hope these reflections encourage researchers to see patients and informal caregivers not only as participants, but as potential co-creators of knowledge, while recognising and supporting the different ways in which people may wish and be able to contribute.
