The next century of spin
Authors: Ilya Kuprov, Warren S. Warren
Published: 2026

It has now been 100 years: Just over a century ago, George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit, both graduate students at the University of Leiden, proposed a simple-looking explanation for the fine structure in the hydrogen atom spectrum that Schrödinger’s equation did not at the time predict. They tentatively ascribed to the electron an intrinsic angular momentum (the discussions at the time literally referred to “a spinning electron”) and used a relativistic effect that astronomers had already understood well but quantum mechanics yet lacked—spin-orbit coupling.

They were briefly made to regret their courage. Colleagues pointed to a missing factor of two, the proposal made no classical physics sense (the implied spinning rate was enormous), and the symmetry properties were so strange that Pauli branded it “eine neue Kopenhagener Irrlehre” (a new Copenhagen heresy). Thankfully, the issues were quickly resolved. By 1928, the concept of spin was physically and mathematically robust, even if an intuitive interpretation was (and you could say still is) lacking.

So, what exactly is spin? Textbooks are full of nonsense. Briefly put, Minkowski space-time has more ways of rotating things than Euclidean space: In Special Relativity, a rotation may also be accomplished by a superposition of Lorentz boosts. This makes rotations applicable to point objects, modifies conservation laws, and creates a new quantity; the rest is mathematical detail. Eccentric as it may be, spin has an astonishingly wide range of applications, driven principally by the magnetic moment it imparts on elementary particles.

Science Advances asked the people at the forefront of those applications to write reviews. To our surprise, everyone agreed and told their friends, so many excellent research papers piled in as well. This collection includes papers on dark matter and exotic interactions, superfluidity and neutron stars, chemical structure and dynamics, nuclear hyperpolarization, quantum optimal control, medical and technical imaging, long-lived quantum states, spintronics and magnetic materials, quantum chromodynamics, and cardiac magnetometers.

The first century was spent working out what spin is and how to use it. The next century… well, we may need to sort a few things out first.
