ZnR/GPR39 regulates hepatic insulin signaling, tunes liver bioenergetics and ROS production, and mitigates liver fibrosis and injury
Authors: Anil Khushalrao Shendge, Israel Sekler, Michal Hershfinkel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdequate supply of zinc is essential for hepatic function and its deficiency is associated with acute liver injury (ALI) and chronic nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). However, how zinc controls hepatic function is unknown. We found that the zinc sensitive ZnR/GPR39, a mediator of zinc signaling, enhances hepatic phosphorylation of ERK1/2, which is reduced in ZnR/GPR39 deficient livers. Surprisingly, livers from ZnR/GPR39 knockout (KO) mice exhibited elevated insulin receptor expression and downstream AKT activation. Moreover, ZnR/GPR39 KO mice had higher blood fasting glucose level, pronounced hepatic lipid accumulation, increased hepatocyte oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. These data suggest that ZnR/GPR39 modulates insulin receptor signaling, a major pathway in hepatic metabolism. Associated with the impaired signaling, ZnR/GPR39 KO livers exhibited increased tissue fibrosis, manifested by marked elevation of collagen expression, compared to wildtype (WT). Additionally, we found alteration of hepatocyte junctional proteins that was accompanied by increased macrophage infiltration and higher liver inflammation in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice. To determine the role of ZnR/GPR39 in ALI, we applied a mild LPS challenge that induced profound decrease in hepatic OCR, also leading to higher ROS generation in ZnR/GPR39 KO hepatocytes, but not in WT. We further found increased serum IL-2 and AST/ALT ratio only in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice. Our findings reveal a role of ZnR/GPR39 in controlling hepatic insulin receptor signaling and mitigating liver fibrosis and inflammation, thus underscoring the important role of ZnR/GPR39 in liver signaling and function.Keywords: Zinc, ZnR/GPR39, Insulin receptor, Hepatic inflammation, Tight junctions, Macrophage infiltration, Mitochondrial dysfunction

Introduction

Being the second most prevalent trace element in the human body, zinc plays pivotal roles in numerous biological functions including cellular, metabolic, and immune functions [1,2]. Zinc is absorbed from the food via the intestines, the liver then rapidly accumulates this mineral in cellular vesicles or attached to proteins such as metallothionein or albumin [3]. Zinc from the liver is exchanged with the serum, as demonstrated using tracer imaging techniques following administration of Zn65 [4], making the liver a primary organ responsible for zinc metabolism, and systemic zinc homeostasis [5]. Consequently, liver diseases affect zinc levels, while zinc deficiency may contribute to their pathogenesis [[6], [7], [8]] and hepatic zinc deficiency has been correlated with impaired liver function and regeneration [9]. Zinc deficiency is documented in patients with cirrhosis [5,10], nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Zinc deficiency is suggested to induce oxidative stress in patients with NASH and further contribute to iron overload, increased insulin resistance, and liver fibrosis progression [15], and to contribute to inflammation of the hepatic parenchyma [5]. Finally, zinc supplementation to patients with viral or alcoholic hepatitis and chronic liver diseases is considered beneficial and is also effective in animal models [6,[16], [17], [18]]. Furthermore, regulation of cellular zinc levels by the zinc transporter ZIP14 were associated with reduced hepatocyte apoptosis and liver fibrosis [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Recently, zinc supplementation was associated with changes in several zinc transporters expression that reduced liver damage induced by LPS-mediated inflammatory challenge in chicken embryos [23]. While zinc signaling is involved in a wide range of physiological function [24], its role in the liver is not well understood.

The ZnR/GPR39, zinc sensing receptor, is a Gαq-coupled receptor belonging to the ghrelin receptor subfamily and expressed in various tissues including the gastrointestinal tract, liver, and pancreas [25,26]. Signaling by extracellular Zn2+ is predominantly mediated through the ZnR/GPR39 receptor [[26], [27], [28]]. Using a GPR39 knockout mouse model [29], it has been shown that this receptor is involved in regulating gastrointestinal motility, cholesterol metabolism [29], and epithelial integrity [30,31]. In colon epithelial cells, ZnR/GPR39 controls Cl− absorption, which leads to increased water loss in ZnR/GPR39 knockout (KO) mice induced with diarrhea compared to WT [32]. Activation of ZnR/GPR39 by Zn2+ also enhances expression of tight junction proteins in colonocytes, thereby providing protection from epithelial erosion in a model of ulcerative colitis [30,33]. The liver tight junction proteins are essential for its function, and disruption of these junctional proteins contributes to higher susceptibility towards liver inflammation and damage [[34], [35], [36]]. However, a role for ZnR/GPR39 in the etiology of liver inflammation is not well understood.

A link between ZnR/GPR39 and liver disease is suggested by its downregulation in nonalcoholic fatty liver diseases (NAFLD) patients (GSE63067, 18 patients), where a p value of 0.0002 and LogFC −0.723 indicate a significant link between ZnR/GPR39 and normal liver function [37]. In addition, the ZnR/GPR39 was associated with metabolic disease, exhibiting lower expression in obese type 2 diabetes patients [38] and impaired insulin secretion and adipocyte lipolytic functions in mice deficient in ZnR/GPR39 [39,40]. The involvement of ZnR/GPR39 in pancreatic acini cells and the pathogenesis of biliary dependent acute pancreatitis was recently suggested, where genetic deletion of ZnR/GPR39 in mice ameliorated the inflammatory condition [41]. But a role for ZnR/GPR39 in hepatocytes, which secrete bile acids, is unknown.

In the current study we demonstrate that in liver tissue and primary hepatocytes ZnR/GPR39-dependent signaling controls insulin receptor expression and metabolic functions. In addition, we show that ZnR/GPR39 regulates junctional proteins and maintains tissue integrity, thus reducing LPS-induced inflammation and acute liver injury (ALI).

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

All experiments involving animals were approved by the committee for the Ethical Care and Use of Animal in Experiments at Ben-Gurion University of the Negev (approval # IL-26-04-2023C). 11–13 weeks old, C57BL/6 male ZnR/GPR39+/+ (WT) and ZnR/GPR39−/− (KO) mice, described previously [29,32], were used for the animal studies. Genotyping of mice was done using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), using tail biopsy samples that were incubated for 30 min at 95 °C in lysis buffer containing: 50 mM KCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH-8.3, 25 mM MgCl2, 0.45 % NP-40, 0.45 % Tween-20 and fresh proteinase K (Sigma-Aldrich). PCR was done with Rapid Taq master mix (Vazyme-P222-03-AA) according to the manufacturer's protocol using the following primers: WT-Forward 5′-AACAGCGTCACCATCAGGGTT-3′ and WT-Reverse 5′-TGCGAGAGAGGTTGCAGTTGA-3′ (Sigma-Aldrich) that amplified a 445 bp band from the WT allele. ZnR/GPR39 KO primers were forward 5′-GGAACTCTCACTCGACCTGGG-3′ and reverse 5′-GCAGCGCATCGCCTTCTATC-3′ (Sigma-Aldrich) amplified a 262 bp band. After amplification, samples were loaded in electrophoresis agarose gel and bands were analyzed using chemiluminescence (ECL) (Cyanagen-XLS142). All animals were kept in an accredited pathogen-free animal facility with a 12 h light/dark cycle at room temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, and fed with a standard chow diet and water ad libitum.

Localization of ZnR/GPR39 in liver

Expression of V5-tagged ZnR/GPR39 in liver sections was determined using proximity ligation assay (PLA), using the Naveniflex MR 100 kit (mrb-12523, Navinci Diagnostics). Briefly, 7 μm paraffin-embedded liver sections underwent deparaffinization and antigen retrieval was performed using 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH-6) for 10 min at 95 °C. Sections were washed three times with 1x PBS for 5 min each and then incubated with 100 μl of the blocking solution. Tissue sections were incubated with rabbit-anti V5 (ab-206566) and mouse-anti V5 (genescript-01724) antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, buffers and enzymes from the kit were utilized according to the manufacturer's protocol. Finally, the sections were mounted with DAPI fluoromount-G (SouthernBiotech-0100-20) solution and allowed to dry before being subjected to confocal microscopy for visualization and analysis (Nikon-Eclipse Ti).

Immunofluorescence staining

For staining, paraffin-embedded liver tissues were prepared as described above. Antigen retrieval was performed using either 10 mM citrate buffer (pH-6) for 10 min at 95 °C (for E-cadherin, Occludin, and F4/80) or Tris-EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris base, 1 mM EDTA, 0.05 % Tween 20, pH-9) for 10 min at 95 °C (for ZO-1, and Insulin receptor). Following antigen retrieval, the sections were washed with 3 changes of 1x PBS, 3 min each and incubated with 5 % BSA (mp-160069) in 0.05 % tween-20 in 1x PBS solution for 1 h at room temperature to block non-specific binding sites. Subsequently, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C, either with anti-E-cadherin (cst-3195), anti-occludin (Thermo-711500), and anti-zonula-1 (ZO-1, ab-221547), anti-F4/80 macrophage marker (cst-70076), and anti-insulin receptor (ab-137747) antibodies. Alexa-594-labeled secondary antibody (mouse-jackson-115585062, rabbit-jackson-111035144) was incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Following secondary antibody incubation, the slides were washed with 1x PBS and mounted with DAPI fluoromount-G solution and images were acquired using confocal microscope. All the images were quantified using ImageJ software.

Histological staining

Hematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) and Picro Sirius Red (PSR) staining were performed on 7 μm paraffin-embedded liver sections to assess liver tissue morphology and collagen deposition, respectively. For H&E staining, deparaffinized liver sections were immersed in hematoxylin solution (sigma-HHS16) for 3 min and allowed to develop a stain by putting slides under tap for 2–3 min. Further, the slides were dipped into acid fast ethanol (0.25 % HCl in 70 % ethanol) several times and rinsed again under a tap water. The slides were then incubated in Eosin solution (eosin-PRC/R/68) for 45 s, followed by dehydrated with 95 % and 100 % ethanol respectively for 5 min each. Finally, the slides were incubated with xylene 3 times, 15 min each and mounted with DPX permount (sigma-06522) and allowed to dry before capturing microscope images (Olympus CKX41). Similarly, the hydrated slides were incubated with PSR (direct red) solution (sigma-365548) for 1 h, followed by dehydration and mounting. Oil Red O (sigma-0625) staining was performed on frozen sections to assess the lipid droplet accumulation. 7 μm thick frozen liver section were air dried for 30 min and incubated with 4 % PFA for 10 min. The slides were then dipped into 60 % isopropanol quickly and further incubated with the Oil Red O solution for 15 min at RT. Further, the slides were dipped into 60 % isopropanol once and in deionized water. The tissues were counter stained with hematoxylin for 3 min, washed with deionized water and tissues were mounted with 90 % glycerol gel. The images were captured on Olympus CKX41 using Motic Images Plus 2.0 software.

Western blotting

After perfusion with PBS, one portion of the liver was dissected and digested with RIPA buffer. The resulting lysates were centrifuged at 10,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration in lysate was determined using BCA protein measurement kit (Thermo scientific-23227) by following the manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of protein was resolved on sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) using 1x running buffer (25 mM tris base, 190 mM glycine, 0.1 % SDS) [42]. Following electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membrane (Pall corporation- 66485) using 1x transfer buffer (25 mM tris base, 190 mM glycine, 20 % methanol). Subsequently, the membranes were blocked with 5 % BSA in tris-buffered saline with 0.01 % tween (TBS-T) solution for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with either anti-collagen-1 (MD biosciences-203002), anti-E-cadherin (cst-3195), anti-β-catenin (cst-9562), anti-p-ERK1/2 (cst-4370), anti-t-ERK1/2 (cst-4696), anti-p-AKT (cst-4060), anti-t-AKT (cst-2920), anti-p-P38 (cst-9211), anti-t-P38 (cst-9212), anti-insulin receptor (ab-137747), Cox-2 (Invitrogen-MA5-14568), anti-β-actin (mp-69100) antibodies. After overnight incubation, the membranes were washed with 1x TBS-T three times, 5 min each and incubated with respective HRP-conjugated anti-mouse (Jackson-115035146) or anti-rabbit (Jackson-111035144) secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were further washed with TBS-T three times, 5 min each and blots were developed with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (Cyanagen-XLS142) and images were captured using imaging system (Vilber-fusion soloX).

Measurement of IL-2 and LDH

Serum levels of interleukin-2 and lactate dehydrogenase from LPS-treated and untreated mice were evaluated using the commercially available ELISA kits. Prior to assay, the serum samples were diluted at a ratio of 1:2 for IL-2 assay and 1:10 for LDH assay and further processed using the manufacturers protocol (IL-2, ab-100706, LDH, ab-102526). Absorbance for IL-2 and LDH were recorded at 450 nm.

Measurement of liver enzymes in LPS and control tissues

To assess the role of ZnR/GPR39 in acute liver injury (ALI), WT and ZnR/GPR39 KO mice were subjected to treatment with 3 μg/kg and 6 μg/kg of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) (Sigma Aldrich- L2880) and D-GalN (25 mg/kg) for the duration of 6 h [43]. Then, blood samples were collected from both treated and untreated mice via cardiac puncture and serum was obtained by centrifugation at 3900×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The collected serum was utilized to measure the levels of serum markers (Beckman Coulter AU5800, Clinical Chemistry Analyzer) specifically alanine aminotransferase (ALT, cat #OSR 6107), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, cat #OSR6209), Alkaline phosphatase (ALP, cat #OSR6104), and total bilirubin (cat #OSR6212). The levels were determined using standard protocol, hemolysis was determined by the manufacturer protocols (LIH, cat #OSR62166), and the absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically at 340 nm for ALT and AST and at 578 nm for ALP and bilirubin. Following blood collection, mice were euthanized and liver tissue was collected for staining.

Primary hepatocyte isolation

Three-month-old male WT and ZnR/GPR39 KO mice were utilized for primary hepatocyte isolation. Briefly, mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane and perfusion was initiated using Hanks’ Balanced Salt solution (HBSS) (Sartorius- 02-018-1A) at a constant flow of 5 mL/min for 6–7 min through the inferior vena cava, while the portal vein was cut to allow release of blood. Subsequently, flow was shifted to liver digest medium (Gibco- 17701-034) and perfusion of the liver continued for additional 6–7 min. The gallbladder was carefully excised from the liver and remaining liver was then excised from the body and placed directly in a Petri dish containing plating medium (10 % FBS, 2 mM sodium pyruvate and 2 % pen-strep in DMEM). The liver sac was incised and hepatocytes were collected and filtered using 70 μm nylon cell strainer (Falcon, 352350). The collected cells were centrifuged at 110×g for 5 min at 4 °C. Further, supernatant containing non-hepatocyte fraction cells was removed and subsequently, 10 ml of plating medium and 10 ml of percoll solution (Santa Cruz Biotechnology- 500790A) were added on pellet, mixed gently and centrifuged again at 110×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The medium was aspirated and the pellets were washed twice with 20 ml of plating medium using same low speed centrifugation as mentioned above. Once washing was done, 20 ml of plating medium were added and pellets were resuspended gently and cells were counted using trypan blue solution.

Mitochondrial respiration and reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurement

Seahorse (Agilent Seahorse XFe96 Cell Mito Stress Test) measurements were used to elucidate the role of ZnR/GPR39 in respiration by monitoring oxygen consumption rate (OCR) [44]. Hepatocytes were seeded for seahorse experiments in 96 well plate with a density of 7000 cells/well. The medium was then replaced with maintenance medium (0.2 % BSA, 2 mM sodium pyruvate and 2 % pen-strep in DMEM) after 2–3 h to remove unattached hepatocytes and plate was incubated again for additional 12–16 h. On the following day, the cells were treated with or without LPS (3 μg/ml) for 1 h. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with XF DMEM assay media (103575-100), pH 7.4 supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, 10 mM glucose and 1 mM sodium pyruvate and cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Finally, the plate was loaded into the Seahorse machine and mitochondrial stress experiment was conducted by injecting 3 μM oligomycin, 3 μM trifluoromethoxy carbonyl-cyanide phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and 2 μM rotenone/antimycin at a specific time points as described [44,45].

ROS levels were assessed in WT and ZnR/GPR39 KO primary hepatocytes using fluorescent DCFDA staining (ab-113851). Hepatocytes were seeded in either 96-well plates or on coverslips, and ROS measurement was performed according to the manufacturer instructions. Briefly, 16 h following seeding, cells were washed with 1x buffer provided in the kit, treated with 20 μM DCFDA, and incubated for 45 min in a CO2 incubator. The DCFDA solution was then removed, and the wells were gently washed with 1x buffer before treatment with or without LPS (3 μg/ml) or TBHP (200 μM, as a positive control) and further incubated for 30 min in the CO2 incubator. Following incubation, coverslips were analyzed using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus-IX73-Q Imaging Retiga 6000) with excitation/emission settings of 485/535 nm, and the fluorescence intensity of the 96-well plate was immediately measured using a fluorescence plate reader at the same excitation and emission.

Glucose tolerance test (GTT)

To investigate the role of ZnR/GPR39 in glucose homeostasis, three-month old male WT and ZnR/GPR39 KO mice were subjected to glucose tolerance test (GTT). Blood samples were collected from the tail vein and glucose level (Basal) was monitored using a glucometer (Abbott- FreeStyle Freedom Lite). Mice were fasted for 12 h and blood glucose was measured again (Fasting). Then, an intraperitoneal glucose injection was applied (2 g/kg of body weight) and blood glucose levels were monitored at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min.

Statistical analysis

All data are reported as mean ± S.D. of at least three experiments. The statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad prism 6. The relationship between the groups were evaluated using unpaired t-test with Welch's correction or one-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey's test where appropriate. In all analyses, a p-value of ≤0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, ****p ≤ 0.0001.

Results

ZnR/GPR39 is functional in liver tissue

To determine if zinc has a signaling role via ZnR/GPR39 in the liver, we first asked if the receptor is present in the liver. Since GPR39 antibodies fail to show specificity in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice, we used a CRISPR knock-in V5 to tag GPR39. Using proximity ligation assay (PLA), we found red puncta that illustrate the presence of ZnR/GPR39 in the liver of V5 tagged mice, which was absent in wildtype mice (Fig. 1A and B). To study the functional relevance of ZnR/GPR39, we monitored the well-established ZnR/GPR39-dependent phosphorylation of extracellular regulating kinase (ERK1/2) [42]. We expect that endogenous Zn2+ found in the tissue will trigger ZnR/GPR39 activation, particularly during cell death that occurs upon tissue dissection [46]. We indeed found increased phospho-ERK1/2 expression in WT liver tissue that is diminished in tissue from ZnR/GPR39 KO tissue (Fig. 1C and D), indicating that ZnR/GPR39 signaling is functional in liver tissue.

Increased proliferation and insulin receptor signaling in liver of ZnR/GPR39 KO mice

Activation of ERK1/2 by ZnR/GPR39 has a prominent role in proliferation of epithelial cells, among them colonocytes and skin keratinocytes [27,30,46]. Considering the importance of hepatocyte proliferation in maintaining liver homeostasis, we asked if ZnR/GPR39 regulates proliferation in the liver tissue using Ki-67 immunofluorescence. Surprisingly, we monitored about 70 % increase in Ki-67 positive cells in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice compared to the WT liver, suggesting that proliferation of hepatocytes is increased in the absence of ZnR/GPR39 (Fig. 2A and B). Previous studies suggest a link between zinc and insulin receptor signaling [47,48,49], we therefore asked if the insulin receptor expression on hepatocytes of ZnR/GPR39 KO tissue is altered. To assess this, we performed immunofluorescence staining and western blotting analysis of insulin receptor in WT versus ZnR/GPR39 KO liver sections and lysates. The expression level of insulin receptor was dramatically higher in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice compared to WT (Fig. 2C–F). In addition, ZnR/GPR39 KO livers exhibit lower total AKT expression but increased phosphorylated AKT, normalized to total AKT (Fig. 2E–G). Our data suggest that ZnR/GPR39 modulates the insulin receptor and its downstream AKT signaling [50], leading to abrogated insulin signaling in the absence of ZnR/GPR39.

Insulin receptor signaling plays a major role in lipid metabolism and glucose storage [50,51], we therefore asked if metabolism is also altered in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice. Using the GTT assay we found no significant difference in blood glucose levels between WT and ZnR/GPR39 KO mice in the fed state (baseline conditions) or following glucose load (Supplementary Fig. 1). Nevertheless, fasting blood glucose levels were higher in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice compared to WT (Fig. 3A). This may be explained by increased hepatic insulin clearance in ZnR/GPR39 KO liver, leading to higher serum glucose following fasting [51]. Importantly, and also consistent with the increased hepatic insulin receptor expression, we monitored greater accumulation of lipid droplets in ZnR/GPR39 KO livers compared to WT (Fig. 3B and C). Based on the increased proliferation and metabolism observed in ZnR/GPR39 KO hepatocytes, we hypothesized that ZnR/GPR39 is involved in regulating mitochondrial respiration. A Seahorse analysis, using hepatocytes isolated from WT and ZnR/GPR39 KO mice, showed that basal oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was higher in ZnR/GPR39 KO hepatocytes compared to WT (Fig. 3D and E). Similar increased OCR results were obtained for ATP-linked OCR (Fig. 3F). Increased mitochondrial activity may result in higher production of reactive oxygen species due to electron premature leak from the electron transport chain [81]. Using DCFDA fluorescence, we found higher ROS levels in ZnR/GPR39 KO hepatocytes under baseline conditions (Fig. 3G–H). Using tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP) to induce oxidative stress, we also found increased ROS levels in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice, in agreement with previous findings that steatotic hepatocytes are more susceptible to oxidative damage [52]. Altogether, we find that ZnR/GPR39 regulates the insulin receptor signaling, and that in the absence of ZnR/GPR39 the insulin receptor pathway is hyper-activated, with increased hepatic proliferation, augmented lipid accumulation, and elevated oxygen consumption and ROS production.

ZnR/GPR39 controls expression of junctional proteins and tissue integrity

Considering the role of tight junction proteins in liver tissue integrity [35,53,54], we next asked whether ZnR/GPR39 regulates the expression of tight junctions in liver tissue. We found an almost 50 % lower expression of occludin in ZnR/GPR39 KO liver compared to WT (Fig. 4A and B). Similarly, ZO-1 proteins, linking the transmembrane occludin with the cytoskeleton, exhibit lower expression by about 20 % in ZnR/GPR39 KO liver compared to WT (Fig. 4C and D). Our results therefore suggest compromised tight junction expression, which may affect integrity of liver tissue of ZnR/GPR39 KO mice.

In addition to the tight junctions, adherens junctions also play a crucial role in liver physiology [55,56], and loss of E-cadherin is associated with inflammatory reactions and liver damage [57]. We next found that periportal expression of E-cadherin in WT liver sections was well preserved and intact, however dramatically lower expression of E-cadherin, by about 60 %, was observed in ZnR/GPR39 KO tissues (Fig. 5A and B). This finding was further supported by western blot data, which demonstrated compromised E-cadherin levels (Fig. 5C and D), in addition to a decrease in the expression of its regulatory protein β-catenin in tissue deficient of ZnR/GPR39 (Fig. 5C–E).

Junctional proteins collectively contribute to maintenance of liver morphology and downregulation of these proteins is associated with tissue fibrosis [58,59], we therefore asked if loss of ZnR/GPR39 results in increased collagen expression. Liver sections from WT mice stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) exhibited a normal and well-defined histology, with hepatocytes nuclei arranged in distinct plates. In contrast, the ZnR/GPR39 KO liver sections displayed damaged and fuzzy liver morphology and loss of plate organization (Fig. 6A and B). Furthermore, PSR staining of collagen fibers, revealed notable differences between WT and ZnR/GPR39 KO liver sections, with enhanced PSR-positive reddish stain in the ZnR/GPR39 KO compared to WT (Fig. 6C). Expression of collagen-1 was quantified via western blotting, consistently showing higher expression of collagen-1 in ZnR/GPR39 KO liver tissue compared to WT (Fig. 6D and E). Our findings collectively suggest that the absence of ZnR/GPR39 results in decreased junction protein expression and increased collagen deposition, highlighting the importance of ZnR/GPR39 in maintaining liver tissue integrity.

Role of ZnR/GPR39 in liver inflammation

To determine if increased collagen deposition in ZnR/GPR39 KO livers is associated with inflammation, we initially studied recruitment of hepatic macrophages, which are established targets for treatment of liver diseases [82]. Our analysis revealed notable higher number of macrophages, determined by F4/80 fluorescence, in ZnR/GPR39 KO liver sections compared to WT counterparts (Fig. 7A–C). Specifically, there was approximately 80 % increase in F4/80 signals in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice compared to WT mice. To assess the inflammatory status of ZnR/GPR39-deficient mice, we performed western blot assays for cyclooxygenase-2 (Cox-2) and P38 MAPK, which are involved in production of inflammatory mediators [60]. We found ∼50 % higher protein expression of Cox-2 (Fig. 7D and E) and ∼40 % increased phosphorylation of P38 (pP38, Fig. 7D–F) in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice, revealing higher liver inflammation in the absence of ZnR/GPR39.

Protective role of ZnR/GPR39 in LPS-induced inflammation

To determine if the protective role of ZnR/GPR39 in hepatocytes, which was found under baseline conditions is also maintained during acute liver inflammation (ALI), we applied an LPS model of ALI [43]. We observed increase in the number of macrophages (F4/80 fluorescence) in WT liver upon LPS treatment (3 μg/kg) compared to their untreated counterparts. However, no significant difference was observed in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice with or without LPS treatment, indicating that the already heightened macrophage recruitment in ZnR/GPR39 deficient mice could not be further increased by this LPS treatment (Fig. 8A and B). In addition, we observed a marked increase in phosphorylated P38 level in WT liver tissue following LPS treatment which was further increased in ZnR/GPR39-deficient livers (Fig. 8C and D).

LPS also modulates mitochondrial respiration in mice [61,62], and decreases hepatic OCR thereby inducing bioenergetic failure associated with changes in mitochondrial function [63]. We therefore asked if ZnR/GPR39 regulates mitochondrial respiration in LPS-induced injury. Hepatocytes from WT mice did not exhibit significant changes in OCR levels following the LPS treatment (3 μg/kg), suggesting normal respiration was maintained under the inflammatory stress induced by this concentration of LPS (Fig. 9A–C,E). In contrast, hepatocytes from ZnR/GPR39 KO mice showed a significant decrease in oxygen consumption rate upon this LPS treatment compared to untreated ZnR/GPR39 KO hepatocytes (Fig. 9B–D,E). Interestingly, we observed similar trend in ATP-dependent OCR (Fig. 9F–H). Altogether, we find that ZnR/GPR39 KO animals exhibit a dramatic drop in OCR while WT mice were not affected by this 3 μg/kg LPS treatment (Fig. 9E–H). To determine whether these alterations in mitochondrial respiration affect ROS production, we again measured ROS levels using DCFDA. We find that LPS treatment induced a dramatic higher DCFDA fluorescence in ZnR/GPR39 KO tissue compared to WT (Fig. 9I and J). In agreement with the OCR measurements, the LPS treatment did not significantly alter ROS levels in WT mice compared to their untreated counterparts, but in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice the LPS treatment led to a marked increase in ROS compared to untreated ZnR/GPR39 KO mice (Fig. 9J). These findings suggest that ZnR/GPR39 plays a crucial role in regulating inflammatory signaling, mitochondrial respiration and ROS production, particularly under injury.

ZnR/GPR39 protection from LPS-induced liver damage

Serum levels of IL-2 and LDH serve as crucial indicators of liver damage, reflecting both acute and chronic inflammation in tissue [[64], [65], [66]]. In baseline conditions, ZnR/GPR39 knockout mice exhibited higher (around 24 %) IL-2 levels compared to WT mice, which aligns with our previous data indicating higher liver damage and macrophage infiltration in ZnR/GPR39 KO liver. Consistent with the previous results, IL-2 level remained relatively stable in LPS-treated WT mice compared to untreated counterparts. In contrast, IL-2 significantly rose by almost 40 % in LPS-treated ZnR/GPR39 KO mice compared to untreated ZnR/GPR39 KO mice, indicating a heightened sensitivity to inflammation and liver damage in the absence of this receptor (Fig. 10A). Surprisingly, the LDH level was reduced by almost 33 % in ZnR/GPR39 KO compared to WT under baseline conditions, and no significant difference was observed by LPS treatment neither in WT nor in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice (Fig. 10B). Finally, we directly monitored biomarkers for liver injury: ALT, AST, ALP, and total bilirubin. Similar ratio of AST/ALT, of ∼1.7 in WT and ∼1.5 in ZnR/GPR39 KO, was found under baseline conditions. Following LPS treatment a ratio of ∼1.7 was still observed in WT mice, but a notable increase of about 65 %, to a ratio of ∼2.5, was observed in the LPS treated ZnR/GPR39 KO mice (Fig. 10C). This trend was maintained following treatment with 6 μg/kg LPS (Fig. 10D). We did not find significant changes in ALP or total bilirubin between the genotypes at both low and high concentration of LPS (see Supplementary Fig. 2). Altogether, our results indicate that in the LPS model of ALI, ZnR/GPR39 mitigates the hepatic inflammatory response and confers partial protection from liver damage.

Discussion

Extracellular zinc, activates ZnR/GPR39 and promotes ERK1/2 mediated signaling leading to epithelial and cancer cell proliferation [26]. Here, we find ZnR/GPR39-dependent ERK1/2 activation in liver tissue from WT mice but not from ZnR/GPR39 KO, suggesting ZnR/GPR39 is functional in this tissue. Surprisingly, while moderate hepatocyte proliferation was observed in WT liver, ZnR/GPR39-deficient mice exhibited increased hepatocytes proliferation, contrary to the previously shown role of ZnR/GPR39 on epithelial proliferation [30]. This unexpected finding was consistent with a dramatic increase in the expression level of the insulin receptor and activation of downstream AKT, a kinase that is a candidate for enhancing proliferation in the liver [67]. Previously, zinc was suggested to act as an insulin-mimetic, affecting adipocyte lipid accumulation by regulating insulin signaling [47,68]. A role for intracellular zinc changes, via regulation of a zinc and manganese transporter, ZIP14, was also associated with glucose uptake in hepatocytes [69]. Our results suggest that the effects of zinc on insulin signaling are mediated, at least in part, by ZnR/GPR39 modulation of insulin receptor expression. While most studies investigated the effect of insulin receptor KO on metabolism, our results provide a unique model for addressing the role hepatic insulin receptor overexpression on liver and global metabolism. In the liver, insulin receptor-dependent activation of AKT, upregulates the expression of lipogenic enzymes and facilitates biogenesis of lipid droplets in hepatocytes [51,70]. Consistent with the increased insulin receptor expression, we show higher lipid accumulation, as seen with oil red O staining, in the absence of ZnR/GPR39 in mouse liver. These results agree with previous studies where ZnR/GPR39 deficient mice exhibited increased body weight and fat accumulation under a high-fat diet, which was suggested to occur via decreased energy expenditure and reduced adipocyte lipolytic activity [40]. Our results further link this fat accumulation to hepatic increase in insulin receptor and AKT activation. The higher blood glucose in ZnR/GPR39 KO fasting mice, may be linked to the increased hepatic insulin receptor expression and hepatic insulin clearance [50,51]. Together with the increased glucose, and lipid accumulation we also find higher OCR in ZnR/GPR39 KO hepatocytes. This increased metabolic activity is consistent with enhanced proliferation in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice compared to WT.

End-stage liver disease show enhanced insulin receptor activity in the liver, which may be associated with the tissue fibrosis [51]. Our results showing increased insulin receptor expression and hepatic lipid accumulation in the ZnR/GPR39 KO mice, suggested that the mice are prone to liver injury. An important role in liver injury is also mediated by the tight junction proteins that are protective against chronic liver disease, cirrhosis and cancer [36,55,58,71]. Our findings show downregulation of the tight junction proteins occludin and ZO-1 in the absence of ZnR/GPR39 in the liver. Alongside tight junctions, adherens junctions are pivotal in maintaining tissue morphology and facilitating cellular signaling [56]. Indeed, loss of E-cadherin disrupts the biliary network leading to cholestatic liver injury and subsequent development of sclerosing cholangitis [57] as well as other liver pathologies [72]. Our results indicate a significant downregulation of hepatic E-cadherin and β-catenin, a component of the surface cadherin protein complex, in the livers of mice lacking ZnR/GPR39. A recent study showed that intracellular zinc chelation reduced β-catenin acetylation but not expression [73], however ZnR/GPR39 activation does not induce cellular zinc changes [33]. Nevertheless, our findings point towards a role for ZnR/GPR39 as an upstream regulator of this pathway in hepatocytes, before cellular zinc levels may be affected. Interestingly, deacetylation of β-catenin was also linked to dietary zinc deficiency in fish that resulted in lipogenesis [73,74], this is consistent with our findings showing that loss of ZnR/GPR39 reduces β-catenin expression and augments lipid accumulation in mouse hepatocytes.

The concurrent downregulation of both tight junctions and adherens junctions, along with β-catenin, underscores the critical role of ZnR/GPR39 in maintaining liver integrity and the resistance to inflammation. Consistent with the downregulation of the junctional proteins, we find in ZnR/GPR39 KO liver tissue increased collagen expression, fibrosis of the tissue. Our findings suggesting that ZnR/GPR39 is essential for maintaining tissue integrity are in agreement with previous findings demonstrating that zinc supplementation significantly suppresses liver fibrosis, collagen deposition, macrophage infiltration and inflammation induced by bile duct ligation (BDL) in mice [75]. We also find increased recruitment of F4/80-expressing macrophages, associated with inflammation and chronic liver disease [76]. In addition to the increased macrophage infiltration in the ZnR/GPR39 KO tissue, we also find activation (phosphorylation) of P38 in liver tissue from ZnR/GPR39 KO mice. Activation of P38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), is a crucial mediator of hemorrhagic liver injury, and its inhibition with FR167653 was shown to mitigate inflammation-related hepatic damage following hemorrhagic shock [77]. Moreover, we find elevated serum IL-2 levels in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice. Altogether our results suggest a higher baseline inflammation in the absence of ZnR/GPR39 compared to WT counterparts.

Upon LPS treatment, WT mice typically exhibit heightened macrophage activation within liver tissue, a mechanism aimed at safeguarding against LPS-induced injury [78]. Notably, while baseline macrophage infiltration was elevated in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice, they failed to recruit more macrophages following LPS exposure. This observation may imply a pre-existing elevated inflammatory baseline in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice, characterized by the heightened macrophage recruitment, which predisposes them to loss of a safeguard mechanism during an inflammatory assault. Another mediator of LPS-induced signaling and cell damage is the P38 MAPK, we find upregulation of P38 expression in WT mice following LPS treatment indicating liver injury by the concentration of LPS used. Intriguingly, around 60 % elevation in P38 expression reflects significantly heightened LPS-mediated inflammation and damage in ZnR/GPR39-deficient mice. Similarly, although we find non-significant difference in serum IL-2 levels between LPS-treated and untreated WT mice, IL-2 levels rose by nearly 40 % in LPS-treated ZnR/GPR39 KO mice compared to untreated counterparts, indicating a markedly heightened inflammatory state in the absence of ZnR/GPR39. Changes in levels of AST and ALT liver enzymes are widely utilized as biomarkers for liver injuries, including those induced by LPS [79]. We did not observe any significant alterations in the basal levels of these enzymes in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice compared to WT. In contrast, a markedly higher AST/ALT ratio was detected in LPS-treated ZnR/GPR39 KO mice, but not in WT mice. This result indicates that the baseline higher inflammatory index of the ZnR/GPR39 KO mice results in higher susceptibility to even low concentration of LPS.

Reduced mitochondrial respiration in hepatocytes has been associated with chronic LPS exposures. For example, in skeletal muscle and macrophages treatment with LPS alters mitochondrial oxygen consumption and the effect is reversed in presence of antioxidants [62]. In the current study we applied mild LPS insult on WT and ZnR/GPR39 hepatocytes. As expected we did not observe a difference in OCR between LPS-treated and untreated WT mice, suggesting minimal impact of LPS on mitochondrial function on WT hepatocytes. In contrast, we observed a large decrease in OCR in hepatocytes derived from ZnR/GPR39 KO mice following LPS treatment compared to untreated counterparts. Thus, despite the higher OCR requirement of the ZnR/GPR39 KO mice under basal conditions, we found that even a relatively mild LPS exposure triggered a strong reduction of mitochondrial function. Mitochondrial respiration is closely linked to increased ROS levels due to the inefficient operation of the mitochondrial electron transport chain [80,81]. We find increase in ROS levels in ZnR/GPR39 KO mice, already under baseline conditions and particularly under LPS-mediated inflammation. These results underscore the critical role of ZnR/GPR39 in regulating mitochondrial function and oxidative stress. The significant differences in mitochondrial respiration between WT and ZnR/GPR39 KO mice suggest that the absence of ZnR/GPR39 leads to impaired electron transport chain efficiency, resulting in greater electron leakage and subsequent ROS formation. These findings highlight the potential importance of ZnR/GPR39 as a modulator of mitochondrial activity and oxidative stress, suggesting that its absence predispose the mice to oxidative damage, especially under inflammatory conditions such as those induced by LPS.

Conclusion

Altogether our results indicate that ZnR/GPR39 regulates liver tissue organization and integrity, and its absence also leads to an increase in hepatic insulin receptor expression leading to hepatic lipid accumulation which further leads to fibrosis and inflammatory state. Moreover, ZnR/GPR39 deficient mice are sensitive to even a mild LPS challenge, which impairs their mitochondrial function, enhances their liver inflammation and leads to more severe liver injury. Thus, our findings point towards a potential role of this receptor in mitigating LPS-induced ALI.
