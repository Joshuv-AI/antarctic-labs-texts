NBM: an Open Dataset for the Acoustic Monitoring of Nocturnal Migratory Birds in Europe
Louis Airale, Adrien Pajot, Juliette Linossier

Abstract
The persisting threats on migratory bird populations highlights the urgent need for effective monitoring techniques that could assist in their conservation. Among these, passive acoustic monitoring is an essential tool, particularly for nocturnal migratory species that are difficult to track otherwise. This work presents the Nocturnal Bird Migration (NBM) dataset, a collection of 13,359 annotated vocalizations from 117 species of the Western Palearctic. The dataset includes precise time and frequency annotations, gathered by dozens of bird enthusiasts across France, enabling novel downstream acoustic analysis. In particular, we demonstrate that a two-stage object detection model, tailored for the processing of audio data, can be trained on our dataset to retrieve localized bounding box coordinates around each signal of interest in a spectrogram. This object detection approach, which is largely overlooked in the bird sound recognition literature, allows important applications by potentially differentiating individual birds within audio windows. Further, we show that the accuracy of our recognition model on the 45 main species of the dataset competes with state-of-the-art systems trained on much larger datasets. This highlights the interest of fostering similar open-science initiatives to acquire costly but valuable fine-grained annotations of audio files. All data and code are made openly available.

The reason for this is the required level of expertise and the prohibitive cost of precise annotations, which still limits the size or the species range of richly-annotated avian sound bases

We argue however that addressing bird sound recognition as an

This is especially true in the case of nocturnal migratory species which tend to emit short, well-defined calls in a generally clear acoustic environment.
Training an object detection model on bird calls however requires a large amount of finely annotated data which are crucially missing, especially for the case of European birds which were the primary focus of the present study.
To overcome these challenges, the NBM project (for Nocturnal Bird Migration) was launched in 2020 as a crowd-sourcing initiative to share the annotation effort over privately collected recordings, complying with a standardized annotation procedure.
This procedure targeted the calls of migratory birds emitted at night, which were annotated both in time and frequency, allowing for the downstream training of fine-grained object detection models.
We present in this article the result of this work, the NBM-Dataset, a collection of 13,359 finely annotated vocalizations containing (predominantly) nocturnal flight calls and songs from 117 species.
As a proof of concept of the utility of this type of annotation, we trained a two-stage object detection model to precisely localize bird calls on input spectrograms.
This model is derived from a FPN Faster-RCNN architecture
[
31

32
]
, adapted for sound event detection in the two following ways: we equip the model with an attention mechanism to leverage the acoustic context of a signal; we provide the frequency position and the temporal scale of each vocalization with respective positional encodings, as spatial invariance is not desirable here.
The resulting NBM detection model shows very competitive results with the BirdNet multi-label classification model
[
16
]
which was trained on a much larger, weakly-labeled corpus.

3
The NBM Dataset

3.1
Data collection and Annotation

The NBM project emerged as an initiative to create a comprehensive dataset of nocturnal migratory birds vocalizations that would be accessible and easily usable by a wide community of birders and computer scientists.
It also ought to be open to contributions through a unified and straightforward annotation process.
The project quickly brought together a community of bird experts who shared their nocturnal soundscape recordings and the annotated calls they contained such that the resulting data collection may be used to train automatic recognition systems able to reproduce their annotating procedure.
This implied concretely drawing fine time and frequency bounding boxes using the Audacity software
[
41
]
around each bird call or song
pattern
, defined as the smallest (in terms of bounding box surface on a spectrogram) element required to identify a given species.
Those are similar to the
phrases
defined in BirdDB
[
36
]
.
A degree of interpretation was naturally admitted given the variability and complexity of bird vocalizations and the heterogeneity of the recording conditions.
Yet the specificity of the targeted nighttime acoustic environment, with a prevalence of bird calls over songs and generally less parasitic sounds of human origin, helped reduce the observed variance between different annotators.

Leveraging the French Birding Community.

The original goal was to compile at least one hundred calls from the most common migratory bird species in Europe, and a perimeter of 60 frequently recorded species was identified.
Facing the important workload that the annotation of 6,000 vocalization units would represent, we sought to engage the French birding community in a collective effort.
A call to action was issued in December 2020 and the webinar introducing the project attracted 50 participants eager to contribute to the annotation process.
A web platform
3
3

3

https://nocturnal-bird-migration.com/fr
was subsequently developed for the submission of audio and annotation files, incorporating a basic verification system to check annotation consistency, each being later manually inspected.
Since the launch of the project, dozens of volunteers have contributed 11,381 annotated vocalization units from 97 species, mainly migratory birds but also nocturnal singers, e.g. from the Strigidae family, and other more common species susceptible to vocalizing at night.

Addressing the Dataset Biases.

Beyond enabling a formidable dataset compilation task by splitting the collection and annotation effort, resorting to a community of annotators also introduced biases and heterogeneity in the dataset.
The latter, which comprises recording equipment and location, is usually considered as an asset to improve the robustness of machine learning models, provided a sufficient amount of training data.
However, we inevitably encountered a large imbalance in the proportion of annotations between species, with common birds being disproportionately annotated.
In some cases, we also faced an overrepresentation of a few annotated species in a single location, with the risk of biasing the database towards a restricted pool of individuals.
This was addressed by curating additional audio samples from the Xeno-Canto database
[
28
]
with a focus on increasing the diversity of the original dataset towards more balance between species and higher intra-class variability.
As XC recordings come with a single class label but often feature vocalizations from diverse species, a manual time and frequency annotation process was conducted on these additional samples to align them with the standards of our database.

3.2
Database properties

The main figures regarding the properties of the NBM database are reported in
fig.

1
(left), together with the share represented by its two components, the original NBM base (NBM-Orig.) and the files originating from XC (NBM-XC), in terms of recording duration, file, and annotation number.
The overall database contains almost 38 hours of recording, while total file and annotation numbers amount respectively to 2,077 and 13,359 for a total of 117 bird species.
Among these, 86 species possess at least 10 annotated calls and 56 species at least 100.
Additionally, 63 species have been annotated across at least 10 distinct files and 16 in more than 50 files.
Being an ongoing project, these figures only provide a snapshot of the NBM dataset as additional files get annotated, with the ultimate objective of covering the diversity of migratory calls of European birds as faithfully as possible.

3.3
NBM Test Set

Along with the training dataset described above, we introduce a test set to facilitate future research and monitor the improvements in both data collection and machine learning recognition models.
The scope of the test set is restricted to the 45 species for which we considered the amount of training data to be sufficient (see
section

4.2
for details).
Six audio files were extracted for each species from XC and manually annotated, for a total of 270 test files.
Should the test dataset be enriched with additional data to follow the evolution of the training set, a versioning system will be used to ensure the traceability and replicability of the results.

4
The NBM Detection Model: an Object Detection Baseline

The original annotation procedure of the NBM dataset enables one to train object detectors to precisely localize bird vocalizations in a spectrogram, akin to what constitutes a large body of literature for natural images.
A perfect object detector would be theoretically able to isolate each call, segmenting the input spectrogram in a way that could, for instance, allow one to differentiate between individuals of the same species, enabling a whole range of downstream applications.
This is noticeably different from the usual multi-label classification approach followed for avian sounds
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
34
]
, which only informs on the presence or absence of a given species in a window of several seconds of recording.
Examples of our model’s outputs on the NBM test set can be found in
fig.

2
, displaying the time and frequency localization of detected calls.

In this section, we present the two-stage model that we trained on the NBM dataset, where we brought the problem in the image domain by performing object detection on spectrograms.
The description of the network is followed by the results of quantitative evaluations both on a standard object detection objective and against BirdNet
[
16
]
on a multi-label classification task on sliding windows.

The very competitive results that we obtain on most of the considered species highlight the relevance of the proposed framework, and especially of the NBM database.

4.1
Model Architecture

Figure 3:
Overview of the presented NBM bird sound detection model.

Object detection neural networks can come either as one-stage
[
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
or two-stage models
[
47
,
48
,
31
,
49
]
.
The latter involves a pass through a first network which is tasked with retrieving all potential Regions of Interest (RoIs) in an input image, corresponding to object positions.
In a second stage, another network performs classification on the RoIs.
One-stage object detectors, on the other hand, directly predict a class for each position, or anchor, in the image, including an extra background class.
Both approaches rely on an initial feature extraction through a backbone network, whose depth and expressive power directly impact the quality of the detection.

Owing to preliminary experiments,
we found two-stage models to be significantly easier to train
on our avian sound detection dataset.
We hypothesize that this is due to the overrepresentation of the background class within samples, and that this class is not always correlated with an absence of signal: all sorts of background noises, starting with raindrops, will appear as peaks of intensity in the input spectrograms.
It is therefore an easier task for the model to tackle avian signal detection and classification as two separate problems.

In light of these findings, we built our detection model on the FPN-FasterRCNN (hereafter FRCNN) architecture
[
31
,
32
]
, which we tailored for the detection of bird calls as follows.
The overall architecture can be found in
fig.

3
.

Backbone & FPN.
The proposed NBM detection model is built upon a modular architecture where each of the blocks represented in
fig.

3
operates independently from the others, allowing the exploration of diverse architectures within each block.
In the following, an EfficientNetV2 backbone
[
50
]
is used in place of the VGG16 network of the seminal FRCNN paper.
Similarly, one may explore using several variants of the Feature Pyramid Network (FPN,
[
32
]
), whose primary goal is to feed lower layers of the network with semantic information through a top-down path (
[
51
,
44
]
to cite a few).
We found in our experiments that, for a fixed computational budget, increasing the size of the output channels of a single original FPN layer performed better than stacking layers of more complex FPN variants.

Self-Attention Module.
Contrary to natural images, there is a strong correlation between bird vocalizations that can be found in a given time frame.
Equipping the model with an attention mechanism allows each local prediction to leverage the contextual information in which the call was emitted, leading to overall better predictions.
This is done by performing self-attention
[
52
,
53
]
separately at each intermediate output from the backbone, prior to the FPN (second block of
fig.

3
).

Detection Head.
The detection head is composed of two networks: a Region Proposal Network (RPN) and a Regional CNN (RCNN), which processes and classifies the outputs from the RPN (we refer the reader to the original paper for more details).
One particularity of object detection on spectrograms is the relaxation of the spatial invariance assumption over the vertical axis: similar patterns emitted at two distinct fundamental frequencies are likely to belong to two distinct species.
We propose to encode the absolute frequency position and the relative time position in the RCNN network, when RoIs are extracted from the FPN outputs and pooled to a unique shape
[
54
]
.
This is done by concatenating time and frequency positional encodings
[
53
]
in the channel axis and adding them to the extracted RoI, providing the network with information related to the shape and position of the latter, as depicted in
fig.

4
.

4.2
Model Evaluation

We experimentally evaluate the performances of our detection model trained on the NBM dataset.
Beyond the validation of the architectural choices presented above, these experiments aim to show the relevance of the proposed dataset on the task of precisely localizing migratory bird calls in acoustic recordings.

Evaluation Scope.

As described in
section

3
, collecting the NBM dataset is an ongoing project, where the annotation effort is partly driven by the frequently re-evaluated needs for species suffering from a lack of data samples or diversity.
For a fair evaluation, it is therefore necessary to restrict the scope of the study to species that present both a sufficient number of data samples and that appear in a sufficient number of input files.
This avoids the biases caused by the over-representation of a small number of individuals in a few long training files.
We set the minimal thresholds to 100 training samples and 20 source files, to which we add three migratory species that closely approach the line (
Ixobrychus minutus
&
Anthus pratensis
with 96 samples, and
Nycticorax nycticorax
with 18 files and 85 samples) for a total of 45 species, which can be found in
table

2
.

Technical Details.

All recordings were resampled to 44100 Hz, and we used 1024 points and a hop size of 132 in the STFT to build the spectrograms.
The input size to the model was set to
375\times 1024
, corresponding to a frequency ranging from 500 Hz to 13000 Hz and 3.07 s of audio, a large input size that contains more contextual information for the model.
As we consider objects of smaller size than for the typical detection task, we start extracting activation maps from the second intermediate layer
\mathcal{P}_{2}
of the backbone (corresponding to a reduction by a factor 2 of the input spatial dimensions), up until
\mathcal{P}_{6}
(factor
2^{5}
, respectively).
Finally, experiments also showed that, compared to the original FRCNN configuration, additional anchor aspect ratios (akin to
[
43
]
) and larger RPN/RCNN batch sizes provided further improvements.

4.2.1
Performances on the Detection Task

We calculate the mean average precision (mAP) over the 45 target species on the 271 hand-annotated files from the xeno-canto database that compose the NBM test set.
We found a
mAP value @
\text{IoU}_{0.5}
of 0.67
, which will constitute the baseline for future improvements of both the model and the dataset.

4.2.2
Comparison with BirdNet

In this section, the evaluation is carried out on a multi-label classification formulation of the problem, which allows to compare the performances of the NBM model with the state-of-the-art BirdNet model.
BirdNet builds on an EfficientNet
[
55
]
backbone, and is trained on a dataset that includes the whole XC database to predict the presence of bird species within 3-second time frames.
As our test set originates from XC itself, at least the oldest recordings that compose it likely belong to BirdNet’s training base, and therefore the results presented here should be treated with caution.
However they still provide valuable insights.
The two models are not directly comparable as their scope differs, the NBM model tackling bird sound recognition through the object detection paradigm.
It is possible however to cast its outputs in the multi-label classification setting of BirdNet by aggregating all detected sound events in a given window and attributing the maximum detection confidence to the corresponding species.
The resulting AP scores per species and overall mAP are reported in
table

1
.
Despite the orders of magnitude of difference in the scale of their respective training sets, the NBM model results are highly competitive with those of BirdNet with 0.07 points of difference in mAP.
These results highlight the potential of a precisely annotated, medium-scale dataset (the average number of individual sound events for these 45 species is 306) compared to a much larger-scale, weakly annotated one.
This suggests that limited, well-thought-out annotations efforts can have an important and positive impact on the model’s performance.

4.3
Discussion & Limitations

The results from
table

1
display a high AP variance among species, which mainly illustrates the sensibility of our model to out-of-distribution samples, e.g. in the cases of
Athene noctua
,
Emberiza citrinella
,
Turdus merula
or
Pluvialis apricaria
.
For these species, the distribution of training samples does not fully encompass that of test samples, which may be an indication of a lack of diversity in the recording conditions.
Such observations are crucial to better drive the future annotation effort towards a higher intra-class diversity of training samples.
A second avenue of improvement concerns the species coverage which, when insufficient, can be a source of prediction errors even for more common species as the model may tend to assign unknown vocalizations to the closest samples in the training set.
To mitigate this, we aim to increase the current scope of species meeting the threshold described in
section

4.2
to all nocturnal migratory birds of Europe, along with common species that are susceptible to vocalizing during nighttime.

5
Conclusion

We presented in this work the NBM database, a crowd-sourced dataset of nocturnal migratory bird calls and nocturnal bird songs collected by a community of volunteers across France and completed by hand annotations from Xeno-Canto.
This dataset contains 13,359 precise time and frequency annotations split across 2,077 files, making it the first of its kind for birds of the Western Palearctic.
We further demonstrated the relevance of this original annotation method by training an object detector model able to precisely localize avian sounds on a spectrogram, while being very competitive on multi-label classification with the acclaimed BirdNet model on the 45 most common species of the dataset.
Margins of improvement remain regarding the coverage of the NBM dataset, which we aim to address through the ongoing collection and annotation project.
As the scarcity of precisely annotated avian sounds databases is still a persisting limitation for the improvement of current automatic recognition methods, we believe that the presented dataset will be a valuable contribution to the bioacoustics community.
