 Two friends have I, so grand, so gay,
 A finer pair you never knew,
 A prouder pair you never saw;
 And one of them is Lord Macaw
 And one is Lady Cockatoo.

 And she is dressed in white and gold
 And he is dressed in gold and blue,
 Yet will they deign to shake a claw—
 The great and stately Lord Macaw,
 The handsome Lady Cockatoo.
 ROSE FYLEMAN.

 THE WONDERFUL KNIGHT _By ELEANOR FARJEON_


There was once the most wonderful Knight in Sussex, which is as much as
to say in the world. He came of a race of knights, as a man might come
of a race of farmers, or of fools. And as of such a one you would say,
“He is a born farmer” or “He is a born fool,” so of my hero you would
say, “He is a born knight.”

His name was Sir John-o'-Dreams, and he was such a great knight that he
spent his life in dreaming of the greatest adventure in the world, an
adventure which should be the very top of all that is meant by
knighthood. Several times a day he polished his shield for the adventure
to come; for he would never, he said, undertake it until his shield was
bright enough. He knew that this adventure, when it came, would claim
not only the strength of his arm and the swiftness of his eye: it would
be a call upon his life, which was the lamp of honour, and on his
spirit, which was the light in the lamp.

One morning in May Sir John walked forth to see the world, and to see it
the better he walked on the top of a wall. As he walked he looked up at
the sun till the air was bedazzled. Through the dazzle he presently
heard the flutiest whistle that ever came from a blackbird in Spring.
Suddenly the whistle stopped, and a voice said, “Take care, sir, you'll
fall.” Even as it was said, Sir John stumbled, and kicked over a bucket
of mortar that was on the top of the wall; and if his ankle had not been
gripped at the same moment he would have fallen after it. But by a great
effort he righted himself, and then he saw that his foot was held by a
boy who was straddling the wall.

The fall of the bucket had caused some commotion on the ground, where a
little black pig with a belly like a ball and a tail like a corkscrew,
and a handsome young pullet of the Light Sussex breed, had been
wallowing and picking in the earth at great peace. The boy himself had
carroty hair and blue eyes.

Sir John recovered his balance, and said kindly, “I've disturbed you.”

“That's all right, sir,” said the boy, “I can get on with my job when
you're by.”

“What are you doing?” asked Sir John.

“Holding your foot, begging your pardon, sir.”

“What _were_ you doing?” asked Sir John.

“Mending the wall, sir, and shall be again.”

“What is your name?” asked Sir John.

“Dick Dowett,” said the boy.

“And what is your business?”

“I clean out the pigs and the chickens.”

“It is good work,” said Sir John.

“Well, it's got to be done,” said Dick. And he began once more to
out-whistle the blackbirds.

“You're happy,” said the Knight.

“As the day is long, sir,” said the boy.

“You sleep at night?”

“Like a top.”

“And dream?”

“Never a wink.”

“I wonder,” said Sir John musingly, “whether I would change my state for
yours?”

“Mine, sir!” said Dick. “What for?”

“I don't know,” said the Knight. And he walked on.

He soon reached the end of the wall, and while he was considering
whether to stay up or to jump down, he heard the ringing of a bell in
the countryside. At the sound of it, the folk came running with all
their might from every quarter, and by the time a great crowd had
gathered the Town-Crier had come up. With his right hand he swung his
big bell to and fro, with his left he led by the wrist a girl in tears.
She was a splendid lass in a blue cotton frock, sixteen years old, but
tall for her age. She had a thick plait of brown hair, and the eyes
which she constantly wiped with her apron were browner still.

The Town-Crier stood the girl in full view of the crowd, rang his bell
three times, and cried:

“Oyez! oyez! oyez! This is Audrey of Picketty, who has lost her ball. To
him who will recover it, she offers all she has.”

“And what is that?” cried the crowd in one breath.

“Herself in the gown she stands up in,” said the Town-Crier.

Then the women in the crowd tossed their heads, and said with one
accord, “We must get back to our duties!” And they turned on their heels
and went to where they had come from.

But the men stayed where they were, and looked at Audrey of Picketty in
her faded blue gown, and thought she was no bad reward for a ball. So
they asked:

“What is her ball like?”

The Town-Crier rang his bell again, and said to Audrey:

“What is your ball like?”

Audrey wiped her eyes once more with her apron, and said: “My ball is a
blue-and-green ball with pictures on it—stars and flowers, and cherubs,
and children, as pretty as life. It was given me in my cradle by my
godmother; I grew up playing with it, and it is the only toy I ever
had.”

Then the men asked:

“Where did she lose her ball?”

And the Town-Crier rang his bell, and said to Audrey:

“Where did you lose your ball?”

And Audrey said: “I was playing with it in Picketty, and I tossed it
higher and higher, till the wind took it, and I couldn't see where it
fell. It may have blown a hundred miles, and rolled another hundred.”

Then the men asked:

“When did she lose her ball?”

The Town-Crier rang his bell, and said:

“When did you lose your ball?”

And Audrey said, “Ten years ago, when I was six years old.”

“Bless me!” cried Sir John, from the top of the wall, “and have you been
weeping for it ever since?”

“Oh no, sir,” said Audrey, curtseying. “I forgot all about it till a
week ago, and then for seven nights running I dreamed that it had fallen
in the Murray River.”

“What river is that?” asked Sir John.

“I do not know, sir. But I dreamed it was the biggest river in Sussex,
and full of dangers from beginning to end, and haunted by a sprite.”

Sir John looked down from left to right on the crowd in the meadows, but
no man said a word. Then Sir John cried so clearly that every man could
hear him: “I undertake this adventure. Go home, good folk.”

When the meadow was empty, Audrey of Picketty looked lovingly at Sir
John, and said, “Sir, if you will let me, I will follow you to the
Murray River, and through all the dangers too.”

She spoke with such sweetness and courage that Sir John reached down his
hand to her, saying, “Come, you brave child.” With one nimble leap she
stood beside him on the wall. Then Sir John flung up his head, like a
spirited horse, and strode back along the middle of the wall, looking
neither to the right nor the left, but only at the bright blue sky.

Suddenly he felt his shoulders seized by two soft hands, and his ankle
gripped by one strong one, and looking first behind him and then before
him he saw both Audrey and Dick holding him fast, and regarding him with
affection and anxiety. The mortar-bucket was on the ground again.

“What's the matter with you, children?” said the Knight.

“What's the matter with _you_, sir?” said Dick; for Sir John's cheeks
were wet with tears. “Are you hurt or in trouble?”

“I am well and in joy, Dick,” said the Knight, “for I am about to follow
my heart through a wonderful adventure.”

“Then why do you weep, sir?”

“With looking on the sun,” said the Knight.

“What did you see, sir?”

“I saw danger, and myself going to meet it.”

“Danger, Sir John?”

“Yes, Dick.”

Dick looked bothered, whistled a note or two, and then said, “If you
please, sir, might I go too?”

The Knight's eye kindled, and he laid his hand on the boy's head. “Do
you also hanker for danger?” he asked.

“Not a bit, sir,” said Dick.

“Then why do you wish to come?”

Dick got rather red, cleared his throat once or twice, and said, “To
catch hold of your foot, sir.”

“Yes?” said Sir John inquiringly.

“Yes, if you please, sir. I've finished my job, and I expect we'll be
home in time to feed the pigs and coop up the hens.”

[Illustration: _He strode back along the middle of the wall._]

“I do not know when we shall be home,” said Sir John. “Perhaps never.”

“Just as you please, sir,” said Dick. “Mind where you step.”

“Yes,” said Audrey from the rear, “do be careful, dear Knight.”

“Come, children,” said Sir John.

And he stepped over the boy and passed along the wall, leaving Dick and
Audrey face to face. And Dick, who had not been aware of her until he
heard her clear voice and almost immediately after beheld her rosy face,
scratched his head and said, “Oh dear me!” But Audrey looked after her
Knight with something of the happy care which a young mother feels for
her baby the first time it toddles, and said:

“Will he fall?”

“Not if I can help it,” said Dick.

“Thank you, you good boy,” said she.

Then Dick picked up his bucket and went after Sir John, and Audrey went
after Dick; and the round black pig, whose name was Paul, and the Light
Sussex pullet, whose name was Emily, ran below them beside the wall
until they reached the end of it and had to jump off.

Now here follows the tale of their adventures, hour by hour.


 TWELVE O'CLOCK

The sun was lazing in a blue-and-white sky bathed in his own gold steam;
but the men and the girl stepped briskly, and as for the pig and the
hen, they gambolled on the road. It happened, however, that Sir John's
eagerness carried him a few steps in advance of his companions, who thus
had all the talk to themselves. Presently, they came to Chiltington, the
prettiest village in England, as, in fact, every second village in the
West of Sussex is. But for all its prettiness it was like a fiddle
without its strings, for not a living soul was to be seen there, indoors
or out.

“A strange place,” said Sir John, pausing. “Time, children, is like a
lamplighter who comes at night to kindle his lamps, and comes by day to
put them out again. Once this dumb blind village was full of life.”

“And not long since, sir,” said Dick, “for there's a spud and a rake in
that garden, and a packet of dwarf beans spilled where somebody has
dropped 'em to be off in a hurry.”

“A hundred years ago, perhaps,” said Sir John dreamily, “and his hurry
is ended.”

“The earth's fresh turned, sir,” said Dick. As he spoke the silence was
broken by a small weeping and whimpering at the back of the cottage.

“Someone is unhappy,” said Audrey.

“I'll just go and see,” said Dick.

So, followed by the others, he went round to the back, and found a very
Old Man chopping down a bed of mustard-and-cress with an axe. The
mustard-and-cress was planted in the name of

 BARBIE

and as he chopped the Old Man whimpered and wept.

“Do you want that cut down, gaffer?” asked Dick.

“Yes, boy.”

“All of it, gaffer?”

“Yes, boy.”

“There's more than you'll need to your salad,” said Dick, taking out his
penknife.

“I do like mustard-and-cress,” said Audrey.

“You're welcome to it all, pretty. No more salads for me.”

“Thank you, gaffer,” said Dick. “We'll take a bunch, then, for it goes
down nicely for supper.” And he put a handful into his bucket.

Audrey said, “What are you crying for?” and wiped the Old Man's eyes
with her pocket-handkerchief.

“Her,” said the Old Man, and pointed to the name. “Let cress go, since
she be gone.”

“I'm so sorry,” said Audrey, and wiped her own eyes with her
pocket-handkerchief.

“Heaven's a good place they say,” said Dick, and wiped his eyes with the
back of his hand.

“Ay, if 'twere heaven,” sobbed the Old Man, “instead of betwixt and
between.”

“Where's that?” said Dick.

“Top of windmill sail,” said the Old Man, “and never a stir in the air.
All the folk is gone to blow her down, but I misdoubt me they'll do it.”

“We've come in the nick of time,” said Sir John. “Dick, here's work to
be done!” And he sat down on a chopping block and began to polish his
shield with great energy.

“We'll just go along, sir,” said Dick; but the Knight was already lost
in the adventure to come, and did not hear him.

So Dick and Audrey went through the village and came to the windmill,
where the villagers were assembled, hissing and blowing with all their
might. Their chests panted and their cheeks puffed as though they would
burst. But they could not budge the sails, to the topmost of which clung
a little girl, whose silky hair flew out all round her head like bubbles
of sunlight. With one arm she squeezed to her breast an orange cat with
golden eyes.

“Oh Dick!” said Audrey, and clasped her hands.

“You stay here,” said Dick, and ran into the mill; but she followed at
his heels. They mounted the stairs, and when they were come to the top
Audrey caught his sleeve, and said again, “Oh Dick!”

“Well, it's got to be done,” said Dick. “There's no call to be
frightened, lass.” For her cheeks had turned as white as linen laid to
bleach on the grass in the sun.

Dick clambered out to the sails, and all the people below began to
cheer, and Audrey watched him with her heart in her mouth. When he
brought them in, the child was frowning and the cat mewing. But Audrey
held out her arms and took them both to her, and then the child began to
smile and the cat to purr.

“Oh, Barbie!” said Audrey, “what _did_ you get up there for?”

“I wanted to see,” said the child.

“Did you see?” said Dick.

“Yes,” said Barbie, “I saw the Murray River.”

“That's where we're going,” said Audrey, with joy in her face.

“Then I will come too,” said Barbie, “and so will Rufus.”

They went down the stairs, and as they came out of the mill the people
cheered more than ever, and a woman ran to meet them.

“Mother,” said Barbie, “I'm going to the Murray River with the big boy
and girl.”

“Very well, pet,” said the woman, “but be back by bedtime.”

“She shall be, ma'am,” said Audrey.

Sir John, who at this moment arrived, stepped forward with his bright
shield, looked earnestly at the child, and said, “Do you then know the
way to the Murray River?”

“Yes, I do,” said Barbie, “as far as the Round-House; and then it was
tea-time. But I'm going all the way to-day, if it's tea-time or not.”

“Dick,” said the Knight, his eyes shining, “heaven has sent us an angel.
Come, children.”

The child kissed her mother farewell, and away strode Sir John, and
Dick, and Audrey after him, with Barbie skipping between them; and Paul
the pig, and Emily the hen, and Rufus the cat brought up the rear.

“What a bright shield he's got,” said Barbie.

“Yes,” said Dick, “it's as bright as a new shilling. There's no man in
Sussex with a shield so bright, is there?” He looked at Audrey, and she
looked tenderly after her Knight, and said, “No man.”


 ONE O'CLOCK

The child pointed the way over a hot, open common, where the sun had
already, as it seemed, begun to drop a shower of golden sparks among the
gorse.

Then under her guidance they crossed a warren and a highroad, and
suddenly entered a cool wood, where sparkles of sunlight could only be
seen here and there when the leaves shook, and the trees stood in a
shimmer of green dreams. And their steps grew slower and their voices
lower, as it must always be in woods. At the end of it they entered a
sunlit lane with another wood beyond. The unexpected light made their
sight dance, so that at first they could not see properly, but they
could hear, at a little distance, the sound of young owls hooting,
“Tu-wick! tu-wick!” and their mother answering, “Tu-whoo-whoo-oo!” And
then they saw that the hooting came from a small circular building on
the edge of the forest. The building was of stone, and had one door in
its wall, and a very small, barred window above it. Here Barbie stopped.

“I don't know which way now,” said she. “This is the Round-House, and I
didn't get farther than that.”

“Perhaps the owls know,” said Audrey, “for they're wiser than most.
Owlets!” cried she, “tell us which way to the Murray River.”

“Tu-wick! tu-wick! tu-wick!” answered the owlets, like a merry taunt.

“Let's go in and see the little owls,” said Barbie.

Dick tried the door. “It's locked,” said he, “and too thick to break
down. We must look for help to others than owlets.”

So they went a little way up the road, and soon stumbled on Sir John,
who had gone ahead as usual, and was standing motionless with one hand
upheld as though bidding them to listen. And what they now heard was
husky calls of “Heave-O! heave!” uttered every half-minute; but the
calls were broken by great gulpings and chokings. The sounds came from
behind a little farm which stood half in the lane, half in the wood; its
doorways were open back and front, and a tunnel of air and light ran
through the farmhouse. You could look straight through from the sunny
lane on this side to the greenwood trees on that.

“See, children,” said Sir John, “how brief is the passage through the
house of life; from the light of our coming to the twilight of our going
it is but a dozen steps, no more. And the end is lovelier than the
beginning. Shall we pass through?”

“I think we'd better, sir,” said Dick; “there seems to be some bother
out there, doesn't there?”

For by this time the choking and gulping had got the upper hand of the
calling, and the “Heave-Os!” were almost drowned in sobs.

“Oh hurry!” said Audrey.

They hurried; and in a strip of kitchen-garden that lay between the
farmyard and the trees they found a middle-aged Farmer and his men
hauling on a lettuce with a rope. The Farmer stood gripping at the
nearest lettuce, and his three men stood behind him. When he cried
“Heave!” they set their jaws and hove, and the lettuce came up and they
fell down. Then they picked themselves up and dusted their breeches and
noosed another lettuce and hung on to the rope, awaiting the Farmer's
signal. But as often as not they had to guess at it, because of the
choke in his throat.

When the men saw the little procession troop through the doorway they
paused in their labour, wiping the sweat from their brows with
red-and-white handkerchiefs.

“Can I help you pull your lettuces, Farmer?” said Dick.

“You can, young man,” groaned the Farmer.

“Every man Jack of them?”

“Every man Jack of them.”

“You'll have a rare feed,” said Dick, beginning to pull up the lettuces,
of which there were quite eighty, with his two hands.

[Illustration:  ... _they paused in their labour_.]

“Lettuces are delicious,” remarked Audrey.

“Take what you please, young lass. They'd but stick in my throat.”

“We're much obliged to you, Farmer,” said Dick, “for a lettuce suits
well with a salad of cress.” And he popped three fat-hearted lettuces
into his bucket, while the Farmer gulped and choked.

“What makes you so unhappy?” asked Audrey.

“Because I shall see my little daughter Christie no more in this life.
And now the sight of these lettuces, which she planted, is more than I
can bear.”

“Alas!” said Audrey, and turned her head away.

Dick stood first on one foot and then on the other to show his sympathy,
and asked, “When did it happen, Farmer?”

“An hour ago,” said he, “and the fault was mine. For nothing would
content her but she must be away to the Murray River, where time and
again I've told her she'd catch her death of cold. But it's as though
that water were fire to the blood of all young things, and when they
will go they will go. So at last I locked her up in the Round-House in
the wood, and for safety I tied the key to the handle of the
well-bucket; but when next I went to draw water I forgot it was there,
and, as luck would have it, the bucket slipped from its buckle, and is
now swimming underground, as you can see for yourself.” And he kicked
the cover from the well, and there, on the dark water, swam the tilted
bucket, forty feet down.

Sir John had listened attentively to the Farmer's tale, and now his eyes
kindled with their fine fire. “Dick,” said he, “here's an adventure
ready to my hand.”

“Yes, sir,” said Dick, “The key must be got, for the door can't be
broken; I've tried it already.”

But Sir John did not heed him, for he was already polishing his shield
with might and main.

Then Dick took the well-rope in his hand and tested it carefully, and
found it sound; and next he fastened the swivel of the buckle to his
leather belt.

“Oh Dick!” exclaimed Audrey, and clutched his shoulders with all the
strength of her two hands.

“It's got to be done,” said he. “There's no call to be frightened,
Audrey.” For her face was as blanched as skim milk when it foams one way
from the separator, while the yellow cream slides another.

He signed to the men, and they came forward and slowly lowered him into
the well, while he gripped the rope above his head; and Audrey shut her
eyes and counted a hundred-and-fifty. Then she opened them and leaned
over the well, and hers were the first hands to help him as he came to
the top, key, bucket, and all; though when he saw her stooping above him
he cried, “Go back, silly!” for fear she should fall. However, in
another moment he stood beside her in the yard; but her knees shook so
that she went down on them under pretence of unfastening the buckle from
his belt.

“Now we'll let out the little girl,” said Dick; and they hastened to the
Round-House where the young owls were “Tu-wicking!” louder than ever.

“Owlet!” cried Audrey again, “we're come to let you fly, if only you
will tell us the way to the Murray River!”

At that a lovely little fair face, full of dreamy mischief, with soft,
short hair falling loose about its ears, peeped through the bars.

“Tu-wick!” said she. “I won't come out, and I won't tell, unless you let
me and Mike come too.”

“I'll answer for that,” said Dick, and he turned the key in the lock;
and the child came running out with a wiry Irish terrier at her heels; a
lean, excitable dog with loving eyes.

Her father caught her to his heart and kissed her repeatedly, and she
returned his kisses, saying, “Good-bye, father, because I'm going now.
I'm going to the Murray River with these people and animals.”

“Ay, so you shall, you shall!” said the Farmer; “but do not be home
after dark.”

“I'll see to it, sir,” said Audrey.

By this time Sir John had joined them with his gleaming shield, and,
looking gravely at Christie, he said, “It is you, dear child, that can
lead us to the Murray River?”

“Oh yes,” said Christie, looking as gravely at him; “as far as the
garden in the wood. But I've never been right down the river yet.”

“Dick,” said the Knight, “the fairies are guiding us. Come, children.”

And he strode the way Christie pointed her finger, and she and Barbie
ran after him, and Dick and Audrey kept close behind them; and Paul the
pig, and Emily the hen, and Rufus the cat, and Mike the dog brought up
the rear.

“How well you do owls,” said Barbie to Christie.

“Yes,” said Christie; “and what a beautiful shield your father's got.”

She looked from Barbie to Audrey as she spoke, but Dick said: “He isn't
their father, but his shield is as bright as the full moon. There's not
a man in all England with such a beautiful shield, is there?” And he
turned to Audrey, who looked wistfully after her Knight, and then on the
ground, and said, “Not in all England.”


 TWO O'CLOCK

The sun was beginning to get tangled in a smear of cloud as they turned
out of the white lane into a green one. At the end of it was an old
gate, opening into a garden in the middle of the wood, as pretty as the
Garden of Eden. But this Eden also had its sorrows, for from somewhere
inside it a sturdy hullabalooing greeted their ears, interrupted with
the sound of small explosions.

“See, children,” said Sir John, “how deceptive is even the earthly
paradise in its promise of eternal joy. For of what more blessed and
lovely surroundings than these could any man dream? Yet he who dwells
here is the prey of grief. Let us look into this man's trouble.”

“Yes, sir,” said Dick, as a louder explosion than usual rent the air,
“for I doubt he's up to some mischief.”

So they went round a hedge of flowers, and found the miserable Adam of
this Eden seated in the middle of a small square of earth filled with
one of everything that could be thought of in a little space by a little
mind: such as one sweet pea and one spring onion, one lupin, one potato,
one broad bean, one double daisy, and one of whatever else you fancy.
But its four sides were framed in radishes, red and white, which an
extremely small boy, with the darkest brown eyes that ever smudged a
dark brown face with tears, was endeavouring to blow up with paper caps
full of gunpowder.

“Do you want those radishes up, sonny?” said Dick.

“'Ss!” hissed the little boy through his tears.

“What, all the lot?”

“'Ss!”

“You'll have a pain,” said Dick, beginning to pull up the radishes.

“Sha'n't eat 'em!” said the little boy.

“Don't you like radishes?” said Audrey. “_I_ do.”

“Then you eat 'em!” said the little boy.

“Thank you, sonny,” said Dick, “so we will; for radishes look pretty in
a red-and-white ring round a lettuce on a garland of cress.”

Saying which, he dropped a couple of handfuls in his bucket.

“Why were you blowing the radishes up?” asked Audrey tenderly, taking
the baby on her knee and drying his dirty face.

“'Cause they're _Hers_!” shouted the little boy, pointing suddenly at
the plank at the foot of the garden. “And she won't let me paddle
without grown-ups.”

And then he stuck his finger in his mouth, and stared from Dick to
Audrey, and abruptly rolled out of her lap and tumbled down the garden,
crying, “Sylvia! _they've come!_ so may I paddle _now_?”

Down the garden the company trooped after him; and standing on the plank
that lay over the brook, they saw the loveliest little creature in all
the kingdoms of the young; though whether she was sprung from the
kingdom of angels, of fairies, or of children none of them knew. For her
eyes were as merry as fairies and as sweet as angels, and the odd little
smiles that sat on her cheeks and in the corners of her mouth were both
tricksy and tender; and she had a lovely little body, clad in a single
blue-green garment that left her slender brown limbs bare to shoulder
and knee. She was standing with her feet close together and her head
bent, and her elbows lay straight by her sides, but her hands were
outstretched, palm uppermost, clasping the two ends of a hazel-fork,
which she held over the brook.

[Illustration: Color illustration of a woman in a blue dress and a small
child in an orange top walking across a wooden plank or bridge over a
stream in a wooded setting.]

“Wilf! Wilf!” said she. “How do I know if you can paddle till I know if
there's water here?”

And she fixed her eyes on the prong of the hazel-fork, and it began to
turn downwards, until at last it pointed directly at the brook flowing
under the plank. Then this child, for a child she was as well as a
sprite, glanced swiftly from the dark baby tugging her garment to the
people standing on the bank, and said, very seriously, but as though her
little mouth were pinching back some secret laughter, “Yes, there is
water here, and you may paddle if they will too.”

“Of course we will!” said Audrey, clapping her hands.

“Hurrah!” said Dick, swinging his bucket.

“Oh!” said Christie and

“Oh!” said Barbie, jumping for joy.

“It's very, very dangerous,” said Sylvia. “There are rapids and rocks,
and jungles that grow from bank to bank where you must crawl in the
water; and there are wrecks and whales and alligators and sea-serpents
and sirens and midges. You'll get bitten and drowned and dashed to
pieces and eaten up and put under a spell for ever and ever unless——”

“Unless what?” cried everybody.

“Unless I like,” said Sylvia, with her funny little smile.

By this time they were all barefoot, with their stockings and shoes
slung round their necks.

“Who's afraid?” cried Dick.

“Not me!” cried Audrey, Barbie, Christie and Wilf.

And Paul said “Umph!” and Emily “Cluck!” and Rufus “Miaow!” and Mike
“Wough! wough!”

“Follow me then!” cried the elfin Sylvia with a shrill sweet laugh; and
she dropped from the plank into the stony channel of the brook that was
sunk so deep between its banks of hazel and alder, with all their tangle
of undergrowth between, that it could scarcely be seen from the garden.
And after her splashed Wilf, and after him Barbie clutching Rufus, and
then Christie dragging Mike, followed by Audrey carrying Emily, with
Dick last of all and Paul tucked under his arm. They were all shouting
with glee.


 THREE O'CLOCK

The sun was like a golden boat afloat upon a blue sky speckled with
white waves when they came out at the source, scratched and tattered and
soaked to the waist. They were still shouting with glee.

Christie had lost her ribbon, and Barbie had a cut on her ankle, and
Wilf had bumped his head, and Audrey's plait was all astray, and a
fragment of bramble, caught in the web of her hair, still hung at her
neck; and Dick's shirt was torn at the shoulder. But Sylvia came out as
whole as she went in.

The first thing they saw when they scrambled up the bank was Sir John,
sitting under an oak-tree rubbing his shield. He had walked along the
bank by the meadow and wood through which the brook ran, and here he
awaited their coming.

“Well, children,” said he, with his gentle smile, “has none of you
perished?”

“Neither man, woman, nor child, sir,” said Dick.

“It was because of Dick,” said Sylvia. “He's the cunningest mariner I
ever knew. He overcame all the dangers and protected the others.”

“It was because of Sylvia,” said Dick. “She's the kindliest water-sprite
that ever guarded poor mortals in peril. She wouldn't let harm touch us.
Though, to be sure, Mother'll have to put a patch on my shirt, all along
of the sharks.”

“And the sirens stole my ribbon!” said Christie eagerly.

“And the dragons bit my foot!” said Barbie proudly.

“And the giants bumped my head!” muttered Wilf darkly.

“And the witches caught me by the hair!” said Audrey excitedly.

“And Emily laid an egg,” said Sylvia. “And Dick saved them all, and the
egg too.”

“Hard-boiled egg looks nice on a salad,” said Dick, “cut into quarters
like white-and-gold canoes a-sail on a green lettuce sea, did we but
know where to boil it.”

“The Hermit of Hardham will boil it,” said Sylvia, “for he lives on eggs
and onions.”

“Where does the Hermit dwell?” asked Sir John.

“In a curl of the river,” said Sylvia.

“Then his way lies our way,” said the Knight. “Come, children.”

And he went ahead where Sylvia pointed; and she, turning him with a
touch now to the left, now to the right, tripped at his heels, and Wilf
toddled at hers, with Christie and Barbie in line like follow-my-leader,
Dick and Audrey coming abreast; and Rufus, wet and dejected, and Mike,
wet and in ecstasy, and Paul, wet and phlegmatic, and Emily, wet and in
a perfect panic of triumph, brought up the rear.

“Hasn't your grandfather got a grand shield?” said Sylvia over her
shoulder.

The children laughed, and Dick answered: “He has neither child nor
grandchild, but his shield is as bright as his own dreams. Why, there
isn't a man in the world with so splendid a shield, is there?” And he
smiled at Audrey, but she looked sorrowfully after her Knight, and then
she looked at Dick, and shook her head.


 FOUR O'CLOCK

Led by Sylvia, the little company came out upon the road, which, after
all their little forest ways and lanes and by-paths seemed like the very
highroad of the world; and soon after they reached a great bridge with a
broad river flowing under it. Sir John's face lit up as though it had
been a cloud through which the sun had passed, and he cried “Ha!” and
stood stock still in the middle of the bridge.

“Is this the river, child, at last?” he said to Sylvia.

“Yes,” said she, “and the Hermit is over there behind the old canal
bed.”

“There let him bide,” said Sir John, “for now my heart dances in the
very shadow of its adventure.” And taking Audrey's hand, he added, “For
your sake, dear child, to whom I am pledged, I will follow this water
till I find the ball!”

She looked at him steadily, and said in her clear voice: “And I too have
given you my word. So marry me when you will.”

“I will marry you,” said Sir John, “as soon as we can find one to wed
us.”

“The Hermit of Hardham will do that,” said Sylvia, “for he sits all the
year round hoping for a wedding, and he has never wed anyone yet.”

“It is a pity to disappoint him,” said Sir John; “yet I will not lose my
river for a thousand hermits.”

“You won't lose it,” said Sylvia, “if you go by Hardham you'll find it
again at Pulborough Bridge as the crow flies.”

“So be it,” said the Knight. “Come, children.”

They went forward swiftly through the high, narrow path by the sunken
bed of the old canal, and at the other end climbed up the grassy
hillocks where the hermit of Hardham sat hoping for a wedding in a
little chapel of stone archways, with neither roof nor doors nor
windows, but with the bright air of heaven to serve him as all three.
Within the chapel was an altar decked with flowers, and flowers hung in
garlands and posies about the old stone-work, so that the chapel
blossomed like a bride's bower. And the Hermit sat on a boulder in the
evening sunlight, clad in a white robe, with a golden ring on either
hand, and a bowlful of crumbs on his left, and a dish of cabbage leaves
on his right, and a saucer of sugar and water at his sandalled feet. And
all about him were small rabbits at play, whom he fed with green leaves
from his right, and amongst them hopped a multitude of birds picking up
the crumbs he scattered with his left, some of the birds sitting on his
wrist and his knee to pick the crumbs from his hand and robe. And a
chaffinch was even clinging to the long, white beard on his breast where
a few crumbs had fallen, and before she flew off she pulled out three
hairs for her nest, and he suffered her, looking after her with his
gentle eyes. And a host of tiny insects hummed and hovered and sipped at
the saucer of sweet water. And a very small calf had got into the chapel
and had knocked down the Hermit's pitcher of supper milk, and was
licking it up. And this too he suffered with a loving look from his blue
eyes. And the tender sunshine bathed him from the crown of his head to
the sole of his foot.

But when he saw the company mounting the slope he said, “Friends, here
come brothers and sisters.” And he rose up so softly in his place that
not a bird or beast or insect was fluttered, and he stepped forward with
his old hands outstretched, and took Audrey's in his right, saying,
“Welcome, sweet bride.” Then he turned to Dick, and taking him by the
left hand said, “And you, young groom, welcome! to the loveliest
adventure in life.”

Audrey hung down her head, while Dick threw up his, and looked to Sir
John to speak. But at the word adventure Sir John had sat down and begun
to polish his shield.

There was a very long pause, in which nothing happened, and nobody
except Sir John knew what to do.

And then Audrey, in great confusion, whispered, “Oh Dick!”

Dick scratched his head, looked once more at Sir John, and said, “Well,
it's got to be done. There's no call to be frightened, dear.” For her
cheeks had turned as pale as wild roses before they are about to fall.

So Dick and Audrey went into the chapel with the Hermit of Hardham, and
the children and the animals went too. There the Hermit took from his
own hands the two gold rings, and wedded Dick and Audrey.


 FIVE O'CLOCK

When Dick and Audrey were wedded, they came out of the chapel amid the
singing of birds and the humming of insects, and the cheeping and
laughing of animals and children, who played together in the grass.

[Illustration: _Sir John began to polish his shield._]

And Sir John looked up from his shield, which shone less brightly than
their eyes, and said, “How happy you look!” So they explained why. And
he looked thoughtfully at Audrey, and said, “Who then will find you your
ball?” And she said she didn't mind about it any more. “Yet,” said Sir
John, “I must seek it still, for to find that ball of stars and flowers
at the end of the Murray River is still my dream, dear child, if it is
no longer yours.”

He rose to go, but Dick said wouldn't he stay to the wedding-party
first? And they made a green bowl of lettuce-leaves, and lined it with a
nest of cress, and set the radishes in a circle of scarlet and snow upon
the nest, and hard-boiled Emily's egg, and laid it in gold and silver
boats upon the lettuce-leaves. And the Hermit of Hardham fetched a
butterfly-net, and with it caught the globe of a great Spanish onion
that was dangling among many others from a cornice of the chapel, and
with a knife he divided it star-wise into eight points of the compass,
and he opened back the points till the onion lay unfolded like a
water-lily, and he set it in the centre of the salad within the crown of
radishes. As he did it he wept, for neither joy nor sorrow.

This beautiful salad was the wedding-party, and they all had some.


 _Six O'Clock_

The sun was almost asleep and the moon very nearly awake when the feast
was ended, and Dick and Audrey said it was time to take the children
home. But Sir John said he must go his own way by himself. He kissed the
children and Audrey farewell, and, taking Dick's hand, said, “Do your
best to look after her without me.”

“I'll try, sir,” said Dick Dowett.

“I would stay and help you,” said Sir John-o'-Dreams, “but my shield is
bright enough at last, and I must find and follow the Murray River.”

He strode away in the evening light up the road to Pulborough where a
river runs under a bridge. And as they watched his glittering shield out
of sight, Christie murmured to Barbie, “But we found and followed the
Murray River.” And Barbie whispered, “Yes, that's the Arun over there.
He's going the wrong way.” And Sylvia smiled her little screwed-up
smile, and Wilf was fast asleep, and nobody else heard.


 SEVEN O'CLOCK

When the children were all safe in their homes, and Dick and Audrey were
walking back to theirs, he took something from his pocket and put it in
her hand.

“What's this?” said Audrey.

“It's a wedding-present,” said Dick. “I found it in the brook when we
were paddling.”

And Audrey laughed for joy and said: “Why, it's my ball! Look! there are
the flowers and children on the green side, and the stars and cherubs on
the blue side, as fresh as when my godmother gave it me. Who would have
thought it would keep so fresh and beautiful?”

She tossed it in the air, caught it, kissed it on the green side and the
blue side, and put it in her pocket.

 His nose is in the air,
 And people look at him and smile;
 He seems so haughty all the while,
 I hope he doesn't care.

 There isn't any chain at all
 Where he is turned towards the wall;
 It doesn't go all round.

 His spots are big and shining black,
 His eyes are black as well;
 I often think that he may mind
 The way his chain leaves off behind,
 But I shall never tell.

 THE FIVE KNIGHTS _By HUGH CHESTERMAN_


 SIR TUSH: THE INDISCREET KNIGHT

 Three gay Crusaders rode to war,
 Sir Twist and stout Sir Tarrymore.
 Of better men you've never heard.
 (Sir Tush of Tooting made the third.)

 They sallied forth in brave array—
 O passing fierce and fleet were they!
 “Our duty's plain,” they said, “we've got
 To show the Saracen What's What.”

 And when they'd gone, fair ladies sighed
 For news of them at every tide,
 And naught their tears could stay or stem,
 For never more came news of them.

 Alack! the first upon my list
 To fall in fight was great Sir Twist,
 And stout Sir Tarrymore was vexed
 (And rightly) when his turn came next.

 Sir Tush lived on, till once at Sceeva
 He called a Turk an unbeliever.
 The Paynim, with a whispered “Hush!”
 Decapitated rash Sir Tush.


 SIR BRUIN BYPH: THE FOOLISH KNIGHT

 Sir Bruin Byph, a witless peer,
 To Palestine went riding,
 A foolish knight—you see him here
 His mettled steed bestriding.

 His squire was a personage
 Of very few pretensions;
 A pensive, inoffensive page,
 A youth of no dimensions.

 And when Sir Bruin smiled and smirked
 The page would raise no laughter;
 And if Sir Bruin quipped and quirked
 The page came silent after:

 And silent kept till fond Sir Byph
 Was slaughtered by a Paynim,
 O then he laughed, _and_ laughed, as if
 No power could restrain him!

 SIR BEVILLE BLEYDE: THE AUDACIOUS KNIGHT

 Of all the knights of Southern France
 Who measured sword or broke a lance
 In tilt or tourney,
 Count Beville Bleyde would bow to none:
 No matter how—he always won.
 And he would journey,
 On bellicose adventure bent,
 To places on the Continent.
 He knew the Lowlands, Russia, Spain,
 Made war in Flanders, and had slain
 A hundred knights of Acquitaine—
 _And_ single-handed.
 And once he kept, at Tinchebrai,
 A Norman regiment at bay:
 You could not count his blows, they say,
 So fast they landed.
 O he was brave; but rumour hints
 (And as he has been dead long since
 I may be candid)
 That there was one whose temper made
 The bold Sir Beville sore afraid—
 It was his Countess, Madam Bleyde.

 SIR TIVOLI TRAIL: THE INDOLENT KNIGHT

 Slack is the snail and most sluggish the sloth;
 As a slow-motion picture the tortoise beats both;
 But worse than the tortoise, the sloth, or the snail
 Was that indolent warrior, Tivoli Trail.
 Late for his breakfast, his tea, and his dinner,
 At hustling or bustling—well, any beginner
 Would put to the blush this unpunctual sinner.

 THE SILVER SHIP _By ROY MELDRUM_


On a September day—many hundred years ago—a boy, named Robin, was
playing alone in the great forest of Stoke and Sayes. He was perched on
a long branch of an oak tree, and balanced on his hands and knees so
still that not a leaf trembled. He looked like a large green squirrel
which had lost its tail; and, like a squirrel, he was listening, as if
his life depended on it, for every sound he could hear in the still
depths of the forest. Close above his head he heard the sound of a jay's
wing, as she wandered from one tree to another; and below, the rustle of
deer as they cropped the grass in a sunny glade near by. From far away,
echoing under the leaves, he heard the faint murmur of bird and beast,
broken now and again by the quick tap of a woodpecker on some distant
hollow trunk, and beyond all these sounds he seemed to hear for a moment
one unlike all the rest—one which puzzled and troubled him. The next
moment it had gone. But he still crouched, waiting to hear it again.

Meanwhile, the deer were coming slowly into sight, one after another,
step by step, eating their way nearer to his tree; and sometimes one,
sometimes another would throw up its head, listening like Robin for the
same strange sound. But after a while they seemed to think no more of
it, and then, when they were all grazing peacefully, and the nearest was
some fifty yards away, he played his first trick on them. He lowered
himself slowly from the branch, until he hung at arm's length, and then
just as slowly raised himself again, and then inch by inch crawled along
the branch, until he was hidden from the deer by the trunk. There he
lowered himself again, and this time dropped to the ground, and peering
round the trunk, saw that not one of the deer had raised its head, and
he smiled with sudden pleasure. He smiled, because he was sure that, if
the deer could not see or hear him, no man, not even the cleverest,
could track him down or find out the secret that was hidden in that oak
tree.

[Illustration: _But he still crouched, waiting to hear it again._]

To him it was a most important secret. For though he was still a boy,
scarce fourteen years old, and as light of heart as he was light of
foot, already in his life he had seen strong castles set on fire, and
soldiers without pity rob peaceful towns, and drive men, women, and
children out to die in winter snows. And so, though he was the son of an
Earl and page to the rich Baron of Sayes, he did not trust himself to
them; and he took great care that nobody at all knew where he went as
often as he slipped through the shadows of the forest; and on these
journeys sometimes he would carry with him a coil of rope, sometimes a
saw, sometimes a sheepskin, or a crock, or a knife, and many other
things besides; and he took them all to that great oak tree against
which he was now leaning; for above in the broad fork of its branches he
had made himself a hiding-place where he could sleep and, if need be,
store his food and live in perilous seasons. That was his forest secret.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of dense trees or a low
hedge with a small figure seated underneath the foliage on the right.]

The deer came still closer, and again he played a trick on them. This
time he laughed like a yaffle, and this time one or two heads rose in
quick suspicion, and he knew that his laugh was not as much like a
bird's as it should be, and he frowned, and for a long time he stood
leaning by the tree, thinking over the mistake he had made; and all the
while the deer were glancing round them anxiously. Then of a sudden in
the dark stillness beyond the sunny glade a twig snapped with a sharp
echo, and before the echo had gone, the deer one and all bounded past
him in full flight, and he crouched against the roots of the tree, to
watch.

The next minute a man came out from the shadows beyond the glade, and
after looking carefully round about him, began to run towards the tree.

“A poor runner for all he is a big man and wears a red beard,” thought
Robin, as he watched him lumbering over the grass. Nearer and nearer he
came, until at length he was close behind the tree. Then there was a
soft thud on the ground, and Robin, gliding a little way round the
trunk, saw the runner lying on his face, his big shoulders rising and
falling as he struggled for breath. Robin waited, suspiciously watching
every deep shadow round the glade.

Presently the man raised himself on his elbow, and sighed in weariness,
as though he was speaking to the ground, “I cannot go further. They must
take me.”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a large leafy tree with a
person lying on the ground beneath it and another figure standing
nearby.]

When he heard those words, Robin stepped forward, burning with a boyish
pity for one who was being pursued. “By your grace, Sir,” he said
defiantly, “they must take me too then.”

The man stared, as dazed as if the oak itself had spoken aloud.

“How far are they behind?” asked Robin eagerly.

The man shook his head.

“Have they hounds with them?”

“I am not certain,” said the man, laying one hand on the knotted trunk
and rising slowly to his feet.

“Follow me then,” said Robin; “I can save you.”

The man smiled, and shook his head at this confident boy; but for all
that he followed him. Listening for every sound upon the air, Robin
glided away from his oak tree, and past another of great girth, whose
branches spread into those of the first, until he came to a little bank,
at the bottom of which under an archway of branches ran a stream. Into
this he stepped up to his knees.

“Come down to me and climb on my back,” he said, setting his arms
against the bank, as if he were playing leapfrog. The man obeyed in
silence. “And now swing up to the third branch above your head. Quick!”

The man looked up into the tree, and shook his head once again, but once
again he could not resist the eager boy who was trying to help him, and
praying that his strength would hold out yet a little while, he planted
his dripping foot on Robin's shoulders, and with much labour and
snapping of twigs hoisted himself into the tree. Robin waited, listening
impatiently to the noise, until the man had reached the third thick
bough, and then swung himself up too in his turn, and being nimbler of
foot and much lighter in weight, quickly took the lead. First they
climbed up and up, and then, when at last they reached a main stem, the
man saw what cunning was in the boy's plan. High above the lower
branches it was as though a pathway had been made from one tree to the
other, and along this they passed cautiously, listening lest at any
moment they might hear the sound of voices through the leaves below. So
they moved, foot by foot, towards the fork of that first big oak tree
where Robin had his forest home, and at length reached it, before the
pursuers had come in sight. There the stranger sank down, and nodding as
if to say, “I thank you,” closed his eyes in utter weariness. For three
days and three nights he had not slept, and his strength was spent.

[Illustration: _Hurrying along behind a large hound._]

Meanwhile, Robin was peering through the branches, and as he caught
sight of a flash of red and silver, he quivered with a new excitement.
These were serving-men of the proud Abbot of Dore, nearly a dozen, armed
with swords and bows, hurrying along behind a large hound, which ran
close to the earth straight towards the oak tree. There for a moment it
seemed to hesitate, and then, with a low cry, it headed fast for the
stream. What an age it seemed to Robin, as he heard the voices arguing
and shouting to one another round the shadows of the next tree, and what
a relief when he heard the sound of splashing water, and at length the
voices dying away into the forest! After much wrangling, the pursuers
had decided that the fugitive had gone along the stream to put them off
his scent, and get back into his old tracks again, and that way they
went also.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a person with short hair
holding a bowl from which steam or smoke rises.]

So once again stillness came over the great woodland. But now Robin's
secret was no longer his own. At first, as he watched the stranger
soundly sleeping among his own sheepskins, he felt rather jealous of
him; but then he remembered that it had all been his own plan, and he
became ashamed that he should grudge help to anyone, who was in danger
from the Abbot of Dore; for of the Abbot's evil ways he had heard many a
poor man tell. Then he grew inquisitive. For what had this stranger run
away? And at last, eager to know this mysterious thing, he crept over to
him, and tried to waken him; but the man only opened his eyes and smiled
sleepily and shut them again. He then looked at the slant of the
sunbeams through the trees, and knew he must go, if he would reach the
Castle in time to put on his livery and wait at supper; for waiting at
meals was one of his duties as a page, and supper in those days was at
five o'clock.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a landscape with a arched
bridge, buildings on the left, a castle on a hill, and a person
walking.]

So, making up his mind to come back at daybreak and question the
stranger then, he laid what little food he had left by his hand, and
stole away back across the stream, and thence through a mile of dense
trees, and came at length to open spaces on the brow of a hill, and from
there across the plain he could see, far down to the left, the three
towers of the Abbey of Dore, and three miles away to the right the grey
Castle keep of Sayes, guarding the long bridge that crossed the river
Ley.

Down the hill he began to run, skipping over fallen trunks and through
the high, golden bracken, until the woods ended, and he came on the
king's highroad, and along its wide green border he took his way to the
Castle.

He crossed the drawbridge over the moat. All was quiet within the great
courtyard, for messengers had come with the news that the Baron would
not arrive before sundown. Robin had even hoped that his master might be
kept at the King's Council for yet another day or more, for when he
returned the Castle would be filled again with guests, and then there
was little time for running away to the forest. But guest or no guest,
as he strode into the hall, where the servants were already making the
first preparations for a feast that evening, he made an oath with
himself that nothing would keep him at daybreak from the stranger he had
left in the forest.

Then he went up the hall, past the dais, and through a door leading to
the Baron's retiring room. There he found the chamberlain. The
chamberlain, who was old and fussy, and almost overcome with the thought
of his master's return, bade him straight begone to the kitchens and
learn his duties from the steward. So he went back the way he had come.
Serving in the kitchens was not one of his duties; he served only at the
high table, where he attended on his master or his guests, and carved
the trenchers and even now was allowed to carve the simpler meats, each
according to rule; and at times he played the harp also, but this not
often, for not even the threat of flogging would ever make him practise
enough in his leisure time. Still, he was ready enough now to go to the
kitchens for many reasons. He was hungry; he loved its bustle, as much
as he disliked the tedious ceremonies of the hall; and, more than all,
he wanted to gather a store of food for the morrow.

As he came singing to the passage that led from the hall to the
kitchens, he met coming from them the Baron's young niece, a girl of
twelve, named Marian. Gaily dressed she was, with scarlet gown and white
silk sleeves; and Robin, thinking that her nose was tipped too high with
pride, tweaked one of her long black plaits in tune to his singing; and
before she could scold him, had darted along the passage to the
kitchens. Here, under the high roof all the noise in the Castle was
gathered. There was the roar of the big fires, the clatter of pots and
pans, the hissing of the spits, as they turned roasting their many
meats, partridge, goose, and mallard, mutton, chickens, and venison.
Everywhere was heat and hurry and the pungent fumes of cookery. At the
thick wooden benches the cooks were preparing dish upon dish, and all
about them lay a plenty of materials: wines and spices, flour and
butter, almonds and nuts, saffron and ginger, eggs and onions, dates and
raisins, milk and vinegar, and countless other things.

[Illustration: _Had darted along the passage to the kitchens._]

As he roved about among all this plenty, keeping out of the way of the
steward, Robin gathered a store of food, and finding an empty crock, he
filled it, and setting it in a cupboard asked his favourite cook to take
great care that it was not touched that night.

At sundown the Baron and his company returned, and after he had washed
off the dust of the journey and arrayed himself in soft raiment, the
minstrels welcomed him to the hall, and the feast began. But it lasted
no longer than two hours, for the long journey had wearied the
new-comers, and so the Baron retired early to his chamber. Robin crept
up to the gallery over the hall, where in a little recess by the door of
the Baron's sleeping room he had his own couch, and there putting off
his gold and silver livery, and praying that the Saints would waken him
before the sun, he stretched himself on his mattress and fell asleep.

It was a sharp, frosty night, and the cold air, pouring through the
draughty gallery, made him restless long before the dawn; and while it
was yet a grey twilight, he woke, and taking a bag and a length of rope
from the chest in which he kept his clothes, he felt his way along the
gallery, and down the stairs into the hall. Here upon the rush-strewn
floor lay groups of sleeping servants, and such a snoring they set up,
that he was glad when he had threaded his way over their sprawling
bodies and out into the kitchens. There he filled his bag with the food
he had gathered the evening before, and then climbed through a window
out into the courtyard, and stole like a shadow along the dark
buttresses of the wall to the gatehouse. There, opening a door in one of
its thick towers he listened. The gatekeeper was breathing in a deep
sleep, and his dog tapped its tail once or twice gently, as a lazy sign
of friendship. So Robin tiptoed past them to a staircase leading up the
tower itself. Ten steps up he went and there came to a window, narrowing
to a slit, through which he could just squeeze his body. From this
window ran a ledge round the outside of the tower to the outside of the
gateway itself, and many a time had he used this ledge to pass in and
out, when the great gates were locked, and the bridge was not drawn up.
If the bridge was drawn up, it meant swimming the moat also, and for
that he had no mind in the bitter cold of the early morning. To his joy,
when he had come through the window, he saw the outline of the bridge
suspended across the water, and hanging the bag over his shoulder, he
quickly worked his way along the ledge, and came to the side of the
bridge, and then sprang down, and a minute after he was lost in the
white mist of the meadows by the river.

[Illustration: _He quickly worked his way along the ledge._]

The bag of food he knew would be too heavy to carry as fast as he wanted
to go to the forest, and so he had brought the length of rope with him;
for somewhere in the meadows grazed a horse called Saracen, that he
often rode and for whom he had made with his own hands a leathern
headstall. He would have whistled, but already he heard sounds of voices
through the mists. It was the swineherds driving the long-legged village
pigs to feed in the woods. So, keeping silence, he wandered unseen over
the drenching grass until he came upon his horse. He fastened the rope
to its headstall, leapt on its back, and after picking up the bag he had
left by the moat, he was away to the forest, as the first beams of the
sun broke crimson through the silver clouds.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a herd of pigs moving
across a landscape.]

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of an owl perched inside a
window frame with textured patterns above and around it.]

But for all his care he had not gone unseen. High in the keep slept
Marian with the two daughters of her uncle, the Baron of Sayes; and at
the time when Robin was down in the meadows, an owl had perched on the
sill of their window and hooted and startled them from their beds. And
after it was driven away, and the other two had fallen asleep again,
Marian still lingered by the window, wondering and downcast about many
things, which she did not understand. She wondered, why for many months
no news had come from her mother in the North; why her aunt never spoke
of her mother now; why her uncle no longer spoke of her father, who had
gone to the wars in France; and she was troubled too by the stern look
he had fixed on her when he was talking to his wife in their private
chamber that very night. She knew by his look that it must be herself
they were speaking of, but she had heard nothing, except once or twice
the words “the Lord Abbot of Dore.” It was all a mystery to her; and
only one thing she knew for certain, and that was, that she wished she
were riding fast to her home in the North.

And, as the wish was in her heart, and her eyes looked down on the bank
of mist that lay below round the river sides, she saw a tiny horse move
through it like a ghost, and then suddenly spring out into the dim
golden sunlight. That horse and rider, her eyes told her, could be no
other than Saracen and Robin. And with an angry sigh she left the
window. Why couldn't she too go free like a boy? Why should Robin
torment her thus again? She had not forgiven him for catching hold of
her plait that evening. He deserved to have all his tricks and escapades
told to her uncle. But she could not punish him even in that way. For
then she would be telling tales herself. Truly, she sighed, it was all
very tiresome.

Meanwhile, suspecting nothing, Robin had tethered his horse far within
the forest, and was tramping with his bag, as hot now as he had been
cold, down the deeply-wooded side of the hill, beyond which lay his
secret oak tree; and at last he came down to the stream near by it, his
heart beating with excitement. His quick eye glanced to the second
trunk, and saw something move in the branches above it. The stranger was
still there.

In less time than it would take to tell, Robin was in the tree, and the
stranger was making a meal; and as he ate, he glanced with an admiring
eye at the boy, who had thus helped him; and ever and anon he questioned
him. Robin, on his side, met his eye with a steady gaze. He liked the
man's rugged, bearded face, and answered him frankly. At last the man
seemed to be satisfied with his questioning, and putting his hand into
his tunic, he drew out the end of a chain, on which hung a little silver
ornament shaped like a ship, and he said simply:

“I am called Brian of the Bow.”—Robin started with surprise, for he had
heard foresters speak of that name with awe.—“You have once given me my
life to keep, if I can, and right glad I am, for I hope to teach my son
to loose a shaft better than his father before I die.”

“How old may he be?” said Robin.

“Sixteen years, and six feet high already, and he can shoot as straight
as I can,” said Brian of the Bow, with a proud nod of his head. Robin's
countenance fell; and he set his chin. He had himself shot with a man's
long bow, but the Baron's archers had laughed, as his arrow flew short
and wide of the mark. Even a stag would sit on his haunches and laugh
aloud, they had told him, if it saw the flight of such an arrow; and
here was news of one, but two years older, already a man in skill. He
was downcast.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a stag looking at an
arrow.]

“I shall teach him, too, one other thing,” said Brian, seeing the shadow
on Robin's face, “to remember that he would never have been John of the
Bow if his father had perished yesterday,” and then, knitting his brows,
he went on: “You have saved my life once. I shall put it once again in
your hands, and, it may be, the life of another also. You see this
silver ship. That was entrusted to me by the lady I serve and her name
you will have heard many a time, if you have been long at yonder Castle.
She is Catharine of Arlington, and her husband is the brother of your
master.”

“Marian——” said Robin.

“Her mother,” said Brian. “It is now many months since she had news of
her daughter, and all her messengers have been driven back and cruelly
used, and she is sick almost to death with fear that some evil may be
hanging over this same Marian. You saw the men who followed me
yesterday, and whose men they were. My mistress fears that their master,
the Lord Abbot, intends to trap her daughter into his power. Many are
his plottings, and time is too short to tell you them now. You will know
enough if I tell you but this: I was sent to carry this silver ship to
my lady's daughter. This ship says 'return'!” and he took it off the
chain, and held it up for Robin to see closely. “If I could give it my
lady's daughter this forenoon, she would be far into the forest by
nightfall, and six leagues to the north I know a woodman who would give
us rest and shelter, and again six leagues and we should be in a
friendlier country, by the forest of Barnwood.”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a person standing beside
a dark horse in an outdoor landscape with trees.]

“Give it to me,” said Robin impetuously, holding out his hand, “and by
noon, if it be as you say, she will be here by this tree, and perchance
two horses with her.”

“Never make too high a promise,” said Brian, and Robin's cheeks flamed
at the rebuke; “but this I know,” he went on, “you may succeed now where
I am like to fail,” and with these words he gave Robin the silver ship,
and commended him to God. Robin had already swung out on a branch, and
dropped to the ground, when Brian called after him in a low voice, “Do
this for me, and in days to come I will teach you to loose a shaft as
well as any man.” Cheered by these words, Robin set off hot-foot for his
horse Saracen, and when he came to him, where he was still peaceably
grazing, he stood leaning by his shoulder and thought awhile, until
Saracen in his turn stopped grazing, and stood and seemed to think also.
Thus they stood and stared together into space, while Robin tried to
imagine what was likely to happen at the Castle of Sayes that day. At
length he seemed to make a decision, and jumping on Saracen, rode back
towards the Castle; but when he came to the wide common land that lay by
the bridge, he dismounted, and turning Saracen loose, made the rest of
the way on foot.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a person walking away
with arms crossed near a door.]

As he came to the gatehouse, a rider in the Abbot's livery thundered out
over the wooden planks of the drawbridge.

“Good day,” said Robin, with a twinkling eye, and wanting very much to
flourish the silver ship in front of his face. The man passed him
without answering.

Then through the gateway Robin saw the Baron striding up and down the
narrow lawn that lay under the shadow of the hall, and he saw, too, that
there was deep anger on his brow, and he trembled, as he drew near,
trying to swagger along with a bold step. Still the Baron strode to and
fro, and all the time watched Robin with a stern eye. Once that morning
already he had called for him to prepare his bath, and there had been no
answer. But still, as he strode to and fro, he did not speak. Dark and
gloomy secret thoughts were vexing his heart and making his tongue
silent. For he had just heard that the sister of the Abbot of Dore was
coming that day to go a-hawking, and he knew that she would return that
night with his niece Marian a captive in her train. He knew that it had
been planned that while Marian was out with the hawking party, she
should be drawn into the woods, in order that the Abbot's serving-men
might seem to rescue her, and carry her off for safety to the Abbey. All
this he knew, and yet he could do nothing; for he had consented to the
plot, unhappy man, afraid that the Abbot would ruin him in the King's
Councils. So without speaking, and with the least twist of a bitter
smile on his lips, he watched his truant page swagger past him to the
great hall door.

A deep sigh of relief came from Robin's heart as he swung the heavy door
of the hall back to its socket. But he was happy too soon. For, as he
shut the door, a small, commanding voice bade him open it again, and he
turned to meet Marian and one of the Baron's daughters, dressed for
riding. And worse still, they began to laugh at him, as though they had
a secret about him that amused them. Two girls foolishly laughing, and
Brian of the Bow waiting in desperate suspense!

For a moment he lost temper, and was tempted to swing the door open
again, and let them go. Instead, he stood fidgeting against it, and at
last he stammered with downcast eye:

“I have seen a silver ship this morning.”

Marian's lip fell, and the dimples fled from her cheek. Then the latch
of the door clattered and the Baron entered brushing Robin aside.

“Father,” cried his daughter, stepping forward to him with a mocking
laugh, “have you seen the silver ship this morning?” The Baron stopped
suddenly in his stride.

“Who has?” he growled.

“Robin,” faltered his daughter, frightened by the harsh change in his
voice.

The Baron for a moment stared at Robin, his face set as hard as stone,
and then he abruptly walked away. “Get them their ponies,” he called
over his shoulder, and again, in a still sterner tone, “Let me never
hear those words again.”

Scared by her mistake, his daughter hastened out into the courtyard,
calling Marian after her; but Marian did not move, for Robin had caught
her by the sleeve. He watched the Baron disappear through the further
end of the hall, and then whispering, “Brian of the Bow gave me that,”
he pushed the silver ship into her hand. For a moment she stood
speechless and amazed, as though she saw her mother actually standing
before her. Then her eyes turned from the ship to Robin's face, and from
his face to the ship. “Where is he? Tell me,” she begged.

“I'll tell you, as we saddle the ponies. Can you get some food, for it
will be a long journey?”

“A long journey! He is waiting!” she cried joyously, so joyously that
Robin's heart grew light too, as though all this serious adventure was
turning into a mischievous game of hide-and-seek.

It was then close upon nine, and at ten dinner would be served, and in
less than an hour the Abbot's sister would be coming into the hall with
her retainers. So the two rushed away to the pantry, and while for a few
breathless minutes they ate, Robin stuffed a pie into the fold of his
tunic and another into his hood, and then they fled after the Baron's
daughter, and a fine excuse it made for hurrying past the surly and
suspicious gatekeeper. As they crossed the moat, the Baron's daughter,
sauntering ahead, was already close to the long, level strip of turf
where the girls daily exercised their ponies under the eye of the
watchman on the walls. But Robin did not mean that Marian should follow
her there. Instead, he beckoned her after him into the main stables,
which stood across the highroad opposite the gatehouse. There the ponies
were quickly saddled, and Marian mounting hers, Robin took its bridle,
and bidding a groom lead the other before them, he and Marian followed
so slowly that when they came out on the highway, they were already some
way behind. There he stopped, and calling to the others that he wanted
to fetch his own horse Saracen from the common land over the bridge, he
turned that way.

“I can come with you,” called Marian, in a voice that all who wished to
hear might hear, and when she saw the groom bow his head in consent to
her call, she turned her pony slowly after Robin; and so the two passed
to the bridge, and still slowly moved out of sight.

As soon, as he saw their heads appear above the parapet, Saracen had
pricked up his ears, and with two or three flourishes with his heels,
and two or three boisterous circlings, he pranced up to them.
Straightway Robin unknotted the rope from his headstall, and the next
moment Marian and he were racing to the nearest point in the forest.

“How lovely!” she cried breathless, as they plunged under the
sun-dappled trees. “Robin, I'm free!”

[Illustration: _Next moment Marian and he were racing to the nearest
point in the forest._]

“Not yet,” he said soberly, his eyes looking eagerly for familiar marks
by which to guide their path. Steadily they moved up towards the crest
of the hills, and then threaded their way along just beneath it,
catching glimpses of the Castle, as it grew smaller and smaller in the
distance. And still they saw nobody setting out in pursuit. And thus at
length they came near the secret oak, and the only trouble Robin had was
to stop Marian startling everything around with her laughing voice. The
serious-eyed quiet Marian had gone, and a new, merry, jolly,
light-hearted Marian had appeared in her place. He was filled with
wonder. Her laughter was so gay that he felt that even the rabbits would
forget their cares, and all their holes would shake with silent jokes.

As they crossed the stream, Brian of the Bow rushed from his
hiding-place, and to Saracen's surprise threw himself on his knees
before them, tears of joy coursing down his brown cheeks, now that the
long suspense was over.

“The saints be praised, my little mistress!” he cried, and Marian jumped
from her pony and flung herself into his great arms, while Robin rescued
the pies, that had danced to many pieces in the folds of his tunic.

There was little time for all that they wished to say then. Every minute
they waited made their course more dangerous, and so all was soon
hurriedly prepared for setting out. All the stores that were of use from
the oak tree were put into two bundles and slung on a rope over
Saracen's back; for Robin, though it cost him a pang, bade Brian ride
him as far as the woodman's hut six leagues through the forest, and then
set him loose to find his own way back.

“God thank you always,” said Brian solemnly, before he mounted; “and
when we meet again I will string a bow for you.”

“Robin,” said Marian shyly in her turn, “I like you very much to-day,”
and he beamed at her with pleasure as she turned to ride away.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a person hiding behind a
large tree in a dense forest, with figures visible in the distance.]

So they started, and as he watched them disappear Robin wished that he
also had been starting with them on this new adventure, over the hills
and far away. But he had to bide his time; and all the way, as he
returned through the woods back towards the highway and kept watch
against the coming of the hawking party, lest he should be seen, he was
trying to plan how that good time might come to him.

There was hue and cry both in the Castle and in the Abbey that night.
Riders galloped this way and that. Rumours flew. But when midnight came,
only Saracen was on his way back through the dark trees; and Marian and
Brian, after a few hour's sleep in the woodman's hut were already
starting on the second stage of their journey, and no servant of the
Abbot had either seen or heard of them. And in the Castle at the same
time Robin was preparing his master's bed, so drowsy that sometimes for
a moment he dreamed that he was tossing in a silver ship, and sometimes
that he was riding in the forest under the stars. Suddenly a voice smote
his ears.

“I will do that for myself,” it said, and Robin, blinking in the
candlelight, saw the Baron standing in front of him.

“Go and rest, go and rest,” the Baron said, laying his hand in a kindly
way on his shoulder. “You have served me well enough already for one
day. Go and rest.”

Years after Robin came to understand how he had served the Baron well
that day; how he had done for the Baron what he could not do for
himself, and saved him from a mean and treacherous act; and years after
he used the bow as skilfully as Brian's great son, Little John. But that
was when men called him Robin Hood, the outlaw of Sherwood, and Marian
had become Maid Marian.

 “THE ORDER OF THE BATH” _By MABEL MARLOWE_


Every Saturday night the gnomes all went down to the river to bathe.
They used the archway under the wooden bridge for a dressing-room,
putting their clothes in little heaps on the stones.

The gnomes that could swim wore red bathing drawers, and were very proud
of them, too. Those who could take one foot off the bottom wore blue
bathing drawers; and those who could only paddle and splash, wore white
ones.

At seven o'clock they all went into the water, where it was shallow at
the edge, and grew deep (but not too deep). For half an hour they
splashed and swam, and dived, and kicked. Then Humpy the Weather-gnome,
who was the Master-of-the-Bath, stood up, and clapped his hands.

“Ready,” he called, and he ran under the bridge and fetched a piece of
soap from his pocket. For “batheing” night was also bath night.

Then the gnomes all stood in a row in the water, where it was deep (but
not too deep). They stood in a row, with the tallest at the back, and
the shortest at the front, and all the others in their right places
according to size. Then Humpy gave the piece of soap to Lanky Tom, who
was always at the back, and scuttled to his place in front of Toby.

“All ready?” he said.

“Yes. All ready.”

“Then—Soap!”

Then Lanky Tom dipped the soap in the water to make it froth. He soaped
the back of Toby. Then Toby took the soap and soaped the back of Humpy.
Humpy soaped Sut. Sut soaped Barney. Barney soaped Lob. And Lob soaped
Dump the dwarf, who was always in the front. Then all the gnomes dipped.

Fizz! went the froth. The river sounded like sherbet, and grew the
colour of milk. The little wafer of soap that was left went floating
away down stream. After that, the gnomes all ran under the bridge, and
dried and dressed, chatting all the time.

But one Saturday night a fuss began. Lanky Tom, the ladder-gnome,
commenced to grumble.

“It isn't fair,” he said.

“What isn't fair?” asked Humpy.

“It isn't fair that I am always at the back. I never get any soap.”

“Why, you get the soap first of all.”

“Yes, I know. But I soap Toby, and Toby soaps Humpy—but nobody ever
soaps me. For twelve years I have had no soap. It isn't fair.”

“H'm! No, it doesn't seem fair,” mumbled Humpy. “It certainly is not
fair. But I do not see how we can help it. It isn't our fault that you
are the tallest one.”

“No, you can't blame us,” said the other gnomes, all talking at once.
Then they began to argue—and they grew so hot about it that they all
steamed dry.

At last Dump the dwarf (who was always in the front) raised his voice.

[Illustration: 'Black]

“Listen to me,” he cried. “I may be the littlest one here, but I have
got the most sense. Let's all go into the water again—but let's go the
other way round, with the tallest in the front.”

“OF COURSE!” said Humpy. “That is a very bright idea. Line up! Quick
March! Left, Right! Now, RIGHT-ABOUT-FACE!”

Solemnly the gnomes marched into the stream in their usual order, and
then faced about, so that Lanky Tom was in the front, and Dump the dwarf
was at the back. Then Humpy took another tablet of soap and gave it to
Dump.

Dump soaped Lob. Lob soaped Barney. Barney soaped Sut. Sut soaped Humpy.
Humpy soaped Toby. And Toby soaped Lanky Tom. He lathered his shoulders,
and rubbed his back, and gave him such a frothing that when he dipped
the whole river boiled and sizzled like a steam engine.

All the willows shook in the breeze, and the fishes gave their tails a
mighty flick, and were off.

Back scuttled the gnomes to the bridge, and began to dress.

“Well, are you satisfied now?” asked Humpy, towelling his head.

“Yes,” replied Lanky Tom. “Yes, I am satisfied now. I have had a good
wash at last.”

“So that's settled,” said Humpy, and he began to pull his socks on.

“No, indeed! It isn't settled! It isn't fair!” cried Dump the dwarf,
getting red in the face.

“What isn't fair?” asked all the others in chorus.

“I have only had one soaping, and the rest of you have had two.”

“I have only had one, and I am quite satisfied,” said Lanky Tom.

“One is quite enough,” said Barney.

“THAT'S NOT THE POINT,” cried Dump, growing excited. “The point is this.
ONE gnome was being treated unfairly before. We all admit that. But now
TWO gnomes have been treated unfairly. Matters are worse than they were
before.”

“It's your own fault. You suggested it. You have got us into a fine
muddle with your interference,” said Humpy.

“Yes, indeed!” said Lob. “I've got a complaint to make, too. I've been
washed twice.”

“So have all the rest of us,” cried the others, all speaking at once.

“What a waste of soap. Two pieces used instead of one!”

“H'm! That's a serious thing about the soap,” said Humpy. “If we use two
tablets each time the soap will only last for half a year, and we shall
have to stop grimy for six months at a time.”

“Let's bath once a fortnight,” said Sut.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a tall, thin man walking
in profile.]

“No! I don't like that idea at all,” said Barney. “There is no sense in
that—to have two washes in one day, and then none for two weeks.”

“We've done it to-day,” said Sut. “I've been washed when I was perfectly
clean.”

“I MAY BE THE LITTLEST ONE HERE,” shouted Dump, “BUT THAT IS NO REASON
WHY I SHOULD BE TREATED WORSE THAN ANYONE ELSE.”

“Tut-ut-tut!” said Toby. “What a fuss! What a silly commotion! And all
about nothing! I suggest that we go on the same way as we used to do. We
have done it for twelve years. We can do it for twelve years more.”

“OH! CAN WE?” said Lanky Tom, in an awful voice. “Can we INDEED!” And he
stumped up the bank and went off in a huff.

Oh, the consternation under that wooden bridge! The water chattered over
the stones; the rushes whispered together, and all the echoes of that
dim archway sounded like the moans of a gathering storm.

“Dear, dear! What shall we do?”

“Let's bath by ourselves.”

“No. We can't reach our own backs. Our arms are too short.”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a small figure peering
from behind a wall while another bearded figure crouches on rocks
below.]

“I shall resign,” said Humpy, shaking his beard. “For twelve years I
have been Master-of-the-Bath, but now I shall resign.”

Gloomily, with many sighs, the gnomes dressed and went away. One by one
they disappeared along the lane, and at last Humpy was left alone.

He sat on a stone in the shadows, watching the water as it lapped at the
pebbly banks. Twilight settled over the land. Stars began to glimmer in
the sky. But Humpy sat under the bridge, and did not move.

He felt very sad, and very old.

Suddenly he heard a voice calling his name.

“Humpy! Humpy! Where are you?”

Mrs. Humpy came running down the bank, peering anxiously under the arch
of the bridge.

“I'm here.”

“Oh, I thought you must be drowned. I watched the other gnomes coming
home—and they looked very grave—no smiles or anything—and it made me
feel afraid that something had happened.”

[Illustration]

“Something has happened,” said Humpy, and he drew her down beside him,
and told her all about it.

She listened to the end without a sound. Then—she laughed aloud.

“Oh, you silly, foolish Humpy,” she cried. “You need not resign at all.
There is a much simpler way than that. Next week, when the gnomes come
down to bath—make them stand in a _ring_.”

“OF COURSE!” said Humpy slowly.


If you should pass by the stream on a Saturday evening, you will see a
happy sight. A ring of gnomes will be standing in the water, like a
clump of rushes, where it is deep, but not too deep.

Listen, and you will hear Humpy, the Master-of-the-Bath, give a loud,
clear order.

“SOAP!”

Then Lanky will soap Toby, and Toby will soap Humpy, and Humpy will soap
Sut, and all round the ring the soap will go. Each gnome will be soaped
fairly and well, and when they dip you will hear a fizzling, and see a
frothing. The stream will sputter, and the edges of the water will cream
and hump as if with snow. Rainbow bubbles will go sailing along under
the willows, like a fairy fleet, and the silver fish will hide in the
pools, keeping very still.

Not a word will there be of unfairness. Not a grumble. Not a single
complaint.

And Humpy the Weather-gnome is still the Master-of-the-Bath.

 A-swing to scan the sea and sky,
 While far below the Ship-man stands
 And shouts to me between his hands.

 I make a sign to him
 That now the coast grows dim
 And sinks from sight;
 Now from our dipping bows
 The dark-blue water flows
 All silver-bright.

 Whither we steer I do not care,
 Crete, Alicant or Finisterre,
 For casks of wine or russet bales,
 So we have fair winds for our sails.

 The wind that I love best,
 Blow it from east or west
 Across the foam,
 Is that which bears us toward
 Grey tower and daisied sward,
 Harbour and home.

 Faces of bronze and domes of gold,
 Green spires that clang with brazen sound
 Above the temples of Mahound.[1]

 But Dick my brother now
 Jogs whistling by the plough
 And marvelleth sore
 That I should choose again
 To brave the wind and rain
 So far from shore.

 I am no 'prentice, for I've made
 Three voyages, thrice seen England fade
 Astern, and thrice beheld it shine
 Ahead on the horizon-line.

 But Wat my brother sits
 In school, and cracks his wits
 O'er A and Z,
 And envieth me much,
 Me who have stuffed none such
 Into _my_ head.

 Salt beef and stiff, dry bread and brown,
 Will be my meed when I climb down;
 I dare not doze upon my perch—
 A rope's no softer than a birch!

 Yet not with Dick or Wat
 I'd choose to change my lot,
 Nor any boy;
 Though I be fain, no doubt,
 When homeward bound I shout—
 Ho, Land Ahoy!

 The boys ashore have some delights;
 They need not wake and watch o' nights,
 Nor clamber where the sharp winds blow
 To hang the lanthorn on the bow;

 No decks have they to scour
 White as a lily-flower,
 No chains to clean;
 And orchard raids are theirs
 For plums and sweet brown pears
 And pippins green.

 Yet rather than on shore to stay
 And trudge a furrow half the day,
 Or plod in school at clerkish craft,
 I'd sail alone upon a raft!

 Oh, it is brave to watch
 Our great square mainsail catch
 A humming breeze,
 And golden amidst blue
 To see our prow cleave through
 The crested seas!

 Five candles oft our Master vows
 To St. Bartholomew his house;[2]
 But to St. Nicholas[3] one will burn
 This night, I know, for my return;

 My Mother never yet
 That candle did forget,
 And never will;
 Nor to that Saint to call
 To guard me and us all
 From every ill.

 When waves are gentle and winds soft

 The solemn kindling of the stars.

 Holden in lanthorns quaint
 Of silver glass;
 Mayhap they are lit by thee,
 Thou friend of boys at sea,
 St. Nicholas!

 OLD JOE _By WALTER DE LA MARE_


The house in which old Mr. Bolsover lived was of a faded primrose
colour, and, apart from a high gable in which showed the windows of the
guest-room and an attic, it was long and low. Its other back windows
looked out across meadows which now lay spreading out before them into
the distance, bright green beneath a merry morning sun. A narrow terrace
skirted the house. At either end of this was a worn, grey stone pedestal
on which stood two dancing fauns, for ever piping one to another above
the wallflowers and the pinks. And it was the pinks that were now in
full bloom, filling the air with their fragrance.

Along the terrace leaning down from the house wall was a canopy made of
copper, and painted green; and this was supported by slim pillars,
around which clematis and jessamine clambered. It was just after
breakfast-time, and old Mr. Bolsover at this very moment was coming out
at the French windows of the breakfast-room with his small niece,
Letitia. She had a nimble way of walking and a quick way of turning her
head that was like a bird, and she was chattering away to her old uncle
as she stepped along beside him. They came to a standstill together,
looking out across the meadows.

“Oh, Uncle Tim, what a _perfectly_ lovely morning!” said she.

“A lovely morning,” repeated Uncle Tim. “One of the most beautiful I can
remember, simply because a small person of the name of Letitia is here
to share it with me.”

“Oh, Uncle Tim,” said Letitia; “that's flattery.”

“No, my dear, not exactly flattery,” replied Uncle Tim, squinnying at
her from under the glasses of his spectacles; “but just the plain,
beautiful, solemn truth.”

“Ah, I know all about that!” said she.... “And to think, Uncle Tim, it's
exactly a year since I was here before. Why, you wouldn't believe a
single flower was different. And there,” she went on hastily, twisting
her head on her slender neck—“why there's that curious old Guy Fawkes
thing still, over there by the willows.”

“Why, yes, so it is,” said Uncle Tim.

Letitia continued to gaze at it. “You know, he _is_ a little alarming,
Uncle Tim, if you look at him long enough. And you don't seem to
remember,” she went on solemnly, “that the very last morning I was here
you promised faithfully to tell me what it's there _for_. And then you
didn't, because Mother came in just when I was asking you, and you
forgot all about it.”

“Well, so I did,” said Uncle Tim. “That's just what comes of the
piecrust which promises are made of. It melts in your mouth.... That's
Old Joe right enough. _Old_ Joe indeed, poor soul, for he must be at
least as old as I am. Ninety-nine, Letitia, if he's a day!”

“You're please not to say that, Uncle Tim. You are the youngest, kindest
Uncle Tim there ever was. So there! But what _is_ Old Joe? Where did he
come from? What is he for? Isn't there a tune about an Old Joe, Uncle
Tim? Is that him? Tell me _now_,” cried Letitia. “Let's sit down here
comfily on the stones—they're as warm as toast in the sun. And now go
straight on. _Please._”

Down sat old Mr. Bolsover; down sat Letitia—side by side like an ancient
Pharaoh and his bride. And this is his story of Old Joe:

“I must tell you first, Letitia,” he began, “that when I was about your
age I used frequently to stay with an old friend of my mother's—your
grandmother's, that is—whose name was Mrs. Lumb. She was a stout woman,
and used, I remember, to wear an amethyst-coloured velvet cap flat over
her ears, and a lace thingummy over her shoulders. I can see her now:
her wide face all creased up in smiles, and her fat fingers with the
emeralds and the sapphires; and even the cameo brooch at her neck. She
wasn't even so much as my godmother, but she was immensely kind to me.
She had a cook, too, that could make every sort of prime rich cake there
is. Jams, jellies, raspberry-fool, apple charlotte, tipsy-cake—they were
the best I've ever tasted. So were her stuffed eggs.”

“Oh, Uncle Tim,” said Letitia, “you greedy thing!”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of an elegantly dressed
woman carrying a small tray of food toward a young boy standing on
grass.]

“You shall see for yourself at lunch,” said her uncle; “for if I'm not
an unprophetic Dutchman, I can smell apple charlotte. But never mind
about that. You'll agree, at any rate, that my friend Mrs. Lumb was the
right kind of person to stay _with_. It was always during the holidays,
and in those days, while there was plenty of honest hard tack at
school—impots, ferrules, bread-and-scrape and what-not—there was no such
thing as a holiday task. You hadn't, I mean, to sit and hate reading a
book you'd have loved to read for its own sake if you had never been
told to. But never mind about that either. At Mrs. Lumb's I did just
what I liked. Hers was an old, roaming house; a good deal older than
this, and twice the size; and there was beautiful country all around it.
Wide sweeping hills with ploughed fields stretching down their sunny
slopes; little woods and copses, too, on the crests and in the folds and
valleys; and a stream meandering its way along and actually brawling
over stones at the foot of our long, sloping garden. If ever I go to
heaven, my dear, I hope to see that house and garden again.”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a boy holding out a
string or worm toward a bird in a hanging cage.]

“But isn't it here _still_?” said Letitia. “I mean wherever in the world
it used to be?”

“Alas, my dear, no,” said old Mr. Bolsover. “The house was destroyed by
fire—burned clean to the ground—forty years ago. But not, I am thankful
to say, before Mrs. Lumb had left it and had gone out to live with her
younger brother in Ceylon—where the strong tea comes from.

“In those days I was uncommonly fond of birds; too fond of them to carry
a catapult, but not too fond of them to set traps and try to catch and
keep them, poor creatures, for pets. How would you like to be a linnet
or a starling or a blackbird barred up inside a cage by an oaf of a boy
like me when I was nine or thereabouts?”

“Not at all,” said Letitia; “though I would sooner be kept by you than
by any other boy.”

“Thank you, my dear,” said Mr. Bolsover; “that's a bargain. Still,
caging wild birds is usually very poor fun for the wild birds. But I was
a boy and I did as boys do, bless their hearts. My traps were scattered
everywhere, sometimes in places where they had no business to be. What I
was after was _rare_ birds, and deep down my one desire was for a bird
unspeakably marvellous in colour and appearance and song, and one that
nobody had ever seen. Which means I was a little cracked on the subject.
There was a particular place that I had in mind for a trap for a long
time before I ventured to make the attempt. It was at the edge of a
field where I used to notice a great many birds were accustomed to
visit, and that in spite of Old Joe there.”

“_That_ Old Joe _there_?” enquired Letitia, pointing with her finger at
the queer, stooping figure with its head and old hat on one side which
stood blankly gazing at them from under the willow where she had noticed
him before.

“Yes, that same Old Joe _there_,” said Uncle Tim. “Between you and me,
my dear—though please not to look his way for a minute, as I shouldn't
like to hurt his feelings—Old Joe is a Scarecrow, an antiquated
hodmadod. And he has never been anything else; though, after all these
years he and I have been together, of course he's now a kind of twin
brother. He's Joseph, you see (in the coat), and I'm Benjamin; and as
you see, my dear, we are much alike.”

“How _can_ you say such things, Uncle Tim,” cried Letitia, pushing her
hand in under his arm. “You know quite well that's a kind of flattery,
too—of yourself, you bad thing!”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a man tipping his top hat
to a scarecrow in a grassy field.]

“No,” said Uncle Tim, “but of Old Joe. But never mind about that. The
first time I saw him, he gave me a real fright. I had come creeping
along on the other side of the hedge, keeping a wary eye open for
anybody that might be in the field; even though there was very little
likelihood of this, for, unless the field was being ploughed or harrowed
or sown or hoed, there never _was_ anybody. The field belonged to a
farmer called Jones. It was a good-sized, sloping, forty-acre field,
with a plantation of spruce down along all one side of it.

“That particular morning was in March; and the field was just
waiting—and on a morning rather like this, but much colder—for its crops
to come up. I was skirting along the hedge, as I say, carrying the snare
I had with me under my jacket, and hardly able to breathe for
excitement. For, thought I to myself as I peered through the hedge in
which the thornbuds were just breaking, that's the place for me. I could
just see across to the lower edge of it, where lay a ditch and another
hedge that had been allowed to sprout. It was truly a little paradise of
birds, my dear. There seemed to be hundreds busying and disporting and
delighting themselves there in the early sunshine.

“As bold as paint I turned the corner by the old gate, and at that
precise moment caught sight of what I supposed was either Farmer Jones
himself or one of his men staring at me across the field not thirty
yards away. I fairly jumped in my clodhoppers at sight of him, turned
hot, then cold, and waited, staring back. It seemed almost as if I could
see his very eyeballs moving. But not he! No common human he! It was Old
Joe. My Old Joe—_that_ Old Joe _there_!

“I have seen a good many scarecrows in my time, but never one at which
you must look longer before you can be perfectly sure he is not
real—alive. After all, what _is_ life, Letitia?”

“That's perfectly true, Uncle Tim,” whispered Letitia, edging a little
closer. “He _might_ be alive at this very moment.”

“You must remember, too,” went on Mr. Bolsover, “all this was in Old
Joe's better days. He was young then. He has been peacocked up in many a
new suit of old clothes since then, and more hats than I could count on
my fingers. But then he was in his heyday—in the very bloom of his
youth, the glass of fashion and the Knave of Trumps! But still, even as
he stands now under his old willow there, I wouldn't part with him not
for a bulging fat bag of guineas. Not for twenty bags. Chiefly for his
own sake; but in part, my dear Letitia—in part because one doesn't too
often actually see fairies in this world.”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a young person creeping
cautiously past a bush or hedge in a wooded area, looking towards a
scarecrow standing in a distant field among bare trees.]

Letitia burst out laughing. “Fairies, Uncle Tim!” she cried, dragging in
her amusement her skirts tight over her knees. “Why, you don't mean to
say, you poor thing, that Old Joe's a fairy?”

“No, I don't mean to say quite that,” said Mr. Bolsover. “Then, as now,
Old Joe was the scaringest of scaring scarecrows I have ever set eyes
on. But like the primrose in the poem he was nothing more. No, indeed,
it wasn't Old Joe was the fairy. But he was all but next best, for he
_was_ one of her rendez-vouses, as the old word goes. That morning he
was wearing immense loose great trousers and a greenish-black coat very
wide on the shoulders. Apart from the stick for his arms, another had
been pushed up one of his coat-sleeves for a cudgel. Another with a lump
at the top made his head, and on that was a high, hard, battered, square
hat like the hats old-fashioned farmers and churchwardens used to wear.
He was stooping forward, cudgel in hand. And the eyes from under the
penthouse brim of the shadowy battered hat seemed to be fixedly staring
straight towards the corner by the gate and at me.

“I hugged the wire snare closer and stared back. And whether it was due
to the hot air coming up from the flinty soil, or some cheating effect
of the light on the white field, I can't say, Letitia. But even while I
watched him, his head seemed to be ever so gently turning on his
shoulders, as if for all the world he were furtively endeavouring to get
a better view of me without my noticing it.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a boy running ahead while
an older man with a walking stick follows behind across a field.]

“I was still pretty well startled, I can tell you, my dear. For in those
days young trespassers on seedling fields might find themselves in for a
smart walloping, if not for something worse. The amusing thing is that
this Old Joe was alarming me a great deal more than he was alarming the
birds. They seemed to be perfectly used to him. A couple of tits were
positively playing hide-and-seek, with him for goal. And there were
rooks in their black coats pacing up and down and preening themselves in
the sunshine not more than a few paces away from where he stood.

“Well, at last, after confronting him like this for a minute or two
together—at last I became sure he was not an angry farmer, but merely
Old Joe. Yet, something in the look of him was different from any other
scarecrow I had seen up to that moment. Even from where I stood I could
spy a few wild flowers already blooming at his feet, and I felt, too,
that even if there were no real eyes in his head, somebody or something
_was_ actually looking at me from under that hat or from out of his
sleeve—from somewhere.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a boy sitting on a grassy
mound, looking across a landscape toward a distant scarecrow.]

“Already the birds were becoming accustomed to me being there, simply
because I stood so still. I squatted on my haunches and began to set the
trap, but the whole time I was thinking of that queer old creature
half-way up the field, and knew I was being watched. And whenever I
stooped, I took a little secret look at him either over my shoulder or
between my legs. And then I sat down on the grass of the bank under the
hedge and steadily fixed my eyes on him for I should think a full
half-hour.

“Nothing happened, however. The sun climbed a little higher into the
sky; the shadows cast by the trees shifted a little on the
wide-stretching field, and at last I turned back home again. At the
corner under an old thorn-tree I stooped down for yet another moment and
had yet another look. And at that very instant I was perfectly sure I
had caught a glimpse of something moving there. It was as if a face had
peered out and had as swiftly been withdrawn again into hiding.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a rabbit leaping across a
grassy field against the backdrop of a large round moon.]

“All the rest of that day I could think of nothing else but Old Joe,
assuring myself that my eyes had deceived me, or that perhaps a bird had
fluttered down, or that a little breeze had moved his sleeve—something
of that kind. And yet I knew inside me that this wasn't true.”

“It might,” said Letitia—“it might, you know, of course, have been not a
bird but a little animal you saw, mightn't it, Uncle Tim? I once saw a
hare skipping about in the middle of a field. Or mightn't a bird have
been building its nest in him? Robins, you know, will build their nests
even in an old boot. And look! There's a bird actually sitting on Old
Joe's shoulder now.”

“Well, you shall hear,” said Uncle Tim. “I am sure, my dear, if you had
seen Old Joe that morning you would have agreed that there was something
different about him from what he looks like now. I can't quite explain,
except that it was the difference between an empty house and one with
people living in it. The difference between a pond with fish in it and
one with none. The difference between you yourself when you are really
asleep and when you are only foxing and pretending to be. And sure
enough, I was right.

“I had my bedtime at Mrs. Lumb's, and always an apple and a glass of
milk. But she was not one of those Uncle Tims who want everything done
exactly at the right moment. She didn't wait till the clock struck eight
and them come peeping in to see if one was safe in bed.”

“Why, you know very well,” said Letitia, “you don't do that yourself.”

“Ha, ha! Never mind,” said Mr. Bolsover. “That evening, about seven
o'clock, I made my way down to the field again, just creeping from bush
to bush, tree to tree as cautiously as I could—so cautiously that I
actually nearly trod on a bunny's tail that happened to be enjoying a
dandelion on the other side of a bramble. And when I reached my
hawthorn, I sat down by its root very low to the ground, having made up
my mind I would watch until it really began to grow dark. It was towards
the end of April, you will remember, so the evenings were a good deal
shorter and duskier than they are now. And as it began to be twilight,
it seemed almost that Old Joe was steadily moving nearer. And then at
the very instant when I noticed the first star in the sky, I saw—well,
what do you think I saw?”

“Why, the fairy, of course,” said Letitia, and sighed.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a boy tumbling or falling
headfirst down a grassy hill as a rabbit runs nearby.]

“Full marks, my dear,” cried Mr. Bolsover. “The fairy. The odd thing is
that I can't really describe her. Maybe the light was not very good;
maybe my young eyes were strained with watching. But it was not only
that. For I seemed to be looking at her partly as if I was imagining
her, though I knew she was really there. She was leaning a little
forward, and reached, I should say, about up to what you might call Old
Joe's waist. Her face was rather long and narrow, and there was fair
hair hanging down from side to side, something between gold and grey,
rather like the colour you see on a phosphorescent fish in the dark,
only more golden. She stood unearthly still, looking out towards my
thorn. And the very sight of her filled me with a sensation I simply
cannot describe.

“It was as if I was in the middle of a dream in another world, and cold
prickles went down my back just as if unawares I had heard the sound of
enchanted music. There was not a breath stirring, everything about me
seemed suddenly to grow sharp and clear, even though the light was dim.
The flowers were different; the trees; the birds. Like that old story of
the Cap of Invisibility, it was as if I knew deep down what the flowers
were feeling, what it was like to be a tendril of ivy creeping up a
tree-stem, or to have feathers all over me, and to be looking with a
bright small eye at my own bird-world. I can't explain it, my dear, but
I am sure you will understand.”

Letitia gave two solemn nods. “_I think_ so, Uncle Tim—a little.”

“Now I believe,” Uncle Tim went on, “that the fairy knew I was there,
but that she could not delay what she wanted to do any longer. For
presently—after a minute or two—she drew back and began to hasten away
over the field towards the utmost corner furthest away from me, keeping
all the time so that Old Joe stood in between us and prevented me from
seeing her clearly, however much I dodged my head from side to side. Now
that, you will agree, was a difficult thing to do, and I don't know how
she did it. Only a fairy could—without once looking back.”

“And what was she like from behind?” said Letitia.

“She was like wood-smoke going up from a bonfire; she was like a puff of
wind over snow; or a silver cloud in the sky. She moved, I mean, my
dear, as if she were hovering on her way, and yet she never left the
ground. Far, far lighter than a gazelle, and so lovely to watch it
fairly took my breath away. And me only a clodhopping boy of ten.”

Mr. Bolsover took a large coloured silk handkerchief out of his pocket
and blew his nose. “I ought to say, at once,” he continued, pushing the
handkerchief back into his pocket, “that this is not really a story at
all.”

“Well, _I_ think, Uncle Tim, it's a lovely story,” said Letitia. “Please
go on.”

“Well, you see, Letitia, a story ought to be like a piece of embroidery:
it ought to have a beginning and a middle and an end. It ought to be
like a whiting with its tail in its mouth. But this one just goes off
into nothing.”

“I think I understand,” said Letitia once more. “But please go on with
the fairy, Uncle Tim.”

“Well,” continued Uncle Tim, “as soon as the fairy was out of sight my
one desire was to steal into the field and take a look at Old Joe at
close quarters. And yet I hadn't the courage. He was _her_ dwelling; her
hiding-place; her habitation—that was certain. Now that she was absent,
had hied away, the very look of him had changed. He was empty, vacant, a
husk.

“I fear, Letitia, you must be thinking I was a remarkably stupid boy.
Indeed I was. And, anyhow, I must confess that I simply could not make
up my mind to go any nearer. Old Joe was quite empty now, but after what
I had seen I felt a dreadful strangeness all about me. I felt I had been
a spy, and that every living thing around me knew it, and hated my
company. I didn't even go to my trap—and when I went again it had
disappeared.

“I talked about fairies next morning to my old friend Mrs. Lumb at
breakfast, just to see if she knew anything about them. She believed in
them right enough, but she had never seen one. I asked her what she
thought a fairy would be like if she ever did see one, and I can see her
now as she sat there in her chair, looking out of the window munching
her toast. 'Well, between you and me, my dear Tim,' she said—_crunch,
crunch_—'I never have cared much about those little flibbety-gibbety
creatures that are supposed to find a water-lily as big as we human
beings find a four-post bed. And I don't believe myself that they'd pay
much attention to _me_. I expect'—_crunch, crunch_—'there are not very
many of them left now—not in these parts. Mr. Lumb, as you know was an
entomologist. He'd have been able to tell you more about it. And he—had
once seen a ghost.'”

“A ghost, Uncle Tim?”

“That's what Mrs. Lumb said, my dear. And I asked her what _that_ was
like. 'Well, it was like,' she said (and I can remember her very words),
'it was like'—_crunch, crunch_—'seeing something with your eyes shut. It
made him feel very cold; the room grew darker, but he was not
frightened.'”

Letitia sidled yet a little closer to her uncle. “Between you and me,
Uncle Tim,” she said, “I think that ghost would have given _me_ the
shudders. But please let's go back to the fairy. Did you tell Mrs. Lumb
about that?”

“I never breathed so much as a syllable; though, if you were to ask me
why, I couldn't say. It's just like small boys, I suppose, and small
girls too, eh?”

“I think I'd have told _you_, Uncle Tim,” said Letitia. “And what
happened then?”

“Two whole days went by before I went near the field again, though I
doubt if an hour went by without my thinking about it. I forgot about my
bird-traps and everything else, and just loafed about wondering if
perhaps all that I had seen had not been a dream or a trick of fancy.
And then on the third afternoon, I was so ashamed of myself that I
determined to go down to the field and keep watch again.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of winged fairies resting or
standing inside open water lilies floating on a dark pond among lily
pads.]

“This time I made my way to its upper corner by the plantation where I
had seen the fairy disappear. I crept down under some bushes, and had
taken with me a little pocket telescope that my father had given me.
Through this I could see everything that happened near Old Joe as
clearly as if I was at the bottom of the field. It was later in the
evening than before, and already the sun had set. But my legs and arms
were cramped and my left eye tired out with being kept shut long before
anything unusual occurred.

“And then suddenly I knew not only that the fairy was preparing to take
her walks abroad, but that she knew also I was there. She had stolen out
of her hiding-place, though I had seen no actual movement, and was
openly and steadily gazing across the faint green of the sprouting wheat
of the field in my direction. After a while she advanced a few paces
towards the corner of the field in which I was lying hid.

“Then in a breath she hesitated; turned and sped away, just as she had
done on that first evening, towards the very thorn-tree from which I had
first spied out on her. And somehow the conviction that she was doing
this in order to avoid coming near me awakened my curiosity more than
ever, and gave me courage. She was setting her wits against mine, and I
determined to try and meet her face to face. Even then, I confess, the
thought of what I should do if we did so meet was not very pleasant. I
lay there till it was almost dark, in the hope of seeing her come back,
and then made my way to the house and crept in at a side door a good two
hours after my bedtime.

“It was a warm still evening for the time of year, and while I was
undressing a full-moon began to rise. I could see it shining through my
bedroom blind, for I had not lit my candle. I drew up the blind and
looked out of the window, and the whole world looked as if it was
enchanted. There was not a sound in the air, and though here I was in
Mrs. Lumb's house, it seemed as if no human being had ever been there
before. And, what is more, Letitia, the same queer feeling came over me
as when I first caught sight of Old Joe, and I knew in my very bones, as
they say, that the fairy was concealing herself somewhere not very far
from the house, and was watching my window.”

“Oh, Uncle Tim,” said Letitia, “and did you go out?”

“No, my dear, I didn't dare. But not from fear. No; I stood steadily
watching until presently a bird began to sing, a bird which I can't
think was a nightingale. It was not too early in the year for
nightingales, mind you; and there was a hurst or thicket near the house
to which they always resorted. The song was as sweet and sorrowful, too,
as theirs; but it was more shrill, more human, than a nightingale's, and
there seemed to be more tune in it—more melody, you know. And a curious
sadness came over me at the sound of it, and even when I got to bed it
was hours before the echo of it had died away in my mind and I was able
to fall asleep.

“It was just my stupidity, perhaps, to persist in persecuting the fairy.
But then I had become used to persecuting birds, and perhaps I didn't
quite realize that my company in her field was as disquieting as hers
would be to us if she came to afternoon tea.”

“Oh, Uncle Tim, if only she would!”

“Well, Letitia,” said her uncle, “it's no good denying it: they don't.
We ourselves may pine for them; but not they, alas, for us. She didn't
want me there. Old Joe was company enough. And her own world's solitude.
But I met her face to face, none the less. It was the day before I had
to go home again, and two or three visits to the field had been quite in
vain. I could tell by now almost at first glance at Old Joe whether she
was there or not. Just as you could tell at one glance at me if _I_ was
here or not. Not just my body and bones, I mean, eyes and nose and mouth
and so on, but the spirit of me which is really and truly, I hope, your
Uncle Tim.”

“Yes,” said Letitia.

“This particular evening I was in just as morose and sulky a temper as a
small boy can be. My legs were aching because, I suppose, the ground was
damp on which I had been lying, and night after night had been so
crowded with dreams that I had had little real sleep. It seemed that the
fairy had deserted the field. It seemed that all my hope and longing and
watching had been in vain. I scowled at Old Joe as if _he_ were to
blame. Just vanity and fretfulness. Besides, Mrs. Lumb had found out
that I was creeping into the house while she was at dinner. And though
she never scolded me, she had a tart tongue, and could smile at you with
her handsome face and be uncommonly sarcastic at the same time.”

“There's a mistress at school, Miss Jennings, just like that,” cried
Letitia. “And then ... you saw her?”

“Yes, I saw her, face to face. I was on my way back through the copse at
the upper corner where two hedges met at the end of a little lane. And
as I came stumping along, I suddenly went cold all over, and I really
believe my cap got pushed up a little on top of my head, for certainly
my hair seemed to stand up beneath it. And yet I was not really
frightened. Startled, but not frightened. It was the loveliness of that
face. I can't even tell you, Letitia, what clothes she was wearing, but,
as I recall her, it was as if she were surrounded with a haze like that
of wood-smoke. She was the colour of a drift of bluebells in the
distance. That may or may not be, but I did see her face, for I was
staring steadily into her eyes. They were blue, like the blue of flames
in a wood-fire, and they were gazing at me out of a face all pale and
wan, the hair on either side hanging in one long strand down to the
narrow sloping shoulder.

“All else then in the world was completely forgotten. Just me, a small,
uncouth human animal, looking as if into another world into those set
unearthly eyes. There was no movement between us; not the least stir of
recognition or reproach or apprehension on her face. But as I looked—how
can I describe it, my dear?—there came a faint far-away change in her
eyes. It was as if, while you were looking out to sea some summer's
evening, a flight of distant sea-birds appeared in the blue and
vanished. We poor mortals can smile with our eyes alone, but not like
that. That was _her_ way of smiling at me. It told me in my heart of
hearts that she was not unfriendly to me, and yet that she was
entreating me to trespass no more near her lair. There was what she must
do in the world to consider, she was assuring me, though I hadn't then,
nor have now, the least notion of what it was. And those eyes were
telling me also as best she could—for she had no language she could
share with my dull wits—that though she wished me no harm, all she asked
me was not to keep a watch upon her, not to spy, not to intrude. And
then she was gone.

“It was easy to take hiding in the evening dusk of the woods, and the
copse and the hedges grew dense just there. She was gone, my dear, and I
have never seen her since—nor her like.... But you can't call _that_ a
story,” added Uncle Tim, vigorously polishing his spectacles with his
silk handkerchief and blinking at his small niece rather like an owl in
the morning sun.

Letitia remained silent. “I _do_ call it a story, Uncle Tim,” she said
at last. “And oh, how I wish.... But then what about Old Joe?”

“Ah, Old Joe, the rascal! Look at him there. You see, my dear, I never
forgot that evening. Years and years afterwards—I suppose I must have
been then in my early twenties—I stayed a night or two with my old
friend Mrs. Lumb again. She was very much older then, alas—and the first
walk I took was to my field.

“Would you believe it? There was Old Joe in his customary place, though
the barley-crop he was guarding that particular summer was now well
above his knees. And whether I myself was changed or had gone a little
blind in some respects; or whether, as I think, the fairy had long since
given up her hiding-place; or whether perhaps this had always been
merely her way of getting into and out of our world, I don't know. Old
Joe in any case looked, well, just as he looks now, absolutely
empty—forsaken, abandoned. He had brand-new old clothes on, of course,
and a rare old wide-brimmed hat, green as a robin's back. And what do
you think I did?”

“You didn't steal him, Uncle Tim?” whispered Letitia.

“No, Letitia; I purchased him; I bought him,” said Uncle Tim. “But 'ssh!
I went to the old farmer—old Farmer Jones—and asked him how many guineas
he'd take for his hodmadod as a curiosity. Just told him I knew Old Joe
when I was a boy, and had a sentimental affection for him.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a man pointing toward a
distant scarecrow while standing and talking with an older gentleman
holding a walking stick.]

“Farmer Jones was as fat as a porpoise, with a large mulberry red face
and eyes like bits of agate. He laughed at me. And what do you think he
charged?”

Letitia pondered rapidly, blinking her eyes. “I _suppose_ £5 would be a
good deal, wouldn't it, Uncle Tim? Even for Old Joe. Though, of course,”
she added in a slightly louder voice, in case the old creature should
hear, “it would be 'strordinarily cheap, too.”

“Guess again, my dear! Nothing like £5—nothing like even tuppence. 'Give
me a pipe of that plug tobacco of yours,' said the farmer, 'and he's
yours for ever.'

“So mine he was; and I am glad it wasn't money.”

[Illustration: “_I set her on my pacing steed...._”]

“And so am I,” said Letitia. “A pipe of tobacco was much better than
money. It doesn't hurt your feelings, Uncle Tim. And you never saw the
fairy again?”

“In a manner of speaking,” said Uncle Tim in a rather solemn voice, “I
don't think, Letitia, I have ever really seen anything else.
Nothing—even in the dreams that flock into my old head nowadays—nothing
ever resembles in loveliness that face, those deep-dark, unsmiling eyes.
_I set her on my pacing steed, and nothing else saw all day long...._
You know how it goes, my child; but I dare say in _your_ dreams....”

Letitia shook her head violently. “Oh no! Oh, how I wish,” she cried;
then fell silent. “You know, I _believe_, Uncle Tim,” she went on in a
low ruminative voice—“_I_ believe it was almost as if you must have
fallen in love with that fairy. Was it that, Uncle Tim?”

“Ah, my dear!” replied old Mr. Bolsover. “But if you don't think the
long grass under the willows over there is still too wet with the
night's dew, we might venture across and ask Old Joe.”

 THE DRAGONS _By MAXWELL ARMFIELD_


 Within my black and scarlet hall
 Four golden dragons live.
 Their names are Richard, Timothy,
 Mirandola and Paul
 (These I myself did give).

 Their real names no one ever says,
 They are so very grand.
 Their real names if you would pronounce
 You must fast forty days,
 With your mouth full of sand;

 Then, with the names all written out,
 Climb to the topmost hills,
 Where you unwind from rock to rock,
 With a triumphant shout,
 Their strange disyllables.


[Illustration: Black and white circular illustration featuring the name
"RICHARD" at the top, enclosing a dark crescent moon dotted with small
stars and featuring a bird-like head facing right.]

 RICHARD

 Richard will meet you when you come;
 He is the dearest thing.
 At first you might think that he was dumb,
 But if you stroke his golden tail
 From left to right with your finger-nail,
 Richard will silently sing.
 He sings of the moon and shooting stars,
 And “how I wonder what you ares,”
 And all that kind of thing.


[Illustration: Black and white circular illustration featuring the name
"TIMOTHY" inside a dark round background, with a patterned, checkered
sleeve, a hand pointing right, a small animal head on the left, and
decorative, flowing curved elements extending from the top right.]

 TIMOTHY

 Timothy guards the electric light,
 Criss-crossed like a Harlequin;
 They say he is only a dragon at night,
 And by day a Pandolin;
 But he always looks the same to me,
 At breakfast, luncheon, dinner or tea,
 So I guess he’s a dragon all right.


[Illustration: Black and white circular illustration featuring an
abstract, swirling design with concentric lines, striped and dark
shapes, and MIRANDOLA winding along the outer border.]

 MIRANDOLA

 Mirandola, Mirandolore,
 She comes from where you’ve been before:
 She comes from Caithness to the Nore,
 Mirandola, Mirandolore.

 Slowly, slowly, and very pale
 (I think her cousin was a snail),
 She lives beside the bathroom door.
 She bathes in summer, in winter more:
 She LOVES the cold tiles on the floor,
 Mirandola, Mirandolore.


[Illustration: Black and white circular illustration featuring a spotted
dragon curving around the border with its tongue sticking out, next to
the name "PAUL" set against a dark background.]

 PAUL

 When mostly all are gone to bed,
 And YOU sit up talking-and-talking instead,
 And you’re both of you one large vacant YAWL,
 From the crown of your head to your feet on the floor—
 If you want to pass the kitchen door,
 Then FIRST you must pass the place of Paul.
 There he circles round and round,
 Chasing a tail that is never found,
 Guarding the kitchen without a sound.
 And if you want to make friends with Paul
 You had better start in right now, with ALL
 The gumption you can commandeer....
 For the ways of Paul are decidedly queer!

 MABEL IN QUEER STREET _By COMPTON MACKENZIE_


“Will you walk on the pavement like a little lady, Miss Mabel, and not
keep on bobbing up and down in and out of the gutter as if I hadn’t have
told you thirteen times to the dozen every day of your life how to walk
properly.”

Nurse was feeling very cross this morning, because the strings of her
bonnet would keep blowing about, and Baby would keep throwing his rattle
out of the perambulator, and Robert would keep shuffling his feet, and
she had had an argument with Cook about the way Cook had burnt a
milk-pudding; and her sister had written to say that she could not come
to see her, after all, this week, but she hoped she’d be able to come
next week, but she wasn’t sure, and she couldn’t come the week after,
what’s more, because the family was going down into the country.

Mabel made matters worse by being argumentative:

“Well, a dozen is the same as twelve, Nannie,” she pointed out, “so I
don’t think you could have told me thirteen times to twelve.”

“Will you not be so argumentative, Miss Mabel! I’m sure, ever since you
won that blessed prize for writing a letter to that blessed paper
there’s been no doing anything with you, such a dictatorial,
domineering, proctorising little girl as it’s made you.”

[Illustration: “_Will you walk on the pavement!_”]

But Mabel liked to be called all these long words. It made her feel
years older and yards taller to be so many long words that she didn’t
know how to spell, and was pretty sure Nannie didn’t know how to spell
either; because she had asked her once how to spell “dictatorial” and
Nannie had said, “Hush, what’s that?” which was what she always said
when she didn’t want to answer an uncomfortable question.

“Well, you couldn’t,” Mabel obstinately repeated.

“Now, that’s quite enough, Miss! One more word from you and straight up
to bed you go the very moment we get home.”

Mabel was bursting to say that she couldn’t go straight up to bed,
because she had to turn round and walk along two landings before she
could reach her bedroom. However, Nannie was in such an unpleasant
humour that she held her tongue. To tell the truth, before coming out
this afternoon, Mabel had caught a glimpse in the pantry of a
particularly pleasant-looking pink cake, which was likely to appear at
tea, but most unlikely to appear in her bedroom.

“What’s the harm in hopping up and down from the gutter?” she asked.

“Why, before you know where you are, you’ll be getting your foot stuck
in a drain, and there you’ll be till doomsday, and that’ll be a nice
state of affairs.”

[Illustration: _A big crowd would collect._]

As a matter of fact, it _would_ be rather nice, Mabel thought; and she
walked along, imagining what a big crowd would collect to watch a little
girl with her foot stuck in a drain till doomsday, and making up in her
head all sorts of remarks that the people would be saying about her when
they saw her plight. By this time they had reached the place where they
always crossed the road to go into Kensington Gardens. Robert was picked
up and plumped down into the perambulator opposite Baby; and when Nurse
had looked first to the right and then to the left, and then behind her,
and then in front of her, she called out in a voice trembling with
excitement, “Come along now and mind how you step or we shall all be run
over.” Mabel was always thrilled by this crossing. It made her think of
the “Charge of the Light Brigade” which the Vicar had read aloud last
Christmas at the children’s party in the Parish Hall, and which she had
read lots of times since to herself.

“Half-a-league! Half-a-league! Half-a-league onward,” Mabel murmured as
they swept on toward the greenness and the safety of the Gardens.

“Don’t chatter,” Nurse snapped, panting on across the wide road while a
most enormous policeman held up the traffic for them.

“Suppose he fell right down dead at this moment?” Mabel asked herself.
“Then all the buses would come charging on, and there’d be nothing left
of the pram, or of Baby, or of Nannie, or of Robert, or of me.”

“Will you not dawdle, you bad girl!” Nurse was gasping irritably. And
just then with a bump the perambulator landed safely on the opposite
pavement.

“Charge for the guns, Nolan said,” Mabel dreamily murmured.

“What _are_ you muttering to yourself now?” Nurse asked.

“Poetry,” Mabel replied loftily. Then she returned to the subject of the
gutter. “But suppose I didn’t hop up and down, Nannie?” she asked.
“Suppose I just walked all the time in the gutter and didn’t ever walk
on the pavement at all? What would happen then?”

“What would happen then? Why, you’d precious soon find yourself in Queer
Street,” Nurse told her. But before Mabel had time to ask any more
questions about Queer Street Nannie’s attention was occupied by Robert,
who had put in his mouth a broken piece of shell that he had picked up
from the path.

“You dare do such a thing ever again, you naughty boy,” she exclaimed,
“and in the pram you’ll stay for the rest of the afternoon.”

Robert scowled; but he spat out the piece of shell, and he did nothing
more that was naughty until he began to stroke the tyres of the
perambulator-wheels.

Mabel gazed longingly at some little ragged children who were climbing
up the railings beside the path and jumping off them, and balancing
themselves on the top of them, and rolling over and over in the grass on
the other side.

“Do those children live in Queer Street?” she asked.

“They live anywhere,” said Nurse. “And if I’ve told you once, Miss
Mabel, not to turn round and stare after common children I’ve told you
twenty times.”

Mabel yawned.

“Well, will you tell me a story, Nannie?”

“I’ll tell you a story about good Queen Victoria when she was a little
girl and lived in Kensington Palace.”

Mabel yawned again.

“If you’ve told me that story once, you’ve told me twenty times,” she
ventured to say.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a girl dancing or leaping
dynamically with arms outstretched.]

Mabel had an idea that she was being rather rude in thus mimicking
Nannie’s way of talking; but she was really astonished to find quite how
angry it did make her. Nannie’s face turned such a bright purple that
Mabel backed away from her in alarm, because she thought that she was
going to be picked up as Robert was picked up and plumped down in the
perambulator opposite Baby. And that would have been a most terrible
disgrace. Luckily at that moment two large dogs began to fight close by.
Nurse who was always afraid of dogs picked up Robert instead and,
turning the perambulator quickly round, hurried away from the noise and
confusion.

Mabel never knew just how it happened, but all of a sudden she was lost.

The last thing she remembered was hurrying after Nannie away from the
confusion and crashing into a lot of other people that were running
towards it. And then, when all was quiet again she found herself
standing in the middle of the Broad Walk completely alone. Her heart
began to beat very fast, and an uneasy lump came into her throat and she
found that she was making a strange little noise that wasn’t exactly
crying, but was rather like it. She twirled round and round in despair;
but Nurse and Robert and Baby and the perambulator had utterly vanished.
Crowds of most enormous trees stared down at her until she felt smaller
and smaller and more and more frightened. Crowds of strange nurses and
crowds of strange children went walking past. And then she saw coming
towards her a KEEPER.

“A keeper,” she whispered in horror. Oh, where could Nurse have gone? A
KEEPER!

She turned and fled.

[Illustration: A quick black-and-white ink sketch of a girl running.]

Now, of course, if Mabel had been a sensible little girl, she would have
waited for the keeper and told him that she was lost. Unfortunately she
had often been so naughty during walks in Kensington Gardens that Nannie
had threatened her more than once that, if she could not behave herself
better, the keeper would come and lock her up in his little green house.
She had no reason to believe that the keeper was not a savage and brutal
man, because several times she had heard him shout in a very loud and
angry voice at boys who were misbehaving themselves. She had seen him
shake his stick, and once she had actually dared to peep into his little
green house and had seen inside it a live baby. Ugh!

My goodness, how fast Mabel did run, and when she stopped running she
was more lost than ever. At least, that was the way she felt she ought
to feel, though as a matter of fact, after she had got over her first
fright she found that she had reached one of the gates of the Gardens
and began to say to herself that if the worst did come to the worst she
could find her way home alone.

“My name is Mabel Evangeline Marjorie Deane, and I live at Forty-three
Cherrington Gardens, and my father is Doctor Deane, and I’m seven and a
half years old.”

It sounded extremely grand when she repeated all that quickly over to
herself.

“And it wasn’t my fault,” she went on, “it was Nannie who ran away from
the dogs. It wasn’t me who ran away from Nannie.”

While she was trying to make up her mind to take the plunge out of the
quiet green Gardens and set out home along the noisy street, a ragged
boy came by, playing a tune on a penny whistle. Naturally, Mabel took
advantage of being lost to stare at him as hard as she could. The ragged
boy did not appear to pay much attention to her; but when he had passed
her he turned right round and came marching by again, still playing his
jolly tune. This time he did look at her and deliberately winked one of
his blue eyes. Mabel turned rather pink and felt inclined to rush off.
She had never in all her life been able to stare long enough at a ragged
boy to be winked at, and really she did not quite know what she ought to
do. Perhaps if she had been able to wink well she might have winked
back; but Uncle Tom had laughed at her so much the last time she had
tried to wink for his benefit that Mabel had decided to be content with
being able to make a noise like a cork by putting her forefinger in her
cheek and letting it go with a pop. Uncle Tom had told her that she did
this splendidly. So, while the ragged boy walked up and down before her,
whistling and winking, Mabel could only stand still and look pink and
uncomfortable.

“What’s the matter, kid?” said the ragged boy at last. “Lost your
mammy?”

“No, she’s at home; but I’ve lost my nurse.”

The ragged boy went off into another tune that made his listener want to
go dancing along beside him anywhere he chose to play her on.

“What are you going to do about it, kid?”

“Well, I’m going to walk home by myself,” Mabel announced.

The ragged boy piped a long trill on his whistle that seemed to say
better than any words could have done how very much astonished he was to
hear this.

“Ain’t you afraid the copper’ll nab you?” he asked.

Mabel, who had never before heard a policeman called a copper and had
not the least idea what “nab” meant, but had no intention of admitting
to a ragged boy that she could be frightened of anything, shook her
head.

“Coo! You’re a plucky kid. I’ll walk along with you a bit of the way if
you like. My name’s Bill Hawkins. Only mostly they calls me Piping
Billie.”

Mabel hesitated a moment before she accepted this offer. She was really
most anxious to enjoy the ragged boy’s company, but she felt rather
afraid of meeting any of her little girl friends in case they should
fancy that he was a relation of hers. It would be terrible if it went
round the dolls’ tea-parties of Kensington that Mabel Evangeline
Marjorie Deane had such a very ragged relation. Still, there was that
crossing to be managed, and she did not feel quite so much as if she was
riding at the head of the Light Brigade, now that she might have to
cross the road all by herself.

“You can come with me to the pavement over there, if you like,” she told
him.

[Illustration: _... a red coat and cocked hat._]

The ragged boy struck up the jolliest tune that she had ever heard and
led the way. Mabel wondered if the policeman who was holding back the
great grumbling buses and the irritable taxis supposed that she was a
princess. It was a pity that Bill Hawkins was not wearing a red coat and
a cocked hat and white gaiters. She wished that next Christmas she could
have enough money in her money-box to buy him such a dress. If she
could, she would ask if she might not keep him always to march before
her and play these jolly tunes. Where would he sleep, though? There were
only two spare-rooms, and they were nearly always full of uncles and
aunts. To be sure, as he was dressed at present, he might easily sleep
in the coal-cellar; but if he ever had that lovely uniform he could
hardly sleep there. The larder was too small, and besides he might get
smeared with bacon fat. The pantry would do, if Mary didn’t mind. But
Mary was a bit fussy about her china, as she called it. Why shouldn’t he
sleep outside her own little room? No need then to be afraid of fire, or
of witches, or of burglars, or of cats, or even of murderers.

Mabel walked on toward Cherrington Gardens, dreaming away to the tunes
that Bill Hawkins was piping on his whistle. Once she nearly grazed her
forehead on an old gentleman’s watch-chain, and once she had to take a
step to the right and then a step to the left and then another to the
right and then another to the left, while a lady in a great hurry coming
from the other direction did just the opposite until at last she and
Mabel discovered that if both of them stepped to the right at the same
time they could pass each other quite easily. Apart from these two small
accidents Mabel threaded her way along the busy pavements as easily as
if there was nobody in all the world except herself and the ragged boy.
She did not want to talk to him. Just to walk along behind his playing
was as good as the most interesting talk that was ever known. Once he
stopped for a moment and asked Mabel where she lived, and when she told
him he nodded his head and started to play Cherry Ripe.

The pavements were not so crowded now that they had passed the big
shops, and presently they turned off the main road into an old-fashioned
square. Mabel found that she was once more beginning to hop up and down
from the gutter as she walked along. And then she began to take two
steps in the gutter for one she took on the pavement. And then she began
to take only one step on the pavement for half-a-dozen in the gutter.
And then Bill Hawkins began to play a jollier tune than any he had yet
played so far. And then Mabel forgot to step back on the pavement at
all, but went walking along all the time in the gutter. And then the
most extraordinary thing happened, because she suddenly heard a
tremendous argument going on, and when she looked up to see who it was
she saw that all the railings of the square were quarrelling with each
other.

“I don’t care,” one rusty old railing was saying to another very smart
railing who had just been painted a rich peacock blue, “beauty’s only
paint deep, and if you rail at me I’ll rail at you.”

“Old iron! Old iron!” retorted the painted railing. “Don’t you speak in
that ironical tone of voice to me.”

Mabel didn’t at all like the voices of these quarrelsome railings, which
grated on her ears horribly.

“Don’t you stare at me, little girl,” one of them creaked.

“Pooh!” said Mabel. “You want a good oiling, you disagreeable, rusty old
thing.”

At this the railings turned palings, and Mabel wondered if railings
objected to being given oil as much as she objected to being given
castor-oil when she would go on being naughty. But she had no time to go
into this question, because she suddenly realized that she was once more
quite alone. She began to wish that she had not spoken so rudely to the
railings, and she crossed the road to the side of the Square where the
houses lived. But now another extraordinary thing happened. When she
tried to step up on the opposite pavement her foot at once came back
into the gutter. She tried jumping on it with both feet, but the
pavement might have been made of indiarubber the way she bounded off
again back into the gutter.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a girl standing on a
narrow path or ledge, facing a line of several tall, thin figures
wearing top hats standing along a wall.]

“Oh, well,” said Mabel aloud, trying to appear calm. “I may as well be
phisolophical.”

This was what her mother said when Mary came in to tell her that the cat
had broken one of the best glasses. Mabel had practised the word in bed,
but this was the first time she had ventured to say it right out in
broad daylight.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a girl running away in
panic down a paved path as an anthropomorphic grandfather clock wearing
a top hat pursues her from behind.]

“If I can’t walk on the pavement, I’ll just have to walk in the gutter,”
she went on.

And then she gave her shoulders a most tremendous shrug. After all,
there was something to be said for being lost. At any rate, you could
shrug your shoulders without being lectured for doing so.

Mabel walked on in the direction of Cherrington Gardens, hoping every
moment to hear the sound of Billie Hawkins’ piping. In spite of her
philosophical shrugs she was beginning to feel just a little bit uneasy.
Somehow or other the square did not look the same as usual, and when she
saw one of the front-doors suddenly leave its hinges and come walking
down the little front garden, its letter-box stretched in a wide grin,
and wave its handle at her, she started to run. But the faster she ran,
the faster the door bolted after her, and if Mabel had not found a funny
little passage too narrow for the door to follow her it would soon have
caught her.

“SLAM!” the door ejaculated angrily, when it perceived that Mabel had
escaped.

Mabel, however, did not stop to listen to its exclamations of baffled
rage, but hurried along the narrow passage until she came out in a
street that she had never seen before in all her walks, and looking up
to find out what its name was, she saw a huge pointed finger write QUEER
STREET on a signpost.

And it certainly was.

All the houses were a different colour and they had the most
extraordinary shapes; but Mabel could not see anybody about who might
tell her how to reach Cherrington Gardens and when she got to the other
end of Queer Street she found that there was no way out and that a
bright red house as round and nearly as tall as a lighthouse took up all
the space. Mabel did not feel at all inclined to venture back by the
small passage down which she had run to escape from the door. However,
when she found that she could not get out of Queer Street at the far end
she went back cautiously to see if she could somehow muster up enough
courage to discover if the door was still hanging about. But things were
getting queerer every minute. The passage was gone now. There was
nothing but an immense blank wall on which was written in gigantic
letters: NO ADMITTANCE EXCEPT ON OMNIBUSINESSES.

[Illustration: Color illustration of a street scene featuring whimsical
buildings shaped like household items and food, including a giant red
post box, a police station, and a baker]

Mabel really was frightened now. There was nobody to be seen at any of
the windows of these strange-coloured houses, and she simply did not
know what to do next. It had sounded rather exciting when Nannie had
told her that she would one day find herself in Queer Street. Now that
she really was in Queer Street it was horrid. At last she collected
enough courage to make up her mind to ring the bell of one of the houses
and, if anybody answered it, to ask the way out of Queer Street. She
finally decided to try the tall red house, which at first she had
thought was rather like a lighthouse, but which now that she examined it
more closely was apparently an enormous pillar-box. The door was painted
white and covered with sums. There was only one window that she could
see; and that was very high up and shaped like the hole up to which she
used often to be lifted to post the letters when she was quite a little
girl. Mabel could not find any sign of a bell or a knocker, but after
she had walked round the red house several times she found a white plate
on which was printed LICK AND STAMP.

“Yes, but what am I to lick?” Mabel asked herself.

“The licker’s broken,” came a gruff voice from what seemed miles above,
where she was standing. Mabel stepped back to look up and see who it was
speaking; but she had only just time to catch sight of a head
disappearing inside as the flap of the window shut with a clang.

“Bother!” Mabel exclaimed.

She wasn’t allowed to say “bother” at home, but being in Queer Street
she did not care how much she let herself go. No sooner had she stepped
back to the door than the gruff voice was heard again from above.

“As the licker’s broken, half a stamp will bring an answer.”

“But I haven’t got a stamp,” Mabel shouted.

“You’ve got feet, haven’t you?” the gruff voice called down.

Once more Mabel stepped back to try to catch sight of the owner; but
once more the flap of the window dropped with a clang.

“You’ve got feet, haven’t you,” the gruff voice repeated when Mabel was
once more outside the door.

“Yes, I’ve got feet.”

“Then you’ve got a stamp. And if you’ve got a stamp you’ve got half a
stamp.”

“Do you mean I ought to stamp with one foot?” Mabel shouted.

“Certainly not. That’s a whole stamp. Of course, if you like to waste
shoe-leather you can use a whole stamp, but you won’t get the answer any
quicker if you do. You may just as well stamp with the sole only. Don’t
bother about putting on your gum-boots.”

Mabel was anxious to get an answer as soon as possible, so she stamped
as hard as she could. Whereupon the door flew back and she saw standing
in the entrance behind a cage, a lump of wood dressed in dark blue and
wearing a cap peaked in front and behind.

“Yes, good-morning. I’m the Postman. What can I do for you?”

“I want to find the way home to Cherrington Gardens, please,” said Mabel
very politely.

“You’ll have to address yourself first,” the Postman snapped.

“But how can I address myself?”

“You can dress yourself, can’t you?”

“Well, nearly everything,” said Mabel. “Only, my nurse helps me with my
frock.”

“You can undress yourself, can’t you?”

“Oh, yes,” said Mabel eagerly. “At least I can if Nurse undoes the
buttons at the back and if there aren’t any knots.”

[Illustration: “_Good-morning. I’m the Postman...._”]

She rather wished that she had kept off the subject of knots, because
the Postman’s face was full of them. However, he did not seem to mind
the allusion.

“Then you ought to be able to address yourself,” he told her severely.
“Addressing is twice as easy as dressing or undressing. Can you undress
an envelope?”

“No, I don’t think I could.”

“Can you dress an envelope?”

“No, I’m sure I couldn’t.”

“You can address one, can’t you?”

“Yes, if the lines are ruled,” Mabel said.

“There you are,” the Postman cried triumphantly. “So the sooner you set
about addressing yourself the sooner you’ll get home. In fact, if you
can address yourself in another minute, you’ll get home by the first
delivery to-morrow morning.”

“But where shall I be all night?” Mabel gasped.

“You’ll be addressed and put to bed.”

“Yes, but where?” Mabel persisted.

“In my pillow-box.”

“But I thought it was a pillar-box.”

“It’s a pillar-box by day, stupid, but by night it’s a pillow-box, of
course. Why, I knew that long before I was your age. I knew that when I
was quite a twig. There’s only one thing you want to look out for. Sleep
well up in the corner of the pillow-box, otherwise you’ll be getting
woken up all the time by parcels coming back late and tumbling down on
top of you.”

“Yes, but Mother will be anxious if I don’t come home all night,” said
Mabel, who didn’t at all relish the idea of going to bed with a lot of
parcels. “Couldn’t you tell me the way to walk home?”

[Illustration: “_... parcels tumbling down._”]

The Postman shook his wooden head so vigorously that his neck squeaked.

“Well, I think I’ll ask at another house first if you don’t mind,
because really Mother will be terribly anxious....”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of an eccentric,
hunched-over figure pointing forward.]

But Mabel need not have bothered herself about being polite, for the
Postman shut the door abruptly and vanished.

A moment afterwards she heard his gruff voice again from above:

“This _was_ a letter-box,” he called down angrily. “But since you won’t
be sensible, as far as you’re concerned, in future it’s a
don’t-letter-box.”

Mabel turned round and decided to try another house. She chose a green
one. This was much more like an ordinary house than the Postman’s, and,
indeed, except that it was green all over and that the windows were like
cucumber-frames it might not have been situated in Queer Street. She
rang the bell, and presently the door was opened by a bulbous-nosed man
in a mouldy suit with lawn sleeves, and an apron of green bays. There
was only one thing that was very queer about him which was that he had a
grass moustache.

“Please, could you tell me the way to Cherrington Gardens?” Mabel asked.

“I could,” said the Greengrocer, for that was who it was, of course.

“Oh, thank you!” Mabel exclaimed.

“But it’s a very heavy weigh from here. It’s a good three pecks, and
perhaps a pint or two over. You take the second turnip to the right and
the first turnip to the left beyond that, and then if you’re lucky
you’ll find a taxi-cabbage or a motor-carrot to lift you the rest of the
weigh. It’ll be quite a nice little spinach for you.”

“But, please....” Mabel began.

“Do you mean ‘peas’?” the Greengrocer interrupted.

“Well, I didn’t exactly,” said Mabel.

The Greengrocer shook his head.

“Politeness weighs nothing, you know,” he said severely. “Didn’t your
nurse ever tell you that?”

“But I did say ‘please.’”

“You ought to have said ‘peas.’”

Mabel thought she would be wise to humour him.

“Peas, Mr. Greengrocer....” she began again.

“Do you mean ‘peas, Mr. Greengrow, sir?’”

Poor Mabel was getting so fussed by all these interruptions that she
could have cried.

“Peas, Mr. Greengrow, sir, I can’t find any turnings—I mean
turnips—anywhere.”

“Well, of course, if you can’t find them, you can’t take them, can you?”

“No, I couldn’t, could I?”

“And that’s that?”

She nodded her head dolefully.

“Well, we all have our disappointments,” said the Greengrocer. “I’ve
tried for years to find a future for beans. But there isn’t one. So
there it is, and we’ll leave it at that.”

With which the Greengrocer retired into his house and shut the door in
Mabel’s face. She waited for a moment or two outside in the hope that he
would come out again; but a shower of brussels-sprouts came tumbling
down on her from one of the upper windows and drove her away from the
green-house.

Once more Mabel walked up and down Queer Street trying to find a way
out. But the great blank wall was still there, and she began to think
that she should have to spend all the rest of her life looking vainly at
these strange-coloured houses. The tears rolled down poor Mabel’s pink
cheeks, and there’s no knowing how much she would have cried if she had
not discovered that she had lost her pocket-handkerchief. So she even
had to stop crying, which made her feel terribly sad. At last, after
another long examination of the various houses, she decided to try a
white house with a zinc door and curious glass windows which instead of
having curtains were half full of milk. When Mabel rang the bell she
heard the most tremendous jangling you can imagine, such a jangling that
she was afraid she had broken something inside, and she was just going
to turn round and run away when the door was opened by a very tall, thin
man in a long white coat and a face that looked exactly as if it was
made of cheese, which in fact it was.

“Please, I can’t....” Mabel began.

“My business is ‘cans,’” he sang. “I’ve nothing to do with ‘can’ts.’ And
just mind where you’re treading. You’re trampling down all my buttercups
and dairies.”

“I beg your pardon,” said Mabel, moving quickly to one side.

“You can borrow my pardon,” sang the Milkman. “Or if you’re quick enough
you can steal my pardon. But don’t start begging it, if you don’t want
the Policeman in the blue house next door to jug you on the spot.”

“Please, _can_ you tell me the way out of Queer Street?”

“Which way? The milky way, the buttery way, or the curds and way?”

“Oh, any way,” Mabel sighed in desperation.

“Then you’d better take the buttery one. You can slip out more easily
that way,” he sang in a very high voice.

“Well, which is the way to the buttery way?”

“Take the first churning to the left or the first churning to the right,
whichever you cheese. Then skim the ground and tell the first person you
meet the cream of the joke.”

“Yes, but supposing I don’t meet anybody?” poor Mabel asked.

“Why, if it’s a question of meat, you’d better enquire from the Butcher
who lives at the flesh-coloured house two doors down. You can’t mistake
it. The garden is full of cowslips, bullrushes, and ox-eyed daisies. But
mind he doesn’t chop you up when he’s chopping them down.”

The zinc door closed behind him with a rattle and jangle.

“Well, wherever I ask,” Mabel decided, “I won’t ask the Butcher
anything. Or the Policeman either.”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of an elderly woman while a
chef looks on.]

Accordingly, she walked past the flesh-coloured house and the blue house
without lingering for a moment. The next house was a brown one shaped
like a cottage loaf. There was a breakfast Rolls-Royce car outside the
gate, which was much more like a fender than a gate. Mabel was on the
point of asking at the oven-door if the car could drive her home when
the oven-door opened and a woman with her hair done in a hot cross bun
puffed out, followed by such a terribly crusty old man that Mabel was
afraid to make any enquiries here, and determined to give the Baker and
his wife as wide a berth as the Butcher and the Policeman. The next
house she came to was the silver house of the Fishmonger. He was leaning
over his gate, and as he looked fairly pleasant Mabel asked him to tell
her the way out of Queer Street.

[Illustration: Black and white line illustration of a portly, comical
figure standing with arms out and feet together, wearing an apron.]

“You might row out,” he suggested in a strange gurgling kind of a voice
like water running out of a bath. “But it’s not at all a soft row, you
know. Oh no, it’s a very hard row. Yes, Queer Street is a very fishy
plaice to row out of.”

“But couldn’t I walk out?”

“Oh, you could. But you’ll wear out your soles _and_ your eels long
before you walk out of Queer Street. Take my advice, young lady, for I
wouldn’t cod you, and trust to boats not boots if you want to get out of
Queer Street. Still, if you insist on walking, why, take the first
turbot to the right, and the second turbot to the....”

But Mabel put her hands up to her ears and ran away.

“I think all these people must be mad,” she said to herself. “Oh dear, I
wish I hadn’t ever walked in the gutter. Nannie always says so many
things that _don’t_ come true, but this did come true.”

While Mabel was talking to herself, she suddenly became aware that it
was growing dark, and just as she settled to risk being jugged (whatever
that might mean) by the Policeman for begging the Greengrocer’s pardon
and ask him to take her home, she heard close behind her a horrible
voice that sounded like somebody being sick. A moment later she was
popped into a large black bag, swung up in the air, and carried off.

“Help! Help!” Mabel shrieked, but the louder she shrieked the more her
mouth was filled with some kind of dust. And then she turned perfectly
black with fright, for she suddenly realized that it must be the Sweep
who had caught her up like this and popped her in his bag. She was on
the point of fainting right away, when she was hauled out of the bag by
the scruff of the neck and found herself (at least, she didn’t exactly
find herself because it was too dark to find anything) in a black room.

“So you want to find your way home, do you?” the horrible voice
demanded.

“Yes, please, Mr. Sweep,” she whispered.

“Then up the chimney you’ll go,” the voice whooped.

“Oh, please, I’d rather not go up a chimney,” Mabel screamed.

“Up the chimney you’ll go,” the voice whooped. “We don’t want
inquisitive little girls like you in Queer Street.”

“I won’t ever be inquisitive again, Mr. Sweep, if only you’ll show me
another way home.”

But it was no use for Mabel to beg for mercy. She felt a pair of bony
arms seize her and gasping for breath she found herself being pushed
further and further up the chimney by a long pole. Up and up she was
pushed, and the further up she got the narrower the chimney became, and
then just as she felt she simply must burst she heard the sound of
Billie Hawkins’ whistle. The sound seemed to be tumbling down on her
like delicious drops of water and looking up she found herself lying on
the grass under a great big tree. There was Nannie bending over her with
a cup in her hand, and beyond her a little ragged boy was playing a
penny whistle, and beyond him a lot of other little ragged boys and
girls were staring at her.

“Why ever don’t you look where you’re going?” Nannie was saying. “You
gave me a terrible turn when you bumped into that tree and knocked
yourself insensible for I don’t know how long.”

“I’m sorry I gave you such a terrible turbot, Nannie, I mean turnip,
Nannie.”

“Pore little mite, she’s still feeling queer,” a woman in the crowd
said.

“Oh, shan’t I ever get out of Queer Street?” Mabel cried, bursting into
tears.

[Illustration: _The dolls’ tea-parties in Kensington._]

“Why, you silly girl, you’re in Kensington Gardens.”

“Was I quite insensible, Nannie?” she asked.

And I really can’t tell you how proud Mabel was of having been knocked
insensible and how jealous all her little girl friends were when Mabel
used to boast at the dolls’ tea-parties in Kensington about her exciting
adventures in Queer Street.

 KING OF THE CASTLE _By MADELEINE NIGHTINGALE_


 Under the bushes by the gate
 Is where I live in royal state,
 And lovelier my palace stands
 Than any palace made with hands;
 For houses made with hands, you see,
 Must be what hands can make them be,
 But those you build inside your head
 Can be just what you like instead.

 And so, because to be a king
 Is such a very pleasant thing,
 Beneath the biggest bush I sit
 And rear a palace out of it.
 The walls with arras-cloth are hung
 And all the woven scenes among
 Are magic trees that wave about,
 And magic birds hop in and out.

 And though the ceiling’s chiefly green,
 Magic the spaces in between,
 Which, as you watch, all through the day
 Are changing—white and blue and grey—
 Whilst on the floor are neatly laid
 Carpets of brown and gold brocade;
 And golden velvet cloths are strewn
 To keep quite clean my ivory throne.

[Illustration: _King of the castle._]

 And, oh, no king has anywhere
 More courtiers than I have there;
 Indeed, so many at my call
 There are I cannot count them all.
 In hose and doublet, silk and lace,
 Grandly they move about the place,
 Till lawns and paths are gayer far
 Than all the gardener’s flower-beds are.

 And yet (it’s very strange but true!)
 The gardener and the postman too
 And milk-boys clattering to and fro
 Across my courtyard never know
 What splendid folk they walk among,
 But pass them as they go along,
 For all the world as though there were
 Just no one walking, talking there.

 Nor do they know—’tis plain to see—
 That this small boy who looks like me
 Is really King of all the land
 And could, did he but lift a hand,
 Cast them in deepest dungeons or
 Hang, draw and quarter them in four.
 Or they’d not dare to shout “Good-day!”
 In such a very casual way.

 THE BIRD-BATH _By MADELEINE NIGHTINGALE_


 Under the quince-tree’s shade, away
 From garden-paths—half-green, half-grey—
 The bird-bath stands, and dipping in it
 Sparrow and blackbird, thrush and linnet,
 So fierce they flutter wide and far
 Scattered the silver splashes are;
 You’d hardly think there left could be
 One single drop of little me.
 And yet when every bird that will
 Has dipped and splashed and drunk his fill,
 Never I go, with either hand
 To hold the bowl and tip-toe stand
 And peep, but there with leaves and sky
 All mixed—there always still am I.

 CARAWAY CAKE _By MABEL MARLOWE_


Biddy the Brownie-cook stood in her little garden, talking over the
fence to her neighbour.

“I am going to make a cake for my husband,” she said.

“What kind of cake?”

“A caraway cake.”

“I shouldn’t if I were you,” said the neighbour. “Never make a caraway
cake for your husband on Midsummer Eve. The cookery book tells you not
to.”

“Oh, I never bother about a cookery book,” said Biddy the Brownie-cook.
“I cannot fuss with all the rules, and all the weights and measures. I
simply go to the larder and take out all the things that I have, and mix
them in a basin, and make a cake—and it is always a nice cake, too.”

She gave her neighbour a smile, and went into her house. She looked in
the larder, and found some flour, some butter, some sugar, some spice,
and a little packet of caraway seeds.

She put all the things on the table, beside her wooden pastry bowl. Then
she looked on the shelf for an egg, but the only egg to be seen was a
greenish one, which she had once found in a deserted nest by the
mulberry tree.

“Never mind, I must make this do. An egg is an egg all the world over,”
said Biddy the Brownie-cook. She took the egg between her finger and
thumb, held it over the bowl, and gave it a smart tap with a fork.

[Illustration: “_Never make a caraway cake on Midsummer Eve...._”]

POP! The egg flew into a thousand pieces, and out fluttered a tiny
yellow bird.

“Tweet! I see some butter,” said the bird, and it flew down to the table
and began to peck at the butter.

“Stop that at once! It is my butter,” cried Biddy the Brownie-cook,
clapping her hands at the bird.

“Never make caraway cake on Midsummer Eve,” said the bird. It dropped a
yellow feather from its tail, then it went on eating the butter. It
pecked and it pecked! It ate and it ate! It grew and it grew until it
was the size of a missel-thrush.

“Dear, dear! How shall I make my cake now, with no egg and no butter?”
said Biddy the Brownie-cook.

“Swee-eet! I see some flour,” said the thrush, and it hopped on to the
flour bag, and began to eat up the flour.

“Stop that at once! It is my flour,” cried Biddy, reaching for the
rolling-pin, and holding it up like a stick.

“Never make caraway cake on Midsummer Eve,” said the thrush. It dropped
a nut-brown feather from its tail, then it went on eating the flour. It
pecked and it pecked! It ate and it ate! It grew and it grew, until it
was the size of a pigeon.

“Dear dear! How shall I make my cake now, with no egg, no butter, and no
flour?” said Biddy the Brownie-cook.

“Coo-ooo! I see some sugar,” said the pigeon, and it hopped upon the
sugar bag, and began to eat the sugar.

“Stop that at once! Stop it this moment! It is my sugar. Don’t you dare
to touch it, you bothersome bird!” cried Biddy, waving the rolling-pin
about.

[Illustration: Color illustration of a girl standing on a small wooden
stool peering into a bowl on a table as a yellow bird hatches and flies
out of it, while a white mouse wearing a blue bow stands on the floor
below looking up.]

“Never make caraway cake on Midsummer Eve,” cooed the pigeon, and it
steadily set to work to eat up all the sugar. It dropped a grey-green
feather from its tail. It pecked and it pecked! It ate and it ate! It
grew and it grew until it was the size of a peacock.

“Dear dear! How shall I make my cake now, with no egg, no butter, no
flour, and no sugar?” wailed poor Biddy the Brownie-cook.

“Cluck-a-cluck-a-clickety! I see some spice,” said the peacock, dipping
its beak into the spice box.

“Stop that at once! Stop it this moment! It is my spice. Don’t you dare
to touch it, you good-for-nothing, bothersome bird!” cried Biddy,
banging on the table with the rolling-pin. “Don’t you dare to eat my
spice, or I will crack you over the head with my rolling-pin.”

“Never make caraway cake on Midsummer Eve,” mumbled the peacock, with
his beak half full of spice. It dropped a beautiful peacock’s feather
from its tail, then it went on eating the spice. It pecked and it
pecked! It ate and it ate! It grew and it grew, until it was the size of
an ostrich.

“Dear dear! How shall I make my cake now, with no egg, no butter, no
flour, no sugar, and no spice?” cried poor Biddy, wiping her eyes.

“Hallo-ullo-ullo-ullo! I see some caraway seeds,” said the ostrich.

“Don’t you dare to touch them! They are my caraway seeds. You have eaten
my butter. You have eaten my flour. You have swallowed my sugar and
gobbled up my spice. But you shall not have my caraway seeds, indeed you
shall not!” cried Biddy the Brownie-cook.

“Never make caraway cake on Midsummer Eve,” clucked the ostrich. He
dropped a trailing tail-feather upon the table. Then he picked up the
bag of caraway seeds, and swallowed them at one gulp.

[Illustration: “_Hullo ... I see some caraway seeds._”]

“You thieving, bothersome, good-for-nothing rascal!” cried Biddy. “How
shall I make my cake now, I wonder, with no egg, no butter, no flour, no
sugar, no spice, and no caraway seeds? What will my hungry husband say
when he comes home?”

“Better make some oat-scones,” said the ostrich.

Then Biddy the Brownie-cook went into the larder, dropping tears as she
went. She reached a bag of oatmeal down from the shelf, and came back
into the kitchen.

The ostrich was gone.

The caraway seeds, the spice, the sugar, the flour, and the butter were
lying on the table beside the wooden pastry bowl. The shell of the
little greenish egg was in the bottom of the bowl cracked neatly in
half, quite empty.

“Goodness me! I must have been dreaming,” said Biddy the Brownie-cook.

But lying beside the caraway seeds were five feathers: a yellow one, a
nut-brown one, a greenish-grey one, a peacock’s one, and a beautiful
drooping, ostrich feather.

 THE WITCH _By ROSE FYLEMAN_


 She has a little funny house
 Beside a little stream;
 It really, truly seems to me
 It’s half a house and half a tree,
 Like something in a dream.

 She has a pointy, pokey nose,
 A pokey, pointy chin;
 Her little door is open wide—
 I do not dare to peep inside,
 Lest she should be within;

 Lest she should smile and nod and say,
 “Good-day, my pretty daughter,”
 And then unhook her wicked broom
 And chase me quickly from the room
 And push me in the water.

[Illustration: Silhouette illustration of a small, rustic cottage with
bare branches growing from its roof and laundry hanging on a line, with
a hunched figure walking across a rope bridge toward it over a stream.]

 THE PRINCESS WHO COULD NOT LAUGH _By A. A. MILNE_


There was once a King who had an only daughter, the pride of his heart.
She was sweet, she was good, she was beautiful, and the King would have
said that she was perfection itself, but for one thing. She never
laughed. Nothing seemed to amuse her.

Her father, the King, was often amused. The court fool had only to open
his mouth, and His Majesty was leaning back in his throne roaring with
laughter. Nobody was so quick as His Majesty to see the point of a good
story or a clever riddle. But the Princess would listen gravely, and
then say “Fancy!” or “Did he really?” or “Well, what happened next?”
when the end of the joke had been reached. “My darling,” the King would
say, wiping away the tears of laughter which had come into his eyes,
“don’t you understand? It’s a _joke_!” And the Princess would answer,
“Yes, I see it’s a joke, Father, but why do you make that noise about
it?”

For Her Royal Highness always said that she saw a joke as well as
anybody. What made her different from the King was that when His Majesty
saw a joke he had to make a loud roaring noise, whereas when _she_ saw
one, she didn’t want to make any noise at all. Did that matter very
much?

“My darling,” said the King, “it isn’t a question of _wanting_ to. If
you have a sense of humour, you _have_ to laugh.”

“_I_ don’t,” said the Princess.

[Illustration: Color illustration featuring a split scene: the upper
panel shows a person reclining on a throne while a jester looks up from
below, and the lower panel shows a woman wearing a patterned, flowing
gown and headdress standing near a curtain, with a jester standing
nearby.]

“I know,” sighed the King; “you don’t.”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a cloaked, hunched figure
with a long nose and glasses, holding their hands out forward.]

And this made him very miserable. Nothing is so pleasant as to tell a
funny story to somebody whom you love, to watch the smile coming on her
face, and to hear her sudden laughter, and then to linger with her, your
laughter catching new life from hers, in happy enjoyment of the joke.
And this pleasure the King did not have.

He did his best. He read straight through to her, from beginning to end,
that amusing book _One Thousand Merrie Jestes collected from Manye
Sources_, pausing for a moment at the end of each story so that her
laughter should not run over on to the next story. But she never laughed
once. He then read it through again; still she did not laugh. He gave
her to read for herself, _Diverting Tales from Divers Lands_, and
watched her face anxiously. She did not smile. Nothing that he could say
or do, nothing that the court fool could say or do, changed the gravity
of her face. She was sweet, she was good, she was beautiful—but she
could not laugh.

A time came when the King could not bear it any longer, and he felt that
somehow or other the young Princess must be moved to laughter. In his
difficulty he consulted his Chancellor, than whom there was supposed to
be no wiser man in His Majesty’s kingdom. Now the Chancellor had a son,
the young Count Hoppo, a fellow of no great learning, nor beauty, nor
bravery—a fellow, indeed, of no personal attraction whatsoever, but a
young man esteemed in his family as a jester. The Chancellor therefore
spoke as follows:

“I would suggest Your Majesty, that it be announced to Your Majesty’s
faithful people that he who is first able to move Her Royal Highness to
laughter be rewarded by the hand of Her Royal Highness and half your
Majesty’s kingdom.”

“What do you say, my love?” said the King to his daughter.

“As you please,” said the Princess. “I shall not laugh, for it seems
that I have no wish to laugh, nor do I greatly desire to marry. But as
you please.”

The King turned to his Chancellor.

“Let it be so announced,” he commanded. “At noon henceforward, for the
space of one half-hour, those with humorous riddles or merry jests to
tell shall have audience before the whole court, and he who first moves
Her Royal Highness to laughter shall be rewarded by her hand in
marriage.”

“_And_ half the kingdom?” said the Chancellor anxiously.

“Is that usual?” asked the King.

“Entirely, Your Majesty.”

“Very well. So be it. To-morrow at noon we shall await the competitors.”

At noon, then, all those with good stories to tell, or merry riddles to
propound, attended at the Palace, and foremost amongst them was the
young Count Hoppo. So one by one they put their riddles or told their
stories, and Her Royal Highness listened to them.

[Illustration: “_Why does a dragon cross the road?_”]

“Tell me, Your Royal Highness,” said Count Hoppo, “why does a dragon
cross the road?”

The King, who knew this one, began to chuckle to himself.

“I suppose,” said the Princess, “that he crosses the road in order to
get to the other side.”

“Y-yes,” said Hoppo, a little annoyed. “Yes, exactly.”

“Well?” said the Princess.

“That’s all,” said Hoppo crossly.

“Was it funny?” she said, turning to the King.

“My dear,” said her father, “you missed the point of the joke. The point
of the joke was that if _you_ said—I mean, what he _expected_ you to
say—then _he_ would have said—only you didn’t.”

“But why else _should_ a dragon cross the road?”

“Quite so,” said the King quickly. “Next competitor.”

Another young man stepped forward and enquired of Her Royal Highness why
a silver bowl was like a wasp’s nest.

“It isn’t,” said the Princess. She turned to the King. “Is it, father?”

“It’s a riddle, dear,” explained His Majesty, and he murmured to
himself, “Why is a silver bowl like a wasp’s nest—like a wasp’s nest.”

“I still think it isn’t,” said the Princess.

“Well?” said the King to the questioner. “Why _is_ a silver bowl like a
wasp’s nest?”

“Because there’s a ‘B’ in ‘BOTH,’ Your Majesty.”

His Majesty roared with laughter.

“Not in a _wasp’s_ nest,” said the Princess, “surely!”

The King explained that the _letter_ “B” was in the _word_ “BOTH.”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a figure in medieval or
Renaissance attire, wearing a feathered hat, patterned tunic, and
holding a long sword horizontally across their body.]

“Then why drag in the wasps’ nest?”

The King explained that that was the joke.

“I see,” said the Princess, but she did not laugh.

Then a third young man came forward, and this was the Count Rollo. Tall
and handsome he was, and of a pleasant smiling countenance, so that the
Princess thought to herself: “If only _he_ would win, and not that
horrid Count Hoppo.” Count Rollo, it seemed, desired to know the
difference between a fly and a bird.

“A fly and a bird,” murmured the King to himself, “a fly and a bird, a
fly and a bird, a fly and a bird.”

The Princess hastened to tell him.

“A bird,” she said, “has feathers on its wings, but a fly has not. A
bird lays eggs, and a fly—no, that’s wrong, because flies do lay eggs.
But a fly hasn’t got a beak. And it doesn’t build a nest. Oh, and it’s
much smaller than a bird.”

“All this is true!” smiled Rollo, “but there is another difference, Your
Royal Highness.”

“Well?” said the King.

“A bird can fly, Your Majesty, but a fly cannot bird.”

“Ha-ha-ha!” went the King. “Ha-ha-ha-ha-ha! Ha-ha!”

“Cannot _what_?” asked the Princess.

“Cannot bird, Your Royal Highness.”

“But what _is_ ‘birding’?”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a fly with its hands
raised to its head in a distressed or dramatic pose.]

“That is what the poor fly never knew, Your Royal Highness, and often
wondered. It seems so unfair, does it not, that if a bird can fly
whenever it likes, the fly can never bird, however much it wants to.”

“It does seem rather unfair,” said the Princess, nodding to him in a
friendly way.

But she did not laugh.

So, in their turn, all the young men of the country put their riddles
and told their stories, not only on that day, but on many other days.
And, one by one, they grew tired of trying to make laugh a Princess who
could not laugh, and they came no more to the court at the hour of noon.
But Count Hoppo and Count Rollo came always, for that Hoppo wished
mightily for the half of His Majesty’s kingdom, and Rollo loved and was
loved by the Princess. So in the end they two stood there alone.

Now this was to be the last trial between them, wherefore each had spent
the night in anxious preparation for the morrow, Rollo wondering which
of the few stories he had left untold would be most likely to amuse the
Princess, and Hoppo bethinking him of a plan whereby at the same time he
might win the Princess and discomfort his rival.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a large procession of
figures in historical attire walking up a grand stairway toward the
arched entrance of a palace.]

On the morrow, then, before the whole court Count Hoppo and Count Rollo
made final trial for the hand of the Princess. And the Princess looked
at them as they stood there before her, and wished with all her heart
that it might be Count Rollo who should make her laugh. So Count Rollo
came forward to tell her his story.

“There was,” he said, “a stranger who came into a far country, and he
desired to know the way to the King’s palace, for he had a present to
lay before the King. So he stopped one whom he met on the road, and said
to him: ‘Pray, sir, do you know the way to the King’s palace?’ Whereupon
the man, wishing to be thought humorous, replied, ‘Yes, I do,’ and
walked on. But a moment later, fearing lest his joke should be mistaken
for rudeness, he hurried after the stranger, and said, ‘I beg your
pardon, sir, but do you wish to know the way to the palace?’ ‘No,’ said
the stranger, ‘I don’t,’ and left him there.”

To the King this seemed such a delightfully silly joke that it was some
minutes before he could recover his gravity, but the Princess could only
look sadly and wistfully at Count Rollo.

“Well, well,” said the King, “you shall have one more turn later. Now it
is for Count Hoppo to try.”

Now Count Hoppo was minded to try a trick with which he had often amused
the members of his own family. For it had long been a habit of his, when
some unwelcome guest was arriving at his father’s door, to prepare a
butter-slide in the hall, so that, treading unwarily upon it, the
visitor should slip, and his feet should fly into the air, and he should
fall heavily to the ground, to the great enjoyment of those of the
family who were looking on. So for this occasion he had prepared a most
amusing butter-slide a few paces from where he and Count Rollo stood,
and had warned his father, the Chancellor, to take care that no members
of the court trod upon it before the trial began. So now he stepped
forward, feeling well assured that in one and the same moment he would
win the hand of the Princess and put to shame and laughter the hated
Count Rollo.

[Illustration: _He had prepared a most amusing butter-slide._]

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a person tumbling or
falling head over heels through the air.]

“I propose,” he said, “to show Your Royal Highness a small humorous joke
of a practical nature, which cannot fail to move Your Royal Highness to
loud and hearty laughter. Perhaps, before I begin, the Count Rollo would
oblige me by moving a little farther away, so as to give me more room
for my joke.” He turned to Rollo. “If you would be so good as to
withdraw a few paces towards the window——”

He indicated with a careless gesture the necessary direction, and bowed
to Rollo. Rollo bowed to him in reply; bowed gracefully to the King and
still more gracefully to the Princess. Then, still bowing with the
utmost ease and elegance, he retired a few paces backwards and stepped
gracefully upon the butter-slide. There was a loud crash.

“Oh!” gasped the Princess. “Oh-ho-ho-ho-ho-ho! Oh-ha-ha-ha-ha-ha!
Oh-he-he-he-he-he!” It was as if somebody had suddenly pressed a button
inside her. Her laughter pealed round the palace walls. She laughed up
and up and up and up, and she laughed down and down and down and down.
She trilled and gurgled and choked, and choked and gurgled and trilled.
It seemed that there was no end to her laughter.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a princess wearing a
long, flowing gown and a patterned cap, holding her hands up near her
mouth with a surprised or joyful expression.]

The King too was laughing, the Chancellor was laughing, Count Hoppo was
laughing. Save for one man the whole court was laughing.

Count Rollo did not laugh. Feeling ashamed and miserable and angry, he
picked himself up and gave the Princess one reproachful look.

“I’m s-s-sorry,” gasped the Princess. “I didn’t mean to laugh, I don’t
know why I did laugh, I don’t want to laugh, I——” And then she trilled
and gurgled and choked, and choked and gurgled and trilled all over
again.

As soon as quiet was restored, the Chancellor stepped forward.

“Your Majesty,” he said, “on behalf of the young Count Hoppo I claim the
promised reward.”

“Count Hoppo?” said the King in surprise. “But it was just an accident,
was it not?”

“Not so, Your Majesty,” replied Hoppo proudly. And he explained how,
with great cleverness, he had prepared the butter-slide, and how, with
great cunning, he had arranged for Count Rollo to step upon it. And all
the court said “Well, well, well!” and “Fancy that now!” And the King
frowned and considered the matter.

“Ah, well, in that case,” said His Majesty, “you may fairly claim to
have succeeded in moving Her Royal Highness to laughter.” He turned to
Count Rollo. “You agree that that is so, Count Rollo?”

Rollo looked at the Princess, and she looked back at him, and their eyes
spoke to each other. As plainly as if she were speaking her eyes cried
to him, “Save me from this Count Hoppo!” And Rollo smiled back at her,
and gave a little nod, as if he would say, “It will be all right.” For
an idea had come to him.

So he turned like one in thought, and walked a few paces off. Then,
turning again to the King, he said:

“Your Majesty, since I have seen Her Royal Highness day by day, these
many days, I have dared to love her. If Your Majesty says now that she
is to be wedded to the Count Hoppo, it will be better that I should not
look upon her again. But I venture to make this last request of her. I
ask her to come down from her throne and say farewell to me here, not as
Princess to subject, but as maid to man.”

And he went down on one knee and waited for her.

“I will come,” said the Princess softly.

So she came to him; and, as she drew near, she stepped suddenly upon the
butter-slide, and her feet shot into the air, and she fell heavily upon
the floor.

There was a cry of anger from the Princess, followed by a cry of horror
from all the court. A dozen hands helped her to her feet, and supported
her again to the side of her father, the King, who comforted her with
royal words.

“But this is passing strange!” said Rollo loudly. “How comes it that Her
Royal Highness does not laugh? Was it not a funny jest?”

The King looked at him with sudden suspicion.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a princess wearing a
long, flowing dress and a patterned cap, standing with one hand raised
to her chest and looking upward.]

“What does this mean?” said the King sternly. “This talk of saying
farewell, was it just a plot to make Her Royal Highness fall?”

“Certainly, Your Majesty,” said Rollo pleasantly. “I hoped that Count
Hoppo’s delightful jest would amuse us all again.” He turned to the
Princess. “Was Your Royal Highness not amused?”

“We were not amused,” said Her Royal Highness coldly.

“Certainly not,” said the King with dignity.

“But Your Royal Highness was amused before,” said Rollo gently.

“That was altogether different,” said the King.

Then Count Rollo smiled at them all. “In that case, Your Majesty, it was
not Count Hoppo’s butter-slide which made Her Royal Highness laugh, but
my extremely humorous way of kicking my feet in the air and falling to
the ground.”

Then it was the turn of the Princess to smile, as she saw what Rollo
would be at.

[Illustration: _Dropped twenty feet below, splash! into the royal fish
pond!_]

“Why, yes,” said the King in surprise. “That had not occurred to me, but
indeed it would seem to be so. What do you say, my dear?”

“Of course it is, father!” said the Princess quickly. “It was Count
Rollo who made me laugh, not Count Hoppo.”

But now Count Hoppo, seeing how things were going against him, cried out
suddenly:

“It _was_ me, Your Majesty, it _was_ me! If _I_ had fallen down, _I_
would have made the Princess laugh, just as loudly as Count Rollo did!
I’ll make her laugh now! Watch me!” And with these words, he ran across
the butter-slide so swiftly that his feet leapt in the air, and he shot
heavily through the window and dropped twenty feet below, _splash!_ into
the royal fishpond!

“How vulgar!” said the Princess, with her nose in the air.

So young Count Rollo married the Princess, and they lived and loved and
laughed happily together for more years than I can remember. But what
happened to Count Hoppo when they had dragged him out of the fishpond, I
never heard; nor, indeed, does it matter very much.

 “WISH TO GOODNESS!” _By LAURENCE HOUSMAN_


In spite of his name, Mr. Peter Comely was a very ordinary-looking
person; and but for one initial circumstance his life had not contained
any event worth recording. The initial circumstance took place upon the
day of his birth: though the son of poor but honest parents, he managed
by accident to get a fairy-godmother.

This is how it came about. His mother, having previously had eleven
daughters, in pairs or in triplets, remarked at the moment when she was
most expecting him: “Well, I hope to gracious goodness that this time
they’ll bring a boy.”

Now it so happened that the fairy Gracious Goodness was passing by at
the time—out of employment and looking for something to do; and hearing
her name thus invoked, she stopped and gave the matter her attention.

Peter Comely was born a boy, and without accompaniments, as his mother
had desired; and the fairy, having seen him into the world, in order
that she might know him again when necessary, made a neat finish of the
matter by appearing before the eyes of his astonished parents, to whom
she announced her name and calling. Then, having bestowed upon the child
a birthday gift suitable to his station in life, she vanished into thin
air, and seemed thenceforth to have washed her fairy hands of him.

The gift she left to him, though to his parents it seemed humdrum and
useless, to Peter himself was quite satisfactory. She gave him
contentment; and so through life contented he remained though poor, and
therefore—in spite of the efforts of his parents to sow in him the seeds
of a criminal ambition—honest, meek, unassuming, and to most people
rather uninteresting.

As he grew up, having few wants, he had also few ideas; and though upon
each of his birthdays, unknown to his parents, his fairy-godmother paid
him a private visit to ask whether he wanted for anything, his answer
always was that he wanted nothing; nor did he even trouble to tell
anybody of the fairy’s visits, or give a thought himself to the
brilliant bargains he might have got out of life from the offers made to
him.

So when he came to man’s estate, though he might have had anything for
the asking, he turned to and worked for a weaver’s wage, as his father
had occasionally done before him, and lived as all other poor men have
to do—with only this unfortunate difference, that he remained quite
contented with his lot; and though he worked well, and was worth much
more than his master paid him, he never asked for a rise; which, while
it mattered little to him, was bad for his master.

That, however, is only by the way; but I don’t want any of my readers to
think that contentment is everything, or good under all circumstances.
Disturbance is sometimes far better: water, in a kettle that doesn’t
boil over, makes bad tea.

Now one day—one of his birthdays—a certain young person of the village,
who had a sharp eye for the main chance, happened to look in and see him
over his loom, talking to a beautiful lady with wings; and all of a
sudden it came to her that a certain queer story she had heard told by
his disreputable parents, and had never believed, was true after all;
and that poor simple Peter Comely really had a fairy-godmother. It
seemed also—and this made the discovery more valuable—that the fairy
still took an interest in him.

Instantly she made up her mind to marry him; and Peter, though he would
have been quite content to remain a bachelor, was equally content when,
by the various ways available to that end, the young woman managed to
become first “_his_ young woman,” as the saying goes, and then his wife.

Having got him, Mrs. Comely lost no time in tackling him on the subject
of his fairy birthright. Peter was quite simple and open about it; and
his wife discovered that, having been given a contented nature from his
birth, he had made no further use of it in spite of the opportunities
which the fairy’s repeated visits afforded him.

“I wish to goodness you’d told me sooner!” was her complaining remark,
when she found that it was only on his birthday that the fairy visited
him. For now there was nearly a year to run; and with the knowledge that
her married circumstances could so easily have been altered for the
better, had only the right remedy been applied at the start, Mrs. Comely
became deeply discontented with her surroundings, with Peter’s wages as
a weaver, and with Peter himself as a man. In fact, there was nothing
now that pleased her; and what pleased her least of all was her
husband’s contentment with things as they were.

The consequence was she was always in a bad temper; and, as is the way
with people when they are far from a state of goodness themselves, “Wish
to goodness!” became the daily expression of her impatience against one
who took everything so patiently. But nothing she could do—though she
tried her best—ever made him discontented.

[Illustration: Color illustration featuring a long-haired woman in a red
gown holding a thin wand, standing in an indoor workshop with a seated
person in a blue tunic working near spools of thread and a loom.]

Behind her back, friends—who saw what sort of a wife he had become
saddled with—would come to condole. “Your wife’s a bit of a handful,
isn’t she?” one would say. And “Oh, aye!” Peter would answer; “a nice
handful she is, for sure—or would be, if you tried to hold her. But let
her go her own way, it’s no odds to me.”

“Hard to please, isn’t she? I’ve been told so.”

“I’ve never seen anything please her yet,” said Peter, smiling, “except
to tell me what a fool she thinks me! And put it that way, you might say
that I do manage always to please her. Well, if that suits her, it suits
me all right; gives her something to talk about, and me something to
listen to that I can understand. For I’m not clever, you know, and if
I’d any doubt about it myself, I’ve got her word for it.”

“Peter, you are too easy,” said his friend. “You’ve got your senses like
all the rest of us. The mistake you make is to think too little of
yourself. The fact is your wife has got a bad temper.”

“Oh yes,” said Peter, “her temper’s bad, I know. But it’s beautifully
bad; it suits her.”

And just then Mrs. Peter put her head in at the door, and cried: “Wish
to goodness, you wouldn’t have lazy giddygaddies coming in talking and
wasting your time all day, when you ought to be at work. Goodness
gracious! I might as well have married a turnstile, the way you let folk
in for the asking, as ought to be stopped if they don’t know any better
themselves, coming in work-time.”

Before she had finished her commentary, Peter’s friend had slunk away,
having had enough of it.

But Peter only said: “We were talking about you, my dear.”

“And what were you saying about me, I’d like to know?”

“I was saying that whatever you did suited you.”

“Did? Suited me? Like what?”

“Well, for instance—losing your temper.”

“If I ever lost my temper with a fool like you, there’d be no finding it
again.”

“No, my dear; but you never do. When you are with me you are always _in_
a temper.”

“And reason enough, too!”

“Yes, my dear; I’ve never denied it.”

“Better not. If you did there’d be trouble.”

“Yes, my dear; and I don’t like giving trouble, you see.”

“_Giving_, indeed!” she cried; “it’s _I_ have to pay for it!” and off
she flounced. Try as she would, she could never get at him. Peter always
remained amiable and contented, and also—which is what tried her
most—slightly amused. Whatever she did to make him do otherwise, Peter
always appreciated her qualities at their full worth. Once, losing all
patience with him, she gave him quite a hard knock over the head with a
rolling-pin.

Peter looked at her with admiration.

“Now I should never have thought you could hit me as hard as that,” he
said—“a little woman like you. I wonder if I could hit you as hard?”

“You’d better not try!” she retorted.

“No, my dear: _you_ mightn’t like it,” explained Peter, smiling, as he
put court sticking-plaster on his forehead, tied a piece of string round
it to keep it in place, and stuck it with sealing-wax for a neat finish.

Mrs. Comely was beaten, and knew it. “I don’t know what’s the matter
with him!” she would say to herself sometimes. “It’s not human; it’s not
reasonable. It’s that fairy of his has gone and got into his blood, and
made him into something that’s more like a ball of wool or a piece of
putty than a man. And I wish to goodness his birthday would come round
quick, so that I could put things right for us!”

Poor woman! during that time of waiting she was sadly tried. But at last
his birthday did come round again.

Mrs. Comely was full of expectation and hope. She was almost nice to him
on the morning of the day, feeling her cares so nearly at an end; for,
of course, now, being his wife and upon the spot, she meant to do the
wishing, and had instructed him accordingly. When the fairy came he was
to be sure to call for her before doing anything.

But it so happened that Peter, being short of yarn for his weaving, had
to go out and buy more.

Mrs. Comely let him go, for she could not bear to see him not working;
but she called after him to be quick home again, being afraid that the
fairy might come while he was out.

Unfortunately he met the fairy before he had got back from his errand.
Just as he was coming through the wood, she slid down the tree on which
she had been waiting for him, and standing before him, radiant with
gracious goodness, cried: “Many happy returns of the day, Peter. Is
there anything you want?”

Peter stood considering: “No,” he said, “there’s nothing I want for
myself; but my wife wants me to be quick home again.”

And scarcely had he said it, when home he was, and the fairy—having done
her duty by him—nowhere to be seen for the whole of another year.

[Illustration: “... _My wife wants me to be quick home again_,”]

[Illustration: _and scarcely had he said it, when home he was._]

“Gracious goodness! you have been quick!” cried his wife, as he came in.
“I’ll say that for you.”

“Yes, I have rather,” said Peter, as he moved off to his work. Then he
bethought him, and stopped to explain. “You see, it happened like this:
while I was out I met godmother. She said, Did I want anything? I said
_I_ didn’t, but you wanted me quick home again. So here I am. Very kind
of her, wasn’t it?”

What Mrs. Comely said then, cannot be set down upon the printed page.
And what she continued to say, for a whole year whenever she thought of
it, was so similar that the only thing one can do in decency is to leave
it at that, and let the year go by unrecorded till it bring us to the
morning of Peter’s next birthday.

Peter was in bed with a cold, an extremely bad cold—the sort of cold
that is called double pneumonia, which, though it sounds like the name
of a flower, is really something much more ugly and dangerous and hard
to get rid of. And only the night before, the Doctor had come and told
Mrs. Comely that he was very much afraid Peter was going to die.

Mrs. Comely was much put out by the news. “Can’t you make him live just
for another day, Doctor?” she enquired complainingly; and the Doctor
said he would do his best.

“You see, he hasn’t made his will,” said Mrs. Comely by way of
explanation, not wishing to let more be known about her actual
expectations than was necessary.

To Peter, also, she said much the same thing. “I do really think,” she
exclaimed, “after all I’ve had to put up with, marrying you, and only
finding out after what a fool you are, that you might at least do that
for me—keep alive till your godmother has come to make it more worth
while to me than it’s ever been yet.”

[Illustration: _The Doctor thought he would die...._]

“What exactly is it you want this time?” enquired Peter. “You’d better
let me know so that I don’t make any mistake.”

“Well, I want you to keep alive,” she replied; “you do that, and I’ll
see to the rest.”

And having him there in bed, and she being herself on the spot to answer
the door, she made sure that this time at all events the situation was
fool-proof, and that all would be right.

But things did not happen quite as she had expected, she not being
accustomed to the ways of fairies, who can, if they like, be quite
independent of such gradual methods of approach as front-doors with
door-mats, and door-bells with some one to answer them; a fairy who
really knows her job is right in the middle of things before you know
that she is there at all.

And so, when Mrs. Comely went down to make some beef-tea for her
husband, the fairy arrived, this time not down a tree but down the
chimney; and very sorry to find him so ill, wished Peter much happier
returns of the day than could be said of the present one. “And what,”
she then enquired, “can I do for you?”

Peter said he didn’t know that there was anything she could do for him.
He had got double pneumonia, and had got it so well that the Doctor—so
his wife told him—thought he would die of it.

“And I don’t know,” he added, “whether she wants me not to die of it.
All she has said she does want is that I may live for another day.”

“You shall live for another day,” said the fairy, “and for many another
day too. Here’s life to you! Take your own time, and get well! A year
hence I will come again and see how you are doing.”

Scarcely had the fairy departed when Mrs. Comely returned with the
beef-tea. Her husband, though with the fairy’s word for it he might now
be said to be on the mend, showed so little sign of a change for the
better that she would not have noticed it, had he not himself told her.

“You have got your wish, my dear,” he said. “I am not going to die
to-day. She has just been and promised that I shan’t—that I am going to
get well.”

Mrs. Peter was so amazed and so angry to think that she had again missed
meeting the fairy through her husband’s stupidity, that with a flounce
and a bounce she upset the soup over him.

“Well, that’s an easy way of taking it, isn’t it?” he remarked, as he
watched it slide over the bed-clothes in a large shifting pattern of
brownish yellow. “I suppose if you leave it it’ll soak in all right.”

“It’s my belief,” snapped his wife, ignoring the discomfort she had
caused him, “that you haven’t got a fairy godmother at all, and that
what I saw was only a young woman out of the circus, and one no better
than she should be—with wings stuck on for her to act with; and that
you’ve been deceiving me all this time!”

“Very well, my dear,” replied Peter, “believe what you like to believe,
if it makes you happier. But you’ll have to believe your eyes presently,
when you see me get well. Only yesterday, you told me, the Doctor said I
was dying.”

“Doctors make mistakes,” she retorted. “I almost wish you were going to
die!”

“Now what a pity,” said Peter, “that I didn’t know that sooner, and then
I might have asked for it. But what you told me, you know, my dear, was
just the contrary. You wanted me to live, you said.”

“Only a day!” said his wife. “And now I’ve got to have another year of
you, and then perhaps find out that it’s all a lie, and that
fairy-godmother of yours just straw and moonshine, like all the rest of
you. Goodness gracious! I wish I’d never married you!”

She had now got it so fixed in her head that he had only been deceiving
her, that she said the same thing several times—most days, in
fact—during the year. And especially she would say it whenever he was in
a state not of doing anything that she could find fault with, but just
being himself. It was his quiet, contented character which most
infuriated her. For, as she said to herself when she considered him in
this sure setting which Heaven had bestowed on him: “There’s no getting
at him! He’s so made you’ve just got to lump it or leave it.”

So when for the third time his birthday came round, and Mrs. Comely,
waking as the alarm-clock went off, sat up in bed and looked at him
still peacefully sleeping, in spite of the racket which her friend the
alarm-clock was making—so like herself in its way, yet only doing its
duty—as she sat up and looked at him lying there undisturbed by the
noise, all her stored-up resentment against him broke out once more,
and, “Goodness gracious!” she cried, “I wish I weren’t married to him!”

Now it so happened that on this particular birthday Goodness Gracious
had come early, having a good many other things in the day that she
wanted to do. So there she was already in the room when the alarm-clock
sounded, sitting on the foot of the bed where Mrs. Comely’s hot-water
bottle had not yet got cold, and Peter’s feet, which had no hot-water
bottle to comfort them, were sticking out from under the coverlet.

So sitting there, unnoticed as yet by Mrs. Comely, who was too much
occupied looking at her husband and hating him, she gave a sharp pinch
to one of Peter’s toes which made him wake up.

“Yes, yes, my dear!” said Peter, thinking it was his wife giving him his
birthday greeting. “The same to you, and more of them!”

“Peter,” said the fairy—and Mrs. Peter, starting up, stared at her in
sudden hope and astonishment—“Peter, your wife says she wishes she
wasn’t married to you. Do you wish it too?”

“I’ve no wish in the matter for myself,” said Peter. “It’s not for me to
decide. But if she wishes it, then I do.”

“Then you shall have your wish, both of you,” said the fairy, while Mrs.
Comely, seeing too late the mistake she had made, started screaming and
protesting that that was not the thing she wished most, or first, or
indeed at all, if the fairy would only wait and let her think a minute.

But fairies don’t wait, or only when it suits them in order to drive
home a moral. And here the fairy was going to drive home a moral simply
by not waiting—having it already up her sleeve, as you shall now hear.

“Very well, Mrs. Comely,” she said; “then as you wish it you shall not
be married to him any longer. And as there are only three ways in which
that can possibly be brought about I now give you your choice of them.
Either he must die, or you must die, or you must have been married to
somebody else before you married him, somebody about whom you may have
entirely forgotten.”

“I never was married to anybody else!” exclaimed Mrs. Peter, fastening
upon the last alternative with great indignation. “Of what wickedness
are you accusing me?”

“Of none,” said the Fairy; “as I say, you may have entirely forgotten
it.”

But this did not appease Mrs. Peter. “I’m not likely to forget a thing
like that,” she protested. “I’m no fool like him; and if you think so,
you’re the same, and nothing to choose between you! Goodness gracious!
if you haven’t more sense than that, and you a fairy, I wish I’d never
set waking eyes on you!”

“Have your wish!” said the fairy. “You never have.” And so saying, she
vanished.

Mrs. Peter rubbed her eyes. It was hard to believe, but it was true. The
meeting with the fairy had been a dream. And now she was gone—or the
dream had gone—and there was nothing to be done for another year.

Mrs. Comely turned in bewilderment to her husband. “I’ve been having a
dream,” she said.

“Have you, my dear?” replied Peter cautiously. “I hope it has done you
good. What was it about?”

“It was about that fairy of yours. I don’t believe in her. She’s only a
dream. I had my suspicions about it before; and now I know.”

“Very well then, my dear, we must do without,” said Peter. “It’s much
easier to be contented with life if you don’t expect anything. Your
believing that I had a fairy-godmother who could do things for me has
made you discontented. Now you’ll feel better.”

Mrs. Peter would not admit it; but as a matter of fact it did. Having
nothing miraculous to look forward to in her married life, she became
resigned to it; and for a year was just an ordinary wife as other
people’s wives are, only complaining of Peter twice as often as she had
cause, which was not often, he being such a very easily contented and
accommodating character.

And on Peter’s next birthday, having no curiosity or expectation left
about the fairy, whom she no longer believed in, she left Peter, without
instructions, to see the fairy alone. And alone he was when the fairy
called on him.

“Well, Peter,” said the fairy, “is there anything you want this time?”

“Not that _I_ want,” said Peter; “but last year you gave my wife a
disappointment. She doesn’t believe in you any more, she thinks you are
only a dream. She’s been very good hiding it; but I can’t help thinking
she feels it.”

“Well, what do you want for her?” enquired the fairy.

“If it isn’t too much to ask,” said Peter, “and if it isn’t too much
trouble, can’t you make her contented like you’ve made me?”

“Of course I can,” said the fairy. “Why didn’t you ask that before.”

Peter thought awhile.

“She didn’t want it,” he said. “She’d got other ideas. Now that she’s
got none left, I can think for her.”

“Very well,” said Goodness Gracious, “then from this moment your wife
shall be as contented as you are.”

And just then, from outside came Mrs. Peter’s voice calling to him, as
she mopped the kitchen floor.

“Goodness gracious, Peter, who’s that you’re talking to?”

The question embarrassed him. He felt it was so much better to let
sleeping dogs lie.

“Never you mind!” he said roguishly.

“Oh, all right!” replied his wife, and went on with her mopping.

“There!” said the fairy.

“Goodness Gracious!” cried Peter. “_You’ve done it!_”

 THE COLOURED LANDS _By G. K. CHESTERTON_


Once upon a time there was a little boy whose name was Tommy. As a
matter of fact, his name was Tobias Theodore; the former because it was
an old name in the family, and the latter because it was an entirely new
name in the neighbourhood. It is to be hoped that the parents who called
him Tobias Theodore, moved by a natural desire to keep it quiet, agreed
to call him Tommy; and anyhow we will agree to call him Tommy. It is
always assumed in stories that Tommy is a common name for a boy; just as
it is always assumed that Tomkins is a common name for a man. I do not
really know very many boys named Tommy. I do not know any man named
Tomkins. Do you? Does anybody? But this enquiry would lead us far.

Anyhow, Tommy was sitting one very hot afternoon on a green lawn outside
the cottage that his father and mother had taken in the country. The
cottage had a bare white-washed wall; and at that moment it seemed to
Tommy very bare. The summer sky was of a blank blue, which at that
moment seemed to him very blank. The dull yellow thatch looked very dull
and rather dusty; and the row of flower-pots in front of him, with red
flowers in them, looked irritatingly straight, so that he wanted to
knock some of them over like ninepins. Even the grass around him moved
him only to pluck it up in a vicious way; almost as if he were wicked
enough to wish it was his sister’s hair. Only he had no sister and,
indeed, no brothers. He was an only child and at that moment rather a
lonely child, which is not necessarily the same thing. For Tommy, on
that hot and empty afternoon, was in that state of mind in which
grown-up people go away and write books about their view of the whole
world, and stories about what it is like to be married, and plays about
the important problems of modern times. Tommy, being only ten years old,
was not able to do harm on this large and handsome scale. So he
continued to pull out the grass like the green hair of an imaginary and
irritating sister, when he was surprised to hear a stir and a step
behind him, on the side of the garden far away from the garden gate.

He saw walking towards him a rather strange-looking young man wearing
blue spectacles. He was clad in a suit of such very light grey that it
looked almost white in the strong sunlight; and he had long loose hair
of such very light or faint yellow that the hair might almost have been
white as well as the clothes. He had a large limp straw hat to shade him
from the sun; and, presumably for the same purpose, he flourished in his
left hand a Japanese parasol of a bright peacock green. Tommy had no
idea of how he had come on to that side of the garden; but it appeared
most probable that he had jumped over the hedge.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a person wearing glasses
and a jacket, holding an open umbrella overhead as their hat blows off
in the wind, with a dense hedge in the background.]

All that he said was, with the most casual and familiar accent, “Got the
blues?”

Tommy did not answer and perhaps did not understand; but the strange
young man proceeded with great composure to take off his blue
spectacles.

“Blue spectacles are a queer cure for the blues,” he said cheerfully;
“but you just look through these for a minute.”

Tommy was moved to a mild curiosity and peered through the glasses;
there certainly was something weird and quaint about the discoloration
of everything—the red roses black and the white wall blue, and the grass
a bluish green like the plumes of a peacock.

“Looks like a new world, doesn’t it?” said the stranger. “Wouldn’t you
like to go wandering in a blue world once in a blue moon?”

“Yes,” said Tommy, and put the spectacles down with a rather puzzled
air. Then his expression changed to surprise; for the extraordinary
young man had put on another pair of spectacles, and this time they were
red.

“Try these,” he said affably. “These, I suppose, are revolutionary
glasses. Some people call it looking through rose-coloured spectacles.
Others call it seeing red.”

Tommy tried the spectacles, and was quite startled by the effect; it
looked as if the whole world were on fire. The sky was of a glowing or
rather glaring purple, and the roses were not so much red as red-hot. He
took off the glasses almost in alarm, only to note that the young man’s
immovable countenance was now adorned with yellow spectacles. By the
time that these had been followed by green spectacles, Tommy thought he
had been looking at four totally different landscapes.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a person leaning backward
while holding an open umbrella and hat in one hand, reaching down to
touch a young boy sitting on a grassy slope.]

“And so,” said the young man, “you would like to travel in a country of
your favourite colour. I did it once myself.”

Tommy was staring up at him with round eyes.

“Who are you?” he asked suddenly.

“I’m not sure,” replied the other. “I rather think I am your long-lost
brother.”

“But I haven’t got a brother,” objected Tommy.

“It only shows how very long-lost I was,” replied his remarkable
relative. “But I assure you that, before they managed to long-lose me, I
used to live in this house myself.”

“When you were a little boy like me?” asked Tommy with some reviving
interest.

“Yes,” said the stranger gravely. “When I was a little boy and very like
you. I also used to sit on the grass and wonder what to do with myself.
I also got tired of the blank white wall. I also got tired even of the
beautiful blue sky. I also thought the thatch was just thatch and wished
the roses did not stand in a row.”

“Why, how do you know I felt like that?” asked the little boy, who was
rather frightened.

“Why, because I felt like that myself,” said the other with a smile.

Then after a pause he went on:

“And I also thought that everything might look different if the colours
were different; if I could wander about on blue roads between blue
fields and go on wandering till all was blue. And a wizard who was a
friend of mine actually granted my wish; and I found myself walking in
forests of great blue flowers like gigantic lupins and larkspurs, with
only glimpses now and then of pale blue skies over a dark blue sea. The
trees were inhabited by blue jays and bright blue kingfishers.
Unfortunately they were also inhabited by blue baboons.”

“Were there any people in that country?” enquired Tommy.

The traveller had to reflect for a moment; then he nodded and said:

“Yes; but, of course, wherever there are people there are troubles. You
couldn’t expect all the people in the Blue Country to get on with each
other very well. Naturally there was a crack regiment called the
Prussian Blues. Unfortunately there was also a very energetic semi-naval
brigade called French Ultramarines. You can imagine the consequence.” He
paused again for a moment and then said: “I met one person who made
rather an impression on me. I came upon him in a place of great gardens
shaped in a crescent like the moon, and in the centre above a fringe of
blue-gum trees there rose a great blue lustrous dome, like the Mosque of
Omar. And I heard a great and terrible voice that seemed to toss the
trees to and fro, and there came out between them a tremendously tall
man, with a crown of huge sapphires round his turban, and his beard was
quite blue. I need not explain that he was Bluebeard.”

“You must have been frightened,” said the little boy.

“At first perhaps,” replied the stranger, “but I came to the conclusion
that Bluebeard is not so black—or perhaps so blue—as he is painted. I
had a little confidential talk with him, and really there was something
to be said on his side of the case. Living where he did, he naturally
married wives who were all blue-stockings.”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a man wearing glasses
being blown through the air by strong wind, holding an umbrella in one
hand and losing his hat with the other.]

“What are blue-stockings?” asked Tommy.

“Naturally you don’t know,” replied the other; “if you did you would
sympathize more with Bluebeard. They were ladies who were always reading
books. They even read them aloud.”

“What sort of books were they?”

[Illustration: Black and white illustration featuring a giant figure
with an immense, wild beard and turban, towering over a smaller person
holding an umbrella, with a miniature figure reading in the background.]

“Blue books, of course,” replied the traveller. “They are the only kind
of book allowed there. That is why I decided to leave. With the
assistance of my friend the Wizard, I obtained a passport to cross the
frontier, which was a very vague and shadowy one, like the fine shade
between two tints of the rainbow. I only felt that I was passing over
peacockcoloured seas and meadows, and the world was growing greener and
greener, till I knew I was in a Green Country. You would think that was
more restful, and so it was, up to a point. The point was when I met the
celebrated Green Man, who has given his name for so many excellent
public houses. And then there is always a certain amount of limitation
in the work and trade of these beautiful harmonious landscapes. Have you
ever lived in a country where all the people were greengrocers? I think
not. After all, I asked myself, why should all grocers be green? I felt
myself longing to look at a yellow grocer. I saw rise up before me the
glowing image of a red grocer. It was just about this time that I
floated insensibly into the Yellow Country; but I did not stay there
very long. At first it was very splendid, a radiant scene of sunflowers
and golden crowns; but I soon found it was almost entirely filled with
Yellow Fever and the Yellow Press discussing the Yellow Peril. Of the
three I preferred the Yellow Fever; but I could not get any real peace
or happiness even out of that. So I faded through an orange haze until I
came to the Red Country, and it was there that I really found out the
truth of the matter.”

“What did you find out?” asked Tommy, who was beginning to listen much
more attentively.

[Illustration: Black and white illustration of a man standing with one
foot on an open, upturned umbrella on the grass, holding a tray with
food while reaching up toward a thatched roof, as a small boy looks on.]

“You may have heard,” said the young man, “a very vulgar expression
about painting the town red. It is more probable that you have heard the
same thought put in a more refined form by a very scholarly poet who
wrote about a rose-red city, half as old as time. Well, do you know, it
is a curious fact that in a rose-red city you cannot really see any
roses. Everything is a great deal too red. Your eyes are tired until it
might just as well all be brown. After I had been walking for ten
minutes on scarlet grass under a scarlet sky and scarlet trees, I called
out in a loud voice, ‘Oh, this is all a mistake.’ And the moment I had
said that the whole red vision vanished, and I found myself standing in
quite a different sort of place; and opposite me was my old friend the
Wizard, whose face and long rolling beard were all one sort of
colourless colour like ivory, but his eyes of a colourless blinding
brilliance like diamonds.

“‘Well,’ he said, ‘you don’t seem very easy to please. If you can’t put
up with any of these countries of any of these colours, you shall jolly
well make a country of your own.’

“And then I looked round me at the place to which he had brought me; and
a very curious place it was. It lay in great ranges of mountains, in
layers of different colours; and it looked something like sunset clouds
turned solid and something like those maps that mark geological soils
grown gigantic. And all along the terraces of the hills, they were
trenched and hollowed into great quarries; and I think I understood
without being told that this was the great original place from which all
the colours came, like the paint-box of creation. But the most curious
thing of all was that right in front of me there was a huge chasm in the
hills that opened into sheer blank daylight. At least, sometimes I
thought it was a blank and sometimes a sort of wall made of frozen light
or air and sometimes a sort of tank or tower of clear water; but anyhow
the curious thing about it was that if you splashed some of the coloured
earths upon it they remained where you had thrown them, as a bird hangs
in the air. And there the Wizard told me, rather impatiently, to make
what sort of world I liked for myself, for he was sick of my grumbling
at everything.

“So I set to work very carefully; first blocking in a great deal of
blue, because I thought it would throw up a sort of square of white in
the middle; and then I thought a fringe of a sort of dead gold would
look well along the top of the white; and I spilt some green at the
bottom of it. As for red, I had already found out the secret about red.
You have to have a very little of it to make a lot of it. So I just made
a row of little blobs of bright red on the white just above the green;
and as I went on working at the details, I slowly discovered what I was
doing; which is what very few people ever discover in this world. I
found I had put back, bit by bit, the whole of that picture over there
in front of us. I had made that white cottage with the thatch and that
summer sky behind it and that green lawn below, and the row of the red
flowers just as you see them now. That is how they come to be there. I
thought you might be interested to know it.”

 EVENSONG _By KATHARINE TYNAN_


 When sweet day is over
 I creep into bed
 Under my rosy cover,
 My prayers all said.

 My nice bath taken,
 I lie cool and sweet,
 Wrapped in sweet-smelling linen
 From head to feet.

 I watch Nanna’s candle
 Through a chink in the door,—
 Nanna, sitting and sewing,
 Her shadow on the floor.

 The Moon comes to see me
 And the Evening Star;
 All night long the Corncrake
 Croaks near and far.

 I think I’ve been sleeping
 Just a minute or two
 Before Cuckoo shouts beside me—
 Wake up! Cuckoo!
