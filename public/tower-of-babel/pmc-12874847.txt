Identifying determinants of unmet healthcare needs in South Korea: a comparison of machine learning approaches for precision public health
Authors: Young Gyu Kwon, Seokmin Ji, Dongwoo Huh, Mankyu Choi, Munjae Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite a comprehensive national health insurance system in South Korea, achieving universal healthcare access remains challenging. Unmet healthcare needs reflect a confluence of individual-level barriers and broader structural constraints. This study aimed to identify and compare the most influential factors determining unmet healthcare needs using multiple machine learning techniques.MethodsThis study included 1,489,560 adults aged ≥ 20 who participated in the Community Health Survey between 2016 and 2022. The variables were classified into predisposing, enabling, and need factors according to Andersen’s behavioral model. Five machine learning algorithms—Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost— were used to predict unmet healthcare needs. The model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score metrics alongside confusion matrices, with special emphasis on minimizing false negatives.ResultsLightGBM exhibited the highest accuracy (0.9222) with superior recall (0.7802) and F1-score (0.7772), demonstrating optimal performance in identifying individuals with unmet healthcare needs. Age, household income, subjective health status, frequency of alcohol consumption, education level, employment status, and walking frequency were found to be the most influential predictors of unmet healthcare needs.ConclusionsThe findings provide data-driven insights that enable policymakers to develop tailored interventions addressing the needs of vulnerable populations. This machine learning framework facilitates precision public health by reducing unmet healthcare needs, enhancing healthcare equity, and optimizing healthcare resource allocation.Keywords: Unmet healthcare needs, Machine learning, Andersen’s behavioral model, Health disparities, Public health policy, Precision public health

Introduction

Ensuring equitable healthcare access is a shared societal responsibility with significant economic and ethical implications [1–3]. In South Korea, the National Health Insurance program, which has progressively expanded since the late 1970 s, has substantially increased healthcare utilization and alleviated financial barriers [4]. Nevertheless, the healthcare needs of various demographic subgroups remain unmet, indicating that individual characteristics, such as socioeconomic status, health perceptions, and educational attainment, continue to critically influence healthcare access [5–7]. This underscores the ongoing challenges of achieving universal healthcare coverage.

Unmet healthcare needs, defined as necessary medical care not received (whether identified by patients or healthcare professionals), have been systematically examined since the 1970 s [8, 9]. Early investigations predominantly focused on individual-level socioeconomic and demographic determinants of foregone care [10–12]. Subsequent research has highlighted the substantial impact of contextual factors, such as community resources and cultural norms, on personal health perceptions and healthcare utilization [13]. Despite national policy interventions, the persistence of these needs suggests that individual barriers remain inadequately addressed.

Within this complex landscape, machine learning offers a potent analytical complement to conventional statistical techniques [14]. Advanced algorithms can reveal nonlinear interactions among demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral variables, enhancing the understanding of the multifaceted factors that contribute to unmet healthcare needs [15]. Although Andersen’s behavioral model has provided a conceptual foundation for analyzing healthcare utilization [12, 16], its integration with modern data mining approaches remains poorly explored in the context of Korean health system.

This study aimed to assess the primary determinants of unmet healthcare needs in South Korea by applying multiple machine learning models to large-scale individual-level data. These findings will support policymakers and healthcare providers formulate targeted, evidence-based interventions that reinforce the national health insurance system and promote equitable healthcare access across diverse population groups.

Methods

Study design and data source

This repeated cross-sectional study analyzed data from the Community Health Survey (CHS) collected between 2016 and 2022. The CHS, established in 2008, is conducted annually by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) to facilitate nationwide health planning and program evaluation. The CHS employs standardized protocols with trained interviewers conducting face-to-face surveys, implementing rigorous quality control procedures to minimize self-reporting biases [17]. The analytical framework (Fig. 1) comprised several sequential steps. First, following Andersen’s behavioral model [18], independent variables were classified into three categories: predisposing factors (gender, age, education level, marital status, household type, basic livelihood security status), enabling factors (household income, employment status, type of region), and need factors (subjective health status, chronic disease, depressive symptoms, health behaviors). The dependent variable was the presence or absence of unmet healthcare needs.

Study Workflow Diagram

In the preprocessing stage, nominal variables were transformed into binary indicator vectors via one-hot encoding and implemented feature selection to optimize data representation. The dataset was then split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets. Five machine learning algorithms were employed—Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost—to predict unmet healthcare needs. Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score) and confusion matrices, with particular emphasis on minimizing false negatives to avoid overlooking individuals with unmet needs. The best-performing model was selected for subsequent feature importance analysis to identify key determinants of unmet healthcare needs, thereby informing targeted interventions to reduce unmet needs and foster health equity.

Study participants

The CHS employs a rigorous two-stage probability proportional to size sampling strategy. In the first stage, 255 administrative districts were selected in proportion to their population size to ensure broad representativeness. In the second stage, households within each district were systematically sampled, targeting individuals aged ≥ 19 years. This dual-stage approach yielded nationally representative data with respect to demographic and geographic distributions.

Of the original 1,604,568 survey respondents, individuals aged < 20 years (n = 27,152) were excluded because they were below the legal adult age in South Korea. Subsequently, participants who did not respond or refused to answer survey questions (n = 87,856) were also excluded. This two-step exclusion process resulted in a final analytical sample of 1,489,560 adults (Fig. 2). All survey procedures followed standardized KDCA protocols, and the necessary approvals for data access and analysis were obtained.

Variables

The dependent variable was unmet healthcare needs, as determined by a “Yes” response to the question, “During the past year, have you needed (non-dental) medical care but did not receive it?” Meanwhile, a “No” response indicated successful receipt of required care.

Independent variables were selected based on Andersen’s behavioral model and previous studies [19] and were grouped into predisposing, enabling, and need factors. The predisposing factors included demographic and socioeconomic attributes (age, sex, educational level, marital status, household type, and basic livelihood security status). Enabling factors capture resource availability and situational elements (household income, employment status, and region). Need factors included health-related indicators (subjective health status, chronic diseases, depressive symptoms, smoking frequency, alcohol consumption frequency, influenza vaccination, walking frequency, and weight control). Machine learning methods and rationale

Five tree-based machine learning algorithms were employed and selected based on their theoretical foundations, computational characteristics, and relevance to healthcare predictive modeling (Table 2). Data preprocessing included transforming nominal variables into multiple binary indicator vectors via one-hot encoding to preserve categorical distinctions and enable algorithms to capture latent patterns more effectively [20].

Characteristics of machine learning algorithms

Decision Tree models were implemented as a baseline approach owing to their interpretability and ability to handle non-linear relationships without prior assumptions about data distribution [21, 22]. The transparent decision paths make them valuable in healthcare contexts where model explainability is critical for clinical adoption and trust. To address the inherent overfitting limitations of single trees, a Random Forest was utilized, which aggregates multiple Decision Trees trained on randomly selected dataset subsets [23]. This ensemble approach enhances model diversity, while its ability to process high-dimensional data without feature scaling made it well-suited for the diverse variable set spanning demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral factors. For advanced predictive performance, three gradient-boosting algorithms were implemented. XGBoost was selected for its iterative improvement of predictions by focusing on previously misclassified instances [24]. Its regularization features and optimization for computational efficiency make it particularly effective for the class imbalance present in our healthcare utilization dataset. LightGBM, optimized for speed and memory efficiency, employs a leaf-wise splitting strategy well-suited for high-dimensional data [25, 26]. For our nationwide dataset of 1.5 million individuals, we utilized default hyperparameters based on empirical evidence showing their robustness in large-scale healthcare applications. Probst et al. [27] demonstrated that default parameters often achieved near-optimal performance, while providing computational efficiency and reproducibility critical for healthcare implementations. Finally, CatBoost was included for its advanced handling of categorical variables through ordered boosting, which mitigates prediction shifts caused by target leakage [28]. This feature is especially valuable when analyzing temporal trends in healthcare access, although it potentially increases computational costs compared to other algorithms.

This diverse algorithm selection allowed us to comprehensively evaluate different machine learning approaches for predicting unmet healthcare needs while identifying the most suitable model for clinical application in resource allocation and intervention targeting.

Model evaluation

The performance of each model is quantified using multiple metrics to ensure a thorough and clinically relevant assessment. Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score serve as comprehensive indicators of model performance Accuracy quantifies the overall proportion of correctly classified cases (both positive and negative), precision measures the proportion of correctly identified individuals with unmet needs among all predicted cases, recall quantifies the model's ability to identify all individuals with actual unmet needs, and F1-score provides a balanced measure of precision and recall. For our large-scale nationwide health survey dataset, these comprehensive evaluation metrics provide statistically sound performance assessment without the computational burden of complex validation schemes, since empirical evidence indicates that substantial sample sizes enable direct performance metrics to yield reliable results comparable to intensive cross-validation approaches [29]. The confusion matrices were also examined to differentiate the distributions of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives. Considering the critical clinical implications of failing to identify individuals who require medical care, minimizing false negatives was prioritized.

Feature importance analysis

To quantify the relative influence of predictors on unmet healthcare needs, feature importance analysis was employed using the “gain” metric from tree-based models. This metric measures each variable’s contribution to accuracy improvement when used in splitting decisions. Feature importance analysis enhances the interpretability of complex ensemble models, providing insights into the relative impact of individual variables while preserving predictive advantages of sophisticated algorithms. In healthcare policy contexts, this approach offers practical utility by identifying high-priority factors for targeted interventions while maintaining scientific rigor [30, 31]. By ranking the resulting importance scores, the most influential determinants of unmet healthcare needs were identified, establishing an evidence-based foundation for potential policy recommendations and resource allocation decisions.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The proportion of individuals reporting unmet healthcare needs was decreased from 11.7% in 2016 to 5.8% in 2022.

The proportion of female respondents consistently exceeded that of male respondents by approximately 55%. The proportion of individuals aged ≥ 70 years was increased from 20.4 to 25.5%, whereas the proportion of those aged 40–49 years decreased from 18.1 to 14.6%. The proportion of respondents with elementary-level education or below was decreased from 24.8 to 21.8%, whereas the proportion of those with college-level education or above increased from 35.7 to 37.4%. Finally, one-generation households became more common, increasing from 43.5 to 53.4%.

Regarding enabling factors, although most households continued to earn under 1,000,000 KRW per month, the proportion of those earning ≥ 6,000,000 KRW was increased from 10.5 to 15.8%. Employment rates were decreased from 64.0%to 62.5%, and metropolitan residency marginally increased from 47.6 to 47.9%.

Regarding need factors, subjective health status rated as “fair” slightly declined from 42.5 to 41.8%, whereas the chronic disease rate substantially increased from 30.5 to 37.4%. The incidence of depressive symptoms was increased from 6.1 to 7.8%. Meanwhile, daily smoking rates were decreased from 16.9 to 14.3%, and complete abstinence from alcohol consumption increased from 32.3 to 39.8%. Influenza vaccination rates were increased from 48.6 to 57.8%, and weight maintenance behaviors increased from 14.1 to 18.4%. Discussion

This study explored the unmet healthcare needs through individual-level analyses by integrating multiple machine-learning algorithms within Andersen’s behavioral framework using CHS data from 2016 to 2022 to demonstrate the feasibility of large-scale data-driven research for informing healthcare policy. To overcome interpretational limitations of existing machine learning models [32], several technical enhancements were introduced, including one-hot encoding for nominal variables, along with the use of accuracy metrics and confusion matrices, to refine data representation and ensure a more robust evaluation. Among the five machine learning models evaluated, LightGBM and XGBoost achieved the highest accuracy. While both showed identical accuracy scores, the LightGBM model demonstrated superior performance in recall (0.7802) and F1-score (0.7772), excelling at eliminating false negatives, which is a critical feature in clinical settings where missed cases can cause severe health consequences. This finding aligns with existing literature, which highlights the superior accuracy and interpretability of advanced ensemble models for health-related predictions [33, 34].

Age, household income, subjective health status, frequency of alcohol consumption, education level, employment status, and walking frequency were critical predictors of unmet healthcare needs, which aligned with prior research indicating that older age, lower educational attainment, and reduced household income significantly elevated the risk of unmet healthcare needs, underscoring the effect of behavioral factors and demographic and economic barriers on access to essential medical services [35, 36]. Furthermore, poor subjective health status heightens individuals’ susceptibility to unmet care needs, underlining the central role of self-perceived health in healthcare-seeking behaviors [7, 37]. Alcohol consumption frequency has also emerged as a key predictor, suggesting underlying health or psychosocial concerns [38]. In contrast, regular exercise as well as avoiding smoking and high-risk drinking were correlated with a reduced likelihood of unmet healthcare needs, illustrating the importance of maintaining healthy behaviors [39]. Finally, employment status proved pivotal, since unemployed individuals faced more pronounced difficulties in obtaining necessary healthcare than their employed counterparts [40–42]. Notably, unmet healthcare needs decreased during the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic (from 6.1% in 2019 to 5.3% in 2021), contrasting with expected access deterioration. This paradoxical finding aligns with Korean studies showing that rapid telemedicine adoption improved access for older individuals and rural populations [43], while the National Health Insurance’s strategic fee adjustments, covering over 96% of COVID-19 testing costs and 80% of isolation-related expenses, effectively reduced financial barriers to care [44]. However, this decrease may reflect altered healthcare-seeking behaviors and changing perceptions of “necessary care” during lockdowns rather than genuine access improvements, highlighting the complexity of interpreting self-reported measures during health system disruptions.

The application of machine learning in this study underscores its ability to detect subtle and nonlinear relationships between various predictors, thereby facilitating the identification of high-risk subgroups with greater precision [45, 46]. By integrating these computational approaches with Andersen’s behavioral model, this study provides a refined framework for developing targeted interventions aimed at reducing unmet healthcare needs. This integration enhances predictive accuracy and deepens our understanding of the multifaceted determinants of healthcare access.

Therefore, data-driven strategies offer a pathway to further minimize unmet healthcare needs within existing national health programs in Korea. While foundational measures, such as universal coverage and basic financial support, remain indispensable, ensemble machine learning models enable more precise identification of high-risk communities and individuals. By incorporating the principles of precision public health, policymakers can target limited resources toward specific socioeconomic and behavioral barriers, such as unemployment, low income, and limited health literacy, which underlie unmet healthcare needs [47, 48].

In practice, the risk profiles produced by these models can guide community health centers in refining participant selection criteria for smoking cessation, alcohol reduction counseling, and physical activity programs. These predictive insights could be operationalized through standardized risk-scoring systems derived from the machine learning models to enable healthcare providers to quantify an individual’s probability of experiencing unmet healthcare needs. Such risk scores could facilitate evidence-based triage in community health settings, prioritizing outreach to high-risk individuals and tailoring intervention intensity according to predicted vulnerability levels. Similarly, telehealth services, personalized follow-ups, and enhanced chronic disease management can help narrow the gaps left by one-size-fits-all approaches. Evidence from international initiatives, such as Thailand’s Thai Health, underscores the efficacy of income-targeted subsidies and preventive interventions in addressing socioeconomic disparities [49]. Furthermore, advanced ensemble models, such as LightGBM, facilitate more accurate resource allocation, spanning financial investments, workforce deployment, and preventive services to populations and regions with greatest needs [50]. The translation of complex model outputs into actionable clinical decision support tools represents a critical next step for implementing these findings in real-world healthcare settings. Integrating these data-driven approaches into Korea’s policy framework will foster a more responsive, equitable, and resilient healthcare environment [51].

This study has some limitations. First, the 2016–2022 temporal scope may not capture long-term trends in unmet healthcare needs, necessitating extended timeframe analyses in future research. Second, while self-reported survey data introduces potential recall and social desirability biases [52], the concept of unmet needs inherently incorporates patient subjective experiences that cannot be fully captured through objective measures alone. Third, while ensemble machine learning models demonstrate strong performance, they present interpretability challenges and potential algorithmic bias that warrant further validation across demographic subgroups. Fourth, the methodological choices—using default hyperparameters [27] rather than formal cross-validation and threshold optimization instead of specialized class balancing—represent limitations despite their support in the literature for large-scale datasets with moderate imbalance [29]. Fifth, this analysis focused primarily on demographic and socioeconomic variables, excluding important factors such as healthcare quality and provider trust. Finally, the COVID-19 pandemic-related decrease in unmet needs during 2020–2021 remains inadequately explained [53] and would benefit from additional qualitative investigation.

Conclusions

Machine learning provides additional insights into the multifaceted factors contributing to unmet healthcare needs, enabling enhanced analytical precision with balanced performance metrics that complement conventional statistical approaches. By identifying significant determinants through ensemble models, this study contributes to the evidence base for tailored interventions and optimized resource allocation in healthcare systems. The integration of precision public health principles with these analytical methods can support the development of more equitable data-driven strategies. Further research should adopt multi-level approaches incorporating healthcare system factors and temporal dynamics, particularly pandemic-related changes in healthcare access patterns, to develop comprehensive frameworks for evidence-based policy decisions.
