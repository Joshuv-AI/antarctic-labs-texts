Tea and coffee consumption in relation to depression and anxiety symptoms: findings from the multicentric LIPOKAP study
Authors: Mohammad Matin Mahjourian, Ghazaleh Bahrami, Noushin Mohammadifard, Fahimeh Haghighatdoost, Farid Najafi, Hossein Farshidi, Masoud Lotfizadeh, Tooba Kazemi, Hamidreza Roohafza, Nizal Sarrafzadegan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Given the increasing mental health challenges and distinct drinking traditions in Iran, this study aimed to shed light on the association between tea and coffee consumption and depression and anxiety. In this cross-sectional study, 1994 adults were recruited via stratified multi‐stage cluster sampling across five major Iranian cities from February 2018 to July 2019. Tea and coffee consumption were assessed based on self-reported daily or weekly intake, with tea classified into three categories and coffee classified as non-consumers versus consumers. Depressive and anxiety symptoms were measured by the Iranian‐validated Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Logistic binary logistic regression models estimated odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for HADS‐defined depressive and anxiety symptoms, with stepwise adjustment for key confounders to minimize bias. No significant associations were observed between tea consumption and depressive or anxiety symptoms either in crude or adjusted models (p-trend > 0.05). In terms of coffee, crude analyses suggested an inverse association for depression (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.43–0.84; p = 0.003) and anxiety (OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.54–0.99; p = 0.045) in coffee consumers (≥ 1 cup/d) compared with non-consumers (< 1 cup/d). However, adjustment for potential confounders attenuated the association, while in the full-adjusted model coffee consumption tend to lower depression risk (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.49–1.01; p = 0.057), and no association was observed for anxiety. While tea consumption was not associated with depression and anxiety, coffee intake tends to lower depression symptoms. Prospective cohort studies are needed to confirm our results.

Introduction

Mental disorders, particularly depression and anxiety, significantly impact various dimensions of life, often disrupting daily functioning and overall well-being1. Depression is a prevalent and debilitating mental health disorder characterized by a persistently low mood, anhedonia, and a constellation of emotional, cognitive, and somatic symptoms. Globally, depression is estimated to affect approximately 5% of the population2,3, and more than 301 million people are estimated to experience mood or anxiety disorders at some point in their lives1. In Iran, the prevalence of depression is rising progressively and is evident across all age groups and diverse geographical regions4,5. Mental disorders have also contributed substantially to the national burden of disease, accounting for 10.3% of all disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2019, an increase from 4.6% in 19906. The rising prevalence of mental disorders has increased interest in identifying modifiable risk factors, particularly dietary factors, that may influence their development6,7.

Diet and nutrition are closely linked to mental health, with dietary changes having the potential to influence mental well-being7–11. Coffee and tea, as two of the most widely consumed beverages, have been a focus of recent investigations exploring their relationship with depression and mental health12, though findings are inconclusive. For instance, while several studies have reported an inverse association13–17, some others have either reported a positive or a null association18–21. These inconsistencies may stem from methodological differences, cultural variations in beverage consumption, or unmeasured confounding factors. Additionally, patterns of tea and coffee consumption, as well as their preparation, vary considerably across countries and are influenced by cultural, social, and economic factors. In Iran, during recent years, despite a notable shift towards higher coffee drinking compared to the last decades, coffee consumption is much lower than that of Western countries, and tea continues to be the most commonly consumed beverage among Iranians22. These factors, in turn, may lead to variations in the health outcomes of these beverages. To date, the majority of studies investigating the association between tea and coffee consumption and mental disorders have been conducted in Western or Southeast Asian populations, and research from the Middle East remains limited23,24. Furthermore, while previous studies have predominantly focused on either tea or coffee, our research examines the individual effects of both beverages on mental disorders14–17. To the best of our knowledge, no study in Iran has specifically investigated the relationship between tea consumption and psychological disorders. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the associations of tea and coffee intake with depression as well as anxiety within the Iranian population.

Materials and methods

Study population

This research was carried out as part of the Nowledge and Practice Of dyslipidemia prevention, management, and control (LIPOKAP) project, which focuses on the knowledge and practices related to the prevention, management, and control of dyslipidemia. The study was conducted across five major cities in Iran between February 2018 and July 2019. The sample included individuals from a diverse range of regions, including Birjand (east), Bandar Abbas (south), Kermanshah (west), and Isfahan and Shahrekord (central Iran). To determine the final sample size in each region, the total population size and the distribution of urban and rural areas within the cities were taken into account. Clusters were then randomly selected from existing healthcare centers. A specific sample size was allocated to each cluster based on its population distribution. Afterward, participants were randomly chosen and invited to take part in the study by trained interviewers. Finally, a total of 2456 adults (18≥) were selected through a stratified multi-stage random cluster sampling method. The exclusion criteria for this study included the presence of systemic diseases or conditions related to dyslipidemia. Additionally, individuals with chronic kidney disease, liver disorders, cancer, immune system abnormalities, or those who exhibited significant under- or overestimation of energy intake (< 800 Kcal/day or > 4200 Kcal/day) were excluded. These exclusion criteria were specifically applied to reduce the potential for reverse causality. Consequently, the final analytical sample consisted of 1994 eligible participants. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in writing prior to their inclusion in the study. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (Protocol No. IR.MUI.RC.1395.4.077). Sociodemographic information, such as age, gender, smoking status, and socioeconomic position (SES), was collected using a self-administered questionnaire. Further details on the study design, participants’ characteristics are provided elsewhere25,26. The assessment of physical activity was conducted using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), with activity levels quantified in metabolic equivalent hours per week (MET-h/week)27.

Dietary assessment

Dietary intake over the previous year was assessed using a validated, semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) consisting of 110 items28. Trained interviewers conducted face-to-face interviews to complete the FFQ. For each item, participants reported their usual intake based on standard portion sizes and a nine-point frequency scale ranging from ‘rarely or never’ to ‘more than six times per day’. Daily consumption (in grams) of each food item was calculated by multiplying the portion size by its corresponding frequency. Subsequently, total energy and nutrient intakes were estimated using the Nutritionist IV software, which had been modified to accommodate the nutrient composition of local Iranian foods. Habitual tea and coffee consumption over the past year was assessed through separate questions regarding the number of cups consumed per day or per week.

Assessment of mental health

Mental health status was evaluated using the Iranian-validated version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), a widely used instrument designed to screen for symptoms of anxiety and depression. This concise questionnaire consists of two distinct sections, each comprising seven items, with responses rated on a four-point Likert scale (0 to 3). Each subscale yields a total score ranging from 0 to 21, where higher scores reflect more severe symptomatology. Individuals with scores of 7 or lower were classified as having no symptoms of anxiety or depression, indicating a healthy mental state. Conversely, scores of 8 or above were interpreted as indicative of the presence of anxiety or depressive symptoms29.

Statistical analysis

Participants were stratified into three categories (< 1, 1 to < 2, and ≥ 2 cups/day) based on tea consumption, while coffee intake was categorized into two groups (non-consumers vs. consumers) due to its relatively low prevalence in the population. Baseline characteristics of participants were compared across tea intake categories and coffee consumption groups using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables (reported as mean ± standard deviation) and chi-square tests for categorical variables (reported as percentages). Differences in dietary intakes were evaluated using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with adjustment for age, sex, and total energy intake. For depression and anxiety outcomes, mean scores were initially compared using ANOVA in unadjusted models, followed by ANCOVA in adjusted models. Binary logistic regression was employed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between tea and coffee consumption and symptoms of depression and anxiety. Three sequential adjustment models were utilized: Model 1 was controlled for age, sex, energy intake, and city of residence. Model 2 was further adjusted for marital status, socioeconomic status, physical activity, and smoking status. In Model 3, dietary factors, including folate, cobalamin, iron, magnesium, pyridoxine, and zinc, were additionally adjusted. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics software (Version 26.0, IBM Corp.), and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Table 1 presents the general characteristics of participants across tea and coffee consumption categories. Compared to those in the lowest tea consumption group, participants in the highest category were generally older, more likely to be current smokers, and had lower educational attainment (less than 6 years), while also exhibiting lower socioeconomic status. Regarding coffee consumption, participants who consumed ≥ 1 cup/day were more physically active, were more likely to be current smokers, and had higher levels of education (more than 12 years) and socioeconomic status compared to non-consumers.

The dietary intakes of participants across different categories of tea and coffee consumption are presented in Table 2. Participants in the highest tea consumption category had significantly lower energy and fat intakes, as well as lower consumption of fish and seafood, compared to those in the lowest category. Regarding coffee, individuals who consumed ≥ 1 cup/day had significantly higher intakes of energy, fat, dietary fiber, fish and seafood, zinc, and cobalamin, and lower carbohydrate intake than non-consumers.

Table 3 presents the means and standard errors (SE) of depression and anxiety across categories of tea and coffee consumption. No statistically significant differences were observed in mean depression or anxiety scores across categories of tea intake, either in the crude or in the adjusted models (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). For coffee consumption, participants in the higher intake category exhibited significantly lower mean depression scores compared to those in the lower intake category in the crude model (non-consumers = 4.55 ± 0.09 vs. consumers = 3.83 ± 0.17; p = 0.001). However, this association was attenuated by adjustment for potential confounders, and remained no longer significant (p > 0.05). No significant associations were found between coffee intake and mean anxiety scores in any of the models (p > 0.05).

Table 4 summarizes odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for depressive and anxiety symptoms across categories of tea and coffee intake in crude and multivariable-adjusted models. No significant association was observed between tea consumption and either outcome in any of the models (Model III: OR 0.1.19; 95% CI 0.70–0.2.01; p-trend = 0.592). In the crude model, individuals in the higher category of coffee intake had 40% lower odds of depressive symptoms comapared with those in the lower category (OR 0.60; 95% CI 0.43–0.84; p-trend = 0.003). Adjustment for city, sex, and energy intake weakened this inverse association (OR 0.72; 95% CI 0.51–1.03; p-trend = 0.070), while further control for demographic and lifestyle factors led to a significant inverse association (OR 0.70; 95% CI 0.49–0.99; p-trend = 0.045). However, after additional adjustment for micronutrients related to mental health, higher coffee consumption tended to be associated with lower risk of depressive symptoms (OR 0.70; 95% CI 0.49–1.01; p-trend = 0.057). With respect to anxiety, higher coffee intake was associated with a 27% lower odds of anxiety symptoms in the crude model (OR 0.73; 95% CI 0.54–0.99; p-trend = 0.045), but no in the multivariable adjusted models (all adjusted models p-trend > 0.05).

Discussion

Our findings suggested a null association between tea consumption and depression and anxiety. However, higher coffee intake showed a tendency toward lower symptoms of depression compared with lower consumption after taking into account various confounders.

Although several studies have been conducted to assess the relationship between tea and coffee drinking and mental disorders, findings are inconsistent. In addition, considering variations in tea and coffee preparation and consumption, it would be worthwhile to explore their associations in different populations. To our knowledge, there are no studies on tea and limited data on coffee in relation to mental disorders in Iranians17,23,24. While our findings revealed no association between tea and depression and anxiety risk in line with several studies20,30, some others have suggested an inverse or even a positive association13,17,18,31. For example, in a 4-year cohort study conducted in Singapore, long-term tea consumption was associated with a significant reduction in depression rate31. However, the study was limited by a small number of cases and a lack of adjustment for multiple nutritional factors31. Similarly, a longitudinal study in China indicated a protective effect of coffee consumption on depressive symptoms; however, the adjusted models did not account for nutritional factors, which may explain some of the inconsistencies in previous research13. These discrepancies are also evident for coffee consumption. For instance, a cross-sectional study by Yu et al. reported a positive association between higher coffee consumption and depressive symptoms among women, whereas no significant relationship was found among men32. Consistently, another study identified a positive correlation between greater tea and coffee intake and higher depression scores; however, the authors did not adjust for dietary nutrient intake, leaving open the possibility that unmeasured nutritional factors may have confounded the results15. This detrimental association was also confirmed by a 5-day intervention trial in which higher coffee consumption was associated with elevated depressive symptoms33. In a Taiwanese cohort, while tea consumption appeared protective at baseline, this effect dissipated after eight years of follow-up, suggesting that initial associations may not be sustained over time34. These findings collectively imply that any potential mental health benefits of tea or coffee may be contingent upon contextual factors such as duration of exposure, stability of consumption habits, or residual or unmeasured confounding variables, rather than reflecting a straightforward protective or detrimental effect. Additionally, differences in population characteristics, including the prevalence of depression and anxiety or the mean age of participants, may partially explain the inconsistencies observed across studies.

The favorable association found for coffee and depression risk might be related to its caffeine content, as a major psychoactive compound that may influence mental health through multiple neurobiological pathways35. Caffeine primarily acts as a non-selective antagonist of adenosine receptors, particularly A1 and A2A subtypes, in the central nervous system, which stimulates the release of neurotransmitters such as dopamine and norepinephrine. This, in turn, enhances alertness, mood elevation, and cognitive performance35,36. Furthermore, higher levels of dopamine, as a neurotransmitter critically involved in reward, motivation, and mood regulation, may enhance mood and mitigate depressive symptoms per se37. However, the chronic and long-term consumption of caffeine induces physiological adaptations that may attenuate its initial psychoactive benefits38. Repeated caffeine exposure leads to the development of tolerance, characterized by upregulation of adenosine receptors—especially A1 receptors—and increased coupling efficiency between adenosine receptors and inhibitory G-proteins (Gi). These compensatory mechanisms enhance the brain’s sensitivity to adenosine in the absence of caffeine, ultimately diminishing the stimulant and mood-modulating effects of caffeine over time38–40. The development of caffeine tolerance may partially explain why long-term consumption of tea and coffee is not consistently associated with a lower risk of depression and anxiety in epidemiological studies. Moreover, genetic variability in caffeine metabolism represents an important biological factor that may influence the psychological effects of tea and coffee consumption41. Between-subjects genetic variations not only affect the rate at which caffeine is cleared from the body but also modulate the neuropsychological responses to its intake. Therefore, the heterogeneity in caffeine metabolism across individuals may attenuate or obscure the potential associations between tea and coffee consumption and mental health outcomes.

This study offers several distinct strengths. The recruitment of a relatively large, representative sample substantially enhances the external validity and generalizability of our findings to the wider Iranian population. Furthermore, the comprehensive adjustment for a diverse range of potential confounding variables in the statistical analyses minimizes the risk of bias and ensures more reliable associations. The implementation of the validated FFQ was another strength of this study, enabling accurate assessment of micronutrient intakes and allowing for their adjustment in the analysis to better control confounders and clarify the association between exposure and outcome. Despite these strengths, certain limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional nature of this study inherently limits causal inference, precluding definitive conclusions regarding the directionality of the relationships between coffee and tea consumption and mental health outcomes such as depression and anxiety. Therefore, future prospective cohort studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are essential to establish temporality and causality. The exclusion of participants with chronic conditions, including dyslipidemia, may have led to a healthier study population, which could reduce the generalizability of the findings to individuals with existing metabolic or chronic diseases. Although this approach was intended to minimize potential reverse causation, it may have introduced some restriction in external validity. Additionally, reliance on self-reported measures for both dietary intake and psychological status introduces the potential for recall bias. Another important limitation is the lack of detailed assessment regarding the specific types and quality of tea and coffee consumed, factors that may independently influence mental health outcomes. Finally, the relatively low prevalence of coffee consumption among the study participants could have limited the statistical power of analyses related to coffee intake.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while tea consumption showed no significant association with symptoms of depression and anxiety, higher coffee intake tended to be associated with lower symptoms of depression after adjustment for potential confounders. Further research is warranted to explore potential underlying mechanisms and account for variations in consumption patterns and participant characteristics.
