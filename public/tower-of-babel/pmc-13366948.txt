Introduction

Odor pollution in pig production systems has become an increasingly serious concern. Indole and skatole are major odorous compounds generated in pig farms and are characterized by low olfactory thresholds and strong diffusibility [1]. These substances can induce respiratory, intestinal, and nervous system disorders in pigs, impair overall health, and reduce production efficiency, while also contaminating the surrounding environment through atmospheric dispersion [2]. Thus, measures to reduce indole and skatole emissions are essential for sustainable livestock development.

Host genetic background and pig breed are key determinants of gut microbiota composition [3]. Gut microbial diversity is influenced by developmental stage and intestinal segment [4, 5]. Significant differences in the relative abundances of Bifidobacterium, Megacoccus, Fusobacterium, and Mitsuokella have been reported between Tibetan pigs and black pigs. Network analysis indicated that the microbial community structure of Tibetan pigs shows greater complexity and modularity than that of black pigs [6]. Gut microbial diversity has been reported to be higher in Tibetan pigs than in Yorkshire pigs [7]. The large intestine of pigs comprises three distinct segments: cecum, colon, and rectum. Rotsaert reported that in the lower digestive tract, bacterial densities significantly increased, with the highest loads in the distal colon (1.55 × 1010 cells/mL) and rectum (6.82 × 1010 cells/mL) [8], indicating significant differences in microbial composition among gastrointestinal segments,

Indole and skatole levels vary among pig breeds, with higher concentrations reported in Duroc pigs than Pietrain pigs [9]. Variations in skatole concentrations have also been observed among four commercial pig breeds (Large White, Landrace, Pietrain, and Duroc) as well as Czech Black pigs [10]. Researchers have found that the production of indole and skatole in pigs is closely associated with the abundance of gut microbiota. For example, the production of indole is regulated by Escherichia coli, Clostridium spp., and Bacteroides spp. [11]. The high abundance of Clostridium and Desulfovibrio is positively correlated with skatole production [12]. High abundances of Sphingobacterium, Moraxella, and Proteiniclasticum are negatively correlated with skatole production, whereas high abundances of Enterococcus, Desulfovibrio, Veillonella, and Streptococcus promote skatole production [13]. Despite these results, research on functional microorganisms specifically targeting indole and skatole emission reduction in pigs remains limited, highlighting the need for systematic screening of microbial strains with deodorization efficiency.

The Hezuo pig is a local breed from Gansu Province and belongs to the Tibetan pig lineage. This breed is characterized by low environmental pollution, reduced energy consumption, and a distinctive gut microbial structure [7]. In this study, the concentrations of indole and skatole in the cecum, colon and rectum of Hezuo and DLY pigs were compared, and the differences in microbial community characteristics between the two pig breeds was analysed by 16 S rRNA, aimed to screen the potential functional gut microbiota in reducing indole and skatole production.

Materials and methods

Animal administration and sample collection

Six Hezuo pigs (75-day-old, Diebu County, Gannan, Gansu Province) and six Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire (DLY) pigs (75-day-old, Yongjing County, Linxia, Gansu Province) in good health were used as donor animals. Pigs were fed three times daily at 08:00, 12:00, and 20:00, with ad libitum access to water. The feeding period lasted 25 days. The nutrient composition of the basal diet (early-growth finishing pig compound feed, Zhengda Feed Co., Ltd.) is presented in Table 1. After the feeding period, four 100-day-old pigs from each breed were randomly selected for slaughter. After slaughter, the large intestine was rapidly excised, ligated into three segments: the cecum, colon, and rectum. After inverting and mixing thoroughly, fresh cecal, colonic, and rectal contents were quickly and aseptically collected from the middle of each segment, placed in 5 mL cryo tubes, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at − 80 °C.

Detection of indole and skatole

Indole and skatole concentrations were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Chromatographic conditions were as follows: column, Syncronis™ C18 (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA); injection volume, 20 µL; column temperature, 30 °C; flow rate, 1.0 mL/min; mobile phase, methanol/water (55:45, v/v); excitation wavelength, 254 nm. emission wavelength, 350 nm. Cecal, colonic, and rectal content samples (0.5 g each) were dissolved in 20 mL of methanol and subjected to ultrasonic extraction for 30 min. Samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane before analysis. Analytical standards of indole and skatole (≥ 99% purity) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Trading Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for standard curve preparation.

16 S rRNA sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from cecal, colonic, and rectal content samples using the Fast DNA® Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals, USA) by following manufacturer protocol. DNA integrity, purity, and concentration were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, Nanodrop® ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), respectively. The V3–V4 regions of bacterial 16S rRNA were amplified using universal primers 314F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). PCR products were gel-purified, quantified, pooled, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequences were spliced and filtered to obtain high-quality data, followed by operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering and taxonomic classification. Alpha diversity (Chao1, Observed-species, Shannon, and Pielou-e indices) and beta diversity (principal coordinate analysis based on Bray–Curtis distances) were calculated. Differential microbiota between pig breeds were identified using LEfSe analysis (LDA > 3, p < 0.05), and functional profiles were predicted using PICRUSt. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to assess associations between the abundance of differential microbiota and indole and skatole concentrations. Sequencing data have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under accession ID PRJNA1396999.

Statistical analysis

Indole and skatole concentrations were compared using the Student’s t-test in SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) for each group. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. For Spearman correlation analysis, associations were considered significant when p < 0.05 and the correlation coefficient |r| > 0.5.

Results

Comparison of indole and skatole concentrations between Hezuo pigs and DLY pigs

As shown in Fig. 1A, compared with DLY pigs, indole concentrations in the cecum of Hezuo pigs were extremely significantly reduced (p < 0.01), and indole levels in the colon and rectum were significantly reduced (p < 0.05). Also, skatole concentrations in the cecum of Hezuo pigs were significantly lower (p < 0.05) than DLY pigs, and skatole levels in the colon and rectum were extremely significantly lower (p < 0.01) than DLY pigs (Fig. 1B).

Diversity of microorganisms of Hezuo pigs and DLY pigs

Alpha diversity analysis showed Pielou-e of HZCE and HZCO was significantly higher than HZRE (p = 0.049, Fig. 4A), indicating higher evenness in cecal and colonic microorganisms of Hezuo pigs. There was no significant difference among the cecal, colonic and rectal contents of DLY pigs (Fig. 4B). Also, significant differences between pig breeds were observed in the cecal contents, where Hezuo pigs exhibited a significantly higher Pielou-e and Shannon diversity index compared to DLY pigs (p = 0.045, p = 0.04, Fig. 4C). This difference indicated that higher cecal microbial diversity and uniformity in Hezuo pig. Although there were no significant differences in Chao1 and Observed species between the two pig breeds, the microbial richness of Hezuo pigs among different large intestine segment was greater than that of DLY pigs. A principal component analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance revealed a tighter clustering of the microbe across different large intestine segments within the same pig breed. PCoA analysis revealed a significant separation of microbial communities between Hezuo pigs and DLY pigs (Fig. 4D). The observed distribution may be closely related to the differences in physiological functions of different large intestine segments.

Discussion

Indole and skatole are major odorous compounds generated in pig farms and their emissions affect the sustainable development of pig industry seriously [2]. The concentrations of indole and skatole vary among pig breeds [14]. In our study, the concentrations of indole and skatole in the gastrointestinal tract of Hezuo pigs were significantly lower than those in DLY pigs, indicating distinct differences in these odor-related compounds between the two pig types and suggesting a lower odor potential in Hezuo pigs. Within Hezuo pigs, indole and skatole concentrations showed a progressive increase from the cecum to the rectum. In DLY pigs, indole levels also increased along the intestinal tract and reached the highest level in the rectum, whereas skatole concentration peaked in the colon. Knarreborg reported that skatole concentration in the porcine digestive tract increases sequentially from the stomach to the small intestine, cecum, and distal colon, with the highest level observed in the distal colon [15]. Indole and skatole are metabolites produced by the metabolism of tryptophan via gut microbiota [16]. Previous studies have shown that there are significant differences in microbial structure, composition, and function among different segments of the gastrointestinal tract [17]. We further speculate that the differences in indole and skatole levels between the two pig breeds may result from variations in the efficiency of tryptophan metabolism by the microbiota in each large intestine segment.

The animal gut microbiota and the host mutually select each other, forming a relatively stable organism. Among them, host genetics, diet, environment, antibiotic usage and other factors can affect the composition and function of the gut microbiota. Metagenomic analysis of the gut microbiota in Tibetan pigs and black pigs identified differences in the abundance of 18 genera and 141 species, and network analysis revealed higher complexity and modularity in the microbiota of Tibetan pigs [6]. Li reported that microbial diversity in the large intestine of Laiwu pigs was higher than that of DLY pigs [18]. In this study, the microbial composition of Hezuo pigs and DLY pigs differed significantly among different large intestinal segments (cecum, colon, and rectum). Moreover, diversity results showed that the microbial diversity and richness of Hezuo pigs were higher than those of DLY pigs, revealing that host genetic background plays an important role in shaping the composition and diversity of the gut microbiota. The differences in indole and skatole levels between Hezuo pigs and DLY pigs may be related to variations in gut microbial composition. We further speculate that the large intestine of Hezuo pigs may harbor more abundant tryptophan-metabolizing bacteria, whereas DLY pigs, despite lower microbial diversity, may be enriched in skatole-producing bacteria.

This study reveals the key role of specific high-abundance bacterial genera in the low skatole production of Hezuo pigs. Higher abundances of Treponema_D, Lactobacillus, Coprococcus_A, Ruminococcus_E, Acetatifactor, Anaeroplasma, and Faecalibacillus in the Hezuo pigs were observed and the Lactobacillus ultunensis, Lactobacillus amylovorus, Anaeroplasma abactoclasticum, Anaeroplasma, Faecalimonas phoceensis, Faecalimonas, and Faecalibacillus were negatively correlated with indole and skatole concentrations. Consistent with these findings, previous studies have demonstrated that Treponema participates in cellulose and lignin degradation, producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), reducing intestinal pH, and suppressing harmful bacterial growth [19]. Treponema succinifaciens has been reported to enhance carbohydrate metabolism in the pig gut [20]. Other studies show that the abundances of Bacillota and Lactobacillus were higher in Tibetan pigs compared with black pigs [6], and that Lactobacillus abundance was greater in Tibetan pigs than in DLY pigs [3]. Lactobacillus, as a probiotic genus, has been reported to support intestinal function and maintain microbial homeostasis, improving ecological stability and adaptability of the gut microbiota [21]. Previous studies have reported that increased abundance of Coprococcus is associated with improved mental health–related indicators and improved exercise motivation in mice [22]. Coprococcus_A is a butyrate-producing genus, and butyrate is a key SCFA involved in the maintenance of gut homeostasis, modulation of inflammatory responses, and preservation of intestinal barrier integrity [23]. Ruminococcus has been reported to show positive correlations with glucose-6-phosphate, maltose, arachidonic acid, 9,12-octadecadienoic acid, oleic acid, and phosphate, indicating its involvement in carbohydrate degradation processes [24]. Acetatifactor, recognized as a SCFA-producing genus, contributes to gut health maintenance [25]. Anaeroplasma has been reported to increase weight gain and feed intake while reducing diarrhea in early-weaned piglets [26], and preliminary findings by Florenciao suggest that Anaeroplasma may confer resistance against African swine fever [27]. Faecalibacillus comprises strictly anaerobic bacteria with probiotic potential and produces short-chain fatty acids, such as butyric acid, through fermentation of dietary fibers. This metabolite plays a key role in preserving intestinal barrier integrity, regulating immune responses, and suppressing inflammation [28]. Murine studies have reported that Faecalibacillus isolates provide effective alleviation of constipation [29]. Alao, a strong positive associations between Faecalibacillus and acetic, propionic, and butyric acids [30]. These results suggest that Hezuo pigs harbor a higher abundance of beneficial bacteria, which may contribute to enhanced carbohydrate metabolism, preservation of intestinal homeostasis, and improved adaptation to high-altitude hypoxic conditions. The high prevalence of these beneficial bacteria promotes the production of SCFAs (such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate), thereby lowering gut pH and creating an acidic environment unfavorable for the colonization of harmful microorganisms, which in turn inhibits the synthesis of malodorous substances such as indole and skatole.

In this study, Escherichia, Streptococcus, Catenibacterium, Peptococcus, Corynebacterium, and Enterococcus_B showed higher abundances in DLY pigs than in Hezuo pigs. Spearman correlation analysis indicated that Streptococcus hyointestinalis, Catenibacterium, Catenibacterium mitsuokai, Corynebacterium, and Enterococcus_B were positively associated with indole and skatole levels. Consistent with these findings, previous studies have shown that aromatic amino acids can be decarboxylated by gut microbiota, including Escherichia–Shigella, forming indole derivatives [31], and that indole and skatole levels are positively associated with pathogenic genera such as Escherichia–Shigella [32]. Other reports indicate that Desulfovibrio and Streptococcus can worsen inflammatory responses, aggravating inflammatory bowel disease [33]. The abundance of Streptococcus has been associated with increased disease severity in bronchiolitis, reduced microbiome stability, and positive associations with high-risk bronchiolitis–related metabolites [34]. Pajarillo reported significant differences in gut microbiota composition among purebred Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire pigs, with Duroc pigs showing higher abundances of Catenibacterium, Phascolarctobacterium, and Subdoligranulum [35]. Multiple studies have documented increased Catenibacterium abundance in infectious and autoimmune disorders [36], and its enrichment has been linked to an elevated risk of rheumatoid arthritis, supporting its association with pro-inflammatory responses [37]. Peptococcus abundance has shown strong positive associations with growth-related traits, including body weight and average daily gain, in growing pigs [38]. Corynebacterium has been reported to be involved in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and other central nervous system disorders [39, 40]. Enterococcus has been shown to participate in tryptophan metabolism, promoting the production of indole and the skatole precursor indoleacetic acid [41]. These results suggest that DLY pigs harbor a higher abundance of potentially harmful bacteria and microbiota involved in dysregulated metabolism across gut segments. The enrichment of these taxa may exacerbate metabolic imbalances, enhance intestinal inflammation, and consequently lead to metabolic disturbances. Furthermore, these harmful microorganisms can redirect tryptophan metabolism toward the production of indole and skatole.

These findings provide theoretical support for the efficient reduction of indole and skatole emission in pigs. Moreover, the development of probiotics derived from Hezuo pigs may become an effective strategy for mitigating indole and skatole production. However, this study only identified specific gut microbes regulating indole and skatole levels using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Future work will utilize in vitro fermentation experiments to probe specific gut microbial functions. The identified functional microbial suggest actionable targets for probiotic supplementation to mitigating odor emissions in pigs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, significant differences in indole and skatole concentrations exist between Hezuo and DLY pigs, with Hezuo pigs showing lower odor potential. Specific gut microbiota, including Lactobacillus ultunensis, Lactobacillus amylovorus, Anaeroplasma, Faecalimonas and Faecalibacillus are associated with reduced indole and skatole levels, indicating their potential as probiotics for mitigating odor emissions in pig production.
