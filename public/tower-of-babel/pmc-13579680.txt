Exome Sequencing in Patients with Loin Pain Hematuria Syndrome and Persistent Isolated Hematuria
Authors: Bhanu Prasad, Ahmed M Soliman, Aarti Garg, Aditi Sharma, Clara Schott, Dervla Connaughton, Matthew B Lanktree
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLoin pain hematuria syndrome (LPHS) is characterized by chronic loin pain and hematuria without a urological cause. We hypothesized that rare genetic variants in genes impacting the glomerular filtration barrier (endothelial cells, glomerular basement membrane, or podocytes) may contribute to LPHS.MethodsExome sequencing was performed in 29 consecutive patients; 17 with LPHS, and 12 with persistent painless isolated hematuria (IH). We first evaluated a virtual gene panel of 130 hematuria-linked genes curated from the literature followed by an exome-wide analysis. Variant interpretation was performed using clinical criteria.ResultsOf 17 patients with LPHS, 13 had gross hematuria, 4 had microscopic hematuria, all with chronic pain. Of these, 9 had a history of kidney stones, but none had obstruction on current imaging; 8 had a family history of kidney stones, 3 of hematuria, and 2 of LPHS. Of 17 patients with LPHS, 4 (23%) had a heterozygous pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variant—1 in each of COL4A4, MSTO1, FLNA, and APPL1. Of 12 patients with IH, 4 (33%) with painless hematuria had a P/LP variant—1 in each of COL4A3, COL4A5, FLNA, and UQCRC1. Broadening to include the whole exome, no additional P/LP variants believed to be involved in LPHS were identified.ConclusionsLPHS appears to be the result of multiple heterogeneous mechanisms. Our analysis did not identify a common monogenic cause, but rare variants were observed in multiple genes among affected individuals. Pathogenic variants in type IV collagen genes (COL4A3-5) were identified in 1 of 17 patients with LPHS and 2 of 12 patients with IH.Keywords: glomerular basement membrane, hematuria, loin pain hematuria syndrome, podocytes

LPHS was first described in 1967 as a rare, complex, and poorly understood painful condition that predominantly affects young women.1 LPHS severely reduces quality of life.2, 3, 4 Patients experience debilitating loin pain along with hematuria in the absence of urological or primary kidney pathology.2, 3, 4 Patients with IH without proteinuria or pain have been studied,5, 6, 7 but the etiology and pathophysiology of LPHS remains unknown. Patients with LPHS struggle to achieve pain relief through non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, narcotics, nerve blocks, and surgical intervention.2, 3, 4 They typically have repeated episodes of gross hematuria, often coinciding with exacerbation of loin pain, but pain can also be chronic and not associated with gross hematuria events.2, 3, 4 Kidney morphology is normal in these patients, and they typically do not have associated proteinuria or microalbuminuria.2, 3, 4 In contrast, isolated microscopic hematuria is typically persistent, painless, and does not have proteinuria or microalbuminuria.5, 6, 7

Glomerular hematuria occurs when red blood cells traverse the 3-layered filtration barrier consisting of glomerular endothelial cells, the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), and podocytes.8,9 Type IV collagen is the major collagen constituent of the GBM, and heterozygous variants in autosomal COL4A3 and COL4A4, or COL4A5 on the X chromosome, have been associated with persistent microscopic hematuria (formerly called benign familial hematuria or thin basement membrane disease).8 Targeted testing by our group for type IV collagen variants in 14 patients with LPHS did not identify any responsible variants.3 Spetie et al.10 proposed glomerular hematuria as the instigating event, sequentially leading to tubular obstruction, back leak of glomerular filtrate, and local parenchymal edema that promotes compression of adjacent tubules and subsequent capsular stretch leading to pain. Electron microscopy examination of the kidney biopsies, emphasizing the GBM, has shown structurally normal or nonspecific changes, which have not explained the cause of hematuria.3 Consequently, the precise mechanisms underlying hematuria and its eventual association with pain remain unclear. It is plausible that yet unidentified defects and/or proteins in the endothelium and GBM lead to the leakage of red cells from the vasculature into the urinary filtrate.

The glomerular filtration barrier composed of endothelial cells, GBM, and podocytes. It is the most feasible pathologic site in glomerular hematuria, thus we hypothesized that variants in genes impacting the glomerular filtration barrier may contribute to the LPHS phenotype. Although common-variant genome-wide association studies have been performed evaluating hematuria in large population cohorts,11 exome sequencing studies examining rare variants in well-phenotyped patients with the rare LPHS phenotype are lacking. There is no identifiable inheritance pattern among patients with LPHS, but familial clustering has been reported.4 There is no known difference in LPHS prevalence across ancestries or founder populations that would support a genetic etiology, but awareness or cultural factors may lead to variable disease recognition around the world.

Although variants in COL4A3/4/5 are frequently recognized as a cause of thin basement membrane disease and microscopic hematuria,8 multiple other monogenic disorders can also present with painless asymptomatic persistent hematuria, such as podocyte-related (LMX1B, INF2, MYH9), ciliary and cystic kidney diseases, and complement-mediated causes (CFHR5). We included an IH cohort as a comparator group, allowing for direct comparison of genetic yield and variant spectra between painful versus painless hematuria, rather than comparing only to population data bases (i.e., gnomAD).

As the pathophysiology of LPHS remains poorly understood, we wanted to explore whether an underlying genetic contribution might explain the conundrum, especially in comparison to isolated painless persistent hematuria. We hypothesized that exome sequencing could uncover genetic etiologies in patients with LPHS. In addition to type IV collagen genes (COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5), we expanded our search to focus on other proteins in the GBM, endothelium, and podocytes, and the whole exome.

Methods

Study Population and Design

Patients with LPHS or isolated painless hematuria were referred to a nephrology clinic that specializes in LPHS in Regina, Saskatchewan, Canada. This clinic is a tertiary referral center for LPHS and receives between 7 and 8 referrals annually from across Canada. Written informed consent was obtained from all 29 study participants on the basis of the LPHS inclusion and exclusion criteria defined by Spetie et al.10 (Supplementary Methods). This included consent for return of incidental findings. All patients were aged ≥ 18 years with physician-diagnosed LPHS or IH. LPHS was defined as the presence of chronic or recurrent flank pain associated with hematuria, in the absence of identifiable urological or structural causes after comprehensive evaluation, including imaging and, when indicated, kidney biopsy (as per criteria described by Spetie et al.10 and previously reported by our group).3 Patients with a history of kidney stones were eligible for inclusion, provided that the stones were not obstructing urinary flow at the time of evaluation. Patients with IH were defined as those with persistent microscopic hematuria on repeated urinalyses, but no associated loin pain, and without identifiable urological, structural, or functional causes. The study was approved by the Saskatchewan Health Authority Research Ethics Board (REB 18-29).

Clinical, Phenotypic, and Biological Characteristics

Age, sex, and comorbidities, including hematuria and pain, were collected. Sex was defined as the biological sex documented in the patient's medical record, corresponding to the chromosomal sex (XX or XY) and was also verified by analysing the X-linked variant during exome sequencing (FLNA, and COL4A5). A detailed family history of chronic diseases or pain was obtained from charted patient self-reports, as reported by our group.4 Medication history was ascertained. Biochemical laboratory data were collected, including estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), calculated by the race-free Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 formula, and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio.

Exome Sequencing and Bioinformatic Gene Panel

Given the rarity of LPHS, all consecutive eligible patients (17 with LPHS and 12 with persistent, IH without pain) referred during the study period were included. Venous blood samples were collected in PAXgene Blood DNA Tubes (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Genomic DNA was extracted using the New England Biolabs Monarch Genomic DNA Purification Kit (New England Biolabs, Inc., Ipswich, MA, USA) as per the protocol by Kruhøffer et al.12 DNA libraries were constructed using the Illumina DNA Prep with Enrichment, Tagmentation kit, and IDT xGen Hyb Panel v2 with xGen Universal Blockers—NXT Mix and dual unique barcodes (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Paired-end sequencing (2x150 bps) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System at 100X depth. Sequencing reads that passed filtering metrics were mapped to the human reference genome (GRCh38-gencode release 43) using the BWA aligner (v0.7.17) with quality assessment performed using Samtools (v1.16.1).13, 14, 15 Copy Number Variants (CNVs) were called using the GATK-recommended GermlineCNVCaller workflow (GermlineCNVCaller - GATK) (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360035531152--How-to-Call-rare-germline-copy-number-variants) using the standard protocol.

Bioinformatic analysis, annotation, and interpretation of the variants were performed using the hereditary pipeline of the QIAGEN Clinical Insight Interpret Translational (QIAGEN) software [https://variants.ingenuity.com/qci/] and the Franklin platform [https://franklin.genoox.com/clinical-db/home]. Variant pathogenicity was determined by the Franklin pipeline according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) guidelines. All candidate variants and CNVs were prioritized by Franklin and in silico tools were manually evaluated according to ACMG/AMP guidelines.16 Variants were divided into i) P/LP variants and ii) variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Manual curation after Franklin followed ACMG/AMP guidelines and included (1) independent review of Franklin outputs (population data, ClinVar submissions, in silico predictions); (2) application and/or adjustment of evidence criteria with gene- or disease-specific considerations where applicable; (3) consensus classification by 2 nephrologists with genetics expertise; and (4) for CNVs, annotation through GATK gCNVCaller and classification per ACMG/ClinGen CNV guidelines.

We applied a virtual gene panel approach, focusing on a preselected list of 130 genes previously associated with disorders in GBM, podocytes, and endothelial cells. This list was developed from recently published literature, including 4 key studies (Supplementary Table S1).11,17, 18, 19 Second, we evaluated all identified pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants identified across the exome. Incidental findings were also analyzed in 81 genes according to ACMG secondary findings v3.2 (2023) (Date of analysis: 30 June 2025) (Supplementary Table S2).20 Unfortunately, family members were unavailable for variant cosegregation analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range) and compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann-Whitney U) test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using Fisher exact test. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software, Release 18.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). All tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical, Phenotypic, and Biological Characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the 29 sequenced patients are summarized in Table 1. The 17 patients with LPHS had a median age of 40 years (interquartile range 35–46), whereas the 12 patients in the IH group had a median age of 58 years (interquartile range 53–62). Most patients in both groups were female (88% in LPHS; 83% in IH) and primarily of European ancestry (94% in LPHS; 92% in IH). Among the patients with LPHS, 9 (53%) had a history of kidney stones, but none had stones on current imaging, whereas none of the patients with IH did. Gross hematuria was common in the LPHS group (13/17, 77%) but rare in the IH group (1/12, 8%). In accordance with the inclusion criteria, chronic pain was reported in all patients with LPHS and in none of the IH group. There was no proteinuria and eGFR was normal in both the LPHS and IH groups; patients with LPHS had a median eGFR of 105 ml/min per 1.73 m2 and a median urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 0.9 mg/mmol, whereas patients with IH had a median eGFR of 90 ml/min per 1.73 m2 and a median urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 0.9 mg/mmol.

P/LP Variants in Hematuria Genes Among Patients With LPHS and IH

P/LP variants were identified in 4 of 17 (24%) patients with LPHS—a pathogenic heterozygous missense variant impacting a glycine residue in type IV collagen alpha 4 chain (COL4A4 NM_000092.5:c.3044G>A, p.Gly1015Glu), a pathogenic heterozygous missense variant in misato mitochondrial distribution and morphology regulator 1 (MSTO1 NM_018116.4:c.971C>T, p.Thr324Ile), a heterozygous likely pathogenic frameshift deletion in adaptor protein phosphotyrosine interacting with PH domain and leucine zipper 1 (APPL1 NM_012096.3:c.1930_1933del, p.Arg644∗) and a pathogenic heterozygous X-linked missense variant in FLNA encoding filamin A (FLNA NM_001110556.2:c.586C>T, p.Arg196Trp) in a female (Table 2). Reverse phenotyping of LPHS-13 patient with the pathogenic COL4A4 p.Gly1015Glu variant identified conductive deafness. Other extrarenal manifestations including myopathy symptoms, mature-onset diabetes in youth, and periventricular nodular heterotopia were not observed in patients with MSTO1, FLNA, and APPL1 variants, respectively.

Heterozygous P/LP Variants in Genes With Autosomal Recessive Modes of Inheritance

Individuals with heterozygous pathogenic variants in genes associated with autosomal recessive inheritance are typically considered asymptomatic carriers. However, as is the case with COL4A3 and COL4A4, there is a possibility of a milder subclinical phenotype among heterozygous individuals.21 We observed a nondiagnostic heterozygous pathogenic frameshift deletion in the Alström syndrome gene (ALMS1 NM_001378454.1: c.10772del (p.Thr3591Lysfs∗6)) in one patient with LPHS. In patient LPHS-17, we observed a heterozygous pathogenic nonsense variant in integrin alpha 3 (ITGA3 NM_002204.4: c.2794C>T, p.Arg932∗) associated with nephrotic syndrome in the context of interstitial lung disease and epidermolysis bullosa. Of all patients, 4 patients with LPHS (LPHS-4, LPHS-8, LPHS-17, LPHS-22) and 3 patients with IH (IH-5, IH-6, IH-11) were carriers of the extensively studied heterozygous pathogenic missense variant in podocin (NPHS2 NM_014625.4: c.686G>A, p.Arg229Gln), that appears to contribute to nephrotic syndrome only when in trans with another pathogenic variant in NPHS2, but is not expected to cause a phenotype on its own.

Missense VUS in Hematuria Genes

A total of 33 missense VUS were found in 26 of 130 genes on the hematuria panel in patients with LPHS or IH (Supplementary Tables S3–S5). A total of 21 VUS in 17 genes were identified in 15 of 17 patients with LPHS. In comparison, 12 VUS in 10 genes were identified in 7 of 12 patients with IH. Determining the pathogenicity of any of these VUS is not possible at present.

Digenic effects have been reported in patients with Type IV collagen variants, and we looked for patients with multiple variants. Patient LPHS-22 had 3 VUS in addition to heterozygous APPL1 and NPHS2 likely pathogenic variants. Patient IH-11 with IH had 3 VUS in addition to a heterozygous NPHS2 likely pathogenic variant. In addition, 4 patients with LPHS had 2 VUS, whereas 3 patients with IH had 2 VUS. We are unable to determine if these combinations of variants contributed to the LPHS phenotype in these patients.

Incidental Findings

Examining genes beyond the preselected bioinformatic gene panel, including genes listed for incidental reporting across the exome, no reportable findings were identified.20 Interestingly, there was a VUS in tuberous sclerosis 2 (TSC2 NM_000548.5: c.821A>G p.Tyr274Cys) in LPHS-1. This variant is rare (maximal allele frequency of 0.004% in gnomAD) with 4 previous uncertain submissions to Clinvar. Although tuberous sclerosis can lead to recurrent hematuria in the context of angiomyolipoma, this patient had no identifiable features of tuberous sclerosis.

CNVs

We identified 4 heterozygous CNV duplications in type IV collagen genes in 3 patients with LPHS (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S5). All were classified as VUS—a heterozygous duplication of 494 bp in COL4A5 (NC_000023.11:g.108580533_108581027dup) in a female LPHS patient (LPHS-10), a large 20.5 kb duplication in COL4A3 (NC_000002.12:g.227263780_227284345dup) and a large 15 kb duplication in COL4A2 (NC_000013.11:g.110458091_110473150dup) in LPHS-20, and a duplication of 668 bp in COL4A1 (NC_000013.11:g.110200854_110201522dup) in LPHS-14. A large hemizygous pathogenic X-linked deletion in filamin A (FLNA, NM_001110556.2: g.154219816_155545276del, ∼1.3 Mb) was found in a male patient with IH (IH-5) (Table 3). This patient did not show the corresponding periventricular nodular heterotopia clinical phenotype typically associated with X-linked FLNA deletion. No other phenotypically relevant P/LP CNVs were identified in the gene panel or exome (Supplementary Table S6).

Clinical Characteristics Between Patients With LPHS With and Without Identified Pathogenic Variants

We compared the phenotypes of patients with LPHS with and without identified variants (n = 4 variant-positive vs. n = 13 variant-negative). Qualitative comparison indicates similar phenotypes regardless of an identified variant, but power to determine a difference is limited by sample size. There was no significant difference in age at onset, pain duration or quality, opioid use, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, or eGFR (all p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

Exome sequencing in 17 patients with LPHS identified 4 P/LP variants in 4 different genes (COL4A4, MSTO1, FLNA, and APPL1). Only 1 of 17 patients with LPHS had a pathogenic variant in COL4A3/4/5. In comparison, we found that 4 of 12 (33%) patients with persistent isolated microscopic hematuria had a heterozygous P/LP variant (COL4A5, COL4A4, FLNA, and UQCRC1). None of the individuals had phenotypes consistent with classical Alport syndrome, but they represent the hematuria phenotype now described as within the Alport risk spectrum.22

Pathogenic variants in COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5 lead to abnormalities in the synthesis of the type IV collagen α3, α4, and α5 chains, disrupting the synthesis of the GBM collagen network. This disruption and destabilization lead to a wide spectrum of phenotypes, from microscopic hematuria to Alport syndrome.23 A total of 3 modes of inheritance are well documented in Alport syndrome—autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant, and X-linked. A total of 3 variants in type IV collagen genes were detected—1 heterozygous missense COL4A4 variant in a patient with LPHS, 1 X-linked heterozygous missense COL4A5 variant in a female, and 1 heterozygous frameshift COL4A3 variant among patients with IH. None of these 3 patients had other clinical features of classical Alport syndrome.24,25 There were also duplications in COL4A3 and COL4A5 that were classified as VUS. Heterozygous variants in COL4A3/4 and female heterozygous variants in COL4A5 do not typically have extrarenal manifestations.21, 22, 23

Advanced stages of kidney disease with hematuria are typically associated with microalbuminuria or proteinuria.5 However, as is the case in LPHS, hematuria and proteinuria do not always coincide, and other kidney diseases exist with hematuria without proteinuria involving glomerular, vascular, and tubular compartments. These include thin basement membrane disease and early Alport syndrome, Nutcracker syndrome and arteriovenous malformations, early autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, tubular disorders including hypercalciuria, hyperuricosuria, and hyperoxaluria even without visible stone formation on imaging, and early complement-mediated CFHR5 nephropathy, often in “synpharyngitic” episodes.5, 6, 7, 8, 9,18,22

Patient LPHS-22 has a likely pathogenic heterozygous frameshift variant in APPL1 NM_012096.3: c.1930_1933del (p.Arg644∗) expected to result in nonsense-mediated mRNA decay and reduction of APPL1 protein production. APPL1 acts as a protective factor against podocyte injury, specifically in conjunction with hyperglycemia.26 This loss-of-function variant may disrupt AMPK signaling, increase podocyte liability to injury, promote podocyte apoptosis, and ultimately result in hematuria.26 Loss-of-function mutations in APPL1 have been linked to mature-onset diabetes in youth,27 which can also lead to GBM damage and hematuria, but this patient had not developed diabetes and has no known family history of diabetes.

Podocyte function requires a continuous energy supply, and mitochondrial variants have been implicated in glomerular disease.28 We identified a heterozygous likely pathogenic missense variant in UQCRC1 NM_003365.3:c.826C>T (p.Arg276Cys) in patient IH-6, which is needed for oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production.29 Another mitochondrial pathogenic heterozygous variant, MSTO1 NM_018116.4: c.971C>T (p.Thr324Ile) was detected in patient LPHS-16. MSTO1 encodes a cytoplasmic protein that plays a crucial role in the fusion and distribution of mitochondria.30 This p.Thr324Ile variant is reported in ClinVar and linked to mitochondrial myopathy-cerebellar ataxia-pigmentary retinopathy syndrome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/RCV000505816/). However, this patient did not have any symptoms of mitochondrial disease. There are no studies evaluating the role of MSTO1 in podocyte mitochondria.

Identification of 2 cases (LPHS-6 and IH-5) with distinct predicted deleterious variants in FLNA is particularly noteworthy. FLNA encodes filamin A, an important cytoskeletal protein that is highly expressed in mesangial cells of the glomerulus (Human Protein Atlas; https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000196924-FLNA/tissue).31 In addition, FLNA is highly conserved and intolerant to variation (gnomAD missense z-score = 5.85; Loss-of-Function Observed/Expected Upper Bound Fraction = 0.083). Given the high clinical burden of LPHS, despite preserved kidney function and low risk of progression to kidney failure, further examination of FLNA is warranted.

Heterozygous P/LP variants in genes associated with autosomal recessive inheritance have sometimes been reported to lead to subclinical or mild phenotypes that only emerge later in life, representing an additive inheritance pattern. For example, this additive inheritance is observed in the heterozygous or autosomal dominant phenotype linked to type IV collagen genes. In 1 patient with LPHS, we observed a heterozygous pathogenic frameshift variant in ALMS1 (ALMS1 NM_001378454.1: c.10772del (p.Thr3591Lysfs∗6)) with a combined annotation dependent depletion score of 23 and a gnomAD allele frequency of 0.00004. ALMS1 plays a role in ciliary function and maintains the structural dynamics of the centrosome and basal body.32,33 Homozygous or compound heterozygous variants in ALMS1 are associated with autosomal recessive Alström syndrome.32 Homozygous ALMS1 knockout rats exhibit increased glomerular capillary pressure, higher tubuloglomerular feedback sensitivity, and hypertension, possibly contributing to glomerular damage.33 ALMS1 heterozygous carriers could exhibit subclinical kidney phenotypes because of impaired ciliary function in podocytes or endothelial cells.33

We also found 4 patients with LPHS (LPHS-4, LPHS-8, LPHS-17, LPHS-22) and 3 patients with IH (IH-5, IH-6, IH-11 who carried the same likely pathogenic heterozygous missense variant NPHS2 (NPHS2 NM_014625.4: c.686G>A p.Arg229Gln). NPHS2 encodes podocin, which anchors nephrin (NPHS1) in lipid rafts to construct functional microdomains critical for slit diaphragm assembly and stability.34 This relatively common variant (minor allele frequency ∼ 3%) has been described in the context of nephrotic syndrome when combined with a second pathogenic allele in trans. The p.Arg229Gln variant’s presence in patients with LPHS or IH has not been reported, and its relevance remains unclear. A heterozygous pathogenic nonsense variant in ITGA3 NM_002204.4: c.2794C>T p.Arg932∗) was detected in LPHS-17. However, because biallelic variants are required for the established ITGA3-related podocytopathy, this finding is most consistent with carrier status or a subclinical phenotype.35 Integrin α3β1 is the dominant integrin on the basolateral surface of podocyte foot processes and helps organize and stabilize the GBM structure during glomerular development and in adulthood.36 ITGA3-knocked-out human podocyte cell line AB8/13 showed a significant reduction in podocyte spreading and adhesion, and gene expression analysis showed downregulation of most integrin α3 interactions leading to exacerbated glomerular damage.36

The current study used exome sequencing in patients with LPHS and IH, primarily of European ancestry, and recruited from a single center (although we receive referrals across Canada). We used variant interpretation, as well as bioinformatic evaluation with commonly used tools. Unfortunately, additional family members for linkage analysis or cosegregation analysis were unavailable. We did not directly evaluate the functional impact of identified variants in model systems, a process inaccessible to most nephrology genetics clinics. It is possible that pathogenic variants may still exist among those classified as VUS because of insufficient functional evidence in the current literature, as is true of all sequencing studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, 4 P/LP variants in GBM and podocyte related genes (COL4A4, APPL1, FLNA, and MSTO1) were observed in 17 patients with LPHS. P/LP variants in COL4A3, COL4A4, FLNA, and UQCRC1 were observed in 4 of 12 patients with persistent painless isolated microscopic hematuria. Thus, 21 of 29 (72%) patients with persistent hematuria, with or without pain, had no pathogenic variant identified. Heterozygous P/PLP variants in genes with autosomal recessive inheritance, such as ALMS1, ITGA3, and NPHS2, were found that could potentially contribute to hematuria. Interpreting VUS remains difficult, and 22 of 29 (76%) patients had a VUS in a gene a priori selected on a hematuria gene panel. Broader exome sequencing did not reveal additional variants likely to be responsible beyond the initial gene panel.
