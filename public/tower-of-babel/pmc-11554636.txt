Dual-frequency countercurrent ultrasonic-assisted extraction of the cold brew coffee and in situ real-time monitoring of extraction process
Authors: Huimin Liu, Dandan Liu, Wuli Wang, Xinyan Zhang, Jamila Tuly, Hanhan Li, Haile Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Coffee is a highly sought-after beverage and the second most valuable commodity after oil, due to its distinct flavor and taste [1], [2]. The coffee brewing process typically entails grinding roasted coffee beans and extracting them at the appropriate temperature, time, and material-to-liquid ratio. There are two main types of brews: hot brew and cold brew, differentiated by the extraction temperature. Coffee grind is steeped in water at low temperature for an extended time to produce cold brew coffee [3], [4]. Cold brew coffee (CBC) has a smoother and less bitter taste compared to hot brew coffee, with intensely sweet, chocolatey, fruity, and floral flavors [3], [5]. However, CBC extraction method is typically lengthy (ranges from 6 to 24 h), inefficient, and expensive, which impedes its industrial-scale production [6]. Therefore, it is imperative to explore a straightforward and effective green extraction method that can reduce the duration of the extraction process of CBC, while also guaranteeing its quality and flavour.

As a type of non-thermal physical processing, ultrasonic extraction offers several advantages over conventional extraction processes, including low energy and solvent consumption and short time. The cavitation effect generated by ultrasound induces a robust shear force in the cavitation region, which results in the rupture of the cell wall, the release of intracellular material into the solvent, and promotion of the release of active ingredient [7], [8]. In addition, ultrasound can also enhance the mass transfer between solute and solvent. Numerous recent studies have demonstrated that ultrasonic treatment accelerates the release of soluble solids and other bioactive substances during coffee cold extraction, significantly reducing extraction time [9], [10]. However, current ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UE) CBC, which uses single-frequency probe-based or water bath ultrasonic devices with a fixed frequency, has limitations including uneven distribution of sound field, high temperature rise and single optional working mode [11]. The multi-mode ultrasound devices that our research group has been working on and has found the extraction advantage of low-frequency combinations is more pronounced. Owing to the advantages of rapid evaluation and environmental friendliness, the development of near-infrared (NIR) as a credible technology for in situ, real-time, and online monitoring of critical information in the food production process, such as changes in caffeine, water, and volatile compounds during the coffee roasting process [12], [13], [14], [15]. Real-time monitoring of extraction process has grown simplified with the recent development of miniature NIR fiber optic probes and spectroscopic equipment. In recent years, NIR has been widely used for monitoring the extraction process of lycium barbarum protein [16], walnut meal proteins [17], and vitamins [18]. However, NIR has rarely been reported in the extraction process of coffee. Therefore, in this work, we applied NIR to the UE process to monitor the extraction endpoint in real-time.

In this study, a countercurrent ultrasonic device was used to enhance extraction of CBC, thereby reducing the extraction time while ensuring the quality and flavor of the CBC. Total dissolved solids content (TDS) and extraction yield (EY) were chosen as indicators for evaluating the effectiveness of UE. In addition, the quality of UE CBC was compared with that of traditional extraction (TE) of CBC through an investigation of physicochemical, nutritional, and flavor indices. Finally, a NIR online monitoring model of TDS and chlorogenic acid (CGA) was established to realize real-time monitoring during the extraction process. This approach facilitates a more comprehensive understanding of the extraction endpoint and provides model support for the intelligent control system of the production process.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Medium to dark roasted Arabica beans and TE coffee liquid were provided by Kingcat Co. Ltd (Suzhou, China). DL-tartaric acid, malic acid and succinic acid were purchased from Shanghai ANPEL LAB Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Fumaric acid, citric acid and gallic acid were from Beijing Wokai Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Beijing, China). Caffeine solution in methanol, CGA, trigonelline, DPPH, ABTS, and 4-methyl-2-pentanol were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-technology Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Other chemical reagents were analytically pure from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Ultrasonic-assisted extraction of CBC

Medium to dark roasted coffee beans were ground to a size of 600–1000 μm (50 % or more), then extracted at 1:15 material-liquid ratio at 4 °C for 5 h. The UE process was optimized based on the countercurrent device, which was manufactured at Jiangsu University. This device has five fixed ultrasonic generator and transducer with different frequencies (20, 28, 35, 40, and 52 kHz), allowing for the operation with mode of single or different frequency combinations. In the treatment chamber, the direction of ultrasonic emission is opposite to the flow direction of the extraction liquid. The flow of the UE process is shown in Fig. 1. Ultrasound works continuously throughout the extraction process. The procedure involved cold extraction at 4 °C via a peristaltic pump cycle. In the mono-frequency and dual-frequency ultrasonic modes, the ultrasonic power densities were set to 240, 280, 320, 360, and 400 W/L. The material-liquid ratios were set to 1:11, 1:13, 1:15, 1:17, and 1:19, and the cold extraction times were set to 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min, respectively. After the extraction was completed, the coffee liquid was roughly filtered out at 4000 × g and centrifuged for 15 min to obtain the coffee supernatant for further analysis. The coffee sample without ultrasound treatment under the same conditions while at negative pressure was used as a control. All the experiments were carried out in triplicates. TDS and EY were indicators for optimizing the ultrasound conditions.

Cold brew coffee quality assessment

Physicochemical properties

TDS was measured using a refractometer (DFT-F10V55H23, Shenzhen Liushu Technology Co., ltd, Shenzhen, China); pH was measured using a pH meter (PH5S, San-Xin Instrument Ltd., Shanghai, China); viscosity was measured using a viscometer (LC-NDJ-1T, Shanghai Lichen Instrument Technology Co., ltd, Shanghai, China); color (L, a, b values) were measured using a chroma meter (Hunter Associates Laboratory, Inc., America). EY was calculated using the following formula:(1)EY%=TDS×m1m0×100%where TDS is total dissolved solids content of coffee, %; m1 defines the total mass of coffee liquid, g; m0 is the initial coffee powder mass used in the extraction, g.

Nutritional characteristics

Total lipid content

Total lipid content of coffee liquid after drying was determined based on the Soxhlet extraction method following the national standard GB/T 5009.6-2016.

Total protein content

Total protein content was measured by the automatic Kjeldahl method following the national standard GB/T 5009.5-2016.

Total sugar content

Total sugar content was determined by the phenol sulfuric acid method described by Chow et al. [19] with slight modifications. One milliliter of 5 % (v/v) phenol and 5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid were mixed with a dilution of 2 mL of coffee solution. The mixture was left to stand for 10 min, followed by a 5 min room temperature water bath. The absorbance was calculated at 490 nm by a UV spectrophotometer (TU-1810, Puxi General Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The blank was prepared by replacing the sample solution with an equal volume of distilled water. The measured absorbance (490 nm) was used to compute the content of total sugar and a standard curve calibrated with glucose (y = 0.0146 x − 0.0045, R2 = 0.9947, where y represents the absorbance, and x is the glucose concentration).

Total acid content

Total acid content was calculated using the method of Zhai et al. [20] with slight modifications. Three drops of 1 % (w/v) phenolphthalein indicator were added to a dilution of 20 mL of coffee liquid and incubated with 0.1 mol/L NaOH solution until the sample turned red and remained for at least 30 s. The total acid content (expressed as CGA) was calculated using the following formula:(2)Totalacidcontent%=C×V×K×NM×100%where C defines the concentration of NaOH solution (mol/L); V is the volume of NaOH solution consumed (mL); K is the acid conversion factor, which is 0.059 for CGA; N is the dilution factor; and M is the mass of coffee powder corresponding to 20 ml of coffee liquid (g).

Total phenol content

Total phenol content was calculated using the procedure of Cordoba et al. [3] with slight modifications. One milliliter of coffee solution dilution was added with 2 mL of 7.5 % (w/v) Na2CO3 solution, 0.5 mL of Folin-Phenol, and 6.5 mL of water, respectively. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min and then left in a 70 °C water bath for 30 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 750 nm, with an equal volume of distilled water used as a blank. The standard curve was plotted using different concentrations of gallic acid solutions, and the total phenol content was calculated by the following formula:(3)y=0.0102x+0.0167,R2=0.9925where y is the absorbance at 750 nm; x is the gallic acid concentration at that absorbance, µg/mL.

Antioxidant activity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity was measured by the method of Bilge et al. [20]. An appropriate amount of 0.2 mM DPPH ethanol solution and sample were mixed in equal parts and incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was calculated at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer. An equal volume of ethanol was used to replace the DPPH reagent for the sample control. An equal volume of water was used to replace the sample for the blank control. The DPPH radical scavenging activity was determined as follows:(4)DPPHradicalscavengingactivity%=1-Ai-AjAc×100%where Ai is the absorbance of the sample; Aj is the absorbance of the sample control and Ac is the absorbance of the blank control.

The ABTS radical scavenging activity was determined according to the method of Górecki et al. [22]. Some 0.2 mL of coffee dilution was added to 3.8 mL of ABTS reagent and then kept at 25 °C in the dark for 6 min. The absorbance was determined at 734 nm using a spectrophotometer. The blank control was set up using water, instead of the sample. The ABTS radical scavenging activity was determined as follows:(5)ABTSradicalscavengingactivity%=A0-AxA0×100%where Ax is the absorbance of the sample and A0 is the absorbance of control.

Flavor

Non-volatile flavor

Caffeine, CGA, trigonelline, and organic acids were determined by HPLC (Agilent 1260, Agilent Tech., Ca, USA) using a C18 column (Welch XB − C18, 4.6 mm × 250 mm, particle size 5 μm, Agilent Technologies, Inc., USA). The mobile phase flow rate of 1.0 mL/min with an injection volume of 10 µL. Caffeine: the determination column temperature was 25 °C, with a detection wavelength of 272 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 24 % methanol (mobile phase A) and 76 % ultrapure water (mobile phase B). CGA: the determination column temperature was 35 °C, with a detection wavelength of 327 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 90 % 0.5 % (v/v) acetic acid (mobile phase A) and 10 % acetonitrile (mobile phase B). Trigonelline: the determination column temperature was 30 °C, with a detection wavelength of 260 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 12 % methanol (mobile phase A) and 88 % ultrapure water (mobile phase B). Organic acid: the determination column temperature was 30 °C, with a detection wavelength of 210 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 25 % methanol (mobile phase A) and 75 % 0.1 % (v/v) phosphoric acid (mobile phase B).

Volatile flavor

The volatile compounds in coffee were obtained by a triple quadrupole gas chromatograph (Shimadzu, TQ8040, Japan), according to the method of Cheong et al. [23], with slight modifications. Five milliliters of coffee liquid was filled into a 25-mL headspace container (4-Methyl-2-pentanol was used as an internal standard). After being at 40 °C for 3 min, the temperature was raised to 130 °C at 5 °C/min without grasping 230 °C at 4 °C/min for 4 min, and 250 °C at 10 °C/min for 5 min. There was no shunt, the pressure was 2.4 KPa, and the carrier gas (He) flow rate was 1 mL/min. The ion source was subjected to electron bombardment, with an electron energy of 70 eV, an ion source temperature of 230 °C, an activation voltage of 1.5 V, and a mass scanning range of 35–550 m/z for the mass spectrometry.

Based on the qualitative and quantitative analysis of the volatile components in the coffee liquid and the aroma thresholds of these components reported in the literature, the OAV of each substance was calculated as follows:(6)OAVi=CiOTiwhere OAVi defines the aroma activity value of substance i; Ci is the concentration of substance i in coffee liquid, μg/L, and OTi is the aroma threshold value of substance i in water, μg/L.

Near-infrared in situ real-time monitoring

The in situ real-time online monitoring device for NIR spectroscopy used in this study is shown in Fig. 1. Under optimal ultrasound process conditions, 50 g of coffee grounds and 750 mL of water were placed into a 1000 mL beaker for 90 min of UE. The fiber optic probe was immersed in the solution, with spectral information automatically saved every minute. Simultaneously, 3 mL samples were taken every time during the extraction process, totaling 90 samples. Each sample was centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 rpm, and the supernatant was retained for chemical analysis at −20 °C. Spectral collection parameters were as follows: spectral range of 900–2500 nm, resolution of 6.4 nm, optical path of 4 mm, with 3 scans average to obtain the original spectral information. Prior to every NIR measurement, the background spectrum was gathered using distilled water and the identical experimental setup. TDS and CGA were measured for 90 samples, and the collected NIR spectral information was processed and analyzed using NIRSA to select the best preprocessing based on the modeling results and to establish an in situ monitoring model by dividing the calibration set and prediction set.

Statistical analysis

Every measurement was performed in triplicate and data were expressed as means ± standard errors. Using SPSS software 22.0, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Durkan's test at a 5 % level of significance were executed. The NIR spectra were analyzed and processed using Matlab 2009b software. Plotting of the findings data was accomplished using Origin 2024 and Microsoft Excel 2021.

Results and discussion

Effect of ultrasonic-assisted extraction on CBC extraction level

The UE CBC process was optimized using TDS and EY as evaluation indexes. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the different ultrasonic modes had a significant effect on the TDS and EY of CBC (p < 0.05). Among the tested mono- and dual-frequency ultrasonic modes, the CBC supported by the dual-frequency (20/28 kHz) occurred the highest TDS (1.53 %) and EY (19.38 %), followed by the mono-frequency of 28 kHz, while the lowest TDS and EY was found at the dual-frequency (20/52 kHz). The results showed that multi-modal ultrasonic treatment could effectively enhance the coffee extraction during the cold extraction process. This may be attributed to the cavitation and mechanical effects of ultrasound, which can accelerate the extraction of bioactive substances by breaking the cell wall, reducing the particle size, and expediting the mass transfer process [24]. Ultrasonic frequencies used for extraction generally range from 20 to 100 kHz, and several studies have shown that lower frequency combinations are more effective in UE [15], [25]. Therefore, dual-frequency (20/28 kHz) was chosen as the optimal ultrasonic treatment frequency.Fig. 2Effect of ultrasound operating mode (a), power density (b), material-to-liquid ratio (c) and ultrasound time (d) on the cold brew effect (total dissolved solids content and extraction yield) of coffee. Bars with different letters are significantly different (p < 0.05).

The effect of ultrasonic power density on the TDS and EY of CBC was investigated under the conditions of dual-frequency (20/28 kHz), with a material-to-liquid ratio of 1:15 and ultrasonic treatment time of 40 min. As depicted in Fig. 2 (b), the TDS and EY of CBC increased significantly (p < 0.05) with the increased ultrasonic power density, attaining the highest levels of 1.53 % and 19.38 % at 320 W/L, respectively. This may be attributed to the increased ultrasonic power density enhancing the mechanical effect, thereby accelerating the dissolution and release of soluble substances. However, when the ultrasonic power density exceeded 320 W/L, some active substances were degraded due to the vigorous action of ultrasound, resulting in a decrease in the TDS and EY [8], [26], [27]. Therefore, 320 W/L was chosen as the optimal ultrasonic treatment power density.

The TDS of CBC significantly decreased as the material-to-liquid ratio increased (p < 0.05) under the treatment conditions of dual-frequency (20/28 kHz), power density of 320 W/L, and ultrasonic time of 40 min. Conversely, the EY initially increased and subsequently decreased (Fig. 2c). Poor permeation and mass transfer result in a low EY when low material-liquid ratio [8]. However, the EY is significantly enhanced when the material-liquid ratio is appropriately increased. Conversely, when the material-liquid ratio was excessive, the internal diffusion distance was extended by the increased solvent volume, which had a detrimental impact on the mass transfer effect and resulted in a lower EY [7], [28], [29]. Therefore, 1:15 was chosen as the optimal ultrasonic treatment material-to-liquid ratio.

Under the conditions of dual-frequency of 20/28 kHz, power density of 320 W/L, and material-liquid ratio of 1:15, TDS and EY maintained an increasing trend with longer ultrasonic time (Fig. 2d). The maximum values of TDS and EY were reached at 50 min of ultrasonic time, which were 1.63 % and 20.52 %, respectively. Extending the duration of ultrasound and cavitation could enhance the diffusivity of solvents in planar matrices, thereby increasing the fragmentation and solubility of bioactive compounds [30]. However, excessively long treatment times could also lead to enhanced thermal and mechanical effects of ultrasound, leading to the decomposition of bioactive and negatively affecting EY [31], [32]. Therefore, 50 min was chosen as the optimal ultrasonic treatment time.

In summary, the optimized conditions for UE were dual-frequency (20/28 kHz), power density of 320 W/L, material-liquid ratio of 1:15, and ultrasound time of 50 min. Under these conditions, the coffee TDS and EY reached 1.63 % and 20.52 %, respectively. Comparatively, the TDS and EY of 5-h continuous negative pressure cold brew in the factory were 1.61 % and 20.33 %, respectively, with no significant difference (p > 0.05) between the two extraction methods. Achieving the corresponding TDS and EY, UE CBC increased efficiency by 83.33 %, contributing to the reduction of production costs and energy consumption. Next, the quality and flavor of the UE and TE CBC in this study were systematically analyzed.

Comparison of the quality of the UE and TE CBC

Physicochemical quality

Radar illustrations were employed to visualize the multidimensional data, which included a variety of quality indicators for the coffee liquid obtained from UE and TE CBC (Fig. 3a). The pH value after ultrasonic treatment (5.21) was non-significant (p > 0.05) to the control (5.22), which is fitting with the findings revealed by Claudimara et al [33]. Generally, the pH values of the coffee liquid from different coffee bean varieties brewed using varying methodologies were around 5.5, suggesting that ultrasonic treatment did not induce pH changes during the coffee extraction process [34], [35].Fig. 3Radar chart of physicochemical, nutritional, and antioxidant indicators of ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UE) cold brew coffee (CBC) and traditional extraction (TE) CBC (a), color comparison (b).

Radar chart of physicochemical, nutritional, and antioxidant indicators of ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UE) cold brew coffee (CBC) and traditional extraction (TE) CBC (a), color comparison (b).

During the roasting process, the Maillard reaction and Strecker's degradation transform coffee bean from green to caramel [34]. Consequently, the CBC showed hazelnut and caramel colors, as shown in Fig. 3(b). Colorimeter results indicated that CBC from UE had slightly higher L and a values and slightly lower b values compared with CBC from TE, indicating that UE had brighter colors with a shift towards the red and blue direction. Additionally, the ultrasonically treated samples appeared light brown and caramel-colored, while non-ultrasonically treated samples were dark brown and chocolate-colored, as shown in Fig. 3(b). This difference may be due to the emulsification of oil by ultrasound, resulting in more oil extraction and thus color variation [10], [21], [36].

The viscosity after ultrasonic treatment was also lower than that of control coffee. This could be explained by the cavitation effect of ultrasound, which broke down the cell walls and extracted more soluble substances, such as polysaccharides and lipids. The viscosity of the CBC under the appropriate ultrasonic treatment could be reduced by the oils' ability to encapsulate the fat-soluble vitamins and aromatics, resulting in the formation of an emulsion [20].

Nutritional quality

The lipid content of the two cold extraction procedures differed significantly (p < 0.05), with UE CBC having a lipid content than was over 4 times that of TE CBC. This increased lipid content impacts taste and contributes to color variations. During extraction, the lipids form emulsions that encapsulate volatile constituents and fat-soluble vitamins, resulting in a more persistent aroma and influencing the aroma, stability, and viscosity of the coffee liquid [37]. The protein content after ultrasonic treatment was slightly reduced, and certain studies had demonstrated that there was no necessary correlation between protein content and coffee flavor quality [20]. Phenols contribute to the antioxidant properties and also impart a more astringent taste. The total polyphenol content of ultrasonic treatment CBC was slightly higher than that of control. CBC exhibited a higher TDS under the appropriate ultrasonic treatment, with sugar serving as the main component, which led to a higher total sugar content compared to TE CBC. In comparison with the control, the total acid content of CBC after ultrasonic treatment was significantly decreased (p < 0.05), which may indicate a weaker acidity in UE CBC. Total acid content is a critical factor in evaluating acidity. These characteristics suggest that UE CBC may have lower acidity, higher sweetness, richer flavor, and a smoother mouthfeel.

Antioxidant activity

The difference in ABTS radical scavenging rate between the two cold-brewed liquid was not significant (P > 0.05), while the DPPH radical scavenging rate of UE CBC was lower than that of TE CBC. This finding suggests that the scavenging rate of the DPPH radicals was significantly influenced by the various cold brew methods, whereas the ABTS radicals were not significantly affected. The antioxidant capacity of coffee is affected by a variety of factors, including the content of CGA, organic acids, total phenolics, and coffee melanoidins, which all contribute to the overall potency of antioxidant properties, despite the fact that ultrasound aids in the release of phenolics.

The cavitation effect of ultrasound and the mechanical breaking effect compensated for the time of TE, resulting in differing impacts on the yields of various compounds in coffee. The extraction rates of total polyphenols, total sugars, and total lipids were higher under the appropriate ultrasonic treatment compared to the control. Conversely, the extraction rates of total acids and total proteins were slightly lower in UE CBC. Since total sugars and total lipids significantly influence flavor and taste, UE CBC with a slightly sweet and soft flavor profile. Considering the extraction efficiency and yield of each active substance, ultrasonic treatment could serve as a viable alternative method to improve extraction efficiency and accelerate the commercialization of CBC.

Flavor quality

Non-volatile flavors

Caffeine, CGA, and trigonelline are the three main bioactive substances in coffee. Caffeine and trigonelline are the main alkaloids in coffee, both contributing to coffee's characteristic bitterness [38]. Caffeine is particularly crucial in defining the coffee flavor. The acidic compounds in coffee consist of CGA and various organic acids. The taste receptors are unable to perceive CGA, which is responsible for the primary astringency of coffee. In contrast, other organic acids collaborate to produce the bright acidic flavor of the coffee. The content of non-volatile flavor compounds is shown in Table 1. The caffeine content of UE CBC was slightly lower than that of TE CBC, resulting in a moderately bitter and more palatable taste for UE CBC. The slightly higher levels of CGA and trigonelline in UE CBC may have enhanced its astringency. UE CBC had slightly higher levels of DL-tartaric and gallic acids and slightly lower levels of malic, citric, succinic and fumaric acids. Volatile flavors

HS-SPEM-GC/MS was used to qualitatively and quantitatively analyze the volatile contents in the coffee liquid obtained from the two extraction methods. The contents and types of volatile components were retrieved and analyzed by the NIST17 spectral library, as shown in Table 2 and Fig. 4. A total of 87 volatile compounds were detected in the two coffee samples, including alcohols (5), phenols (6), ketones (14), pyrroles (5), acids (4), esters (5), aldehydes (6), pyrazines (11), pyridines (3), furans (10), and other compounds (18). Seventy volatile compounds were detected in UE CBC, compared to 59 volatile compounds in TE CBC. Furans, aldehydes, and alcohols were the most abundant volatile components, contributing roasted, almond, burnt, and bitter-spicy flavors to the coffee, respectively. Furans were formed by the thermal degradation of carbohydrates, ascorbic acid, or unsaturated fatty acids during coffee roasting [39]. The highest levels in the coffee liquid were 2-Furancarboxaldehyde, 5-methyl- and ethanone, 1-(2-furanyl)-, which provide a distinctive roasted flavor. Although furfural and 2-Furanmethanol were present at high levels, their higher sensory thresholds give the coffee a certain almondy and burnt flavor, respectively. Among the esters, 2-Furanmethanol, acetate was the most abundant, imparting a fruity flavor to the coffee. Pyrazine formed by thermal decomposition of amino acids, contributed a rich aroma of nuts and roasting. In the composition of coffee flavors, phenolic compounds released during coffee roasting process are of significant importance. Heterocyclic compounds such as pyridine and pyrrole imparted characteristics of grilled meat and cookies, respectively, as well as some burnt and smoky flavors. Among the phenolic and ketone compounds, phenol, 2-methoxy- and 2,3-pentanedione were dominant, yielding caramel and cheese flavors. Modeling of TDS and CGA during UE using NIR in situ real-time monitoring

Analysis of off-line determination of TDS and CGA during UE process

The changes in TDS and CGA content during the cold extraction process are shown in Fig. 5(a). With the prolongation of extraction time, the content of all indicators showed a fluctuating upward trend. TDS increased with increasing ultrasonic time, indicating a continuous extraction from the coffee and a resulting increase in concentration. The change of CGA was more gradual, showing a slow increase in the first 40 min of ultrasound. Following this, there was a modest decrease between 40 and 50 min, followed by stabilization and gradual increase beyond 50 min. This trend may be due to the strong cavitation effect of ultrasound partially decomposing the CGA while simultaneously extracting new CGA. The maximum TDS and CGA observed during the extraction process were approximately 1.60 % and 1.24 mg/mL, respectively. Effect of different raw spectral pre-processing methods on the constructed model

The raw NIR spectra of the UE are shown in Fig. 5(b). The raw spectra collected in the NIR contained background information and noise, necessitating preprocessing to develop a stable, reliable, and accurate model. Four different pre-processing methods, 1stDer, 2ndDer, SG, and centralization, were selected to process the raw spectra, and the results are shown in Fig. 5(c)–(f). 1stDer and 2ndDer processing amplified the signal and noise of the spectra, leading to the loss of some effective information due to over-processing. In contrast, SG preprocessing produced spectral curves that relatively smooth, effectively eliminating noise and background interference, enhancing spectral features, and concentrating spectral information. The predictive potential and stability of the model were enhanced by the centralization preprocessing, which increased the differences between sample spectra. The preprocessed spectra were modeled using PLS to compare the modeling effects among the 4 spectral preprocessed methods, the 2ndDer pre-processing yielded the lowest Rc (0.9169) and Rp (0.8696) and highest RMSECV (0.0773) and RMSEP (0.0878), indicating the poorest modeling effect. Centralization pre-processing resulted in the highest Rp (0.9514) and lowest RMSECV (0.0694) and RMSEP (0.0631), indicating the best modeling effect. Therefore, centralization pre-processing was selected for subsequent modeling.

Quantitative modelling and predictive modelling of target parameters

Before building the corresponding correction and prediction models, the spectral information needed to be divided into sample sets. A total of 90 sets of spectral data were collected during UE, and the optimal division of the sample set might vary due to the different feature spectra of TDS and CGA. After removing three anomalous samples, were compared the results of the 4 algorithms: K-S, DUPLEX, RS, and SPXY. The sample set was divided with a correction set to a prediction set ratio of 2:1. The model constructed by dividing the sample set in these 4 methods exhibited good stability, as evidenced by the high correlation between the calibration set and the prediction set (Table 4). However, the prediction ability of the models was better when the TDS sample set was divided using SPXY and the CGA sample set was divided using RS. Therefore, SPXY and RS were chosen to divide the TDS and CGA sample sets, respectively. Conclusions

UE has the potential to considerably enhance the efficiency of coffee production. Compared to the control group, the 20/28 kHz dual-frequency mode of operation of a countercurrent ultrasonic device could shorten the extraction time, and the cold brew efficiency increased by 83.33 %. Notably, UE CBC had a higher total phenol and total sugar content, and the total lipid content was higher significantly (p < 0.05) compared to that of the control, which allowed coffee to exhibit a unique light brown and caramel color. Overall, UE CBC maintained the quality and flavor profile of TE CBC. In addition, an in situ monitoring model was developed to monitor TDS and CGA levels. TDS was a critical metric for assessing the yield and quality of coffee during production. Additionally, CGA contributes to astringency, which was mitigated to ensure the extraction yield of coffee. The real-time monitoring of the extraction endpoints facilitated the intelligent control system of food processing. On the whole, this study attempts to contribute to the field of food processing and extraction technologies by introducing a green and efficient extraction method and a real-time monitoring approach, which may be applied in the industrial development and smooth production systems of cold brew coffee.
