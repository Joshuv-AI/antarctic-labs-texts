Positivity and long-term behaviour of a diffusion model with measure-valued nonlocal reaction term: applications in bioscience and engineering
Xiao Yang, Qiyao Peng, Sander C. Hille

Abstract
The behaviour is investigated of solutions to a diffusion equation on the real line with nonlocal and singular reaction term, i.e., given by a Dirac source or sink at the origin. It gives a simplified representation of for example a control system that senses concentration at a distance, but “intervenes” at the origin. Positivity of solutions (for positive initial conditions) cannot be guaranteed for all parameter settings in the model. We determine a parameter regime and conditions on the positive initial condition in terms of monotonicity and symmetry, that do allow us to conclude the positivity of the solution for all time. In addition, we provide conditions that ensure convergence of the system to a constant steady state (pointwise), outside the region of observation. Technically, we extensively use Laplace transform arguments to achieve these results. Keywords: reaction-diffusion equation, Laplace transform, positivity, Dirac delta measure, negative feedback, point source

Introduction

In various settings in (bio)science and engineering a modeller is confronted with the question how to represent a system in which “
entities
” (e.g., a chemical compound, type of biological cells) diffuse in the environment of a structure that can release and take up these entities, controlled by “observations” of their abundance nearby. For instance, cell-to-cell communication through signalling molecules follows this general pattern, for which the mathematical work uses mostly continuum modelling, or so-called macroscopic models, where cells are modelled by cell density in the partial differential equation (PDE). These models consider the uptake of the compounds by describing ligand-receptor binding kinetics on the cell “boundary”, i.e., the cell membrane. The governing equations include the dynamics of the cells, receptors on the cell membrane and the concentration of signalling molecules (e.g., ligand); see
[
23
,
18
]
.

One may also think for example of the vascular system in animals that exchanges various compounds (e.g., oxygen, carbon dioxide, drugs) between blood and surrounding tissue, regulating the permeability of the blood vessel wall; or how immune cells spread to regions where they are most needed, signalled by so-called cytokines, e.g., immune response in wound healing known as the inflammation phase. In this phase of wound healing, immune cells such as macrophages and lymphocytes enter the blood vessels from other parts of body and leave the blood circulation from the broken vessels at the wound edge to clear the bacteria
[
27
]
. Another example related to “exchanging behaviour” between blood vessels and their direct environment is provided by cancer cell metastasis. In every metastatic journey, intravasation and extravasation are the fundamental steps. That is, the cancer cell enters the blood circulation (intravasation) from one part of body, then it exits at the other (extravasation) to develop the secondary tumour via the junction between the epithelial cells of the blood vessels
[
33
,
15
]
; see Fig.
1.1
for an illustration.

In chemical engineering, one may wish to control the concentration of a compound in a solution (or the temperature) as measured by a few localized sensors in the bath, by adjusting the concentration (or temperature) in a liquid flowing through a permeable tube system in the bath; see
[
14
]
.

A mathematical model of such a system would describe the dynamics of the density of entities (e.g., concentration of the compound) in the environment of the exchanging structure most naturally as a diffusion equation with suitable (dynamic) boundary conditions at the interface between structure and environment. If the structure has complicated or changing geometry in time, theoretical tractability easily becomes cumbersome, followed by various challenges in numerical simulations, such as computational efficiency and different numerical schemes to handle the changing and moving geometry (e.g., cutFEM
[

and weighted shifted boundary method

If the exchanging structure has symmetries, reduction of the dimensionality of the computational domain may also be made, resulting in a substantial increase in computational efficiency – and often an improvement in theoretical tractability. For example, if the above mentioned vasculature or tubing in the bath consists of parallel tubes over a sufficiently long stretch, a three-dimensional description may be replaced well by a diffusive model for the density in a two-dimensional cross section orthogonal to the tubing. If the tubing is in a plane, a further reduction to a one-dimensional description for the average concentration perpendicular to this plane is reasonable (see Fig.

These considerations of model reduction, motivated by the aforementioned examples from biology and engineering, led us to study the following “prototypical” initial value problem, which is central in the current paper. It concerns an isotropic diffusion equation on
\mathbb{R}
with a nonlocal and non-autonomous reaction term that is singular, in the sense that it is Dirac-measure-valued:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}v&=D\partial_{x}^{2}v+\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}[v]\delta_{0},\quad&&\mbox{for }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R},\\

This paper expands on work that considered (in two-dimensional space) the diffusion equation with forced point sources or sinks – i.e., independent of the solution (cf.

•

Exhibiting an approach towards positivity of solutions to this particular type of nonlocal equations that allows providing conditions on the model data that guarantee positivity of the solution – generally not on all of

•

Providing conditions on

Our approach to both employs a Laplace transform argument. As we also view the positivity as “controlling the solution below a desired threshold”, it brings feedback control systems in mind, in which the variable being controlled is measured and compared with a preset target.

In a linear feedback system, by Laplace transform the study of its behaviour boils down to studying the so-called
transfer function
. Consider for example the system depicted in Fig.
1.2
.

The Laplace transformed system variables, denoted
X,Y,Z,W
, are related by the algebraic equations (for holomormic functions):

\left\{\begin{aligned} Z&=X-W,\\
W&=H_{1}Z,\\
Y&=H_{2}Z.\end{aligned}\right.

The first two equations result in

Z=\frac{X}{1+H_{1}},\qquad\mbox{hence}\quad X=(1+H_{1})Z.

The transfer function for this feedback system between input

H:=\frac{Y}{X}=\frac{H_{2}}{1+H_{1}}.

It will be similar transfer functions of which the study is essential for obtaining the positivity results for our model; see Section

. In particular, the key idea is, that one can study the behaviour of the solution by considering a convenient system with the same transfer function.

1.1
Mathematical assumptions

We make the following fundamental assumptions and choices for the intensity functional
\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}[v]
. First, we assume that it depends on
v
only through the solution at time
t
:
v_{t}:=v(t,\cdot)
. Thus, no time delay is considered. Next, the nonlocal dependence on
v_{t}
is limited to its value (“measurement”) at two distinct points, taken symmetrically around the Dirac point
x=0
for convenience, say at
x=\pm R
. Note that point evaluation is continuous in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, because of the Sobolev embedding of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
into
C_{b}^{0}(\mathbb{R})
(see
[
1
]
, Theorem 4.12, p.85). Hence, we assume:

(R1)

There is a function
(t,x_{1},x_{2})\longmapsto f_{t}(x_{1},x_{2})
such that

\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}[v]=f_{t}\bigl(v_{t}(-R),v_{t}(R)\bigr).

Since the modelled system is thought to be symmetric around the Dirac point
x=0
,
f_{t}
is symmetric:
f_{t}(x_{1},x_{2})=f_{t}(x_{2},x_{1})
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and all
t\geqslant 0
.

Moreover, we assume the existence of a (positive) “
asymptotically steady state
’:

(R2)

There exists
\bar{v}\geqslant 0
such that

f_{t}(\bar{v},\bar{v})\rightarrow 0,\quad\mbox{as }t\rightarrow+\infty.

(1.2)

This will ensure that the solution
v_{t}
to System
(
1.1
)
will converge (in appropriate sense) to the constant function
\bar{v}
as
t\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447pt}+\infty
. Note that the constant functions are not in the state space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, generally, System
(
1.1
)
is not autonomous. Hence, it will not have a steady state in the usual sense.
Since we wish to focus on establishing an approach for proving positivity of solutions to nonlocal equations of the class studied, we want to take
f_{t}
as simple as possible:

(R3)

f_{t}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\nabla f_{t}(x,x)
is independent of
t
.

Note that the symmetry of
f_{t}
from (R1) implies (with the first part of (R3)) that
\partial_{x_{1}}f_{t}=\partial_{x_{2}}f_{t}
on the diagonal
\{(x,x)\mid x\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Then, for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
, and writing
u:=v-c
for the deviation of
v
from
c
, one has

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}[v]

\displaystyle=f_{t}(c,c)+\nabla f_{t}(c,c)\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{u_{t}}(-R)\\
u_{t}(R)\end{array}\right]+\mathrm{h.o.t.}

\displaystyle\approx\Phi_{c}(t)+a_{c}\bigl[u_{t}(-R)+u_{t}(R)\bigr]

(1.5)

for small
u_{t}(\pm R)
, for some
a_{c}\in\mathbb{R}
that is independent of time, because of the second part of Assumption (R3).

1.2
Question of positivity of solution in relation to a control problem

Consider System
(
1.1
)
with
\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}[v]
satisfying (R1) and (R3). Assume that it has the linearized form in Expression
(
1.5
)
. The constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
may represent a particular threshold value for the state variable
v
. In an application, the equations can represent a system in which the value of the state variable at position
x\in\mathbb{R}
are controlled by change at
x=0
only, based on deviation of the state variable from a desired overall value
c
(i.e., “
u
”) “measured” at location
x=-R
and
x=R
. A related control problem then is:

For given
\Phi_{c}(t)
and
v_{0}(x)\leqslant c
, is it possible to find a parameter value
a_{c}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
v
remains below the threshold
c
for all time on (at least) the spatial domain
\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus(-R,R)
?

In the context of this problem it is then convenient to define
u:=c-v
, as above, such that the condition
v\leqslant c
is satisfied if we show positivity of
u
on
\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus(-R,R)
. Then,
u
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}u&=D\partial_{x}^{2}u+\Psi_{t}[u]\delta_{0},\quad&&\mbox{for }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,x)&=u_{0}(x),&&\mbox{for }x\in\mathbb{R},\end{aligned}\right.

(1.6)

with
u_{0}:=c-v_{0}\geqslant 0
and

\Psi_{t}[u]:=\Phi(t)-a\bigl[u(t,-R)+u(t,R)].

(1.7)

Natural conditions are that
\Phi(t)\geqslant 0
, representing that the system tends to increase
u
, i.e., to move
v
to a lower value than
c
. A parameter value
a\leqslant 0
represents yet another tendency for the system to move
v
below
c
. It is clear intuitively, that then
u
will remain positive for all time when
u_{0}\geqslant 0
. The interesting case is, whether positivity of
u
can be guaranteed for some
a>0
. This represents a situation in which the system tends to increase
v
, based upon the measurements at
x=\pm R
, bringing it “close” to
c
, while at the same time “steadily” decreasing
v
through the forcing
\Phi(t)
. Thus, in the setting
a>0
there is negative feedback.

Non-dimensionalization of the model

We shall be concerned with System
(
1.6
)
–
(
1.7
)

We non-dimensionalize System

Then System

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{\tau}\hat{u}&=\partial^{2}_{\xi}\hat{u}+\hat{\Psi}[\hat{u}](\tau)\delta_{0},\quad&&(\tau,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R},\\

\hat{\Psi}[\hat{u}](\tau):=\hat{\Phi}(\tau)-\hat{a}\bigl[\hat{u}(\tau,1)+\hat{u}(\tau,-1)\bigr]

\hat{\Phi}(\tau):=\frac{t^{*}}{u^{*}R}\Phi(t),\qquad\hat{a}:=\frac{t^{*}a}{R}=\frac{aR}{D}.

such that
\lim_{\tau\rightarrow+\infty}\hat{\Phi}(\tau)=1

From this point we shall work on the dimensionless model. For ease of presentation, we shall drop the accents and return to write

)

In

Remark 2.5

Within the range of

[\frac{1}{2},1]
. Thus, it violates the monotonicity condition on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
of Theorem
2.2
. As a consequence of this strong asymmetry and violation of the mentioned monotonicity, during the diffusion process, the point source drives the solution
u
below zero.

3
Employing Laplace transform for proving positivity

Due to the nonlocal term in the reaction-diffusion equation, classical methods to obtain positivity of solutions cannot be applied to System
(
1.6
)
–
(
1.7
)
. Moreover, it can be anticipated that the nonlocal character inhibits the
global
positivity of the solution. However, since that system is intended as approximation of a model with a spatially extended object that secretes and takes-up compounds from its environment, only the values and behaviour of the solution in our model on the part of space that is “external” to the object is of interest. As the overview of main results shows, we can guarantee positivity of the solution on this external space in a particular regime for the uptake parameter
a
.

To do so, in this work, we utilize extensively the Laplace transform, enabled by the essentially linear character of the uptake. In this section we shall briefly recall the results from Laplace Transform Theory that are key to our approach. Our main reference for these is
5
.

Let
f\in L_{{\mathrm{loc}}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0})
. One says that
f
has Laplace transform
F:=\mathcal{L}\{f\}
if for some
s\in\mathbb{C}
the limit

F(s)=\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s):=\lim_{T\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-st}f(t)\mathrm{d}t

(3.1)

exists. The
abscissa of convergence
of
f
is

\mathrm{abs}(f):=\inf\bigl\{{\mathrm{Re}}(s)\mid s\in\mathbb{C},F(s)\mbox{ exists}\bigr\}.

With the convention that
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
,
f
has a Laplace transform if and only if
\mathrm{abs}(f)<+\infty
. In that case, the Laplace integral
(
3.1
)
converges for any
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
{\mathrm{Re}}(s)>\mathrm{abs}(f)
(see
[
5
]
, Proposition 1.4.1). If
f
is of
exponential order
, i.e., there exists
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and
M\geqslant 0
such that

|f(t)|\leqslant Me^{\omega t}\quad\mbox{for a.e. }t\geqslant 0,

then
\mathrm{abs}(f)\leqslant\omega
.
F(s)
is a holomorphic function on
\{s\in\mathbb{C}\mid{\mathrm{Re}}(s)>\mathrm{abs}(f)\}
(see
[
5
]
, Theorem 1.5.1).

The function essentially uniquely determines its Laplace transform, and vice versa:

Theorem 3.1

(Uniqueness Theorem)
.

Let
f,g\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
with
\mathrm{abs}(f)<+\infty
and
\mathrm{abs}(g)<+\infty
. Let
r_{0}>\max\bigl(\mathrm{abs}(f),\mathrm{abs}(g)\bigr)
. If
\mathcal{L}\{f\}(r)=\mathcal{L}\{g\}(r)
for all
r\in(r_{0},+\infty)
, then
f=g
.

A slight rephrasing is given by

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
f\neq g\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
. If
\mathcal{L}\{f\}
and
\mathcal{L}\{g\}
exist, then
\mathcal{L}\{f\},\mathcal{L}\{g\}
do not coincide for some
s\in\mathbb{C}
.

Thus, the Laplace transform is invertible on the set

\mathcal{H}_{hs}^{\mathcal{L}}:=\mathcal{L}\bigl\{\{f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})\mid\mathrm{abs}(f)<+\infty\}\bigr\},

which consists of functions
\mathbb{C}\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathbb{C}
that are holomorphic on some halve space. One says that
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F\}:=f
is the
inverse Laplace transform
of
F
, and that
f
and
F
are a
Laplace transform pair
.
The range
\mathcal{H}_{hs}^{\mathcal{L}}
is hard to describe precisely, but a class of holomorphic functions can be identified that is obtained through Laplace transform:

Theorem 3.3

(Paley-Wiener)
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and
F:\{s\in\mathbb{C}\mid{\mathrm{Re}}(s)>\omega\}\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447pt}\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function. If

\sup_{\sigma>\omega}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}|F(s)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s<+\infty,

then there exists
f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
and
C>0
such that
\mathcal{L}\{f\}=F
and
|f(t)|\leqslant Ce^{\omega t}
.

Proofs of Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
can be reduced immediately from the results in
5
, Theorem 1.7.3 and Theorem 1.8.3, respectively.

Thus, Laplace transform converts functions from a “time domain” (often named as
t-
domain) to a complex frequency domain (often named as
s-
domain). For any function
F(s)
in the
s-
domain, if it is the Laplace transform of a function
f(t)
in
t
-domain, then
F(s)
is a holomorphic function on some complex right half-plane
\{s\in\mathbb{C}\mid{\mathrm{Re}}(s)>\omega\}
. Properties of the holomorphic function
F(s)
translate into associated properties of
f(t)
in the time domain; Figure
3.1
sketches a typical flowchart of applying Laplace transform to answer a question.

Figure 3.1:
Flowchart of applying Laplace transform, where
\mathcal{L}
represents Laplace transform and
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
is inverse Laplace transform.

Particularly, for linear ordinary differential equations as found for example in Electrical Engineering, Laplace transform helps simplify the problem by converting the differential equations to algebraic equations. In this paper, the use of the Laplace transform derives similarly from its property to turn convolution of functions into ordinary multiplication of (holomorphic) functions:

\mathcal{L}\{f*g\}(s)=\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s)\cdot\mathcal{L}\{g\}(s).

The solution to the diffusion equation on
\mathbb{R}
is given by a convolution integral equation in the time domain. Applying Laplace transform turns this into an algebraic equation of holomorphic functions in the
s-
domain.

Positivity of a function in the
t
-domain can be characterised by the property of complete monotonicity of its Laplace transform. A function
g(x)
is called
completely monotonic
on the interval
(x_{0},+\infty)
in
\mathbb{R}
if

(-1)^{n}g^{(n)}(x)\geqslant 0\qquad\mbox{for all }x>x_{0},n\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.2)

See also
[
5
]
, Section 2.7.
The necessity of this property is exhibited as follows

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
with
\mathcal{L}\{f\}
exists for some
s_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
. If
f\geqslant 0
almost everywhere, then
F:=\mathcal{L}\{f\}
is completely monotonic on
({\mathrm{Re}}(s_{0}),+\infty)
.

Proof.

By the above,
F
is analytic on
({\mathrm{Re}}(s_{0}),+\infty)
. Therefore, for any
x>{\mathrm{Re}}(s_{0})
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}F}{\mathrm{d}s^{n}}(x)=\left.\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial s^{n}}(e^{-st}f(t))\mathrm{d}t\,\right|_{s=x}=(-1)^{n}\int_{0}^{+\infty}x^{n}e^{-xt}f(t)\mathrm{d}t.

The integrand
x^{n}e^{-xt}f(t)
is nonnegative a.e.

\blacksquare

The former result can be strenghtened to a full characterization of positivity of
f
, by means of the Post-Widder Inversion Theorem.

Proposition 3.5

(Characterization of positivity by Laplace transform)
.

Let
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
such that
\mathrm{abs}(f)<+\infty
. Then,
f\geqslant 0
if and only if
\mathcal{L}\{f\}
is completely monotonic on
(\mathrm{abs}(f),+\infty)
.

Proof.

The necessity has been shown already in Proposition
3.4
. For sufficiency, note that almost every
t\geqslant 0
is a Lebesgue point of
f
. Take such a point
t>0
. For sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n/t>\mathrm{abs}(f)
. The Post-Widder Inversion Formula (see Theorem
A.4
) yields

f(t)=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}}\left({\frac{n}{t}}\right)^{n+1}F^{(n)}\left({\frac{n}{t}}\right)\geqslant 0,

by complete monotonicity. Thus,
f\geqslant 0
in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
.

\blacksquare

The following result is crucial for properly rephrasing later on our mathematical problem at hand.

Lemma 3.6

(
5
Prop. 1.6.8, p.40)
.

Assume
f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
of exponential order. Let

g(t):=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\tau e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t^{3}}}f(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau.

Then
g\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
is well-defined,
G:=\mathcal{L}\{g\}
also exists, and
G(s)=F(\sqrt{s})
.

Proof.

By
(
C.3
)
in Lemma
C.2
,

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\tau e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t^{3}}}f(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\right)e^{-st}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\tau e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t^{3}}}e^{-st}\mathrm{d}t\right)f(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\sqrt{s}\tau}f(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau=F(\sqrt{s}).

Note that we apply Fubini-Tonelli theorem in order to swap the integrals.

\blacksquare

Corollary 3.7
.

Given
f,\xi(\cdot,\tau)\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
that are of exponential order and
\mathcal{L}\{\xi(\cdot,\tau)\}(s)=e^{-\eta(s)\tau}
, then

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{F\circ\eta\}(t)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\xi(t,\tau)f(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau.

4
Positivity of solutions on part of the domain

For a nonlocal model like System
(
1.6
)
–
(
1.7
)
one cannot expect to find that solutions for positive initial conditions, with positive forcing
\Phi(t)
to remain positive on the whole domain
\mathbb{R}
, in general. See e.g., the counter-example at the end of Section
2
. However, numerical simulations suggest that positivity can be ensured in many cases
on a subdomain of
\mathbb{R}
that contains “the environment” of the original “exchanging structure”, i.e.,
\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus(-1,1)
. This will be satisfactory in applications, as the system is intended to approximate a model that is defined on this subdomain (only).

The mathematical techniques that we are aware of that are employed to prove positivity of solutions for PDEs with local reaction terms, like those e.g., in
[
32
,
11
,
22
]
, are not suited to prove positivity only on a subdomain, nor are able to deal with nonlocal reaction terms. Therefore, in this work we invented an approach suitable for the nonlocal reaction term in System
(
1.6
)
–
(
1.7
)
and applicable to subdomains.

4.1
Pointwise positivity on the boundary suffices

Recall that we work with the non-dimensional equations, which – improperly speaking – effectively amounts to taking
R=1
and
D=1
. Let
I
be an open interval in
\mathbb{R}
whose closure does not contain
0
, the support of the Dirac measure in the reaction term. We approach the question of positivity of a solution of System
(
1.6
)
–
(
1.7
)
on the subdomain
I
by means of the observation:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
u_{0}\geqslant 0
on
I
. The mild solution
u
to System
(
1.6
)
–
(
1.7
)
is positive on
I
for all
t\geqslant 0
if and only if
u(t,p)\geqslant 0
for all boundary points
p
of
I
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Note that the mild solution
u(t)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and that by a Sobolev Embedding (see
[
1
]
Theorem 4.12, p.85)
u
is continuous. Hence, point evaluation of
u(t)
at
p\in\partial I
is defined.
Since
0\not\in I
,
u
on
I
is the solution to
\partial_{t}w=\Delta w
with initial condition
w(0)=u(0)|_{I}\geqslant 0
and Dirichlet boundary conditions
w(t,p)=u(t,p)
at
p\in\partial I
for all
t\geqslant 0
. According to the comparison principle (see Corollary 2.5 of
[
31
]
), if
u(t,p)\geqslant 0
for all
t\geqslant 0
and
p\in\partial I
, then
w(t)\geqslant 0
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

\blacksquare

Define

u_{+}(t):=u(t,1)+u(t,-1),\qquad u_{-}(t):=u(t,1)-u(t,-1).

(4.1)

As an immediate consequence of Lemma
4.1
one obtains that “
u_{+}(t)\geqslant 0
for all
t\geqslant 0
” is a necessary condition for positivity of the solution
u
on the subdomain
\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus(-1,1)
. The following corollary formulates a slightly stronger result.

Corollary 4.2
.

The following are equivalent:

(
i
)

u(t,x)\geqslant 0
for all
t\geqslant 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus(-1,1)
.

(
ii
)

u(t,-1)\geqslant 0
and
u(t,1)\geqslant 0
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

(
iii
)

u_{+}(t)\geqslant|u_{-}(t)|
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

“(
i
)
\Leftrightarrow
(
ii
)” is Lemma
4.1
. For “(
ii
)
\Rightarrow
(
iii
)”, observe that
u(t,1)+2u(t,-1)\geqslant u(t,1)
, because
u(t,-1)\geqslant 0
. Then, by subtracting
u(t,-1)
on both sides, one gets
u_{+}(t)\geqslant u_{-}(t)
. Similarly, one can start with
2u(t,1)+u(t,-1)\geqslant u(t,-1)
and arrive at
u_{+}(t)\geqslant-u_{-}(t)
. The condition in (
iii
) implies that both
u(t,1)+u(t,-1)\geqslant u(t,1)-u(t,-1)
and
u(t,1)+u(t,-1)\geqslant u(t,-1)-u(t,1)
must hold. This gives (
ii
).

\blacksquare

We shall therefore start by finding conditions that ensure the positivity of
u_{+}
, which are necessary for
u
to be positive on
\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus(-1,1)
. Then we proceed to study the positivity on the boundary, “pointwise” at
x=-1
and
x=1
. This requires a careful analysis of the integral kernels in the expressions of
u(t,1)
and
u(t,-1)
.

4.2
Positivity of the sum:
u_{+}

Let
(e^{\Delta t})_{t\geqslant 0}
be the diffusion semigroup in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, generated by the Laplacian
\Delta
on the domain
D(\Delta):=H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. It is given explicitly in this setting as convolution with the Gauss-Weierstrass or heat kernel on
\mathbb{R}
given by

\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,\beta):=\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t}},\qquad(t>0,\beta\in\mathbb{R}),

as

e^{\Delta t}f(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,x-\tilde{x})f(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}.

The Sobolev space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
embeds continuously into
C_{b}^{0}(\mathbb{R})
, because of spatial dimension 1 (see Sobolev Embedding Theorem, e.g.,
[
1
]
, Theorem 4.12, p.85). Thus, point evaluation at
x=-1
and
x=1
are defined on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and yield continuous functionals.
In view of the Variation of Constants Formula
(
2.2
)
and substituting

\mathfrak{F}(u(\tau),\tau)=(\Phi(\tau)-a(u(\tau,1)+u(\tau,-1)))\delta_{0},

we obtain for
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
:

u(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}_{1}(t-\tau,x)(\Phi(\tau)-au(\tau,1)-au(\tau,-1))\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,x-\tilde{x})u_{0}(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}.

(4.2)

From the latter equation, we can obtain a renewal equation for
u_{+}
.

4.2.1
A Renewal Equation for
u_{+}

Since the mild solution
t\longmapsto u(t):\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447pt}H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is continuous, by the observation made above that point evaluation is continuous, we obtain that
t\longmapsto u_{+}(t)
is a continuous function. From Equation
(
4.2
)
, we derive that it solves the integral equation

u_{+}(t)=f_{0}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}h(t-\tau)u_{+}(\tau)\mathrm{d}s=v(t)+h*u_{+}(t),

(4.3)

where

h(t-\tau):=-2a\mathcal{K}_{1}(t-\tau,1),

(4.4)

and

f_{0}(t):=2\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{K}_{1}(t-\tau,1)\Phi(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,1-\tilde{x})+\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,1+\tilde{x}))u_{0}(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}.

(4.5)

We used here the symmetry
\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,-x)=\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,x)
. Note that
v\geqslant 0
under our assumptions (i.e.,
\Phi(t)\geqslant 0
and
u_{0}\geqslant 0
). It is a
Volterra integral equation of the second kind
, for which a comprehensive solution theory exists; see
[
25
]
. Our case is a more particular instance of the
convolution type
, also known as a (linear)
renewal equation
. The kernel
h
defined in
(
4.4
)
is in
L^{1}_{{\mathrm{loc}}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
as well as
\Phi\in L^{1}_{{\mathrm{loc}}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
. Hence, its
n
-fold convolution product,
h^{(*n)}:=h*\dots*h
, is defined. Consequently, we can solve
u_{+}
via

u_{+}=f_{0}+\mathcal{R}*f_{0},\qquad{\text{with }}\mathcal{R}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h^{(*n)},

(4.6)

if a suitable interpretation can be given to the series defining the so-called
resolvent kernel

\mathcal{R}
of the renewal equation (see e.g.,
[
17
]
, Section I.2).

First of all, we need the following results for later use:

Lemma 4.3
.

f_{0}
is of exponential order.

Proof.

For all
t>0
, the first integral in
(
4.5
)
is bounded by
C_{1}e^{\gamma_{1}t}
for some
C_{1},\gamma_{1}
, because
\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,1)
is bounded as a function of
t
and
\Phi\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
of some exponential order
\gamma_{1}
. Therefore, we just need to show that the second integral satisfies the same property for all
t>0
. In fact, we can show that the integral of each term is bounded. Because there is an embedding
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq C_{b}^{0}(\mathbb{R})\subseteq L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
(see the discussion at the beginning of Section
1.1
), we can apply Hölder’s inequality,

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,1\pm\tilde{x})u_{0}\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}\right|\leqslant\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}.

Due to Gauss integral, it is well known that
\|\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}}=1
independent of
t
. Thus,
f_{0}
is of exponential order, and hence belongs to
L^{\infty}_{{\mathrm{loc}}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 4.4
.

\mathcal{R}
is of exponential order.

Proof.

Inductively, we have

|h^{(*n)}(t)|\leqslant\frac{\|h\|_{\infty}^{n}t^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}.

Then

|\mathcal{R}(t)|\leqslant\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\|h\|_{\infty}^{n}t^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\|h\|_{\infty}{e^{\|h\|_{\infty}t}}.

In fact,
\mathcal{R}
is even a smooth integral kernel and also in
L^{\infty}_{{\mathrm{loc}}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
.

\blacksquare

Corollary 4.5
.

u_{+}
is of exponential order.

Proof.

As we explained above, Equation
(
4.3
)
can be solved by
(
4.6
)
. Then it follows from Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
. In particular,
u_{+}\in L^{\infty}_{{\mathrm{loc}}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
as well.

\blacksquare

If
a\leqslant 0
, then
h\geqslant 0
and consequently,
u_{+}\geqslant 0
, as can be immediately seen from
(
4.6
)
. However, the negative feedback (
a>0
) really complicates the analysis of positivity in the system, because now the series defining
\mathcal{R}
is alternating.

4.2.2
An expression for
u_{+}
in the
s
-domain

Corollary
4.5
guarantees that
u_{+}
is of exponential order. Thus, applying the Laplace transform to study integral equation

is now a most natural step to take. Necessary fundamental definitions and properties concerning Laplace Transform can be found in Section
3

U_{+}(s):=\mathcal{L}\{u_{+}\}(s),\quad\Theta(s):=\mathcal{L}\{\Phi\}(s),\quad F_{0}:=\mathcal{L}\{f_{0}\}(s),\quad\mbox{and}\quad Q(s,\beta):=\mathcal{L}\{\mathcal{K}_{1}(\cdot,\beta)\}(s),

where all Laplace transforms exist because the functions that are transformed are of exponential order (see Corollary
4.5
, Lemma
4.3
, and
(
C.2
)
). The convolution equation
(
4.3
)
then becomes an algebraic relation for the Laplace transforms:

U_{+}(s)=F_{0}(s)-2aQ(s,1)U_{+}(s),

(4.7)

where

Q(s,\beta)=\frac{e^{-\beta\sqrt{s}}}{2\sqrt{s}}

(4.8)

(computed in Appendix

F_{0}(s)=2Q(s,1)\Theta(s)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(Q(s,|1-\tilde{x}|)+Q(s,|1+\tilde{x}|))u_{0}(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}.

(4.9)

Comparison of

)

P_{a}(s,\beta):=\frac{Q(s,\beta)}{1+2aQ(s,1)}

U_{+}(s)=2P_{a}(s,1)\Theta(s)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}(P_{a}(s,|1-\tilde{x}|)+P_{a}(s,|1+\tilde{x}|))u_{0}(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}.

(4.11)

4.2.3
Outline of the approach to proving positivity of
u_{+}

If
p_{a}(t,\beta):=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\{P_{a}(.\cdot\beta)\}(t)
exists, apply inverse Laplace transform formally to
(

)
. The desired solution in the

u_{+}(t)=\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}2p_{a}(\tau,1)\Phi(t-\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau}_{u_{+}^{\mathrm{tp}}}+\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}}(p_{a}(t,|1-\tilde{x}|)+p_{a}(t,|1+\tilde{x}|))u_{0}(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}}_{u_{+}^{\mathrm{sp}}}.

(4.12)

Thus, the question about positivity of
u_{+}
can be related to properties of its holomorphic Laplace transform
U_{+}
, which essentially boils down to similar properties of the functions
P_{a}(s,\beta)
, through Expression
(
4.11
)
. We observe the sufficient condition:

If the inverse Laplace transform of

exists and is nonnegative for all

and is nonnegative.

Analysing positivity through the Laplace transform is not straightforward, though. Proposition

provides an entrance, but it is difficult to have control over the signs of all derivatives, required for complete monotonicity. Therefore, we take a different approach.

For

P_{a}(s,\beta)=\frac{\frac{e^{-\beta\sqrt{s}}}{2\sqrt{s}}}{1+2a\frac{e^{-\beta\sqrt{s}}}{2\sqrt{s}}}=\frac{e^{-\beta\sqrt{s}}}{2\sqrt{s}+2ae^{-\sqrt{s}}}.

(4.13)

A crucial observation, formulated in Lemma

, to the better analyzable

\tilde{P}_{a}(s,\beta):=\frac{e^{-\beta s}}{2s+2ae^{-s}}.

(4.14)

Accordingly (cf.
(
4.8
)
), we also define

\tilde{Q}(s,\beta):=\frac{e^{-\beta s}}{2s}.

(4.15)

It allows to conclude that

If the inverse Laplace transform of
\tilde{P}_{a}(s,\beta)
exists and is positive, then so is that of
P_{a}(s,\beta)
,

according to Lemma
3.6
. Thus, the question is now reduced to studying
\tilde{P}_{a}(a,\beta)

5
Convergence to a Steady State

First of all, in order to expect a nonnegative steady state of the system that makes sense for real life application, we shall assume
a\in(0,e^{-1}]
. Moreover, a necessary condition for the existence of a steady state is that the temporal limits of the boundary values
u(t,\pm 1)
at the “cell boundary” should exist. However, we shall see in the following that to ensure the existence of steady states stronger regularity conditions on
u_{+}
and
u_{-}
are needed – specifically, particular regularity of their derivatives.

Note that
u_{+}
and
u_{-}
do possess derivatives in
L_{{\mathrm{loc}}}^{1}
. Because we have already computed their Laplace transforms in Section
3
and Section
4
, and differentiation in the
t
-domain corresponds to multiplication by
s
in the
s
-domain, it follows that their derivatives also belong to
L_{{\mathrm{loc}}}^{1}
. So do
u^{\prime}(t,\pm 1)\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Assume that
u_{+}^{\prime},u_{-}^{\prime}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
. If
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u_{+}(t)
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u_{-}(t)
exist, then the mild solution
u(t)
to System
(
1.8
)
converges pointwise on
[1,+\infty)
to the steady state value
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u(t,1)
, respectively to
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u(t,-1)
on
(-\infty,-1]
.

Proof.

Due to symmetry, it is enough to show that if
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u(t,1)
exists, then
u
has a steady state on
[1,+\infty)
. By the comparison principle (see Corollary 2.5 of
[
31
]
), we can equivalently consider the diffusive system of
u_{R}
on the semi-infinite real line
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
whose boundary condition at
x=0
is forced by the given solution of
u(t,1)
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial u_{R}}{\partial t}&=\frac{\partial^{2}u_{R}}{\partial x^{2}}&&\text{for }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\\
u_{R}(t,0)&=u(t,1)&&\text{for }t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\\
u_{R}(0,x)&=u_{0}(x+1)\quad&&\text{for }x\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\end{aligned}\right..

Substitute
u_{R}
by
\tilde{u}_{R}+u(t,1)
to obtain a system with homogeneous boundary condition

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\tilde{u}_{R}}{\partial t}&=\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{u}_{R}}{\partial x^{2}}+u^{\prime}(t,1)&&\text{for }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\\
\tilde{u}_{R}(t,0)&=0&&\text{for }t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\\
\tilde{u}_{R}(0,x)&=u_{0}(x+1)-u(0,1)\quad&&\text{for }x\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\end{aligned}\right..

(5.1)

Evaluating the Variation of Constants Formula
(
2.2
)
in the setting of System
5.1
, we get

\tilde{v}_{R}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}}\mathcal{K}_{1,\mathrm{semi}}(t-\tau,x,\tilde{x})u^{\prime}(\tau,1)\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}}\mathcal{K}_{1,\mathrm{semi}}(t,x,\tilde{x})(u_{0}(\tilde{x}+1)-u(0,1))\mathrm{d}\tilde{x},

(5.2)

where the heat kernel for
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
with Dirichlet boundary condition at
x=0
is given by

\mathcal{K}_{1,\mathrm{semi}}(t,x,\tilde{x}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\left(e^{-\frac{(x-\tilde{x})^{2}}{4t}}-e^{-\frac{(x+\tilde{x})^{2}}{4t}}\right)

according to
[
12
]
, Section 18, p.33. The second integral of
(
5.2
)
converges to zero similar to Lemma
5.2
. The first integral of
(
5.2
)
can be further computed as

\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{4(t-\tau)}}\right)u^{\prime}(\tau,1)\mathrm{d}\tau,

where the
error function
is defined as

\mathrm{erf}(x):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-y^{2}}\mathrm{d}y.

All conditions of Lemma
C.8
are satisfied. Then we can show that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{u}_{R}(t,x)=0
pointwise. Eventually,
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u(t,x)=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u(t,1)
pointwise.

A similar result is obtained on
(-\infty,-1]
:
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u(t,x)=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u(t,-1)
pointwise.

\blacksquare

We shall now establish the integrability conditions for
u^{\prime}_{+}
and
u^{\prime}_{-}
that are assumed in Proposition
5.1
. First, we prove this for
u_{-}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}u_{-}(t)=0
, and
u_{-}^{\prime}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
.

Proof.

Apply Hölder’s inequality to
(
4.31
)
. One gets

|u_{-}(t)|\leqslant\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\cdot\sqrt[4]{\frac{2}{\pi t}},

which approaches to
0
when
t\rightarrow+\infty
.

For the absolute integrability of
u_{-}^{\prime}(t)
, we just need to show that the following integral is finite

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{|1-\tilde{x}|^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}-\frac{e^{-\frac{|1+\tilde{x}|^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\right)u_{0}(\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}\right|\mathrm{d}t.

We compute

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{|1\pm\tilde{x}|^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\right)=\frac{e^{-\frac{|1\pm\tilde{x}|^{2}}{4t}}}{4\sqrt{4\pi t^{5}}}(|1\pm\tilde{x}|^{2}-2t).

As
u_{0}
is in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then we can apply Lemma
C.4
to conclude the integral is finite on the interval
(1,+\infty)
.

By the property of the heat kernel,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,|x-y|)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{K}_{1}(t,|x-y|)}{\partial x^{2}},

(5.3)

and Stokes’ theorem (integration by parts), it is now enough to show that the following integral is finite

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{|1-\tilde{x}|^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t^{3}}}\frac{|1-\tilde{x}|}{2}+\frac{e^{-\frac{|1+\tilde{x}|^{2}}{4t}}}{\sqrt{4\pi t^{3}}}\frac{|1+\tilde{x}|}{2}\right)|u_{0}^{\prime}(\tilde{x})|\mathrm{d}\tilde{x}\mathrm{d}t.

This is true due to Lemma
C.3
.

\blacksquare

Establishing a similar result for

is more complicated. Naturally, we should require that the temporal limit of the secretion term,
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\Phi(t)=\Phi_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}

that has the indicated limit. Otherwise, the cell would keep secreting more and more compound indefinitely, preventing the system from reaching equilibrium. However, we shall see that an additional regularity on

\blacksquare

This paper investigates a time-evolving biological system of compound-exchange cells modeled by a nonlocal PDE with negative feedback. The study is motivated by the two-dimensional models in
[
28
]
and
[
36

, which we reduce to a one-dimensional setting for analytical tractability. We focus on the positivity and steady-state behavior of the system. Numerical evidence further confirms and supports our analytical results.

Appendix B
Analysis of a Delayed Differential Equation

The following linear delayed differential equation (DDE) with nonconstant coefficients

\left\{\begin{aligned} y^{\prime}(t)&=A(t)y(t-\tau),\quad&&t\in(t_{0},+\infty),\\
y(t)&=h(t),&&t\in[t_{0}-\tau,t_{0}],\end{aligned}\right.

(B.1)

where
h(t)
provides the history condition for
t\in[t_{0}-\tau,t_{0}]
, has been extensively studied (see e.g.,
[
21
,
16
]
, and various references in the latter). It, or the even more general version with multiple and variable delays (see
(
4.21
)
), occurs as an essential object of study in various questions, like it does (in much simplified form) in our question, about positivity of solutions of the System
(
1.6
)
–
(
1.7
)
. Properties of the solution (like positivity or being oscillatory, i.e., having zeros at arbitrarily large times) then characterize properties of solutions to the original question.

For constant delay and continuous coefficient function
A(t)
and history
h(t)
, the existence of a unique solution
y:[t_{0}-\tau,+\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is immediate by means of integration by steps. By shifting time when necessary and redefining coefficient and history functions accordingly, we may assume without loss of generality that
t_{0}=\tau
. For the convenience of conducting analysis, we non-dimensionalize Equation
(
B.1
)
by rescaling as follows:

t^{*}=\frac{t}{\tau},\quad y^{*}(t^{*})=\frac{y(t)}{\hat{y}},\quad A^{*}(t^{*})=\tau A(t),\quad h^{*}(t^{*})=\frac{h(t)}{\hat{y}}.

(B.2)

Then the dimensionless equation is given by

\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}y^{*}}{\mathrm{d}t^{*}}&=A^{*}(t^{*})y^{*}(t^{*}-1),\quad&&t^{*}\in(1,+\infty),\\
y^{*}(t^{*})&=h^{*}(t^{*}),&&t^{*}\in[0,1].\end{aligned}\right.

For our question of interest, we need to obtain those
A\in\mathbb{R}
for which the solution to the specific version of Equation
(
B.1
)
with
\tau=1
,
t_{0}=1
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} y^{\prime}(t)&=Ay(t-1),\quad&&t\in(1,+\infty),\\
y(t)&=1,&&t\in[0,1].\end{aligned}\right.

(B.3)

is positive on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. We denote this solution by
\eta_{A}(t)
. Note that in view of the rescaling
(
B.2
)
, the case of Equation
(
B.1
)
with constant coefficient and history function is then covered by
(
B.3
)
.

Lemma B.1
.

Suppose that the coefficient function
A(t)
and the history function
h(t)
are continuous. If
A(t)
is bounded on
[t_{0}-\tau,+\infty)
, then the solution
y(t)
to
(
B.1
)
satisfies

|y(t)|\leqslant Me^{\omega(t-t_{0}+\tau)}\quad\mbox{for all }t\geqslant t_{0}-\tau,

(B.4)

where
M:=\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\tau,t_{0}]}|h(t)|
and
\omega:=\sup_{t\geqslant t_{0}-\tau}|A(t)|
.

Proof.

Put
t_{k}:=t_{0}+k\tau
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. We shall prove the statement by induction. For
t\in[t_{-1},t_{0}]
, by construction
|y(t)|=|h(t)|\leqslant M\leqslant Me^{\omega(t-t_{-1})}
.
Suppose that
(
B.4
)
has been proven for all
t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}]
, for all
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
. Let
t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
. Then

\displaystyle|y(t)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|y(t_{n})|+\bigl|\int_{t_{n}}^{t}y^{\prime}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi\bigl|\leqslant Me^{\omega(n+1)\tau}+\omega\int_{t_{n}}^{t}\bigl|y(\xi-\tau)\bigr|\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\leqslant Me^{\omega(n+1)\tau}+\omega\int_{t_{n}}^{t}Me^{\omega(\xi-\tau-t_{-1})}\mathrm{d}\xi

\displaystyle\leqslant Me^{\omega(n+1)\tau}+Me^{-\omega\tau}\bigl(e^{\omega(t-t_{-1})}-e^{\omega(n+1)\tau}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant Me^{\omega(n+1)\tau}\bigl(1-e^{-\omega\tau}\bigr)+Me^{\omega(t-\tau-t_{-1})}

\displaystyle\leqslant Me^{\omega(t-t_{-1})}\bigl(1-e^{-\omega\tau}+e^{-\omega\tau}\bigr)

\displaystyle=Me^{\omega(t-t_{-1})}.

Hence, the result follows.

\blacksquare

It is clear from integration by steps, that
\eta_{A}>0
for
A\geqslant 0
. The case
A<0
requires a more subtle analysis. Because the coefficient function is constant, we can apply Laplace transform to gain insight into the positivity of
\eta_{A}
.

The common approach to prove positivity of the solution to a specific system of ordinary differential equations or partial differential equations is to exhibit the solution as a limit of an iteration procedure (like Picard’s Iteration) and prove that positivity is preserved in each step of this procedure (see e.g.,
[
32
,
11
,
22
]
). This strategy did not work well for the DDE
(
B.3
)
. Instead we employ a positivity result by Diblìk, Lemma
B.4
.

The solution
\eta_{A}(t)
to
(
B.3
)
may be obtained through integration by steps, yielding

\eta_{A}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}A^{m}\frac{(t-m)^{m}}{m!}\theta(t-m).

(B.5)

This expression may be obtained through Laplace transform as well, because the coefficient function,
A(t)=A
, is constant.

Integration by steps yields that the solution
y(t)
will be piecewise-
C^{1}
. Lemma
B.1
implies that
y
is exponentially bounded:

\mbox{For any }A\in\mathbb{R},\qquad|\eta_{A}(t)|\leqslant e^{|A|t}\qquad\mbox{for all }t\geqslant 0.

(B.6)

Thus,
y
and
y^{\prime}
have Laplace transforms. Put
Y:=\mathcal{L}\{y\}
. One can also apply the Laplace transform to
(
B.3
)
. The Uniqueness Theorem (Theorem
3.2
) yields

sY(s)-y(0)=Ae^{-s}Y(s),

(B.7)

where
y(0)=1
. Then
Y(s)
can be expressed as

Y(s)=\frac{1}{s-Ae^{-s}}=\frac{\frac{1}{s}}{1-\frac{A}{s}e^{-s}}.

(B.8)

Hence, for
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\displaystyle\left|\frac{A}{s}e^{-s}\right|<1
,

can be expressed as a geometric series:

Y(s)=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}Y_{n}(s):=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{A^{m}e^{-ms}}{s^{m+1}}.

(B.9)

In particular, the series converges for
s>|A|
. Define

y_{n}(t):=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{A^{m}(t-m)^{m}}{m!}\theta(t-m).

Then
y_{n}
is exponentially bounded of the same type for all

:

|y_{n}(t)|\leqslant\sum_{m=0}^{n}|A|^{m}\frac{(t-m)^{m}}{m!}\theta(t-m)\leqslant\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{|A|^{m}}{m!}t^{m}\leqslant e^{|A|t}\quad\mbox{for all }t\geqslant 0.

(B.10)

Hence,
y_{n}
has Laplace transform (see Table
A.1
)

\mathcal{L}\{y_{n}\}(s)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}A^{m}\frac{e^{-ms}}{s^{m+1}}=Y_{n}(s).

Moreover,
y_{n}
converges uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
to
\eta_{A}
in
(

)
. Because of the uniform exponential order bound for the

satisfies the same growth bound. Moreover, because of the pointwise convergence of the geometric series for

,
[
5
]
Theorem 1.7.5 (Approximation) now implies that
\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s)=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}Y_{n}(s)=Y(s)
. Thus,
y(t)=\eta_{A}(t)
.

Lemma B.2
.

The spectral problem of System
(
B.3
)
, given by
\lambda-Ae^{-\lambda}=0
, has two negative real roots when
A\in[-e^{-1},0)
: the larger root is in
[-1,A)
, and the smaller root is in
(A^{-1},\ln(-A)]
.

Proof.

Denote the spectral equation as function

f_{A}(\lambda)=\lambda-Ae^{-\lambda},

with
A\in[-e^{-1},0)
. Take its derivative

f_{A}^{\prime}(\lambda)=1+Ae^{-\lambda}

which has a unique root
\lambda=\ln(-A)
. Thus,
f_{A}(\lambda)
increases in
\lambda\in(\ln(-A),+\infty)
and decreases in
\lambda\in(-\infty,\ln(-A))
. Notice that for
A
in the given range,
A^{-1}<\ln(-A)\leqslant-1<A
. Denote these four points from left to right on the real axis by
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}
respectively. To obtain the desired statement, we just need to show that
f_{A}(\lambda_{1})>0
,
f_{A}(\lambda_{2})\leqslant 0
,
f_{A}(\lambda_{3})\leqslant 0
, and
f_{A}(\lambda_{4})>0
.

We can determine the signs of
f_{A}
at last three points easily:

f_{A}(\lambda_{2})=\ln(-A)+1\leqslant 0
,
f_{A}(x_{3})=-1-Ae^{-1}\leqslant 0
,

f_{A}(\lambda_{4})=A(1-e^{-A})>0
. It remains to show that

f_{A}(\lambda_{1})=\frac{1}{A}-Ae^{-\frac{1}{A}}>0
. Set
\tilde{A}:=-\frac{1}{A}\in[e,+\infty)
. Then

f_{A}(\lambda_{1})>0\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;-\tilde{A}+\frac{e^{\tilde{A}}}{\tilde{A}}>0\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;e^{\tilde{A}}>\tilde{A}^{2}.

Since

e^{\tilde{A}}+e^{-\tilde{A}}=2\cosh(\tilde{A})=2+\tilde{A}^{2}+\text{(nonnegative h.o.t.)}\geqslant 2+\tilde{A}^{2},

we obtain

e^{\tilde{A}}\geqslant\tilde{A}^{2}+(2-e^{-\tilde{A}})>\tilde{A}^{2},

as
0<e^{-\tilde{A}}<1
for
\tilde{A}>0
. Hence, the claim.

\blacksquare

Proof.

\blacksquare

Let

(

Lemma C.1
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2n}e^{-ax^{2}}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{(2n-1)!!\sqrt{\pi}}{2^{n}\sqrt{a^{1+2n}}}.

(C.1)

Proof.

By the well-known Gaussian integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ax^{2}}\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}.

Take partial derivative with respect to
a
on both side,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial a^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ax^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial a^{n}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}\right)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial^{n}e^{-ax^{2}}}{\partial a^{n}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial a^{n}}\left(\sqrt{\pi}a^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(-1)^{n}x^{2n}e^{-ax^{2}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{2n-1}{2}\right)\left(-\frac{2n-3}{2}\right)\cdots\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a^{1+2n}}}.

Note that
(2n-1)!!:=(2n-1)(2n-3)\cdots 1
by definition and
(2\cdot 0-1)!!:=1
by convention. We obtain the result.

\blacksquare

Lemma C.2

\displaystyle Q(s,\beta):=\,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\;\;

\displaystyle Q(s,\beta)=\frac{e^{-\sqrt{s}\beta}}{\sqrt{4\pi s}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{s}{t}}+\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4t^{3}}}\right)e^{-\left(\sqrt{st}-\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{2}}{4t}}\right)^{2}}\mathrm{d}t

Moreover, we can compute the higher derivatives of Laplace transform of the heat kernel,

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{\sqrt{4\pi}}s^{-n-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{n-\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\frac{(\sqrt{s}\beta)^{2}}{4t}-t}\mathrm{d}t.

(C.4)

Lemma
C.8
shows that the convolution between an
L^{1}
-function and a
C_{0}^{0}
-function is in
C_{0}^{0}
. By general theory of Fourier transform, the Fourier transfomr of an
L^{1}
-function is a
C_{0}^{0}
-function (see Theorem 8.22 on Page 249 of
[
19
]
). Let
F
be the Fourier transform of
f
.
F
is
C_{0}^{0}
.

For example, take

, which has Fourier transform
