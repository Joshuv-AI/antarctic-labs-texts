Analyzing Symmetries of Swarms of Mobile Robots Using Equivariant Dynamical Systems
Raphael Gerlach, Sören von der Gracht

Abstract
In this article, we investigate symmetry properties of distributed systems of mobile robots. We consider a swarm of n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} robots in the 𝒪 ​ ℬ ​ ℒ ​ 𝒪 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{OBLOT} model and analyze their collective ℱ \mathcal{F} sync dynamics using of equivariant dynamical systems theory. To this end, we show that the corresponding evolution function commutes with rotational and reflective transformations of ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . These form a group that is isomorphic to 𝐎 ⁡ ( 2 ) × S n \mathbf{O}(2)\times S_{n} , the product group of the orthogonal group and the permutation on n n elements. The theory of equivariant dynamical systems is used to deduce a hierarchy along which symmetries of a robot swarm can potentially increase following an arbitrary protocol. By decoupling the Look phase from the Compute and Move phases in the mathematical description of an LCM cycle, this hierarchy can be characterized in terms of automorphisms of connectivity graphs. In particular, we find all possible types of symmetry increase, if the decoupled Compute and Move phase is invertible. Finally, we apply our results to protocols which induce state-dependent linear dynamics, where the reduced system consisting of only the Compute and Move phase is linear.

1
Introduction

Due to improved availability and decreased production costs of robotic units, the research area of coordination of
swarms
of mobile, autonomous robots has seen great activity in recent years. Depending on their area of application, it can be economic or even the only possibility to employ large swarms of very simple and therefore cheap robots instead of highly specialized and capable units. A common theme in distributed computing is to overcome the lack of individual capabilities by clever coordination, that enable the swarm to collectively solve given tasks. This approach has seen great success
[
13
,
5
]
. However, it comes with limitations or problems. In particular, when the swarm exhibits symmetries these can typically not be overcome by the collective. Here, we focus on mobile robots whose tasks consist of forming specific patterns based only on the observed positions of other robots. We apply the mathematical theory of dynamical systems to qualitatively investigate symmetries and their evolution.

The
\mathcal{OBLOT}
Model

We consider swarms of autonomous, mobile robots according to the the well-known
\mathcal{OBLOT}
model
[
5
]
. Robots are assumed to be deterministic points that are
oblivious
(no memory),
anonymous
(no identities and indistinguishable),
homogeneous
(execute the same algorithm), and
disoriented
(no global coordinate system). We further assume robots to be
silent
(no active communication).
Robots typically have a limited, constant
viewing range
, within which they can perceive other robots. Identifying these positions is the only form of communication between robots.
The robots act in discrete time steps in which each of them performs a full LCM-cycle consisting of a
Look
- (observe surroundings), a
Compute
- (calculate target), and a
Move
- (move to target) phase.
These cycles are assumed to happen fully synchronously over all robots, which is referred to as the
fully-synchronous
(
\mathcal{F}
sync
) time model.
We consider only
rigid
movements (robots always reach their designated target).
Powerful pattern formation algorithms have been developed in the
\mathcal{OBLOT}
model. The most general result to our knowledge states that any pattern can be formed by a swarm, if the initial condition is such that all robots are within the viewing range of every other robot (which can be realized by a distributed (
Near
-)
Gathering
algorithm in a previous phase)
[
12
]
.
We would like to point out that numerous models with additional robot capabilities have been considered in the literature (e.g.
[
3
,
14
,
16
]
) and the theory developed below can readily be adapted to many of them.

Symmetries in Distributed Systems

The problem with symmetries in this distributed setup can informally be summarized as –
robots that see the same thing, do the same thing
. More precisely, if two robots within their respective individual coordinate systems observe neighbors in exactly the same (relative) positions, they necessarily perform the same computations and move to the same point for the next round. As this is true for all robots, the symmetry of the swarm that relates the two robots remains intact in the next round and consequently for all future times. In the context of pattern formation by mobile robots, it has consequently been shown, that robot swarms can only form patterns which have the same or more (Euclidean) symmetries (as point clouds in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
), which is a direct consequence of these informal considerations. For further background, see
[
17
,
7
,
19
,
20
,
6
,
18
]
.

The mathematical theory of
equivariant dynamical systems
investigates the interplay between dynamics and symmetry properties and offers a wide set of tools for the formalization of symmetries of robot swarms and their temporal development. One of the main goals of this manuscript is to properly introduce this language and to set it up for further use in the study of arbitrary protocols in the
\mathcal{OBLOT}
model.
In
[
8
]
, this mathematical approach has been used to develop protocols which solve the
Near-Gathering
problem while not increasing the symmetries of a swarm.

Our Contribution

In this paper, we take a broader perspective. In particular, we describe the collective dynamics induced by an arbitrary distributed protocol in terms of a discrete dynamical system
\mathbf{z}^{+}=F(\mathbf{z})
, where
\mathbf{z}
is the collection of all robots’ positions in an arbitrary global coordinate system. This system comes with natural symmetry properties, which relate to symmetries of the swarm. We investigate the interplay of both to unveil a hierarchical ordering by which symmetries might be gained in course of the evolution (cf.
\Cref
thm:hierachy). This hierarchy and the corresponding structural properties are subsequently further clarified by investigating only a single round and decoupling the
Look
phase. This allows to describe the possible symmetries that can be gained in terms of automorphisms of the connectivity graph (cf.
\Cref
prop:Gamma_G). Finally, we make these considerations even more explicit in the case that the
Compute
phase involves linear computations only.

Outline

The article is organized as follows.
In
\Cref
sec:evolutionfunction, we derive the mathematical description in terms of dynamical systems induced by arbitrary protocols according to the outlined robot model (
\mathcal{OBLOT}
,
\mathcal{F}
sync
,
LCM
) and analyze its equivariance properties. We further unveil the mechanisms by which the symmetries of a robot swarm change through the induced collective dynamics.
In
\Cref
sec:interpretation, we interpret the symmetries (and their variations) in terms of the potential emerging behavior such as early collisions and invariant formations. We further deduce the hierarchy describing which symmetries can be gained by a given configuration.
By decoupling the
Look
phase, we refine these considerations by effectively studying fixed interaction topologies. This is illustrated at the hand of a special class of protocols in
\Cref
subsec:LD.
We conclude the article in
\Cref
sec:conclusion with final remarks and an outlook.
Some technical proofs, examples, and details can be found in
\Cref
appendix.

2
Characterizing Protocols as Equivariant Dynamical Systems

In this section, we mathematically describe a swarm protocol as a discrete dynamical system. We show that it is equivariant with respect to certain combinations of rotations, reflections, and permutations. Then, we formalize the notion of a symmetry of a swarm and provide first insights into the evolution of a swarm’s symmetries under the collective dynamics. As most of these considerations have already been published in
[
8
]
, the presentation is streamlined.

2.1
Evolution Function

We begin by recalling the three phases of a synchronous
LCM
cycle. In the considered model, each robot synchronously

Look:

observes the positions of other robots in relative coordinates within its vision range,

Compute:

computes a target point using the relative visible robots’ positions, and

Move:

moves to the computed target point.

For our analysis, we take the stance of an external observer describing the dynamics in global coordinates. Given a swarm of
n\in\mathbb{N}
robots we denote their positions in round
t
by
{z_{1}^{t}=(x_{1}^{t},y_{1}^{t}),\dotsc,z_{n}^{t}=(x_{n}^{t},y_{n}^{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
in an arbitrary
global coordinate system
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The (column) vector of all robot positions
\mathbf{z}^{t}=(z_{1}^{t},\dotsc,z_{n}^{t})^{T}\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{n}\equiv\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is the

configuration
of the entire swarm in round
t
.
During the analysis, we sometimes identify a robot with its position (e.g., saying that robot
z_{i}
moves to its target point).

We now formalize the behavior of the swarm as a distributed system in the language of dynamical systems, which benefits the analysis in terms of the symmetry properties of the swarm. Recall that robots move according to synchronous
LCM
cycles, which can most generally be described by a dynamical system of the form

z_{i}^{k+1}=f(z_{i}^{k};\mathbf{z}^{k})\quad\text{for }i=1,\dotsc,n,

for some suitable function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Here, the new position
z_{i}^{k+1}
depends on its own as well as on (potentially)
all other robots’
positions (e.g., if the robot currently sees the whole swarm and the protocol leads it to move towards the center of gravity of visible robots). As all robots perform the same protocol, the function
f
does not depend on the index
i
. However, since all robots have their own coordinate system they must to be able to distinguish their own position from all other positions in the swarm.
This is reflected in the explicit first argument given to
f
.
The dynamics of the entire configuration is then described by the so-called
evolution function
of the protocol
F:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
given by

\mathbf{z}^{k+1}=F(\mathbf{z}^{k})=\begin{pmatrix}f(z_{1}^{k};\mathbf{z}^{k})\\
\vdots\\
f(z_{n}^{k};\mathbf{z}^{k})\end{pmatrix}.

(1)

To shorten notation we may drop the explicit time superscript
t
and abbreviate
{z_{i}^{+}=f(z_{i};\mathbf{z})}
where
z_{i}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
indicates the
“next”
position of robot
i
.
In the same way,
{\mathbf{z}^{+}=F(\mathbf{z})}
denotes the
“next”
configuration under application of the evolution function
F
to some configuration
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. In mathematical terms, (
1
) induces a
discrete dynamical system
.

2.2
Symmetries in Protocols

As pointed out in the introduction, symmetries play an important role when analyzing the collective behavior of a swarm. Most prominently, rotational and reflectional symmetries within the swarm in combination with permutations of robots turn out to lead to interesting dynamical phenomena.
To this end, we formalize the notion of a
swarm symmetry
in such a way that we can apply the theory of
equivariant
dynamical systems to analyze how a protocol’s evolution function influences those symmetries. This generalizes the well-known notion of symmetricity
[
7
]
(cf.
\Cref
rem:symmetricity below).

Equivariance of the Collective Dynamics

Without loss of generality we assume the swarm to be centered at the origin of the global coordinate system. As the robots are unaware of the global coordinate system that we choose as the external observer, their computations and movements are insensitive to
rotations
and
reflections
of the global coordinates in the sense that collectively rotating or reflecting all robots’ positions via a rotation or reflection
\rho\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
causes each robot to move to a rotated or reflected image of its original target point. Combined this implies that all
orthogonal
symmetries
\mathbf{O}(2)
are respected.

Remark 1
.

Occasionally, it is assumed that all robots share a common sense of
chirality

[
12
]
. In this setting, robots are not insensitive to reflections, as these transformations reverse the chirality. Hence, only rotations
\mathbf{SO}(2)
are respected. The arguments in the remainder of this article remain to hold in this restricted setup without any additional effort.

On the other hand, recall that the robots are indistinguishable, which implies that, if a robot observes another robot in a certain position, it does not know which label this robot has. More precisely, the computations and movements of every single robot depend on the set of the other robots’ positions rather than their ordered tuple. In other words, redistributing the labels via arbitrary permutations
\kappa\colon\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\}\to\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\}
does not alter the computed target points.
In terms of the function governing the dynamics of all robots
f
, these observations can be reformulated as follows.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\dotsc,\zeta_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
be arbitrary. The function governing the dynamics of all robots
f
has the following properties:

[(i)]

1.

f(\rho\eta;\rho\zeta_{1},\dotsc,\rho\zeta_{n})=\rho f(\eta;\mathbf{\zeta})
for all rotations and refelections
\rho\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
;

2.

f(\eta;\zeta_{\kappa(1)},\dotsc,\zeta_{\kappa(n)})=f(\eta;\mathbf{\zeta})
for all permutations
\kappa\colon\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\}\to\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\}
.

Remark 3
.

For the sake of completeness, note that the computations and movements are also insensitive to
(uniform) translations
of all robots’ positions, i.e.,

[(iii)]

1.

f(\eta+\xi;\zeta_{1}+\xi,\dotsc,\zeta_{n}+\xi)=f(\eta;\mathbf{\zeta})+\xi
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
;

We typically neglect this property, as it has no practical implications in terms of
invariant
configurations (cf.
\Cref
def:symmetry) in the analysis below.

To shorten the statement in
\Cref
lem:invariance we lift the rotation
\rho
and permutation
\kappa
to the configuration space
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2n}
using the following block matrices
M_{\rho}
and
M_{\kappa}
(
n\times n
matrices with entries in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
) generated by
\rho
and
\kappa

\displaystyle\begin{split}M_{\rho}=\mathbf{I}_{n}\otimes\rho&=\begin{pmatrix}\rho&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\rho&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}&\rho\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }(M_{\kappa})_{ij}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{I}_{2},\quad&\text{if }\kappa(i)=j\\
\mathbf{0},\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(2)

Therein,
\mathbf{I}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times l}
denotes for the
l\times l
identity matrix,
\mathbf{0}
the zero matrix of suitable dimensions, and
\otimes
the Kronecker product. In an abuse of notation, we identify both matrices with their
\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
counterparts.
This allows us to restate the properties of
f
in
\Cref
lem:invariance for a configuration as follows.

[(i)]

1.

f(\rho\eta;M_{\rho}\mathbf{\zeta})=\rho f(\eta;\mathbf{\zeta})
for all rotations and reflections
\rho\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
;

2.

f(\eta;M_{\kappa}\mathbf{\zeta})=f(\eta;\mathbf{\zeta})
for all permutations
\kappa\colon\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\}\to\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\}
.

On the global scale in regard of the entire configuration, the symmetry properties of
f
imply that the evolution is insensitive to arbitrary rotations, reflections, and permutations of the robots in the following sense: It does not matter if first all robots perform a round of a
LCM
cycle and then rotate/reflect/permute or do it the other way around.
We define the set of all
potential symmetries

\Gamma=\left\{\,\gamma=M_{\kappa}M_{\rho}\mid\kappa\text{ permutation},\rho\text{ rotation or reflection}\,\right\}.

Theorem 4

(Protocols are equivariant)
.

The evolution function
F
is symmetric – or
equivariant
– with respect to all potential symmetries of formations
M_{\kappa}M_{\rho}\in\Gamma
, i. e,

F\circ(M_{\kappa}M_{\rho})=(M_{\kappa}M_{\rho})\circ F.

(3)

The proof of this statement can be found in
[
8
]
and for completeness also in
\Cref
proof:equiv.

(Global) Symmetries of Robot Swarms

Intuitively, a symmetry of a swarm’s configuration should be a transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which leaves the
set
of robot positions unchanged. However, as symmetries of sets are somewhat in the mathematical dynamical analysis, extra care has to be taken to reformulate this idea in terms of tuples of positions. In particular, a transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a symmetry of a configuration
\mathbf{z}
if it yields an arbitrarily permuted tuple of the same positions. In particular, we define

Definition 5

(Symmetry of a Configuration)
.

Consider a configuration
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Then
\gamma=M_{\kappa}M_{\rho}
is a
symmetry of the configuration
\mathbf{z}
if and only if

\gamma\mathbf{z}={M}_{\kappa}{M}_{\rho}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}
.
The subset
\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}\subseteq\Gamma
of all such
\gamma\in\Gamma
are called
(actual) symmetries
of the configuration
\mathbf{z}
, i.e.,

\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}=\left\{\,\gamma=M_{\kappa}M_{\rho}\in\Gamma\mid M_{\kappa}M_{\rho}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}\,\right\}.

.

1.

\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}=\Gamma
, if and only if it is given by a swarm in which all robots have collided.

D_{3}

\Cref

Remark 7
.

A commonly used measure for “how symmetric” the configuration of a given robot swarm is is the notion of
symmetricity

[

. It is defined by partitioning the robots into concentric regular polygons with the same number of sides (if possible). The largest possible number of sides for which such a partition exists is the symmetricity of the swarm. In other words, the symmetricity is the number of rotational symmetries of the swarm. Note that this number can also be deduced from our notion of symmetry by counting the number of different rotations

Definition 8

A

g_{1},g_{2},\dotsc\in G
.

The set of all potential symmetries forms a group with the binary operation given by matrix multiplication. In particular,

\Gamma\cong\mathbf{O}(2)\times S_{n}
,
where
\mathbf{O}(2)

\mathbb{R}^{2}

.

Evolution of Symmetries

With this formalization at hand, we can study how the actual symmetries of a configuration evolve under the collective dynamics of the swarm induced by a given protocol. The
mathematical framework for the investigation of dynamical systems with symmetry properties as in
\Cref
thm:equiv is given by so-called
equivariant dynamical systems
, for which there exists a well developed theory to investigate the interplay of dynamics and symmetries (e.g.
[
10
,
2
]
). For example, it can readily be shown that a protocol never causes the
loss
of symmetries. In other words, symmetry cannot be broken. More precisely, we state the following result and refer to
\Cref
proof:noloss for its proof.

Theorem 11
.

Consider the dynamics of a configuration
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
according to an arbitrary protocol mathematically described as in (
1
). Then the configuration after one round cannot have fewer symmetries than the initial one, i. e,
\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}\subseteq\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{+}}
.

In other words, symmetries cannot decrease for any protocol, but can potentially increase. In fact, this happens frequently in commonly investigated distributed protocols for example in achieving gathering via consecutive pairwise collisions (see for example the discussion of the
Go-To-The-Center
protocol in
[
1
]
).
In the following section, we further investigate what symmetry increase means for a given swarm configuration.

Conversely, we also state that protocols which induce
invertible
evolution functions
preserve
symmetries
[
8
]
. Invertibility is to be understood on the level of the configuration in the eyes of the passive observer.
This means that there exists a function
F^{-1}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
such that
F(F^{-1}(\mathbf{z}))=F^{-1}(F(\mathbf{z}))=\mathbf{z}
for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Typically,
F^{-1}
is not the evolution function of a distributed protocol.
In particular, a robot does not have to be able to determine its previous position (on its own).
The term “locally” further specifies that the inverse function needs to be defined only locally, meaning for all configurations that are sufficiently similar to a given one. For a more in-depth discussion, we refer to
[
8
]
.

Proposition 12
.

If the evolution function
F
is additionally (locally) invertible, then we also cannot
gain
symmetries, i.e.,
\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{+}}=\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}
.

3
A Closer Look on the Evolution of Symmetries

In this section, we take a more in-depth look at implications of the equivariance properties of the evolution function (
\Cref
thm:equiv) on the symmetries of a swarm (
\Cref
def:symmetry). We begin by describing immediate consequences of
\Cref
thm:noloss, i.e., of the fact that symmetries cannot be broken via collective dynamics. Then, we describe precisely how the symmetries of a swarm may increase if
\Cref
prop:preserve does not hold.
Everything discussed so far holds for arbitrary evolution functions
F
and therefore for arbitrary protocols. To avoid uninteresting special cases—and the repetitive need to exclude those—we restrict ourselves to protocols which preserve connectivity. In particular, whenever two robots are within each other’s perception range, they will remain visible to each other for all future times.

3.1
Preservation of Structure

It is well-known that certain structural properties of a swarm do not disappear in its temporal evolution. These include symmetries but can occasionally be interpreted in terms of geometrical shapes which remain intact (while scaling and rotating). To make more sense of this general statement consider the following examples which are illustrated in
\Cref
fig:preservation.

Early collisions:

Once two (or more) robots have collided, they cannot resolve this situation and move to different points in subsequent rounds.

Regular polygons:

When a swarm is arranged in the shape of a regular
m
-gon, it will remain in the form of a regular
m
-gon. If
m\lneq n
this arrangement requires early collisions.

Straight lines

A collinear swarm cannot break its collinearity.

Reflection symmetries:

Similarly, reflection symmetries cannot be broken in the most general
\mathcal{OBLOT}
model (cf.
\Cref
rem:chirality).

Star shapes

If the swarm can be partitioned into two concentric
m
-gons, the formation can be interpreted as a star. This shape will remain but can degenerate into two concentric
m
-gons with the same radius (cf.
\Cref
subsec:increase below).

Arbitrary combinations of regular polygons:

The same holds true for finitely many regular
m
-gons, which may only degenerate into
m
-gons with the same radius.

In particular, for any subgroup
H\leq\Gamma

\Fix(H)=\left\{\,\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\mid\gamma\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}\text{ for all }\gamma\in H\,\right\},

F(\Fix(H))\subseteq\Fix(H).

(4)

\mathbf{z}\in\Fix(\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}})

In fact, the dynamical invariance of fixed point subspaces is equivalent to the statement in
\Cref

Each of the preserved structural properties listed above precisely relates to a fixed point subspace of a suitable subgroup of transformations of the configuration space:

Early collisions:

A configuration in which two robots are in the same position is fixed under
M_{\kappa}
, where
\kappa
is the transposition of the corresponding labels. When multiple robots are collided, the configuration is invariant under arbitrary permutations of their labels.

Regular polygons:

A configuration arranged in a regular
m

M_{\kappa}M_{\rho}
, where
\rho
is the rotation by
\frac{2\pi}{m}
and
\kappa
suitably rearranges the labels (e.g. shifts them by one if the robots were labeled consecutively along the polygon). The isotropy subgroup emerges by forming arbitrary products (powers) of
M_{\kappa}M_{\rho}

Straight lines:

A collinear swarm is fixed by
M_{\rho}
, where
\rho
is the reflection through the line through the robots.

Reflection symmetries:

Similarly, a configuration with a reflection symmetry is fixed under
M_{\kappa}M_{\rho}
, where
\rho
is the reflection and
\kappa
suitably rearranges the labels.

Star shapes

A star shape has the same rotational symmetry as a polygon with vertices given by the outer vertices of the star. The argument then holds as above.

Arbitrary combinations of regular polygons:

The same holds true for finitely many concentric regular
m
-gons, which have the same rotational symmetry as one
m
-gon.

It is likely that more structural properties or shapes of a configuration can be identified in terms of symmetry properties. In the next subsection, we further elucidate the interaction of different patterns.

Remark 13
.

Recall from
\Cref
prop:preserve that symmetries of a configuration cannot increase when the protocol is such that the evolution function is (locally) invertible. This can be interpreted similarly. In particular, a swarm cannot evolve into configuration that is an element in one of the fixed point subspaces if it was not in there initially. For instance, any collisions (including complete gathering) or the emergence of regular polygons is impossible in this situation. Instead, configurations can only converge towards such a structure.

3.2
Hierarchical Increase of Symmetries

In this section, we will make precise
how
symmetries of a swarm may increase, if
\Cref
prop:preserve fails to hold, that is, if the evolution function
F
is not (locally) invertible. This precise description holds for a general protocol whose evolution is not (locally) invertible. Therefore, the discussion demonstrates
all possible
mechanisms by which symmetry may increase. A given protocol does not have to increase symmetries along those lines (or even at all for that matter,
\Cref

Example 14

Consider a swarm of
16

This specific example will be discussed in more detail in

4
Temporary Fixed Interaction Topology – A One-Step Analysis

In this section, we further investigate possible symmetry gains in view of
\Cref
sec:interpretation that can occur in
one step
of the underlying protocol (
1
).
To this end, we decouple the
Look
phase from the
Compute
and
Move
phases in the
LCM
cycle.
More precisely, we consider an arbitrary but fixed initial configuration
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
of the swarm and assume that in the
Look
phase a
connectivity graph

G=(\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\},E)
is constructed.
The vertices are given by the set of robots and
e=(i,j)\in E
if and only if the robot
i
and
j
perceive each other. By the properties of the
\mathcal{OBLOT}
model, this interaction is
symmetric
in the sense that
(i,j)\in E\iff(j,i)\in E
.
We assume that the construction of
G
is invariant under rotations and reflections, meaning the same graph is generated for the initial configuration
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
as for the transformed configuration
M_{\rho}\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
for any rotation/reflection.
For instance, this is the case if
e=(i,j)\in E
if and only if
\left\lVert z_{i}-z_{j}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\mathcal{C}
for a predefined fixed viewing range
\mathcal{C}>0
, which is typically considered.
We emphasize that the complete connectivity graph
G
is only available for the global external observer, whereas each robot only has local information on its neighbors.

The remaining
Compute
and
Look
steps are mathematically described by a reduced evolution function
F_{G}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, i.e., the update of
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}^{+}=F_{G}(\mathbf{z}).

(6)

By removing
Look
from
LCM
the interaction structure is temporarily fixed, which decreases the number of symmetries of the system, as, in general, the function computing the target point of each robot
f
is not invariant under
every
permutation
\kappa:\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\}\to\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\}
anymore (cf.
\Cref
lem:invariance).
Rather, the reduced system is only invariant with respect to permutations
\kappa_{G}:\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\}\to\left\{\,1,\dotsc,n\,\right\}
that respect the graph
G
, which are called
graph automorphisms

\displaystyle\Aut(G)=\left\{\,\kappa_{G}\in S_{n}\mid(i,j)\in\iff(\kappa(i),\kappa(j))\in E\,\right\},

i.e., one-to-one permutations that preserve the edge-vertex connectivity.
Combined with the remaining
\mathbf{O}(2)
symmetry, the reduced system (
6
) is only equivariant with respect to

\displaystyle\Gamma(G)=\left\{\,\gamma=M_{\kappa}M_{\rho}\mid\kappa\in\Aut(G),\rho\text{ rotation or reflection}\,\right\}\cong\mathbf{O}(2)\times\Aut(G).

It follows that the previous results of
\Cref
sec:evolutionfunction,sec:interpretation hold for the reduced group
\Gamma(G)
.
In particular, if
F_{G}
is invertible symmetries in
\Gamma(G)
cannot
be gained according to
\Cref
prop:preserve.
However, additional symmetries
\gamma\in\Gamma=\mathbf{O}(2)\times S_{n}
that are
not
in the subgroup
\Gamma(G)
can still occur, even if
F_{G}
is invertible, as the reduced system is
not
equivariant with respect to the entire group
\Gamma
.
We state this important observation in the following proposition.

Proposition 17
.

Consider the reduced system
F_{G}
in (
6
) with graph
G
and initial configuration
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Assume that the full evolution function
F
is not invertible. Then

[i]

1.

Symmetry gain from
\Gamma\setminus\Gamma(G)=\mathbf{O}(2)\times\left(S_{n}\setminus\Aut(G)\right)
is (always) possible.

2.

If
F_{G}
is not invertible, symmetry gain from
\Gamma(G)=\mathbf{O}(2)\times\Aut(G)
is possible.

3.

If
F_{G}
is invertible,
\Gamma\setminus\Gamma(G)=\mathbf{O}(2)\times\left(S_{n}\setminus\Aut(G)\right)
is the only set of symmetries that can be added.
That is, if
\gamma\in\Gamma_{z^{+}}
and
\gamma\notin\Gamma_{z}(G)
then
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma(G)=\mathbf{O}(2)\times\left(S_{n}\setminus\Aut(G)\right)
.

In particular,
\Cref
prop:Gamma_G provides a more in-depth understanding of the lattice structure of isotropy subgroups discussed in
\Cref
thm:hierachy. Heuristically speaking, if
F_{G}
is invertible, novel symmetries can only emerge involving permutations that were not automorphisms of the connectivity graph. For example, collisions are only possible of robots, that were not the image of each other under any automorphism.

Remark 18
.

The reasoning behind decoupling
Look
from
Compute
and
Move
is that it allows for a fine grained analysis of the symmetries.
In general, the evolution function
F
in (
1
) is
not
invertible, which allows for a large set of possible additional symmetries.
However, the reduced evolution function
F_{G}

can be
invertible for some initial configurations
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, respectively connectivity graphs
G
.
Thus, the set of possible additional symmetries is easier to analyze because symmetry gain from
\Gamma(G)
is not possible (cf.
\Cref
prop:Gamma_G iii).
Moreover, checking for invertibility is generally simpler for the reduced system (
6
) since, for instance, it can be linear in special cases which is studied in the upcoming
\Cref
subsec:LD.

4.1
State-Dependent Laplacian Dynamics

While in the previous section a general evolution function
F
, respectively reduced evolution function
F_{G}
, is considered, we now apply the results to an explicit
class
of protocols called
State-Dependent Laplacian Dynamics
.
Here, we assume that the connectivity graph
G
, which is constructed in the
Look
phase, is weighted by a (configuration-dependent)
weight matrix

W(\mathbf{z})=(w(\mathbf{z})_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, which again by the properties of the
\mathcal{OBLOT}
model is
symmetric
, i.e.,
W=W^{T}
.
Note that this weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is only available for the external observer as is the graph
G
itself.
In particular, the
i
-th row is computed by the
i
-th robot using only its local information.
In the
Compute
phase, the entries of this matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
serve as coefficients for a
linear
combination of neighboring robots to compute target points
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Finally, in the
Move
phase each robot moves an
h
-fraction to that computed position
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
h\in[0,1]
is a predefined (fixed) step size.
Since a negative weight corresponds to the neighboring position
reflected at the origin
and would force a robot to intentionally
move away
from that neighbor, which contradicts connectivity and/or (possible)
Gathering
properties, it is reasonable to restrict to non-negative weights
w_{i,j}\geq 0
.
All of these steps can be modeled by a system of the form

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} z_{i}^{+}&=(1-h)z_{i}+h\sum_{j=1}^{n}w(\mathbf{z})_{i,j}z_{j},\quad\text{resp. in collective coordinates \eqref{eq:general_configuration}},\\
\mathbf{z}^{+}&=(1-h)\mathbf{z}+h(W(\mathbf{z})\otimes\mathbf{I}_{2})\mathbf{z}=\left[\left((1-h)\mathbf{I}_{n}+hW(\mathbf{z})\right)\otimes\mathbf{I}_{2}\right]\mathbf{z}.\end{aligned}

(7)

This is a discrete state-dependent linear dynamical system with state-dependent system matrix
{\mathbf{A}_{h}(\mathbf{z})=\left((1-h)\mathbf{I}_{n}+hW(\mathbf{z})\right)\otimes\mathbf{I}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}}
.

It is well known that the model (
7
) solves the
Gathering
problem, if and only if, for every configuration
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
the weight matrix
W(\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
has a simple eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=1
with eigenvector
(1,\dotsc,1)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and all other eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfy
\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert<1
. In this case, each robot
i
computes a
convex
combination of its neighboring robots’ positions, as the eigenvalue/-vector condition implies

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{i,j}=1\quad\text{for all }i=1,\dotsc,n.

(8)

Afterwards, it moves an
h
-fraction towards that target point.
In the following, we only consider gathering protocols, particularly

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=1\text{ and }\left\lvert\lambda_{j}\right\rvert<1\text{ for all }j=2,\dotsc,n\text{ and }\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

(9)

Note that since
W=W^{T}
by construction, the eigenvalues are in fact real, i.e., we may assume
\lambda_{j}\in[-1,1]
from now on.

Following the argumentation in
\Cref
sec:application the reduced collective dynamics of (
7
) is

\begin{split}\mathbf{z}^{+}&=F_{G}(\mathbf{z})=(1-h)\mathbf{z}+h(W\otimes\mathbf{I}_{2})\mathbf{z}=\left[\left((1-h)\mathbf{I}_{n}+hW\right)\otimes\mathbf{I}_{2}\right]\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{A}_{h}\mathbf{z},\end{split}

(10)

which models the
Compute
and
Look
phases decoupled from the
Look
phase as a linear system with (temporary) fixed system matrix
\mathbf{A}_{h}=\left[\left((1-h)\mathbf{I}_{n}+hW\right)\otimes\mathbf{I}_{2}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
(cf. (
6
)).
Again, note that this update is only valid for the current step with initial configuration
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

To apply the results derived in
\Cref
sec:application we have to check, when the dynamics described by (
10
) is (not) invertible. Fortunately, invertibility of the reduced system (
10
) is simply characterized by the spectrum of the system matrix
\mathbf{A}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
whose (doubled) eigenvalues
\sigma_{j,h}\in\mathbb{C}
depending on
h\in[0,1]
, are computed as the spectral shift:

\displaystyle\sigma_{j,h}=1-h+h\lambda_{j}=1+h(-1+\lambda_{j})\quad\text{for }j=1,\dotsc,n,

(11)

Now, the dynamics in (
10
) is not invertible if and only there is a zero eigenvalue, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma_{j,h}=0\iff\lambda_{j}=\frac{h-1}{h}.

(12)

Thus, by
\Cref
prop:Gamma_G symmetries in
\Gamma_{G}
are preserved if
\lambda_{j}\neq\frac{h-1}{h}
for all
j=1,\dotsc,n
. For instance, this is the case when
h<\frac{1}{2}
since the protocol is assumed to be gathering (cf. (
9
)). We summarize this first result in the following lemma,

Lemma 19
.

A one-step reduced protocol of the form (
10
) with connectivity graph
G
preserves its symmetries in
\mathbf{O}(2)\times\Aut(G)
, if the step size is chosen small enough
h<\frac{1}{2}
.

Further analyzing the invertibility condition (
12
) shows that only non-positive eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}\in[-1,0]
can cause non-invertibility of the dynamics.
Indeed, by continuity in
h
and the intermediate value theorem, in this case there is always a step size
h\in[0,1]
such that the reduced system becomes non-invertible.
According to
\Cref
lem:h¡0.5 this step size is bounded from below by
h\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
We formalize this result in the following lemma.

Lemma 20
.

Consider a one-step reduced protocol of the form (
10
) with weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and assume
\lambda_{j}\in[-1,0]
is an eigenvalue of
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then, there is a step size
h\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
such that the protocol is non-invertible in this step. In this case,
\cref
thm:hierachy is applicable and symmetry gain with respect to
\mathbf{O}(2)\times\Aut(G)
is possible.

To summarize, in order to guarantee invertibility for the decoupled
Compute
and
Move
system, it is sufficient to either assume
h<\frac{1}{2}
or
\lambda_{j}\notin[-1,0]
for
j=1,\dotsc,n
. For the latter case the step size
h\in[0,1]
can be chosen arbitrary.

In the linear case, one can answer the question, where do additional symmetries come from?
To this end, we consider the case where
F_{G}
is not invertible, i.e., there is a zero eigenvalue
\sigma_{j,h}=0
with corresponding
k
-dimensional null space
\ker(\mathbf{A})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. In particular, the swarm state space
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
can be decomposed into two subspaces such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2n}=\ker(\mathbf{A})\oplus V\text{ for a suitable $n-k$-dimensional subspace $V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}$}.

(13)

This allows us to uniquely express the initial configuration
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{v}_{0}+\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},\quad\text{where }\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\ker(\mathbf{A})\text{ and }\mathbf{v}\in V.

Note that this decomposition is
invariant
under the dynamics, particularly,
\mathbf{A}v\in V
.
Let
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{+}}(G)
be a symmetry of the updated configuration
\mathbf{z}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
which is
not
a symmetry of
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
itself, i.e., it is an additional
new
symmetry. Further assume
\gamma\in\Gamma(G)
which is possible since
F_{G}
is non-invertible (cf.
\Cref
prop:Gamma_G). By evolving
\mathbf{v}\in V
under the dynamics we can compute

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{v}_{0})=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}^{+}=\gamma\mathbf{z}^{+}=\gamma\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}=\gamma\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{v}_{0})=\gamma\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{A}\gamma\mathbf{v},

where we used the symmetry properties of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Now,
\mathbf{v}\in V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
does not contain any kernel vector of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
by definition of the decomposition (
13
) and we conclude
\gamma\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}
.
Thus, the part
\mathbf{v}\in V
which is
not
in the kernel of
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
already has the new symmetry
\gamma\in\Gamma(G)
.
In this sense, kernel vectors
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\ker(\mathbf{A})
are responsible for symmetry gain in
\Gamma(G)
as they decrease the symmetry of
\mathbf{v}\in V
by superposition. As they vanish under the dynamics (
10
) the “actual” symmetry of
\mathbf{v}\in V
prevails for the entire configuration
\mathbf{z}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

5
Discussion & Conclusion

In this article we investigated symmetry properties of arbitrary distributed swarm systems following the
\mathcal{F}
sync

\mathcal{OBLOT}
model and derived a hierarchy along which symmetries can emerge under iteration. In particular, they cannot disappear (cf.
\Cref
thm:noloss).
This hierarchy follows the lattice of isotropy groups and the corresponding fixed point spaces (
5
).

We exemplary illustrated our findings by decoupling the
Look
phase from the
Move
and
Compute
phases in the
LCM
cycle as invertiblity of the reduced system is presumably more likely to hold true (cf.
\Cref
prop:preserve), particularly, if the reduced system is linear.
In this situation, we showed that kernel vectors are responsible for additional new symmetries: They vanish under the dynamics and only the non-kernel part with those new symmetries remains.
Certainly, this is a special case and the analysis of more complex protocol remains for future research. We claim that for general non-linear protocols configurations that prevent the evolution function to be injective play the analogous as the kernel vectors.
In particular, we are convinced that that the presented approach provides a theoretical pathway for in-depth analysis of explicit non-linear protocols such as
Go-To-The-Center
and could help with design of new distributed algorithms (e.g. for pattern formation
[
17
,
19
]
).

Finally, in this work we considered the
\mathcal{F}
sync
model. It remains an interesting open question if the results can be extended to
asynchronous
time models
[
7
,
4
]
.

A.2
Lattice Structure

In this appendix, we provide some additional details regarding the lattice structures of isotropy subgroups and fixed point subspaces laid out in
\Cref
subsec:increase. In general, a
lattice
is a
partially ordered set
together with a
join operation
(to compute the least upper bound of two elements) and a
meet operation
(to compute the greatest lower bound of two elements).

The set of isotropy subgroups is partially ordered with respect to the subgroup relation
\leq
. The join operation on this set is given by

\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}\vee_{\mathrm{iso}}\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}=\left\langle\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}\right\rangle,

which is the subgroup of
\Gamma
that emerges from taking arbitrary products of elements of both subgroups
\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}
and
\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}
. The meet operation in the lattice is given by intersection

\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}\wedge_{\mathrm{iso}}\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}=\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}\cap\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}.

The set of fixed point subspaces, on the other hand, is partially ordered with respect to the subspace relation
\subseteq
. The join operation on this set is given by the direct sum

\Fix\left(\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}\right)\vee_{\mathrm{fix}}\Fix\left(\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}\right)=\Fix\left(\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}\right)\oplus\Fix\left(\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}\right).

The meet operation is given by intersection

\Fix\left(\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}\right)\wedge_{\mathrm{fix}}\Fix\left(\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}\right)=\Fix\left(\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}}\right)\cap\Fix\left(\Gamma_{\mathbf{z}^{\prime}}\right).

To be fully precise, one typically considers the lattice of
conjugacy classes
of isotropy subgroups: Two subgroups
H,H^{\prime}\leq\Gamma
are conjugate if there exists an element
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
\gamma^{-1}H\gamma=H^{\prime}
. The partial order arises in terms of the subgroup relation of suitable representatives of the conjugacy classes. This lattice is called the
isotropy lattice
. In that sense, it emerges from the above construction by summarizing all conjugate isotropy subgroups into their respective conjugacy class. In the same way, the fixed point subspaces can be summarized into classes of fixed point subspaces with conjugate isotropy subgroups. Both of these reduced lattices are in fact finite. For full details on the theoretical background, we refer to
[
10
]
§10.
