An anterior-first two-stage surgical strategy for neglected traumatic facet dislocation of subaxial cervical spine: a technical note
Authors: Zhechen Li, Yaoqing Chen, Huaqun Zeng, Changyi Jiang, Zhibin Chen, Yiping Chen, Ruomiao Chen, Weihong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeglected subaxial cervical traumatic facet dislocation (TFD) presents unique surgical challenges due to fibro-osseous ankylosis and severe segmental instability. Traditional three-stage procedures are effective in complex cases but may increase operative complexity because of repeated patient repositioning and multiple surgical corridors. This study aimed to describe an anterior-first two-stage algorithm for neglected TFD and to report preliminary feasibility observations, including the selective use of a single-screw zero-profile cage as an anterior spacer.MethodsFive patients with neglected subaxial TFD (≥ 3 weeks post-injury) underwent surgery between May 2021 and February 2024. Stage 1 involved anterior discectomy, scar and osteophyte release, and traction-based alignment assessment. If realignment was achieved, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) was performed; if not, a zero-profile cage secured with a single cranial screw was inserted before proceeding to Stage 2 posterior release, reduction, and lateral mass fixation. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for neck pain, and Neck Disability Index (NDI). Radiographic alignment, fusion, and perioperative parameters were recorded.ResultsFive patients (4 men, 1 woman; mean age 49.6 years; mean delay 40.8 weeks) were included. Three patients underwent ACDF plus posterior fixation, whereas two required single-screw zero-profile cage placement before posterior reduction and fixation. Mean operative time was 196 min, mean blood loss was 180 mL, and mean hospital stay was 8 days. Mean VAS decreased from 4.4 to 0.4, mean NDI decreased from 23.8 to 1.6, and three patients improved by one ASIA grade. Mean anterior translation decreased from 44.6% preoperatively to 4.7% at final follow-up, and mean local segmental alignment improved from 18.0° of kyphosis to 0.5° of lordosis. Radiographic evidence of fusion or stable arthrodesis was observed in all patients, with no cage migration, implant loosening, redislocation, or loss of correction.ConclusionsThis technical note suggests that an anterior-first, traction-guided two-stage strategy may be technically feasible in selected patients with neglected subaxial cervical TFD. Owing to the small sample size and lack of controls, efficacy, safety, and superiority cannot be established.Keywords: Neglected traumatic facet dislocation, Subaxial cervical spine, Anterior-first staged surgery, Zero-profile cage, Circumferential stabilization

Introduction

Traumatic facet dislocation (TFD) of the subaxial cervical spine most commonly results from a high energy flexion–distraction injury that disrupts the posterior ligamentous complex and injures the intervertebral disc [1]. This produces intrinsic instability that threatens the neural elements through segmental malalignment and disc extrusion. In the acute setting, initial management of TFD often begins with closed reduction using skull traction under continuous neurological and fluoroscopic surveillance. If closed reduction is unsuccessful or contraindicated, open reduction is performed via an anterior or posterior approach, followed by instrumented stabilization [2].

Neglected TFD of the subaxial cervical spine, generally defined as ≥ 3 weeks after the index injury, poses a distinct challenge [3]. With increasing delay, progressive fibroconnective scarring and osseous bridging may develop around the intervertebral disc, the uncovertebral joints, and facet joints converting the locked facets into a fixed deformity that resists closed reduction [4]. To achieve adequate release, realignment, and stabilization, many authors advocate combined anterior-posterior staged procedures, most commonly anterior–posterior–anterior (A-P-A) or posterior–anterior–posterior (P-A-P) approaches [5–8]. Although effective, these three-staged surgeries require repeated patient repositioning and prolonged operative time, which may increase the risk of complications and morbidity associated with surgery.

Against this background, we propose an anterior-first two-stage surgical strategy for neglected TFD of the subaxial spine (Fig. 1). The primary contribution of this approach is an adaptive decision-making algorithm that uses intraoperative traction assessment after anterior release to guide the subsequent reconstruction pathway. Specifically, patients achieving satisfactory realignment proceed to definitive anterior reconstruction followed by posterior stabilization, whereas those with persistent malalignment receive anterior support using a single-screw zero-profile cage before posterior reduction and fixation.

Methods

This technical note retrospectively reviewed five cases of neglected subaxial cervical traumatic facet dislocation (TFD) treated with an anterior-first, two-stage surgical strategy between May 2021 and February 2024. Neglected subaxial cervical TFD was defined as a facet dislocation diagnosed or treated at least 3 weeks after the index injury. Patients were included if they had radiographically confirmed subaxial cervical TFD, available pre- and postoperative imaging, and a minimum follow-up of 12 months. Patients with pathological dislocation, active infection, severe vertebral body destruction requiring corpectomy, multilevel rigid deformity requiring osteotomy beyond the dislocated segment, or incomplete records were excluded. All cases were reviewed by a senior spine surgeon to ensure diagnostic and procedural consistency. Surgical treatment was considered in patients with persistent neck pain, neurological deficit or radiculopathy, progressive deformity, radiographic instability, fixed or partially fixed facet dislocation, or imaging evidence of spinal canal compromise. The anterior-first sequence was selected when anterior decompression and release were considered necessary before definitive posterior fixation. This decision was guided by the presence or suspicion of anterior mechanical obstacles to reduction, including ventral scar tissue, osteophyte formation, or traumatic disc disruption, which could interfere with safe reduction if not adequately addressed beforehand. All consecutive patients treated with this anterior-first strategy during the study period were assessed against the stated eligibility criteria. Five patients were identified, all met the eligibility criteria, none met an exclusion criterion, and all were included in the analysis (Fig. 2).

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital (Approval No. 2015084-3). The study was performed under the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Each included patient signed a written informed consent form to participate in this work and to publish personal data and images.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with multimodal intraoperative neuromonitoring. A Gardner–Wells tong was applied and skull traction (approximately 10 kg) was maintained throughout the procedure.

Stage 1 (anterior release and traction-based assessment)

A standard Smith–Robinson approach was used. After confirming the surgical level, contracted anterior soft tissues were released. The longus colli muscles were detached and elevated from the vertebral bodies using bipolar cautery. A Caspar distractor was applied to open the disc space. Thorough discectomy was then performed at the injured level, including removal of intervertebral disc material, posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL) resection, release of fibrous scar tissue, and osteophytectomy around the disc space and uncovertebral joints. Following anterior release, reduction was attempted with additional in-line traction applied by an assistant via the skull tong, combined with gradual neck extension under fluoroscopic and neuromonitoring surveillance.

For intraoperative decision-making, satisfactory realignment was defined fluoroscopically as: (1) restoration of acceptable sagittal alignment without obvious focal kyphosis; (2) substantial reduction of anterior translation to a near-anatomic relationship relative to the adjacent vertebral bodies; (3) restoration of facet overlap or apposition without gross residual incongruence; and (4) adequate disc-space preparation allowing safe placement of a definitive interbody cage without forcing the reduction. Persistent malalignment was defined as obvious residual anterior translation, persistent facet incongruence, residual locked or perched facet configuration, or focal kyphosis indicating that posterior release/reduction was required before a rigid anterior construct could be safely finalized. If satisfactory alignment was achieved after anterior release and traction-based assessment, definitive ACDF was performed during Stage 1. If persistent malalignment remained despite adequate anterior release, a zero-profile interbody cage was inserted as an anterior spacer and secured with a single cranial screw before proceeding to Stage 2 posterior release, reduction, and fixation. (Figures 1 and 3) The single screw was used primarily as an anti-migration anchor during patient repositioning and posterior manipulation, whereas definitive stability was expected to be achieved by subsequent posterior screw–rod fixation. This modification was inspired by prior reports of using a single-screw zero-profile cage as anterior support during staged cervical kyphosis correction [9].

Stage 2 (posterior reduction and stabilization)

The patient was repositioned prone, and a standard midline posterior cervical exposure was performed. If acceptable reduction had already been achieved in Stage 1, the posterior surgery consisted of definitive stabilization with lateral mass screw-rod instrumentation and posterolateral fusion. If facet dislocation remained incompletely reduced after Stage 1, further posterior release was carried out, including removal of fibrotic adhesions and osteophytes around the locked facets, and performing partial facetectomy if required to fully mobilize the segment. Lift reduction was then performed to dislodge and realign the facet joints, followed by lateral mass screw insertion and rod fixation to stabilize the reduction (Fig. 3). Local bone graft was placed for posterolateral fusion.

Postoperatively, all patients were managed with a hard cervical collar for 12 weeks.

Outcome measures

Neurological status was graded using the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale. Neck pain was assessed with a 10-point Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and neck-specific disability was evaluated using the Neck Disability Index (NDI). These scores were recorded preoperatively and at final follow-up. Perioperative variables, including total operative time, estimated blood loss, length of hospital stay, and any complications, were recorded.

Radiographic assessment

Radiographic assessment was performed using preoperative, immediate postoperative, and final follow-up radiographs and/or CT images. Segmental alignment was assessed by local kyphotic/lordotic angle and anterior translation at the injured level. The local segmental angle was measured as the Cobb angle between the superior endplate of the cranial vertebra and the inferior endplate of the caudal vertebra at the injured segment. Lordosis was recorded as a negative value, whereas kyphosis was recorded as a positive value. Anterior translation was measured as the anterior displacement of the posteroinferior corner of the cranial vertebral body relative to the posterior vertebral line of the caudal vertebral body on lateral radiographs. To reduce magnification-related error, translation was also expressed as a percentage of the anteroposterior diameter of the caudal vertebral body. Facet reduction was assessed by restoration of facet apposition or overlap. Fusion and construct stability were assessed at the final follow-up using the available imaging modality for each patient, including CT or radiographs. The imaging modality used for final fusion assessment in each case is listed in Table 3. Fusion was considered achieved when bridging bone across the operated segment was observed on CT. In patients without available CT at final follow-up, stable arthrodesis was defined based on maintained alignment, absence of radiographic instability, and no evidence of implant loosening, cage migration, or loss of correction on follow-up radiographs. Radiographic measurements were independently reviewed by two spine surgeons. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. However, the assessment was retrospective and was not performed as a blinded radiological review. Global cervical sagittal parameters, such as C2–7 Cobb angle, C2–7 sagittal vertical axis, and T1 slope, were not routinely analyzed because the primary purpose of this study was to evaluate reduction and stabilization at the injured segment rather than global cervical alignment, and full standardized standing lateral radiographs were not consistently available in this retrospective series.

Statistical analysis

Given the small sample size and the technical case-series design, only descriptive statistics were used. Continuous variables were summarized using mean, standard deviation, and range only to provide an overview of the cohort, and categorical variables were reported as counts. No hypothesis testing, statistical comparison, or inferential analysis was performed. Changes in VAS, NDI, neurological status, and radiographic parameters were interpreted descriptively only.

Results

Five consecutive patients were assessed for eligibility; no patient was excluded, and all five were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2). The cohort comprised 4 men and 1 woman (Table 1). Mean age was 49.6 years (range, 17–80), mean follow-up duration was 29.3 months (range, 15–47) and mean injury-to-surgery interval was 40.8 weeks (range, 6–106). Mechanisms of injury included motor-vehicle collision (n = 2), direct impact to the neck by a heavy object (n = 2), and fall from height (n = 1). Delayed presentation was due to missed diagnosis or misdiagnosis (n = 2), inappropriate conservative management with collar immobilization only (n = 2), or postponed surgery owing to severe polytrauma (n = 1). Dislocation levels were at C4–5 (n = 2), C3–4 (n = 1), C5–6 (n = 1), and C6–7 (n = 1). No patient demonstrated a posteriorly extruded disc behind the dislocated vertebral body. All cases showed radiographic evidence of osteophyte formation around the affected facet joints; two patients had prominent anterior osteophytes at the dislocated level. Clinically, all patients reported chronic neck pain and restricted neck motion prior to surgery. One patient had a preoperative incomplete spinal cord injury (ASIA grade C), two had mild deficits (ASIA D), and two were neurologically intact (ASIA E).

All five patients completed the anterior-first, two-stage procedure (Table 2). During Stage 1, satisfactory realignment was achieved under traction in three patients; these underwent definitive ACDF in Stage 1 followed by posterior screw–rod fixation in Stage 2. In the remaining two patients, satisfactory realignment was not achieved after anterior release and traction; a single-screw zero-profile cage was inserted in Stage 1 before proceeding to Stage 2, where extensive posterior release and reduction were required (Figs. 4 and 5). Discussion

Neglected subaxial cervical traumatic facet dislocations (TFD) differ significantly from acute dislocation because progressive scar formation and osseous bridging can create a fixed deformity that is resistant to closed reduction [2, 10, 11]. A recent review found that only 17 of 74 delayed cervical facet dislocations (23%) were successfully reduced with skeletal traction, indicating a 77% failure rate [11]. Moreover, neglected TFD represents a three-column injury pattern requiring both adequate release and durable stabilization [1]. Therefore, combined approaches, such as A–P–A [5, 7] or P–A–P [6, 8], have been commonly used to achieve decompression, reduction, and fusion. These approaches are effective in many complex cases, particularly when reduction cannot be achieved through a single corridor, such as in cases with extensive circumferential ankylosis. However, three-stage workflows require repeated patient repositioning, may prolong operative time, and may increase surgical complexity.

Several two-stage techniques for chronic cervical dislocations have been described, each involving trade-offs among reduction control, construct stability, and operative complexity [3, 12, 13]. Posterior-first surgery is a valuable option, especially when posterior facet ankylosis is the dominant obstacle to reduction and no major ventral compression is present. However, in neglected subaxial cervical TFD, a posterior-first sequence may create an intraoperative dilemma if satisfactory reduction is not achieved after posterior release. Applying definitive posterior instrumentation at that point may fix the segment in a residual malaligned position and limit the effect of subsequent anterior release, whereas deferring posterior fixation until after anterior decompression and reduction may require a planned return to the posterior corridor, resulting in a P-A-P sequence. A modified P-A technique has been proposed by Liu et al. [3], in which partial facetectomy and posterior wiring are performed before anterior decompression, reduction, and fusion. This strategy may reduce the need for a third surgical stage. However, posterior wiring provides less rigid posterior stabilization than modern screw–rod fixation and may leave the anterior construct to bear a greater proportion of the load; partial loss of correction has been reported. Conversely, an A-P approach using a non-structural anterior graft has also shown encouraging clinical results [12]. Nevertheless, because the graft is not fixed before posterior manipulation, there may be a theoretical risk of graft migration or loss of anterior column support during repositioning and posterior reduction. In light of these considerations, we proposed an anterior-first, traction-guided two-stage algorithm followed by definitive posterior fixation. The primary novelty of this approach lies in the adaptive decision-making algorithm rather than the individual surgical components themselves. The key concept is to reassess reducibility after complete anterior release and decompression, and then select the anterior reconstruction strategy according to the immediate post-release alignment. In our practice, the anterior-first sequence was selected when anterior decompression and release were considered necessary before definitive posterior fixation. Typical scenarios included traumatic disc herniation or residual disc material at the injured level, ventral scar tissue, osteophytes, uncovertebral fibrosis, anterior bony bridging, or other anterior column pathology that could limit safe reduction. By addressing these ventral obstacles first, the strategy allows decompression-before-reduction and traction-guided reassessment of reducibility before the definitive posterior screw–rod construct is applied.

In our series, all patients underwent the same initial anterior release, decompression, and traction-guided reassessment. Three patients achieved near-anatomic alignment after Stage 1 and underwent definitive ACDF before posterior fixation. In two patients, obvious malalignment persisted after anterior release and traction; therefore, a single-screw zero-profile cage was used as an anterior spacer before posterior reduction. Within this algorithm, the single-screw zero-profile cage serves as a technical adjunct for anterior support in patients with persistent malalignment after anterior release, rather than representing an independent implant innovation. The biomechanical and technical rationale for this configuration is as follows. First, regarding construct stability, the cage was intended to maintain disc-space height and provide anterior column support. It was not intended to function as a definitive stand-alone anterior fixation device. Definitive stability was expected to be achieved by the subsequent posterior screw–rod construct after reduction. Second, regarding migration resistance, the single cranial screw was used primarily as an anti-migration anchor to reduce the likelihood of gross cage displacement during patient repositioning and posterior manipulation. This differs from an unsecured interbody graft or cage, which may be more vulnerable to displacement before posterior stabilization is completed. Third, regarding preservation of reduction flexibility, the single-screw configuration was intended to avoid fully locking an incompletely reduced segment before posterior reduction. A standard locked two-screw zero-profile construct, or a rigid anterior plate-cage construct, may prematurely fix the injured segment in its residual malaligned position and reduce the controlled intersegmental mobility needed for posterior facet release, distraction, and reduction. In contrast, the single-screw cage may provide anterior support while preserving limited controlled mobility for subsequent posterior maneuvers. Nevertheless, potential mechanical limitations of this configuration should be acknowledged. Compared with conventional two-screw zero-profile devices or plate-cage constructs, a single-screw fixation provides less resistance against rotational and pull-out forces. Therefore, cage migration, rotation, or loss of anterior support may theoretically occur, particularly in the setting of excessive distraction or forceful manipulation. Accordingly, it was used as a compromise between an unsecured spacer and a fully locked anterior construct. Until validated biomechanically, the single-screw zero-profile cage pathway should be considered a technical modification requiring further biomechanical validation rather than an established or recommended fixation method.

The practical implications of this algorithm compared with conventional staged strategies include the following. First, anterior decompression and release are performed before forceful reduction, allowing ventral obstacles to be addressed before posterior reduction maneuvers [14]. Second, traction-guided reassessment after anterior release allows the reconstruction pathway to be tailored to intraoperative reducibility while avoiding premature rigid fixation of an incompletely reduced segment. Third, final reduction and definitive stabilization are completed through the posterior stage, potentially avoiding a planned return to the anterior corridor while achieving circumferential stabilization in two stages [15].

In this small series, all five patients showed clinical improvement, improved segmental alignment, and radiographic evidence of fusion or stable arthrodesis were observed during follow-up, with no perioperative complications. However, these observations should be interpreted as preliminary feasibility findings in selected patients rather than evidence of efficacy, safety, or superiority over other strategies. Moreover, this strategy should not be considered a universal solution for all neglected subaxial cervical TFDs. Patients with severe osteoporosis precluding reliable screw purchase, medical instability precluding staged anterior-posterior surgery, or deformity requiring planned three-stage circumferential reconstruction may not be suitable candidates for this approach.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a small retrospective case series without a control group, which limits statistical power and precludes statistical inference, comparative effectiveness assessment, or conclusions regarding treatment efficacy. Changes in VAS, NDI, neurological status, and radiographic parameters should therefore be interpreted as descriptive observations only. In particular, because no conventional A-P-A or P-A-P cohort was included, we cannot determine whether the proposed strategy offers advantages in operative time, morbidity, fusion, maintenance of alignment, or neurological outcomes. Second, the cohort was heterogeneous with respect to age, injury chronicity, dislocation level, neurological status, and reducibility. These factors may influence surgical complexity, fusion potential, neurological recovery, and maintenance of alignment, and therefore limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, although intraoperative decision-making was clarified using real-time assessment of segmental alignment, these criteria remain partly qualitative and surgeon-dependent because intraoperative imaging lacks a calibrated scale and is affected by magnification. Therefore, strict millimeter-based thresholds were not used in this preliminary series. Future studies with larger cohorts are required to establish quantitative criteria and validate the reproducibility of this decision algorithm. Fourth, we did not perform cadaveric testing or finite-element analysis to validate whether the single-screw zero-profile cage construct truly preserves controlled reduction mobility or reduces migration risk. Therefore, the single-screw cage should be interpreted as a technical hypothesis and feasibility observation rather than a validated biomechanical advantage. Larger multicenter studies and biomechanical analyses are needed.

Conclusion

This technical note suggests that an anterior-first, traction-guided two-stage strategy may be technically feasible for selected patients with neglected subaxial cervical TFD. The proposed algorithm incorporates anterior decompression and release before reduction assessment and may provide a framework for circumferential stabilization without a planned return to the anterior corridor. The single-screw zero-profile cage may serve as an anterior spacer in cases with persistent malalignment after anterior release; however, this remains a technical hypothesis requiring biomechanical validation. Because of the very small sample size, retrospective design, and lack of a control group, no conclusions can be drawn regarding efficacy, comparative safety, generalizability, or superiority over established approaches. Larger studies are needed to validate patient selection, reproducibility, and long-term outcomes.
