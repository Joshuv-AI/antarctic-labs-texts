Categories of Games and their Fraïssé Theory Thanks: 1 Supported by FAPESP (2019/16357-1 and 2021/13427-9)
Matheus Duzi 1, Paul Szeptycki, Walter Tholen

Abstract
. Relying on recent generalizations of the Fraïssé theory to a broader category-theoretic context, we study the class of abstract finite games played between two players and show the existence of an infinitetly countable game which is ultrahomogeneous and universal with respect to said class. Certain peculiarities of our game categories which clash with the usual framework found in the literature then lead us to formulate weaker category-theoretic properties which still yield a universal and ultrahomogeneous Fraïssé limit, thus further generalizing the categorical framework for a Fraïssé theory. Keywords: infinite games, finite games, Fraïssé theory, category theory, model theory, topological dynamics, Ramsey theory.

1.
Introduction

The study of the combinatorics of Fraïssé classes, started in
[
5
]
, has received a
great deal of attention, especially from the perspective of the Pestov-Kechris-Todorcevic correspondence established in
[
7
]
. Our goal in this work is to study Fraïssé classes related to some recently introduced categories of infinite games.

Fraïsse theory is classically set in a model-theoretic context. However, in
[
9
]
, Fraïssé classes have been studied in a broader categorical framework. In light of the categorical theory of mathematical games introduced and studied in
[
4
]
, we consider here approaches to developing the Fraïssé theory of games from both a model-theoretic and categorical perspective, with an emphasis on the later (for reasons made apparent below).

In Section
2
we recall the basic concepts of Fras̈sé theory in the classical model-theoretic context, introduce the finite objects that we wish to consider (namely, the proper finite games) and describe an example that shows that the model-theoretic approach cannot be applied to our class of finite games.

Section
3
applies the categorical framework developed in
[
9
]
and presents a Fraïssé sequence of finite games and its (direct limit, our candidate for the) Fraïssé limit,
G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
.

In Section
4
we present a modification of the model-theoretic framework that addresses the shortcomings of the latter approach, culminating in:

Theorem

(Theorem
4.10
)
.

G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
is the unique (up to isomorphism) game which:

•

is
countable
(in the sense that its game tree and payoff set are countable),

•

contains a copy of every finite game,

•

is ultrahomogeneous w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
.

We return in Section
5
to the categorical framework and introduce the notion of
weak finite smallness
, highlighting its key role in ultrahomogeneity of the Fraïssé limit in the classical context, characterizing which games satisfy such property and thus showing that, despite our Fraïssé limit of finite games being ultrahomogeneous (as seen in the previous section), not every finite game is weakly finitely small.

We single out in Section
6
two categorical properties weakening weak finite smallness for all small objects that, together, guarantee ultrahomogeneity of the Fraïssé limit (thus, providing a generalization to the categorical Fraïssé theory).

At last, relying on the characterization shown in Section
5
and on a KPT-correspondence, we show that, provided with a certain topology, the automorphism group of our Fraïssé limit of finite games is not extremely amenable and that the class of finite games which are trivial for one of the two players does not satisfy a Ramsey-type combinatorial property.

2.
Model-theoretic approach

Let us first recall the model-theoretic notion of a Fraïssé class (we follow
[
6
]
as reference).

Let
L
be a countable signature and
D
an
L
-structure (for the sake of existence of initial objects, we allow empty structures as long as
L
has no constants). The
age
of
D
is the class
{\mathcal{K}}
of all finitely generated structures which can be embedded into
D
. We say that
D
has
countable age
if its age
{\mathcal{K}}
is, up to isomorphisms, countable and we say that
D
is
ultrahomogeneous
if for every finitely generated
L
-structure
A
and all embeddings
f,g\colon A\to D
there is an automorphism
h\colon D\to D
such that
h\circ f=g
.

Now let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a class of finitely generated
L
-structures. Then we say that
{\mathcal{K}}
has the

•

Hereditary property (HP for short) if for all
A\in{\mathcal{K}}
and
B
finitely generated substructure of
A
,
B\in{\mathcal{K}}
;

•

Joint embedding property (JEP for short) if for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{K}}
there is a
C\in{\mathcal{K}}
such that both
A
and
B
embed into
C
.

•

Amalgamation property (AP for short) if for all
A,B,C\in{\mathcal{K}}
and embeddings
f\colon A\embed B
,
g\colon A\embed C
there is a
D\in{\mathcal{K}}
and there are embeddings
U\colon B\embed D
,
v\colon C\embed D
such that
uf=vg
.

Note that AP implies JEP in the case where the class of all finitely generated
L
-structures has a minimum.

The main result from
[
5
]
, states:

“Let
L
be a countable signature and let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a non-empty at most countable set of finitely generated
L
-structures which has the HP, the JEP and the AP. Then there is an
L
-structure
\mathsf{Flim}(L)
, unique up to isomorphism, such that

•

\mathsf{Flim}({\mathcal{K}})
is at most countable,

•

\mathsf{Flim}({\mathcal{K}})
has age
{\mathcal{K}}
,

•

\mathsf{Flim}({\mathcal{K}})
is ultrahomogeneous.”

Any class of
L
-structures that is at most countable (up to isomorphisms), has the HP, the JEP and the AP is called a
Fraïssé class
.

Now let us get back to games. We are particularly interested in the class of
finite
games.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G=(T,A)
be a game. We say that
G
is
finite
if
|{\mathrm{Run}}(T)|
is finite.

The motivation for defining finite games as in
2.1
should be clear: since a game with its set of runs being finite has only finitely many outcomes, each player has only finitely many options to choose from at every turn of the game and, from some inning
n<\omega
onward, both players will have a
single
option – so that we might as well consider the game to be over at such inning. In other words, our finite games are, in essence,
actual finite games
.

Moreover, let
\mathbf{CUltMet}_{1}
be the category of complete ultrametric spaces with diameter at most
1
whose morphisms are
1
-Lipschitz maps and then consider
\mathsf{Sub}\left({\mathbf{CUltMet}_{1}}\right)
as the
functor-structured category
(see Definition 5.40 in
[
1
]
) of the forgetful functor
\mathrm{U}\colon\mathbf{CUltMet}_{1}\to\mathbf{Set}
, i.e., the category whose objects are pairs
(X,A)
, with
X
object in
\mathbf{CUltMet}_{1}
and
A\subseteq X
, and whose morphisms
f\colon(X,A)\to(Y,B)
are
1
-Lipschitz maps
f\colon X\to Y
such that
f[A]\subseteq B
. Then it was shown in
[
4
]
that
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
is equivalent to a full coreflective subcategory
\mathbf{MetGame}
of
\mathsf{Sub}\left({\mathbf{CUltMet}_{1}}\right)
, and one easily shows that the finite games defined here are precisely those which are mapped to finite ultrametric spaces by the equivalence presented in
[
4
]
.

For a signature
L
, let
\mathbf{S}_{L}
be the category whose objects are
L
-structures and morphisms are
L
-embeddings. Let
\mathbf{Game}_{emb}
be the category whose objects are games and morphisms are game embeddings. We will now show that the classical model-theoretic approach to studying the Fraïssé Theory of our category of finite games is not possible:

Proposition 2.2
.

There is no signature
L
and injective functor
\mathrm{F}\colon\mathbf{Game}_{emb}\to\mathbf{S}_{L}
satisfying all the conditions below:

•

for every chain of games
{\left({G_{n}:n<\omega}\right)}
,
\mathrm{F}(\bigcup_{n<\omega}G_{n})=\bigcup_{n<\omega}\mathrm{F}(G_{n})
,

•

if
G
is a finite game, then
\mathrm{F}(G)
is a finitely generated
L
-structures.

Proof.

Consider first the following example:

Example 2.3
.

Let

c_{00}(\omega)=\left\{\,{R\in\omega^{\omega}:\text{ $R$ is eventually $0$}}\,\right\}=\left\{\,{R_{n}:n<\omega}\,\right\}.

Then, for each
n<\omega
, consider

\displaystyle T_{n}=\left\{\,{R_{i}\restrict m:m\in\omega,i\leq n}\,\right\},

\displaystyle A_{n}=\left\{\,{R_{i}:i\leq n}\,\right\},

\displaystyle\text{and }G_{n}=(T_{n},A_{n}).

If we let
G_{\mathsf{Flim}}=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}G_{n}=(\bigcup_{n\in\omega}\omega^{n},c_{00}(\omega))
, then the constant
{\left({1:n<\omega}\right)}\in{\mathrm{Run}}(\bigcup_{n\in\omega}\omega^{n})=\omega^{\omega}
is a run in which
Bob
wins. Hence,
G=(T,\emptyset)\leq G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
, with
T=\left\{\,{{\left({1:i<n}\right)}:n<\omega}\,\right\}
. However,
G
does not embed into any
G_{n}
, as
Alice
wins in every run of
G_{n}
for every
n<\omega
.

That this example concludes the proof, can be seen by recalling that if
L
is a signature,
\left\{\,{A_{n}:n<\omega}\,\right\}
is a chain of
L
-structures and
X
is a finitely generated substructure of
\bigcup_{n<\omega}A_{n}
, then there is an
n<\omega
such that
X
is a finitely generated substructure of
A_{n}
.

∎

As mentioned above, the main conclusion from Proposition
2.2
is that a direct application of the classical model-theoretic approach to studying the Fraïssé Theory of our category of finite games is not possible. Of course, denoting
(\bigcup_{n\in\omega}\omega^{n},c_{00}(\omega))
by
G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
is suggestive: this will be an analogous Fraïssé limit that we will obtain later on, so we still have some options left.

3.
Category-theoretic approach

In
[
9
]
, Kubiś presents a categorical framework for the study of Fraïssé classes and their limits which we now apply to our categories of games.

The use of the term “
limit
” in “
Fraïssé limit
”, within the model-theoretic context, is due to the fact that said object can be obtained by taking the colimit of a cleverly constructed tower

D_{0}\embed D_{1}\embed\cdots\embed D_{n}\embed\cdots

It is then based on the construction of said tower that the notion of “
Fraïssé limit
” is generalized to a broader categorical context in
[
9
]
– to better suit the work done in later sections, we shall apply it within the following general setting: while working within an ambient category
{\mathbf{C}}
, we select a class
{\mathcal{M}}
of
{\mathbf{C}}
-morphsisms (meant to play the role of “embedding-like” morphisms, although the framework provided in
[
9
]
can be used in broader contexts, such as that of Projective Fraïssé Theory) and a full subcategory
{\mathbf{F}}
of
{\mathbf{C}}
(meant to play the role of selecting the “finite-like” objects which we are interested in). We will also assume (unless stated otherwise) that
{\mathbf{C}}
is closed under colimits of
\omega
-sequences in
{\mathbf{F}}_{\mathcal{M}}
(which denotes the category whose objects coincide with
{\mathbf{F}}
and whose morphisms coincide with those in
{\mathbf{F}}
which are also in
{\mathcal{M}}
). In this case, it should be clear that the construction of the aforementioned tower should take place in
{\mathbf{F}}_{\mathcal{M}}
:

Definition 3.1
.

A sequence
\mathrm{F}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
is called a
Fraïssé sequence
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(U)

for every object
x
in
\mathbf{F}
there is an
n<\omega
such that
{\mathcal{M}}(x,\mathrm{F}(n))\neq\emptyset
,

(A)

for every
n<\omega
and for every morphism
f\colon\mathrm{F}(n)\to x
in
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
there is an
m\geq n
and a morphism
g\colon x\to\mathrm{F}(m)
in
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
such that
\mathrm{F}_{n}^{m}=gf
(where
\mathrm{F}_{n}^{m}
denotes the morphism
\mathrm{F}(n\leq m)
).

Thus, our goal here is to show that, even though Proposition
2.2
tells us that we cannot explore any meaningful
Fraïssé Theory
of finite games through the lens of model theory, we can do so through the aforementioned category-theoretic approach.

But first, let us recall a few generalizations of the basic concepts seen in Section
2
.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
has the
joint embedding property
(JEP for short) if for all pairs of objects
A,B
in
\mathbf{F}
there are morphisms
a\colon A\to C
and
b\colon B\to C
in
{\mathbf{F}}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

We say
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
satisfies the
amalgamation property
(AP for short) if for all
f\colon A\to B
and
f^{\prime}\colon A\to B^{\prime}
in
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
there are
g\colon B\to C
and
g^{\prime}\colon B^{\prime}\to C
in
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
such that
g^{\prime}\circ f^{\prime}=g\circ f
.

It goes without saying that JEP holds if
{\mathbf{F}}_{\mathcal{M}}
has binary coproducts, with coproduct injections lying in
\mathcal{M}
; and that the condition on the coproduct injections is necessary for JEP if the class
\mathcal{M}
is left cancellable (that is, if
f\circ i\in\mathcal{M}
always implies
i\in\mathcal{M}
). Likewise, AP may be verified using a pushout of the given span of morphisms in
\mathcal{M}
, provided that the pushout injections again belong to
\mathcal{M}
, which is a necessary condition under the right cancellation property of
\mathcal{M}
. Furthermore, the AP in
{\mathbf{F}}_{\mathcal{M}}
is equivalent to the JEP holding in all of the comma categories
(A/\mathbf{F})_{\mathcal{M}}
.

Now, returning to our game-theoretic context: let us denote the class of all game embeddings in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
by
\mathrm{emb}
. Furthermore, let
\mathbf{Fin}\mathbf{Game}\subset\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
be the (full) subcategory of
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
comprised of finite games.

Since
\mathbf{FinGame}_{\mathrm{emb}}
is closed by coproducts and pushouts (for these constructions in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
, we refer to Section 9 in
[
4
]
), it follows that
\mathbf{FinGame}_{\mathrm{emb}}
satisfies the JEP and the AP.

We also need the following variation of the well known concept of
injective object
(see Section 6 of
[
8
]
).

Definition 3.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a class of morphisms in a category
\mathbf{C}
. We say that an object
X
in
\mathbf{C}
is injective with respect to
\mathcal{M}
if every
f\colon A\to X
in
{\mathbf{C}}
can be extended through any
g\colon A\to B
in
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

The game
G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
defined in Example

is injective in
\mathbf{Game}_{\mathrm{emb}}
w.r.t. the class of all morphisms in
\mathbf{FinGame}_{\mathrm{emb}}
.

Proof.

Item (a) in the following lemma is essentially the proof, but we will need item (b) later, in Theorem
3.7

Lemma 3.5

Let

be finite games and

. Then there is an embedding
\tilde{f}\colon G^{\prime}\embed G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
such that:

(a)

\tilde{f}
extends

;

(b)

for all
k<n<\omega
such that there is a
t^{\smallfrown}x\in T^{\prime}\setminus T
with
\tilde{f}(t^{\smallfrown}x)=\tilde{f}(t)^{\smallfrown}n

and
\tilde{f}(t^{\smallfrown}y)=\tilde{f}(t)^{\smallfrown}k

Suppose

Then we recursively define

•

Obviously,
\tilde{f}({\left({\,}\right)})={\left({\,}\right)}
.

•

Suppose

is defined up to

. Then there are only finitely many elements

\displaystyle F=\left\{\,{x_{0},\dotsc,x_{m}}\,\right\}=\left\{\,{x:t^{\smallfrown}x\in T^{\prime}}\,\right\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle H=\left\{\,{n<\omega:f(t^{\smallfrown}x_{i})=f(t)^{\smallfrown}n,i\leq m,t^{\smallfrown}x_{i}\in T}\,\right\}.

In this case, we recursively define

,

–

4.
Ultrahomogeneity by an analogous approach

We saw in Section
3
how one constructs the analogue to the tower of “finite-like” objects which gives rise to the Fraïssé limit in the model theoretical context, so that the category
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
is usually comprised of these so called “finite-like” objects – we shall now tackle the matter of computing the actual direct limit of such Fraïssé sequences in order to obtain a so-called “Fraïssé limit” in a more general categorical context.

We start by noting that, even though we build the tower only with classes of “finite-like” structures in the model theoretical context, the Fraïssé limit (taken as the direct limit of such special towers) of these classes is most often not a finite structure itself – thus, it is only natural to assume that we will not obtain Fraïssé limits of a category
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
with a Fraïssé sequence
\mathrm{F}
within
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
, in most cases. This is why we started Section
3
by considering
{\mathbf{F}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}
within an ambient category
\mathbf{C}
.

One may wonder whether we should consider the ambient
{\mathbf{C}}
as comprising only of the morphisms in the desired class
{\mathcal{M}}
. However, such approach will often run into problems with the colimit-taking process:

Example 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{Met}
be the category of metric spaces, whose morphisms non-expanding maps,
\mathbf{FinMet}
be its full subcategory of finite metric spaces, and
\mathrm{mon}
be the class of injective non-expanding maps (i.e., its class of monomorphisms). Then it can be easily demonstrated that there are sequences in
\mathbf{FinMet}_{\mathrm{mon}}
which do not have any colimit cocone in
\mathbf{Met}_{\mathrm{mon}}
– however, every sequence in
\mathbf{FinMet}_{\mathrm{mon}}

does
have colimit cocone in
\mathbf{Met}
.

Therefore, in order to incorporate such a crucial detail to our theory, we will normally be working in the following setting:

Definition 4.2
.

Suppose
\mathbf{F}
is a full subcategory of
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathcal{M}
is a class of morphisms in
\mathbf{C}
such that every sequence in
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
has a colimit in
\mathbf{C}
. We then say that an object
F
is a
Fraïssé limit of the pair
(\mathbf{F},\mathcal{M})
in
\mathbf{C}
if there is a colimit cocone
(f_{n}\colon\mathrm{F}n\to F)_{n<\omega}
in
\mathbf{C}
of a Fraïssé sequence
\mathrm{F}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
in
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
(here, we note that our chosen terminology extends the model-theoretic terminology by explicitly determining the morphisms which are being considered).

Nevertheless, even within the above setting, other problems arise: there is no guarantee that the resulting Fraïssé limit has the desired properties of the classical Fraïssé limit. Namely:

Definition 4.3
.

Suppose
\mathbf{F}
is a full subcategory of
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathcal{M}
is a class of morphisms in
\mathbf{C}
.

We say that an object
U
in
\mathbf{C}
is
ultrahomogeneous w.r.t.
(\mathcal{M},\mathbf{F})
, if for every object
A
in
\mathbf{F}
and morphisms
f,g\colon A\to U
in
\mathcal{M}
there is an automorphism
u\colon U\to U
in
\mathbf{C}
such that
u\circ f=g
.

We should point out that the concept of ultrahomogeneity as described in Definition
4.3
will be found in the literature only with
\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{M}}=\mathbf{C}
– we consider the possibility of a strictly smaller class
{\mathcal{M}}
in
\mathbf{C}
based on the fact that we are only interested to check ultrahomogeneity for embeddings

and, while it is usually the case that in the model theoretic approach every morphism is an embedding, we are interested in exploring more general category theoretical contexts in which this does not hold. In order to see this, we first explicitly compute the colimits of sequences in

\tilde{f}=\bigcup_{n<\omega}f_{n}\colon T_{1}\to T_{2}
is an isomorphism, which concludes the proof (because each
f_{n}
extends
f
).

Indeed,
\tilde{f}
is injective, because each
f_{n}
is injective. Moreover, we constructed
\left\{\,{f_{n}:n<\omega}\,\right\}
in such a way that the image of
f_{n+1}
contains
G_{2}^{n}
, so
\tilde{f}
is surjective (because
\left\{\,{G_{2}^{n}:n<\omega}\,\right\}
is an exhaustion of
G_{2}
).

Finally, let
R\in{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{1})
. Then, because
\left\{\,{G_{1}^{n}:n<\omega}\,\right\}
is an exhaustion of
G
,
R\in A_{1}
if, and only if,
R
is a run won by
Alice
in some
G_{1}^{n}
. But
R
is a run won by
Alice
in
G_{1}^{n}
if, and only if,
\overline{f_{n}}(R)\in A_{2}
(because
f_{n}
is an embedding). Hence, because
\tilde{f}
extends
f_{n}
for every
n<\omega
, it follows that
R\in A_{1}
if, and only if,
\overline{\tilde{f}}(R)\in A_{2}
, from which we conclude that
\tilde{f}
is an isomorphism.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

A game
G=(T,A)
is ultrahomogeneous with respect to
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
if, and only if, it is injective in
\mathbf{Game}_{\mathrm{emb}}
w.r.t. embeddings in
\mathbf{FinGame}
.

Proof.

Just apply Lemmas
4.6
and
4.7
with
G_{1}=G_{2}=G
.
∎

Now, going back to our candidate for
Fraïssé limit
:

Corollary 4.9
.

G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
is ultrahomogeneous w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
.

Proof.

We showed in Theorem
3.4
that
G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
is injective w.r.t.
\mathbf{FinGame}_{\mathrm{emb}}
’s morphisms and, in view of Corollary
4.8
, this suffices to show that
G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
is ultrahomogeneous w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
.
∎

Summarizing the above results we obtain a result analogous to the one which is is true in the model theoretical approach:

Theorem 4.10
.

G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
is the unique (up to isomorphism) game which:

•

is
countable
(in the sense that its game tree and payoff set are countable),

•

contains a copy of every finite game

•

is ultrahomogeneous w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
.

Proof.

That
G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
satisfies all the above follows from Proposition
2.2
, Lemma
3.5
and Corollary
4.9
.

Now, suppose
G
satisfies the above. Then
G
is injective w.r.t.
\mathbf{FinGame}_{\mathrm{emb}}
’s morphisms, so we get the desired result by applying Lemma
4.7
.
∎

5.
Weakly finitely small games

The proofs seen in the literature for the fact that the colimit in
\mathbf{C}
of a Fraïssé sequence
\mathrm{F}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
is ultrahomogeneous with respect to
({\mathcal{M}},\mathbf{F})
relies heavily in the fact that objects in
\mathbf{F}
are somewhat
{\mathcal{M}}
-
small
in
\mathbf{C}
. To be precise:

Definition 5.1
.

Suppose
\mathbf{F}
is a full subcategory of
{\mathbf{C}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}
is a class of
{\mathbf{C}}
-morphisms. We say that an object
X
in
\mathbf{C}
is
weakly finitely small
with respect to
({\mathcal{M}},\mathbf{F})
if for every sequence
\mathrm{S}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
with colimit cocone
q_{n}\colon\mathrm{S}n\to\colim\mathrm{S}
in
\mathbf{C}
and morphism
f\colon X\to\colim\mathrm{S}
in
{\mathcal{M}}
there is an
n<\omega
and an
\tilde{f}\colon X\to\mathrm{S}n
in
{\mathbf{F}}_{\mathcal{M}}
such that
q_{n}\circ\tilde{f}=f
.

When
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{M}}=\mathbf{C}
, we simply say that
X
is weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{C}
.

We should point out that the above definition is a generalization of the concept of
finite smallness
which can be seen in, e.g.,
[
12
]
and, more recently,
[
2
]
, which states that an object
X
in
\mathbf{C}
is finitely small if the Hom functor
\mathbf{C}(X,-)\colon\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{Set}
preserves colimits of sequences of monomorphisms. Definition
5.1
is then a weakening of said property in the case which
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{M}}=\mathbf{C}

because we just ask that
\bigsqcup_{n<\omega}\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}(X,X_{n})\to\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{M}}(X,X_{\infty})
is surjective.

As it was hinted:

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{C}}
,
{\mathbf{F}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}
are such that every object in
\mathbf{F}
is weakly finitely small w.r.t.
({\mathcal{M}},\mathbf{F})
and
\mathrm{F}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
is a Fraïssé sequence in
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
with colimit
F
in
\mathbf{C}
. Then
F
is ultrahomogeneous w.r.t.
({\mathcal{M}},\mathbf{F})
.

Proof.

Fix a colimit cocone
{\left({f_{n}\colon\mathrm{F}n\to F}\right)}_{n\in\omega}
in
\mathbf{C}
and let
g,h\colon A\to F
in
{\mathcal{M}}
be given with
A
in
\mathbf{F}
. Since
A
is weakly finitely small w.r.t.
({\mathcal{M}},\mathbf{F})
, there must be
n_{g},n_{h}\in\omega
together with
\tilde{g}\colon A\to\mathrm{F}n_{g}
and
\tilde{h}\colon A\to\mathrm{F}n_{h}
in
\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
such that
g=f_{n_{g}}
and
h=f_{n_{h}}
. Without loss of generality, let us assume that
n_{g}=n_{h}=N
.

Consider the sequences
\mathrm{S}\colon\omega\to\mathrm{F}_{\mathcal{M}}
such that

•

\mathrm{S}0=\mathrm{S^{\prime}}0=A
and

\mathrm{S}n=\mathrm{S^{\prime}}n=\mathrm{F}(N+n-1)

for every
n\geq 1
.

•

\mathrm{S}_{0}^{1}=\tilde{g}\colon A\to\mathrm{F}N
,
\mathrm{S^{\prime}}_{0}^{1}=\tilde{h}\colon A\to\mathrm{F}N
and

\mathrm{S}_{n}^{n+1}=\mathrm{S^{\prime}}_{n}^{n+1}=\mathrm{F}_{N+n-1}^{N+n}\colon\mathrm{F}(N+n-1)\to\mathrm{F}(N+n)

for every
n\geq 1
.

Then the cocones
{\left({g_{n}\colon\mathrm{S}n\to F}\right)}_{n\in\omega}
and
{\left({h_{n}\colon\mathrm{S^{\prime}}n\to F}\right)}_{n\in\omega}
in
\mathbf{C}
with
g_{0}=g\colon A\to F
,
h_{0}=h\colon A\to F
and

g_{n}=h_{n}=f_{N+n-1}\colon\mathrm{F}(N+n-1)\to F

for every
n\geq 1
, are colimit cocones in
\mathbf{C}
(since, from
n=1
onward, they are equal to a final segment of the fixed colimit cocone of
\mathrm{F}
in
\mathbf{C}
). In this case, the universal property of
{\left({g_{n}\colon\mathrm{S}n\to F}\right)}_{n\in\omega}
and
{\left({h_{n}\colon\mathrm{S^{\prime}}n\to F}\right)}_{n\in\omega}
applied to one another provides us with the desired isomorphism
\varphi\colon F\to F
in
\mathbf{C}
.
∎

But, in view of Example
2.3
, not every finite game is weakly finitely small w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
. It is then only natural to ask which are these weakly finitely small games w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
and what kind of Fraïssé theory emerges from them.

Instead of starting by tackling this matter, though, we will first focus on the (harder) problem of which games are weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
, so that the answer relating to
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
will follow from it (see Theorem
5.8
).

Our goal now is to show:

Theorem 5.3
.

A game
G=(T,A)
is weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
if, and only if,
G
is a finite game that is trivial for
Alice
, that is,
A={\mathrm{Run}}(T)
.

In order to do this, given a non-empty game tree
T
and
t\in T
, let

\displaystyle\overline{t}=\left\{\,{R\in{\mathrm{Run}}(T):R\restrict|t|=t}\,\right\},

\displaystyle T(t)=\left\{\,{s\in T:s\subseteq t\text{ or }t\subseteq s}\,\right\}.

If
{\mathrm{Run}}(T)
is infinite, one can recursively find a family
\left\{\,{t_{n}\in T:n\in\omega}\,\right\}
such that
\left\{\,{\overline{t_{n}}:n\in\omega}\,\right\}
partitions
{\mathrm{Run}}(T)
with
\overline{t_{n}}\neq\emptyset
for every
n\in\omega
. In this case:

Proposition 5.4
.

If
G=(T,A)
is such that
{\mathrm{Run}}(T)
is infinite, then
G
is not weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
.

Proof.

Fix a family
\left\{\,{t_{n}\in T:n\in\omega}\,\right\}
such that
\left\{\,{\overline{t_{n}}:n\in\omega}\,\right\}
partitions
{\mathrm{Run}}(T)
with
\overline{t_{n}}\neq\emptyset
for every
n\in\omega
. We let
\mathrm{S}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
be such that

•

\mathrm{S}n=(T_{n},A_{n})
with
T_{n}=\bigcup_{k\leq n}T(t_{k})
and
A_{n}=A\cap{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{n})
;

•

\mathrm{S}_{n}^{m}\colon G_{n}\embed G_{m}
is the inclusion map.

Then
{\left({i_{n}\colon\mathrm{S}n\embed G}\right)}_{n\in\omega}
, where
i_{n}\colon\mathrm{S}n\to G
is also the inclusion map, is clearly the colimit cocone of
\mathrm{S}
in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
.

Now consider
\id_{G}\colon G\to G
. Then there can be no
n\in\omega
and
f\colon G\to\mathrm{S}n
such that
i_{n}\circ f=\id_{G}
, since
\id_{G}[T]=T
, but
i_{n}[T_{n}]=\bigcup_{k\leq n}T(t_{k})
and
T\setminus\bigcup_{k\leq n}T(t_{k})\neq\emptyset
because
\overline{t_{n+1}}\neq\emptyset
for every
n\in\omega
.

Hence,
G
is not weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
.
∎

For a non-empty game tree
T
and
t\in T
, let

\lfloor t\rfloor=\left\{\,{s\in T:t\subsetneq s}\,\right\}.

Proposition 5.5
.

If
G=(T,A)
is such that
{\mathrm{Run}}(T)\setminus A\neq\emptyset
, then
G
is not weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
.

Proof.

Fix
R\in{\mathrm{Run}}(T)\setminus A
and
b
such that
b\notin\mathrm{M}(T)
. Then we let
\mathrm{S}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
be such that

•

\mathrm{S}n=(T_{n},A_{n})
, where

\displaystyle T_{n}=T\setminus\lfloor R\restrict n\rfloor\cup\left\{\,{R\restrict k^{\smallfrown}{\left({b:i\leq m}\right)}:k\leq n,\,m\in\omega}\,\right\}

\displaystyle A_{n}=A\cap{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{n}).

•

\mathrm{S}_{n}^{m}\colon T_{n}\embed T_{m}
is the inclusion map.

Let
i_{n}:\mathrm{S}n\embed(T^{\prime},A))
be the inclusion map for each
n
, where

T^{\prime}=T\setminus\cup\left\{\,{R\restrict k^{\smallfrown}{\left({b:i\leq m}\right)}:k,m\in\omega}\,\right\}.

Then the sequence of maps
{\left({i_{n}}\right)}_{n\in\omega}
is clearly the colimit cocone in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
of
\mathrm{S}
.

Now consider the inclusion map
i\colon G\embed G^{\prime}
. Then there can be no
n\in\omega
with
f\colon G\to\mathrm{S}n
such that
i_{n}\circ f=i
, since
R\in\overline{i}[{\mathrm{Run}}(T)]
, but
R\notin\overline{i_{n}}[{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{n})]
for every
n\in\omega
. Hence,
G
is not weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
.
∎

To finish the proof of Theorem
5.3
, it remains to show that every finite game which is trivial for
Alice
is weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
. To this end, we need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\mathrm{S}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
be a sequence with
\mathrm{S}n=(T_{n},A_{n})
, colimit cocone
\left\{\,{q_{k}\colon\mathrm{S}k\to G_{\mathrm{S}}:k\in\omega}\,\right\}
. Suppose
x\in A_{k}
and
R\in A_{\mathrm{S}}
are such that
\overline{q}_{k}(x)\neq R
.

Then there are
M\geq k
and
y\in A_{M}
such that
\overline{q}_{M}(y)=R
and

\Delta(y,\overline{\mathrm{S}_{k}^{M}}(x))=\Delta(R,\overline{q}_{k}(x)).

Proof.

By the definition of
A_{\mathrm{S}}
, there are
m\geq k
and
R^{\prime}\in A_{m}
such that
\overline{q}_{m}(R^{\prime})=R
. Let
S=\overline{\mathrm{S}_{k}^{m}}(x)
and then
l=\Delta(R,\overline{q}_{m}(S))-1
. Then, by definition of
\Delta(R,\overline{q}_{m}(S))
,
R\restrict l=\overline{q}_{m}(S)\restrict l
.

In this case, because
q_{m}
is chronological,

\Delta(R^{\prime},S)\leq\Delta(\overline{q}_{m}(R^{\prime}),\overline{q}_{m}(S))=\Delta(R,\overline{q}_{k}(x)).

But
q_{m}(R^{\prime}\restrict l)=R\restrict l=\overline{q}_{k}(x)\restrict l=\overline{q}_{m}(S)
, so
R^{\prime}\restrict l\sim S\restrict l
and thus there is an
M\geq m
such that
\mathrm{S}(m\leq M)(R^{\prime}\restrict l)=\mathrm{S}_{m}^{M}(S\restrict l)=\mathrm{S}_{k}^{M}(x\restrict l)
.

Finally, by letting
y=\overline{\mathrm{S}_{m}^{M}}(R^{\prime})
, we get that

\Delta(y,\overline{\mathrm{S}_{k}^{M}}(x))\geq l+1=\Delta(R,\overline{q}_{k}(x)),

so that the conclusion follows from the fact that
q_{M}\colon\mathrm{S}M\to G_{\mathrm{S}}
is chronological.
∎

At last:

Proof of Theorem
5.3
:

Let
\mathrm{S}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
be a sequence with
\mathrm{S}n=(T_{n},A_{n})
. Consider its colimit cocone
\left\{\,{q_{k}\colon\mathrm{S}k\to G_{\mathrm{S}}:k\in\omega}\,\right\}
as in Lemma
4.4
.

Now, in order to show that
G
is weakly finitely small, we can assume that
G\leq G_{\mathrm{S}}
and it suffices to show that the inclusion
i\colon G\embed G_{\mathrm{S}}
factors through some
q_{M}
. So enumerate
{\mathrm{Run}}(T)=\left\{\,{R_{i}:i\leq N}\,\right\}\subset A_{\mathrm{S}}
.

For each
i\leq N
we will recursively find
m_{i}\in\omega
and
\left\{\,{S_{j}^{i}:j\leq i}\,\right\}
such that
\overline{q_{m_{i}}}(S_{j}^{i})=R_{j}
and
\Delta(S_{j_{1}}^{i},S_{j_{2}}^{i})=\Delta(R_{j_{1}},R_{j_{2}})
. For
i=0
we may simply use the definition of
A_{\mathrm{S}}
to find an
S_{0}^{0}
in some
A_{m_{0}}
with
\overline{q_{m_{0}}}(S_{0}^{0})=R_{0}
.

Suppose the work is done for
i<N
. Then we may use Lemma
5.6
to find
k_{0}\geq m_{i}
and
y_{0}\in A_{k_{0}}
such that
\overline{q}_{k_{0}}(y_{0})=R_{i+1}
and

\Delta(y_{0},\overline{\mathrm{S}(m_{i}\leq k_{0})}(R_{0}^{i}))=\Delta(R_{i+1},R_{0}).

We then may use Lemma
5.6
again to find
k_{1}\geq k_{0}
and
y_{1}\in A_{k_{1}}
such that
\overline{q}_{k_{1}}(y_{1})=R_{i+1}
and

\Delta(y_{1},\overline{\mathrm{S}(k_{0}\leq k_{1})}(R_{1}^{i}))=\Delta(R_{i+1},R_{1}).

Note that

\Delta(y_{1},\overline{\mathrm{S}(m_{i}\leq k_{1})}(R_{0}^{i}))=\Delta(R_{i+1},R_{0}).

By repeating this process
i
times we then get the desired
m_{i}\in\omega
and
\left\{\,{S_{j}^{i}:j\leq i}\,\right\}
.

Let
M=m_{N}
. It is then easy to check that the mapping
\overline{f}\colon{\mathrm{Run}}(T)\to A_{\mathrm{S}}
such that

\overline{f}(R_{i})=S_{i}^{N}

defines an
A
-morphism
f\colon G\to\mathrm{S}M
such that
q_{M}f
is the identity over
G
, which concludes the proof.

The proof is then concluded with Propositions
5.4
and
5.5
.

\square

As previously mentioned, we are particularly interested in determining which are the weakly finitely small games w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
. However, note that we, purposefully, only used game embeddings in the proof of Proposition
5.5
, so the same proof works to show that

Proposition 5.7
.

If
G=(T,A)
is such that
{\mathrm{Run}}(T)\setminus A\neq\emptyset
, then
G
is not weakly finitely small w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
.

Finally:

Theorem 5.8
.

A game
G=(T,A)
is weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\mathrm{emb}}
w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
if, and only if,
G
is a finite game that is trivial for
Alice
.

Proof.

It remains to show that finite games which are trivial for
Alice
are weakly finitely small w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
.

So let
G=(T,A)
be a finite game which is trivial for
Alice
and suppose
\mathrm{S}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{Fin}\mathbf{Game}_{\mathrm{emb}}
is given with colimit cocone
{\left({q_{n}\colon\mathrm{S}n\to G_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)}
in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
and an embedding
f\colon G\embed G_{\mathrm{S}}
.

By Theorem
5.3
,
G
is weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
, so there must be an
n\in\omega
and
g\colon G\to\mathrm{S}n
in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
such that
q_{n}\circ g=f
. In this case, since
f
is a game embedding, so must
g
. Thus,
g
attests that
G
is weakly finitely small w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{FinGame})
for
\mathrm{S}
,
{\left({q_{n}\colon\mathrm{S}n\to G_{\mathrm{S}}}\right)}
and
f\colon G\embed G_{\mathrm{S}}
.
∎

Consider the functor
\mathrm{Free}\colon\mathbf{Gme}\to\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
such that
\mathrm{Free}T=(T,{\mathrm{Run}}(T))
and

\mathrm{Free}\left(T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}T^{\prime}\right)=(T,{\mathrm{Run}}(T))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\to}}(T^{\prime},{\mathrm{Run}}(T^{\prime})).

Then
\mathrm{Free}
is clearly an isomorphism over its image in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
, which comprises of the full subcategory of
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
whose objects are games that are trivial for
Alice
. Thus, if
\mathbf{Fin}\mathbf{Gme}
is the full subcategory of
\mathbf{Gme}
comprised of trees with finitely many runs, then
\mathrm{Free}[\mathbf{Fin}\mathbf{Gme}]
is
exactly
the full subcategory of
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
comprised of finite games which are trivial for
Alice
, i.e. (in view of Theorem
5.3
), the full subcategory of
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
comprised of those games which are weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
.

Thus, even though
\mathrm{Free}\colon\mathbf{Fin}\mathbf{Gme}\to\mathrm{Free}[\mathbf{Fin}\mathbf{Gme}]
is an isomorphism, the following result highlights the crucial role of the ambient category
\mathbf{C}
in Definition
5.1
:

Proposition 5.9
.

A decision tree
T
is weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Gme}
if, and only if,
T=\emptyset
.

Proof.

As
T=\emptyset
is the initial object in
\mathbf{Gme}
, it is clear that it is weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Gme}
.

The rest of the proof will be pretty much the same as the proof of Proposition
5.5
:

Fix
R\in{\mathrm{Run}}(T)
and
b
such that
b\notin\mathrm{M}(T)
. Then we let
\mathrm{S}\colon\omega\to\mathbf{Gme}
be such that

•

\mathrm{S}n=T_{n}
, where

\displaystyle T_{n}=T\setminus\lfloor R\restrict n\rfloor\cup\left\{\,{R\restrict k^{\smallfrown}{\left({b:i\leq m}\right)}:k\leq n,\,m\in\omega}\,\right\}.

•

\mathrm{S}_{n}^{m}\colon T_{n}\embed T_{m}
is the inclusion map.

Then
{\left({i_{n}\embed\mathrm{S}n\to T^{\prime})}\right)}_{n\in\omega}
with

T^{\prime}=T\setminus\cup\left\{\,{R\restrict k^{\smallfrown}{\left({b:i\leq m}\right)}:k,m\in\omega}\,\right\}

and each
i_{n}
being the inclusion map is clearly the colimit cocone in
\mathbf{Gme}
of
\mathrm{S}
.

Now consider the inclusion map
i\colon T\embed T^{\prime}
. Then there can be no
n\in\omega
with
f\colon G\to\mathrm{S}n
such that
i_{n}\circ f=i
, since
R\in\overline{i}[{\mathrm{Run}}(T)]
, but
R\notin\overline{i_{n}}[{\mathrm{Run}}(T_{n})]
for every
n\in\omega
. Hence,
T
is not weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Gme}
.
∎

At last:

Theorem 5.10
.

There is a Fraïssé sequence
\mathrm{F}_{\textsc{A}}\colon\omega\to\mathrm{Free}[\mathbf{Fin}\mathbf{Gme}]_{\mathrm{emb}}
. Moreover, the colimit in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
of
\mathrm{F}_{\textsc{A}}
is
G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
.

Proof.

Let
G_{n}=(T_{n},A_{n})
be as in Example
2.3
and then define
\mathrm{F}_{\textsc{A}}\colon\omega\to\mathrm{Free}[\mathbf{Fin}\mathbf{Gme}]_{\mathrm{emb}}
as:

•

On objects,
\mathrm{F}_{\textsc{A}}n=G_{n}
.

•

On morphisms, let
{\mathrm{F}_{\textsc{A}}}_{n}^{m}
be the inclusion of
G_{n}
into
G_{m}
(recall that
G_{n}\leq G_{m}
).

Then it is clear that
{\left({i_{n}\colon G_{n}\embed G_{\mathsf{Flim}}}\right)}
is the colimit cocone in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
of
\mathrm{F}_{\textsc{A}}
, where each
i_{n}\colon G_{n}\embed G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
is the inclusion map.

Now let us show that
\mathrm{F}_{\textsc{A}}
is a Fraïssé sequence.

In order to show condition (a) of Definition
3.1
, let
G
be a finite game that is trivial for
Alice
. Then, because
G
is finite, it follows from Lemma
3.5
that there is an embedding
f\colon G\embed G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
. So condition (a) follows from the fact that
G
is weakly finitely small in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
and that
G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
is the colimit in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}

∎

\mathcal{E}^{*}

\mathcal{E}
for

of

as a single branch which is not on the image of

Thus, we have now provided a categorical proof of the previously shown:

Corollary 6.13
.

G_{\mathsf{Flim}}
is ultrahomogeneous w.r.t.
(\mathrm{emb},\mathbf{Fin}\mathbf{Game})
in
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
.

And it should be emphasized here that, while the proof provided in Section

”, it can also be seen as “

” – since it relies a lot on the particularities of the objects and embeddings of

We have already explained the importance of considering a proper subclass

of

Example 6.14
.

Consider

\displaystyle T_{1}=\left\{\,{{\left({0:k<n}\right)}:n<\omega}\,\right\},\quad

\displaystyle G_{1}=(T_{1},\emptyset)

\displaystyle T_{2}=\left\{\,{{\left({i:k<n}\right)}:i\in\{0,1\},\,n<\omega}\,\right\},\quad

and

and

be such that

and

for every

(with the identity being the connecting maps).

In this case, if we consider the unique chronological

with the cocone

in

.

7.
A brief study of the KPT-correspondence over finite games

The
KPT-correspondence
established in
[
7
]
relates properties of the topological automorphisms group of the Fraïssé limit of an
L
-structure to combinatorial properties of the class of finitely generated substructures. We end this subsection by fully describing
\Aut_{\mathbf{Game}}(G_{\mathsf{Flim}})
in terms of a known group (we don’t specify here
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{A}}
or
\mathbf{Game}_{\textsc{B}}
because the automorphism group is the same in both categories).

Let

S=\{0\}\cup\left\{\,{1/n:n>0}\,\right\}

and denote by
{\mathcal{U}}_{S}
the class of all finite ultrametric spaces whose distance function’s range is contained in
S
.

Then
{\mathcal{U}}_{S}
is a Fraïssé class in the classical model theoretical sense. In fact, such classes, as well as its Fraïssé limit and its automorphism group, were studied in depth in
[
11
]
.

Note that
\mathrm{Met}(G_{\mathsf{Flim}})=((\omega^{\omega},d),c_{00}(\omega))
, where

d(R,R^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\Delta(R,R^{\prime})+1}\text{ if $R\neq R^{\prime}$,}\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then it can easily be shown that
\mathsf{Flim}({\mathcal{U}}_{S})
is isomometric to
c_{00}(\omega)
with the induced subspace metric, so we may consider
\mathsf{Flim}({\mathcal{U}}_{S})=c_{00}(\omega)
.

On the other hand,
(\omega^{\omega},d)
is the completion of the subspace
c_{00}(\omega)
. In this case, any isometry
f
from
c_{00}(\omega)
into itself can be uniquely extended to an isometry
\tilde{f}
from
\omega^{\omega}
into itself. Moreover, note that
f\in\Aut_{\mathsf{Sub}\left({\mathbf{CUltMet}_{1}}\right)}((\omega^{\omega},d),c_{00}(\omega)))
if, and only if,
f
is an isometry from
\omega^{\omega}
into itself that restricts to an isometry from
c_{00}(\omega)
into itself. Hence, in view of Theorem 7.3 in
[
4
]
:

Proposition 7.1
.

The group
\Aut_{\mathbf{Game}}(G_{\mathsf{Flim}})
is isomorphic to
\Aut_{\mathbf{UltMet}_{1}}(\mathsf{Flim}({\mathcal{U}}_{s}))
. Namely, the map
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Aut_{\mathbf{Game}}(G_{\mathsf{Flim}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Aut_{\mathbf{UltMet}_{1}}(\mathsf{Flim}({\mathcal{U}}_{s}))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{f}|_{c_{00}(\omega)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

is a group isomorphism.

However, the so called KPT-correspondence explores the properties of the
topological
automorphisms group of the Fraïssé limit and we have not yet imbued
\Aut_{\mathbf{Game}}(G_{\mathsf{Flim}})
with any topology.

In view of Proposition
7.1
, we can induce a topology
\tau_{\varphi}
over
\Aut_{\mathbf{Game}}(G_{\mathsf{Flim}})
by the group isomorphism
\varphi\colon\Aut_{\mathbf{Game}}(G_{\mathsf{Flim}})\to\Aut_{\mathbf{UltMet}_{1}}(\mathsf{Flim}({\mathcal{U}}_{s}))
, considering that
\Aut_{\mathbf{UltMet}_{1}}(\mathsf{Flim}({\mathcal{U}}_{s}))
is taken with the classical topology of pointwise convergence.

Recall that a topological group
G
is said to be
extremely amenable
if every continuous action
G\curvearrowright X
over a compact space
X
has a fixed point. Model-theoretic KPT-correspondence tells us that the automorphisms group of a Fraïssé limit is extremely amenable if, and only if, its Fraïssé class is
Ramsey
(see
[
7
]
). As we are not within the model-theoretic context, though, we need to rely on some generalizations from
[
3
]
:

Definition 7.2
.

[Definition 2.1 in
[
3
]
]
Suppose
\mathbf{F}
is a full subcategory of
{\mathbf{C}}
and
{\mathcal{M}}
is a class of
{\mathbf{C}}
-morphisms. Let
\mathrm{S}\colon\omega\to{\mathbf{F}}_{\mathcal{M}}
be a sequence, and

.
