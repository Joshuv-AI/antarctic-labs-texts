Dropout Neural Network Training Viewed from a Percolation Perspective
Finley Devlin, Jaron Sanders

Abstract
In this work, we investigate the existence and effect of percolation in training deep Neural Networks (NNs) with dropout . Dropout methods are regularisation techniques for training NNs , first introduced by G. Hinton et al. (2012). These methods temporarily remove connections in the NN , randomly at each stage of training, and update the remaining subnetwork with Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) . The process of removing connections from a network at random is similar to percolation, a paradigm model of statistical physics. If dropout were to remove enough connections such that there is no path between the input and output of the NN , then the NN could not make predictions informed by the data. We study new percolation models that mimic dropout in NNs and characterise the relationship between network topology and this path problem. The theory shows the existence of a percolative effect in dropout. We also show that this percolative effect can cause a breakdown when training NNs without biases with dropout; and we argue heuristically that this breakdown extends to NNs with biases.

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI)

According to the Eurostat, around 30% of large

Eurostat:2024:Use

These technologies improve worker productivity—potentially bridging a gap between low- and high- skilled workers—and can now perform many tasks better than humans
Maslej.ea:2024:AI

Machine Learning (ML)
underpins many of these

Modern

Dar.ea:2021:Farewell

This also means that many of the commonly used

Developing solid theory behind

We focus in this paper on
Neural Networks (NNs)
—a prominent technology in
ML
.
The largest practical challenge with
NNs
is to train the model.
This requires solving a high dimensional, non-convex optimisation problem and is thus computationally expensive.
For this reason, more computationally efficient methods from stochastic approximation are used for training
NNs
—the most common of which is
SGD

Kushner.ea:2003:Stochastic
.
Another part of the challenge of

is to ensure that the model makes high quality predictions on unseen data, and not just the data it was trained on, known as generalisability.
For this, many

techniques are employed that modify the training process

.

More precisely, we study a regularisation technique known as
dropout

Hinton.ea:2012:Improving
.
Dropout methods temporarily omit parts of the
NN
at random, at each step of training.
A stochastic approximation algorithm is then used as normal to update the weights in the remaining sub-network.
The idea behind this is to force different parts of the network to rely less on one another, enabling them to learn features more independently, and thus improving the generalisability.
Although there is a fair amount of research on dropout’s regularisation properties, there is not as much work on the behaviour of dropout as a stochastic approximation method in its own right
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
.
As such, the analytical picture of how and why dropout works well is not yet complete, despite its abundant use in practice.

The random omission of parts of a network relates to

.
Percolation is a paradigm model of statistical physics

Percolation theory studies whether paths cross the medium, and if so, the fluid is said to percolate.
Seeing the

If information does not flow across the
NN
, then it cannot make predictions informed by data.

This paper contributes to the understanding of dropout by formalising and investigating this problem.

II.1
Deep learning

We consider feedforward

Each layer in a network is comprised of activation functions and parameters known as weights and biases.
These activation functions

In

refers to the choices

W_{0}
,
\dots
,
W_{L+1}
,
\sigma_{1}
,

,
\sigma_{L+1}
.
This architecture forms a family of

parametrised by

For our work, we always assume layers are fully connected and the networks have constant width

\ell=0,\dots,L+1
.
These constant width, fully connected models are also found in recent theoretical literature

;

Historically, much focus was placed on

(when

) over deep

(when

.

In this work, we consider the potential issues with training increasingly deep

These issues are connected with percolation theory and for this, we consider the limiting case as

This forms an important step in understanding the finite depth case.

II.2
Dropout

Dropout is a class of methods used for training
NNs
in combination with
SGD
:

Definition II.3

(
SGD
)
.

Let
w\mapsto R(w)
be an
objective function
over parameter space
\mathcal{W}
.

w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\alpha_{t}g(w_{t},\xi_{t})

(2)

is known as
stochastic gradient descent
if the following assumptions are satisfied:

Assumption II.1
.

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}[\left\lVert g(\,\cdot\,,\xi_{t})\right\rVert^{2}]<\infty

Assumption II.2
.

\mathbb{E}\left[g(\,\cdot\,,\xi_{t})\right]=\nabla R(\,\cdot\,)
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}

Assumption II.3
.

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}=\infty
,
\alpha_{t}\geq 0
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha_{t}\to 0
as
t\to\infty

The idea is that at each training step, nodes or weights in the network are removed (filtered) randomly, then the remaining parameters are updated using some stochastic approximation method.
The proposed purpose of this is to reduce the correlation between different weights, meaning neurons can learn different features and improve generalisation of the network
Hinton.ea:2012:Improving
;
Srivastava.ea:2014:Dropout
;
Wan.ea:2013:Regularization
.
This is more broadly known as regularisation, which refers to any technique that tries to reduce overfitting and improve generalisation.

Dropout was first proposed in
Hinton.ea:2012:Improving
, in which each neuron and all their associated weights are filtered independently with probability
1/2
.
Following this, many different dropout algorithms have been studied, notably
Wan.ea:2013:Regularization
introduced so-called
dropconnect
which filters each weight independently with probability
p
.
In
Wan.ea:2013:Regularization
, dropconnect showed improved performance and reduced overfitting of digit recognition of the MNIST database
Deng:2012:MNIST
.
In this work, we refer to any dropout algorithm simply as dropout, but in particular we focus on dropconnect and the original dropout for their respective analogues with bond and site percolation.
Below we define a family of stochastic recursions that we call
dropout
SGD

.

Definition II.4

(Dropout
SGD
)
.

Let

v_{t+1}=v_{t}-\alpha_{t}g(v_{t},\xi_{t}),

(3)

under Assumptions
II.1
,
II.2
, &
II.3
, and
f:\Omega\to\mathcal{W}
be a random vector with law
\lambda
such that each element belongs to
\left\{0,1\right\}
, known as the
filter vector
.
The stochastic recursion

\displaystyle w_{t+1}=w_{t}-\alpha_{t}f_{t}\odot g(f_{t}\odot w_{t},\xi_{t})

(4)

is known as
dropout
SGD

.
Here,
\odot
denotes the component-wise product.

In general, the law
\lambda
defines the type of dropout algorithm.
We focus on two dropout algorithms, one of which, dropconnect, is given by
\lambda
such that each
weight
component of
f
is independently 0 with probability
p
and
1
with probability
1-p
.
In the original dropout the filters are not independent: each neuron is filtered with probability
p
, and if a neuron is filtered, then all the connected
weights
are filtered in
f
.
Note that biases are not typically filtered.
Two examples of filtered networks for both dropconnect and original dropout can be found in
Figure

3
.

(a)
Dropconnect with
p=0.5

(b)
Original dropout with
p=0.5

Figure 3:
Two schematics of
NNs

F(\cdot,f\odot w)
, with two different distributions of dropout filters.
Observe that edges are dropped from each layer for dropconnect, but only in the hidden layers for original dropout.
The unfiltered
NN

F(\cdot,w)
is depicted in
Figure

1
.

We importantly note that by modifying the stochastic approximation algorithm, the gradient estimates no longer correspond to the
SGD
objective
R(\cdot)
but rather some different implied
dropout
objective.
This dropout objective is characterised in
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
, along with convergence guarantees, and is defined as the solution of an ODE arising from the expected gradient estimates.
In
Section

V
, we discuss the dropout objective in the case of the ideal gradient estimate when the objective
R(w):=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(X,w),Y)\right]
is defined in terms of a
loss function

(x,y)\mapsto\ell(x,y)

Our work revolves around the following potential issue with dropout.
Consider the scenario that the sampled filters

are such that no path connects the input and output layers in the
NN
connectivity graph
G(F(\cdot,f\odot w))
.
Consequently,

is a constant function (
Lemma

Under this scenario, a key component of supervised learning is lost: we have labels but with no corresponding input.
Thus, philosophically, no learning is contained in the dropout gradient estimate in this scenario.
Showing that such an issue exists, and how it may be avoided, constitutes the work of
Section

V
.
However, before this, we must consider the probability that this scenario occurs.
This informs our percolation problem in which we investigate the probability that a path exists across the
NN

G(F(\cdot,f))
, constituting the work of
Section

IV
.

This model is known as

The classical question on this model is whether there exists an infinitely large connected component.

We can define another percolation model known as

In this work we consider both bond and site percolation for their respective connections to dropconnect and original dropout.
In

In percolation literature, edges are typically removed with probability

II.3.1
Model

We now define percolation models that mimic the connectivity graphs of our
NN

These graphs consist of
L

W

Additionally, our

are feedforward and thus the edges between neurons are directed.

Definition II.5

(Rectangular Layered Network)
.

Let
L,W\in\mathbb{N}
and define the set of vertices
V:=\left\{1,\dots,W\right\}\times\left\{0,\dots,L+1\right\}
.
Let
E
be the edge set containing the directed edges
(v_{(i,\ell)},v_{(j,\ell+1)})\in E
for all
i,j=1,...\dots W
and
\ell=0,\dots,L
.
Then
G=(V,E)
is known as the
W\times L
Rectangular Layered Network
.
Additionally,
L
is known as the
length
and
W
the
width
of the graph, where there are
L+2
layers each of
W
vertices.
Vertex
v_{(i,\ell)}\in V
denotes the
i
th node in the
\ell
th layer.
The 0th and
L+1

input
and

Remark II.2
.

We make the simplification to constant width for the ease of the readability and analysis of our later results.
Extensions to general layer widths are possible, but would be more cumbersome, while adding little additional insight.

By construction, the rectangular layered network is the same as the connectivity graph of our
NN
model (fully connected, constant width, and feedforward) when all the weights are non-zero.
When we train the network with dropconnect, at each time step some weights are filtered by setting the weight to zero.
A zero weight is equivalent to there being no connection between the two corresponding vertices and therefore we can consider this edge temporarily
deleted
.
This is the precise link between the process of dropconnect and bond percolation, leading to the definition of the following percolation model:

Definition II.6

(Rectangular Layered Bond Percolation)
.

Let
L,W\in\mathbb{N},p\in[0,1]
, and
H=(V,E)
be the
W\times L
Rectangular Layered Network.
Let
E^{\prime}
be a random subset of
E
such that

with probability
1-p
independently for all
e\in E
.
Then we say that the graph
G=(V,E^{\prime})
has distribution
G\sim G^{\text{bond}}(p,W,L)
, which we call the
W\times L

bond percolation model
with parameter

.

Similarly, when training with the original dropout algorithm, for each vertex that is filtered, all of the weights associated with this vertex are also filtered and so we can consider the corresponding edges as temporarily
deleted
.
This yields the following site percolation model:

Definition II.7

(Rectangular Layered Site Percolation)
.

Let
L,W\in\mathbb{N},p\in[0,1]
and
H=(V,E)
be the
W\times L
Rectangular Layered Network.
Let

be a random subset of

with probability

Then define

to be the induced subgraph of

.
We say that the graph

with parameter

Note that, in original dropout, only vertices in

This is represented in

II.3.2
Crossing probability

As remarked at the end of
Section

II.2
, our work on percolation, and its connection to dropout, is rooted in whether there exists a path between the input and output layers of the connectivity graph induced by the dropout filters.
The exact connection between the existence of paths and the performance of dropout is detailed in
Section

V
.

The probability that a path connects the input and output layers is known as the
crossing probability
.
In the canonical model, this crossing probability is characterised in the Russo–Seymour–Welsh theorem, which considers the probability that there exists a path from left to right of some
n\times kn
box belonging to the lattice.
In our model, the crossing probability depends on the percolation parameter
p
and network size
W\times L
.
We define this relationship the
percolation function
:

Definition II.8

(Percolation function)
.

Let
W,L\in\mathbb{N},p\in[0,1]
and
G\sim G(p,W,L)
.
Let
\mathcal{C}(G)
be the set of vertices in the output layer that are connected to the input layer, thus

\mathcal{C}(G):=\left\{j\in[W]:(i,0)\leftrightarrow(j,L+1)\text{ for some }i\in[W]\right\}.

(5)

We define the map
(p,W,L)\mapsto\theta(p,W,L)
known as the
percolation function
by

\theta(p,W,L):=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}(G)\right|>0\right).

(6)

Note that the percolation function depends on the percolation model, which, when the distinction is necessary, will be denoted by
\theta^{\text{bond}}
or
\theta^{\text{site}}
.

Figure

6
depicts the shape of the site percolation function as each parameter changes, with the other two parameters fixed.

These shapes are informed by our characterisation of the site percolation function in
Proposition

IV.1
.
As
L
grows large, each potential path becomes longer and is thus less likely exist.
On the other hand, as
W
grows, so too do the number of potential independent paths, and therefore the probability that one of them reaches the output layer tends to 1.
We also see that the percolation function undergoes a rapid change around some value of
p
.
This alludes to the existence of a so-called
phase transition
of the percolation function in
p
.

Figure 6:
Three figures depicting the shape of the site percolation function
\theta(p,W,L)
when each of
p,W,L
is varied.
The shapes are given by
Proposition

IV.1
.

One of the most interesting properties of percolation models is the existence of phase transitions, however these are typically properties of percolation on infinite graphs.
As such, to investigate phase transitions in our models we must consider limits of the percolation function as the size of the network grows.
We do so by letting the width and length
W,L:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be maps of some index to network size, and then consider the limiting case of these scaled networks.
The concept of a phase transition is formalised below with the definition of the critical threshold.

Definition II.9

(Critical percolation threshold)
.

Let
W=W(n),\;L=L(n)
be such that
W(n),L(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
G_{n}\sim G(p,W(n),L(n))
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and define
\theta_{n}(p):=\theta(p,W(n),L(n))=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}(G_{n})\right|>0\right)
.
The
critical percolation threshold

p_{c}
is then given by

p_{c}:=\text{inf}\left\{p\in[0,1]\;:\;\lim_{n\to\infty}\theta_{n}(p)=0\right\}.

(7)

Note that
\theta_{n}(p)\in[0,1],\theta_{n}(1)=0,
and
\theta_{n}(0)=1
, thus
p_{c}
is well-defined.
The critical threshold defines a concept of phase transition because for
p\geq p_{c}
there is no path with high probability, but below the threshold there is a positive probability that a path exists and thus a small change in the parameter yields a large change in the behaviour of the system.
It is important to observe that the critical threshold
p_{c}=p_{c}(L,W)
depends on the specific scalings of
L,W
.
In
Section

IV
we let
L(n)=n
and analyse the dependence of
p_{c}
on the scaling of
W
.

In the above scaling regime we choose to fix
p
rather than scale it.
This means we fix the dropout algorithm and instead focus on the effect of scaling the topology, rather than scaling both the topology and algorithm.
We believe this facilitates a clearer relationship between the dropout parameter
p
and the performance of dropout seen in our results.
However, our results are easily extendable to the case where
p
scales, which is briefly discussed in
Remark

A.1
.

Related literature

III.1
Percolation theory and random graphs

To our knowledge, our proposed percolation model does not appear in the literature.
The model contains a number of features which are studied individually in literature, but not as a combination.

Most attention in percolation research belongs to the size of the giant component, although the crossing probability has also seen much attention across, for example,
Watts:1996:crossing
;
Cardy:1992:Critical
;
Langlands.ea:1992:universality
;
Kohler-Schindler.ea:2023:Crossing
.
Much of this work considers
planar
percolation models which operate upon an infinite lattice, similar to the canonical model.
In our problem, one of the most important features is that the degree of each vertex grows as the graph scales, and thus we must consider models with growing dimension.

Graph models with growing degrees are a typical object in random graphs.
Our model includes vertices with infinite degrees in limit, which is a feature of, for example,
Esker.ea:2005:Distances
which considers the distances in the configuration model for a power law degree distribution with
\tau\in(1,2)
.
These power laws are studied due to their observation in real world networks, first noted about the Internet in
Faloutsos.ea:1999:power-law
.
However, our degree distribution is still much larger.
In a power law, few vertices have large degree, but in our model, the probability the degree is finite tends to 0 as the graphs grow wide.
The binomial degree distribution of our model is akin to the canonical
Erdős–Rényi
random graph model
Erdos.ea:1959:Random
, although this is typically only studied in the sparse case.
Recent work
Lichev.ea:2024:Percolation
characterises the size of the giant component in bond percolation on the configuration model with dense degrees.
This model aligns with many of the features of our own model in terms of degrees and scaling, but is fundamentally different due to the geometry of our layered networks.

One such problem with growing dimension and emphasis on the geometry, is bond percolation on the hamming hyper-cube
\left\{0,1\right\}^{m}
.
This looks to find the critical probability for the existence of a giant connected component of size
\Theta(2^{2m/3})

Hofstad.ea:2017:Hypercube
.
Although, the geometry of the cube is substantially different to our layered structure.
Percolation on a layered graph structure is considered in
Guha.ea:2016:Spanning
, which stacks independent and identically distributed samples of percolation models on top of one another, and with edges between stacked layers.
In our model, each layer has no structure of its own and edges are removed from between the layers.
This makes the models rather different under bond percolation, but there is some similarity in site percolation since in
Guha.ea:2016:Spanning
an edge between layers only exists if both of the corresponding vertices in the adjacent layers have not been removed.
Site percolation in two-layered networks is similarly studied in
Cao.ea:2021:Percolation
, although our model scales both the number of layers and number of vertices in a layer.

Finally, our graph is directed, which, in the case of the canonical model, reduces the symmetry and adds further complications necessitating its own study, see for example
Henkel.ea:2008:Non-Equilibrium
.
Although, directness actually simplifies our path problem since it removes the existence of loops.

III.2
Convergence of dropout

Literature on the convergence of dropout is the most relevant to our work.
This literature is sparse but has seen recent attention.

The first stochastic approximation perspective on dropout is found in
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
in 2020.

(
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
, Proposition 6)
proves that the iterates of dropout
SGD
, for
NNs
without biases, converge to the limiting set of an
Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE)
.
Under sufficient regularity of the activation functions and dropout algorithm, this shows that the iterates converge almost surely to the set of critical points of the dropout objective.
This result used the classical stochastic approximation tool known as the
ODE
method

(
Kushner.ea:2003:Stochastic
, Chapter 5)
.
The result largely avoided any specific issues of percolation and concluded that dropout algorithms are well behaved asymptotically.

Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
connects the dropout parameter with the rate of convergence of dropout and dropconnect.

(
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
, Proposition 7)
specifically characterises convergence in a weak sense, and shows that the rate of convergence to stationary points increases with a larger percolation probability.
Finally,
(
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
, Proposition 9)
gives a strong characterisation for the rate of convergence of dropout to the minimiser when the underlying network topology forms an
arborescence
.
The result finds exponential decay with a rate that is much smaller than
O((1-p)^{n})
, which shows a clear dependence on the dropout probability.

The difference between our work and
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
lies in the perspective on where
“issues”
with dropout may lie.
We focus on the critical breakdown in performance of dropout due to the percolation effect that no path crosses the network, whereas
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
argues that dropout is well-behaved asymptotically and investigates whether the convergence is slowed by dropout.
For our perspective, we pay attention to specific scaling of the topology and how training time must be scaled with the topology to avoid such a breakdown.
In our scaling regime, dropout may not be so asymptotically well-behaved (
Theorem

V.3
) and the dropout objective may be of poor quality (
Section

V.2.2
).

At a similar time,
Senen-Cerda.ea:2022:Asymptotic
;
Mianjy.ea:2020:Convergence
independently studied the convergence of dropout in shallow
NNs
, although with different techniques and assumptions.
In
Senen-Cerda.ea:2022:Asymptotic
, the convergence rate of linear shallow
NNs
is shown, under the assumption that the iterates follow the trajectories of the gradient flow identified by the
ODE
method.
In particular, it is shown that under this gradient flow assumption, the trajectories converge exponentially fast, with an exponent depending on the dropout parameter, when close to the manifold of minimisers.

Mianjy.ea:2020:Convergence
studies the convergence of dropout in shallow ReLU
NNs
and shows that
\varepsilon
-optimality of the objective can be reached with
O(1/\varepsilon)
training steps.
This result is shown under the assumption of data that is separable by some margin, and by the so-called
lazy training
assumption.
Lazy training refers to assuming that the iterates of
SGD
do not move far from their initialisation.
Different to
Senen-Cerda.ea:2022:Asymptotic
, the convergence rate obtained in
Mianjy.ea:2020:Convergence
is independent of the percolation probability, which they argue is due to separability.
We make no such assumptions, and in particular, we consider deep
NNs
to connect to the critical effects of percolation.

In 2022,
Mohtashami.ea:2022:Masked
obtained convergence results for a generalised version of
SGD
which allows for perturbations and filters, which namely generalises dropout.
In particular,
Mohtashami.ea:2022:Masked
shows that
\varepsilon
-optimality of the averages of
\lVert\nabla R(w_{t})\rVert^{2}
can be reached in
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
training steps.
Choosing dropout filters recovers a result similar to
(
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
, Proposition 7)
.
Characterising the average of means has precedent as a convergence result for non-convex objectives, for example
(
Bottou.ea:2018:Optimization
, Theorem 4.8)
, which can be recovered by
(
Mohtashami.ea:2022:Masked
, Theorem 1)
.
Although this is a weak characterisation of convergence, it enables a general result with mild assumptions.
In
Section

V
we characterise convergence more directly by looking at the evolution of the parameters themselves.

Most recently in 2024,
Shalova.ea:2024:Singular-limit
shows convergence of a general class of
noisy
gradient descent algorithms, which includes dropconnect and the original dropout.
In particular,
Shalova.ea:2024:Singular-limit
shows that when the learning rate and dropout parameter are small, the time scaled iterates follow the trajectories of an
ODE
towards the manifold of minimisers, when starting in its basin of attraction.
Moreover, they characterise the behaviour of the scaled iterates when they reach the MM and continue to evolve along it.
Although the results of
Shalova.ea:2024:Singular-limit
hold for a general class of
noisy
gradient descent algorithms, the results are less applicable to
Section

V
.
There, we scale the processes in a different fashion;
Shalova.ea:2024:Singular-limit
focuses on scaling the hyper parameters of the algorithms, whereas we focus on scaling the topology of the
NNs
.

III.3
Regularisation of dropout

Regularisation refers to any method used to prevent or minimise over-fitting in
NNs
, with the goal of improving the network’s performance on unseen data—known as its
generalisability
.
The majority of existing work on dropout has focused on its regularisation properties, largely due to the fact that this is the intended purpose of dropout from a practical perspective.
The regularisation property was observed in the first work on dropout
Hinton.ea:2012:Improving
;
Srivastava.ea:2014:Dropout
and again for dropconnect in
Wan.ea:2013:Regularization
.
The property has since been well studied across, for example,
Baldi.ea:2013:Understanding
;
Baldi.ea:2014:dropout
;
Wager.ea:2013:Dropout
;
Mianjy.ea:2018:Implicit
;
Wei.ea:2020:implicit
amongst others.

In this work we do not consider the regularisation properties of dropout and focus on percolation induced issues with convergence and the implied objectives.

IV.2
Bond percolation

We next study the dropconnect–bond percolation model.
However, this is more challenging.
There can now be cuts in the graph which are not just across one layer, but rather across many layers.
This is illustrated in
Figure

8

Observe in

In bond percolation, there is similarly a cut if the entire layer of edges is missing (c), but additionally, the cut may instead extend across many layers as given in

Fortunately, we can still obtain an exact characterisation of the bond percolation function.
Computing the crossing probability is akin to path counting.
However, because the paths overlap, there are dependencies amongst them.
To circumnavigate this issue, we can exploit the layered structure and directness of the graph.
This allows one to obtain a recursive result by conditioning on the number of vertices reached by a path in each layer.

Let

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1},...n_{L+1}=1}^{W}\prod_{\ell=0}^{L}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{\ell+1}=n_{\ell+1}\mid N_{\ell}=n_{\ell}\right)

N_{0}=W

V
Breakdown of Dropout due to Percolation

In this section, we detail the effect of percolation on the behaviour of dropout.
In particular, we show that a lack of paths across the network causes a critical breakdown in the performance of dropout in which no learning occurs.
We refer to this percolation–induced breakdown as
“the percolation problem”
.

This problem is more clearly defined as a result of
Theorem

V.3
.

Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
shows that, under various regularity conditions on the networks and algorithms, dropout behaves well and converges to the critical points of the dropout risk function.
To show this,
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
uses the
ODE
method, which requires first taking the limit in iteration steps of the dropout algorithm.
As seen in
Section

IV
, the critical effects of percolation are only present when the networks become large.
It follows that for any fixed
NN
topology, there is enough time to recover from the path related issues of dropout if we first take the limit in iteration steps.
On the other hand, if we instead take the limit in topology first, then with high probability there is no path across the network.
The network could therefore never learn anything because the lack of paths means the output is independent of the input—which we establish rigorously in
Lemma

V.7
.

To bridge this gap between the order of the limits, we ask whether there is some intermediate scaling of both topology and training time that exhibits a non-trivial percolation problem.
This concept of an intermediate scaling is depicted in
Figure

10
, which shows that this acts as a boundary between the two trivial cases from the ordering of the limits.

Figure 10:
A diagram depicting the effect of the order of limits in iterations (training time) and topology on the percolation problem.

Our intermediate scaling perspective on the dropout problem constitutes one of the key insights in this chapter.
We believe that this scaling also has a better
ML
interpretation than taking either limit first.
In practice,
NNs
are only trained for finitely many steps, and with more parameters, yet ever larger
NNs
may need more training.
In this way, the scaling says for how long a given sized network should be trained.

In
Section

V.1
,
Theorem

V.3
connects the scaling of topology and training time, showing that as the networks become deeper, they must be trained for longer to avoid critical issues in the behaviour of dropout.
We also discuss specific cases of this theorem that pertain to the dropout–percolation models of
Section

IV
.
In
Section

V.2
, we discuss interpretations of our assumptions and for which models and algorithms our assumptions and results hold.

V.1

T(n)\in\mathbb{N}
steps, using the dropout algorithm

w^{(n)}_{t+1}=w^{(n)}_{t}-\alpha_{t}^{(n)}f^{(n)}_{t}\odot g^{(n)}(f^{(n)}_{t}\odot w^{(n)}_{t},\xi_{t}^{(n)}),

(9)

for independent and identically distributed filters
f^{(n)}\sim\lambda^{(n)}
under
V.1
and
V.2
.
Define the percolation probability as

\theta(n):=\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{C}(G(F_{n}(\cdot,f^{(n)})))|>0\right).

(10)

Then there exists an
M>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert{w_{T(n)}^{(n)}-w_{0}^{(n)}}\rVert\right]\leq M\theta(n)\sum_{t=0}^{T(n)-1}\alpha_{t}^{(n)}.

(11)

Theorem

V.3
characterises the expected difference in norm of the parameters after being trained for
T(n)
time steps.
In particular, it connects the dropout–percolation probability of a
NN
with the behaviour of the algorithm, thus connecting the scaling of the topology in
\theta(n)
and scaling of training time
T(n)
.
Observe that if the network is trained for too few steps, or if the time steps are too small, and
\theta(n)\to 0
more rapidly than
\sum_{t=0}^{T(n)-1}\alpha_{t}^{(n)}
grows large, then the parameters do not move from their initial condition.
This means that if scaled incorrectly, the lack of paths causes no learning to happen.

The choice of topology scaling and
T(n)
such that
\theta(n)\sum_{t=0}^{T(n)-1}\alpha_{t}^{(n)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
gives an intermediate curve as in
Figure

10
such that the percolation problem exists.
As such, to avoid this percolation problem, the networks must be trained for
T(n)
time steps such that
\sum_{t=0}^{T(n)-1}\alpha_{t}^{(n)}\gg 1/\theta(n)
.
However, this condition does not guarantee that dropout behaves well; it only guarantees the avoidance of the collapse of dropout given by this theorem.
In this sense, the condition forms a lower bound on
T(n)
.

V.1.1
The case of dropconnect

Theorem

V.3
is written in general terms, but if we specify the filter distribution and network topologies, then we can find the largest scaling of
T(n)
such that the percolation problem is not avoided.
In
Corollary

V.4
we use our percolation results from
Section

IV.2
to find a closed form scaling of
T(n)
when using dropconnect.

Corollary V.4
.

Assume the conditions of
Theorem

V.3
.
Let each
NN

F_{n}
have depth
n
and width
W(n)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and choose filters
f_{t}^{(n)}
distributed according to dropconnect with parameter
p\in(0,1)
.
Then there exists an
M>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert{w_{T(n)}^{(n)}-w_{0}^{(n)}}\rVert\right]\leq M\exp(-np^{W(n)^{2}})\sum_{t=0}^{T(n)-1}\alpha_{t}^{(n)}.

(12)

In particular, assuming that
np^{W(n)^{2}}\to\infty
, then the following holds:

i)

Let
\rho\in[0,1),\;c\in(0,(1-\rho)^{-1})
and
\alpha>0
.
If
\alpha_{t}^{(n)}=\frac{\alpha}{(t+1)^{\rho}}
and
T(n)=O(\exp(cnp^{W(n)^{2}}))
, then
\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert{w_{T(n)}^{(n)}-w_{0}^{(n)}}\rVert\right]\to 0
as

.

ii)

Let
c\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>0
.
If
\alpha_{t}^{(n)}=\alpha/(t+1)
and
T(n)=O(\exp(\exp(cnp^{W(n)^{2}})))

\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert{w_{T(n)}^{(n)}-w_{0}^{(n)}}\rVert\right]\to 0
as

.

In
Section

IV.2
we demonstrated the existence of a percolation effect in dropconnect and identified topological scalings under which this occurs.
This corollary states that the percolation effect induced by these scalings causes an issue whereby no learning happens at all, when training with dropconnect.
The condition
np^{W(n)^{2}}\to\infty
is namely satisfied if
p\in(0,1),\tau\in(0,1/2),
and
W(n)=O(\log^{\tau}(n))
and thus the training steps

necessary to avoid the percolation problem grow large.
More details on the specific scalings can be found in
Proposition

IV.5
and discussion therein.

Let us also highlight the case that
W
is constant and with common learning rate
\alpha_{t}=\frac{\alpha}{t+1}
.
For this case, the growth of
T(n)
is depicted in
Figure

11
.

Depiction of the growth of the training time required to avoid the percolation problem for constant width

.
The log training time axis is used to show the difference in exponential and doubly exponential.

To avoid the percolation problem in the scenario of

As such, when deciding whether to add depth to the network, one should consider that many more training steps are needed if the network is already deep.
However, quantitatively, if
p

p^{W^{2}}
is significantly smaller and so these issues can be quantitatively mitigated by the choice of

.
Moreover, by

V.4

to grow more slowly.
Also, if the network is much wider than it is deep, then there is no percolation problem as given by

.

V.1.2
Discussion

Learning rates form continuous time axis.

The term
\sum_{t=0}^{T(n)-1}\alpha_{t}^{(n)}
in
Theorem

V.3
can be thought of as a transformation of the discrete training time axis into a continuous time axis.
In particular, each update is weighted by the learning rate, and thus we can instead think about these updates as continuously driving the process by some gradient for a time
\alpha_{t}
.
As such, the bound in
Theorem

V.3
can be seen as a product of the transformed time and the percolation probability.
This continuous time scaling perspective is also taken by the
ODE
method
(
Kushner.ea:2003:Stochastic
, Chapter 5)
.

Theorem

V.3
implies
lazy training
holds.

Theorem

V.3
shows that for very deep networks, the parameter process does not move far from the initialisation.
This is similar to the idea of lazy training which is the assumption that when training in the strongly over-parameterised case, the parameters move little from their initial value.
As such, the
NN
can be approximated well by a linear model around the initialisation.
Under regular
SGD
, this holds if the curvature of the loss function is much larger than that of the
NN

Chizat.ea:2019:Lazy
.
Lazy training is used in practice for cheaper training steps, but it is also used in theoretical work to obtain convergence results due to the convenient properties of linear models
Mianjy.ea:2020:Convergence
;
Chizat.ea:2019:Lazy
.

Theorem

V.3
implies that for a sufficiently deep network, we can bound the probability that the process stays within an arbitrarily small region around the initialisation, upon which the
NN
can be approximated well by a linear function.
We may be able to use such a linear approximation to further characterise the parameter process.

Gradient norm bounds are alone an incomplete picture.

The related works
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
;
Mohtashami.ea:2022:Masked
characterise the averages of the dropout gradient.
This is a weak characterisation of convergence and does not imply convergence of the iterates to the minimiser of the intended objective as demonstrated by our results.
In particular, the gradient estimate decays with percolation probability
\theta(n)
as the networks grow large and thus the convergence obtained from these results may be induced by the percolation probability.

Note that the boundedness assumptions of
(
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
, Proposition 7)
are similar to
V.1
, and the lack of biases and choice of ideal gradient estimate imply
V.2
(as discussed in
Section

V.2.1
).
Thus under similar assumptions to the related work, we observe that there exists
M>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert f\odot\nabla R(f\odot w,\xi)\right\rVert\right]\leq\theta_{n}(p)M,

(13)

and so we clearly see that the gradients decay with the percolation function. This corresponds to the geometry of the dropout objective becoming flat as the percolation function becomes small.

Intuitively, the dropout algorithm enforces that filtered weights are not updated, and so on average a fraction
p
of the entries of the gradient estimate are equal to zero.
Therefore, as
p
grows large, the magnitude of the gradient estimate decreases.
Our percolation results suggest that for network topologies susceptible to percolation effects, the fraction of zero gradients is much larger than
p
due to this percolation probability.

Theorem

V.3
implies convergence to a global minimiser.

As seen in (
13
) and discussed in the remark immediately above, under the conditions of
Theorem

V.3
, the gradient estimates tend to 0 for all parameters
w\in\mathcal{W}
due to the percolation probability.
As such, the implied objective must be some constant with respect to the parameters
w
(for details see (
V.2.2
) in
Section

V.2.2
) and therefore
Theorem

V.3
implies convergence to a trivial global minimiser.

Corollary

V.4
holds for original dropout.

By replacing
W(n)^{2}\mapsto W(n)
in
Corollary

V.4
, we see that it also hold for original dropout through our results on site percolation in
Propositions

IV.1
and
IV.2
.
This means that
T(n)
must grow even larger to avoid the percolation problem when each neuron is filtered independently with probability

p
.

V.2
Interpreting
V.2

We next show in
Section

V.2.1
that networks without biases satisfy
V.2
under standard choices of gradient estimates and a mild assumption on the activation functions.
Alternatively, in
Section

V.2.2
, we provide a secondary interpretation which makes no restriction on the architecture of the
NNs
, but modifies the dropout algorithm such that
V.2
holds.
We heuristically argue that this algorithm may perform better than unmodified dropout.
This would therefore suggests that some form of the observed percolation problem may exist for general dropout algorithms applied to a general class of
NNs
.

V.2.1
Networks without biases

Consider the following two assumptions:

Assumption V.5
.

F(x,w)=\sigma_{L+1}\circ A_{L+1}\circ\dots\circ\sigma_{1}\circ A_{1}(x)
where
w=(A_{1},\dots,A_{L+1})
and
\sigma_{\ell}(0)=0
for all
\ell=1,\dots,L+1
.

Assumption V.6
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
((X_{t,i},\allowbreak Y_{t,i}))_{t\in\mathbb{N},i=1,\dots,m}
be a sequence of random variables with
(X_{t,i},Y_{t,i})\sim\mu
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
i=1,\dots,m
.
Then let
I_{t}
be a random subset of
\left\{1,\dots,m\right\}
and choose

g(w,\xi_{t})=\frac{1}{\left|I_{t}\right|}\sum_{i\in I_{t}}\nabla\ell(F(w,X_{t,i}),Y_{t,i}).

(14)

The first,
V.5
, specifies a class (architecture) of
NNs
.
This class has no biases and a mild restriction on the activation functions.

NNs
without biases are an important class for this problem because dropout filters only affect the weights and not the biases.
This also has precedent since the previous work
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
only considers
NNs
without biases; and
(
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
, Proposition 9)
uses
\sigma(x)=x
thus satisfying our condition on the activation functions.
We use the assumption to enforce that
F(0,w)=0
and ensures zero gradients when there is no path.

V.6
covers the most common choices in theory and practice.
If we have a dataset
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
where we assume
(x_{i},y_{i})\sim\mu
, then we may choose
(X_{t,i},Y_{t,i})=(x_{i},y_{i})
for all
t\in\mathbb{N},i=1,\dots,m
.
Alternatively, if all samples are independent and identically distributed, then this covers the ideal choice in stochastic approximation theory such as in
Senen-Cerda.ea:2020:Almost
;
Fehrman.ea:2020:Convergence
.
The choice of distribution of
I_{t}
also allows for the important modification of mini-batching.

In order to prove that
V.2
follows from
V.5
and
V.6
, we require the following
Lemma

V.7
, which says that if there is no path across a
NN
, then its output does not depend on its input.
We have referenced this lemma throughout the work as it is key to formalising the intuition that no paths mean no flow of information across the
NN
.

Lemma V.7
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{W}
, if
\mathcal{C}(G(F(\cdot,w)))=\emptyset
, then
F(x,w)=F(0,w)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

The following
Lemma

V.8
confirms that
NNs
without biases of the type in
V.5
, trained with standard choices of gradient estimates as in
V.6
, satisfy
V.2
and thus the results of
Theorem

V.3
.

Lemma V.8
.

If
V.5
and
V.6
hold, then
V.2
holds.

V.2.2
Modified dropout

We may alternatively enforce
V.2
with our choice of dropout algorithm.
We do so by stipulating that whenever there is no path across the network, one does not update the weights.
By doing so, we require no further assumptions on the network architecture and so the networks may include biases and any type of activation function.

The modification sets the gradient estimate to zero at step
t
if the filter samples at
t
yield no path across the network.
This results in a different filter distribution.
Consider filters
f
, then the modified filters are given by
\tilde{f}:=\mathbbm{1}_{E}f
where
E=\left\{\left|\mathcal{C}(G(F(\cdot,f)))\right|>0\right\}
.
Since this is a modification of the filters, we also require no further assumptions on
g(\cdot,\xi)
(other than
V.1
).

Note that changing the dropout filters changes the dropout objective.
Specifically, this modified algorithm is trying to minimise a different objective function.
In the following, we argue that, philosophically, the modified objective is
“better”
than the original objective.
If this modified algorithm tries to minimise a
“better”
objective and still exhibits the percolation problem, we argue that this suggests a percolation problem also exists for the unmodified algorithm.
Although, this percolation problem may not exhibit itself in the same manner as
Theorem

V.3
because the unmodified algorithm may still update the parameters even if there is no learning.
We do not have a proof for these claims, but we can argue them by looking at the objectives.

Consider dropout with filters
f\sim\lambda
and ideal
SGD
gradient estimate
g(w,\xi)=\nabla\ell(F(X,w),Y)
.
We can decompose the dropout objective into two parts depending on the event
E
.
Let
\theta(\lambda):=\mathbb{P}\left(E\right)
be the percolation probability under filters
f
, then

\displaystyle D(w)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(X,{f}\odot w),Y)\right]

(15)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(E\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(X,{f}\odot w),Y)\mid E\right]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{E}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(X,{f}\odot w),Y)\mid\overline{E}\right]

(16)

\displaystyle=\theta(\lambda)D_{\text{path}}(w)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+(1-\theta(\lambda))D_{\text{no path}}(w),

(17)

with
D_{\text{path}}(w),D_{\text{no path}}(w)
defined accordingly.
By
Lemma

V.7
,

\displaystyle D_{\text{no path}}(w)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(X,{f}\odot w),Y)\mid\overline{E}\right]

(18)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(0,{f}\odot w),Y)\mid\overline{E}\right]

(19)

which is independent of the input data and therefore cannot be a
“good”
objective.
If the
NN

F
is deep and
\theta(\lambda)
is small, then
D(w)
is dominated by this poor objective
D_{\text{no path}}(w)
and so the majority of the geometry (including the minimisers) is not influenced by the input data.
On the other hand, the objective
\tilde{D}(w)
for the modified filters
\tilde{f}
can be deconstructed as such,

\displaystyle\tilde{D}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(X,\tilde{f}\odot w),Y)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(E\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(X,\tilde{f}\odot w),Y)\mid E\right]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{E}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(X,\tilde{f}\odot w),Y)\mid\overline{E}\right]

\displaystyle=\theta(\lambda)\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(X,f\odot w),Y)\mid{E}\right]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+(1-\theta(\lambda))\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(F(X,0),Y)\right]

\displaystyle=\theta(\lambda)D_{\text{path}}(w)+\text{ Constant}.

(20)

Therefore, the modified objective is similar, but is only dependent on
D_{\text{path}}(w)
, which contains all the information from the input
and
output data.
Therefore, this objective looks closer to the intended objective with the original filters, but removes the case that there is no path, which we do not want to optimise for.

Ideally, we would like to move beyond
V.2
and show that a percolation problem exists for general
NNs
with biases and any dropout algorithm.
This requires future research, as new mathematical tools are required for this.

VI
Conclusion

We proposed and explored the problem of percolation in training deep
NNs
with dropout.
We established on which
NN
topologies dropout and dropconnect filters exhibit percolation behaviour, whereby no paths connect the input and output layers of the
NN
.
We showed that this percolation behaviour can cause a critical breakdown in the performance of dropout in
NNs
without biases, in which no learning is achieved by training the
NN
.
Next, we discussed that this problem should exist for general
NNs
with biases.
The lack of paths causes the dropout objective to be weakly dependent on the input data and thus does not capture the goal of supervised learning.

A few aspects of this research are left open to future work.
In
Section

IV.2
, the picture of the dropconnect–percolation problem can be refined to establish tight bounds for the scaling of the width such that the critical percolation threshold is non-trivial.
Building on
Section

V
, an exploration into the performance of modified dropout would give insight into the heuristics used in
Section

V.2.2
.
Additionally, a numerical study comparing (modified) dropout would be valuable to the applicability of the results of
Section

V
to real world
NNs
, both with and without biases.

Appendix B
Remaining proofs

Proof of
Proposition

IV.1
.

Since each vertex is fully connected to every vertex in the next layer, as long as there is at least 1 vertex in each layer, there is a path across the network.
As such, we see that the probability that there is a path across the network is just the probability that there are no vertical cuts in the graph.
The probability that there is at least one vertex in layer
\ell
is equal to
1-p^{W}
.
Since there are
L
hidden layers of vertices,
\theta^{\text{site}}(p,W,L)=(1-p^{W})^{L}
.
Note that there are
L+2
total layers, but the first and last layers do not have vertices removed.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

IV.2
.

.

.

Proof of

IV.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
be the set of vertices in layer
\ell
that are connected to any vertex in the input layer, defined as such

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\ell}:=\left\{j\in[W]:(i,0)\leftrightarrow(j,\ell)\;\text{for some $i\in[W]$}\right\}.

(31)

Then it follows that

\displaystyle\theta^{\text{bond}}(p,W,L)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{L+1}\right|>0\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{L+1}\right|>0\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{L}\right|>0\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{L}\right|>0\right)

(32)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{L+1}\right|>0\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{L}\right|>0\right)\theta^{\text{bond}}(p,W,L-1).

(33)

We can bound this conditional probability from above and below to get a recursive relationship.
For this we condition on the exact number of vertices in the
\ell
th layer as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell+1}\right|>0\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|>0\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{W}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell+1}\right|>0\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|>0,\;\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|=k\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|=k\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|>0\right)

(34)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{W}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell+1}\right|>0\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|=k\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|=k\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|>0\right)

(35)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{W}(1-p^{kW})\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|=k\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|>0\right).

(36)

This may be lower bounded by

\sum_{k=1}^{W}(1-p^{kW})\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|=k\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|>0\right)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{W}(1-p^{W})\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|=k\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|>0\right)=1-p^{W},

(37)

and upper bounded by

\sum_{k=1}^{W}(1-p^{kW})\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|=k\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|>0\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{W}(1-p^{W^{2}})\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|=k\mid\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|>0\right)=1-p^{W^{2}}.

(38)

Consequently, using the recursion above, we conclude that

(1-p^{W})^{L+1}\leq(1-p^{W})\theta^{\text{bond}}(p,W,L-1)\leq\theta^{\text{bond}}(p,W,L)\leq(1-p^{W^{2}})\theta^{\text{bond}}(p,W,L-1)\geq(1-p^{W^{2}})^{L+1}.

(39)

Note that
L
is the number of hidden layers, and so there are
L+1
layers of edges between the
L+2
total layers of vertices.
This means that
\theta^{\text{bond}}(p,W,0)=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{1}\right|>0\right)
which may be upper and lower bounded as above.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

IV.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}:=\left\{j\in[W]:(i,0)\leftrightarrow(j,\ell)\;\text{for some $i\in[W]$}\right\}
and
N_{\ell}:=\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\right|
.
By conditioning on the random vector
N=(N_{1},\dots,N_{L+1})
and observing that
N_{\ell+1}
depends only on
N_{\ell}
, we find that

\displaystyle\theta^{\text{bond}}(p,W,L)=\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{C}_{L+1}|>0\right)

(40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{L+1}=0}^{W}\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{C}_{L+1}|>0\mid N_{1}=n_{1},\dots,N_{L+1}=n_{L+1}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(N_{1}=n_{1},\dots,N_{L+1}=n_{L+1}\right)

(41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{L+1}=0}^{W}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{n_{L+1}>0\right\}}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{1}=n_{1},\dots,N_{L+1}=n_{L+1}\right)

(42)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{L+1}=0}^{W}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{n_{L+1}>0\right\}}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{L+1}=n_{L+1}\mid N_{1}=n_{1},\dots,N_{L}=n_{L}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(N_{1}=n_{1},\dots,N_{L}=n_{L}\right)

(43)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{L+1}=0}^{W}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{n_{L+1}>0\right\}}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{L+1}=n_{L+1}\mid N_{L}=n_{L}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(N_{1}=n_{1},\dots,N_{L}=n_{L}\right)

(44)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{L+1}=0}^{W}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{n_{L+1}>0\right\}}\prod_{\ell=0}^{L}\mathbb{P}\left(N_{\ell+1}=n_{\ell+1}\mid N_{\ell}=n_{\ell}\right).

(45)

Suppose now that
N_{\ell}=n_{\ell}
.
Then for each vertex in the
(\ell+1)
st layer, there are
n_{\ell}
possible edges that could connect it to a path and thus it is connected with probability
1-p^{n_{\ell}}
independently of every other vertex in its layer.
Then the number of vertices in this layer connected to the previous layer is the sum of
W
independent Bernoulli trials each with success probability
1-p^{n_{\ell}}
.
Consequently,

.
We also know that if
n_{\ell}=0
, then
1-p^{n_{\ell}}=0
and thus the sum over
n_{\ell}
need only run from
1
to
W
.
∎
