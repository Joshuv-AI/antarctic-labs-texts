Nonequilibrium Theory for Adaptive Systems in Varying Environments
Ying-Jen Yang, Charles D. Kocher, Ken A. Dill

Abstract
Biological organisms thrive by adapting to their environments, which are often unpredictably changeable. We apply recent results from nonequilibrium physics to show that organisms’ fitness parses into a static generalist component and a nonequilibrium tracking component. Our findings: (1) Environmental changes that are too fast or too small are not worth tracking. (2) Well-timed anticipatory tracking enhances fitness in coherent environments. (3) We compute and explain the optimal adaptive strategy for a system in a given environment, such as bet hedging or phenotypic memory. Conversely, (4) We compute and explain the optimal way for an environment to control a given system, for example for designing drug regimens to limit the growth of pathogens. By connecting fitness, adaptive strategy, and environmental variability, this work provides the foundations for a generic physical theory of adaptivity.

Example 4: Anticipatory Tracking Adapts to Coherent Environments

So far, we have considered a current system that acts in its current environment. What if the system has the capability to account somehow for past environments? In our Markov modeling above, knowing how long the environment has been in a particular state does not help to predict the timing of the next switch. Here, we consider environments that have some degree of predictability, such as periodicity. Some adaptive systems can learn to anticipate future environmental changes and respond proactively to counter sensory and response delays, either through precursor signals
Mitchell et al. 2009
;
Tagkopoulos et al. 2008

Saigusa et al. 2008
;
Mi et al. 2013

Pershin et al. 2009
;
Yang et al. 2015

Example 5: How to Control Environments to Manipulate Systems

So far, we considered adaptive system degrees of freedom in unpredictable environments. Here, we consider the reverse: the system is given, and we can control the environment. We illustrate this with a toy model of drug resistance.

When drugs are applied to killing populations of pathogens, such as bacteria, cancer, or viruses, the resulting selective pressure leads the organism to become drug-resistant. A sufficiently strong treatment of a drug will drive the rise of a resistant mutant, and subsequent treatment will fail. Accordingly, drug resistance can be viewed as an evolutionary game that we drug-givers are playing against the disease pathogen
Stein et al. 2023
. An important successful example is “integrated pest management”
Whelan and Cunningham 2020
;
Gatenby and Brown 2020
, where the problem of pesticide resistance has been controlled through better treatment schedules and other strategies explicitly designed to fight resistance. We now show how a toy model of drug resistance, similar to the one in
Robertson-Tessi et al. 2023

Consider a pathogen with drug-sensitive population
N_{S}
and drug-resistant population

\displaystyle=[g-(1-f)\penalty\ \delta]N;

\displaystyle\dot{f}

\displaystyle=f(1-f)\penalty\ \delta+(1-f)\penalty\ r,

Summary and Discussion

Parsing fitness into generalism and tracking components reveals key principles of adaptive dynamics: it explains optimal strategies like bet-hedging and phenotypic memory; clarifies why tracking gain diminishes in fast or biased environments, but can be boosted by well-timed anticipation in coherent ones; and shows how pathogenic populations can be optimally controlled by using fast environmental switching and optimal time-fractioning to independently suppress tracking and generalism.

We note two potential generalizations: extending the framework to multi-state systems or environments (Appendix C in SM
SI
) and incorporating system-environment feedback mechanisms from finite resource or the system’s active changes of environment
Friston 2010
;
Friston et al. 2012
like migration. We also note a conceptual parallel to neuroscience, where the distinction between generalism and tracking is analogous to that between rate and spike-time coding—a choice of strategy in sensory neurons that itself can depend on the stochasticity of the environment
Mainen and Sejnowski 1995
;
Taillefumier and Magnasco 2013
.
