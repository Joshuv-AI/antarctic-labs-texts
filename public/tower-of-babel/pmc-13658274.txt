Long-term cyclic ectopic adrenocorticotropic hormone syndrome with extreme fluctuations and adrenal insufficiency
Authors: Soon Young Choi, Hyounjung Chin, Soyeon Yoo, Sang Ah Lee, Gwanpyo Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 77-year-old woman presented with worsening glycemic control and hypokalemia and was found to have adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)-dependent hypercortisolism with loss of diurnal cortisol variation and failure of suppression on low- and high-dose dexamethasone suppression tests. Computed tomography (CT) demonstrated bilateral adrenal enlargement, whereas pituitary magnetic resonance imaging and chest CT failed to identify a source of ACTH secretion, leading to a diagnosis of occult ectopic ACTH syndrome. During 15 years of follow-up, she exhibited cyclic fluctuations in ACTH and cortisol concentrations, including episodes of severe hypercortisolism with ACTH levels exceeding 2000 pg/mL (>440 pmol/L) (reference range, 7.2-63.6 pg/mL [1.6-14.0 pmol/L]) and cortisol concentrations of 30-60 μg/dL (828-1656 nmol/L) (reference range, 6.2-19.4 μg/dL [171-535 nmol/L]), alternating with spontaneous remission. Serial imaging revealed dynamic adrenal morphological changes, with enlargement during hypercortisolemic phases and regression during remission. During treatment with ketoconazole, mitotane, and osilodrostat, the patient experienced recurrent significant hypocortisolic episodes, some of which were likely treatment-associated and required hydrocortisone replacement therapy. This case highlights an exceptionally prolonged course of cyclic ectopic ACTH syndrome characterized by extreme hormonal oscillation, reversible adrenal hyperplasia, and recurrent hypocortisolism, emphasizing the importance of long-term biochemical surveillance and individualized therapeutic adjustment.Keywords: cyclic Cushing syndrome, occult ectopic ACTH syndrome, ectopic ACTH secretion, adrenal hyperplasia, hyperpigmentation, adrenal insufficiency

Introduction

Cushing syndrome (CS) is a disorder caused by chronic exposure to excessive glucocorticoids. Cyclic Cushing syndrome (CCS) is a rare and diagnostically challenging subtype of CS characterized by intermittent fluctuations in cortisol secretion, with alternating periods of hypercortisolism and remission. Although no universally accepted definition exists, CCS is generally defined by the presence of at least 2 to 3 distinct peaks of cortisol excess separated by spontaneous periods of biochemical remission. Because hormonal secretion fluctuates over time, biochemical evaluation performed during a trough phase may lead to delayed or missed diagnosis. Furthermore, cyclic hormonal secretion may result in discrepancies between clinical manifestations and biochemical findings, thereby complicating diagnostic interpretation and lesion localization studies [1, 2].

Most reported cases of CCS have been associated with pituitary corticotroph adenomas, whereas cyclic ectopic adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) syndrome is less common [3, 4]. The mechanisms underlying cyclic cortisol secretion remain incompletely understood. Proposed explanations include intermittent tumor hemorrhage, fluctuating tumor viability, alterations in hypothalamic–pituitary regulation, episodic secretory activity of neuroendocrine tumors, and cyclic changes in tumor blood flow [5-7]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the periodicity of cyclic hormonal oscillations is highly variable, ranging from several hours to several years [8, 9]. In addition, some patients may experience prolonged remission phases that give the appearance of disease resolution, further complicating the diagnostic process [10, 11].

The diagnosis of cyclic ectopic ACTH syndrome is particularly challenging because the results of dynamic endocrine testing and localization studies may vary substantially according to the phase of hormonal activity at the time of evaluation, potentially leading to inconsistent or misleading findings [2, 12]. In particular, dexamethasone suppression tests, inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS), and imaging studies may exhibit considerable temporal variability during cyclic disease activity [1, 12]. Consequently, repeated biochemical assessments and prolonged longitudinal follow-up are often required to establish the diagnosis [1, 2, 13].

Herein, we report a patient with cyclic ectopic ACTH syndrome who exhibited an exceptionally prolonged clinical course characterized by marked fluctuations in ACTH and cortisol concentrations, spontaneous remission phases, persistent skin hyperpigmentation, variable bilateral adrenal hyperplasia, and clinically significant relative adrenal insufficiency (AI) following steroidogenesis inhibitor therapy. This case provides a unique longitudinal illustration of the biochemical, clinical, and morphological variability associated with cyclic ectopic ACTH syndrome over more than a decade of follow-up.

Case presentation

In July 2011, a 77-year-old woman with a 20-year history of diabetes mellitus and an 8-year history of hypertension was admitted because of worsening glycemic control. Her blood glucose levels had increased progressively during the preceding 10 days despite ongoing treatment with voglibose, metformin, and glimepiride. She reported no specific symptoms other than hyperglycemia. Physical examination revealed mild bilateral pretibial pitting edema without other remarkable findings.

Diagnostic assessment

On admission, laboratory evaluation revealed a random glucose level of 162 mg/dL (9.0 mmol/L) (reference range, 70-110 mg/dL [3.9-6.1 mmol/L]), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) of 8.4% (68 mmol/mol) (reference range, 4.0-6.0% [20-42 mmol/mol]), serum creatinine of 1.1 mg/dL (97.2 μmol/L) (reference range, 0.50-0.90 mg/dL [44.2-79.6 μmol/L]), and serum sodium of 146 mmol/L (reference range, 135-145 mmol/L). Serum potassium was markedly decreased at 2.1 mmol/L (reference range, 3.5-5.3 mmol/L). A single measurement showed that plasma renin activity (PRA) was suppressed at 0.17 ng/mL/h (0.047 ng/L/s) (supine reference range, 0.32-1.84 ng/mL/h [0.089-0.511 ng/L/s]; upright reference range, 0.60-4.18 ng/mL/h [0.167-1.161 ng/L/s]), and an aldosterone concentration of 16.96 ng/dL (470 pmol/L) (supine reference range, 6.75-24.86 ng/dL [187-689 pmol/L]; upright reference range, 9.78-35.91 ng/dL [271-996 pmol/L]). Although the aldosterone concentration was within the laboratory reference range, it was considered inappropriately nonsuppressed in the setting of suppressed PRA, profound hypokalemia, and severe hypercortisolism.

Morning plasma ACTH and serum cortisol levels were markedly elevated at 182.70 pg/mL (40.2 pmol/L) (reference range, 7.2-63.6 pg/mL [1.6-14.0 pmol/L]) and 61.24 μg/dL (1689 nmol/L) (reference range, 6.2-19.4 μg/dL [171-535 nmol/L]), respectively. Repeat measurements obtained at approximately 4:00 Pm showed persistently elevated ACTH and cortisol levels of 85.75 pg/mL (18.9 pmol/L) and 58.92 μg/dL (1625 nmol/L), respectively, indicating loss of normal diurnal variation.

To confirm the diagnosis of CS and determine its etiology, dexamethasone suppression tests (DSTs) were performed. Serum cortisol was not suppressed during the low-dose DST, and neither serum cortisol nor 24-hour urinary free cortisol was suppressed during the high-dose DST (Table 1). Given these findings together with the markedly elevated basal ACTH concentration, ectopic ACTH syndrome was strongly suspected.

Abdominal computed tomography (CT) demonstrated bilateral adrenal enlargement without an identifiable extra-adrenal mass lesion (Fig. 1). Chest CT and sellar magnetic resonance imaging likewise failed to identify a thoracic or pituitary source of ACTH secretion. Inferior petrosal sinus sampling was planned to differentiate ectopic ACTH secretion from pituitary Cushing disease; however, the procedure could not be performed because the patient and her family declined it. Somatostatin receptor-based functional imaging was not performed because it was unavailable at our institution. Based on the marked ACTH elevation, severe hypokalemia, lack of cortisol suppression during the high-dose DST, and negative conventional imaging findings, occult ectopic ACTH syndrome was considered the most likely diagnosis.

Treatment

Medical therapy was initiated with oral ketoconazole to suppress endogenous cortisol production (Fig. 2). Ketoconazole was administered at a dose of 200 mg twice daily from July 14, 2011, to August 4, 2011, and subsequently increased to 400 mg twice daily from August 5, 2011, to July 10, 2012. Shortly after treatment initiation, ACTH and cortisol concentrations decreased transiently, and a relative remission phase was observed (Fig. 2).

In June 2012, follow-up adrenal CT revealed a newly developed left adrenal lesion (Fig. 3). Because malignancy could not be excluded, laparoscopic left adrenalectomy was performed on July 11, 2012. Histopathological examination revealed a well-circumscribed adrenocortical nodule measuring 1.7 × 1.5 cm, suggestive of an adrenocortical adenoma. According to the pathology report, the remaining adrenal parenchyma was grossly unremarkable, and no cortical hyperplasia was reported. Ketoconazole therapy was subsequently discontinued (Fig. 2). Serial follow-up imaging demonstrated dynamic changes in right adrenal morphology, with enlargement during recurrent hypercortisolemic phases and regression during remission (Fig. 4).

Following recurrence of severe hypercortisolism in 2014, mitotane therapy was initiated to reduce endogenous cortisol production. During treatment, the patient developed anorexia, generalized weakness, hyperkalemia, and progressive skin hyperpigmentation (Fig. 5), raising concern for AI. Mitotane was therefore discontinued after approximately 6-7 months. Recurrent hypercortisolism in 2020 prompted reintroduction of mitotane at a low dose of 125-500 mg/day; however, treatment was again discontinued after approximately 9-10 months because of concern regarding treatment-induced AI (Fig. 2).

Skin hyperpigmentation observed during the adrenal insufficiency phase after mitotane treatment. (A) Facial hyperpigmentation noted during follow-up after prolonged mitotane exposure. (B) Hyperpigmentation involving the dorsal surfaces of both hands and fingers. (C) Persistent pigmentation on the palmar surfaces and finger creases, including areas not typically exposed to sunlight. These findings, together with anorexia, generalized weakness, and hyperkalemia, suggested clinically significant functional adrenal insufficiency associated with suppression of cortisol synthesis.

In January 2026, low-dose osilodrostat (1-2 mg/day) was initiated for recurrent hypercortisolism. The drug was temporarily discontinued during a subsequent remission phase and later restarted at the same dosage following recurrence of hypercortisolism. However, recurrent anorexia, generalized weakness, and marked reductions in cortisol concentrations developed, prompting permanent discontinuation of osilodrostat because of suspected AI (Fig. 2).

Hydrocortisone replacement therapy was initiated on April 24, 2026, at a dose of 100-150 mg/day intravenously. Following clinical stabilization, treatment was transitioned to oral hydrocortisone 10 mg twice daily on April 29, 2026, tapered to 5 mg twice daily on May 1, 2026, and then to 5 mg once daily from May 2 to May 5, 2026. Because of persistent AI concerns, maintenance therapy with hydrocortisone 5 mg twice daily was resumed on May 6, 2026, and has been continued thereafter. Following hydrocortisone replacement, anorexia and generalized weakness improved markedly.

Outcome and follow-up

After hydrocortisone administration, the patient's symptoms, including generalized weakness and anorexia, improved markedly. This period was therefore considered a clinically significant relative AI phase. The hydrocortisone dose was subsequently tapered gradually and converted to oral maintenance therapy, which has been continued to the present time (Fig. 2). At the most recent follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable while receiving hydrocortisone replacement therapy and undergoing continued biochemical surveillance.

Discussion

We describe an exceptionally prolonged occult ectopic ACTH syndrome characterized by marked cyclic fluctuations in ACTH and cortisol secretion over approximately 15 years. The patient repeatedly transitioned between severe hypercortisolism and clinically significant hypocortisolism, accompanied by substantial metabolic and electrolyte disturbances. Several features distinguish this case from previously reported cases of CCS [1, 14-16].

First, the duration and magnitude of hormonal fluctuation were remarkable. Although cyclic hormonal oscillations in CCS have been reported to range from hours to several years [8, 9], prolonged longitudinal observations extending beyond a decade remain uncommon [17-20]. Our patient exhibited recurrent episodes of severe ACTH-dependent hypercortisolism over approximately 15 years, with ACTH concentrations exceeding 2000 pg/mL (>440 pmol/L) and serum cortisol levels of 30-60 μg/dL (828-1656 nmol/L). These episodes were accompanied by profound hypokalemia, hypernatremia, hyperglycemia, edema, and progressive adrenal enlargement, indicating substantial biologic activity of the underlying ACTH-secreting source.

An additional biochemical finding was the inappropriately nonsuppressed aldosterone concentration despite suppressed PRA and profound hypokalemia. Although sustained severe hypercortisolism usually suppresses both renin and aldosterone through cortisol-mediated mineralocorticoid receptor activation and volume expansion, ACTH can acutely stimulate aldosterone secretion from the adrenal zona glomerulosa. Transient hyperaldosteronism with suppressed renin followed by aldosterone suppression has been reported during a rapidly evolving phase of ectopic ACTH syndrome [21, 22]. Because PRA and aldosterone were measured only once, ACTH-mediated aldosterone stimulation could not be confirmed, and concomitant renin-independent aldosterone secretion could not be excluded.

Second, this case demonstrated unusually broad bidirectional endocrine oscillation. Whereas CCS is typically characterized by alternating periods of hypercortisolism and biochemical remission [4], our patient repeatedly developed clinically significant hypocortisolic states accompanied by anorexia, generalized weakness, reduced insulin requirements, and transient hyperkalemia. Serum cortisol concentrations decreased to 0.446-2.10 μg/dL (12-58 nmol/L) during several episodes. These findings suggest that cyclic ectopic ACTH syndrome may occasionally extend beyond simple remission and manifest as clinically meaningful cortisol deficiency, creating additional diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

Another notable feature was the exaggerated clinical response to relatively low-dose steroidogenesis inhibitors. Clinically significant hypocortisolic transitions occurred during treatment with ketoconazole and low-dose mitotane or osilodrostat, highlighting the difficulty of balancing adequate control of hypercortisolism against the risk of overtreatment in patients with unstable cortisol dynamics. Frequent biochemical monitoring and careful clinical assessment are therefore essential during treatment [23, 24].

The observed hormonal variability likely reflected both intrinsic disease activity and treatment effects. Marked fluctuations in ACTH and cortisol were documented even during periods without steroidogenesis inhibitor therapy, supporting an intrinsic cyclic secretory pattern. However, mitotane and osilodrostat may have contributed to or accentuated some of the hypocortisolic episodes. A block-and-replacement regimen might have provided more stable cortisol control and reduced the risk of treatment-associated hypocortisolism [24]. Bilateral adrenalectomy was not pursued because of the patient's advanced age and concerns regarding the burden and potential complications of lifelong glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement.

This report has several limitations. The source of ACTH secretion could not be definitively established because the patient declined IPSS and no causative lesion was identified on conventional imaging. Gallium-68–labeled DOTA-(Tyr3)-octreotate positron emission tomography/CT can facilitate the localization of ectopic ACTH-secreting neuroendocrine tumors [25]. However, this imaging modality was unavailable at our institution and could not be performed in this patient. Consequently, an ectopic ACTH-secreting tumor at another site could not be completely excluded, and histopathologic confirmation of ectopic ACTH secretion was unavailable. Thus, occult ectopic ACTH syndrome remained the most likely, rather than definitively established, diagnosis. In addition, serum cortisol measurements obtained after the initiation of hydrocortisone replacement could not reliably distinguish endogenous cortisol secretion from exogenously administered hydrocortisone and were therefore not interpreted as evidence of recurrent hypercortisolism.

In conclusion, this case illustrates an exceptionally prolonged and highly oscillatory occult ectopic ACTH syndrome characterized by recurrent transitions between severe hypercortisolism and clinically significant hypocortisolism. The case broadens the recognized clinical spectrum of cyclic ectopic ACTH syndrome and emphasizes the importance of long-term biochemical surveillance and individualized treatment strategies in patients with unstable cortisol dynamics.
