Combined Anterior Cruciate Ligament Tensioned Remnant Suspension Repair and Autograft Reconstruction for Sherman I‐II Anterior Cruciate Ligament Injuries
Authors: Zirong Huang, Wenhan Yang, Wenzhe Feng, Yankan Ou, Weimin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArthroscopic anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction with tensioned remnant preservation is a simple, reproducible strategy designed to enhance graft biology and stability in appropriately selected patients while maintaining anatomic tunnel accuracy. Prioritized for acute cases with functional remnants (Sherman I‐II), the approach leverages the remnant's “biologic scaffold” and “fluid‐seal” effects to promote graft synovialization, revascularization, and reinnervation, reduce synovial fluid washout into the tunnels, and potentially improve rotational control. When applied under strict indications, this remnant‐preserving, tensioning strategy can achieve functional outcomes comparable to standard anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction while providing additional benefits for graft integration and joint stability.

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is among the most common traumas in sports medicine, and ACL reconstruction (ACLR) is widely recognized as the standard surgical approach to restore knee stability and function. 1 Nevertheless, postoperative residual instability and graft rerupture remain prevalent, prompting ongoing efforts to optimize surgical techniques. 2 In recent years, ACLR with preservation of the tibial remnant has attracted increasing attention due to its potential “biological sleeve” effect: histological evidence indicates that the ACL remnant retains synovial blood supply, fibroblasts, and mechanoreceptors, which may facilitate graft synovialization, revascularization, and reinnervation, while the “seal effect” may reduce joint fluid ingress into the bone tunnels, thereby potentially improving graft integration and joint stability. 3 , 4 , 5

Despite these theoretical advantages, clinical outcomes of remnant‐preserving ACLR remain heterogeneous. Some studies suggest enhanced postoperative stability, lower graft failure rates, and accelerated functional recovery, whereas others report no significant differences compared with conventional techniques. Additionally, concerns have been raised regarding limited visualization, challenges in accurate tunnel placement, and an increased risk of cyclops lesions associated with remnant preservation.

It is critical to note that the benefits of remnant preservation depend on the “quality‐load” status of the remnant. Mechanoreceptors are primarily located in the subsynovial layer around the tibial footprint, and their number declines with increasing time from injury; moreover, functionally loaded, longer remnants exhibit greater reinnervation potential. Therefore, tensioned remnant preservation in patients with a shorter interval from injury and a higher proportion of functional remnant may maximize both biological and stability‐related benefits. 5 Building upon this evidence and recent advances, we propose—under strict indications and targeting acute injuries with Sherman type I‐II remnants—an arthroscopic technique that is simple and efficient while achieving both “complete preservation and tensioning.” This approach aims to maximize preservation of peripheral synovial‐vascular‐neural structures without compromising anatomic tunnel placement, to mitigate the risks of cyclops lesions and tunnel malposition, and to achieve overall functional outcomes comparable to standard ACLR with superior rotational stability. 2 , 6

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Step 1: Positioning and Anesthesia

Under combined spinal‐epidural anesthesia, the patient is placed supine. The operative limb is positioned to meet 2 intraoperative requirements: (1) effortless placement into the hyperflexion position for femoral tunnel preparation and (2) the ability for the knee to hang freely off the table edge to facilitate tendon harvest and handling. Step 2: Graft Harvest and Preparation

With the knee flexed approximately 90°, a 2 cm oblique incision (45°) is made about 2 cm medial to the apex of the tibial tubercle. Layered dissection is performed to harvest the gracilis and semitendinosus tendons. After complete removal of adherent muscle fibers, the tendons are doubled to form a 4‐strand construct according to available length. The graft diameter (preferably ≥8 mm) and effective length (preferably ≥7 cm) are measured. A No. 1 absorbable Vicryl suture is used to whipstitch the free ends, and the graft is loaded onto an adjustable‐loop cortical suspension device for later passage.

Step 3: Portal Establishment and Remnant Assessment

Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals are established. A thorough arthroscopic assessment of the ACL remnant is performed, documenting length and tear pattern. The accessory medial portal is created between the medial femoral condyle and the medial tibial plateau. Under anterolateral visualization, a guide pin is used to simulate the trajectory for the femoral tunnel to avoid iatrogenic chondral injury from instrument impingement on the medial femoral condyle and to secure adequate working space and visualization for precise tunnel placement.

Step 4: Femoral Tunnel Preparation

Loose remnant fibers are selectively debrided to expose the femoral footprint; radiofrequency may be used to mark the planned tunnel entry if needed. The knee is flexed to approximately 120° and placed in hyperflexion position. With the scope in the central (intercondylar) portal and instruments through the medial portal, a 4 mm guide pin is advanced from the center of the femoral remnant footprint to establish the femoral tunnel. After drilling, a depth gauge is used to measure tunnel length. Based on the graft diameter, an appropriately sized reamer is used to create a 2 cm femoral socket (Figure 1A). A femoral passing suture is then shuttled from the intra‐articular tunnel aperture to the skin, ensuring a straight, untwisted path (Figure 1B).

Step 5: Tibial Tunnel Preparation

With the knee flexed to 90°, a 60° tibial guide is positioned, placing its intra‐articular tip at the posterior‐central region of the ACL tibial remnant footprint. A 2.0 mm guide pin is advanced, followed by sequential reaming to match the graft diameter. As the reamer approaches breakthrough at the tibial tunnel aperture, forward pressure is reduced and drilling is slowed to avoid remnant injury, thereby preserving continuity for subsequent remnant coverage and tensioning.

Step 6: Remnant Suturing and Creation of Traction Pathway

Through the central portal, a suture passer introduces high‐strength suture (Figure 2). Two continuous bites are placed across the superior portion of the ACL remnant to achieve robust tissue purchase (Figure 3). Using a suture retriever, both limbs of the high‐strength suture along with the previously placed shuttle suture are retrieved through the central portal, and the high‐strength suture is tied to the shuttle (Figure 4A). Through the tibial tunnel, both the shuttle and the 2 high‐strength suture limbs are pulled out to the tibial side, completing the traction pathway (Figure 4B).

Step 7: Graft Passage and Remnant Coverage

The adjustable‐loop (ETButton Adjustable Loop Titanium Plate, Rejoin Mastin Medical Devices) traction suture is passed into the shuttle loop. The 2 high‐strength remnant sutures—designated to wrap and tension the remnant over the graft—are each passed through different openings of the adjustable loop and then loaded into the shuttle (Figure 5A‐C). The shuttle is pulled steadily from the femoral side to draw the graft sequentially through the tibial tunnel, across the joint, and into the femoral tunnel. A probe is used to gently mobilize the remnant to ensure graft visualization and smooth seating into the femoral socket. The cortical button is flipped on the lateral femoral cortex, and the adjustable loop is tightened to seat the graft securely within the socket. The 2 high‐strength remnant sutures are then tensioned to achieve complete remnant coverage of the graft with appropriate tension. A knot pusher is used to secure the remnant sutures deep to the adjustable loop, finalizing an integrated construct of “remnant tensioning with full graft coverage.” (Figure 5D).

The patient is in the supine position, with the right knee hanging at the edge of the operating table. The traction suture of the adjustable titanium loop is passed through the traction suture loop (A). Then, the 2 high‐strength sutures used to bundle the remnant are each threaded into the openings at both ends of the titanium loop (B) and placed into the traction suture loop for later retrieval (C), ultimately forming the suspensory fixation shown in (D).

Step 8: Tibial‐Side Fixation

With longitudinal graft tension maintained, a 1.5 mm guidewire is placed at the tibial aperture. The knee is positioned at approximately 20° of flexion, and an interference screw (ReliaFix PEEK Interference Screw, Rejoin Mastin Medical Devices) is inserted to secure the distal graft. Graft tension, range of motion, and remnant coverage are reassessed arthroscopically to confirm the absence of impingement or mechanical catching, completing the intra‐articular portion of the procedure. Postoperative Rehabilitation

Postoperative rehabilitation begins with protected partial weight‐bearing on postoperative day 1. From weeks 0 to 2, prioritize achieving full knee extension while gradually increasing flexion to 90°. Between weeks 2 and 4, progress flexion to approximately 120° and emphasize quadriceps isometric contractions and straight‐leg raises. From weeks 6 to 12, advance to closed‐chain functional exercises and proprioceptive training to improve gait and stair negotiation. At approximately 3 months, initiate low‐impact activities such as jogging, swimming, and cycling while avoiding cutting, sudden stops, and jumping. Approximately 1 year postoperatively, consider return to sport when knee stability is restored, isokinetic strength symmetry and functional testing meet criteria, and no significant quadriceps atrophy is present.

DISCUSSION

Preservation of the ACL remnant confers clear biologic and biomechanical advantages. First, the remnant serves as a natural scaffold that promotes graft integration with surrounding tissues and reduces micromotion of the graft during knee flexion‐extension. Second, the residual tissue can share load, thereby decreasing graft stress and, over the long term, enhancing the mechanical stability of the reconstruction. 7 However, although some prior techniques advocated remnant preservation, they did not achieve sufficient remnant coverage over the graft or maintain effective remnant tension, and thus failed to fully realize the advantages of promoting graft synovialization. 8

From a surgical standpoint, more complete remnant preservation combined with tensioning enables improved coverage of the femoral and tibial tunnels by the remnant, mitigating the synovial fluid “washing” effect, which can otherwise lead to tunnel enlargement and adversely affect synovialization. 9 Based on this, precise selection of surgical indications is critical: we recommend prioritizing patients in the acute phase of injury with arthroscopic classification of the remnant as Sherman type I‐II.

Regarding concerns about tunnel placement, current evidence suggests that remnant preservation does not compromise accuracy. Kosy et al. reported that maintaining the remnant does not affect the precision of tibial tunnel placement. 10 By contrast, femoral tunnel placement is more challenging due to remnant masking; nevertheless, a three‐dimensional computed tomography‐based study confirmed that even with a substantial remnant, anatomic tunnel positioning can still be achieved when employing remnant‐preserving techniques. 11

Another common concern is the development of cyclops lesions. Bierke et al. study indicated that, in remnant‐preserving reconstructions, these lesions are often caused by torn bundles of the tendon graft. 6 In our experience, cyclops lesions more frequently occur in Sherman type III‐IV cases, in which the remnant is too short to be suspended under tension. Over time, the remnant may kink and contract anterior to the knee, predisposing to lesion formation. These observations further clarify our indications: prioritize Sherman type I‐II cases; perform judicious debridement or radiofrequency shrinkage for excessively lax torn bundles; and exercise caution in patients with a narrow intercondylar notch, as remnant preservation may increase the risk of graft impingement.

In summary, patient selection and remnant management directly influence outcomes. We recommend selecting acute phase patients with Sherman type I‐II remnants for remnant‐preserving reconstruction and tensioning the remnant arthroscopically so that it fully covers the graft without overtensioning, thereby optimizing the balance between promoting synovialization and ensuring mechanical stability.
