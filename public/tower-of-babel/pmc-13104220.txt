Application of melanin and its engineered nanomaterials in tumor radiotherapy: a review
Authors: Cheng Chen, Yong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiotherapy (RT) is the cornerstone of cancer treatment, but its clinical efficacy is frequently hampered by radiation-induced damage to normal tissues and the radioresistance of tumors. As a natural biopolymer, melanin demonstrates the potential for a bidirectional role in radioprotection and radiosensitization due to its unique physicochemical properties.Main contentThis review systematically analyzes the bidirectional roles of melanin and engineered melanin nanomaterials (EMNMs) in tumor RT. Under physiological conditions, melanin predominantly functions as a radioprotector by absorbing radiation, scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), chelating metal ions, and regulating redox states. Conversely, in pigmented tumors such as melanoma, melanin overproduction leads to RT resistance. This resistance is driven by mechanisms including the “ROS sponge” effect, hypoxia induction, metabolic reprogramming, and interference with immune activation. To circumvent RT resistance, research strategies have expanded from inhibiting melanin synthesis to its functional engineering. Through synthetic biology and nanotechnology, melanin—especially melanin-like substances such as polydopamine (PDA)—can be atomically doped, structurally designed, and functionally loaded to construct multifunctional nanoplatforms. Consequently, these EMNMs can convert RT resistance into RT sensitization by enhancing physical energy deposition, catalyzing chemical reactions, regulating the hypoxic tumor microenvironment (TME), and synergizing with immunotherapy (IT). Furthermore, their utility can be extended to broad-spectrum sensitization applications in non-pigmented tumors.ConclusionMelanin and EMNMs are emerging as highly promising platforms for realizing “bidirectional precision RT” (i.e., targeted tumor eradication concurrent with precise normal tissue preservation) and broad-spectrum RT sensitization. Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT) is the cornerstone of modern oncology, playing an indispensable role in the comprehensive treatment of over 50% of cancer patients. It relies on ionizing radiation (IR) to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) to induce irreversible DNA damage, thereby inhibiting tumor progression [1]. Although RT technologies have progressed with the times, their clinical efficacy remains constrained by major challenges, including the irreversible damage of RT to surrounding healthy tissues and the resistance barrier constructed by hypoxia, antioxidant TME, and complex DNA damage responses in solid tumors [2, 3]. To overcome these dual limitations, there is an urgent need for intelligent biomaterials capable of simultaneously protecting normal tissues in cancer patients and enhancing tumor radiosensitivity. In this pursuit, research into melanin and its engineered derivatives has yielded remarkable progress, spanning physiological melanin, tumor-associated melanin, and engineered exogenous melanin/melanin-like nanomaterials (hereinafter referred to as engineered melanin nanomaterials, EMNMs).

Melanin is an evolutionarily conserved, heterogeneous biopolymer with an amorphous structure [4–6], widely distributed across diverse life forms ranging from fungi and plants to humans [4, 7–9]. Based on differences in precursor molecules and biosynthetic pathways, melanins are broadly classified into subtypes, including eumelanin, pheomelanin, neuromelanin, and allomelanin [4, 7–9]. At the molecular level, melanin consists of a basic diffraction unit composed of planar graphite assemblies [10], which are bound together through a complex network of covalent bonds, hydrogen bonds, cation-π interactions, and aromatic interactions [11]. Melanin possesses unique physicochemical properties, including broadband light absorption, paramagnetism, charge transport, and excellent structural stability [6, 8]. These characteristics make melanin an effective natural radioprotective agent, functioning primarily through ultraviolet (UV) absorption and ROS scavenging [12]. Furthermore, melanin exhibits potential as a photosensitizer, as ROS production is detected when irradiated with ultraviolet A (UVA) (320–400 nm) [12], suggesting its viability for programmable radiation responsiveness.

Tumor-associated melanin shares essentially identical core chemical structures and biosynthetic pathways with physiological melanin, yet it exhibits profoundly distinct pathophysiological implications. In malignant melanoma, the aberrant activation of the tyrosinase (TYR) pathway not only drives excessive melanin deposition but also leads to cytotoxic accumulation of intermediate metabolites [13]. This fosters an immunosuppressive TME, thereby promoting tumor invasion, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [13]. For the purposes of this review, both physiological melanin and tumor-associated melanin are categorized under the umbrella of natural melanin.

EMNMs can be synthetically produced through molecular engineering or by utilizing precursors such as DOPA and dopamine (DA) [9]. For example, through molecular engineering, selenium (Se) can be incorporated to replace sulfur (S) in eumelanin, yielding selenomelanin with a structural profile highly analogous to that of native eumelanin [7]. The formation of polydopamine (PDA) shares several common characteristics with the melanin biosynthetic pathway. Thus, PDA is considered a melanin-like material [14], with its assembly mechanism following the DA oxidation pathway [15]. These engineered materials retain the primary advantages of natural melanin while being abundantly functionalized with various active functional groups such as hydroxyl, amine, and carboxyl groups. These features facilitate targeted delivery and enable precise treatment of non-pigmented tumors [9], thereby potentially achieving a transition from melanin-based RT protection to RT sensitization.

Neither physiological melanin nor tumor-associated melanin inherently possesses the “bidirectional function” of automatically switching between protecting normal tissues and sensitizing tumor cells to RT. Such “bidirectionality” can only be achieved through exogenous engineering design. By primarily relying on structural modulation and functionalization strategies, EMNMs can achieve selective biodistribution and functional switching at normal versus tumor sites. Consequently, “bidirectional precision RT” constitutes an engineering-driven, system-level concept. It exhibits modularity and scalability, extending beyond singular RT applications to integrate with chemotherapy (CTx), targeted therapy, immunotherapy (IT), photothermal therapy (PTT), and other modalities, ultimately forming a comprehensive theranostic strategy.

It is noteworthy that melanin and EMNMs currently remain predominantly in the preclinical research phase. The majority of research evidence originates from in vitro and small animal studies, lacking large-scale clinical trials. Crucially, translating these materials into clinical practice faces formidable hurdles, including the complex engineering required for bidirectional regulation, the intrinsic resistance of the tumor microenvironment, and the stability and biosafety concerns associated with theranostic applications such as radiolabeled imaging. Therefore, this review aims to systematically explore and prospectively evaluate the clinical potential of melanin and EMNMs in the RT of both pigmented and non-pigmented tumors. This review incorporates personal perspectives on potential challenges and future directions, aiming to promote discussion among relevant researchers and arouse interest in this research direction.

Physicochemical properties and radiobiological basis of melanin

Structural heterogeneity and engineering potential

Various melanin subtypes show significant differences in structure and function, primarily associated with pigmentation and UV protection [9, 16–18]. It is generally believed that eumelanin confers a photoprotective effect on pigmented tissues, whereas pheomelanin has phototoxicity [5, 19].

The biosynthesis of melanin involves complex cascades of enzymatic and biochemical reaction pathways, collectively known as the Raper-Mason pathway [20, 21]. Within this pathway, melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) activity regulates the synthesis of two melanins in melanocytes [17, 19, 22]. Tyrosinase (TYR), tyrosinase-related protein-1 (TRP-1), and tyrosinase-related protein-2 (TRP-2) serve as the three master regulators of melanin production [16]. As the core rate-limiting enzyme of this pathway, TYR dictates the formation efficiency of dopaquinone (DQ) and the subsequent diversion of melanin types (Fig. 1A) [16].

Eumelanin

Eumelanin is formed through the oxidative polymerization of 5,6-dihydroxyindole (DHI) and its 2-carboxylic acid derivative (DHICA), forming an extended π-conjugated system. Its broadband monotonic absorption, stable paramagnetism, and electron exchange properties make it an excellent electron acceptor or donor (Fig. 1A, C) [5, 6, 8, 9, 18]. Notably, the effective defense barrier against UV-induced damage is largely attributed to the presence of DHICA [17]. DHICA not only affects the stacking mode of melanin polymers but also determines the kinetic characteristics of their antioxidant and free radical-scavenging [11, 17, 19]. Concurrently, the DHI-to-DHICA ratio also profoundly influences the antioxidant activity and metal-chelating ability of eumelanin [5, 18]. Significantly, DHICA can promote DNA fragmentation and inhibit the activity of DNA repair enzymes [12], thereby acting as a photosensitizer. This has led researchers to realize that eumelanin is not only a radioprotectant, but also may be one of the basic structural components of RT-sensitizing materials.

Pheomelanin

Pheomelanin is derived from the oxidative polymerization of 5-S-cysteine dopa (5-S-CD) and 2-S-cysteine dopa (2-S-CD), comprising benzothiazine and benzothiazole units (Fig. 1A and C) [5, 9, 19]. Importantly, pheomelanin exhibits a lower ionization threshold (3.9 eV) compared to eumelanin (4.6 eV), alongside a higher oxidation potential (+ 0.5 V vs. -0.2 V for eumelanin, relative to the standard hydrogen electrode) [19, 24]. This indicates that pheomelanin is more prone to electron transfer and possesses higher pro-oxidative potential. Such structural instability suggests it can transition from an antioxidant to a prooxidant under specific high-energy radiation excitation. In addition, the biosynthesis of pheomelanin imposes a substantial metabolic burden. Studies have shown that the synthesis of pheomelanin requires a large consumption of antioxidants, which will directly weaken the cell’s capacity to scavenge free radicals [19, 25]. The pro-oxidative state induced by pheomelanin may exacerbate genomic instability under radiation exposure. Studies indicate that the pheomelanin pathway is involved in UV-independent melanoma carcinogenesis via oxidative DNA damage mechanisms [5, 22]. Therefore, the instability and pro-oxidation potential of pheomelanin are the inspiration for the subsequent design of biomimetic sensitizing materials for pigmented and non-pigmented tumors. By synthesizing nanomaterials structures analogous to pheomelanin, researchers can intentionally induce oxidative stress under IR to amplify RT sensitization.

Neuromelanin and allomelanin

Neuromelanin, localized in the substantia nigra of the mammalian brain, exhibits a unique “core-shell” hybrid structure: the pheomelanin is the core and the surface is covered with the eumelanin [26]. This structure enables it to seal cytotoxic catecholamines and redox-active metals, conferring a distinct cytoprotective effect [9].

Conversely, allomelanin is mainly found in plants and microorganisms (e.g., fungi and bacteria) (Fig. 1C) [9]. Certain variants, such as pyomelanin, demonstrate extreme thermal stability and photostability [27]. This structural stability stems from its unique aromatic carbon-carbon (Car-Car) linkages, which enable it to maintain electron buffering function under extreme physical pressure [27]. This provides a new material perspective for understanding how organisms leverage distinct melanin configurations to withstand environmental pressure.

Physical mechanism of radiation interception

From the perspective of physics, melanin is regarded as an amorphous organic semiconductor (Fig. 2B) [6, 10]. Turick et al. demonstrated via electrochemical experiments that fungal-derived eumelanin undergoes a continuous oxidation reaction in a γ-ray field, producing a quantifiable electrical current [28]. This current is positively correlated with both radiation dose and melanin concentration, providing critical evidence that IR directly alters the redox potential of melanin [28]. Bioelectronics defines melanin as an efficient “electron-ion mixed conductor.” [27]. During the redox cycle, electron release or capture is accompanied by the redistribution of protons within the polymer matrix until equilibrium is established. This dual-conductive mechanism enables efficient charge storage and energy conversion at the femtogram level (Fig. 2B) [27]. These physical properties originate from a “local order-global disorder” model (Fig. 2C). Its π-stacked molecular system, along with hydrogen-bonding and ion-bonding nanostructures, endows it with graphite-like electronic properties [6, 8, 10].

Melanin can interact with a variety of electromagnetic radiation frequencies. Its high electron cloud density enables it to interact with high-energy photons via mechanisms such as Compton scattering, facilitating rapid energy dissipation (Fig. 2A) [29]. Schweitzer et al. proposed that this energy dissipation is controlled [30]. The aromatic oligomers within melanin’s extensive multi-π electron systems enable Compton recoil electrons generated by IR to dissipate energy via stepwise transitions. This process continues until their energy is sufficiently attenuated to be captured by stable free radical traps, effectively preventing the cascade of secondary ionization and ROS from the source (Fig. 2A) [30]. For synthetic eumelanin, over 99.9% of the absorbed photons undergo non-radiative dissipation under UV excitation [31]. This mechanism is due to the extremely high “electron-phonon coupling” efficiency of melanin, which can rapidly convert the absorbed high-energy radiation into thermal energy through molecular vibration modes or phonons [8]. This serves as one of the core kinetic foundations of melanin as a physical shielding agent.

Natural melanin can exhibit an efficient “weak acid cation exchanger” property (Fig. 2F), conferring unique metal chelating ability, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), and characteristic X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) [6, 18]. Spectroscopic analyses reveal that melanin can strongly capture metal ions via octahedral coordination, utilizing its high affinity sites of phenolic hydroxyl and carboxyl groups [18, 32]. Specifically, Fe(III) chelation is predominantly mediated by hydroxyl group at pH 2.0–7.0, whereas Cu(II) chelation is driven by carboxyl and/or amino groups at pH 7.0–11.0 [33, 34]. Given that the TME is usually acidic (pH 6.4–7.0) [35], this means that melanin may mainly chelate redox-active metals (e.g., iron and copper) via phenolic hydroxyl groups in the tumor, thereby modulating local Fenton/Fenton-like reactions (The Fenton reaction refers to the chemical reaction that occurs between hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and Fe(II). Fenton-like reaction refers to the redox reaction similar to the traditional Fenton reaction, but the catalyst used is not iron ions), inhibiting metal ion reduction, and preventing oxidative stress [4].

The coordination of metal ions profoundly affects the radiochemical behavior of melanin. Evidence confirms that Zn(II) can significantly enhance the oxygen consumption and superoxide-generating capacity of melanin [19]. Furthermore, melanin’s energy dissipation capacity is highly correlated with the atomic number (Z) of its constituent elements [7]. Studies indicate that the radiation absorbed dose can be reduced by up to 50% by laying a high-Z materials at the radiation-incident surface [36]. Based on this principle, synthetic biology approaches have been employed to introduce high-Z elements, augmenting the physical shielding effectiveness of native melanin, designing a new generation of high-efficiency radiation shielding materials based on the “Z-lifting” strategy [7, 30, 37].

Dynamic redox of melanin

“Free radical sponge” and “electron buffer pool”

Melanin has a redox balance among its quinone, semiquinone, and hydroquinone states (Fig. 2E). This enables it to quickly and repeatedly cycle between the oxidized and reduced states [5, 24]. At the same time, the disproportionation balance maintains the concentration of reactive melanin free radical at an exceptionally low level (see the formula (1.1)) [6].

The redox balance and disproportionation balance endow melanin with the characteristics of “free radical sponge” and “electron buffer pool.” It can not only provide electrons to hydroxyl radicals (· OH) but also capture electrons from superoxide anions (O2⁻), thereby rapidly quenching primary ROS produced by water radiolysis and restoring itself to steady state (Fig. 2D) [5, 8, 38, 39]. Remarkably, even after receiving an ultra-high dose of IR up to 30,000 cGy, the stable free radical concentration and EPR spectral characteristics in the synthetic melanin remained constant [30]. However, the capacity of this buffer pool is finite. Prolonged exposure to UVA or blue light irradiation induces the photodegradation of both eumelanin and pheomelanin, accelerating the production of ROS, especially O2⁻ and singlet oxygen (1O2), and transforms melanin from antioxidant to prooxidant (Fig. 1B) [5, 18, 19]. The characteristics of a “free radical sponge” and “electronic buffer pool” determine that melanin itself is not a simple antioxidant but also a two-way electronic switch. Understanding this switching mechanism is the key to achieving the transformation from resistance to sensitization.

Environmental dependence of melanin antioxidant

The antioxidant function of melanin is highly dependent on the environment. In the steady state, melanin inhibits the Fenton and Fenton-like reaction by chelating free iron and/or copper ions [40], thereby reducing the generation of highly toxic · OH [18]. However, within the H₂O₂-enriched environment produced by RT, melanin may promote the formation of highly toxic · OH (see the formula (1.2)) [32].

The interaction between H₂O₂ and Fe(II) produces highly active · OH [32].

This catalytic reaction mediated by the “metal-melanin axis” constitutes a key chemical switch, inverting melanin from a radioprotectant to a radiosensitizer (or prooxidant).

Furthermore, a “dark chemistry” mechanism dominates in the pheomelanin-rich environment. Pheomelanin can be used as a redox catalyst, actively accelerating the depletion of key antioxidants such as glutathione (GSH) and reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) in cells through a radiation-independent electron transfer pathway [19]. The pro-oxidative activity of pheomelanin may maintain a chronic oxidative stress state through a redox buffering mechanism [41]. This persistent oxidative stress mechanism explains why pheomelanin-overproducing tumor cells may be naturally in a redox imbalance state, thereby interfering with the RT-induced ROS eradicating threshold.

The radioprotection applications of melanin and EMNMs

While eradicating tumors through high-energy photons or particles, IR inevitably causes radiation-induced damage to surrounding healthy tissues, especially the sensitive hematopoietic system and gastrointestinal (GI) tract [42, 43]. Exploiting the unique physicochemical properties described in section “Physicochemical properties and radiobiological basis of melanin”, melanin and EMNMs demonstrate substantial clinical potential for protecting non-tumorous and healthy tissues against IR-induced injury [29, 42, 43].

The viability of melanin as a safe radioprotectant also lies in its differential biodistribution. By controlling the size of EMNMs within the 20–100 nm range, the self-filtration properties of the human reticuloendothelial system (RES) can be leveraged to allow passive targeting and accumulation in radiation-sensitive hematopoietic tissues, such as bone marrow [43, 44]. This distribution pattern covers the hematopoietic system, thereby achieving passive targeted protection. Furthermore, for common intestinal injuries during abdominal RT, melanin formulations and EMNMs can locally scavenge free radicals in the GI tract, thereby protecting intestinal stem cells and mitigating radiation damage [29, 42]. Importantly, while these nanoparticles may be taken up by RES organs like the liver and spleen, unmodified EMNMs exhibit restricted penetration into the dense stroma of solid tumors. This limited tumoral accumulation drastically minimizes the risk of unintended radioprotection of tumors themselves [44]. Ultimately, this differential biodistribution not only explains the safety profile of EMNMs as systemic radioprotectants but also dictates a critical design imperative: if these materials are to be used for tumor RT sensitization, fine external surface engineering must be carried out to break through the physiological barrier.

RT resistance of melanin

“ROS sponge” effect and lethal threshold remodeling

As previously established, a primary tumoricidal mechanism of RT involves the radiolysis of intracellular water molecules by high-energy radiation, which generates a surge of ROS to induce irreversible DNA damage. However, functioning as a strong “ROS sponge,” melanin efficiently absorbs radiation and eliminates RT-generated ROS. This directly reduces RT-induced DNA oxidative damage while suppressing intracellular ROS baseline levels below the lethal threshold [38]. Furthermore, high concentrations of melanin not only directly scavenge free radicals but also construct a TME with high antioxidant capacity by upregulating intracellular GSH and peroxidase [45, 46]. This environment makes it exceedingly difficult for RT to trigger the oxidative stress threshold required for apoptosis, thereby promoting the survival of cancer cells (Fig. 3A).

Melanogenesis-driven hypoxia and metabolic reprogramming

The therapeutic effect of IR depends on the “oxygen fixation hypothesis,” which posits that oxygen can fix DNA damage caused by radiation and make it permanent and irreparable. However, melanin synthesis itself is an extremely oxygen-consuming process, and its metabolism may be one of the reasons for the failure of the “oxygen fixation effect.” [12].

Hypoxia

TYR and its downstream TRP-1 play an important role in this metabolic consumption. Studies have demonstrated that the high TRP-1 expression is significantly and positively correlated with the nuclear accumulation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) (p = 0.045) [47]. HIF-1α can upregulate a cascade of genes governing tumor angiogenesis, metastasis, and antioxidative stress [48]. In addition, the activation of melanin synthesis can significantly inhibit mitochondrial respiration, precipitating severe local cellular hypoxia (Fig. 3B) [12]. This oxygen depletion directly reduces the oxygen enhancement ratio (OER) of the tumor, predisposing the pigmented melanoma cells to a state of innate hypoxic radioresistance and rendering pigmented tumor regions highly susceptible to necrosis [46, 47].

Metabolic reprogramming

The excessive accumulation of endogenous melanin in the human body is not only the accumulation of isolated metabolites but also the result of abnormal activation of signaling pathways. IR can activate cyclic adenosine monophosphate/protein kinase A (cAMP/PKA), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt), and wingless/Int-1 and beta-catenin (Wnt/β-catenin) signaling pathways, which collectively converge on the microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) to drive melanogenesis [49, 50]. Notably, under normoxic conditions, MITF can directly bind to the E-box sequence on the Hif1a gene promoter, strongly driving the expression of HIF-1α. This pre-initiate the defense mechanism of anti-radiation and pro-angiogenesis, without waiting for the occurrence of physical hypoxia [51]. Furthermore, research indicates that HIF-1α transiently upregulates MITF expression in the early stage of hypoxia, which in turn activates hypoxia-responsive genes [52]. This suggests that in the early stage of RT, tumor cells not only did not stop melanin synthesis, but also improved glycolysis and angiogenesis by transiently enhancing MITF function. This dual adaptation not only maintains the antioxidant barrier required for radioresistance but also strengthens the metabolic survival advantage (Fig. 3B).

DNA repair interference and immune escape

Physical binding interferes with DNA repair

Beyond absorbing radiation energy, melanin can bind directly to DNA molecules. This physical binding sterically hinders DNA repair enzymes from approaching the damage site [53]. Although this may seem to increase damage, in tumor cells, this mechanism may force error-prone repair pathways, ultimately giving rise to more aggressive radioresistant phenotypes (Fig. 3A).

Concealing “danger signals” and immunosuppression

Current literature highlights the immune-activating properties of RT [54], revealing that its therapeutic efficacy partially depends on inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD). ICD triggers the release of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and activates T cells [55, 56], thereby transforming “cold” tumors (characterized by weak immunogenicity and less tumor infiltrating T cells) into “hot” tumors (characterized by strong immunogenicity and rich T cell infiltration) to enhance anti-tumor immunity [54, 57].

However, the profound ROS-scavenging ability of melanin weakens the oxidative stress signal required by ICD. Melanin may directly adsorb the damage-associated molecular patterns released by RT, thereby masking TAAs and leading to the formation of “cold” tumors [58]. In-depth mechanistic analyses reveal that melanin’s ROS clearance directly blocks the activation of the cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) (cGAS-STING) signaling pathway. Normally, cytoplasmic DNA, TAAs, and RT-induced ROS activate STING, driving type-I interferon (IFN-1) production and anti-tumor immune infiltration [59]. However, melanin inhibits the STING-dependent transcription process by maintaining low intracellular ROS levels, making it impossible for tumor cells to send “danger signals” to recruit immune cells (Fig. 3C) [45]. This also be one of the important reasons for the primary resistance of melanoma to RT combined IT.

Two-way intervention based on the nature of melanin: from melanin removal to engineering empowerment

Given the physical shielding, metabolic reprogramming, and immunosuppression mediated by melanin in tumor RT (see section “RT resistance of melanin”), single-modality RT mode often exhibits limited efficacy. Strategies to overcome this RT resistance are shifting from simple physical confrontation to precise intervention based on molecular mechanisms. Excitingly, nanoengineering is transforming melanin into a multifunctional RT-sensitizing platform, which holds broad applicability for treating both pigmented and non-pigmented tumors. This section will discuss how to manipulate melanin to enhance the efficacy of RT from three dimensions: inhibition synthesis, targeted utilization, and engineering sensitization.

Blocking the synthetic pathway

Considering melanin’s role in RT resistance, researchers have proposed the hypothesis of inhibiting melanin production to improve the sensitivity of melanoma to RT [60, 61]. The primary approach involves inhibitors that directly target TYR. Beyond traditional N-phenylthiourea (PTU), various natural or synthetic compounds with better bioavailability (e.g., resorcinol derivatives and kojic acid derivatives) significantly reduce intracellular melanin levels via competitive or non-competitive inhibition of TYR activity [16, 62–64]. A secondary approach regulates upstream signaling pathway. Interfering with MC1R/alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH), PI3K/Akt, Wnt/β-catenin, and other pathways to inhibit the transcriptional activity of MITF can fundamentally block melanin synthesis at its source [16, 50].

Targeted melanin

The highly specific expression of melanin in melanoma makes it an ideal target for radiopharmaceutical theranostics [65]. Radionuclide therapy (RNT) can specifically target melanin using radiolabeled small molecule drugs (e.g., benzamide compounds) or radioimmunotherapy (RIT) [66, 67]. These small molecules and antibodies can recognize and bind to melanin or melanin-like structures within tumors, enhancing tumor penetration to effectively eradicate tumor cells while minimizing damage to healthy melanin-containing tissues [66, 68, 69].

Ultimately, whether chemically cutting off melanin synthetic or utilizing it as a target for radionuclides, the core purpose is to break the radioresistance homeostasis constructed by melanin.

Two-way intervention strategy and engineering empowerment

Although the intervention strategy against melanin has weakened the tumor defense system to a certain extent, the inherent limitations of natural melanin in low-Z element composition and lack of functional diversity preclude its direct use as an efficient RT sensitizer.

Since melanin is an ideal carrier due to its excellent biocompatibility, can it be functionally engineered by nanotechnology? Section “Two-way intervention strategy and engineering empowerment” to “EMNMs for RT sensitization: unique advantages and universal design principles” will explore engineering transformation based on melanin-like materials, such as PDA. Rather than viewing melanin merely as an obstacle to RT, researchers have upgraded it into a multifunctional platform to overcome RT resistance via atomic doping, structural design, and multimodal loading. This engineering approach can be categorized into three dimensions: physicochemical sensitization, hypoxic TME regulation, and systemic synergy.

Physicochemical sensitization

Although natural melanin can dissipate energy via Compton scattering, its main low-Z element composition limits the ability to intercept high-energy X-rays. Studies demonstrate that the introduction of high-Z elements can enhance the Compton effect and photoelectric effect, generating more photoelectrons and Auger electrons [70, 71]. These scattered secondary electrons can directly damage DNA and produce ROS via water radiolysis [72]. For example, Aishwarya et al. constructed selenium melanin nanoparticles (SeMNPs), which promote ROS production and induce DNA double-strand breaks. Furthermore, these nanoparticles regulate Checkpoint Kinase 1/Checkpoint Kinase 2 (CHK1/CHK2) phosphorylation to induce G2/M phase arrest, ultimately activating Caspase-3 to trigger apoptosis [73].

On the other hand, chemical sensitization based on metal ion catalysis has emerged as a research hotspot. Leveraging the excellent chelating ability of PDA, diverse metal ions can be integrated into the nano-skeleton to catalyze the Fenton/Fenton-like reactions in the TME, amplifying ROS production for tumor destruction [74, 75]. Notably, these reactions encompass metal with multiple redox states, such as Fe, Cu, manganese (Mn), cobalt (Co), chromium (Cr), and cerium (Ce) [76]. This catalytic process not only directly converts H2O2 into highly toxic · OH, but is often accompanied by the consumption of endogenous antioxidants. Consequently, this generates an intracellular ROS storms that significantly magnifies RT lethality on tumor cells. Prominent examples include biomimetic Cu-doped PDA nanoparticles, Mesoporous polydopamine (MPDA)-gold (Au)-manganese dioxide (MnO2) nanoplatform, Fe(II)-doped Fe(II)-PDA grafted natural glucose oxidase (See the specific mechanism of action in Table 1) [74, 75, 77].

Regulating the hypoxic TME

By introducing high-Z elements and catalyzing Fenton and Fenton-like reactions, EMNMs have successfully created a double blow of physical dose enhancement and chemical ROS storms in cells. However, the pervasive hypoxic TME in solid tumors may offset most of the ROS-mediated radiation damage due to the lack of oxygen fixation effect (see section “Melanogenesis-driven hypoxia and metabolic reprogramming”). Therefore, alleviating tumor hypoxia is a critical breakthrough for optimizing these sensitization strategies. It is worth noting that studies have shown that continuous low-dose inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) gas therapy significantly enhances RT, primarily by increasing blood perfusion and oxygen supply in the TME, a synergy primarily attributed to the OER [2]. Because RT depends on ROS generation to kill cancer cells, severe hypoxia makes tumor eradication nearly impossible. iNO overcomes this challenge by inducing vasodilation to increase oxygen delivery to hypoxic zones while concurrently inhibiting cellular respiration. This dual action spares available oxygen, prolonging its presence in the tissue to ensure radiation can successfully generate DNA-damaging ROS. Based on the above mechanisms, Ding et al. developed a melanin-based nanoplatform that generates abundant ROS under near-infrared light irradiation and co-promote NO release within the TME characterized by high GSH expression. NO further upregulates the soluble guanylate cyclase-cyclic guanosine monophosphate (sGC-cGMP) signaling pathway, inducing tumor vasodilation and alleviating tumor hypoxia. Additionally, the cascade release of ROS and NO yields can generate highly lethal reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which initiate tumor cell apoptosis [80]. The platform demonstrates the feasibility of integrating direct cytotoxicity with hypoxia regulation.

To address radiation resistance, EMNMs designs further focus on systematically integrating direct attack with the regulation of the hypoxic TME. The engineering strategies for regulating the hypoxic TME are mainly divided into two types: alleviating hypoxia and utilizing hypoxia.

The first strategy focuses on directly reversing hypoxia to restore sensitivity to oxygen-dependent therapies. For instance, Zhan et al. utilized an oxygen bubble-template method to synthesize hollow PDA (HPDA) capable of encapsulating and delivering oxygen to the tumor sites. Simultaneously, the acidic TME triggers the release of the encapsulated photosensitizer, Zn phthalocyanine, thereby enhancing PDT efficacy [69]. Furthermore, Wang et al. utilized an MnO2 shell to as a catalase-like nanozyme, decomposing intratumoral H2O2 and generate oxygen. Guided by the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-specific dimer affinity, this system achieved active tumor targeting and subsequent downregulation of HIF-1α [81]. Similarly, Gong et al. combined HPDA with platinum nanoparticles to catalyze oxygen generation. Loading this nanoplatform with the apoptotic protein [100–109] and verteporfin significantly enhanced both apoptosis induction and PDT outcomes [82].

Theranostics

In clinical reality, standalone RT often struggles with immunosuppression and a weak abscopal effect [59, 83]. The abscopal effect refers to the phenomenon where RT at a localized site elicits a clinical response in distant, unirradiated lesions [1]. Because natural melanin is immunologically inert and cannot directly activate immune cells, it is incapable of reversing RT-induced immunosuppression [84]. However, EMNMs, leveraging their superior photothermal conversion efficiency and drug-loading capacity, successfully overcome this immune inertness. These EMNMs drive the development of combinatorial therapeutic strategies, achieving functional complementary and sensitizing effects in diagnosis and treatment through multi-mechanistic approaches [85, 86]. These EMNM-based combinations typically utilize the nanoparticle framework to integrate chemotherapeutics, PTT, photodynamic therapy (PDT), imaging contrast agents, immunomodulators, and vaccine components [9].

High-performance imaging is a prerequisite for accurate RT and efficacy evaluation. Shariati et al. synthesized hyaluronic acid-functionalized PDA chelated with Gd(III) and targeted modification. This engineering modification significantly improves the lesion detection and visualization capabilities of this investigational contrast agent [79]. Moreover, as an endogenous chromophore, melanin inherently produce photoacoustic (PA) signals in vivo [87]. In order to enhance the photoacoustic imaging of tumor blood vessels, Ju et al. changed the pH value to make EMNMs accumulate in biological tissues, thereby enhancing the PA signal [87].

The synergistic effect is not only reflected in the superposition of physical and chemical therapy but also in the deeper sense of reshaping the immune TME and inducing the abscopal effect. As described in section “Concealing “danger signals” and immunosuppression”, RT efficacy partially relies on the perception and signal transduction of cGAS-STING. Yet, RT-induced STING signaling pathway activation is transient and cannot maintain the robust immune activation [88]. However, ICD induced by PTT or RT [78, 89], when combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-L1 and anti-PD-1), immune adjuvants, or STING signal activators [80], can transform the original “cold” tumor into “hot” ones. This transformation activates a systemic anti-tumor immune response and prolongs the effective time of efficacy.

Additionally, melanin and EMNMs also show great potential as cancer vaccines carriers. Due to PDA’s excellent biocompatibility and lymph-node-targeting ability [90], encapsulating TAAs within PDA not only protect TAAs from enzymatic hydrolysis but also act as an immune adjuvant to activate dendritic cells (DCs) and induce long-lasting CD4+ and CD8+ T cell immune responses [91, 92]. Moura et al. constructed multivalent nano-conjugated cancer vaccines based on melanin scaffolds. This cancer vaccine rescues the host’s immune response to melanoma by delivering tumor-associated antigens [90].

EMNMs for RT sensitization: unique advantages and universal design principles

Melanin’s natural redox-switching capability provides a bidirectional functional mechanism that is exceedingly difficult to replicate in synthetic polymers. Furthermore, unlike conventional synthetic nanoparticles that require complex surface engineering to detect TME signals, melanin is inherently TME-responsive. Melanin’s redox status, metal-binding affinity, and other properties undergo changes under acidic or oxidative conditions. These dynamic properties enable pH-triggered drug release, hypoxia-regulated pro-oxidant conversion, and H2O2-responsive degradation. This intrinsic responsiveness facilitates the design of “smart” materials capable of autonomously switching between normal tissue RT protection and tumor RT sensitization based on localized microenvironmental signals. Simultaneously, this built-in environmental sensitivity minimizes the reliance on external triggers and significantly streamlines nanomaterial design.

While traditional RT sensitizers based on high-Z elements typically rely on singular mechanism for physical dose enhancement and lack chemical catalytic or immunomodulatory functions [70, 71], EMNMs overcome this limitation. By incorporating different high-Z elements, EMNMs provide a chemically active scaffold that seamlessly integrates both physical and chemical sensitization mechanisms into a single nanoparticle. This simplifies the synthesis process and helps reduce batch-to-batch variability.

Moreover, the broadband absorption and robust photothermal conversion efficiency of melanin are intrinsic. In contrast, other nanomaterials (e.g., gold nanoparticles and carbon nanotubes) require precise control over size and/or shape to achieve similar performance [95, 96]. Melanin’s natural pigmentation and metal-binding capacity render it an optimal carrier for imaging probes. Furthermore, most conventional RT sensitizing materials focus on enhancing direct tumor cell cytotoxicity without engaging the immune system. However, EMNMs can serve as carriers for immune adjuvants.

While both liposomes and melanin exhibit good biocompatibility, compared to stable melanin, liposomes are considered moderately unstable colloidal systems. Liposomes are prone to drug leakage due to lipid oxidation, hydrolysis, or liposome fusion, and their preparation methods and sterilization techniques have not been fully established [97].

Despite the diverse mechanisms of RT sensitization across various platforms, this review summarizes the following four potentially universal design principles for EMNMs: (a) enhancing physical radiation dose via high-Z element doping; (b) regulating hypoxic TME via localized oxygen delivery or generation; (c) boosting chemical catalytic activity via Fenton and Fenton-like reactions; (d) loading anti-tumor drugs to directly eradicate tumor cells or activate the immune system.

Discussion and outlook

Toward bidirectional precision in RT

Future developments in RT should advance toward the concept of bidirectional precision. This means achieving precise tumor destruction while simultaneously enabling precise protection of healthy tissues. Owing to its inherent radioprotective properties and engineerable RT sensitization potential, melanin is poised to become a key medium for realizing this vision. Through meticulous regulation of their composition, structure, surface properties, and delivery systems, “smart” EMNMs exhibiting spatiotemporally selective behavior in vivo can be designed. Functional modifications enable EMNMs to preferentially accumulate in tumor regions [98]. Upon triggering by the TME, their sensitizing function is activated, thereby locally amplifying the cytotoxic effects of RT [93]. Furthermore, leveraging the passive enrichment tendency of EMNMs or further strengthening their accumulation in key sensitive tissues via surface modification can maximize their RT protection effects and mitigate the acute and chronic toxicity associated with RT [43, 44].

Broad-spectrum sensitization of all tumor RT

Although melanin plays a significant role in pigmented tumors, the application of EMNMs is not limited to this. For non-pigmented tumors, exogenously introduced EMNMs can serve as “artificial radiation receptors” and “TME regulators.” Pyrrole-2,3,5-tricarboxylic acid (PTCA)/total melanin (TM) and thiazolium-2,4,5-tricarboxylic acid (TTCA)/PTCA may be used as biomarkers of RT sensitivity (Fig. 1A).

By doping with high-Z elements, EMNMs can enhance their mass attenuation capability for X-rays and γ-rays. Through amplified Compton scattering and photoelectric effects, high-Z element-doped EMNMs generate more secondary electrons locally within tumors, directly damaging DNA and improving radiation dose deposition [70, 71]. This represents a physical RT sensitization mechanism independent of tumor histology, potentially serving as a foundational design starting principle for broad-spectrum RT sensitizers. In this review, the primary high-Z elements utilized for physical RT sensitization are Se and Au [73, 75], whereas other high-Z elements are more frequently employed for chemical sensitization (e.g., Fe, Cu, Mn) [74, 75, 77], multimodal imaging (e.g., Gd, Tc, I) [79, 85], and hypoxic TME regulation (e.g., Pt, Mn) [75, 82].

By utilizing EMNMs loaded with catalytically active components (such as Fe, Cu, Mn), Fenton/Fenton-like reactions can be specifically initiated within the TME. This catalytic process generates highly toxic · OH while depleting endogenous antioxidants, thereby chemically amplifying RT-induced oxidative stress and accelerating tumor eradication [74, 75, 77]. This design rationale may be applicable to a majority of solid tumors with similar TME characteristics.

Moreover, EMNMs can act as delivery carriers for immunomodulators. When combined with RT-induced ICD, they can synergistically reverse the immunosuppressive TME and activate systemic anti-tumor immunity [78]. This capability holds broad-spectrum significance for overcoming RT resistance in immunologically “cold” tumors and suppressing tumor distant metastasis.

Challenges and future directions

Limitations of preclinical models

The vast majority of cited studies in this review are limited to in vitro assays or small animal models. However, substantial biological differences exist between small animal experiments and human applications. Variations in immune system function, metabolic rates, and tumor biology across species may unpredictably alter the in vivo functionality and efficacy of EMNMs.

Structural differences

Currently widely used melanin-like materials (such as PDA) still exhibit structural differences from natural melanin. Although PDA is facile to synthesize, its macromolecular structure primarily consists of DHI and lacks the critical DHICA subunit found in natural eumelanin [11]. This implies that radiobiological conclusions drawn from PDA-based studies may not fully replicate the in vivo behavior of natural melanin. It is worth noting that DHICA exhibits both radioprotective properties and a potential for photosensitization. Regardless of whether the objective is to develop materials for RT sensitization or radioprotection, DHICA should be regarded as an essential structural building block.

Degradation, pharmacokinetics, and long-term toxicity

To date, there have been no reports on the natural enzymatic or microbial degradation of melanin [27]. Interestingly, as early as 1990, Wakamatsu et al. indicated that 5-S-CD, a precursor of pheomelanin excreted in urine, serves as a biochemical marker for melanoma progression [99]. The eumelanin-related metabolites 5-hydroxy-6-methoxyindole-2-carboxylic acid (5H6MI2C) and 6-hydroxy-5-methoxyindole-2-carboxylic acid (6H5MI2C) may be excreted in urine as conjugates with glucuronic acid and sulfate [99]. Therefore, tracking melanin metabolites in urine may aid in the pharmacokinetic optimization and biodistribution design of EMNMs. Simultaneously, it could also possibly serve as a biological indicator for evaluating potential risks.

Despite these potential tracking strategies, overall current research on melanin degradation remains scarce, and its precise underlying mechanisms are still largely unelucidated [100]. Moreover, while these EMNMs are generally considered biocompatible, careful consideration of their in vivo distribution and potential long-term toxicity (tissue-specific and general) is imperative, particularly in the context of chronic treatments. Therefore, the long-term in vivo retention, degradation profiles, potential immunogenicity, and impacts on distal organs of EMNMs still require systematic exploration and evaluation. Special attention must be paid to the chronic toxicity of materials doped with metal ions or radionuclides. Future research directions should prioritize the design of programmable degradable EMNMs that disassemble in a predetermined manner after fulfilling their therapeutic function. Additionally, integrating biomarker-responsive elements could enable these materials to alter their properties or trigger self-clearance mechanisms upon detecting potential toxicity risks.

Complexity of “real-world” combinatorial regimens

Furthermore, the complex interactions between EMNMs and chemotherapeutic agents, targeted drugs, IT, and radiation within combinatorial regimens need to be elucidated. Such intricate drug-material dynamics could introduce unpredictable toxicities or alter pharmacokinetics, thereby limiting the seamless application of EMNMs in “real-world” clinical settings. Due to the complex and poorly defined regulatory pathways associated with multifaceted nano-therapies, demonstrating the safety and clinical efficacy of these integrated platforms will require innovative clinical trial designs and close collaboration with regulatory agencies.

Technical hurdles of “bidirectional” activation

A core technical challenge lies in how to precisely activate the RT-sensitizing function of melanin-based materials at the tumor site, while simultaneously maintaining their radioprotective function in normal tissues throughout a continuous treatment course. Overcoming this demands the development of sophisticated, stimuli-responsive smart materials (e.g., radiation-triggered or TME-activated nanosystems) coupled with highly accurate targeted delivery strategies.

Heterogeneity and personalized medicine

Intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity also poses a significant challenge to achieving this dual functionality. Patient-specific variables, including tumor perfusion, immune status, and genetic background, may compromise the efficacy of EMNMs. Therefore, future research should explore the “programmable” nature of EMNMs. This entails tailoring personalized EMNMs with specific RT-sensitizing mechanisms (e.g., targeting particular metabolic pathways or modulating specific immune checkpoints) based on the patient’s unique molecular tumor profile (e.g., mutational spectrum, metabolic status, and immune phenotype) and the specific RT regimen.

Emerging technologies for personalized EMNMs

Emerging technologies, including Physics-Informed Deep Learning (PIDL) and Artificial Intelligence (AI), stand to significantly accelerate the personalized design of EMNMs. PIDL ensures physically consistent and interpretable predictive outcomes for diagnostics and therapeutics by integrating fundamental physical principles with the powerful pattern recognition capabilities of data-driven methods [101]. AI can optimize the design of nanoparticles and nanobiosensors to effectively target cancer biomarkers while minimizing toxicity [101]. Ultimately, the convergence of nanomedicine and AI-driven radiomics, and hybrid modeling approaches will provide a powerful tool for achieving more precise and personalized medicine in the future.

In summary, extensive preclinical evaluations using large animal models are imperative prior to the widespread clinical application of melanin and EMNMs. Subsequently, rigorously optimized design and statistically powered clinical trials must be conducted to verify their safety and efficacy in human patients.

Conclusion

Melanin is a ubiquitous biopolymer that exists in nature. Its unique physicochemical properties and exceptional biocompatibility make it a potential multifunctional material in the field of tumor RT. This review has systematically analyzed the dualistic roles of natural melanin and EMNMs in RT. On the one hand, natural melanin acts as a formidable RT resistance barrier in melanoma and other pigmented tumors, driven by mechanisms such as radiation energy dissipation, ROS scavenging, and metabolic reprogramming. On the other hand, through synthetic biology and nanotechnology, melanin can be transformed into “smart” theranostic nanomaterials that can protect normal tissues and sensitize tumor therapy. This conceptual transition from RT resistance to RT sensitization not only provides a new strategy for overcoming RT resistance of pigmented tumors, but also provides a new idea for applying melanin to broad-spectrum RT sensitization of non-pigmented tumors.
