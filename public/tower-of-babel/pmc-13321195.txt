Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair: the effect of cuff tear size on outcomes and recovery trajectories
Authors: Yen Chin Jeremy Tan, Shi Ting Chiu, Andrew Hwee Chye Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRotator cuff (RC) tear size is known to influence post-operative outcomes and recovery following arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR). However, the trajectory of recovery has not been fully explored. This study aims to evaluate patient-reported outcomes pre-operatively, 6 months (6ms) and 2 years (2ys) after ARCR, stratified according to tear size.MethodsA retrospective cohort study evaluated outcomes of 136 patients who underwent ARCR. RC tear size was categorized into small (<1 cm), medium (1-3 cm), and large (3-5 cm) groups. Outcome measures included range of movement forward flexion and range of movement abduction, visual analog scale (involved shoulder), Constant score, University of California, Los Angeles score and Oxford Shoulder Score. Paired t-tests were conducted within each tear-size group, comparing pre-operative with 6m outcomes and 6m with 2y outcomes. Between-group differences in change scores were examined using one-way analysis of variance, and multivariate regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of outcome. Effect size analysis using Cohen d was performed to complement primary statistical findings, given the underpowered cohort sizes.ResultsAll groups displayed significant improvement in all measured outcomes from baseline to 6 months (P < .05). Small and medium tears demonstrated continued significant improvements in several outcomes from 6 months to 2 years (Constant score +7.6 and +7.4, respectively, P < .05; Oxford Shoulder Score +4.3 and 0.017, respectively, P < .05) while large tears did not (P > .05). Between-group analysis of variance demonstrated no significant difference in the degree of change scores for both time intervals (P > .05). Multivariate regression analysis concluded that tear size is not an independent predictor of 2-year outcomes (P > .05). Effect size analysis revealed predominantly moderate-to-large early effect sizes across all groups.ConclusionARCR are associated with substantial early improvement across all tear sizes at 6 months, with diminishing returns thereafter. Although larger tear sizes have lower post-operative readings, the equally low pre-operative baseline readings result in similar net numerical improvements across all groups.Keywords: Rotator cuff repair, Arthroscopic, Cuff tear size, Outcomes, Satisfaction, Recovery rate

Rotator cuff (RC) tears commonly present with functional impairment and shoulder pain. When conservative management fails, arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) is commonly performed and has demonstrated reliable significant improvement in pain and function. While revision rates are low, post-operative outcomes remain varied and the identification of independent predictors of recovery trajectories remain a priority for surgeons and patients.13

Initial tear size has emerged as a clinically significant predictor of outcomes after ARCR.10 Larger tear sizes have been noted to have higher rates of fatty infiltration and higher rates of post-operative retears as compared to small and medium tears, suggesting that healing integrity can be mechanically and biologically influenced by tear dimensions.1 While, theoretically, smaller tears are associated with better functional outcomes, this is not universal across the literature, indicating that the prognostic influence of tear size might evolve with time.6

Despite the growing literature of how tear size or morphology might influence post-operative functional outcomes, the temporal trajectory of improvement within different tear sizes post-ARCR remains poorly characterized. Most studies evaluating outcomes focus on a single follow-up time point instead of longitudinal changes, which does not capture whether improvements happen early and plateau, continuously accrue over time or present at different stages of recovery for different tear-size groups. Understanding the trajectory would allow clinicians to advise patients when they might expect improvements and the duration of said improvements.5

Therefore, this study aimed to supplement this by evaluating how functional outcomes and pain evolve post-ARCR, comparing baseline values to 6-month (6m) and 2-year (2y) time points for the following 3 tear-size categories: small (<1 cm), medium (1-3 cm), and large (3-5 cm).

Materials and methods

The study was approved by our institutional review board (2022/2681). This was a retrospective study involving 145 patients who underwent ARCR by a single surgeon in a single tertiary hospital between October 2012 and October 2023. Only 136 patients returned for all follow-ups, resulting in a loss-to-follow-up of 6.2%. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) completion of ARCR for articular-sided full-thickness RC tear, (2) availability of tear size information intraoperatively, and (3) availability of pre-operative, 6m post-operative and 2y post-operative range of movement forward flexion (ROMFFI) and range of movement abduction (ROMABI), Constant score (CONS), visual analog scale (involved shoulder) (VASI) of pain, University of California, Los Angeles score (UCLA), and Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS). Patients who had glenohumeral osteoarthritis, acromioclavicular osteoarthritis, or prior shoulder surgery were excluded. Tear sizes were measured intraoperatively and categorized into small (<1 cm), medium (1-3 cm) and large (3-5 cm). Cofield and DeOrio defined massive RC tears as >5 cm – this was excluded from our study due to the small population number.21

For all patients, the RC tears were mobilized in the humeroscapular motion interface and articular side, up to the level of the glenoid, so as to prevent repair under tension. For small tears, single-row with mattress sutures was used. For medium and large tears, double-row knotless anchors were used. For U- and L-shaped tears, margin convergence was performed. The biceps tendon was largely retained, without tenodesis or tenotomy, as Moorthy and Tan9 demonstrate that biceps tendon surgery need not be routinely performed for RC repairs. The literature demonstrated how acromioplasty was not associated with outcomes, and hence not routinely done.14 Post-operatively, patients were given rehabilitation that encouraged early passive mobilization of the operated shoulder.

CONS is a 100-point scale consisting of the following 4 subgroups: 15 points assess pain, 20 points assess activities of daily living, 25 points measure strength, and 40 points measure range of movement, with higher scores indicating better outcomes.12 VASI is a continuous linear scale ranging from 0 to 10 (no pain – worst pain). UCLA is a 35-point instrument with 10 points toward function, 10 points for pain, and 5 points each for active forward flexion, strength of forward flexion and satisfaction.19 OSS assesses patient symptoms and daily function with 12 questions, each ranging from 1-5 (worst – best).4 These patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) questionnaires were handed to patients to be independently filled up at each follow-up visit.

Results

Pre-operative patient demographics are shown in Table I. Out of the total 136 patients within the study analysis, 15 patients had tears <1 cm, 112 patients had tears measuring 1-3 cm and 9 patients had tears 3-5 cm. Although varied, there was a slight female predominance overall and among the 3 tear size groups (46-66% vs. 33-53%). There was a generally low prevalence of smoking among patients, with <1 cm group having the greatest percentage of smokers at 26.7% (Table I).

Tears <1 cm

Patients with tears <1 cm exhibited significant improvements in all outcomes from pre-operative baseline to 6m (Table II) – ROMFFI (+34.8, 95% confidence interval [CI] 22.4-47.2, P < .001), ROMABI (+43.3, 95% CI 28.9-57.7, P < .001), CONS (+26.8, 95% CI 18.6-35.0, P < .001), UCLA (+11.7, 95% CI 8.9-14.5, P < .001), OSS (+17.4, 95% CI 12.2-22.6, P < .001), and VASI (−4.7, 95% CI -5.9—3.5, P < .001). From 6 m to 2y, ROMFFI (+8.4, 95% CI 0.4-16.4, P = .041), CONS (+7.6, 95% CI 0.2-15.0, P = .047), and OSS (+4.3, 95% CI 0.3-8.3, P = .039) demonstrated further significant improvement. The remaining outcomes, however, demonstrated numerical improvement without significance. Moderate-to-large effect sizes were observed in the early time interval with the largest effects seen in UCLA (d = 2.53), OSS (d = 1.55), VASI (1.67), and small-moderate effect sizes seen in CONS (d = 0.77), ROMFFI (d = 0.23), and ROMABI (d = 0.04). Meanwhile, the later time interval generally demonstrated smaller effect sizes with ROMFFI (d = 0.70), ROMABI (d = 0.56), UCLA (d = 0.58), OSS (d = 0.59), and VASI (d = 0.11), implying that the majority of pain improvement occurred in the first 6m post-operatively (Supplementary Table S1).

Tears 1-3 cm

Patients within this category demonstrated statistically significant improvement from pre-operative baselines to 6m (ROMFFI [+36.7, 95% CI 31.1-42.3, P < .001], ROMABI [+45.4, 95% CI 38.9-51.9, P < .001], CONS [+27.1, 95% CI 22.4-31.8, P < .001], UCLA [+11.3, 95% CI 9.7-12.9, P < .001], OSS [+17.0, 95% CI 14.1-19.9, P < .001], VASI [−4.6, 95% CI -5.1—4.1, P < .001]) and continued significant improvement beyond that to 2y (Table III — ROMFFI (+10.3, 95% CI 3.4-17.2, P = .018), ROMABI (+10.3, 95% CI 3.6-17.0, P = .021), CONS (+7.4, 95% CI 1.1-13.7, P = .022), UCLA (+2.5, 95% CI 0.4-4.6, P = .019), OSS (+4.6, 95% CI 1.0-8.2, P = .017), and VASI (−1.1, 95% CI -2.1—0.1, P = .034). Similar to small tear sizes, early post-operative period demonstrated large effect sizes across all PROMs other than ROMFFI (d = 0.39) and ROMABI (d = 0.44) – CONS (d = 0.83), UCLA (d = 1.80), OSS (d = 1.28), and VASI (1.52). Unlike small tears, later longitudinal analysis revealed continued moderate-to-large effect sizes in CONS (d = 1.12), UCLA (d = 1.00), ROMFFI (d = 0.77), ROMABI (d = 0.68), OSS (d = 0.78), and VASI (d = 0.60), indicating that moderate-sized tears continue to accrue functional improvement beyond the initial 6m post-operative period (Supplementary Table S1).

Tears 3-5 cm

Patients in this grouping showed statistically significant early improvement from pre-operative to 6m (Table IV) — ROMFFI (+39.8, 95% CI 15.4-64.2, P = .002), ROMABI (+45.5, 95% CI 18.2-72.8, P = .004), CONS (+25.7, 95% CI 9.9-41.5, P = .003), UCLA (+11.1, 95% CI 4.1-18.1, P = .004), OSS (+15.8, 95% CI 5.7-25.9, P = .003), and VASI (−4.2, 95% CI -6.1—2.3, P = .001). This improvement continued numerically in the later comparisons but was no long significant (P > .05). Moderate-to-large effect sizes were seen in early and later time intervals for PROMs — CONS (d = 1.39 and 3.32), UCLA (d = 2.26 and 1.50), OSS (d = 2.44 and 0.44), and VASI (d = 1.65 and 0.84) (Supplementary Table S1).

Analysis of variance

ANOVA comparing each tear size group's early and late outcomes demonstrated no statistically significant difference in the magnitude of improvements (P > .05) (Tables V and VI). For pre-operative to 6m interval, there were no between-group differences in all outcomes — ROMFFI (P = .714), ROMABI (P = .063), CONS (P = .443), UCLA (P = .636), OSS (P = .615), and VASI (P = .660). Similarly, for the 6m to 2y interval, ANOVA did not demonstrate any significant differences in change scores for all PROMs — ROMFFI (P = .891), ROMABI (P = .790), CONS (P = .949), UCLA (P = .442), OSS (P = .35), and VASI (P = .176).

Multivariable linear regression analysis

Multivariable regression analysis showed that tear size was not an independent predictor of all PROMs after adjusting for age, gender, and smoking status — ROMFFI (P = .78), ROMABI (P = .53), CONS (P = .61), UCLA (P = .22), OSS (P = .29), and VASI (P = .14) (Table S2).

Discussions

All 3 tear size groups displayed significant early improvements from preoperational to 6m (Table II, Table III, Table IV). Moreover, the change in mean values from preoperational to 6m was approximately similar for all outcomes, across all tear sizes. Rashid et al11 demonstrated that only massive tears >5 cm were independent predictors of poorer post-ARCR outcomes and that tear sizes <5 cm experienced similar rates of recovery. Additionally, it was age, rather than tear size, that was a bigger predictor of poorer postsurgery outcomes. Our early results across all 3 tear size groups correspond with Kurowicki et al’s8 findings whereby their study demonstrated 89% improvement in pain, 81-88% improvement in function and 78% improvement in ROM by 6 months, regardless of tear size.

Small and medium tear sizes of up to 3 cm demonstrated continued significant improvement in some outcomes beyond 6m (Tables II and III). The current literature suggests that the majority of functional and pain recovery occurs up to 12 months post-operatively. In medium-sized tears (1-3 cm), patients experience continued strength recovery beyond 6m until 18 months post-operatively.16 It can be noted that in small tears, only the CONS and OSS showed significant improvement from 6m to 2y, while the P value for mean change over the same period for UCLA was 0.056. This likely reveals a ceiling-effect for UCLA, whereby the maximum score is 35 and the 6m mean of 30.6 approaches the upper limit, limiting sensitivity in detecting additional functional gains. Additionally, Xu et al20 revealed that the minimal clinically important difference at 2y from pre-operatively would be 6.3 for CONS, 2.9 for UCLA, and 2.7 for OSS. Hence, a mean change of +2.2 in UCLA from 6m to 2y in our dataset could be insufficient change to be noted as significant.

Large tear sizes 3-5 cm, on the other hand, did not show any significant improvement in all outcomes beyond 6m. This could be due to patients with larger tears tending to be older patients with greater fatty infiltration and muscle atrophy, which, in turn, correspond to poorer quality of musculotendinous unit and biomechanical properties. Fatty infiltration and muscle atrophy have been associated with worse post-operative outcomes.2

Given the underpowered cohort sizes, particularly large tear-size (n = 9), solely conventional significance testing would be insufficient to accurately characterize recovery trajectories. Effect size analysis using Cohen d thresholds of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 as benchmarks7 for small, moderate, and large effects, respectively, was employed to complement and analyze whether within-group changes were clinically meaningful, independent of sample size.17

All 3 tear-size groups demonstrated majority moderate-to-large effect sizes across most PROMs in the first 6m, supporting the ANOVA findings of equivocal early mean changes in PROMs (Table V). For small tears, ROM measurements had modest effect sizes (ROMFFI d = 0.23, ROMABI d = 0.04) as compared to functional and pain PROMs (UCLA d = 2.53, VASI d = 1.67, OSS d = 1.55), suggesting that symptomatic relief precedes biomechanical recovery.8 This is further substantiated by how VASI Cohen d decreases from 1.67 to 0.11 beyond 6m, indicating that pain recovery is largely complete within 6m. Conversely, medium tears yielded moderate-to-large effect sizes across all outcomes that persisted beyond 6m, corroborating existing literature by Shin et al16 pertaining to continued recovery up to 18 months post-operatively.

Large tears observed predominantly large early effect sizes despite poorer baseline scores. Although prior significance testing did not generate anything meaningful beyond 6m, moderate-to-large effect sizes were observed across several outcomes (CONS d = 3.32, UCLA d = 1.50, OXF d = 0.44, VASI d = 0.84). These effect sizes, especially CONS, should be interpreted with caution. A small cohort size of 9 patients indicates that small absolute changes in mean scores can result in disproportionately large Cohen d values.3 Nevertheless, the overall pattern of moderate-to-large late effect sizes supports the possibility of persistent clinically meaningful functional improvement in large tears beyond 6m, despite a lack of statistical significance.15

Analysis of variance and multivariate linear regression analysis

ANOVA analysis comparing the 3 tear sizes demonstrated no significant difference in the magnitude of improvement for both early and late post-operative periods. Our findings concur with Holtedahl et al6 who noted that while small/medium tear cohorts demonstrated better eventual outcomes post-operatively, the extent of improvement in absolute outcomes scores was similar compared to large tear cohorts. Likewise, multivariate linear regression analysis demonstrated a lack of statistically significant improvements in all outcomes after accounting for age, gender and smoking status as confounders (Table S2). This agrees with Watson et al's18 findings that tear size is not an independent predictor of change scores.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Firstly, the retrospective nature introduces risks of selection bias and runs the risk of loss-to-follow-up bias. Secondly, the marked imbalance in tear size cohort sizes limits statistical power and increases the risk of Type II error. Button et al3 explains that low statistical power reduces the possibility of detecting true effects and obtaining statistically significant results. Moreover, small cohort sizes may artificially inflate effect sizes.

Thirdly, this paper did not account for several factors pertaining to RC tears — degree of fatty infiltration, tear chronicity, tear type, and tear index. Such factors could have served as confounding factors for multivariate regression analysis which limit conclusions drawn pertaining to tear size as an independent predictor of outcomes. Post-operatively, no routine imaging was performed to assess tear integrity due to cost concerns. Clinical symptoms were the main determining factor of a possible retear that would prompt further evaluation with imaging.

As this study involved a single surgeon, the generalizability of findings to other surgeons and the broader population might be affected. Nevertheless, the single-surgeon design meant that surgeon variables were minimized and that outcomes were more reflective of patient factors instead.

Conclusions

ARCR provides patients with substantial improvements in function, pain, and range of movement regardless of tear sizes. While larger tear sizes have lower post-operative readings, the pre-operative baseline readings are equally lower, resulting in similar net numerical improvements across all tear-size groups. Additionally, the majority of recovery progress happens within the first 6m post-operatively. While the multivariate analysis suggests equivocal outcomes between tear-size groups, the underpowered cohort sizes warrant caution when interpreting these findings. More research can look into analyzing larger sample sizes while incorporating follow-up imaging and confounders like fatty infiltration to overcome limitations seen in this paper and draw more definitive conclusions.
