To the Editor:

Chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) remains the principal limitation to long-term outcomes after lung transplantation [1]. Once established, therapeutic options are limited, and at present no commonly accepted effective treatment exists [1–7]. This raises a practical clinical question: rather than searching for a novel, universally applicable CLAD therapy, should we instead identify a patient treatment-responder profile in whom CLAD evolution, once diagnosed, is modifiable by treatments currently available?

Current CLAD diagnosis and classification rely primarily on pulmonary function tests and radiological phenotyping, and CLAD progression is commonly evaluated by assessing the evolution of forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) [1]. While clinical CLAD phenotype and rate of FEV1 decline may provide prognostic information, reliable prediction of treatment efficacy remains challenging. We therefore explored whether clinical and biological risk factors for CLAD might better inform possible response to lymphocyte depleting therapy with rabbit-derived anti-human T-lymphocyte globulin (rATLG) (Grafalon, Neovii Biotech, Lexington, MA, USA), a possible rescue therapy in progressive CLAD [2–7]. Prior reports on the use of rATLG, or the alternate formulation anti-thymocyte globulin (rATG) (Thymoglobulin, Genzyme-Sanofi, Cambridge, MA, USA), in CLAD primarily described post-treatment spirometric trajectories and survival, but did not systematically assess determinants of response, which were highly variable [2–7]. Across these studies, potentially relevant clinical and biological factors such as chronic airway infection, prior acute cellular rejection, presence of donor-specific antibodies, and inflammatory biomarkers were inconsistently reported or addressed only indirectly through exclusion criteria. Consequently, the possible relationship with these alloimmune and non-alloimmune divers of disease and response to rATLG/ATG treatment in patients with progressive CLAD has remained unclear [2–7]. In this context, we aimed to investigate disease velocity, immune-mediated features, infection history, inflammatory parameters, and treatment-related variables within a single framework, to more comprehensively evaluate the clinical phenotype of rATLG-responsiveness than has been performed in prior reports.

We performed a retrospective analysis of lung transplant recipients treated with rATLG for progressive CLAD in UZ Leuven (Belgium) between 2015 and 2024 (outcomes censored as of January 2026). Three different response definitions were evaluated at 6 months following rATLG: 1) any attenuation of FEV1 decline (mL per month), 2) ≥50% reduction in rate of FEV1 decline (mL per month) [8], and 3) <10% decline in FEV1 (L) (i.e. ≥10% FEV1 decline is considered CLAD progression) [9]. Patients who died or required retransplantation within 6 months post-rATLG were classified as non-responders to mitigate survivor bias. Time-to-event outcomes were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank testing. Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Univariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with treatment response, including pre-treatment spirometric decline, blood inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, blood eosinophils and lymphocytes), CLAD phenotype and stage, infection and acute cellular rejection history, donor-specific antibodies, treatment-related variables, and time-to-event timepoints. Continuous predictors were analysed on their original scale, with pre-rATLG FEV1 slope modelled inversely for interpretability. Owing to the small sample size, multivariable analyses were not performed. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

A total of 32 CLAD patients received rATLG, of whom 4 (12.5%) died prior to 6 months post-rATLG, with no retransplants within the 6-month timeframe. 18 patients had bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (56.25%), 10 restrictive allograft syndrome (31.25%), and 4 mixed phenotype (12.5%).

Across response definitions, post-rATLG graft survival differed depending on the definition applied. When response was defined as any attenuation of FEV1 decline, no significant difference in survival was observed between groups (log-rank p=0.249), with median survival of 4.52 years in non-responders and 2.95 years in responders (figure 1a). In contrast, when response was defined as a ≥50% reduction in FEV1 decline, responders demonstrated significantly improved survival (p=0.042), with a median survival of 4.22 years compared with 2.17 years in non-responders (figure 1b). Similarly, using the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT)-based definition (<10% FEV1 decline), responders exhibited significantly better survival (p=0.036), with median survival not reached, whereas non-responders had a median survival of 2.17 years (figure 1c).

Time from CLAD diagnosis to rATLG administration did not differ significantly between responders and non-responders across any of the three response definitions. For attenuation of decline, time from CLAD diagnosis to rATLG was 3.3 months (interquartile range (IQR) 1.9 to 5.5) in responders compared with 6 months (IQR 2.8 to 38.4) in non-responders (p=0.26). Similar findings were observed for the ≥50% reduction definition, with responders 2.8 months (IQR 1.9 to 3.6) versus 5.2 months (IQR 2.8 to 19.4) in non-responders (p=0.08), and for the ISHLT definition, with responders 4.6 months (IQR 2.7 to 6.5) versus 2.6 months (IQR 1.5 to 4.9) in non-responders (p=0.13).

A consistent pattern was observed across response-definitions, with responders overall exhibiting a steeper decline in lung function 6 months prior to rATLG initiation. For attenuation of decline, this was a median of −181 mL per month (IQR −290 to −91) in responders versus −61 mL per month (IQR −71 to −42) in non-responders (p=0.006), for the ≥50% reduction definition, this was −220 (IRQ −313 to −134) versus −75 mL per month (IQR −124 to −56; p=0.004), and for the ISHLT definition −118 (IQR −206 to −63) versus −169 mL per month (IQR −259 to 74; p=0.440).

Post-rATLG lung function decline was significantly attenuated in responders across all definitions. For attenuation of decline, post-rATLG FEV1 slope was −44 mL per month (IQR −71 to −19) in responders compared with −214 mL per month (IQR −262 to −192) in non-responders (p<0.001). Similar findings were observed for the ≥50% reduction definition, with responders −32 mL per month (IQR −57 to −14) versus −135 mL per month (IQR −216 to −59) in non-responders (p<0.001), and for the ISHLT definition, with responders −32 mL per month (IQR −51 to −8) versus −75 mL per month (IQR −192 to −56) in non-responders (p=0.006).

Univariable logistic regression analyses identified only limited and definition-dependent predictors of response to rATLG (figure 1d). When response was defined as a ≥50% reduction in FEV1 decline, both higher CLAD stage at rATLG (OR 2.35, 95% CI 1.02 to 5.38, p=0.044) and steeper pre-rATLG FEV1 decline (OR 4.57×104, 95% CI 5.44 to 3.85×108, p=0.020) appeared significantly associated with treatment response. However, no other clinical, infectious, or inflammatory variables demonstrated significant associations with response to treatment. Across all definitions, most candidate predictors (including infection status, rejection history, inflammatory biomarkers, and timing variables) were not consistently associated with response, and effect estimates were often variable with wide confidence intervals.

In this small cohort study, we demonstrate that both the apparent efficacy of rATLG and the clinical interpretation of treatment response in CLAD are highly dependent on the response definition applied. While no survival difference was observed when response was defined as attenuation of FEV1 decline, responders exhibited significantly improved survival when more stringent definitions were used (≥50% reduction in decline and ISHLT-based criteria). Across definitions, a consistent signal emerged whereby patients with more rapidly progressive disease prior to treatment derived the greatest functional benefit, as reflected by steeper pre-treatment FEV1 decline and marked attenuation of post-treatment decline. In contrast, traditional clinical, infectious, and inflammatory variables were not consistently associated with response, and effect estimates were often variable.

These findings suggest that treatment response in CLAD is not uniform, but instead reflects an underlying disease trajectory that may remain modifiable in a subset of patients. Importantly, our results highlight that the choice of response definition is not merely methodological, but may have direct implications for both outcome interpretation and patient stratification, which is critical for the design of future CLAD treatment studies.

This exploratory analysis is limited by its retrospective design and small sample size. In addition, recent or concurrent acute allograft injury, including acute cellular rejection or antibody-mediated rejection, may have confounded interpretation of apparent treatment response. Therefore, attenuation of FEV1 decline after rATLG could, in some patients, partly reflect treatment or resolution of an acute process superimposed on CLAD, rather than a direct disease-modifying effect on established CLAD. Nevertheless, it highlights two key unmet needs in the management of CLAD: 1) the lack of a standardised and clinically meaningful definition of treatment response, and 2) the limited ability to predict therapeutic response based on readily available clinical and immunological markers. Early identification of patients with modifiable disease trajectories may enable more precise selection for rescue therapies, improving outcomes in those likely to benefit, while directing non-responders towards alternative strategies, including enrolment in clinical trials of novel agents [9]. Future studies should incorporate hypothesis-driven biomarkers to better define treatment-responsive phenotypes and advance personalised therapeutic approaches in CLAD [9].
