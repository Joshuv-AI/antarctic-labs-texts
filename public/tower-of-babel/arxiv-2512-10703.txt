Sub-Bath Cooling in Bosonic Systems: Gaussian Constraints and Non-Gaussian Enhancements Preprint: APS/123-QED
Wen-Han Png, Xueyuan Hu, Valerio Scarani

Abstract
Cooling quantum systems with finite resources is a central task in quantum technologies and has been extensively explored in discrete-variable settings. As continuous-variable (CV) platforms play an increasingly important role in quantum information processing, it becomes crucial to understand the fundamental limitations of cooling bosonic systems. In this work, we develop a general framework for cooling CV systems, identifying both the constraints imposed by Gaussianity and the advantages enabled by non-Gaussian interactions. We derive a reachable bound on the cooling performance of Gaussian operations that applies to arbitrary cooling architectures. By optimizing over all protocols saturating this bound, we further identify the most efficient scheme, which minimizes dissipated energy for a given number of ancilla modes. Beyond Gaussian operations, we show that p p -excitation exchange exploits non-Gaussian resources to achieve a p p -fold enhancement of the cooling limit. Our results establish the fundamental limits of CV heat-bath algorithmic cooling and reveal the crucial role of non-Gaussianity in surpassing Gaussian cooling barriers.

These experimental advances naturally raise a broader theoretical question: what are the fundamental cooling capabilities of Gaussian and non-Gaussian operations?
A notable step in this direction was made in Ref.
34

However, the multi-mode generalization of the Gaussian constraint in cooling, as well as the corresponding bounds under finite-time and finite-resource constraints, are still unknown. Beyond Gaussian operations, non-Gaussian resources have been recognized as indispensable in other quantum tasks, including universal quantum computation

Recent advances in the precise control of nonlinear interactions in CV platforms—such as parametric amplification and second-harmonic generation have opened new opportunities for the integration of non-Gaussian operations into cooling protocols. Nevertheless, the impact of the nonlinearity order on achievable cooling limits has yet to be fully characterized, leaving the potential of non-Gaussian resources to enhance cooling as an open question.

Heat-bath algorithmic cooling has been employed as a general framework to quantitatively study the third law in finite-dimensional quantum systems, demonstrating reachable cooling limits

We also optimize the heat dissipation for Gaussian cooling over the machine spectrum.

3.

II
Preliminary

We consider a generic framework of HBAC for bosonic systems, where a
N
-mode machine
M
works cyclically between a target mode
S
and a multi-mode reservoir
R
to carry the entropy from
S
to
R
(Fig.
1
). The system and machine are described through bare Hamiltonians
H_{S}=\omega_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
and
H_{M}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{j}
with
\omega_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\omega_{N}
, respectively. The protocol is made up of several rounds, each comprises of a recharging routine, where a unitary operator
V
acts collectively on
S
and
M
, and a thermalizing routine, where
SM
interacts with
R
via an energy-preserving unitary
U
.
Before the protocol starts, the state of
SMR
is initialized to a Gibbs state
\tau_{S}\otimes\tau_{M}\otimes\tau_{R}
at inverse temperature
\beta
, i.e.,
\tau_{X}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{X}}}{\mathrm{tr}(e^{-\beta H_{X}})}
. Because the reservoir is large, it is assumed to be relaxed to the Gibbs state in every round, while
SM
can preserve its state in the output of the thermalizing routine to the next round. After the whole process ends, the state of the machine would be reset to the Gibbs state for latter use.
In the Gaussian scenario (Sec.
III
), we work on the most generic Gaussian HBAC framework, i.e., the only restriction we consider is that both
V
and
U
are Gaussian. It leads to the fundamental restriction on cooling limit and efficiency imposed by Gaussianity. In the non-Gaussian scenario (Sec.
IV
), we showcase the advantage of non-Gaussianity in cooling by considering a specific protocol, where
N=1
, the recharging routine is driven by the multi-excitation exchange Hamiltonian
H_{I}^{(p)}\propto\hat{a}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}p}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b}^{p}
, and the thermalizing routine is simply full thermalization of the machine mode.

The purpose of HBAC is to realize sub-bath cooling, i.e., to cool the system mode below the temperature of the reservoir.
In general, the output state of the system mode may not be a Gibbs state. Thus, for a general single-mode state, we introduce the definitions of thermal excitation and effective temperature, which will serve as key indicators of cooling performance throughout this work.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{1}^{G}
denote the set of single-mode Gaussian unitary operators. The thermal excitation of a single-mode state
\rho_{1}
is

\mathfrak{n}(\rho_{1}):=\min_{W\in\mathcal{U}^{G}_{1}}\mathrm{tr}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}W\rho_{1}W^{\dagger}),

(1)

and the corresponding effective inverse temperature is

\beta_{eff}(\rho_{1}):=\frac{1}{\omega}\ln\frac{\mathfrak{n}(\rho_{1})+1}{\mathfrak{n}(\rho_{1})}.

(2)

For a single-mode Gaussian state
\rho^{G}
,
\mathfrak{n}(\rho^{G})
accounts for the thermal excitation contribution to the mean excitation number
\bar{n}(\rho^{G})=\mathrm{tr}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\rho^{G})
, aside from the contributions from squeezing and displacement. Moreover,
\rho^{G}
can be transformed by a Gaussian unitary to the Gibbs state
\tilde{\tau}(\rho^{G})
with inverse temperature
\beta_{eff}(\rho^{G})
, whose mean photon number is
\mathfrak{n}(\rho^{G})
(see for example Ref.
41
, or Appendix
A.1
for a brief proof). Notably, because
\tilde{\tau}(\rho^{G})
is related to
\rho^{G}
by a Gaussian unitary, the von Neumann entropy
S(\rho^{G}):=-\mathrm{tr}(\rho^{G}\ln\rho^{G})
and purity
P(\rho^{G}):=\mathrm{tr}[(\rho^{G})^{2}]
are also monotonic on
\mathfrak{n}(\rho^{G})
. Thus, the thermal excitation
\mathfrak{n}(\rho^{G})
can also be employed as an effective measure of non-equilibrium or purity of the Gaussian state
\rho^{G}
.
Every non-Gaussian state
\rho^{NG}
has a Gaussian counterpart
\tilde{\rho}^{G}(\rho^{NG})
, such that the first and second moments of
\rho^{NG}
and
\tilde{\rho}^{G}(\rho^{NG})
coincide. Thus, it is natural to employ
\mathfrak{n}[\tilde{\rho}^{G}(\rho^{NG})]
to measure the thermal excitation in
\rho^{NG}
, which equals to
\mathfrak{n}(\rho^{NG})
by Definition
1

25
.

III
Cooling with Gaussian operation

III.1
Limitation of Gaussian cooling

The unitary operations in both recharging and thermalizing routines are restricted to be Gaussian in this section, so we first consider the ability of a multi-mode Gaussian unitary in minimizing the thermal excitation in the first mode, and prove the following lemma (the proof is in Appendix
A.2
).

Lemma 1
.

Consider a
J
-mode system initially in a Gaussian product state
\rho_{J}=\otimes_{j=1}^{J}\rho_{j}
.
After the action of a global Gaussian unitary, the minimum of the thermal excitation of Mode 1 in the output is

\min_{W\in\mathcal{U}^{G}_{J}}\mathfrak{n}\left(\mathrm{tr}_{\backslash 1}(W\rho_{J}W^{\dagger})\right)=\min_{j}\mathfrak{n}(\rho_{j}),

(3)

where
\mathcal{U}^{G}_{J}
is the set of
J
-mode Gaussian unitary operators.

Applying Lemma
1
to the scheme of HBAC, we obtain Theorem
1
, which specifies the necessary and sufficient condition on sub-bath cooling via Gaussian operations.

Theorem 1
.

By Gaussian HBAC protocols based on coherent control, the effective inverse temperature of
S
can reach
\beta^{*}=\lambda\beta

(\lambda>1)
if and only if
\omega_{N}\geq\lambda\omega_{0}
.

Proof.

First, we notice that the unitary
U
in the thermalizing routine is both Gaussian and energy-preserving, which requires that the reservoir modes that can exchange energy with the machine should be resonant with the machine modes. It means that the frequencies of the effective reservoir modes are no larger than
\omega_{N}
.

From Fig.
1
(a), the output state
\rho^{(l)}_{SM}
of
SM
after the
l
th iteration is related to
\rho^{(l-1)}_{SM}
as
\rho^{(l)}_{SM}=\mathrm{tr}_{R_{l}}[W_{l}(\rho^{(l-1)}_{SM}\otimes\tau_{R_{l}})W_{l}^{\dagger}]
, where
W_{l}=U_{l}V
,
V
acts on
SM
,
U_{l}
acts on
MR_{l}
, and
\rho^{(0)}_{SM}=\tau_{S}\otimes\tau_{M}
. It follows that after
L
iterations,

\rho_{S}^{(L)}=\mathrm{tr}_{MR}[W(\tau_{S}\otimes\tau_{M}\otimes\tau_{R})W^{\dagger}],

(4)

where
\tau_{R}=\otimes_{l=1}^{L}\tau_{R_{l}}
, and
W=W_{L}\cdots W_{1}
is a Gaussian unitary. By Lemma
1
, the mean excitation number in the system mode is bounded as
\mathfrak{n}(\rho^{(L)}_{S})\geq\min_{j}\mathfrak{n}(\tau(\omega_{j}))=\mathfrak{n}(\tau(\omega_{N}))
. It follows that the effective temperature of the system mode satisfies

\beta^{*}=\beta_{eff}(\rho^{(L)}_{S})\leq\frac{\omega_{N}}{\omega_{0}}\beta.

(5)

Therefore, for Gaussian HBAC protocol to reach
\beta^{*}=\lambda\beta
, it is necessary that
\omega_{N}\geq\lambda\omega_{0}
. This proves the “only if” direction. Conversely, if
\omega_{N}\geq\lambda\omega_{0}
, then
\beta^{*}
can be reached by setting the recharging routine as
V=\exp[-i\chi(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{N}+\hat{a}\hat{b}_{N}^{\dagger})t]
for some proper
t
.
∎

This theorem directly implies the necessity of a machine for sub-bath cooling under Gaussian operations, which is consistent with the no-go theorem
34
stating that cooling below the bath temperature via Gaussian thermal operations is impossible. Our results unveil further restrictions of Gaussian operations in cooling protocols.

In algorithmic cooling (also known as non-equilibrium concentration in the context of resource theories), one starts from
J
copies of state
\rho
, applies a joint unitary
U
on them, and aims at purifying the output state
\rho^{\prime}=\mathrm{tr}_{\backslash 1}(U\rho^{\otimes J}U^{\dagger})
of the first system, such that
S(\rho^{\prime})<S(\rho)
or
P(\rho^{\prime})>P(\rho)
. This task is achievable for
J\geq 3
if
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
are qubit states
22
, and for
J\geq 2
if
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
are
d
-dimensional (
d\geq 3
) systems
18
. In contrast, Lemma
1
indicates that it is impossible to do algorithmic cooling using only Gaussian unitary operations.

In an incoherent control scenario of HBAC, only covariant unitary that preserves the total energy is allowed. Therefore, the recharging routine is realized by an energy-preserving interaction between the system and two machines – one at a lower temperature
\beta
and the other at a higher temperature
\beta_{H}<\beta
. Since Gaussian interactions are induced by interacting Hamiltonian of quadratic form, they are energy preserving only if the system mode is resonant with the machine modes. Then Lemma
1
implies that the system cannot be cooled beyond
\beta
, in contrast to the discrete-variable case
6
where HBAC protocols based on incoherent control can achieve the same cooling limit as those based on coherent control.

In the PPA-like protocols, the machine is composed of two parts, the reset part, which is refreshed to the Gibbs state in the thermalizing routine in each round, and the scratch part, which preserves the state to the next round and thus carries forward memory. For both qubits case (where the system is a single qubit and the machine is composed of qubits)
31
and arbitrary finite-dimensional case
36
, it is shown that the memory effect of the scratch can provide an exponential advantage over the memoryless scenario. On the contrary, Theorem
1
implies that the memory effect does not improve the cooling limit in the Gaussian setting.
From Theorem
1
, when
\omega_{N}=\lambda\omega_{0}
, the inverse temperature of the system can at most reach
\beta^{*}=\lambda\beta
by Gaussian HBAC protocols, which include the PPA-like protocol as a special case.
On the other hand, this limit can be achieved in one round by simply swapping the states of
S
and
M_{N}
in the recharging routine, which is memoryless.
Hence, our result shows that, in order to activate the advantage of the memory effect, non-Gaussianity in the operations is necessary.

III.2
Energy efficiency of Gaussian cooling

As shown in the last subsection, the cooling limit can be achieved within one round. During the recharging routine, the system and the machine interact via a Gaussian unitary
V
, and the joint state becomes
\rho_{SM}^{\prime}=V(\tau_{S}\otimes\tau_{M})V^{\dagger}
.
During the thermalizing routine, the machine is reset to the Gibbs state for latter use, which is in general irreversible. By the second law,
\beta Q>\tilde{\Delta}S_{S}
, where
Q=\mathrm{tr}[H_{M}(\rho_{M}^{\prime}-\tau_{M})]
is the amount of heat dissipated to the reservoir, and
\tilde{\Delta}S_{S}=S(\tau_{S})-S(\rho^{\prime}_{S})
is the decrement of entropy in the target system. The difference is usually called the entropy production and can be calculated as

\Sigma_{N}(V,H_{M}):=\beta Q-\tilde{\Delta}S_{S}=D[\rho^{\prime}_{M}||\tau_{M}]+I_{S:M}(\rho^{\prime}_{SM}),

(6)

where
I_{S:M}
is the mutual information and
D[\cdot\|\cdot]
is the relative entropy. A lower value of
\Sigma_{N}(V,H_{M})
indicates the higher efficiency of the protocol. In the following, we derive the optimal Gaussian unitary
V
in the recharging routine and the optimal spectrum
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{N-1}
of the machine, such that
\Sigma_{N}(V,H_{M})
reaches the minimum for given
N
.

For an arbitrary choice of the spectrum of the machine, the following theorem identifies successive bi-mode swaps as the most efficient unitary in the recharging routine. The proof is in Appendix
A.3
.

Theorem 2
.

For any choice of
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{N-1}
, among all the protocols that can achieve the cooling limit
\beta^{*}=\frac{\omega_{N}}{\omega_{0}}\beta
, the most efficient unitary in the recharging routine is

\displaystyle V^{*}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathrm{arg}\min_{V\in\mathcal{U}^{G}_{N+1}}\Sigma_{N}(V,H_{M})

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textsc{swap}(S,M_{N})\circ\dots\circ\textsc{swap}(S,M_{j_{0}}),

where
\textsc{swap}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the unitary which swaps the states of two modes,
j_{0}
is the minimum index such that
\omega_{j_{0}}>\omega_{0}
. Besides, the minimum entropy production reads

\Sigma_{N}^{*}(H_{M})=D[\tau(\omega_{0})\|\tau(\omega_{j_{0}})]+\sum_{j=j_{0}+1}^{N}D[\tau(\omega_{j-1})\|\tau(\omega_{j})],

(8)

where
\tau(\omega_{j})=(1-e^{-\beta\omega_{j}})\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-n\beta\omega_{j}}|n\rangle\langle n|
is the Gibbs state of a harmonic oscillator with frequency
\omega_{j}
and inverse temperature
\beta
.

Prior to this result for general Gaussian cooling protocols, to the best of our knowledge, the most efficient recharging unitary had been tackled analytically only for the collisional cooling for qubits
37
where the system is a single qubit, the machine consists of
N
qubits, and the system qubit interacts successively with machine qubits, one at a time and each qubit only once.

Clearly, the machine modes with
\omega_{j}\leq\omega_{0}
do not play any role in improving the cooling limit or energy efficiency. Therefore, for the purpose of optimizing
\Sigma_{N}^{*}(H_{M})
for a given
N
, one should set
\omega_{1}>\omega_{0}
. With this, Eq. (
8
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{N}^{*}(H_{M})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}D[\tau(\omega_{j-1})\|\tau(\omega_{j})]

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\bar{n}_{j-1}+1)\ln\frac{\bar{n}_{j}+1}{\bar{n}_{j-1}+1}+\bar{n}_{j-1}\ln\frac{\bar{n}_{j-1}}{\bar{n}_{j}},

where
\bar{n}_{j}:=\bar{n}(\tau(\omega_{j}))=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega_{j}}-1}
.
Next, we focus on optimizing the entropy production over the machine spectrum
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{N-1},

\Sigma_{N}^{**}=\min_{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{N-1}}\Sigma_{N}^{*}(H_{M}).

(10)

We verify that
\frac{\partial^{2}\Sigma_{N}^{*}(H_{S})}{\partial\bar{n}_{j}^{2}}=\frac{\bar{n}_{j-1}}{\bar{n}_{j}^{2}}-\frac{\bar{n}_{j-1}+1}{(\bar{n}_{j}+1)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{j}(\bar{n}_{j}+1)}>0
for
\bar{n}_{j-1}\geq\bar{n}_{j}
, and hence,
\Sigma_{N}^{*}(H_{S})
is a strictly convex function of
\bar{n}_{1},\dots,\bar{n}_{N-1}
for
\bar{n}_{0}\geq\bar{n}_{1}\geq\dots\geq\bar{n}_{N-1}\geq\bar{n}_{N}
. Therefore, the minimum of
\Sigma_{N}^{*}(H_{S})
is reached if and only if
\frac{\partial\Sigma_{N}^{*}(H_{S})}{\partial\bar{n}_{j}}=0
, which gives

g_{j+1}-g_{j}=(e^{g_{j}-g_{j-1}}-1)\cdot\frac{1-e^{-g_{j}}}{1-e^{-g_{j-1}}},\ j=1,\dots,N-1,

(11)

where we define
\beta\omega_{j}=g_{j}(j=0,\dots,N)
for convenience, and
g_{0}
and
g_{N}
are the boundary condition. Eq. (
11
) can be solved numerically and plotted in Fig.
2
, see also Fig.
5
in Appendix
A.3
. The results indicate a substantial reduction in heat dissipation even for finite
N
. In particular, the dissipation decreases by more than half with the introduction of just one additional machine mode (
N=2
).

Plot of
\Sigma_{N}^{**}
versus
\lambda
, with the initial mean excitation number fixed at

In the limit where
N
is large, such that
(g_{j}-g_{j-1})^{k}N^{2}
is infinitesimal for

, Eq. (

) can be solved analytically as

g^{*}_{j}=2\ln\left\{-\coth\left[\frac{j}{2N}\ln\tanh\frac{g_{N}}{4}+\frac{N-j}{2N}\ln\tanh\frac{g_{0}}{4}\right]\right\}.

(12)

Therefore, the optimal entropy production for large
N
reads

\Sigma^{**}_{N\gg 1}=\frac{1}{2N}\left[\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\tanh(\beta\omega_{N}/4)}{\tanh(\beta\omega_{0}/4)}\right]^{2}+o(\frac{1}{N^{2}}).

(13)

The details of the calculation can be found in Appendix
A.3
.

Eq. (
13
) shows that
\Sigma_{N}^{**}
converges to zero in the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
, demonstrating that the Landauer’s limit can be approached when the time cost diverges, which is consistent with the result in Ref.
35
. Besides, Eq. (
13
) scales as
1/N
. It extends the result in Ref.
37
, where
1/N
scaling is also observed when the optimality of entropy production is discussed for collisional model in finite-dimensional systems, to the continuous variable regime. Furthermore, since our optimization is over both the genuine multipartite Gaussian unitary operators and the spectrum of the machine, Eq. (
13
) actually gives the general and reachable lower bound of dissipated energy in Gaussian cooling for given
\omega_{0}
,
\omega_{N}
, and
N\gg 1
. Our result shows that the collective advantage
32
disappears when the Gaussian condition is imposed, complementing the result for thermodynamics length
1
, where the continuous time evolution of a system is studied. Our results show that Gaussian HBAC cannot surpass the
1/N
convergence rate towards Landauer limit, thus offering no collective advantage. This limitation arises because Gaussian operations lack the strong nonlocal, long-range interactions required for collective enhancement
32
. This observation also complements the findings on thermodynamic length in classical Gaussian dynamics
1
.

IV
Cooling with Non-Gaussian operation

IV.1
Single-shot cooling

In this section, we investigate the cooling enhancement offered by
p
-excitation exchange operation induced by the interacting Hamiltonian
H_{I}^{(p)}(\chi)=\chi(\hat{a}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}p}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b}^{p})
with
p\in\{1,2,3,...\}
. The case
p=1
corresponds to a beam-splitter-type interaction which yields the optimal recharging routine among all Gaussian operations. For
p\geq 2
, the dynamics become non-Gaussian, and is realizable through multi-mode mixing in experiments
10
;
5
.
In particular, the quantum absorption refrigeration
25
;
7
, which relies on three-wave mixing,
has been demonstrated in trapped-ion systems
24
.

Specifically, we consider a single-mode system and machine, each initialized in a Gibbs state (
\tau_{S}
and
\tau_{M}
) at inverse temperature
\beta
. Their bare Hamiltonian is
H_{0}=\omega_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\omega_{1}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}
. The system and the machine then interact through the unitary
V^{(p)}(t)=\exp(-iH^{(p)}t)
generated by
H^{(p)}=H_{0}+H_{I}^{(p)}(\chi)
. Obtaining a closed analytical expression for the exact total excitation under this nonlinear interaction is extremely challenging. As a result, earlier works have either focused on short-time dynamics with coherent or Fock initial states
4
, or relied on effective long-time equilibration toward a steady state. In our work, we follow the short-time approach but consider the more realistic case in which the initial states are Gibbs states. Now, the single-shot cooling operation is defined as

\mathcal{E}_{t}(\tau_{S})=\mathrm{tr}_{M}\left[V^{(p)}(t)\left(\tau_{S}\otimes\tau_{M}\right)V^{(p){\dagger}}(t)\right].

(14)

In the Heisenberg picture, the mean excitation number of the system evolves as

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{S}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left[\tau_{S}\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}(\hat{n}_{S})\right]

(15)

where
\hat{n}_{S}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
and
\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}(\hat{n}_{S})=\mathrm{tr}_{M}\left[(\mathbb{I}\otimes\sqrt{\tau_{M}})V^{(p){\dagger}}(t)(\hat{n}_{S}\otimes\mathbb{I})V^{(p)}(t)(\mathbb{I}\otimes\sqrt{\tau_{M}})\right]
. In the
\chi t\ll 1
limit, the mean excitation number is approximated as (see Appendix
B.1
)

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{S}(t)

\displaystyle\approx\bar{n}_{S}-\chi^{2}t^{2}\left[(1+\bar{n}_{M})^{p}\bar{n}_{S}-\bar{n}_{M}^{p}(1+\bar{n}_{S})\right]p!,

(16)

where
\bar{n}_{S}:=\bar{n}_{S}(0)
and
\bar{n}_{M}:=\bar{n}_{M}(0)
are the initial mean excitation numbers of the system and machine respectively. After a single short
V^{(p)}
interaction, the system and machine become correlated, leading to a non-thermal transient state. As we show in Appendix
B.1
, the thermal excitation of
\rho^{\prime}_{S}(t)=\mathcal{E}_{t}(\tau_{S})
equals to its mean excitation number, i.e.,
\mathfrak{n}(\rho^{\prime}_{S}(t))=\bar{n}_{S}(t)
. Henceforth, we employ
\bar{n}_{S}(t)
to quantify the cooling performance.
If
\bar{n}_{S}(t)<\bar{n}_{S}
for
\chi t\ll 1
, we say that the system is cooled beyond its initial temperature, i.e.,
\beta_{eff}(\rho^{\prime}_{S}(t))>\beta
. The necessary and sufficient condition for sub-bath cooling with the
p
-excitation exchange Hamiltonian is given by the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Under a single-shot
p
-excitation exchange cooling operation
\mathcal{E}_{t}
with
\chi t\ll 1
, the necessary and sufficient condition for sub-bath cooling is
p\omega_{1}>\omega_{0}
.

Proof.

From Eq. (
16
), the sub-bath cooling
\bar{n}_{S}(t)<\bar{n}_{S}
is equivalent to
\chi^{2}t^{2}\left[(1+\bar{n}_{M})^{p}\bar{n}_{S}-\bar{n}_{M}^{p}(1+\bar{n}_{S})\right]p!>0
, which gives

\bar{n}_{S}>\frac{\bar{n}_{M}^{p}}{(1+\bar{n}_{M})^{p}-\bar{n}_{M}^{p}}.

(17)

Because
(1+\bar{n}_{M})/\bar{n}_{M}=e^{\beta\omega_{1}}
, the right-hand side is equal to
\frac{1}{e^{\beta p\omega_{1}}-1}
. From
\bar{n}_{S}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega_{0}}-1}
, Eq. (
17
) holds if and only if
p\omega_{1}>\omega_{0}
.
∎

,

is less restrictive than the one in Theorem

,
\omega_{1}>\omega_{0}
.
This implies that cooling with a machine whose frequency is equal to or lower than the system frequency is possible for
V^{(p)}(t)
with

. In Fig.

The lower bound of the cooling condition in Theorem

V^{(p)}

IV.2
Iterative cooling

In the previous subsection, we showed that
V^{(p)}(t)
provides cooling advantage in the short-time regime. We now investigate the cooling behavior when this operation is applied repeatedly. Within the HBAC framework, we iterate the recharging step
V^{(p)}(t)
, followed by full thermalization of the machine for
L
rounds (see Fig.
1
(b)). In each iteration, the system interacts with a refreshed machine
\tau_{M}
with a fixed machine gap for the same short duration
\chi t\ll 1
. Since
\mathcal{E}_{t}
is a dissipative channel, the system state converges to a steady state after sufficiently many iterations. To determine whether this steady state is thermal, we evaluate to thermal Fano factor
25
. We find that it approaches zero in the large-
L
limit, indicating that the steady state converges to a Gibbs state (see Appendix
B.3
). Thus, in the asymptotic limit, the repeated action of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
maps any initial Gibbs state to another Gibbs state, while allowing for non-equilibrium dynamics during intermediate iterations. Since the steady state in the large-
L
limit is thermal, its temperature can be characterized entirely by its mean excitation. Using this fact, we derive the HBAC cooling limit for the
p
-excitation exchange operation.

Theorem 4
.

In the large
L
limit, the Markovian collisional model governed by the unitary interaction
V^{(p)}(t)
maps a Gibbs state to another Gibbs state. The asymptotic effective inverse temperature can reach
\beta^{*}=\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{0}}p\beta
.

Proof.

The mean excitation number after
L
iterations for
p
-excitation exchange is given by

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{S}^{(L)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\Big[\tau_{S}\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}}_{L\ \text{times}}(\hat{n}_{S})\Big].

(19)

From this, we derive the closed-form expression of the mean excitation number after
L
iterations of cooling as (see Appendix
B.2
)

\bar{n}_{S}^{(L)}\approx\bar{n}_{S}^{(0)}(1-a)^{L}+b\frac{1-(1-a)^{L}}{a}

(20)

where
a=\chi^{2}t^{2}[(1+\bar{n}_{M})^{p}+\bar{n}_{M}^{p}]p!
and
b=\chi^{2}t^{2}\bar{n}_{M}^{p}p!
.
In the large-
L
limit, the mean excitation approaches

\bar{n}_{S}^{(\infty)}:=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\bar{n}_{S}^{(L)}=\frac{1}{e^{p\beta\omega_{1}}-1},

(21)

which saturates to the minimum allowed mean excitation of the system for sub-bath cooling. Hence it cannot be reduced even further by
p
-excitation interaction from Theorem
3
.
Besides, because the asymptotic state of the system is a Gibbs state, we have
\bar{n}_{S}^{(\infty)}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta^{*}\omega_{0}}-1}
.
Eq. (
21
) then implies
\beta^{*}=\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{0}}p\beta
.
∎

Theorem
4
highlights that the chain of non-Gaussian collisions
\mathcal{E}_{t}
achieves the cooling limit equivalent to a single excitation exchange with a machine possessing a
p
-fold increased energy gap (see Fig.
1
(d)). With increasing nonlinearity
p
, not only does the asymptotic mean excitation decreases exponentially, but the convergence rate towards the asymptotic mean excitation is also increased by
(p!)
-fold (see Fig.
4
).
For
p=1
, we recover
\bar{n}_{S}^{(\infty)}=\bar{n}_{M}
. Since only single-excitation exchanges are permitted, the energy exchange per interaction is limited. This agrees with our previous result that the most optimal Gaussian recharging operation fails to surpass the limit
\beta^{*}=\frac{\omega_{1}}{\omega_{0}}\beta
. For
p\geq 2
, however, the
p
-excitation exchange Hamiltonian allows multiple quanta to be transferred in a single interaction. This accelerates the cooling process, enabling the system temperature to converge more rapidly and decrease a factor of
p
. Since higher
p
-nonlinearity implies stronger non-Gaussianity, the observed
p
-fold cooling enhancement emphasizes that non-Gaussianity is a key resource for surpassing the cooling limits imposed by Gaussian dynamics.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we have investigated the fundamental cooling limits of multi-mode Gaussian operations and the cooling advantage offered by a specific non-Gaussian operations, the
p
-excitation exchange operation. Optimizing over all Gaussian protocols, we prove that the most optimal cooling strategy is successive
swap
operations with machine modes of increasingly larger energy gaps. Since
swap
interactions exchange only a single excitation per round, Gaussian protocols are intrinsically limited in the control complexity required for more energy extraction during the recharging routine. Consequently, Gaussian operations cannot cool a target system when the available machine modes have degenerate or smaller gaps than the system. This limitation rules out non-equilibrium concentration, excludes any cooling advantage from incoherent Gaussian control, and shows that Gaussian protocols gain no benefit from memory effects.

Beyond single-excitation exchange, we demonstrate that the
p
-excitation exchange yields a p-fold cooling enhancement. This improvement arises from genuine non-Gaussianity, which which leverages increased control complexity to extract more energy. By enabling multiple excitations to be exchanged in each interaction, these protocols also reduce the minimum required machine-mode energy gap by a factor of
p
.
Our work provides a systematic HBAC framework for continuous-variable systems, enabling experiments to improve the cooling performance based on the available machine modes and control complexity.

In the future work, the cooling efficiency of the
p
-excitation exchange operation and the corresponding collective advantage remain to be established. The potential of memory effects induced by the
p
-excitation exchange to further enhance cooling will be also addressed in the future.

Appendix A
Appendices for Gaussian cooling

A.1
Preliminaries: Gaussian states and Gaussian unitary operations

Consider a
J
-mode bosonic system, whose Hamiltonian is written as
H=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\omega_{j}\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}
, where
\hat{a}_{j}
,
\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}
, and
\omega_{j}
are the annihilation and creation operators, and the frequency of the
j
th mode.
A Gaussian state of this system is fully characterized by its first and second moments defined as
\mathbf{r}_{j}=\mathbf{r}_{l+j}^{*}=\langle\hat{a}_{j}\rangle
for
j=1,\cdots,J
and
\mathbf{M}_{j,k}=\mathbf{M}_{k+J,j+J}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\}\rangle-\langle\hat{a}_{j}\rangle\langle\hat{a}_{k}^{\dagger}\rangle
,
\mathbf{M}_{j,k+J}=\mathbf{M}_{j+J,k}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\{\hat{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{k}\}\rangle-\langle\hat{a}_{j}\rangle\langle\hat{a}_{k}\rangle
for
j,k=1,\cdots,J
, respectively.
Accordingly, the first moment vector
\mathbf{r}
and the second moment matrix
\mathbf{M}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}\vec{\alpha}\\
\vec{\alpha}^{*}\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu^{*}&\nu\\
\nu^{*}&\mu\end{pmatrix},

(22)

where
\vec{\alpha}
is a
J
-dimensional vector and
\mu
and
\nu
are
J\times J
-dimensional matrices that are Hermitian and symmetric, respectively.
Moreover, the second moment must obey the uncertainty principle, which corresponds to
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Z}\geq\mathbf{0}
with a
2J\times 2J
matrix
\mathbf{Z}:=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&-\mathbb{I}\end{pmatrix}
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix. With this, the mean excitation number
\bar{n}_{j}:=\langle\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j}\rangle
can be written as

\bar{n}_{j}=|\alpha_{j}|^{2}+\mu_{jj}-\frac{1}{2}.

(23)

As an example, a single-mode Gibbs state with mean excitation number
\bar{n}
can be described by
\mathbf{r}=0
,
\mu=\bar{n}+\frac{1}{2}
, and
\nu=0
.

In this representation, a
J
-mode Gaussian unitary operations is described by a
2J
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{d}=\begin{pmatrix}\vec{\alpha}_{\mathbf{d}}&\vec{\alpha}_{\mathbf{d}}^{*}\end{pmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}
and a
2J\times 2J
unitary matrix
\mathbf{G}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{C}^{*}&\mathbf{S}\\
\mathbf{S}^{*}&\mathbf{C}\end{pmatrix}
satisfying
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{Z}
, or equivalently,

\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}-(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\dagger})^{*}=\mathbb{I},\ (\mathbf{S}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger})^{\mathrm{T}}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}.

(24)

which acts on the first and second moments as
\mathbf{r}\rightarrow\mathbf{G}\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{d}
and
\mathbf{M}\rightarrow\mathbf{G}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger}
. It is directly checked that
|\det(\mathbf{G})|=1
and further
\det(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{G}^{\dagger})=\det(\mathbf{M})
, i.e., the determinant of the second moment does not change under the action of Gaussian unitary operations.

In the following, we will use the tuple
(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{S})
to represent a multi-mode Gaussian unitary operation. For example, a displacement is described by
(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{G}=\mathbb{I})
; a passive unitary, which is composed of phase-shifters and beam-splitters, is described by
\mathbf{d}=0
,
\mathbf{S}=0
, and a unitary matrix
\mathbf{C}
; a phase shifter further requires
\mathbf{C}=\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\phi_{1}},\dots,e^{-i\phi_{J}})
. For single-mode squeezing acting on each mode,
\mathbf{d}=0
,
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{\Lambda_{C}}=\mathrm{diag}(\cosh r_{1},\dots,\cosh r_{J})
, and
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{\Lambda_{S}}=\mathrm{diag}(\sinh r_{1},\dots,\sinh r_{J})
. With this, some known results can be inferred directly. For example, Eq. (
24
) implies that the singular value decompositions of
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{C}}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{C}}^{\dagger}
and
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{S}}^{\dagger}
should satisfy
\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{S}}^{*}
,
\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{S}}
, and
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}}^{2}-\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{S}}^{2}=\mathbb{I}
. It follows that any
\mathbf{G}
in a Gaussian unitary can be decomposed as
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{ps}_{2}}\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{sq}}\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{ps}_{1}}
, where
\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{sq}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}}&\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{S}}\\
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{S}}&\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\mathbf{C}}\end{pmatrix}
describes a single-mode squeezing,
\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{ps}_{1}}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{C}}^{\dagger*}\oplus\mathbf{V}_{\mathbf{C}}^{\dagger}
and
\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{ps}_{2}}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{C}}^{*}\oplus\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{C}}
are passive unitary operators. This immediately leads to the well-known result that any Gaussian unitary is decomposable as passive unitaries, single-mode squeezings and displacements.

Besides, consider a single-mode Gaussian state
\rho^{G}
with first and second moments
(\alpha,\mu,\nu)
. It can be transformed to a Gibbs state
\tau(\rho^{G})
with
\mu^{\prime}=\sqrt{\mu^{2}-|\nu|^{2}}=\sqrt{\det(\mathbf{M})}
by a single-mode Gaussian unitary composed of a displacement by
-\alpha
, a phase shifter with
\phi=-\frac{1}{2}\arg(\nu)
, and a squeezing with
r=-\frac{1}{2}\arg\tanh\frac{|\nu|}{\mu}
. It follows that
\bar{n}(\rho^{G})=|\alpha|+\mu-\frac{1}{2}\geq\sqrt{\det(\mathbf{M})}-\frac{1}{2}=\bar{n}[\tau(\rho^{G})]
. Hence, the thermal excitation in
\rho^{G}
is
\mathfrak{n}(\rho^{G})=\bar{n}[\tau(\rho^{G})]
from the definition.

The representation based on
\{\hat{a}_{j},\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\}
described above will be employed to simplify the proofs of our results. It is equivalent to the more widely used representation based on
\{\hat{x}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\hat{a}_{j}+\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}),\hat{p}_{j}=\frac{1}{i\sqrt{2}}(\hat{a}_{j}-\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger})\}
by a simple transformation.

Next we will focus on the ability of multi-mode Gaussian unitary operations in manipulating the mean number of excitations in each mode. Lemma
2
below will be used in our proof of Lemma
3
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathbf{L}
be a
J\times J
matrix satisfying
\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}\geq\mathbb{I}
, and
O
be a completely positive semi-definite matrix of the same dimension. The eigenvalues of
O
in the descending order are labeled as
o_{j}^{\downarrow}
, while the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L}O\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}
in the descending order are labeled as
o_{j}^{\prime\downarrow}
. Then for all
j
, the following relation holds

o_{j}^{\prime\downarrow}\geq o_{j}^{\downarrow}.

(25)

Let
\{o^{\prime\prime}_{j}\}
be the diagonal elements of
\mathbf{L}O\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}
, and then

\sum_{j=k}^{J}o_{j}^{\prime\prime}\geq\sum_{j=k}^{J}o_{j}^{\downarrow},\ k=1,\dots,J.

(26)

Proof.

By the Courant–Fischer–Weyl min-max principle, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}^{\downarrow}
(where
\downarrow
indicates the descending order) of a Hermitian matrix
Q
can be calculated as

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{\downarrow}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{H}\\
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{M})=j\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x\rangle\in\mathcal{M}\\
\langle x|x\rangle=1\end{subarray}}\langle x|Q|x\rangle,

(27)

where
\mathcal{H}
is the Hilbert space on which
Q
acts.

From
\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}\geq\mathbb{I}
, we have
\|\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}|x\rangle\|^{2}=\langle x|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}|x\rangle\geq 1
for any normalized vector
|x\rangle
. Defining
|x_{\mathbf{L}}\rangle=\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}|x\rangle/\|\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}|x\rangle\|
, we then obtain

\langle x|\mathbf{L}O\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}|x\rangle=\langle x_{\mathbf{L}}|O|x_{\mathbf{L}}\rangle\cdot\|\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}|x\rangle\|^{2}\geq\langle x_{\mathbf{L}}|O|x_{\mathbf{L}}\rangle.

(28)

Besides,
\mathbf{L}
is full-rank, so for any
j
-dimensional space
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{L}}\equiv\{\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}|x\rangle\big||x\rangle\in\mathcal{M}\}
is also
j
-dimensional. It follows that for any normalized vector
|x\rangle\in\mathcal{M}
, there is a normalized vector
|x_{\mathbf{L}}\rangle\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{L}}
, such that Eq. (
28
) holds. Therefore, for any
j
-dimensional space
\mathcal{M}
, there is a
j
-dimensional space
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{L}}
such that

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x\rangle\in\mathcal{M}\\
\langle x|x\rangle\end{subarray}}\langle x|\mathbf{L}O\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}|x\rangle\geq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x_{\mathbf{L}}\rangle\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{L}}\\
\langle x_{\mathbf{L}}|x_{\mathbf{L}}\rangle\end{subarray}}\langle x_{\mathbf{L}}|O|x_{\mathbf{L}}\rangle.

(29)

It follows immediately that

o_{j}^{\prime\downarrow}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}\in\mathcal{H}\\
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{M})=j\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x\rangle\in\mathcal{M}\\
\langle x|x\rangle\end{subarray}}\langle x|\mathbf{L}O\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}|x\rangle\geq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{L}}\in\mathcal{H}\\
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{L}})=j\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x_{\mathbf{L}}\rangle\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbf{L}}\\
\langle x_{\mathbf{L}}|x_{\mathbf{L}}\rangle\end{subarray}}\langle x_{\mathbf{L}}|O|x_{\mathbf{L}}\rangle=o_{j}^{\downarrow}.

(30)

Further, Eq. (
26
) is obtained by recalling that the diagonal elements
\{o^{\prime\prime}_{j}\}
and the eigenvalues
\{o^{\prime\downarrow}_{j}\}
of
\mathbf{L}O\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}
are related as
\sum_{j=k}^{J}o^{\prime\prime}_{j}\geq\sum_{j=k}^{J}o^{\prime\downarrow}_{j}
.

∎

Lemma 3
.

Consider a system consisting of
J
independent harmonic oscillators with frequency
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{J}
and initially in the Gibbs state at inverse temperature
\beta
. The mean excitation number of the
j
th mode is
\bar{n}_{j}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega_{j}}-1}
. Under any Gaussian unitary
(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{S})
, the mean excitation number
\bar{n}_{l}^{\prime}
in the output satisfies

\sum_{j=k}^{J}\bar{n}_{j}^{\prime}\geq\sum_{j=k}^{J}\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow},\forall k=1,\dots,J,

(31)

where
\{\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}\}
is
\{\bar{n}_{j}\}
in the descending order.
The equalities are saturated for all
k
when
\mathbf{d}=0
,
\mathbf{S}=0
and
\mathbf{C}
is a permutation which puts
\bar{n}_{j}
in the descending order.

This lemma is related to Lemma 1 in Ref.
17
, where the relation between the diagonal elements and the symplectic eigenvalues of a covariant matrix has been demonstrated. Here we are more interested in the conversion of the mean excitation numbers of each mode under the action of a multi-mode Gaussian unitary operation. For completeness, we provide our comparably simpler proof.

Proof.

Because the system is initially in the Gibbs state at inverse temperature
\beta
, the second moment of the
J
-mode initial state is in the following form

\mu=\mathrm{diag}(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{J}),\nu=0,

(32)

where
\mu_{j}=\bar{n}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}
, and
\bar{n}_{j}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\omega_{j}}-1}
is the mean excitation number of the
j
th mode.
After a joint Gaussian unitary
\mathbf{G}
, the second moment becomes

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\mu\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}+\mathbf{S}^{*}\mu\mathbf{S}^{*\dagger},

(33)

\displaystyle\nu^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{*}\mu\mathbf{S}^{*\dagger}+\mathbf{S}\mu\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}.

(34)

The second moment of the
j
-th mode is then calculated as

\displaystyle\mu_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j^{\prime}}(|\mathbf{C}_{jj^{\prime}}|^{2}+|\mathbf{S}_{jj^{\prime}}|^{2})\mu_{j^{\prime}},

(35)

\displaystyle\nu_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j^{\prime}}\mathbf{C}_{jj^{\prime}}^{*}\mathbf{S}_{jj^{\prime}}\mu_{j^{\prime}}.

(36)

It follows that for all
k=1,\dots,J
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{J}\mu_{j}^{\prime}=\sum_{j=k}^{J}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{J}(|\mathbf{C}_{jj^{\prime}}|^{2}+|\mathbf{S}_{jj^{\prime}}|^{2})\mu_{j^{\prime}}\geq\sum_{j=k}^{J}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{J}|\mathbf{C}_{jj^{\prime}}|^{2}\mu_{j^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=k}^{J}\mu_{j}^{\prime\prime},

(37)

where
\{\mu_{j}^{\prime\prime}\}
are the diagonal elements of the matrix
\mathbf{C}\mu\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}
. From Eq. (
24
),
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}\geq\mathbb{I}
.
It follows from Lemma
2
that

\sum_{j=k}^{J}\mu_{j}^{\prime\prime}\geq\sum_{j=k}^{J}\mu_{j}^{\downarrow}
for all
k=1,\dots,J
, and then
\sum_{j=k}^{J}\mu_{j}^{\prime}\geq\sum_{j=k}^{J}\mu_{j}^{\downarrow}
. Recalling that
\bar{n}_{j}^{\prime}\geq\mu_{j}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}
is the mean excitation number of the
j
th mode in the output, and
\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}=\mu_{j}^{\downarrow}-\frac{1}{2}
is the mean excitation number in the input, we arrive at Eq.
31
.
It is noted that the left hand side means the summation of the mean excitation numbers of arbitrary
k
modes in the output.
∎

A.2
The cooling limit of the Gaussian HBAC

Two consequences of Lemma
3
are observed. The first is Lemma
1
in the main text, which we restated below.

Lemma 4
.

Consider a
J
-mode system initially in a Gaussian product state
\rho_{J}=\otimes_{j=1}^{J}\rho_{j}
.
After the action of a global unitary, the minimum of the thermal excitation of Mode 1 in the output is

\min_{W\in\mathcal{U}^{G}_{J}}\mathfrak{n}\left(\mathrm{tr}_{\backslash 1}(W\rho_{J}W^{\dagger})\right)=\min_{j}\mathfrak{n}(\rho_{j}),

(38)

where
\mathcal{U}^{G}_{J}
is the set of
J
-mode Gaussian unitary operators.

Proof.

From
\rho_{j}=W_{j}\tau(\rho_{j})W_{j}^{\dagger}
,
\mathfrak{n}(\rho_{j})=\bar{n}[\tau(\rho_{j})]
, and the definition of the thermal excitation, we have for all
J
-mode Gaussian unitary
W
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{n}\left(\mathrm{tr}_{\backslash 1}(W\rho_{J}W^{\dagger})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{W}_{1}\in\mathcal{U}^{G}_{1}}\bar{n}\left(\tilde{W}_{1}\mathrm{tr}_{\backslash 1}(W(\otimes_{j=1}^{J}W_{j})(\otimes_{j=1}^{J}\tau(\rho_{j}))(\otimes_{j=1}^{J}W_{j}^{\dagger})W^{\dagger})\tilde{W}_{1}^{\dagger}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{n}\left[\mathrm{tr}_{\backslash 1}\left(\bar{W}(\otimes_{j=1}^{J}\tau(\rho_{j}))\bar{W}^{\dagger}\right)\right]\geq\min_{j}\bar{n}[\tau(\rho_{j})]=\min_{j}\mathfrak{n}(\rho_{j}),

where
\bar{W}=\tilde{\bar{W}}_{1}W(\otimes_{j=1}^{J}W_{j})
and
\tilde{\bar{W}}_{1}

Lemma 5
.

Consider a
J
-mode Gibbs state
\tau=\otimes_{j=1}^{J}\tau_{j}
with the mean excitation number of each mode in the descending order
\{\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}\}
, and a
J
-mode Gaussian state
\rho=U^{G}\tau U^{G\dagger}
obtained from
\tau
via a Gaussian unitary
U^{G}
with the mean excitation numbers
\bar{n}_{j}^{\rho}=\mathrm{tr}(\rho\hat{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{j})
. Let
\vec{K}=(K_{1},\dots,K_{J^{\prime}})
(

) be a real vector with its elements in the increasing order. If
\bar{n}_{1}^{\rho}=\bar{n}_{J}^{\downarrow},\dots,\bar{n}^{\rho}_{J-{J^{\prime}}}=\bar{n}_{{J^{\prime}}+1}^{\downarrow}

\sum_{j=1}^{J^{\prime}}K_{j}\bar{n}_{J-{J^{\prime}}+j}^{\rho}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{J^{\prime}}K_{j}\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}.

(40)

The equality is saturated when
\bar{n}^{\rho}_{J-{J^{\prime}}+j}=\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}
for
j=1,\dots,{J^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

For
{J^{\prime}}=J

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}K_{j}\bar{n}_{j}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}K_{j}\left(\sum_{l=j}^{J}\bar{n}^{\rho}_{l}-\sum_{l=j+1}^{J}\bar{n}_{l}^{\rho}\right)

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{1}\sum_{l=1}^{J}\bar{n}^{\rho}_{l}+\sum_{j=2}^{J}(K_{j}-K_{j-1})\sum_{l=j}^{J}\bar{n}^{\rho}_{l}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle K_{1}\sum_{l=1}^{J}\bar{n}^{\downarrow}_{l}+\sum_{j=2}^{J}(K_{j}-K_{j-1})\sum_{l=j}^{J}\bar{n}^{\downarrow}_{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}K_{j}\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}.

In the third line, we have used Lemma
3
. The equality is saturated when
\bar{n}_{j}^{\rho}=\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}
for all
j
.

For
{J^{\prime}}<J
, define
\vec{K}^{\prime}
such that
K^{\prime}_{j}=K_{j}
for

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J^{\prime}}K_{j}\bar{n}_{J-{J^{\prime}}+j}^{\rho}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J^{\prime}}K^{\prime}_{j}\bar{n}_{J-{J^{\prime}}+j}^{\rho}+\sum_{j={J^{\prime}}+1}^{J}K^{\prime}_{j}\bar{n}_{J-j+1}^{\rho}-\sum_{j={J^{\prime}}+1}^{J}K^{\prime}_{j}\bar{n}_{J-j+1}^{\rho}

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}K^{\prime}_{j}\bar{n}_{j}^{\rho^{\prime}}-\sum_{j={J^{\prime}}+1}^{J}K^{\prime}_{j}\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}K^{\prime}_{j}\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}-\sum_{j={J^{\prime}}+1}^{J}K^{\prime}_{j}\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}=\sum_{j=1}^{J^{\prime}}K^{\prime}_{j}\bar{n}_{j}^{\downarrow}.

where

B.1
Evolution of mean excitation number under Heisenberg picture

The mean excitation number of target after single operation of
V^{(p)}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{S}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[V^{(p)}(t)(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{M})V^{(p)\dagger}(t)(\hat{n}_{S}\otimes\mathbb{I})\right]

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left[\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho_{S})\hat{n}_{S}\right]

where
\mathcal{E}_{t}(\rho_{S})=\mathrm{tr}_{M}[V^{(p)}(t)(\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{M})V^{(p)\dagger}(t)]
. Equivalently in Heisenberg picture, we have

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{S}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left[\tau_{S}\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}(\hat{n}_{S})\right]

(56)

where
\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}(\hat{n}_{S})=\mathrm{tr}_{M}\left[(\mathbb{I}\otimes\sqrt{\tau_{M}})V^{(p){\dagger}}(t)(\hat{n}_{S}\otimes\mathbb{I})V^{(p)}(t)(\mathbb{I}\otimes\sqrt{\tau_{M}})\right]
. In short time regime
\chi t\ll 1
, the mean excitation number is approximate using the Baker-Campbell Haussdorf (BCH) expansion

V^{(p){\dagger}}(t)(\hat{n}_{S}\otimes\mathbb{I})V^{(p)}(t)\approx\bar{n}_{S}+i[H^{(p)},\hat{n}_{S}]t-\frac{1}{2}[H^{(p)},[H^{(p)},\hat{n}_{S}]]t^{2}

(57)

where

[H^{(p)},\hat{n}_{S}]=\chi(\hat{a}b^{{\dagger}p}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}b^{p})

(58)

[H^{(p)},[H^{(p)},\hat{n}_{S}]]=2\chi^{2}\left[\hat{n}_{S}[\hat{b}^{p},\hat{b}^{{\dagger}p}]-\hat{b}^{{\dagger}p}\hat{b}^{p}\right]+\chi(\omega_{0}-p\omega_{1})(\hat{a}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}p}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{b}^{p})

(59)

If the initial states of the system and machine are assumed to to Gibbs state
\tau_{S}
,
\tau_{M}
with mean excitation number
\bar{n}_{S}
and
\bar{n}_{M}
respectively, the evolution of the mean excitation number of target
\bar{n}_{S}(t)
and machine
\bar{n}_{M}(t)
are approximate to (in the small time perturbation).

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{S}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{S}-\left[\bar{n}_{S}\langle[\hat{b}^{p},\hat{b}^{{\dagger}p}]\rangle_{\tau_{M}}-\langle\hat{b}^{{\dagger}p}\hat{b}^{p}\rangle_{\tau_{M}}\right]\chi^{2}t^{2}+\mathcal{O}(t^{4})+...

(60)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{S}-\left[(1+\bar{n}_{M})^{p}\bar{n}_{S}-\bar{n}_{M}^{p}(1+\bar{n}_{S})\right]p!\chi^{2}t^{2}+\mathcal{O}(t^{4})+...

where we have used

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{n}^{n}}{(\bar{n}+1)^{n+1}}\langle\hat{b}^{{\dagger}p}\hat{b}^{p}\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{n}^{n}}{(\bar{n}+1)^{n+1}}\frac{n!}{(n-p)!}

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p!\bar{n}^{p}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{n}^{n}}{(\bar{n}+1)^{n+1}}\langle\hat{b}^{p}\hat{b}^{{\dagger}p}\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{n}^{n}}{(\bar{n}+1)^{n+1}}\frac{(n+p)!}{(n!)}

(62)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p!(1+\bar{n})^{p}

Using
tr[\tau_{S,M}a^{m}b^{{\dagger}n}]=0
, the contributions from Eq. (
58
) and the second term in Eq. (
59
) vanish. Thus,
\bar{n}_{S}(t)
is also valid for non-resonance case
\omega_{0}\neq p\omega_{1}
if (i) the initial state
\tau
is state that is diagonal in the Fock basis. (ii) short-time perturbation limit.
Following similar calculations, we show that the first moment
\alpha(t)=\mathrm{tr}(\tau_{S}\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}(\hat{a}))=0
and the off-diagonal element of the second moment
\nu(t)=\mathrm{tr}(\tau_{S}\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}(\hat{a}^{2}))=0
. Therefore, the thermal excitation of the output state
\rho_{S}^{\prime}(t)=\mathcal{E}_{t}(\tau_{S})
equals to its mean excitation number, i.e.,
\mathfrak{n}(\rho^{\prime}_{S}(t))=\bar{n}_{S}(t)
.

B.2
Cooling limit for iterative cooling (matrix algebra)

The cooling protocol in previous section is then repeated with

\tau_{S}\otimes\tau_{M}
with fixed machine gap
\omega_{S},\omega_{M}

L

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{S}^{(L)}=\mathrm{tr}\left[\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{t}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}}_{L\text{times}}(\rho_{S})\hat{n}_{S}\right].

Equivalently in the Heisenberg picture, we have

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{S}^{(L)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left[\rho_{S}\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}}_{L\text{times}}(\hat{n}_{S})\right],

(64)

and further,

Then for

\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}}_{L\text{ times}}(\hat{n}_{s})=\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}}_{(L-1)\text{ times}}(\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}(\hat{n}_{s})),

\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}}_{L\text{times}}(\hat{n}_{S})\approx\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}}_{(L-1)\text{ times}}(\hat{n}_{S})-g^{2}t^{2}\left[\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}}_{(L-1)\text{ times}}(\hat{n}_{S})(4\bar{n}_{M}+2)-2\mathbb{I}\bar{n}_{M}^{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(t^{4}).

(67)

Plugging back into Eq.
64

\bar{n}_{S}^{(L)}\approx\bar{n}_{S}^{(L-1)}-g^{2}t^{2}\left[\bar{n}_{S}^{(L-1)}(4\bar{n}_{M}+2)-2\bar{n}_{M}^{2}\right]+\mathcal{O}(t^{4}).

(68)

\bar{n}_{S}^{(L)}\approx\bar{n}_{S}^{(0)}(1-a)^{L}+b\frac{1-(1-a)^{L}}{a}

B.3
Thermality of the system after many iterations

It’s apparent that the system evolves into a transient state after a single collision which is not Gaussian nor thermal. In previous section, we found that the the mean excitation of the system converge to a thermal excitation. This raises a question whether this is coincidence or the final state actually equilibrates to a Gibbs state. To verify that, we use Fano factor
q(t)
defined as (Eq.9 in
25
)

q(L)=\frac{\langle\hat{n}_{S}^{2}\rangle_{L}-\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{L}^{2}}{\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{L}(\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{L}+1)}-1

(70)

where
\langle\bullet\rangle_{L}=\mathrm{tr}(\tau_{S}\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\dagger}}_{L\text{times}}(\bullet))
. If
q(t)=0
then the system state has Gibbs distribution at the
L
th iteration . In the following calculation, we are interested in whether
\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}q(t)=0
, where

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}q(L)=\frac{\langle\hat{n}_{S}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}-\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{\infty}^{2}}{\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{\infty}(\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{\infty}+1)}-1,

(71)

The analytical form of
\langle\hat{n}_{S}^{2}\rangle_{1}
and
\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{1}
are given by

\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{1}=(1-a)\bar{n}_{S}^{(0)}+c,

(72)

and

\langle\hat{n}_{S}^{2}\rangle_{1}=(1-2a)\bar{n^{2}}_{S}^{(0)}+b\bar{n}_{S}+c,

(73)

where
\bar{n}_{S}^{(0)}=\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{\tau_{S}}
and
\bar{n^{2}}_{S}^{(0)}=\langle\hat{n}_{S}^{2}\rangle_{\tau_{S}}
. Here,
a=\chi^{2}t^{2}[(1+\bar{n}_{M})^{p}-\bar{n}_{M}^{p}]p!
,
b=\chi^{2}t^{2}[(1+\bar{n}_{M})^{p}+3\bar{n}_{M}^{p}]p!
and
c=\chi^{2}t^{2}\bar{n}_{M}^{p}p!
. After
L
iterations, the
\langle\hat{n}_{S}^{2}\rangle_{L}
and
\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{L}
in term of initial mean excitation read

\bar{n}_{S}^{(L)}\approx\bar{n}_{S}^{(0)}(1-a)^{L}+c\frac{1-(1-a)^{L}}{a}

(74)

and

\displaystyle\bar{n^{2}}_{S}^{(L)}

\displaystyle=(1-2a)^{L}\bar{n^{2}}_{S}^{(0)}+\bar{n}_{S}^{(0)}b\frac{1-a}{a}\Big[(1-a)^{L}-(1-2a)^{L}\Big]

(75)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{bc}{a}\left(\frac{1-(1-2a)^{L}}{2a}-\frac{(1-a)^{L}-(1-2a)^{L}}{a}\right)+c\,\frac{1-(1-2a)^{L}}{2a}.

(76)

When
L\rightarrow\infty
and
a\ll 1
(as implies by
\chi t\ll 1
), we have

\langle\hat{n}_{S}\rangle_{\infty}=\bar{n}_{S}^{(\infty)}\approx c/a

(77)

\langle\hat{n}_{S}^{2}\rangle_{\infty}=\bar{n^{2}}_{S}^{(\infty)}\approx\frac{c}{2a}\left(1+\frac{b}{c}\right)

(78)

Plugging this into the Eq.
71
and using the relation
1+b/c=2(1+2c/a)
, we have

\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}q(L)=0,\forall p.

(79)

Therefore, when the cooling condition for
p
-excitation exchange is satisfied, its collision in larger
L
limit equilibrates towards a Gibbs state for all nonlinearity order
p
. We verify the result with numerical example with
p=2,3
(see Fig.
6
), where one can see that the transient state (finite
L
) does not exhibit Gibbs distribution, however in large
L
limit the equilibrated state exhibits Gibbs distribution. Effectively, the collisional model with
p
-excitation exchange map a Gibbs state to Gibbs state, while in between the evolution can be out-of equilibrium,

\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{t}\circ...\circ\mathcal{E}_{t}}_{L\rightarrow\infty}(\tau_{S}(H_{S},T_{S}))=\tau_{f}(H_{f},T_{f}),

(80)

where
H_{f}
is also a quadratic Hamiltonian, i.e., a harmonic oscillator.
