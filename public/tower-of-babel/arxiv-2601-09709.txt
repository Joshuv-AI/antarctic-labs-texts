Social Determinants of Health Prediction for ICD-9 Code with Reasoning Models
Sharim Khan, Paul Landes, Adam Cross, Jimeng Sun

Abstract
Social Determinants of Health correlate with patient outcomes but are rarely captured in structured data. Recent attention has been given to automatically extracting these markers from clinical text to supplement diagnostic systems with knowledge of patients’ social circumstances. Large language models demonstrate strong performance in identifying Social Determinants of Health labels from sentences. However, prediction in large admissions or longitudinal notes is challenging given long distance dependencies. In this paper, we explore hospital admission multi-label Social Determinants of Health ICD-9 code classification on the MIMIC-III dataset using reasoning models and traditional large language models. We exploit existing ICD-9 codes for prediction on admissions, which achieved a 89% F1. Our contributions include our findings, missing Social Determinants of Health codes in 139 admissions, and code to reproduce the results.

Introduction

Social Determinants of Health are non-medical factors that account for an estimated 80–90% of modifiable contributors to health outcomes and disparities
(
9
)
. Social Determinants of Healths may be adverse or protective. Adverse Social Determinants of Healths are factors that create an additional social work or resource support need for patients
(
4
)
. Healthcare providers often document these factors in unstructured notes in Electronic Health Records, which limits their utility. The International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision classification system includes V-codes designed to capture social circumstances such as homelessness, unemployment, and inadequate family support. However, physicians diagnose Social Determinants of Health V-codes as little as 2%, even in populations with documented social needs
(
11
;
5
)
.

This underutilization often stems from documenting social risk factors within clinical narratives, which require substantial time and expertise to diagnose as billing codes
(
11
)
. Reasoning-capable language models present an opportunity to predict Social Determinants of Health by using coding guidelines in prompts and training on V-codes (a subset of ICD-9 codes used by insurance companies for billing) as labels already diagnosed by physicians in the MIMIC-III dataset as shown in Figure
1
.

Existing large language models studies on Social Determinants of Health classify text into broad social risk categories such as housing or social support, as shown by
4
. This approach does not operate at the admission level nor predict specific billing codes. In contrast, our work directly predicts fine-grained ICD-9 V-codes from the entire set of clinical documents for an admission, bridging the gap between unstructured Social Determinants of Health documentation and structured diagnostic coding.

The semantics of the clinical notes with the diagnosed ICD-9 V-codes map to the descriptions of the labels from the annotation guide provided by
4
as shown in Table
1
. Undomiciled and unemployed are the only adverse Social Determinants of Health categories with overlap between this study and the work done by
4
. This study evaluates the feasibility and performance of reasoning language models to predict V-codes from clinical documentation across open-source and closed frontier models.

Related Work

Initial approaches to Social Determinants of Health identification employed rule-based systems and traditional machine learning methods to detect Social Determinants of Health from clinical text
(
3
)
. However, these methods demonstrated limited generalizability due to institution-specific documentation patterns. Subsequent advances in neural architectures, including convolutional models
(
10
)
and transformer architectures such as ClinicalBERT
(
1
)
, improved automated medical coding performance on diagnosis and procedure codes.

Researchers have explored large language models for Social Determinants of Health mention classification in clinical notes.
4
achieved 70% macro-F1 for adverse Social Determinants of Health classification at the sentence-level. Their work identified 93.8% of patients with adverse Social Determinants of Health compared to 2.0% captured by ICD-10 codes. Their taxonomy of labels maps to broad ICD-10 Z-code categories.

Hybrid architectures combining language models with traditional neural networks have demonstrated improvements in both accuracy and computational efficiency for Social Determinants of Health extraction
(
8
)

We identified admissions with notes containing the V-codes listed in Table

or Social Determinants of Healths not diagnosed by physicians.
We labeled these admissions with the additional V-codes for related Social Determinants of Healths, which we refer to this as “amended codes”. For example, “He is
unemployed

We evaluate the models on the amended codes to avoid penalizing for missing undiagnosed Social Determinants of Healths.

Few-shot prompting
(
2
)
is used to predict Social Determinants of Health labels from clinical documentation.
Our prompts’ role is a certified medical coder, specify labeling criteria, and include coding example (both positive and negative). Our prompts are given in Appendix

Evaluation

We evaluated seven large language models spanning frontier closed-source, open-source, reasoning-capable, and traditional architectures on multi-label classification of V-codes that represent adverse Social Determinants of Health labels. We processed each clinical note through the model independently across admission, then aggregated predictions at the admission level.

5
Results

The results of the models we tested are in Table
3
.
GPT-5-mini achieved the highest overall performance with 72.2% exact match and 89.1% micro-F1, which demonstrates frontier models’ capability in extracting V-codes in clinical documentation. Open-source models demonstrated competitive capabilities, with Qwen3-32B and GPT-OSS-20B achieving exact match scores of 65.5% and 65.2%, respectively. This performance suggests that effective Social Determinants of Health prediction does not require proprietary systems.

Traditional language models showed significantly lower performance, with Llama-3.3-70B and GPT-3.5 achieving exact match scores of 35.5% and 14.4%, respectively. The performance difference between traditional and reasoning-capable models indicates that multi-label Social Determinants of Health classification presents challenges that benefit from advanced reasoning capabilities, particularly when processing lengthy clinical narratives with distributed evidence.

Reasoning models leverage the chain-of-thought, which is a series of intermediate reasoning steps the model performs before producing an output
(
12
)
. These models perform better given the length of the prompt, and complexity of conjunctions (
i.e.
inclusion and exclusion clauses), and negative examples. For example, our prompt contains hierarchical code exclusion rules (such as identifying
homelessness
in an example, but instructing the exclusion of
inadequate material resources
).

Label-Specific Performance

GPT-5-mini’s performance varied across Social Determinants of Health categories (Table
4
). The model achieved high performance for
homelessness
and
family alcoholism
, where documentation typically follows consistent terminology. Moderate performance was observed for
unemployment
and
inadequate material resources
. Performance decreased for labels requiring inference across scattered evidence, including
no family caregiver
,
legal problems
, and
emotional abuse
. While this pattern suggests labels with explicit documentation are more reliably predicted, the conservative prompting strategy for codes with low support may have limited the model’s ability to capture more nuanced cases, and further prompt optimization could improve performance on complex inference tasks.

Evaluation Against Original Coding

We evaluated model performance on the amended codes and the non-amended codes (Table
5
). Evaluated on the amended codes, GPT-5-mini achieved 72.2% exact match and 89.1% micro-F1. Performance against non-amended codes showed 48.9% exact match and 67.0% micro-F1. The difference in performance reflects instances where clinical notes contained evidence for additional Social Determinants of Health labels beyond those originally diagnosed.

6
Discussion

The evaluation metrics support that large language models can predict adverse Social Determinants of Health V-codes from clinical notes with high accuracy (89.1% micro-F1). Furthermore, we identified clinical notes that contained adverse Social Determinants of Health semantics beyond those originally diagnosed. The performance difference between the prediction of amended codes and the non-amended codes suggests non-agreement in the diagnosis of V-codes among physicians. There is potential for these multi-label classification models to bridge the gap between unstructured documentation of Social Determinants of Healths and missed diagnoses.

The model performed best on labels with standard terminology and struggled with recall when documentation varied, such as
history of emotional abuse
.
However, further experimentation is needed improve precision-recall tradeoff. Open-source models demonstrated competitive performance, suggesting effective V-code prediction may be achievable without requiring commercial models.

7
Conclusion

Reasoning large language models can effectively predict adverse Social Determinants of Health labels from clinical documentation through few-shot prompting approaches. The competitive performance of open-source models compared to commercial models demonstrates that effective Social Determinants of Health prediction does not require proprietary systems, making these capabilities accessible to resource-constrained healthcare settings. Our identification of admissions with documented but uncoded Social Determinants of Health factors highlights a gap between clinical documentation and coding practices.
As healthcare systems increasingly recognize Social Determinants of Health as critical quality indicators, classification models can improve both clinical care and population health management.

8
Limitations

The use of MIMIC-III admissions potentially limits generalizability to other institutions with varied documentation practices. The study examined eight ICD-9 V-codes, a subset of available Social Determinants of Health codes. The evaluation dataset was developed by a single annotator without an inter-rater reliability assessment.

The notes used in our experiments are limited to those exported in the MIMIC-III dataset, which include only discharge summaries and those from the ICU department
7
. Notes during the hospital stay for patients that are moved to other departments during their recovery are omitted and likely have an impact on model performance.

Appendix B
Annotation Guide for Social Determinants of Health ICD-9 V-codes

B.1
V60.0: Homelessness

Definition:
Patient has no permanent residence or lives in transient arrangements such as streets, shelters, or motels.

Approximate Synonyms:
Homeless, currently homeless.

Applies To:
Hobos, tramps, vagabonds, social migrants, transients.

Inclusion Criteria:
Explicit statements such as “Patient is homeless,” “Lives in shelter,” or “No permanent address.”

Exclusion Criteria:
Financial hardship only (V60.2), statements like “lives alone” or “poor” without loss of housing.

Examples:

•

Positive: “Lives in a homeless shelter after eviction.”

•

Negative: “Struggling to pay rent but living with family.”

B.2
V60.2: Inadequate Material Resources

Definition:
Patient lacks financial means for necessities such as food, medication, or housing.

Approximate Synonyms:
Financial problem.

Applies To:
Economic problem, poverty not otherwise stated.

Inclusion Criteria:
Explicit financial statements (e.g., “Cannot afford medications,” “No money for food”).

Exclusion Criteria:
Any homelessness (V60.0), implicit hardship without direct evidence.

Examples:

•

Positive: “Financial hardship preventing purchase of insulin.”

•

Negative: “Homeless and can’t afford meds.” (V60.0 only)

B.3
V60.4: No other household member able to render care

Definition:
Indicates no family or caregiver available to provide care or support.

Applies To:
Family member too ill or unsuited to render care, partner temporarily away, or person away from usual home.

Inclusion Criteria:
Explicit lack of family support (e.g., “No family contact and no one to help with care.”).

Exclusion Criteria:
“Lives alone,” “independent,” or “widowed” without need for care.

Examples:

•

Positive: “No family or friends available to assist after surgery.”

•

Negative: “Lives alone, independent in activities of daily life.”

B.4
V62.0: Unemployment

Definition:
Patient currently lacks employment.

Excludes:
Main problem due to economic inadequacy (V60.2).

Inclusion Criteria:
Direct mention of unemployment or job loss (“unemployed,” “laid off,” “no job”).

Exclusion Criteria:
Retired, student, stay-at-home parent, or on medical leave; “used to work.”

Examples:

•

Positive: “Unemployed, seeking work.”

•

Negative: “Previously worked as nurse.”

B.5
V62.5: Legal Problems

Definition:
Current involvement with legal or judicial processes (criminal, civil, custody, or guardianship).

Approximate Synonyms:
Arrested, charged with crime, imprisonment, litigation, parole, bail, probation, court case pending.

Applies To:
Imprisonment, legal investigation, litigation, prosecution.

Inclusion Criteria:
Active legal process (arrest, trial, custody case, guardianship hearing).

Exclusion Criteria:
History of crime or incarceration without current legal process.

Examples:

•

Positive: “Currently on probation after DUI arrest.”

•

Negative: “History of incarceration in 2010.”

B.6
V15.41: History of Physical or Sexual Abuse

Definition:
Past exposure to physical or sexual violence inflicted by another person.

Approximate Synonyms:
History of physical abuse, sexual abuse, child abuse, domestic violence, rape.

Applies To:
Rape.

Inclusion Criteria:
Mentions such as “History of sexual abuse” or “Physically abused by spouse.”

Exclusion Criteria:
Accidents, self-injury, patient as aggressor, emotional abuse only (V15.42).

Examples:

•

Positive: “Reports childhood sexual abuse by uncle.”

•

Negative: “Verbal abuse from partner.” (V15.42)

B.7
V15.42: History of Emotional Abuse or Neglect

Definition:
History of psychological, emotional, or neglectful abuse without physical or sexual component.

Approximate Synonyms:
Emotional abuse, neglect, psychological abuse, verbal abuse, child neglect.

Applies To:
Neglect.

Inclusion Criteria:
“Emotionally abusive relationship,” “childhood neglect,” “verbal abuse.”

Exclusion Criteria:
Physical or sexual abuse (V15.41), depression/anxiety/PTSD alone.

Examples:

•

Positive: “Endured years of emotional abuse from spouse.”

•

Negative: “PTSD from physical assault.” (V15.41)

B.8
V61.41: Family History of Alcoholism

Definition:
Indicates alcoholism or alcohol dependence in the patient’s family.

Approximate Synonyms:
Family history of alcohol dependence, FHX of alcoholism, adult child of alcoholic.

Inclusion Criteria:
Mentions such as “Father alcoholic,” “Mother with ETOH abuse,” or “Family history of alcoholism.”

Exclusion Criteria:
Patient’s own alcohol use; negations (“Denies family history of alcoholism”).

Examples:

•

Positive: “Mother and uncle both heavy drinkers.”

•

Negative: “Patient with long history of alcohol use disorder.”

General Annotation Rules

1.

Precision over recall: only label with explicit evidence.

2.

Mutual exclusions: V60.0 supersedes V60.2; V15.41 and V15.42 are mutually exclusive.

3.

Each code requires independent evidence.

4.

When in doubt, do not assign a code.
