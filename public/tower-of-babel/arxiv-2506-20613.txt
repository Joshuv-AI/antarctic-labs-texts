On likelihood of a Condorcet winner for uniformly random and independent voter preferences
Boris Pittel

Abstract
. We study a mathematical model of voting contest with m m voters and n n candidates, with each voter ranking the candidates in order of preference, without ties. A Condorcet winner is a candidate who gets more than m / 2 m/2 votes in pairwise contest with every other candidate. An “impartial culture” setting is the case when each voter chooses his/her candidate preference list uniformly at random from all n ! n! preferences, and does it independently of all other voters. For impartial culture case, Robert May and Lisa Sauermann showed that when m = 2 ​ k − 1 m=2k-1 is fixed ( k = 2 k=2 and k > 2 k>2 respectively), and n n grows indefinitely, the probability of a Condorcet winner is small, of order n − ( k − 1 ) / k n^{-(k-1)/k} . We show that when m m grows indefinitely and m ≫ n 4 m\gg n^{4} , the probability of a Condercet winner is 1 − O ⁡ ( n 2 / m 1 / 2 ) → 1 1-O(n^{2}/m^{1/2})\to 1 .

1.
Condorcet winner

Suppose we have
m
voters and
n
possible candidates to vote for. Each voter ranks candidates in the most to the least preferred. A Condorcet winner is a candidate
j\in[n]
such that for every
j^{\prime}\in[n]\setminus\{j\}
the number of voters who prefer
j
to
j^{\prime}
is
strictly
above
m/2
. There can be at most one Condercet winner. Suppose that the voters’ preferences are random and, more specifically, uniform and independent of each other.
In the literature, this is known as an “impartial culture” case.
Let
Q_{m,n}
denote the probability that there is a Condorcet winner. Sauermann
[
5
]
proved recently that, for
k
fixed and
n
large,
winner is given by

(1.1)

Q_{2k-1,n}=C_{k}\cdot n^{-(k-1)/k}+O_{k}\Bigl(\tfrac{(\log n)^{1/k}}{n}\Bigr).

Here
0<C_{k}<\infty
is given by

(1.2)

C_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{x}\geq\mathbb{0}}\exp(-\sigma_{k,2k-1}(\mathbb{x}))\,d\mathbb{x},\quad\mathbb{x}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{2k-1}\},

and
\sigma_{k,2k-1}(\mathbb{x})=\sum_{I\subset[2k-1],\,|I|=k}\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}
is the usual
k
-th elementary symmetric polynomial of the variables
x_{1},\dots x_{2k-1}
. Thus, the probability of a Condercet winner tends to zero at an exact rate
n^{-(k-1)/k}
, which for
k>2
considerably strengthens a result of May
[
4
]
, proved in 1971.
As also pointed out in
[
5
]
, for
k=2
the above claim, with a weaker remainder term, was proved in

[
4
]
, leading to an explicit
C_{2}=0.5\pi^{3/2}
.

In this paper we consider the case when the number of voters is large, far exceeding the number of candidates.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
m\to\infty
and
n=o(m^{1/4})
, then
Q_{m,n}=1-O(n^{4}/m)\to 1
.

Note.
Thus, for
m\gg n^{4}
, in the impartial-culture case a Condorcet winner exists with probability asymptotic to
1
. Most of the argument requires
m\gg n^{2}
only, but we have no working intuition as to whether
m\gg n^{2}
may
already be sufficient for validity of our claim.

In
[
5
]
the reader will find other results for the case
m=2k-1
fixed, and a thought-provoking review of the related work throughout the years, such as Gehrlein
[
3
]
, Tsetlin, Regenwetter, and Grofman
[
6
]
. It was proved
in
[
6
]
that for
n=3
, the impartial culture “maximizes the probability of (majority) cycles within the class of all cultures of indifference”. (
A\to B\to C\to A
is called a majority cycle among
three candidates
A,B,C
if
A
beats
B
beats
C
beats
A
.) That is, an“irrational” intransitivity of a voting-induced binary relation between candidates is the likeliest for the impartial culture case among “cultures of indifference”. Our result implies directly that, for the impartial-culture case (with
m
uniformly random and independent
n
-long voter’s preference lists for candidates) the probability of a majority cycle of length
n
approaches
0
if
m\gg n^{4}
.

1.1.
Proofs

Following
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
, we generate the random instance of of
impartial culture preferences via an
m\times n
matrix
X=\{X_{a,b}\}
with the entries
X_{a,b}
independent,
[0,1]
-Uniforms. Each voter
a\in[m]
ranks the
n
candidates from most preferred to least preferred in the increasing order of the entries of the
a
-th row
\{X_{a,1},\dots,X_{a,n}\}
. Let us evaluate
Q_{m,n}
, the probability that candidate
1
is a Condercet winner. The candidate
1
is preferred by strictly more than
m/2
voters to a candidate
j>1
if and only if
\sum_{a\in[2k-1]}\mathbb{I}(X_{a,1}<X_{a,j})>m/2
, where
\mathbb{I}(A)
denote the indicator of an event
A
. The
(n-1)
events in question are not independent, but they are independent, if
conditioned
on the event
A:=\{X_{1,1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{2k-1,1}=x_{2k-1}\}
. Now, the
(n-1)
sums

(1.3)

N_{j}(\mathbb{x}):=\sum_{a\in[m]}\mathbb{I}(x_{a}<X_{a,j}),\quad 2\leq j\leq n,

are
equidistributed independent sums of independent terms
. Therefore, introducing
C_{m}=[0,1]^{m}
,
the probability of a Condorcet winner is given by

(1.4)

Q_{m,n}=n\int_{\mathbb{x}\in C_{m}}\mathbb{P}^{\,n-1}(N_{2}(\mathbb{x})>m/2)\,d\mathbb{x},

as there can be at most one Condorcet winner. Probabilistically,

Q_{m,n}=n\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbb{P}^{n-1}(N_{2}(\mathbb{x})>m/2)\big|_{\mathbb{x}=\mathbb{X}}\bigr],

where
\mathbb{X}=\{X_{a}\}_{a\in[m]}
,
X_{a}
being independent
[0,1]
-Uniforms.

Note.
Of course, we could have chosen any other continuous distribution instead of the
[0,1]
-Uniform. But the uniform distribution is perfect for the asymptotic analysis. Here is one immediate benefit of our choice:
\tilde{X}_{i,j}:=1-X_{i,j}
are also
[0,1]
-Uniforms, and
\tilde{X}_{a,b}<\tilde{X}_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
if and only if
X_{a,b}>X_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}
. This implies that
Q_{m,n}
is also the probability of a Condorcet “loser”, i. e. a candidate who gets at most
m/2
votes in a pairwise contest with every other candidate.

For certainty, we consider the case
m=2k-1
. In this case, Condorcet winner is a candidate who gets at least
k
votes in every pairwise contest.

To analyze an asymptotic behavior of the integral (
1.4
), we will use the following classic theorem by Esseen
[
1
]
, cf. Feller
[
2
]
. Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{2k-1}
be independent random variables such that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{a}]=0
,
\mathbb{E}[Y_{a}^{2}]<\infty
,
\mathbb{E}[|Y_{a}|^{3}]<\infty
. Denote
s_{2k-1}^{2}=\sum_{a}\mathbb{E}[Y_{a}^{2}]
,
r_{2k-1}=\sum_{a}\mathbb{E}[|Y_{a}|^{3}]
. Then

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\sum_{a}Y_{a}}{s_{m}}\geq y\right)-G(y)\right|\leq\frac{6t_{2k-1}}{s_{2k-1}^{3}},\quad G(y):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{y}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}/2}\,dz.

In the case of
N_{j}(\mathbb{x})
, we have
Y_{a}=Y_{a}(\mathbb{x})=\mathbb{I}(x_{a}<X_{a,j})-(1-x_{a})
, with
\mathbb{E}[Y_{a}]=0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{a}^{2}]

\displaystyle=x_{a}(1-x_{a}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Y_{a}|^{3}]

\displaystyle=(1-x_{a})x_{a}^{3}+x_{a}(1-x_{a})^{3}\leq x_{a}(1-x_{a}).

So,
\sigma^{2}_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})=\sum_{a}x_{a}(1-x_{a})
, and

\frac{t_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})}{s_{2k-1}^{3}(\mathbb{x})}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})}{\sigma_{2k-1}^{3}(\mathbb{x})}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})}.

Therefore, denoting
y(\mathbb{x}):=\frac{k-\sum_{a}(1-x_{a})}{\sigma_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})}
,

\mathbb{P}(N_{2}(\mathbb{x})\geq k)=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{N_{2}(\mathbb{x})-\sum_{a}(1-x_{a})}{\sigma_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})}\geq y(\mathbb{x})\right)\\
=G(y(\mathbb{x}))+O(\sigma_{2k-1}^{-1}(\mathbb{x})),

uniformly over
\mathbb{x}\in C_{2k-1}
. Consequently, for a fixed constant
\alpha
,

\mathbb{P}^{\,n-1}(N_{2}(\mathbb{x})\geq k)\!=G^{n-1}(y(\mathbb{x}))\\
+O\!\left(\sum_{\nu=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{\nu}G^{n-\nu}(y(\mathbb{x}))\alpha^{\nu}\sigma^{-\nu}_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})\!\right).

Neglecting
G^{n-\nu}y(\mathbb{x})
, and integrating over
C_{2k-1}
, we get

(1.5)

n^{-1}Q_{2k-1,n}=\mathbb{E}[G^{n-1}(y(\mathbb{X}))]\\
+O\!\left(\sum_{\nu=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{\nu}\alpha^{\nu}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\sigma^{-\nu}_{2k-1}(\mathbb{X})\bigr]\!\right).

Lemma 1.2
.

For
\nu\ll k
, we have

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\sigma_{2k-1}^{-\nu}(\mathbb{X})\bigr]=O((c/k)^{\nu/2}),\quad c>e.

Proof.

Recall that
\sigma_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})=\left(\sum_{a}x_{a}(1-x_{a})\right)^{1/2}
, thus it is zero at all
2^{2k-1}
corners of
C_{2k-1}
. For
A\subseteq[2k-1]
, let
C_{2k-1}(A)\!=\!\{\mathbb{x}\in C_{2k-1}\!:\!A=\{a\in[2k-1]:x_{a}\leq 1/2\}\}
. For
\mathbb{x}\in C_{2k-1}(A)
, we have

\sum_{a}x_{a}(1-x_{a})\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in A}x_{a}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in A^{c}}(1-x_{a}).

So,

\int\limits_{\mathbb{x}\in C_{2k-1}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sigma^{-\nu}_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})\,d\mathbb{x}\leq 2^{\nu/2}\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{A\subseteq[2k-1]}\,\int\limits_{\mathbb{x}\in C_{2k-1}(A)}\left(\sum_{a\in A}x_{a}+\sum_{a\in A^{c}}(1-x_{a})\right)^{-\nu/2}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!d\mathbb{x}\\

Switching to
\xi_{a}=2x_{a}
for
a\in A
and
\xi_{a}=2(1-x_{a})
for
a\in A^{c}
, and denoting
s(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\sum_{a}\xi_{a}
, we obtain

(1.6)

\int\limits_{\mathbb{x}\in C_{2k-1}}\!\!\!\!\sigma^{-\nu}_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})\,d\mathbb{x}\leq 2^{\nu/2}\!\!\sum_{A\subseteq[2k-1]}\!\!2^{-(2k-1)}\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in C_{2k-1}}\!\!\!\!\!s(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-\nu/2}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi}\\
=2^{\nu/2}\!\!\!\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in C_{2k-1}}\!\!\!\!s(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-\nu/2}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi},

as the number of subsets of
[2k-1]
is
2^{2k-1}
. It remains to upper bound the last integral.

The integral equals
\mathbb{E}[S_{2k-1}^{-\nu}]
, where
S_{2k-1}=\sum_{a\in 2k-1}X_{a}
. Consider first

C(1)=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in C_{2k-1}:s(\boldsymbol{\xi})\leq\beta k\}
,
\beta
to be chosen later. Since the density of

S_{2k-1}
is below
s^{2k-2}/(2k-2)!
, we see that

\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in C(1)}\!\!\!\!s(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-\nu/2}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi}\leq\frac{1}{(2k-2)!}\int_{0}^{\beta k}s^{2k-2-\nu/2}\,ds\\
=\frac{(\beta k)^{2k-1-\nu/2}}{(2k-2)!\cdot(2k-1-\nu/2)}=O\left((\beta k)^{-\nu/2}\left(\frac{e\beta}{2}\right)^{2k}\right),\\

since
\nu\ll k
and
(2k-1)!=\Theta\bigl(k^{1/2}((2k-1)/e)^{2k-1}\bigr)
. As

\int\limits_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in C_{2k-1}\setminus C(1)}\!\!\!\!s(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-\nu/2}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi}\leq(\beta k)^{-\nu/2},

we see that the integral over the whole
C_{2k-1}
is also of order at most
(\beta k)^{-\nu/2}
if we choose
\beta<2/e
.
Thus, the equation (
1.6
) yields

(1.7)

\int\limits_{\mathbb{x}\in C_{2k-1}}\sigma^{-\mu}_{2k-1}(\mathbb{x})\,d\mathbb{x}=O\Bigl(\left(\frac{2}{\beta k}\right)^{\nu/2}\Bigr).

∎

Next

Lemma 1.3
.

For
k\gg n^{4}
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[G^{n-1}(y(\mathbb{X}))\bigr]=\frac{1}{n}+O(nk^{-1/2}),

Proof.

By the definition,
y(\mathbb{X})=\frac{k-\sum_{a}(1-X_{a})}{\sqrt{\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})}}
.
Observe that the mean and the variance of the numerator are
1/2
and
(2k-1)/6
, and
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})\right]=(2k-1)/6
. So, intuitively
y(\mathbb{X})
is asymptotically
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, the standard normal with zero mean and unit variance.
To prove this crucial fact, introduce

(1.8)

y^{*}(\mathbb{X}):=\frac{k-\sum_{a}(1-X_{a})}{\sqrt{\frac{2k-1}{6}}}.

Then,

(1.9)

y(\mathbb{X})-y^{*}(\mathbb{x})=\left(k-\sum_{a}(1-X_{a})\right)Z_{k}(\mathbb{X}),\\
Z_{k}(\mathbb{X})=\frac{\sqrt{\frac{2k-1}{6}}-\sqrt{\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})}}{\sqrt{\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})}\sqrt{\frac{2k-1}{6}}}\\
=\frac{\frac{2k-1}{6}-\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})}{\sqrt{\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2k-1}{6}}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})}+\sqrt{\frac{2k-1}{6}}\right)},\\

so that

|Z_{k}(\mathbb{X})|\leq\frac{\left|\frac{2k-1}{6}-\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})\right|}{\sqrt{\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})}\frac{2k-1}{6}}.

Then, by Cauchy-Schwartz inequality,

(1.10)

\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{X})]\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[\left(\frac{2k-1}{6}-\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})\right)^{4}\right]\\
\times E^{1/2}\left[\left(\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})\right)^{-2}\left(\frac{2k-1}{6}\right)^{-4}\right]\\

Now,
\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})
is the sum of the
bounded
independent random variables, with zero mean, and variance equal
(2k-1)\text{Var}(X(1-X)
. Therefore

\frac{\frac{2k-1}{6}-\sum_{a}X_{a}(1-X_{a})}{(2k-1)^{1/2}\text{Var}^{1/2}(X(1-X))}

converges,
with all the central moments
, to
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, meaning that the top expectation in (
1.10
) is of order
k^{2}
.

This stronger version of the central limit theorem can be proved by using the Laplace transform of the distribution of
X_{a}(1-X_{a})
, rather than the characteristic function, i. e. the Fourier transform of that distribution.

As for the bottom expectation in (
1.10
), by the inequality (
1.7
), it is of order
k^{-6}
.
Hence
\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{X})]=O(k^{-2})
. So, applying Cauchy-Schwartz inequality to the top RHS in (
1.9
),
we get a crucial estimate

(1.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[|y(\mathbb{X})-y^{*}(\mathbb{X})|\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left[\left(k-\sum_{a}(1-X_{a})\right)^{2}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}^{1/2}[Z_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{X})]

\displaystyle=O(k^{1/2}k^{-1})=O(k^{-1/2}).

Note.
In the preceding inequalities we could have used a more general H’́older inequality
\mathbb{E}[|UV|]\leq\mathbb{E}^{1/p}[|U|^{p}]\mathbb{E}^{1/q}[|U|^{q}]
,
(1/p+1/q=1)
, picking up the best
p
and
q
in each case. We tried, but the
O(k^{-1/2})
bound did not budge.

To proceed, define

y^{**}(\mathbb{X})=\frac{(2k-1)/2-\sum_{a}(1-X_{a})}{\sqrt{\frac{2k-1}{6}}};

clearly
|y^{**}(\mathbb{X})-y^{*}(\mathbb{X})|=O(k^{-1/2})
.
Since the mean and the variance of
\sum_{a}(1-X_{a})
are
(2k-1)/2
and
\frac{2k-1}{6}
, by the central limit theorem
y^{**}(\mathbb{X})
is asymptotically
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. In other words,
introducing
F_{y{**}(\mathbb{X})}(y)
, the distribution function of
y^{**}(\mathbb{X})
, we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}F_{y{**}(\mathbb{X})}(y)=F(y):=1-G(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-z^{2}/2}\,dz.

Moreover, since
\mathbb{E}[|X-1/2|^{3}]<\infty
, by a general theorem (Feller
[
2
]
, Ch. XVI, Section 4): uniformly for all
y
,

F_{y^{**}(\mathbb{X})}(y)=F(y)+\frac{\mu_{3}}{6\sigma^{3}k^{1/2}}(1-y^{2})\frac{e^{-y^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+o(k^{-1/2}).

in our case,
\sigma^{2}=1/6
,
\mu_{3}=0
, i.e.
F_{y{**}(\mathbb{X})}(y)=F(y)+o(k^{-1/2})
. In fact, since the characteristic function
\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{iu(X-1/2)}\bigr]
is square integrable, the density
p_{y^{**}}(y)
of
y^{**}(\mathbb{X})
satisfies

p_{y^{**}}(y)=\frac{e^{-y^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}-\frac{\mu_{3}}{6\sigma^{3}k^{1/2}}(y^{3}-3y)\frac{e^{-y^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+o(k^{-1/2})\\
=\frac{e^{-y^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+o(k^{-1/2}),

whence
p_{y^{**}}(y)
is uniformly bounded for all
k
and
y
; (
[
2
]
, Ch. XVI, Section 2). So, recalling that
|y^{**}(\mathbb{X})-y^{*}(\mathbb{X})|\leq ck^{-1/2}
, we have

F_{y^{*}(\mathbb{X})}(y)=\mathbb{P}(y^{*}(\mathbb{X})\leq y)\leq F_{y^{**}(\mathbb{X})}(y+ck^{-1/2})\\
=F_{y^{**}(\mathbb{X})}(y)+\int_{y}^{y+ck^{-1/2}}p_{y^{**}(\mathbb{X})}(z)\,dz)\\
=F_{y^{**}(\mathbb{X})}(y)+O(k^{-1/2}).

Likewise,
F_{y^{*}(\mathbb{X})}(y)\geq F_{y^{**}(\mathbb{X})}(y)+O(k^{-1/2})
, so that
F_{y^{*}(\mathbb{X})}(y)=F_{y^{**}(\mathbb{X})}(y)+O(k^{-1/2})
, enabling us to conclude that

F_{y^{*}(\mathbb{X})}(y)=F(y)+O(k^{-1/2})
,
uniformly for all
y
and
k
.
Therefore

\mathbb{E}\bigl[G^{n-1}(y^{*}(\mathbb{X}))\bigr]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G^{n-1}(y)\,dF_{y^{*}(\mathbb{X})}(y)\\
=G^{n-1}(y)F_{y^{*}(\mathbb{X})}(y)\big|_{-\infty}^{\infty}-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(G^{n-1}(y))^{\prime}F_{y^{*}(\mathbb{X})}(y)\,dy\\
=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl[(G^{n-1}(y))^{\prime}(F(y)+O(k^{-1/2}))\bigr]\,dy\\
=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(G^{n-1}(y))^{\prime}F(y)\,dy+O\bigl[k^{-1/2}\bigl(G^{n-1}(-\infty)-G^{n-1}(\infty)\bigr)\bigr]\\
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G^{n-1}(y)\frac{e^{-y^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dy+O(k^{-1/2})\\
=\frac{G^{n}(y)}{n}\big|_{\infty}^{-\infty}+O(k^{-1/2})=\frac{1}{n}+O(k^{-1/2}).

Finally,

\big|G^{n-1}(y(\mathbb{X}))-G^{n-1}(y^{*}(\mathbb{X}))\big|\leq(n-1)\big|G(y(\mathbb{X}))-G(y^{*}(\mathbb{X}))\big|\\
=O\bigl(n|y(\mathbb{X})-y^{*}(\mathbb{X})|).

So, using
\mathbb{E}\bigl[|y^{*}(\mathbb{X})-y(\mathbb{X})|\bigr]=O(k^{-1/2})
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\bigl[G^{n-1}(y(\mathbb{X}))\bigr]=O(nk^{-1/2})+\mathbb{E}\bigl[G^{n-1}(y^{*}(\mathbb{X}))\bigr]=\frac{1}{n}+O(nk^{-1/2}).

∎

Combining Lemma
1.2
and Lemma
1.3
, we transform (
1.5
) into

\ n^{-1}Q_{2k-1,n}=\frac{1}{n}+O(nk^{-1/2})+O\!\left(\sum_{\nu=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{\nu}\left(\alpha\sqrt{\frac{c}{k}}\right)^{\nu}\right)\\
=\frac{1}{n}+O(nk^{-1/2})+O\biggl(\left(1+\alpha\sqrt{\frac{c}{k}}\right)^{n-1}-1\biggr)=\frac{1}{n}+O(nk^{-1/2}).\\

Thus
Q_{2k-1,n}=1+O(n^{2}k^{-1/2})
. The proof of Theorem
1.1
is complete.
