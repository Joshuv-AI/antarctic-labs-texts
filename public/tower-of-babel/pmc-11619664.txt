Porphyromonas gingivalis inducing autophagy-related biological dysfunction in alveolar epithelial cells: an in vitro study
Authors: Qian Zhao, Xueyuan Wang, Wenyan Liu, Huan Tian, Hongjia Yang, Zuomin Wang, Zhiqiang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a respiratory disease with high morbidity and mortality. Notably, the pathogenesis and progression of COPD are related to lung infection, inflammatory response, and biological dysfunction in alveolar epithelial cells. Studies also found that periodontitis is an independent risk factor for COPD. The inhalation of periodontal pathogens into the respiratory system is the most common method for periodontal pathogens to promote the development of COPD. Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis), the keystone pathogen in periodontitis, has been found to migrate to the lungs, triggering inflammatory reactions and causing decreased lung function. However, the impact of P. gingivalis infection on the biological function of alveolar epithelial cells remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of P. gingivalis infection on the biological functions of alveolar epithelial cells.MethodsMouse alveolar epithelial cells (MLE-12) were co-cultured with P. gingivalis and treated with autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) or LC3 siRNA in vitro. MTT assay and EdU staining were used to detect cell viability, and the TUNEL assay kit and Annexin V-FITC/PI method were used to detect cell apoptosis. Western blot was used to detect autophagic markers LC3 and P62, and mRFP-GFP-LC3 was used to observe autophagic flux.ResultsP. gingivalis inhibited the viability of alveolar epithelial cells in a dose- and time-dependent manner. P. gingivalis also promoted autophagy and apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells in a dose-dependent manner. Interestingly, we found that inhibiting autophagy using CQ or silencing LC3 with siRNA significantly reduced cell apoptosis and viability damage induced by P. gingivalis. Thus, these data indicated the synergistic effect of autophagy in P. gingivalis-induced biological dysfunction of alveolar epithelial cells.ConclusionP. gingivalis infection can cause biological dysfunction of alveolar epithelial cells, manifested as decreased cell viability, increased autophagy and apoptosis. Notably, the up-regulation of autophagy induced by P. gingivalis plays a synergistic role in this dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05253-y.

Introduction

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is a highly prevalent chronic respiratory disease with pathological changes involving the bronchi and alveoli [1]. Importantly, the pathogenesis of COPD is heterogeneous. Although cigarette smoke is the greatest COPD risk factor, emerging evidence suggests that the biological dysfunction of alveolar epithelial cells is implicated in disease pathogenesis [2]. Studies have demonstrated that excessive apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells can lead to the destruction of lung tissues and a reduction in host defenses, gradually leading to a decline in lung function and the progression of COPD [3–5]. Moreover, aberrant autophagy can induce abnormal alveolar epithelial cell function and cell death, contributing to COPD’s pathogenesis [6]. Therefore, abnormal biological function of alveolar epithelial cells may promote the occurrence and progression of COPD.

Periodontitis is a chronic infectious disease that begins with the invasion of periodontal pathogens and causes the destruction of tooth-supporting tissues [7]. Epidemiological and clinical studies have shown that periodontitis is also a risk factor for COPD [8]. Due to the direct connection between the oropharynx and the respiratory system, it is widely accepted that aspiration of periodontal pathogens present in oropharyngeal secretions into the respiratory system is the most common method by which periodontal pathogens promote the development of COPD [9]. Furthermore, the virulence factors produced by periodontal pathogens can also transfer to the lungs through aspiration and blood, thus exacerbating lung damage and the progression of COPD [10].

Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis), one of the most critical periodontal pathogens, has been detected in the subgingival plaque of approximately 91.5% of patients with periodontitis [11]. Additionally, the prevalence of P. gingivalis in patients with COPD is significantly higher than in patients without COPD, and the content of P. gingivalis in the subgingival plaques of patients with COPD is negatively associated with lung function [12]. Animal studies have also demonstrated that in mice infected with P. gingivalis in the periodontium, P. gingivalis can reach the lung tissue and stimulate inflammatory response in the lungs [13, 14]. However, the effect of P. gingivalis infection on the biological function of alveolar epithelial cells remains unclear. Although P. gingivalis typically thrives in anaerobic environments and has limited survival in the respiratory tract, understanding its effects on alveolar epithelial cells can shed light on potential pathways of respiratory pathology.

This study investigates whether P. gingivalis infection, even in its inactivated form, can affect alveolar epithelial cell functions through mechanisms of autophagy and apoptosis. By comparing the changes in viability and apoptosis rate of alveolar epithelial cells before and after inhibition of cell autophagy, the role of autophagy on P. gingivalis-induced cellular biological functional changes was also analyzed. Importantly, the research results provide evidence for the potential impact of P. gingivalis infection on COPD.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The effect of P. gingivalis on the biological function of alveolar epithelial cells

MLE-12 alveolar epithelial cells were cultured in vitro and stimulated with P. gingivalis at 1, 10, and 100 multiplicity of infection (MOI) [15]. MLE-12 cells were cultured separately for the control group. MTT assay was used to detect cell viability (time points: 24, 48, and 72 h); EdU staining was used to detect cell proliferation; TUNEL staining and Flow cytometry were used to detect cell apoptosis; Western blot was used to detect autophagy proteins LC3 and P62; mRFP-GFP-LC3 was used to observe autophagic flux.

Screening of LC3 siRNA (si LC3)

The si LC3 (RIBOBIO, China) knockdown fragment and si LC3 negative control were transfected into MLE-12 cells, and then the cells were collected for qRT-PCR detection of LC3 expression. We found that the transfection of the LC3 knockdown fragment significantly reduced LC3 expression, with the si LC3-2 fragment having the best effect (Fig. 1). Therefore, we chose the si LC3-2 fragment for further experiments.

Preparation of inactivated P. gingivalis

Heat-inactivated P. gingivalis was used to assess whether its components alone can induce cellular responses independent of active infection. The freeze-dried P. gingivalis ATCC33277 (Beijing Beina Chuanglian Biotechnology Research Institute) was dissolved in sterile physiological saline, and the sterile ring was picked and divided into zones and planted on CDC solid culture medium. After 5–7 days of cultivation under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C (80% N2, 10% H2, 10% CO2), morphological identification of the cultured bacteria was performed. P. gingivalis was subcultured on a solid medium to form a single colony and then transferred to a liquid medium for anaerobic cultivation at 37 °C for 2 days. Subsequently, the cultured P. gingivalis liquid culture medium was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 20 min, the supernatant was discarded, and an appropriate amount of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was resuspended. The OD value of the culture medium was measured using a spectrophotometer, and the bacterial concentration was calculated by referring to the bacterial concentration standard curve. Furthermore, the bacteria were inactivated by heating at 60 °C for 1 h and stored at -80 °C until use.

In vitro culture of alveolar epithelial cells

The MLE-12 cell line (mouse alveolar epithelial cell HTX1967) was provided by the Respiratory Research Institute of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital, affiliated with Capital Medical University. After thawing in a 37 °C water bath, the frozen cells were transferred to a sterile test tube in a complete culture medium (DMEM medium, 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin). Following centrifugation, the cells were transferred to a culture dish and cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. The culture medium was removed when the cells were cultured to about 80 − 90% fusion, and the adherent cells were digested using 0.25% trypsin. A complete culture medium was added to terminate digestion when the cells were digested until round without floating. After centrifugation, the cells were evenly dispersed into three culture dishes, moved to 37 °C, and cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were selected, centrifuged after digestion, and resuspended with a serum-free cell cryopreservation solution to obtain a cell concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Furthermore, cells were transferred to a 1.5 mL cell cryopreservation tube and placed in a refrigerator at -80 °C or liquid nitrogen for long-term storage.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assay

Total RNA was extracted from the MLE-12 cells using TRnaZol Reagent (Vazyme, China). Subsequently, we reverse-transcribed RNA into cDNA using the HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme, China). Following this step, qRT-PCR was performed. The total volume was 20 μL, including 10 μL 2 × ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China), 0.4 μL forward primer, 0.4 μL reverse primer and 2 μL cDNA. The relative expression of mRNA was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, using ACTB as an internal reference control. The primers used in qRT-PCR were as follows: Mus ACTB-F: 5’-GTCCCTCACCCTCCCAAAAG-3’; Mus ACTB-R: 5’-GCTGCCTCAACACCTCAACCC-3’; Mus LC3-F: 5’-TGATCATCGAGCGCTACAAG-3’; Mus LC3-R: 5’-GGATGATCTTGACCAACTCG-3’.

MTT assay

The MTT assay, a colorimetric method measuring mitochondrial activity, was employed to assess the viability of MLE-12 cells. After the alveolar epithelial cells grew to 80% confluence, they were digested with 0.25% trypsin and collected by centrifugation. We diluted the cell density with culture medium to 3 × 104 cells/mL, 100 μL/well onto a 96-well plate. The following morning, intervention was conducted according to the experimental groups and continued in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. We added 10 μL MTT solution (5 mg/mL) to each well at 24, 48, and 72 h with the culture medium in a volume ratio of 1:10 and continued to incubate for 4 h. Then, we discarded the liquid from the orifice plate, added 150 μL DMSO, read the absorbance at 595 nm, and calculated the cell survival rate.

EdU staining

We preheated the EdU (C0071S, Beyotime, China) working solution at 37 °C for 20 μM. We added an equal volume to a 6-well plate so that the final concentration of EdU in the 6-well plate was 10 μM. We continued to incubate the cells for 2 h. Following the EdU labeling of cells, we removed the culture medium and added 1 mL of fixed solution at room temperature for 15 min. After washing the cells three times, we incubated each well with 1 mL of transparent solution at room temperature for 15 min. Following an additional 2 washes, we added 0.5 mL of Click reaction solution to each well and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. After a third round of washing the cells, we added 1 mL of 1X Hoechst 33342 solution to each well and incubated the samples at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. We then performed fluorescence detection after washing the cells three times.

TUNEL assay

To explore the effect of P. gingivalis on apoptosis in MLE-12 cells, a TUNEL assay was performed to detect apoptotic cells by enzymatic labeling of DNA strand breaks with fluorescein dUTP and TdT. The TUNEL Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGen Biotech, China) was used. We soaked the naturally dried cell samples in 4% paraformaldehyde fixative, fixed them at room temperature for 30 min, and rinsed them three times with PBS. The sample pieces were immersed in 1% Triton X-100 permeate solution, promoted at room temperature for 5 min, and rinsed three times with PBS. We dripped 100 μL of DNase I reaction solution onto each sample slide, treated them at 37 °C for 30 min, and rinsed them three times with PBS. We added 50 μL of TdT enzyme reaction solution dropwise to each sample and reacted at 37 °C in the dark for 60 min. We dripped 50 μL Streptavidin Fluorescein labeling solution onto each sample for reaction in a dark environment at 37 °C for 30 min. DAPI staining solution was used to re-stain the nucleus, followed by fluorescence microscopy detection.

Flow cytometry

After the cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI, the apoptosis rate was detected using flow cytometry. MLE-12 cells were prepared into a single-cell suspension, and the cell concentration was detected and adjusted with a cell counting plate. The apoptosis was detected with an Annexin V-FITC cell apoptosis detection kit (KeyGen Biotech, China). The cells were resuspended in 500μL binding buffer, mixed with 5μL annexin V FITC in the dark, and added with 5μL propidium iodide. After reacting at room temperature for 10 min in the dark, the cells were placed on ice for testing and then put into a flow cytometer (BD, USA) within 1 h.

Western blot

The total protein of the cells was extracted, and the total protein concentration was detected using the BCA Protein Quantitative Kit (P0010, Beyotime, China). Following protein denaturation and sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), the separated proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Following transfer, membranes were incubated with corresponding primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight as follows: LC3 (ab192890, Abcam) (1:2000), P62 (ab109012, Abcam) (1:10000), and β-actin (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech) (1:10000). After being washed three times with TBST, the transferred membranes were incubated with secondary antibody HRP-labeled Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (A0216, Beyotime, China) (1:1000) at 37 °C for 2 h. Finally, these membranes were rewashed with TBST and detected using a fully automated chemiluminescence analyzer (Tanon, China).

Autophagic flux analysis

For the detection of autophagosome and autolysosome, cells were pre-cultured in 6-well plates and transiently transfected with 20 MOI of mRFP-GFP-LC3 adenovirus (HB-AP210001, Hanbio, China). The fluorescent image was captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Japan) 24 h after infection.

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between different groups was determined by one-way-ANOVA using GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

P. gingivalis inhibited MLE-12 cell viability

We treated MLE-12 cells with increasing concentrations of P. gingivalis at different time points and then evaluated cell viability. Higher doses and prolonged exposure to P. gingivalis led to a marked decrease in alveolar epithelial cell viability, as measured by the MTT assay (Fig. 2A). In addition, the EdU staining results also showed that following co-culturing with P. gingivalis at 1, 10, and 100 MOI for 24 h, the proliferation of cells was significantly decreased compared with uninfected control (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B).

P. gingivalis promoted MLE-12 cell autophagy

To explore the relationship between P. gingivalis infection and the cell autophagy, we examined the expression of LC3 and P62. Exposure to higher concentrations of P. gingivalis led to a marked upregulation of LC3-II/LC3-I (p < 0.001) and a marked downregulation of P62 (p < 0.01 or 0.001) in alveolar epithelial cells, as assessed by Western blot (Fig. 3A). Following transfection with mRFP-GFP-LC3, the number of fluorescent LC3-positive spots in cells was significantly increased with higher levels of exposure to P. gingivalis (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). Obviously, P. gingivalis induced MLE-12 cell autophagy in a dose-dependent manner.

P. gingivalis upregulated MLE-12 cell apoptosis

Cells incubated with 100 MOI P. gingivalis for 24 h showed a 21.81% apoptosis rate, as measured by TUNEL assay (Fig. 4A). The apoptosis rate detected by flow cytometry under the same exposure to P. gingivalis was 23.48%, generally similar to that of the TUNEL assay (Fig. 4B). Additionally, cell apoptosis rates were significantly higher in infected cells compared to controls (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4A, B). In summary, P. gingivalis induced a dose-dependent increase in alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis.

CQ or silencing LC3 with siRNA inhibited P. gingivalis-induced MLE-12 cell autophagy

To further analyze the relationship between autophagy, cell viability, and apoptosis, we observed the effects of autophagy inhibitor CQ or silencing LC3 with siRNA (si LC3) on the autophagy level of cells. We found that when P. gingivalis infected cells were co-cultured with CQ or si LC3, LC3-II/LC3-I was downregulated (p < 0.001) and P62 was upregulated (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5A). Moreover, the number of fluorescent LC3-positive spots that indicated autophagy was significantly decreased (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B) compared with the Pg + si LC3 NC group. These experimental results suggest that CQ or silencing LC3 can inhibit cell autophagy induced by P. gingivalis.

Inhibiting autophagy with CQ or silencing LC3 with siRNA improved P. gingivalis-induced viability decrease and apoptosis increase in MLE-12 cells

To investigate the effect of autophagy regulation on the biological function of infected cells, we tested the cell viability and apoptosis after adding CQ or si LC3. Our results demonstrated that adding CQ or si LC3 significantly increased the viability of infected cells (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6A). In contrast, the apoptosis rate of infected cells decreased to 14.45% and 16.64%, respectively, significantly lower than that (22.54%) in the Pg + si LC3 NC group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6B, C). To summarize, inhibition of autophagy by CQ or silencing LC3 with siRNA can improve the reduced cell viability and increased apoptosis induced by P. gingivalis.

Discussion

Up to now, research on the effects of P. gingivalis on alveolar epithelial cell biological function has been limited. In this study, the primary pathogen of periodontitis, P. gingivalis, was co-cultured with mouse alveolar epithelial cells. The effects of P. gingivalis on cell viability, autophagy, and apoptosis were observed. Moreover, CQ or LC3 siRNA was used to inhibit cell autophagy, and the changes in cell viability and apoptosis were determined before and after inhibiting autophagy. The results directly demonstrated that P. gingivalis causes alveolar epithelial cell biological dysfunction by inhibiting cell viability and promoting cell autophagy and apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, the inhibition of P. gingivalis-activated LC3, or the addition of CQ leading to decreased autophagy, could improve cell viability and inhibit cell apoptosis (Fig. 7).

P. gingivalis is anaerobic and unlikely to survive in the human lower respiratory tract [18]. Therefore, we investigated the effect of heat-inactivated P. gingivalis on the biological function of alveolar epithelial cells in this study. After infection with alveolar epithelial cells by P. gingivalis, we measured the viability of MLE-12 cells using the MTT assay. As time and dose increased, the cell proliferation level gradually decreased compared with the control group, demonstrating that P. gingivalis infection would damage the vitality of alveolar epithelial cells. This finding is consistent with previous research results, which indicated that outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) produced by P. gingivalis could induce abnormal changes in alveolar cell morphology and thus affect cell vitality [19]. Previous studies have also shown that P. gingivalis can promote apoptosis of epithelial cells, such as brain and vascular endothelial cells [20, 21]. In this study, P. gingivalis infection significantly increased the fluorescence brightness by TUNEL assay. Additionally, the cell apoptosis rates detected by flow cytometry and TUNEL assay were similar, consistent with previous research results that P. gingivalis OMVs destroyed the alveolar epithelial cell barrier system and induced cell apoptosis [19]. The study also found that P. gingivalis disrupts epithelial cell adhesion by degrading integrin β1 and inducing caspase-independent mitochondrial apoptosis [22]. In the early stage of infection with P. gingivalis, alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis was temporarily suppressed, promoting P. gingivalis proliferation in cells [23]. However, the later stages of increased pathogen replication lead to insufficient intracellular nutrients to meet the pathogen’s nutritional needs, resulting in cell apoptosis [24]. Therefore, our present results indicate that even inactivated P. gingivalis still has cytotoxic effects on alveolar epithelial cells, leading to apparent biological dysfunction of cells.

Autophagy is a vacuolar process of cytoplasmic degradation by lysosomes to maintain cell homeostasis and organelle self-renew [25]. LC3 and P62 are autophagy markers, and the modification of LC3 is necessary for autophagosome formation [26]. In this study, it was found that in P. gingivalis infected alveolar epithelial cells, autophagy marker LC3 was overexpressed, and the percentage of cells containing LC3-positive autophagosome was significantly increased, suggesting that autophagy in alveolar epithelial cells was involved in P. gingivalis-induced biological dysfunction of cells. LC3 is divided into cytoplasmic type LC3-I and membrane type LC3-II. When the level of autophagy increases, LC3-I in the cytoplasm undergoes a ubiquitination reaction and combines with phosphatidylethanolamine to convert into LC3-II distributed on the membrane of autophagic bodies. Hence, the ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I increases [27]. P62 can bind to LC3-II to form a complex and transfer the protein to be degraded into autophagic lysosomes. With the degradation of proteins in autophagosomes, P62 will gradually decrease. Thus, the level of P62 protein is inversely proportional to autophagic activity [28]. In this study, when alveolar epithelial cells were co-cultured with P. gingivalis, the LC3-II/LC3-I values of MLE-12 cells significantly increased, and the expression level of P62 protein significantly decreased compared to the control group. These findings further suggest that P. gingivalis infection increased alveolar epithelial cell autophagy levels.

The relationship between autophagy and apoptosis remains debatable. When cells are in pathological conditions, autophagy activation can either increase or decrease apoptosis [29, 30]. One study found that specific suppression of LC3 by siRNA effectively downregulated the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and significantly increased the apoptosis in HUVEC upon stimulation with P. gingivalis so that the autophagy may protect HUVEC from P. gingivalis-induced apoptosis [15]. However, other studies have found that lipopolysaccharide (LPS) can activate the expression of the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) in alveolar epithelial cells, activating autophagy and promoting cell apoptosis. Inhibiting autophagy with 3-MA can reduce LPS-induced cell apoptosis and lung damage [31]. The different effects of autophagy on cell apoptosis may be related to cell types and various states of autophagy. Recent studies have proposed that when P. gingivalis invades non-phagocytic cells, the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes is impaired due to lysosome efflux, and P. gingivalis survives and replicates in these double-membrane autophagosome vacuoles [32]. Based on these data, we hypothesize that P. gingivalis infection of alveolar epithelial cells may induce a “defective” autophagy, which promotes the formation of autophagic vesicles in cells, allowing bacteria to hide in these membrane structures to seek survival and harm lung cells. Therefore, our findings suggest that P. gingivalis induces autophagy in alveolar epithelial cells, which may initially serve as a protective mechanism but eventually leads to cell viability damage and apoptosis.

There are some limitations in this study. This study’s use of murine alveolar cells limits the generalizability of the findings to human biology. Future studies could employ primary human alveolar cells or organoid models to verify these results. Moreover, the current study only investigated the relationship between P. gingivalis-induced autophagy and apoptosis at one time point. It was reported that autophagy had various effects on apoptosis at different time points [33]. Future studies could investigate autophagy and apoptosis at different time intervals to reveal the temporal dynamics of these pathways in response to P. gingivalis infection, providing a deeper understanding of the sequence of cellular event.

Conclusions

This study highlights that P. gingivalis induces autophagy and apoptosis in alveolar epithelial cells, with autophagy appearing to facilitate apoptosis and cellular dysfunction over time. Modulating autophagy may therefore be a promising strategy for reducing P. gingivalis-induced cell damage. Targeting autophagy pathways may help improve cell viability and protect against the damaging effects of P. gingivalis on alveolar epithelial cells.
