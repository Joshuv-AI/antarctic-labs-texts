What is the meaning of interpregnancy interval after caesarean section for women planning further pregnancies: a mixed method systematic review protocol
Authors: Alison M Canty, Hazel Keedle, Annemarie Hennessy, Angela Makris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCaesarean section rates are increasing globally. Caesarean section and repeat caesarean section increase risk in the next pregnancy. Risks associated with next pregnancy after caesarean section for both the mother and fetus are well documented including placentation anomalies, uterine rupture, small for gestational age neonates and preterm birth. Interpregnancy interval may play a role in reducing risk in the next pregnancy. It is therefore important to understand the meaning of interpregnancy interval to women planning further pregnancies after caesarean section. Appreciating interpregnancy interval from women’s perspective can improve the sharing of interpregnancy interval recommendations relevant to women for informing their decision making.MethodsThe review will include all studies exploring women’s knowledge, understanding and decision making when planning another pregnancy after caesarean section and will follow the JBI methodology for mixed methods reviews using a convergent integrated approach, reported as per the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analysis (PRISMA). A search strategy will be used to find published and unpublished studies in CINHAL, Medline, Embase, Psycinfo and SCOPUS. No limitation will be placed on study type, language of publication, or date range. All studies will be screened against the inclusion criteria, all data extracted critically appraised for methodological quality using JBI tools by two independent reviewers. Integrated findings will be presented as line of action statements. Confidence in the textual synthesis will be presented in a table.DiscussionThis review aims to integrate research exploring the meaning of interpregnancy interval to women after a caesarean section when planning further pregnancies. Background

Caesarean section (CS) rates are increasing across the world. Betran et al [1] demonstrate an increasing worldwide trend from 1990 and this is projected to be 28.5% of births across the world and 45% in Australia by 2023 [2]. Current Australian data shows CS rates continue to rise from 32.3% in 2011 to 38.2% in 2021 [3]. The repeat CS rate in Australia in 2021 was 87% with the most common primary reason given being previous CS [3], adding to the overall burden of CS. Conversely, Norway has a CS rate reported at 12.7% in 1990 to 15.9% in 2018 [1] which is largely consistent with the World Health Organization (WHO) Statement on CS rates of 10% to 15% [4].

There is much literature reporting the risk to the next pregnancy for both women and their infant’s following a CS, repeat CS changes risk further. Interpregnancy interval (IPI) has a role to play in reducing risk to women and the fetus in a pregnancy following CS. Known risks associated with repeat CS include placenta praevia and placental accreta spectrum disorders (accreta, increta and percreta) [5] the incidence of which in middle- and high-income countries has been associated with rising CS rates [6, 7] and uterine rupture [5]. Further to risk posed by CS alone is IPI, which has been widely studied in the context of risk. A systematic review of CS morbidity found association with vaginal birth after caesarean (VBAC); reporting an increased risk of uterine rupture with an IPI of < 6 months (aOR 3.05; 95%CI, 1.36, 6.87) in comparison to an IPI of 18–59 months [8]. Furthermore, after all previous modes of birth uterine rupture in a Chinese population data study was found to have an aRR of 1.31 (95%CI 1.08, 1.40) with an IPI of 6 months and no significant adjusted risk at 18 months [9]. Moreover, composite of maternal morbidity and mortality was shown in a population-based study in Canada to have an increased risk aRR 1.18 (95%CI, 1.09–1.27) with an IPI of 6 months as compared to 18 months after all modes of birth [10].

Adding to confusion around IPI, variation is evident in the recommendation of IPI after any live birth. The recommendation by the WHO is 24 months [11] specifically in line with united nations international children’s emergency fund (UNICEF) & WHO breastfeeding recommendation of two years or beyond [12]. This varies from the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RANZCOG) best practice statement guidance of interdelivery interval of at least 18 months [13], the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) inter-delivery interval of less than 12 months [14] for women planning a vaginal birth after caesarean [13], in relation to the potential of increasing the risk of uterine rupture [14]. There is some evidence suggestive of a longer IPI reducing the risk of uterine rupture [15].

Risks associated with IPI for the neonate include preterm birth and small for gestational age (SGA) infants. Without specifying type of previous birth an IPI of approximately 6 month was shown to have an effect size of ≥ 1.2 for preterm birth in countries classified as very high on the United Nations Human Development Index (UNHDI) [16]. This was reinforced in a Canadian population study finding spontaneous preterm birth had an aRR of 1.59 (95%CI 1.56–1.2) with an IPI of 6 months [10]. An increased risk of low birth weight (LBW) or SGA infants and shorter IPIs have also been shown in several analysis [9, 10, 17]. LBW defined as < 2500gm and IPI < 6 months had an aOR of 1.44 [1.30, 1.61] and aOR 1.12 [1.08, 1.17] for > 6 months were found in one meta analysis [17]. Once again systematic review of studies in high resource settings found significant aORs for SGA infants, defined as less than the 10 centile, for IPIs approximately < 6 months [16]. While no significant difference was found in stillbirth in a systematic review of high resource settings [16]; conversely a meta-analysis including low- or middle-income countries found IPI after any birth was associated with stillbirth, random-effects OR of 1.35 [1.07, 1.71] [17]. Furthermore, a significant increased risk of early neonatal death was found both by Ahrens et al. 16 and Wendt et al [17]. Additionally, findings from a population-based study across 46 countries on perinatal death (from 28 weeks gestation to 7 days after birth) after an IPI of < 6 months was found to have an all-inclusive hazard ratio of 2.72 (95% CI 2.52–2.93) compared to 18 to 23 months and no significant difference in perinatal death between an IPI of 18 to23 months and 24 to 59 months [18].

Currently there is little focus on pregnancy planning after CS outside of knowledge of and access to available contraception. Understanding what IPI means to women after a CS, including knowledge of IPI recommendations and how these fit with their expected family size. Understanding if women’s perception of risk for their next pregnancy effects pregnancy planning, will work toward facilitating maternity care clinicians sharing relevant evidence-based information to inform pregnancy planning after CS.

Awareness of IPI as a risk factor amongst women surveyed in the UK has been shown as low [19]; additionally, women surveyed in Canada identified previous caesarean section as a preconception risk although IPI was not included in this survey [20]. Furthermore, an integrative review of perceived barriers or facilitators to VBAC identified knowledge and concern regarding risk, sociocultural belief and organizational health system approach to both negatively and positively affect planning birth after CS [21]. Adding to this, next birth planning is identified by Munro et al [22] as complex with knowledge sought either directly prior to or after a primary CS.

While there is much research addressing morbidity and mortality risk to both the woman and the fetus. What does all this mean to women? Pregnancy planning after CS is multifactorial and can include women’s birth experience, intended family size, maternal age, co-morbidity and unintended pregnancy. Currently there is limited evidence on women’s understanding of IPI after CS and the impact this has on pregnancy planning. In this convergent integrated review, we will explore women’s views on knowledge of IPI, acceptable and perceived risk when planning their next pregnancy after CS and how IPI factors into next pregnancy planning.

A preliminary search of PROSPERO was undertaken in which no systematic reviews on meaning (understanding) of IPI after CS to women were located.

Aim

The aim of this systematic review is to explore what interpregnancy interval means to women who have experienced a caesarean section in relation to their reproductive life plans. If inadequate data is found regarding IPI after CS the aim will then be broadened to: What interpregnancy interval means to women who have experienced a previous pregnancy and birth in relation to their reproductive life plans.

Methods and analysis

This systematic review will be conducted in line with the JBI methodology for mixed methods systematic reviews (MMSR) of evidence [23]. This protocol has been written following the PRISMA-P guidance [24, 25]. A convergent integrated approach has been chosen for this review as both qualitative and quantitative research methodologies can be addressed by the question [23, 26] (Table 1); What is the meaning of interpregnancy interval after caesarean section for women planning further pregnancies? This approach will bring together quantitative study findings and qualitative evidence [23] of the meaning of IPI to women, strengthening the usefulness of the findings.

Eligibility criteria

Population of interest

This review will consider studies that include women who have had a pregnancy or plan to have another pregnancy after a caesarean birth.

Phenomena of interest

This review aims to gain an insight into women’s knowledge, understanding or practice of birth to pregnancy interval (time from last birth to next pregnancy conception) or Interpregnancy interval (time spent not pregnant prior to the next pregnancy conception) [11].

Context

This review will consider all studies exploring pregnancy planning in the context of previous caesarean section. If less than three studies exploring pregnancy planning after CS are located the context will be broadened to include studies exploring pregnancy planning after a previous birth.

Outcome

This review will consider all publications and studies that included women’s knowledge of IPI, considerations for planning a next pregnancy and perception of risk after caesarean section.

Search strategy and information sources

The search strategy (Table 3) aims to locate published and unpublished studies. Development of the search strategy was undertaken with the assistance of a university librarian. An initial search of MEDLINE (Ovid) and EMBASE (Ovid) was undertaken finding literature on the topic of interest. Index terms from literature addressing the research aims were used to develop the full search strategy. The search strategy will be modified to suit each database, Medline, CINHAL, Embase, PsychINFO and SCOPUS. Reference lists of systematic reviews that include data relevant to the aims of this review protocol will be screened for additional studies. Unpublished studies and grey literature will be searched for via fully automated internet search using proprietary software.

Search strategy

Study records and selection of studies

All studies identified by systematic searching will be uploaded into EndNote 20, 2020 (Clarivate Analytics, PA, USA) [27] where duplicates will be removed. The review inclusion criteria will be used to pilot test title and abstract screening by two reviewers on a sample of records to assess consistency. Following confirmation of congruence in screening decisions, the remaining records will be screened by a single reviewer. After discarding by title and abstract, if less than three studies are found to answer the original question the review will then focus on the broader question of the meaning of IPI to women who have experiences a previous birth.

Studies that meet the inclusion criteria in title and abstract screening will then be sought for full text and imported into the JBI System for the Unified Management, Assessment and Review of Information (JBI SUMARI) [28]. Two or more reviewers will thoroughly assess the selected full text publications using the inclusion criteria. Full text articles that do not meet the inclusion criteria, will have the exclusion reason reported in the systematic review. Resolution of any disagreements that arise during selection of articles for review, will be reached through discussion by the reviewers or with an additional study team member. A PRISMA flow diagram [29], will be used in the reporting of this systematic review, including both search results and the process of inclusion.

Data collection

Studies selected for inclusion will have their methodological validity reviewed prior to inclusion. Methodological quality and credibility of qualitative data will be assessed using the standard JBI critical appraisal instruments for qualitative research [30] and for analytical cross sectional studies [31] (Appendix 1a and 1b). Two independent researchers will conduct critical appraisal of each of the selected studies. If data is missing or needs further explanation, authors of the papers will be contacted for available data to be included in analysis. The researchers will be blinded to each other’s appraisals; any lack of consensus will be discussed, if a resolution is not reached a third member of the study team will be consulted. Data extraction and where possible data synthesis will be undertaken for all included studies with results of critical appraisal reported in narrative form and in a table.

Data extraction and transformation

Data will be extracted from papers included in the review using the JBI mixed methods data extraction form following a convergent integrated approach [23] (Appendix 2) by two independent reviewers. The data will include cultural and geographic context, population, study methods and data address the objectives of IPI knowledge or meaning and pregnancy planning. Quantitative data extracted will consist of descriptive and/or inferential statistics. Qualitative data extracted will consist of themes and/or subthemes with their corresponding illustrations, these will be given a level of credibility as per JBI data extraction guidance of unequivocal, credible, or not supported [30]. Qualitative data that is rated as not supported will be excluded from synthesis of the data. If missing data or additional data is needed, a request will be put to the author of the paper. If the same data is found to be presented in more than one paper, this data will only be included once. Discussion will be entered into to resolve any disagreement, or a third study team member will be consulted. Transformation of quantitative data will then occur using textual or narrative interpretation and description that responds directly to the aim of this review.

Data synthesis and integration

Using the JBI convergent integrated approach for mixed methods systematic reviews [23]. Where relationships between the data are evident the qualitized data will be brought together with the qualitative data into categories, creating integrated findings as line of action statements.

Discussion

Following this MMSR protocol provides a clear guide to exploring the evidence of women’s interpretation of IPI after CS in their newfound context of risk. Publication of this protocol increases transparency in reporting of this MMSR following a JBI methodology. From the synthesised evidence of this MMSR, recommendations can be made addressing access to information and evidence-based resources for women. Results from this MMSR will be used in a co-design/co-create project. This project aims to bring together the integrated findings of the meaning of IPI to women after caesarean section and population outcome data. From this to design acceptable, accessible information resources for both birthing families and clinicians that work towards facilitating informed decision making.
