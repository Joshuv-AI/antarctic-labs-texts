Quantum Speedup for Nonreversible Markov Chains
Baptiste Claudon, Jean-Philip Piquemal, Pierre Monmarché

Abstract




Abstract

Quantum algorithms can potentially solve a handful of problems more efficiently than their classical counterparts. In that context, it has been discussed that Markov chains problems could be solved significantly faster using quantum computing. Indeed, previous work suggests that quantum computers could accelerate sampling from the stationary distribution of reversible Markov chains. However, in practice, certain physical processes of interest are nonreversible in the probabilistic sense and reversible Markov chains can sometimes be replaced by more efficient nonreversible chains targeting the same stationary distribution. This study uses modern quantum algorithmic techniques and Markov chain theory to sample from the stationary distribution of nonreversible Markov chains with faster worst-case runtime and without requiring the stationary distribution to be computed up to a multiplicative constant. Such an up-to-exponential quantum speedup goes beyond the predicted quadratic quantum acceleration for reversible chains and is likely to have a decisive impact on many applications ranging from statistics and machine learning to computational modeling in physics, chemistry, biology and finance.

.0.1
Introduction

Quantum algorithms recently gained attention due to their asymptotic advantage over the best known classical methods for certain problems
Shor 1994
;
Grover 1996
. Such speedups turned quantum computing into an active field of research. Monte Carlo simulations both offer a wide range of applications and promise to be accelerated by quantum algorithms
Akhalwaya et al. 2023
. The speedup is typically provided by the Quantum Amplitude Estimation (QAE) routine
Brassard et al. 2002
;
Grinko et al. 2021
. The routine requires access to samples from a target probability distribution
\pi
. Arguably, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is among the most popular methods to provide such samples
Dongarra and Sullivan 2000
. The present introductory paragraph uses common definitions, which are recalled in the Methods section
Methods
. In MCMC, we start by designing an ergodic Markov chain that converges to the sought distribution
\pi

Hastings 1970
. Performing sufficiently many steps of the chain yields an approximate sample from
\pi
. The main difficulty is that the mixing time, the minimum number of steps for the sample to be distributed according
\pi
, may be large. It is well-known that, to each reversible chain, we can associate a quantum walk with quadratically larger spectral gap
Szegedy 2004
. It is then possible to construct the reflection through the stationary distribution in the inverse spectral gap of this quantum walk (see for example
Claudon 2024
). Since the mixing time is of the order of the inverse spectral gap of the chain, the reflection can readily be used to provide a sample from
\pi
with quadratic speedup
Yoder et al. 2014
.

First and foremost, we are often interested in studying physical processes that are nonreversible in the probabilistic sense. Such processes may have a known stationary distribution, as for the underdamped Langevin dynamics, or not, as in the study of out-of-equilibrium systems in statistical physics
Bordenave et al. 2018
;
Blassel and Stoltz 2023
. In addition, reversible chains are known to show a diffusive behavior
Levin et al. 2006
. It is sometimes possible to construct a nonreversible chain that converges faster than the reversible counterpart to the same stationary distribution
Diaconis et al. 2000
;
Gao et al. 2020
. One way to do this is to use a so-called lifting procedure. An optimal lift can offer up to a quadratic speedup (like the quantum algorithms)
Chen et al. 1999
;
Eberle and Lörler 2024
but typically requires a detailed knowledge of the chain
Apers et al. 2017
. Because there is no downside to using lifts, such nonreversible processes are generally preferred for sampling purposes
Gouraud et al. 2024
.

This work provides two ways to construct an approximate reflection through the target coherent state from the Szegedy quantum walk operators
Szegedy 2004
. None of the two methods requires reversibility. Amplitude amplification algorithms use such a reflection to prepare the sought state
Martyn et al. 2021
. Since QAE rely on such a state preparation unitary
Brassard et al. 2002
;
Grinko et al. 2021
, the reflection is readily usable in Quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) routines
Akhalwaya et al. 2023
. QMC requires quadratically less samples from the stationary distribution than classical MCMC, and each sample can be provided more efficiently.

The first method requires knowledge of
\pi
up to a multiplicative constant. It prepares the reflection in the square root of the mixing time of the multiplicative reversibilization of the kernel. This first result is stated in Proposition
1
. The second method does not require any additional knowledge about
\pi
and produces an approximate reflection. This second result is stated by Proposition
5

The first method can be applied to any kernel whereas the second requires theoretical justification. We introduce a Markov kernel called the geometric reversibilization of the chain and a condition of reversibility on
\pi
-average. This condition guarantees both the efficiency of the method and the quality of the produced reflection.
Indeed, if this condition is met, the geometric reversibilization reaches its stationary distribution in a time of the order of the mixing time of the additive reversibilization of the chain, the process that goes forward and backward in time with equal probabilities. This stationary distribution is called the most reversible distribution and is guaranteed to be close to
\pi
. Figure

-average is verified long before the mixing time. Figure

displays that the eigenvalues of the geometric reversibilization, whose properties govern the runtime of the quantum algorithm, rapidly approach those of the additive reversibilization of the kernel. Figure

By efficiently constructing reflections through the stationary distribution of nonreversible chains, this study expands the set of Markov chain computations that can be achieved faster using a quantum computer. These reflections could ultimately be applied to accelerate molecular dynamics, with implications in drug design
Höllmer et al. 2024
;
Ma et al. 2021

Jayachandran et al. 2006
;
Casares et al. 2022
;
Krauth 2021

Technical background

A Markov kernel on a finite set
\mathbb{S}
is a matrix
P
of positive numbers such that each row sums to
1
. The Markov chain with initial condition
X_{0}\in\mathbb{S}
associated with
P
is a stochastic process
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
with values in
\mathbb{S}
such that when it is in state
x\in\mathbb{S}
, it goes to
y\in\mathbb{S}
with probability
P(x,y)
. If the kernel allows to reach any state
y\in\mathbb{S}
from any state
x\in\mathbb{S}
, we say that it is irreducible. If it has a single eigenvalue on the unit circle we say that it is aperiodic. If
P
is both irreducible and aperiodic, it is said to be ergodic. An ergodic Markov kernel is such that, after waiting for a sufficiently long time
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
, the probability distribution of the system state
X_{\tau}
is approximately given by a probability distribution
\pi
that is independent from the starting state (see the Methods
Methods
for a precise definition of the mixing time
\tau
). This distribution is called the stationary distribution. A sufficient condition for an ergodic kernel
P
to have stationary distribution
\pi
is to be reversible with respect to
\pi
: such that
\pi(x)P(x,y)=\pi(y)P(y,x)
for any states
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
. MCMC algorithms compute the expectation values of functions of random variables sampled according to
\pi
. Therefore, the number of Markov chain steps required to provide a sample from
\pi
is a natural performance metric.

By contrast, modern quantum algorithms do not simply perform steps of the chain sequentially. For instance, Generalized Quantum Eigenvalue Transform (GQET) and Generalized Quantum Singular Value Transform (GQSVT) respectively manipulate the eigenvalues and singular values of matrices encoded within a quantum circuit
Motlagh and Wiebe 2024
;
Sünderhauf 2023
. Let us define such encodings. A unitary
U
and two (partial) isometries
\square_{L},\square_{R}
are said to be a projected unitary encoding
(U,\square_{L},\square_{R})
of
A
if
\square_{L}^{\dagger}U\square_{R}=A
(the superscript
†
indicates the conjugate transpose matrix). If
A
is symmetric,
\square_{L}=\square_{R}=\square
and
U
is also symmetric, then
(U,\square)
is said to be a symmetric unitary projected encoding of
A
. Given
(U,\square)
a symmetric unitary projected encoding of
A
, define the qubitized walk operator
\mathcal{W}=(2\square\square^{\dagger}-1)U
. This operator is going to be particularly useful when
A
is the projector on a state of interest. Indeed, if
A=\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}
for some state
\ket{\phi}
, then
\left(\mathcal{W}^{2},\square\right)
is a symmetric projected unitary encoding of
2\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}-1
(see
Claudon 2024
for a comprehensive exposition).

GQET and GQSVT apply polynomials to the eigenvalues and singular values of encoded matrices. For each degree
d
polynomial
\upsilon(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}T_{k}(x)
with complex coefficients defined in the Chebyshev basis, define the signal processing polynomial
\Upsilon
by
\Upsilon(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}z^{k}
. Denote the condition
\max_{z\in\partial B_{1}(0)}|\Upsilon(z)|\leq 1
as the scaling condition. Note that if
\upsilon
does not satisfy the scaling condition, there exists a smallest
\beta>1
such that
\upsilon/\beta
satisfies the scaling condition.
\beta
is called the scaling factor and grows at most logarithmically with
d

Sünderhauf 2023
. If
\upsilon
satisfies the scaling condition and
(U,\square)
is a symmetric projected unitary encoding of the symmetric operator
H
, then the GQET allows to construct a symmetric projected unitary encoding
(\mathcal{U},\square)
of
\upsilon(H)
using
d
times the unitary operator
U
,
2\square\square^{\dagger}-1
and single qubit gates. Note that if a polynomial
\upsilon
sends a single eigenvalue
H
to
1
and all the others to
0
, the square of the qubitized walk operator associated with
\mathcal{U}
acts as a reflection.

Let us now consider the general case where
(U,\square_{L},\square_{R})
is a projected unitary encoding of
A
. Define
\overline{U}
and
\overline{\square}
by:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \overline{U}&=\left(\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes U+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes U^{\dagger}\right)(X\otimes 1),\\
\overline{\square}&=\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\square_{L}+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\square_{R}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

Here,
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
define the orthonormal basis of a two-dimensional Hilbert space and
X=\ket{0}\bra{1}+\ket{1}\bra{0}
. Then,
\left(\overline{U},\overline{\square}\right)
is a symmetric projected unitary encoding of the hermitian matrix
\overline{A}=\left(\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes A+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes A^{\dagger}\right)(X\otimes 1)
. Let
\upsilon
be a degree
d
real and even polynomial satisfying the scaling condition. The GQSVT algorithm allows to construct a symmetric projected unitary encoding
(\mathcal{U},\ket{01}\otimes\square_{R})
of

A=W^{\dagger}\Sigma V
is the singular value decomposition of

, with

Our goal is to construct a reflection through the coherent state associated with the stationary distribution
\ket{\pi}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\sqrt{\pi(x)}\ket{x}
, for some known basis
\{\ket{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{S}}

\{\ket{x,y}\}_{x,y\in\mathbb{S}}

\ket{\psi_{x}}=\ket{x}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S}}\sqrt{P(x,y)}\ket{y}

\pi

. As demonstrated in
Lemieux et al. 2020
;
Loke and Wang 2017

Camps et al. 2022

Singular value transformations of the flat discriminant

Implementing

If

\mu=\text{argmax}_{\nu}\braket{\nu|D|\nu},

(2)

where the argmax ranges over all strictly positive probability distributions on

\forall x,y\in\mathbb{S}:Q(x,y)=\sqrt{\frac{\mu(y)}{\mu(x)}}\frac{D(x,y)}{\braket{\mu|D|\mu}}

Sequences of flat discriminants

If
P
is reversible, then

and its spectral properties are clear. If
P
is nonreversible, then

may contain no information about the stationary distribution. Indeed, consider a Markov chain on the
N

is half the identity matrix. However, if

for each

and

(D_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of the flat discriminants associated to
(P^{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
instead of simply
D=D_{1}
. We will show examples of chains that become reversible on
\pi
-average,
1-\braket{\pi|D_{j}|\pi}\ll\gamma(Q_{j})
, long before the mixing time. In this regime, Proposition
5
constructs an approximation of the sought reflection significantly faster than the mixing time of the chain.

and proven in the Appendix, shows that if either a kernel or its adjoint is sufficiently mixed, then the reflection through the most stationary distribution can be constructed with constant cost. In the Appendix, we give an example of a Markov chain such that the reflection construction time is exponentially smaller than the mixing time.

Let us now assume that the Markov chain possesses a group structure, then
\left\|D_{j}-(\mathcal{D}^{j}+(\mathcal{D}^{\dagger})^{j})/2\right\|=1-\braket{\pi|D_{j}|\pi}
. This implies that the eigenvalues of the flat discriminant are all within distance
1-\braket{\pi|D_{j}|\pi}
of an eigenvalue of
(P^{j}+(P^{\star})^{j})/2
. A precise statement is given in Proposition
6
. For many applications it is therefore sufficient to focus on
\braket{\pi|D_{j}|\pi}
.

Note that
\braket{\pi|D_{j}|\pi}
is the
\pi

P^{j}(x,\cdot)
and
(P^{\star})^{j}(x,\cdot)
as
x
follows
\pi

\braket{\pi|D_{j}|\pi}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\braket{P^{j}(x,\cdot)|(P^{\star})^{j}(x,\cdot)}\right]
. In practice, a chain with local moves tends to stay for a long time near a local maxima of probability (according to the distribution
\pi

P^{\star}
. In such regions, the overlap between the laws of
P
and of

tends to
1
very fast (1 minus the overlap is in fact the square of a distance). For
x\in\mathbb{S}
in a low probability region,
P^{j}(x,\cdot)
and
(P^{\star})^{j}(x,\cdot)
can remain different until
j
equals the mixing time. Such ideas are made rigorous using the concept of quasi-stationary distributions in Proposition

rapidly tends to
1
. A numerical example is given in the Appendix. Many discrete approximations of stochastic differential equations also typically have quasi-stationary distributions and rapidly increasing
\braket{\pi|D_{j}|\pi}

Discussion

The first algorithm prepares the reflection through the sought stationary distribution with a worst case runtime that is quadratically smaller than the worst case mixing time of a generic Markov chain. We have shown that normal kernels (in particular, reversible kernels) are all quadratically accelerated and gave an example of exponentially accelerated nonreversible chain. However, kernels that are not accelerated also exist. For example, the kinetic walker (introduced in the Methods section
Methods
) in a constant potential is such that both algorithms have runtime
\mathcal{O}(|\mathbb{S}|)
. Indeed, this Markov chain is precisely designed so that it does not commute with its adjoint until it is mixed. This feature is arguably made possible by the fact that the target potential is flat. Non-trivial potentials typically prevent this phenomenon to occur.

The second algorithm allows to approximate the reflection through the stationary distribution of a Markov chain without requiring its knowledge up to a multiplicative constant. In particular, it allows to sample from the stationary distribution of kinetic processes subject to non-conservative forces, for which the stationary distribution is not known, with quadratic speedup.

To conclude, this study extends the application of quantum algorithms to Markov chain problems. They can now accelerate nonreversible processes of wide interest and could, for example, speed up molecular dynamics simulations. It is among the early examples where the GQET framework not only unifies algorithms, but allows to design new ones. It also opens many questions in Markov chain theory, inviting practitioners to study the flat discriminants of the kernels they are interested in. In all likelihood, it is possible to improve the perturbation theoretic results and derive tighter upper bounds for the second largest eigenvalue of the flat discriminant. It would also be interesting to understand whether optimal lifts can match the performances of quantum algorithms and, if yes, if they can be systematically constructed. Future work will be directed to the design of efficient quantum circuits for
R
and
R^{\star}
in order to compare the end-to-end complexities of classical and quantum methods. Finally, reaching such a quantum speedup has ultimately profound implications for quantum computing, potentially enabling a variety of new quantum algorithms across various fields, from statistics and machine learning to biological, chemical and financial computational modeling.

Let us start by recalling central definitions. Let
\mu,\nu:\mathbb{S}\to[0,1]
be such that
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\mu(x)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}\nu(x)=1
. The total variation distance separating them is defined as
d_{TV}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{S}}|\mu(x)-\nu(x)|

\epsilon>0

\epsilon

\tau(\epsilon)=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}:\max_{x\in\mathbb{S}}d_{TV}(P^{t}(x,\cdot),\pi)\leq\epsilon\}

\epsilon=1/4
so that the notation
\tau
means
\tau=\tau(1/4)
. A kernel

is said to be lazy if
P(x,x)\geq 1/2
for each

. The spectral gap
\gamma
of

is defined as the second smallest singular value of

1
is the identity matrix and

is the curved discriminant of

The quantum algorithm described in Paragraph
