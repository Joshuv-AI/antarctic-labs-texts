Through a Filter of Empathy and Understanding: Patients' Experiences of Missed Nursing Care
Authors: Maja Thomsen Albrechtsen, Lone Jørgensen, Ingrid Poulsen, Hanne Mainz, Britt Laugesen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo explore how patients experience, interpret, and respond to missed nursing care during hospitalization in a Danish hospital context.DesignA qualitative design was employed.MethodsIndividual semi‐structured interviews were conducted with fifteen patients who had been admitted to medical or surgical wards at a Danish university hospital. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.FindingsThe overarching theme, ‘Nursing care experienced through a filter of empathy and understanding’, captured how patients perceived omissions and delays as understandable responses to systemic pressures rather than neglect. Although they experienced missed nursing care, patients rationalized these omissions as inevitable, adapting to organizational constraints with empathy toward nurses, awareness of workload pressures, and a wish not to be a burden. Three subthemes illustrated this process: ‘Not wanting to be a burden: negotiating physical needs’, ‘Moderating expectations: the subtle importance of psychosocial care’ and ‘Waiting patiently: a sense of shared responsibility for timely care’.ConclusionPatients' empathy and adaptation sometimes obscured unmet needs and contributed to the normalization of missed nursing care. Missed nursing care thus emerged as a relational, co‐constructed phenomenon shaped by both system pressures and patients' efforts to avoid burdening nursing staff.ImplicationsReducing missed nursing care requires action beyond staffing, addressing relational and cultural dimensions across clinical, organizational and policy levels through proactive, person‐centered approaches.ImpactBy foregrounding patients' perspectives, this study reframes missed nursing care as co‐constructed by both system constraints and patient adaptations, offering new insight to guide policy, leadership, and practice efforts toward more responsive and person‐centered nursing care.Reporting MethodReporting followed the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research.Patient or Public ContributionPatients contributed as interview participants but were not involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of the study.

Introduction

Healthcare systems increasingly operate under pressure: staff shortages, rising patient complexity, aging populations, and limited resources challenge the delivery of consistent, high‐quality nursing care. Under such conditions, nurses are often required to prioritize care activities, which can result in necessary patient care being delayed, partially omitted, or fully omitted, defined as missed nursing care (MNC) (Kalisch et al. 2009). Growing evidence has established MNC as a persistent and global phenomenon, closely linked to workforce constraints, workload intensity and organizational pressures within contemporary healthcare systems (Chaboyer et al. 2021; Gong et al. 2025). These pressures are further influenced by staff‐related conditions, including job satisfaction, presenteeism and burnout, which have been associated with the occurrence of MNC (Chaboyer et al. 2021; Dirgar et al. 2024; Jafari‐Koulaee et al. 2024). As a result, MNC poses significant risks to patient well‐being and the overall quality of healthcare delivery (Chaboyer et al. 2021; Recio‐Saucedo et al. 2018).

Although these dynamics are well documented from nurses' perspectives, a critical gap remains in understanding how patients themselves experience, interpret, and respond to MNC within such pressured care environments. Existing quantitative research has largely focused on measuring the occurrence of MNC, demonstrating that patients report MNC across settings and countries, but qualitative studies exploring their lived experiences are still scarce (Bagnasco et al. 2020). Addressing this gap is crucial for developing a more comprehensive understanding of MNC and its impact on patients.

Background

Research demonstrates that MNC can function as a key mediator between nurse staffing levels and patient outcomes (Connell et al. 2026; Griffiths et al. 2018; Kalisch et al. 2012). When nursing care activities are deprioritized due to factors such as staffing shortages, workload pressure, rising acuity and competing demands (Gong et al. 2025), patients face delayed recovery, increased symptom burden and higher risk of complications and mortality (Mandal et al. 2020; Recio‐Saucedo et al. 2018). Moreover, MNC can negatively affect patients' perceptions of nursing care, reducing trust in nurses and overall satisfaction during hospitalization (Karadaş et al. 2024; Sancar and Dogan Aktas 2025). Given that staffing levels are consistently identified as one of the strongest predictors of MNC (Griffiths et al. 2018), the projected global nursing shortage (World Health Organization 2020) underscores the urgency of mitigating MNC.

Although studies from patients' perspectives remain limited, survey research has shown that patients report MNC across healthcare systems (e.g., Albrechtsen, Jørgensen, Poulsen, Bruun, et al. 2025; Dabney and Kalisch 2015; Karadaş et al. 2024; Sarpong et al. 2025), confirming that MNC is not only a professional concern but also a patient‐experienced phenomenon. Across these studies, patients report omissions and delays in fundamental aspects of care such as assistance with mobility, hygiene and nutrition, as well as communication and emotional support. Yet, while surveys provide valuable information on the frequency and patterns of MNC, they are limited in their ability to capture the contextual, relational and emotional dimensions of patients' experiences.

To date, qualitative studies exploring patients' perspectives have typically focused on specific aspects of nursing care, such as communication, information exchange, patient involvement or pain management, rather than examining MNC in a broader and more integrated sense (Bagnasco et al. 2020; Gustafsson et al. 2020). Notably, Kalisch et al. (2012) conducted one of the first qualitative studies directly investigating patients ‘perceptions of MNC, showing that patients can provide reliable accounts of multiple elements of nursing care and identifying commonly missed aspects such as mouth care, ambulation and patient education. More recently, a mixed methods study adopting a broader perspective further highlighted that patients’ experiences of MNC are closely linked to their need for dignity, individuality, and sense of control, as well as to how they attribute its causes (Cohen et al. 2025).

Despite these valuable contributions, there remains a lack of qualitative evidence that explores not only whether patients experience MNC, but also how they interpret and respond to these experiences during hospitalization. Qualitative research is crucial for providing deeper insights into patients' perspectives, capturing latent attitudes, personal meanings and relational dimensions of nursing care that cannot be fully revealed through surveys with closed‐ended questions. Such studies can uncover subtle aspects of MNC, thereby offering a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon.

Evidence from diverse health care contexts is also needed, as organizational routines, cultural norms and resource availability vary across systems, influencing both the occurrence and the perceived significance of MNC. In this respect, qualitative research necessarily develops cumulatively, with in‐depth studies conducted within specific healthcare settings constituting essential building blocks in the development of a broader evidence base. The present study contributes to this body of knowledge by offering context‐specific insights into how patients experience MNC during hospitalization, how they interpret and make sense of these situations in relation to their needs and the care environment, and how they respond when care is omitted or delayed. In doing so, the study complements existing research while underscoring the continued need for qualitative investigations across diverse healthcare contexts. Understanding these contextual factors is essential for interpreting findings within specific national settings and for developing strategies to improve nursing care at local, national and international levels.

The Study

The study aimed to explore how patients experience, interpret, and respond to MNC during hospitalization.

Methods

Design

This study adopted a qualitative design to enable in‐depth exploration of patients' perspectives on MNC. Qualitative methods were considered appropriate for capturing the subjective meanings patients ascribe to these situations, including relational and contextual aspects that cannot be adequately addressed through surveys alone. Relational factors refer to interactions between patients and nursing staff (e.g., communication and responsiveness), whereas contextual factors encompass organizational routines, staffing conditions and the broader clinical environment in which care is delivered (Kitson 2018). The study was informed by a constructivist epistemological orientation (Denzin et al. 2023), in which patients' experiences of MNC are understood as socially constructed and context‐dependent rather than objective or fixed phenomena.

The study is part of a larger research project comprising multiple sub‐studies on MNC including a concurrent survey of patients' perspectives (Albrechtsen, Jørgensen, Poulsen, Bruun, et al. 2025). Reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) (Tong et al. 2007).

Study Setting, Participants and Recruitment

Patients admitted to 21 different medical or surgical wards at a publicly funded Danish university hospital were eligible for inclusion in the study. In this hospital setting, nursing care is primarily delivered by registered nurses and nurse assistants working within interdisciplinary teams, whereas overall responsibility for nursing care rests with nurse leaders at both ward and hospital management levels.

Participants were a subsample of respondents from a survey on patients' perspectives of MNC (Albrechtsen, Jørgensen, Poulsen, Bruun, et al. 2025). Inclusion criteria for the survey were: (1) hospitalization for a minimum of 48 h, (2) Age 18 years or older, (3) physically and cognitively able to provide informed consent and (4) able to understand and communicate in Danish. Potential participants were identified through a question at the end of the survey asking if they would be willing to participate in an in‐depth interview. Respondents who answered affirmatively provided consent to be contacted for an interview; in total, 90 survey respondents indicated interest. From this pool, participants were purposefully selected using maximum variation sampling to ensure diversity in experiences. Variation criteria included sex, age, educational level, type of admission (medical or surgical) and length of stay.

The sample size was pragmatically planned to include approximately 15 participants, balancing feasibility with the need for sufficient depth and variation in the data. This decision was informed by the concept of information power, whereby sample adequacy is evaluated in relation to the study aim and the richness and relevance of the data obtained (Malterud et al. 2016), rather than being determined by data saturation. A total of 28 potential participants were contacted by the first author by mail or telephone and invited to participate in an individual interview. Seven declined or were unable to participate (e.g., due to readmission or death), and six did not respond. Ultimately, 15 participants were included in the study.

Data Collection

Data were collected through qualitative, semi‐structured, face‐to‐face individual interviews (Kvale and Brinkmann 2015), allowing participants to share their narratives and provide rich, nuanced insights into how they experienced, interpreted and responded to MNC during hospitalization. Interviews were conducted between June and July 2023. To accommodate participants' preferences, the time and location were chosen by the participants themselves. Thirteen interviews took place in participants' homes and two in a hospital meeting room. All interviews were conducted in undisturbed settings, although relatives were present in two of the home interviews.

The interviews were conducted by the first author (female, PhD student with a background in public health and training and experience in qualitative research methods), who had no prior relationship with the participants and no clinical background in nursing. Participants were informed about the interviewer's role as a PhD student and the purpose of the study prior to participation. The interviews were guided by a semi‐structured interview guide inspired by the Danish version of the MISSCARE Survey–Patient (Albrechtsen, Jørgensen, Poulsen, Shifaza, et al. 2025), which includes 23 items covering three domains of nursing care: physical care (hands‐on assistance such as hygiene, nutrition and mobility), psychosocial care (e.g., communication, empathy, dignity and involvement) and timeliness (promptness in responding to patients' needs). The interview guide was developed in collaboration with the research team, discussed and refined prior to data collection, and only slightly adjusted during the interview process, primarily in terms of wording, without altering its overall structure or focus.

The interview guide was structured around open‐ended questions addressing patients' experiences of nursing care during hospitalization, how this care aligned with their needs, their perceptions of the timing of care, and their expectations of nursing care. The intention was to encourage open reflection on these experiences rather than to assess MNC as a predefined concept. Accordingly, the term ‘missed nursing care’ was deliberately not introduced during the interviews to avoid imposing predefined terminology and to support participants in articulating their experiences in their own words. Additional probing questions (e.g., ‘How did you feel?’, ‘How did you respond?’, ‘Looking back, is there anything that could have made the situation better for you?’ were used to explore specific situations in greater depth).

To facilitate recall and reflection, a set of cue cards was used as visual prompts during the interviews. The cue cards contained short phrases derived from the survey items and reflected both physical care activities (e.g., help with personal hygiene, mobilization and nutrition) and psychosocial aspects of nursing care (e.g., being listened to, treated with dignity, and involved in decisions). The cue cards were introduced flexibly as conversational prompts rather than as a checklist, allowing participants to select and discuss aspects they found most relevant to their own experiences. In several interviews they were only briefly referred to or not used at all when participants spontaneously elaborated on their experiences. When used, participants typically expanded beyond the brief phrases on the cards, offering more nuanced and in‐depth accounts of how they experienced nursing care during hospitalization.

All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim by the first author. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment, and no field notes were taken, as the recordings and transcripts were considered sufficient to capture participants' accounts. The duration of interviews ranged from 44 to 90 min, with a mean length of 57 min.

Data Analysis

The interview data were analysed using reflective thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2022) with the aim of developing, analysing and interpreting patterns across the dataset. Reflexivity, understood as the ongoing critical reflection on the researchers' roles, assumptions and influence on the research (Braun and Clarke 2022), was an integral part of the process.

The analysis proceeded in iterative phases following Braun and Clarke's six‐phase framework (Braun and Clarke 2022). First, the interviews were transcribed, listened to, and reread to ensure familiarity with the data. This was followed by systematic coding at a relatively semantic level, capturing distinct meanings and concepts. Two researchers (first and last author) participated independently in the coding process to enhance reflexivity, strengthen interpretation, and ensure a nuanced understanding of the material. Based on the coded data, patterns of shared meaning were identified and grouped into candidate themes, which were subsequently reviewed and refined through iterative discussions involving the entire research team. Reflexivity was operationalized throughout the analytical process through critical examination of analytic decisions and discussion of alternative interpretations. This process ensured that the themes were coherent, meaningful and consistent with both the coded extracts and the dataset as a whole. In the next step, themes were further defined and named to articulate their essence and relevance to the research aim. Finally, the findings were written up in a narrative account that wove together themes with illustrative quotations, thereby grounding the interpretations in the participants' own voices. Ethical Considerations

The study adhered to ethical guidelines for the protection of personal data and followed the principles of the World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association 2024). Participants received both verbal and written information about the study before the interview, including their right to withdraw at any time without providing a reason and without any consequences for their future nursing care and treatment. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Interview transcripts were anonymized, and identifying information was stored separately from the data. All materials were stored securely on a protected server. Formal ethical approval was not required according to Danish law and the Danish Research Ethics Committee System. However, the study was registered in the North Denmark Region's record of processing activities (ID number: F2022‐111) in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2016).

Rigour and Reflexivity

To enhance trustworthiness according to the criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability (Nowell et al. 2017), several strategies were applied throughout the study. Transferability was supported through maximum variation sampling, ensuring inclusion of patients with diverse backgrounds and hospitalization experiences. Descriptions of participants and the study context were provided to enable readers to assess whether the findings may be transferable to other contexts. The sample size was pragmatically planned to include approximately 15 participants and was further informed by the concept of information power (Malterud et al. 2016), meaning that adequacy was evaluated in relation to the richness and relevance of the data in addressing the study aim. Credibility was enhanced by researcher triangulation: two researchers independently engaged in the coding process. Emerging themes were continuously reflected upon and discussed within the research team to ensure that interpretations remained grounded in the data. Dependability was supported by documentation of methodological and analytical decisions and by ongoing dialogue among the research team throughout the process. Confirmability was addressed through collaborative analysis and team reflections, which helped ensure that interpretations were not shaped by a single researcher's perspective. Furthermore, the inclusion of illustrative participant quotes demonstrates a clear link between the findings and the raw data, thereby enhancing transparency and allowing readers to trace the analytical process. Reflexivity was considered in all phases of the study. The interviewer's background in public health and lack of clinical nursing experience may have reduced the risk of profession‐based preconceptions influencing data collection and analysis, whereas also posing potential challenges related to limited clinical familiarity, including the possibility that subtle clinical cues or the significance of specific nursing care activities might be less readily recognized. These challenges were mitigated through continuous reflexive dialogue within the research team, the rest of whom are registered nurses with clinical expertise, contributing clinical perspectives that supported contextual interpretation during data analysis.

Findings

Characteristics of the Sample

The sample comprised nine men and six women aged between 24 and 94 years, with diverse educational backgrounds from primary school to higher education. Seven participants had been admitted for medical conditions and eight for surgical procedures. Length of hospital stay ranged from 3 to 196 days (median 7 days), reflecting experiences across different phases of hospitalization, from shorter admissions to prolonged stays involving more extensive contact with nursing care. Participants' level of physical functioning and dependence on nursing care varied, ranging from being largely self‐sufficient to requiring assistance with activities such as mobilization, eating and personal hygiene. Interviews were conducted 3–49 days (median 21 days) after discharge, allowing participants time to recover while ensuring experiences remained recent. Nursing Care Experienced Through a Filter of Empathy and Understanding

Through the analysis, an overarching theme was developed: Nursing care experienced through a filter of empathy and understanding. At first glance, participants' accounts of the nursing care they received during hospitalization appeared overwhelmingly positive, with few explicit reports of MNC. Most participants described their overall experiences as good and expressed satisfaction with the nursing care provided. However, a deeper analysis revealed more nuanced narratives. Participants often acknowledged situations where aspects of care were delayed, incomplete or omitted, but these were typically reframed as understandable oversights rather than failures. This reframing was closely tied to their empathy for the nursing staff and their awareness of workload pressures and busy ward environments.

Three interrelated subthemes were constructed, which together elaborate and support the overarching theme: ‘Not wanting to be a burden: negotiating physical needs’, ‘Moderating expectations: the subtle importance of psychosocial care’ and ‘Waiting patiently: a sense of shared responsibility for timely care’ (Figure 1). These subthemes illustrate how participants experience, interpret, and respond to MNC, emphasizing the relational and contextual factors shaping their perceptions. Although conceptually distinct, the subthemes are united by a shared interpretive lens, as participants consistently framed experiences of missed or delayed care through empathy and understanding. Together, they capture complementary aspects of how participants experienced, interpreted, and responded to MNC during hospitalization.

Not Wanting to Be a Burden: Negotiating Physical Needs

Most participants described the nursing staff as kind, attentive and proactive, often recalling how staff checked in and encouraged them to use the call bell. However, despite this openness, many participants expressed a pronounced reluctance to request help. This reluctance often involved an internal negotiation between their own physical needs and their perception of staff workload:I often felt like such a burden because I needed help with everything, so I would rather call one time too little than one time too many. I would just stretch it out a bit, so I didn't have to call so often. (Participant 10)

I often felt like such a burden because I needed help with everything, so I would rather call one time too little than one time too many. I would just stretch it out a bit, so I didn't have to call so often. (Participant 10)

This quote reflects how feelings of dependency were closely intertwined with concerns about overburdening nursing staff. This sense of not wanting to inconvenience nursing staff led some participants to hold back or delay requests, even when doing so could have benefited their comfort or recovery. Their restraint reflected an awareness of nursing staff's workload and a reluctance to appear demanding. As one participant explained, they consciously evaluated whether it was acceptable to interrupt the nursing staff:Well, it wasn't anything really, as I said before, I don't call that often – it's only if you absolutely have to… I try to manage on my own as best I can. (Participant 3)

Well, it wasn't anything really, as I said before, I don't call that often – it's only if you absolutely have to… I try to manage on my own as best I can. (Participant 3)

Together, these accounts illustrate how participants' reluctance to ask for help was not driven by lack of need, but by a deliberate weighing of their own needs against perceived staff workload. Participants actively assessed whether their needs were ‘important enough’ to justify requesting help, and how they consequently often downplayed their own needs to avoid disturbing the staff. By doing so, participants demonstrated a kind of personal triage—a self‐imposed prioritization in which their comfort or support came second to staff capacity. This negotiation was not only about when to ask for help, but also about what level of nursing care quality to expect:They use the time they have available, and that's fine – they get done what they need to, they just have to run a little faster. (Participant 3)

They use the time they have available, and that's fine – they get done what they need to, they just have to run a little faster. (Participant 3)

The participant accepts a faster pace and reduced thoroughness as an inevitable consequence of staff busyness, rather than as a shortcoming of care. Nursing care was still delivered, but its quality subtly reflected the surrounding workload. Others expressed similar reflections, noting that some nursing care activities were completed, yet not with the same attention or precision they would have preferred. One participant described how teeth brushing, though carried out, did not meet their usual standard:Well, it wasn't done as properly as when I do it myself, no. I would have liked to, but I just couldn't manage. (Participant 12)

Well, it wasn't done as properly as when I do it myself, no. I would have liked to, but I just couldn't manage. (Participant 12)

Even when recognizing such differences in quality, participants rarely expressed dissatisfaction. Instead, they justified them as understandable outcomes of an overworked environment. Although this pattern was common, participants' capacity to manage independently varied, which influenced how strongly they felt the need to negotiate requests for help and how they interpreted the quality of care received. This empathetic stance was also evident in more serious situations such as medication errors:I was given the wrong medication because everything was moving so fast. Luckily, it wasn't dangerous, but I received ten times the dose I should have had. It could have been bad. The nurse was almost in tears when she realized – she just couldn't keep up. It was obvious she was running so fast that she didn't notice the mistake, and I think that's just terrible. (Participant 4)

I was given the wrong medication because everything was moving so fast. Luckily, it wasn't dangerous, but I received ten times the dose I should have had. It could have been bad. The nurse was almost in tears when she realized – she just couldn't keep up. It was obvious she was running so fast that she didn't notice the mistake, and I think that's just terrible. (Participant 4)

Rather than focusing on the potential harm, the participant emphasized the nurse's distress and the systemic pressures that led to the mistake. Through such empathy, participants distanced themselves from a narrative of neglect and instead framed omissions as understandable consequences of external strain. At the same time, some participants described situations in which they reminded nursing staff about missed medications or other aspects of care, indicating that patients occasionally contributed to identifying omissions when they felt able to speak up. However, even missed or minimal nursing care—such as assistance with walking—was accepted as a matter of prioritization rather than interpreted as an omission:There might be patients who would have liked to go for a walk down the hallway, but if they can't do it themselves, there just isn't time for that. I could only go because I could do it on my own. (Participant 3)

There might be patients who would have liked to go for a walk down the hallway, but if they can't do it themselves, there just isn't time for that. I could only go because I could do it on my own. (Participant 3)

Similarly, when participants did notice small omissions, they tended to excuse them rather than interpret them as inadequate nursing care:There could be days where some things slipped through (…) but it wasn't often, and I just thought, well, they are probably busy, so I could skip it that one time. (Participant 10)

There could be days where some things slipped through (…) but it wasn't often, and I just thought, well, they are probably busy, so I could skip it that one time. (Participant 10)

This quote exemplifies how participants normalized and rationalized omissions by attributing them to busyness rather than neglect. By framing such instances as understandable oversights, they maintained trust in the nursing staff's intentions and upheld a sense of gratitude rather than disappointment. This interpretive flexibility reflects how participants adjusted their expectations and accepted that nursing care, though sometimes incomplete, was still ‘good enough’.

In sum, this theme illustrates how participants actively negotiated their physical care needs by weighing the legitimacy of their requests, downplaying their needs, adapting expectations to align with perceived staff workload, and rationalizing the quality of care received including direct omissions or mistakes. Together, these strategies constituted adaptive responses through which participants accommodated care constraints, shaping how MNC was experienced. Although these experiences varied according to participants' functional status and dependence on nursing assistance, they were consistently framed through empathy for the nursing staff and an understanding of systemic pressures. Participants often accepted that nursing care was delivered ‘well enough’ rather than ideally, thereby preserving a positive view of the nursing staff and protecting themselves from the discomfort and distress of interpreting their care as inadequate.

Moderating Expectations: The Subtle Importance of Psychosocial Care

Just as participants carefully navigated their physical care needs to avoid being a burden, they approached their psychosocial needs with similar caution. Many expressed subtle desires for emotional support, yet they remained acutely aware of nursing staff's time constraints and the pressures of the clinical environment.

Several participants described moments when their psychosocial needs were clearly recognized and met. They emphasized the value of being acknowledged and understood, and highlighted the importance of nursing staff being empathetic, attentive, and responsive to their individual situations. Participants' preferences varied: some placed trust in the nursing staff to make decisions on their behalf, whereas most appreciated being included and actively engaged in their care. However, not all participants felt adequately listened to or involved. One participant described a conflict over her acrylic nails, which held deep personal meaning:They were going to measure something on my finger, which is difficult through acrylic nails. The nurse said she would cut one off, and I told her she could forget it (…) Then she got really angry and said, ‘It's not like I'm taking your firstborn’. (…) but she couldn't understand that when you're sick, it's one of the few things you still have some control over. I looked awful because I was so sick, (…) but at least I still had my nails, and she couldn't understand that at all. (Participant 10)

They were going to measure something on my finger, which is difficult through acrylic nails. The nurse said she would cut one off, and I told her she could forget it (…) Then she got really angry and said, ‘It's not like I'm taking your firstborn’. (…) but she couldn't understand that when you're sick, it's one of the few things you still have some control over. I looked awful because I was so sick, (…) but at least I still had my nails, and she couldn't understand that at all. (Participant 10)

This episode illustrates how seemingly minor details from nurses' perspective can carry great emotional weight from a patient's point of view. A rigid procedural focus can inadvertently undermine patients' sense of dignity and identity, leaving them feeling unseen.

A related dimension was the role of clear and continuous communication. For many participants, being well informed was essential for feeling secure and maintaining a sense of control. Information helped them preserve their agency, build trust and feel reassured. Conversely, unclear or insufficient communication often led to anxiety and frustration. One participant described how the lack of explanations left them uncertain and unsettled:What I find difficult about the healthcare system is that we end up in a situation where someone takes over (…) it's like someone takes over and assumes your responsibility, and they are so kind and do a lot of good things, but you don't really get an explanation as to why. (Participant 5)

What I find difficult about the healthcare system is that we end up in a situation where someone takes over (…) it's like someone takes over and assumes your responsibility, and they are so kind and do a lot of good things, but you don't really get an explanation as to why. (Participant 5)

This quote highlights how lack of information can erode patients' sense of control and understanding, even when nursing care is perceived as kind and competent. As the same participant explained, this could lead to a feeling of being disempowered:You feel a bit disempowered because you feel someone is taking over. (Participant 5)

You feel a bit disempowered because you feel someone is taking over. (Participant 5)

Here, communication is not merely about transferring information but about supporting patients' autonomy and their role in their own care.

Participants frequently noted that psychosocial care, although present, was shaped by time constraints. Several appreciated nursing staff's attentiveness despite their visible busyness. Participants' awareness of nursing staff's busyness was grounded in a combination of observable cues in everyday care situations and their own interpretations of organizational constraints and prioritization. Participants described noticing nursing staff's pace, interruptions during care encounters, and cues such as phones or call systems, whereas also inferring time pressure from their understanding of ward routines and competing demands:When they [the nursing staff] come into the room and then suddenly they stand like this, checking whether their phone has beeped (…) and so often you can see that it does beep, but they still finish what they are doing (…) but I know that there are two or three other patients who have also called. (Participant 6)

When they [the nursing staff] come into the room and then suddenly they stand like this, checking whether their phone has beeped (…) and so often you can see that it does beep, but they still finish what they are doing (…) but I know that there are two or three other patients who have also called. (Participant 6)

This illustrates how participants actively interpreted everyday interactions as signs of limited time availability, rather than relying on explicit statements from staff. Such observations shaped how participants understood nursing staff's limited time for psychosocial care and contributed to their tendency to moderate expectations regarding emotional presence and extended interaction. Yet many also expressed a wish for more time and personal contact, particularly during vulnerable moments:It would always be nice if there was more time for just general conversations, because I think what I felt best about (…) was when they had the time to just come in, and we could talk for fifteen minutes and just get everything off my chest with someone who could understand it better than my family can. (Participant 10)

It would always be nice if there was more time for just general conversations, because I think what I felt best about (…) was when they had the time to just come in, and we could talk for fifteen minutes and just get everything off my chest with someone who could understand it better than my family can. (Participant 10)

This highlights how participants simultaneously valued brief moments of emotional presence and adjusted their expectations in recognition of nursing staff's constrained time. Although short, unhurried interactions could provide meaningful emotional support, participants nonetheless expressed an unmet need for greater attention to psychosocial aspects of care. At the same time, they often rationalized the limited time available for such nursing care, acknowledging the systemic pressures faced by nursing staff:It [psychosocial care] becomes somewhat secondary, because you have to quickly attend to seven people in one room. It ends up being just quickly checking blood pressure and so on before moving to the next (…) but again, when you are busy, I know exactly how that feels, so you just can't do everything, and that's probably where something gets lost or left out – it will happen. (Participant 1)

Another participant echoed this understanding while still noting what was missing:Of course, they don't have time for that because it's their job to take care of these acute tasks (…) but still, there is this aspect of care and presence that may have been somewhat lacking. (Participant 4)

Of course, they don't have time for that because it's their job to take care of these acute tasks (…) but still, there is this aspect of care and presence that may have been somewhat lacking. (Participant 4)

These accounts suggest that nursing care was at times experienced as predominantly task‐oriented, with nursing staff focusing on completing essential procedures and routines, leaving limited time for the relational and psychosocial aspects of nursing care. At the same time, participants demonstrated a dual awareness: they recognized the prioritization of acute tasks, whereas acknowledging that this could come at the expense of emotional connection and presence. By adjusting their expectations and rationalizing missed elements, they protected both themselves and the staff from perceived shortcomings. Some even reframed negative interactions as symptoms of systemic strain rather than personal failings:Nurses need proper working conditions and satisfaction at work, which I don't think they always have. Then they get stressed, and yes, they may snap a bit – it's normal, they need to let it out. (Participant 15)

This empathetic stance reveals how participants interpret psychosocial shortcomings within the broader organizational context. Finally, several participants reflected on how structural factors, such as shared rooms, shaped their psychosocial experiences. Some expressed discomfort with the lack of privacy:I don't really like it when they come in and talk about whether I've been to the bathroom today, and I just think, I don't want the rest of the ward to hear that. (Participant 14)

For this participant, the lack of privacy felt invasive, yet such situations were often accepted as inevitable:If you need confidentiality, well, that's impossible, I'd say it's almost impossible. How on earth are you supposed to do that with the conditions they have today? You would need single rooms, and we don't have those. (Participant 7)

If you need confidentiality, well, that's impossible, I'd say it's almost impossible. How on earth are you supposed to do that with the conditions they have today? You would need single rooms, and we don't have those. (Participant 7)

These reflections highlight that psychosocial care is not only relational but also structurally conditioned by the physical and organizational environment. Participants did not perceive these issues as something staff could change, further moderating their expectations.

Taken together, these findings suggest that participants deeply valued emotional presence, empathy, involvement, clear communication and privacy. However, they often moderated their expectations in light of nursing staff's workload and structural constraints. By navigating psychosocial care in this way, participants maintained empathy for nursing staff, reframed unmet needs, and preserved their own sense of dignity and trust within the care environment.

Waiting Patiently: A Sense of Shared Responsibility for Timely Care

Participants generally described that when they expressed a need for assistance, nursing staff responded promptly. Although many found it difficult to estimate exact waiting times, most recalled nursing staff as arriving ‘right away’. Although some noticed delays, these were not perceived as excessive, and participants rarely expressed frustration or dissatisfaction. Instead, they rationalized waiting by referring to nursing staff's workload, competing demands, and their own non‐urgent conditions.

One participant recalled an incident where a nurse was delayed, but quickly excused the situation:She came in one morning to help wash me down below, and she had everything ready but needed to fetch a diaper or a cloth (…). Then she ran into someone outside who needed help with something, and of course, she said yes – and then she forgot all about me. It took about forty‐five minutes before she came back, but that's just how it is. (…) I wasn't in pain or in any critical condition. (Participant 15)

She came in one morning to help wash me down below, and she had everything ready but needed to fetch a diaper or a cloth (…). Then she ran into someone outside who needed help with something, and of course, she said yes – and then she forgot all about me. It took about forty‐five minutes before she came back, but that's just how it is. (…) I wasn't in pain or in any critical condition. (Participant 15)

This participant's account reflects an understanding of nursing staff's competing priorities and acceptance of delays as reasonable under the circumstances. More generally, participants acknowledged that nursing staff could only be in one place at a time, showing empathy for the constraints of their work:There are always some things that don't happen exactly when you'd like them to, but you can see it from both sides – they can only be in one place at a time. (Participant 8)

Taken together, these reflections show how participants contextualized waiting as an unavoidable consequence of staffing levels rather than as neglect. They patiently adapted to systemic constraints, framing delays empathetically and recognizing that nursing staff's time was distributed among many patients. A similar awareness emerged when participants compared their own situation to that of others:If I pull the cord because I need to go to the toilet or something, well, it's not certain that I'll be helped first – there could be someone even sicker than me who needs help before me. In this way, waiting was not merely understood as a delay but as part of a moral order of nursing care, in which empathy for others justified postponing one's own needs when others were perceived to be worse off. Participants thus participated in the implicit triage logic of the ward, assessing whose needs should come first and demonstrating awareness of the limitations of staff capacity.

However, not all experiences reflected this balance of patience and understanding. For some, delays were prolonged and distressing, particularly when participants were unable to summon help. Another participant similarly reflected:There was one day where it was really very quiet; I didn't see the nurse I had for a long time, but I thought that if I don't say anything, she probably won't come by to check in either. This account highlights a subtle but important dynamic: participants often internalized responsibility for maintaining contact with nursing staff, recognizing that staff presence could not be taken for granted and that the timeliness of nursing care often depended on their own willingness or ability to make their needs visible.

Altogether, these experiences illustrate differences in how timely nursing care was experienced depending on participants' ability and willingness to express their needs. Although participants often described nursing care as responsive when they actively requested assistance, others recounted situations in which receiving help was delayed when they were reluctant or unable to signal their needs. Across participants' accounts, waiting was frequently rationalized by referring to the nursing staff's workload and systemic pressures, with participants describing how they waited patiently while adjusting their expectations accordingly. Timely nursing care thus emerged as an interaction between nursing staff's attentiveness and participants' own efforts to make their needs visible, with participants sometimes assuming responsibility for initiating or sustaining contact when nursing care was delayed.

Discussion

This study explored how patients experience, interpret, and respond to MNC during hospitalization. By foregrounding patients' perspectives, the study offers novel insight into how MNC is not only experienced but actively negotiated and normalized in everyday care contexts. The findings indicate that patients actively interpret, rationalize, and sometimes internalize omissions and delays in nursing care. Although they did not always receive the nursing care they needed, they rarely framed these omissions as neglect or failure. Instead, their accounts reflected empathy toward nurses and an acute awareness of the visible strain on the healthcare system.

Participants in this study overwhelmingly interpreted MNC as a consequence of systemic pressures rather than as the result of individual nurses' actions or attitudes. This finding aligns with previous research showing that patients express considerable empathy toward nurses and attribute omissions in care to broader systemic issues (Chiappinotto et al. 2023; Cohen et al. 2025). For instance, Cohen et al. (2025) found that patients perceived both themselves and nurses as ‘shared victims’ of an overburdened healthcare system, whereas Chiappinotto et al. (2023) similarly reported that patients often linked omissions to system‐level changes such as cost‐containment policies and workforce reductions. Correspondingly, El‐Haddad et al. (2020) showed that patients across a range of clinical contexts were comfortable openly criticizing the health system as a whole while conceptually separating it from the individual clinicians they encountered. This tendency to distinguish between structural and individual responsibility was clearly reflected in the present study. Instances of insufficient nursing care, errors or situations where nurses ‘snapped’ at patients were often rationalized as understandable consequences of workload pressures rather than personal failings.

Despite recounting episodes of delayed or omitted care, most participants described being generally satisfied with the nursing care they received. This apparent paradox may be partly explained by patients' limited understanding of what constitutes high‐quality nursing care (Kitson 2018). Without explicit knowledge of care standards or rights, patients may accept the level of nursing care they receive as normal, even when their needs remain partially unmet. Another possible explanation could be the role of expectations in shaping healthcare experiences. According to Thompson and Suñol (1995), patient expectations can be divided into ideal and predicted expectations. Although ideal expectations reflect patients' aspirations and desired standards of nursing care, predicted expectations concern what patients realistically anticipate based on previous experiences, others' accounts, and public narratives. In the context of this study, the persistent public discourse highlighting nurse shortages and resource constraints (World Health Organization 2020)—along with the busy reality observed during admission—may have lowered patients' predicted expectations, normalizing the perception that limitations in care provision are inevitable.

This understanding is supported by findings from a study conducted in Denmark and Norway, which demonstrated a high degree of congruence between patients' expectations of and experiences with psychosocial aspects of care, with incongruency mainly occurring when patients' experiences exceeded their expectations (Ingstad et al. 2023). Although these findings may suggest that nursing care delivery aligns well with patient expectations, they could also indicate that patients' expectations are relatively low. This may be shaped by the prevailing societal understanding of strained healthcare resources. If so, this alignment might reflect accommodation to systemic limitations rather than genuine satisfaction with care quality. This adaptive process may serve as a coping mechanism, helping patients maintain trust in the healthcare system, but it also risks obscuring gaps in nursing care by normalizing them.

In the current study, adaptation was not limited to accepting limitations—it evolved into a form of collaboration, in which patients took part in managing care demands and preserving system order. Participants frequently expressed a sense of shared responsibility for receiving timely and appropriate nursing care. Although they expected nurses to be attentive and responsive, they also felt obligated to help maintain order and avoid ‘bothering’ busy staff with what they perceived as minor needs. Instead, they waited patiently and refrained from using the call bell despite encouragement to do so or tried to manage without support due to fear of being a burden. This reciprocal awareness, although rooted in empathy and altruism, occasionally led participants to downplay their own discomfort or delay requesting help. This finding extends previous literature on the ‘good patient’ ideal, where patients aim to be undemanding, grateful, and considerate of busy staff (Hope et al. 2022; Maben et al. 2012). In such contexts, patients engage in emotional labour to minimize disruption, avoid burdening staff, and maintain positive relationships (Hope et al. 2022; Maben et al. 2012). At the same time, such behaviours may also reflect the inherent power asymmetry of the nurse–patient relationship (Delmar 2012). In situations where patients depend on nursing staff for essential care, expressions of empathy, compliance, and restraint may function as relational strategies that help patients navigate this asymmetry and sustain cooperative relationships with staff.

Importantly, this study adds to existing literature by demonstrating how such ‘good patient’ behaviours are not merely individual attitudes but are embedded in and reinforced by organizational conditions characterized by visible busyness, time pressure, and expectations of patient self‐advocacy. Although this behaviour demonstrates agency and empathy, it can also mask vulnerability and silence legitimate needs. The expectation to self‐advocate assumes physical and psychological capacity to do so—an assumption not all patients can meet. For those who are frail or uncertain about their entitlement to care, the responsibility for asking for help or support may inadvertently increase the risk of MNC. Although patients are uniquely positioned to identify and flag MNC (Kalisch et al. 2014), omissions may go unrecognized when patients remain silent out of consideration for staff workload while nurses simultaneously rely on them to articulate needs. At the same time, participants' accounts suggested that patients sometimes did speak up when they felt able to do so. Some described reminding nursing staff about missing medications, thereby contributing to the identification of omissions. However, other accounts illustrated how errors could be interpreted primarily through empathy for nursing staff. For example, one participant described receiving a tenfold medication dose—an error with clear potential for patient harm—but framed the incident in terms of the nurse's distress and workload. Such a strong sense of consideration for staff may unintentionally reduce opportunities for patients to contribute to the early recognition of potential safety problems. In this way, well‐intentioned empathy and restraint may paradoxically perpetuate a system in which omissions remain both unreported and normalized, highlighting a structural tension: systems that depend on patient activation may inadvertently disadvantage those least able to self‐advocate.

This tension aligns with principles articulated in person‐centered care frameworks, including the Fundamentals of Care framework, which explicitly positions the delivery of person‐centered fundamental care as the goal of nursing practice (Kitson 2018). Within this framework, high‐quality care depends on integrating patients' physical, psychosocial and relational needs through a trusting nurse–patient relationship and responsiveness to individual preferences and vulnerabilities (Kitson 2018). Ideally, nursing care should be adapted to the patient. However, when patients instead adapt to systemic limitations—by rationalizing omissions, lowering expectations, or postponing help‐seeking—the sense of responsibility subtly shifts. Rather than nursing care being structured around patients' needs, patients adjust to fit the system's capacity. This shift not only challenges the principles of person‐centered care but also helps explain how omissions in nursing care can become normalized within everyday practice. More broadly, these mechanisms can be situated within a prevailing ‘flow culture’ (Kirk and Nilsen 2015) in healthcare, where efficiency and patient throughput are prioritized over attentiveness and responsiveness to individual needs. Within such systems, nursing care risks becoming primarily task‐oriented, with care delivery guided by routines, procedures, and workflow demands rather than by patients' psychosocial and relational needs. Consequently, nurses' ability to provide person‐centered, fundamental care and prevent or respond to MNC may be constrained not only by time and staffing shortages but also by an underlying organizational logic that values efficiency over presence.

Over time, the normalization of missed or delayed nursing care may contribute to a subtle devaluation of nursing care itself. As omissions come to be seen as an inherent and inevitable part of everyday nursing practice, the threshold for what is considered ‘adequate nursing care’ gradually shifts downward, embedding MNC more deeply within the system. In turn, this devaluation risks eroding the professional identity of nursing by narrowing its scope to the completion of tasks while diminishing the visibility and perceived importance of its relational and psychosocial dimensions.

International initiatives such as the International Learning Collaborative, a global network established to develop, implement, and advance the Fundamentals of Care framework, have increasingly recognized these challenges and seek to re‐emphasize the relational and person‐centered foundations of nursing practice (Kitson 2018). These efforts highlight the need to reaffirm relational and psychosocial care as integral to everyday nursing in order to counteract the normalization of MNC and sustain nursing's holistic values. In this way, MNC can be understood as emerging from the interplay of structural constraints, professional practices and patient adaptations, whereas ongoing initiatives aim to restore the centrality of person‐centered care.

In sum, this study advances the understanding of MNC as a relational and co‐constructed phenomenon. Its unique contribution lies in showing how patients' empathy, self‐regulation, and sense of shared responsibility can inadvertently obscure unmet needs and contribute to the normalization of MNC. These findings underscore the importance of fostering communication and relational openness that enable patients to voice their needs without fear of burdening staff. Efforts to reduce MNC should therefore combine structural improvements with relational and cultural strategies that empower both patients and nurses to co‐create care environments where patients' needs can be safely expressed and addressed.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is its in‐depth qualitative approach, which enabled rich and nuanced insights into how patients experience, interpret, and respond to MNC in everyday hospital contexts. Rather than focusing solely on whether MNC occurred, the study explored how patients understood and interpreted such situations in relation to their expectations, needs, and the broader care environment. In doing so, it extends the conceptual understanding of MNC beyond measurement and prevalence. Furthermore, the sample included patients with varying conditions and lengths of stay, allowing for diverse perspectives. Another strength lies in the analytic process, which involved several researchers through ongoing discussions and reflections, thereby enhancing the credibility of the interpretations through researcher triangulation.

However, the study also has limitations. Findings are based on self‐reported experiences, which may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability, particularly given patients' strong empathy and gratitude toward nursing staff. Although the interviews aimed to create a safe and open atmosphere, it cannot be ruled out that some participants may have moderated their accounts to protect nurses or maintain a positive relationship with the healthcare system. Moreover, it remains a broader methodological challenge whether patients can always recognize or articulate MNC, particularly when they view omissions as understandable and inevitable, or when they are unsure about their entitlements and what constitutes high‐quality nursing care. It is also possible that participants who chose to volunteer for interviews were those who felt relatively comfortable reflecting on their experiences, whereas patients who were more dissatisfied or frustrated with their care may have disengaged and chosen not to participate. Similarly, patients with severe illness, cognitive impairment, or communication difficulties—who may be at increased risk of experiencing MNC—were not represented in the sample. As a result, the study may more strongly reflect the perspectives of patients who were able and willing to articulate their experiences and engage in reflective dialogue. Finally, as the study was conducted within the Danish healthcare context, findings may not be directly transferable to systems with different cultural or organizational norms.

Recommendations for Further Research

Future research should examine how patients' adaptive strategies—such as rationalizing MNC or moderating help‐seeking—manifest across different healthcare settings and patient groups. Mixed‐methods approaches are particularly well suited to capture both the depth of patients' experiences and broader patterns of nursing care delivery, linking qualitative insights to measurable outcomes.

Research should also investigate how communication practices, ward culture, and organizational norms shape patients' willingness and perceived legitimacy to express needs. Building on this, studies are needed to identify barriers and facilitators to person‐centered nursing care, including how relational, cultural and structural factors influence the ability of nursing staff to recognize and respond to patients' expressed and unspoken needs. Such studies could inform interventions that enable patients and staff to co‐create care environments where all needs can be safely voiced and addressed.

Implications

The findings of this study highlight the need to address MNC not only as a question of staffing and resources but also as a relational and cultural issue within nursing practice. Reducing MNC therefore requires coordinated action across multiple levels, combining structural improvements with increased attention to the relational, communicative, and person‐centered dimensions of nursing care. Although this study does not provide a basis for specifying or evaluating interventions, it points to key areas that warrant attention.

At the clinical level, the findings suggest the importance of proactive relational practices in which nurses do not rely solely on patients to articulate their needs but actively create opportunities for patients to express concerns and expectations. The findings further underscore the importance of recognizing psychosocial and relational care as integral components of nursing practice, even in time‐constrained settings. At the organizational level, the findings indicate a need to more clearly acknowledge and support the relational dimensions of nursing care within everyday clinical work. This includes ensuring that such aspects are not rendered invisible in prioritization processes and that nursing staff are supported in balancing technical, administrative and relational responsibilities. At the educational and policy levels, the study points to the relevance of strengthening attention to relational and person‐centered care as core professional competencies.

The findings also suggest that patients may internalize systemic limitations, leading to lowered expectations of what constitutes adequate nursing care and, in some cases, a reluctance to express unmet needs. Such normalization risks obscuring instances of MNC and diminishing the visibility of nursing care as a professional contribution to patient recovery and well‐being. Addressing this challenge requires that policymakers, nursing leadership, and educators explicitly recognize person‐centered care—including integration of physical, psychosocial and relational elements of care—as a core priority of care rather than a secondary concern. Strengthening the importance and visibility of person‐centered care may support professional identity and contribute to more responsive and comprehensive nursing care. However, further research is needed to develop and evaluate how such approaches can be effectively supported and sustained in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study advances understanding of MNC by foregrounding patients' perspectives and conceptualizing it as a relational, co‐constructed phenomenon. Rather than viewing omissions and delays as neglect or individual failure, patients often responded with empathy, rationalization and adaptive strategies that preserved trust and cooperation within strained care environments.

By demonstrating how patients actively interpret and negotiate omissions in nursing care, the study highlights that well‐intentioned adaptations can obscure unmet needs and inadvertently normalize MNC. The findings underscore that addressing MNC requires moving beyond resource‐focused explanations to also consider the relational and cultural processes that shape how nursing care is experienced and enacted in everyday practice.

Understanding MNC through patients' lived experiences further reveals how nursing care delivered under pressure may lead patients to downplay or suppress their own needs to avoid burdening nursing staff. Efforts to address MNC should therefore foster care environments that proactively invite and respond to patients' needs, rather than relying on patients to self‐advocate.
