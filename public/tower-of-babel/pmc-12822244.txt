O-CCR: oriented cervical canal region detection framework toward cervical change assessment in transvaginal ultrasound
Authors: Minseo Hwangbo, Yeong-Eun Jeon, Kyong-No Lee, Keun-Young Lee, Jae Jun Lee, Ga-Hyun Son, Dong-Ok Won
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe cervix undergoes morphological and structural changes during pregnancy in preparation for delivery. Assessing the progression of these changes using transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) is crucial for preterm birth prediction. However, existing methods such as cervical length have limitations in capturing subtle tissue changes. Although tissue analysis using TVUS has been explored to address these limitations, achieving consistent and reproducible results in quantitative analysis remains challenging due to high inter-observer variability and a lack of standardized region of interest (ROI) definitions. This study proposes an oriented cervical canal region (O-CCR) framework that identifies regions containing ultrasound features while preserving anatomical spatial information.MethodsWe utilized 1436 TVUS images for training, validation, and testing, with 189 additional images from a different hospital for external validation. CCR was defined to include the cervical canal and its surrounding region after aligning the IO and EO parallel to ensure anatomical consistency in the cervix. To validate the effectiveness of O-CCR in handling various orientations, we applied five oriented object detection models (Oriented R-CNN, ReDet, S2A-Net, R3Det, and Oriented RepPoints) and evaluated their CCR localization performance.ResultsWe compared the performance of five models implemented within O-CCR framework. Among them, Oriented RepPoints achieved the highest average precision (AP) of 0.981 at the intersection over union (IoU) threshold of 0.5, compared to Oriented R-CNN (0.968), S2A-Net (0.962), ReDet (0.964), and R3Det (0.980) on the test dataset. Notably, Oriented RepPoints demonstrated superior performance even at higher thresholds of 0.6 (0.931) and 0.7 (0.743) and the lowest average orientation error (AOE) of 9.1980 in CCR localization.ConclusionThe proposed O-CCR framework exhibited reliable performance in CCR localization regardless of varying orientations and morphological configurations, while providing standardized regions that preserve the anatomical spatial information of the cervix. The consistent CCR could be applied to quantitative analysis of cervical tissue properties in future research. Ultimately, this approach could support the development of automated cervical change assessment for prenatal care.keywords: Deep learning, Oriented object detection, Cervix, Cervical canal region (CCR), Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS)

Introduction

Preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation, is a major risk factor accounting for at least 50% of all neonatal deaths [1]. Furthermore, serious complications resulting from preterm birth, such as chronic lung disease, hearing and visual impairments, and neurodevelopmental disabilities, often lead to long-term functional impairment and impose a substantial burden on healthcare systems [2]. The cervix plays a crucial role in successful delivery [3]. As delivery approaches, the cervix undergoes changes in tissue composition including collagen remodeling and increased water content, which lead to morphological and structural alterations that facilitate fetal passage [4, 5]. However, various risk factors such as cervical incompetence and infectious agents can cause premature cervical changes, substantially increasing the risk of preterm birth [6–9]. Therefore, evaluation of cervical changes can be effectively utilized for analyzing various preterm birth risk factors, making it crucial for early identification of high-risk populations and prevention of preterm birth through appropriate interventions.

In current clinical practice, cervical length (CL) measured by transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) is widely used as a representative indicator for cervical change assessment [10]. However, as a length-based indicator, it has limitations in sufficiently reflecting subtle changes in tissue composition. To comprehensively and accurately evaluate cervical changes resulting from various factors, additional ultrasonic imaging features need to be considered alongside CL. TVUS can visualize changes such as speckle patterns and the presence or absence of cervical gland area (CGA) according to tissue compositional changes, which can be manifested in ultrasound images through pixel-based analysis including echogenicity and texture [11–14]. These features demonstrate that morphological and structural changes in cervical tissues as well as microscopic pixel-level alterations can serve as important indicators alongside CL.

Nevertheless, unlike CL, these features are difficult to quantify and require numerical analysis of distribution patterns for specific regions within the entire image. Moreover, if the regions are not consistently defined, it becomes difficult to compare between studies and quantitatively interpret tissue changes. Previous studies have manually designated regions of interest (ROI) and analyzed features from them [11–15]. One study indicated that excluding interfering elements such as fetal head and probe areas enabled more focused cervical feature analysis [15]. However, most of these approaches define ROI based on expert manual annotation, requiring additional labeling processes for practical implementation, which limits automation. Furthermore, the cervix in TVUS images exhibits low contrast with surrounding tissues, making boundary delineation difficult. This can lead to inter-observer variability in region definition and requires considerable time and manpower for manual extraction. Moreover, spatial information within the ROI may be lost during the quantification process. Particularly, different anatomical structures of the cervix such as anterior, posterior, cervical canal, internal os (IO), and external os (EO) exhibit different patterns of change. This necessitates structure-specific ROI designation for comparative analysis. Therefore, establishing consistent ROIs that encompass various features of change is essential for effectively analyzing subtle cervical changes according to anatomical structure.

Recent studies have demonstrated that deep learning-based approaches achieve high performance in localizing organs and lesions from various ultrasound images [16–19]. These techniques show promise for effective utilization in automated cervical extraction from TVUS images [20–25]. Several recent studies have demonstrated the potential for automation in cervical region analysis by segmenting cervical areas using deep learning technology [20–22]. However, segmentation-based approaches have the limitation that detailed anatomical information is not reflected within the extracted regions, requiring redefinition of separate ROIs for comparative analysis of region-specific features. Another study proposed an approach for anatomical structure-based segmentation of the cervix [23–25]. Such research is advantageous for obtaining accurate information about morphology and size through detailed extraction of specific structures. However, standardization processes are necessary for cervical features that appear in various orientations and forms through TVUS. Additionally, segmentation methods require precise mask labeling for training data collection. This poses a burden on actual clinical workflows, as complex labeling demands considerable time and effort from experts. Additionally, due to the high variability between devices and operators inherent in ultrasound imaging, retraining optimized for each hospital’s protocol is essential for clinical implementation [26]. Therefore, automated extraction methods for consistent and standardized ROIs that encompass various key areas without additional labeling can be critically important for analyzing preterm birth risk factors and consistently extracting cervical changes in actual clinical practice.

In this study, we propose an oriented cervical canal region (O-CCR) framework that consistently detects the regions containing sonographic features while preserving anatomical spatial information. The proposed approach provides a standardized cervical canal region (CCR) as an ROI by aligning various cervical morphologies based on anatomical reference points, the IO and EO. Furthermore, we implemented oriented object detection models to effectively detect the simple oriented box-shaped ROIs without complex labeling. The proposed framework is expected to facilitate the consistent comparison and analysis of structure-specific tissue changes by providing a standardized ROI based on anatomical landmarks. In particular, it can be effectively applied to actual clinical environments without imposing clinical burden by normalizing various cervical morphologies through simple oriented box labeling, thereby eliminating the need for precise segmentation masks.

Methods

Dataset acquisition

From 2019 to 2022, pregnant women who underwent TVUS examinations at Hallym University Kangnam Sacred Heart Hospital and Chungnam National University Hospital during the gestational periods of 16–24 or 36–40 weeks. The TVUS procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1(a). Afterward, pregnant women who gave birth at the same hospital or provided comprehensive delivery information were retrospectively included in the study. Gestational age was determined from the last menstrual period and validated by measuring crown-rump length using first-trimester ultrasonography.

Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) examination procedure, representative TVUS image, and anatomical illustration of cervical structures within the ultrasound plane (a). Overview of the O-CCR framework (b)

Still-cut grayscale cervical images were extracted from the collected ultrasound scans from the image storage and communication database system in JPEG format. We eliminated inappropriate images using the following criteria: 1) images of low resolution or poor quality that hindered the identification of the cervical shape (e.g., out-of-focus or shadowed); 2) images displaying cervical cerclage knots; and 3) images presenting anomalies.

We also excluded patients with the following conditions that could influence delivery outcomes: 1) twin pregnancy; 2) uterine anomalies; 3) fetal anomalies or confirmed chromosomal abnormalities; 4) patients who received cervical cerclage after collecting the ultrasound scan and delivered at term; and 5) medically indicated preterm births.

Among the images acquired, those acquired at approximately 20 weeks of gestation were selected. Grayscale images of individuals were incorporated in the study, and all images were anonymized. First, we obtained ultrasound images at resolutions ranging from 715 × 536 to 1494 × 959 pixels. The actual sizes of the images were produced using pixelcms, a picture archiving and communication system that specifies precise centimeters per pixel.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Hallym University Kangnam Sacred Heart Hospital (IRB No. 2023–06-007).

O-CCR framework

The IO and EO of the cervix are rarely positioned horizontally, and both their orientation and shape vary among individuals. To address this variability, we propose a deep learning-based O-CCR framework for anatomically consistent CCR detection in TVUS images (Fig. 1(b)).

The framework consists of two parts: 1) CCR definition based on IO-EO alignment, which reflects cervical orientation, and 2) automated CCR localization using object detection models. The model is trained on preprocessed TVUS images with ground truth annotations derived from IO-EO alignment. During inference, it automatically predicts rotated bounding boxes that precisely localize the CCR without manual intervention.

Oriented object detection for O-CCR

In this study, we apply existing oriented object detection models to CCR detection in TVUS images. The oriented object detection pipeline operates through several key stages. First, backbone networks of models extract high-level feature maps from input TVUS images, encoding rich visual information essential for object detection. Second, different types of detectors employ distinct strategies for handling rotated objects: two-stage detectors generate oriented region proposals through modified region proposal networks (RPNs), single-stage detectors perform direct regression to oriented bounding boxes, while anchor-free models utilize point-based representations. Third, detectors incorporate their respective specialized techniques, such as rotated RoI pooling, feature alignment modules, or rotation-equivariant representations, to handle the geometric variations of rotated objects. Fourth, the extracted features are used to classify objects and perform precise regression of the five parameters defining the bounding box , where θ represents the clockwise rotation angle in radians measured from the horizontal axis. Finally, rotated IoU-based Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) is applied to eliminate redundant detections and select the most confident oriented bounding boxes.

During training, these models utilize preprocessed TVUS images as input and convert the four corner coordinates extracted from the preprocessing stage into the rotated bounding box format used as ground truth. At inference, the trained models predict precise CCR in new TVUS images without manual IO and EO marking, enabling consistent automated detection while maintaining clinical accuracy. To evaluate automated CCR detection performance, we selected five representative oriented object detection models.

Baseline model: Oriented R-CNN Oriented R-CNN is a model that extends the traditional regions with convolutional neural networks features (R-CNN) framework to effectively detect rotated objects [27]. Figure 2 (a) shows the structure of Oriented R-CNN. It is a two-stage detection framework for oriented object detection that achieves a practical balance between speed and accuracy. In the first stage, it generates multiscale feature maps using a residual network (ResNet) backbone and feature pyramid network (FPN) [28]. It then efficiently generates high-quality oriented region proposals using an oriented RPN. In the second stage, the oriented standard ROI head classifies each proposal and utilizes a rotated ROI align to adjust the position and size of each candidate region precisely. This model was selected as our baseline model because it maintains structural simplicity while demonstrating stable performance across various benchmarks.

Architectures of representative models employed in the O-CCR framework: oriented R-CNN (a) as baseline model and oriented RepPoints (b) as point-based model

Comparison with other O-CCR detection models Various innovative approaches have been proposed to detect oriented objects. Rotation-equivariant detector (ReDet) is a two-stage model that leverages a rotation-equivariant backbone, ReResNet, and ReFPN to extract rotation-equivariant features. These features are converted into oriented bounding boxes via an RPN and ROI Transformer (RT), and the final predictions are made using rotation-invariant ROI Align (RiROI Align). Benefiting from rotation-equivariant representations throughout the pipeline, ReDet achieves consistent performance on arbitrarily oriented objects [29].

Single-shot alignment network (S2A-Net) and R3Det are single-stage rotated object detectors. S2A-Net consists of a feature alignment module (FAM) and an oriented detection module (ODM). The FAM aligns anchor boxes with feature maps using an anchor refinement network and alignment convolution, addressing spatial misalignment. The ODM employs active rotating filters to generate orientation-sensitive and orientation-invariant features, reducing the gap between classification and localization. This design enables high detection accuracy in complex rotational scenarios [30].

Refined rotation RetinaNet (R3Det) adopts a coarse-to-fine regression strategy for accurate localization. To address feature misalignment, it introduces a feature refinement module (FRM), which reconstructs feature maps using pixel-wise interpolation guided by refined bounding boxes. Furthermore, an approximate SkewIoU loss is employed for accurate angle regression. These enhancements lead to robust performance on objects with high aspect ratios, dense layouts, and arbitrary orientations [31].

Oriented RepPoints, shown in Fig. 2 (b), is an anchor-free, single-stage model that learns representative points for classification, regression, and orientation prediction through a shared head. It applies adaptive point assessment and assignment (APAA) to select high-quality point samples. By combining point deformation and adaptive representation, the model captures geometric properties more precisely and achieves strong performance in challenging rotational settings [32].

Experimental setting

As shown in Fig. 3, we used 1436 images, excluding 33 images, from Hallym University Kangnam Sacred Heart Hospital. These images were divided into 1006 training, 143 validation, and 287 test images in a ratio of 7:1:2. For external validation, we employed 189 images collected from the Chungnam National University Hospital. All the images were resized to 1024 × 1024 pixels and normalized. We then applied random vertical, horizontal, and diagonal flip augmentations to enhance the model generalization across various situations. We implemented five oriented object detection models using MMRotate [33]. The models were retrained using pre-trained weights from the dataset for Object detection in aerial images (DOTA), a large-scale aerial image dataset [34]. Training was performed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 Ti GPU using a SGD optimizer (momentum: 0.9, weight decay: 0.0001) with a learning rate of 0.001, batch size of 2, and 15 epochs.

Evaluation metrics

To evaluate model performance, we calculate the Intersection over Union (IoU) between predicted and ground truth bounding boxes. The IoU is computed by dividing the area of intersection between two bounding boxes by the area of their union. Detections with IoU ≥ threshold are considered True Positives.

Based on this, we compute precision, recall and average precision (AP). Precision represents the proportion of actual objects among all detections made by the model, while recall indicates the proportion of actual objects correctly detected by the model. AP is a metric that comprehensively reflects the relationship between precision and recall at various confidence thresholds. We calculated AP using the all-point integration method as the area under the precision-recall curve.

The performance evaluation results are presented in tables for IoU thresholds of 0.5, 0.6, and 0.7, showing both recall and AP values. As the IoU threshold increases, the positional accuracy requirements become more stringent.

Following Oriented RepPoints [32], we utilized the average orientation error (AOE) to evaluate orientation accuracy. For detections with IoU above a threshold, we calculated the angle difference between ground truth and predicted bounding boxes. Angles were normalized to 0 to π radians, and we computed absolute differences. When differences exceeded π/2, we used the complementary angle (π minus difference). These values were converted to degrees, and their mean was used as the AOE metric.

Discussion

This study proposes the O-CCR framework for consistently detecting regions containing ultrasonographic features while preserving anatomical spatial information in TVUS. The proposed framework provides significant improvements compared to existing cervical automatic extraction studies in TVUS. Deep learning-based segmentation studies perform segmentation for specific cervical regions [20–25]. This approach is similar in that it automatically extracts ROI in TVUS and has the advantage of being able to extract accurate morphology and size. However, within the extracted ROI, it is difficult to determine the location of organs or the directionality of structures. Therefore, even when extracting the same organ, there are difficulties in comparing the extracted regions. Additionally, there is a limitation that data collection is relatively difficult due to the need for precise mask labeling for training. Our framework significantly improved clinical applicability by automatically extracting aligned, simple box-shaped regions while preserving anatomical spatial information. In particular, it is differentiated from existing studies by providing ROI that enables comparative analysis of change patterns by structure through standardization of different anatomical structures such as anterior, posterior, and cervical canal through an alignment method using IO and EO as anatomical reference points.

We employed five oriented object detection models to identify diverse orientations of the cervix, all of which successfully detected CCR. Among these, Oriented RepPoints achieved the highest AP at IoU thresholds of 0.5 (0.981), 0.6 (0.931), and 0.7 (0.743) and the lowest AOE (9.1980), accurately capturing the position and orientation of CCR in the test dataset (Tables 1, 3). In external validation, all models experienced performance degradation at IoU 0.7 due to inter-hospital protocol differences and different manufacturer equipment characteristics. Nevertheless, they still demonstrated meaningful performance at IoU 0.5 (S2A-Net: AP 0.858) and 0.6 (S2A-Net: AP 0.751) (Table 2). Since the purpose of this study is standardized alignment of anatomical structures rather than precise pixel-level boundary extraction, detection results in the IoU 0.5–0.6 range can be clinically utilized sufficiently if alignment through IO and EO reference points is performed accurately. As shown in Fig. 6, even when the detected CCR is at IoU 0.5–0.6 level, it can provide reliable ROI for subsequent analysis by appropriately aligning major anatomical structures of anterior, posterior, and cervical canal through excellent orientation estimation. Therefore, the proposed O-CCR framework demonstrated the clinical applicability of automated aligned CCR detection that can be used for analysis by comprehensively evaluating AP and AOE.

Our study has several limitations. First, detection accuracy tended to decrease when the cervical canal was not clearly visible in ultrasound images due to speckle noise, shadows, and distortions (Figs. 4, 5 (b)). The cervical canal, which represents the interface between the two mucosal layers, appears as an echogenic central line in the ultrasound image [35]. As illustrated in Fig. 7, detection ambiguities may arise when the echogenic line of the cervical canal appears indistinct or when echo patterns resembling the cervical canal are present in non-canal regions. Second, incorporating TVUS image data from diverse hospitals and devices is necessary. Ultrasound equipment can have different characteristics, as manufacturers have their own image processing methods such as filtering and speckle reduction [26]. Approximately 50% of our external validation data was collected using ultrasound equipment by different manufacturers from those used for the training data (Fig. 5 (b)). These images from different equipment showed brighter echogenicity and lower contrast. Additionally, although standardized ultrasound examination protocols are established, detailed procedural variations can occur between hospitals or examiners. Nevertheless, these limitations are problems commonly faced by ultrasound image-based automatic analysis systems, and it is expected that the generalization performance of the model can be further improved through multi-institutional dataset construction and application of data augmentation techniques through future multi-institutional collaboration. In particular, the simple box annotation method of this study presents a practical solution by minimizing the data collection burden necessary for such extension studies.

The O-CCR framework has important clinical significance in several aspects. First, by standardizing various forms of cervix through anatomical reference points of IO and EO, it consistently identifies regions containing important ultrasonographic features of the cervix. Through this, fine tissue change patterns at the pixel level, such as echogenicity and texture, can be easily used for structural analysis showing different change patterns such as anterior, posterior, cervical canal, internal os, and external os. Second, it reduces the workload of clinicians and minimizes inter-examiner variability through an automated system that does not require manual ROI setting. This enables reproducible analysis by providing consistent ROI and improving the efficiency of the clinical workflow. Third, training data construction is possible with simple box annotation instead of complex mask labeling, making data collection optimized for various ultrasound devices and hospital environments easy. These characteristics lower the barriers to adoption in actual clinical settings. Therefore, the O-CCR provides a solution in normalized cervical region detection, offering a promising approach for more consistent and precise analysis of cervical changes in ultrasound imaging.

Conclusion

This study proposes a novel O-CCR framework for automated extraction of consistent ROI by normalizing anatomical spatial information through alignment of various cervical morphologies in TVUS images. The proposed method establishes standardized CCR by aligning the cervix based on IO and EO in TVUS images to normalize anatomical spatial information and automatically extract these regions. Additionally, we evaluated CCR localization and orientation estimation performance using oriented object detection models, and the results suggest that the proposed framework has potential for useful application in automated CCR identification in clinical environments. Specifically, the proposed framework can be effectively utilized for analyzing anatomical variation patterns by standardizing various cervical presentations observed in TVUS. This demonstrates clinical value by providing a ROI that can reflect pixel-level morphological changes across the entire cervix. Furthermore, this framework can reduce clinical workload through automated analysis without requiring additional labeling by clinicians, and unlike existing segmentation-based models, it facilitates easy collection of additional training data considering compatibility across various ultrasound systems and hospital environments, thereby accelerating adoption in real clinical settings. Although future research should consider constraints such as imaging characteristic differences between various ultrasound equipment, it still shows potential for effective utilization in actual clinical workflows. Consequently, this research can be effectively utilized for analyzing preterm birth risk factors through TVUS-based structural change patterns and may contribute to the development of automated monitoring systems for high-risk preterm birth detection.
