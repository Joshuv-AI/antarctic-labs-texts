Salinity-responsive hyperaccumulation of flavonoids in Spirodela polyrrhiza, resultant maneuvering in the structure and antimicrobial as well as azo dye decontamination profile of biofabricated zinc oxide nanoentities
Authors: Santanu Gupta, Abir Das, Kuhely Ganguli, Nilakshi Chakraborty, Mohammad Reza Fayezizadeh, Sudipta Kumar Sil, Malay Kumar Adak, Mirza Hasanuzzaman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Duckweeds (Spirodela polyrrhiza) are free-floating macrophytes that grow profusely in nutrient-rich waters. Under ideal conditions, they exhibit a rapid growth rate and can absorb a substantial amount of nutrients, macromolecules, and pollutants from bodies of water. Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) synthesized from plant extracts, particularly under stress conditions, have opened new research avenues in the field of nanotechnology. Under salinity stress, the accumulation of flavonoids in duckweeds can affect the structure of ZnO NPs, helping researchers ascertain their antimicrobial role. In our study, we exposed mid-log phase duckweed monocultures to 75 mM NaCl in a full-strength Murashige and Skoog medium for 7 days, followed by a 15-day recovery period. We observed significant overexpression of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide as reactive oxygen species. As a result, chlorophyll and certain metabolites were produced in lesser amounts, while flavonoid and phenol content increased by 12% and 8%, respectively. This overproduction persisted up to 10 days into the recovery treatment period but dropped by 8% and 5%, respectively, by the 15th day. The flavonoid coating transformed the NPs into rosette clusters, which exhibited reduced antimicrobial activity against Aeromonas hydrophila, a Gram-negative, fish-pathogenic bacterium. Herein, we discuss potential mechanisms for the conformational transformation of ZnO NPs into finer dimensions in response to NaCl-induced oxidative stress in duckweed. In this study, the azo dye degradation capacity of salinity-treated plants increased as the flavonoid profile became enriched. Zinc oxide nanoparticles, both prior to and after salinity treatment, were found to be efficient in scavenging azo dye and mitigating its toxicity, as evidenced by improved germination, growth, and overall plant morphometry.

Background

Duckweed, a free-floating macrophyte of the Lemnaceae family, grows profusely in waters rich in nutrient content. The plant is represented by several species, including Lemna, Wolffia, Spirodela, Wolfiella, and Landoltia1. Under ideal conditions, duckweed exhibits rapid growth and can absorb a substantial amount of nutrients, macromolecules, and pollutants from bodies of water. Its resilience to extreme conditions, such as metal toxicity, means it can significantly contribute to the decontamination of polluted bodies of water or wastewater-containing micropollutants2. Duckweed generally prefers stagnant bodies of fresh water, such as ponds, ditches, and lakes, for their luxuriant growth, as these environments favor better solvation of inorganic pollutants. The growth of duckweed can be notably accelerated by the accumulation of phosphorus and nitrogen within their vacuolar sap in the form of oligocyclic and high-molecular phosphate residues2,3.

Spirodela polyrrhiza (L.) Schleid, commonly known as greater duckweed or giant duckweed, is cosmopolitan in its occurrence in eutrophic bodies of water in tropical countries. The fronds of S. polyrrhiza (L.) Schleid are characterized by the highest degree of structural reduction, particularly when exposed to diverse environmental pollutants4. Similarly, salt stress impacts plants through osmotic and ionic stress, as high salt concentrations diminish soil’s water-holding potential, leading to reduced water uptake by plant roots5,6. Conversely, an excessive accumulation of ions such as Na+ and Cl− in plant cells results in toxicity or nutritional disorders7. Salinity tolerance in plants can activate and alter the expression of numerous genes of various functional groups that are responsible for minimizing the effects of excess salt8,9. These genes are associated with photosynthetic proteins, proteins linked to transport into the vacuole, the synthesis of osmolytes, membrane channels, and the activation of protectors against reactive oxygen species (ROS), along with signaling or regulatory elements, including transcription factors. In previous studies, the overexpression of genes involved in ROS removal resulted in decreased cell damage, maintenance of photosynthetic efficiency, and improved root growth under saline conditions10,11. A similar effect has been observed with the overexpression of H+ and potassium antiport channels, which facilitate the accumulation of biomass and enhance tolerance to salinity12.

Flavonoids, also known as vitamin P, constitute a vast class of secondary metabolites in plants, encompassing over 10,000 structures. They are characterized by a low molecular weight and a general structure comprising three rings, including two phenyl rings (A and B) and a heterocyclic ring (C). With different substituent groups, flavonoids can be categorized into seven subgroups: chalcones, flavones, flavonols, flavandiols, anthocyanins, condensed tannins, and aurones13–15. Certain plant species synthesize specialized forms of flavonoids, such as isoflavonoids16,17. In recent developments, nanoparticles (NPs) have been synthesized using various methods, including chemical, physical, and arc methods that require direct current power supplies and commercially available copper rods18. However, biogenic synthesis, a well-accepted method of synthesis that employs medicinal plant parts, has gained in popularity for producing biocompatible NPs19, which are known to enhance biological functions. Metal oxide NPs, particularly ZnO NPs, have garnered interest for their antimicrobial and biocidal properties20. Plant-mediated synthesis has been recognized for being mild, eco-friendly, and cost- and time-effective, with plant phytochemicals and bioactive components serving as reducing, capping, and stabilizing agents21,22. Various types of flavonoid nanocarriers are currently utilized in cancer therapy, including polymeric NPs, nanocapsules, metallic NPs23–25, and other metal oxide-supported lipid nanocarriers26. Plant compounds found in plant extracts function as both reducing agents and capping or stabilizing agents in the bioreduction process, in which metal ions or metal oxides are reduced to zero-valence metal NPs with the aid of plant-secreted phytochemicals, such as polyphenolic compounds, alkaloids, polysaccharides, amino acids, vitamins, and terpenoids27–29.

Azo dyes play an important role in modern dye-based industries, such as textiles, paper, cosmetics, and stationary items. In confectionaries, food-grade dyes are used. Although considered safe for use, long-term exposure poses a threat to humans and to the environment30. The deleterious effects of azo dyes on humans and aquatic flora and fauna have triggered urgent calls for the treatment of effluents containing these dyes to either degrade them or convert them into less toxic substances31. Azo dyes with more azo bonds have a higher molecular mass, and it has been established that the higher the molecular mass, the greater the toxicity of these dyes32. Efforts are being made to decontaminate water sources polluted with azo dyes using various biomaterials or biofabricated NPs33. The extracts of various plants, often with the addition of bacteria, have been employed for this purpose. Additionally, attempts have been made to enhance the efficacy of plants in scavenging azo dyes through different treatments, such as salinity, stress application, and metabolite modulation. These treatments aim to upscale the biosynthesis of suitable metabolites for azo dye degradation.

In this study, we investigated the possible effects of salinity treatment at previously reported concentrations, noting a wide range of responses from both physiological and nanobiotechnological perspectives. We examined the structure and application-oriented aspects of biofabricated zinc oxide (ZnO) NPs prior to and after salinity treatment. This examination was crucial for understanding their potential formulations and for outlining a strategy for their production and utilization. It was observed that salinity triggered the accumulation and diversification of flavonoids, which, in turn, induced the agglomeration of ZnO NPs into the form of rosette structures. These structures exhibited weak antimicrobial activity; however, upon sonication, they disintegrated into fine particles that showed enhanced potential against A. hydrophila. It was also observed that salinity induces azo dye decontamination efficacy not only in plant extracts but also in ZnO NPs biofabricated in response to salinity treatment and consequent flavonoid hyperaccumulation. Thus, an aquatic plant with luxuriant growth capability can also be employed in the detoxification of natural water reservoirs through multichannel approaches: antimicrobial activity, countering ROS, and azo dye degradation. This study is a pioneering attempt to explore the counter-pollution potentiality of weed-like plants with profuse growth rates in the tropics and subtropics, particularly in the region examined in this study.

Materials and methods

Sampling and cultivation

Duckweed specimens were sampled from five perennial freshwater bodies in the Malda district, West Bengal, India. The plant samples, collected from the Central National Herbarium (CNH) located at Shibpur in the AJC Bose Indian Botanic Garden, Kolkata, India (CAL), were identified as Spirodela polyrrhiza (L.) by Santanu Gupta. The specimen deposition letter reference number was CNH/Tech.II/2018/14, and the voucher specimen number was SG-04. The samples were cultured on Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal medium for 45 days under the following conditions: a temperature of 24 ± 2 °C, a light intensity of 2,500 lx, and a 16/8-h photoperiod34.

Biomass collection and extraction

Biomass from the mid-log phase of growth (15–18 days post-inoculation) was harvested, with each sample weighing 2 g. The biomass was extracted with deionized water and then lyophilized34,35 using a Labogene Scanvac CoolSafe 55 − 4 freeze dryer to form a powder.

Biosynthesis of NPs and biofabrication

The synthesis of ZnO NPs was achieved using a biofabrication approach, as detailed in a previous study36. Two hundred milligrams of the lyophilized powder was redissolved in 20 mL of deionized water and warmed to 60 °C. A mixture of 5 mL of 0.1 N caustic soda and 5 mL of 1% aqueous zinc acetate solution was added to the plant extract at room temperature. The reaction mixture was vortexed for 3 h and allowed to stand overnight at room temperature. The prepared suspensions were centrifuged twice at 5,000 rpm at room temperature37. The residue was resuspended in 5 mL deionized water, precipitated, oven-dried at 80 °C, and incubated at 50 °C for 7 days. The dried granules were analyzed under scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to determine the shape and size of the NPs.

Assay for polyphenols, flavonoids, and DPPH activity

For the determination of polyphenols and antioxidant activity, 0.5 g of fresh sample was incubated for 24 h with 95 mL of ethyl alcohol (98%)38. To evaluate antioxidant activity, 0.1 mL of plant extract was mixed with 2.9 mL of 60 µM 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and incubated for 180 min for a complete reaction. The absorbance was read at 515 nm. Data processing was performed according to the following formula:

Chromogenic absorbance testing

The suspension’s chromogenic absorbance39 was measured across a wavelength range of 190–900 nm using an Agilent Cary 60 UV-Vis spectrophotometer.

Salinity treatment

In a parallel experimental setup, an equal amount of biomass (2.0 g) was treated with a 75 mM aqueous NaCl solution, maintaining the same physicochemical parameters as the control group. This salinity-treated biomass was processed similarly for the extract preparation and biofabrication of NPs.

Characterization of NPs

The chemical identity and hydrodynamic size of the NPs were assessed through energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis, respectively. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy was used to identify the functional groups involved in surface interactions with the NPs under both salinity stress and non-stress conditions40,41.

Flavonoid and amino acid profiling

Hydromethanolic extracts (1:1 v/v) with and without salinity treatment were subjected to liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) for flavonoid spectrum analysis using 50% aqueous acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid in a Milli-Q water solvent system (80:20 v/v) on a ZORBAX column42. Amino acid profiling was conducted using an HPLC (Perkin Elmer® LC300) with a C-19 Zodiac column43.

DPPH assay

A DPPH assay was performed to evaluate free radical formation in the freshly collected biomass, steady-state, salinity stress treated, and recovery phase cultures of duckweed species. To evaluate antioxidant activity, 0.1 mL of plant extract was mixed with 2.9 mL of 60 µM DPPH and incubated for 180 min for a complete reaction. The absorbance was read at 515 nm44.

Estimation of total phenols

The total phenol content was determined as follows: 0.1 mL of leaf extract was mixed with Folin–Ciocâlteu reagent, and then 7.5% NaCO3 was added. After 90 min of incubation, the samples were read using a spectrophotometer at 760 nm. The results were interpreted according to the calibration curve as gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry mass35,46.

Dye decolorization and degradation tests

Dye decolorization and degradation tests were conducted47. In this study, methyl orange (MO) was used as the azo dye. In a standard decolorization test, 0.10 g of the die was dissolved in 100 mL of solution and mixed in separate tests with the following combinations.

Prior to and after saline treatment, 20 mL of double-filtered aqueous plant extract were separately incubated in 10 mL of aqueous solution for 5 h in the dark with occasional shaking. At regular intervals, the test mixtures were visually observed for color changes. Supernatants (6,000 rpm, 4 °C) were then processed for optical density values (Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer) at a wavelength of 464 nm.

Similar experiments were carried out with ZnO NP suspensions biofabricated with untreated and saline-treated duckweed biomass (500 ng mL−1). The absorbance (at a wavelength of 464 nm) of the dye was checked at intervals of 30 min, starting from 0 to 600 min at 4 °C. The dye decolorization percentage (DD%) was calculated using the following formula:

An FTIR spectroscopy spectrum of the 0.1% w/v dye solution and the 15-h incubation product was analyzed to check for the presence or absence of the wave number (1,525 cm−1) corresponding to the azo bond (N = N).

Assessment of intact and degraded dye toxicity

Toxicity assessment was conducted using two methods48: evaluating seed germination efficacy and assessing the root-to-shoot ratio of germinated seeds. For the germination efficacy test, three experimental sets were prepared in triplicate. Each set consisted of petri dishes packed with thin pads of absorbent cotton presoaked in Hoagland and Knop medium (HiMedia PT126) and 15 mL of experimental liquid (1.0% w/v dye solution, 10-h incubated dye-degraded solution, and deionized water). Twenty-five seeds of Vigna radiata L. were placed on the cotton pads for germination at 24 ± 2 °C. After germination, the seeds were allowed to grow for 72 h under conditions of 24 ± 2 °C, 2,500 lx light intensity, a 16/8-h photoperiod, and 80% relative humidity. The root-to-shoot ratio was then measured.

Results

UV-visible characterization of ZnO NPs

The UV-visible spectra of ZnO NPs biofabricated from a fraction of duckweed extract are presented in Fig. 1. The ZnO NPs exhibited a broad absorption peak of 362–372 nm, indicating the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) characteristic of ZnO as a semiconductor. This peak suggests a shift in the optical band gap energy, typically expressed in electron volts (eV), associated with the transition from bulk material to nanostructure. The visible color change accompanying this shift indicates the reduction of Zn2+ ions into ZnO NPs. The intensity variation in the color of the nanomixture reflects changes in the SPR band. Spectral measurements were taken within the wavelength range of 350–400 nm, with a specific absorption peak of 362–372 nm49 and maximum absorbance at 370 nm. This peak at 370 nm could be attributed to particle-specific ZnO NP behavior, with a decrease in absorption peak intensity suggesting particle aggregation into roseate shapes (Fig. 1).

SEM images and EDX spectroscopic analysis of ZnO NPs

Scanning electron microscope imaging revealed the diverse shapes of the biofabricated ZnO NPs, which included rectangular, diagonal, spherical, cylindrical, and irregular anisotropic forms, with some forming small roseate structures (Fig. 2A, C). These NPs appeared more dispersed than those derived from the control duckweed extracts (Fig. 2A–D). The size range of the salinity-induced NPs was 0.01–0.40 μm. EDX mapping showed a significant presence of Zn (Fig. 2E), indicated by atomic zinc signals between 2.5 and 3.0 KeV, constituting around 50% of the signals. Other elements, such as oxygen, potassium, silicon, iron, and magnesium, were detected in smaller quantities (Fig. 2E).

Characterization of functional groups by FTIR analysis

FTIR spectroscopy analysis identified the functional groups contributing to the stability of the biofabricated ZnO NPs from duckweed extract (Fig. 4A, B). Peaks within the 800–1700 cm−e range were observed, with N-H and O-H groups evident around 1,200.75 cm−1, indicating C-H stretching. Peaks near 1,500–1,650 cm−1 suggested the presence of alkenes (C = C), secondary alcohols (R-OH)50, aromatic hydroxyl groups (–OH), and methyl residues (–CH3). Peaks around 2,800–3,000 cm−1 indicated–CH3 stretching and carboxyl (C = O) group attachment. Additional peaks near 1,300 cm−1 were indicative of secondary amines and alcohols, while those around 500–600 cm−1 suggested the determination of –NH2 or –COO groups, signifying a predominance of –COO in the biofabrication process51.

Physico-optical characteristics of synthesized ZnO NPs

Zeta potential analysis derived from DLS revealed the size distribution and surface charges of ZnO NPs47. The particles were mostly uniform, ranging from 50 to 100 nm (Fig. 5A–F). Notably, both finer and rosette-shaped structures were abundant in the aqueous solution, with the latter being more prevalent (Fig. 5E). DLS also indicated the polydispersity index, with a range in particle size from 35.3 nm to 88.7 nm and occasional larger dimensions up to 102.5 nm. This size range corresponds to low Zn solubility in the aqueous extract. TEM imaging showed that the ZnO NPs in the extract were larger than the sizes indicated by DLS, highlighting a larger hydrodynamic radius owing to their affinity for water molecules, thereby suggesting a polydispersive nature. The dispersion values and zeta potential suggested moderate stability in aqueous solutions with a tendency toward aggregation in plant extracts. X-ray diffraction analysis confirmed the high purity of the nanoscale sample, with predominant zinc and oxygen content and minimal contamination (Fig. 6). The size of the NPs seemed to exhibit a trend of incremental growth, suggesting the possibility that they were shrouded with coatings of metabolites produced in response to the salinity treatment.

Involvement of biomolecule synthesis under saline stress in the fabrication of ZnO NPs

The marked overexpression of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide as ROS, total chlorophyll, carbohydrate, protein, and free amino acids decreased in response to salinity stress, but flavonoid and phenol content recovered (12% and 8% increases, respectively). This overproduction was observed to continue for up to 10 days of recovery treatment but was reduced by 8% and 5%, respectively, by the 15th day. In another experiment, ZnO NPs were synthesized using duckweed plant extract from an aqueous solution of saline, caustic soda, and Zn acetate. Amino acid profiling (Fig. 9) showed significant increases (p = 0.05) in histidine, proline, isoleucine, phenyl alanine, arginine, and glycine. The increase in flavonoids can be correlated with the increased endurance of the duckweed against ROS-mediated physio-biochemical constraints. The quantification of 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl was negatively correlated with antioxidant activity. In the study, the trend of accumulation of flavonoids was also responsible for the augmentation of the ROS-scavenging capability of the duckweed biomass pre-exposed to salinity for a finite period (Figs. 10 and 11), followed by culturing in MS basal medium under optimum ambient conditions.

Dye decolorization test results

The optical density of the experimental solution corresponding to the wavelength of 464 nm showed a gradual decline in absorbance with time of incubation. The 0.1% w/v aqueous MO solution exhibited the lowest degree of dye decolorization (29.46%) after 10 h of incubation. The increasing order of DD% is as follows: salinity-untreated biomass extract (MOC), salinity-treated biomass (MOS), ZnO NPs biofabricated using MOS, and ZnO NPs biofabricated using MOC. The scores were 72.14%, 80.53%, 94.06%, and 95.29%, respectively. Complete degradation of MO was recorded after 15 h (Fig. 12A–C).

Assessment of toxicity level of dye and dye-degraded products by seed germination and root-to-shoot ratio

The germination percentage shows significant differences in the potency of the different experimental liquids (MO, MOC, MOS), whereas the two NP suspensions, namely “methyl orange dye solution treated with ZnO NPs synthesized from salinity un-treated duckweed biomass” (MOCZN) and “methyl orange dye solution treated with ZnO NPs synthesized from salinity treated duckweed biomass” (MOSZN), did not show any significant difference. The germination medium was obtained from the filtered experimental reaction mixture after 10 h of incubation at room temperature (24 ± 2 °C). After 10 h of incubation, the dye solution was filtered and used for seed germination. The filtrates of MOCZN and MOSZN supported significantly higher germination, scoring higher values than other treatments such as MOS, MOC, and MO. The most promising germinated seed was allowed to grow for 72 h under the physical conditions mentioned. The root-to-shoot ratios observed are presented in Table 4. The ratio decreased by over 40% in the MO dye treatment set compared to the control set. However, the ratio doubled in the dye-degraded solution set. This observation could indicate the rectification of dye toxicity. The growth-promoting morphometric attributes also suggest that ZnO NPs have the capacity to mitigate azo dye toxicity in aquatic microcosms. Discussion

Efficacy of biofabricated nanomaterials and their physiological relevance

The biofabrication of nanomaterials involves significant chemical alterations, primarily focusing on size and shape. Despite the drawbacks of cost inefficiency and hazardous procedures associated with synthesis and application, biofabrication offers a sustainable alternative to chemical synthesis. The use of biological materials, including plant extracts, exudates, and leachates from green algae, molds, and cryptogamic flora, as well as bacterial secretions and various plant parts, has proven more effective36,48. These biological agents act as stabilizing agents, maintaining the desired shape of the nanomaterial. Notably, the coating or capping agents on nanostructures often contain functional groups, such as −NH2, −OH, −COOH, −SH, −CH3, and C = O, as revealed by FTIR analysis. These groups can significantly influence the material’s efficacy37,53. A notable example is the experiment involving duckweed extract, which transformed crude nanomaterials into chemically modified residues with altered physio-optical properties. This transformation was more effective when the plant extract was derived from plants under salinity or stress conditions, as demonstrated with duckweed in this study. Stress conditions such as salinity can unveil antioxidizing potentials, particularly through the induction of reducing moieties such as non-thiols, small peptides, and amino residues38,54.

Consequently, the development of salinity-induced residues enriched with reducing groups in duckweed extract is anticipated to be highly effective in producing stabilizing agents for ZnO nanomaterials. These residues might contribute to enhanced hydrodynamic properties, increased depressiveness, and improved aggregation capabilities in the capping agent, particularly due to −SH-projecting residues39,55 from compounds such as cysteine and homocysteine40,56. Furthermore, biofabrication with plant extracts yields enhanced antioxidizing potential, reduced redox, and superior antimicrobial capacities, making extracts promising candidates in terms of biosafety and biocompatibility. Our findings align with those of other studies in which duckweed has been shown to have potential as a biosorption material for metal remediation by demonstrating enhanced biosynthetic pathways for metabolites, stress tolerance, and pathogen recognition41,57. Thus, it is essential for researchers to consider the size, shape, and surface properties of plant-based biofabricated nanomaterials with a focus on changes in catalytic efficiency in biological processes. Green biofabricated nanomaterials offer advantages such as biocompatibility, simple preparation, mild reaction conditions, and eco-friendliness. However, certain metal transitions in nanoforms may possess high oxidizing capacities that are potentially toxic to biological systems. Encapsulation by biofabricated residues can mitigate this toxicity, transforming these materials into less toxic, slow-releasing compounds42,58. This opens avenues for the biogenic synthesis of a wide array of inorganic NPs and nanocomposites with enhanced effectiveness.

Antimicrobial activity

Biofabricated NPs synthesized through eco-friendly, cost-effective green methods offer significant advantages in the regulation of the ROS that underlie their antimicrobial activity. These NPs, often synthesized from medicinal plants using green extracts, have been extensively evaluated for their antibacterial and antimicrobial properties. Notably, silver and copper NPs are frequently synthesized using these methods43,59. Many medicinal plants are known to produce secondary metabolites, such as terpenes, flavonoids, alkaloids, and tannins, which act as natural reducing agents, effectively reducing metal ions to nanoconformations44,60. Silver NPs are particularly efficient at generating ROS, contributing to their bactericidal activities. When these NPs are capped with phytochemicals from plant extracts, the efficiency of ROS generation is further regulated. ZnO NPs fabricated with specific fractions of duckweed extract show enhanced potential in producing and regulating ROS, thereby improving their antimicrobial activity45,46,61,62. Duckweed is known for its ability to quench toxic metals and xenobiotics, mostly by sequestering them in apoplastic or noncellular spaces, preventing their incorporation into metabolic pathways. The synthesis of nanomaterials from duckweed, with modifications to functional groups or chemical properties, remains an area in need of further exploration47,63.

The chelation of bacterial cell wall residues (e.g., peptidoglycan, teichoic acid, LPS, and murein) with functional groups projecting from nanomaterials could enhance their efficiency against bacterial strains. The redox changes in synthesized NPs, influenced by nucleophilic attraction to the electron-dense residues of microbial cells, are enhanced when modified with biomolecules. Phytochemicals from plant extracts play a significant role in forming bonds that stall bacterial growth, primarily through antioxidant properties48,64. Recent advances include modifying antibiotics that inhibit DNA replication (e.g., cyclothialidine and quinolones) with complex secondary metabolites from plant extracts. These modified compounds exhibit compatibility with NPs when biofabricated using the same extracts. Therefore, studying the activities of ZnO NPs with different complex biomolecules from various plant species could further improve antimicrobial activity. Silver NPs are particularly notable for their antifungal, anti-inflammatory, and anti-angiogenesis activities, mostly attributed to their antioxidation properties.

This observation was consistent with other parameters observed during the experiment. The synthesis of epigenin, naringin, vitexin, and other flavonoids in duckweed under salinity stress suggests their potential as capping agents for ZnO NP biofabrication. Similarly, amino acid profiles under stress may contribute to the residues in biofabricated ZnO NPs49,65. Secondary metabolites, organic acids, glycosides, aminated residues, unsaturated hydrocarbons, and certain fatty esters are commonly used as capping agents, influencing the shape, dispersivity, and hydrodynamic properties of biogenic NPs. These nanoformulations exhibit advanced antimicrobial activities and antioxidation reactions; their antifungal properties make them efficient biocontrol agents. Plant extracts from different plants, such as Abutilon, Saraca, Rheum, and Pisum, when treated with these nanoformulations, induce the production of starch and electron bridge-reducing residues50,66.

Green chemistry principles in the fabrication of NPs/composites highlight their activity with specific biomolecules while maintaining ideal size, shape, surface properties, and other physico-optical properties. In some cases, activated phenols and flavonoids used as stabilizing agents are efficient in the catalytic elimination of xenobiotics, such as synthetic azo dyes and nitrophenols. Therefore, biostimulants such as stress-induced bioactive compounds (flavonoids, complex phenolics, tannins, and proteins) can effectively be used as capping or reducing agents for nanoformulations51,67. Their effectiveness is enhanced due to their nontoxic nature, excellent biocompatibility, high absorption capacity, and modified surface characteristics at the nanoscale, leading to improved antioxidant properties. This was clearly demonstrated with ZnO NPs in the current study.

During the experimental process, a notable change was observed in the color of the reacting mixture, transitioning from pale yellow to brownish, which was indicative of the progression of the reaction. The maximum absorbance of the NP-containing suspension was measured at 368 nm, signifying the successful biofabrication of ZnO NPs in the aqueous phase.

The use of biostimulants derived from stress-induced bioactive compounds, such as flavonoids, complex phenolics, tannins, and proteins, is pivotal in a green chemistry approach to nanoparticle fabrication47,68. These compounds, when used as capping or reducing agents, contribute to the development of NPs that are not only effective in their desired functions but also environmentally friendly and safe for biological applications. Their ability to catalytically eliminate xenobiotics, such as synthetic azo dyes and nitrophenols, further underscores their utility in diverse scientific and industrial applications52,69.

The synthesis of NPs using these biocompatible agents aligns with the principles of green chemistry, emphasizing the creation of products that are sustainable, safe, and efficient. The properties of these NPs, such as size, shape, surface characteristics, and physico-optical properties, are finely tuned to meet specific requirements, making them ideal for various applications, including antimicrobial treatments, catalysis, and environmental remediation48,70.

Efficacy of saline-treated and untreated duckweed biomass and biofabricated NPs in azo dye degradation

Azo dyes are characterized by the presence of auxochrome and chromophore linked with an azo bond (N = N). The number of such linkages determines the physico-chemical properties of the dye molecule. Methyl orange, for instance, is an azo dye with one such bond. It is a comparatively simple molecule in the azo group of compounds30. Different plant extracts and microorganisms have been reported to quench azo compounds, especially the dyes53,54,71,72. In this study, flavonoids were aggravated in response to salinity, which, in turn, upgraded the performance of azo-dye degradation. This is also observed in other plants, such as seaweeds55,73, Salvinia sp56,74. , Monotheca sp57,75. , and other biomaterials. ZnO NPs and other nanoconformations are efficient azo dye degraders58,76. This study not only demonstrated the azo-dye degrading capability of untreated and salinity-treated duckweed extracts but also showed that flavonoid-capped ZnO NPs in solitary and clustered spatial conformation shapes can decontaminate azo dye-containing water. Therefore, duckweeds are misnamed “weeds” in that they can serve as a potentially useful bioresource.

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents a protocol for synthesizing ZnO NPs using duckweed extracts, particularly under stress conditions, thus unveiling a new vista in the field of nanobiotechnology. The exploration of different plant species and their unique bioactive compounds could lead to the development of a wide range of biofabricated NPs with enhanced properties and applications. Furthermore, this study explores the efficacy of salinity-treated and untreated plant extracts, as well as the ZnO NPs biofabricated from these extracts, which were found capable of degrading MO, an azo dye, in an aqueous medium within 15 h of incubation. Such a finding may point the way to multiple applications in the field of environmental biotechnology, highlighting how biomaterial consider wasteful to the layman can be a valuable eco-resource.
