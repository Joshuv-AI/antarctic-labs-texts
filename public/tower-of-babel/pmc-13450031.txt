A three-metabolite microbiota-associated signature for early risk stratification of gestational diabetes mellitus

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes and long-term metabolic and cardiovascular risk. However, oral glucose tolerance testing at 24–28 gestational weeks limits early risk stratification. Gut microbiota-associated metabolites may reflect early metabolic abnormalities, including those relevant to cardiometabolic health, but robust early-pregnancy biomarkers remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter nested case-control and prospective study involving 2,693 pregnant women. Untargeted metabolomics and metagenomics were integrated to identify GDM-associated metabolites and gut microbial alterations. Candidate algorithms were compared using repeated 10-fold cross-validation, and a final generalized linear model was externally and prospectively validated.ResultsWomen who later developed GDM showed an adverse early-pregnancy metabolic profile, including higher BMI, triglycerides, and platelet count. Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy and has become an increasingly important public health issue worldwide. Its prevalence has risen steadily in parallel with increasing rates of obesity and adverse lifestyle patterns, and current estimates suggest that more than 14% of pregnancies may be affected globally [1–3]. GDM is associated with substantially increased risks of adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, cesarean delivery, large-for-gestational-age birth, preterm delivery, neonatal hypoglycemia, and stillbirth [2, 4, 5]. Beyond these immediate pregnancy-related complications, GDM is also increasingly recognized as a clinically informative window into maternal metabolic health. Women with GDM have a higher long-term risk of type 2 diabetes and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, suggesting that pregnancy may unmask an underlying susceptibility to broader cardiometabolic dysfunction [6–9]. However, current clinical diagnosis still relies primarily on the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) performed at 24–28 gestational weeks [10], which limits the opportunity for early risk stratification and intervention. Therefore, identifying robust biomarkers for early-pregnancy prediction of GDM remains an important unmet clinical need [11–13].

In recent years, metabolomics has emerged as a powerful tool for investigating early metabolic perturbations preceding overt dysglycemia and has shown considerable promise for GDM prediction [14–20]. Previous reviews have consistently indicated that women who later develop GDM already exhibit metabolic abnormalities before clinical diagnosis, particularly in lipid, amino acid, and energy metabolism pathways [15–17]. For example, in a longitudinal metabolomics study including 30 women with GDM and 30 healthy controls, Lu et al. reported that several phospholipid-related metabolites, including phosphatidylcholine, lysophosphatidylcholine, and lysophosphatidic acid, were consistently elevated in GDM, whereas 2,3-dihydroxypropyl dihydrogen phosphate was reduced [21]. They further found that allantoic acid remained persistently increased from early to mid-pregnancy and was positively correlated with blood glucose, highlighting its potential as an early predictive biomarker. In addition, in a prospective test-validation study, Zhu et al. performed fasting serum untargeted metabolomics in a discovery set of 91 GDM and 180 non-GDM participants, a validation set 1 of 42 GDM and 372 non-GDM participants, and a validation set 2 of 35 GDM and 70 non-GDM participants, and found that purinone metabolites at gestational weeks 10–13 and 16–19, as well as amino acids, amino alcohols, hexoses, indoles, and pyrimidine metabolites at gestational weeks 16–19, were positively associated with GDM risk [22]. Notably, their 17-metabolite panel at gestational weeks 10–13 outperformed a model based on conventional risk factors, including fasting glycemia, supporting the translational value of metabolomic profiling for early risk assessment [22]. Moreover, in the HAPO Study including 2,290 women, Liu et al. analyzed fasting and 1-hour serum samples obtained after a 75-g glucose load at approximately 28 weeks of gestation and showed that maternal circulating metabolites were associated not only with GDM during pregnancy but also with postpartum metabolic traits, including fasting glucose, 2-hour glucose, insulin resistance, and disorders of glucose metabolism in follow-up, further linking dysregulated maternal metabolism during pregnancy with subsequent glucose dysregulation [23].

More recently, increasing attention has focused on the interaction between gut microbiota and host metabolism in GDM. In an integrative metagenomic and metabolomic analysis including 50 women with GDM and 54 healthy controls, Ye et al. found that circulating 2-hydroxybutyric acid and L-α-aminobutyric acid were significantly elevated in GDM and were accompanied by alterations in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing genera, including Faecalibacterium, Prevotella, and Streptococcus, as well as several species such as Bacteroides coprophilus, Eubacterium siraeum, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Prevotella copri, and Prevotella stercorea, supporting a close association between microbial dysbiosis and host metabolic remodeling [24]. Similarly, Pinto et al., in a prospective analysis including 44 women who later developed GDM and 350 controls, showed that months before diagnosis, future GDM cases already exhibited elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, reduced fecal short-chain fatty acids, and altered microbiome structure, indicating that microbiota-associated inflammatory and metabolic disturbances arise early in disease development [25]. These observations are consistent with broader evidence that gut microbiota and their metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids and bile acid-related products, are important regulators of glucose homeostasis, insulin sensitivity, and metabolic inflammation [26, 27].

Despite these advances, several important gaps remain. First, many previous metabolomics or microbiome studies were conducted in relatively limited cohorts, with insufficient external validation and limited generalizability [28–30]. Second, although gut microbiota–metabolite interactions are increasingly implicated in GDM, systematic biomarker discovery and predictive modeling based specifically on microbiota-associated circulating metabolites remain scarce [28, 31, 32]. Third, many existing models emphasize predictive performance but provide limited biological interpretability, particularly regarding how early metabolic abnormalities may relate to broader maternal cardiometabolic health [33–35]. To address these gaps, the present study adopted a multicenter, multi-cohort nested case-control and prospective design, integrating early-pregnancy untargeted metabolomics with metagenomic analysis to identify microbiota-associated circulating metabolites related to GDM. We further constructed and validated an early-pregnancy prediction model based on three metabolites—3-hydroxydecanoic acid, γ-Glu-Leu, and propionic acid—across internal, external, and prospective cohorts. This strategy aimed not only to improve early GDM risk stratification, but also to provide a biologically interpretable framework linking gut microbial alterations, circulating metabolic signatures, and early metabolic abnormalities relevant to cardiometabolic health in pregnancy.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a multistage, multicenter study designed to identify first-trimester serum metabolite biomarkers associated with early metabolic dysregulation and subsequent GDM risk, develop an early prediction model, and evaluate its performance across independent populations. The overall design comprised retrospective case-control phases for biomarker discovery, replication, and model development, followed by independent external and prospective validation phases. The study was conducted from January 2024 to December 2024 across three hospitals in China, with a total of 2,693 pregnant women enrolled. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital (Approval No.: NFKSL-077) and the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University (Approval No.: 2024ZDSYLL299-P01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

All participants underwent serum sampling at 8–14 gestational weeks and a standard 75-g OGTT at 24–28 gestational weeks. GDM was diagnosed according to the 2020 American Diabetes Association criteria: fasting blood glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-h blood glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-h blood glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L after OGTT. Eligible participants were 18–45 years of age, had singleton pregnancies, completed prenatal registration in early pregnancy, and had complete clinical information and qualified biospecimens available for analysis. Women with pregestational diabetes, prior GDM, multiple pregnancy, major pregnancy complications, recent use of medications affecting metabolism or gut microbiota, severe systemic disorders, malignancy, or incomplete data were excluded. Across the retrospective case-control phases, 362 women who later developed GDM and 364 women with normal glucose tolerance were included.

The study was conducted in five sequential phases. First, a discovery phase was performed in a discovery cohort comprising 92 GDM cases and 88 healthy controls from Nanjing Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital. In this cohort, serum samples were collected in both the first trimester (8–14 weeks) and the second trimester (24–28 weeks), and fecal samples were obtained from a subset during the second trimester. This phase was used to identify metabolites showing reproducible dysregulation across gestation by untargeted metabolomics, while metagenomic sequencing was used as a supportive analysis to explore microbiota-associated patterns related to the metabolite changes.

Second, a replication phase was performed in two independent first-trimester case-control cohorts to assess whether candidate metabolites identified in the discovery phase showed consistent directional changes in additional populations. Independent cohort 1 included 50 GDM cases and 70 healthy controls from Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University, and independent cohort 2 included 48 GDM cases and 49 healthy controls from Changzhou Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital. These cohorts were used for first-trimester replication of candidate metabolite signals rather than for de novo biomarker discovery.

Third, a targeted quantification and model development phase was conducted using the discovery cohort together with the two independent cohorts. Based on the untargeted metabolomics results from these three cohorts, three metabolites with consistent biomarker potential were selected for targeted LC-MS/MS quantification: 3-hydroxydecanoic acid, γ-Glu-Leu, and propionic acid. The pooled targeted data from these cohorts were then used for model comparison, model training, and internal validation.

Fourth, an external validation phase was conducted in two additional first-trimester case-control cohorts reserved exclusively for independent validation. External validation cohort 1 included 96 GDM cases and 81 healthy controls from Nanjing Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital, and External validation cohort 2 included 76 GDM cases and 76 healthy controls from Changzhou Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital.

Fifth, a prospective validation phase was conducted in an independent early-pregnancy cohort recruited consecutively from the obstetrics clinic of Zhongda Hospital, Southeast University. A total of 335 pregnant women were enrolled in this cohort, among whom 55 were subsequently diagnosed with GDM and 280 remained normoglycemic. First-trimester serum samples were collected before GDM diagnosis and were used to evaluate the performance of the prespecified metabolite panel and final locked model in a real-world longitudinal setting. Serum metabolomics analysis

Serum metabolomics analyses comprised untargeted discovery profiling and targeted quantification of candidate biomarkers. Fasting serum samples collected in the first and/or second trimester were processed under a standardized workflow, and pooled quality-control samples were used to monitor analytical stability. Untargeted metabolomics was performed using UHPLC–high-resolution mass spectrometry in both positive- and negative-ion modes. Raw data were processed for feature extraction, alignment, filtering, missing-value imputation, normalization, and metabolite annotation based on accurate mass and MS/MS matching. Subsequently, three candidate metabolites (3-hydroxydecanoic acid, γ-Glu-Leu, and propionic acid) were quantified by targeted LC–MS/MS using internal standard correction and calibration-based quantification. Detailed sample preparation, instrument settings, data processing parameters, metabolite annotation criteria, targeted quantification procedures, and calibration strategies are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Detailed differential metabolite results for the discovery and independent cohorts are provided in Supplementary Data 1–4.

Metagenomic sequencing and microecological analysis

Fecal samples collected from a subset of participants in the discovery cohort during the second trimester underwent metagenomic sequencing. Samples were collected, transported under cold-chain conditions, and processed using standardized nucleic acid extraction procedures. Sequencing libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer’s workflow and sequenced on the MGI-2000 platform. Raw reads were quality filtered, taxonomically annotated at the genus and species levels, and normalized for downstream diversity and differential abundance analyses. Detailed procedures for sample handling, nucleic acid extraction, sequencing, quality control, taxonomic annotation, and diversity analysis are described in the Supplementary Methods. Detailed differential taxonomic results from the matched metagenomic subset are provided in Supplementary Data 5.

Identification of key microbial taxa and multi-omics association analysis

To identify microbial taxa associated with GDM-related metabolic remodeling, differential abundance analysis and co-occurrence network analysis were performed at the genus and species levels. Network topology metrics were used to prioritize high-connectivity taxa for downstream integration. Associations among key microbial taxa, persistent differential metabolites, and clinical metabolic parameters were then evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis with multiple-testing correction where appropriate. Detailed criteria for differential abundance screening, network construction, topological prioritization, correlation thresholds, and visualization are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

GDM prediction model development and evaluation

Three metabolites, namely 3-hydroxydecanoic acid, γ-Glu-Leu, and propionic acid, were selected for model construction because they showed consistent directional changes across the discovery cohort and two additional independent first-trimester cohorts. Targeted LC-MS/MS quantification of these three metabolites was then performed in the discovery cohort and the two independent cohorts, and the pooled targeted data from these three cohorts were used as the development dataset for model construction.

The development dataset was divided into a training cohort (70%) and an internal validation cohort (30%) by stratified random sampling according to GDM status. All preprocessing and model development procedures were performed using the training cohort. Specifically, the three metabolite variables were standardized using Z-score normalization, with the mean and standard deviation estimated from the training cohort only. The same scaling parameters were then applied unchanged to the internal validation cohort, the external validation cohorts, and the prospective cohort. Within the training cohort, candidate algorithms including generalized linear model (GLM) [36], random forest (RF) [37], and support vector machine (SVM) [38] were compared using repeated 10-fold cross-validation (10 folds, 3 repeats), with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as the primary performance metric. Formal model comparison was further performed using the Friedman test followed by pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction. After model comparison, the final GLM was fitted in the training cohort. Predicted probabilities were generated for the training cohort, and the optimal cutoff value was determined from the training-set ROC curve using the Youden index. This fixed cutoff was subsequently applied unchanged to the internal validation cohort, the two external validation cohorts, and the prospective cohort.

For external and prospective validation, the same three metabolites were quantified by targeted LC-MS/MS, standardized using the scaling parameters estimated from the training cohort, and then entered into the locked GLM without further model refitting or threshold re-estimation. To benchmark the final linear model against a more complex nonlinear algorithm, ROC curves of GLM and RF were additionally compared using DeLong tests in the validation cohorts.

Model performance was evaluated using AUC with 95% confidence intervals, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and balanced accuracy. ROC curves were generated for the training, internal validation, external validation, and prospective cohorts. In addition, the distribution of predicted risk scores in the internal validation cohort was visualized using a boxplot with the fixed cutoff overlaid for risk stratification.

As a clinical benchmarking analysis, we further evaluated whether the three-metabolite panel provided predictive information beyond available baseline clinical characteristics. Three logistic regression models were constructed: a clinical-only model including maternal age, first-trimester body mass index (BMI), and gestational week at sampling; a metabolite-only model including 3-hydroxydecanoic acid, γ-Glu-Leu, and propionic acid; and a combined clinical + metabolite model including both the baseline clinical variables and the three metabolites. The metabolite-only model corresponded to the final three-metabolite generalized linear model (GLM) described above. These models were first fitted in the training cohort and then evaluated in the internal validation cohort, the two external validation cohorts, and the prospective cohort using the same validation framework as the primary model.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.3.0). Continuous variables were compared using the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate, and categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Unless otherwise specified, all tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed information on software packages, multivariate metabolomics analysis, metagenomic statistics, correlation analysis, and model comparison procedures is provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria and confirming GDM status by OGTT in the second trimester, 92 women who later developed GDM were identified, corresponding to an occurrence rate of 12.06%. To establish a comparable control group, 88 pregnant women with normal glucose tolerance were selected as healthy controls (HCs), matched for age and gestational week. Untargeted metabolomic analysis was then performed on serum samples collected in the first trimester (E-phase) and second trimester (M-phase), and their clinical parameters were compared (Table 2).

Clinical characteristics of the discovery cohort

In the first trimester, women who later developed GDM were characterized by higher BMI (P = 0.050), triglycerides (TG; P = 0.008), and platelet count (PLT; P = 0.023), whereas no significant differences were observed for the remaining clinical parameters. These findings indicate that adiposity- and lipid-related metabolic risk features were already detectable before clinical diagnosis.

In the second trimester, at the time of GDM diagnosis, the GDM group had, in addition to higher blood glucose levels (FBG, OGTT-1 h, and OGTT-2 h), higher BMI, PLT, and hemoglobin (HGB) than the HC group (P < 0.05), while no significant differences were observed for the other clinical indicators. Overall, these results suggest that women who later developed GDM already exhibited an adverse metabolic background in early pregnancy, involving metabolic features relevant to broader cardiometabolic health, which became more evident by the time of diagnosis in the second trimester.

Serum metabolomics reveals metabolic characteristics and dynamic changes in GDM patients during early and mid-pregnancy

To characterize the metabolic profile of GDM and its temporal changes during pregnancy, untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed on serum samples collected from 92 GDM patients and 88 HC pregnant women during the first trimester (8–14 weeks) and second trimester (24–28 weeks). Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) revealed significant separation in metabolic profiles between the two groups at both timepoints, indicating that metabolic disturbances manifest prior to GDM diagnosis (Fig. 2A-B, Supplementary Fig. 1A-D).

A total of 2,751 metabolic features were identified in first-trimester samples. Based on adjusted P < 0.05 and variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, 131 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were identified (76 upregulated, 55 downregulated; Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table 6). In the second trimester, 2,686 metabolic features were detected, yielding 118 DEMs (79 upregulated, 39 downregulated; Fig. 2D and Supplementary Table 6). Notably altered metabolites were primarily organic acids, amino acids/derivatives, lipids, and steroids (Fig. 2E). First-trimester-specific metabolites (e.g., N-Acetylornithine, Acetyl-L-carnitine, Oxaloacetic acid, Allantoin) reflected disruptions in energy and amino acid metabolism, whereas second-trimester-specific metabolites (e.g., Lipoic acid, Glycerol 3-phosphate, Leukotriene C4) implicated oxidative stress, lipid regulation, and steroid synthesis.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis corroborated these findings: first-trimester metabolic aberrations were enriched in protein digestion/absorption, amino acid synthesis, and carbon metabolism pathways, while second-trimester anomalies predominantly involved steroid hormone synthesis, aromatic compound degradation, and endocrine signaling networks (Fig. 2F and G). These results demonstrate a distinct temporal pattern of GDM-associated metabolic dysregulation. The data suggest that early metabolic disturbances may involve energy and amino acid metabolism, whereas mid-pregnancy alterations may be more related to endocrine, lipid, and inflammation-associated pathways.

Screening and analysis of key metabolites in GDM patients across early and mid-pregnancy

To identify robust metabolic biomarkers consistently altered across pregnancy stages, intersection analysis of DEMs from both trimesters was conducted, revealing 14 metabolites with persistent differential expression (10 upregulated, 4 downregulated; Fig. 3A-C). These metabolites exhibited stable directional changes throughout disease progression, suggesting their potential as early predictive markers.

KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these 14 metabolites were associated with metabolic pathways, tyrosine metabolism, cofactor synthesis, β-alanine metabolism, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis (Fig. 3D)—processes closely linked to insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and amino acid dysregulation in GDM. ROC curve evaluation demonstrated their discriminative capacity for GDM prediction, with AUC values of 0.700–0.973 in the first trimester and 0.665–0.957 in the second trimester (Fig. 3E). Notably, metabolites like Decanoic acid showed reduced predictive efficacy in the second trimester, highlighting stage-dependent variations in biomarker utility. These findings suggest that temporally persistent metabolic signatures may be useful for early GDM risk stratification.

Metagenomic characterization and bacterial co-occurrence network patterns of gut microbiota in GDM

To characterize gut microbial alterations associated with GDM, metagenomic sequencing was performed on fecal samples from 32 women with GDM and 32 HC pregnant women in the discovery cohort. At the overall community level, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in alpha diversity (Shannon, Simpson, and Invsimpson indices) or beta diversity (PCoA) at either the genus or species level (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating that global microbial diversity remained broadly comparable between groups.

Differential abundance analysis further revealed compositional shifts in specific taxa. At the genus level, several taxa were significantly enriched in the GDM group, including Staphylococcus (log2FC = 1.66, P = 3.5 × 10− 5) and Propionibacterium (log2FC = 2.31, P = 5.1 × 10− 5), whereas Odoribacter (log2FC = − 1.63, P = 4.8 × 10− 5), Alloprevotella (log2FC = − 1.67, P = 7.3 × 10− 5), Alistipes (log2FC = − 2.17, P = 5.6 × 10− 5), and Paraprevotella (log2FC = − 1.60, P = 2.68 × 10− 4) were reduced in the GDM group (Supplementary Fig. 3). At the species level, representative depleted taxa included Alistipes shahii (log2FC = − 3.03, P = 1.8 × 10− 5), Odoribacter splanchnicus (log2FC = − 1.63, P = 4.8 × 10− 5), and Paraprevotella xylaniphila (log2FC = − 2.08, P = 2.5 × 10− 5), whereas Neisseria subflava (log2FC = 5.28, P = 4.2 × 10− 5), Actinomyces oris (log2FC = 1.61, P = 5.3 × 10− 5), and Staphylococcus aureus (log2FC = 1.89, P = 1.5 × 10− 4) were enriched in GDM. These findings indicate that, despite relatively stable overall diversity, the gut microbiota of GDM patients exhibited marked restructuring at the taxon level.

To further explore microbial interaction patterns, co-occurrence networks were constructed separately for the GDM and HC groups using the top 50 most abundant genera and species, based on Spearman correlations with false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |r| > 0.3. Core taxa were operationally defined as nodes with degree ≥ 15. At the genus level, representative core genera in the GDM network included Anaerotignum, Merdibacter, and Massilioclostridium, whereas representative core genera in the HC network included Intestinibacillus, Gemmiger, and Lactonifactor (Supplementary Figs. 4 A and 4B). Network topology analysis further showed reduced eigenvector centrality and lower global transitivity in the GDM genus-level network, indicating a less cohesive interaction structure than that observed in HC controls (Supplementary Figs. 4 C–F).

At the species level, representative core taxa in the GDM network included Bacteroides eggerthii (degree = 18), Bacteroides dorei (degree = 17), and Odoribacter splanchnicus (degree = 17), whereas representative core taxa in the HC network included Roseburia inulinivorans (degree = 19), Clostridioides difficile (degree = 19), and Bacteroides clarus (degree = 18) (Fig. 4A, B). Comparison of species-level network topology further demonstrated distinct distributions of weighted degree, eigenvector centrality, weighted closeness, and global transitivity between the GDM and HC groups (Fig. 4C–F), indicating that GDM was associated with an altered microbial interaction architecture at the species level. To prioritize taxa for downstream multi-omics integration, species with degree ≥ 15 in both the GDM and HC networks were intersected, yielding five shared high-connectivity species: Bacteroides eggerthii, Odoribacter splanchnicus, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, Paraprevotella clara, and Bacteroides clarus. All five species showed lower abundance in the GDM group, among which Odoribacter splanchnicus (log2FC = − 1.63, P = 4.8 × 10− 5), Paraprevotella clara (log2FC = − 1.36, P = 0.0011), and Bacteroides eggerthii (log2FC = − 1.20, P = 0.0022) showed particularly evident reductions (Fig. 4G). These species were therefore retained as key taxa for subsequent association analyses with serum metabolites and clinical metabolic parameters.

Association patterns of key microbial taxa and metabolites with clinical metabolic parameters in GDM

To explore the relationships between gut microbial signatures and clinical metabolic parameters in GDM, we performed Spearman correlation analyses at both the genus and species levels. At the genus level, several key genera, including Faecalibacterium, Eubacterium, Subdoligranulum, Hungatella, and Phascolarctobacterium, showed predominantly negative correlations with FBG and TG. In addition, Faecalibacterium and Eubacterium also tended to be negatively associated with BMI (Fig. 5A). Overall, these results indicate that multiple genera shared broadly similar inverse association patterns with glycemic and lipid-related traits.

At the species level, the representative GDM-associated species highlighted in our analysis, including Bacteroides clarus, Bacteroides eggerthii, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, Odoribacter splanchnicus, and Paraprevotella clara, also exhibited predominantly inverse correlations with FBG, with some species additionally showing negative associations with BMI or other metabolic indicators (Fig. 5B). Overall, these findings suggest coordinated microbiota–host metabolic variation in GDM and provide a hypothesis-generating basis for future mechanistic studies.

To further investigate the relationships between gut microbial alterations and circulating metabolic disturbances in GDM, we performed correlation analyses between the 14 differential metabolites and the key microbial taxa identified above. At the genus level, several representative genera exhibited highly similar association patterns with the metabolite panel. Among these metabolites, formylkynurenine showed consistent positive correlations with most genera, whereas N-acetylneuraminic acid, γ-Glu-Leu, D-pantothenic acid, 3-hydroxydecanoic acid, 3-hydroxybutyric acid, and 2-oxobutyric acid were predominantly negatively correlated with these genera. Propionic acid also showed an overall positive association trend, although its correlation strength was relatively modest compared with formylkynurenine (Fig. 6A).

A similar pattern was observed at the species level. The five representative species highlighted in our analysis, namely Bacteroides clarus, Bacteroides eggerthii, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, Odoribacter splanchnicus, and Paraprevotella clara, displayed broadly concordant correlations with the differential metabolites (Fig. 6B). Specifically, these species were generally positively associated with propionic acid and formylkynurenine, but negatively associated with N-acetylneuraminic acid, γ-Glu-Leu, D-pantothenic acid, 3-hydroxydecanoic acid, 3-hydroxybutyric acid, and 2-oxobutyric acid. By contrast, several other metabolites, such as tyramine, Trp-Phe, decanoic acid, arg-gln, 11-beta-hydroxyandrosterone-3-glucuronide, and (+/-)-metanephrine, showed relatively weaker or more heterogeneous association patterns across taxa.

Consistent with these findings, the Sankey diagram further summarized the significant links between the five representative species and the metabolite panel, illustrating that these key GDM-associated species were connected to multiple metabolites rather than isolated metabolite-specific relationships (Fig. 6C). Overall, these results suggest that gut microbial alterations in GDM are accompanied by coordinated changes in multiple circulating metabolites, supporting a broad microbiota–metabolite association pattern in this disease.

Construction of an early prediction model for GDM based on microbiota-associated circulating metabolites

To construct an early-pregnancy prediction model for GDM, we first integrated the untargeted metabolomics results from the discovery cohort and two additional independent first-trimester cohorts, with the untargeted results of the two independent cohorts summarized in Supplementary Table 6. Cross-cohort comparison identified six commonly upregulated metabolites, namely 3-hydroxydecanoic acid, hydroxyhexanoylcarnitine, itaconic acid, γ-Glu-Leu, N-acetyl-L-glutamic acid, and neuraminic acid, together with one commonly downregulated metabolite, propionic acid. Among these common metabolites, 3-hydroxydecanoic acid and γ-Glu-Leu also remained persistently elevated in the second trimester in the discovery cohort, whereas propionic acid was the only metabolite showing a concordant downward trend across all three first-trimester cohorts (Fig. 7A). Based on this combination of cross-cohort consistency and temporal stability, 3-hydroxydecanoic acid, γ-Glu-Leu, and propionic acid were retained for model construction. In addition, these three metabolites were each associated with key microbial taxa identified in the metagenomic analyses, providing biological support for their selection as gut microbiota-related candidate biomarkers.

Targeted quantitative assays were subsequently established for these three metabolites, and the pooled targeted data from the discovery and two independent cohorts (190 GDM and 207 HC) were used as the development dataset. The development dataset was then divided into a training cohort and an internal validation cohort in a 7:3 ratio by stratified random sampling. Within the training cohort, GLM, RF, and SVM were compared using repeated 10-fold cross-validation. As summarized in Supplementary Table 7, the mean cross-validated AUCs were 0.854 ± 0.044 for RF, 0.837 ± 0.061 for GLM, and 0.845 ± 0.067 for SVM. However, formal statistical comparison showed no significant overall difference among the three candidate models by Friedman testing (statistic = 3.068, df = 2, P = 0.216; Supplementary Table 8). Pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests further showed that RF did not significantly outperform GLM (raw P = 0.127, adjusted P = 0.382), and no significant pairwise difference was observed between RF and SVM or between GLM and SVM (both adjusted P = 0.560; Supplementary Table 8).

To determine the final model, we conducted a comprehensive comparison and found that, although RF yielded the highest mean AUC during repeated cross-validation, its advantage over GLM was not statistically significant (Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). Further cohort-wise comparison of GLM and RF demonstrated that GLM provided better external and prospective discrimination, while also offering stronger interpretability for this compact three-metabolite panel (Fig. 7B; Supplementary Fig. 5; Supplementary Table 8; Supplementary Table 9). Accordingly, GLM was selected as the primary final model, and RF was retained as a nonlinear comparator.

After fitting the final GLM in the training cohort, the model achieved an AUC of 0.838 (95% CI, 0.791–0.885), with an accuracy of 0.758, sensitivity of 0.854, and specificity of 0.673 (Fig. 7C; Table 3). Subsequently, the optimal cutoff value for the GLM was determined in the training cohort according to the Youden index derived from the training ROC curve, yielding a fixed threshold of 0.368 (Fig. 7D). When this fixed cutoff was applied to the internal validation cohort, the model maintained stable performance, with an AUC of 0.840 (95% CI, 0.769–0.911), accuracy of 0.742, sensitivity of 0.839, and specificity of 0.656 (Fig. 7E; Table 3).

External validation and prospective evaluation of the early prediction model for GDM

To evaluate the generalizability of the final GLM-based model, two independent external case-control cohorts and one prospective cohort were analyzed using the locked model and the fixed cutoff established in the training cohort. In External Validation Cohort 1, the model achieved an AUC of 0.955 (95% CI, 0.925–0.985), with an accuracy of 0.814, sensitivity of 1.000, and specificity of 0.593 (Fig. 7F; Table 3). In External Validation Cohort 2, the model yielded an AUC of 0.917 (95% CI, 0.875–0.958), with an accuracy of 0.809, sensitivity of 0.921, and specificity of 0.697 (Fig. 7G; Table 3).

For prospective validation, a total of 335 pregnant women were consecutively recruited in early pregnancy, and all 335 participants underwent targeted LC-MS/MS quantification of the three metabolites. Among them, 55 women were ultimately diagnosed with GDM. The baseline clinical characteristics of the prospective cohort are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. When these first-trimester metabolite data were entered into the locked GLM, the model achieved an AUC of 0.969 (95% CI, 0.937–1.000), with an accuracy of 0.851, sensitivity of 0.964, specificity of 0.829, PPV of 0.525, NPV of 0.991, and balanced accuracy of 0.896 (Fig. 7H; Table 3). Together with the cohort-wise overview in Fig. 7B, these findings indicate that the three-metabolite GLM retained discriminative performance across independent retrospective cohorts and in the prospective cohort.

To further benchmark the final linear model against the nonlinear RF comparator, ROC differences between GLM and RF were formally assessed using DeLong tests (Supplementary Table 9). No significant difference was observed in the internal validation cohort (P = 0.712). In contrast, GLM significantly outperformed RF in External Validation Cohort 1 (raw P = 3.20 × 10− 8, Holm-adjusted P = 1.28 × 10− 7), External Validation Cohort 2 (raw P = 0.001, Holm-adjusted P = 0.003), and the prospective cohort (raw P = 7.71 × 10− 7, Holm-adjusted P = 2.31 × 10− 6). Consistent with these statistical comparisons, Supplementary Fig. 5 shows that although RF remained competitive during model development, its AUCs in External validation cohort 1, External validation cohort 2, and the prospective cohort were 0.799, 0.820, and 0.894, respectively, all lower than those of the final GLM.

Incremental predictive value of the three-metabolite panel beyond baseline clinical factors

To further evaluate whether the three-metabolite panel provided predictive information beyond available baseline clinical characteristics, we performed an additional multivariable logistic regression analysis incorporating maternal age, first-trimester body mass index (BMI), and gestational week at sampling. A clinical-only model and a combined clinical + metabolite model were constructed and compared with the previously established three-metabolite model.

The clinical-only model showed limited discriminatory ability across cohorts. In the training cohort, the clinical-only model achieved an AUC of 0.671 (95% CI, 0.613–0.729). Similar modest performance was observed in the internal validation cohort, External Validation Cohort 1, External Validation Cohort 2, and the prospective cohort, with AUCs of 0.649 (95% CI, 0.566–0.732), 0.695 (95% CI, 0.621–0.769), 0.667 (95% CI, 0.590–0.744), and 0.683 (95% CI, 0.602–0.764), respectively (Table 4). These results suggest that baseline clinical factors alone provided only moderate early discrimination for GDM. After integrating the three metabolites with baseline clinical variables, the combined model achieved AUCs of 0.853 (95% CI, 0.808–0.898) in the training cohort and 0.849 (95% CI, 0.780–0.918) in the internal validation cohort. In the two external validation cohorts, the combined model maintained high discriminatory performance, with AUCs of 0.962 (95% CI, 0.935–0.989) and 0.926 (95% CI, 0.888–0.964), respectively. In the prospective cohort, the combined model achieved an AUC of 0.975 (95% CI, 0.946–1.000) (Table 4). DeLong tests showed that the combined model significantly outperformed the clinical-only model across all cohorts (all DeLong P < 0.001) (Table 4). However, compared with the previously established metabolite-only model, the AUC improvement of the combined model was small and did not reach statistical significance in the training cohort, internal validation cohort, External Validation Cohort 1, External Validation Cohort 2, or the prospective cohort (DeLong P = 0.137, 0.228, 0.316, 0.284, and 0.341, respectively) (Table 4).

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, BMI remained associated with GDM risk (OR, 1.202; 95% CI, 1.091–1.325; P < 0.001). Importantly, all three metabolites also remained significantly associated with GDM after adjustment for age, BMI, and gestational week at sampling, including 3-hydroxydecanoic acid (OR, 1.431; 95% CI, 1.126–1.819; P = 0.003), γ-Glu-Leu (OR, 1.328; 95% CI, 1.048–1.683; P = 0.018), and propionic acid (OR, 0.737; 95% CI, 0.581–0.936; P = 0.013) (Supplementary Table 10). Discussion

This multicenter, multi-cohort study integrated serum metabolomics and gut metagenomic profiling to identify microbiota-associated circulating metabolites related to early GDM risk. By combining cross-stage metabolite screening, targeted LC-MS/MS quantification, and validation across internal, external, and prospective cohorts, we identified a compact three-metabolite panel consisting of 3-hydroxydecanoic acid, γ-Glu-Leu, and propionic acid. The panel showed reproducible discriminatory performance for early-pregnancy GDM risk stratification. Importantly, these metabolites were not selected solely on the basis of statistical performance, but also showed consistent relationships with early metabolic abnormalities and gut microbial features. Thus, our findings support the presence of detectable metabolic dysregulation before routine OGTT-based diagnosis and provide a biologically interpretable framework linking early GDM risk with broader metabolic alterations relevant to cardiometabolic health in pregnancy.

Our findings indicate that women who later develop GDM already exhibit detectable metabolic perturbations in early pregnancy, before routine clinical diagnosis by OGTT. The observed differences in BMI, triglycerides, and platelet count are consistent with an unfavorable baseline metabolic profile rather than newly emerging abnormalities at the time of diagnosis. Beyond these clinical indicators, untargeted metabolomics revealed persistent alterations involving energy metabolism, amino acid metabolism, oxidative stress-related pathways, and lipid-associated metabolic signals. These changes are consistent with the concept that GDM develops on the basis of early metabolic disequilibrium rather than appearing abruptly in mid-pregnancy [21–23, 39–41]. Recent metabolomic studies have likewise shown that first-trimester or early-to-mid-pregnancy metabolite panels can improve GDM prediction beyond conventional risk factors and may reflect biologically heterogeneous metabolic phenotypes [22, 23, 40, 41]. In our study, the identification of 14 metabolites with persistent directional changes across gestation further supports coordinated metabolic remodeling involving peptide turnover, lipid handling, oxidative balance, and host–microbial co-metabolism [42–47].

At the microbial level, we did not observe significant differences in overall α- or β-diversity, but we detected remodeling at the taxonomic and network levels. This pattern suggests that GDM-related microbial alterations may be reflected more by changes in specific taxa and microbial interaction architecture than by global diversity indices alone. Similar observations have been reported in previous GDM microbiome studies, in which specific taxa and ecological interactions were more informative than diversity metrics for explaining metabolic phenotypes [24–27, 48, 49]. In our data, several high-connectivity taxa, including Bacteroides eggerthii, Odoribacter splanchnicus, and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, showed reduced abundance in GDM and were associated with circulating metabolites and clinical metabolic traits. This is consistent with recent work linking GDM-associated gut dysbiosis to reduced short-chain fatty acid availability, altered adipose tissue macrophage polarization, and disturbed glucose homeostasis [50]. However, because the microbiome and metabolomic data were observational and were not temporally aligned in all participants, these associations should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence of causality.

One of the meaningful aspects of this study is that the three metabolites ultimately selected for model construction were not chosen solely because of predictive performance; they also retained biological relevance in the context of host–microbiota metabolic interaction. Among them, propionic acid is particularly notable because it is a representative short-chain fatty acid generated through microbial fermentation and has recognized anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing effects [27, 50–52]. Its reduction in GDM, together with its association with SCFA-related microbial taxa, suggests that altered microbial fermentation-related metabolic signatures may accompany an unfavorable metabolic environment early in disease development. γ-Glu-Leu was consistently elevated in GDM and correlated with adverse metabolic features such as BMI and triglycerides, which is compatible with altered peptide turnover, amino acid handling, and redox-related metabolic stress [53–55]. 3-hydroxydecanoic acid was also increased in GDM and represents another biologically informative signal. Previous work has linked elevated circulating 3-hydroxydecanoic acid to type 2 diabetes and to activation of GPR84-dependent inflammatory pathways [56]. In our dataset, these three metabolites were embedded within the same broader microbiota-associated metabolic pattern, suggesting that inflammatory activation, lipid metabolic perturbation, and microbial ecosystem remodeling may be interconnected components of the early GDM phenotype. Taken together, these results support a coordinated microbiota–metabolite association pattern in GDM, while further mechanistic studies are required to determine whether these taxa or metabolites actively contribute to disease development or primarily serve as biomarkers of early metabolic dysregulation.

From a translational perspective, the final three-metabolite model is clinically attractive because it is compact, biologically interpretable, and based on targeted LC-MS/MS quantification. This is important because many previous GDM prediction models either relied heavily on conventional clinical variables or used larger, less interpretable molecular panels [18, 19, 33, 34]. The predictive value of this panel should be considered in relation to established clinical risk factors. In the present study, women who later developed GDM already showed an adverse early-pregnancy metabolic profile, including higher BMI and triglyceride levels. However, available baseline clinical variables alone provided only modest discrimination in our benchmarking analysis, whereas the three-metabolite panel showed stronger performance across validation cohorts. The limited additional improvement observed after combining clinical variables with the metabolite panel suggests that these metabolites may capture biological information not fully represented by routine clinical predictors. Nevertheless, maternal age, BMI, triglycerides, glycemic indices, family history, and obstetric history remain clinically important, and future implementation should evaluate the metabolite panel alongside comprehensive clinical risk scores.

The higher AUCs observed in the external and prospective cohorts than in the training cohort should also be interpreted cautiously. Several factors may have contributed to this pattern, including differences in cohort composition, case-control contrast, center-specific metabolite distributions, and sampling variability across moderate-sized validation cohorts. In addition, AUC measures relative discrimination rather than absolute calibration; therefore, a higher AUC in an external cohort does not necessarily imply superior clinical transportability. These findings support the reproducibility of the metabolite signal, but further validation in broader and more heterogeneous populations will be required before clinical implementation. Notably, despite the high AUC and NPV observed in the prospective cohort, the moderate PPV indicates that the panel should currently be viewed as an early risk stratification tool rather than a standalone diagnostic test.

The broader relevance of early GDM biomarkers should be viewed within the context of maternal cardiometabolic health. OGTT remains the standard diagnostic test for GDM, but it is time-consuming, inconvenient, and restricted to mid-pregnancy, which inevitably limits earlier preventive intervention [5, 57, 58]. Although our study did not evaluate postpartum cardiovascular outcomes, GDM is increasingly recognized as a pregnancy-related marker of later-life metabolic and cardiovascular vulnerability [8, 9, 59–65]. The early-pregnancy metabolic alterations identified here, particularly those related to lipid handling, inflammatory activation, microbial co-metabolism, and insulin resistance-related traits, may therefore represent an early biochemical window into this unfavorable metabolic trajectory. In this sense, the main value of the three-metabolite panel is not to predict cardiovascular disease directly, but to improve early recognition of women with GDM-related metabolic dysregulation who may benefit from closer antenatal management and postpartum cardiometabolic follow-up.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, gut microbiome data were available only in the second trimester, which limited assessment of microbial dynamics in early pregnancy and restricted temporal alignment between microbiome features and first-trimester metabolomic signals. Second, pregnancy itself is characterized by profound physiological metabolic adaptation, and some metabolite shifts observed across gestation may therefore reflect normal pregnancy-related remodeling in addition to GDM-related changes. Third, although we performed clinical benchmarking analyses incorporating available baseline clinical variables, other established risk factors, including detailed dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, family history of diabetes, genetic background, and detailed obstetric history, were not fully incorporated. Fourth, the present population was drawn predominantly from coastal regions of Eastern China, and further validation in more geographically and ethnically diverse populations is warranted. Fifth, although the study was designed to support early GDM risk stratification, postpartum metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes were not available. Therefore, the current data cannot determine whether the three-metabolite panel predicts long-term cardiometabolic outcomes after pregnancy. Finally, although the three metabolites were associated with key microbial taxa and retained predictive value for GDM, the observational nature of the microbiome–metabolite analyses precludes causal inference. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether these microbiota-associated metabolites actively contribute to GDM development or primarily serve as informative biomarkers of early metabolic dysregulation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study shows that GDM is accompanied by early and persistent metabolic perturbation, coordinated changes in second-trimester gut microbial features, and reproducible changes in a small set of microbiota-associated circulating metabolites. By integrating biological interpretability with multicenter validation, our work provides a potential framework for early-pregnancy GDM risk stratification and highlights the relevance of early metabolic screening to broader cardiometabolic health in pregnancy.
