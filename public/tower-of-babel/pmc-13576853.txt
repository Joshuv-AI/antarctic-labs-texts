Identification of Novel Co-Occurring ZNF711 and SRCAP Variants in a Child with Neurodevelopmental Disorders Through Exome Sequencing
Authors: Tingting Liu, Mingtao Huang, Min Dai, Yixuan Liang, Cuiping Zhang, Jianxin Tan, Fengchang Qiao, Yan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobal developmental delay (GDD)/intellectual disability (ID) represent a group of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) with onset in childhood with a global prevalence of 1–3%. The etiology of GDD/ID is complex, with genetic factors accounting for about half of cases. Exome sequencing (ES) has proven to be an effective diagnostic tool for genetically heterogeneous disorders such as GDD/ID. This study aims to identify the genetic etiologies using ES in a Chinese patient with NDD.MethodsClinical data were collected through physical examination. Trio-ES was conducted to investigate the potential genetic causes, followed by Sanger sequencing for validation.ResultsThe proband exhibited facial dysmorphisms, motor and language development delay, ID, autism spectrum disorder, and attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder symptoms. Trio-ES identified a novel maternal inherited hemizygous nonsense variant c.205G>T (p.E69*) in ZNF711, and a novel de novo missense variant c.673C>T (p.R225C) in SRCAP. Both of variants were classified as likely pathogenic. Phenotype dissection revealed ZNF711 dominated neurodevelopmental abnormalities (Intellectual developmental disorder, X-linked 97, XLID97) and SRCAP contributed to multisystemic manifestations (developmental delay, hypotonia, musculoskeletal defects, and behavioral abnormalities, DEHMBA).ConclusionTo our knowledge, this study reports the first case of concurrent ZNF711 and SRCAP variants in China, expanding variant spectrum of both genes. It highlights the critical value of ES for detecting multi-gene variants in probands with complex phenotypes.

Background

Global developmental delay (GDD)/intellectual disability (ID) is a group of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) with onset in childhood, which not only imposes a significant impact on society but also increases the family burden.1 The global prevalence of GDD/ID is approximately 1% to 3%.2 GDD typically refers to significant developmental delay in two or more developmental domains in children under five years old, including gross or fine motor skills, speech and language, cognition, personal-social functioning, and activities of daily living.3 ID is defined as obvious limitations in intellectual function (such as reasoning, learning, and problem-solving abilities) and adaptive behavior (such as conceptual, social, and practical skills) that manifest before the age of eighteen.3 GDD/ID may be accompanied by autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and anatomical abnormalities in other organ systems.4 The etiology of GDD/ID is complex, with the main causes categorized into acquired and genetic factors. Acquired factors involve perinatal trauma, intrauterine infections, toxic exposure and postnatal events, whereas genetic etiologies encompass single-gene variants, copy number variations, chromosomal abnormalities, epigenetic abnormalities and others.4,5 Notably, approximately half of all cases are attributed to genetic factors,6,7 highlighting the significant role of hereditary influences in these conditions.

Exome sequencing (ES) has been recommended as the first-tier genetic evaluation for patients with GDD/ID according to updated American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines.8 Recent studies have shown that ES achieves a diagnostic rate of 27% to 43% for GDD/ID,9–12 underscoring its essential role in the etiological diagnosis of these disorders. Furthermore, emerging research has shown that synergistic genetic effects have been implicated in the pathogenesis of certain NDDs. Djenifer et al13 suggested that an interaction between LPHN3 and NTAD genes is involved in the underpinnings of attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) susceptibility and stability. Li et al14 also reported that digenic variants contribute to ID. Collectively, these findings indicate that multi-gene interactions represent an important molecular mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of diverse NDDs.

Herein, we report a rare case with co-occurring novel likely pathogenic variants in ZNF711 and SRCAP genes, leading to a genetic diagnosis of intellectual developmental disorder, X-linked 97 (XLID97) and developmental delay, hypotonia, musculoskeletal defects, and behavioral abnormalities (DEHMBA). In addition, we conducted a literature review of previously reported XLID97 and DEHMBA syndromes and summarized that the proband exhibited a combination of phenotypic features from both syndromes. This reinforces the utility of ES in diagnosing complex and non-specific phenotypes.

Methods

Clinical Information

The boy was born to non-consanguineous parents via cesarean operation at 37 weeks of gestational age, with a birth length of 49 cm (−0.47 SD) and a birth weight of 2990 g (−0.52 SD). There was no history of genetic diseases in the family (Figure 1A). The proband was referred to Center of Prenatal Diagnosis at Nanjing Women and Children’s Healthcare Hospital for further assessment at two years and five months of age due to GDD/ID. Physical examination revealed that he was 96 cm (+1.45 SD) in height, weighed 13.5 kg (+0.25 SD), had a head circumference of 47 cm (−1.36 SD) and a thoracic circumference of 55 cm (+2.64 SD). These findings verified that the child had a normal weight and height, but exhibited thoracic overdevelopment compared with peers. Besides, the proband presented with facial dysmorphisms, including long face, narrow palpebral fissures, epicanthal folds, periorbital fullness, wide nasal bridge and thick and everted lower lip. The Chinese Developmental Scale for children aged 0–6 years indicated delays in both gross motor and language development, along with impaired social communication. Neurodevelopmental assessment confirmed moderate ID and ASD, and indicated clinically significant ADHD symptoms. Brain MRI and EEG excluded other causes of neurodevelopmental delay, and both showed unremarkable findings. Additionally, due to extremity hypotonia and motor developmental delay, he was unable to walk steadily until the age of two (Figure 1B). During a follow-up at age five, the child was noted to have acquired the ability to jump by age four. He still had no verbal output but could understand simple instructions, and presented with a barrel-shaped chest, joint hypermobility, and hypotonia, accompanied by anger outbursts and challenging behaviors. Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the parents. The utilization of clinical data and medical records in this study complied with ethical standards, and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanjing Women and Children’s Healthcare Hospital ((2019)KY-081) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

DNA Isolation and Exome Sequencing

Peripheral blood was collected from the proband and his parents in EDTA tube. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood using an Automated Nucleic Acid Extractor and the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) based on procedure of manufacturer. The absorbance ratio (A260/A280) of the extracted DNA was confirmed to range from 1.8 to 2.0. A total of 500 ng was taken for subsequent genetic analysis, and the remaining qualified samples were stored at −20 °C. The genomic DNA was clipped into target fragments by ultrasonication, followed by amplification for sequencing library construction. The libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing data were aligned to the Human genome GRCh37/hg19 using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner36 (BWA.0.6.2) to detect single nucleotide variations and insertions/deletions with Toolkit 3.4 (GATK). Common polymorphisms were filtered out through the 1000 Genomes Project database (https://www.internationalgenome.org/), Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC, https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), and the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD, https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/). All identified variants were annotated making use of ANNOVAR software. Finally, the sequencing variants were classified into five categories: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance, likely benign and benign according to the guidelines of ACMG.15

Sanger Sequencing

Sanger sequencing was performed to validate the candidate variants identified in the proband and his parents. PCR primers were designed to amplify exon 5 of the ZNF711 gene (Forward: 5’-CCCAATTCCTGGTTGTTGGTGA-3’, Reverse: 5’-CCTCCTCTGATACCATTGTGGG-3’) and exon 7 of the SRCAP gene (Forward: 5’-CTTTCCTAAGAGGCCAGCCC-3’, Reverse: 5’-AGTGGCAATTCTCCCTCACG-3’).

Protein Structure Prediction

We used AlphaFold 3 to predict the three-dimensional structures of the wild-type ZNF711 and SRCAP proteins and the corresponding variant proteins. The structures were visualized using PyMOL.

Results

The ZNF711 gene is associated with XLID97 (OMIM #300803), and the influenced individuals usually manifest mild to moderate ID, with half of them accompanied by ASD.16 The proband harbored a novel hemizygous variant in the ZNF711 gene (NM_001330574.2: c.205G>T (p.E69*)), which was maternally inherited. This variant was a nonsense mutation, resulting in the premature termination of polypeptide chain synthesis and thereby impairing the normal function of the ZNF711 protein (PVS1). Structural prediction using AlphaFold 3 revealed that the ZNF711 (p.E69*) variant disrupted the C2H2 domains, thereby compromising protein function (Figure 3A). This variant was not recorded in ExAC, 1000G or gnomAD database (PM2_Supporting), excluding it being a common polymorphism. Therefore, the variant in ZNF711 gene was classified as a likely pathogenic variant (PVS1+PM2_Supporting) according to ACMG guidelines.15 Figure 3Prediction of the effect of ZNF711 (A and B) and SRCAP (C and D) variants on the protein using AlphaFold. The SRCAP gene follows an autosomal dominant inheritance and is related to Floating-Harbor syndrome (FHS) and DEHMBA. The proband harbored a novel heterozygous variant in the exon 7 of SRCAP gene (NM_006662.3: c.673C>T (p.R225C)), and the proband’s core phenotypes were highly consistent with the manifestations of DEHMBA. Parental testing via trio-ES confirmed de novo occurrence, and the phenotype is specific to DEHMBA. Meanwhile, this de novo variant has not been reported previously (PS2). The frequency of this variant in the population was absent from ExAC, 1000G or gnomAD database (PM2_Supporting). This variant was predicted to be deleterious to protein function by multiple bioinformatics tools, with a REVEL score of 0.761 (PP3). AlphaFold 3 showed that SRCAP protein structure was changed (Figure 3B). Following ACMG guidelines, this variant was classified as likely pathogenic (PS2+PM2_Supporting+PP3).15

To systematically assess the variant spectrum of the ZNF711 and SRCAP genes, we analyzed a total of 55 XLID-97-associated ZNF711 variants and 69 DEHMBA-associated SRCAP variants in the ClinVar database (accessed March 11, 2026). The ZNF711 variants were classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic (n = 7, 12.7%), variants of uncertain significance (n = 29, 52.7%), benign/likely benign (n = 18, 32.7%), and conflicting classifications of pathogenicity (n = 1, 1.8%). Among the pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants, six were frameshift variants and one was a nonsense variant, all of which were predicted to cause loss of function, consistent with our findings. A similar analysis was performed for SRCAP variants. The SRCAP variants were classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic (n = 6, 8.7%), uncertain of significance (n = 53, 76.8%), benign/likely benign (n = 2, 2.9%), and conflicting classifications of pathogenicity (n = 8, 11.6%). The pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants consisted of frameshift variants (n = 4, 66.7%) and nonsense variants (n = 2, 33.3%), both of which were considered truncating variants. Although most pathogenic SRCAP variants were truncating variants, White-Brown et al17 first reported a missense variant causing SRCAP-related NDD outside the FHS locus. Consistently, the c.673C>T (p.R225C) variant identified in our case provides additional evidence supporting this finding.

Discussion

In this study, we report a male patient with facial dysmorphisms, hypotonia, ASD, speech delay, GDD and ID. ES revealed that the patient harbored two novel likely pathogenic variants, c.205G>T in ZNF711 gene and c.673C>T in SRCAP gene. To our knowledge, this represents the first case of compound likely pathogenic variants in both genes.

The ZNF711 gene is localized to the Xq13.3-q21.2 region and encodes the ZNF711 protein, which belongs to the Zinc finger protein family, and is a group of proteins widely involved in transcriptional regulation and modulation of gene expression.18,19 Highly expressed in brain tissues, ZNF711 is well implicated in XLID97.16,20 As a transcriptional activator, the ZNF711 protein can upregulate the expression of the KDM5C gene and recruit the PHF8 protein. Notably, KDM5C expression is co-regulated by ZNF711, PHF8 and ARX, and variants in these four genes (ZNF711, PHF8, ARX, KDM5C) are all implicated in XLID-related NDDs.21 KDM5C gene encodes JmjC-domain protein acting essential for dendritic spine plasticity. Collectively, given the high expression of ZNF711 in brain tissues, its variants may impact proliferation of nerve cells and the formation of synapses, which may contribute to the neurodevelopmental abnormalities observed in the proband.22–25 A total of 46 XLID97 cases with ZNF711 variants (including our proband) were identified16,20,23,25,26 (Table 2). According to records, ID was highly prevalent, affecting 44 (95.7%) patients, including 25 (54.3%) with mild ID and 19 (41.3%) with moderate‑severe ID. Additionally, 13 (28.3%) patients were diagnosed with ASD, suggesting a notable association between ASD and XLID97 cases. The SRCAP (SNF2-related CREBBP activator protein) gene is located at 16p11.2, and encodes the core catalytic ATPase component of the chromatin-remodeling SRCAP complex, which regulates transcription of target genes by incorporating H2A.Z-H2B dimers into nucleosomes.27,28 SRCAP plays a crucial role in the development of multiple organ systems, including the nervous, skeletal, and muscular system.29–31 Truncating variants in exons 33 and 34 of SRCAP cause FHS, while DEHMBA syndrome is a distinct developmental disorder arising from SRCAP variants outside the FHS locus.29 Compared with FHS, DEHMBA syndrome is more likely to present with ASD, psychiatric and behavioral problems, and hypotonia.29,32,33 The novel de novo missense variant c.673C>T (p.R225C) in SRCAP gene, identified in the proband outside of the FHS associated locus, has been reported to be responsible for DEHMBA.34 A further review of DEHMBA syndrome summarized 40 individuals (including our proband) with SRCAP-related NDD outside the FHS locus14,17,29,32,35–37(Table 3). Among them, 10 (25.0%) presented with pectus abnormalities, 20 (50.0%) with joint hypermobility, 23 (57.5%) with hypotonia, 27 (67.5%) with ID, 11 (27.5%) with ADHD, and 16 (40.0%) with ASD. These findings suggest that in this cohort, ID is the most prevalent phenotype, followed by hypotonia, joint hypermobility and ASD, while ADHD and pectus abnormalities are relatively less frequent. SRCAP modulates chromatin remodeling to facilitate transcription factor binding, while the ZNF711 protein functions as a transcription factor. These two proteins act on distinct but complementary steps of transcriptional regulation. SRCAP remodels chromatin to make regulatory regions accessible, and ZNF711 binds to target promoters to activate transcription.38,39 Therefore, we speculate that these two proteins may jointly contribute to the normal operation of transcriptional programs critical for development. However, this remains a hypothesis that requires functional validation. Specifically, the neurodevelopmental abnormalities in our proband are consistent with those reported in individuals carrying ZNF711 variants, supporting the evidence that ZNF711 regulates neuronal proliferation and synapse formation, and its variants typically result in mild to moderate ID.22 In contrast, phenotypes including ADHD symptoms, hypotonia, pectus abnormalities, and some specific facial dysmorphisms have been reported in association with SRCAP variants, as SRCAP plays a critical role in multi-organ development (skeletal, muscular, and facial structures).29–31

The proband’s complex neurodevelopmental and multisystemic phenotypes are likely attributable to concurrent likely pathogenic variants in the ZNF711 and SRCAP, with each gene potentially contributing distinct yet partially overlapping features. Phenotypes such as non-specific facial dysmorphisms, moderate ID, ASD speech delay and motor delay may be attributable to the overlapping effects of both genes. Correspondingly, the thick and everted lower lip, pectus abnormalities, hypotonia, and joint hypermobility, accompanied by anger outbursts and challenging behaviors observed in this child, are most probably attributable to the SRCAP gene variant.

Currently, ZNF711 in conjunction with PHF8 regulates downstream KDM5C gene, modulating the KDM5C-H3K4me3 pathway. Experimental evidence has demonstrated that suberanilohydroxamic acid, a potent reversible pan-histone deacetylase inhibitor, can correct the pathway in vitro and in vivo treatments.23 Unfortunately, no effective pharmacological treatments have been identified for DEHMBA symptoms, and rehabilitation therapy remains the primary intervention, providing certain therapeutic benefits for this proband.

The mother is a carrier of the X-linked ZNF711 variant. Each male offspring has a 50% risk of being affected, while each female offspring has a 50% chance of being a carrier. Comparatively, the variant in SRCAP is de novo, and the estimated recurrence risk for future pregnancies is approximately 0.5% to 2%. This risk may result from recurrence by chance, gonadal mosaicism, or somatic-gonadal mosaicism.40 Hence, during subsequent pregnancies, preimplantation genetic testing is the preferred approach to prevent recurrence, and prenatal diagnosis is crucial to accurately assess the recurrence risk and provide comprehensive genetic counseling.

Conclusions

Taken together, we report a Chinese family with a proband harboring concurrent likely pathogenic variants in the ZNF711 and SRCAP genes, thereby expanding the variant spectrum of both genes. This single case suggests that concurrent variants in multiple genes might contribute to the complex phenotype. However, given that the findings are based on a single patient, further studies are needed to confirm the potential combined effect of these variants. For patients with non-specific clinical phenotypes, genetic testing may be useful in clarifying the etiological diagnosis, which may facilitate precise treatment and provide valuable references for genetic counseling in similar families.
