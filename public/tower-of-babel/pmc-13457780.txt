A preliminary experimental study of venoactive compounds on human lymphatic endothelial cells: cellular response and therapeutic potential
Authors: Hyunmin Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLymphedema is a common complication following surgical procedures, particularly in breast cancer patients. However, no effective medical treatment has been clearly established. INTRODUCTION

Lymphedema is a commonly encountered condition in surgical patients, particularly as a complication following oncologic procedures. It frequently develops after axillary lymph node dissection or radiation therapy in breast cancer patients. The reported incidence of lymphedema following breast cancer treatment ranges from approximately 6% to 30% [1]. Lymphedema is also observed after lymph node surgery in other malignancies. For example, studies in melanoma patients have demonstrated that more than 20% of patients develop clinically significant limb swelling following nodal dissection [2].

Current management of lymphedema primarily relies on conservative approaches, including compression therapy, manual lymphatic drainage, and exercise, which aim to alleviate symptoms and slow disease progression. However, these treatments do not address the underlying pathophysiology and are highly dependent on patient adherence. Notably, there is no well-established medical therapy that effectively prevents or reverses the progression of lymphedema [3]. Given these limitations, venoactive compounds (venoactive drugs, VADs) have been used as an alternative option for the management of lymphedema. Originally developed for the treatment of chronic venous insufficiency, VADs are known to improve venous tone, reduce capillary permeability, and exert anti-inflammatory effects [4,5].

Lymphedema is caused by the accumulation of protein-rich fluid in the interstitial space due to damage or obstruction of lymphatic vessels. Lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), including human LECs (HLECs), play a critical role in maintaining lymphatic vascular integrity and fluid homeostasis. Beyond their structural function, HLECs actively regulate lymphatic permeability, immune cell trafficking, interstitial fluid clearance, and inflammatory signaling through coordinated intracellular pathways involving vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGF-C)/VEGF receptor 3 (VEGFR-3), phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT), and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) [6]. Dysfunction of HLECs contributes to impaired lymphatic drainage, persistent inflammation, and progressive tissue fibrosis, which are hallmarks of chronic lymphedema [7,8]. Therefore, evaluation of HLEC behavior and related intracellular signaling pathways may provide important insight into the mechanisms underlying lymphatic dysfunction and potential therapeutic targets. In this context, although VADs have historically been used based on their anti-inflammatory and microvascular stabilizing effects rather than direct stimulation of lymphangiogenesis, their potential influence on HLEC function and signaling pathways remains poorly understood. Accordingly, we investigated the effects of commonly used VADs on HLECs to better understand whether these agents may exert functional effects on lymphatic endothelial biology beyond simple reduction of capillary leakage.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study utilized commercially available HLECs obtained from a certified vendor. The study did not involve any direct interaction with human participants or the use of identifiable private information. Therefore, in accordance with institutional guidelines and the definition of human subjects research, this study was exempted from review by the Institutional Review Board of Chung-Ang University Gwangmyeong Hospital (No. 2604-321-069).

Experimental design

In this study, 3 widely used venoactive compounds (diosmin, calcium dobesilate, and Vitis vinifera extract) were selected to evaluate their effects on LEC function. HLECs were cultured under standard conditions and used as an in vitro model to investigate cellular responses to these agents.

Cell viability was examined using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay across a range of concentrations for each compound. Based on the observed dose-response relationships and cytotoxicity profiles, concentrations that did not induce significant cytotoxic effects were selected for subsequent analyses.

Lymphangiogenesis was evaluated through functional assays, including cell migration and tube formation. Cell migration was assessed using a Transwell assay (Corning) to quantify the migratory behavior of HLECs following treatment. Tube formation assays were performed on Matrigel (Corning) to evaluate the ability of HLECs to form capillary-like networks, and quantitative analysis was conducted by measuring total mesh area.

Further mechanistic insights were obtained through Western blot analysis of key signaling pathways involved in cell survival and proliferation. In particular, the activation states of the AKT, extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and p38 MAPK pathways were examined by assessing the expression levels of their phosphorylated and total protein forms. These analyses were performed to determine whether venoactive compounds modulate intracellular signaling pathways associated with LEC function.

All experiments were independently performed at least 3 times. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Materials

Diosmin (61386) and calcium dobesilate (SML0488) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, whereas Vitis vinifera extract was kindly provided by Hanlim Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. MTT (M5655) and crystal violet solution (V5265) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, and Matrigel (354234) was purchased from Corning. Primary antibodies against phospho-AKT (Ser473; #4060), AKT (#4691), phospho-ERK (Thr202/Tyr204; #4370), ERK (#4695), phospho-p38 (Thr180/Tyr182; #4511), and p38 (#8690) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. An anti-α-tubulin antibody (sc-5286) was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. MG132 and G418 sulfate were purchased from Assay Designs Inc. and InvivoGen, respectively.

Cell culture

HLECs (#2500) were purchased from ScienCell Research Laboratories. Cells were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM; #1001, ScienCell) supplemented according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Culture dishes were precoated with fibronectin (#8248, ScienCell). HLECs were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% air (Fig. 1).

Human lymphatic endothelial cells in culture (Human lymphatic endothelial cells in culture, ×200).

MTT assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. HLECs were treated with various concentrations of diosmin, calcium dobesilate, and Vitis vinifera extract for 24 hours. Following treatment, cells were incubated with MTT solution (0.1 mg/mL) at 37 ℃ for 2 hours. The culture medium was then removed, and the resulting insoluble formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm using a SPARK 10M multimode microplate reader (TECAN).

Cell migration

Cell migration was evaluated using a Transwell system (24-well plates, 8.0 µm pore size). The lower chambers were filled with 700 µL of ECM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). HLECs (5 × 103 cells) were seeded in the upper chamber in 200 µL of ECM containing 1% FBS and treated with either 25 µM diosmin, 100 µM calcium dobesilate, or 50 µg/mL Vitis vinifera extract. After 24 hours of incubation at 37 ℃, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes and stained with crystal violet. Non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed, and migrated cells on the lower surface were counted under a microscope.

Tube formation

For the tube formation assay, 200 µL of Matrigel was added to each well of a 48-well plate and allowed to polymerize at 37 ℃ for 30 minutes. HLECs (2 × 104 cells) were seeded onto the Matrigel-coated wells and treated with either 25 µM diosmin, 100 µM calcium dobesilate, or 50 µg/mL Vitis vinifera extract. After 16 hours of incubation, tube-like structures were visualized, and the total mesh area was quantified using the Angiogenesis Analyzer in ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Western blot analysis

HLECs were treated with either 25 µM diosmin, 100 µM calcium dobesilate, or 50 µg/mL Vitis vinifera extract for 24 hours. Whole cell lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (R2002, Biosesang) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (P8340, Sigma-Aldrich). Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore) at 200 mA for 1 hour on ice. Membranes were blocked with either 5% of the skim milk or 5% bovine serum albumin (for phospho-proteins) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T) for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Vector Laboratories). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagent (Thermo Scientific). Alpha (α)-tubulin was used as a loading control to ensure equal protein loading across all lanes. The expression levels of phosphorylated proteins were normalized to their corresponding total protein levels, and total protein levels were verified using α-tubulin.

RESULTS

Cell viability and dose optimization

Although a cell viability threshold of greater than 80% is commonly used to define acceptable experimental conditions, the determination of maximal non-cytotoxic concentration in this study was based on a comprehensive assessment, including dose-response patterns, experimental reproducibility, and suitability for downstream functional assays. Final concentrations were established through repeated experiments and consultation with experimental specialists.

Cell viability of HLECs following treatment with 3 venoactive compounds—diosmin, calcium dobesilate, and Vitis vinifera extract—was evaluated using the MTT assay across a range of concentrations (Fig. 2). Diosmin exhibited a concentration-dependent effect on cell viability. No significant cytotoxicity was observed at lower concentrations. However, a significant reduction in cell viability was detected at concentrations above 50 µM (P < 0.047). Based on these findings, 25 µM was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments, as it preserved cell viability without inducing significant cytotoxic effects (Fig. 2A). Calcium dobesilate did not show a significant decrease in cell viability within the tested concentration range. Notably, no cytotoxic effects were observed even at the highest concentration tested. Accordingly, the maximum concentration of 100 µM was selected as the optimal concentration for further experiments (Fig. 2B). Treatment with Vitis vinifera extract also demonstrated a dose-dependent decrease in cell viability. Although a mild reduction in viability was observed at 50 µg/mL, cell viability remained above the commonly accepted threshold for non-cytotoxic conditions (>80%). In contrast, concentrations above 100 µg/mL resulted in a more pronounced and statistically significant reduction in viability (P < 0.001). Consequently, 50 µg/mL was selected as the optimal concentration (Fig. 2C).

Human lymphatic endothelial cell (HLEC) viability. Cell viability of HLECs treated with (A) diosmin, (B) calcium dobesilate, and (C) Vitis vinifera extract were assessed using the MTT assay. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from 3 independent experiments. NS, not significant. *P < 0.05, ***P < 0.001.

Inhibition of human lymphatic endothelial cell migration

The effects of venoactive compounds on the migratory behavior of HLECs were investigated using a Transwell migration assay. Representative microscopic images of migrated cells are presented in Fig. 3A. In the control group (no treatment), a relatively high density of cells was observed on the lower surface of the membrane, indicating active migration. In contrast, all treatment groups exhibited a noticeable reduction in the number of migrated cells. The distribution of cells in the treated groups appeared less dense and less uniform compared to the control, suggesting diminished migratory capacity. Quantitative analysis of migrated cells is shown in Fig. 3B. When normalized to the control group (100 ± 0.10), treatment with 25 µM diosmin resulted in a significant decrease in migration to 77 ± 15.31 (P = 0.010). A more pronounced reduction was observed in the 100 µM calcium dobesilate-treated group, with migration reduced to 68 ± 1.68 (P < 0.001). The greatest inhibitory effect was detected in cells treated with 50 µg/mL Vitis vinifera extract, which exhibited a migration value of 63 ± 18.32 (P = 0.003). All treatment conditions demonstrated statistically significant reductions in cell migration compared to the control, with varying degrees of inhibition among the compounds. Although these venoactive compounds were anticipated to enhance LEC migration, all treatments consistently resulted in a reduction of migratory activity under the experimental conditions. This trend was observed in both qualitative microscopic findings (Fig. 3A) and quantitative measurements (Fig. 3B).

Human lymphatic endothelial cell (HLEC) migration. (A) Representative microscopic images of migrated HLECs in the Transwell assay (Corning; ×200). (B) Quantification of migrated cells. Diosmin, calcium dobesilate, and Vitis vinifera extract significantly reduced cell migration compared to the control. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from 3 independent experiments. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

No significant change in human lymphatic endothelial cell tube formation

The effects of venoactive compounds on the tube-forming ability of HLECs were evaluated using a Matrigel-based tube formation assay. Representative microscopic images of tube-like structures are shown in Fig. 4A. Quantitative analysis of tube formation was performed by measuring the relative total mesh area, as presented in Fig. 4B. When normalized to the control group (1.00 ± 0.082), treatment with 25 µM diosmin resulted in a value of 1.05 ± 0.036, which was not statistically significant (P = 0.423). Similarly, treatment with 100 µM calcium dobesilate showed a value of 0.95 ± 0.042 (P = 0.433), and 50 µg/mL Vitis vinifera extract yielded a value of 0.99 ± 0.236 (P = 0.950). None of the treatment groups demonstrated a statistically significant difference compared to the control. Overall, treatment with venoactive compounds did not significantly alter the tube-forming capacity of HLECs under the experimental conditions.

Human lymphatic endothelial cell (HLEC) tube formation. (A) Representative microscopic images of tube-like structures formed by HLECs on Matrigel (Corning; ×200). (B) Quantitative analysis of relative total mesh area. No significant differences were observed among diosmin, calcium dobesilate, and Vitis vinifera extract treatment groups compared to the control. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from 3 independent experiments. NS, not significant.

Modulation of AKT, ERK, and p38 signaling pathways

To investigate the molecular effects of venoactive compounds on intracellular signaling pathways in HLECs, Western blot analysis was performed to assess the activation status of AKT, ERK, and p38 MAPK pathways. Protein expression levels were quantified as the ratio of phosphorylated to total protein and normalized to the control group (set as 1.00). For the AKT pathway (Fig. 5A), treatment with 25 µM diosmin resulted in a relative expression level of 1.21 ± 0.231, which did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.072). Similarly, 100 µM calcium dobesilate showed a value of 0.91 ± 0.096 (P = 0.076), indicating no significant change compared to the control. In contrast, treatment with 50 µg/mL Vitis vinifera extract significantly reduced AKT phosphorylation, with a relative expression level of 0.51 ± 0.249 (P = 0.002). Analysis of the ERK pathway (Fig. 5B) revealed a similar trend. Diosmin (0.93 ± 0.167, P = 0.377) and calcium dobesilate (0.91 ± 0.153, P = 0.216) did not significantly alter ERK activation. However, Vitis vinifera extract markedly decreased ERK phosphorylation to 0.51 ± 0.352, reaching statistical significance (P = 0.015). For the p38 MAPK pathway (Fig. 5C), diosmin treatment led to a modest but statistically significant increase in phosphorylation levels (1.13 ± 0.088, P = 0.014). Calcium dobesilate showed no significant effect (1.03 ± 0.117, P = 0.634). In contrast, Vitis vinifera extract significantly suppressed p38 phosphorylation, with a relative expression level of 0.50 ± 0.239 (P = 0.003). Overall, among the tested venoactive compounds, Vitis vinifera extract consistently induced significant downregulation of AKT, ERK, and p38 signaling pathways. Diosmin exhibited a selective effect, showing a significant increase in p38 activation without affecting AKT or ERK, while calcium dobesilate did not produce significant changes in any of the signaling pathways examined.

AKT, ERK, and p38 signaling in human lymphatic endothelial cells. (A) Relative expressions of p-AKT/AKT, (B) p-ERK/ERK, and (C) p-p38/p38 determined by Western blot analysis. Vitis vinifera extract significantly reduced phosphorylation of AKT, ERK, and p38, whereas diosmin increased p38 phosphorylation. Calcium dobesilate showed no significant effect. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from 3 independent experiments. AKT, protein kinase B; ERK, extracellular signal-regulated kinase; p, phosphorylated. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01.

DISCUSSION

The lymphatic and venous systems are anatomically and functionally interconnected, forming an integrated lymphovenous network that plays a crucial role in maintaining tissue fluid homeostasis. While the venous system is responsible for returning blood to central circulation, the lymphatic system facilitates the clearance of interstitial fluid, macromolecules, and immune cells [7,9,10,11]. Dysfunction of either system can lead to fluid accumulation and edema, and increasing evidence indicates that chronic venous insufficiency and lymphatic dysfunction frequently coexist and share common pathophysiological mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and impaired microcirculation [4,5,7]. Based on this close relationship, venoactive compounds originally developed for the treatment of chronic venous disorders have been considered a potential therapeutic option for lymphedema. These agents are known to improve venous tone, reduce capillary permeability, and exert anti-inflammatory effects, thereby providing a theoretical basis for their use in conditions associated with impaired fluid drainage [12,13,14,15]. Importantly, VADs are understood to be primarily vasostabilizing agents. However, despite this rationale, the direct effects of VADs on LECs and lymphangiogenesis remain poorly understood.

Lymphangiogenesis, the process by which new lymphatic vessels form from pre-existing vessels, plays a fundamental role in maintaining tissue fluid balance and regulating immune responses. This process is primarily governed by the VEGF-C/VEGFR-3 signaling axis and involves the proliferation, migration, and differentiation of LECs. In addition, intracellular signaling pathways such as AKT, ERK, and p38 MAPK are critically involved in regulating LEC survival, migration, and tube formation [16,17,18]. Lymphangiogenesis may serve as a compensatory mechanism to restore impaired lymphatic drainage. When appropriately regulated, it has the potential to improve lymphatic flow and reduce interstitial fluid accumulation, making it an attractive therapeutic target. Indeed, experimental approaches such as VEGF-C-based therapies and strategies aimed at promoting lymphatic regeneration have demonstrated promising potential in preclinical models [18,19,20]. Accordingly, demonstrating that venoactive compounds can directly regulate LEC function and modulate lymphangiogenesis beyond their established vascular effects would provide a stronger mechanistic basis for their use in lymphedema management.

Diosmin, a well-established flavonoid commonly used in chronic venous disorders, has been reported to exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, as well as to improve microcirculation and lymphatic drainage. These properties have led to the assumption that diosmin may promote LEC function [21,22,23,24]. However, in our study, diosmin did not significantly activate the AKT or ERK signaling pathways, which are closely associated with cell survival and proliferation. Instead, a significant increase in p38 MAPK activation was observed. Given that the p38 pathway is typically associated with cellular stress responses and inflammatory signaling, this finding suggests that diosmin may primarily exert a modulatory or stress-related effect rather than directly promoting lymphangiogenic activity. Consistently, diosmin treatment resulted in a reduction in cell migration without significantly affecting tube formation, indicating a limited or context-dependent role in regulating LEC function.

Calcium dobesilate is known to improve microvascular permeability, reduce blood viscosity, and inhibit VEGF-related signaling, thereby contributing to vascular stabilization [25,26]. In the present study, calcium dobesilate did not induce significant changes in AKT, ERK, or p38 MAPK signaling pathways. Furthermore, no meaningful alterations were observed in either cell migration or tube formation assays. These findings suggest that calcium dobesilate does not directly stimulate LEC activation or lymphangiogenesis under the experimental conditions tested. Rather, its therapeutic effects may be mediated through indirect mechanisms, such as modulation of vascular permeability and attenuation of inflammatory responses, rather than direct regulation of LEC behavior.

Vitis vinifera extract, which is well known for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, demonstrated a distinct pattern of effects [27,28,29]. Treatment with Vitis vinifera extract showed a tendency toward suppression of AKT, ERK, and p38 MAPK signaling pathways. Given that the AKT and ERK pathways play critical roles in cell survival, proliferation, and migration, this broad inhibition is likely to have contributed to the observed reduction in cell migration. However, despite the suppression of these signaling pathways and migratory capacity, no significant change was observed in tube formation. Although this pattern may initially appear inhibitory in healthy HLECs, it may alternatively reflect a vasostabilizing or anti-inflammatory effect under pathological conditions characterized by chronic endothelial activation and increased permeability, such as lymphedema. In this context, suppression of excessive signaling activity may represent modulation of pathological over-activation rather than a purely negative effect on LEC function.

The results of this study demonstrate that venoactive compounds do not uniformly enhance LEC function or lymphangiogenesis. Instead, each compound exhibits distinct and, in some cases, opposing effects on cellular behavior and signaling pathways. These findings challenge the conventional assumption that venoactive agents may universally benefit lymphatic disorders and highlight the need for a more nuanced understanding of their mechanisms of action. Although the present in vitro findings suggest that venoactive compounds do not directly promote LEC function or lymphangiogenesis, their potential clinical benefits in lymphedema should not be completely excluded. Venoactive compounds may still contribute to symptom improvement indirectly by reducing capillary permeability, improving venous return, and decreasing the overall interstitial fluid load that the lymphatic system must transport [13,30]. Therefore, the discrepancy between cellular-level effects and potential clinical outcomes should be interpreted with caution. These results should be interpreted within a revised mechanistic framework in which VADs are not lymphangiogenesis-modulating agents but vasostabilizing drugs. Furthermore, our findings suggest that the therapeutic effects of venoactive compounds are unlikely to be mediated through direct stimulation of LEC growth or lymphangiogenesis. This interpretation is consistent with the known pharmacological actions of many venoactive agents, which primarily target the blood microcirculation rather than the lymphatic endothelium itself. Previous studies have shown that venoactive compounds exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, improve capillary resistance, and reduce microvascular permeability. Through these mechanisms, they may decrease plasma extravasation and the formation of interstitial fluid, thereby reducing the volume of fluid that must be cleared by the lymphatic system [31,32,33]. Consequently, clinical improvement in patients with lymphedema may occur without any direct enhancement of LEC proliferation, migration, or tube formation.

This study has several limitations. First, as an in vitro study using HLECs, it may not fully reflect the complex in vivo environment of lymphedema. Second, the effects of venoactive compounds were evaluated at a single time point, limiting the assessment of time-dependent responses. Third, lymphangiogenesis was assessed using migration and tube formation assays, which represent only partial aspects of this complex process. Furthermore, these functional assays, including Transwell migration and Matrigel tube formation, primarily reflect endothelial motility and morphogenesis and are commonly used in growth factor-driven angiogenesis models. However, they may not fully capture the vasoprotective mechanisms of venoactive compounds. In particular, endothelial barrier function and oxidative stress-related effects were not directly evaluated, and additional permeability assays or reactive oxygen species measurements could have provided further mechanistic insight. Finally, only 3 venoactive compounds were examined, and the in vitro concentrations may not directly correspond to clinical conditions.

In conclusion, VADs do not appear to act via direct stimulation of lymphangiogenesis. Their clinical benefit likely relies on indirect mechanisms, such as reducing capillary filtration and inflammation, which were not captured in this HLEC-specific model. Overall, our findings support the concept that venoactive compounds indirectly contribute to vascular and tissue stabilization through antioxidant, permeability-reducing, and anti-inflammatory effects at the microvascular and tissue levels. Instead, their use should be carefully individualized based on patient response, with close monitoring to evaluate therapeutic efficacy. As a preliminary experimental study, this work provides a foundation for understanding the role of venoactive compounds in lymphedema. Further studies incorporating diverse experimental conditions, in vivo models, and clinical validation are needed.
