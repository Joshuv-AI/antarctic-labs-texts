Modulating superconductivity in elementary materials by doping
Authors: Simin Nie, Xiting Zhang, Jiaxuan Guo, Fritz B. Prinz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Influenced by the discovery of high-temperature superconductivity in compressed hydrides, the search for superconductors with high transition temperatures has received growing attention. Here, based on first-principles calculations, the influence of doping on superconducting properties of five elementary materials (i.e., Mo, Nb, Pb, Al, Zr) is studied. The electron–phonon coupling strength and transition temperature can always be effectively increased due to both phonon softening and increased contribution of some specific phonons either by electron doping or hole doping. For each material, there is an optimum doping level with highest transition temperature. Our work highlights the crucial role of doping in raising the transition temperature of traditional Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer superconductors.

Introduction

Superconductivity, one of macroscopic quantum phenomena, has attracted enormous and persistent interest since its first observation in mercury by Kamerlingh Onnes in 19111. Below a critical temperature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document}, a superconductor features two main attractive characteristics: zero resistance to electrical current and perfect diamagnetism expelling magnetic field lines from its interior. Although the Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer (BCS) theory2 cannot be applied to properly explain cuprate superconductors3–6 and iron-based superconductors7–10 with much higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document} as compared with conventional superconductors at ambient pressure, it has achieved great success in precisely explaining the physical properties of conventional electron–phonon superconductors. One of the findings in the application of BCS theory is that in 1968 metallic hydrogen was predicted by Ashcroft to be the first room temperature superconductor11, which has been regarded as the “Holy Grail” in the field of condensed matter physics. However, to metallize hydrogen by breaking strong covalent H-H bonds, extraordinary high pressure (at the order of megabar) is needed. In 2004, Ashcroft proposed that the hydrogen-rich compounds with chemical precompression through alloying hydrogen with other metal elements can decrease the pressure needed to induce room temperature superconductivity12.

Recently, guided by the second proposal of Ashcroft, researchers have achieved significant progress in highly-compressed hydrides with near room temperature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document} at high-pressure13–25. The initial breakthrough is the discovery of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {SH}_3$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document} around 200 K at a pressure of 155 GPa26, breaking the temperature record for superconductivity held by cuperates for almost 30 years27. Soon after, another hydride, lanthanum superhydride \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {LaH}_{{10}}$$\end{document}, exhibited an even higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {T}_c$$\end{document} of 260 K at 190 GPa28, further motivating the study of high-temperature superconductivity. To date, an increasing number of clathrate hydrides with high \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document} have been experimentally confirmed or theoretically predicted, such as, yttrium superhydride \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {YH}_9$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {T}_c$$\end{document} of 262 K at 182 GPa29, calcium hydride \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CaH}_6$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {T}_c$$\end{document} of 210 K at 160–190 GPa30, etc. It is worth noting that theoretical calculations have played a crucial role in discovering new high-temperature superconductors because of big advances in computational methods and the corresponding numerical realization13. Most high \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document} hydrides were first theoretically predicted and then confirmed experimentally.

Although high-pressure techniques can effectively increase \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document}, it is still difficult to achieve the high pressure required by most hydrides. Moreover, the requirement of such extreme condition makes the actual application of hydrides virtually impossible. Finding alternative ways to increase \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document} is of great promise. Because elemental materials represent the simplest systems with only one type of atom, they provide ideal platforms for studying the strategies towards enhancing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document}. In this work, based on first-principles calculations, we have studied the doping effect in elementary superconductors. The critical temperature can be significantly increased for all investigated materials either by electron doping or hole doping. Surprisingly, even a small amount of electron doping in Mo leads to an increase of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document} from 0.9640 K to 19.4414 K (about a 20-fold increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document}). This result of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document} increase is mainly due to both phonon softening and the enhancement of electron–phonon coupling (EPC) of some specific phonons. For each material, there is an optimum doping level with highest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document}, away from which the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document} will decrease due to the decrease of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _{log}$$\end{document}.

Next, the phonon-related properties of Mo are explored. Figure 3a shows the calculated phonon spectra along the principal symmetry directions, together with the experimental dispersion. It is obvious that our result is consistent with previous experimental result34, which was measured by the technique of coherent inelastic neutron scattering. Because the primitive unit cell only has one Mo atom, there are a total of three acoustic phonon bands tending to zero in the long wavelength limit, which include one longitudinal acoustic branch and two transverse acoustic branches. No imaginary frequencies were found in the phonon spectra, indicating that the structure is dynamically stable. One feature of the phonon bands is that both the H and P points exhibit threefold degenerate frequencies due to the crystalline symmetries, indicating the possible presence of topological surface phonon modes on the boundary35,36. Another significant feature of the phonon dispersion is the phonon softening near the H point. To investigate the EPC, the isotropic Eliashberg spectral function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha ^2F(\omega )$$\end{document} is calculated, as shown in Fig. 4a. It is obvious that the soft phonons around the H point have significant contributions to the EPC constant. Next, we evaluate the influence of doping effect on the phonon properties of Mo. The phonon bands of doped Mo are calculated (Fig. 3a,b). During the hole doping process, a few phonon modes (for example, the highest phonon mode at the N point) are softened and most phonon modes are hardened. In contrast, electron doping usually leads to phonon softening. Especially, the phonons around the H point is significantly softened. The peak of Eliashberg spectral function due to the softening at the H point will shift towards lower energies, leading to the increase of EPC strength \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}. This can be understood as follows: assuming that the overall shape of the Eliashberg spectral function is invariant, its low-energy shift leads to the decrease of the denominator in Eq. (1) and finally to the increase of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document}. To clearly show its evolution in the doping process, Eliashberg spectral functions of doped Mo are calculated, as shown in Fig. 4a. The peaks around 21 meV due to the phonon softening at the H point indeed move to the low-energy direction. Moreover, the peak magnitudes are increased. To understand it, the contribution of each phonon to the EPC (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{\textbf{q}\nu }$$\end{document}) is calculated, as shown in Fig. 3. Considering the strong intrinsic SOC strength in some materials (such as Pb, Ta), we also study the influence of SOC on the transition temperature (Table 1). It is clear that the transition temperature doesn’t change too much for all studied elementary superconductors. Therefore, for these materials, it is safe to ignore SOC when EPC is considered.

As Mo-4d orbitals have strong electron-electron correlations, and some materials have shown a strong influence of correlations on phonons45–47, we also calculated the EPC and superconducting transition temperature of Mo using PBE+U method with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {U}=\text {6}$$\end{document} eV for Mo-d orbitals. For Mo, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document} are computed to be 0.39636 and 1.0512, respectively, which are very close to the results shown in Table 1. Therefore, it is safe to ignore the influence of correlations in Mo-4d on the EPC. The possibility of magnetic states induced by doping has also been investigated. For Pb and Al, it is difficult to induce magnetic order since their valence electrons occupy only s and p orbitals. In contrast, Mo, Nb, and Zr have partially filled d orbitals, and doping may induce magnetic moments. To assess this, we performed spin-polarized calculations at the doping levels considered. While the initial magnetic moments were allowed in the calculations, all systems converged to non-magnetic ground states. We therefore conclude that magnetic effects can be safely neglected for all five elemental superconductors studied.

Conclusion

In summary, we have performed first-principles calculations to study the influence of doping on the properties of elementary superconductors. Interestingly, the transition temperature can be significantly increased for all studied materials either by electron doping or hole doping caused by both phonon softening and the enhancement of EPC of specific phonons. Moreover, each material has an optimum doping level with the highest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_\text {c}$$\end{document}. Our work provides a promising path to increase the transition temperature of superconductors.

Methods

We used first-principles calculations to obtain optimized structures and converged ground states, employing the QUANTUM ESPRESSO package49,50. The electronic wave functions were represented by a plane-wave basis set with ultrasoft pseudopotentials51. The exchange-correlation functional was treated within the generalized gradient approximation parameterized by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof (PBE)52. The cutoff energies for the kinetic energy of the plane-wave basis and charge density were set to 100 and 400 Ry, respectively. A dense \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$24\times 24\times 24$$\end{document} k-point grid was used to sample the Brillouin zone in the self-consistent calculations. The structures were fully relaxed until the residual forces on each atom were less than 0.001 eV/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {\text{\AA }}$$\end{document}. To study the doping effect, the homogeneous background method was employed53,54. For a periodically repeated charged system, the total energy diverges. To satisfy the requirement of charge neutrality, a compensating uniform background was added as part of the method. The calculations exhibit slow convergence of total energies with respect to the size L of the supercell. To compensate for this shortcoming, the method incorporates a first-order correction to the energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^2 q^2 \alpha /L\epsilon$$\end{document}, where q is the net charge of the system, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} the Madelung constant of a point charge q placed in a homogeneous background charge -q, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document} the dielectric constant of the system.
