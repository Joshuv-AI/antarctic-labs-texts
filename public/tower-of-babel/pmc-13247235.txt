Contribution of the anterior lateral motor cortex to predictive postural control in rats
Authors: Akira Konosu, Kaito Fukuhara, Kenta Ouchida, Tetsuro Funato, Dai Yanagihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredictive postural control is a motor function that enables animals to manage expected mechanical disturbances. The rodent anterior lateral motor cortex (ALM) may contribute to this function through interactions with the cerebellum; however, the underlying cortical mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we examined whether the ALM contributes to the emergence of experience-dependent improvements in predictive postural control in a rat platform-tilt paradigm. Rats received bilateral muscimol injections into the ALM (muscimol group) or vehicle injections (vehicle group) before completing sixty conditioned platform-tilting trials. The initial upright posture and reactive responses to the disturbances did not differ between groups. However, both segmental kinematics and regression-based analyses of the center-of-mass angle revealed that experience-dependent reduction in postural response, which is consistent with the acquisition of predictive postural control, was attenuated in the muscimol group. Furthermore, unlike the control, the muscimol group did not develop forward shank inclination, which is a preparatory adjustment to counteract the upcoming disturbance. These results indicate that ALM activity plays an important role in the emergence of experience-dependent improvements in predictive postural control, while exerting little effect on baseline standing. Our findings highlight the role of ALM in feedforward regulation of posture and motivate future investigations into broader networks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49148-0.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Humans and animals, with their nervous systems being subject to delays and noise, need to predictively control their bodies based on their experiences with their bodily state and environment1. In particular, the ability to predictively maintain an upright balance forms the foundation of daily activities and sports performance, attracting considerable attention with regard to fall prevention and rehabilitation in the elderly and in patients with neurological disorders2–4.

Clinical studies in patients with cerebellar disorders5–7 and animal experiments8,9, together with theoretical frameworks1,10, suggest that cerebellar plasticity is central to the acquisition of predictive postural control4,11. Studies have also suggested that the cerebral cortex and basal ganglia contribute to predictive postural control, particularly during the initiation and timing of postural adjustments12–14. It has been hypothesized that the cerebral cortex and the cerebellum jointly generate postural commands by exchanging information about action goals and the timing/coordination of muscle recruitment11, yet the detailed mechanisms remain unclear.

A rat experimental paradigm that promotes the acquisition of predictive postural control by applying a platform-tilt disturbance was recently developed15. Using this paradigm, it was demonstrated that the reactive responses evoked by the disturbance were mechanically similar to those observed in humans, and that lesions of the cerebellar vermis impaired the acquisition of predictive controls16. The next challenge is to identify the cortical regions that contribute to predictive postural control through possible cooperation with the cerebellum.

In non-human primates, the frontal cortex has been well documented to show preparatory neural activity preceding overt movement17–19. In humans, converging lines of evidence suggest that the supplementary motor area (SMA) contributes to predictive control, including postural adjustments13,20–22. Similarly, in rodents, a relatively restricted region of the frontal cortex has been implicated in predictive function, with the anterior lateral motor area (ALM) constituting a prominent part23. The ALM was first identified through optogenetic screening across the cortex as the region whose perturbation most profoundly impaired performance on the sensorimotor discrimination task24 and has been described as a premotor region25. Further studies have revealed that it constitutes a distinct cluster based on axonal tracer-derived connectivity analyses26. The ALM receives projections from the cerebellar fastigial nucleus via the ventral medial (VM) nucleus of the thalamus and reciprocally projects to the cerebellar cortex through the basal pontine nucleus27–29. Optogenetic manipulations during conditioning tasks have demonstrated that preparatory activity in the fastigial nucleus and vermis is necessary for ALM activity, and vice versa27,29,30. Taken together, these results suggest that the ALM plays a critical role in predictive postural control through reciprocal interactions within a neural loop involving the vermis and fastigial nucleus.

In the present study, we examined the contribution of the ALM to predictive postural control by inactivating the ALM in the rat postural task. We hypothesized that ALM inactivation would impair the emergence of experience-dependent improvements in predictive postural control, while having little influence on the baseline upright posture.

Results

On the day of behavioral testing, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane for marker placement (Fig. 1b, f) and solution injections into the ALM (muscimol in the muscimol group, vehicle in the vehicle group; Fig. 1d, e). After a 30-min recovery period, the animals underwent 60 repeated platform-tilt disturbance trials (Fig. 1a–c). Figure 2 illustrates the representative motion trajectories following disturbance onset (Fig. 2a) and time-series changes in body segmental angles during the three initial and terminal trials (averaged across trials; Fig. 2b). In the initial trials, most rats in both groups exhibited backward rotation of the foot, shank, thigh, and lower trunk segments (in the same direction as the platform tilt), whereas the head segment rotated forward (Figs. 2b and 3). The upper-trunk segment rotated forward in some animals and backward in others. A comparison of the segmental angle changes during the first 0.3 s after disturbance onset (TD–T0.3) revealed no significant differences between the vehicle and muscimol groups for any segment (Table 1). Likewise, the segmental angles at disturbance onset (TD) did not differ between the groups. These findings indicate that ALM inhibition does not appreciably alter the upright posture or the reactive response to disturbances under naïve conditions.

Across the 60 repeated disturbance trials, both groups exhibited behavioral adaptation, including a reduction in postural response (Figs. 2a,b and 3). Figure 3 compares the segmental angles and their changes between the three initial and terminal trials. When comparing the segmental angle changes induced by the disturbance (TD–T0.3), the amplitude of rotation in the foot (vehicle: p = 0.008; muscimol: p = 0.025), shank (vehicle: p < 0.001; muscimol: p = 0.008), and head (vehicle: p = 0.001; muscimol: p = 0.029) segments decreased in both groups, while the thigh (p = 0.011) and lower trunk (p < 0.001) segments also showed reduced rotation in the vehicle group. These results indicate that suppression of the postural response was more pronounced in the vehicle group than in the muscimol group. At the timing of the conditioned cue (TC), segmental angles did not differ significantly between the initial and terminal trials in the muscimol group. In contrast, the shank segment in the vehicle group was more forward-tilted during the terminal trials (p = 0.008). This forward inclination likely represents a predictive postural adjustment that counteracted the backward rotation of the shank segment induced by the disturbance31. When comparing segmental angle changes during the cue period (TC–TD), no significant differences were observed across trials in any segment of the vehicle group. However, in the muscimol group, the upper trunk (p = 0.049) and head (p = 0.010) segments showed smaller rotational amplitudes in the terminal trials. In both groups in the initial trials, the observed segmental movements—characterized by forward rotation from the shank to the upper trunk and backward rotation of the head—likely reflect a process through which animals explore an optimal upright posture. In the muscimol group, the reduced rotational amplitudes of the upper trunk and head segments across trials may indicate a decline in mobility.

To evaluate the learning rate of postural adaptation at the whole-body level, we analyzed the center-of-mass (CoM) angle as an index of overall postural response (Fig. 4). Typically, the CoM angle increased monotonically after the onset of the disturbance, indicating a backward shift of the CoM, and the rate of increase gradually decreased across trials (Fig. 4a, b). Therefore, the CoM angle at 0.3 s after disturbance onset (T0.3) was used as a representative measure of postural displacement, and its relationship with trial number was examined by regression analysis (Fig. 4c). For each animal, three models described by Eqs. 1–3 (see Methods) were fitted to the data, and the optimal model was selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC; Fig. S1). The CoM angle predicted by the optimal model at trial 0 (), representing the naïve condition before the disturbance experience, did not differ between the groups (p = 0.344; Fig. 4d). This finding was consistent with the absence of group differences in the segmental rotation amplitudes (TD–T0.3) observed in the initial trials (Table 1). In contrast, the normalized expected CoM angle after 60 disturbance trials () was significantly greater in the muscimol group than in the vehicle group (p = 0.013; Fig. 4d). Additionally, to directly evaluate the learning rate as a time constant, the coefficients A (predicted CoM angle at trial 0) and B (learning rate) that were obtained by fitting the model defined in Eq. (1) were compared between the groups (Fig. 4e). Coefficient A did not differ between the groups (p = 0.159), whereas log10-transformed B was significantly smaller in the muscimol group (p = 0.049). These results indicate a slower learning rate for predictive postural control in animals with ALM inhibition.

Discussion

In the present study, we inactivated the ALM in rats and examined its effect on the acquisition of predictive postural control. During the initial phase of the repeated disturbance trials, the muscimol group showed no significant differences from the vehicle group in the standing posture or reactive responses to the disturbance (Table 1). After repeated trials, the number of segments in which the reactive segmental rotation diminished was smaller in the muscimol group than in the vehicle group (Fig. 3, bottom row). Regression analysis of the CoM angles demonstrated that attenuation of the postural response across repeated trials was smaller in the muscimol group (Fig. 4c–e). Furthermore, a forward inclination of the shank segment preceding the disturbance was observed in the vehicle group, but not in the muscimol group (Fig. 3, top row). Because shank rotation substantially affects CoM displacement15, and similar forward inclinations have been reported in humans as preparatory adjustments counteracting platform tilts31, this pattern likely indicates impaired acquisition of predictive postural control in the muscimol group. Taken together, our results demonstrate that while ALM inactivation exerts little effect on postural maintenance or reflexive and muscle-level postural responses, it plays a critical role in the emergence of experience-dependent improvements in predictive postural control, supporting our initial hypothesis.

During the cue period, the rotational amplitudes of the upper trunk and head segments decreased in the ALM-inactivated rats (Fig. 3, middle row). The segmental rotations observed in both the ALM-inactivated and vehicle-injected rats in the initial trials likely reflect exploratory movements performed after the animals had adopted a bipedal stance, possibly to search for an optimal posture while maintaining the mouth position. One explanation for this reduced optimization behavior in the ALM-inactivated rats is that such movements are kinematically disadvantageous for coping with disturbances. Indeed, segmental rotations during the cue period can amplify subsequent postural responses induced by disturbances. However, forward rotation of the upper trunk during the cue period may be advantageous in counteracting the backward rotation associated with the postural response. Therefore, the above-mentioned explanation alone is insufficient. Another possibility is that the ALM-inactivated rats had adapted to other kinematically less-visible bodily factors. Increased antagonist muscle co-contraction during platform-disturbance tasks has been reported in patients with Parkinson’s disease12 and cerebellar disorders5. Therefore, the ALM-inactivated rats may have increased predictive antagonist co-contraction as a compensatory strategy for impaired motor functions, including prediction, leading to increased joint stiffness and reduced mobility. In any case, the ALM-inactivated rats likely required a distinct postural strategy to deal with the observed larger postural responses after learning.

To elucidate how a given brain region contributes to predictive control, two classical experimental approaches have been widely employed: (1) perturbation after learning to assess its involvement in the expression or retention of predictive control, and (2) perturbation during learning to evaluate its contribution to experience-dependent acquisition. The present study adopts the latter approach, suppressing ALM activity through repeated exposure to postural disturbances. However, this design does not allow the behavioral deficits observed after training to be attributed to impairments in expression or acquisition. Previous studies using the former approach have consistently demonstrated that the ALM contributes to the execution of predictive motor commands24,27,30; it is likely that the ALM supports at least the expression function. Moreover, the fundamental principle of motor learning is that internal models are updated by comparing the predicted sensory consequences of a motor command with the actual sensory feedback, and this computation requires the execution of motor commands32,33. Accordingly, the ALM appears to contribute to the acquisition of predictive postural control as well. To clearly dissociate these functional roles, experimental approaches that combine transient perturbation after learning (to assess expression) with perturbation during learning and subsequent performance evaluation after recovery (to assess acquisition) will be instrumental and represent an important direction for future research.

A previous study16 reported that lesions of the cerebellar vermis attenuate the gradual reduction in postural responses across repeated disturbance trials, a result similar to that observed for ALM inactivation in the present study (Fig. 4c–e). Although the learning rates in the two studies were not comparable due to differences in lesion procedures and regression models employed, the findings suggest that in terms of the experience-dependent emergence of predictive postural control captured by this approach, the ALM and the cerebellum appear to make similar contributions. In line with these observations, studies using sensorimotor tasks in mice have reported that optogenetic perturbation of either the ALM or cerebellar activity similarly degrades learned discrimination performance24,27, and that predictive neural activities in the two regions are mutually dependent27,29,30. Similar studies using eyeblink conditioning have demonstrated that disrupting either the medial prefrontal cortex (PFC) or the cerebellum prevents the acquisition of conditioned responses34,35. Together, these findings suggest that, in predictive postural control, the frontal cortex and cerebellum cooperatively support the generation of feedforward motor commands and their associated learning by sharing predictive information.

At a more detailed level, the previous study16 also reported that in the disturbance-naïve state, the shank segment in vermis-lesioned rats was tilted backward compared with controls. In contrast, in the present study, no significant differences in the segmental angles were observed between the ALM-inactivated and vehicle-injected rats (Table 1). The same study reported that animals with lesions extending to the cerebellar nuclei were unable to stand bipedally on their hind limbs. These findings suggest that whereas the cerebellum is involved in broader aspects of postural control, including upright posture maintenance, the ALM is more specialized for higher-order motor controls involving prediction and planning.

From a clinical standpoint, impairments in predictive postural control have been well-documented in patients with cortical lesions11. For instance, patients with hemiparetic stroke with premotor cortex damage exhibit delayed tibialis anterior activation during forward stepping tasks36. Similarly, in Parkinson’s disease, abnormal cortico–basal ganglia loop activity involving the SMA and PFC has been implicated in deficits of predictive controls13,37,38. Particularly in studies using moving-platform paradigms, heightened short-latency responses (60–80 ms post-disturbance) as well as excessive co-contraction of antagonist muscles have been demonstrated12,39. In our experiment, postural responses also began within 80 ms after the disturbance onset (Fig. 2b), suggesting that a similarly rapid, subcortically mediated response exists and that impaired learning in ALM-inactivated rats may reflect a disruption in the cortical modulation of this mechanism. These observations suggest that ALM dysfunction models key aspects of the cortical contributions to human postural disorders, providing a mechanistic link between cortical dysfunctions and clinically observable postural impairments.

Advances in the anatomical characterization of circuits surrounding the ALM now make it possible to consider its role in predictive postural control in terms of specific input–output pathways. First, the rat rostral forelimb area, which exhibits partial overlap with the ALM, receives projections from the posterior parietal cortex (PPC)40. This pathway may have a sensorimotor transformation function analogous to that of the PPC–premotor projections described in primates41,42. Therefore, within our experimental paradigm, the ALM may receive PPC-derived signals related to the visual cue that specify the timing of the predictive motor output. Second, the primary and secondary motor cortices in rodents, including the ALM, project to the pontomedullary reticular formation43, a brainstem structure that contributes to postural control44. It is therefore possible that some of the predictive activity generated through ALM–cerebellar processing manifests as alterations in muscle tone via the reticulospinal system. Further studies using pathway-specific manipulations are necessary to determine whether cortical–cerebellar–brainstem circuits centered on the ALM are involved in predictive postural control.

The present results provide a basis for formulating hypotheses on cerebro–cerebellar contributions to predictive postural control and for experiments designed to test them. At the learning stage, repeated exposure to the external environment enables the cerebellum to acquire an internal model linking motor commands, postural dynamics, and reward1,45. This model incorporates environmental information such as the context-dependent timing of disturbances46. During real-time movement, the frontal cortex, centered on the ALM, interacts recurrently with the cerebellum to generate feedforward motor commands that minimize postural deviations. These predictive processes manifest both as preparatory adjustments that counteract the expected disturbance, and as modulation of short-latency response gains that attenuate the ensuing reactive response. To dissect the computational principles underlying the cerebro–cerebellar loop, three complementary strategies appear particularly promising. First, the direction of information flow can be examined through pathway-specific manipulations such as perturbing the VM or pontine relay nuclei47,48, as well as through simultaneous ALM–cerebellar recordings that allow for coherence and causality analyses49,50. Second, temporally selective optogenetic perturbations may uncover the temporal organization and hierarchical dependencies among predictive neural activities29. Finally, in recent years, constructive approaches that infer control strategies by reproducing rodent postural trajectories through simulations have advanced considerably15. Applying this approach to the perturbation data will further accelerate the identification of the principles underlying this loop.

Methods

Experimental animals

Two groups of male Wistar rats (vehicle group: n = 8, 16 ± 4 weeks old, 420 ± 40 g; muscimol group: n = 8, 19 ± 2 weeks old, 460 ± 23 g) supplied by CLEA Japan (Tokyo, Japan) were used in this study. The rats were housed under a constant temperature with a 12 h light/dark cycle and given ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of the University of Tokyo and conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for Research with Experimental Animals of the University of Tokyo. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines 2.0.

Surgical and experimental procedures

Habituation and behavioral measurements were performed in a transparent acrylic box placed on a tilting platform (Fig. 1a, b). Prior to behavioral measurements, the rats were trained for at least 8 days of habituation to maintain an upright posture on their hind limbs for more than 10 s. The habituation paradigm was identical to that used for the measurement sessions (Fig. 1c), except that the platform was fixed. The height of the water spout was individually adjusted for each rat so that the heels remained in contact with the platform and the hips did not touch it during the standing posture (vehicle group: 24.4 ± 0.2 cm; muscimol group: 24.7 ± 0.2 cm).

A dual 26-gauge guide cannula was implanted into the ALM at least two days before the behavioral measurements (2–9 days prior) under 3.0–5.0% isoflurane anesthesia. The stereotaxic coordinates (3.7 mm anterior and 2.0 mm lateral from bregma, and 1.5 mm in depth from the cortical surface; Fig. 1d, e) were determined according to Guo et al.24, who first defined the ALM, and Chen et al.51, who reported that predictive neural activity is prominent in the deeper cortical layers. After surgery, the rats were allowed to recover in their home cages until the day of measurement.

On the day of the behavioral measurements, the rats were lightly anesthetized with 2.0–3.0% isoflurane. After shaving the right side of the body, the anatomical landmarks—the 4th metatarsophalangeal joint, lateral malleolus, knee joint, greater trochanter, iliac crest, scapula, midpoint between the iliac crest and scapula, and the temporomandibular joint—were marked with black ink for motion capture (Fig. 1f). Rats in the muscimol group received a muscimol solution (50 mM muscimol, corresponding to 5.7 g/L, containing 4% Pontamine Sky Blue in PBS), whereas rats in the vehicle group received a vehicle solution (4% Pontamine Sky Blue in PBS). The solution was infused bilaterally through internal cannulae connected to a microsyringe pump at a rate of 10 µL/h (1.0 µL per hemisphere). The infusion volume, concentration, and rate were chosen with reference to the ranges reported in previous studies that performed pharmacological inactivation of specific frontal cortical regions in rats and mice52,53. After infusion, the internal cannula was left in place for 5 min to prevent backflow.

Behavioral measurements began approximately 30 min after the rats were fully awake. Each rat underwent 60 consecutive tilting trials within a 2 h session. The trial paradigm is shown in Fig. 1c. At the beginning of each trial, a light positioned in front of the rat was turned on and 4% sucrose water was supplied as a reward through the overhead spout. The rats stood upright on their hind limbs, facing the light, to drink water. After confirming that the rats’ posture was stable, the experimenter manually triggered the control system. This trigger turned the light off as a conditional cue (TC) and initiated water delivery that lasted about 6 s. Approximately 0.9 s (0.92 ± 0.04 s) after the trigger, the platform began to rotate backward (Fig. 1b) as an external disturbance (TD), rotating by 8.8° over 0.25 s with a constant angular velocity. During the water delivery period, the rats continued licking; however, in trials exhibiting large postural responses, the mouth detached from the spout. After reaching the tilted position, the platform remained tilted for approximately 6 s and then returned to the horizontal position over 14 s. The interval between full recovery of the platform and light onset in the next trial was 10 ± 1 s. No additional manual intervention was required to restore the rats to the upright posture. The sucrose water was delivered using a water pump (OPR-7100, O’Hara & Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), controlled by Operant Task Studio V2 (O’Hara & Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

After completing the behavioral experiments, the rats were euthanized by overdose of isoflurane. Transcardial perfusion with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde was then performed. The brains were cryoprotected with sucrose and sectioned coronally at a thickness of 40 μm. The sections were stained with Nissl stain to verify the location of the injection sites (Fig. 1d).

Measurement and data processing

During the behavioral measurements, six high-speed cameras (Prime 13 and Prime 13 W, NaturalPoint Inc., USA) recorded the movements of the right side of the body at 200 Hz. Two-dimensional coordinates of the body markers and snouts were extracted from the video images using DeepLabCut54, and three-dimensional coordinates were reconstructed using the direct linear transformation method55. The resulting trajectories were smoothed with a fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 15 Hz cutoff frequency. Sagittal plane kinematic analyses were performed using a rigid-link model consisting of the foot, shank, thigh, lower trunk, upper trunk, and head segments (Fig. 1f). In addition to TC and TD, we defined T0.3, the time point 0.3 s after TD, for the analyses (Fig. 1c). The 0.3-s interval was determined as the longest duration for which all rats could reliably maintain a bipedal stance during the disturbance.

To quantify the learning rate as the rate of reduction in postural fluctuation across trials, we computed the CoM angle, defined as the angle of the vector from the toe to the CoM, with time 0 corresponding to the onset of the disturbance (Fig. 4a). Segmental mass ratios of the rats were adopted from a previous study15. Postural fluctuations varied considerably among the trials, and some rats showed a rapid reduction in the first few trials (Fig. S1)15. These characteristics suggest that regression-based prediction is a valid approach for the analysis. Statistical analyses

The segmental angles at TD, angular changes from TD to T0.3 (both averaged over the initial three trials) were compared between the vehicle (n = 8) and muscimol (n = 8) groups (Table 1). Additionally, model-derived parameters, including the predicted CoM angles and and the coefficients A and B (log10-transformed B) in Eq. (1), were also compared. The logarithmic transformation of B was applied to approximate normality16. The normality of data distribution in each group was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When both groups satisfied the normality assumption, Welch’s t-test was applied; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. For within-group comparisons, the mean values of the initial three trials and the terminal three trials were compared for the segmental angles at TC and for the angular changes between TC–TD and between TD–T0.3 (Fig. 3). When the data satisfied the normality assumption according to the Shapiro–Wilk test, a paired t-test was used; otherwise, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. For all statistical tests, the significance level was set at p < 0.05.
