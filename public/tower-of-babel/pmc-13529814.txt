Safety reporting in chronic kidney disease clinical trials: a comparison of ClinicalTrials.gov registries and published reports
Authors: Nevada Denton, Lydia Hashemi, Daniel Archer, Rachel Hazlitt, Logan Corwin, Ryan Sherry, Kellen Keefer, Alicia Ito Ford, Matt Vassar
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Transparent reporting of adverse events (AEs) is essential for evaluating the safety of interventions from chronic kidney disease (CKD), a population especially vulnerable to treatment-related harm because of polypharmacy, reduced renal clearance, and high comorbidity burden. Since 2007, the FDA Amendments Act (FDAAA 801) and its 2017 Final Rule have required applicable clinical trials to submit structured harms data to ClinicalTrials.gov [1]. Although discrepancies between registry and published harms data have been documented in other clinical domains, whether this registry information is faithfully reflected in the peer-reviewed literature for CKD trials specifically had not been examined [2].

We performed a cross-sectional registry–publication comparison of 142 CKD trials with results posted on ClinicalTrials.gov (2009–2024) and matched USA-based publications, drawn from 843 initially identified records. Two reviewers independently extracted AE data in duplicate, and reporting concordance was assessed descriptively, with Bland–Altman and funnel plots used to visualize agreement, and segmented regression used to evaluate change around the Final Rule’s 2017 implementation date.

Serious adverse event (SAE) totals were consistent between ClinicalTrials.gov and the matched publication in only 11% of studies (16/142), with underreporting in the publication accounting for the large majority of mismatches. Patient-count mismatches occurred in 56% of studies, again more often favoring higher counts in the registry. SAE reporting was significantly more frequent on ClinicalTrials.gov than in publications both before (59% vs 26%; P < .001) and after (53% vs 31%; P = .041) the Final Rule. Critically, regression analysis showed that the improvement in a composite AE reporting score after the Final Rule took effect was not significant (NS) (adjusted estimate −0.265, P = .183), and the proportion of trials failing to report any SAEs on ClinicalTrials.gov itself rose from 37% pre-rule to 47% post-rule. Other adverse events (OAEs) followed a similar pattern, with significantly higher OAE reporting and non-reporting rates on ClinicalTrials.gov than in publications both pre- and post-Final Rule (P < .001). These results suggest that reliance on the published literature alone provides an incomplete, and often more favorable, picture of treatment-related harm in CKD trials than the registry record. This gap is particularly concerning given the narrow therapeutic margins and high-risk comorbidity profiles typical of CKD patients, for whom incomplete safety data can distort shared decision making and guideline development. Our finding of stagnant reporting quality nearly a decade after the Final Rule also raises questions about the adequacy of current enforcement mechanisms, consistent with prior estimates of billions of dollars in uncollected noncompliance fines [3].

We recommend that nephrology guideline developers and systematic reviewers routinely incorporate ClinicalTrials.gov harms data rather than relying solely on publications, and that journals request submission of registry AE tables alongside manuscripts to facilitate reconciliation before publication.[4] Structured, predefined AE reporting plans consistent with CONSORT Harms guidance, along with stronger regulatory enforcement, may be needed to close this persistent transparency gap and better safeguard CKD patients relying on the published evidence base [5]. Limitations of our analysis include exclusion of non-USA trials and reliance on published, English-language results, which may limit generalizability to the broader international CKD trial landscape.
