Executive dysfunction and cognitive disorganization in schizophrenia: Case-control and cluster analyses of a Korean Wisconsin Card Sorting Test cohort
Authors: Kyungwon Kim, Byung Dae Lee, Young Min Lee, Eunsoo Moon, Hwagyu Suh, Si Yul Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveExecutive dysfunction is a hallmark cognitive deficit in schizophrenia, yet phenotypic heterogeneity within the disorder complicates clinical translation. This study aimed to identify discrete executive profiles within a large Korean schizophrenia cohort using the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST) and to evaluate their clinical and functional correlates.MethodWe analyzed WCST performance in 276 individuals with schizophrenia and 361 healthy controls. A principal component analysis (PCA)-derived executive composite was validated against chart-reviewed clinical variables in a patient subset (n = 145). K-means cluster analysis was utilized to characterize cognitive heterogeneity, stratifying patients into distinct neurocognitive profiles.ResultsPatients with schizophrenia exhibited severe, broad executive impairment compared to controls, particularly in total errors (Hedges' g = 2.268), categories achieved (g = 2.122), and conceptual-level responses (g = 1.751). Within the patient group, cluster analysis robustly identified three distinct executive profiles: mild/near-normal (n = 83), intermediate (n = 88), and severe (n = 105). While age was independently associated with the general executive dysfunction composite (β = 0.254, p = 0.004), conceptual disorganization showed a prominent, theoretically consistent trend-level association (β = 0.166,p = 0.065). Exploratory neurodevelopmental burden and mediation pathways did not show robust statistical signals.ConclusionsExecutive dysfunction in schizophrenia is characterized by reproducible, clinically interpretable sub-profiles rather than a uniform deficit. Stratifying patients into mild, intermediate, and severe executive clusters provides a practical framework for neurocognitive heterogeneity, which may facilitate target selection for individualized cognitive remediation and functional rehabilitation strategies.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Executive function, Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, Cluster analysis, Cognitive heterogeneity, Cognitive remediation

Introduction

Schizophrenia is fundamentally characterized by multifaceted neurocognitive deficits that robustly predict long-term functional outcomes (Tandon et al., 2013; Barch and Ceaser, 2012). Recent large-scale meta-analyses confirm that cognitive deficits, particularly in executive domain, represent the strongest determinant of real-world community functioning and disability in schizophrenia (Green et al., 2019; Vita et al., 2022; Harvey and Strassnig, 2023; Green, 1996). Among these impairments, executive dysfunction represents a critical clinical dimension, impairing abstract reasoning, cognitive flexibility, response monitoring, and behavioral adaptation to changing environmental contingencies (Barch and Ceaser, 2012). These cognitive control mechanisms are crucial not only for laboratory-based neuropsychological testing but also for determining a patient's capacity to navigate real-world educational, occupational, and interpersonal challenges (Green, 1996; Bowie and Harvey, 2006).

The Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST) represents a gold-standard neuropsychological instrument for probing executive control networks, specifically requiring set-shifting, rule induction, and the strategic utilization of corrective feedback (Heaton et al., 1993). Extensive meta-analytic evidence has conclusively demonstrated marked WCST performance deficits across both first-episode and chronic schizophrenia populations (Wang et al., 2018; Forbes et al., 2009). While numerous case-control studies have established gross WCST performance deficits in schizophrenia, often implicating compromised prefrontal and frontostriatal circuitries (Pantelis et al., 1997), a critical gap remains in translating these group-level observations into patient-specific clinical utility. In recent years, attention has shifted toward parsing cognitive heterogeneity in schizophrenia, with growing recognition that distinct neurocognitive subtypes exist within the patient population (Carruthers et al., 2019; Geisler et al., 2021). Characterizing neurocognitive heterogeneity within the schizophrenia population is essential, as the clinical presentation ranges from individuals with relatively preserved cognitive architecture to those experiencing pervasive, treatment-resistant impairment (Nuechterlein et al., 2004; Kahn and Keefe, 2013; Keefe and Fenton, 2007; van Os and Kapur, 2009).

Identifying meaningful cognitive phenotypes requires models that simultaneously differentiate the disease state from health and capture variance within the patient population (Milev et al., 2005; Reichenberg and Harvey, 2007). In this context, conceptual disorganization is highly relevant; it reflects a core clinical fragmentation of thought architecture (Liddle, 1987) that may share underlying pathophysiological mechanisms with the impaired abstraction and loss of executive set captured by the WCST.21,22.

To address these questions, the present study utilizes a large, well-characterized Korean schizophrenia and healthy control sample to systematically delineate diagnostic impairment and intra-illness cognitive heterogeneity. We sought to move beyond simple case-control comparisons by applying an elegant mathematical framework—combining data reduction via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with k-means clustering—to isolate discrete, clinically interpretable neurocognitive sub-profiles. We explicitly defined primary hypotheses and exploratory secondary objectives: We hypothesized that (1) individuals with schizophrenia would demonstrate severe, multi-domain WCST impairments compared to healthy controls, and (2) intra-illness variation would settle into reproducible executive profiles linked to conceptual disorganization. As secondary exploratory objectives, we evaluated whether executive sub-profiles mapped onto secondary psychotic symptoms, whether executive dysfunction mediated functional outcomes, and whether exploratory neurodevelopmental burden markers acted as upstream determinants (Braff, 1993).

Methods

Participants and ethical approval

This study utilized an archival clinical and neuropsychological database collected at Pusan National University Hospital (Busan, Republic of Korea) between 2010 and 2022. The total case-control sample comprised 276 patients diagnosed with schizophrenia and 361 healthy controls who completed standardized WCST assessment and were included from this database. A subset of 145 schizophrenia patients with complete, detailed electronic medical record (EMR) chart-review data available was utilized for within-schizophrenia clinical correlates, cluster-association testing, and exploratory neurodevelopmental analyses (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Diagnosis was established by consensus clinical evaluations conducted by board-certified psychiatrists according to the DSM-IV-TR or DSM-5 criteria (Tandon et al., 2013). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Pusan National University Hospital (IRB No. 0908–003-001) and conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants or their legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Neuropsychological Assessment (WCST)

Executive function was measured using the standard 128-card computerized version of the WCST (WCST-64/128 architecture; Psychological Assessment Resources). The primary analyzable indices included number of correct responses, percent correct, total errors, percent errors, trials to complete the first category, total categories achieved, percent conceptual-level responses, failures to maintain set, and mean response time (seconds). To maintain rigorous data integrity, perseverative response and perseverative error variables were excluded from the final analytic structure due to a high rate of missing or un-reconstructable observations within historical database exports.

Operationalization of clinical variables (chart-review subset)

Due to the archival nature of the EMR database, structured dimensional scales (e.g., PANSS) were not uniformly available across all historical records. Therefore, clinical variables in the chart-review subset (n = 145) were systematically extracted by two trained clinical researchers (authors KK and BDL) using a standardized extraction protocol and operationalized as follows:

•Psychotic Symptoms (Delusions, Hallucinations, Conceptual Disorganization): Evaluated by psychiatric evaluations during index admission/assessment and operationalized as binary variables (0 = Absent, 1 = Present). Conceptual disorganization was defined as documented formal thought disorder, circumstantiality, derailment, or loss of goal-directed speech.

Statistical analysis

Executive dysfunction composite (PCA)

To minimize alpha error inflation from multiple testing, core WCST metrics were aggregated into a singular, psychometrically robust phenotype. All constituent indicators were directionalized so that higher values uniformly indicated greater executive impairment (e.g., correct responses, categories achieved, and conceptual-level responses were reverse-coded, while total errors and response times were preserved). Standardized directional variables were subjected to Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and the first principal component (PC1) was extracted as the primary executive dysfunction composite.

Case-control and intra-illness correlates

Diagnostic group differences were evaluated using Welch's independent-samples t-tests to account for unequal variances. Effect sizes were expressed as impairment-oriented Hedges' g. An age- and sex-adjusted linear regression was implemented to confirm the diagnostic predictive power of the WCST composite.

Within the patient population (n = 142 complete cases), a multivariable linear regression model evaluated clinical predictors of the executive PC1 composite. Standardized β coefficients were calculated to compare the relative impact of individual clinical domains. Missing data across individual clinical metrics were addressed using a complete-case analytic framework (listwise deletion for specific models).

K-means cluster analysis

To map cognitive heterogeneity, k-means clustering was performed on the standardized directional WCST data. Mathematical solutions from k = 2 to k = 5 were iteratively evaluated based on silhouette width, Calinski-Harabasz indices, Davies-Bouldin metrics, minimum cluster allocation stability, and clinical utility. The optimal three-cluster model was validated across both the full schizophrenia sample and the chart-review subset. Group differences across clusters were analyzed using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical distributions.

Supplementary sensitivity analyses

Exploratory neurodevelopmental burden scores (reconstructed from historical indicators of learning difficulties, ADHD history, obstetric complications, and early neurological history) were examined via conservative missing-as-zero and observed-case paradigms. Cross-sectional bootstrap mediation models (5000 resamples) were exploratory tools evaluating indirect pathways between symptoms, executive function, and functional outcomes.

Results

Case-control WCST performance

Delineated in Table 1, individuals with schizophrenia displayed profound, broad-spectrum executive impairments across all assessed dimensions compared to healthy controls (p < 0.001). The magnitude of diagnostic separation was substantial for total errors (Hedges' g = 2.268), percent errors (g = 2.202), percent correct (g = 2.202), categories achieved (g = 2.122), and conceptual-level responses (g = 1.751). Failures to maintain set showed a modest but statistically significant elevation in the patient group (g = 0.189,p = 0.022). In the fully adjusted diagnostic regression model, the schizophrenia diagnosis remained a robust independent predictor of executive impairment (coefficient = 1.040, 95% CI: 0.937 to 1.143, p < 0.001).

Extraction of the executive dysfunction component

Within the patient cohort, the first principal component derived from the PCA captured 73.8% of the total variance across core WCST parameters (Table 2). Factor loadings were uniformly high and in the expected direction for accuracy metrics, categories achieved, and conceptual responses. Notably, mean response time demonstrated a negligible loading (0.037), indicating that processing speed/bradyphrenia and executive abstraction accuracy represent largely orthogonal cognitive dimensions within schizophrenia.

Multivariable clinical correlates

The full multivariable model predicting the executive PC1 composite (n = 142) is detailed in Table 3. Advancing chronological age was independently tied to severe executive dysfunction (β = 0.254, 95% CI: 0.081 to 0.427, p = 0.004). Among primary psychotic symptom dimensions, conceptual disorganization demonstrated the most prominent positive relationship with executive impairment (β = 0.166, 95% CI: −0.010 to 0.343, p = 0.065). Although this fell slightly above the traditional p < 0.05 significance threshold after rigorous multivariable adjustment, it represented a notable statistical trend. Age at onset, delusions, hallucinations, occupational scores, and gross SES indices did not display independent associations (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 5).

When mapping chart-review clinical variables across these three mathematical groups, the clusters were predominantly separated by their distinct neuropsychological profiles rather than broad psychiatric symptom brackets. Chronological age significantly differed across the groups (ANOVA p < 0.001), with older patients overrepresented in the intermediate cluster (Supplementary Table S2). Rates of conceptual disorganization differed across clusters—being lowest in the mild cluster (7.14%), highest in the intermediate cluster (22.2%), and moderate in the severe cluster (15.1%)—though this did not reach omnibus statistical significance (p = 0.117). Positive psychotic symptoms and functional indicators did not vary significantly across the clusters.

Exploratory mediation and neurodevelopmental burden

Sensitivity testing of the neurodevelopmental burden metrics revealed extensive data sparsity. Under the conservative missing-as-zero approach, no reliable link was established with the executive PC1 composite or cluster phenotypes (Supplementary Table S3). Similarly, all cross-sectional mediation models evaluating indirect pathways between conceptual disorganization, executive PC1, and final occupational or socioeconomic outcomes yielded bootstrap confidence intervals that spanned zero, indicating the absence of a cross-sectional mediation signal.

Discussion

This study integrated a rigorous case-control evaluation of executive control with advanced intra-illness cluster analyses in a large Korean schizophrenia sample. Four central findings emerge from this architecture. First, diagnostic group separation on the WCST is wide, characterized by large effect sizes across accuracy and conceptual domains. Second, a dominant principal component accounts for nearly three-quarters of the variance in WCST accuracy, cleanly diverging from processing speed. Third, cluster analysis mapped three reproducible neurocognitive sub-profiles (mild/near-normal, intermediate, severe) that capture the observed landscape of cognitive heterogeneity. Fourth, conceptual disorganization emerged as the most prominent symptom-level correlate of this executive composite, showing a statistically non-significant trend-level association.

The large effect sizes observed for total errors (g = 2.268) and categories achieved (g = 2.122) confirm that executive dysfunction in schizophrenia is not a subtle deviation but a foundational cognitive deficit (Pantelis et al., 1997; Mesholam-Gately et al., 2009). The multi-domain nature of this impairment—spanning accuracy, feedback utilization, and conceptual execution—underscores a pervasive disruption in the distributed frontostriatal networks that subserve working memory and behavior monitoring.5,10 Importantly, our PCA demonstrated that response speed and sorting accuracy represent separate domains, reinforcing the notion that executive deficits in schizophrenia are driven by true impairments in abstract rule induction rather than simple psychomotor slowing.

The primary contribution of this study lies in its shift toward characterizing intra-illness cognitive heterogeneity (Kahn and Keefe, 2013; Keefe and Fenton, 2007). The three identified executive clusters were not minor statistical variations; the mild/near-normal cohort exhibited preserved abstraction capacities akin to healthy controls, whereas the severe cohort demonstrated an absolute failure in rule completion and feedback integration (Reichenberg and Harvey, 2007). Crucially, these clusters did not neatly mirror external clinical variables like delusions or coarse functional categories. This independence establishes that WCST-derived executive clusters capture a distinct neurocognitive phenotype rather than acting as a simple proxy for overall disease severity or positive symptom expression (Milev et al., 2005).

From a therapeutic perspective, this three-tier stratification offers a practical framework for clinical trials and personalized cognitive rehabilitation. While the Intermediate and Severe clusters overlap on aggregate PC1 score (Fig. 4), their underlying error architecture is qualitatively distinct (Table 4). Patients in the Intermediate cluster retain partial conceptual capability (∼22% conceptual responses) but suffer from shifting/maintenance errors, suggesting they may benefit most from goal-management training and error-reduction cognitive remediation. In contrast, the Severe cluster demonstrates profound abstraction failure (∼5% conceptual responses), requiring intensive environmental scaffolding and compensatory cognitive strategies. Patients in the Mild cluster likely possess the cognitive reserve required for standard psychosocial interventions.

The trend-level relationship between the executive composite and conceptual disorganization (β = 0.166,p = 0.065) aligns with theoretical models linking formal thought disorder to impaired executive control. Conceptual disorganization reflects fragmented thought and loose associations, while the WCST requires mental set maintenance and inhibition. However, because this association did not reach the conventional p < 0.05 significance threshold, it must be interpreted with caution as a preliminary exploratory finding requiring replication with standardized rating scales like the PANSS.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, detailed chart-review metrics were restricted to a patient subset (n = 145), reducing statistical power for intra-illness regression modeling. Second, clinical variables were reconstructed retrospectively from EMR records as binary or ordinal metrics rather than continuous, standardized symptom ratings (Nieuwenstein et al., 2001). Third, formal education duration had >35% missingness in archival charts and was excluded from multivariable models to prevent severe sample size reduction, which represents a notable limitation given the known influence of education on cognitive testing. Fourth, critical confounding variables—including lifetime antipsychotic exposure, chlorpromazine equivalents, negative symptom profiles, and premorbid IQ—were not uniformly available (Woodberry et al., 2008). Fifth, perseverative indices could not be evaluated due to historical data omissions. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes any definitive causal or temporal inferences regarding the relationship between executive subtypes and long-term functional decline.

In conclusion, executive dysfunction in schizophrenia is a robust diagnostic feature that exhibits highly structured, reproducible heterogeneity within the patient population. The delineation of mild, intermediate, and severe executive clusters provides an empirical framework for stratifying cognitive deficits in schizophrenia. This neurocognitive taxonomy may enhance the precision of cognitive remediation protocols and functional rehabilitation targeting. Future prospective studies utilizing longitudinal designs and dense, standardized clinical tracking are needed to confirm whether these specific executive profiles predict long-term treatment response and real-world recovery trajectories.
