M6PR/IGF2R double-knockout supplies therapeutic sources for mucolipidosis II and Niemann-Pick C2
Authors: Toshiharu Fujita, Seigo Terawaki, Takahito Moriwaki, Takanobu Otomo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLysosomal storage disorders (LSDs) are intractable rare diseases caused by lysosomal dysfunction due mainly to defects in lysosomal enzyme genes. For many lysosomal enzymes to be transported correctly into lysosomes, their mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) labeling by GlcNAc-1 phosphotransferase is crucial. M6P-modified lysosomal enzymes are captured by M6P receptors and transported to lysosomes. The M6P-dependent pathway is utilized not only for newly synthesized lysosomal enzymes but also for the intracellular transport of exogenously administered lysosomal enzymes to lysosomes. In this study, we performed gene knockouts targeting the M6PR and IGF2R genes, which encode the M6P receptors. Cells with a double knockout of these genes secrete M6P-modified proteins into the culture supernatant double KO (dKO sup), and we investigated its potential for therapeutic application. I-cell disease (mucolipidosis II, ML-II) arises from GNPT deficiency and is a disorder in which dozens of lysosomal enzymes are deficient within the cell. Treating ML II cells with the dKO sup restored lysosomal enzyme activity, reduced inclusion bodies, and improved autophagic function. ML II patient-derived fibroblasts exhibited increased lysosomal enzyme activities, normalized morphology, and improved cryo-viability with the treatment. Niemann-Pick disease type C2 (NPC2) protein is not a lysosomal enzyme but functions cooperatively with NPC1 protein to mediate cholesterol transport in lysosomes. NPC2 deficiency causes NPC2, and the dKO sup restored the cellular function of the disease by supplementing the NPC2 protein. These results indicate that M6PR/IGF2R double-deficient cells provide a simple platform for supplying M6P-modified proteins that can be applied to the treatment of a broad range of LSDs.Keywords: enzyme replacement therapy, GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase, I-cell disease, lysosomal storage disorder, lysosome, mannose 6-phosphate, mucolipidosis II, Niemann-Pick C2

Lysosomes are small vesicular organelles that play a crucial role in cellular homeostasis by degrading and recycling various biological macromolecules (1, 2). They contain a diverse array of acid hydrolases that degrade proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, or nucleic acids. Mutations in the genes encoding these lysosomal enzymes lead to decreased degradation activities and result in lysosomal storage disorders (LSDs) (3, 4, 5). Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) is currently established in several LSDs, including Gaucher disease, Fabry disease, Pompe disease, lysosomal acid lipase (LAL) deficiency, and part of neuronal ceroid lipofuscinoses and mucopolysaccharidoses (4, 6). ERT is aimed at restoring cell functions by administering the recombinant enzymes that are genetically deficient in patients as a drug, to promote the breakdown of accumulated substances in the lysosomes.

In addition to genetic abnormalities in hydrolytic enzymes, deficiencies in other lysosomal functional proteins involved in lysosomal enzyme transport, activation, and substrate or metabolite delivery can also cause LSDs. Disruptions of the machinery responsible for transporting enzymes to the lysosome result in the loss of multiple hydrolytic enzymes in the lysosomes. Most of the newly synthesized lysosomal enzymes are modified with mannose 6-phosphate (M6P) to the non-reducing end of the N-linked oligosaccharide by UDP-N-acetylglucosamine--lysosomal-enzyme N-acetylglucosaminephosphotransferase (GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase, hereafter GNPT [EC 2.7.8.17]) in the Golgi apparatus. The M6P-modified enzymes are captured by the M6P receptors in the trans-Golgi network and are transported to lysosomes via clathrin-coated vesicles (7). A functional defect in GNPT causes impaired M6P modification of lysosomal proteins, leading to their disrupted transport to lysosomes and their secretion into the extracellular space.

Mucolipidoses type II and III (ML II, ML III) are caused by a genetic deficiency of GNPT, where undigested substrates accumulate in lysosomes, forming inclusion bodies in the cytosol (8, 9, 10, 11). Niemann-Pick disease type C2 (NPC2) is caused by a genetic defect of the NPC2 gene, which works for transporting free cholesterol in the lysosome in coordination with NPC1. These diseases are not lysosomal enzyme deficiencies, and fundamental treatment, such as ERT, has not been developed yet. Here, we report that cells depleted of two types of M6P receptors, M6PR and IGF2R, via genome editing secrete M6P-modified proteins into the culture supernatant. These secreted proteins not only serve as a source of ERT for ML II, but can also be applicable for the treatment of other enzyme-deficient LSDs and NPC2, by restoring the cellular function of the patients.

Results

Depletion of M6P receptors releases lysosomal enzymes into the extracellular space

Components of GNPT are encoded by two genes, GNPTAB and GNPTG. Biallelic pathogenic variants in either of these genes cause ML II or its attenuated form, mucolipidosis type III (11, 12, 13). GNPT adds GlcNAc-P to the glycan chain of lysosomal enzymes, and the N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphodiester alpha-N-acetylglucosaminidase (uncovering enzyme, [EC 3.1.4.45]) removes the terminal GlcNAc from the glycan chain, exposing the M6P residue on the lysosomal enzyme (14, 15). The uncovering enzyme is encoded by the NAGPA gene, and no diseases caused by mutations in this gene have been reported in humans (14, 16). To understand the interaction between the M6P residue and M6P receptor in lysosomal enzyme transport and to develop therapeutic strategies for ML II and ML III, gene-modified cells were established using CRISPR/Cas9 (17) and analyzed.

In the GNPTAB or GNPTG gene knocked out (KO) HeLa cells, intracellular lysosomal enzyme activities were significantly reduced for α-mannosidase, α-fucosidase, α-galactosidase, β-hexosaminidases, β-glucuronidase, and α-iduronidase (Fig. S1A). As β-glucosidase is known to be transported to lysosomes in an M6P-independent manner, the intracellular enzyme activity in GNPTAB or GNPTG KO cells was intact in this study (Fig. S1A), which is consistent with the previous report (18, 19). The β-galactosidase activity was significantly reduced in GNPTAB KO cells but not in GNPTG KO cells. The gamma subunit of GNPT is suggested to recognize substrates, while the alpha and beta subunits possess phosphotransferase activity (14, 20). The mechanism by which intracellular β-galactosidase activity is not reduced by GNPTG KO remains unclear.

In NAGPA KO cells, intracellular activities of β-hexosaminidase measured using the 6-sulfated (6S) substrate and α-iduronidase were reduced, but no apparent decrease was observed for other enzymes (Fig. S1A). To date, human disease linked to NAGPA gene deficiency has not been reported, and NAGPA KO mice grow normally without histologic abnormalities (16). These findings suggest that the NAGPA gene had minimal effects on the transport of most lysosomal hydrolases.

To verify the effects of clonal variation on the phenotype, cell lines with four different biallelic pathogenic mutations introduced into the GNPTAB gene were established and tested (Table S1); all of them showed similar reduced enzyme activity profiles (Fig. S1B) and complete impairment of autophagic function (Fig. S1C), consistent with the previous studies (21, 22, 23). These results indicate that the model cells properly mimic patient cells, and thus clone #1 was used for the subsequent investigations in this study.

The M6P residue on the lysosomal enzymes is recognized and captured by the M6P receptors in the trans-Golgi network, initiating the journey to lysosomes. Two types of M6P receptors are known: cation-dependent M6P receptor (encoded by the M6PR gene) that requires divalent cations to efficiently recognize lysosomal hydrolases, and cation-independent M6P receptor (insulin-like growth factor II receptor, encoded by the IGF2R gene) that can recognize enzymes without requiring cations (24). To investigate the impact of M6P receptor deficiency on lysosomal enzyme transport, we established M6PR KO, IGF2R KO, and M6PR/IGF2R double KO (dKO) cells and measured the lysosomal enzyme activities in the culture medium of these cells.

M6PR KO had little effect on enzyme activity in the culture medium, whereas IGF2R KO represented significant increases in activities in part of the lysosomal enzymes compared with WT cells (Fig. 1A). In M6PR/IGF2R dKO cells, further increases in enzyme activities were observed in the culture medium for almost all enzymes tested (Fig. 1A), which are comparable to those of GNPTAB KO cells. Conversely, the intracellular enzyme activities other than β-glucosidase of GNPTAB KO and M6PR/IGF2R dKO cells significantly decreased compared with the WT cells (Fig. 1B). These results indicated that the various lysosomal enzymes transported in the M6P-dependent manner were secreted into the supernatant of M6PR/IGF2R dKO cells. To assess the impact of enzyme transport deficiency on lysosomal degradation substrates, we also examined electron micrographs of the cells tested above. The results showed that abnormal lysosomes, with an increase in number and/or size, were observed in the M6PR/IGF2R and GNPTAB KO cells (Fig. 1C).

The results above demonstrate that lysosomal enzyme transport mediated by M6P is impaired when either the M6P modification on the enzymes or the M6P receptor is deficient. Consequently, the enzymes are secreted outside the cell, causing the cells to exhibit a similar phenotype.

Proteomic characterization revealed the secretion of lysosomal proteins from GNPTAB KO and M6PR/IGF2R dKO cells

All cells secrete various proteins via exocytosis and exosome-mediated pathways, and so on. To know the profiles of proteins, which are secreted in the culture supernatant of M6PR/IGF2R dKO cells (hereafter dKO sup), and to determine their relative abundances, we performed proteomic analysis. Using serum-free medium, we collected culture supernatants from WT, M6PR/IGF2R single or double KO, and GNPTAB KO cells and conducted comprehensive protein profiling by LC-MS/MS (Supplemental data set). The results revealed a marked enrichment of lysosomal proteins in the culture supernatants of GNPTAB KO cells and M6PR/IGF2R dKO cells compared to those of WT cells. A total of 3088 translational products derived from different genes were detected from the samples, and the known lysosomal proteins were highly ranked in abundance (Fig. 2A). Among the top 100 detected proteins, 21 were major lysosomal proteins, such as proteases and glycosidases. Furthermore, an additional 11 lysosomal enzymes were identified within the top 300 or so (Fig. 2B). The profiles of the cell culture supernatants from GNPTAB KO cells and M6PR/IGF2R dKO cells were very comparable, indicating that disruption of the M6P-dependent pathway similarly leads to the extracellular secretion of lysosomal proteins.

Supernatant of M6P receptor dKO cells (dKO sup) contains M6P-modified lysosomal enzymes capable of treating lysosomal diseases

The development of ERT for ML II and III patients has not been established. This is because, in these diseases, numerous lysosomal enzymes that rely on M6P for transport is simultaneously deficient, making it impractical to produce large quantities of lysosomal enzymes and replenish them all at once. Since M6P receptor KO is thought not to affect the M6P modification of lysosomal enzymes, the lysosomal enzymes secreted into the dKO sup are expected to be normally M6P-labeled. Since M6P-modified lysosomal enzymes could serve as a source for ERT for LSDs, including ML II, we next investigated the usability of the lysosomal enzymes in the dKO sup.

The dKO sup was collected after 2 days of incubation with regular fresh culture medium fetal bovine serum (FBS-containing DMEM) with these cells. Then, the GNPTAB KO cells were cultured with the dKO sup (hereafter, dKO sup treatment) for 24, 48, and 72 h. The increase in enzyme activity due to dKO sup treatment was observed in a time-dependent manner in GNPTAB KO cells, with some enzymes recovering to levels comparable to normal cells after 72 h of treatment (Fig. S2). According to this result, the dKO sup treatment was fixed at duration of 72 h (3 days) for further experiments. To examine the duration of the effects of enzyme supplementation, the culture medium was switched back from the dKO sup to regular culture medium after 3 days of dKO sup treatment, and the time course of withdrawal of lysosomal enzyme activities was observed. The increased enzyme activity by the dKO sup treatment gradually declined and returned to baseline levels for the GNPTAB KO cells within approximately 4 days (Fig. 3A). To confirm that the increase in lysosomal enzyme activity observed with dKO sup treatment was indeed mediated by M6P receptor-mediated enzyme uptake, we added various concentrations of M6P concurrently with dKO sup treatment and observed competitive inhibition effects. Compared to the untreated group (0 mM M6P), the addition of 5, 10, and 20 mM M6P inhibited lysosomal enzyme uptake into GNPTAB KO cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3B). These results indicate that dKO sup treatment resulted in the uptake of multiple M6P-modified lysosomal enzymes into GNPTAB KO cells via the M6P receptors.

Furthermore, we confirmed that dKO sup treatment has the potential to restore enzyme activities in other lysosomal enzyme-deficient cells besides GNPTAB KO, MAN2B1 KO cells (a model for α-mannosidosis), FUCA1 KO cells (fucosidosis), GLB1 KO cells (GM1 gangliosidosis), IDUA KO cells (mucopolysaccharidosis type I), HEXA KO cells (Tay-Sachs disease), and HEXB KO cells (Sandhoff disease) (Fig. 3C) that were established by genome editing using the CRISPR/Cas9 method (Table S1). These results suggest that the dKO sup can broadly treat pathological conditions caused by the dysfunction of the individual enzymes that are transported in an M6P-dependent manner.

Since the activity of lysosomal enzymes is measured using cell lysates, it is not possible to verify whether the enzymes taken up into the cells from the dKO sup are properly transported to the lysosomes, processed, and exhibit enzymatic activity. Therefore, we examined the processing level of cathepsin B, cathepsin D, and the hexosaminidase β subunit by Western blotting using cell lysates from WT, GNPTAB KO, and GNPTAB KO cells treated with dKO sup. The results showed that in GNPTAB KO cells, many samples exhibited bands corresponding to precursor (p) or intermediate (i) forms, or irregular bands thought to result from abnormal glycosylation. In contrast, in GNPTAB KO cells treated with dKO sup, bands corresponding to the mature (m) forms of all three enzymes were observed, suggesting that enzymes taken up from the extracellular space were properly processed in the acidic environment of the endosomes/lysosomes (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, we examined intracellular β-galactosidase activity using the SPiDER probe, which can detect lysosomal enzyme activity in situ. The signal from the SPiDER-β-Gal probe colocalized with LysoTracker, and the SPiDER signal in GNPTAB KO cells was significantly weaker than that in WT cells. When GNPTAB KO cells were treated with the dKO sup, a significant recovery of the SPiDER signal was observed, and these signals colocalized with LysoTracker. These results support the notion that β-galactosidase in dKO sup is taken up by GNPTAB KO cells, properly transported to the lysosomes, and exerts enzymatic activity (Fig. 3E).

dKO sup treatment restores cellular function in GNPTAB-deficient cells

Autophagy is an essential process for maintaining cellular homeostasis by degrading obsolete proteins and organelles that have lost their proper functions within the cell, and it is dependent on the lysosomal degradative functions (25, 26, 27). In the ML II patient cells, autophagic function is significantly impaired due to reduced lysosomal function (22, 28). Similarly, in GNPTAB KO cells, the compromised lysosomal functions lead to a significant decrease in autophagic activity compared with WT, gene non-edited HeLa cells. When GNPTAB KO cells were treated with the dKO sup for three days, autophagic activity was significantly restored in both nutrient-rich and starved conditions (Fig. 4A, upper). After removal of the dKO sup, the improved autophagic activity returned to the original level of GNPTAB KO cells by the fourth day, in accord with the reduction of the lysosomal enzyme activity (Fig. 4A, lower).

LysoTracker staining represents stronger signals in the GNPTAB KO cells compared to the WT cells, reflecting abnormal lysosome accumulation containing undigested materials (21, 22, 28, 29). The enhanced LysoTracker signal was reversed to almost normal levels upon the dKO sup treatment (Fig. 4B). Similarly, an electron microscopic analysis revealed the accumulation of undegraded substances (i.e., "inclusion bodies") and a 2–3-fold increase in lysosomal diameter in the GNPTAB KO cells, which can be attributed to impaired lysosomal degradation. Notably, these accumulated materials were largely eliminated following the dKO sup treatment (Fig. 4C). Altogether, these findings suggest that the dKO sup can effectively restore intracellular abnormalities associated with lysosomal dysfunction in the ML II.

The dKO sup treatment restores cellular function in skin fibroblasts derived from ML II patients and improves their viability rate after cryopreservation

ML II patient fibroblasts exhibit a swollen morphology and are characterized by numerous inclusion bodies in the cytoplasm under phase-contrast microscopy observation. These phase-dense bodies became the origin of the disease name inclusion cell disease (I-cell disease) (30, 31, 32). When ML II skin fibroblasts (SF) were subjected to dKO sup treatment for three days, a significant recovery in the activity of lysosomal enzymes was observed, as shown in the HeLa model cells (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, the abnormal accumulation of cholesterol, which is markedly evident in the ML II SF, was resolved by the dKO sup treatment (Fig. 5B). Along with this restoration of cellular function, the morphology of the ML II SF was much improved, adopting the typical elongated shape of normal skin fibroblasts without inclusion bodies (Fig. 5C).

A characteristic feature of patient-derived ML II cells is their extremely low viability after cryopreservation, which often poses an obstacle in ML II research (33, 34). The viability of the untreated ML II SF after a freeze-thaw cycle was less than 5% of the input before cryopreservation. In contrast, ML II SF treated with the dKO sup before freezing showed a significant improvement in cell viability after thawing (Fig. 5D). We further tested the effects of dKO sup treatment on the viability by adding it immediately after thawing, but the cell viability did not show any significant changes from that of untreated specimens. We also tested both pre-freeze and post-thaw dKO sup treatments, and the results were similar to cells treated only with the pre-freeze treatment (Fig. 5D). Taking these results together, it is important to improve the condition of cells by treating them with dKO sup before cryopreservation, which may confer resistance against the physical stress of freezing, and no additional effect is expected from post-thaw treatment.

We additionally confirmed whether ML II lysosomes are fragile. We coated beads with a non-liposome-based lipid transfection reagent used for DNA transfection and allowed them to be internalized via endocytosis. Beads internalized into the endo-lysosome system permeabilize the vesicle membrane. When the lysosomal lumen, containing sugar-rich glycoproteins like Lamp1/2, communicates with the cytoplasm, the cytoplasmic lectin galectin-3 (Gal3) accumulates on the sugar chains within the lysosome. Gal3-positive vesicles can be identified as vesicles with ruptured membranes (35, 36, 37). In ML II patient cells, a higher proportion of lysosomal membranes were observed to be ruptured compared to WT (Fig. 5E). This leads to the conclusion that lysosomes in ML II are fragile. This finding may provide one explanation for the low cryo-viability observed in ML II.

These results indicate that mucolipidosis cells exhibit abnormalities in the composition of plasma membranes, and that treatment of Mucolipidosis cells with the dKO sup restores normal plasma membrane dynamics through improving lysosomal functions.

The dKO sup treatment restores cellular function in NPC2-deficient cells

Abnormal metabolism of cholesterol is known in LSDs such as ML II, LAL deficiency, and Niemann-Pick disease. Next, to more broadly validate the efficacy of the dKO sup treatment, we investigated disease model cells with the KO of cholesterol metabolism-related genes.

As reported in the patient-derived SF (28, 38), intracellular cholesterol levels were higher in GNPTAB KO cells compared to WT cells. The treatment of GNPTAB KO cells with the dKO sup significantly reduced cholesterol accumulation (Fig. 6A). Consistent with this, fluorescent microscopic analysis also revealed that the intracellular free cholesterol visualized by Filipin III (39) was also reduced after the treatment with the dKO sup (Fig. 6B).

In addition to GNPTAB KO cells, LIPA, NPC1, and NPC2 KO cells were established (Table S1). The LIPA gene encodes LAL, a primary enzyme responsible for hydrolyzing cholesteryl esters and triglycerides within lysosomes (40), which is also one of the representative hydrolases that is transported to lysosomes via the M6P-dependent pathway (41). The accumulation of esterified cholesterol observed in GNPTAB KO and LIPA KO cells can be attributed to the failure of proper transport of LAL to the lysosomes, resulting in impaired hydrolysis of esterified cholesterol. dKO sup treatment reduced the accumulation of esterified cholesterol in these cells (Fig. 6A), suggesting that M6P-modified LAL was included in the dKO sup and supplied to the lysosomes of the recipient KO cells.

NPC, caused by mutations in the NPC1 or NPC2 genes, is characterized by the accumulation of free cholesterol and other lipids in the endo/lysosomal compartments (42). NPC2 binds and transfers cholesterol to the luminal side of the lysosomal transmembrane protein NPC1, and NPC1 facilitates the transport of cholesterol across the lysosomal membrane to other cellular compartments. This cooperative process is essential for maintaining cellular lipid balance (43). Filipin III staining confirmed the free cholesterol accumulation in both NPC1 and NPC2 KO cells (Fig. 6B). NPC is not a disease caused by enzyme abnormalities but rather by defects in lipid transport proteins. The dKO sup treatment successfully reduced the accumulated cholesterol in NPC2 KO cells. In contrast, no significant intracellular cholesterol clearance was observed in NPC1 KO cells (Fig. 6B). It has already been reported that adequate M6P modification is necessary for the lysosomal targeting of NPC2 (44). M6P-modified NPC2 protein is also secreted extracellularly from the M6P receptor dKO cells (Fig. 2), and it suggests that the addition of the dKO sup treatment could replenish functional NPC2 in the NPC2 KO cells. To test this possibility, NPC2 protein levels in the culture supernatants of WT, GNPTAB KO, and M6PR/IGF2R dKO cells were examined by western blotting. The results showed that highly shifted bands of NPC2 at molecular size, compared with WT cells, which were EndoH resistant and PNGaseF sensitive, were detected in GNPTAB KO cells (Fig. 6C), indicating that NPC2 protein modified with complex-type sugar is being secreted extracellularly due to GNPT deficiency. Conversely, in M6P receptor dKO cells, larger amounts of NPC2 of the same size as WT cells were secreted. Furthermore, NPC2 KO cells treated with dKO sup showed restored intracellular NPC2 levels (Fig. 6D). These results support the validity of our hypothesis for NPC2 delivery.

Discussions

Our current study revealed that depletion of M6P on the ligand side (lysosomal enzymes) and depletion of the M6P receptor on the receptor side produce similar enzyme activity profiles in their culture supernatants and endo-lysosomal structures. While the phenotypes in lysosomal enzymatic activities were clear when both M6PR and IGF2R were depleted, the effect of M6PR KO was generally milder than that of IGF2R KO. Furthermore, for some enzymes like α-fucosidase, dependence on IGF2R was relatively high; depletion of IGF2R reduced activity in cells to only about one-quarter of that in normal cells. These observations are consistent with the previous report (45) and support their conclusion that M6PR and IGF2R do not exhibit complete functional redundancy in lysosomal enzyme transport. Differences in enzyme activities of the supernatant and inside the cell between the M6PR and IGF2R single KO may reflect their distinct affinities for individual lysosomal enzymes. The precise differences in affinity for M6P receptors between individual lysosomal enzymes represent a crucial issue not only for understanding disease pathogenesis but also for the development of enzyme replacement therapy. Further studies are needed.

In this report, we established the M6PR/IGF2R dKO cell supernatant (the dKO sup) as a source of M6P-modified lysosomal enzymes, which can be used to restore lysosomal function in various types of LSDs. Although the degree of recovery of activities varies by enzyme, ranging from about 10% to complete recovery of WT levels, previous reports have shown that approximately the remaining 10% enzymatic activity was sufficient to prevent disease onset for some lysosomal disorders (46, 47), suggesting the therapeutic potential of the dKO sup would be sufficient for ERT. The approach of the single or double-depletion of the M6P receptors is not only applicable for developing efficient enzyme production methods in ERT, but also for ex vivo genetically modifying donor cells to efficiently release enzymes extracellularly during hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

The potential for ERT in ML II and III has already been demonstrated (28). In that report, a lysosomal enzyme mixture was collected from the culture supernatant of normal cells treated with ammonium chloride (NH4Cl). NH4Cl disrupts the intracellular membrane transport environment, facilitating the leakage of M6P-modified lysosomal enzymes into the culture supernatant. While this method requires removal of NH4Cl from the culture medium, the M6PR dKO strategy presented in the present study eliminates the need for NH4Cl removal steps via dialysis or ultrafiltration, making it highly convenient and enabling continuous, higher-efficiency enzyme recovery from the production cells. In ML II or III, which is caused by GNPT deficiency, multiple lysosomal enzymes are deficient within cells, and the single-enzyme ERT strategy currently attempted for LSDs such as Fabry disease, Gaucher disease, and mucopolysaccharidoses cannot be applied to ML II or III. As GNPT is a hexameric protein complex encoded by the two genes and is composed of 2 sets of three subunits after intracellular processing and assembly (14, 20), this makes it difficult to supply active GNPT enzyme to the proper subcellular location where this enzyme functions from outside the cell. Therefore, the approach of the dual depletion of M6P receptors demonstrated here could become a powerful tool for the treatment of ML II and III.

Although the precise reason why ML II cells fail to survive cryopreservation remains unclear, lysosomal dysfunction could alter lipid or protein composition within plasma and organelle membranes, which can compromise their integrity through several mechanisms. An impaired intracellular membrane trafficking reported in ML II cells (28) may be responsible for the reduced plasma membrane fluidity and self-repair capacity (48). Lysosomal enlargement and membrane destabilization can trigger lysosomal membrane permeabilization, leading to the release of lysosomal enzymes such as cathepsins into the cytosol. lysosomal membrane permeabilization -induced cathepsin release has also been implicated in the initiation of both apoptosis and necrosis (49, 50). Collectively, these combined defects may render cells more vulnerable to external stresses, including freeze-thaw cycles. The dKO sup treatment is not effective unless it is performed before freezing ML II cells. To enhance viability after cryopreservation, it is necessary to improve the cellular pathology, possibly by eliminating accumulated substances and normalizing lipid composition before freezing. This enables the preservation and utilization of valuable research resources like ML II patient-derived skin fibroblasts. In addition, the results in Figure 3 suggest that the enzyme activity levels in the cells return to those of the untreated diseased cells more than 4 days after thawing and resumption of culture. These cells can be used for pathological analysis of GNPTAB KO or ML II after a certain period of subculture. This method for achieving reversible, temporary recovery of lysosomal functions has a significant practical advantage in research in this field.

Although treatments such as cyclodextrin, as therapeutics aimed to reduce accumulated cholesterol in NPC, are currently in trials (51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56), only symptomatic treatments are available for this disease. Previous reports have shown that the NPC2 protein reduces cholesterol accumulation in fibroblasts from NPC2 patients (57, 58, 59) and ameliorates the pathological condition in NPC2 KO mice (60). Our study demonstrates that the NPC2 protein from human resource is properly modified and actually taken up by cells, and then reduces cholesterol accumulation, which clearly establishes the therapeutic concept of protein replacement therapy. These findings open the door to an entirely new potential curative strategy for NPC2. However, it is important to note that our research findings are merely a proof of concept and cannot be directly linked to clinical applications. In particular, as with conventional ERT, delivering treatment to the central nervous system and bones will likely remain challenging. Furthermore, to maintain long-term intracellular enzyme activity, it will be necessary to explore optimization strategies, such as concentrating and purifying the M6P-modified protein or increasing the dosage. Moving forward, it is necessary to investigate in greater detail the effects of the dKO sup on cellular metabolic systems and to explore its potential for clinical application through in vivo therapeutic experiments using animal models of ML II, LAL deficiency, and NPC disease.

Experimental procedures

Cell lines and cell culture

HeLa-Kyoto cells (referred simply to as HeLa cells in this manuscript), established as a sub-line of the original HeLa cells at Kyoto University, are now globally distributed without any limitation, and we obtained them through EMBL. Normal human skin fibroblasts were obtained from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific), Lonza, and Kurabo. The skin fibroblast lines derived from mucolipidosis patients were established from the remaining specimens for diagnostic or other research purposes for which written informed consent was obtained. In Figure 5, A–D, Pathogenic variants in the GNPTAB gene in ML II patients #1 and #2 were c.310C4T (p.Q104X)/c.2544delA (p.K848fs) and c1120T>C (p.F374L)/c.3565C>T (p.R1189X), respectively. As for the patient-derived skin fibroblasts used in Figure 5E, they were clinically diagnosed, but their genotypes were not determined. The use of the patients’ specimens was approved by the institutional ethics review boards (Osaka University: 703 and Kawasaki Medical School: 5889). The human subject research reported in this manuscript complies with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. HeLa cells and the skin fibroblasts were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator in high-glucose DMEM (D5796; Merck) supplemented with 10% FBS (173012; Merck) and penicillin/streptomycin (100 units/ml and 100 μg/ml, respectively; 09367-34; Nacalai Tesque, Japan) for normal growth conditions (nutrient-rich). For nutrient starvation, cells were cultured in EBSS (E2888; Merck). All cell lines used this study were confirmed as mycoplasma free.

Antibodies

Primary antibodies used were: β-actin (1:5000, M177-3; MBL), LC3 (1:1500, PM036; MBL), NPC2 (1:1000, 19888-1-AP; Proteintech, IL), and Gal3 (1:1000, sc-23938), cathepsin B (1:1000, AF953, R&D, MN), cathepsin D (1:1000, AF1014, R&D), hexosaminidase β subunit (1:1000, sc-376781). horseradish peroxidase -conjugated secondary antibodies used for immunoblotting were: Goat anti-rabbit IgG (31460, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Goat anti-mouse IgG (31430, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Reagents

Commercially available compounds were obtained as follows and used in this study: Bafilomycin A1 (BafA1; 11038, Cayman Chemicals), Mannose-6-phosphate (M6P; M6876, Merck), U18666A (218-01441, Fujifilm-Wako). 4MU-conjugated lysosomal enzyme substrates for β-glucosidase (M3633, Merk), β-hexosaminidase (M2133, Merk), α-mannosidase (M3657, Merk), α-fucosidase (M8527, Merk), α-galactosidase (M7633, Merk), β-galactosidase (M1633, Merk), and β-glucuronidase (M9130, Merk), α-hexosaminidase (44009, Glycosynth), α-iduronidase (44076, Glycosynth).

Measurement of lysosomal enzyme activity

The 4-MU enzyme assay was performed as described previously (61). Cell lysates (“cell”) prepared at a protein concentration of 2 mg/ml, or 10 μl of culture supernatant (“sup”), were used for the reactions. For measurement of total β-hexosaminidase activity, cell lysates were diluted to 20 μg/ml. Samples were incubated with artificial substrates in acidic phosphate or citrate buffer at 37 °C for 1 h, and fluorescence was measured at Ex/Em 365/450 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO; TECAN). Enzyme activities are described as nmol/mg protein/h based on 4-MU standards.

Proteome analysis of cell culture supernatants

Serum-free culture supernatants from WT, GNPTAB KO, M6PR KO, IGF2R KO, and M6PR/IGF2R dKO cells were obtained by culturing the cells in OPTI-MEM (31985062, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 24 h. Proteomic analysis of the collected supernatants and control medium was performed by Creative Proteomics. Protein samples were subjected to 4D-direct DIA proteomic analysis. Proteins were extracted in 8 M urea containing protease inhibitors and quantified using a bicinchoninic acid assay. The samples were digested with trypsin using an iST Sample Preparation Kit (PreOmics), followed by peptide cleanup according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting peptides (200 ng) were analyzed on a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system coupled to a timsTOF HT mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics). The Peptides were separated on an IonOpticks Aurora C18 column (75 μm × 8 cm, 1.7 μm particles) using a 30-min gradient at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. DIA data were acquired in diaPASEF mode. Raw data were processed using Spectronaut version 20 (Biognosys), and spectra were searched against the UniProt human database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, whereas oxidation of methionine and protein N-terminal acetylation were set as variable modifications. Trypsin was specified as the digestion enzyme. False discovery rates at the precursor, peptide, and protein levels were controlled at 1%. Protein abundances were estimated using the MaxLFQ algorithm implemented in Spectronaut.

Enzyme supplementation by dKO sup

M6PR/IGF2R double-knockout cells were cultured in normal growth medium in a 10 cm dish until they reached 100% confluency. The medium was replaced with 10 ml of fresh complete DMEM, and cells were cultured for an additional 2 days. Conditioned medium was collected, centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm pore filter (180E03; Sartorius). Supernatants were stored at −20 °C until use (dKO sup). For the dKO sup treatment, the conditioned medium was mixed at a 1:1 ratio with complete DMEM and applied to cells for 3 days.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in SDS-PAGE sample buffer (56 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8, 6% glycerol, 2% SDS, 0.1 M DTT, 0.04% Bromophenol Blue) and boiled for 5 min. Culture supernatants were cleared by centrifugation (2000 rpm, 5 min), mixed with one-third volume of 4x SDS-PAGE sample buffer, and boiled for 5 min. Samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (IPVH00010; Merck). Membranes were blocked in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 and 3% skim milk and incubated with primary antibodies, followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Chemiluminescent signals were developed with Immobilon Forte (WBLUF0100; Merck) and imaged on the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

LC3 flux assay

The LC3 flux assay was conducted according to the autophagy assay guidelines (62). Briefly, confluent cells were washed with PBS and incubated for 2 h in EBSS, either in the presence or absence of 125 nM bafilomycin A1 (BafA1). The cells were then washed with PBS, lysed with SDS-PAGE sample buffer, and subjected to Western blotting. The samples were separated on a 13% acrylamide gel and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The membranes were incubated separately with antibodies against LC3 and β-actin for detection. Lipidated LC3-II is recruited to the autophagosome membrane upon autophagy activation and is degraded by autophagy. Therefore, the difference in the amount of LC3-II between samples treated with and without BafA1 represents the autophagic degradation of LC3 flux over 2 h.

Enzymatic deglycosylation analysis

Cell lysate and culture supernatant samples prepared in SDS-PAGE sample buffer were treated with Endo H (P0702S; NEB) or PNGase F (P0704S; NEB) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 μl of SDS-PAGE sample was mixed with 1 μl of Endo H or 1 μl of PNGase F and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. For PNGase F treatment, samples were preincubated in the Glycoprotein denaturing buffer (supplied with the enzyme) at 100 °C for 10 s, and 1% NP-40 was added to the reaction.

Imaging analysis

Bright-field images were acquired on an IX83 inverted microscope (OLYMPUS) equipped with a UPlanFLN 4 × /0.13 PhL objective and a DP80 digital camera (OLYMPUS). Live cells were imaged directly in culture medium without fixation. For lysosome analysis, cells grown on coverslips for 24 h were treated with 200 nM LysoTracker Red DND-99 (L7528; Invitrogen) for 30 min, then washed with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at room temperature. Cellular cholesterol was visualized by Filipin staining. Cells on coverslips were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at room temperature. Fixed cells were treated with 100 μg/ml Filipin III (F4767; Merck) at room temperature for 1 h. In situ β-glucosidase activity was visualized with SPiDER-β-Gal (Dojindo) (63). 1 μM of the SPiDER probe was added to the culture medium for the last 1 h of incubation, and the cells were co-stained with LysoTracker. The coverslips were washed and fixed as described above. After washing with PBS, samples were mounted and imaged either on an IX83 inverted microscope (OLYMPUS) equipped with a PlanApo N 60 × /1.42 NA oil-immersion objective, Zyla sCMOS camera (ANDOR), and U-HGLGPS fluorescence light source (OLYMPUS) or on LSM900 with a PlanApo 63 × /1.4 NA oil-immersion objective lens (Carl Zeiss). Images were acquired using cellSens software (OLYMPUS) or ZEN software (Zeiss), and quantified using Fiji (https://imagej.net/Fiji), or CellProfiler ver. 4.2.8 (https://cellprofiler.org).

Electron microscopy

Cells were rinsed with phosphate buffer and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer for 2 h, followed by post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h at 4 °C. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in Epon resin for 3 days at 60 °C. Ultrathin sections of 70 nm were cut using an ultramicrotome (EM UC7; Leica) and mounted on mesh grids. Sections were stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 20 min at room temperature, followed by lead citrate staining for 5 min at room temperature. After drying, samples were examined using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400, JEOL) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

Cholesterol quantification

Cellular cholesterol levels were determined using the Amplex Red Cholesterol Assay Kit (A12216; Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. To minimize intracellular cholesterol accumulation derived from the culture medium, cells were pre-cultured in medium supplemented with 2% FBS for 48 h, and then treated with the dKO sup in 5% FBS medium. Cells were lysed in distilled water containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (04693132001; Roche) by sonication. Ten micrograms of each lysate were incubated with the Amplex Red working solution. Fluorescence was measured at Ex/Em 540/580 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO).

Endosome/lysosome rupture assay

The assay was performed as previously described (64, 65). Effectene (301425, QIAGEN)-coated polystyrene beads (09850; Polysciences, PA) were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, as for DNA transfection. Cells were allowed to internalize the beads for 3 h, then fixed and stained with anti–Gal3 antibody after 3 h and 12 h chase periods. The proportion of damaged endosomes/lysosomes was quantified by counting the total number of internalized beads per cell and determining the fraction that was Gal3.

Statistics

Parametric comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t test or Welch’s t test depending on variance. For comparisons among multiple independent groups, parametric data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. Nonparametric data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests with p-values adjusted using the Holm method. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001). Statistical details are provided in the figure legends, and reported values are presented as means and SD.
