Uniform spectral gaps, non-abelian Littlewood-Offord and anti-concentration for random walks Thanks: OB has received funding from the European Research Council (ERC) under the European Union’s Horizon 2020 research and innovation programme (grant agreement No. 803711).
Oren Becker, Emmanuel Breuillard

Abstract
. We show that random walks on semisimple algebraic groups do not concentrate on proper algebraic subvarieties with uniform exponential rate of anti-concentration. This is achieved by proving a uniform spectral gap for quasi-regular representations of countable linear groups. The method makes key use of Diophantine heights and the Height Gap theorem. We also deduce a non-abelian version of the Littlewood–Offord inequalities and prove logarithmic bounds for escape from subvarieties. In a sequel to this paper, we will show how to transform this uniform gap into uniform expansion for Cayley graphs of finite simple groups of bounded rank G ⁡ ( p ) G(p) over almost all primes p p .



1.
Introduction

Given a discrete group
\Gamma
, a unitary representation
\pi
is said to have the
spectral gap property
if there is
\varepsilon>0
and a finite set
S\subset\Gamma
such that for every unit vector
v

(1.1)

\max_{s\in S}\|\pi(s)v-v\|\geq\varepsilon.

This notion plays a key role in infinite group theory, in the ergodic theory of group actions and in the theory of group
C^{*}
-algebras. For instance, to say that the regular representation
\lambda_{\Gamma}
has the spectral gap property is equivalent to the
non-amenability
of
\Gamma
. Similarly, to say that every unitary representation without invariant unit vector has the spectral gap property is equivalent to
Kazhdan’s property (T)
for
\Gamma
. In dynamics, one is naturally interested in group actions on measure preserving systems
(X,m)
and is led to study the so-called
Koopman representation

\lambda_{X}
on
L^{2}(X,m)
given by
\lambda_{X}(\gamma)f(x)=f(\gamma^{-1}x)
. If
m
is a probability measure, one also considers the restriction
\lambda_{X}^{0}
to the hyperplane of zero mean functions. The spectral gap property for
\lambda_{X}
or
\lambda_{X}^{0}
implies exponential decay of correlations, rates of mixing and rates of convergence for the Kakutani central limit theorem for the associated random products. It is also closely related to the notion of
strong ergodicity
. We refer the reader to the book
[
6
]
, the surveys
[
9
,
40
,
61
]
and to the articles
[
39
,
8
]
for background and references. We shall call a pair
(\varepsilon,S)
as in (
1.1
) a pair of
Kazhdan parameters
for
\pi
. The parameter
\varepsilon>0
is the
Kazhdan constant
and
S
the
Kazhdan set
for
\pi
.

It is of interest to determine which unitary representations have the spectral gap property. When the property holds it is interesting to work out explicit Kazhdan parameters
(\varepsilon,S)
. In this paper, we establish a
uniform spectral gap property
for certain unitary representations of linear groups. We will show that these representations have a
uniform spectral gap property
in that the Kazhdan parameter
\varepsilon
can be chosen to be uniform over all Kazhdan sets
S
.

We shall derive several consequences of this uniform gap. Most notably, we shall obtain logarithmic bounds on escape from subvarieties (see Corollary
1.5
) as well as uniform anti-concentration estimates for random walks on linear groups. The latter (see Theorem
1.3
and
1.4
) establishes a strong non-commutative version of the classical Littlewood-Offord estimates
[
65
]
. In a sequel to this paper we deduce from it uniform expansion results for finite simple groups of bounded rank
[
5
]
.

1.1.
Uniform gap for quasi-regular representations

For a subgroup
H\leq\Gamma
we let
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
be the
quasi-regular representation
of
\Gamma
with isotropy group
H
. Its representation space is
\ell^{2}(\Gamma/H)
and
\Gamma
acts by left translations. Eymard
[
36
]
studied the spectral gap property for quasi-regular representations and coined the term
co-amenable
to describe a subgroup
H\leq\Gamma
for which
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
does not have the spectral gap property. See
[
36
,
44
,
63
,
42
,
54
,
7
]
. While it is futile to attempt to classify all co-amenable subgroups of an arbitrary linear group (such as the free group), one can seek geometric conditions on
H
that are enough to guarantee the spectral gap property for
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
and even the uniform spectral gap property.

A preliminary remark is that the spectral gap property is inherited from quotient group actions and so are the Kazhdan parameters. In other words, if
X
and
Y
are
\Gamma
-sets and
\phi:X\to Y
is a
\Gamma
-equivariant map, the spectral gap property for
\lambda_{Y}
with Kazhdan parameters
(\varepsilon,S)
implies the spectral gap property for
\lambda_{X}
with the same parameters (see Lemma
6.5
). So in establishing the uniform spectral gap property we may always pass to a suitable quotient.

Let
K
be a field and
\Gamma\leq\GL_{d}(K)
a countable group. If
\Gamma
is virtually solvable, that is, contains a solvable subgroup of finite index, then it is amenable and no unitary representation of
\Gamma
has the spectral gap property. Otherwise,
\Gamma
admits a quotient that is Zariski-dense in a semisimple algebraic group. In view of the preliminary remark above, it is therefore natural to assume that
\Gamma
is Zariski-dense in a semisimple algebraic group
\mathbb{G}\leq\GL_{d}
defined over
K
. Passing to a subgroup of bounded index, one can further assume
\mathbb{G}
to be connected, see
1.5.4
. We can thus state our main result in this setting.

Theorem 1.1

(uniform spectral gap for quasi-regular representations)
.

There is
\varepsilon_{d}>0
depending on
d
only, such that the following holds. Let
K
be a field and
\mathbb{G}\leq\GL_{d}
a connected semisimple algebraic
K
-group. Suppose
H\leq\Gamma
are countable subgroups of
\mathbb{G}(K)
, with
H
not Zariski-dense in
\mathbb{G}
. Given
S\subset\Gamma
, for every unit vector
v\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma/H)
, there is
s\in S
such that

\|\lambda_{\Gamma/H}(s)v-v\|\geq\varepsilon_{d}

provided
S
is finite and generates a Zariski-dense subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
.

We stress that
\varepsilon_{d}
does not depend on the field
K
nor its characteristic. Although we made no attempts at optimizing it, we shall give an explicit lower bound on
\varepsilon_{d}
in terms of the height gap constants from
[
22
]
(see (
6.1
), (
6.8
) and (
6.10
) in Section §
6
). The height gap constants are effective and, although they have not been stated in
[
22
]
, explicit bounds in terms of
d
can be traced from the arguments therein.

The uniformity in Theorem
1.1
allows us to obtain a uniform upper bound for the operator norm of an arbitrary probability measure on the group. We let
\|.\|_{\op}
be the operator norm on
\ell^{2}(\Gamma/H)
. For a probability measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
we set:

\beta(\mu):=1-\sup_{g\in\Gamma,L\lneq\mathbb{G}}\mu(gL\cap\Gamma)

where
L
runs over all proper algebraic subgroups of
\mathbb{G}
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Set
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{d}=\varepsilon_{d}^{2}/2
. Let
H\leq\Gamma
be countable subgroups of
\mathbb{G}(K)
with
H
not Zariski-dense in
\mathbb{G}
. If
\mu
is any probability measure on
\Gamma
, then

\|\lambda_{\Gamma/H}(\mu)\|_{\op}\leq 1-\beta(\mu)\varepsilon^{\prime}_{d}\,\,.

Again, we stress that this bound is uniform over all
\Gamma
and
H
: the only way
\|\lambda_{\Gamma/H}(\mu)\|_{\op}
can be close to
1
for some
\Gamma
and
H
is if
\mu
is almost entirely supported on a coset of a proper algebraic subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
.

1.2.
Anti-concentration estimates for random walks

We now state some consequences of the above uniform spectral gaps for the classical question of bounding the probability that a product of independent random variables lies in some algebraic subvariety of
\mathbb{G}
. Let
k
be a local field (i.e.
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, a
p
-adic field, or a field of Laurent series over a finite field).

Theorem 1.3

(anti-concentration on algebraic subvarieties)
.

Let
G=\mathbb{G}(k)\leq\GL_{d}(k)
be a connected semisimple algebraic
k
-group. For a
G
-valued random variable
X
let

\beta(X):=1-\sup\{\mathbb{P}(X\in gL)\mid L\textnormal{ proper algebraic subgroup of }G,g\in G\}.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent
G
-valued random variables. Then for every proper closed algebraic subvariety
V
of
G
we have

(1.2)

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\cdots X_{n}\in V)\leq C_{V}\cdot e^{-c\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta(X_{i})},

where one can take
C_{V}=(1+\dim V)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\deg(V)
(
C_{V}=1
if
V
is a coset of an algebraic subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
) and
c=\varepsilon^{\prime}_{d}4^{-d^{2}}
.

Here
\deg(V)
denotes the degree of
V
in some fixed projective embedding of
\mathbb{G}
, see Section
3
. In Theorem
1.3
the probability distributions are arbitrary and not assumed to be symmetric nor supported on a countable set. Although the result will be deduced from the special case of finitely supported measures, the reduction is not routine due to the fact that there are uncountably many cosets
gL
.

A direct corollary of Theorem 1.3 is the following novel uniform anti-concentration estimate: For a fixed
k\geq 3
, if each
X_{i}
is distributed uniformly on a set
S_{i}
of cardinality
k
, and if
S_{i}^{-1}S_{i}
generates a Zariski-dense subgroup of
G
for each
i
(implying
\beta(X_{i})\geq 1/k
), then for every
V
as above, the rate of exponential anti-concentration depends only on
d
and
k
:

(1.3)

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\cdots X_{n}\in V)\leq C_{V}e^{-cn/k}.

We will derive Theorem
1.3
directly from Theorem
1.1
. An important special case of the above theorem is when
V
is a proper algebraic subgroup. In this case, the theorem follows directly from Theorem
1.1
. Anti-concentration on subgroups is a key ingredient in establishing spectral gap estimates for Cayley graphs of large finite groups via the so-called Bourgain-Gamburd machine; see
[
17
]
. In
[
5
]
we will use Theorem
1.3
to find new families of uniform expanders. In turn, this will be a crucial ingredient in
[
3
]
where we study character varieties of random groups.

Anti-concentration estimates on varieties for i.i.d. random walks have been independently studied in
[
2
]
as well as in
[
13
,
10
,
24
]
, where the theory of random matrix products is used to derive an exponential estimate for a certain special class of varieties. However, these methods have shortcomings inasmuch as they often require special hypotheses, such as proximality, and they are not uniform in the probability law of the random variables. In
[
53
]
and
[
47
]
exponential non-concentration of random walks on subvarieties is shown for arbitrary subvarieties using a sieving procedure together with the spectral gap estimates for finite quotients that follow from super-strong approximation
[
58
,
18
,
43
,
20
]
. See
[
25
]
for a description of this method. Theorem
1.3
above recovers all these results, but proves something of a much stronger nature. Indeed, a key feature of Theorem
1.3
, which is absent from all earlier works, is the uniformity of the rate of exponential decay. In the example (
1.3
) for instance, the rate depends only on the dimension
d
and the cardinality
k
of the support of the probability measures.

Note that our bound is uniform regardless of the magnitude of the
X_{i}
and depends on the variety only through the multiplicative constant and only via the degree of the variety. Inasmuch, it belongs to the family of results that is usually attached to the names of Littlewood and Offord, who studied such anti-concentration bounds for sums of independent random variables in their study of real roots of random polynomials
[
52
]
. In that commutative context, anti-concentration on varieties has recently been studied in
[
64
,
38
]
, where the authors obtained inverse square root bounds. Inverse square root bounds are optimal without further assumptions on the distribution of the random variables, but as Theorem
1.3
shows, much stronger exponential bounds hold under mild assumptions of semisimplicity and non-concentration on algebraic subgroups.

1.3.
Non-commutative Littlewood-Offord estimates

In the special case when the variety
V
is a point, the above estimate can be seen as a genuine non-abelian version of the Littlewood–Offord inequality
[
52
,
65
,
55
]
. Recall that this inequality (improved by Erdős in
[
34
]
as follows), says that if
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are real numbers and
X_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, are independent random variables taking values
a_{i}
or
-a_{i}
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
, then

(1.4)

\sup_{b\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(X_{1}+\ldots+X_{n}=b)\leq 2^{-n}{n\choose\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}).

The key features of the Littlewood–Offord bound (as opposed to classical normal approximation limit theorems such as the Berry-Esseen bound for which moment assumptions are required) are that it holds for independent but not necessarily identically distributed random variables and that the bound is uniform regardless of the magnitude of the
a_{i}
’s.

A non-abelian version of the Littlewood–Offord inequality was obtained by Tiep and Vu in
[
67
]
(see also
[
56
,
64
]
) for products of independent random variables in
\GL_{d}(\mathbb{C})
with a similar inverse square-root bound under very mild assumptions on the
X_{i}
(that there should not be too many of small order). More recently, Juskevicius and Semetulskis
[
49
]
gave a very short proof of (
1.4
), that works uniformly for all torsion-free (not necessarily abelian) groups.

The example of the free group tells us that we should expect a much stronger bound under further non-commutativity assumptions. The version below yields such a stronger bound, which is typically exponential in the number of variables, provided the
X_{i}
are not too heavily supported on virtually solvable subgroups of
\GL_{d}(k)
. Below
k
is an arbitrary local field.

Theorem 1.4

(non-abelian Littlewood–Offord inequality)
.

There is a constant
c_{d}>0
such that the following holds. Let
k
be a local field,
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be
n
independent
\GL_{d}(k)
-valued random variables. Let
\beta_{i}:=1-\sup\{\mathbb{P}(X_{i}\in gL)\mid g\in\GL_{d}(k),L\leq\GL_{d}(k)\textnormal{ virtually solvable}\}
. Then

\sup_{g\in\GL_{d}(k)}\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\cdots X_{n}=g)\leq e^{-c_{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}}.

For example, if
X_{i}
takes values
a_{i},a_{i}^{-1},b_{i},b_{i}^{-1}
with probability
\frac{1}{4}
and if each pair
a_{i},b_{i}
generates a non virtually solvable subgroup of
\GL_{d}(\mathbb{C})
, then
\beta_{i}\geq\frac{1}{4}

(indeed, if a symmetric set
S
is contained in a coset
gL
then
\langle S^{2}\rangle
is contained in
L
and has index at most
2
in
\langle S\rangle
). While Theorem
1.4
will be derived from Theorem
1.1
, it does not require consideration of quasi-regular representations, only the regular one. Inasmuch, it can also be derived from the uniform Tits alternative
[
21
]
.

1.4.
Escape from subvarieties

We conclude by stating another consequence of Theorem
1.3
regarding
escape from subvarieties
. This phenomenon originates from a lemma due to Eskin-Mozes-Oh
[
35
]
that has been essential in work on growth of linear groups and approximate subgroups, e.g.
[
15
,
18
]
. It asserts (see Lemma
3.1
) that if a finitely generated subgroup of an algebraic group is Zariski-dense, then there is an upper bound on the smallest word length of an element that lies outside a given proper algebraic subvariety, and this bound depends only on the degree and dimension of the subvariety. There are various proofs available in the literature for this lemma. For instance,
[
35
]
uses Bezout’s theorem while
[
18
]
uses an ultralimit argument. We provide yet another proof, based on a very simple linearisation procedure, see Lemma
3.1
. This proof gives an explicit polynomial bound in the degree of the subvariety. While this lemma is needed in the proof of our main results Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
, it turns out that these results can be used
a posteriori
to drastically improve the bound in the escape lemma by providing a logarithmic bound in the degree aspect:

Corollary 1.5

(Logarithmic escape)
.

Given
d\geq 2
, there is
C_{d}>1
such that if
K
is a field,
\mathbb{G}
is a connected semisimple algebraic
K
-group of dimension at most
d
,
S\subset\mathbb{G}(K)
is a finite subset generating a Zariski-dense subgroup,
V
is a proper closed subvariety of
\mathbb{G}
of degree at most
N
, and
n\geq 1
is an integer such that
S^{n}\subset V
, then
n\leq C_{d}\log(1+N)
.

1.5.
Some further remarks.

1.5.1.
Weak containment

One may wonder to what extent the quasi-regular representations
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
can be controlled already by the regular representation
\lambda_{\Gamma}
. If
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
is weakly contained in
\lambda_{\Gamma}
(in the sense of Fell), then
\|\lambda_{\Gamma/H}(\mu)\|_{\op}=\|\lambda_{\Gamma}(\mu)\|_{\op}
for every symmetric probability measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
. One side of the equality follows from weak containment and the other side from Kesten’s criterion, or in the setting of Theorem
1.1
for center-free
\mathbb{G}
from the
C^{*}
-simplicity of
\Gamma

[
19
]
showing that weak containment implies weak equivalence. So if
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
is weakly contained in
\lambda_{\Gamma}
, the spectral gap of the quasi-regular representation is controlled by that of the regular representation. This is the case when
H
is an amenable subgroup of
\Gamma
, e.g.
[
70
, 7.3.7]
. However, this is the only case, because if
H
is any non-amenable subgroup of
\Gamma
, then
\|\lambda_{\Gamma/H}(\mu)\|_{\op}=1>\|\lambda_{\Gamma}(\mu)\|_{\op}
for any symmetric
\mu
supported on a generating set of
H
. This shows that for non-amenable
H
, the spectral gap for
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
proven in Theorem
1.1
cannot be deduced from a spectral gap for
\lambda_{\Gamma}
alone.

1.5.2.
The spectral gap property and invariant means

For the Koopman representations
\lambda_{X}
with
(X,m)
discrete and
m
the counting measure, a celebrated theorem of Tarski
[
66
,
32
,
27
]
asserts that
\lambda_{X}
does not have the spectral gap property if and only if
X
admits a
\Gamma
-invariant mean and if and only if
X
does not have a paradoxical decomposition. As shown in
[
27
]
these equivalences can be made quantitative in a certain sense. Our proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on a new characterization of the spectral gap property via a result, which we call the ping-pong lemma with overlaps (Lemma
2.1
). It yields explicit gap estimates. While we only use the forward direction here, in a subsequent paper
[
4
]
we will show that a converse holds by introducing the notion of
action with few overlaps
and show that
X
has few overlaps if and only if
\lambda_{X}
has the spectral gap property.

1.5.3.
Proving the spectral gap property without uniformity

The fact that
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
has the spectral gap property for a given pair
H\leq\Gamma
as in Theorem
1.1
is well known. A quick proof can also be given along the lines of Furstenberg’s proof of the Borel density theorem
[
41
]
. This strategy was used by Shalom in
[
62
]
to give a direct proof (without relying on the Tits alternative) of the non-amenability of non virtually solvable linear groups. It goes as follows. By Chevalley’s theorem one finds a projective representation
V
of
\Gamma
where
H
fixes a line. If there is no spectral gap, an invariant mean exists on
\Gamma/H
, which then becomes a
\Gamma
-invariant probability measure on
\mathbb{P}(V\otimes k)
for each choice of local field extension
k
of
K
. Choosing
k
for which
\Gamma
is unbounded allows to apply Furstenberg’s lemma
[
70
, Lemma 3.2.1]
and contradict the irreducibility of
\Gamma
on
V
. This method can be pushed to yield uniformity of the Kazhdan constant when the isotropy group
H
varies. We heard from Hadari and Shalom
[
46
]
that they have used this method to establish the spectral gap property for
\Gamma/H
for a wide variety of pairs
H\leq\Gamma
with uniformity in
H
. Uniformity in
H
can also be deduced from super-strong approximation
[
43
]
, but should rather be seen as an ingredient in the proof of super-strong approximation, see
[
25
]
. A proof with uniformity in
H
was given by the second author via random matrix products in an unpublished note
[
24
]
. However, all these methods fail to get uniformity with respect to
\Gamma
or
S
. While it is certainly worth contemplating a non-standard analysis approach to Theorem
1.1
, attempts in that direction have failed so far. Even if they were successful, they would not provide an explicit bound on the gap as we obtain in Theorem
1.1
.

1.5.4.
A reformulation of Theorem
1.1

A subgroup of
\GL_{d}
is said to be strongly irreducible if it does not permute a finite family of proper vector subspaces. Given two countable linear groups
H\leq\Gamma\leq\GL_{d}
we say that
H
is
irreducibly dense
in
\Gamma
if every strongly irreducible representation of a finite index subgroup
\Gamma_{0}
of
\Gamma
remains strongly irreducible in restriction to
H\cap\Gamma_{0}
. This is equivalent to requiring that
\mathbb{H}^{0}Rad(\mathbb{G})
is a proper subgroup of
\mathbb{G}^{0}
, where
\mathbb{H}
(resp.
\mathbb{G}
) denotes the Zariski closure of
H
(resp.
\Gamma
),
Rad(\mathbb{G})
is the solvable radical and
\mathbb{G}^{0}
the connected component of the identity.

With this definition we have the following reformulation of Theorem
1.1
.
Let
K
be a field and
H\leq\Gamma
countable subgroups of
\GL_{d}(K)
with
H
not irreducibly dense in
\Gamma
. If
S\subset\Gamma
is a finite set with
\langle S\rangle
irreducibly dense in
\Gamma
, then
S
is an
\varepsilon_{d}
-Kazhdan set for
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
. Here
\varepsilon_{d}>0
depends only on
d
.

To see this from Theorem
1.1
, note first that looking at the conjugation action of
\Gamma
on
\mathbb{G}^{0}/Rad(\mathbb{G})
, and in view of Lemma
6.5
, one may assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{G}^{0}
is semisimple and
\mathbb{G}\leq Aut(\mathbb{G}^{0})
. Next recall that
Aut(\mathbb{G}^{0})
contains
Inn(\mathbb{G}^{0})
as a subgroup of bounded index. Indeed there are at most
\rk\mathbb{G}\leq d
simple factors of
\mathbb{G}^{0}
that may be permuted and for each of them the group of outer-automorphism identifies to the symmetry group of the Dynkin diagram, hence has size at most
6
(achieved for
D_{4}
). In particular
[Aut(\mathbb{G}^{0}):Inn(\mathbb{G}^{0})]\leq d_{0}:=6^{d}d!
. So we may assume that
\mathbb{G}^{0}
has index at most
d_{0}
in
\mathbb{G}
. As is well-known (e.g.
[
18
, Lemma C.1]
)
\Sigma:=(S\cup S^{-1})^{2d_{0}}\cap\mathbb{G}^{0}
generates
\langle S\rangle\cap\mathbb{G}^{0}
. So if
\Sigma
is
\varepsilon
-Kazhdan for
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
, then
S
is
\frac{\varepsilon}{2d_{0}}
-Kazhdan. And the restriction of
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
is a direct sum of at most
d_{0}
quasi-regular representations of
\Gamma\cap\mathbb{G}^{0}
with non-Zariski-dense isotropy group. By Theorem
1.1

\Sigma
is
\varepsilon_{d}
-Kazhdan for each of them. Hence it is
\frac{\varepsilon_{d}}{\sqrt{d_{0}}}
-Kazhdan for
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
as desired.

1.5.5.
Other sufficient geometric conditions on the isotropy group

For a countable linear group
\Gamma\leq\GL_{d}
, one may ask for other geometric conditions on the isotropy group
H
for
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
to have the spectral gap property or its uniform version. It turns out that when
H
is
algebraic
in the sense that
H=\Gamma\cap\mathbb{H}
, where
\mathbb{H}
(resp.
\mathbb{G}
) is the Zariski-closure of
H
(resp.
\Gamma
), then one can give a necessary and sufficient condition for
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
to have the spectral gap property. This holds if and only if
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{H})
is a proper subgroup of
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{G})
, where
\mathcal{C}()
denotes the
perfect core
, namely the last term in the derived series of the connected component of the identity, see
[
61
, 3.23]
. In a sequel to this paper, we will show how to push Theorem
1.1
to this more general setting and prove the uniformity of the spectral gap for such actions. This requires extending Theorem
1.1
to perfect algebraic groups and dealing with affine representations where we only deal with projective ones in this paper (as in e.g.
[
43
]
). While our method does extend to this case, it is at the cost of a significantly more involved argument (due to the failure of (
5.9
) in a non semisimple context), which we postpone to a forthcoming sequel. Similar difficulties arose in L. Pham’s thesis
[
57
]
in the case of the affine group
\SL(2,\mathbb{Z})\ltimes\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

1.5.6.
Anti-concentration on other sets and neighborhoods

Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
deal with anti-concentration on points and varieties. It would interesting, in the spirit of
[
64
,
38
]
to extend these results to semi-algebraic sets or more generally to sets definable in an o-minimal structure. The original Littlewood-Offord theorem concerned anti-concentration on intervals rather than points. Under the assumption that the support of the
\mu_{i}
in Theorem
7.6
contain generators of a
discrete
non-amenable subgroup, one can easily deduce a similar anti-concentration estimate for compact sets instead of points. More challenging would be to derive anti-concentration estimates for pre-images of an interval (over
\mathbb{R})
or a disc (over
\mathbb{C}
) under a polynomial map (Theorem
1.3
being the case when the interval is reduced to a point) under a similar discreteness assumption. See
[
29
]
for a result of this type in the classical Littlewood-Offord setting. The very strong uniformity afforded by Theorem

allows the use of the effective nullstellensatz to prove such estimates when the interval is of very small size (super-exponential in

) without any discreteness assumption on the supports. See
[
23
]
for this argument.

1.5.7.
One-percent versus ninety-nine percents

Corollary
1.2
in the case
H=1
(or Theorem
7.6
) says that for a probability measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
a small spectral gap
1-\|\lambda_{\Gamma}(\mu)\|_{\op}
implies that
\mu
is heavily supported on a coset of a proper algebraic subgroup (even an amenable subgroup). We may call this a
99\%
statement. The
1\%
analogue would then be the following, which we leave as an open question. The
1\%
problem: Does there exist a function
\delta_{d}(\varepsilon)>0
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
every field
K
and every probability measure
\mu
on
\Gamma\leq\GL_{d}(K)
such that
\|\lambda_{\Gamma}(\mu)\|_{\op}\geq\varepsilon
we have
\mu(gA)\geq\delta_{d}(\varepsilon)
for some coset
gA
of an amenable subgroup of
\Gamma
. Even the case when
\Gamma
is a fixed free group and
\mu
a uniform probability measure on a finite subset seems challenging.

1.6.
An overview of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

One way to establish explicit spectral gaps for group actions is to exhibit an explicit paradoxical decomposition, for example by constructing a free subgroup acting freely. While we will not do this, we will use a closely related but more versatile argument establishing a version of the ping-pong lemma that does not produce a free subgroup, but provides enough separation to guarantee a quantitative spectral gap. This will be the topic of Section
2
, where we prove the
ping-pong with overlaps lemma
, Lemma
2.1
.

Unlike the classical ping-pong lemma used to produce free subgroups where ping-pong partners
\gamma_{i}
are chosen to have a contracting action on a set
X
with disjoint attracting and repelling neighborhoods
A_{i},R_{i}
, this result directly gives an upper bound on the spectral norm of the sum
\gamma_{1}+\cdots+\gamma_{r}
of
r
group elements, under the assumption that their action satisfies similar conditions as in the ping-pong lemma. The key difference is that the attracting and repelling neighbourhoods
A_{i},R_{i}
are now allowed to have some overlap, yet with multiplicity much smaller than
r
.

Back to the group action of
\Gamma
, by Chevalley’s theorem, given a non-Zariski-dense subgroup
H
of
\mathbb{G}
, there is a linear representation
V
of
\mathbb{G}
without
\mathbb{G}
-invariant line such that
H
fixes a line in
V
. When
K
has characteristic zero,
V
can be chosen to be absolutely irreducible. Only mild modifications are required in positive characteristic (e.g. see Lemma
6.6
) and for simplicity, we assume char
K=0
in this overview. Only finitely many possible
V
’s are necessary to consider, so it is enough to deal with one of them. Starting with the finite set
S
we shall construct an element
\gamma
and suitable conjugates
\gamma_{i}:=g_{i}\gamma g_{i}^{-1}
all expressible as short words of bounded length in
S
, and find a suitable local field extension
k
of
K
for which
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{r}
“play ping-pong with overlaps” on projective space
X=\mathbb{P}(V\otimes_{K}k)
.

The element
\gamma
need not be semisimple or
k
-proximal (that is, with a unique top eigenvalue); it just needs to have one eigenvalue that is not of modulus
1
in
k
. This flexibility is key to the success of the method and is afforded thanks to the great generality of the ping-pong with overlaps lemma. The
g_{i}
are chosen so that they send the generalized eigenspaces of
\gamma
in the most transverse possible way. Of course, subspaces of small codimension will typically intersect, and consequently the attracting and repelling neighbourhoods (which are thickenings of these generalized eigenspaces) will intersect. But the irreducibility of the action is enough to guarantee that the overlap will have bounded multiplicity.

The fact that one can indeed find some local field extension
k
, where the
\gamma_{i}
play ping-pong with overlaps, is the beef of the main argument. While some local completions
k
may provide the required separation and overlap control for some of the
\gamma_{i}
, it is not a priori clear that a single completion can be chosen to achieve the required separation and overlap control simultaneously for all
\gamma_{i}
. In fact, examples exist of families of finite subsets
\{S_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
of certain real simple Lie groups such as
\SO(2n,1)
for which, although
\langle S_{i}\rangle
is (Hausdorff) dense, all words in
S_{i}
of length at most
i
remain elliptic elements (with all eigenvalues of modulus
1
), see
[
14
, Example 10.3]
. To find a suitable
k
, we thus need to argue globally and exploit the various cancellations and compensations that occur when considering all completions together. An essential preliminary reduction is thus to first argue that, without loss of generality when proving Theorem
1.1
, the field
K
can be assumed to be a global field (e.g., a number field in characteristic zero).

A second reduction is that
S
can be assumed to be of bounded cardinality, as every finite set generating a Zariski-dense subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
has a subset of size at most
2\dim\mathbb{G}-1
with the same property. See Lemma
3.4
, whose proof uses a non-euclidean Helly-type theorem.

Then the argument proceeds by considering all possible generalized eigenspaces of
\gamma
and estimating the projective distance between them in each of the (finitely many) possible completions of
K
for which
\gamma
is not elliptic. This has to be done at the local level; that is, for each completion, concrete estimates for distances in each local field need to be worked out.

For instance, one needs estimates for the distance from a point to the intersection of subspaces in terms of the distance to each subspace, c.f. Lemma
4.3
. We discuss a natural notion of orthogonality for this purpose in §
4.1
that makes sense uniformly over all local fields. In the non-archimedian case, it is essential to obtain sharp constants in these estimates.

The local quantities are then summed up into a height-like quantity of the form:

t:=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}t_{v}

where
t_{v}\geq 0
is the local quantity at hand, e.g. the sum of the logarithms of the projective distances between the relevant subspaces. Here
V_{K}
is the set of all places (i.e. equivalence classes of absolute values) of
K
and
n_{v}
is the local degree
[K_{v}:\mathbb{Q}_{v}]
, where
\mathbb{Q}_{v}
and
K_{v}
are the completions of
\mathbb{Q}
and
K
with respect to
v
. In Section
5
this weighted sum of local contributions is shown to be solely controlled by the height of
S
, namely:

(1.5)

t\leq C_{1}h(S)+C_{2},

for parameters
C_{1},C_{2}
depending only on
d
, where

h(S):=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}\log\|S\|_{v}

is the height of
S
, and
\|S\|_{v}:=\max_{s\in S}\|s\|_{v}
for
\|s\|_{v}
the operator norm of
s
in
\GL_{d}(K_{v})
associated with the standard Euclidean or
v
-adic norm. For (
1.5
) to hold, it is essential to properly define the projective distances between subspaces and most of Section
4
is devoted to this definition and its basic properties.

Three main ingredients are used at this point. The first is the effective Bochi inequality
[
11
,
26
]
, which is a local result and asserts that the joint spectral radius

R_{v}(S):=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\|S^{n}\|_{v}^{\frac{1}{n}}

is almost (within a multiplicative constant close to
1
) achieved by the spectral radius (largest modulus of an eigenvalue) of a word of bounded length in
S
. See (
5.6
).

The second ingredient is a global result about heights on semisimple algebraic groups, the so-called
Height gap theorem
proved by the second author in
[
22
]

(see also
[
28
]
for a recent new proof). It asserts that the normalized height

\widehat{h}(S):=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}h(S^{n})=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}\log R_{v}(S)

is bounded away from
0
by some positive quantity
gap_{d}>0
, which depends only on
d
(and not on
K
nor
S
) provided
S
generates a Zariski-dense subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
. This result can be interpreted as a kind of adelic Margulis lemma.

The last crucial ingredient is the
quasi-symmetrisation theorem
from
[
22
]
. This asserts that the normalized height, which is always bounded above by the height
h(S)
and is invariant under conjugation by elements in
\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{Q}^{al})
can be achieved (up to multiplicative constants) by the height of a suitable conjugate of
S
in
\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{Q}^{al})
. Namely:

(1.6)

\widehat{h}(S)\leq\inf_{g\in\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{Q}^{\al})}h(gSg^{-1})\leq\kappa_{d}\widehat{h}(S)

for some constant
\kappa_{d}
independent of
S
.

The last two ingredients combined with (
1.5
) allow, after a suitable conjugation of
S
in
\mathbb{G}(\mathbb{Q}^{al})
to assume that

t+h(S)\ll_{d}\widehat{h}(S).

Now this must imply the existence of at least one place
v
for which
t_{v}+\log\|S\|_{v}
is controlled by
\log R_{v}(S)
. Applying the effective Bochi inequality (
5.6
), we then obtain a suitable
\gamma
in a bounded power of
S
, which has a large eigenvalue gap between the top and bottom eigenvalues at the place
v
, and for which all the subspaces associated with the
\gamma_{i}
are well separated. This ensures that the conditions of the ping-pong with overlaps lemma on
\mathbb{P}(V\otimes_{K}K_{v})
are satisfied and ends the proof of the theorem.

2.
Ping-pong with overlaps

Let
M\geq 0
. We will say that a finite subset
F
of a group
\Gamma
acting on a set
X
is in
M
-
ping-pong
position if there are subsets
\mathbf{R}_{\gamma},\mathbf{A}_{\gamma}
of
X
for each
\gamma\in F
such that

(i)

\gamma(X\setminus\mathbf{R}_{\gamma})\subset\mathbf{A}_{\gamma}
for all
\gamma\in F
, and

(ii)

\sum_{\gamma\in F}1_{\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}}(x)\leq M
and
\sum_{\gamma\in F}1_{\mathbf{A}_{\gamma}}(x)\leq M
for all
x\in X
.

The following simple lemma gives a general upper bound on the norm of Markov operators associated to finite sets in ping-pong position.

Lemma 2.1

(ping-pong with overlaps)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a group acting on a discrete set
X
. Let
F\subset\Gamma
be a finite subset in
M
-ping-pong position. Let
\lambda
be the unitary representation of
\Gamma
acting on
\ell^{2}(X)
, namely
(\lambda(\gamma)f)(x)=f(\gamma^{-1}x)
. Then

\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}\lambda(\gamma)\|^{2}\leq 4M|F|.

We call this lemma the
ping-pong lemma with overlaps
because it differs from the classical ping-pong lemma (as in
[
68
]
or
[
31
]
) inasmuch as it does not imply that the elements of
F
generate a free subgroup, but it yields a bound for the operator norm of the operator
\sum_{\gamma\in F}\lambda(\gamma)
which is as good (up to a multiplicative factor) as Kesten’s bound for free groups. Recall that Kesten’s bound
[
50
]
gives
\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}\lambda(\gamma)\|^{2}=4(|F|-1)
if
F=\{\gamma_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,\gamma_{k}^{\pm 1}\}
and the
\gamma_{i}
generate a free group of rank
k
. It follows that our bound is asymptotically sharp up to the factor
M
as
|F|
grows. Its main advantage is that conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
are
more widely applicable and much easier to verify than those of the classical ping-pong lemma.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Let
P_{\gamma}
and
Q_{\gamma}
be the orthogonal projections
onto
\ell^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{\gamma}}}\right)
and
\ell^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R_{\gamma}}}\right)
,
respectively, i.e.
P_{\gamma}f=1_{A_{\gamma}}f
and
Q_{\gamma}f=1_{R_{\gamma}}f
.

For every family
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{f_{\gamma}\in\ell^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)}}\right\}_{\gamma\in F}
,
we have by Cauchy–Schwarz and Assumption (ii) that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{\gamma\in F}{\bf 1}_{{\bf A}_{\gamma}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)f_{\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{\gamma\in F}{\bf 1}_{{\bf R}_{\gamma}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)f_{\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}

are bounded from above by

M\sum_{\gamma\in F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f_{\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)}}\right|^{2}

for all
x\in X
. So

\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}P_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}\|^{2}\leq M\sum_{\gamma\in F}\|f_{\gamma}\|^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}Q_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}\|^{2}\leq M\sum_{\gamma\in F}\|f_{\gamma}\|^{2}\,\,\text{.}

Thus

\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}P_{\gamma}\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)\|^{2}\leq M\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|F}}\right|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}Q_{\gamma}\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)^{*}\|^{2}\leq M\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|F}}\right|\,\,\text{.}

By the triangle inequality,

\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)\|\leq\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}P_{\gamma}\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)\|+\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-P_{\gamma}}}\right)\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)\|\,\,,

and thus it remains to prove that

\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-P_{\gamma}}}\right)\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)\|\leq\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)Q_{\gamma}\|=\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}Q_{\gamma}\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)^{*}\|

(the equality follows since
Q_{\gamma}^{*}=Q_{\gamma}
).

Fix
f\in\ell^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X}}\right)
,
\|f\|=1
. Assumption (i) implies
that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-P_{\gamma}}}\right)\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)f=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-P_{\gamma}}}\right)\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)Q_{\gamma}f\,\,\text{.}

Furthermore,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-P_{\gamma}}}\right)\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)Q_{\gamma}f}}\right|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\leq\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)Q_{\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f}}\right|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\qquad\forall x\in X\,\,\text{.}

Thus

\displaystyle\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-P_{\gamma}}}\right)\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)f\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\sum_{\gamma\in F}\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)Q_{\gamma}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f}}\right|\|\leq\|\sum_{\gamma}\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)Q_{\gamma}\|

∎

Allowing
M>1
is essential for our use of the above lemma in this paper. The parameter
M
, which controls the overlap, will be bounded in terms of the dimension of the ambient space, while the set
F
will be allowed to be as large as required.

In the next subsection, we prove a general transversality result for linear actions. When we will apply Lemma
2.1
in the proof of Theorem
1.1
we will do so with the using neighborhoods of the projective subspaces from the families
\mathcal{F}^{\pm}
constructed below.

2.1.
Weak general position

Let
V
be a finite dimensional vector space over a field
k
. In this subsection

is an arbitrary field. A subspace of
V
is called non-trivial if it is neither
V
nor
\{0\}
. A family
\mathcal{F}
of non-trivial subspaces of

is said to be in

if for every subfamily
I

\codim\bigcap_{W\in I}W\geq\min\{\dim V,|I|\}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Given integers
d>1,r>0
there is an integer
M=M(d,r)
such that for every field
k
, every subset
S\subset\GL_{d}(k)
with
1\in S
the following holds. If
\Gamma
denotes the connected component of the Zariski-closure of the subgroup generated by
S
, and if
H^{+},H^{-}\leq k^{d}
is a pair of non-trivial subspaces that do not contain a non-zero
\Gamma
-invariant subspace, there are
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\in S^{M}
such that each family
\mathcal{F}^{+}:=\{g_{1}H^{+},\ldots,g_{r}H^{+}\}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-}:=\{g_{1}H^{-},\ldots,g_{r}H^{-}\}
is made of

distinct subspaces in weak general position.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on

. When

we can set
g_{1}=1
. Assume that the lemma holds for
r
and we have found
g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}
. For each

, let

be the intersection of the
g_{i}H^{+}
for

, and define
H^{-}_{I}
similarly.
If
H^{+}_{I}\neq 0
, then
|I|<d
and we may pick a non-zero
v^{+}_{I}\in H^{+}_{I}
. Similarly for
H_{I}^{-}
. Now
\cup_{I,H^{+}_{I}\neq 0}\{g\in\GL_{d}\mid g^{-1}v^{+}_{I}\in H^{+}\}
is an algebraic subvariety

of

whose degree is bounded in terms of

and

only. We similarly define

. But

is not entirely contained in

would be contained in

there is

and

such that

for each

and sign

.
This means that

and that

∎

3.
Escape from subvarieties

In this section we present a new proof of the
escape from subvarieties lemma
of Eskin-Mozes-Oh
[
35
, Proposition 3.2]
. This new proof gives much improved, indeed polynomial bounds in the degree aspect. We also discuss generation of Zariski-dense subgroups in semisimple algebraic groups and how to control the size of a generating set (Lemma
3.4
and Proposition
3.3
).

In order to state the escape lemma, we recall the notion of degree of a subvariety.

Degree of an algebraic subvariety.
Let
K
be an algebraically closed field.

Given a linear algebraic
K
-group
\mathbb{G}
, we view it as a closed subvariety of
\GL_{N}
for some
N
. It is convenivent to view
\GL_{N}
as a closed subvariety of some affine space, so we embed
\GL_{N}
in
\SL_{N+1}
in the standard block diagonal way. This way
\mathbb{G}
embeds is
\mathbb{A}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N+1}}\right)^{2}}
. Viewing this affine space as an open set in
\mathbb{P}^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N+1}}\right)^{2}}
allows us to consider projective closures of subvarieties of
\mathbb{G}
,
and gives rise to the notion of their projective degrees. We define
the degree of an irreducible subvariety
V
of projective space in
the standard way as the cardinality of the intersection of
V
with
a general linear subspace of complementary dimension. In general, we define the degree of a subvariety to be the sum of the degrees of its irreducible components.
If
V
is a hypersurface in projective space defined by a homogeneous polynomial
f
, then
\deg(V)\leq\deg(f)
with equality if
f
is irreducible.

Lemma 3.1

(Escape lemma)
.

For all integers
d
and
N\geq 3
there is
C_{d,N}\leq N^{d^{2}}
such that if
V
is a closed subvariety of
\GL_{d}
of degree at most
N
and
S\subset\GL_{d}(K)
is a finite set generating a subgroup not entirely contained in
V
, then there is
k\in[0,C_{d,N}]
, such that
S^{k}
is not entirely contained in
V
.

The proof is based on the following lemma. Let
M(d,m)
be the dimension of the space
K_{\leq m}[V]
of polynomial functions on
V
with degree at most
m\geq 3
, namely
M(d,m)={m+d\choose d}\leq m^{d}+1.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\GL\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U}}\right)
be a finite
set with
1\in\Sigma
, where
U
is a
K
-vector space of dimension

d
. Let
u\in U
and
f
a polynomial function of degree at most

m
on
U
. Assume that
f
does not vanish identically on the

Proposition 3.3

(coset escape)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a connected semisimple algebraic group over
K
, and let

be a finite subset generating a Zariski-dense subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
. Then there is

.

We note that this improves
[

, Lemma 3.5]
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Before we delve into the proof, we note that it is possible to find infinite groups with a finite generating set
S
such that
S^{k}S^{-k}
is contained in a finite subgroup for all
k
. Consider for instance the lamplighter group
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}=\{((u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\sigma^{i})\mid i\in\mathbb{Z},u_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\}
, where
\sigma
is the shift and
S=\{(\delta_{0},\sigma)\}
.
Therefore, the proof of the lemma must exploit some special features of Zariski-dense subgroups of semisimple algebraic groups.
We shall see that the finiteness of the groups
\langle S^{k}S^{-k}\rangle
is precisely the obstruction to the conclusion of the lemma, and that this obstruction does not occur when
S
is as in the hypothesis of the lemma.

Let
H_{k}
be the Zariski closure of the subgroup generated by
S^{k}S^{-k}
. First observe that
1\in SS^{-1}
and hence
S^{k}S^{-k}\subset S^{k+1}S^{-(k+1)}
. So
H_{k}\leq H_{k+1}
. Also, given
s\in S
and
i\leq k
,
S^{i}\subset H_{k}s^{i}\subset H_{k}\langle s\rangle
.

We will show, by induction on
d=\dim\mathbb{G}
, that
H_{k}=\mathbb{G}
when
k
is larger than some integer
C=C(d)\geq C(d-1)
to be determined.

We shall soon see that there is
B=B(d)
depending on
d
only such that
H_{k}
is infinite for all
k\geq B
.
Setting
C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right),C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d-1}}\right)}}\right\}+d
,
there must be
k\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)-d,C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)}}\right]

such that
\dim H_{k}=\dim H_{k+1}
and
H_{k}^{0}
is of positive
dimension. Then
sH_{k}^{0}s^{-1}\subset H_{k+1}^{0}=H_{k}^{0}
and
so
sH_{k}^{0}s^{-1}=H_{k}^{0}
for all
s\in S
, and thus
H_{k}^{0}

is a normal subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
. By the induction hypothesis,
H_{k}

surjects onto
\mathbb{G}/H_{k}^{0}
and thus
H_{k}=\mathbb{G}
.

Now, assume by way of contradiction that
H_{k}
is finite. We will derive a contradiction
provided
k\geq B
,
B=B\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d}}\right)
to be chosen later. Projecting to a simple factor of
\mathbb{G}
we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{G}
is simple and without center. The algebraic group
\mathbb{G}
embeds into
\GL_{n_{d}}
for some

depending only on
d
and we may also assume that it acts irreducibly on
K^{n_{d}}
.

The argument now differs according to the characteristic of the field. Suppose first that we are in characteristic zero.
Jordan’s theorem says that there is an abelian subgroup
A_{k}
of
H_{k}
such that
[H_{k}:A_{k}]\leq J
, where
J=J(n_{d})
is Jordan’s constant. But
A_{k}
is made of semisimple elements, so it is contained in a maximal torus
T_{k}
of
\mathbb{G}
. Fix
s\in S
, write
s=s_{s}s_{u}
for the Jordan decomposition, and find a maximal torus
T
containing
s_{s}
and a one-dimensional unipotent subgroup

containing
s_{u}
.

So we obtain that for all

,

|X_{k}|\leq J
. All maximal tori are conjugate. Hence the Zariski closures of

and

are varieties whose degree is bounded independently of

N
. They also are proper subvarieties for dimension reasons (as
\dim\mathbb{G}>2\dim T+1
unless
\mathbb{G}
has rank
1
, but in that case either

is central or

is trivial). However this contradicts Lemma
3.1
when

is large enough, say

.

Now assume that the field has positive characteristic. Let
Z_{k}
be the centralizer of

in
\mathbb{G}
. If
z\in Z_{k}
, then

the centralizer of

in
\mathbb{G}
. Equivalently,

belongs to the closed subvariety

only and is proper if and only if

(since
\mathbb{G}
has no center). Lemma
3.1
applies and we find

such that

is not entirely contained in

for some

. This means that
Z_{k}
is trivial. In particular
Z_{k}
is trivial for all
k\geq C_{2}
. Now if

is finite and if there is a basis of

such that the elements of
H_{k}
have their matrix coefficients in the algebraic closure

of the prime field of

, then there is a power

of the Frobenius automorphism

which fixes
H_{k}
pointwise. In particular, as
sH_{k-1}s^{-1}\leq H_{k}
for each

, we get that

for all

. This implies that

is the subfield of

It remains to justify why we may find such a basis. If

acts irreducibly on

. See for example

where it is noted that as

is finite, eigenvalues and traces of its elements belong to

. For the same reason, this holds if

is completely reducible. Let us show that

is completely reducible for some

with

. The smallest dimension of a non-zero

. Suppose that it remains constant on an interval of size at least

. This must happen for some

with

. Then if

is a non-zero

is

-invariant of minimal dimension. This implies that the subspaces

,

, …,

are in direct sum until

lies entirely in the direct sum

of the pevious

. But then

is invariant under

and under

, hence under

, since

and

together generate a Zariski-dense subgroup of

by our assumption. As

is irreducible,

and

Lemma 3.4

(size reduction)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Zariski-connected semisimple algebraic group defined over a field

. Let
S\subset\mathbb{G}(K)
be a finite subset generating a Zariski-dense subgroup. Then there is

with
|S^{\prime}|\leq 2\dim\mathbb{G}-1
that already generates a Zariski-dense subgroup.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\mathbb{G}
is simple, for if
\mathbb{G}=G_{1}\ldots G_{k}
are the simple factors of
\mathbb{G}
and one finds
S_{i}\subset S
of size at most
\dim G_{i}
whose projection to
G_{i}
generates a Zariski-dense subgroup, then the union of the
S_{i}
has the appropriate size and generates a Zariski-dense subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
. Note that
\mathbb{G}
embeds in
\SL_{d}
for some
d\leq\dim\mathbb{G}
, for example via the adjoint representation. By Lemma
3.5
below we may thus find a subset
S^{\prime}\subset S
with
|S^{\prime}|\leq\dim\mathbb{G}
that generates an infinite subgroup. The connected component
H
of its Zariski closure is positive dimensional and unless

already, the normalizer of

is a proper closed subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
, because
\mathbb{G}
is simple. Therefore some

lies outside this normalizer. This means that the Zariski closure of
\langle H,s\rangle
has dimension strictly higher than that of

. We may continue this way at most
\dim\mathbb{G}-\dim H
times until we reach all of
\mathbb{G}
. This adds at most
\dim\mathbb{G}-1
elements to

. The result follows.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
K
a field. Suppose

is a finite set that generates an infinite subgroup. Then there is
S^{\prime}\subset S
with
|S^{\prime}|\leq d
, that generates an infinite subgroup.

Proof.

We may assume that
K
is finitely generated over its prime field and embed

in a local field

in such a way

becomes unbounded in
\GL_{d}(k)
. Without loss of generality we may assume that every element of

is torsion, so that
\det(s)
is a root of unity for each

. Then the image

of
\langle S\rangle
in

must also be unbounded. Discarding as many elements of

as necessary, we may further assume that

is finite for each

. In this situation we now need to show that
|S|\leq d
. By way of contradiction imagine that
|S|>d
. Then every subset of
d
elements in

would generate a finite subgroup and therefore would have a common fixed point in the Bruhat-Tits building
X_{d}
of
\PGL_{d}(k)
. This means that the family of convex sets

,

, of the fixed point sets of each

in

has the property that any

of them intersect non-trivially. But

is a

-dimensional contractible simplicial complex and as such is

[

,

]
and so this would force the

.

Lemma 3.6

Let

be an algebraically closed field and

generating a subgroup that acts strongly irreducibly on

. Then there is

of size at most

Let
\mathbb{G}
be the Zariski-closure of
G:=\langle S\rangle
. To say that
G\leq\SL_{d}
is strongly irreducible is equivalent to say that
\mathbb{G}^{0}
is semisimple and acts irreducibly on
V=K^{d}
. As is well-known (e.g.
[
69
]
) there is a finite subgroup
F\leq\mathbb{G}
such that
\mathbb{G}=F\mathbb{G}^{0}
. By Lemma
3.5
we find
S^{\prime}\subset S
of size at most
d
that generates an infinite subgroup. Proceeding as before, we may add elements
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
of
S
to
S^{\prime}
one by one until the connected component
H
of the Zariski-closure of
\langle S^{\prime},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\rangle
is normal in
\mathbb{G}
. This happens first with some
n\leq\dim H
. This means
H
is a product of some simple factors of
\mathbb{G}^{0}
and that
\mathbb{G}
permutes those simple factors. It must then permute the remaining factors as well. They thus form a normal subgroup
H_{2}\leq\mathbb{G}
. Note then that
\mathbb{G}=FH_{2}H
and
\mathbb{G}/H
is a central quotient of

. In particular
\mathbb{G}/H
embeds in
\PGL_{d}(V)
. Note that Lemma
3.5
also holds for subsets of
\PGL_{d}(K)
with the same argument. This means that unless

, we may find
d
elements in
S
that generate an infinite group modulo

. We may then repeat this process as many times as needed until we reach

. The total number of elements needed will be at most
dk+\dim\mathbb{G}
, where

is the number of times we had to repeat the procedure, which is at most the number of simple factors of
\mathbb{G}
, that is

(comparing ranks). The requested bound follows. Further note that the Zariski-closure of
\langle S^{\prime}\rangle
has
\mathbb{G}^{0}
as connected component of the identity, the same as the original

.
∎

4.
Local considerations

In this section, we gather some preliminary considerations regarding projective transformations and their dynamics on projective space over a local field.

4.1.
Orthogonality over local fields

Let
k
be a local field. We endow
k^{d}
with its standard norm,
i.e. the Euclidean or Hermitian norm when
k=\mathbb{R}
or
k=\mathbb{C}
, respectively,
and the supremum norm
\|x\|=\max_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|x_{i}}}\right|
when
k

is non-archimedean. In the latter case, we let
\mathcal{O}
be the ring
of integers,
\mathfrak{m}
its maximal ideal, and we set
V_{\mathcal{O}}=V\cap\mathcal{O}^{d}

for a vector subspace
V\leq k^{d}
.

For a non-archimedean local field
k
, we have a bijection

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\text{ linear subspaces of $k^{d}$ }}}\right\}\leftrightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\text{ direct summands of $\mathcal{O}^{d}$ }}}\right\}

given by
W\mapsto W_{\mathcal{O}}
and
M\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}k\mapsfrom M

(this follows from the structure theorem for finitely generated modules
over a PID). We say that subspaces
U,W
of
k^{d}
are
orthogonal

if
U\cap W=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0}}\right\}
(equivalently,
U_{\mathcal{O}}\cap W_{\mathcal{O}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0}}\right\}
)
and one (hence all) of the following conditions holds:

(1)

U_{\mathcal{O}}\oplus W_{\mathcal{O}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U+W}}\right)_{\mathcal{O}}
.

(2)

For all
u\in U
and
w\in W
, if
u+w\in\mathcal{O}^{d}
then
u,w\in\mathcal{O}^{d}
.

(3)

U_{\mathcal{O}}\oplus W_{\mathcal{O}}
is a direct summand of
\mathcal{O}^{d}
.

Note that if
U
and
W
are orthogonal then
U^{\prime}
and
W^{\prime}
are
orthogonal for all subspaces
U^{\prime}\subset U
and
W^{\prime}\subset W
. For
a subspace
U
of
k^{d}
, the natural map
U_{\mathcal{O}}/\mathfrak{m}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U+W}}\right)_{\mathcal{O}}/\mathfrak{m}

is injective, and an application of Nakayama’s lemma shows that
\rank_{\mathcal{O}}U_{\mathcal{O}}=\rank_{\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{m}}U_{\mathcal{O}}/\mathfrak{m}
,
and that subspaces
U
and
W
of
k^{d}
are orthogonal if and
only if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U+W}}\right)_{\mathcal{O}}/\mathfrak{m}=U_{\mathcal{O}}/\mathfrak{m}\oplus W_{\mathcal{O}}/\mathfrak{m}
.

More abstractly, a pair
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,L}}\right)
, where
V
is a finite-dimensional

k
-vector space and
L\subset V
is an
\mathcal{O}
-submodule isomorphic
to
\mathcal{O}^{\dim_{k}V}
, gives rise to a notion of orthogonality between
subspaces of
V
(as above). If
U
is a linear subspace of
V
,
then
U\cap L\cong\mathcal{O}^{\dim_{k}U}
as
\mathcal{O}
-modules, and subspaces

A,B
of
U
are orthogonal in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U,U\cap L}}\right)
if and
only if they are orthogonal in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,L}}\right)
.

Remark 4.1
.

Note that every subspace
U
of

k^{d}
has an orthogonal complement
W
in
k^{d}
(since
U_{\mathcal{O}}

has a direct complement
M
in
\mathcal{O}^{d}
, and
W=M\otimes_{\mathcal{O}}k

satisfies
W_{\mathcal{O}}=M
). Thus given subspaces
U\subset V
of
k^{d}
, there is an orthogonal
complement
W
for
U
in
V
, and the decomposition
V=U\oplus W

is orthogonal both in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,V\cap\mathcal{O}^{d}}}\right)
and in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k^{d},\mathcal{O}^{d}}}\right)
.

We say that vectors
u,w\in k^{d}
are orthogonal if they are contained
in orthogonal subspaces of
k^{d}
. Equivalently,
u,w
are orthogonal
if
\spn_{k}u
is orthogonal to
\spn_{k}w
.

For a local field
k
and orthogonal
u,w\in k^{d}
, if
k
is archimedean we have

\|u+v\|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|u\|^{2}+\|v\|^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}

while if
k
is non-archimedean, we have

\|u+v\|=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|u\|,\|v\|}}\right\}

Indeed,
\GL_{d}\mathcal{O}
preserves the
sup-norm on
k^{d}
, and there are nonzero scalars
\alpha,\beta\in k
, satisfying

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\alpha}}\right|=\|u\|
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\beta}}\right|=\|v\|
, such
that
\mathcal{O}^{d}
has a basis containing
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\alpha^{-1}u,\beta^{-1}v}}\right\}
.

The norm on
k^{d}
extends naturally to wedge powers
\Lambda^{n}k^{d}
using the standard basis. If
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{d})
is the standard basis of
k^{d}
, the standard basis of
\Lambda^{n}k^{d}
is given by the collection of all

e_{I}:=e_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{i_{n}},

where
I=\{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{n}\}\subset[1,d]
and the norm on
\Lambda^{n}k^{d}
is defined as before, using this basis. It is well-known that for any two pure vectors
\mathbf{v}=v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{n}
and

\mathbf{w}=w_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge w_{m}
we have

(4.1)

\|\mathbf{v}\wedge\mathbf{w}\|\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|\|\mathbf{w}\|

with equality if and only if the subspace
V
(spanned by the
v_{i}
) and
W
(spanned by the
w_{i}
) are orthogonal.

Proof of (
4.1
).

The archimedean case is classical, so we give a proof in the ultrametric case only. If
V
is the subspace corresponding to
\mathbf{v}
and
W
to
\mathbf{w}
, we can assume that
V\cap W
is
0
. Up to scaling
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{w}
we can assume they are tensors of unit norm and we can also replace
\mathbf{v}
by
e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{k}
where the
e_{i}
form a basis of the
\mathcal{O}
-module
V_{\mathcal{O}}
and same with
W
so
\mathbf{w}
is
f_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge f_{l}
for some
\mathcal{O}
-basis
f_{i}
of the
\mathcal{O}
-module
W_{\mathcal{O}}
. Then we are left to show that the norm of the wedge of the
e_{i}
and
f_{j}
is at most
1
. To do this, one can proceed in stages and consider the subspace
V_{k+1}
spanned by
V
and
f_{1}
,
V_{k+2}
the span of
V
and
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, etc. In
V_{k+1}
, complement the
e_{i}
basis by a vector
e_{k+1}
so as to get an
\mathcal{O}
-basis of
(V_{k+1})_{\mathcal{O}}
. Then
f_{1}=\alpha_{1}e_{k+1}+u_{k+1}
, where
u_{k+1}\in V
. One can do the same for
V_{k+2}
and get
e_{k+2}
so that the
e_{i}
for
i\leq k+2
form a
\mathcal{O}
-basis of
(V_{k+2})_{\mathcal{O}}
and
f_{2}=\alpha_{2}e_{k+2}+u_{k+2}
with
u_{k+2}\in V_{k+1}
, etc. Note that
e_{k+1}
is orthogonal to
V
and
e_{k+2}
to
V_{k+1}
, so the norm of
f_{1}
(which is
1
by assumption) is
\max\{|\alpha_{1}|,||u_{k+1}||\}
. In particular
|\alpha_{1}|\leq 1
with equality if and only if
f_{1}
is orthogonal to
V
. Similarly
|\alpha_{2}|\leq 1
with equality if and only if
f_{2}
is orthogonal to
V_{k+1}
, etc. At the end we obtain
\mathbf{v}\wedge\mathbf{w}=(\prod_{i=1}^{l}\alpha_{i})e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{k+l}
and each
|\alpha_{i}|\leq 1
, hence the desired inequality. For the equality case, note that this would mean that all
\alpha_{i}
have absolute value
1
. But by our construction of the
e_{j}
, this is equivalent to
(V+W)_{\mathcal{O}}\leq V_{\mathcal{O}}+W_{\mathcal{O}}
, namely to the orthogonality of
V
and
W
.
∎

4.2.
The projective distance

We will use the following classical notion of distance on the projective space
\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
. For
x,y\in k^{d}\setminus\{0\}
we set:

d(x,y):=\frac{\|x\wedge y\|}{\|x\|\|y\|}.

We note that
d(x,y)
depends only on the lines
kx,ky
. It is well-known (e.g. see
[
12
, 2.8.18]
) that it satisfies the triangle inequality, and thus is a distance on
\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
. We extend this definition to pairs of non-zero subspaces
V,W\leq k^{d}
by setting:

d(V,W):=\|\mathbf{v}\wedge\mathbf{w}\|.

where
\mathbf{v}
(resp.
\mathbf{w}
) are unit vectors denoting the wedge of a basis of
V
(resp.
W
). We set
d(V,W)=1
if one or both of
V
and
W
is zero. Note that
d(V,W)\in[0,1]
. Note further that
d(V,W)=0
if and only if
V\cap W\neq 0
and that
d(V,W)=1
if and only if
V
and
W
are orthogonal.
For
x\in k^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, we shall write
d(x,V)
for
d(kx,V)
.
The function
d
is a reasonable notion of distance between
V
and
W
when they intersect trivially. To account for the case when they do not, we set,

(4.2)

\delta(V,W)=\sup_{V^{\prime}\oplus(V\cap W)=V}d(V^{\prime},W).

We see that
\delta(V,W)>0
for all subspaces
V,W
. Note also that
\delta(V,W)=1
if
V\leq W
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
V
and
W
be linear subspaces of
k^{d}
, and let
U
be
an orthogonal complement to
V\cap W
in
k^{d}
. Then

\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V,W}}\right)=d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap U,W}}\right)=d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap U,W\cap U}}\right)\,\,\text{.}

Proof.

Let
{\bf vu}
,
{\bf vw}
,
{\bf uw}
and
{\bf w}
be pure unit
wedges representing
V\cap U
,
V\cap W
,
U\cap W
and
W
, respectively.
We start with the equality on the right: Since
W=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap W}}\right)\oplus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U\cap W}}\right)

and
V\cap W
is orthogonal to
U
, we have

d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap U,W}}\right)=\|{\bf vu}\wedge{\bf vw}\wedge{\bf uw}\|=\|{\bf vu}\wedge{\bf uw}\|=d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap U,W\cap U}}\right)\,\,\text{.}

We now prove the equality on the left. We have
V=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap U}}\right)\oplus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap W}}\right)
.
Take
V^{\prime}
such that
V=V^{\prime}\oplus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap W}}\right)
, and let
{\bf v}^{\prime}

be a pure unit wedge representing
V^{\prime}
. We need to show that
d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V^{\prime},W}}\right)\leq d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap U,W}}\right)
.
Let
n=d-\dim V\cap W
. The orthogonal decomposition
k^{d}=U\oplus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap W}}\right)

gives rise to an orthogonal decomposition

\Lambda^{n}k^{d}=\Lambda^{n}U\oplus\underbrace{\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\Lambda^{n-i}U\wedge\Lambda^{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap W}}\right)}_{\eqqcolon A}\,\,\text{.}

and we have

{\bf v}^{\prime}=\alpha{\bf vu}+{\bf r}

where
\alpha\in k
and
{\bf r}\in A
(while
{\bf vu}\in\Lambda^{n}U
).
In particular,
{\bf r}
and
{\bf vu}
are orthogonal and
{\bf r}\wedge{\bf w}=0
.
Thus
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\alpha}}\right|\leq 1
since if
k
is archimedean then

\|{\bf v}^{\prime}\|^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\alpha}}\right|^{2}\|{\bf vu}\|^{2}+\|{\bf r}\|^{2},
and
if not then
\|{\bf v}^{\prime}\|=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\alpha}}\right|,\|{\bf r}\|}}\right\}
.
Hence

\displaystyle d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V^{\prime},W}}\right)

\displaystyle=\|{\bf v}^{\prime}\wedge{\bf w}\|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\alpha}}\right|\cdot\|{\bf vu}\wedge{\bf w}\|\leq d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\cap U,W}}\right)\,\,\text{.}

∎

From the definition of
\delta
and Lemma
4.2
, we have the following facts, proved below:

(4.3)

\textnormal{If}\,\,\,\,V\cap W=\{0\}:\,\,\,\delta(V,W)=d(V,W)

(4.4)

\delta(V,W)=\delta(W,V)

(4.5)

\textnormal{If}\,\,V^{\prime}\leq V\textnormal{ is orthogonal to }V\cap W\,:\,\,\,\delta(V^{\prime},W)\geq\delta(V,W)

(4.6)

\textnormal{If}\,\,\,\,x\in k^{d}\setminus\{0\}:\,\,\,d(kx,W)=\inf_{y\in W\setminus\{0\}}d(x,y)

Facts (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) are immediate from the definition and Lemma
4.2
, respectively.
In light of Lemma
4.2
, to prove (
4.5
) it suffices to prove that
d(V^{\prime},W)\geq d(V,W)
: Let
U
be an orthogonal complement to
V^{\prime}
in
V
, and let
{\bf v^{\prime}}
,
{\bf u}
and
{\bf w}
be pure wedges of unit norm corresponding to
V^{\prime}
,
U
and
W
, respectively. Then
{\bf v}\coloneqq{\bf v^{\prime}}\wedge{\bf u}
is a pure wedge of unit norm corresponding to
V
, and
d(V,W)=\|{\bf v}\wedge{\bf w}\|=\|{\bf v^{\prime}}\wedge{\bf u}\wedge{\bf w}\|\leq\|{\bf v^{\prime}}\wedge{\bf w}\|=d(V^{\prime},W)
.
Finally, to prove
(
4.6
)
, note first that
d(kx,W)\leq d(x,y)
for all
y\in W

as we have just shown.
To see that equality is realized for some
y\in W\setminus\{0\}
, pick an orthogonal complement
W^{\prime}
to
W
in
k^{d}
and write
x=w^{\prime}+w
,
w^{\prime}\in W^{\prime}
,
w\in W
. If
w=0
we are done.
Otherwise,
d(x,w)=d(kx,W)
since
\|x\wedge\mathbf{w}\|=\|w^{\prime}\wedge\mathbf{w}\|=\|w^{\prime}\|=\|x\wedge w\|/\|w\|
.

We let
C_{k}=1
if
k
is ultrametric and
C_{k}=d
if
k
is archimedean. The following lemma will be essential. It formulates quantitatively the intuition that if a point is close to each subspace in a given family of subspaces, then it must be close to their intersection. The fact that the multiplicative constant
C_{k}
is equal to
1
in the non-archemdean case will also be essential when we upgrade this inequality to the adelic setting in the next section. And so will be the explicit form of the multiplicative constant as a power of
\delta
in the lower bound.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}
be subspaces in
k^{d}
and
x\in k^{d}
. Then

d(x,V_{1}\cap\ldots\cap V_{n})\cdot\delta^{\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil}\leq C_{k}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\{d(x,V_{i})\}

where
\delta=\inf_{I,J}\delta(V_{I},V_{J})
, where
I,J\subset[r]
and
V_{I}:=\cap_{i\in I}V_{i}
.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that
\|x\|=1
.
We shall prove by induction on
n
the same statement with
C_{k}
replaced by
C^{\prime}_{k}(n)=n
when
k
is archimedean and
C^{\prime}_{k}(n)=1
otherwise. Clearly this implies the result, because the intersection of
n
proper subspaces coincides with that of at most
d
of them. So we now set out to prove the statement with
C^{\prime}_{k}(n)
for all
n
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
n
is a power of
2
; simply add as many copies of the same subspace, say
V_{n}
, as required. Suppose now that we have proved the statement for
n=2
and for
n=2^{m}
and let us prove it for
2n=2^{m+1}
. Set
W_{1}
the intersection of the
V_{i}
for
i\leq n
and
W_{2}
the intersection of the remaining
V_{i}
. By the
n=2
case, we have:

d(x,V_{1}\cap\ldots\cap V_{2n})\cdot\delta(W_{1},W_{2})\leq C^{\prime}_{k}(2)\max\{d(x,W_{1}),d(x,W_{2})\}.

Now by induction hypothesis, we have

d(x,W_{1})\delta_{1}^{m}\leq C^{\prime}_{k}(n)\max_{i\leq n}\{d(x,V_{i})\}

and

d(x,W_{2})\delta_{2}^{m}\leq C^{\prime}_{k}(n)\max_{n<i\leq 2n}\{d(x,V_{i})\}

where
\delta_{1}=\inf_{I,J\subset[n]}\delta_{I,J}
,
\delta_{2}=\inf_{I,J\subset[2n]\setminus[n]}\delta_{I,J}
, and
\delta_{I,J}:=\delta(V_{I},V_{J})
. Note that
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
and
\delta(W_{1},W_{2})
are
\geq\delta
. Since
C^{\prime}_{k}(2n)=C^{\prime}_{k}(2)C^{\prime}_{k}(n)
, combining the above inequalities yields the result.

It remains to prove the statement for
n=2
. We give the proof in
the case when
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
do not intersect trivially, and
leave the entirely analogous case when they do to the reader. Choose
subspaces
V_{1}^{\prime}
and
V_{2}^{\prime}
of
k^{d}
giving orthogonal decompositions

V_{1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{1}\cap V_{2}}}\right)\oplus V_{1}^{\prime}
and
V_{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{1}\cap V_{2}}}\right)\oplus V_{2}^{\prime}
.
Assume first that
x\in V_{1}+V_{2}
, and write
x=x_{1}+x_{12}+x_{2}
,
where
x_{i}\in V_{i}^{\prime}
and
x_{12}\in V_{12}
. Then

d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,V_{12}}}\right)=\|x_{1}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}+x_{2}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}\|\leq\begin{cases}\|x_{1}\|+\|x_{2}\|&\text{$k$ archimedean}\\
\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|x_{1}\|,\|x_{2}\|}}\right\}&\text{$k$ non-archimedean}\end{cases}

But

d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,V_{1}}}\right)=\|x\wedge{\bf v}_{1}\|=\|x_{2}\wedge{\bf v}_{1}\|=\|x_{2}\|d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{2},V_{1}}}\right)\geq\|x_{2}\|\delta

by (
4.5
), and similarly,

d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,V_{2}}}\right)=\|x\wedge v_{2}\|\geq\|x_{1}\|\delta

and the result follows. Finally, assume that
x\notin V_{1}+V_{2}
,
choose an orthogonal complement
Y
for
V_{1}+V_{2}
in
k^{d}
,
and write
x=x_{v}+x_{y}
, where
x_{v}\in V_{1}+V_{2}
and
x_{y}\in Y
.
We have just shown that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\delta\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}\|\leq\begin{cases}\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{1}\|+\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{2}\|&\text{$k$ archimedean}\\
\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{1}\|,\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{2}\|}}\right\}&\text{$k$ non-archimedean}\end{cases}\,\,\text{.}

On the other hand,
t\coloneqq\|x_{y}\|=\|x_{y}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}\|=\|x_{y}\wedge{\bf v}_{1}\|=\|x_{y}\wedge{\bf v}_{2}\|
.
If
k
is archimedean then

\displaystyle d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,V_{12}}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}\|^{2}+\|x_{y}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}\|^{2}=\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}\|^{2}+t^{2}

since
x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}
and
x_{y}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}
are
orthogonal, as is evident from the orthogonal decomposition
\Lambda^{\ell}k^{d}=\bigoplus_{i}\Lambda^{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{1}+V_{2}}}\right)\wedge\Lambda^{\ell-i}Y

for
\ell=1+\dim_{k}V_{1}\cap V_{2}
. Similarly,
d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,V_{1}}}\right)^{2}=\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{1}\|^{2}+t^{2}

and
d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,V_{2}}}\right)^{2}=\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{2}\|^{2}+t^{2}
.
Thus, noting that
\delta\leq 1
and combining (
4.7
) with Minkowski’s
inequality yields:

\displaystyle\delta d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,V_{12}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}\|^{2}+t^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\delta^{2}\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}\|^{2}+t^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{1}\|+\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{2}\|}}\right)^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t+t}}\right)^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{1}\|^{2}+t^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{2}\|^{2}+t^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,V_{1}}}\right)+d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,V_{2}}}\right)

as required. If
k
is non-archimedean then
d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,V_{\alpha}}}\right)=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{\alpha}\|,\|x_{y}\|}}\right\}

for
\alpha\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2,12}}\right\}
, while
\delta\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{12}\|\leq\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{1}\|,\|x_{v}\wedge{\bf v}_{2}\|}}\right\}

and the result follows (noting again that
\delta\leq 1
).

∎

4.3.
Dynamics of projective transformations

In order to construct the finite set of ping-pong players appearing in the “ping-pong with overlaps lemma”, Lemma
2.1
, it is important to get very good control of the dynamics of powers of projective transformations on projective space. It will be essential to do this in a way that is uniform with respect to the local field. It will also be essential that the constants appearing in the calculations disappear when we deal with ultrametric local fields. Our aim here is the following result.

Let
k
be a local field and
\gamma\in\GL_{d}(k)
. For
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
we let
A_{\gamma}\leq k^{d}
(resp.
R_{\gamma}\leq k^{d}
) be the sum of the generalized eigenspaces of
\gamma
with eigenvalues of modulus
\geq\omega
(resp.
<\omega
). Note that although generalized eigenspaces are only defined over a finite extension of
k
, the subspaces
A_{\gamma},R_{\gamma}
are indeed defined over
k
as they are stable under Galois.
Write
\Lambda_{\omega}(\gamma)
and
\lambda_{\omega}(\gamma)
for the moduli of the smallest eigenvalue
\geq\omega
of
\gamma
and largest eigenvalue
<\omega
of
\gamma
, respectively
(so
\lambda_{\omega}(\gamma)<\omega\leq\Lambda_{\omega}(\gamma)
).
The operator norm is denoted by
\|\gamma\|
, while the largest modulus of an eigenvalue of
\gamma
is denoted by
\Lambda(\gamma)
. As before, we let
C_{k}=1
if
k
is ultrametric and
C_{k}=d
if
k
is archimedian.

Lemma 4.4
.

For every
n\geq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
we have

d(\gamma^{n}x,A_{\gamma})\cdot d(x,R_{\gamma})\cdot d(A_{\gamma},R_{\gamma})^{2}\leq(\|\gamma\|\cdot\|\gamma^{-1}\|)^{d-2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[C_{k}^{2}\frac{\lambda_{\omega}(\gamma)}{\Lambda_{\omega}(\gamma)}}}\right]^{n}\,\,.

A version of this lemma appears in
[
21
, Lemma 4.6]
. We shall now provide a complete proof for convenience.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\gamma\in\GL_{d}(k)
. There is
h\in\SL_{d}(k^{al})
such that
\|h\gamma h^{-1}\|\leq C_{k}\Lambda(\gamma)
and
\max\{\|h\|,\|h^{-1}\|\}\leq(\|\gamma\|/\Lambda(\gamma))^{\frac{d-1}{2}}
.

Proof.

This is
[
21
, Lemma 4.9]
. We include the proof here for completeness. First we claim that if
\lambda\in k^{al}
an eigenvalue of
\gamma
, there is some
h_{0}\in\SL_{d}(k^{al})
such that
\|h_{0}\|=\|h_{0}^{-1}\|=1
and
h_{0}\gamma h_{0}^{-1}
is lower triangular with top left entry equal to
\lambda
. To see this, consider a full flag
\mathcal{F}
in
(k^{al})^{d}
fixed by
\gamma^{T}
, the transpose of
\gamma
. We can choose
\mathcal{F}
so that it starts with the line
k^{al}v,
where
v
is an eigenvector of
\gamma^{T}
with eigenvalue
\lambda.
Full flags are conjugate under
\GL_{d}(k^{al})
, so
\mathcal{F}=g\mathcal{F}_{0}
for some
g\in\GL(k^{al})
with
ge_{1}=v
, where
\mathcal{F}_{0}
is the flag generated by the canonical basis of
(k^{al})^{d}
. The Iwasawa decomposition reads
\GL_{d}(k^{al})=\mathbb{K}_{k^{al}}B_{0}
where
B_{0}
is the Borel stabilizing

\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
\mathbb{K}_{k^{al}}:=\{g\in\GL_{d}(k^{al}),\|g\|=\|g^{-1}\|=1\}
. So we may assume that
g\in\mathbb{K}_{k^{al}}
and take
h_{0}=\alpha g^{T}
for a suitable unit
\alpha\in k^{al}
with
|\alpha|=1
chosen so that
\det h_{0}=1
.
Now note that

\max_{i,j}|a_{ij}|\leq\|a\|\leq C_{k}\max_{i,j}|a_{ij}|
for every
a\in\GL_{d}(k^{al})
. If we let
h_{1}:=t^{\frac{d+1}{2}}diag(t^{-1},...,t^{-d})\in SL_{d}(k^{al}),
where
t\in k^{al}
is such that
|t|_{k}\Lambda(\gamma)=\|h_{0}\gamma h_{0}^{-1}\|=\|\gamma\|,
then
\max\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|h_{1}}}\right\|,\|h_{1}^{-1}\|\}=|t|_{k}^{\frac{d-1}{2}}=(\|\gamma\|/\Lambda(\gamma))^{\frac{d-1}{2}}
and the
coefficients of
h_{1}h_{0}\gamma h_{0}^{-1}h_{1}^{-1}
are of modulus
\leq\Lambda(\gamma).
So setting
h=h_{1}h_{0}
, we are done.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Write
d_{a}=\dim A_{\gamma}
and
d_{r}=\dim R_{\gamma}
. We may
decompose
x\in k^{d}\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0}}\right\}
as
x=x_{A}+x_{R}

according to the direct sum
k^{d}=A_{\gamma}\oplus R_{\gamma}
.
The statement follows by multiplying the following inequalities, which are proved below:

(4.8)

\displaystyle d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma^{n}x,A_{\gamma}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|x_{R}\|}{\|\gamma^{n}x\|}(\|\gamma\|\cdot\|\gamma^{-1}\|)^{d_{r}-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{k}\lambda_{\omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}}\right)^{n}

(4.9)

\displaystyle d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{\gamma},R_{\gamma}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\gamma^{n}x\|}{\|\gamma^{n}x_{A}\|}

(4.10)

\displaystyle d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{\gamma},R_{\gamma}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|x\|}{\|x_{R}\|}

(4.11)

\displaystyle d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,R_{\gamma}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\gamma^{n}x_{A}\|}{\|x\|}(\|\gamma\|\cdot\|\gamma^{-1}\|)^{d_{a}-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{k}\Lambda_{\omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)^{-1}}}\right)^{n}

Let
{\bf a}
(resp.
{\bf r}
) be a unit pure wedge vector representing

A_{\gamma}
(resp.
R_{\gamma}
). Then

\displaystyle d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma^{n}x,A_{\gamma}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\gamma^{n}x\wedge{\bf a}\|}{\|\gamma^{n}x\|}=\frac{\|\gamma^{n}x_{R}\wedge{\bf a}\|}{\|\gamma^{n}x\|}\leq\frac{\|\gamma^{n}x_{R}\|}{\|\gamma^{n}x\|}

and, using (
4.5
) in the last inequality,

d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma^{n}x,R_{\gamma}}}\right)=\frac{\|\gamma^{n}x\wedge{\bf r}\|}{\|\gamma^{n}x\|}=\frac{\|\gamma^{n}x_{A}\wedge{\bf r}\|}{\|\gamma^{n}x\|}\geq d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{\gamma},R_{\gamma}}}\right)\frac{\|\gamma^{n}x_{A}\|}{\|\gamma^{n}x\|}

so (
4.9
) follows. By applying Lemma
4.5
to
\gamma
on
R_{\gamma}
and noting
that
\Lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma_{\mid R_{\gamma}}}}\right)=\lambda_{\omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)
,

\|\gamma^{n}x_{R}\|\leq\|h^{-1}\|\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h\gamma h^{-1}}}\right)^{n}\|\|hx_{R}\|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\|\gamma{}_{\mid R_{\gamma}}\|}{\Lambda(\gamma{}_{\mid R_{\gamma}})}}}\right)^{d_{r}-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{k}\lambda_{\omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)}}\right)^{n}\|x_{R}\|

and (
4.8
) follows. By applying the same lemma to

\gamma^{-1}
on
A_{\gamma}
and noting that
\Lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma_{\mid A_{\gamma}}^{-1}}}\right)=\Lambda_{\omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)^{-1}
,

\|x_{A}\|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\|\gamma_{\mid A_{\gamma}}^{-1}\|}{\Lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma_{\mid A_{\gamma}}^{-1}}}\right)}}}\right)^{d_{a}-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{k}\Lambda_{\omega}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\gamma}}\right)^{-1}}}\right)^{n}\|\gamma^{n}x_{A}\|\,\,,

and (
4.11
) follows since
d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,R_{\gamma}}}\right)\leq\frac{\|x_{A}\|}{\|x\|}
.
Finally, by (
4.5
),
d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{\gamma},R_{\gamma}}}\right)\leq d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A_{\gamma},x_{R}}}\right)=\frac{\|{\bf a}\wedge x_{R}\|}{\|x_{R}\|}=\frac{\|{\bf a}\wedge x\|}{\|x_{R}\|}\leq\frac{\|x\|}{\|x_{R}\|}
,
and (
4.10
) follows.
∎

4.4.
Local conditions for ping-pong

We keep the notation of the previous paragraph, in particular
C_{k}=d
if
k
is archimedian and
1
otherwise. For
\gamma\in\GL_{d}(k)
we write

\alpha_{\omega}(\gamma)\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{\omega}(\gamma)}{\Lambda_{\omega}(\gamma)}\in(0,1),

where, as before,
\lambda_{\omega}(\gamma)
and
\Lambda_{\omega}(\gamma)
are modulus of the eigenvalues of
\gamma
on either side of
\omega>0
. The goal of this subsection is to prove:

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\gamma,g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\in GL_{d}(k)
and
\gamma_{i}:=g_{i}\gamma g_{i}^{-1}
. Assume that each family
\{g_{1}A_{\gamma},\ldots,g_{r}A_{\gamma}\}
and
\{g_{1}R_{\gamma},\ldots,g_{r}R_{\gamma}\}
is made of
r
distinct subspaces in weak general position. Let

\delta:=\min_{I,J\subset[r]}\{\delta(A_{\gamma},R_{\gamma}),\delta(A^{I}_{\gamma},A^{J}_{\gamma}),\delta(R^{I}_{\gamma},R^{J}_{\gamma})\},

\Delta:=\max_{i\leq r}\{\|\gamma\|,\|\gamma^{-1}\|,\|g_{i}\|,\|g_{i}^{-1}\|\}^{2},

where
A^{I}_{\gamma}=\cap_{i\in I}g_{i}A_{\gamma}
and
R^{I}_{\gamma}=\cap_{i\in I}g_{i}R_{\gamma}
.
Then for each
n\geq 1
,
F:=\{\gamma_{1}^{n},\ldots,\gamma_{r}^{n}\}
is in
d
-ping-pong position on
X=\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
, provided

(4.12)

(C_{k}^{2}\alpha_{\omega}(\gamma))^{n}<\frac{\delta^{2+2\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil}}{C_{k}^{2}\Delta^{3(d-2)}}.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and let
\mathbf{A}_{\gamma_{i}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{P}(k^{d})\mid d(x,A_{\gamma_{i}})\leq\varepsilon\}
and similarly
\mathbf{R}_{\gamma_{i}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{P}(k^{d})\mid d(x,R_{\gamma_{i}})\leq\varepsilon\}
, where
A_{\gamma_{i}}:=g_{i}A_{\gamma}
and
R_{\gamma_{i}}:=g_{i}R_{\gamma}
. We claim that if
\varepsilon<\delta^{\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil}/C_{k}
, then condition
(ii)
from the Section
2
is satisfied with
M=d
(even
d-1
). Indeed, imagine that
x\in\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
belongs to
\mathbf{R}_{\gamma_{i}}
for at least
d
indices
i
, say for
i=1,\ldots,d
. By the weak general position assumption, the intersection of the
R_{\gamma_{i}}
is trivial, and hence in view of Lemma
4.3
, we obtain
\delta^{\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil}\leq C_{k}\varepsilon
, a contradiction. The same holds with
A_{\gamma_{i}}
in place of
R_{\gamma_{i}}
. We now check condition
(i)
. Assume that
x\notin\mathbf{R}_{\gamma_{i}}
, so that
d(x,R_{\gamma_{i}})\geq\varepsilon
and apply Lemma
4.4

d(\gamma_{i}^{n}x,A_{\gamma_{i}})\cdot\varepsilon\delta^{2}\leq(\|\gamma_{i}\|\cdot\|\gamma_{i}^{-1}\|)^{d-2}(C_{k}^{2}\alpha_{\omega})^{n}\leq\Delta^{3(d-2)}(C_{k}^{2}\alpha_{\omega})^{n}.

So
\gamma_{i}^{n}x\in\mathbf{A}_{\gamma_{i}}
provided
(C_{k}^{2}\alpha_{\omega})^{n}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\delta^{2}}{\Delta^{3(d-2)}}
. Now under the assumption of the proposition, there clearly exists a suitable

5.
Heights

Let
K
be a number field. The set of places of
K
will be denoted by
V_{K}
and for
v\in V_{K}
we denote by
K_{v}
the corresponding completion of
K
. If
K_{v}
is
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
, we say that
v
is an infinite place and set
\mathbb{Q}_{v}=\mathbb{R}
, while if
K_{v}
is non-archimedean (i.e. a
p
-adic field) we say that
v
is finite and set
\mathbb{Q}_{v}=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, where
p
is the characteristic of the residue field of
K_{v}
. We let
n_{v}=[K_{v}:\mathbb{Q}_{v}]
, and let
|\cdot|_{v}
be the unique absolute value on
K_{v}
that extends the standard absolute value on
\mathbb{Q}_{v}
. The (absolute) Weil height of
x\in K
is defined as:

h(x)=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}\log^{+}|x|_{v},

where
\log^{+}=\max\{\log,0\}
. The
product formula
asserts that for
x\neq 0

(5.1)

\prod_{v\in V_{K}}|x|_{v}^{n_{v}}=1.

The Weil height is defined for
x\in K
, but one of its appealing features is that its definition is independent of the choice of number field containing
x
. We refer the reader to Bombieri and Gubler’s book
[
12
]
for this and all the background we shall need about heights. In particular we note that for all
x,y\in K
,

h(xy)\leq h(x)+h(y)

and

(5.2)

h(x+y)\leq h(x)+h(y)+\log 2.

5.1.
Height of matrices

On each
K_{v}^{d}
we use the standard norm, namely
\|x\|_{v}=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}|x_{i}|_{v}
if
v
is finite and
\|x\|_{v}^{2}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}|x_{i}|_{v}^{2}
if
v
is infinite. This allows to define a height on
K^{d}
by

h(x)=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}\log^{+}\|x\|_{v}.

On the space of
d
-by-
d
matrices
M_{d}(K)
we define analogously:

h(A)=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}\log^{+}\|A\|_{v}

where
\|A\|_{v}
is the associated operator norm.

It is easy to check that for every place
v\in V_{K}
, we have

\|A\|_{v}=\|A^{T}\|_{v}
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|a_{ij}}}\right|_{v}\leq\|A\|_{v}
for every entry
a_{ij}
of
A
, and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\lambda}}\right|_{v}\leq\|A\|_{v}
for every eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A
.
Therefore
h(A)=h(A^{T})
,
h(a_{ij})\leq h(A)
and
h(\lambda)\leq h(A)
. From this it follows easily that
h(A)=0
if and only if every row and column of
A
has at most one non-zero entry, which needs to be a root of unity. Indeed
h(A)=0
forces
\|A\|_{v}\leq 1
for each place
v
.

For any
A,B\in M_{d}(K)
, by submultiplicativity of the operator norm:

h(AB)\leq h(A)+h(B)

and at each place the triangle (ultrametric for finite
v
) inequality gives

(5.3)

h(A+B)\leq h(A)+h(B)+\log 2.

5.2.
Height of subspaces

When dealing with projective subspaces, it will be convenient to introduce the following variant of the Weil height, the so-called Arakelov height. For a point
x=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1}:\cdots:x_{d}}}\right)\in\mathbb{P}(K^{d})
it is defined as

h_{\Ar}(x)=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}\log\|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d}}}\right)\|_{v},

It is clear from
(\ref{prodform})
that
h_{\Ar}(x)
does not depend on the choice of projective coordinates, so it is well defined on
\mathbb{P}(K^{d})
, does not depend on the choice of
K
, and is non-negative (as we can assume one of the
x_{i}
to be equal to
1
). Now if
W\leq K^{d}
is a vector subspace, we define

h_{\Ar}(W)=h_{\Ar}(\Lambda^{\dim W}W)

where
\Lambda^{\dim W}W
is a line in the vector space
\Lambda^{\dim W}K^{d}
endowed with the canonical basis given by the wedges of vectors from the canonical basis of
K^{d}
. The following is well-known (
[
12
, 2.8.13]
):

Lemma 5.1

(submodularity of Arakelov height)
.

If
W,V\leq K^{d}
are two subspaces, then

h_{\Ar}(V\cap W)+h_{\Ar}(V+W)\leq h_{\Ar}(V)+h_{\Ar}(W).

Proof.

This follows from the local inequality at each place

\|u\|_{v}\|u\wedge v^{\prime}\wedge w^{\prime}\|_{v}\leq\|v\|_{v}\|w\|_{v}

where
V^{\prime}
(resp.
W^{\prime}
) is an orthogonal complement to
U:=V\cap W
in
V
(resp.
W
),
u,v^{\prime},w^{\prime}
are pure wedge vectors in
\Lambda^{*}K_{v}^{d}
representing
U
,
V^{\prime}
and
W^{\prime}
respectively, and
v:=u\wedge v^{\prime}
,
w:=u\wedge w^{\prime}
. This inequality is a direct consequence of (
4.1
) noting that
\|u\|_{v}\|w^{\prime}\|_{v}=\|w\|_{v}
.∎

If
A\in M_{d}(K)
has rank
r
then

(5.4)

h_{\Ar}(\image A)\leq r\cdot h(A)

Indeed there are
r
elements of the canonical basis of
K^{d}
, say
e_{i_{1}},\ldots,e_{i_{r}}
such that
\image A
is spanned by the
w_{j}:=Ae_{i_{j}}
. But
\|w_{j}\|_{v}\leq\|A\|_{v}
, so
\|w_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge w_{r}\|_{v}\leq\|A\|^{r}_{v}
for all places and
(\ref{arbd})
follows.

The following will also be helpful:

Lemma 5.2
.

If
V\leq K^{d}
is a
K
-subspace of dimension
r
, then there are
r
linearly independent lines
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r}
in
V
such that
h_{\Ar}(\ell_{i})\leq h_{\Ar}(V)
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
r
. Let
E_{k}
be the
K
-span of the elements
e_{k+1},\ldots,e_{d}
of the canonial basis of
K^{d}
. Note that
h_{\Ar}(E_{k})=0
. Also
V\cap E_{0}=V
,
V\cap E_{d}=0
, and
\dim V\cap E_{k+1}\geq\dim V\cap E_{k}-1
, so there is
k<d
such that
\dim V\cap E_{k}=1
. Let
\ell_{1}=V\cap E_{k}
. By Lemma
5.1

h_{\Ar}(\ell_{1})\leq h_{\Ar}(E_{k})+h_{\Ar}(V)=h_{\Ar}(V)
. Now for
i\in[1,d]
let
H_{i}
be the hyperplane spanned by all
e_{j}
,
j\neq i
. There is
i
such that
\ell_{1}\notin H_{i}
. Clearly
\dim H_{i}\cap V=r-1
. Also, since
h_{\Ar}(H_{i})=0
, we have
h_{\Ar}(V\cap H_{i})\leq h_{\Ar}(V)
. We may now use the induction hypothesis to find the remaining linearly independent lines in
V\cap H_{i}
.
∎

We now introduce the Arakelov distance between two
K
-subspaces
V,W
in
K^{d}
as follows:

\delta_{\Ar}(V,W)=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}\cdot\log\frac{1}{\delta_{v}(V,W)}

where
\delta_{v}
is the projective distance over
K_{v}
defined in
(\ref{projdi})
of §
4.2
and
\delta_{v}(V,W)
is shorthand for
\delta_{v}(V\otimes_{K}K_{v},W\otimes_{K}K_{v})
. Similarly, we write
d_{v}(V,W)
for
d_{v}(V\otimes_{K}K_{v},W\otimes_{K}K_{v})
.

Recall that
\delta_{v}(V,W)\in(0,1]
, so
\delta_{\Ar}(V,W)\geq 0
. The Arakelov distance is not a distance in the usual sense (it does not satisfy the triangle inequality and can be zero for example if
V\leq W
or
W\leq V
or if
W
and
V
are orthogonal at all places) but it is non-negative and is a measure of how arithmetically transverse the two subspaces
V
and
W
are. Note that each term in the sum is non-negative and only finitely many of them are non-zero. Indeed, if
V^{\prime}
is a subspace of
K^{d}
and
V^{\prime}\oplus(V\cap W)=V
, then for each place
v
,
\delta_{v}(V,W)\geq d_{v}(V^{\prime},W)
by definition of
\delta_{v}.
But the wedge vectors
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{w}
representing
V^{\prime}
and
W
have coordinates in
K
, so
\|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\wedge\mathbf{w}\|_{v}=\|\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\|_{v}=\|\mathbf{w}\|_{v}=1
for all but finitely many
v
. So
d_{v}(V^{\prime},W)=1
and hence
\delta_{v}(V,W)=1
for all but finitely many places.

When
V\cap W=0
then
d_{v}(V,W)=\delta_{v}(V,W)
, so rearranging the terms we see that

(5.5)

\delta_{\Ar}(V,W)=h_{\Ar}(V)+h_{\Ar}(W)-h_{\Ar}(V+W).

It will be important for us to consider the general case, when
V
and
W
can intersect. Then we have:

Lemma 5.3
.

For every two subspaces
V,W\leq K^{d}
, we have:

\delta_{\Ar}(V,W)\leq h_{\Ar}(V)+h_{\Ar}(W^{\prime})-h_{\Ar}(V+W),

for any linear subspace
W^{\prime}
complementing
V\cap W
in
W
. Furthermore,

\delta_{\Ar}(V,W)\leq d\max\{h_{\Ar}(V),h_{\Ar}(W)\}.

Proof.

By definition of
\delta_{v}
, at each place
v\in V_{K}
we have
\delta_{v}(V,W)\geq d_{v}(V,W^{\prime})
. Summing over all places and using
(\ref{trans})
the first inequality follows. To prove the second inequality,
we are allowed to assume that
V\cap W\neq\{0\}
.
We need to choose a complement
W^{\prime}
with small height.
By Lemma
5.2
, there are
\dim W
linearly independent lines in
W
, each of Arakelov height at most
h_{\Ar}(W)
.
We pick
\dim W-\dim V\cap W
of them that span a linear subspace
W^{\prime}
complementing
V\cap W
in
W
.
By subadditivity of
h_{\Ar}
(as follows from Lemma
5.1
), we get
h_{\Ar}(W^{\prime})\leq(\dim W-\dim V\cap W)h_{\Ar}(W)
. The result then follows from the first inequality.
∎

If
W^{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal to
W
in the dual of
K^{d}
identified with
K^{d}
via the canonical pairing
\langle x,y\rangle=\sum x_{i}y_{i}
, then it is easy to check that
h_{\Ar}(W^{\perp})=h_{\Ar}(W)

[
12
, 2.8.10]
. As a result, if
A\in M_{d}(K)
is a matrix with rank
r
, we obtain from
(\ref{arbd})
that

h_{\Ar}(\ker A)=h_{\Ar}((\ker A)^{\perp})=h_{\Ar}(\image A^{T})\leq r\cdot h(A^{T})=r\cdot h(A)

More generally we can upper bound the height of the generalized eigenspaces as follows:

Lemma 5.4
.

If
A\in M_{d}(K)
and
W\leq(\mathbb{Q}^{\al})^{d}
is an
A
-invariant subspace, then

h_{\Ar}(W)\leq d^{2}(2h(A)+\log 2).

Proof.

Note that
W
is the sum of generalized eigenspaces
E_{\lambda}:=\ker(A-\lambda)^{n_{\lambda}}
, where
n_{\lambda}
is the largest size of a Jordan block of
A
with eigenvalue
\lambda
. Hence by
(\ref{arbd})

h_{\Ar}(E_{\lambda})\leq dh((A-\lambda)^{n_{\lambda}})\leq dn_{\lambda}(h(A)+h(\lambda)+\log 2)

and summing over
\lambda
yields the result.
∎

5.3.
Height Gap

In
[
22
]
the second named author introduced the notion of normalized height of a finite set of matrices and proved a uniform lower bound (height gap theorem) valid for generating sets of non-virtually solvable subgroups. We now briefly recall these results. Given a finite subset
S\subset\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Q}^{\al})
we define its height as:

h(S):=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}\log^{+}\max_{A\in S}\|A\|_{v}\,\,.

In particular if
S
consists of a single matrix
A
, then
h(S)
coincides with the height of the matrix
A
introduced in §
5.1
. We define the normalized height of
S
as:

\widehat{h}(S):=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}h(S^{n}).

The limit exists and as shown in
[
22
]
,

\widehat{h}(S^{n})=n\widehat{h}(S)

for every integer
n\geq 1
. The normalized height is invariant under conjugation. For future reference, note that if
g\in\SL_{d}(\mathbb{Q}^{\al})
and
\widehat{h}(g)>0
then
g
has at least two distinct eigenvalues (this follows, e.g., since
\widehat{h}(g)
is equal to the Weil height of the projective point
[1:\lambda_{1}:\cdots:\lambda_{d}]
).

One may also view the normalized height as a sum of local contributions at each place. Indeed:

\widehat{h}(S)=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}\log^{+}R_{v}(S)

where
R_{v}(S)
is the
joint spectral radius
of
S
at the place
v
, namely:

R_{v}(S)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\|S^{n}\|_{v}^{1/n}

where we denote
\|Q\|_{v}=\max_{A\in Q}\|A\|_{v}
for a subset
Q\subset M_{d}(K_{v})
.

A key feature of the joint spectral radius
R_{v}(S)
is the
Bochi inequality
. This says that, when
v
is archimedean,

(5.6)

\frac{1}{2}R_{v}(S)\leq\max_{k\leq 16d^{4}}\Lambda_{v}(S^{k})^{1/k}\leq R_{v}(S)

where, for
Q\subset M_{d}(K_{v})
we denote by
\Lambda_{v}(Q)
is the maximum modulus of an eigenvalue of an element of
Q
. When
v
is non-archimedean, on the other hand, one has:

(5.7)

R_{v}(S)=\max_{k\leq d^{2}}\Lambda_{v}(S^{k})^{1/k}.

We refer the reader to
[
26
, Theorem 5 and 7]
for these facts.

In the proof of our main results we will make key use of the
Height gap theorem
from
[
22
]
. This asserts (see
[
22
, Theorem 3.1]
) that there is a constant
gap_{d}>0
(the
height gap
) such that

(5.8)

\widehat{h}(S)\geq gap_{d}

as soon as
S
generates a non-virtually solvable subgroup,
and thus whenever
S
generates a subgroup acting strongly irreducibly on

(\mathbb{Q}^{\al})^{d}
.
Another essential result is the
quasi-symmetrisation bound
of
[
22
, Proposition 1.1]
: there is a constant
\kappa_{d}>0
such that

(5.9)

\widehat{h}(S)\leq\inf_{g\in\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Q}^{\al})}h(gSg^{-1})\leq\kappa_{d}\widehat{h}(S)

provided the Zariski-closure of the subgroup
\langle S\rangle
is semisimple. In fact, the proof of
[
22
, Proposition 1.1]
gives
\kappa^{\prime}_{d}>0
such that
\inf_{g\in\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Q}^{\al})}h(gSg^{-1})\leq\kappa^{\prime}_{d}(1+\widehat{h}(S))
, so we may take

(5.10)

\kappa_{d}=\kappa^{\prime}_{d}(1+\gap_{d}^{-1}).

Remark.
In
[
22
]
we did not give an explicit bound on
gap_{d}
and
\kappa^{\prime}_{d}
, but with the help of
[
26
]
it is now possible to do so by carefully going through all estimates in the proofs of
[
22
]
. We plan to address this point in the near furture. See also
[
28
]
for another proof of (
5.8
) and a discussion of explicit bounds on
gap_{d}
.

5.4.
Positive characteristic

If
K
is now a global field of positive characteristic (i.e. a finite extension of the field of Laurent series with coefficients in a finite field), then the height function
h(S)
on matrices and its normalized version
\widehat{h}(S)
can be defined in the same way as above. The main difference is that all places are now non-archimedean and this simplifies some estimates. For example, the height is then genuinely subadditive: (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) hold without
the
\log 2
term. The product formula
5.1
continues to hold as well as all the lemmas above. Lemma
5.4
holds without the
\log 2
term, and (
5.7
) holds. The key difference however is that there is no height gap in positive characteristic, see the example below. But crucially, the quasi-symmetrisation bound does hold verbatim, namely:

(5.11)

\widehat{h}(S)\leq\inf_{g\in\GL_{d}(K^{\al})}h(gSg^{-1})\leq\kappa^{\prime}_{d}\,\widehat{h}(S)

provided the Zariski-closure of the subgroup
\langle S\rangle
is semisimple. The constant
\kappa^{\prime}_{d}
is the same as in (
5.10
) and the proof given in
[
22
]
holds verbatim.

Example.
(Failure of height gap in positive characteristic) Let
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
and let
S=\{u,u^{T}\}
where
u
is the unipotent in
\SL_{2}(K^{al})
given by
u(e_{1})=e_{1}
,
u(e_{2})=t^{\frac{1}{n}}e_{1}+e_{2}
. Then
S
generates a Zariski-dense subgroup of
\SL_{2}(K^{al})
. However
\widehat{h}(S)\leq h(S)=\frac{h(t)}{n}
. The last inequality results from the fact that, because there are only non-archimedean places,
\max_{i,j}h(A_{ij})\leq h(A)\leq\sum_{i,j}h(A_{ij})
for any matrix
A
, and
h(t^{\frac{1}{n}})=\frac{1}{n}h(t)
.

6.
Proof of the uniform spectral gap

The main technical statement we will prove is the following:

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
r,d\geq 2
. There is
N=N(d,r)\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds. Let
K
be a number field and
S\subset\GL_{d}(K)
be a finite symmetric set containing the identity and such that the subgroup
\langle S\rangle
it generates acts strongly irreducibly on
(K^{\al})^{d}
. Then there is a completion
k=K_{v}
of
K
and a subset
F\subset S^{N}
with
|F|=r
in
d
-ping-pong position on
X=\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
.

Recall that strongly irreducible means that
\langle S\rangle
does not preserve a finite union of proper subspaces, or equivalently that the connected component
\mathbb{G}
of the Zariski-closure of
\langle S\rangle
acts irreducibly. Note then that
\mathbb{G}
must be reductive for it must preserve the fixed point set of its unipotent radical, which is therefore trivial. Also its image in
\PGL_{d}
is semisimple.

We stick to number fields for now and will explain at the end of this section how to deal with global fields of positive characteristic.

We will obtain an explicit bound on
N(d,r)
in terms of the height gap
gap_{d}
(assumed in
(0,1)
) and quasi-symmetrisation constant
\kappa^{\prime}_{d}
from (
5.8
), (
5.9
)and (
5.10
):

(6.1)

N(d,r)\leq\kappa^{\prime}_{d}(1+2gap_{d}^{-1})4^{4rd}e^{(4d)^{5}(\log d)^{2}gap_{d}^{-1}}\leq\kappa^{\prime}_{d}(1+2gap_{d}^{-1})\exp(O(rd+d^{6}gap_{d}^{-1})).

Note that we may always pass to a finite extention of
K
without loss of generality, because if
F\subset\GL_{d}(k)
is in
d
-ping-pong position on
\mathbb{P}(k^{\prime d})
for some extension field
k^{\prime}
, then it is obviously also in
d
-ping-pong position on
\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
using the regions obtained by intersecting
\mathbf{A}_{\gamma},\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}
with
\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
.
Similarly, considering the subgroup generated by the
\overline{s}
,
s\in S
where
\overline{s}=(\det s)^{-1/d}s
, we obtain a subgroup of
\SL_{d}(K^{\prime})
for some finite extension
K^{\prime}
of
K
with the same image in
\PGL_{d}(K)
as
\langle S\rangle
and which is again strongly irreducible. So we may assume without loss of generality that
S\subset\SL_{d}(K)
.

We pass to the proof of Theorem
6.1
and first describe its outline. The strategy is to work our way to put ourselves in a situation where we can apply Proposition
4.6
at a suitably chosen place
v\in V_{K}
and for suitably chosen elements
\gamma
and
g_{i}
. Candidates for
\gamma
will be drawn from all possible elements in a bounded power
S^{L}
of the generating set
S
with
L
bounded in terms of
d
only. Setting
\gamma_{i}=g_{i}\gamma g_{i}^{-1}
, Proposition
4.6
guarantees that the family
\{\gamma_{1}^{n},\ldots,\gamma_{r}^{n}\}
will be in
d
-ping-pong position provided
n
is larger than a bound that depends on the maximal squared norm
\Delta
of
\gamma
and the
g_{i}
, on the largest eigenvalue gap
\alpha_{\omega}(\gamma)^{-1}>1
and on the
separation

\delta
, that is on the projective distance between the subspaces
g_{i}A_{\gamma}
and
g_{i}R_{\gamma}
and their intersections:

(6.2)

n>\frac{(2+2\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil)\log\delta^{-1}+\log C_{k}^{2}\Delta^{3(d-2)}}{|\log(C_{k}^{-2}\alpha_{\omega}(\gamma)^{-1})|}.

We want to find a
\gamma
and
g_{i}
for which the right hand side of (
6.2
) is bounded in terms of
d
and
r
only. To achieve the maximal possible eigenvalue gap, we may pick
\gamma
so that its top eigenvalue is as large as possible, which by the Bochi inequality (
5.6
) can be taken to be of size comparable to the joint spectral radius of
S
for some
\gamma
of word length at most
(2d)^{4}
. The problem is that there is no guarantee that the separation
\delta
locally at that place for this choice of
\gamma
be controlled at all. Only when taking into account all places globally can we hope to bound the separation in terms of the height of the generating set
S
. The main idea is then to sum over all places and upper bound the sum
s
of all the quantities involved (norms of elements of
S
and separation) by the normalized height
\widehat{h}
(or
e
in the lemma below) of
S
, making crucial use of the height gap theorem (
5.8
) and the quasi-symmetrixation inequality (
5.9
) at this step. The chosen place
v
will then be either archimedean or non-archimedean. Its existence will be established thanks to the following simple combinatorial observation:

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose
K
is a number field with set of places
V_{K}
and suppose that we are given non-negative quantities
e_{v}
and
s_{v}
for each
v\in V_{K}
. We set

e=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}e_{v}

and
e_{\infty}
(resp.
e_{f}
) for the contribution of the infinite places (resp. finite places) in this sum, so that
e=e_{\infty}+e_{f}
. We define
s,s_{\infty}
and
s_{f}
similarly. Now assume that

s\leq Ce

for some
C>0
, then one of the two options holds:

(1)

there is
v\in V_{f}
with
e_{v}>\frac{1}{2C}s_{v}
, or

(2)

there is
v\in V_{\infty}
with
e_{v}\geq\frac{s_{v}}{2C}+\frac{e}{2}
.

Proof.

Suppose option
(1)
does not hold. Then, for all
v\in V_{f}
we have
s_{v}\geq 2Ce_{v}
, hence
s_{f}\geq 2Ce_{f}
. By assumption
s\leq Ce
, so
s_{\infty}+s_{f}\leq Ce_{\infty}+Ce_{f}
. Hence
s_{\infty}+2Ce_{f}\leq Ce_{\infty}+Ce_{f}
, which means that
s_{\infty}+Ce_{f}\leq Ce_{\infty}
. In other words
s_{\infty}\leq Ce_{\infty}
and
s_{\infty}+C(e-e_{\infty})\leq Ce_{\infty}
. So

e_{\infty}\geq s_{\infty}/2C+e/2=\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{\infty}}n_{v}(\frac{s_{v}}{2C}+\frac{e}{2}),

because
\sum_{v\in V_{\infty}}n_{v}=[K:\mathbb{Q}]
. The left and right hand sides are both averages over all infinite places, so there must be at least one
v\in V_{\infty}
for which
e_{v}\geq\frac{s_{v}}{2C}+\frac{e}{2}
.
∎

We now pass to the details of the proof of Theorem
6.1
, assuming as we may that
|S|\leq 2d^{2}-3
. As a first step we note that, according to
(\ref{qs})
, we may assume, up to conjugating
S
by a suitable element of
\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Q}^{\al})
, that

(6.3)

Given
\gamma\in\GL_{d}(\mathbb{Q}^{\al})
, we will say that a non-zero proper subspace

is
\gamma
-admissible
, if it is a sum of generalized eigenspaces of
\gamma
corresponding to eigenvalues that are not all roots of unity. Note that since
\gamma
has at most
d
distinct eigenvalues, it has at most
2^{d}
distinct
\gamma
-admissible subspaces. If
W
is
\gamma
-admissible, we let
W^{\prime}
be the complement of
W
, which is the sum of the generalized eigenspaces associated to the remaining eigenvalues of
\gamma
. We let
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
be the family of
\gamma
-admissible subspaces.

If
W
and
W^{\prime}
are both admissible, we may apply Lemma
2.2
to them setting
H^{+}=W
and
H^{-}=W^{\prime}
. This yields
g_{1,W},\ldots,g_{r,W}\in S^{M}
,

such that each family
\mathcal{F}_{W}=\{g_{1,W}W,\ldots,g_{r,W}W\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{W^{\prime}}=\{g_{1,W}W^{\prime},\ldots,g_{r,W}W^{\prime}\}
is made of
r
distinct subspaces in weak general position. As announced earlier, we are going to show that, provided
m
is larger than a certain quantity that depends only on
d
, there is a place
v\in V_{K}
, an element
\gamma\in S^{m}
and an admissible subspace
W\in\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
such that
\{\gamma_{1,W}^{n},\ldots,\gamma_{r,W}^{n}\}
is in
d
-ping-pong type position for every large enough

(depending only on

), where
\gamma_{i,W}=g_{i,W}\gamma g_{i,W}^{-1}
.

Without loss of generality we may enlarge the number field
K
so as to guarantee that the characteristic polynomial of each
\gamma\in S^{m}
splits over
K
. This does not affect the statement of Theorem
6.1
and allows us to assume that the
\gamma
-admissible subspaces are defined over
K
. For a place

we denote by
\delta_{W,v}(\gamma)
the quantity denoted by
\delta
in Proposition
4.6
, namely:

(6.4)

\delta_{W,v}(\gamma)=\min_{I,J\subset[r]}\{\delta_{v}(W,W^{\prime}),\delta_{v}(W_{I},W_{J}),\delta_{v}(W^{\prime}_{I},W^{\prime}_{J})\},

where
W_{I}=\cap_{i\in I}g_{i,W}W
and
W^{\prime}_{I}=\cap_{i\in I}g_{i,W}W^{\prime}
.
Then we set:

t_{v}(\gamma):=\sum_{W\in\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}}\log\delta_{W,v}^{-1}(\gamma)

with the convention
t_{v}(\gamma)=0
if
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
is empty. We also set

(6.5)

s_{v}:=2(M+m)\log\|S\|_{v}+\sum_{\gamma\in S^{m}}t_{v}(\gamma)\,\textnormal{ and }\,\,\,e_{v}:=\log R_{v}(S)

and let
t,s,e
be the associated weighted sum over all places; note that

.

Lemma 6.3
.

We have
s\leq C(m)\widehat{h}(S)
, where

(6.6)

C(m):=(2M+2m+|S|^{m}C_{2})\kappa_{d}+|S|^{m}C_{1}/gap_{d}.

and
C_{1}=d^{4}2^{d}4^{r+1}\log 2
and
C_{2}=d^{4}2^{d}4^{r+2}(M+m)
.

Proof.

Taking logs in (
6.4
) and summing over all places, we get:

\frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}\sum_{v\in V_{K}}n_{v}\log\delta^{-1}_{W,v}(\gamma)\leq\delta_{Ar}(W,W^{\prime})+\sum_{I,J\subset[r]}\delta_{Ar}(W_{I},W_{J})+\delta_{Ar}(W^{\prime}_{I},W^{\prime}_{J}).

Applying Lemmas
5.3
and
5.1
we note that

\delta_{Ar}(W_{I},W_{J})\leq d\max_{I\subset[r]}h_{Ar}(W_{I})\leq d^{2}\max_{1\leq i\leq r}h_{Ar}(g_{i,W}W).

This yields:

t(\gamma)\leq 2^{d}d^{2}(1+2^{2r+1})\max_{W\in\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}}\max_{g\in S^{M(d,r)}}h_{Ar}(gW).

Noting that
gW
is invariant under
g\gamma g^{-1}\in S^{2M+m}
, Lemma
5.4
yields:

t(\gamma)\leq d^{4}2^{d}4^{r+1}(2h(S^{2M+m})+\log 2)\leq C_{1}+C_{2}h(S).

Recall from (
5.8
) and (

) that
\widehat{h}(S)\geq gap_{d}
and
h(S)\leq\kappa_{d}\widehat{h}(S)
. Combining the above inequalities we get

as desired.
∎

We can now apply Lemma
6.2
, to conclude that two options can hold:

Option 1:
there is a finite place

of

where

. Then, in particular

and it then follows from (

) that there is

such that

, provided

. The largest eigenvalue gap between the modulus of two eigenvalues of

, which is at least

, as

. Therefore there is a cursor
\omega>0
such that

in the notation of Proposition

for the local field

. Since

. In particular:

C_{k}^{2}\alpha_{\omega}(\gamma)\leq\Lambda(\gamma)_{v}^{-\frac{1}{d-1}}<1.

On the other hand,
A_{\gamma}
and

are given by one
\gamma
-admissible subspace

and its complement

, which is also

-admissible, i.e.

and

, and thus

\|S\|_{v}^{2(M+m)}\delta_{W,v}(\gamma)^{-1}\leq\|S\|_{v}^{2(M+m)}e^{t_{v}(\gamma)}\leq e^{s_{v}}<e^{C(m)e_{v}}\leq\Lambda(\gamma)_{v}^{C(m)}.

In particular, as
2+2\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil\leq 2+2d\leq 3d
, and
\Delta\leq\|S\|_{v}^{2(M+m)}

C_{k}^{2}\Delta^{3(d-2)}\delta^{-1}_{v,W}(\gamma)^{2+2\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil}<\Lambda(\gamma)_{v}^{3dC(m)}\leq(C_{k}^{2}\alpha_{\omega}(\gamma))^{-3d(d-1)C(m)}.

This means that the condition

for ping-pong from Proposition

is satisfied whenever

.

Option 2:
there is an infinite place

of
K
such that

.
In particular,

e_{v}\geq\widehat{h}(S)/2\geq gap_{d}/2
. Let

be the smallest integer greater or equal to

(6.7)

1+2gap_{d}^{-1}(4(d-1)\log d+\log 2).

We have

R_{v}(S^{n_{d}})=R_{v}(S)^{n_{d}}\geq e^{(n_{d}-1)gap_{d}/2}R_{v}(S)\geq 2d^{4(d-1)}R_{v}(S).

Similarly as above but using (

) this time, there is
\gamma\in S^{(2d)^{4}n_{d}}
such that
\Lambda(\gamma)_{v}\geq\frac{1}{2}R_{v}(S^{n_{d}})\geq d^{4(d-1)}R_{v}(S)>R_{v}(S)=e^{e_{v}}
. The largest eigenvalue gap is at least

and there is a cursor

for which

C_{k}^{2}\alpha_{\omega}(\gamma)\leq d^{2}\Lambda(\gamma)_{v}^{-1/(d-1)}\leq\Lambda(\gamma)_{v}^{-1/2(d-1)}.

On the other hand

and as earlier

and

are given by one
\gamma
-admissible subspace

and its complement, so as

\|S\|^{2(M+m)}_{v}\delta^{-1}_{W,v}(\gamma)\leq e^{s_{v}}\leq e^{2C(m)e_{v}}\leq\Lambda(\gamma)_{v}^{2C(m)}

So

\displaystyle C_{k}^{2}\Delta^{3(d-2)}\delta_{v,W}^{-1}(\gamma)^{2+2\lceil\log_{2}d\rceil}

\displaystyle\leq d^{2}e^{3ds_{v}}\leq d^{2}\Lambda(\gamma)_{v}^{6C(m)d}

\displaystyle<\Lambda(\gamma)_{v}^{(6C(m)d+1/2(d-1))}

\displaystyle\leq(C_{k}^{2}\alpha_{\omega}(\gamma))^{-n}

provided

. Therefore condition

from Proposition

holds for all

.

Conclusion:
for a suitable

, we have found a place

of

and

where

Starting with Lemma

to the complementary admissible subspaces

6.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this paragraph we derive Theorem
1.1
from Theorem

.

We start with two initial observations.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable group and
\pi:X\to Y
a
\Gamma
-map of countable
\Gamma
-sets. Let
\varepsilon>0
. If
S\subset\Gamma
is an
\varepsilon
-Kazhdan set for
\lambda_{Y}=\ell^{2}(Y)
, then it is also an
\varepsilon
-Kazhdan set for
\lambda_{X}=\ell^{2}(X)
.

Proof.

This is because the induced map
\ell^{2}(X)\to\ell^{2}(Y)

f\mapsto\bar{f}
, where
\bar{f}(y)=\|f_{|\pi^{-1}(y)}\|
is a
\Gamma
-map such that
\|f\|=\|\bar{f}\|
and
\|\bar{f}-\bar{g}\|\leq\|f-g\|
for all

as follows from the triangle inequality.
∎

As a result, when proving Theorem
1.1
we can always pass to a suitable quotient.

The second observation is that without loss of generality, we may assume either that
H=\{1\}
, or that
H
is infinite, for if
H
is finite then
\ell^{2}(\Gamma/H)
naturally embeds isometrically and equivariantly as the subrepresentation
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)^{H}
of right

-Kazhdan set for
\lambda_{\Gamma}

\varepsilon
-Kazhdan for
\lambda_{\Gamma/H}
.

To exploit Theorem
6.1
, we need the following basic lemma.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a semisimple algebraic group of dimension

\mathbb{K}
and
\mathbb{H}
is an algebraic subgroup. Suppose
\mathbb{H}^{0}
is not normal in
\mathbb{G}
. Then there is a linear representation

of
\mathbb{G}

and at least

\mathbb{G}

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Lie algebra of
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathfrak{h}

\mathbb{H}
. Each wedge representation

\mathbb{H}^{0}

\mathbb{G}

-invariant subspace of largest dimension. This gives a
\mathbb{G}
-representation

-invariant. If

\mathbb{G}
-invariant codimension one subspace

∎

Recall that Chevalley’s theorem

We can thus apply Theorem

But
\nu_{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L\setminus L^{*}}}\right)=\nu_{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L\cap V^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r}}\right)}}}\right)\setminus L^{*}}}\right)

because
\nu_{\ell}
is supported on
V^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r}}\right)}
. Since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(L\cap V^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r}}\right)}}}\right)\setminus L^{*}

is contained in a closed subvariety
W_{L}
of dimension
<r
and
degree controlled by
\deg L
and
\deg V^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r}}\right)}
only,
it remains to prove the following claim: There are integers
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N_{D}}}\right)_{D\geq 1}

such that the following holds almost surely:
for every closed subvariety
W
of
G
of dimension
<r
,

\ell\nu_{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(W}}\right)\leq N_{\deg W}
,
i.e.
W
contains at most
N_{\deg W}
of the points
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell}
.

We can assume without loss of generality that
W
is irreducible.
The proof is by induction on
\dim W+\deg W
. In the base case
\dim W=0
,

W
is a point, and the claim follows since for
i\neq j
,
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}=X_{j}}}\right)=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X_{i}=X_{j}\mid X_{j}}}\right))=0

since
r\geq 1
and thus
\mu^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r}}\right)}
has no atoms. For
the induction step, assume that the claim holds for all
W
of dimension
at most
p-1
, with some
N_{D}=N_{p-1,D}
, and consider now dimension

p
. If there are two distinct irreducible closed subvarieties
W_{1}
and

W_{2}
, of dimension at most
p
and degree at most
D
, containing

X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N_{p-1,D^{2}}+1}
, then
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N_{p-1,D^{2}}+1}

belong to
W_{1}\cap W_{2}
, which is a closed subvariety of dimension

<p
and degree at most
D^{2}
by Bezout (
7.1
). Thus, for every
D\geq 1
, almost
surely there is at most one closed subvariety
W
of dimension at
most
p
and degree at most
D
containing
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N_{p-1,D^{2}}+1}
.
But then, almost surely
X_{N_{p-1,D^{2}}+2}\notin W
since
\mu^{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r}}\right)}

gives measure
0
to subvarieties of dimension
<r
.

It is easier to verify the second assertion of the lemma: indeed for a given
H
, and
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\exists g\in G,X_{i},X_{j}\in gH\setminus(gH)^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{P}(X_{j}\in X_{i}H\setminus(X_{i}H)^{*}|X_{i}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\mu^{(r)}(X_{i}H\setminus(X_{i}H)^{*})/\mu^{(r)}(G))=0

So almost surely we have:
\nu_{k}(L\setminus L^{*})\leq 1/k
for all
L
of the form
L=gH
.
∎

Lemma 7.4
.

If
\mathbb{G}
is a connected linear algebraic group in characteristic
0
, then there is a countable family of proper closed subgroups
\mathbb{H}_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, and a number
N_{\mathbb{G}}
such that every proper algebraic subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
is either contained in
\mathbb{H}_{n}
or contained in a proper algebraic subgroup of degree at most
N_{\mathbb{G}}
. When
\mathbb{G}
is semisimple, only the second option occurs and it also holds in positive characteristic for subgroups
\mathbb{G}
that are not subfield subgroups.

Proof.

Consider first the semisimple case. By
[
45
, Theorem 11.6]
(see also
[
16
, Lemma 4.2]
there is a finite number of finite dimensional irreducible
\mathbb{G}
-modules
V_{\rho_{i}}
with the property that a proper algebraic subgroup
L
of
\mathbb{G}
cannot act irreducibly on all of them unless we are in positive characteristic and
L
is a finite subfield subgroup. The latter means that
L
is conjugate to a subgroup of
\mathbb{G}(F)
for some finite subfield
F
. In the former case
L
must be contained in a parabolic subgroup
\Stab(V)
of
\GL(V_{\rho_{i}})
for some proper subspace
V
of the representation space
V_{\rho_{i}}
. These have bounded degree in
\mathbb{G}
irrespective of the choice of
V
. This proves the lemma in the semisimple case. Now consider the general case in characteristic zero. Write
\mathbb{G}=TSU
a Levi decomposition, where
S
is semisimple,
T
a torus commuting with
S
, and
U
the unipotent radical. There are only countably many proper algebraic subgroups of
T
, say a family
\{T_{n}\}_{n}
(see
[
12
, 3.2.19]
), so we can set
\mathbb{H}_{n}=T_{n}SU
.
Suppose
L\leq\mathbb{G}
is not contained in any
\mathbb{H}_{n}
, so
T\leq L
.
If
LU
is proper, then
L\subset TS^{\prime}U
for a proper algebraic subgroup
S^{\prime}
of
S
and we are done by the semisimple case. If
LU=\mathbb{G}
, then every Levi subgroup
R
of
L
is a Levi
subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
. Indeed,
L\cap U
is the unipotent radical of

L
since it is normal, connected, unipotent and
L/L\cap U=\mathbb{G}/U

is reductive. Hence
L=R\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H\cap U}}\right)
, and so
\mathbb{G}=LU=RU

(and
R\cap U=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1}}\right\}
). Thus
L
is conjugate to a
subgroup containing
TS
since all Levi subgroups of
\mathbb{G}
are conjugate,
and so we may assume that
TS\leq L
.
Hence
L=TSU^{\prime}
for some proper closed subgroup
U^{\prime}\leq U
. Proper closed subgroups of
U
are connected and in characteristic zero are of bounded degree (being bounded products of bounded degree homomorphic images of the additive group
(\mathbb{G}_{a},+)
). This ends of the proof.
∎

We can now finish the proof of Lemma
7.2
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{n}
be the family of all cosets of
\mathbb{H}_{n}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{G}}
the family of all cosets of proper algebraic subgroups of degree at most
N_{\mathbb{G}}
.
In characteristic zero, we can apply Lemma
7.4
and get for all
\ell\geq 1
,

\sup_{L\in\mathcal{L}}\mu_{\ell}(L)\leq\max\{\sup_{L\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{G}}}\mu_{\ell}(L),\sup_{L\in\mathcal{L}_{n},n\geq 1}\mu_{\ell}(L)\}.

In positive characteristic when
\mathbb{G}
is semisimple the same lemma applies except for cosets of finite subfield subgroups. But, since
k
is a local field, there is a largest finite subfield
F
of
k
and hence a uniform bound on finite subfield subgroups in
\mathbb{G}(k)
. So up to updating the value of
N_{\mathbb{G}}
we get the same bound. Hence by Lemma
7.3
, almost surely

\sup_{L\in\mathcal{L}}\mu_{\ell}(L)\leq\max\{\mu_{\ell}(L^{*})\mid L^{*}\in\mathcal{L}_{V}\}+O_{\mu,\mathbb{G}}(1)/\ell

and letting
\ell
tend to infinity, we obtain
\limsup_{\ell}\gamma_{\mathcal{L}}(\mu_{\ell})\leq\max\{\mu(L^{*})\mid L^{*}\in\mathcal{L}_{V}\}\leq\gamma_{\mathcal{L}}(\mu)

because
\mathcal{L}_{V}
is finite and
\mu_{\ell}(L)\to\mu(L)
almost surely for each
L\in\mathcal{L}
.
This completes the proof of Lemma
7.2
.

∎

Remark 7.5
.

As its proof shows, Proposition
7.1
also holds if
\mathcal{L}
is taken to be the cosets of a family of algebraic subgroups, that is closed under intersection and that has maximal subgroups of bounded degree. For example the family of cosets of virtually solvable algebraic subgroups of
\GL_{d}(k)
is such a family. Indeed, up to bounded index such groups can be triangularized. In characteristic zero, this is
[
69
, 3.6, 10.10]
with a bound that depends only on
d
. In positive characteristic, the bound would depend on
k
and also follows from
[
69
, 3.6, 10.10]
and the observation that there is a maximal finite subfield in
k
.

7.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

The proof of Theorem
1.4
is similar to that of Theorem
1.3
. The main spectral ingredient is the following:

Theorem 7.6

(uniform
\ell^{2}
gap)
.

Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
there is
\varepsilon_{d}>0
such that the following holds. Let
K
be a field and
\Gamma\leq\GL_{d}(K)
be countable a subgroup. Suppose a finite set
S\subset\Gamma
generates a non-amenable subgroup. Then for every unit vector
v\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
there is
s\in S
such that
\|s\cdot v-v\|_{\ell^{2}(\Gamma)}\geq\varepsilon_{d}
. In particular, if
\mu
is any probability measure on
\Gamma
, then

\|\mu\|_{\ell^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq\exp(-\beta_{\mathcal{L}}(\mu)\varepsilon_{d}^{2}/4)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the family of all cosets of amenable subgroups of
\Gamma
.

Proof of Theorem
7.6
.

As we already observed (cf. the first paragraph of §
6.2
) if
\pi:\Gamma\to\overline{\Gamma}
is a surjective group homomorphism and
\pi(S)
is a
\varepsilon
-Kazhdan set for
\lambda_{\overline{\Gamma}}
, then
S
is an
\varepsilon
-Kazhdan set for
\lambda_{\Gamma}
. Also if
S\subset\Gamma
is an
\varepsilon
-Kazhdan set for
\lambda_{\Gamma^{\prime}}
, where
\Gamma^{\prime}\leq\Gamma
is a subgroup, then it is also an
\varepsilon
-Kazhdan set for
\lambda_{\Gamma}
. So without loss of generality
S
generates
\Gamma
and if
\mathbb{G}
is the Zariski closure of
\Gamma
and
R
its solvable radical, the quotient
\mathbb{G}/R
is semisimple. It acts by conjugation on its connected component
\mathbb{S}
. As is well-known the index of inner automorphisms in all automorphisms of
\mathbb{S}
is bounded in terms of
d
only. So we obtain a subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}\leq\Gamma
of bounded index and a homomorphism of
\Gamma^{\prime}
to
\mathbb{S}
with Zariski-dense image. There is some bounded
n_{0}=n_{0}(d)
such that
(S\cup S^{-1})^{n_{0}}\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
generates
\Gamma^{\prime}
(cf. see
[
18
, Lemma C.1]
). These remarks allow us to reduce the first assertion of the Theorem to the case when
\Gamma
is Zariski-dense in a connected semisimple algebraic group. Then the result is a special case of Theorem
1.1
.
To prove the second assertion about
\mu
, note that as in the proof of Corollary
1.2
, if
v\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
is a unit vector and
B_{v}=\{s\in\Gamma\mid\|\pi(s)v-v\|<\varepsilon_{d}\}
, then by the above paragraph,
B_{v}
generates an amenable group. Hence
\sup_{v}\nu(B_{v})\leq\sup\{\nu(A)\mid A\leq\Gamma\textnormal{ amenable}\}
, where
\nu:=\mu^{-1}*\mu
. Then

\|\pi(\mu)\|^{2}=\sup_{v}\langle\pi(\nu)v,v\rangle=\sup_{v}1-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}(\|\pi(s)v-v\|^{2})\leq 1-\frac{\varepsilon_{d}^{2}}{2}(1-\sup_{v}\nu(B_{v})).

Finally note that
\nu(A)=\mu^{-1}*\mu(A)\leq\sup_{g\in\Gamma}\mu(gA)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

If the

are finitely supported, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\cdots X_{n}=g)

\displaystyle=\langle\lambda_{\Gamma}(\mu_{1}*\cdots*\mu_{n})\delta_{e},\delta_{g}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\|\lambda_{\Gamma}(\mu_{i})\|_{\op}\leq e^{-2c_{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{\mathcal{L}}(\mu_{i})}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\beta_{\mathcal{L}}(\mu_{i}))^{c_{d}}.

where
c_{d}=\varepsilon_{d}^{2}/8
as follows from Theorem
7.6
. The family
\mathcal{L}
can be taken to be that of all virtually solvable algebraic subgroups of
\GL_{d}
, since by the Tits alternative
[

]
finitely generated amenable subgroups are virtually solvable. To extend the bound to arbitrary random variables, we apply Proposition
7.1
with
f=1_{\{g\}}
,

∎

7.4.
Proof of Corollary
1.5

We conclude by deducing Corollary
1.5
from Theorem
1.3
. In view of Lemma
3.4
, we may assume without loss of generality that
|S|\leq 2\dim\mathbb{G}-1
. Consider the uniform probability measure
\mu
on
S
. According to Proposition
3.3
there is
k\leq C(d)
such that
\mu^{k}
is not supported on a coset of a proper algebraic subgroup of
\mathbb{G}
. In particular
\beta:=\beta(\mu^{k})\geq|S|^{-k}\geq(2\dim\mathbb{G}-1)^{-k}
and Theorem
1.3
applies to
n
i.i.d. variables with law

(
1.2
)

\mu^{kn}(g^{-1}V)\leq d^{1/2}N\exp(-c\beta n).
