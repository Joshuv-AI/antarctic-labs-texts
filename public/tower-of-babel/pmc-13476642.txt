Strengthening participatory migration health research in a changing political environment
Authors: Asres Ioab Kolahta, Nababan Herfina, Trummer Ursula, Amy Jane Stevens, Bonneau Louise, Offe Johanna, Bezawit Temesgen Sima, Eick Frode, Correia Miguel, Mardin Deniz, Sevik Irem, Öcek Zeliha
Published: 2026

Across Europe, rising anti-migrant sentiment is reshaping migration governance and public discourse. Rather than recognised as a potential for development and a matter of human rights, migration is increasingly framed as a security issue.1 This shift is reflected in restrictive migration policies, the criminalisation of migrants and organisations supporting them, and the growing use of health systems as instruments of migration control.1, 2, 3, 4 These developments hinder migrants' access to healthcare, while declining support for civil society organisations (CSOs) and migration health research undermines participatory and cross-sector migration health research.3

To address these challenges, the Network for Research on Migration and Health (NeRMiH), a complementary initiative supporting existing migration health networks5,6 through dialogue and knowledge exchange, convened an international roundtable in May 2026, bringing together migrant communities, CSOs, and academia. The discussions identified three interconnected barriers and highlighted four priorities.

The first barrier is a fragmented research ecosystem. Researchers, CSOs, migrant communities, and policymakers often operate in separate systems with different priorities, timelines, and institutional logics, limiting collaboration and knowledge co-production. Academic research produces evidence too slowly to inform rapidly changing policy contexts, by which time policy windows may already have closed. Although CSOs play a bridging role by connecting community knowledge with research, policy, and practice, this work remains under-resourced and under-recognised.

Second, institutional structures and research funding do not recognise lived experience as a legitimate form of knowledge. Migrants frequently remain sources of data or consultation rather than equal partners in shaping research agendas, implementation, governance, data interpretation, and evidence use. Initiatives such as the Migrant Health Community Research Network face funding and institutional barriers.7 Excluding migrant communities risks reproducing evidence gaps and leaving their realities structurally underrepresented, particularly for those experiencing intersecting structural vulnerabilities based on residence status, age, disability, race/ethnicity, socio-economic position, housing, and immigration detention.

Third, meaningful CSO participation remains inadequately supported by institutional structures and research funding. CSOs' contributions often involve invisible labour, including recruitment, translation, trust-building, contextual interpretation, advocacy, safeguarding, and emotional support, that is seldom recognised through funding, authorship, institutional credit, or decision-making power. Consequently, CSOs lack the protected time and resources needed to engage meaningfully in research collaboration.

Addressing these barriers requires changes in how participatory and cross-sector migration health research is organised, conducted, and supported. First, research should move beyond project-based collaboration towards long-term partnerships among researchers, migrant communities, CSOs, and policymakers, recognising CSOs' role in connecting community knowledge with research, policy, and practice. Funders should incentivise such collaborations, while researchers should acknowledge them in publications. These partnerships should co-produce timely, relevant evidence from agenda-setting to policy and practice, as demonstrated by the National Health Advisors programme of Doctors of the World UK, which embeds people with lived experience of migration as equal partners in health policy, advocacy, and practice.8

Second, migrants' lived experience should be recognised as valuable knowledge, and migrants as equal partners in research, with responsibility shared by researchers, universities, and funders. This requires moving beyond consultation to shared decision-making in agenda-setting, governance, interpretation, and dissemination. Meaningful participation requires translation, accessible and safe spaces, flexibility, sustained support, and remuneration,9 planned and budgeted from the outset rather than treated as an afterthought. Otherwise, participation risks remaining limited to those already able to navigate academic and institutional systems.

Third, participatory research should generate timely, relevant evidence that responds to policy and practice, drawing on the knowledge of CSOs and migrant communities about where and when needed. Academic timelines, shaped by funding and publication incentives, should better align with policy windows for evidence-informed decision-making. From the outset, research should plan policy engagement and knowledge translation with CSOs, migrant communities, and policymakers. This can strengthen ownership of research recommendations, promote evidence-informed policymaking, and help counter misinformation and exclusionary narratives.

Finally, meaningful participation and cross-sector collaboration require institutional support, including remuneration for migrant participants, time for relationship-building, funding for CSO research collaboration, and recognition of CSOs' bridging work. Funding mechanisms, authorship practices, and institutional incentives should support equitable participation and long-term partnerships over tokenistic collaboration.

In today's political environment, participatory and cross-sector migration health research is no longer a methodological choice but a prerequisite for socially accountable evidence. Migration health research risks generating rigorous evidence disconnected from the realities it seeks to address unless lived experience and migrant and CSO expertise are recognised as integral forms of knowledge. This also requires asking who benefits from research, who has access to its findings, and how evidence is shared with and returned to migrant communities and CSOs.
