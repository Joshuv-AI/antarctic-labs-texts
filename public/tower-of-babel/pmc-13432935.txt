Comparative Prognostic Assessment of European Kidney Function Consortium Equations for Mortality Prediction: A Population-Based Cohort Study
Authors: Sitong Chen, Jinjing Shi, Iokfai Cheang, Gengmin Liang, Qiang Qu, Ziqi Chen, Huaxin Yuan, Yiyang Fu, Shengen Liao, Xinli Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe European Kidney Function Consortium (EKFC) has introduced equations for estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) using serum creatinine (Cr) and cystatin C (Cys). While their diagnostic accuracy is established, their comparative prognostic value for long-term mortality in the general population is not well defined. This study compared the prognostic performance of three EKFC equations – creatinine-based (EKFC-eGFRcr), Cys-based (EKFC-eGFRcys), and the combined (EKFC-eGFRcrcys) model – for predicting all-cause and cardiovascular (CV) mortality.MethodsWe analyzed a population-based cohort of 4,519 participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 1999–2002. eGFR was calculated using all three EKFC variations (EKFC-eGFRcr, EKFC-eGFRcys, and EKFC-eGFRcrcys). The primary and secondary endpoints were all-cause and CV mortality, respectively, through December 31, 2019. Predictive improvements were evaluated using integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and number needed to measure (NNM).ResultsThe eGFR value calculated by the EKFC-eGFRcys, EKFC-eGFRcr, and EKFC-eGFRcrcys was 78.92 ± 22.98, 84.45 ± 23.21, and 81.68 ± 22.13 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively. Over a median follow-up of 211 months, all three eGFR models were independent predictors of mortality (all p < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline regression revealed significant non-linear, inverse associations between all eGFR measures and adjusted mortality risks (all p for non-linearity < 0.001). Compared to EKFC-eGFRcr, EKFC-eGFRcys significantly improved risk reclassification for all-cause mortality (categorical NRI = 15.9%; 95% CI: 14.1–17.8%; NNM = 7). The combined EKFC-eGFRcrcys also outperformed the Cr-only model (NRI = 8.9%; NNM = 12). However, EKFC-eGFRcys demonstrated modestly better performance than the combined equation (NRI = 7.0%; NNM = 15), suggesting limited incremental value in adding Cr to Cys. The superiority of EKFC-eGFRcys was primarily driven by improved classification of non-events.ConclusionThe EKFC-eGFRcys equation provides superior prognostic value for all-cause and CV mortality compared to both Cr-based and combined EKFC equations. Notably, the combined model offered no prognostic advantage over Cys alone. These findings highlight the clinical importance of Cys-based eGFR for accurate risk stratification in the general population.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), defined by a sustained decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2) or evidence of renal impairment for at least 3 months [1], remains a critical global health burden associated with high morbidity and mortality. Patients with reduced renal function face a significantly elevated risk of both all-cause and cardiovascular (CV) death [2]. As of 2024, the median global prevalence of CKD is 9.5%, accounting for 2.4% of total deaths, with a disability-adjusted life year rate of 491.4 per 100,000 population [3].

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is the premier indicator of renal function, essential for diagnosing CKD and determining the necessity of renal replacement therapy (GFR < 20 mL/min/1.73 m2) [4]. Beyond diagnosis, GFR serves as a powerful predictor of CV outcomes and mortality [5, 6]. While measuring GFR via the clearance of exogenous markers (e.g., inulin, iohexol) is the gold standard [7], its cost and complexity preclude widespread clinical use. Consequently, eGFR equations based on endogenous markers like serum creatinine (Cr) and cystatin C (Cys) are the clinical standard.

The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines currently recommend the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equations [8, 9]. However, the inclusion of race as a biological variable in these models has sparked significant debate regarding clinical bias and equity [10]. Studies have shown that when using race-based coefficients, black patients may be assigned higher eGFR values than non-black patients with the same Cr levels, potentially delaying access to kidney transplants or specialty care [11]. For example, removing the race coefficient in the 2021 CKD-EPI equation resulted in a median eGFR decrease of 3.7 mL/min/1.73 m2 for black adults, reclassifying many into more advanced stages of CKD [12].

To address these limitations, the European Kidney Function Consortium (EKFC) developed the EKFC-eGFRcr equation [13], which utilizes rescaled Cr levels to control for variations in age and sex without requiring a race-based coefficient. The EKFC model has demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy compared to CKD-EPI and has shown promising prognostic value in specific cohorts [14–16]. Notably, Pottel et al. [15] found that EKFC equations based on cystatin C (EKFC-eGFRcys) are independent of both race and sex, enhancing assessment accuracy. Furthermore, Ye et al. [16] observed that EKFC-eGFRcys outperformed both the Cr-based EKFC and CKD-EPI models in predicting mortality.

Cys is a low-molecular-weight protein produced at a constant rate by all nucleated cells; unlike Cr, it is less influenced by muscle mass, age, or diet, making it a potentially more reliable indicator of kidney function. Inker et al. [17] observed that Cys-based equations (both CKD-EPI and EKFC) outperformed Cr-only models across diverse demographic subgroups. However, while the diagnostic performance of EKFC equations is well-characterized, their comparative prognostic value for long-term mortality risk stratification in the general population remains unclear. Specifically, it is unknown whether the EKFC-eGFRcys equation provides superior risk prediction compared to the Cr-based equation, or if a combined equation (EKFC-eGFRcrcys) offers incremental benefit.

Understanding the variances in these equations is crucial for refining mortality risk assessment. Therefore, we hypothesized that EKFC-eGFRcys would provide superior discrimination and reclassification for all-cause and CV mortality compared to EKFC-eGFRcr and EKFC-eGFRcrcys in the general US population.

Methods

Study Design

This study utilized publicly available data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted during the period 1999–2002 (http://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes). NHANES is a comprehensive program aimed at evaluating the health and nutritional status of the US population. Each 2-year cycle of NHANES involves data collection from a nationally representative sample of approximately 10,000 individuals. Participants undergo household interviews, physical examinations, and collection of biological specimens at mobile centers.

Among 21,004 participants in the combined survey, data on Cr and Cys were obtained from 5,263 participants. Participants were excluded if they were aged <18 years or if they were ineligible for mortality linkage or had missing follow-up information in the public-use linked mortality files (n = 744). After these exclusions, 4,519 participants constituted the final analytic cohort (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart.

Covariates

Information was collected on socio-demographic factors (sex, age, education status, ethnicity, marital status, body mass index), lifestyle (socioeconomic status, smoking status, and physical activity), CV risk factors, and comorbidities (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes). Body measurements were collected in the mobile centers by trained health technicians.

Assessment of Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate

In NHANES 1999–2002, Cr was measured using a Hitachi Model 917 multichannel analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Cys was measured using the Dade Behring N Latex Cys assay, which is an automated particle-enhanced nephelometric assay (PENIA) run on the Dade Behring Nephelometer II (BNII) (Finney et al. [18]). eGFR was calculated using the EKFC equations. EKFC-eGFRcys and EKFC-eGFRcr represent eGFR calculated separately using Cys and Cr-based equations, respectively (see online suppl. Tables S1 and S2; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552852). EKFC-eGFRcrcys is computed as the arithmetic mean of EKFC-eGFRcr and EKFC-eGFRcys.

Endpoints and Follow-Up

The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality, and the secondary endpoint was CV mortality, which is defined using ICD-10 codes for diseases of the heart (I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51) and cerebrovascular diseases (I60–I69) during the follow-up. The mortality data for the current study were extracted from the National Death Index death certificate records provided by National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), and the linked mortality files were updated to December 31, 2019. Follow-up time (in person-months) was calculated from the baseline interview to the date of death or the end of the study period, whichever occurred first.

Statistical Analysis

Participants were divided into two groups according to survival status. Continuous variables are presented as the mean (standard deviation) and were compared using Student’s t-test. Categorical variables are presented as the frequency (percentage) and were compared using the chi-square test.

The eGFR values calculated by the EKFC-eGFRcys, EKFC-eGFRcr, and EKFC-eGFRcrcys were compared using a paired t-test. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to compare all-cause and CV mortality between groups. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to examine the associations between the eGFR and all-cause and CV mortality. Variables with a p value <0.05 in univariate analysis were further incorporated into multivariable adjusted models (Model 1: adjusted for age and sex; Model 2: adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, education level, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, angina, heart attack, stroke, cancer). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were fitted to evaluate potential non-linear relationships between eGFR and the risk of all-cause and CV mortality. Receiver operator characteristic curves and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to assess the predictive value of the different eGFR equations.

To evaluate the incremental predictive value of Cys-based eGFR over Cr-based eGFR for all-cause and CV mortality, we calculated the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and the net reclassification improvement (NRI). Pairwise comparisons were performed between all three equations to evaluate incremental predictive value. The NRI was calculated using a predefined risk threshold of 10% [19] for all-cause mortality, based on predictions from Cox models. Participants were categorized as low risk (<10%) or high risk (≥10%). Number needed to measure (NNM) was calculated to quantify the clinical utility of adopting the new equations. Based on the categorical NRI derived from the 10% risk threshold, NNM was defined as the reciprocal of the absolute net reclassification gain. The NNM values were rounded up to the nearest integer to provide a conservative estimate.

All statistical analyses were consistent with the analytic guidelines of NHANES (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/analyticguidelines.aspx) and conducted in R software (version 4.2.1; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

In this study, 4,519 participants were enrolled, with a median follow-up time of 211 months (IQR: 123–228 months). Of these participants, 2,060 (45.6%) died during the follow-up period, with 549 (12.1%) attributed to CV disease. Baseline characteristics were compared between the two groups stratified by the endpoint event of all-cause death. Significant differences were observed in age, gender, race, education level, SBP, DBP, NT-proBNP, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, angina, heart attack, stroke, cancer) between these groups (Table 1).

eGFR Values

The mean values and standard deviations of eGFR were calculated using different equations. For EKFC-eGFRcr, the mean eGFR was 78.92 ± 22.98 mL/min/1.73 m2, while for the EKFC-eGFRcys, it was 84.45 ± 23.21 mL/min/1.73 m2. The mean eGFR calculated by EKFC-eGFRcrcys was 81.68 ± 22.13 mL/min/1.73 m2. Notably, significant differences were observed among these eGFR values (Fig. 2, all p < 0.001).

Comparison of the eGFR calculated by the European Kidney Function Consortium creatinine equation (EKFC-eGFRcr), the European Kidney Function Consortium cystatin C equation (EKFC-eGFRcys), and arithmetic mean of the two parameters (EKFC-eGFRcrcys). ***p < 0.001.

Association between eGFR and Mortality

The Kaplan-Meier survival curves demonstrated that there was a consistent and graded inverse association between eGFR levels and survival probability across all three equations. The lowest eGFR group (<60 mL/min/1.73 m2) exhibited the poorest survival throughout the follow-up period for both all-cause and CV mortality. Progressively higher survival probabilities were observed among participants with eGFR levels of 60 to <90, 90 to <120, and ≥120 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively. This difference between groups was statistically significant (all log-rank p < 0.001; Fig. 3). Collectively, these findings indicate that lower eGFR, regardless of the EKFC equation used, is associated with increased risks of both all-cause and CV mortality.

Univariate and Multivariate Cox Regression Analyses

In univariate analysis, multiple traditional risk factors and all three eGFR metrics were significantly associated with both all-cause and CV mortality (all p < 0.001; Table 2). In multivariable analysis (Table 3), lower eGFR remained independently associated with increased mortality after full adjustment (Model 2). For participants with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (vs. 60 to <90), the HRs for all-cause mortality were 1.75 (95% CI: 1.57–1.95) for EKFC-eGFRcys, 1.26 (95% CI: 1.13–1.40) for EKFC-eGFRcr, and 1.49 (95% CI: 1.34–1.66) for EKFC-eGFRcrcys. For CV mortality, the corresponding HRs were 2.17 (95% CI: 1.76–2.67), 1.41 (95% CI: 1.14–1.73), and 1.70 (95% CI: 1.37–2.12), respectively (all p < 0.001). For eGFR 90 to <120 mL/min/1.73 m2, only EKFC-eGFRcys showed significantly lower risks for both all-cause (HR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.61–0.95) and CV mortality (HR: 0.49, 95% CI: 0.29–0.83), while associations for the other equations were non-significant. Estimates for eGFR ≥120 mL/min/1.73 m2 were imprecise due to the low number of events, with no CV deaths observed in this group.

RCS regression indicated a stable inverse association between the EKFC-eGFRcys and adjusted all-cause and CV mortality (Fig. 4a, d). Furthermore, RCS regression revealed non-linear, U-shaped associations between EKFC-eGFRcr and adjusted all-cause and CV mortality (p for non-linearity < 0.001; Fig. 4b, e). RCS regression also demonstrated inverse associations between the EKFC-eGFRcrcys with adjusted all-cause and CV mortality (Fig. 4c, f).

Prediction Values of the EKFC Equations

The prognostic value of the three equations of EKFC-eGFRcys, EKFC-eGFRcr, and EKFC-eGFRcrcys, and the mean of both equations was assessed through receiver operator characteristic curve analysis, with the AUC as the metric. The AUC values were as follows: EKFC-eGFRcys (AUC: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.82–0.85) and EKFC-eGFRcrcys (AUC: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.81–0.83) showed higher values compared to EKFC-eGFRcr (AUC: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.76–0.79) for both all-cause mortality and CV mortality. Specifically, for CV mortality, the AUC values were EKFC-eGFRcys: 0.91(95% CI: 0.89–0.92), EKFC-eGFRcrcys: 0.89 (95% CI: 0.88–0.91), and EKFC-eGFRcr: 0.85 (95% CI: 0.83–0.87). This suggests that EKFC-eGFRcys exhibited better specificity and sensitivity in mortality stratification (Fig. 5).

Receiver operator characteristic curve analysis for the prediction of 10-year all-cause mortality (a) and CV mortality (b). AUC, areas under the curve.

The 10-year decision curves showed similar results, indicating that all three formulas were useful for the prediction of all-cause and CV mortality. The net benefit of EKFC-eGFRcys and EKFC-eGFRcrcys was better than that of EKFC-eGFRcr between threshold probabilities of 0–0.7 (Fig. 6).

Reclassification and Discrimination Improvements

EKFC-eGFRcys to EKFC-eGFRcr

Compared with the EKFC-eGFRcr equation, the EKFC-eGFRcys equation significantly improved risk prediction at 10 years (Table 4). For all-cause mortality, integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) was 6.2% (95% CI: 4.9–7.2%) and continuous NRI of 25.8% (95% CI: 19.5–29.5%). For CV mortality, the IDI was 7.7% (95% CI: 5.6–9.6%) and continuous NRI 27.7% (95% CI: 17.8–34.5%) (all p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive comparison of the prognostic utility of three EKFC equations for all-cause and CV mortality in a general population. All three equations emerged as independent mortality predictors. Notably, EKFC-eGFRcys demonstrated superior risk discrimination and reclassification compared to EKFC-eGFRcr, with a categorical NRI of 15.9% and a low NNM of 7 for all-cause mortality. This translates to a significant clinical impact: for every 1,000 individuals assessed using Cys instead of Cr, approximately 159 would be correctly reclassified. Interestingly, EKFC-eGFRcys also modestly outperformed the combined equation (NRI 7.0%; NNM 15), suggesting that adding Cr to Cys provides limited incremental prognostic value in this cohort.

Comparison with Previous Studies

Our findings align with and extend those of Ye et al. [16], who showed that EKFC-eGFRcys outperformed CKD-EPI equations in the same NHANES cohort. While Ye et al. focused on cross-family comparisons (EKFC vs. CKD-EPI), our study provides a granular intra-family analysis. The superiority of Cys-based eGFR aligns with its biological profile as a marker less influenced by muscle mass, age, and diet than Cr [20], thus offering a more accurate reflection of true kidney function [17, 21].

Several previous studies have examined the performance of Cr-Cys combined equation. Fu et al. [22] found that eGFR equations incorporating both markers performed best for GFR estimation in a Swedish cohort, with minimal variation among equations. Similarly, Nateghi Haredasht et al. [23] observed that Cys-based eGFR detected more CKD events than Cr-based eGFR in patients with acute kidney injury, with eGFRcys serving as a predictor of mortality. Unlike these prior studies that focused primarily on diagnostic accuracy for CKD, our analysis uniquely quantifies the long-term clinical impact of switching between EKFC variants using IDI, NRI, and NNM metrics.

Performance of the Combined Equation

A pivotal finding is that the combined EKFC-eGFRcrcys equation did not outperform Cys-based equation. This contrasts with reports indicating that EKFC-eGFRcrcys offers superior diagnostic accuracy for measured GFR [15, 22]. This highlights a critical distinction: while the combined equation may provide the most accurate snapshot of current filtration, Cys-based equation appears superior for capturing long-term mortality risk.

As mentioned, Cys is produced at a constant rate by all nucleated cells and is relatively unaffected by muscle mass. Beyond its role as a filtration marker, elevated Cys levels are linked to systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and preclinical CV damage. Consequently, the biological advantage of Cys likely stems from its independence from non-renal factors such as sarcopenia or dietary protein intake. These factors often attenuate the prognostic accuracy of Cr-based models [15]. The stronger association with CV mortality observed here further suggests that Cys may capture cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic risk factors that remain undetected by Cr.

Practical Considerations

The primary prognostic gain stems from the inclusion of Cys itself; the NNM of 7 for EKFC-eGFRcys versus EKFC-eGFRcr reinforces this. Mechanistically, this improvement was driven by the correct “down-classification” of survivors (non-events). Specifically, 19.4% of survivors were correctly reclassified from high risk to low risk when using Cys-based equation, while only 3.3% were incorrectly moved upward. By reducing false positives, EKFC-eGFRcys could help clinicians avoid unnecessary interventions and reduce patient anxiety.

From a practical standpoint, the NNM of 15 suggests that measuring both biomarkers provides limited incremental prognostic benefit over Cys alone. This suggests that for general population risk stratification, a dual-marker approach may increase certain laboratory costs without significantly enhancing the predictive accuracy.

Future Directions

These benefits must be balanced against the higher cost and limited availability of Cys in some settings. Future research should focus on cost-effectiveness analyses to determine the optimal threshold for adopting Cys-based eGFR across different healthcare systems. Prospective studies are needed to examine whether Cys-guided risk stratification translates into improved clinical outcomes and reduced unnecessary interventions. In addition, further work should identify specific subgroups that may benefit more from the combined equation. Examples include individuals with extremes of muscle mass, malnutrition, or inflammatory conditions. In these populations, the prognostic advantage over Cys-based equation could be more pronounced.

Limitations

While this study demonstrates the superior prognostic value of EKFC-eGFRcys, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, kidney function was assessed via a single baseline measurement, precluding the analysis of eGFR trajectories over time. Second, the lack of albuminuria data in the public-use NHANES files for this period represents a significant limitation as albuminuria is a key component of CKD staging and its absence may lead to residual confounding in risk stratification. Third, Cys was measured only in a subsample of NHANES participants, which may affect the broader generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, the observational design limits our ability to establish causal relationships between EKFC-derived eGFR and mortality. Despite comprehensive adjustment for traditional risk factors, residual confounding from unmeasured variables remains possible. Consequently, external validation in diverse, contemporary cohorts is essential to confirm these results.

Conclusion

The Cys-based EKFC equation provides superior risk discrimination and reclassification for all-cause and CV mortality in the general population compared to the Cr-based and the combined equations. Our findings suggest that the primary prognostic benefit of using the EKFC framework is derived from the incorporation of Cys, with the combined equation offering no significant incremental advantage. The clinical utility of EKFC-eGFRcys is particularly evident in its ability to reduce false-positive risk assessments among survivors. These results support the broader clinical adoption of Cys for more accurate mortality risk stratification and the optimization of healthcare resources.
