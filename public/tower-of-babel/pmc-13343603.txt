From early prevention to invasive treatment: dental care utilization and outcomes according to gestational age in preterm children
Authors: Han Gill Lee, Seong Joon Lee, Ko Eun Lee, Je Seon Song, Hyung-Jun Choi, Jaeho Lee, Chung-Min Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreterm birth is associated with long-term developmental vulnerability, including altered dental development and oral health. Although preterm children tend to visit dental clinics earlier and receive preventive care more frequently than full-term peers, it remains unclear whether such preventive-oriented utilization reduces the need for invasive dental treatment. This study aimed to evaluate dental care utilization patterns and clinical outcomes according to gestational age in preterm children.MethodsThis retrospective observational study analyzed electronic medical records of children who attended a tertiary pediatric dental clinic between January 2020 and December 2024. Participants were classified into four groups based on gestational age: extremely preterm (< 28 weeks), very preterm (28–31 weeks), late preterm (32–36 weeks), and full-term (≥ 37 weeks). Demographic characteristics, age at first dental visit, reasons for visits, preventive and therapeutic dental procedures, sedation and general anesthesia, and early extraction of primary molars were assessed. Group differences were evaluated using chi-square tests and one-way analysis of variance. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to estimate adjusted odds ratios for dental outcomes according to gestational age, controlling for sex, age at first dental visit, and residential area.ResultsA total of 7,352 children were included in the analysis. Preterm children, particularly those born extremely preterm, visited dental clinics at a younger age and received preventive interventions, such as fluoride application, more frequently than full-term children (p < 0.001). Despite higher preventive care utilization, extremely preterm children showed higher frequencies of invasive treatments, including tooth extraction and inpatient general anesthesia. Tooth developmental anomalies were significantly more prevalent in all preterm groups, with the highest risk observed in extremely preterm children (adjusted odds ratio 3.43, 95% confidence interval 2.23–5.26). Early extraction was selectively concentrated in the second primary molars among extremely preterm children.ConclusionsPreterm children, especially those born extremely preterm, demonstrate a distinct dental care trajectory characterized by early preventive utilization but an increased likelihood of invasive treatment once intervention becomes necessary. These findings highlight the importance of risk-stratified surveillance and tailored preventive strategies, with particular attention to developmentally vulnerable teeth, to optimize long-term oral health outcomes in preterm populations.

Introduction

Preterm birth remains a major global public health concern, accounting for a substantial proportion of neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide. Recent estimates indicate that more than 13 million infants are born preterm each year, and this burden persists across both high- and low-income countries despite advances in perinatal care [1]. Improvements in neonatal intensive care have markedly increased the survival of preterm infants [2], shifting focus toward long-term developmental outcomes and healthcare utilization throughout childhood [3]. While neurological and respiratory sequelae of prematurity have been extensively studied, oral and dental outcomes have received comparatively less attention, despite their potential impact on quality of life and long-term health.

Accumulating evidence suggests that children born preterm are at increased risk of adverse dental developmental outcomes, including enamel defects, delayed eruption, and altered tooth morphology [4–9]. Meta-analytic data indicate that the risk of developmental enamel defects is approximately threefold higher in preterm children than in those born at full term [10]. These structural disturbances may compromise enamel integrity and increase susceptibility to environmental challenges, potentially predisposing affected teeth to accelerated disease progression [11]. Dental care utilization patterns are clinically relevant in this context, as they reflect not only disease burden but also access to care, caregiver behavior, and the timing of preventive versus invasive interventions.

However, findings regarding the association between prematurity and dental caries remain inconsistent across studies. Several investigations have reported that although enamel defects and delayed dental development are more prevalent among preterm children, caries experience itself does not differ significantly from that of full-term children [12, 13]. In contrast, other investigations have reported a significantly increased risk of dental caries associated with preterm birth [14]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study design, age at assessment, access to care, and insufficient consideration of the degree of prematurity.

In parallel, studies from various healthcare systems have shown that preterm children tend to access dental services earlier and receive preventive interventions more frequently than full-term children, which may reflect differences in healthcare utilization patterns and proactive health-seeking behavior [15]. These findings raise an important but unresolved question: whether early and frequent preventive dental care in preterm children effectively mitigates the need for restorative or invasive treatment later in childhood. Most existing studies have treated preterm children as a homogeneous group on selected oral health indicators [4, 16], leaving a critical gap in understanding how the degree of prematurity influences preventive care use, invasive treatment occurrence, and risk of early tooth loss. Early loss of primary teeth represents a clinically meaningful outcome, as it may adversely affect occlusal development and long-term oral function.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate how gestational age influences dental care utilization patterns and treatment outcomes in preterm children using large-scale clinical data.

Methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This study was a single-center retrospective observational study using electronic medical records (EMRs) of pediatric patients who attended a tertiary pediatric dental clinic. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University Dental Hospital, Seoul, Republic of Korea (No. 2-2025-0013). Owing to the retrospective nature of this study and the use of de-identified clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Yonsei University Dental Hospital.

Study population

This retrospective observational study included children and adolescents who visited the Department of Pediatric Dentistry at a tertiary pediatric dental clinic at least once between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024. Only patients with available gestational age information at the initial dental visit were eligible for inclusion. Patients were excluded if dental treatment records were insufficient, birth information was missing, or if dental care was limited exclusively to permanent teeth. In addition, patients older than 72 months at their first dental visit were excluded to ensure the validity of analyses focusing on primary dentition–related treatments.

Data collection and variable definitions

Birth-related and sociodemographic information of the study participants was retrospectively collected through a review of EMRs. The collected variables included sex, date of birth, gestational age, birth weight, residential area, disability registration status, and age at first dental visit. Birth weight was recorded as a continuous variable and converted to grams (g) for analysis. The residential area was classified based on administrative district codes recorded in the EMR at the initial visit and categorized into capital area (Seoul) and non-capital area (all other regions). More detailed regional classifications were initially considered; however, several regions contained very small numbers of subjects, leading to substantial imbalance in sample sizes. Therefore, a dichotomized classification was used to enhance analytical stability and interpretability. This categorization was intended to reflect differences in access to tertiary healthcare services. Age at first dental visit was defined as the age at the first visit to the tertiary pediatric dental clinic included in this study, calculated as the interval between the date of birth and the date of the initial visit recorded in the institutional electronic medical record system (months).

Clinical oral characteristics assessed at the first dental visit included the presence of dental caries (recorded as a binary variable based on clinical diagnosis), tooth developmental anomalies, and malocclusion. Tooth developmental anomalies were defined as developmental defects of enamel, including enamel hypoplasia and hypomineralization, based on documented clinical findings in the EMR.

Variables related to dental care utilization and treatment included the reason for visit (multiple responses permitted) and the receipt of preventive and therapeutic dental procedures. Preventive procedures included fluoride application, sealant placement, and prophylaxis or scaling. Restorative treatments comprised glass ionomer restorations, resin restorations, and stainless-steel crowns. Pulp therapy included pulpotomy and pulpectomy, and surgical treatment included tooth extraction. Each treatment variable was defined as a binary outcome based on whether the procedure was performed at least once during the study period. In selected analyses, the number of procedures and the age at first occurrence were additionally considered.

Behavior management and anesthesia-related variables included the use of inpatient general anesthesia, outpatient general anesthesia, nitrous oxide sedation, and midazolam sedation. Each anesthesia or sedation modality was defined as present if it was administered at least once during the study period.

One of the primary outcome variables of this study, early extraction of primary molars, was defined as the removal of primary molars due to pathological causes—such as advanced dental caries or pulpal and periapical pathology—before the expected timing of physiological exfoliation associated with the eruption of permanent successors. The analysis was limited to first and second primary molars, and tooth identification was recorded using the Fédération Dentaire Internationale (FDI) notation system. Early extraction status was determined by considering the patient’s age at the time of extraction and the specific tooth involved, and cases corresponding to physiological exfoliation during the normal exfoliation period were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants according to gestational age groups. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (mean ± SD). Differences in categorical variables across gestational age groups were assessed using the chi-square (χ²) test, while continuous variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA).

To evaluate the association between gestational age and dental care utilization as well as oral health outcomes, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed, with the full-term group used as the reference category. Sex, age at first dental visit, and residential area were included as covariates in the adjusted models. Sex was included as a basic demographic characteristic, age at first dental visit was included because the timing of entry into dental care may influence preventive and therapeutic interventions. Residential area was included as a proxy indicator of potential differences in access to tertiary pediatric dental services. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Regarding sex distribution, the proportion of boys was slightly higher than that of girls across all gestational age groups; however, no statistically significant difference in sex distribution was observed among the groups (p = 0.177).

Birth weight differed significantly according to gestational age (p < 0.001). The mean birth weight was lowest in the EPT group (983 ± 543 g) and highest in the FT group (3,237 ± 416 g). As expected, mean gestational age also differed significantly across the four groups (p < 0.001).

When the residential area was categorized into capital area and non-capital area, a significant difference in distribution was observed across gestational age groups (p < 0.001). The proportion of participants residing in the capital area was 40.2% in the EPT group, 44.4% in the VPT group, 58.1% in the LPT group, and 59.2% in the FT group. Lower gestational age was associated with a relatively lower proportion of capital-area residence. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it may partly reflect referral patterns to tertiary care centers or other potential confounding factors rather than a direct relationship between gestational age and residential distribution.

Disability registration status also differed significantly among gestational age groups (p < 0.001). The proportion of registered disability was highest in the EPT group (7.4%), followed by the VPT group (2.8%), the LPT group (3.8%), and the FT group (1.8%), demonstrating a decreasing trend with increasing gestational age.

Age at first dental visit and patterns of reasons for dental visits

Clear differences were observed in the age at first dental visit and patterns of reasons for dental visits according to gestational age (Table 2). The mean age at first dental visit was significantly lower with decreasing gestational age (p < 0.001). The EPT group had the youngest mean age at first visit (34.1 ± 16.6 months), followed by the VPT group (38.6 ± 19.2 months), the LPT group (45.6 ± 18.6 months), and the FT group (48.2 ± 17.9 months), demonstrating a gradual increase with increasing gestational age.

Analysis of reasons for dental visits revealed significant differences in the distribution of visit purposes within gestational age groups (p < 0.001). Routine check-ups accounted for the largest proportion of visits in the EPT and VPT groups (49.4% and 44.4%, respectively), which were markedly higher than the proportion observed in the FT group (25.8%). In contrast, visits due to dental trauma and surgical reasons were more frequently observed among FT children (11.7% and 17.2%, respectively) than among EPT children (3.9% and 1.9%, respectively).

The proportion of visits prompted by dental caries was also slightly lower in the EPT (24.0%) and VPT (29.6%) groups than in the FT group (31.8%). Overall, these patterns indicate that preterm children, particularly those born extremely or very preterm, tended to present for dental care at an earlier age and were more likely to attend for routine examinations compared with full-term children, whereas full-term children were relatively more likely to present with treatment- or procedure-related concerns such as trauma or surgical conditions.

Figure 2A shows the timing of first primary tooth eruption across gestational age groups. A significant difference was observed (p < 0.001), with delayed eruption—defined as the eruption of the first primary tooth at or after 12 months of age—occurring more frequently among preterm children than among full-term children. Because eruption timing was analyzed using chronological age, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the differences may be attenuated when corrected age is applied.

Figure 2B presents the distribution of tooth developmental anomalies according to gestational age. A significant difference was observed across groups (p < 0.001). Compared with children without developmental anomalies, those with anomalies showed relatively higher proportions of preterm births, indicating a greater representation of preterm children in the anomaly group.

Figure 2C shows no significant difference in gestational age distribution according to the presence or absence of dental caries (p = 0.051), with similar proportions of extremely preterm children in both the caries and non-caries groups.

In contrast, Fig. 2D illustrates the distribution of malocclusion according to gestational age. A significant difference was observed (p = 0.046), with a relatively higher proportion of extremely preterm children in the malocclusion group compared with the normal occlusion group.

Figure 2E and 2F further examine the effect of birth weight within the extremely preterm group (gestational age < 28 weeks), focusing on extremely low birth weight (ELBW; <1000 g). Among the extremely preterm children (n = 122), 91 had ELBW (< 1000 g) and 31 had birth weight ≥ 1000 g. Within this subgroup, children with ELBW showed higher proportions of delayed first tooth eruption and tooth developmental anomalies compared with those with birth weight ≥ 1000 g.

Sedation/anesthesia patterns and treatment frequency by procedure

Sedation/anesthesia patterns and the frequency of dental treatments according to gestational age are summarized in Table 3; Fig. 3. Among preventive procedures, both fluoride application and dental prophylaxis differed significantly across gestational age groups (Fig. 3A). The mean number of fluoride applications was highest in the EPT group (3.63 ± 3.33), followed by the VPT group (1.86 ± 2.54), the LPT group (1.70 ± 2.78), and the FT group (1.26 ± 2.47) (p < 0.001), indicating that preventive fluoride treatment was performed more frequently in preterm subgroups than in the FT group. Dental prophylaxis was also performed more frequently in the EPT group (0.61 ± 1.08) than in the FT group (0.17 ± 0.62) (p < 0.001), with intermediate values observed in the VPT and LPT groups.

Inhalation sedation (nitrous oxide–oxygen) was most frequently used in the FT group (p = 0.035). Discussion

This study analyzed dental care utilization patterns and clinical treatment outcomes according to gestational age using a large-scale dataset derived from pediatric patients who attended a single tertiary pediatric dental center. For context, national birth statistics indicate that preterm births account for approximately 9–10% of all live births in Korea during 2020–2024. In the present cohort, preterm children accounted for 12.8% of the study population, which is slightly higher than the national prevalence. This relatively higher proportion may reflect referral patterns to tertiary care centers and increased healthcare utilization among medically vulnerable children.

Unlike previous studies that treated preterm children as a single homogeneous group, we stratified preterm birth into extremely preterm (EPT), very preterm (VPT), and late preterm (LPT) categories, enabling a more granular evaluation of how the degree of prematurity influences oral health risks and clinical trajectories. This approach moves beyond a dichotomous interpretation of prematurity and supports a risk continuum model in which oral health vulnerability increases progressively with decreasing gestational age, underscoring the need for risk-based oral health management strategies in preterm populations.

In the present study, preterm children—particularly those in the EPT and VPT groups—demonstrated significantly higher frequencies of tooth developmental anomalies compared with full-term children. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing a higher prevalence of enamel hypoplasia and hypomineralization among children born preterm [10, 17]. Perinatal hypoxia, metabolic imbalance, nutritional deficiencies, and exposure to intubation and medications during neonatal intensive care may directly disrupt amelogenesis during critical stages of enamel formation [9, 18–20]. Systemic stressors encountered during sensitive developmental windows are therefore likely to result in long-term structural alterations in tooth tissues, leading to reduced mechanical strength and persistent vulnerability to environmental challenges [21].

Delayed tooth eruption has also been reported among children born preterm. However, because the difference between chronological age and corrected age may reach several months in extremely preterm children—particularly during infancy—the interpretation of first primary tooth eruption should consider this potential discrepancy. When corrected age is applied, the timing of eruption in preterm children may appear later than indicated by chronological age alone.

Notably, however, this biological vulnerability did not translate directly into a higher frequency of dental caries diagnoses in preterm children. In fact, children in the EPT group visited dental clinics at a significantly younger age and received preventive interventions—such as fluoride application and dental prophylaxis—more frequently than their full-term counterparts. These patterns may reflect differences in healthcare utilization between preterm and full-term children, with preterm children potentially entering dental care pathways earlier. Preterm children often require frequent medical follow-up from infancy due to systemic health concerns [3], and this increased engagement with healthcare services may facilitate earlier entry into dental care and more frequent preventive dental visits. Collectively, these observations suggest that preventive-oriented dental care may be initiated relatively early among children born preterm.

Despite this proactive preventive care, our results indicate that once dental treatment became necessary, preterm children—particularly those born extremely preterm—were more likely to undergo invasive procedures. The frequency of early restorative treatments such as resin restorations did not differ substantially across gestational age groups and was, in some analyses, even lower in the EPT group. In contrast, invasive treatments, including pulpotomy and extraction, were significantly more frequent in the EPT group. These findings suggest that in preterm children, the clinical challenge may lie not in the occurrence of dental caries itself, but in the speed and severity of lesion progression. Teeth affected by developmental anomalies are structurally fragile and often exhibit irregular or rough surfaces, which may facilitate rapid caries progression or crown fracture once disease onset occurs, thereby narrowing the window for conservative intervention and increasing the likelihood of invasive treatment [22–24].

This pattern was particularly evident in the analysis of early primary tooth loss. While no gestational age–related differences were observed in early extraction of first primary molars, selective vulnerability was identified in second primary molars. Early extraction of specific second primary molars—most notably the maxillary (#55) and mandibular (#75) second primary molars—was significantly more frequent among preterm children, especially those in the EPT group. The calcification of second primary molars begins during late fetal life and continues shortly after birth [25], overlapping with periods of intense systemic stress and intensive care exposure in extremely preterm infants [20]. This temporal overlap may render second primary molars particularly susceptible to metabolic and environmental insults, leading to a higher concentration of developmental disturbances.

Although posterior primary molars are often considered more challenging for caregivers to clean during early childhood, the tooth-specific extraction pattern observed in this study suggests that brushing accessibility alone may not fully explain the distribution of extractions. Instead, multiple factors—including developmental enamel vulnerability, occlusal morphology, eruption timing, and clinical treatment decision-making—may contribute to the selective pattern of early second primary molar loss observed in extremely preterm children. When caries develops in teeth affected by developmental anomalies, progression may be more rapid than in structurally normal teeth, often resulting in early pulpal involvement, crown breakdown, and ultimately extraction. Thus, the observed pattern of early second primary molar loss likely reflects a combination of biological vulnerability and clinical management factors rather than a single explanatory mechanism. In addition, second primary molars play a critical role in maintaining arch length and occlusal stability during early childhood. Accordingly, their early loss may have more pronounced functional and developmental consequences than the early loss of other primary teeth. From a clinical perspective, this finding highlights the importance of closer monitoring and early intervention for second primary molars in preterm children, particularly those born extremely preterm.

The timing of early primary tooth extraction further supports this interpretation. In the EPT group, the first occurrence of early primary tooth loss was observed at a significantly younger age compared with other gestational age groups, indicating that dental developmental disturbances and disease progression may manifest clinically at earlier stages of life in extremely preterm children. Early loss of primary teeth may adversely affect occlusal development and space maintenance, potentially increasing the long-term risk of malocclusion [26].

Consistent with these findings, children in the EPT group had the highest number of treatment-related dental visits during the study period. This observation may reflect several factors, including earlier entry into dental care pathways and more frequent clinical monitoring in preterm populations. Although early access to preventive care appears common among children born preterm, structural dental vulnerability may contribute to repeated clinical interventions over time. At the same time, patterns of dental service utilization may also be influenced by healthcare access, caregiver health-seeking behavior, and referral patterns within tertiary care settings. Thus, dental service utilization patterns in preterm children likely reflect a combination of early healthcare engagement, biological vulnerability, and healthcare system–related factors rather than prematurity alone.

Sedation and anesthesia patterns further illustrate these clinical challenges. Preterm children—particularly those in the EPT group—were significantly more likely to receive treatment under inpatient general anesthesia rather than outpatient sedation. Conversely, outpatient sedation was more commonly used among full-term children. This difference may partly reflect variations in underlying systemic health conditions between the groups. Children born preterm, especially those born extremely preterm, often present with a higher prevalence of medical comorbidities that may require more cautious perioperative management and favor hospital-based anesthesia care.

In addition, developmental and behavioral characteristics observed in some preterm children may influence treatment planning. Preterm behavioral phenotypes, including heightened anxiety, attention difficulties, and sensory hypersensitivity, may complicate chairside behavior management in outpatient settings [27] and increase reliance on advanced behavior management strategies [28]. However, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously, as sedation and anesthesia decisions may also be influenced by institutional treatment pathways and clinical practice patterns within tertiary care settings.

Emerging evidence also suggests that preterm infants exhibit distinct oral microbiome profiles from birth, with differences that may persist for several months or longer [29–32]. Such early microbial dysbiosis may interact with structurally compromised tooth surfaces to accelerate caries progression, providing an additional biological explanation for the relatively high burden of invasive treatment and early tooth loss observed in preterm children despite active preventive care.

Although some previous studies have reported a higher overall caries risk among preterm children [14, 33], discrepancies across studies likely reflect heterogeneity within preterm populations and differences in study design. By stratifying preterm children according to gestational age and simultaneously examining the timing of first dental visit, preventive care utilization, treatment frequency and intensity, and the timing and distribution of early primary tooth loss, the present study provides a more nuanced understanding of how biological vulnerability and caregiver-driven healthcare utilization interact to shape oral health outcomes in preterm children.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The single-center, retrospective design may limit generalizability. Prematurity represents a heterogeneous condition, and variations in early-life clinical exposures may influence subsequent oral developmental outcomes. Detailed perinatal variables such as length of neonatal intensive care stay, duration of intubation, oxygen therapy, and precise nutritional status were not fully captured. These factors may affect enamel formation and early tooth development, and their absence in the present dataset limits our ability to explore potential biological pathways linking prematurity and dental developmental anomalies. In addition, behavioral factors such as home oral hygiene practices and dietary habits could not be directly assessed. Nevertheless, the large sample size and comprehensive clinical dataset allowed for a robust, gestational age–stratified analysis of dental utilization patterns and outcomes. Future multicenter or population-based cohort studies incorporating detailed perinatal variables may help clarify the mechanisms underlying these findings and further refine predictive models and tailored preventive programs for identifying and managing high-risk oral health trajectories in preterm children.

Conclusion

In this large-scale clinical study, children born preterm—particularly those born extremely preterm—showed distinct oral health trajectories characterized by structural dental vulnerabilities despite early and active preventive care. When treatment was required, lesions may have progressed more rapidly, potentially leading to a higher likelihood of invasive interventions and selective early loss of second primary molars. These findings highlight the importance of risk-stratified surveillance and tailored preventive strategies in preterm populations, with particular attention to the early identification and close monitoring of developmentally vulnerable teeth, especially second primary molars, to support timely intervention and preserve long-term occlusal development.
