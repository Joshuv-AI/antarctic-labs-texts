Single-stage foreskin-flap repair of an extensive post-circumcision urethrocutaneous fistula in a child: A case report
Authors: María Antonia Ocampo, Katherine Arias, Margarita Maria Chancy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe urethrocutaneous fistula is an uncommon complication after circumcision. Two stage urethroplasty is mostly use to correct long urethral defects, with good outcomes and no recurrence. We present the case of a 7-year-old male diagnosed with a urethrocutaneous fistula (UCF) after circumcision. The patient developed progressive voiding difficulty leading to acute urinary retention. A thermal injury of the penile urethra was identified. The patient underwent UCF resection and urethroplasty using a foreskin flap in a single-stage surgery without complications. Single-stage reconstruction is feasible for correcting long urethral defects in selected cases.Keywords: Circumcision, Fistula, Foreskin, Glans penis, Surgical flap

Introduction

Between 1999 and 2010, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 59.1% of newborn males in the United States underwent circumcision.1

Although generally considered a safe surgery, complications may arise. UCF is one of the less common complications and the usual management in infants is the two – stage uretrhoplasty.2 To date, there is no consensus of treatment for the use of the single - stage approach in large defects with the use of a foreskin flap, due the risk of recurrence. Nevertheless, as a result of limited access to healthcare, a case of urethroplasty using a foreskin flap and glans reconstruction in a single-stage surgery is reported.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 7-year-old preschool-aged male with a surgical history of umbilical herniorrhaphy and circumcision. One month after surgery, he presented to the emergency department on several occasions due to voiding difficulty, without response to medical management. Due to persistent symptoms he was referred to our institution. The urethral meatus was not visible at the glans, and a very small subcoronal opening was identified (Fig. 1), cystoscopy revealed a complete urethral disruption, precluding passage of even a ureteral guidewire. A vesicostomy was performed for urinary diversion and to relieve acute symptoms.

Preoperative findings of the urethrocutaneous fistula.

Another cystoscopy was performed through both the vesicostomy and the urethra; however, retrograde access through the urethral meatus was not possible due to its extremely narrow caliber. The lesion was found to be limited to the distal urethra. The patient underwent urethral reconstruction, during which a 2.5 cm complete disruption of the urethra from the glans was observed, likely secondary to thermal injury from electrocautery.

Through a circumcision incision, penile degloving was performed followed by dissection of the penile urethra and a ventral urethrotomy, revealing absence of the urethral plate. Dissection continued until the proximal penile urethra with healthy mucosa identified. To cover the defect, a tubularized foreskin flap was fashioned. A circumcision incision was made to obtain the flap. The external incision extended deep to the skin and superficial to the dartos fascia, down to the penile base. The internal incision extended to the dartos fascia and superficial to Buck's fascia. The entire foreskin was mobilized based on the dorsal dartos pedicle, supplying vascularization. The flap was then mobilized over the vascularized dartos pedicle. The inner foreskin was tubularized to form a neourethral tube, which was anastomosed to the proximal penile urethra and to the urethral meatus. Glans wings were developed, and glanular reconstruction was completed in two layers (Fig. 2a, b, 2c, 2d). The patient was discharged on postoperative day five.

a: Foreskin mobilization over the dartos flap. b: Tubularization of the foreskin over an 8 Fr Foley catheter. c: Proximal anastomosis between the tubularized foreskin flap and the proximal penile urethra. d: Intraoperative image after fistula repair and tubularized foreskin flap reconstruction.

During follow-up, the patient developed a complicated urinary tract infection associated with soft tissue infection. The flap was found to be viable, and adequately integrated.

The urethral catheter was removed 3 months after surgery. Closure of vesicostomy was six months after the last surgical intervention. At 12 months follow – up (Fig. 3a, b, 3c, 3d), the patient presents with adequate voiding through the urethral meatus (Q max: 13 mL/s, Q avg: 11.4 mL/s) (Fig. 4), without new urinary tract infections, pain or hematuria.

Discussion

The occurrence of UCF is most commonly associated with surgical correction of conditions such as hypospadias during the neonatal period, and less frequently as a complication of circumcision.1,3

Some authors report a higher rate of complications related to circumcision among newborns,4 while other studies describe a greater frequency of complications in preschool- and school-aged children.5 The prevalence of this complication specifically associated with circumcision is not clearly described in Latin America. In countries like Afghanistan, Algeria, Pakistan, complications have been linked to procedures performed by untrained personnel or inadequate knowledge of proper surgical techniques, depending on cultural practices.2,6

It is reported that UCF tends to appear within the first 30 days after surgery, according to a study conducted by Lucas et al.7 The fistula may result from a urethral incision, suture injury, thermal burns caused by electrocautery, or infection at the surgical site. These lesions are typically located in the ventral and distal portions of the penis, where the urethra is closest to the skin.8 If the vascular supply is compromised, ischemia and tissue necrosis may occur, leading to the formation of a fistula; explaining why coronal fistulas are usually smaller compared to those in the distal penis.2

Several surgical techniques have been described, including primary closure using Thiersch - Duplay urethroplasty, inverted flap urethroplasty, pedicled island tube flap, and onlay flap techniques. Repairs using local flaps are preferred in cases of a single fistula with intact corpus spongiosum.2

A major surgical challenge is achieving watertight closure of large defects, as in the present case, in which the urethral defect measured 2.5 cm. A fasciocutaneous flap can be mobilized from the distal penis and used as a ventral onlay flap to close the defect over a Foley catheter. Skin coverage is achieved by mobilizing the remaining penile skin.9 Quartey and McAninch described the use of penile fasciocutaneous flaps for urethral reconstruction, based on the axial superficial blood supply of the dartos fascia.10,11

Conclusion

UCF represents a challenge for pediatric urologists, not only because of the outcomes, but the whole process greatly impacts the patient's and family's quality of life. This case highlights the importance of including this entity in the differential diagnosis of atypical urinary symptoms in patients who have undergone circumcision, in order to provide timely intervention and achieve both functional and aesthetic outcomes with a single stage reconstruction. Also representing an alternative for patients with limited access to healthcare where optimal functional outcomes and low complication rates can be ensured.
