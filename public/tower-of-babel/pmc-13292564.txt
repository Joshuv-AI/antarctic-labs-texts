A comparative study on meat quality traits and metabolomic profiles of different duck breeds following water boiling
Authors: Zhengyu Fan, Xiujuan Wu, Hongyu Liu, Ziang Chen, Zhao Yang, Hehe Liu, Liang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuck meat and broth are widely consumed for their distinctive flavors, which are significantly influenced by breed—a critical determinant of consumer preference. Using untargeted metabolomics, this study identified key molecular compounds underlying flavor differences in cooked meat and broth across duck breeds: Cherry Valley Duck (CV), Jinling White Duck (JL), and Liancheng White Duck (LD). The results revealed that JL exhibited intermediate levels of flavor-related metabolites—including cysteine, 4‑butyl‑5-propylthiazole, furaneol A, and several dipeptides—compared to CV and LD, which may contribute to its more balanced and appealing flavor profile. Water boiling induced significant biochemical transformations in the meat, ultimately shaping the characteristic flavor profiles of both the cooked meat and the resulting broth. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that amino acid metabolism, nucleotide catabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and carbohydrate catabolism were the primary metabolic pathways underlying breed-related flavor differences and flavor evolution during cooking. This study demonstrates that untargeted metabolomics is an effective tool for elucidating the molecular basis of meat flavor formation and transformation, offering valuable insights for future research on poultry flavor profiling and the impact of thermal processing on meat quality.Keywords: Ducks, Water boiling, Meat flavor, Meat broth, Untargeted metabolomics

Introduction

Meat quality and flavor are key determinants of consumer purchasing decisions, as they directly determine palatability and general consumer acceptability. Meat flavor originates mainly from lipids and low-molecular-weight water-soluble compounds (Arshad et al., 2018). The characteristic bloody odor of raw meat diminishes during cooking, where Maillard reactions and lipid oxidation synergistically generate distinct flavor compounds (Sohail et al., 2022). Thermal processing has been shown to promote the formation of sulfur-containing compounds, which are crucial for meat flavor (Indriani et al., 2024). Metabolomics enables the profiling of small molecules in meat, such as amino acids and fatty acids, and helps elucidate their roles in meat quality (Ramanathan et al., 2023). It also provides critical insights into how processing affects meat flavor. In metabolomic studies of meat, differential metabolites—including l-palmitoylcarnitine, l-threonine, N-acetyl-l-aspartate, choline, and succinate—have been identified as contributors to variations in meat quality and flavor (Zhao et al., 2024), underscoring their potential as biomarkers for distinguishing meat characteristics.

Cooking induces profound transformations in taste and aroma, resulting in flavor profiles that are highly preferred by consumers. Studies reveal significant changes in fatty acids and water-soluble low-molecular-weight compounds in chicken following boiling (Xiao et al., 2019). Metabolomic analysis of raw and cooked donkey meat by Li et al. revealed 37 differential metabolites; maltotriose, l-glutamate, and l-proline have been proposed as major contributors to the characteristic umami and sweet taste of cooked donkey meat (Li et al., 2022), highlighting the dynamic nature of flavor development during thermal treatment. Similarly, Han et al. identified 12 volatile compounds—such as (E,E)-2,4-decadienal, ethyl hexanoate, dimethyl disulphide, and hexanaletc—in boiled pork from different pig breeds, suggesting their utility as potential flavor markers for discriminating cooked pork products (Han et al., 2020). These identified differential metabolites and flavor markers offer a scientific basis for future improvements in meat quality traits of livestock and poultry.

It is widely acknowledged that variations in meat quality and flavor can be attributed to differences in breeds. Previous studies have revealed differences in meat traits, nutrient composition, and metabolomes among duck breeds/strains (Aronal et al., 2012; Li et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2022). China’s unique geographical and historical context has fostered a rich diversity of indigenous duck breeds. Simmering local duck into soup is a deeply rooted culinary tradition across many regions of China. The Liancheng white duck (LD), a rare native poultry breed in China, is valued for its distinct sensory properties—particularly its flavor and texture—as well as its traditionally recognized health benefits (Zhou et al., 2022). In contrast, the Cherry Valley duck (CV), a commercial meat-type breed, is characterized by rapid growth and high carcass yield. The Jinling White duck (JL), a recently developed breed, was derived from crossing purebred LD with CV (Zhang et al., 2024). Significant differences in meat quality and metabolites were observed between CV and LD (Zhang et al., 2023). There are significant differences among these three breeds in terms of meat quality and flavor, but the sources of these differences remain unknown, especially the extent of improvement in the hybrid offspring (JL) of CV and LD compared to CV. Nevertheless, the key metabolites responsible for meat quality and flavor differences among JL, LD, and CV—particularly before and after boiling—remain to be elucidated.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically compare meat quality traits, metabolite profiles, and metabolic changes in breast meat from CV, JL, and LD before and after boiling, using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)- and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based untargeted metabolomics combined with conventional meat quality trait determinations. The results are expected to provide a theoretical basis for optimizing meat duck breeding strategies and enhancing breed-specific flavor qualities.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the national standard “Guideline on the Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of China. The experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Approval ID: DKY20170913).

Sample collection and preparation

A total of 27 eight-week-old ducks from LD, CV and JL were used in this study, with 9 ducks of each breed. Following a 12-hour fasting period, the ducks were humanely slaughtered, and both pectoralis muscles were rapidly excised. Both pectoral muscle tissues were stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis, while slaughter indices were collected concurrently.

Approximately 5 g of breast muscle (central part of the left breast muscle) was boiled in ultrapure water at 100 °C for 15 min. After cooking, the meat samples were immediately cooled to room temperature to minimize post-cooking biochemical changes. For each breed, 9 ducks were slaughtered and allocated to 3 biological replicates, with 3 ducks per replicate (n = 3), and their meat samples were applied to the measurement of meat quality traits. Concurrently, 5 mL of broth from each cooking group was collected and transferred into EP tubes, labeled according to breed. Samples from each raw meat group, cooked meat group, and broth were separately pooled into composite samples for untargeted metabolomic analysis. For subsequent chemical composition analysis, meat samples were determined by GC-MS, whereas broth samples were analyzed via LC-MS.

Meat quality evaluation

Color parameters (L* [lightness], a* [redness], and b* [yellowness]) of raw meat samples were measured on the inner surface using a chromameter (Model LS173, Shenzhen Linshang Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). within 45 min post-slaughter, three readings were taken at the upper, middle, and lower sections of each sample, and the average value was calculated as the final result.

Tenderness was evaluated according to the Chinese National Standard NY/T 1180-2006 (Ministry of Agriculture of the People’s Republic of China, 2006). Briefly, raw meat samples were trimmed parallel to muscle fibers into 3 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm rectangular pieces, free of tendons, fat, and epimysium. Shear force was measured with a TVT-300XP texture analyzer equipped with a standard blade, positioned perpendicular to the muscle fiber direction to ensure uniform cross-sectional area. Three replicate measurements were performed per sample, and the average value was recorded as the final tenderness score.

Moisture, intramuscular fat, and protein content were analyzed in raw breast meat samples using a near-infrared food analyzer (Xingchuang Zhongpu Instrument Co., Ltd., Guangdong, China). Instrument settings included the "duck meat" calibration model, dynamic acquisition mode, and 5 consecutive scans per measurement. Each sample was tested in triplicate, and the arithmetic mean was used as the final result.

Volatile compound analysis

Volatile compound profiling was conducted using a PEN3 electronic nose (E-nose) system. Prior to analysis, 1.5 g of deboned meat sample was homogenized and transferred into a 20 mL E-nose sampling vial. The sensor probe was inserted into the vial, allowing volatile organic compounds (VOCs) to diffuse through the membrane for detection. The operational parameters were set as follows: sampling interval = 1 s, sensor cleaning time = 20 s, injection duration = 50 s, and sampling flow rate = 1 L/min. The probe was purged between measurements to prevent cross-contamination. Eighteen MOS sensors (S1-S18) were applied to detect and characterize the VOC profile of the samples.

GC-MS for meat

Metabolite extraction

A 50±1 mg sample was transferred into a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube, and 500 μL pre-cooled extraction mixture (methanol/Chloroform, v/v = 3:1) containing the internal standard adonitol (0.5 mg/mL stock solution) was added. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, followed by addition of a stainless-steel grinding ball. The sample was then homogenized at 40 Hz for 4 min using a high-throughput tissue grinder, and subjected to ultrasonic disruption in an ice-water bath for 5 min; this cycle was repeated three times. After centrifugation at 12000 rpm (RCF = 13800 × g, rotor radius = 8.6 cm) at 4 °C for 15 min, 200 μL supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube. The extract was dried under vacuum in a concentrator. For quality control (QC), 50 μL of each sample was pooled. After evaporation in a vacuum concentrator, 50 μL of Methoxyamination hydrochloride (20 mg/mL in pyridine) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 80 °C for 30 min. The sample was then derivatized with 70 μL of BSTFA regent (1% TMCS, v/v) at 70 °C for 1.5 h. After gradual cooling to room temperature, 5 μL of FAMEs (in chloroform) was added to the QC sample. All samples were analyzed using gas chromatograph coupled with mass spectrometer (GC-MS).

GC conditions

GC-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent gas chromatograph coupled with a mass spectrometer and equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column. A 1 μL aliquot was injected in Split Mode (1:1). Helium was used as the carrier gas, the front inlet purge flow was 3 mL/min, and the gas flow rate through the column was 1 mL/min. The initial temperature was held at 50°C for 1 min, then raised to 310°C at 8°C/min, and held for 11.5 min at 310°C. The injection, transfer line, and ion source temperatures were 280, 280, and 230°C, respectively. The energy was −70 eV in electron impact mode. The mass spectrometry data were acquired in full-scan mode over the m/z range of 50-500 at a rate of 12.5 spectra per second, with a solvent delay of 7.2 min.

Data processing and annotation

Raw data processing—including peak extraction, baseline adjustment, deconvolution, alignment, and integration—was performed using ChromaTOF software (V 4.3x, LECO) and LECO-Fiehn Rtx5 (Kind et al., 2009). Metabolites were annotated by matching experimental mass spectra and retention indices against reference standards in the database. Peaks detected in fewer than half of the QC samples or exhibiting relative standard deviation (RSD) values greater than 30% across QC replicates were excluded from further analysis (Dunn et al., 2011).

LC-MS for broth

Metabolite extraction

A 100 μL aliquot of the broth sample was transferred to a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube, mixed with 400 μL of pre-chilled methanol: acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). The mixture was vortexed for 30 s and incubated at −20°C for 30 min. After incubation, the sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Subsequently, 400 μL of the supernatant was collected and dried completely under vacuum. The resulting residue was reconstituted in 150 μL of 50% methanol (v/v) containing 5 ppm 2-chlorophenylalanine as an internal standard, vortexed for 30 s, and centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and the filtrate was transferred to an HPLC vial. Finally, 10-20 μL aliquots from each sample filtrate were pooled to generate a quality control (QC) sample for evaluating instrument stability and data reliability.

Liquid chromatography conditions

Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 Å, 1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min, a column temperature of 40°C, an autosampler temperature of 8°C, and an injection volume of 2 μL. For both positive and negative ionization modes, the mobile phases consist of: mobile phase A-0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B-acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid(v/v). Elution was carried out using a gradient program as detailed in Table S1.

The Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer was used to acquire DDA (data-dependent acquisition) mass spectrometric data in both positive and negative ionization modes under the control of Xcalibur software (version 4.7, Thermo Scientific). A heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source was employed with the following parameters: spray voltage set to 3.5 kV/−3.0 kV, sheath gas flow rate at 40 arb, auxiliary gas flow rate at 15 arb, capillary temperature of 325 °C, auxiliary gas temperature of 300 °C, primary resolution of 60,000, scan range from 100 to 1000 m/z, AGC target set to “Standard”, maximum injection time (Max IT) of 100 ms, the top 4 most intense ions selected for secondary fragmentation, dynamic exclusion duration of 8 s, secondary resolution of 15,000, HCD collision energy at 30%, AGC target set to “Standard”, and Max IT set to “Auto”.

All analytical samples and QC samples were loaded into the equipment according to the above-mentioned chromatography and mass spectrometry methods. Before the formal injection, QC samples were injected 2-4 times to balance the system. During the injection process, one QC sample was injected for every 5-10 samples for subsequent data evaluation and quality control.

Differential metabolite analysis

Clustering analysis of differential metabolite abundances was carried out using the Pheatmap package (version 1.0.12) in R, with heatmaps and trend analysis plots drawn. Venn diagrams for differentially expressed substances in two-group comparisons were created using VennDiagram (version 1.7.3), respectively. Functional characterization of differential metabolites was primarily conducted through KEGG enrichment analysis using clusterProfiler (version 4.6.0), which identified significantly enriched metabolic pathways associated with breed- and treatment-related variations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Graphpad Prism 8. Values are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to determine significant differences among samples (P < 0.05). Pairwise comparisons between the three groups were further evaluated by Student's t-tests, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

The R package Ropls was used to separately perform principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) for dimensionality reduction of the sample data. Score plots, loading plots, and S-plots were produced to illustrate differences in metabolic compositions among samples. Model overfitting was assessed using permutation testing.

Results and analysis

Comparative analysis of conventional meat quality traits among three duck varieties

This study conducted a comparative analysis of breast meat quality traits among three duck breeds (Table 1). The CV exhibited the highest values for redness (a*), shear force, and cooking loss, while the LD showed the lowest levels of redness and shear force. The JL demonstrated the lowest cooking loss among the three breeds. However, no significant difference in cooking loss was detected among breeds (P > 0.05). A significant difference in redness were found between CV and LD (P < 0.05). For shear force, extremely significant differences existed between CV and both JL and LD (P < 0.01), although it did not differ significantly between JL and LD (P > 0.05). Additionally, the LD showed significant differences in breast meat protein content (P < 0.05) and highly significant differences in intramuscular fat content (P < 0.01) compared with the CV and the JL. All other meat quality parameters showed no statistically significant differences among the three breeds.

GC-MS analysis of breast meat from different meat duck breeds

Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling aliquots from all experimental samples to assess analytical reproducibility. The total ion chromatograms (TICs) of QC samples showed high overlap in both peak intensities and retention times (Fig. S3A), with Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 0.9 across replicate injections (Fig. S3B), indicating minimal instrumental variability and excellent experimental repeatability. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed tight clustering of QC samples (Fig. S4A), demonstrating system stability throughout the GC-MS runs.

Raw data were normalized using QC-based correction to mitigate batch effects. Partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) of normalized data (Fig. S4B) clearly segregated the 18 breast meat samples into six distinct clusters corresponding to breed (CV, JL, LD) and cooking status (raw vs. boiled: BoiledCV, BoiledJL, BoiledLD). Complete separation was observed between all raw and cooked sample groups, reflecting pronounced metabolic changes induced by thermal processing. To evaluate potential overfitting of the PLS-DA model, permutation testing (200 iterations) was performed (Fig. S4C). The resulting validation plot showed all permuted Q² values were lower than the original Q² (rightmost point), with a negative intercept of the regression line, thereby confirming model robustness and predictive accuracy (R²Y = 0.719, Q² = 0.448). The evaluation parameters (R2, Q2) obtained from the PLS-DA model after 10 interaction cycle validations are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Under the established analytical conditions, a total of 212 metabolites were detected in breast meat samples across three duck breeds before and after cooking (Supplementary Table S3, Fig. 1). Among these, 173 metabolites (81.6% of total) were successfully annotated and classified into nine major categories: Organic acids and derivatives (53 compounds, 28.8%), Lipids and lipid-like molecules (45, 24.5%), Organo-oxygen compounds (37, 20.1%), Organoheterocyclic compounds (18, 9.8%), Benzenoids and substituted derivatives (14, 7.6%), Nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogues (7, 3.8%), Organic nitrogen compounds (5, 2.7%), Phenylpropanoids and polyketides (3, 1.6%), and Homogeneous non-metal compounds (2, 1.1%).

Metabolic changes of key compounds

Meat primarily consists of proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and trace components. During water boiling, these substances undergo progressive oxidation and decomposition as temperature increases, leading to the formation of characteristic meaty flavor compounds, some of which leach into the broth. Analyzing the dynamic changes of these components in meat of different breeds during cooking reveals distinct, breed-specific metabolic patterns.

In cooked meat from the three duck breeds, differential metabolites—including cysteine, inosine, and palmitoleic acid—exhibited a consistent decrease in peak intensity across all breeds. These compounds were most abundant in CV broth, followed by JL broth, with the lowest levels observed in LD broth (Fig. 6A).

Under both positive and negative ionization modes, the concentration trends of specific compounds in the broth varied according to breed. Flavor-enhancing substances such as 4‑butyl‑5-propylthiazole, taurocholic acid, furaneol A, and certain dipeptides were highest in LD broth, intermediate in JD broth, and lowest in CV broth (Fig. 6B). These compounds contribute positively to meat flavor. In contrast, adenine showed an opposite trend: its concentration was highest in CV broth, followed by JL broth, and minimal in LD broth. Notably, excessive intake of purine derivatives such as adenine may pose health risks, including an increased likelihood of gout development.

These compositional differences likely contribute to the distinct flavor and nutritional profiles of both cooked meat and broth across duck breeds, thereby influencing consumer preference and purchasing decisions.

Metabolic dynamics of duck meat during thermal processing

To elucidate the relationship between thermal processing and dynamic shifts in intramuscular metabolites, we conducted pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 3D). The most significantly enriched metabolic pathways included d-amino acid metabolism, alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, purine metabolism, and carbon metabolism. These pathways are primarily involved in the synthesis and degradation of proteins, nucleotides, lipids, and carbohydrates in meat. We further reconstructed the functional crosstalk among these core metabolic modules (Fig. 7). Proteolysis, which derived free amino acids—particularly aspartic acid and glutamic acid—serve as direct contributors to umami taste in cooked duck meat and its broth. Meanwhile, the thermal degradation of ATP yields inosine monophosphate (IMP), widely recognized as the predominant umami-enhancing nucleotide in meat. Lipid oxidation—especially of polyunsaturated fatty acids such as linoleic acid—generates a diverse array of volatile aroma compounds, including aldehydes, ketones, and hydrocarbons, which collectively define the characteristic broth odor profile. Carbohydrate metabolism contributes pyruvate as a central hub metabolite, enabling bidirectional flux into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and glycolysis. Critically, these pathways do not operate in isolation: their intermediates are extensively interconnected through shared enzymatic nodes and energy-coupling reactions, forming an integrated metabolic network. Thus, flavor formation in boiled duck meat and its broth emerges not from a single metabolic pathway, but from the coordinated, temperature- dependent remodeling of this entire network-where proteolytic, nucleotidic, lipidic, and glycolytic processes synergistically shape sensory quality.

Fig. 7 also shows that the three duck breeds exhibit certain differences under the same cooking conditions. CV have the highest levels of IMP and AMP in the broth after cooking, indicating an adequate accumulation of umami nucleotides. JL have intermediate levels of each metabolite, with a relatively balanced flavor. LD show a significant increase in l-alanine and α-ketoglutaric acid, suggesting a deeper degree of protein degradation. Their metabolic network demonstrates more efficient substance transformation during thermal processing. LD undergo deep protein degradation during cooking, generating a rich array of flavor-enhancing amino acids. The accumulation of TCA cycle intermediates (α-ketoglutaric acid, l-malic acid) provides sufficient substrates for the Maillard reaction, while the moderate retention of IMP works synergistically with amino acids to create a complex flavor profile characterized by a rich umami taste, a strong meaty aroma, and a balanced sweet and sour flavor.

Discussion

Impact of breed on meat quality

Meat color and shear force are key quality traits that directly influence consumer acceptance and exhibit significant variation among duck breeds. While Zhang et al. reported higher shear force, higher pH, and lower cooking loss in 120-day-old LD compared to CV (Zhang et al., 2023), our study in 8-week-old (56-day) ducks revealed the opposite: LD exhibited lower shear force and reduced redness relative to CV. This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in slaughter age. Supporting the breed-specific impact on meat quality, Wang et al. found that Linwu ducks (a Chinese indigenous breed) had higher shear force and lower cooking loss than Pekin ducks (Wang et al., 2020). Meat flavor is closely associated with intramuscular fat (IMF) content, as lower IMF typically reduces the abundance of flavor precursors (Bravo-Lamas et al., 2018). In this study, LD breast muscle contained higher IMF than both CV and JL, which may partly account for the flavor variations among the three breeds.

Impact of duck breed on metabolites in cooked meat and broth

Metabolomic analysis revealed significant breed-dependent differences in the levels of organic acids and derivatives, lipids, nucleotides, nucleosides, and organooxygen compounds in breast meat. β-Alanine can combine with l-histidine to form carnosine, thereby increasing muscular carnosine levels (Boldyrev et al., 2013). Dietary β-alanine supplementation has been shown to reduce shear force (Qi et al., 2018), potentially explaining the lower shear force observed in JL compared to CV. The characteristic volatile flavor of meat is largely derived from lipids such as stearic acid and cholesterol. Stearic acid, a saturated fatty acid typically generated from fat hydrolysis, has been reported to exert neutral or even modest cholesterol-lowering effect in blood (Mensink et al., 2003). This aligns with our findings of significantly higher pre-cooking stearic acid in CV versus JL breast meat, while JL showed higher cholesterol levels. Cholesterol influences the level of 1-octen-3-ol, a key flavor compound derived mainly from the oxidative degradation of linoleic acid (C18:2n6) and arachidonic acid, contributing significantly to meat flavor (Jin et al., 2022). This mechanism likely underlies the distinct flavor profiles between CV and JL.

In broth, concentrations of specific differential metabolites also varied significantly by breed. Notably, the level of prostaglandin E3 (PGE3)—a bioactive lipid mediator derived from eicosapentaenoic acid with recognized anti-cancer and anti-inflammatory properties (Tanaka et al., 2014)—was markedly elevated in LD broth compared to CV and JL. Furthermore, thromboxane B2 (TXB2) and 20-hydroxyleukotriene B4 (20-OH-LTB4) were more abundant in LD broth. Metabolites of arachidonic acid such as prostaglandins and thromboxanes exhibit complex bioactivities affecting inflammation and cellular homeostasis (Chandrasekharan et al., 2016), with 20-OH-LTB4 playing a role in immune regulation. Additionally, taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TCDCA), a primary bile acid with potent anti-inflammatory effects mediated via the TGR5 receptor-cAMP-PKA-CREB pathway (Qi et al., 2020), was enriched in LD broth. Collectively, these findings suggest that LD broth exhibits distinctive nutritional components and potential health beneficial functions relative to CV and JL broths.

Impact of cooking processes on meat flavor profiles

Meat flavor formation is governed by three core processed: amino acids and nucleotides metabolism, lipid oxidation, and microbial metabolism, all of which are distinctly influenced by cooking methods (Yu et al., 2021). Thermal processing generates a wide array of volatile compounds—including alcohols, ketones, aldehydes, furans, esters, hydrocarbons, carboxylic acids, and various nitrogen- or sulfur-containing compounds (e.g., pyrazines, pyrroles, thiazoles) (Kosowska et al., 2017)—that collectively define the characteristic flavor of cooked meat.

Amino acid metabolism and Lipid decomposition are critical pathways governing meat flavor formation during boiling. Amino acid composition determines both flavor and nutritional value. Valine, phenylalanine, leucine, isoleucine, and methionine contribute bitter notes, while glycine, alanine, and threonine impart sweetness, and aspartic acid and glutamic acid provide umami (Ma et al., 2020). Notably, aspartic acid, glycine, and alanine can react with reducing sugars to form flavor-enhancing compounds (Xu et al., 2019). In this study, cooked breast meat showed increased levels of l-methionine, aspartic acid, glycine, l-threonine, l-valine, l-norleucine, l-isoleucine, and l-alanine compared to raw meat after boiling, which enriched the taste substrate composition and improved overall flavor complexity. Conversely, inosine monophosphate (IMP) levels decreased significantly after boiling, presumably due to thermal degradation.

Pathway analysis indicated that following boiling treatment, all three duck breeds shared common enriched metabolic pathways, primarily related to carbon and amino acid metabolism (particularly alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism). Carbon metabolism runs through the whole process of energy substance transformation in muscle tissue, and its metabolic flow direction directly affects the synthesis and accumulation of flavor precursors. Boiling influences lipid oxidation, a process responsible for over half of the volatile compounds in boiled meat and essential for developing species-characteristic meaty aromas (Bravo-Lamas et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2021). Meanwhile, the inherent genetic differences among different duck breeds lead to distinct activation degrees of amino acid metabolic pathways under the same boiling condition. Glutamic acid, a key taste-active component, plays a pivotal role in shaping the final flavor profile (Watanabe et al., 2015).

Conclusions

This study explored the dynamic changes of small-molecule compounds in duck meat during cooking, demonstrating that breed significantly influences conventional quality traits, volatile profiles, and final flavor. At 8 weeks of age, CV showed higher shear force, while LD had higher breast muscle fat content compared with JL. Notably, the hybrid progeny JL demonstrated marked improvements compared with CV: it had significantly lower shear force, and accumulated higher levels of key flavor compounds in broth, including furanone A, 4‑butyl‑5-propylthiazole and glutaric acid. The difference in β-alanine levels may contribute to the observed variation in shear force between JL and CV. Boiling treatment enriched metabolites primarily in amino acid metabolism, TCA cycle and carbon metabolism pathways, which are critical for the formation of heat-induced meat flavor. Collectively, this work provides novel molecular insights into the evolution of meat flavor during cooking.
