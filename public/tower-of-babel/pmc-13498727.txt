Compounded 0.03% Tacrolimus in Cross‐Linked Hyaluronate Improves Ocular Surface Health in Dogs With Evaporative Dry Eye Disease
Authors: Lionel Sebbag, Eunice Q A Neta, Catharina R de Farias, Ana Cláudia S Raposo, Arianne P Oriá
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo assess the clinical effects of a compounded 0.03% tacrolimus in 0.75% cross‐linked hyaluronic acid (XHA) on tear quantity, quality, and ocular surface health in dogs with evaporative dry eye disease (EDED).ProceduresSixteen client‐owned dogs diagnosed with EDED (normal aqueous production yet reduced stability) were enrolled in this prospective, open‐label clinical trial. Patients were treated with the compounded formulation in affected eyes twice daily for 45 days. Ophthalmic assessments, including Schirmer tear test‐1 (STT‐1), tear film breakup time (TFBUT), punctate fluorescein staining (PFS), lissamine green staining, and conjunctival hyperemia scoring, were performed during designated morning appointments at Day 0, Day 15, and Day 45. Data were analyzed using repeated‐measures ANOVA and post hoc tests.ResultsSTT‐1 values increased significantly from 18.7 ± 2.3 mm/min at baseline to 21.7 ± 4.1 mm/min at Day 45 (p = 0.004). Tear film stability improved markedly, with TFBUT rising from 3.3 ± 0.7 s to 5.2 ± 1.4 s (Day 15) and 8.1 ± 2.4 s (Day 45) (p < 0.001). Ocular surface health improved, evidenced by PFS scores decreasing from 1.8 ± 0.9 to 0.3 ± 0.4 (p < 0.001) and lissamine green scores dropping from 1.9 ± 1.1 to 0.1 ± 0.3 (p < 0.001). Conjunctival hyperemia scores declined significantly by Day 45 (p = 0.041).ConclusionsTwice‐daily administration of compounded XHA/tacrolimus resulted in significant improvements in tear quantity, stability, and corneoconjunctival integrity. Combining immunomodulation with long‐acting lubrication in a single formulation offers a promising, practical therapeutic strategy for efficiently managing canine qualitative dry eye disease in clinical practice.Keywords: keratoconjunctivitis sicca, lacrimomimetic, lacrimostimulant, meibomian gland dysfunction, qualitative tear deficiency, tear film breakup time

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a multifactorial ocular surface disorder that includes aqueous‐deficient dry eye (ADDE) and evaporative dry eye disease (EDED) [1, 2, 3]. In dogs, ADDE associated with immune‐mediated keratoconjunctivitis sicca (KCS) has long been recognized as a common cause of ocular surface irritation, excessive mucoid discharge, and sight‐threatening keratitis [4, 5, 6]. Recently, qualitative tear film disorders and EDED have been increasingly reported in dogs, particularly in brachycephalic breeds with meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) and eyelid abnormalities, as well as in dogs with ocular surface inflammatory disorders that may contribute to tear film instability [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. These conditions are characterized by tear film instability and corneoconjunctival staining despite normal aqueous tear secretion [2, 14], highlighting the importance of assessing both tear quantity and quality in canine patients [15].

Management of canine DED, including both aqueous‐deficient and evaporative forms, typically involves two complementary therapeutic approaches: a lacrimomimetic to supplement the tear film and a lacrimostimulant or immunomodulatory agent to enhance endogenous tear secretion and maintain ocular surface homeostasis [16, 17]. Hyaluronic acid (HA), a mucoadhesive glycosaminoglycan with viscoelastic properties, has been shown to enhance tear film stability, promote epithelial wound healing, and alleviate clinical signs in dogs with KCS and other ocular surface diseases [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Cross‐linked HA (XHA) formulations further increase the viscosity and ocular surface residence time of HA, allowing for less frequent dosing and improved clinical efficacy [18, 20, 21, 22, 24, 25]. Tacrolimus, a calcineurin inhibitor, has been shown to increase aqueous tear production and reduce inflammatory cytokines in dogs with KCS [26, 27, 28, 29]. Tacrolimus can be compounded as either an aqueous suspension or an oil‐based formulation. More recently, our group reported that tacrolimus, when administered alongside a hyaluronate‐containing tear supplement, improves tear film stability and ocular surface homeostasis in dogs with EDED, demonstrating measurable benefits in both clinical testing and tear crystallization patterns [7, 8].

Given the complementary mechanisms of HA and tacrolimus, combining both agents into a single topical formulation may offer synergistic benefits for dogs with EDED. Building on previous research where tacrolimus and HA were administered separately in dogs with EDED [7, 8], we hypothesized that a compounded ophthalmic solution containing 0.03% tacrolimus in 0.75% cross‐linked HA (XHA/tacrolimus formulation) would improve tear film stability and ocular surface health in dogs with qualitative dry eye. The aims of this prospective study were to evaluate the effects of twice‐daily treatment with XHA/tacrolimus formulation on tear quantity, tear quality, and other ocular surface parameters over 45 days in dogs with EDED.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Study Design

This was a prospective, open‐label, single‐arm clinical trial conducted in client‐owned dogs diagnosed with qualitative tear film deficiency. Client‐owned dogs were prospectively enrolled after completing a full physical and ophthalmic examination, which included neuro‐ophthalmic testing, slit‐lamp biomicroscopy, indirect ophthalmoscopy, rebound tonometry, Schirmer tear test‐1 (STT‐1), and tear film breakup time (TFBUT). Dogs were eligible for inclusion if one or both eyes demonstrated reduced tear film stability (TFBUT < 5 s) along with adequate aqueous tear production (STT‐1 ≥ 15 mm/min) and at least one clinical feature commonly associated with qualitative tear film deficiency, such as epiphora, mucoid discharge, meibomian gland plugging, conjunctival hyperemia, or keratitis [2, 7, 9, 10, 14, 30]. Dogs were excluded if they had undergone ocular surgery or received topical ophthalmic medications within the previous 6 months, or if they showed evidence of systemic illness that could influence tear dynamics or ocular surface health based on history, examination, and routine laboratory testing. Eyes with corneal ulceration or other primary ocular diseases unrelated to tear film abnormalities were also excluded. All owners provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee on Animal Experimentation of the Federal University of Bahia (protocol # 08/2024).

Treatment

All dogs received a compounded ophthalmic formulation containing 0.03% tacrolimus in 0.75% cross‐linked hyaluronic acid (XHA) vehicle (hereafter referred to as XHA/tacrolimus formulation), prepared by a single compounding pharmacy (Drogavet compounding pharmacy, Brazil) under sterile conditions. Owners were instructed to instill one drop of XHA/tacrolimus formulation in each affected eye twice daily (approximately every 12 h) from Day 0 through Day 45.

Clinical and Tear Film Assessments

At each visit (Day 0, Day 15, Day 45), dogs underwent a standardized ophthalmic examination conducted in the morning hours (08:00–12:00) by the same two examiners (A.R., A.O.) in a single examination room. Ambient temperature and humidity were recorded at each session. All ophthalmic assessments were performed in the same standardized sequence for every dog, following the order in which the diagnostic procedures are described below. A minimum interval of 5–10 min was maintained between tests to allow for tear film replenishment, and on recheck days, all procedures were performed at least 1 h after the administration of the morning dose of the study medication.

Blink rate: Blinking was assessed from a distance by two trained observers, each consistently assigned to the same eye throughout the study to ensure reproducibility. Dogs were observed while at rest, without direct handling or external stimulation. Complete and incomplete blinks were counted separately over a 2‐min period, and the values were divided by two to obtain the complete and incomplete blink rates (blinks/min).

Conjunctival hyperemia and ocular discharge: Each was graded using a previously described 0–3 scale, where 0 = absent, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, and 3 = severe [7, 22]. Ocular discharge included serous, seromucoid, or mucopurulent material.

Corneal tactile sensation (CTS): Corneal sensitivity was measured using a Cochet–Bonnet aesthesiometer (0.12‐mm monofilament). Testing began with a filament length of 6.0 cm, positioned perpendicular to the central cornea and advanced until a slight bend occurred. The length was then shortened in 0.5‐cm increments until a consistent blink response was elicited in at least 3 out of 5 touches. The filament length (cm) required to trigger this response was recorded as the CTS value.

Tear ferning: Each STT strip was placed inside a 0.5‐mL microtube with a pierced bottom, which was then nested within a 2‐mL collection tube. Samples were centrifuged at 2583 × g for 10 min to recover the absorbed tear fluid, which was subsequently stored at 4°C until ferning analysis. Ferning evaluation was conducted by three experienced clinicians masked to clinical details in a controlled laboratory environment (temperature 19°C–24°C, relative humidity 42%–50%) [8]. Tear ferning patterns were independently graded according to both the Rolando and Masmali classification systems, as previously described [31]. For each sample, the final reported grade for each scale reflected the score given by at least two of the three evaluators, ensuring consistency without the need for full consensus.

Tear film breakup time (TFBUT): A 3‐μL aliquot of 1% fluorescein was instilled onto the ocular surface using a calibrated micropipette. After three manual blinks to distribute the dye, the eyelids were gently held open. The dorsotemporal cornea was examined under cobalt‐blue illumination with 16× magnification. TFBUT was measured to the nearest tenth of a second using a stopwatch, defined as the interval from eye opening to the first appearance of one or more dark spots in the fluorescent tear film. Two measurements were obtained per eye, and their mean was used for analysis.

Data Analysis

Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Results from the right and left eyes were compared with paired t‐tests. Given that several clinical outcomes were based on ordinal grading scales, Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were additionally performed to verify the robustness of the inter‐eye comparisons. As no significant differences were noted between eyes for any diagnostic test (p ≥ 0.095), the averages of the measurements from the right and left eyes of each dog were used for further analysis. Data were analyzed using repeated‐measures ANOVA and post hoc Holm‐Sidak tests to assess the effect of time (Day 0, Day 15, Day 45) on the outcomes of interest. Statistical analyses were performed with SigmaPlot 15.0 (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA), and values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The study population comprised various breeds, including Shih Tzu (n = 7), Yorkshire Terrier (n = 2), Pug (n = 1), Cocker Spaniel (n = 1), West Highland White Terrier (n = 1), Dachshund (n = 1), Toy Poodle (n = 1), Pinscher (n = 1), and Golden Retriever (n = 1). There were nine females (8 neutered, 1 intact) and seven males (2 neutered, 5 intact), aged 4.7 ± 3.4 years (range, 1–13) and weighing 8.1 ± 7.6 kg (range, 2.5–35). At baseline, affected eyes primarily exhibited tear film instability with mild conjunctivitis and superficial corneal epitheliopathy, reflected by punctate fluorescein staining. None of the enrolled dogs had advanced chronic keratitis characterized by corneal fibrosis, pigmentation, or other vision‐threatening corneal changes. All 16 dogs completed the 45‐day study period. Owners reported good tolerance to the XHA/tacrolimus formulation, and no ocular or systemic adverse events were observed during the study. Data were normally distributed (p ≥ 0.132). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (minimum‐maximum) and summarized in Table 1.

Values increased to 22.6 ± 3.1 mm/min (15.5–27.0) at Day 15 and 21.7 ± 4.1 mm/min (16.0–28.5) at Day 45. The overall effect of time was significant (p = 0.004; pairwise comparisons: Day 15 versus Day 0, p = 0.005; Day 45 versus Day 0, p = 0.027; Day 45 versus Day 15, p = 0.433).

TFBUT

TFBUT increased significantly during XHA/tacrolimus therapy (Figure 2). Baseline TFBUT was 3.3 ± 0.7 s (range, 2.0–4.5) and increased to 5.2 ± 1.4 s (3.0–8.0) at Day 15, and further to 8.1 ± 2.4 s (4.5–14.8) at Day 45. The overall effect of time was highly significant (p < 0.001; pairwise comparisons: Day 15 vs. Day 0, p = 0.002; Day 45 vs. Day 0, p < 0.001; Day 45 vs. Day 15, p < 0.001).

Additional clinical parameters, including blink rate, ocular discharge scores, corneal sensitivity, and tear ferning patterns, did not show statistically significant changes over time (p ≥ 0.260; Table 1).

Discussion

This prospective, open‐label study demonstrates that a compounded formulation containing 0.03% tacrolimus in 0.75% XHA improves multiple metrics of ocular surface health in dogs with EDED. Specifically, we observed increased STT‐1 values, prolonged TFBUT, reduced corneal staining, conjunctival staining, and conjunctival hyperemia scores over 45 days with twice‐daily therapy. These findings mirror and build upon previous work in which tacrolimus and HA were administered as separate products in dogs with EDED [7, 8], suggesting that directly compounding tacrolimus into an HA vehicle can also provide meaningful benefits for clinical patients. This study also reinforces the growing recognition of EDED in dogs, particularly in brachycephalic breeds and patients with MGD [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Traditional reliance on STT‐1 alone can overlook clinically significant tear film abnormalities, as evidenced by these dogs who exhibited normal aqueous tear production but reduced TFBUT and increased ocular surface staining. Our data support the use of a multimodal tear film assessment, including TFBUT, fluorescein and lissamine staining, and meibomian gland evaluation, to guide diagnosis and monitor response to therapy [15].

The observed increase in STT‐1, despite normal baseline values, is consistent with prior evidence that topical tacrolimus can enhance aqueous tear production in canine eyes [26, 33]. Tacrolimus acts through calcineurin inhibition in T lymphocytes, reducing T cell–mediated inflammation and enhancing glandular function [34]. In cases of EDED, where aqueous tear production is generally within reference limits, modest increases in STT‐1 may still be clinically beneficial, although excessive tearing (i.e., epiphora) may occur in individual patients.

Dogs in this study showed a striking improvement in tear film stability, with the mean TFBUT increasing from 3.3 s at baseline to 5.2 s at Day 15 and 8.1 s at Day 45, corresponding to approximately +58% and +145% increases, respectively. These changes exceed the magnitude of improvement previously reported when tacrolimus was administered alongside a conventional, non‐cross‐linked HA‐based lubricant in dogs with EDED (+57% and +93% at the same time points) [7], suggesting that the XHA vehicle and/or direct incorporation of tacrolimus into the XHA formulation may further enhance tear film stability. Corneal PFS improved in parallel with TFBUT, decreasing from a mean score of 1.8 at baseline to 0.6 and 0.3 at Days 15 and 45, respectively (67% and 83% reductions). PFS is a key marker of DED, reflecting disruption of epithelial tight junctions, increased corneal permeability, or epithelial cell loss; thus, its reduction indicates a restoration of epithelial integrity and a more uniform precorneal tear film. Baseline PFS scores in our cohort were comparable to those reported in brachycephalic dogs, such as Shih Tzus, where most eyes show grade 1–2 punctate staining associated with low TFBUT and brachycephalic ocular syndrome [9]. The near‐complete resolution of PFS by Day 45 therefore suggests not only symptomatic relief but also a meaningful reversal of epithelial surface damage in dogs with EDED treated with the XHA/tacrolimus formulation. Lissamine green staining scores showed a similarly strong response, decreasing from 1.9 at baseline to 0.7 and 0.1 at Days 15 and 45 (63% and 95% reductions). Lissamine green staining highlights devitalized epithelial cells or areas with inadequate mucin protection, with higher scores correlating to more severe tear film deficiency, especially in brachycephalic dogs [32]. Lastly, conjunctival hyperemia also improved over the study period, declining from 0.6 at baseline to 0.3 at Day 15 and 0.1 at Day 45, achieving statistical significance only at Day 45. The slower resolution of hyperemia is consistent with the pharmacologic profile of tacrolimus, which is immunomodulatory but not strongly anti‐inflammatory in the short term. In clinical practice, dogs with EDED who present with more pronounced conjunctivitis or keratitis may benefit from a brief course of topical corticosteroids at the start of therapy to more rapidly control surface inflammation, followed by maintenance with XHA/tacrolimus formulation to support long‐term tear film stability and ocular surface health.

The clinical benefits observed with the current formulation are supported by a substantial body of veterinary evidence regarding XHA on the ocular surface. In a masked randomized trial of canine KCS, XHA produced significantly greater improvements in conjunctival hyperemia, ocular irritation, and discharge compared to a linear HA supplement, and owners rated it as more effective [20]. A controlled study in normal dogs also showed that twice‐daily XHA significantly increased TFBUT by Day 7 and Day 14 compared to saline [35]. In a fluorophotometric study, XHA demonstrated markedly longer ocular surface retention than linear HA, with higher fluorescence intensity and slower clearance throughout the measurement period [24]. From a practical standpoint, combining tacrolimus and XHA into one product offers several advantages over administering two separate medications. First, a single drop simplifies treatment regimens and may substantially improve owner adherence, which is critical in chronic diseases requiring lifelong therapy. Second, the prolonged retention of XHA on the ocular surface likely extends the contact time of tacrolimus with target tissues, potentially allowing for reduced dosing frequency (e.g., once daily) in some patients after an initial induction phase. Similar concepts have been successfully applied when XHA was used as a vehicle for topical antibiotics such as chloramphenicol or cefazolin [25].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was open‐label and uncontrolled, which prevents definitive attribution of all improvements to XHA/tacrolimus formulation alone. However, the magnitude and consistency of changes across multiple objective endpoints, along with the chronic nature of qualitative DED, make spontaneous resolution unlikely. The sample size was modest (16 dogs), limiting our power to detect smaller effects or to perform robust subgroup analyses (e.g., by breed or baseline severity). Additionally, we did not assess the physicochemical stability or uniformity of the compounded formulation over time. Tacrolimus is commonly compounded in oil‐based vehicles (e.g., olive or corn oil) or aqueous suspensions [26, 29], and its distribution within an XHA matrix could theoretically be heterogeneous from drop to drop. Future studies should characterize drug content uniformity, viscosity, and stability throughout the product's intended shelf life.

In conclusion, a compounded ophthalmic solution containing 0.03% tacrolimus in 0.75% XHA administered twice daily led to increased aqueous tear production, improved tear film stability, and reduced corneal and conjunctival staining and hyperemia in dogs with EDED. By combining a lacrimomimetic and an immunomodulatory/lacrimostimulant in a single, long‐acting formulation, the XHA/tacrolimus formulation offers a promising and clinically practical approach for managing canine EDED and other ocular surface disorders.
