Dual-function TA@CaCO3 nanomedicine targeting M2 macrophage lipid peroxidation sensitivity in rheumatoid arthritis therapy
Authors: Qin Zhang, Ziliang Dong, Yanglin Wu, Ruoran Zhou, Quguang Li, Jesscia M. Rosenholm, Cheng Huang, Liangzhu Feng, Jun Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a globally prevalent autoimmune musculoskeletal disease that requires early-stage treatment. Initially, patients with RA exhibit elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and acidic conditions within the articular spaces. These two factors synergistically promote lipid peroxidation in M2 macrophages and lead to increased inflammation. Our work was then designed to engineer a multifunctional nanomedicine that targets the increased lipid peroxidation sensitivity of M2 macrophages. We successfully synthesized a calcium carbonate (CaCO3)-based nanomedicine via a biomineralization approach involving tannic acid (TA) and poly(ethylene glycol)-b-poly(glutamic acid). The TA@CaCO3 nanomedicine exhibited superior ability to neutralize acidity and scavenge ROS both in vitro and in vivo, effectively enhancing the lipid peroxidation resistance of M2 macrophages. Therefore, this nanomedicine significantly protected M2 macrophages and ameliorated the symptoms of arthritis, achieving therapeutic efficacy comparable to that of conventional methotrexate therapy. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is now understood as a chronic immunity-related disease that elicits histological damage predominantly in joints [1, 2]. During the outbreak of RA-related inflammation, joints and other RA-related organs exhibit increased levels of oxidative stress (e.g., lipid peroxidation) and extracellular acidification, as well as dysfunction of multiple immune cells, collectively promoting the destruction of cartilage and bone [3, 4]. Among these RA-related immune cells, macrophages exhibit significantly enhanced synovial infiltration at an imbalanced classically activated M1 to alternatively activated M2 ratio, thereby playing a predominant role in driving the pathogenesis of RA by promoting the production of proinflammatory cytokines and some small molecule mediators (e.g., reactive oxygen species [ROS]) [5, 6]. Therefore, macrophages have been extensively explored as potent targets for the development of antirheumatic drugs [7]. Despite the depletion of proinflammatory immune cells and the inhibition of specific inflammatory mediators, reprogramming macrophages from the M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype represents a promising therapeutic strategy to resolve life-threatening RA inflammation [8].

A recent study demonstrated that M2 macrophages are more susceptible to intensive intraarticular lipid peroxidation than are M1 macrophages, which exhibit high levels of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). This susceptibility of M2 macrophages might cause ferroptotic cell death, thereby contributing to the progression of inflammation [9]. Several ingelligent strategies enabling efficient ROS scavenging by blocking ROS production or inhibiting NDAPH oxidase have therefore shown great potential in resolving inflammation by increasing the population of M2 macrophages [10]. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), an abundant and stable species of ROS in inflammatory tissues, can induce ferroptosis in the presence of ferrous ions (Fe2+), which can decompose H2O2 into hydroxyl groups (·OH) via the Fenton reaction to initiate lipid peroxidation chain reactions [11]. Given that the acidic microenvironment favours the Fe2+-mediated Fenton reaction, we hypothesize that acidification of the articular cavity in RA patients (pH 6.5) could disrupt the imbalanced M1/M2 ratio. This is possibly achieved by facilitating the accumulation of lipid peroxides, which in turn triggers ferroptosis in M2 macrophages.

To this end, we first confirmed that RA patients presented significantly higher serum levels of lipid peroxidation metabolites, such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA), than healthy volunteers did. Consistent with these findings in humans, the collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse model, a classical model of RA, also exhibited significant lipid peroxidation and joint acidification at inflammatory sites. We further demonstrated that M2 macrophages were more susceptible to simultaneous extracellular exposure to protons and H₂O₂, a prototypical reactive oxygen species (ROS). This heightened sensitivity was attributed to the fact that extracellular acidification could augment H₂O₂-induced intracellular lipid peroxidation by downregulating the expression of ferritin heavy chain (FHC) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). Inspired by the excellent ROS-scavenging capacity of natural polyphenols (e.g., tannic acid (TA)) and the acidity neutralization capacity of CaCO3, we constructed a bifunctional CaCO3-based nanomedicine through TA- and poly(ethylene glycol)-b-poly(glutamic acid) (PEG-PGA)-mediated biomineralization processes for RA treatment. The obtained TA@CaCO3 nanomedicine was capable of both neutralizing protons and scavenging ROS, thus protecting M2 macrophages from ferroptosis via the inhibition of intracellular lipid peroxidation in both cellular and animal experiments. When administered intra-articularly, the TA@CaCO3 nanomedicine effectively controlled inflammation and prevented pannus formation, joint ankylosis and cartilage destruction in CIA mice, achieving therapeutic efficacy comparable to that of standard MTX treatment. This work highlights a promising strategy for treating RA with our bifunctional TA@CaCO3 nanomedicine via simultaneous ROS scavenging and acid neutralization.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Calcium chloride (CaCl2), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) and potassium persulfate (K2S2O8) were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China. Tannic acid, 2’,7’-bis-(2-carboxyethyl)-5-(and-6)-carboxyfluorescein, acetoxymethyl ester (BCECF-AM) and 2,2’-azinobis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline 6-sulfonate) (ABTS) were obtained from Sigma‒Aldrich. 1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl free radical (DPPH.) were purchased from Alfa Aesar. PGA5k-PEG10k block copolymers were obtained from Xi’an Ruixi Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS), trypsin-EDTA and antibiotic/antimycotic (AA) solutions were purchased from WelGENE (Gyeongsan, Korea). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) were purchased from HyClone Laboratories, Inc. (IL, USA). All aqueous solutions used in the experiments were prepared with deionized water (18.2 MΩ.cm) sourced from a Milli-Q water purification system.

Synthesis of TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles

To synthesize TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles, 10 mg of TA and 7.17 mg of PGA5k-PEG10k were dissolved in 2 mL of degassed deionized (DI) water and then vigorously stirred at room temperature for 5 min. At an optimized mass feeding ratio of CaCl2 to TA of 1:1, CaCl2 (10 mg mL− 1) and Na2CO3 (10 mg mL− 1) with an equal volume of 1 mL were sequentially added to the above reaction mixture and stirred in a nitrogen atmosphere for 4 h. Later, the obtained TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles were collected and repeatedly washed with DI water by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm. CaCO3 nanoparticles were prepared via a similar procedure to that used for the synthesis of TA@CaCO3, except that TA was not added. The obtained TA@CaCO3 and CaCO3 nanoparticles were stored at 4 °C for further use.

Characterization

The elemental and morphological compositions of the TA@CaCO3 and CaCO3 nanoparticles were determined via a transmission electron microscope (TALOS 200X, Sigma) equipped with high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) imaging and energy dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDX). The UV‒vis‒NIR spectra and dynamic light scattering (DLS) size distribution profiles of the TA@CaCO3 and CaCO3 nanoparticles were measured via a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher) and a Malvern Zetasizer (nano-ZS90), respectively. The elemental composition of TA@CaCO3 was determined via XPS (Thermo Kalpha). The crystalline profiles of the TA@CaCO3 and CaCO3 nanoparticles were determined on an X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean). The concentration of Ca2+ was determined via an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometer (ICP‒OES, Thermo Fisher). The content of TA in TA@CaCO3 was determined via spectrophotometry with Folin, and Den was used as the chromogenic reagent.

ROS scavenging assays

ABTS+. scavenging abilities of TA@CaCO3. The ABTS (7 mM) solution was first dissolved in K2S2O8 (2.45 mM) in the dark overnight to obtain ABTS+., which (34.5 µM) was then incubated with TA@CaCO3 at various concentrations in the dark for 0.5 h. Afterwards, the absorbance of the above solutions at 734 nm was recorded via UV‒Vis‒NIR spectrophotometry. The ABTS+ scavenging efficacy was calculated via the following equation: (A0-A1)/A1*100%, where A1 and A0 represent the absorbances of ABTS+. solutions before and after the incubation of TA@CaCO3, respectively. The TA@CaCO3 scavenging abilities of free TA and CaCO3 were evaluated via the same protocol.

DPPH. scavenging abilities of TA@CaCO3. Freshly prepared DPPH· methanolic solution (100 µM) was mixed with TA@CaCO3 in various solutions and then incubated in the dark for 1 h. Later, the absorbances of the above solutions at 517 nm were recorded via UV‒Vis‒NIR spectrophotometry. The DPPH· scavenging efficacy was calculated via the following equation: (A0-A1)/A1*100%, where A1 and A0 represent the absorbances of the DPPH· solutions before and after the incubation of TA@CaCO3, respectively. The DPPH·-scavenging abilities of free TA and CaCO3 nanoparticles were determined via the same procedure.

·OH scavenging abilities of TA@CaCO3. First, 50 µL of TA@CaCO3 solutions at various concentrations were mixed with 300 µL of ·OH-containing working solution (Solarbio), followed by incubation for 1 h in the dark at 37 °C. The absorbance of the supernatants at 536 nm was subsequently recorded via a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher) after centrifugation.

·O2− scavenging abilities of TA@CaCO3. Twenty microliters of TA@CaCO3 solution at various concentrations were first mixed with 160 µL of WST-8 working solution (Beyotime) and then mixed with 20 µL of xanthine oxidase solution. After being incubated at 37 °C for another 0.5 h, the absorbance of the solutions at 450 nm was measured via a microplate reader.

Acid neutralization capacity of TA@CaCO3

The acidic media (pH 6.5) was prepared by adding diluted HCl solution to commercial DPBS. Then, 20 µL of DPBS, TA, CaCO3 or TA@CaCO3 at equivalent TA or CaCO3 concentrations (TA = 0.13 mg mL-1, CaCO3 = 1 mg mL-1) was added to the acidic solutions, and the real-time pH variations were recorded by using a pH microelectrode.

Cellular experiments

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were first collected from the bone marrow cavity of DBA/1 mice and then treated with LPS (200 ng/mL, Sigma, USA) or IL-4 (10 ng/mL, MCE, USA) for 24 h to obtain M1 or M2 macrophages, respectively, according to standard protocols.

To test the intracellular lipid peroxidation levels, M1 and M2 macrophages were initially cultured in a 12-well plate at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well. The cells were subsequently treated with various agents (TA, CaCO3, TA@CaCO3) in the presence or absence of H2O2 (100 µM) at pH 7.4 or pH 6.5 for 3 h. Afterward, the cells were stained with C11 BODIPY 581/591 (50 µM) for 1 h.

Then, the treated cells were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (10 µg/mL) for another 10 min before being subjected to confocal microscopic observation. Then, the fluorescence of nonoxidized (λex = 581 nm, λem = 591 nm) or lipid-oxidized (λex = 488, λem = 510 nm) cells was semiquantitatively analysed via ImageJ software.

To test cell viability, BMDMs were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 2000 cells per well and treated with various agents (TA, CaCO3, TA@CaCO3) in the presence or absence of H2O2 (100 µM) at pH 7.4 or 6.5 for 3 h. The relative cytotoxicity was then determined via a CCK-8 assay (Beyotime, China).

To test the intracellular levels of GSH, MDA and Fe2+, BMDMs were first seeded on a 96-well plate (2000 cells/well) and subjected to the same treatments described above. Afterwards, the intracellular levels of MDA, GSH, and Fe2+ in the treated cells were separately assessed via commercial MDA colorimetric assay kits, GSH colorimetric assay kits and Fe2+ colorimetric assay kits (Elabscience, China).

To test the intracellular expression levels of xCT, GPX4 and FHC, BMDMs were initially seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 1 × 10⁷ cells per well. Following the same treatment protocol as described above, the cells were then harvested for analysis via a standard Western blotting assay.

Animal experiments

Male DBA/1 mice aged 8–10 weeks were obtained from Heng Xin Chen Company (Suzhou, China). Animal research was performed according to the guidelines approved by the ethics committee of Soochow University (approval number: SUDA20221124A21). The collagen-induced arthritis model was established via two subcutaneous injections of a 0.1 mL mixture of bovine type II collagen solution (2 mg/mL) and complete Freund’s adjuvant (content of M. tuberculosis H-37 RA: 4 mg/mL, first immunization) or incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (content of M. tuberculosis H-37 RA: 0 mg/mL, second immunization) on days 1 and 21. A burst of inflammation in CIA mice usually occurs on day 27.

To test their intraarticular retention behavior, TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles (TA = 0.13 mg mL− 1, CaCO3 = 1 mg mL− 1) were noncovalently labelled with Cy5.5 dye (10 mg µL− 1). Cy5.5-TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles (1 mg mL− 1, 10 mL) were injected into the knee joints of CIA mice, which were then imaged under a Lumina III in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer) (λex = 673, λem = 695 nm) every day for one week.

To test the acid neutralization ability of TA@CaCO3, the knee joints of CIA mice were injected with 10 ml of TA, CaCO3 or TA@CaCO3 at equivalent TA or CaCO3 concentrations (TA = 0.13 mg mL− 1, CaCO3 = 1 mg mL− 1) at 3 days after the second immunization and injected with BCECF-AM solution (10 µM, 10 µL) in the articular cavity of the same knee of each mouse at 7 days after the second immunization. One hour after BCECF-AM injection, the knees of each mouse were collected for ex vivo fluorescence imaging under a Lumina III in vivo imaging system (λex = 440 and 480 nm, λem = 520 nm). The intraarticular pH of healthy and untreated CIA mice was recorded via the same protocol.

To test the ROS scavenging ability of TA@CaCO3, CIA mice subjected to the same treatments described above were injected with DCFH (10 µM, 10 µL) in the articular cavity of the knees at 7 days after treatment. Twenty-four hours later, the knees of each mouse were collected, cryosected and counterstained with DAPI, after which the DCFH fluorescence was recorded via confocal microscopy (OlympusIX73).

To test the therapeutic effects of TA@CaCO3, five groups of CIA mice (n = 8 per group) were injected with ① PBS, ② free TA (0.13 mg/mL, 10 µL), ③ CaCO3 nanoparticles (1 mg/mL, 10 µL), ④ TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles (TA = 0.13 mg/mL, CaCO3 = 1 mg/mL, 10 µL), or ⑤ MTX (5 mg/kg per mouse) 3 days after the second immunization. The CIA mice in groups 2–4 were intraarticularly injected with the corresponding agents in the knee and ankle joints once a week 3 times, whereas the CIA mice in group 5 were intraperitoneally injected with MTX once every 3 days for a total of 6 injections. The clinical scores ranging from 0 to 4 for each paw were as follows: 0): normal paw; 1): one toe with erythema and swelling (black arrow); 2): more than one toe (≥ 2), but not the entire paw with erythema and swelling; 3): entire paw with erythema and swelling; and 4): very severe erythema and swelling or an ankylosed paw. The thickness of the hind paws was recorded via a Vernier calliper at 1 and 21 days after the second immunization. Twenty-one days after the second immunization, blood samples were collected to determine the level of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-a) secretion, and the joints were collected for micro-CT evaluation and pathological analysis.

The osteolysis of ankle joints was evaluated via a microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) scanning system (Skyscan 1176, Bruker, German) according to previously used parameters [12]. Following the scanning process, the 3-dimensional model was constructed via Mimics (version 19.0) software, and quantitative analyses of BV/TV (bone volume to total volume) and Tb.N (number of trabeculae) were performed via CTAn software.

The levels of inflammation and cartilage destruction in the knee joints of the mice subjected to different treatments were analysed via H&E staining, safranin O-fast green staining and immunofluorescence staining. In addition, the expression levels of FHC, GPX4 and xCT in the slices of the collected knee joints were evaluated via standard immunofluorescence staining methods by using primary antibodies against FHC (1:200, ABclonal), GPX4 (1:200, Abcam) and xCT (1:100, Abcam) and corresponding FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies. All the stained slices were imaged via microscopy (OlympusIX73). The OARSI and HSS scores [13] were used to quantify the degree of inflammation and degree of cartilage destruction. The intensity of the fluorescence was analysed via ImageJ software.

Detection of lipid peroxidation in the plasma of humans

Blood samples from RA patients and healthy volunteers at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University were collected to analyse the serum levels of MDA, 4HNE and GSH-Px via commercial MDA (malondialdehyde) ELISA kits (Elabscience, China), lipid peroxidation (4-HNE) assay kits (Abcam, USA), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity assay kits (Elabscience, China), respectively, according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Human research was performed in accordance with the guidelines approved by the ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (approval number: 2024–063), and informed written consent from all participants was obtained prior to the research.

Statistical data analysis

The experiments were performed at least in triplicate. All experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Discrete metrics such as arthritis scores and OARSI and HSS scores were analysed via chi-square tests, with values of *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.005, and ****P < 0.001. Statistical significance among different groups with continuous variables was analysed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a value of *p < 0. 05, **p < 0. 01, ***p < 0. 005 and ****p < 0. 001.

Results

Extracellular acidification promotes H2O2-induced ferroptosis of M2 macrophages

Previous clinical studies have indicated that rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients commonly exhibit enhanced lipid peroxidation metabolism [14], but how lipid peroxidation promotes disease progression has not yet been elucidated. Given that H2O2 can initiate lipid peroxidation of cellular polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) via the Fe2+-mediated Fenton reaction [11], particularly under acidic pH [15], and that M2 macrophages are sensitive to oxidative stress [9], we speculated that the accumulation of acidic species and ROS in inflammatory tissues might disturb the balance of the M1/M2 ratio and promote the progression of disease. Compared with healthy individuals, RA patients had significantly higher serum levels of lipid peroxidation metabolites, such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA) (Fig. 1A). Conversely, the serum levels of the antioxidant glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) were reduced in RA patients (Fig. 1B). These results collectively suggest a positive correlation between lipid peroxidation and RA. Furthermore, we observed a reduced number of M2 macrophages and increased level of lipid peroxidation in the inflamed joint cavity of CIA mice (Fig. 1C - G), which were accompanied by increased levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and decreased pH (Fig. 1H & I). These results indicate that the CIA mouse model is suitable for subsequent studies.

Next, we investigated the effects of concurrent extracellular acidification and H2O2 exposure, which are utilized to mimic the unique microenvironment of the inflamed joint cavity of RA patients, on the viability of both M1 and M2 macrophages. With BODIPY-C11 as the characteristic probe for lipid peroxidation, we found that extracellular acidification (pH 6.5) significantly enhanced H2O2 exposure (100 mM)-induced intracellular lipid peroxidation in M2 macrophages, which was achieved by preincubating BMDMs with IL-4 (10 ng/mL) for 24 h (Fig. 1J, Fig. S1). In contrast, concurrent extracellular acidification and H2O2 exposure induced negligible intracellular lipid peroxidation in M1 macrophages, which was achieved by preincubating BMDMs with LPS (200 ng/mL) for 24 h. However, simple extracellular acidification itself had a minimal effect on the intracellular lipid peroxidation of both M1 and M2 macrophages. Such extracellular acidification promoted H2O2-induced lipid peroxidation in M2 macrophages, which was further confirmed via detection of the levels of MDA, another characteristic marker of lipid peroxidation, in M1 and M2 macrophages subjected to the same treatments via an MDA colorimetric assay (Fig. 1K). Furthermore, concurrent extracellular acidification and H2O2 exposure resulted in the selective ferroptosis of M2 macrophages (Fig. 1L).

Next, we investigated the detailed mechanism underlying how extracellular acidification promotes H2O2-induced ferroptosis of M2 macrophages. The extracellular acidification treatment significantly enhanced the H2O2 exposure-induced downregulation of glutathione (GSH), a key intracellular antioxidant that protects cells from ferroptosis (Fig. 1M). Furthermore, via a ferrous iron colorimetric assay, M2 macrophages subjected to extracellular acidification presented markedly increased intracellular concentrations of labile Fe2+, which is important in the Fenton reaction (Fig. 1N). Western blotting revealed that M2 macrophages subjected to concurrent extracellular acidification and H2O2 exposure presented markedly reduced expression of xCT and GPX4, both of which are important antioxidant proteins involved in ferroptosis (Fig. 1O). More surprisingly, we observed that M2 macrophages subjected to extracellular acidification presented significantly reduced expression of ferritin heavy chain (FHC) (Fig. 1O & P), an important intracellular iron-containing protein [16, 17]. Taken together, these results demonstrate that extracellular acidification could enhance H2O2 exposure-induced ferroptosis of M2 macrophages by concurrently augmenting H2O2 exposure-induced downregulation of intracellular antioxidants (e.g., GSH, xCT, and GPX4) and increasing the intracellular labile Fe2+ content via downregulation of FHC expression.

Fabrication and characterization of proton- and ROS-scavenging TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles

Because extracellular acidification can enhance H2O2 exposure-induced ferroptosis in M2 macrophages, we hypothesized that the concurrent scavenging of extracellular protons and ROS might be capable of protecting M2 macrophages from ferroptosis, thereby relieving intraarticular inflammation. To this end, a bifunctional TA@CaCO3 nanomodulator possessing ROS scavenging and acidity neutralization capacities was fabricated by utilizing the strong coordination interactions between Ca2+ and the phenolic hydroxyl groups of TA and the carboxyl groups of PGA5K-PEG10K via a biomineralization process (Fig. 2A). At an optimized mass feeding ratio of CaCl2 to TA of 1:1, the as-synthesized TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles showed a uniform spherical morphology and a mean size of 110 nm according to transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (Fig. 2B). High-angle annular dark field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) analysis (Fig. 2C) revealed uniform distribution profiles of Ca, C, and O within the TA@CaCO₃ nanoparticles. The existence of Ca2+ in such TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles was ultimately verified by recording their energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra (Fig. S2A&B). Furthermore, through selected area electron diffraction (SAED) analysis, we found that the TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles presented no clear lattice fringes or crystallized peaks, indicating that they feature an amorphous structure (Fig. 2D). In sharp contrast, the CaCO3 particles formed in the absence of TA had a relatively large mean size of 770 nm with a typical calcite crystalline structure (Fig. S2C-F). Collectively, these results highlight a facile strategy to synthesize monodispersed TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles.

Then, via dynamic light scattering (DLS), the obtained TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles exhibited monodispersed size distribution behavior, and their mean diameter was determined to be approximately 150 nm (Fig. 2E), which was consistent with that determined via the aforementioned TEM imaging. The loading of TA in the TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles was calculated to be 13.7% through a commonly used Folin–Denis chromogenic reagent, whereas the content of CaCO3 was determined to be 74.0% via inductively coupled plasma‒optical emission spectrometry (ICP‒OES). The TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles also showed excellent stability in various solutions, including phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 0.9% NaCl, cell medium and H2O (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, the TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles rapidly decomposed upon incubation in a low-pH environment (pH 6.5) because of the instant reaction between the protons and CaCO3. This was confirmed by TEM observations (Fig. 2G), which collectively highlight the pH sensitivity of the TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles. To better evaluate the acid-responsive drug release characteristics of nanomedicine, we examined the release capacity of TA in different acidic solutions (pH 7.4 or 6.5). The result in Fig. S3 showed that in acidic environments, a significant amount of TA was released from the very beginning, demonstrating the acid-responsive property. In contrast, almost no noticeable release of TA was observed in neutral solutions, indicating the stability of TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles.

Thereafter, as monitored by a pH microelectrode, we evaluated the proton scavenging capacity of TA@CaCO3 by recording the pH evolution profile in a low-pH environment (pH 6.5) following the introduction of TA@CaCO3. The addition of TA@CaCO3 or plain CaCO3 nanoparticles rapidly neutralized the acidic solution (Fig. 2H). In marked contrast, the addition of TA molecules alone had a negligible effect on the pH of the solution. The pronounced acid-neutralizing efficacy of TA@CaCO3 was further substantiated by pH-responsive ratiometric fluorescence imaging with a commercial fluorescent probe, BCECF-AM (Fig. 2I&J). Taken together, these results demonstrate the instant proton scavenging capacity of TA@CaCO3, primarily due to the existence of CaCO3 (Fig. 2K).

Next, we evaluated the ROS scavenging performance of TA@CaCO3 by first utilizing the colored oxidized ABTS+· and DPPH· radicals as the modelling ROS according to previous studies [18]. Via spectrophotometry, we found that adding TA@CaCO3 to the ABTS+· solution led to the decay of its green color in a concentration-dependent manner, indicating the effective reduction of oxidized ABTS+· to the original ABTS (Fig. 2L). By quantitative comparison, treatment with TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles (100 µg mL− 1 in terms of TA, 0.5 h) led to an ~ 88.4% reduction in ABTS+·, whereas it was ~ 96.1% for free TA treatment under the same conditions (Fig. 2M, Fig. S4A). However, the plain CaCO3 nanoparticles showed a negligible reduction in ABTS+· (Fig. S4B). The efficient concentration-dependent ROS scavenging ability of TA@CaCO3 was further confirmed by the use of colored DPPH· radicals, superoxide radicals (·O2−) and hydroxyl radicals (·OH) as representative ROS (Fig. 2N-Q, Fig. S4C&D). These results demonstrate that TA@CaCO3 is a broad-spectrum ROS-scavenging agent (Fig. 3R).

TA@CaCO3 protects M2 macrophages from extracellular acidification and H2O2 exposure-induced ferroptosis

We then investigated the ability of TA@CaCO3 to protect M2 macrophages from ferroptosis in vitro. By using a cell-permeable ratiometric pH probe, 2’,7’-bis-(2-carboxyethyl)-5-(and-6)-carboxyfluorescein, acetoxymethyl ester (BCECF-AM, 1 mM), it was first verified that concurrent extracellular acidification and H2O2 exposure led to a reduced intracellular pH inside M2 macrophages, which aligns with the results reported in prior research [19]. Treatment with both CaCO3 and TA@CaCO3 reversed such concurrent extracellular acidification, and H2O2 exposure induced an intracellular pH reduction, whereas the addition of free TA minimally disturbed the intracellular pH of M2 macrophages with concurrent extracellular acidification and H2O2 exposure (Fig. 3A & B). Moreover, the DCFH staining results revealed that the addition of TA@CaCO3 effectively reversed concurrent extracellular acidification, and H2O2 exposure induced intracellular ROS amplification with comparable efficacy to that of free TA (Fig. 3C & D). The addition of plain CaCO3 had a negligible effect on the amplification of these intracellular ROS. These results collectively confirm that TA@CaCO3 is promising for enabling concurrent acid neutralization and ROS scavenging.

After that, we investigated the capacity of TA@CaCO3 to protect M2 macrophages from extracellular acidification, and H2O2 exposure induced intracellular lipid peroxidation and thereby ferroptosis. BODIPY-C11 staining revealed that TA@CaCO3 treatment significantly suppressed intracellular lipid peroxidation in M2 macrophages with concurrent acidification and H2O2 exposure (Fig. 3E & F). The exceptional capacity of TA@CaCO3 to mitigate both extracellular acidification and H2O2 exposure-induced lipid peroxidation in M2 macrophages was additionally validated by quantifying the intracellular levels of 4-HNE and MDA via immunofluorescence and an MDA assay kit, respectively (Fig. 3G - I). Ferroptosis is often accompanied by disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential. The results demonstrate that the nanomedicine effectively stabilizes membrane potential and mitigates its decline, indicating its role in suppressing mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. S5A - B).

Furthermore, TA@CaCO3 treatment reversed the decrease in extracellular acidification, and H2O2 exposure induced a decrease in the levels of the antioxidant species xCT, GPX4 and GSH (Fig. 3J - L, N), as well as FHC (Fig. 3J & M), thereby leading to significantly reduced intracellular liable Fe2+ in M2 macrophages (Fig. 3O) via the methods mentioned above. Consequently, TA@CaCO3 treatment protected M2 macrophages from extracellular acidification, and H2O2 exposure induced cell death (Fig. 3P). Collectively, the findings presented here underscore the potential of TA@CaCO3 as a robust protective agent against ferroptosis triggered by extracellular acidification and H₂O₂ exposure in M2 macrophages. Building upon the confirmed protective effect of TA@CaCO3 on M2 macrophages, through cellular immunofluorescence, we further demonstrated that the nanomedicine can mitigate the release of inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β) (Fig. S6A - D). Moreover, beyond the suppression of inflammatory factors, we observed a significant balance in the polarization of both M1 and M2 macrophages (Fig. S7A - B).

TA@CaCO3 inhibits intra-articular acidification and lipid peroxidation

Initially, we investigated the retention behaviors of TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles within inflamed joints in CIA patients following intra-articular administration. By tracking the fluorescence signal from cyanine 5.5 (Cy5.5), which adheres to TA@CaCO3 in a noncovalent manner, we discovered that these TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles persisted in inflamed joints for more than 7 days (Fig. 4A). Semiquantitative analysis revealed that the fluorescence intensity decreased to approximately 30% within one week (Fig. 4B). Moreover, free TA was shown to be capable of complexing with proteins (e.g., bovine serum albumin) to form nanoparticles of a size comparable to that of TA@CaCO3 (Fig. S8A&B). These results collectively indicate that TA@CaCO3, CaCO3 and free TA upon intraarticular injection could remain inside the inflamed keen joints for a rather long time.

Next, we evaluated the capacity of TA@CaCO3 to scavenge protons and ROS in inflamed joints. To this end, four groups of CIA mice in the acute inflammatory phase were treated with a single intraarticular injection of 10 µL of PBS, TA (1 mg/mL), CaCO3 nanoparticles (1 mg/mL), or TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles (1 mg/mL). Seven days later, these mice were intraarticularly injected with a ratiometric fluorescent pH probe of BCECF and then subjected to in vivo fluorescence imaging. The acidic nature of the inflamed knee joints in CIA mice was initially confirmed, as evidenced by the weaker fluorescence of BCECF-AM excited at 480 nm than that observed in healthy mice (Fig. 4C). The results of ratiometric fluorescence imaging revealed that intraarticular injections of both plain CaCO3 and TA@CaCO3 significantly increased the fluorescence of BCECF-AM excited at 480 nm in these inflamed knee joints, whereas intraarticular injection of free TA did not obviously disturb the fluorescence intensity of BCECF-AM excited at 480 nm (Fig. 4D). Moreover, the DCFH staining results indicated that, compared with those of healthy mice, the inflamed joints of CIA mice presented significantly increased ROS production, and TA@CaCO3 treatment was also capable of effectively reducing intraarticular ROS in a manner comparable to that of free RA treatment (Fig. 4E & F). In sharp contrast, treatment with plain CaCO3 nanoparticles minimally disturbed the intraarticular oxidative stress levels. These results collectively demonstrate that TA@CaCO3 is able to effectively scavenge intraarticular protons and ROS.

The BODIPY-C11 staining results indicated that, compared with no treatment, TA@CaCO3 treatment effectively suppressed intraarticular lipid peroxidation in inflamed keen joints. In contrast, treatment with plain CaCO3 NPs or free TA only slightly suppressed lipid peroxidation inside inflamed keen joints (Fig. 4G & H). In addition, compared with untreated CIA mice, TA@CaCO3-treated CIA mice presented increased expression levels of xCT, GPX4, and FHC (Fig. S9A - F) in inflamed keen joints. Furthermore, the immunofluorescence staining results revealed that, compared with those of untreated CIA mice, the inflamed keen joint slices subjected to TA@CaCO3 treatment presented significantly increased expression of CD206, a characteristic surface marker of M2 macrophages, but markedly reduced expression of iNOS, a characteristic surface marker of M1 macrophages (Fig. 4I & J). Moreover, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed that the TA@CaCO3-treated CIA mice also presented obviously reduced expression of proinflammatory TNF-α (Fig. 4K & L) and increased expression of anti-inflammatory IL-10 (Fig. 4M & N) in these inflamed keen joints. Therefore, these results collectively verify that TA@CaCO3 treatment is able to restore the normal balance of M1/M2 inside the joint by suppressing intraarticular lipid peroxidation through the simultaneous scavenging of protons and ROS.

TA@CaCO3 suppresses inflammation in CIA mice

We then investigated the therapeutic efficacy of TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles in CIA mice (Fig. 5A). To this end, five groups of CIA mice (n = 8 in each group) were subjected to the following treatments: (1) PBS, (2) TA (1 mg/mL), (3) CaCO3 nanoparticles (1 mg/mL), (4) TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles (1 mg/mL), and MTX (5 mg/kg per mouse). The mice in groups 1–4 were intraarticularly injected three times per week, whereas the mice in group 5 were intraperitoneally injected six times every three days. CIA mice treated with PBS presented noticeable paw swelling, as indicated by rapid increases in paw thickness and arthritis scores from day 27 (Fig. 5B, C, E). The arthritis severity score in the PBS group on day 42 was 3.1 ± 0.4. In sharp contrast, TA@CaCO3 treatment effectively suppressed the progression of paw swelling, and the arthritis score on day 42 was determined to be 1.1 ± 0.4, which was comparable to the value of 1.3 ± 0.4 for the MTX-treated mice. Moreover, the ELISA results indicated that TA@CaCO3 treatment was also able to decrease the serum level of inflammatory factors, including TNF-a (Fig. 5D), IL-1β and IL-6 (Fig. S10A - B), which is closely related to the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis.

Given that inflammation in the rheumatoid joint is also prone to cause destruction and resorption of subchondral bones and articular cartilage [20], microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) was utilized to record the bone morphology of the inflamed hind paws of CIA mice subjected to different treatments and healthy control mice. Compared with those of healthy control mice, the metatarsophalangeal joints of CIA model mice injected with PBS exhibited severe osteolysis, whereas those of CIA model mice treated with TA@CaCO3 presented minimal osteolysis (Fig. 5F). Via quantitative analysis, the value of the bone volume (BV)/total volume (TV) of the internal region (ROI, 1.1 × 0.5 × 1.8 mm) in the interphalangeal joints of the CIA model mice injected with PBS, an important index of osteolysis, was determined to be 29.78 ± 1.39% (Fig. S11A). In contrast, the BV/TV of the CIA mice treated with TA@CaCO3 was 39.93 ± 1.07%, which was comparable to the values of 40.99 ± 1.16% for the CIA mice treated with MTX and 44.99 ± 0.84% for the healthy control mice (Fig. S11A). Furthermore, the number of trabecular bones (Tb.N) in CIA model mice injected with PBS, which play crucial roles in maintaining physiological homeostasis and structural stability of the bone [21], was determined to be only 1.24 ± 0.1/mm, which is significantly less than the 1.8 ± 0.09/mm and 1.89 ± 0.07/mm of TA@CaCO3-treated CIA mice and MTX-treated mice, respectively (Fig. S11B). These results collectively verify that TA@CaCO3 treatment is able to suppress inflammation-induced bone destruction.

During the outburst of inflammation, the synovium on both sides of the joint is infiltrated by inflammatory cells, which can cause severe synovial hyperplasia and pannus formation upon spreading into the center of the joints [22]. Therefore, hematoxylin‒eosin (H&E) and Safranin O staining were performed to evaluate the ability of TA@CaCO3 treatment to suppress synovial hyperplasia and pannus formation in these inflamed knee joints. H&E staining revealed that, in contrast to those of healthy mice, the inflamed joints of CIA mice treated with PBS presented pronounced inflammatory infiltration, synovial hyperplasia and pannus formation (Fig. 5G). Conversely, the knee joints of CIA mice treated with TA@CaCO3 presented minimal pathological alterations. This finding was confirmed by the histological synovitis scoring system (HSS), which comprehensively evaluates parameters such as the density of inflammatory infiltration and the synovial surface cell layer (Fig. S11C) [23]. Furthermore, the Safranin O staining and corresponding OARSI score results demonstrated that TA@CaCO3 treatment was also capable of suppressing inflammation-induced cartilage damage, as indicated by the presence of high-density glycosaminoglycans in the articular cartilage of TA@CaCO3-treated CIA mice (Figs. 5H, S11D). Overall, these findings suggest that TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles have significant potential as effective therapeutic agents for treating RA by simultaneously neutralizing protons and scavenging ROS in inflamed joints.

Upon intra-articular injection, such TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles enabled efficient acidity neutralization and ROS scavenging and thus attenuated inflammation and bond destruction in inflamed joints with therapeutic efficacy comparable to that of MTX. However, unlike MTX, which exhibited apparent cytotoxicity to both M2 macrophages and chondrocytes, the TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles had negligible effects on the viability of these two kinds of cells (Fig. S12A & B). Meanwhile, after one month of treatment, HE staining of major organs in mice revealed that TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles had no obvious impact on the important organs of the mice, compared to PBS group (Fig. S13) (Fig. 6).

Discussion and conclusion

The inflamed joint cavities and other related tissues in RA patients are typically characterized by extracellular acidification, elevated oxidative stress levels, and an imbalanced M1/M2 ratio [24]. However, the detailed interplay among extracellular acidification, increased oxidation and an imbalanced M1/M2 ratio has not yet been elucidated. Herein, it was demonstrated that extracellular acidification could cooperate with H2O2, an abundant ROS in inflamed tissues, to induce ferroptosis in M2 macrophages through collectively downregulating the levels of intracellular antioxidants (e.g., GSH, xCT, and GPX4) and increasing the intracellular labile Fe2+ content via the downregulation of FHC expression. Together with the fact that M1 macrophages with high iNOS expression can scavenge ROS by catalytically producing NO, extracellular acidification and elevated oxidative stress levels result in imbalanced M1/M2 ratios.

Tannic acid (TA), a well-documented and widely recognized antioxidant, is characterized by its diverse sources, straightforward preparation, and favorable economic benefits [25, 26]. For these reasons, we selected TA as the drug loaded onto the nanoplatform. Multiple results from our cellular and animal experiments confirm that under the conditions of intense oxidative stress in RA microenvironment, TA effectively reduces the levels of ROS that trigger subsequent inflammation and injury, while preventing the depletion of antioxidants associated with ferroptosis. Furthermore, we discovered that acids can effectively promote ferroptosis and enhance ROS-induced ferroptotic effects.

Recently, numerous studies have demonstrated that CaCO3-based formulations can reverse tumor immunosuppression or attenuate inflammation associated with aging-related tissue damage (e.g., intervertebral disc degeneration) by directly neutralizing excess protons in corresponding extracellular microenvironments [27, 28]. Therefore, in this experiment, CaCO₃ was not only widely utilized as a nanomedicine but also leveraged for its acid-neutralizing capacity. Together with the potent ROS scavenging capacity of TA, a plant polyphenol, we rationally constructed drug-free TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles with excellent proton and ROS scavenging capacities and physiological stability via a PGA-PEG-templated biomineralization process for effective RA treatment. Although acid neutralization alone exhibits limited efficacy in counteracting ROS, the combination of TA and CaCO₃ demonstrated significant inhibition of M2 macrophage ferroptosis and mitigation of inflammatory outbursts. This effect was not only confirmed at the cellular level but, more importantly, validated within the complex inflammatory joint microenvironment in RA mouse model.

As summarized in Fig. 6, in this study, we first revealed that extracellular acidification could increase the degree of oxidative stress-induced ferroptosis of anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages via the collective promotion of the accumulation of cytotoxic lipid peroxides. Next, we developed a concise biomineralization method for preparing TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles to protect M2 macrophages from ferroptosis, highlighting a revolutionary strategy for treating inflammation-related diseases via direct modulation of the extracellular microenvironment of inflamed tissues. Considering that TA is an important component of traditional medicine and that tannins and CaCO3 are widely used pharmaceutical excipients, TA@CaCO3 nanoparticles with excellent biocompatibility hold great promise for future clinical translation.
