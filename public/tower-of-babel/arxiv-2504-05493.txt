Neural network-enhanced integrators for simulating ordinary differential equations
Amine Othmane, Kathrin Flaßkamp

Abstract
Numerous applications necessitate the computation of numerical solutions to differential equations across a wide range of initial conditions and system parameters, which feeds the demand for efficient yet accurate numerical integration methods. This study proposes a neural network (NN) enhancement of classical numerical integrators. NNs are trained to learn integration errors, which are then used as additive correction terms in numerical schemes. The performance of these enhanced integrators is compared with well-established methods through numerical studies, with a particular emphasis on computational efficiency. Analytical properties are examined in terms of local errors and backward error analysis. Embedded Runge-Kutta schemes are then employed to develop enhanced integrators that mitigate generalization risk, ensuring that the neural network’s evaluation in previously unseen regions of the state space does not destabilize the integrator. It is guaranteed that the enhanced integrators perform at least as well as the desired classical Runge-Kutta schemes. The effectiveness of the proposed approaches is demonstrated through extensive numerical studies using a realistic model of a wind turbine, with parameters derived from the established simulation framework OpenFast.



keywords
artificial neural networks, numerical methods, ordinary differential equations, Runge-Kutta

1
Introduction

The need for accurate yet efficient numerical solutions to differential equations remains a cornerstone of modern computational mathematics and engineering. For example, the analysis of fatigue effects and lifetime prediction of complex technological systems, e.g. wind turbines, requires repeatedly solving differential equations under a variety of varying initial conditions and system parameters.
The inherently stochastic characteristics of wind conditions encompassing turbulence, shear, and wake interactions necessitate extensive numerical simulations over a wide range of environmental and operational conditions to enable reliable fatigue assessment.
This example demonstrates the challenges faced in numerical integration: the need for high accuracy in approximating solutions while being constrained by computational resources. The traditional approach, which relies heavily on classical numerical methods such as Runge–Kutta methods (e.g.,
Hairer et al. (1993)
;
Butcher (2016)
), often encounters limitations when scaling to high-dimensional systems or when long-term integration is required.

1.1
State of the art

In order to overcome these limitations, extrapolation techniques have been developed to enhance both the accuracy and efficiency of numerical solvers. One of the earliest such techniques is Richardson extrapolation
Richardson (1910)
;
Richardson (1927)
;
Atkinson (1989)
, which systematically eliminates the leading error term by combining solutions computed with different step sizes. More generally, extrapolation methods form the basis of adaptive algorithms that refine the solution iteratively using estimates of the local error. In particular, the Bulirsch–Stoer algorithm
Stoer and Bulirsch (2002)
employs Richardson extrapolation applied to the modified midpoint rule, efficiently estimating the local error and extrapolating to the zero-step-size limit. These methods have proven particularly effective in handling stiff or high-dimensional systems, as they leverage accurate local error estimates to reduce the overall computational effort without sacrificing precision.
Adaptive step-size control further enhances solver performance. Embedded Runge–Kutta pairs
Dormand and Prince (1980)
dynamically adjust the time step using error estimates computed during the integration process, ensuring that the local error remains within a specified tolerance while optimizing computational effort.

Neural networks possess remarkable approximation properties, as demonstrated by the universal approximation theorem, which asserts that a neural network with a sufficient number of neurons and appropriate activation functions, such as the rectified linear unit, can under mild assumptions approximate any continuous function to any desired degree of accuracy
Cybenko (1989)
;
Hornik et al. (1989)
;
Hornik (1991)
;
Hanin (2019)
;
Yarotsky (2017)
. These properties have been used in recent years to develop neural network‐based methods for approximating the solutions of differential equations. In particular, mesh‐free approaches for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) have been developed and shown to outperform traditional solvers in terms of computational speed and flexibility
Li et al. (2020a)
;
Li et al. (2020b)
. Moreover, hybrid strategies that combine neural networks with classical time‐stepping low-order methods have been investigated as a means to enhance accuracy and stability in the numerical solution of ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
Shen et al. (2020)
;
Huang et al. (2022)
;
Huang et al. (2023)
. The correction of the forward Euler method has been considered and analyzed in
Shen et al. (2020)
;
Huang et al. (2022)
. Simulations using an enhanced Heun method have been considered in
Shen et al. (2020)
.

1.2
Contribution

Taking advantage of the ability of NNs to approximate nonlinear functions, we propose a general methodology in which NNs are trained to learn the integration error of arbitrary order explicit one-step Runge-Kutta methods.
This learned error is then used as an additive correction term for the classical discrete flow to achieve a better compromise between computational load and accuracy.
Such a hybrid approach aims to combine the physics-based classical numerical techniques with the adaptive learning capabilities of neural networks. Our investigation includes an analysis of the theoretical properties of the solvers such as global error, modified differential equation and accuracy-efficiency trade-offs for arbitrary Runge-Kutta methods.
Moreover, by using embedded Runge-Kutta schemes in an adaptive algorithm, we ensure that outside the validity domain of the network, the proposed solver is not destabilized.
It is guaranteed that the enhanced integrators perform at least as well a user defined classical Runge-Kutta schemes.
The numerical experiments for a non-trivial academic example validate the theoretical findings and confirm that the enhanced integrator can outperform classical methods in the accuracy-efficiency trade-off.

1.3
Application case study

The effectiveness of the proposed methods are shows using a wind turbine model developed within the CADynTurb framework
Geisler (2021a)
;
Geisler (2021b)
;
Geisler (2024)
with 16 state components. Designed to capture the nonlinear, multi-degree-of-freedom dynamics of wind turbines, the model incorporates eight generalized coordinates that represent key structural and electromechanical components—including tower displacements, blade flapwise and edgewise bending modes, and drivetrain as well as generator dynamics. Derived from the Euler–Lagrange equations and parameterized using publicly available data such as the NREL 5-MW reference turbine
Jonkman et al. (2009)
, this model is capable of simulating the complex cyclic loading conditions essential for fatigue analysis
Shaler et al. (2023)
;
Ding et al. (2024)
;
Liu and Mavris (2023)
;
Schafhirt et al. (2022)
. Its balance between physical fidelity and computational efficiency makes it ideally suited for extensive simulations required in design optimization and real-time estimation tasks.

(3)

(4)

such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}

Example 1
.

The simplest of all numerical schemes is the forward Euler method.
The discrete flow is then given as

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}+hf(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}).

(5)

Example 2
.

A second-order improvement is provided by the explicit trapezoidal rule, also known as Heun’s method. It evaluates the vector field at both the initial and predicted states such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{h}{2}(k_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})+k_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}))
with

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle k_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),

(6)

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle k_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{x}+hk_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}).

(7)

In the sequel, the error
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})-\nu_{(k+1)h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{x}((k-1)h)

A numerical scheme is said to be of order

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h^{p+1}
(see, e.g.,
Hairer et al. (1993)
).
It is known that reducing the step size or using a high-order integration scheme can reduce the error. However, this yields a critical trade-off between computation speed and accuracy. Different approaches dealing with this trade-off such as extrapolation techniques or variable step sizes have also been developed in the literature
Richardson (1910)
;
Richardson (1927)
;
Atkinson (1989)
;
Stoer and Bulirsch (2002)
;
Butcher (2016)
;
Hairer et al. (1993)
.

Problem statement:

3
Neural network enhanced integrators

In this section, we outline a method to correct numerical integration errors by training artificial neural networks to learn these discrepancies.
The error approximation is then used as an additive correction term to the discrete flow.
Then, enhanced integrators with mitigated generalization risk are proposed.
The theoretical properties of the approaches are analyzed.

3.1
Definition of neural networks

Consider a Lipschitz continuous activation function

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\xi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and define for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
the function
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\Xi:\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto(\xi(x_{1}),\ldots,\xi(x_{n}))^{T}}
, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x_{j}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, is the
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}j
-th component of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{x}
. An artificial neural network of depth
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L\in\mathbb{N}
is a function
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{\mathrm{in}},n_{\mathrm{out}}\in\mathbb{N}
, that maps
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}
to

\mathcal{N}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x})=T_{L}\Xi\left(T_{L-1}\Xi\left(\ldots\Xi(T_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right)\right),

(8)

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T_{l}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
, is an affine-linear transformation defined as

T_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{l-1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}},\;T_{l}x=W^{(l)}x+b^{(l)},\;n_{0}=n_{\mathrm{in}},\;n_{L}=n_{\mathrm{out}},

(9)

with
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{W^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times n_{l-1}}}
the weight matrices and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{b^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}}
the bias vector of the
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}l
-th layer all of which are summarizes in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta
such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\theta=\left\{(W^{(l)},b^{(l)})\right\}_{l=1}^{L}}
. Denote in the following by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Theta
the set of all possible
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{(W^{(l)},b^{(l)})}
, i.e.,

\Theta=\left\{(W^{(l)},b^{(l)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}\times n_{l-1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{l}}|l\in\{1,\ldots,L\}\right\}.

3.2
Learning problem for local error correction

Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{K}}
be a finite subset of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{N}
and consider the set
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathcal{D}=\{(k_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{0i},\boldsymbol{p}_{i})\in\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{S}_{x}\times\mathcal{{P}}\,|\,i\in\{1,\ldots,{N_{\mathcal{D}}}\}\}}
of finite cardinality
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N_{\mathcal{D}}
with time steps, initial conditions, and parameters. For each element in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}
define for a numerical scheme with discrete flow
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}
the local error
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x}
that maps each element
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(k,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
to

r(k,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})={\nu_{(k+1)h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})-\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}\!\left(\!\nu_{kh}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\!\right)}.

(10)

We now employ a neural network, denoted by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
, to construct an approximation of the local error (
10
), with the aim of its subsequent incorporation into an adapted numerical scheme for error compensation.
The parameter set
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta
of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
is computed using the flow
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu_{t}
or a high order approximation of it evaluated at different times and for various initial states and parameter configurations. The generation of the data can be done using exact analytical solutions, high-fidelity numerical solvers, or empirical data. Subsequent validation against simulated trajectories reveals that a small number of trajectories suffice to yield satisfactory approximations. This is discussed in detail in Section
4
.

The parameters
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\theta
of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
are then computed as the solution of an optimization problem

\theta=\argmin_{\tilde{\theta}\in\Theta}\frac{1}{N_{\mathcal{D}}}\sum_{(k,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\theta}}(k,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})

(11a)

with the function
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\theta}}
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\theta}}(k,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})=

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\bigl(\nu_{kh}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\bigr)-\tfrac{1}{h^{p+1}}r\!\bigl(k,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\bigr)\Big\rVert^{2},

(11b)

to approximate the local error, where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is some norm.

Note that the loss function (
11b
) depends on the step size
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h
and thus classical approximation arguments for numerical integrators can be applied for the subsequent analysis.
For a consistent integrator, there exists a function
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta
such that the local truncation error is equal to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h^{p+1}\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{p+2}\right)

Hairer et al. (2006)
. Because the optimization problem (
11a
) uses samples

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
in the entire region spanned by the training data,
the network thus approximates
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
across that
entire state domain. Thus, when the loss function (
11b
) of the optimal solution is of order
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{O}\left(h^{p+2}\right)
the approach
provides first-order approximation in the step size
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h
to the
local error within that region.

3.3
Enhanced solvers

Once the training of the neural network successfully converged to a good minimizer of (
11a
),
the solution of (
2
) can then be approximated using an enhanced numerical scheme defined by

\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k})+h^{p+1}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),

(12)

for all
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k\geq 0
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
.

Using an approximation of the local error to enhance numerical schemes is a well known approach in the classical numerical integration literature. A well known method is the Richardson extrapolation developed in
Richardson (1910)
;
Richardson (1927)
(see also
(
Hairer et al., 1993
, Ch. II.4)
).
Consider a numerical approximation
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
of the solution
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nu_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
of the initial value problem (
2
) obtained using a numerical scheme with step size
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h
. The Richardson extrapolation technique aims to improve the accuracy of this approximation by combining approximations with different step sizes. Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h_{1}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h_{2}
be two different step sizes such that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{h_{2}=\frac{h_{1}}{2}}
. Denote by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}(h_{1})
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}(h_{2})
the approximations obtained with step sizes
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h_{1}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h_{2}
, respectively. The Richardson extrapolated solution
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}^{\mathrm{rich}}
is given by

\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}^{\mathrm{rich}}=\frac{2^{p}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}(h_{2})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}(h_{1})}{2^{p}-1},

(13)

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p
is the order of the numerical scheme used. This extrapolation effectively cancels out the leading error term, resulting in a more accurate approximation of the true solution.

The enhanced numerical scheme (
12
) resembles the idea of extrapolations methods. However, approximating the local truncation error using neural networks instead of the Richardson extrapolation method, for example, yields more design freedom. This extra flexibility given by possible variations of the cost function in (
11
) permits the incorporation of tailored regularization terms, domain-specific weighting, or alternative error norms into the training process, allowing the network to more accurately capture and correct the local error structure. Consequently, such an approach can lead to enhanced robustness and improved generalization of the error correction compared to fixed, classical extrapolation techniques.

3.4
Analysis of enhanced integrators

Proposition 1
.

Consider the initial value problem (
2
) and an artificial neural network

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{p}\;\to\;\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}.

Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}
be a discrete flow of a numerical scheme of order
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p
with step size
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h
,
and denote by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}}
a Lipschitz constant of it.
Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L_{\mathcal{N}}{}_{\theta}
be a Lipschitz constant of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}

and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\epsilon_{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}>0
a scalar such that

\bigl\lVert r(k,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})-h^{p+1}\,\mathcal{N}_{\theta}(\nu_{kh}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\bigr\rVert\;<\;\epsilon_{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}\,h^{p+1},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},\;\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{x},\;\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathcal{{P}},

(14)

with the local error
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}r
defined in (
10
).

Consider the error

\boldsymbol{e}_{k+1}\;=\;\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}\bigr)\;+\;h^{p+1}\,\mathcal{N}_{\theta}\bigl(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\bigr)\;-\;\nu_{(k+1)h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),

with
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
defined by (
12
).
Then, the bound

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{k+1}\right\rVert\;\leq\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\exp\!\Bigl((k+1)\,\bigl(\alpha-1\bigr)\Bigr)\;-\;1}{\alpha-1}\,\epsilon_{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}\,h^{p+1},&\text{if }\alpha>1,\\
(k+1)\,\epsilon_{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}\,h^{p+1},&\text{if }\alpha=1,\\
\dfrac{\epsilon_{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}\,h^{p+1}}{\,1-\alpha\,},&\text{if }0<\alpha<1,\end{cases}

(15)

with
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha=L_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}}\;+\;h^{p+1}L_{\mathcal{N}}{}_{\theta}
, is satisfied for all
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{x}
, and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathcal{{P}}
.

Proof.

Let for notational simplicity
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\coloneqq\nu_{k+1h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})
.
Then from the hypothesis on
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
, for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k=0
we have

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\right\rVert\;=\;\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\;+\;h^{p+1}\,\mathcal{N}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\;-\;\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert\;<\;\epsilon_{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}\,h^{p+1}\;\eqqcolon\;\beta.

(16)

Using the Lipschitz continuity of both
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
and the discrete flow
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}
, one obtains
for any
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{k+1}\right\rVert\;\leq\;\epsilon_{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}\,h^{p+1}\;+\;\Bigl(L_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}}\;+\;h^{p+1}L_{\mathcal{N}}{}_{\theta}\Bigr)\,\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\right\rVert\;=\;\beta\;+\;\alpha\,\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\right\rVert.

(17)

This implies the linear recurrence

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{k+1}\right\rVert\;\leq\;\alpha\,\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\right\rVert\;+\;\beta.

(18)

By standard induction (unrolling this recurrence), we get

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{k+1}\right\rVert\;\leq\;\beta\,\sum_{j=0}^{k}\alpha^{j}.

(19)

We now distinguish three cases:

(i)

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha>1
:
Recalling that
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha^{k+1}\,\leq\,e^{(k+1)(\alpha-1)}
yields

\sum_{j=0}^{k}\alpha^{j}\;=\;\frac{\alpha^{k+1}-1}{\alpha-1}\;\leq\;\frac{\exp\!\bigl((k+1)(\alpha-1)\bigr)\;-\;1}{\alpha-1}.

(20)

Thus,

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{k+1}\right\rVert\;\leq\;\beta\;\frac{\exp\!\bigl((k+1)(\alpha-1)\bigr)-1}{\alpha-1},

(21)

as in the first line of (
15
).

(ii)

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha=1
:
The geometric sum simply becomes
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sum_{j=0}^{k}1=k+1
.
Hence

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}0<\alpha<1

For a neural network
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{O}\left(h^{p+2}\right)

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2})

3.5
Enhanced integrators with mitigated generalization risk

Numerous numerical integration algorithms that adapt the step size to achieve a prescribed tolerance for the local error have been developed (see, e.g.,
(
Hairer et al., 1993
, Ch. II.4)
) and are part of most modern numerical computing toolboxes. The core idea of these approaches is the use of two Runge–Kutta schemes of order
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p+1
, respectively, to compute (at least asymptotically) an estimate for the local error. If the error is not within prescribed bounds, the step size is adjusted to ensure both computational efficiency and desired tolerances. This idea is used in the following to mitigate the risk of large NN prediction errors and to only trust the NN if its prediction roughly agrees with prescribed tolerances.

Consider in the following two numerical flows
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{}_{p+1}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{}_{p}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}
for two integrators of orders
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{p+1}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p
, respectively.

3.5.1
Local error approximation:

An asymptotic estimate for the local error of the less precise method is

{}_{p}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})={}_{p+1}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})-{}_{p}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}).

(27)

Denote by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})_{i}
the value of the
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i
-th component of
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
. As in automatic step size control algorithms, define a scaling factor

\text{sc}_{i}\;=\;\mathrm{Atol}_{i}\;+\;\max\bigl(|(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})_{i}|,\;|({}_{p+1}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}))_{i}|\bigr)\,\mathrm{Rtol}_{i},

(28)

which accounts for absolute and relative tolerances prescribed by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Atol}_{i}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Rtol}_{i}
, respectively. Relative errors are considered for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Atol}_{i}=0
and absolute errors for
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{Rtol}_{i}=0
.

3.5.2
Mitigating the generalization risk of neural networks:

Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
be a neural network with parameters solving (
11
), trained to enhance the solver corresponding to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{}_{p}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}
. The neural network aims to approximate the local truncation error of the classical integrator of order
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p
.

The discrepancy between the true local error and the neural network prediction can be assessed by considering

\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})={}_{p}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})-h^{p+1}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}).

(29)

To ensure the reliability of the network prediction during generalization, we introduce the normalized error

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})=\left(\tfrac{(\boldsymbol{\delta}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}))_{i}}{\text{sc}_{i}}\right)_{i=1}^{n},

(30)

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{sc}_{i}
denotes a component-wise scaling factor (
28
).

Let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
denote an arbitrary vector norm. The training dataset
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{D}
used in (
11
) is employed to define a threshold

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\text{max}}=\kappa\max_{(k,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\in\mathcal{D}}\left\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(k,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\right\rVert,\qquad\kappa\geq 1,

(31)

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\kappa

The quantity
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(k,\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\right\rVert

(32)

the enhanced solver (informed by the network prediction) is employed; otherwise, the algorithm reverts to the classical integrator of order
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p+1

3.5.3
Efficient implementation of hybrid enhanced solvers

Embedded Runge–Kutta formulas are a classical tool to obtain multiple approximations of different orders using a shared set of stage evaluations. Such constructions allow for the efficient implementation of higher-order methods by reusing intermediate computations from lower-order schemes. In particular, given a method of order
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p
, an embedded formula of order
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{p}<p
can often be constructed using the same set of stage values or a subset thereof; see
(
Hairer et al., 1993
, Section II.4)
and
Dormand and Prince (1980)
. These methods can be used to implement the hybrid solvers summarized in Algorithm
1
by using a minimal number of further vector field evaluations. This is illustrated in the following example.

Example 3
.

A three-stage third-order Runge–Kutta method can be constructed by combining evaluations at the initial point, a forward Euler step from Example
1
, and a midpoint evaluation using a trapezoidal predictor from Example
2
(see also, e.g.,
Hairer et al. (1993)
). The numerical scheme is defined as follows:

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle k_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),

(36)

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle k_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle=f(\boldsymbol{x}+hk_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),

(37)

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle k_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle=f\left(\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{h}{4}(k_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})+k_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})),\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\right),

(38)

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}+h\left(\frac{1}{6}k_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{6}k_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{2}{3}k_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})\right).

(39)

For time-invariant systems, i.e. if the vector field does not depend explicitly on time, this scheme can be implemented efficiently by reusing computations from a Heun update. Specifically, the evaluations

k_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),\quad k_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{x}+hk_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\boldsymbol{p}_{0})

(40)

are already required for Heun’s method. To upgrade to the third-order Runge–Kutta method, only one additional evaluation,

k_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})=f\left(\boldsymbol{x}+\tfrac{h}{4}(k_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})+k_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})),\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\right),

(41)

is needed. This makes the higher-order method particularly attractive in contexts where intermediate values from simpler schemes are available or reused across solvers.

Numerical results

Fatigue analysis is a fundamental part of wind turbine design, required to ensure structural integrity over long operational lifetimes. Due to the stochastic nature of wind conditions, including turbulence, shear, and wake interactions, comprehensive fatigue evaluation requires extensive simulations in a wide range of environmental and operational parameters
Shaler et al. (2023)
;
Ding et al. (2024)
;
Liu and Mavris (2023)
.
These simulations quantify fatigue damage by accounting for cyclic loading variations, which are critical in components such as blades and tower structures. Recent studies highlight the importance of parameter sensitivity
Shaler et al. (2023)
, probabilistic modeling under coupled wind wave conditions
Ding et al. (2024)
, and surrogate models to reduce computational cost
Liu and Mavris (2023)
.
In practice, thousands of simulations are often necessary to capture the range of possible loading scenarios, making automated model generation and high-performance computing essential for fatigue-driven design optimization
Schafhirt et al. (2022)
. Thus, a good accuracy vs efficiency trade-off is crucial.

The model equations are derived using Lagrangian mechanics. The dynamics follow from the Euler–Lagrange equations

(43)

In compact matrix form, the equations of motion can be expressed as

\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}},\boldsymbol{p}_{0}),

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})
contains Coriolis and gyroscopic coupling terms, and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{q})
includes conservative forces such as gravitational and elastic restoring forces. The right-hand side vector
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}
encompasses external inputs and excitations, including aerodynamic forces. Wind parameters are summarized in
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{p}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{q}(0)=\mathbf{q}_{0}

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{ref.}}_{k}
, wind speed
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{p}
, is defined as

\mathcal{L}_{\tilde{\theta}}(k,0,\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{ref.}}_{k},\boldsymbol{p})=\left\lVert\mathcal{N}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{ref.}}_{k},\boldsymbol{p})-\tfrac{1}{\tilde{h}^{p+1}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{ref.}}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\tilde{h}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{ref.}}_{k})\right)\right\rVert^{2}.

(46)

The network is trained using mini-batch gradient descent, where the training data is divided into batches of fixed size to improve memory efficiency and training stability. Batch processing enables faster convergence and smoother optimization dynamics, especially in the presence of noisy gradients. The AdamW optimizer
Loshchilov and Hutter (2019)
is used to solve (
11a
).

Data generated using the wind speeds
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\{5,7.2\}$\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{m}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
are used for validating the prediction of the local error and tuning the
ReduceLROnPlateau
method in PyTorch Lightning. This scheduler reduces the learning rate when the loss function (
11b
) evaluated on the validation dataset ceases to improve. If no improvement is observed for a specified number of epochs, the learning rate is reduced by a predefined factor. This mechanism facilitates finer control over the learning process and helps to mitigate overfitting.
The hyperparameter optimization framework Optuna
Akiba et al. (2019)
is employed to identify optimal values for the batch size, learning rate, and scheduler parameters, thereby enhancing training efficiency and predictive performance. The results discussed in the next chapter are derived using the models trained using the best hyperparameters.

To ensure that the correction learned by the neural network is applied consistently across all components of the system state, both the training and validation data are scaled independently. Specifically, each state component is linearly mapped from its minimum and maximum values to the interval
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[0,1]
, ensuring a uniform input and output range across all components.

To preserve this scaling during inference and to integrate it seamlessly into the network architecture, two additional fixed linear layers are appended—one at the input and one at the output of the network. These layers apply the inverse and forward scaling transformations, respectively, allowing the neural network to operate entirely in the normalized space while maintaining compatibility with the original physical scale of the data. The loss function (
11b
) is evaluated on the scaled data, promoting numerical stability and consistent training dynamics across all state dimensions.

4.4
Comparison of numerical schemes

In the following, let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{\textsc{RK}}
and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}
denote the number of evaluations of the vector field
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f
from (
2
), and the number of evaluations of a neural network
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{N}_{\theta}
, respectively, that are required by a numerical integration scheme. For instance, the classical Heun method requires
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{\textsc{RK}}=2
vector field evaluations and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}=0
network evaluations. To assess the efficiency and accuracy of different integration approaches, both the total simulation time and the global error of the resulting trajectories are compared.

To quantify the computational effort to approximate a solution, we introduce the performance indicator

\delta_{t}=\frac{n_{\textsc{RK}}\,\textsc{time}(f)+n_{\mathcal{N}_{\theta}}\,\textsc{time}(\mathcal{N}_{\theta})}{h},

(47)

where
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{time}(\kappa)
denotes the time required by the processor to evaluate a given mapping
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\kappa:x\mapsto\kappa(x)
at an arbitrary input
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x
. The quantity
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta_{t}
thus approximates the total computational effort per unit of simulation time and serves as a proxy for the time efficiency of the numerical method under consideration.

To evaluate the accuracy of each method, let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{ref.}}(kh)
denote a reference solution of (
44
), and let
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}
represent the corresponding approximation obtained by the method under analysis. Denoting by
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{ref.}})_{k,i}
the value of the
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i
-th component of the reference solution at time
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}kh
, the global relative squared error is defined as

\delta_{e}=\frac{1}{nN}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{|(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{ref.}})_{k,i}-(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})_{k,i}|}{|(\boldsymbol{x}^{\text{ref.}})_{k,i}|+\varepsilon}\right)^{2},

(48)

with
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon=10^{-18}
to prevent division by zero,
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N
the number of integration steps, and
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n=16
the dimension of the state vector. The evaluation interval for all methods is fixed to
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[0,20]\,$\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{s}$
.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced a class of neural network-enhanced integrators that leverage data-driven error correction to improve the accuracy of classical numerical methods for solving differential equations. By training neural networks to let them approximate the local truncation error, these enhanced integrators incorporate additive correction terms into standard schemes, thereby reducing local errors and preserving the stability and convergence properties of the underlying integrators.

Our analytical investigation via backward error analysis confirms that the proposed methodology maintains the theoretical guarantees of classical Runge–Kutta schemes while effectively mitigating the error propagation inherent in long-term simulations. Furthermore, the incorporation of embedded Runge–Kutta techniques serves to control the generalization risk, ensuring that the neural network corrections remain robust even when the state trajectory ventures into regions not encountered during training.

Extensive numerical studies, including realistic simulations of wind turbine dynamics based on models parameterized with data from OpenFast, demonstrate that the enhanced integrators not only achieve the desired accuracy but also offer significant improvements in computational efficiency. These results underscore the potential of combining classical numerical analysis with modern machine learning approaches to address complex, high-dimensional dynamical systems.

Future work will focus on further refining the integration of neural network corrections with adaptive error control strategies and extending the framework to a broader class of integrators. Moreover, problems specific cost functions for the training of neural networks shall be derived and the use of the enhanced integrators for load prediction of life-cycle simulations of wind turbines will be considered. The training of the neural network when the entire state is not known but only a function of it deserves future research.
