Closed-form empirical Bernstein confidence sequences for scalars and matrices
Ben Chugg, Aaditya Ramdas

Abstract
We derive a new closed-form variance-adaptive confidence sequence (CS) for estimating the average conditional mean of a sequence of bounded random variables. Empirically, it yields the tightest closed-form CS we have found for tracking time-varying means, across sample sizes up to ≈ 10 6 \approx 10^{6} . When the observations happen to have the same conditional mean, our CS is asymptotically tighter than the recent closed-form CS of 39 . It also has other desirable properties: it is centered at the unweighted sample mean and has limiting width (multiplied by t / log ⁡ t \sqrt{t/\log t} ) independent of the significance level. We extend our results to provide a CS with the same properties for random matrices with bounded eigenvalues.

Introduction

Consider a stream of random variables
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
lying in
[0,1]
with average conditional means
\mu_{t}=t^{-1}\sum_{j\leq t}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}|X_{1},\dots,X_{j-1}]
. We are interested in obtaining nonasymptotic, time-uniform confidence sequences (CSs) for
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 1}
. Given a parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
and an initial peeking time
t_{0}
, a
(1-\alpha)
-CS for
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
is a sequence of sets
(C_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
such that

P(\mu_{t}\in C_{t}\text{ simultaneously for all }t\geq t_{0})\geq 1-\alpha,

(1.1)

or, equivalently,
P(\mu_{\tau}\in C_{\tau})\geq 1-\alpha
for all stopping times
\tau\geq t_{0}
. The study of confidence sequences goes back at least to
24
and
10
, who both gave results for (sub-)Gaussian distributions. CSs are now applied in a wide range of sequential and online settings, and their study has blossomed in recent years.

Confidence sequences for bounded random variables is an important special case. First, many applications naturally yield bounded data. Second, boundedness opens the door to variance-adaptive bounds, which require no distributional knowledge beyond the range itself. This is in contrast to unbounded data, where nonasymptotic CSs require a priori knowledge of an upper bound on some moment- or tail-type quantity of the distribution
[
3
]
. For sub-Gaussian data one needs the sub-Gaussian variance proxy; for data with finite
p
-th moment one needs a bound on this moment, and so on. In many settings such information is unavailable or unrealistic.

For bounded data meanwhile, one can provide CSs which adapt to the unknown variance over time. Such bounds aim to approach the width of the oracle Bernstein bound (or rather its time-uniform version in our case; see
[
14
,
15
]
) which assumes knowledge of the true variance. Such variance-adaptive CSs have come to be known as
empirical
Bernstein CSs.

Empirical Bernstein bounds have enjoyed a tremendous amount of recent attention (e.g.,
[
15
,
39
,
19
,
37
,
35
,
41
,
6
,
23
]
), owing both to their wide applicability and exciting new techniques for constructing them. A longer discussion on related work is held in Appendix
A
, beginning with the original (fixed-time) bounds of
2
and
21
which kick-started this research program.

This article is focused on developing
closed-form
CSs
without assuming a constant conditional mean
. We focus on closed-form bounds because they are straightforward to implement and allow for an asymptotic analysis of their widths. We allow for a non-constant conditional mean because most past work has required a shared mean, yet in many applications the mean drifts over time.

Our main points of comparison will be the empirical Bernstein CS of
39
,
C_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny WSR}}
, which is empirically the tightest closed-form bound in the literature, and
that of
15

C_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny HRMS}}
, which has an optimal
\sqrt{\log\log t/t}
rate of decay.
We give more detail on these two bounds in Section

Let us briefly describe the technical details behind our methodology. Many recent empirical Bernstein bounds are derived based on a bivariate inequality given by
15
, itself an extension of an inequality of
11

Our approach is based on an extension of the latter, which leads to a version the former in which the role of the two variables are “flipped.” This small change opens the door to different techniques for optimizing the parameters of the bound and, in particular, allowing us to more easily leverage the method of mixtures—a technique pioneered by
36

27

Contributions.

To summarize the above discussion, we might enumerate our contributions as follows:

1.

We present a new empirical Bernstein CS
C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
for bounded random variables which tracks a time-varying mean. Empirically,
C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
is the tightest (up to reasonable sample sizes) such CS that we’ve found.

2.

We analyze its limiting width, showing that
C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
is asymptotically tighter than the closed-form CS of
39
. We also analyze the limiting width of several other popular CSs—see Table
1
—providing an analysis which is thus far mostly absent from the literature but that we believe is important for understanding the behavior of these CSs.

3.

We extend our results in two ways by (i) providing a version of
C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
which decays at an iterated logarithm rate (theoretically optimal but often empirically less powerful), and (ii) providing a CS for the maximum eigenvalue of symmetric matrices.

1.2
Notation and conventions

Throughout, we let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
be a sequence of random variables taking values in
[0,1]
. We let
\mu_{t}=t^{-1}\sum_{j\leq t}\mathbb{E}[X_{j}|\mathcal{F}_{j-1}]
be the average conditional mean at time
t
, where
\mathcal{F}_{j}=\sigma(X_{1},\dots,X_{j})
is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the first
j
observations.
Our results place no assumptions on
\mu_{t}
other than measurability. That is, we require that
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
be defined for each
t\geq 1
.
Of course, this encapsulates the case when the observations are iid or have a constant conditional mean.

For a set of distributions
\mathcal{P}
, a
\mathcal{P}
-supermartingale is a stochastic process
(M_{t})
that is adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
(i.e.,
M_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable) and
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}[M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}[M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq M_{t-1}
.
Nonnegative supermartingales lie at the heart of constructing confidence sequences due to Ville’s inequality
[
34
]
, which is a time-uniform version of Markov’s inequality.
Ville’s inequality states that for a nonnegative
\mathcal{P}
-supermartingale
(M_{t})
,
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(\exists t\geq t_{0}:M_{t}\geq 1/\alpha)\leq\alpha\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}}[M_{t_{0}}]
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
t_{0}
. A common strategy for constructing CSs for a parameter
\theta_{t}
is thus to find a nonnegative
\mathcal{P}
-supermartingale
(N_{t})
where (i)
\mathcal{P}
is the set of distributions with this parameter, (ii)
N_{t}
is a function of
\theta_{t}
, and (iii)
N_{0}=1
, and then apply Ville’s inequality. (In other words, we look for suitable
e-processes

[
26
]
). This is also the approach we take here.

In our case,
\mathcal{P}
will be the set of distributions with average conditional mean
\mu_{t}
. We will, therefore, simply refer to nonnegative
\mathcal{P}
-supermartingales as nonnegative supermartingales. Further, if we say that
(C_{t})
is a
(1-\alpha)
-CS for
\mu_{t}
it should be assumed unless stated otherwise that it starts from time
t=1
.

We will occasionally use asymptotic notation in an almost sure sense. In particular, for two functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write
f=O_{\textnormal{a.s.}}(g)
(resp.,
f=o_{\textnormal{a.s.}}(g)
) to mean that
f=O(g)
(resp.,
f=o(g)
)
P
-almost surely, where the measure
P
will be clear from context. We also write
f\sim g
to mean that
\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t)/g(t)=1
. If
f(t)
and
g(t)
are random variables, then the limit should be understood to hold almost surely.

2
A new empirical Bernstein supermartingale

The empirical Bernstein bounds of
15
and
39
are constructed by first considering a deterministic inequality first given by
11
, commonly referred to as
Fan’s inequality
:

\textnormal{Fan's inequality:}\quad\text{For all }\lambda\in[0,1)\text{ and }\xi\geq-1,\;\exp\{\lambda\xi-\psi_{E}(\lambda)\xi^{2}\}\leq 1+\lambda\xi,

(2.1)

where
\psi_{E}(\lambda)=-\log(1-\lambda)-\lambda
. It is tempting to apply Fan’s inequality with
\xi=X_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{t-1}X_{t}
and then take expectations, in which case one obtains that
\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\exp\{\lambda(X_{t}-\mu)-\psi_{E}(\lambda)(X_{t}-\mu)^{2}\}\leq 1
. While the process with increments
\exp\{\lambda(X_{t}-\mu)-\psi_{E}(\lambda)(X_{t}-\mu)^{2}\}
thus defines a nonnegative supermartingale, the resulting CS cannot be solved in closed form for
\mu
.

To solve this problem,
15
proved that one can replace
(X_{t}-\mu)^{2}
with
(X_{t}-\widehat{X}_{t})^{2}
after taking expectations, where
\widehat{X}_{t}\in[0,1]
is predictable (i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable). This gives rise to
Howard’s inequality
:

\text{Howard's inequality:}\quad\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\exp\left\{\lambda(X_{t}-\mu)-\psi_{E}(\lambda)(X_{t}-\widehat{X}_{t})^{2}\right\}\leq 1,

(2.2)

where again
\lambda\in[0,1)
.
The empirical Bernstein bound
15
and the closed-form bound of
39
follow from Howard’s inequality after optimizing for
\lambda
.
15
“stich”
\lambda
over geometrically spaced epochs (see Section
4
where we do something similar), while
39
choose a distinct
\lambda_{t}
at every timestep
t
, which has come to be known as the “method of predictable plug-ins” and can be traced back to
36
.

Our approach is motivated by the following question: Can we reverse the roles of
\lambda
and
\xi
in Fan’s inequality? That is, can we take
\lambda=X_{t}-\widehat{X}_{t}
? The empirical term would thus show up in the argument of
\psi_{E}
and we would be able to optimize over the parameter
\xi
. This is appealing, since the bound looks sub-Gaussian in terms of
\xi
.

Of course, we cannot directly apply Fan’s inequality with
\lambda=X_{t}-\widehat{X}_{t}
, since
\lambda
must be nonnegative. This motivates the following straightforward modification of Fan’s inequality to handle negative values of
\lambda
.

Lemma 2.1

(Modified Fan’s Inequality)
.

For all
\lambda\in(-1,1)
and
\xi\in[-1,1]
,

\exp\left\{\lambda\xi-\psi_{E}(|\lambda|)\xi^{2}\right\}\leq 1+\lambda\xi.

(2.3)

Proof.

If
\lambda\geq 0
, this reduces to (
2.1
). If
\lambda<0
, set
\lambda^{\circ}=-\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\xi^{\circ}=-\xi\geq-1
. Applying (
2.1
) with
\xi^{\circ}
and
\lambda^{\circ}
gives
\exp\{\lambda\xi-\psi_{E}(|\lambda|)\xi^{2}\}=\exp\{\lambda^{\circ}\xi^{\circ}-\psi(\lambda^{\circ})\xi^{2}\}\leq 1+\lambda^{\circ}\xi^{\circ}=1+\lambda\xi
.
∎

Just as Howard’s inequality can be derived from Fan’s inequality, we can derive a modified Howard’s inequality from our modified Fan’s inequality.

Lemma 2.2

(Modified Howard’s inequality)
.

Let
X
be a random variable in
[0,1]
with law
P
and let
\widehat{X}\in[0,1)
be nonrandom. Then, for any
\xi\in[-1,1]
,

\mathbb{E}_{P}\exp\left\{\xi(X-\mathbb{E}_{P}[X])-\xi^{2}\psi_{E}(|X-\widehat{X}|)\right\}\leq 1.

(2.4)

Proof.

Let
Y=X-\mathbb{E}[X]
and
\delta=\widehat{X}-\mathbb{E}[X]

so that
Y-\delta=X-\widehat{X}\in(-1,1)
.
Applying (
2.3
) with
\lambda=Y-\delta
, we obtain
\exp\{(Y-\delta)\xi-\psi_{E}(|X-\widehat{X}|)\xi^{2}\}\leq 1+(Y-\delta)\xi
.
Note that this is a legal choice for
\lambda
since we’ve assumed that
\widehat{X}
is strictly less than 1.
Taking expectations and noting both that
\delta
is predictable and that
\mathbb{E}[Y]=0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\exp\left\{\xi Y-\psi_{E}(|X-\widehat{X}|)\xi^{2}\right\}\leq e^{\delta\xi}(1-\delta\xi)\leq 1,

as claimed.
∎

Lemma
2.2
quickly leads to a new empirical Bernstein-style supermartingale. In particular, if
(\widehat{X}_{t})
is a predictable sequence in
[0,1)
and
(\xi_{t})
is a predictable sequence in
[-1,1]
, the process defined by

B_{t}(\xi_{1}^{t})=\prod_{i\leq t}\exp\left\{\xi_{i}(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{i-1}X_{i})-\xi_{i}^{2}\psi_{E}(|X_{i}-\widehat{X}_{i}|)\right\}.

(2.5)

is a nonnegative supermartingale.
From here, one could follow the predictable plug-in strategy of
39
, choosing
\xi_{t}
based on
X_{1},\dots,X_{t-1}
. Such an approach requires that we take
\mu_{t}
to be constant, however. Further, even under this assumption, we find that this strategy leads to worse results in our case.
Instead, we will obtain our CS by choosing
\xi_{t}=\xi
, and integrating
B_{t}(\xi)
over
\xi
.

3
An empirical Bernstein CS via the method of mixtures

Following an approach which has come to be known as the “method of mixtures”
[
10
,
15
]
, we form another nonnegative supermartingale from
(B_{t}(\xi))
by mixing over the parameters
\xi
.
Fix
\xi=\xi_{t}
and rewrite
B_{t}(\xi)
as
B_{t}(\mu_{t},\xi)=\exp\{\xi(S_{t}-t\mu_{t})-\xi^{2}V_{t}\}

where
S_{t}=\sum_{i\leq t}X_{i}
and
V_{t}=\sum_{i\leq t}\psi_{E}(|X_{i}-\widehat{X}_{i}|)
. For any data-free distribution over
[-1,1]
with density
\rho
, the mixture

M_{t}(\mu_{t}):=\int_{-1}^{1}B_{t}(\mu_{t};\xi)\rho(\xi)\textnormal{d}\xi,

(3.1)

is again a nonnegative supermartingale by Fubini’s theorem. We may thus form a
(1-\alpha)
-CS for
\mu_{t}
by taking our confidence set at time
t
to be
\{m\in[0,1]:M_{t}(m)<1/\alpha\}
, since
M_{t}(\mu_{t})<1/\alpha
with probability
1-\alpha
by Ville’s inequality.

A particularly tight CS comes from taking
\rho
to be the density of a truncated Gaussian distribution over
[-1,1]
with mean 0 and variance
\kappa^{2}
. To state the result, first define

I(y;v):=\int_{-1}^{1}\exp\left\{y\xi-v\xi^{2}\right\}\textnormal{d}\xi.

(3.2)

The function
I(y;v)
is convex with a minimum at
y=0
. One should think of it as behaving roughly as
f(y)=y^{2}+c_{v}
for a constant
c_{v}>0
depending on
v
(and decreasing as
v
grows). Writing the set
\{m:M_{t}(m)<1/\alpha\}
in terms of
I
leads to the following CS.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(X_{t})
be a sequence of random variables in
[0,1]
with average conditional mean
\mu_{t}
. Let
(\widehat{X}_{t})
be any predictable sequence in
[0,1)
. Fix
\kappa>0
and let
Z=\Phi(1/\kappa)-\Phi(-1/\kappa)
. Set
S_{t}=\sum_{i\leq t}X_{i}
and
U_{t}=(2\kappa^{2})^{-1}+\sum_{i\leq t}\psi_{E}(|X_{i}-\widehat{X}_{i}|)
. Then

C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}}:=\bigg\{m:I(S_{t}-tm;U_{t})<\frac{\kappa Z\sqrt{2\pi}}{\alpha}\bigg\},

(3.3)

constitutes a
(1-\alpha)
-CS for
\mu_{t}
. Further, if
\mu_{t}\equiv\mu
is constant over time, the running intersection
(\cap_{j\leq t}C_{j}^{\textnormal{mix}})_{t\geq 1}
is also a
(1-\alpha)
-CS for
\mu
.

The proof is in Appendix
B.1
. As in
C_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny WSR}}
, we recommend taking
\widehat{X}_{t}=t^{-1}(1/2+\sum_{j<t}X_{j})=\widehat{\mu}_{t-1}
for all
t\geq 1
. Notice that since
\widehat{X}_{1}=1/2
,
\widehat{X}_{t}<1
for all
t
, ensuring that the requirements of Proposition
3.1
are met. Further,
\widehat{X}_{t}
depends only on
X_{1},\dots,X_{t-1}
, and is thus predictable. However, it’s worth emphasizing that
\widehat{X}_{t}
might in principle be the result of any algorithm using
X_{1},\dots,X_{t-1}
to predict
X_{t}
.

Remark 3.2
.

One can of course consider distributions other than a truncated Gaussian. For instance, if we take
\rho
to be uniform on
[-1,1]
, then a simple modification of the proof of Proposition
3.1
shows that

C_{t}^{\textnormal{unif}}:=\bigg\{m:I(S_{t}-tm;V_{t})<\frac{2}{\alpha}\bigg\},

(3.4)

is a
(1-\alpha)
-CS for
\mu
. This CS has the benefit of not needing to specify an additional parameter
\kappa
, unlike
(C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}})
. However, we find that in practice
(C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}})
reliably outperforms
(C_{t}^{\textnormal{unif}})
(see the experiments in Appendix
D
), so we work with the former throughout this section. That said, we emphasize that these two CSs differ only in terms of lower-order constants, so the discussion on asymptotics in Section
3.2
holds for both. Additionally, one can show that
\kappa Z=\kappa(\Phi(1/\kappa)-\Phi(1/-\kappa))\to\sqrt{2/\pi}
as
\kappa\to\infty
, to
C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}}
becomes
C_{t}^{\textnormal{unif}}
in the limit.

To better understand how
C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}}
behaves, note that by a change of variables we can write

I(y;v)=\frac{\exp\{y^{2}/(4v)\}}{2\sqrt{v/\pi}}\left(\erf\bigg(\sqrt{v}-\frac{y}{2\sqrt{v}}\bigg)+\erf\bigg(\sqrt{v}+\frac{y}{2\sqrt{v}}\bigg)\right),

(3.5)

where

\erf(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}\exp(-u^{2})\textnormal{d}u.

(3.6)

Since
I(y;v)
is minimized at
y=0
,
m^{*}=S_{t}/t=\overline{X}_{t}\in C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}}
as long as
I(0;U_{t})<\kappa Z\sqrt{2\pi}/\alpha
. And indeed, as a sanity check we can verify that this final inequality holds for all
t
, implying that
C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}}
is never empty. To see this, note that
I(y;U_{t})
is decreasing in
U_{t}
by (
3.5
). Hence,
I(0;U_{t})\leq I(0,1/(2\kappa^{2}))=\kappa\sqrt{2\pi}(\erf(\sqrt{1/(2\kappa^{2})})=\kappa Z\sqrt{2\pi}<\kappa Z\sqrt{2\pi}/\alpha
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Here we’ve used the identity
\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\erf(\sqrt{x}/2)
to conclude that
\erf(1/\sqrt{2\kappa^{2}})=\Phi(1/\kappa)-\Phi(-1/\kappa)
.

The representation in (
3.5
) further implies that
I(y;v)
is symmetric about
y=0
and increasing in
|y|
. It follows that
C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}}=(m^{*}-\widehat{m},m^{*}+\widehat{m})=(\overline{X}_{t}-\widehat{m},\overline{X}_{t}+\widehat{m})
,
for some
\widehat{m}>0
. In fact, since
I(y;v)
is surjective and increasing on
[I(0;v),\infty)
,
\widehat{m}
solves the equation
I(Z_{t}-t\widehat{m};U_{t})=\kappa Z\sqrt{2\pi}/\alpha
, and can thus be found by root finding or binary search. Thus, while the interval
C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}}
cannot be written down explicitly, it may be easily approximated.

Still, in this work we want to derive closed-form CSs, so let us turn to that task next.

3.2
Asymptotic width

One should think of
U_{t}
as growing linearly (see Figure
3(b)
) in which case the width of
C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
(and hence
C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}}
) scales as
O(\sqrt{\log(t)/t})
. To get a more precise sense of how the width of
C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
(and hence
C_{t}^{\textnormal{mix}})
behaves,
we can compare it directly to
\sqrt{\log(t)/t}
as follows. Throughout this section we assume a constant conditional mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[X_{t}]
for all
t
and we assume that the observations are iid.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(X_{t})
be drawn iid from
P
with mean
\mu
. Let
W_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
be the halfwidth of
C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
with
\widehat{X}_{t}=t^{-1}(1/2+\sum_{i<t}X_{i})
. Then

W_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}\sim A_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}:=\sqrt{\frac{2\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)]\log(t)}{t}}.

(3.9)

In particular, letting
R_{t}:=W_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}/A_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
, we have

R_{t}^{2}=\frac{2U_{t}}{t\cdot\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)]}\left(\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\log(t)}+\frac{1}{2}+o_{\textnormal{a.s.}}\left(1\right)\right).

(3.10)

The proof is in Appendix
B.3
. To get a sense of the size of
A_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
, one can show that
\sigma^{2}/2\leq\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)]\leq\log(2)-1/2\approx 0.194
, with Bernoulli(0.5) random variables reaching the upper bound. See Table
2
for several examples. In particular, this shows that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)]\leq 1/2
so
A_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}\leq\sqrt{\log(t)/t}
. See Appendix
C.4
for a discussion of the worst case ratio between
\mathbb{E}\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)
and
\sigma^{2}
and, in particular, a proof that
\mathbb{E}\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)\leq C_{\mu}\sigma^{2}
for a constant
C_{\mu}
depending on
\mu
.

An intriguing fact about Lemma

C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
does not depend on
\alpha

\log(1/\alpha)
is attached to a lower order term in

(it scales with
U_{t}
as opposed to

C.3
.

However, we emphasize that in practice the effect of
\alpha
is not negligible. As shown by (
3.10
), the influence of
\log(1/\alpha)
vanishes with rate
\log(t)
. Thus, even for sample sizes of
10^{6}

\log(1/\alpha)/\log(t)\approx\log(1/\alpha)/13.8

\alpha=0.01

to

\approx 1/9

as compared to

.

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sqrt{\frac{t}{\log t}}W_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}=O_{\textnormal{a.s.}}(1)\text{~ whereas ~}\lim_{t\to\infty}\sqrt{\frac{t}{\log t}}W_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny WSR}}=\infty\quad P\text{-a.s.}

4
Iterated Logarithm Rates

As usual, let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
lie in
[0,1]
and let
(\widehat{X}_{t})\subseteq[0,1)
be predictable.
For a fixed
\xi\in[-1,1]
recall that Section
2
showed that
B_{t}(\mu_{t},\xi)=\prod_{i\leq t}\exp\{\xi(X_{t}-\mu_{t})-\xi^{2}\psi_{E}(|X_{t}-\widehat{X}_{t}|)\}
is a nonnegative supermartingale. Applying Ville’s inequality to both
(B_{t}(\mu,\xi))
and
(B_{t}(\mu;-\xi))
and taking a union bound we obtain that with probability
1-\alpha
, for all
t\geq 1
,

|\overline{X}_{t}-\mu|\leq\frac{|\xi|V_{t}}{t}+\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{t|\xi|},

(4.1)

where, as usual,
V_{t}=\sum_{i\leq t}\psi_{E}(|X_{i}-\widehat{X}_{i}|)
. To obtain a confidence sequence which scales as
O(\sqrt{\log\log(t)/t})
instead of
O(\sqrt{\log(t)/t})
, we apply (
4.1
) in geometrically spaced epochs
[\eta^{j},\eta^{j+1})
,
j=0,1,\dots
, choosing a different parameter
\xi
in each epoch. This technique, known as stitching, was popularized by
15
and has been frequently used in recent works to achieve optimal rates (e.g.,
[
41
,
39
,
7
,
8
,
23
]
).

Stitching is particularly smooth in our case, owing to the sub-Gaussian style behavior of
\xi
in the bound. As we’ve described, other empirical Bernstein bounds have the free parameters
\xi
inside
\psi_{E}
, and it’s challenging to stitch over
\psi_{E}
directly. Indeed, for this reason
15
only provide stitching for sub-gamma distributions. One can always relax
\psi_{E}
to a sub-gamma tail bound by noticing that
\psi_{E,1}(\xi)\leq\frac{\xi^{2}}{2(1-\xi)}
but this will result in a looser CS.

The following result relies on three parameters. The first is the scalar
\eta>1
which, as above, controls the geometry of the epochs. The second is a parameter
L_{0}
which controls the minimum time at which the bound starts. The third is a “stitching function”
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, which controls how much mass is placed on each epoch in the union bound. This function must be increasing, strictly positive, and obey
\sum_{j\geq 0}h^{-1}(j)\leq 1
.
A reasonable choice, and one often used, is
h(j)=\zeta(s)(j+1)^{s}
where
\zeta
is the Riemann-zeta function and
s>1
. This is the choice that was made when presenting
C_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny HRMS}}
in (
1.7
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be increasing and satisfy
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}1/h(j)\leq 1
. Fix
\eta>1
.
Let
(\widehat{X}_{t})
be a predictable sequence in
[0,1)
and set
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
L_{0}\geq 1
.
Then, with probability
1-\alpha
, simultaneously for all
t
such that
V_{t}\geq L_{0}
and
h(\log_{\eta}(V_{t}/L_{0}))\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\exp(V_{t})
,

|\overline{X}_{t}-\mu_{t}|\leq\frac{\sqrt{\eta}+1}{t}\sqrt{V_{t}\left(\log\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)+\log h\left(\log_{\eta}\left(\frac{V_{t}}{L_{0}}\right)\right)\right)}.

(4.2)

The proof is in Appendix

. Let us note that the constraint on

is typically a polynomial in

, hence

is achieved quickly.

To analyze the width of this bound, let us take
L_{0}=1
, and
h(j)=\zeta(s)(j+1)^{s}
. Then
\log(h(\log_{\eta}(V_{t}))=\log(\zeta(s))+s\log(\log_{\eta}(V_{t})+1)=\log(\zeta(s)/\log^{r}(\eta))+\log\log(\eta V_{t}))
so we have that with probability
1-\alpha
, simultaneously for all
t
large enough,
|\overline{X}_{t}-\mu_{t}|<W_{t}^{\textnormal{stch}}
where

W_{t}^{\textnormal{stch}}:=\frac{\sqrt{\eta}+1}{t}\sqrt{V_{t}\left(\log\left(\frac{2\zeta(s)}{\alpha\log^{s}(\eta)}\right)+s\log\log(\eta V_{t})\right)}.

(4.3)

To see explicitly that this bound has iterated logarithm rates, let us compare it against
\sqrt{\log\log(t)/t}
.
Suppose that we are in the fixed mean setting, so
\mu_{t}=\mu
.
First note that
V_{t}\sim tu

P
-almost surely (by calculations in the previous section), where
u=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)]
. Therefore,
\log\log(\eta V_{t})\sim\log\log(\eta tu)=\log(\log(t)(1+\log(\eta u)/\log(t)))-\log\log(2)\sim\log\log(t)
, and

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{t}{\log\log t}}W_{t}^{\textnormal{stch}}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{\eta}+1)\sqrt{\frac{V_{t}\log(2\zeta(s)/\alpha\log^{s}(\eta))+rV_{t}\log\log(\eta V_{t})}{t\log\log(t)}}

\displaystyle\sim(\sqrt{\eta}+1)\sqrt{\frac{stu\log\log(t)}{t\log\log(t)}}=(\sqrt{\eta s}+\sqrt{s})\sqrt{u}.

Letting
\eta
and
s

W_{t}^{\textnormal{stch}}\sim 2\sqrt{u\log\log(t)/t}
. Of course, in practice, small values of
\eta
and
s
inflate the term

which has a noticeable effect on the bound. We suggest using
s=1.4
and
\eta=2
in experiments. These are also the parameters suggested by
15
.

Like

Figure

C_{t}^{\textnormal{stch}}

5
Extension to matrix empirical Bernstein bounds

Here we extend the techniques developed above to give a novel empirical Bernstein CS for the maximum eigenvalue of matrices. Let
\mathcal{S}_{d}
denote the set of
d\times d
symmetric matrices with real entries. We consider a stream
(\mathbf{X}_{t})
of
\mathcal{S}_{d}
-valued random matrices with eigenvalues in
[0,1]
. Let
\mathbf{M}_{t}=t^{-1}\sum_{i\leq t}\mathbb{E}_{i-1}[\mathbf{M}_{i}]
be the average conditional mean at time
t
and
\mathbf{X}_{t}=t^{-1}\sum_{i\leq t}\mathbf{X}_{i}
be the running average of the matrices at time
t
. We denote the maximum and eigenvalues of a matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
by
\gamma_{\max}(\mathbf{X})
and
\gamma_{\min}(\mathbf{X})
respectively.

Following
32
,
33
, we are interested in providing bounds on
\gamma_{\max}(\mathbf{\overline{X}}_{t}-\mathbf{M}_{t})
. Unfortunately, naively following the strategy developed in Section
2
runs into trouble almost immediately. In particular, while a matrix version of the modified Fan’s inequality in Lemma
2.1
exists, a modified Howard’s inequality does not (nor does a matrix version of the original Howard’s inequality). Inspecting the proof of Lemma
2.2
reveals that it uses the commutativity of real numbers; a property that cannot, of course, be lifted to the matrix setting.

Instead, we rely on a result first proved by
14
which gives conditions under which three sequences of matrices forms a supermartingale. We leverage this result and some elementary properties of eigenvalues to prove the following lemma. The proof is in Section
B.6
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
(\mathbf{X}_{t})
be a stream of random matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{d}
with eigenvalues in
[0,1]
. Let
(\mathbf{\widehat{X}}_{t})
be a predictable sequence of matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{d}
with eigenvalues in
[0,1)
. Let
(\xi_{t})
be a predictable sequence in
[-1,1]
. Then
(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a nonnegative supermartingale where

L_{t}:=\textnormal{tr}\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}\xi_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{i-1}[\mathbf{X}_{i}])-\sum_{i=1}^{t}\xi_{i}^{2}\psi_{E}(|\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{\widehat{X}}_{i}|)\right\},

(5.1)

and
L_{0}=d
.
Moreover, for any density
\rho
over
[-1,1]
,
(N_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is upper bounded by a supermartingale with initial value
d
, where

N_{t}:=\int\exp\left\{\xi\gamma_{\max}(\mathbf{S}_{t}-t\mathbf{M}_{t})-\xi^{2}\gamma_{\max}(\mathbf{V}_{t})\right\}\rho(\xi)\textnormal{d}\xi,

(5.2)

where

\mathbf{S}_{t}:=\sum_{i\leq t}\mathbf{X}_{i},\quad\mathbf{M}_{t}:=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i\leq t}\mathbb{E}_{i-1}[\mathbf{X}_{i}],\quad\mathbf{V}_{t}=\sum_{i\leq t}\psi_{E}(|\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{\widehat{X}}_{i}|).

(5.3)

As in Section
3
one can take
\rho
to be a truncated Gaussian and write down the equivalent of Proposition
3.1
(or of Remark
3.2
). Such a bound is useless in the matrix case however, as an implicit bound requires searching over the space of symmetric matrices. Instead, let us turn immediately to the closed-form approximation. The proof of Theorem
5.2
is in Appendix
B.5
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
(\mathbf{X}_{t})
be a sequence of random matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{d}
with eigenvalues in
[0,1]
with average conditional mean
\mathbf{M}_{t}
. Let
(\mathbf{\widehat{X}}_{t})
be any predictable sequence of matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{d}
with eigenvalues in
[0,1)
. Fix
\kappa>0
and take
Z=\Phi(1/\kappa)-\Phi(-1/\kappa)
. Set
U_{t}=(2\kappa^{2})^{-1}+\gamma_{\max}(\mathbf{V}_{t})
.
and
G_{\alpha}=\kappa Z\sqrt{2\pi}/\alpha
.
Let
t_{0}
be the minimum time
t
at which

\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{U_{t}}}\left(\exp\bigg(\frac{bU_{t}}{4}\bigg)-\frac{1}{2}\right)\geq G_{\alpha}.

(5.4)

Then, with probability
1-\alpha
, simultaneously for all
t\geq t_{0}
,

\left|\gamma_{\max}(\mathbf{\overline{X}}_{t}-\mathbf{M}_{t})\right|\leq W_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny mat}},\quad W_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny mat}}:=\frac{2}{t}\sqrt{U_{t}\left(\ell_{\alpha,d}+\frac{1}{2}\log(2U_{t})\right)},

(5.5)

where

\ell_{\alpha,d}:=\log\left(\frac{d\kappa Z/\alpha}{1-\exp(-U_{t}/4)}\right).

(5.6)

Like we did in the scalar case, we can analyze the asymptotic width of this bound.
Let us suppose that the matrices are iid with mean
\mathbf{M}
, so that
\mathbf{M}_{t}=\mathbf{M}
for all
t
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
(\mathbf{X}_{t})
be a sequence of random matrices in
\mathcal{S}_{d}
drawn iid from
P
with eigenvalues in
[0,1]
and mean
\mathbf{M}
. Let
\mathbf{\widehat{X}}_{t}=t^{-1}(\mathbf{I}_{d}/2+\sum_{j<t}\mathbf{X}_{j})
. Then

W_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny mat}}\sim A_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny mat}}:=\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma_{\max}(\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi_{E}(|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{M}|)])\log(t)}{t}}.

(5.7)

The proof of Proposition
5.3
follows precisely the same logic as Lemma
3.4
after showing that
\gamma_{\max}(\mathbf{V}_{t})/t\to\gamma_{\max}(\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi_{E}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{M})])
. It is presented in Appendix
B.7
.

Let us compare Theorem
5.2
to the recent empirical Bernstein CS of
37
, which states
that if
(\lambda_{t})
be a predictable sequence in
(0,1)
. Then, with probability
1-\alpha
, for all
t\geq 1
,

\left|\gamma_{\max}\left(\frac{\sum_{i\leq t}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{M}_{i})}{\sum_{i\leq t}\lambda_{i}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{\log(2d/\alpha)+\gamma_{\max}(\sum_{i\leq t}\psi_{E}(\lambda_{i})(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\mathbf{\widehat{X}}_{i})^{2})}{\sum_{i\leq t}\lambda_{i}}.

(5.8)

We note that Wang and Ramdas state their result as a one-sided bound; we apply a union bound to make it two-sided and more easily comparable to Theorem
5.2
. The bound in (
5.8
) should be considered as the matrix equivalent of
C_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny WSR}}
, insofar as it relies on similar techniques and uses the method of predictable plug-ins. A similar discussion can thus be had on the pros and cons of Theorem
5.2
versus the bound in (
5.8
) as was had in the scalar setting.

If, similarly to the scalar case, one chooses
\xi_{t}\asymp 1/\sqrt{t\log(t)}
and
\mathbf{\widehat{X}}_{t}=\frac{1}{t}(\mathbf{I}_{d}/2+\sum_{j<t}\mathbf{X}_{j})
then the halfwidth of (
5.8
) behaves as
(\log(d/\alpha)+\log\log(t))\cdot\sqrt{\log(t)/t}
(via very similar arguments as in Section
3.2
). Meanwhile, if one chooses
\xi_{t}\asymp\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}
as in
37
and
\mathbf{\widehat{X}}_{t}=\sum_{i<t}\psi_{E}(\xi_{i})\widehat{X}_{i}/\psi_{E}(\xi_{i})
, then the width behaves as
(\log(d/\alpha)+\log(t))/\sqrt{t}
. In both cases
W_{t}^{\textnormal{\tiny mat}}
remains asymptotically tighter.

6
Summary

We have a presented a new closed-form CS for bounded random variables,
C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
, which is easy to implement and tracks a time-varying mean. The CS requires no knowledge of the distribution other than boundedness, adapting to the samples via the function
\psi_{E}(\lambda)=-\log(1-\lambda)-\lambda
. Among such empirical closed-form CSs, we find that
C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
is the tightest in practice, making it a good candidate for ‘out-of-the-box’ applications.

We extended these results to (i) provide a CS whose width decays at the optimal rate of
O(\sqrt{\log\log(t)/t})
and (ii) to provide a CS with similar properties for matrices with bounded eigenvalues. Our approach is based on modifications of Fan’s and Howard’s inequalities
[
11
,
15
]
which led to a new empirical Bernstein-style supermartingale and may be of independent interest.

When multiplied by
\sqrt{t/\log(t)}
,
C_{t}^{\textnormal{apx}}
has limiting width
\sqrt{2\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)}
(or the maximum eigenvalue of that quantity in the matrix case). Since
\sqrt{2\mathbb{E}[\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)]}\geq\sigma
, an intriguing open question is to design a CS with limiting with
\sigma
, which would match that of Robbins’ mixture CS for sub-Gaussian observations, and the oracle Bernstein of
15
(see Table
1
).
We expect that such a CS exists.

Appendix A
Related Work and Applications

Some of the first empirical Bernstein bounds are due to
21
and
2
in 2009, who both work in the fixed-time setting. These techniques were extended to empirical Bernstein inequalities for u-statistics by
25
.
In 2016,
4

provided the first time-uniform results, giving an empirical Bernstein CS that scaled at an iterated logarithm rate.

The bounds of
21
,
2
and
4

were improved both theoretically and empirically by the results of
15
of
39
, who study both fixed-time bounds and confidence sequences. In addition to the closed-form bounds in these works which we’ve compared to here, both of these papers also provide implicit bounds which are usually tighter than the closed-form counterparts, especially at smaller sample sizes. Like us, all the bounds of
15
allow for a time-varying conditional mean, whereas
39
require a constant conditional mean.

Even though we do not study the fixed-time setting in this paper, it’s worth mentioning that the fixed-time, closed-form confidence interval of
39
is “sharp,” in the sense that its width scales as the oracle Bernstein bound in the limit. That is, if
W_{n}^{\textnormal{\tiny WSR}}
is the width of the CI for a fixed
n
, then
\sqrt{n}W_{n}^{\textnormal{\tiny WSR}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\log(2/\alpha)}
.

The techniques developed both by
15
and
39
have spurred a significant amount of recent activity on empirical Bernstein bounds, both in the time-uniform and fixed-time settings. Empirical Bernstein bounds have now been developed for vectors
[
19
,
8
]
, matrices
[
37
]
, self-normalized processes
[
41
,
6
,
18
]

and on other statistics of the data such as the variance
[
20
]
. The scalar case has also been studied further, with
23
developing (implicit) CSs via links to online learning (in particular, the regret of universal portfolio), and
35
recently developing tighter fixed-time intervals using insights that go back to Thomas Cover
[
9
]
.

Related to these implicit CSs, there is a parallel bandit literature on divergence-based confidence bounds (e.g., KL-inf), which can be seen as variance-adaptive via local quadratic approximations; see
[
1
,
13
,
28
]
.

By their very nature, empirical Bernstein bounds are very applicable. This is part of their appeal. Still, it’s perhaps worth noting several places in the literature where they have been employed, either as theoretical tools or as practical bounds. For instance, they have been used to construct adaptive sampling algorithms
[
22
,
5
]
, as tools in learning theory
[
7
,
17
,
31
]
, for boosting algorithms
[
29
]
, to provide generalization bounds in LLMs
[
12
]
, and for inference in contextual bandits
[

]
.

Proof.

(s_{t})
which converges to 0 almost surely such that
\sigma/\widehat{\sigma}_{t-1}=1+s_{t}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq t}\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leq t}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\alpha)}{\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}i\log(i+1)}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}=\sum_{i\leq t}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\alpha)}{\sigma^{2}i\log(i+1)}}(1+s_{i})\sim\sum_{i\leq t}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\alpha)}{\sigma^{2}i\log(i+1)}}.

The proof is then completed by appealing to Lemmas
C.2
and
C.4
.
∎

Finally, we analyze the asymptotics of the empirical variance-like term.

Lemma C.6
.

\sum_{i\leq t}(X_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{i-1})^{2}\psi_{E}(\lambda_{i})\sim\log(2/\alpha)\log\log t,

(C.6)

Proof.

39

\lambda^{2}/(2\psi_{E}(\lambda))\to 1

(note their definition of

is scaled by a factor of 4). Therefore,

\frac{\lambda_{t}^{2}/2}{\psi_{E}(\lambda_{t})}=1+s_{t},

where
s_{t}\to 0

\widehat{\sigma}_{t-1}^{2}/\sigma^{2}=1+r_{t}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq t}(X_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{i-1})^{2}\psi_{E}(\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\leq t}\lambda_{i}^{2}(X_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{i-1})^{2}(1+s_{i})

(C.7)

\displaystyle=\log(2/\alpha)\sum_{i\leq t}\frac{(X_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{i-1})^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{i-1}^{2}i\log(i+1)}(1+s_{i})

(C.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i\leq t}\frac{(X_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{i-1})^{2}}{i\log(i+1)}(1+s_{i})(1+r_{i}).

Let
A_{t}=\sum_{i\leq t}(X_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{i-1})^{2}/(i\log(i+1))
and note that

Letting
w_{i}=(i\log(i+1))^{-1}

by Lemma

. For (ii), note that
\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\mu}_{t-1}-\mu)^{2}]\leq C/t
for some constant

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{i\leq t}\frac{(\mu-\widehat{\mu}_{i-1})^{2}}{i\log(i+1)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leq t}\frac{\mathbb{E}(\mu-\widehat{\mu}_{i-1})^{2}}{i\log(i+1)}\leq\sum_{i\leq t}\frac{C}{i^{2}\log(i+1)}<\infty.

This shows that the sum in (ii) is
O(1)
almost surely. Indeed, if it was

with some positive probability, then the expectation would be infinite.
Finally, (iii) can be handled by a martingale convergence theorem. Note that

where

\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}[M_{t-1}]=\dots=0,

once again implying that

almost surely. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i\leq t}\frac{(X_{i}-\widehat{\mu}_{i-1})^{2}}{i\log(i+1)}(1+s_{i})(1+r_{i})\sim\frac{\log(2/\alpha)}{\sigma^{2}}A_{t}\sim\log(2/\alpha)\log\log(t),

From here, using Lemmas

Meanwhile, we might be curious about the case when the asymptotic width does not depend on

C.2
On the asymptotic width of the predictable plug-in Hoeffding CS

For the curious reader, let us also analyze the asymptotic width of the natural CS for sub-Gaussian random variables, following the predictable plug-in approach. Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
share a constant conditional mean
\mu

\sigma

\mathbb{E}_{t-1}[\exp(\lambda(X_{t}-\mu)]\leq\exp(\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2)

(1-\alpha)

Let

Using the analysis in the previous section, it follows that

\sum_{i\leq t}\lambda_{i}\sim 2\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\alpha)t}{\sigma^{2}\log t}},

C.4
On the relationship between
\psi_{E}

\sigma^{2}

\mathbb{E}\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)\leq C_{\mu}\sigma^{2}

Let
r(x)=\sum_{k\geq 2}x^{k-2}/k
.
Note that
\psi_{E}(x)=\sum_{k\geq 2}x^{k}/k=x^{2}r(x)
. Since
r

x\geq 0
and
|X-\mu|\leq\max\{\mu,1-\mu\}=:g(\mu)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg(|X-\mu|)^{2}\sum_{k\geq 2}\frac{g^{k-2}(\mu)}{k}\bigg)=\sigma^{2}r(g(\mu))=\sigma^{2}\frac{\psi_{E}(g(\mu))}{g^{2}(\mu)}.

Next, we show that this upper bound is achievable. For a fixed

X=1
with probability
\epsilon
and

\mathbb{E}X=\mu

and
\mathbb{E}(X-\mu)^{2}=g^{2}(\mu)\epsilon+(\frac{\mu-\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}-\mu)^{2}(1-\epsilon)=g^{2}(\mu)\epsilon+\epsilon^{2}g^{2}(\mu)/(1-\epsilon)
. Also,

\mathbb{E}\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)=\psi_{E}(1-\mu)\epsilon+\psi_{E}(g(\mu)\epsilon/(1-\epsilon))(1-\epsilon)
.
So,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}\psi_{E}(|X-\mu|)}{\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon\psi_{E}(g(\mu))+\psi_{E}(\frac{g(\mu)\epsilon}{1-\epsilon})(1-\epsilon)}{\epsilon g^{2}(\mu)+\epsilon^{2}g^{2}(\mu)/(1-\epsilon)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi_{E}(g(\mu))+\psi_{E}(\frac{g(\mu)\epsilon}{1-\epsilon})\frac{1-\epsilon}{\epsilon}}{g^{2}(\mu)+\epsilon g^{2}(\mu)/(1-\epsilon)}\xrightarrow{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\psi_{E}(g(\mu)}{g^{2}(\mu)}.

C.5
On the width of two oracle Bernstein CSs

Here we comment on two oracle (i.e., non-empirical) Bernstein CSs, both given by
15
. The first uses the method of mixtures and has width scaling as
O(\sqrt{\log(t)/t})
, and the second uses stitching (see Section
4
) and has width
O(\sqrt{\log\log(t)/t})
. Here we examine their asymptotic widths (multiplied by
\sqrt{\log(t)/t}
and
\sqrt{\log\log(t)/t}
respectively), showing that they are both asymptotically sharp, as defined in the introduction. That is, the mixture bound has limiting with
\sigma
and the stitched bound has limiting width
\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}}
.

To present both bounds, first let us recall that a bounded random variable
X\in[0,1]
satisfies
Bernstein’s
condition with parameter
c=1
. That is,
\mathbb{E}[|X|^{k}]\leq\frac{k!}{2}\sigma^{2}
.
In the language of
15
this implies that the process
(\sum_{i\leq t}(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{i-1}[X_{i}]))_{t\geq 1}
is sub-gamma with
c=1
and variance proxy
V_{t}=t\sigma^{2}
. We can thus apply both the gamma-exponential conjugate mixture boundary
15
and the stitched boundary
15
to obtain two CSs for
\mu_{t}
.

Let us first consider the conjugate mixture boundary.

15
implies that the width
W_{t}
of this CS behaves as

W_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{t}\log\left(\frac{t\sigma^{2}}{2\pi\alpha f^{2}(0)}\right)+o\left(\frac{1}{t}\right)},

where
f
is the density of the gamma distribution with respect to the Lebesgue measure (see Equation (66) in Howard et al. for the precise parameters of the gamma distribution used to construct the CS). Similarly to the analysis done in Section
C.3
, we see that
W_{t}
obeys
\lim_{t\to\infty}\sqrt{t/\log(t)}\cdot W_{t}=\sigma
.

Next, the stitched boundary behaves similarly to the CS presented in Section
1.1
, but with the variance proxy
V_{t}=t\sigma^{2}
instead of the empirical variance. As was done in Section
4
, we can take limits and then let
\eta,s\to 1
to see that the width
W_{t}
obeys
\sqrt{t/\log\log(t)}W_{t}\to\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}}
as
t\to\infty
. Note that, since we’re letting
\eta
and
s
approach 1, it’s really a
sequence
of such bounds that has limiting width
\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}}
. The limit is not achieved by any one bound.
This is also demonstrated by
15
.

Here we present some additional experiments to get a handle on the effect of
