Moth‐Eaten Osteolysis of Multiple Foot Bones After a Conservatively Treated “Stable” Ankle Fracture: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Haotian Wu, Xue Yang, Yao Zhou, Zihan Chen, Liqing Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProgressive multifocal “moth‐eaten” osteolysis involving foot and ankle bones after nonoperative treatment of a stable ankle fracture is rare and warrants evaluation for infectious, neoplastic, inflammatory, vascular, and mechanical causes. Vigilant follow‐up, serial imaging, laboratory testing, vascular assessment, and multidisciplinary review can enable early diagnosis and timely, individualized management.Keywords: ankle fracture, bone metabolism, complication, conservative treatment, moth‐eaten osteolysis

Introduction

Ankle fractures account for approximately 10% of all fractures and occur across age groups, with higher rates among older women and younger men, often resulting from sports‐related activities [1, 2, 3]. With an aging population, the burden of ankle fractures remains substantial worldwide [4].

Treatment strategies for ankle fractures are determined primarily by the stability of the fracture. Stable fractures, characterized by minimal or no displacement, can usually be managed conservatively with cast or splint immobilization, and generally have favorable outcomes [5]. By contrast, multifocal osteolytic lesions of the foot are typically associated with infectious or neoplastic processes [6, 7]. To our knowledge, no previous report has described progressive multifocal osteolysis around the ankle after nonoperative treatment of an initially minimally displaced and radiographically stable ankle fracture in an adult without a known systemic disorder associated with osteolysis. This case is notable for its unusual post‐traumatic setting, progressive involvement of multiple contiguous bones, and the diagnostic challenge of distinguishing the condition from infection, malignancy, inflammatory arthropathy, Gorham–Stout disease (GSD), and other destructive bone disorders.

This case highlights the need for close follow‐up, early imaging, and vascular assessment when pain, swelling, or abnormal bone healing persists when symptoms persist despite stable fracture alignment.

Patient and Observation

Patient Characteristics

A 47‐year‐old man sustained a right ankle injury in a motor vehicle accident in May 2023. After the injury, he developed right ankle pain, swelling, and restricted motion without an open wound. Radiographs obtained the following day (Figure 1a) showed a minimally displaced Weber A‐type fracture with cortical discontinuity and soft‐tissue swelling around the lateral malleolus. At the local hospital, he was discharged with general advice but without formal immobilization. Over the subsequent days, his pain and swelling persisted. Approximately 10 days later, he began weight‐bearing, after which his pain increased, particularly during walking. The ankle swelling persisted and was accompanied by pitting edema and increased local skin temperature.

The patient subsequently sought further treatment at multiple hospitals and clinics, receiving conservative therapies such as topical Chinese herbal preparations, acupuncture, and cold compresses. Despite these interventions, his symptoms did not improve. Because of persistent pain and functional limitation, he presented to the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at our hospital in September 2023 for further evaluation and management.

His medical history was notable for over 30 years of cigarette smoking (approximately 30 cigarettes per day) and a 2‐year history of moderate alcohol consumption (approximately 20 mL/day). He also had hyperlipidemia for more than 10 years and hypertension diagnosed 2 years earlier (peak blood pressure: 150/99 mmHg), for which he was receiving long‐term treatment with amlodipine benzenesulfonate tablets.

Clinical Findings

At admission in September 2023, the patient was afebrile and had no chills, night sweats, or unexplained weight loss. Physical examination revealed mild edema and dark discoloration of the right ankle and dorsum of the foot. Skin temperature was normal over the medial ankle, whereas the lateral fracture site was tender. The dorsalis pedis pulse was palpable and symmetric with the contralateral side. Sensory examination was normal. Active range of motion of the right ankle was severely restricted, with dorsiflexion limited to 5°, toe flexion to 5°, and rotation nearly absent, all accompanied by significant pain.

The T‐SPOT.TB assay was weakly positive. Lipid levels were markedly elevated, with a total cholesterol level of 8.32 mmol/L and a triglyceride level of 9.27 mmol/L. The white blood cell count was 9.08 × 109/L, the erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 12 mm/h, and the C‐reactive protein level was 0.64 mg/L. These values were within the reference ranges. Glycated hemoglobin and other routine laboratory parameters were unremarkable.

Outpatient imaging performed in July 2023 revealed preexisting bone changes in the right foot (Figure 1c). Further imaging during hospitalization included three‐dimensional CT reconstruction of the right ankle, which demonstrated multiple osteolytic lesions and periarticular soft‐tissue swelling. MRI revealed degenerative changes, irregular morphology of the lateral malleolus, potential injury to the anterior and posterior talofibular ligaments, soft‐tissue edema around the ankle and dorsum of the foot, and a small amount of joint effusion (Figure 1e,f). No abscess, sinus tract, discrete soft‐tissue mass, or extensive periosteal reaction was identified. Chest CT and abdominal ultrasonography did not reveal pulmonary abnormalities suggestive of active tuberculosis or an obvious primary malignant lesion. The ankle–brachial index was 0.85, suggesting borderline peripheral arterial insufficiency.

Therapeutic Intervention

The patient was diagnosed with multifocal osteolytic destruction in the right foot, likely exacerbated by delayed and inadequate early management between May and August 2023. Upon admission to our hospital, comprehensive laboratory and imaging assessments were performed.

Given the extent of bone destruction, a multidisciplinary treatment regimen was implemented. Treatment included vitamin D and other bone‐protective medications. Rehabilitation consisted of isometric muscle training, joint mobility exercises, magnetic therapy, medium‐frequency electrotherapy, ultrasound therapy, acupuncture, and gradual weight‐bearing.

By September 28, the patient exhibited significant reduction in swelling and improvement in ankle mobility and pain. He was subsequently discharged from the hospital.

Follow Up

Two months after discharge, the patient was readmitted for coronary artery disease and underwent successful coronary artery stenting. At a 3‐month follow‐up visit, he reported intermittent right ankle pain, particularly after prolonged walking or jogging. CT scans obtained at that time demonstrated evidence of an old fracture with loose bone fragments, degenerative joint changes, and radiographic signs of osteoporosis involving the ankle region (Figure 1g).

Discussion

Case Analysis and Differential Diagnosis

The patient developed progressive osteolysis involving the distal tibia, talus, calcaneus, and navicular during follow‐up after the ankle injury. The differential diagnosis includes bacterial osteomyelitis, tuberculous osteomyelitis, malignancy, GSD, Charcot neuroarthropathy, and alcohol‐related osteonecrosis. Bacterial osteomyelitis was considered. However, the patient had no clinical or laboratory evidence of active infection. Serial CT and MRI showed periarticular soft‐tissue edema and a small joint effusion, without abscess, sinus tract, or marked periosteal reaction. These findings made bacterial osteomyelitis less likely.

Tuberculosis was considered because the T‐SPOT assay was weakly positive. However, the patient had no constitutional or pulmonary symptoms, no documented history of tuberculosis exposure, and no pulmonary abnormalities suggestive of active tuberculosis on chest CT. The lesions remained confined to the injured foot and ankle and were not accompanied by marked systemic inflammation. Active tuberculous osteomyelitis was therefore considered unlikely.

Malignancy was also considered because of the moth‐eaten appearance of the lesions. However, no discrete soft‐tissue mass, aggressive periosteal reaction, obvious primary tumor, or metastatic lesion was identified on available imaging. The close temporal relationship to the injury, regional distribution of the lesions, and subsequent clinical improvement made an aggressive neoplastic process less likely.

GSD was considered because it can cause progressive osteolysis involving multiple contiguous bones [8]. However, the imaging pattern was not typical of massive osteolysis. The affected bones showed diffuse or patchy osteopenia, cortical irregularity, and progressive regional bone loss, but their overall osseous contours remained identifiable. There was no clear segmental disappearance of bone or extensive loss of the cortical and medullary architecture characteristic of the “vanishing bone” appearance [9]. No specific vascular or lymphatic malformation was identified on the available imaging. These features made advanced GSD less likely.

Charcot neuroarthropathy was considered unlikely. The patient had no history of diabetes, sensory deficits, peripheral neuropathy, or any other neurological impairment. Furthermore, the presentation of severe joint pain, in the absence of underlying neuropathic disease, provided additional evidence against this condition. Alcohol‐related osteonecrosis was considered less likely because the patient reported only alcohol consumption and had no history of alcohol dependence.

Overall, the findings favored a multifactorial post‐traumatic process involving vascular vulnerability, local edema, ligament injury, abnormal mechanical loading, and altered bone remodeling. These factors may have contributed to the observed regional osteolysis and moth‐eaten radiographic pattern, although their individual contributions could not be determined in this single case. Because no bone biopsy or microbiological culture was performed, the differential diagnoses could not be confirmed or definitively excluded by tissue or microbiological evidence.

Pathophysiological Mechanisms

The imaging pattern may reflect several interacting factors, including reduced vascular reserve, post‐traumatic perfusion impairment, inadequate immobilization, premature weight‐bearing, inflammation, and abnormal mechanical loading (Figure 2c). These factors may have disrupted the balance between bone formation and resorption in the injured ankle and contributed to the progression from regional osteopenia to multifocal osteolytic and cortical changes.

Local Ischaemia and Hypoxia

The ankle injury may have created a locally compromised perfusion environment. Persistent soft‐tissue edema, ligamentous injury, and local inflammation can increase tissue pressure and impair capillary‐level blood flow around the ankle [10, 11]. These changes may be particularly important during the early healing period, when fracture repair and soft‐tissue recovery require greater oxygen and nutrient delivery [12].

In this case, edema and pain persisted after the injury, and the patient developed marked restriction of ankle motion. Although the dorsalis pedis pulse was palpable and equal to that on the contralateral side, pulse does not exclude tissue‐level or microvascular perfusion impairment. Tissue hypoxia increases reactive oxygen species production in osteoblasts, which induces autophagy and apoptosis and further impairs bone formation and repair capacity [13, 14, 15].

Vascular Disease and Atherosclerosis

The patient had several major vascular risk factors, including more than 30 years of heavy cigarette smoking, hypertension, severe hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, and a borderline ankle‐brachial index. These factors may have contributed to endothelial dysfunction and reduced peripheral vascular reserve.

Systemic vascular vulnerability may not necessarily cause bone destruction in the absence of local injury. However, it may reduce the ability of the peripheral circulation to compensate for the increased metabolic demands associated with fracture healing, soft‐tissue repair, and rehabilitation. In this patient, the vascular risk profile may have increased susceptibility to the local perfusion impairment.

Several studies have confirmed a strong association between atherosclerosis and bone loss [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Mo et al. identified shared molecular mediators between these two pathologies—including microRNAs, COL1A1, and IBSP—highlighting the overlapping biological mechanisms of vascular dysfunction and skeletal deterioration [22].

However, these associations do not establish that systemic atherosclerosis caused the osteolytic changes in this patient. In the present case, vascular disease is best interpreted as a predisposing factor that may have amplified the effects of local trauma and abnormal mechanical loading.

Inflammation and Sympathetic Nervous System Activation

Fracture‐induced pain activates the sympathetic nervous system, increasing noradrenaline release, which can affect β‐adrenergic signaling in osteoblasts and osteoclasts, thereby inhibiting osteogenesis [23]. Concurrently, proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) promote bone resorption and further disrupt normal bone remodeling [24].

In this case, persistent pain and sympathetic nervous system activation were present. However, inflammatory cytokines, catecholamines, and other markers of sympathetic activation were not measured. Accordingly, inflammation and sympathetic activation should be considered possible secondary contributors.

Musculoskeletal Interaction and Mechanical Loading

Bone health depends critically on gravitational loading and muscle contraction [25, 26]. Prolonged immobilization reduces mechanical stimulation of the skeleton, leading to rapid bone mass loss [27]. In this patient, the early rehabilitation course was characterized by inadequate immobilization followed by weight‐bearing approximately 10 days later. Premature weight‐bearing may have placed repetitive, uneven stress on the lateral malleolar fracture and injured ligaments before adequate healing. At the same time, restricted motion may have reduced normal muscle contraction and physiological loading during other periods. This combination of premature overload and subsequent activity restriction may have created an unstable mechanical environment for bone healing.

Overall, systemic vascular vulnerability may have interacted with local post‐traumatic factors, including edema, ligamentous injury, inadequate immobilization, and premature loading. This interaction may have impaired local perfusion and mechanical adaptation, contributing to the progression from regional osteopenia to multifocal osteolytic lesions with cortical irregularity.

Bone Metabolism and Complications During Fracture Healing

Bone Metabolism in Fracture Healing

Fracture healing depends on balanced bone turnover controlled by osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Osteoblasts form new bone matrix, whereas osteoclasts perpetually remove old or damaged bone [28]. These processes are jointly regulated by the endocrine and immune systems through signaling pathways such as RANK‐RANKL‐OPG and Wnt, thereby regulating bone remodeling [29, 30]. Disruption of this balance by aging, vascular disease, or other pathological conditions may contribute to bone loss, osteonecrosis, or osteoporosis [31, 32]. In this patient, reduced vascular reserve, persistent edema, inadequate immobilization, and premature loading may have disrupted this balance.

Osteonecrosis

Osteonecrosis, also known as avascular or aseptic necrosis, results from compromised bone perfusion [33]. Early disease may be difficult to detect because imaging findings can be subtle. With progression, radiographic signs such as sclerosis and subchondral collapse become apparent, while MRI reveals the characteristic “T1 low‐signal band” and “T2 double‐line sign” at the interface between necrotic and viable bone [34].

Because osteonecrosis has diverse etiologies and clinical manifestations, we conducted a focused literature review of osteonecrosis involving the foot and ankle. The search was performed using the China Biology Medicine Database (CBM), Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Wanfang Data and covered publications from 2013 through 2023. Search terms were organized into four conceptual groups: (1) osteonecrosis, avascular necrosis, aseptic necrosis, or ischemic necrosis; (2) foot‐ and ankle‐related anatomical terms, including foot, ankle, talus, navicular, calcaneus, and metatarsal; (3) trauma‐ and treatment‐related terms, including ankle fracture, foot injury, trauma, conservative treatment, immobilization, weight‐bearing, and mechanical loading; and (4) potential etiological or predisposing factors, including vascular insufficiency, atherosclerosis, smoking, alcohol use, corticosteroid exposure, diabetes, systemic lupus erythematosus, inflammatory disease, and idiopathic disease. The terms were combined using the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR,” with database‐specific syntax adapted as necessary.

Reports were eligible if they involved human patients and primarily addressed osteonecrosis or avascular necrosis of the foot or ankle. Studies were considered particularly relevant if they evaluated traumatic or postfracture osteonecrosis, conservative treatment, mechanical loading, vascular factors, systemic disease, or other recognized etiologies of osteonecrosis. Reports unrelated to the foot or ankle, duplicate publications, nonhuman or purely experimental studies, and publications lacking relevant clinical, imaging, laboratory, or etiological information were excluded.

The search identified 92 reports. Because the included publications represented heterogeneous anatomical sites, etiologies, disease stages, and clinical presentations, they were used to summarize the reported causes and clinical characteristics of foot and ankle osteonecrosis.

The literature screening process is shown in Figure 2a. Traumatic and iatrogenic etiologies were frequently reported, whereas corticosteroid‐induced osteonecrosis was a well‐recognized nontraumatic cause involving mechanisms such as vascular damage, adipocyte dysfunction, elevated intraosseous pressure, and bone marrow ischemia [35]. Idiopathic cases often present diagnostic challenges due to the absence of clear precipitating factors. For instance, navicular ischaemic necrosis (known as Köhler's disease in childhood) often follows a self‐limited course with a favorable prognosis, whereas similar lesions in adulthood (termed Müller‐Weiss disease) have a relatively poorer prognosis [36]. Moreover, other site‐specific osteonecrotic disorders include Freiberg's disease affecting the second and third metatarsal heads of the foot and Preiser's disease of the wrist, both present unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

Osteonecrosis of the foot and ankle may coexist with lesions at other skeletal sites (Figure 2b), particularly in nontraumatic cases. This observation underscores the importance of comprehensive skeletal screening for patients diagnosed with osteonecrosis of the foot and ankle and vigilance for secondary lesions in those already diagnosed with osteonecrosis at other sites.

In this case, CT imaging in December 2023 showed residual fracture fragments and high‐density shadows adjacent to the talus, suggesting post‐traumatic structural remodeling and possible osteonecrotic change. Based on the patient's vascular risk profile, mechanical instability, and clinical course, we postulate that chronic microvascular dysfunction combined with systemic atherosclerosis contributed to avascular necrosis in the initially minimally displaced fracture.

Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is a systemic metabolic bone disorder characterized by decreased bone mass and deterioration of trabecular microarchitecture, leading to reduced bone strength and increased fracture risk [37, 38, 39]. It is commonly assessed by bone mineral density measurement using dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry, while quantitative CT can provide volumetric bone density estimates [40]. In addition to age and hormonal factors, immobilization, chronic inflammation, endocrine dysfunction, and vascular disease can also precipitate secondary osteoporosis [41, 42, 43].

In this case, inadequate early immobilization, premature weight‐bearing, persistent local swelling, and a high vascular‐risk profile may have acted together to impair regional bone remodeling. These factors may explain the osteopenic appearance and sparse trabecular pattern observed on imaging.

Clinical Implications

This case highlights that an apparently stable ankle fracture may still require close follow‐up, particularly in patients with substantial vascular risk factors or persistent pain and swelling. Adequate early immobilization and avoidance of premature weight‐bearing are essential, while failure of symptoms to improve should prompt reassessment of vascular status and repeat imaging. The combination of persistent edema, severe pain, restricted motion, and progressive regional osteopenia or osteolytic change should raise concern for infection, malignancy, inflammatory disease, GSD, or post‐traumatic ischemic and disuse‐related bone loss. Vascular studies should be interpreted alongside serial imaging and clinical progression, and rehabilitation should be individualized, with gradual loading initiated only after fracture stability and soft‐tissue recovery have been confirmed.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. First, no bone biopsy, histopathological examination, or microbiological culture was performed. Therefore, the diagnosis remains a clinicoradiographic working diagnosis, and infectious, neoplastic, and inflammatory causes could not be definitively excluded. Second, the postfracture bone changes in this patient likely resulted from the combined influence of multiple interacting factors, including vascular disease, inflammation, and mechanical stress. However, this case report did not quantitatively assess the relative contribution of each factor or their complex interrelationships. Third, because the patient initially received treatment at an external hospital, early imaging and laboratory data were incomplete. As a result, portions of the early disease course could not be fully reconstructed. Only the most representative available radiographs were included. Finally, the follow‐up duration was relatively short. Although the patient was evaluated clinically and radiographically at the 3‐month follow‐up, he was subsequently lost to follow‐up and could no longer be contacted. Consequently, long‐term outcomes, including pain progression, gait, ankle range of motion, and further radiographic changes, could not be assessed.

Conclusion

This case illustrates an unusual pattern of progressive multifocal osteolysis developing during follow‐up after nonoperative treatment of an initially minimally displaced and radiographically stable ankle fracture. Although the precise pathophysiological mechanism remains uncertain, the findings may reflect interactions among systemic vascular vulnerability, post‐traumatic edema, inadequate early immobilization, premature weight‐bearing, and abnormal mechanical loading. These factors may have contributed to impaired local perfusion and altered bone remodeling.

This case underscores the importance of maintaining clinical vigilance during every stage of management, regardless of the apparent severity of the injury. Systematic evaluation, appropriate immobilization, and individualized rehabilitation strategies are essential to prevent irreversible bone destruction. For patients with multiple systemic comorbidities or poor vascular health, treatment should prioritize multidisciplinary management, including early vascular assessment, metabolic optimization, and bone‐protective therapy. Ultimately, the present report enriches current understanding of rare complications following ankle fractures and provides valuable clinical insights for improving diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of postfracture osteolytic conditions.
