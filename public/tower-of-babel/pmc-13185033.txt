Empowering women, strengthening support: a quality improvement project to enhance pelvic floor health awareness
Authors: Rachel Phoy Cheng Chun, Wan Lin Goh, Khaing Mon Nanda Kyaw Isabella, Jason Shau Khng Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) is a prevalent yet under-recognised condition among obstetric women, often presenting as urinary incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse. Despite evidence supporting pelvic floor muscle training as an effective first-line management strategy, awareness and service utilisation remain suboptimal. This quality improvement project, conducted at Singapore General Hospital, aimed to enhance PFD awareness among obstetric patients and nursing staff and to increase physiotherapy uptake from a baseline of 0% to 50% within 6 months.The comprehensive root cause analysis identified three critical barriers to physiotherapy utilisation within our department—inadequate screening procedures, insufficient staff and patient education and poorly defined referral processes. A pre–post intervention design was implemented across outpatient, inpatient and postnatal care settings. Interventions included structured patient education through pamphlets, digital media and discharge materials, systematic PFD screening and strengthened referral pathways. Nursing staff received targeted training and physician engagement was integrated in later cycles to improve patient receptivity. Outcomes were assessed through referral rates, completed physiotherapy appointments and pre/post education surveys with nursing staff.Baseline data revealed inconsistent referrals and low health literacy as key barriers. During the first cycle, many antenatal patients deferred physiotherapy until postpartum, highlighting the need for stronger physician involvement. Adjustments in the second cycle, particularly routine counselling by obstetrics doctors, improved patient acceptance and referral compliance. As a result, physiotherapy uptake increased significantly, reaching the target of 50% within 2 months. Nursing staff also demonstrated improved confidence and knowledge in PFD education.This initiative successfully embedded pelvic floor health into standard maternal care, underscoring the importance of education, multidisciplinary collaboration and physician advocacy in overcoming stigma and systemic gaps. It also establishes a sustainable framework for integrating pelvic floor health into maternity care and improving long-term outcomes for women.Keywords: Audit and feedback, Education, Healthcare quality improvement, Maternal Health Services, PDSA

Problem

Limited awareness of pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) among obstetric women seeking the services of Singapore General Hospital (SGH) revealed a critical gap in maternity care delivery. Many antenatal and postnatal patients experience urinary incontinence (UI) and pelvic organ prolapse (POP) during their pregnancy journey,1 yet they were unaware that these symptoms were due to PFD and did not actively seek medical attention. For a small group of patients who were identified early to have PFD, we noticed an inconsistent physiotherapist referral pattern and underutilisation of existing female pelvic health resources. Although pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) is a well-established first-line conservative treatment2 for PFD, UI and POP, its clinical implementation remains suboptimal. Our objective is to increase PFD awareness among obstetric patients and nursing staff. The goal of this objective will be measured by the increase in uptake rate of pelvic floor physiotherapy service by obstetric mothers in our centre from a median of 0% to 50% within 6 months.

The intervention was implemented in SGH, an insitution equipped with an Obstetrics and Gynaecology centre (OGC) for antenatal care, labour ward and postnatal ward for peripartum care. On average, we deliver approximately 2000 patients per year. As a tertiary hospital, we actively receive high-risk patients’ transfers from other institutions. In our OGC, we have specific services to cater to our wide range of patients, for example pelvic floor physiotherapy and uro-gynaecology services.

The quality improvement (QI) initiative aims to refine the work process for PFD screening in antenatal patients and appropriate referral pathway for optimal resource utilisation. In addition, this project aims to improve nursing staff’s education on PFD.

The primary outcome measure focuses on uptake rates of pelvic floor physiotherapy service, quantified through referral numbers for eligible mothers and those who completed physiotherapy assessments. The target increase to 50% uptake was deemed achievable on the current baseline of 0%, which can be improved given the available physiotherapy capacity. This uptake enhancement directly aligns with the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence3 and reinforced SGH’s commitment to improving maternal health. The secondary outcome measure focuses on nursing staff’s pre-education and post-education survey. We hypothesise that the majority of nurses will have positive feedback in their post-education interview.

The intervention was implemented over the projected study period, featuring weekly progress monitoring and continuous review of implementation strategies. A final evaluation was conducted following the implementation period to measure and assess the intervention’s outcome and effectiveness.

Background

Pelvic floor dysfunction remains a significant health concern for women globally, with prevalence estimates ranging from 1.9% to as high as 46.5%4 depending on population and diagnostic criteria. The syndrome represents a constellation of clinical disorders arising from impaired pelvic floor muscles and connective tissue support, manifesting as UI, faecal incontinence, POP and pelvic-perineal pain syndromes.5 Obstetrics risk factors include multiparity, instrumental births, fetal macrosomia, advanced maternal age, obesity, lifestyle factors and coexisting medical conditions, such as gastrointestinal disease.4 Notably, poor sleep quality and reduced physical performance have been linked to increased incidence and persistence of PFD symptoms,4 both factors which are very common due to the physical and hormonal changes during pregnancy.

Pelvic floor dysfunction substantially impairs quality of life, social participation, mental health, sexual well-being and economic productivity, yet remains underdiagnosed and undertreated due to stigma, low awareness and limited discussion in routine care. Considering the profound negative impact of PFD, incorporating pelvic floor health education into standard maternity care is imperative.

Conservative management is effective, with PFMT established as a cornerstone intervention for both treatment and prevention when initiated during pregnancy and the postpartum period.6 Evidence suggests higher success when PFMT is supported by adjunctive modalities, such as biofeedback,7 and when programmes are delivered with adequate instruction and follow-up.

However, despite its demonstrated benefits, PFMT implementation is undermined by knowledge gaps and suboptimal health literacy among women and healthcare professionals, leading to misconceptions that exercises are ineffective and to poor adherence with self-directed regimens.8 System-level discontinuities in care, such as lack of structured follow-up and fragmented responsibility for pelvic floor counselling, further compromise sustained practice. Targeted interventions that address both patient education and organisational processes are needed to improve awareness, support behaviour change and embed PFMT as a routine component of maternity care.

Measurement

Our target population comprised antenatal patients as early awareness and identification of PFD are believed to help reduce its incidence. Referral numbers and completed physiotherapy appointments served as the primary measures for intervention impact. This measurement framework was established as a systematic mixed-methods approach to identify key barriers to pelvic floor physiotherapy uptake.

The study population included all antenatal patients attending routine outpatient appointments at the OGC during the study period. There were no specific exclusion criteria. Patients who were keen to be educated on pelvic floor health or who were experiencing urinary symptoms were eligible for referral to physiotherapy services.

Baseline data collection took place between 28 January 2025 and 14 February 2025, forming the foundation for intervention assessment. During this period, 172 patients were screened, 79 patients were eligible, 10 of them (13%) were referred to the physiotherapist and 6 attended the session. Intervention group data collection took place between 18 February 2025 and 4 April 2025. During this period, 372 patients were screened. Within Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) Cycle 1 (18 February 2025 to 12 March 2025), 79 patients were eligible, 11 of them (14%) were referred for physiotherapy and 4 attended the session. In PDSA Cycle 2 (17 March 2025 to 4 April 2025), 83 patients were eligible, 20 of them (25%) were given physiotherapy appointments and 6 attended the session. The rest of the eligible patients declined referral to a physiotherapist. The option for physiotherapy referral remained available at subsequent antenatal visits if the patient was keen to be referred.

Staff surveys—including doctors, physiotherapists and nurses—were systematically conducted to identify root causes contributing to suboptimal referral rates and pelvic floor physiotherapy service uptake. These causes are illustrated in a cause-and-effect diagram, which categorises contributing factors according to their impact on process efficiency or resource availability (figure 1). Once the root causes were identified, votes were collected from eight staff members (one doctor, five nurses and two physiotherapists) to determine the top four root causes they perceived as significant. Each staff member was allowed to cast four votes.

For the secondary outcome measure, nursing staff from both outpatient clinic and inpatient ward were interviewed before the implementation began. They were asked about their knowledge of PFD and their confidence in counselling patients on this topic. Pelvic floor health education was then conducted, and posteducation interviews were carried out to obtain feedback from the nurses.

Root causes

Four root causes were identified as fundamental to achieving the target objective. These are discussed according to their significance, illustrating how each informed the intervention measures.

The first root cause involves inadequate integration of pelvic health discussions within maternal care consultations, leading to the consistent omission of pelvic floor health topics during both antenatal and postnatal visits. This practice contradicts evidence-based recommendations from a comparable intervention targeting pelvic floor health outcomes among pregnant Malaysian women, which established that PFMT education should commence during the antenatal period.10 Supporting research demonstrates that regular antenatal exercise incorporating PFMT significantly reduces UI occurrence within 3 months post partum.11 This awareness gap directly impacts both referral rates and uptake of PFMT service uptake, forming a major barrier to implementation.

The second root cause centres on the maternal predominant focus on fetal well-being at the expense of maternal pelvic health. This emphasis results in the absence of routine antenatal and postnatal reviews in outpatient clinic and inpatient ward, respectively. Consequently, doctors do not refer pregnant patients to appropriate healthcare providers and departments capable of offering PFMT services. Research indicated that non-mandated interventions often receive lower priority among competing clinical tasks,8 which explains this systematic oversight. This root cause also impacts referral rates and PFMT service uptake.

The third cause involves the delayed initiation of physiotherapy service, with recruitment commencing only after 20 weeks of gestation. This timing misses substantial antenatal opportunities to enhance patient awareness and understanding of PFD and PFMT, as recommended by Malaysian study.10 This delayed approach undermines patient confidence in the necessity and efficacy of PFMT, primarily affecting service uptake due to reduced appointment completion rates.

The fourth root cause is the absence of pelvic floor health education in both nurse training and ward induction, which creates significant knowledge gaps. These gaps particularly hinder the screening and risk assessment of patients, subsequently influencing referral likelihood and overall intervention effectiveness.

Data collection

Data collection involved pelvic health screening questionnaire (online supplemental appendix 1) distribution to antenatal patients for PFD symptoms identification. The questionnaire assessed UI experiences and patients’ willingness to accept referral to pelvic floor physiotherapists. Subsequently, referral patterns and physiotherapy appointment attendance were systematically tracked. Schedule modifications, cancellations and no-show appointments were monitored through SGH’s online appointment system.

Operational constraints prevented screening at regular intervals. However, all available data from each survey period were included in the analysis without exclusion. Despite varying intervals between screening days, the measurement process had remained standardised and uniform throughout the implementation period. Referral numbers encompassed two patient categories: women exhibiting PFD symptoms who received clinical referrals and women who had shown interest in accessing physiotherapy services. Meanwhile, the number of completed physiotherapy appointments is operationally defined as the number of appointments attended. Data was also collected for patients who subsequently withdrew from physiotherapy services, resulting in cancelled follow-up and patients who failed to attend without prior notification or documented withdrawal reasons, labelled as ‘no-show’.

This measurement framework ensures accurate capture of genuine service engagement while distinguishing between patient-initiated withdrawals and unexplained non-attendance, providing reliable data for intervention assessment and future service planning.

Meanwhile, pre-PFD and post-PFD education survey on nursing staff were conducted via interview by the project team members.

Design

The intervention employed a pragmatic pre–post design to compare referral and uptake rates before and after the implementation of pelvic health education strategies. Similar pre–post methods were used to collect feedback from nursing staff before and after PFD education. This design was selected as optimal for system-level interventions where randomisation is unfeasible, while still enabling iterative assessment.12

We hypothesised that structured pelvic health education to both patients and nurses would lead to an increase in outpatient physiotherapy appointments, whether through patient-led or nurse-led referrals.

A multidisciplinary team comprising OGC nurses, pelvic floor physiotherapists and a pelvic floor specialist was formed to develop the intervention, ensuring project consistency and clinical relevance. The intervention was delivered through three strategic components: screening, education and referrals.

The target population consisted of obstetric patients accessing SGH’s Obstetrics and Gynaecology (O&G) department services, specifically those utilising the OGC and postnatal ward facilities. This comprehensive approach ensured the intervention spanned the full maternal care continuum, from the antenatal to the postnatal period.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Inpatient setting

To enhance PFD screening and reporting in the inpatient setting, inpatient ward nurses were trained to deliver pelvic and bladder health education and to identify PFD symptoms in postnatal patients. Through this guidance, patients were empowered to inform the healthcare team if they experienced difficulty voiding or urinary leakage during sneezing, coughing or before reaching toilet facilities. This approach established a transparent communication pathway to combat PFD stigmatisation and negative perceptions. This practice aligns with research addressing knowledge deficiencies among mothers seeking primary care for PFD.13 Enhanced awareness and understanding proved crucial in dispelling patients’ misconceptions about PFD symptoms, thereby encouraging treatment engagement.14 In addition, this implementation facilitated direct referral to our pelvic floor physiotherapist, further emphasising the importance of outpatient physiotherapy appointments.

Besides verbal education, patients were also given softcopy of our discharge educational materials which were recently updated to incorporate bladder and pelvic health information. This served as a platform for patients to check their pelvic floor exercise techniques and if necessary, correct them, which is a common concern identified in PFMT literature.15 On discharge day, attending staff reinforced these instructions to ensure knowledge retention and proper technique implementation.

Outpatient setting

Outpatient OGC staff were provided with education on female pelvic floor health prior to the intervention. During the study period, antenatal patients were given PFD screening questionnaire, pamphlet-guided education on pelvic floor health by OGC staff and additional educational materials on female pelvic floor health through digital media which was made accessible easily using a quick-response (QR) code. The utilisation of printed educational materials and digital platforms has demonstrated effectiveness in health literacy initiatives and reviews, yielding favourable patient outcomes.13 16 This facet of intervention is based on established practices in health communication and promotion.

Antenatal patients identified with PFD or expressing interest in pelvic floor health were offered outpatient physiotherapy appointments if they were agreeable. This intervention design ensures timely, consistent and accessible pelvic floor healthcare provision, facilitating comprehensive coverage for obstetrics patients.

Postnatal assessment clinic

The postnatal assessment clinic (PAC) provides integrated services from pelvic floor specialist and pelvic health physiotherapist. High-risk postnatal mothers and patients with PFD symptoms were referred to the PAC on discharge. Risk factors include assisted vaginal delivery and/or obstetric anal sphincter injuries. At this clinic, postnatal patients receive comprehensive postnatal assessment, in-depth pelvic floor evaluation, individualised education and structured follow-up care plans.

Strategy

Implementation

The project team implemented PDSA cycles to facilitate weekly progress monitoring and systematic review of implementation strategies. Practice changes were reinforced through targeted staff training sessions and departmental briefings. Rapid adjustments were made when gaps were identified in order to strengthen intervention’s effectiveness.

A comprehensive framework consisting of active engagement from the pelvic floor health team and education integration across outpatient, inpatient and discharge care settings ensures the durability of the intervention beyond the initial implementation period, creating sustained improvements in maternal pelvic healthcare delivery.

Intervention

The project consisted of two implementation cycles.

Cycle 1

The first cycle deployed the intervention as originally designed and was conducted from 18 February 2025 to 12 March 2025. Outpatient OGC staff received training on female pelvic floor health prior to implementation. During the study period, antenatal patients were given a PFD screening questionnaire, pamphlet-guided education on pelvic floor health by OGC staff and educational materials on female pelvic floor health through digital media which was made accessible easily using a QR code. Antenatal patients identified with PFD or those expressing interest in pelvic floor health were offered outpatient physiotherapy appointments, subject to their agreement.

However, early feedback from the outpatient team revealed significant resistance from obstetrics patients, many of whom preferred to postpone physiotherapy sessions until after delivery. This reluctance likely stemmed from a prioritisation of fetal well-being over maternal pelvic health and a general lack of awareness about the importance of pelvic floor care. This feedback highlighted the need for strategic adjustments to better address patient perceptions and increase awareness of pelvic floor health.

Cycle 2

Based on the insights from cycle 1, a second PDSA cycle was initiated with a focus on physician engagement, recognising their pivotal role in communicating the long-term impact of PFD on maternal health. This cycle featured systematic reminders to all O&G department doctors to counsel antenatal patients on female pelvic floor health during outpatient consultations, with the goal of raising patients’ awareness. In addition, revisions to the Antenatal Care Plan (ACP) to incorporate pelvic floor health as a standard component during clinic reviews. The revised ACP ensures that doctors consistently inform and counsel patients about pelvic floor health. It also promotes timely referral of symptomatic patients to pelvic health physiotherapists.

Results

Figure 3 illustrates the daily percentage of referred patients who attended a pelvic floor physiotherapy appointment. The baseline period is shown, followed by the implementation of PDSA Cycle 1 (weeks 1–3) and PDSA Cycle 2 (weeks 4–8). The project led to a significant increase in pelvic floor physiotherapy attendance following the implementation of intervention, rising from a baseline median of 0% to 50%. This substantial improvement reflects the importance of emphasising pelvic floor health and raising awareness of PFD across the care continuum. However, these results were not immediate, as an unexpected barrier emerged during implementation, primarily observed in the outpatient setting.

During the first PDSA cycle, many antenatal patients with PFD symptoms expressed a preference to delay their physiotherapy appointments until the postpartum period. This response was not anticipated by the QI project team. Patients may have perceived PFD as irrelevant to their immediate care needs, as pelvic floor health receives comparatively less emphasis during antenatal visits. Similar findings have been reported in research on misconceptions surrounding PFD, which notes that some patients view PFD as a natural consequence of childbearing.14 This perspective explains patients’ openness to PFMT after delivery while demonstrating resistance during pregnancy.

In response to the unexpected findings from the first PDSA cycle, an improved second PDSA cycle was implemented to address the identified obstacles. This cycle incorporated physician engagement in pelvic floor health counselling. Obstetrics and Gynaecology doctors played a pivotal role in helping patients navigate their uncertainty about undergoing PFMT, effectively demonstrating the impact of physician endorsement in overcoming patient reluctance. This intervention highlights the crucial role of patient-doctor relationship in influencing patient’s decision-making.17 It underscores the importance of involving trusted healthcare providers as advocates for new health initiatives, especially when these initiatives challenge patients’ existing perceptions of care priorities.

The project successfully achieved its primary objective—to enhance PFD awareness and education among antenatal patients and nursing staff. This project further demonstrated the intervention’s effectiveness in addressing systemic barriers to pelvic floor healthcare and in establishing sustainable improvements in maternal health service delivery.

Enhanced antenatal education

Enhanced antenatal education empowered pregnant patients to better recognise PFD symptoms, specifically UI and POP more effectively, and facilitated timely referral to pelvic floor physiotherapists. Patients demonstrated a marked increase in pelvic floor health knowledge, improved ability to identify their own PFD symptoms and a greater willingness to seek help for PFD-related issues. This is evidenced by their increased receptivity to physiotherapy services.

The strong correlation between enhanced patient knowledge and increased physiotherapy service engagement validates the educational approach of the intervention as a critical factor in bridging existing gaps in pelvic floor healthcare.

Improved healthcare providers’ awareness and capability in patient education

The intervention also achieved its objective of equipping healthcare staff with the comprehensive knowledge and skills necessary to screen for PFD symptoms and provide appropriate counselling for early intervention. All nursing staff received education on the importance of pelvic floor and bladder health for obstetric care. The ward induction programme for new nurses now includes pelvic floor health topics as standard components of both obstetric patient care and postpartum discharge education.

Similarly, O&G doctors joining the department now receive induction training on female pelvic floor health education and the appropriate referral pathways. These enhancements directly contributed to increased referral rates and greater PFMT service uptake during the intervention period. Collectively, these implementations establish a new standard of care that integrates a more holistic approach to maternal health.

The systematic integration of pelvic floor health education into staff training programmes ensures sustainable knowledge transfer and consistent care delivery across all healthcare providers involved in maternal care, leading to lasting improvements in service quality and patient outcomes.

This structured approach to screening, raising awareness of PFD and establishing referral pathways directly supports the intervention’s overarching aim, positively influencing both referral rates and PFMT service uptake. The enhanced coordination mechanisms create sustainable frameworks for ongoing collaborative care delivery, ensuring long-term improvements in patient access to appropriate pelvic floor health services.

Strength and limitation

This QI project aimed to enhance the uptake of physiotherapy services for PFD by leveraging patient–doctor relationships and iterative process refinement. The intervention highlighted how trust, open communication and mutual respect between patients and healthcare providers support informed decision-making, engagement in care and adherence to recommendations.

Increased physician engagement during the second PDSA cycle was associated with higher physiotherapy uptake, demonstrating the value of established patient–doctor relationships in promoting acceptance of sensitive or unfamiliar topics such as PFD. Positioning physicians as advocates for physiotherapy emerged as a key enabler of patient engagement and service utilisation. The project also showed that active ongoing communication empowers patients to self-identify PFD symptoms, improving awareness and self-efficacy.

Anticipating implementation challenges was another major learning point. If the team had foreseen that antenatal patients would prioritise fetal well-being and decline physiotherapy during pregnancy, an alternative pathway could have been planned, such as redirecting these women to physician-led counselling (‘Plan B’). Involving a patient advocate in the project design might also have improved alignment between the intervention and patient needs by incorporating direct patient perspectives.

Several strengths contributed to the project’s success. These included an adequate sample size with robust data collection, allowing generation of meaningful results and the achievement of predefined targets within 2 months, earlier than the projected 6 months. The team demonstrated adaptive problem-solving and implementation science principles by promptly recognising obstacles and transitioning to a second PDSA cycle. The initiative also prompted systematic review and updating of patient education materials and fostered multidisciplinary collaboration, particularly through sustained nursing involvement across antenatal and postnatal care.

However, important limitations were noted. The project setting was a tertiary centre managing predominantly high-risk pregnancies, so the patient population may not represent the broader maternity population, and competing clinical priorities might have reduced receptivity to physiotherapy. These contextual factors may limit generalisability of the findings and suggest the need for adaptation before wider implementation in more diverse obstetric settings. The questionnaire used in this study was simple and short; however, it was not validated.

The project faced manpower constraints that limited patient contact and recruitment, reducing exposure to the intervention. An additional barrier emerged in the first PDSA cycle, where lack of early feedback led to poor initial patient response. Similar low prioritisation of pelvic floor health was observed among both professionals and patients, highlighting the need to include patient perspectives at the design stage. Future work should incorporate qualitative pilot studies, early feedback and patient advocates within QI teams to improve patient-centredness, address contextual barriers and co-create sustainable, context-appropriate solutions.

Conclusions

This QI project successfully addressed the critical gap in PFD awareness and care among obstetric patients and nursing staff at SGH. Through structured screening, education and streamlined referral pathways, the initiative achieved its primary goal of increasing physiotherapy uptake from a baseline of 0% to 50% within just 2 months—well ahead of the 6 months projection. The results underscore the effectiveness of patient education, multidisciplinary collaboration and physician engagement in normalising discussions around PFD and encouraging timely intervention.

The project further enhanced the knowledge and confidence of healthcare providers, ensuring that pelvic floor health is now embedded as a standard component of antenatal and postnatal care. While the intervention was limited by its setting in a tertiary hospital with predominantly high-risk patients and by resource constraints, it demonstrated that sustained education, adaptive problem-solving and patient-centred approaches can overcome systemic and cultural barriers to care.

Moving forward, the incorporation of patient perspectives, wider stakeholder engagement and adaptation to diverse maternity care contexts will be essential for scaling and sustaining these improvements. Overall, this project not only met its objectives but also laid the foundation for long-term integration of pelvic floor health into holistic maternal care, ultimately improving quality of life and outcomes for women.
