Maternal exposure to PM2.5 and its components and risk of small for gestational age in women undergoing in vitro fertilization: a propensity score-matched case-control study in Guangzhou, China
Authors: Weiqi Liu, Mengxian Liu, Yaoxiang Tang, Shiqi Yang, Fei Ma, Qiuzheng Yue, Weiling Liu, Guiying Zhuang, Sitao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnant women conceiving via in vitro fertilization (IVF) represent a uniquely vulnerable population, yet the impact of ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and its specific chemical components on their fetal growth remains under-researched. This study aimed to investigate the associations between prenatal exposure to PM2.5 and its key components—sulfate (SO42−), nitrate (NO3−), ammonium (NH4+), organic matter (OM), and black carbon (BC)—with the risk of small for gestational age (SGA) in IVF pregnancies.MethodsA propensity score-matched case-control study was conducted in Guangzhou, China. From a source population of 1,459 women who conceived via IVF, 166 SGA cases were identified and matched 1:1 with controls. Exposure to PM2.5 and its components was estimated using time-weighted average concentrations for each trimester and the entire pregnancy. Logistic regression models adjusted for potential confounders were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs), and mixture analyses were employed to identify the dominant pollutants driving the observed risks.ResultsExposure to PM2.5 during the second trimester (OR = 1.082, 95% CI: 1.005–1.165) and third trimester (OR = 1.075, 95% CI: 1.002–1.154), and the entire pregnancy (OR = 1.124, 95% CI: 1.015–1.245) was significantly associated with an increased risk of SGA. Significant positive associations were also observed for SO42−, OM, and BC. Notably, mixture analyses identified NO3− as the primary driver of SGA risk. Furthermore, subgroup analyses revealed no significant associations with these pollutants.ConclusionsPrenatal exposure to PM2.5 and its components, particularly secondary inorganic aerosols and carbonaceous species, is significantly associated with impaired fetal growth in IVF-conceived pregnancies. These findings emphasize the need for component-specific air quality guidelines and targeted interventions to protect this high-risk population during critical gestational windows.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28800-4.Keywords: Air Pollution, Fertilization in Vitro, Nitrates, Particulate Matter

Introduction

Small for gestational age (SGA) is defined as a neonate with a birth weight below the 10th percentile (P10) for sex and gestational age on validated growth charts. Globally, approximately 17.4% of live births are affected by SGA [1], with prevalence exhibiting significant geographic variation—ranging from 5 to 10% in high-income countries to 20–30% in low- and middle-income countries [2, 3]. Reports from different countries further highlight this heterogeneity. For instance, the rate of term SGA infants in the United States increased by 29.9% between 2002 and 2011 [4], and Japan reported a prevalence of 3.5% [5]. A nationwide study in China showed an overall SGA prevalence of 6.4% [6], with Guangdong Province recording a rate as high as 12.28% [7]. SGA infants not only face a significantly higher risk of neonatal death and complications such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia and necrotising enterocolitis [8–10], but also have an increased likelihood of developing metabolic syndrome in adulthood [11]. Given these substantial short- and long-term health consequences, a deeper understanding of the underlying risk factors for SGA is essential to elucidate its aetiology and inform targeted prevention strategies.

The risk of delivering an SGA infant is associated with several determinants, including advanced maternal age, obesity, prenatal exposure to metals [6, 12, 13], and ambient air pollution [14, 15]. Specifically, regarding fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and its components, growing evidence suggests an association with SGA, however, the epidemiological evidence remains inconclusive. For instance, studies in Peru and China reported that such exposure increased SGA risk [16] and was positively associated with this risk [17], respectively. In contrast, studies from South Korea and the United States, demonstrated that PM2.5 exposure did not increase the risk of SGA [18, 19]. Such inconsistencies may stem from regional variations in the chemical composition of PM2.5, differences in exposure assessment methods, and the diversity of population backgrounds. These factors collectively indicate the need for more targeted research in specific regions. A more critical limitation of existing research is its predominant focus on naturally conceived pregnancies, with insufficient attention to the potentially vulnerable subgroup of in vitro fertilization (IVF) pregnancies. The impact of PM2.5 and its components on the risk of SGA in offspring conceived via IVF remains largely unexplored. This vulnerability stems from IVF-specific features-altered placental angiogenesis, ovarian stimulation-induced pro-inflammation, and epigenetic changes from embryo manipulation-which may lower the threshold for oxidative injury and amplify the toxicity of specific PM2.5 constituents [20, 21].

To address this gap, this study was conducted in Guangzhou, focusing on this IVF population. PM2.5 in Guangzhou is characterised by a high proportion of carbonaceous aerosols, particularly fossil fuel-derived elemental carbon and secondary organic carbon [22]. Given that carbonaceous aerosols are potent inducers of oxidative stress and epigenetic perturbations—pathways central to IVF vulnerability-this unique composition makes the city ideal for investigating component-specific health effects. Notably, the annual mean PM2.5 concentration (37 µg/m3 in January 2026) remains substantially above the WHO air quality guideline, underscoring the urgency of this investigation. Therefore, clarifying the exposure–response relationship under Guangzhou’s typical PM2.5 profile will inform targeted interventions for this vulnerable group and specific emission sources.

Methods

Study population

This study was designed as a propensity score-matched case-control study. The initial source population consisted of 2,096 women who underwent IVF and had a live birth at the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Huadu between 2018 and 2024. Baseline data, including age, occupation, educational attainment, and clinical diagnoses, were obtained from the electronic medical record system. Eligible participants were selected after applying the following exclusion criteria: residence outside Guangzhou or address not recorded (n = 20); implantation procedure performed outside Guangzhou (n = 235); multiple gestation (n = 303); pre‑existing diabetes mellitus (n = 23) or hypertension (n = 10); missing embryo transfer date (n = 2); delivery at < 28 weeks of gestation (n = 7); blood type not tested or invalid entry (n = 9); and missing data on covariates or outcome variables (n = 28). In addition, no participants were excluded due to delivery after December 31, 2024, delivery not occurring in the third trimester, or congenital anomalies. After excluding 637 women in total, 1,459 eligible participants comprised the source population, from which 166 SGA cases were identified (Fig. 1). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Huadu (Approval No.2024-001). As this research involved secondary analysis of anonymized electronic medical records collected during routine clinical care, with no direct interaction with participants and no risk to patient privacy, the requirement for informed consent was waived in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and national regulations on ethical review of biomedical research involving humans.

Definition of small for gestational age

This study defined SGA as a birth weight below the P10 for sex- and gestational age-specific reference values, according to China’s national standard Growth standard for newborns by gestational age (WS/T 800–2022). Individualised gestational age was calculated in completed weeks from the date of embryo transfer—a precise marker of conception in assisted reproductive technology—adjusted for pre-transfer embryo development days based on whether a fresh or frozen embryo was used. Each infant’s birth weight was matched to the sex- and gestational age-specific the P10 reference value from the standard’s appendix (reference range: 24–42 weeks), and those with gestational age ≥ 28 weeks and birth weight below the threshold were classified as SGA. This approach, grounded in an objective and standardised growth assessment framework, ensures accurate and reproducible case classification.

Assessment of prenatal exposure to PM2.5 and its components

This study obtained daily concentration data for PM2.5 and its components—sulphate (SO42−), nitrate (NO3−), ammonium (NH4+), organic matter (OM), and black carbon (BC)—in Guangzhou during the study period from 2018 to 2024. The data were sourced from the Tracking Air Pollution (TAP) near-real-time atmospheric composition dataset (http://tapdata.org.cn/). The TAP team employed a two‑stage machine‑learning inversion system with decision‑tree gap‑filling to produce a 10‑km resolution gridded PM2.5 product [23, 24]; component‑specific concentrations were then derived from chemical transport model estimates with bias corrections using an improved dust module and extreme gradient boosting [25, 26]. This product has demonstrated robust predictive accuracy, with an average out‑of‑bag cross‑validation R² of 0.83 for daily PM2.5 mass concentrations. Although the 10‑km resolution cannot capture intra‑urban micro‑scale variations, any resulting exposure misclassification would be non‑differential with respect to SGA status, biasing effect estimates towards the null and thus rendering our findings conservative.

Due to the precision constraints in geocoding residential addresses to the 10‑km grid, we were unable to reliably match each participant to a specific grid cell; therefore, we used city‑wide daily average concentrations as proxies for individual‑level exposure. The exposure windows during pregnancy were defined based on precise clinical records of embryo transfer and delivery dates, with the transfer date serving as the anchoring point for gestational age estimation. According to standard IVF dating conventions (embryonic age + 14 days) [27] fresh embryo transfers (typically day‑3 cleavage‑stage embryos) correspond to approximately 17 days post‑fertilisation, whereas frozen‑thawed blastocyst transfers (typically day‑5 blastocysts) correspond to approximately 19 days. We strictly adhered to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists guidelines to define the three trimesters: the first trimester from the onset of gestation through 13 weeks + 6 days (i.e., < 14 weeks 0 days), the second trimester from 14 weeks 0 days through 27 weeks + 6 days, and the third trimester from 28 weeks 0 days to delivery [28]. In practical terms, the end date of the first trimester was calculated as the transfer date plus (97 days minus the pre‑transfer embryonic days), where 97 days represents the total days from fertilisation to the conclusion of week 13; no further adjustment was made for the second and third trimesters, as the transfer date provides a fixed and consistent reference for all subsequent windows. To ensure the robustness and accuracy of this window assignment, we used documented clinical dates, excluded pregnancies with incomplete transfer or delivery records, as well as those with gestational dating errors exceeding ± 3 days, followed the widely accepted IVF dating algorithms reported in the literature [1], and calculated time‑weighted average concentrations using daily monitoring data at 10‑km resolution. These measures collectively minimised temporal aggregation errors and ensured the reliability of our exposure estimates.

Covariates

Covariates included in this study were extracted from the electronic medical record system and selected based on established research practice. The selected covariates were as follows: (1) demographic characteristics—maternal age (included both as a continuous variable and categorised according to clinical convention into < 35 and ≥ 35 years), occupation (classified as managerial/professional, self-employed, or other), marital status (dichotomised as married or other), and blood group (recorded as A, B, O, or AB); (2) pregnancy and birth characteristics—parity (classified from obstetric history as multiparous or primiparous); Blastocysts were grouped according to the type of transfer cycle: fresh blastocyst transfer (transfer within the same stimulation cycle) or frozen-thawed blastocyst transfer (transfer of cryopreserved-thawed blastocysts derived from a prior cycle); and infant sex (male or female); (3) pregnancy complications, defined as binary variables (yes, or no ) based on primary and secondary discharge diagnoses recorded in the electronic medical record system, including gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, anaemia, premature rupture of membranes, preterm birth, and thyroid disease; and (4) The meteorological data were sourced from the Global Historical Climatology Network-Daily database, specifically using observations from Guangzhou Baiyun International Airport, including daily temperature, dew-point temperature, and wind speed; (5) Daily ozone (O3) concentration was obtained from the same environmental monitoring network that also provided the PM2.5. Exposure assessment for all meteorological variables and O3 was conducted using the same method as that applied for PM2.5, and these variables were included in the models as continuous variables.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of the participants were described using medians (interquartile ranges) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test or the chi-square test as appropriate. To establish a comparable control group within a case-control framework, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.2. Cases were defined as the 166 infants diagnosed with SGA, and controls were selected from the remaining 1,293 non-SGA women. The propensity score was estimated based on maternal age, occupation, marital status, parity, blastocyst type, and infant sex. After matching, balance between cases and controls was assessed using the standardized mean difference (SMD), with an SMD < 0.1 indicating adequate balance. The final analytic sample consisted of 332 women (166 matched pairs) with complete data for all exposure and covariate variables, thus no imputation was required. Subsequently, we evaluated correlations among the six PM2.5 components using a Pearson correlation matrix. To further quantify the collinearity strength, we additionally calculated variance inflation factors (VIFs) for the full pregnancy exposure window. Given the strong collinearity detected (Figure S1), each component was analysed in separate single-pollutant models. To account for the matched design, conditional logistic regression models were used to examine the associations between PM2.5 and its components with SGA. Both unadjusted and adjusted models were constructed. The adjusted model included the following covariates to further control for residual confounding: maternal blood type, anaemia, preterm birth, premature rupture of membranes, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes mellitus, as well as concurrent levels of dew point temperature, wind speed, and O3. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each exposure window.

To explore potential nonlinear exposure-response relationships for single pollutants, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models (with 3–7 knots) were fitted to full-pregnancy exposure levels, and the optimal model was selected by minimising the Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria (AIC and BIC). Furthermore, to assess the overall impact of the mixture of PM2.5 components (SO42−, NO3−, NH4⁺, OM, and BC) without including total PM2.5 in the models, we applied two complementary approaches: weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression, which assumed a positive direction and used 500 bootstrap samples, and quantile g-computation (qgcomp), which allows unconstrained estimation of component effects. To account for the 1:1 propensity score–matched design, both mixture models included the matched pair identifier (subclass) as an additional covariate (treated as a fixed effect) to control for potential confounding due to matching and to adjust for the paired data structure. All components were categorized into quartiles for these analyses. Finally, subgroup analyses were performed based on infant sex, maternal age, and blastocysts.

To evaluate the robustness of our findings against residual confounding, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. These included: (1) conventional logistic regression using the full unmatched cohort; (2) a stringent PSM-based conditional logistic regression further adjusting for all covariates that remained imbalanced (standardized mean difference [SMD] > 0.1) after matching; and (3) calculation of E-values to quantify the minimum strength of association an unmeasured confounder would need to fully explain away the observed effects.

All statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 4.3.0), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 1,459 pregnant women undergoing IVF treatment were enrolled, of whom 166 (11.4%) delivered SGA. As shown in Table 1, after propensity score matching, most covariates achieved SMDs below 0.1, indicating acceptable balance between the case and control groups for the majority of baseline characteristics. However, a few covariates-including anemia (SMD = 0.255), marital status, blood type, and gestational hypertension-exhibited SMD values exceeding 0.1, suggesting residual imbalances that may require further adjustment in subsequent analyses. As shown in Table 3, after adjustment for potential confounders, exposure to PM2.5 during the second trimester was associated with an increased risk of SGA (OR 1.082, 95% CI 1.005–1.165). Exposure to PM2.5, OM, and BC during the third trimester was significantly associated with increased odds of SGA, with odds ratios of 1.075 (95% CI 1.002–1.154), 1.387 (95% CI 1.064–1.809), and 5.074 (95% CI 1.356–18.99), respectively. An increased risk of SGA was observed with exposure throughout the entire pregnancy to PM2.5 (OR 1.124, 95% CI 1.015–1.245), SO42− (OR 1.797, 95% CI 1.040–3.107), OM (OR 1.552, 95% CI 1.059–2.273), and BC (OR 7.995, 95% CI 1.237–51.67), respectively.

Comparison of RCS models with 3 to 7 knots (Figure S2) indicated that for most pollutants the AIC and BIC were minimised with 3 knots, which aligns with the BIC’s tendency to favour more parsimonious models. Therefore, to adhere to the principle of model parsimony and avoid overfitting, 3 knots were uniformly selected for the RCS analysis of pollutions. As shown in Fig. 3, the exposure–response relationships between PM2.5 and its components and SGA showed no significant nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity > 0.05).

Discussion

This study is the first to systematically evaluate prenatal exposure to PM2.5 and its components in relation to SGA risk among women conceiving through IVF. Exposure to PM2.5 during the second and third trimesters and throughout pregnancy significantly increased SGA risk. Specific components, particularly SO42−, and OM, showed stronger associations. Further analysis identified NO3− as a key driver of PM2.5-related SGA risk, offering new insights into its component-specific toxicity. Subgroup analyses revealed greater susceptibility in female infants and in those conceived via frozen—thawed embryo transfer during the third trimester. These findings provide novel epidemiological evidence on air pollution’s impact on this vulnerable IVF population and suggest that PM2.5 components—especially secondary inorganic aerosols and some carbonaceous species—play a critical role in fetal growth restriction.

Emerging evidence suggests that the association between maternal PM2.5 exposure and SGA may be mediated by biological processes such as oxidative stress and inflammatory responses affecting placental function. Firstly, exposure to PM2.5 can induce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, resulting in impaired placental function. Studies show that PM2.5 exposure is associated with elevated levels of inflammatory markers such as interleukin—6 and tumour necrosis factor—alpha in maternal serum, along with increased lipid peroxidation, processes that can interfere with placental blood flow and nutrient transport, thereby restricting fetal growth [29, 30]. Secondly, epigenetic modifications such as abnormal DNA methylation play a mediating role in the association between PM2.5 exposure and fetal development. For instance, maternal exposure to traffic—related air pollution can reduce placental long interspersed nuclear element—1 methylation levels, which has been linked to lower birth weight [31]. Furthermore, specific components within PM2.5 may further impair placental vascular development and function by inducing endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and elevated blood pressure [32]. Moreover, animal studies indicate that PM2.5 can promote epithelial—mesenchymal transition through specific signalling pathways, leading to abnormal placental structure and restricted fetal growth [33]. Collectively, PM2.5 and its components may increase the risk of SGA through multiple pathways, including inflammatory and oxidative stress responses, epigenetic alterations, endothelial dysfunction, and abnormal placental vascular development—mechanisms that may be potentiated in IVF pregnancies by their altered placental angiogenesis and heightened inflammatory baseline.

This study of IVF pregnancies demonstrates that prenatal exposure to PM2.5 increases the risk of SGA in IVF pregnancies. This finding is consistent with research findings from studies conducted in naturally conceived populations in recent years [34, 35]. This consistent pattern across populations suggests that the toxic effect of PM2.5 on fetal growth shares a common qualitative pathophysiological basis (e.g., oxidative stress and inflammation); however, this shared pathway does not preclude a heightened quantitative susceptibility in IVF pregnancies. Rather, their altered placental angiogenesis, ovarian stimulation—induced pro—inflammatory milieu, and epigenetic lability may lower the toxicological threshold for these insults, thereby amplifying the observed risks [20, 21]. Further analysis showed that the second and third trimesters of pregnancy—particularly the third trimester—are critical susceptibility windows during which PM2.5 exposure affects fetal growth. This result is consistent with previous research on differences in vulnerability across various stages of pregnancy [36, 37]. This may be because the second and third trimesters of pregnancy constitute a period of rapid fetal weight gain. During this critical window, PM2.5 exposure could create a “bottleneck” effect on fetal growth by triggering oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, impairing placental function, or disrupting fetal metabolism. This finding establishes a link between exposure timing and potential biological mechanisms, thereby providing crucial temporal insights for future targeted toxicological research and the development of stage—specific recommendations for prenatal protection.

This study further identified that specific PM2.5 components—particularly BC, OM, SO42− and NO3−—were associated with an increased risk of SGA, consistent with previous evidence on the toxic effects of PM2.5 constituents reported in general populations [38, 39]. These findings suggest that the adverse health effects of these harmful components are likely to be widely applicable. The underlying mechanisms likely involve common pathophysiological pathways, including the induction of oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, maternal endothelial dysfunction, and disruption of placental vascular development and nutrient transport—collectively mediating placental toxicity and impairing foetal development. This study provides direct evidence supporting the biological plausibility that these components adversely affect the foetus through placental toxicity mechanisms.

It is noteworthy that, although nitrate did not reach conventional statistical significance in the single‑pollutant model for the entire pregnancy, this marginal signal approached the threshold and does not undermine its consistent identification as a key contributor in both mixture frameworks, as the strong collinearity among PM2.5 components inflates standard errors and reduces statistical power in single‑pollutant models, whereas mixture approaches such as WQS and qgcomp aggregate correlated signals to enhance the detection of joint effects. Regarding the mixture models themselves, WQS assigned the greatest weight to SO42− while qgcomp indicated the strongest positive effect for NO3−, a divergence reflecting their distinct algorithms—WQS quantifies relative contribution to the mixture whereas qgcomp estimates conditional marginal effects—yet critically, NO3− was positively associated with SGA in both frameworks and showed the largest conditional effect, supporting it as the primary driver with SO42− as a supportive component. This finding aligns with previous studies: a nationwide Chinese survey demonstrated that anthropogenic PM2.5 constituents, including sulphate and nitrate, were associated with increased SGA risk [39]; a domestic twin study similarly found positive associations between sulphate, nitrate, ammonium, black carbon and organic matter and SGA [38]. Furthermore, a Massachusetts‑based study identified elemental carbon as the most toxic species, followed by nitrate, organic carbon and sulphate [40], whereas a recent Lanzhou study using qgcomp suggested black carbon as the most hazardous component [17]. Given nitrate’s established roles in promoting oxidative and nitrosative stress—pathways that can impair placental function and restrict fetal growth—its consistent positive signal across both WQS and qgcomp frameworks further supports its prioritisation as an intervention target among air pollutants in this vulnerable IVF population. Overall, despite ranking discrepancies between the two mixture models, the consistent positive signal of NO3− across both frameworks supports our primary conclusion.

Subgroup analyses by maternal age and fetal sex showed that across all exposure windows, exposure to PM2.5 and its components was not significantly associated with an increased risk of SGA. Existing literature reports inconsistent findings on sex‑specific susceptibility; some studies suggest female infants are more vulnerable to PM2.5 exposure in the first trimester [41], while others report stronger associations in male infants [42] or identify concentration—dependent differences only in males [43]. These discrepancies may stem from multiple interwoven factors. Firstly, as a heterogeneous mixture, PM2.5 exhibits considerable spatiotemporal variation in its chemical constituents, and its diverse components differ in their capacity to cross the placental barrier and provoke oxidative stress responses. Secondly, differences in the delineation of exposure time windows and in the lag structures adopted by statistical models may bias the detection of truly critical sensitive periods. Thirdly, intrinsic disparities among study populations—including racial/ethnic background, pre‑pregnancy body mass index, and socioeconomic status—are also likely to modify the direction and magnitude of the observed pollutant effects. Additionally, we conducted subgroup analyses by embryo transfer type. The results indicated that neither fresh nor frozen—thawed embryo transfer cycles showed statistically significant associations between pollutant exposure and SGA risk across any exposure window. However, because comparable literature is currently lacking, this finding should be regarded as exploratory and warrants confirmation in larger, dedicated IVF cohorts.

Furthermore, to examine the robustness of our core findings against residual confounding, we performed multi—layered sensitivity analyses. The results demonstrated that the associations between PM2.5, OM, and BC with SGA risk remained highly consistent in both direction and statistical significance across all models, with no substantial alteration due to different model specifications or covariate adjustment strategies. Notably, the quantitative assessment of E-values indicated that unmeasured confounders would need to possess implausibly strong associations to fully explain away the observed effects. Collectively, these sensitivity analyses provide robust methodological support for the reliability of our main conclusions and suggest that residual confounding is unlikely to drive our findings.

This study has several limitations. First, although we sought to minimise confounding through propensity score matching and multi-level sensitivity analyses, and the E-value quantification indicated that unmeasured confounding would require an effect size far beyond plausible bounds to overturn our findings, residual confounding—arising from unmeasured lifestyle or genetic factors such as pre-pregnancy body mass index, smoking, alcohol consumption, and household income—cannot be entirely ruled out in an observational design. In addition, we discussed potential biological mechanisms, we did not directly measure intermediary biomarkers of oxidative stress, inflammation, or epigenetic modifications; therefore, our mechanistic interpretations remain largely dependent on the existing literature. Second, exposure assessment based on residential address and a 10‑km resolution grid may not fully capture the fine‑scale spatial variability within Guangzhou, and indoor air pollution or individual time–activity patterns were not incorporated. Such an approach is likely to result in non‑differential exposure misclassification, which generally biases effect estimates towards the null (i.e., odds ratios tending towards unity). Therefore, the significant associations observed in this study should be regarded as conservative estimates. Furthermore, future investigations should employ higher‑resolution exposure models combined with individual spatiotemporal activity data to reduce exposure misclassification, expand the sample size in populations undergoing in vitro fertilisation, perform analyses stratified by specific assisted reproductive technology procedures, and incorporate multi‑omics profiling to further elucidate the precise mechanisms and critical exposure windows. Third, the study population comprised a specific cohort of pregnancies achieved through in vitro fertilisation, and caution is therefore warranted when extrapolating the findings to naturally conceived pregnancies. Furthermore, the effect estimates for black carbon (BC) exhibited marked statistical instability, primarily attributable to the limited number of matched pairs with both high BC exposure and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) outcomes, which resulted in insufficient statistical power and may have induced overfitting and sparse‑data bias in the conditional logistic regression models. Consequently, the effect sizes for BC lack adequate precision and reliability, and should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis‑generating findings that require validation in larger independent cohort studies.

Conclusions

This study identifies nitrate as a principal risk driver of PM2.5 component toxicity for fetal growth restriction in IVF pregnancies, with carbonaceous components showing suggestive but statistically fragile associations. These findings provide novel insights into component-specific risks in this vulnerable population and underscore the need for refined air quality assessments in assisted reproduction cohorts.
