Work Function-Driven Performance Analysis of Silver Nanowire Back Contact Electrodes for Platinum and FTO-Free Dye-Sensitized Solar Cells
Authors: Lokesh Murali, George Jacob
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs) are promising substitutes to conventional solar cells, provided their efficiency, stability, and cost effectiveness can be improved. The conventional fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) back contact electrodes used in DSSCs involve complex fabrication processes, increasing the overall costs. The work function of the back contact electrode plays a critical role in determining the charge extraction efficiency and overall photovoltaic performance in DSSCs. In this study, systematic investigations were performed on the back contact electrode’s work function influence on the efficiency of DSSCs by combining SCAPS-1D simulations with experimental validation. Guided by simulation results, DSSCs were fabricated using silver nanowire (AgNW)-based back contact electrodes, and their performance was compared to that of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) and Pt-coated FTO electrodes. Experimental measurements show that AgNW electrodes achieve an efficiency of 4.2%, closer to 5.3% of Pt-deposited FTO and significantly outperforming the 2% efficiency obtained with bare FTO. The strong correlation between the simulated trends and experimental results confirms that the improved performance originates from the higher effective work function of the AgNW electrode. This work highlights that AgNW-based back contacts not only reduce material and fabrication costs but also provide favorable energetics for efficient charge collection, making them a promising alternative for scalable and FTO-free DSSC development.

Introduction

In recent decades, energy consumption has faced a steady increase. The main factors that drove this rise were population growth and industrialization. This demand continues to grow exponentially even today due to the limited energy supply. Nowadays, renewable energy sources are desired for encountering energy needs because traditional nonrenewable sources are associated with diverse issues, including delimited fuel availability, environmental pollution, and loss of biodiversity. The different renewable energy sources include tidal, , geothermal, , hydro, , wind, and solar energy. The most favored form of green sustainable energy is considered to be solar energy due to the reduced maintenance and installation costs compared to those of other forms of energy. Solar cells are energy-harvesting machines that convert photons from the sun into electricity. They are also relatively affordable to install, so solar cells are seen as a potential answer to the present-day energy demand crisis. ,

Conventional solar cells (first-generation solar cells) transform photonic power from the sun to electrical power using semiconductor technology. The complexity of the production process is a limitation for first-generation solar cells. First-generation solar cells require high-purity silicon ingots for manufacturing crystalline solar cells; manufacturing high-purity silicon ingots is an energy-intensive process and complicates the manufacturing process for first-generation solar cells. Second-generation thin film solar cells, such as CdTe and CIGS were later developed, which exhibited better efficiency compared to that of conventional polycrystalline solar cells; however, the presence of heavy metals and their toxicity made them unfit for conventional utilization. The most promising prospect for the future is third-generation solar cells, which are still in their infant stage of development. This development is hampered by stability issues, high-cost raw materials, and poor encapsulation techniques. Third-generation solar cells are anticipated to replace first-generation solar cells in the near future due to their reduced fabrication cost. Dye-sensitized solar cells are still in the research stage since the commercialization of DSSC still has a long way to go. Research on third-generation dye-sensitized solar cells has shown potential application in building integrated photovoltaics (BIPV).

The key components of a DSSC are transparent conductive oxide, an electron transport layer (ETL), a photosensitizer, a hole transport layer (HTL), and a counter electrode (Figure ). The transparent conductive oxide (TCO) layer used in conventional DSSC is fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO). These FTO-coated glass slides have a very high transparency, allowing light to effectively penetrate the active layer of the DSSC. The conventionally used electron transport layer (ETL) is TiO2 in its anatase form. Due to its mesoporous nature, it facilitates the transport of electrons to the FTO-coated glass slides produced from the photoexcitation of the dye molecules. The mesoporous nature of TiO2 allows the efficient adsorption of photosensitizer dye molecules, increasing light harvesting. The photosensitizer used in DSSC is a ruthenium-based dye referred to as N719 dye. The dye is adsorbed onto the mesoporous TiO2 layer. The charge carriers are produced by N719 dye molecules upon photoexcitation. The charge carriers produced upon illumination are injected from the dye into the TiO2 conduction band. The charge carriers travel through the TiO2 layer to reach the FTO electrode. The electrons that reach the electrode surface are then transported to the external circuit to drive the load in the external circuit, after which these charge carriers reach the counter electrode (CE). The CE plays a substantial role in DSSC by reducing oxidized electrolyte molecules by collecting the electrons from the external circuit. The conventionally used CE is a platinum-based CE in DSSC. The electrons at the counter electrode reach the oxidized dye molecules by passing through the electrolyte medium. The conventional electrolyte layer used in DSSC is the iodide/triiodide electrolyte. The electrolyte layer reduces the oxidized photosensitizer dye molecules, returning them to their original state. To facilitate the commercialization of DSSC, much research has been conducted toward using substrates, semiconductors, , alternative dyes, , electrolytes, , and counter electrodes , for DSSC.

Schematic representation of the components used in dye-sensitized solar cells

The commercialization of DSSCs has faced significant hurdles, primarily due to the high costs associated with the method and materials used for their fabrication. The major cost is the use of FTO-coated glass slides, which serve as the conductive substrate. The use of platinum (Pt) as CE in DSSC has also been a significant factor in limitations faced by DSSC since Pt is scarce and very costly. This calls for alternative materials for FTO and Pt to improve the cost effectiveness of DSSC. In this research, we propose silver nanowires (AgNW) as back contact electrodes to replace FTO as an electrode in DSSC. AgNW also has the potential to be used as CE by substituting Pt; however, the iodide electrolyte used in DSSC is corrosive, which leads to the etching of silver nanowires. To address this setback, PEDOT:PSS was chosen as a protective coating for the AgNW electrode. Over the past few years, PEDOT:PSS has gained importance in advanced solar technologies due to its high conductivity, stability, and compatibility with flexible substrates. For instance, a recent study demonstrated an NREL-certified small-area cell (>0.052 cm2) achieving efficiencies above 26% using PEDOT:PSS as a hole-selective layer in hybrid solar cells. PEDOT:PSS is a highly conductive polymer, and it is very compatible with rigid and flexible substrates. Unlike platinum, it provides better cost reduction and scalability options as well, and it has the potential to play the role of CE, thereby substituting for Pt in DSSC. In our approach, AgNW was drop cast on a glass slide to serve as a back contact electrode, and PEDOT:PSS was drop cast on top of a AgNW back contact electrode to form a conformal coating which imparts electrochemical activity and physical protection to AgNW.

In this study, a DSSC device back contact electrode is engineered and evaluated to study the influence of the work function of the back contact electrode on solar cell performance. The fabricated DSSC devices were analyzed for performance using a solar simulator and further validated using SCAPS 1D software. A comparative study was performed; the performance of AgNW as a back contact electrode in DSSC was compared with that of Pt-coated FTO and a bare FTO-coated glass slide as back contact electrodes. In order to have a fair comparison between the devices, PEDOT:PSS was fixed as the CE for all of the devices. While Pt and FTO dominate DSSC research, recent works ,, highlight the need for low-cost, flexible alternatives. However, few studies integrate AgNW with PEDOT:PSS and validate performance through simulation and experimentation, which this work addresses.

Methodology

Experimental Fabrication

Cleaning of FTO-Coated Glass Slides

The FTO slides were cleaned in a soap solution for 10 min, followed by sonication for 10 min. Next, the FTO-coated glass slides were sonicated with acetone solution for another 10 min. This process was repeated with isopropyl alcohol, which was also sonicated for 10 min.

Fabrication of the TiO2 Photoanode

A total of 250 mg of TiO2 in its anatase form was measured and placed in a mortar. A drop of Triton X-100 was added as a binder for the TiO2 slurry. Around 400 μL of DI water was added to the TiO2 powder to achieve a thin, consistent paste. Nitric acid (150 μL) was also added to the composition. The composition was continuously ground with a pestle until the consistency was suitable for doctor blade deposition. Next, the conductive face of the FTO-coated glass slide was masked by using 3M Scotch tape, leaving an unmasked area of 0.16 cm2. The exposed region was used for depositing the TiO2 paste. The paste was deposited in this unmasked region using the doctor blade deposition technique and heated to 500 °C for 2 h. This improved the binding of TiO2 to the FTO slides. ,

Dye Loading of TiO2

Dye serves as a photosensitizer material that generates charge carriers upon exposure to light. The dye used here to accomplish this task is the N719 dye. A concentration of 0.3 mM N719 dye was dissolved in an ethanol solution, and the fabricated TiO2 photoanode electrodes were submerged in the dye solution for 22 h. This procedure is highly sensitive to light and was maintained under dark conditions.

Fabrication of the Silver Nanowire Electrode

The silver nanowires were synthesized using one-step polyol synthesis. Silver nitrate served as the precursor, and ethylene glycol acted as the reducing agent in this reaction. Initially, 294 mg of PVP (130 K) was added to 20 mL of ethylene glycol and preheated to 100 °C. After 30 min, 40 μL of NaCl solution and 20 μL of KBr solution were added. The temperature was further elevated to 140 °C for an additional 30 min, after which 294 mM silver nitrate dissolved in ethylene glycol was added dropwise. The stirring was stopped when silver nitrate was added. , The reaction lasted for 90 min at a temperature of 140 °C. The obtained solution was centrifuged at 7500 rpm for 10 min using acetone; following the initial wash procedure, the precipitate was redispersed and subjected to a second centrifugation at the same speed and time using ethanol. After the supernatant was removed, the remaining silver nanowire sediment was dispersed in an ethanol solution. This dispersion was further used to fabricate silver nanowire electrodes by using the drop-casting deposition technique.

Microscopic glass slides measuring 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm were used as the substrates. These glass slides were cleaned using the same procedure as that for cleaning the FTO-coated glass slides. Additionally, glass slides were subjected to UV-ozone for 2 h to improve the substrate’s wettability by improving the substrates’ hydrophilic nature. The UV-ozone-treated glass slides were used as substrates for the fabrication of silver nanowire electrodes. Silver nanowires were drop cast on the UV-ozone-treated glass slide and held at 90 °C for 1 h and at 120 °C for 2 h.

Deposition of the Pt Electrode

The platinum electrode was fabricated on a fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass slide. A 10 mM solution of hexacholoroplatinic acid was taken and drop cast on the FTO-coated glass slide. After the solution was drop cast, the FTO-coated glass slide was heated to an elevated temperature of 500 °C to facilitate the thermal decomposition of hexachloroplatinic acid to platinum. To verify the presence of platinum, a few drops of hydrogen peroxide were dropped onto the Pt thin film coating deposited on the FTO-coated glass slide. Bubbles were observed from the region where platinum was deposited, which confirmed the presence of platinum.

Deposition of the PEDOT:PSS Counter Electrode

The counter electrode (CE) layer chosen as PEDOT:PSS was drop cast onto the silver nanowire electrode to form a CE layer. To perform a comparative study, PEDOT:PSS CE was also deposited on a plain FTO-coated glass slide and a Pt-coated FTO-coated glass slide as well. The PEDOT:PSS CE layer was held at 80 °C for 1 h in all of the above-discussed cases. The role of the CE is to return the electrons from the external circuit to the oxidized dye molecules.

Fabrication of the Iodide Electrolyte

An iodide/triiodide complex was used as the electrolyte medium in this work. It was synthesized using 0.5 M lithium iodide, 0.05 M iodine, and 0.1 M 4-tert-butylpyridine, which were all dissolved in acetonitrile solution.

Fabrication of DSSC

The DSSC was fabricated by sandwiching the photoanode and photocathode cells. The photoanode used was TiO2-coated FTO-coated glass slides, while the photocathode cell consisted of silver nanowire-deposited glass slides.

Simulation

SCAPS 1D software was used for simulation and modeling DSSC devices. SCAPS 1D is one-dimensional solar cell simulation software which was developed by Professor Marc Burgelman at the University of Gent. Initially, this software was developed to simulate second-generation solar cells, since the cost of fabrication was too high. Many modules were later added to SCAPS 1D, which extended its simulation capability to silicon-based solar cells and perovskite solar cells as well. In this study, SCAPS 1D is utilized to theoretically simulate dye-sensitized solar cells. Device Architecture

The proposed device in this method has three layers which are TiO2, N719 dye, and PEDOT:PSS. TiO2 serves as a semiconductor, which is an n-type material. N719 is a dye sensitizer material, and PEDOT:PSS serves as a p-type material and constitutes the counter electrode layer. All of the simulations were conducted under AM 1.5G, and the incident light power was 1000 W/m2. The working temperature was maintained at 300 K. In this simulation, a comparison study was conducted by varying the work function values of the back contact region in SCAPS 1D. The back contact electrodes taken for comparison were the FTO-coated glass slide, Pt-coated FTO, and AgNW. The simulation parameters were sourced from closely related materials to reflect realistic values − as shown in Table . All layers were verified for compatibility with SCAPS modeling limits and cross-checked with experimental data. This work suggests three comparative investigations involving different device configurations. In the first device, the front and back contact electrodes were fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO), with a work function of 4.4 eV for both contacts. In the second device, the front contact is FTO while the back contact is Pt-coated FTO. The work function of the front contact was considered to be 4.4 eV, and the rear contact work function was 5.5 eV. In the third device, FTO was taken to be the front contact previously mentioned, and the back contact was taken to be the AgNW-coated glass slide. The work function for the rear contact was taken as 4.7 eV. The thickness of the TiO2 layer was 20 μm, based on the actual thickness measured for the TiO2 layer deposited on FTO. The thickness of the doctor blade-coated TiO2 layer was determined using a surface profiler. Additionally, the thickness value of the drop-cast PEDOT:PSS was determined to be 10.5 μm.

Characterization

Various characterizations were performed to analyze the material and film properties. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed using a D8 advanced Bruker with Cu Kα of wavelength 1.5418 Å. UV–vis spectroscopy was performed with an Analytica Jena, Specord 210 plus instrument. Field emission scanning electron microscopy was performed using a ThermoFisher Scientific FEI Quanta 250 FEG. Sheet resistance was measured with an Everbeing C series starter probe station coupled with a Keithley 4200A-SCS semiconductor parameter analyzer. Ultraviolet photoemission spectroscopy (UPS) was performed with an ULVAC-PHI VersaProbe 4. The photovoltaic measurement of the device was made by analyzing the photocurrent density and voltage (J–V curve) using a solar simulator (Oriel) under test conditions of 1 SUN illumination and 1.5 AM (air mass).

Results and Discussion

XRD analysis was conducted to examine the crystalline nature of the one-step polyol-synthesized AgNW, as shown in Figure a. The XRD peaks were observed at 37.8, 44, 64.25, and 77.2° at the corresponding 2θ angles. These peaks correspond to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystal planes, respectively, which matched JCPDS card no. 99-0094. The crystalline structure of AgNW was determined to be face-centered cubic. The average crystallite size of silver nanowires was calculated by using the Williamson–Hall method to be approximately 198.6 nm. Given that silver nanowires exhibit preferential growth along the (111) crystallographic direction and the dominant XRD peaks correspond to this orientation, the obtained crystallite size corresponds to the longitudinal axis of the nanowires. This is consistent with FESEM analysis shown in Figure c, which shows diameters of around 73 nm and lengths extending into the submicrometer range, confirming the anisotropic growth of AgNWs. These results suggest that the silver nanowires are largely single-crystalline along their length, with minimal grain boundary interruption along the (111) axis.

(a) XRD analysis of AgNW. (b) UV–vis absorbance spectroscopy of AgNW. (c) FESEM image of AgNW.

Figure b shows the UV–vis spectroscopy of one-step polyol-synthesized AgNW. The peaks were observed in the regions of 383.3 and 353.8 nm, which correspond to longitudinal surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) and transverse surface plasmon resonance (TSPR), respectively. The presence of these two peaks in UV–vis spectroscopy indicates the growth of AgNW primarily in one direction.

The work function of the bulk silver as reported by Dweydari and coworkers was 4.74 eV. Silver, being face-centered cubic, has a (100) plane and a (111) plane, and the work function of silver is different in the other two planes, which was reported to be 4.72 and 4.66 eV. According to Dweydari et al., silver-based thin films had a lower work function individually, but after continuous deposition followed by annealing, the work function increased to be similar to that of bulk silver. In this study, the work function of deposited silver nanowires was adopted as 4.7 eV as determined by the UPS. To determine the work function of the fabricated silver nanowire electrode on a glass slide, UPS analysis was performed. UPS measured the kinetic energy of the electrons released from the valence band of the silver atoms, which was recorded, and a graph was plotted as shown in Figure . The work function of the silver nanowire was calculated using the given formula ϕ=hν−(K.E.⁡cutoff−Fermi⁡level⁡Ef) where ϕ is the work function, hν is the incident photon energy (21.2 eV), K.E. cutoff is the x intercept of highest energy in the UPS spectrum, and Fermi level Ef is the x intercept of the initial energy value in the UPS spectrum. For the first device, the second and third devices, the back contact electrodes were FTO, Pt-coated on FTO, and AgNW deposited on a glass slide, respectively, as shown in Figure . All the devices had FTO as the front contact electrode. As mentioned earlier, the devices were experimentally fabricated and theoretically simulated in SCAPS 1D. The J–V curve for experimentally fabricated devices is shown in Figure a, while the J–V curve for SCAPS 1D simulated devices is shown in Figure b. Neutral defects were introduced in the PEDOT:PSS layer to mimic interfacial traps generated during high-temperature Pt annealing, as reported by Gnanasekar et al., 2019. This approach improves the match between simulated and experimental FF. The disparity between the simulated and experimental values of V oc and J sc arises from the idealized assumptions used in the SCAPS simulation. The model does not account for parasitic losses such as interfacial traps, nonzero series and shunt resistances, contact barriers, and optical losses. These simplifications lead to an overestimation of device performance in the simulation compared to real-world experimental results.

The devices were fabricated in the laboratory, and the same architecture was simulated in SCAPS 1D software. J–V parametric values such as the open circuit voltage (V oc ), short circuit current density (J sc ), efficiency, and fill factor were determined for both the fabricated and simulated devices for each electrode taken for comparison. J sc refers to the current flow in the external circuit when the terminal of the solar cell is shorted, hence, the name short circuit current density. J sc is preferred instead of I sc (short circuit current) because J sc neglects the area dependence of the solar cell taken. V oc is the voltage observed at the solar cell terminals when kept open. J sc depicts the ability of charge carrier generation in DSSC, and V oc depicts the charge carrier separation ability of solar cells. The efficiency of solar cells is also interchangeably called the conversion efficiency; it measures the ability of the solar cell to convert the incident power to generated power. Under ideal conditions, the generated charge carriers must be separated, which can be used to drive the external circuit. However, in real time some charge carriers that are generated will undergo recombination, leading to a decrease in the efficiency of the solar cell. As mentioned earlier, PEDOT:PSS was drop cast onto the back contact electrode to form the photocathode of the solar cell. This photocathode of the solar cell was sandwiched with the photoanode to fabricate a dye-sensitized solar cell. The iodide electrolyte was used as the hole transport layer or electrolyte medium. According to Putra and group, when PEDOT:PSS was used as CE in DSSC using Z907 as a dye, an efficiency of 1.7% was achieved with a V oc of 0.61 V and a J sc of 5 mA/cm2. The efficiency of the cells fabricated in this paper is higher since N719 dye was used during fabrication, unlike the Z907 dye used by Putra et al.

The best results in terms of charge carrier separation (V oc ), charge carrier generation (J sc ), and efficiency are observed for Pt, followed by AgNW and FTO while keeping all the other layers in the devices constant. The reason for this variation in efficiency is the work function of the back contact electrodes used. From Figure , it may be observed that the work function of the back contact electrode has a direct relationship with the efficiency of DSSC. In particular, the higher the work function of the back contact, the greater the efficiency of DSSC; therefore, Pt (work function of 5.5 eV) delivered the highest efficiency, followed by the silver nanowire (work function of 4.7 eV), and the lowest efficiency is shown by FTO (work function of 4.4 eV).

Figure illustrates the energy band diagram of the DSSC using different back contact electrodes. The semiconductor layer (TiO2) has a thickness of 20 μm, N719 is given a thickness of 0.2 μm, and PEDOT:PSS is given a thickness of 10 μm in SCAPS 1D. The thickness of each defined layer is observed in the shaded regions of Figure . The x axis indicates the distance of each layer from the front contact electrode, which corresponds to the thickness of each layer. In the energy band diagram, the red line corresponds to the valence band and the blue line corresponds to the conduction band. Near the back contact region, band bending is observed, which is caused by the interaction of p-type material (PEDOT:PSS) with a metal back contact electrode. The vertical red line in Figure b,c shows the start of the bands (conduction and valence) bending toward back electrodes. The significance of the band bending in this case relates to the work function of the back contact electrode. A higher work function results in less band bending near the back contact region, which improves the overall efficiency of the device. Less band bending decreases the chances of charge carrier accumulation at the back contact interface. Among the materials analyzed, Pt shows higher efficiency due to the absence of band bending at the back contact interface. This is followed by AgNW, which shows minimal band bending, and finally FTO shows prominent band bending because of charge carrier accumulation near the back contact interface.

The influence of temperature on the performance of DSSC was simulated using SCAPS 1D. The temperature was varied from 0 to 100 °C, and its influence on the efficiency, fill factor, and open circuit voltage of DSSC devices was analyzed as shown in Figure . The results revealed that temperature had adverse effects on the efficiency of both AgNW- and FTO-based back contact DSSCs. However, the Pt-coated FTO-based back contact DSSC displayed a positive correlation with an increase in temperature, which can be observed in Figure a. The fill factor showed a gradual increase from 0 to 60 °C but tended to decrease when the temperature was elevated to 100 °C for Pt- and AgNW-based DSSC, but for FTO-based DSSC, the fill factor increases in direct proportion to temperature, which is shown in Figure b. Moreover, all devices shared a plunge in open circuit voltage with increasing temperature, as shown in Figure c. From the above-mentioned results, we can interpret that for the AgNW-based back contact electrode, the ideal temperature is 0 °C. At elevated temperatures, the possibility of the recombination of charge carriers is high, hence there is a significant decrease in the efficiency of the device.

Cost analysis was conducted to relate the fabrication cost of a conventional Pt/FTO counter electrode to that of a PEDOT:PSS/AgNW counter electrode on plain glass. All costs were calculated based on the actual quantities of materials consumed during laboratory synthesis and normalized to cost per unit area. For the PEDOT:PSS/AgNW electrode, silver nanowires were synthesized via the polyol method using 200 mg of AgNO3, 294 mg of PVP (Mw ≈ 130 kMwt), 40 μL of NaCl solution, 20 μL of KBr solution, and ethylene glycol as the solvent. Based on commercial laboratory prices, the AgNO3 contribution corresponds to approximately 0.78 USD (US dollars), while the proportional costs of PVP, NaCl, KBr, and ethylene glycol are significantly smaller and together contribute less than 0.15 USD per batch. The AgNW film was deposited on a 2 cm × 2 cm plain glass substrate, followed by coating with PEDOT:PSS as a counter electrode. For PEDOT:PSS, the pricing was 252 USD per 100 g; assuming a typical coating volume of ∼100 μL (∼0.1 g) per 4 cm2 electrode, the PEDOT:PSS consumed was approximately 0.25 USD per device, normalized to the active area. The total fabrication cost of the PEDOT:PSS/AgNW counter electrode was estimated to be ∼0.4 USD cm–2.

For comparison, the platinum counter electrode was fabricated by depositing 200 μL of chloroplatinate onto an FTO-coated glass substrate, followed by thermal decomposition. Using a commercial price of 238 USD per 10 mL of chloroplatinic acid, the precursor cost for Pt deposition amounts to approximately 4.8 USD per device, while the FTO substrate contributes to ∼0.40 USD for a 2 cm × 2 cm active area. When normalized to area, the total fabrication cost of the Pt/FTO counter electrode was approximately 2 USD cm–2. This analysis validates that substituting Pt/FTO with PEDOT:PSS/AgNW on plain glass results in a considerable reduction in material cost without a significant compromise in device performance.

The electrocatalytic activity of the back contact electrodes was further evaluated through cyclic voltammetry (CV) using PEDOT:PSS-coated Pt, AgNW, and FTO substrates immersed in the iodide/triiodide electrolyte. The CV graphs (Figure ) reveal a clear trend in the catalytic performance that correlates strongly with the intrinsic work function of the electrode materials. Platinum, possessing the highest work function (∼5.5 eV), exhibits the most noticeable redox currents and the lowest overpotential for the I–/I3 – redox reaction, which indicates greater catalytic behavior. AgNW electrodes, with an intermediate work function (∼4.7 eV), demonstrated an enhanced catalytic response compared to FTO but lower than that of Pt. FTO, which has the lowest work function (∼4.4 eV), shows the weakest catalytic activity among the three. These results confirm that electrodes with higher work functions facilitate more efficient charge transfer to the electrolyte redox couple, thereby supporting the improved photovoltaic performance observed in devices employing AgNW and Pt back-contact electrodes.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) response of Pt, AgNW, and FTO back-contact electrodes coated with PEDOT:PSS and measured in the iodide/triiodide electrolyte.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed to compare the interfacial properties of the back contact electrode (FTO, Pt-deposited FTO and AgNW). The impedance spectra were fitted using a modified Randal circuit consisting of Rs in series, Cdl in parallel, and series connection of Rct and Wdiff as shown in the inset of Figure . FTO exhibits a series resistance (Rs ) of 1.23 Ω and a charge transfer resistance (Rct ) of 21.34 Ω as shown in Table , which indicates efficient charge transfer. Pt-deposited FTO shows increased Rs of 1.51 Ω compared to FTO and Rct of 24.21 Ω as shown in Table . However, AgNW displays a higher Rs of 4.19 Ω and a higher Rct of 70.41 due to the junction resistance in the nanowires’ percolation network. The Warburg diffusion parameters for AgNW were significantly lower than those for FTO, which indicates more efficient ion diffusion and mass transport characteristics. Additionally, the double-layer capacitance (Cdl ) of AgNW also was less than that for FTO. The Nyquist plot of AgNW as shown in Figure displayed a low-frequency vertical tail which signifies better capacitive behavior and interfacial polarization. The interconnect nanowire pathways in AgNW have more active surface area, which facilitates rapid electron transport while displaying efficient ionic accessibility. Therefore, AgNW displays competitive electrochemical performance and superior diffusion-control behaviors as well.

Conclusions

Silver nanowires are proposed as a suitable and cost-effective back contact electrode for DSSC application. Three different back contact electrodes were taken to prove the above statement, which were FTO, Pt-coated FTO, and AgNW. Each back contact electrode was placed in an identical DSSC, and the efficiency of the device was measured to be 2.46% for FTO, 5.3% for Pt-deposited FTO, and 4.2% for AgNW. To support these findings, a SCAPS 1D solar simulator was used for theoretical simulations, which showed similar efficiency for DSSCs fabricated using different back contact electrodes. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy further confirmed the improved charge transfer and diffusion behavior of AgNW compared to those of FTO, supporting its photovoltaic performance. The work function of the electrodes used has played a significant role in improving the efficiency of the DSSC. The work function for the back contact electrodes was 4.4 eV for FTO, 5.5 eV for PT, and 4.7 eV for AgNW. The higher work function of Pt contributed to its improved efficiency, while FTO’s lower work function limited the overall efficiency to just 2%. Additionally, the charge carrier accumulation near the back contact interface, as observed in SCAPS 1D, negatively impacted FTO’s efficiency. The AgNW-based back contact electrode showed an efficiency of 4.2%, which is closer to that of the Pt-based back contact with significantly better efficiency than for FTO since the work function of AgNW is higher than that of FTO. This study concludes that the AgNW-based back contact electrode can be used as a replacement for FTO, which is being used conventionally in DSSC to reduce its cost. Furthermore, the future outlook of this research supports the fact that AgNW has the potential to replace conventionally used ITO-PET (indium tin oxide-coated polyethylene terephthalate) electrodes which are used for flexible DSSC applications.
