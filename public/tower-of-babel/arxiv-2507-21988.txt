Properties of Algorithmic Information Distance
Marcus Hutter

Abstract
The domain-independent universal Normalized Information Distance based on Kolmogorov complexity has been (in approximate form) successfully applied to a variety of difficult clustering problems. In this paper we investigate theoretical properties of the un-normalized algorithmic information distance d K d_{K} . The main question we are asking in this work is what properties this curious distance has, besides being a metric. We show that many (in)finite-dimensional spaces can(not) be isometrically scale-embedded into the space of finite strings with metric d K d_{K} . We also show that d K d_{K} is not an Euclidean distance, but any finite set of points in Euclidean space can be scale-embedded into ( { 0 , 1 } ∗ , d K ) (\{0,1\}^{*},d_{K}) . A major contribution is the development of the necessary framework and tools for finding more (interesting) properties of d K d_{K} in future, and to state several open problems.



Keywords

Kolmogorov complexity; information distance; metric; isometric embedding; Euclid; Graphs; Hamming; Hilbert space.

Introduction

Metric spaces and embeddings.

Isomorphisms and isomorphic embeddings play an important role in all parts of mathematics:
to relate different mathematical structures or even mathematical sub-fields,
to reduce problems to each other,
to deepen our understanding, and of course for mathematical ‘economy’.
Isometries are isomorphisms between metric spaces that preserve distance.
An isometric embedding
\phi:{\cal V}\to{\cal W}
from a metric space
{\cal V}
with distance
d_{\cal V}

to a metric space
{\cal W}
with metric
d_{\cal W}
by definition
satisfies
d_{\cal W}(\phi(v),\phi(v^{\prime}))=d_{\cal V}(v,v^{\prime})
.
For instance, orthogonal transformations from
{\cal V}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
{\cal W}=\mathbb{R}^{m}

preserve the Euclidean distance
d_{\cal V}=d_{\cal W}=||\cdot||_{2}
.
There also exist universal metric spaces
({\cal U},d_{\cal U})
,
meaning
all
metric spaces can be isometrically embedded into
({\cal U},d_{\cal U})
.
Studying the relation between metric spaces,
and their additional properties has a long history
[
Fre10
,
Ury27
,
Hus08
,
GW85
]

and has even been featured in recreational/artistic work
[
ZXYL20
]
.

Euclidean spaces.

Euclidean spaces such as
(\mathbb{R}^{m},||\cdot||_{2})
for
m<\infty
and
their infinite-dimensional extension,
Hilbert spaces, for
m=\infty
(also called infinite-dimensional Euclidean spaces)
play a particularly important role.
Not all metric spaces are Euclidean.
So whether a metric has the Euclidean property, that is,
whether it can be isometrically embedded into
(\mathbb{R}^{m},||\cdot||_{2})
or a Hilbert space,
is one of the most natural and first questions to ask,
since a lot is known about Euclidean spaces,
and Euclidean spaces enjoy particularly nice properties.
For instance, unlike most other interesting metrics one can impose on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
e.g.
p
-norms, the Euclidean distance on
\mathbb{R}^{m}

has a very large automorphism group (isomorphisms onto itself).
An important application is in machine learning:
If
d(x,y)
measures some (abstract) distance between data points,
then
k_{\lambda}(x,y):=\exp(-\lambda d(x,y)^{2})

is a kernel which measures the similarity between
x
and
y
.

k_{\lambda}
is a positive definite kernel (
\forall\lambda>0
)
if and only if
d
can be isometrically embedded into some Euclidean
space (the kernel trick), which allows one to
apply linear methods for classification (such as SVMs)
or (e.g. logistic) regression in this Euclidean feature space.
Many algorithms have been developed to approximately map points from
a high-dimensional or abstract metric space into a lower-dimensional Euclidean space

[
Mat02
, Chp.15]
.
Not least, for
m\leq 3
this is our local space we live in,
so objects in
\mathbb{R}^{\leq 3}
have convenient representations
(see e.g. the popular
t
-SNE
[
VH08
]
algorithm).

Algorithmic information distance.

The algorithmic information distance
[
BGM+98
]

is a peculiar complexity metric
d_{K}

over the set of finite binary string
\mathbb{B}^{*}
.
It measures the distance of (an object encoded as) string
x

from (an object encoded as) string
y
in terms of the length
of the shortest computer program that transforms
x
into
y
and
y
into
x
.
If
x
and
y
share a lot of structure (in the extreme case
x=y
),
then
d_{K}(x,y)
is small. Otherwise it will be large(r),
e.g. if
x
is a piece of text and
y
is a digitized photo.
But if
y
is a photo of the text
x
,
d_{K}
would be smaller,
since
y
can be compressed with the help of
x

(and
x
is close to trivial given
y
, think OCR).
Most mathematically “natural” distances would not capture this relation.
The distance is universal in the sense that it minorizes most other distances
(like Hamming or Edit distance).
By normalizing it (NID)
[
LCL+04
]
and approximating it
by standard file compressors like gzip (NCD)
[
CV05
]
,
it has led to impressive applications including a completely automatic reconstruction
of the evolutionary tree of 24 mammals based on complete mtDNA,
of the classification tree of 52 languages based on the declaration of human rights,
of the phylogeny of SARS-CoV-2 virus
[
CV22
]
,
and various others
[
CV05
,
JWBL23
,
JYT+23
]
,
without using any domain-specific knowledge.
Its theoretical properties have barely been studied.
It has been shown that it is indeed (nearly) a metric,
and that it minorizes all other metrics with limited neighborhood size
[
LCL+04
, Sec.III]
.
Very recently
d_{K}
has been applied in kernel methods
[
HHO25
]
.
For instance, while we show that
d_{K}
is not Euclidean on all of
\mathbb{B}^{*}
,
it can accommodate any finite Euclidean point set,
so the kernel trick (see above) could be applied to kernel
\exp(-\lambda d_{K}^{2})
on such subspace.

This paper.

The goal of this paper is to initiate a deeper study of
the un-normalized complexity distance
d_{K}
.
We will encounter sufficient obstacles even for this somewhat simpler cousin of NID.
We expect many results to transfer in some form or another to NID,
but this is beyond the scope of this article.
It is known that
d_{K}
is also a metric and has a certain minorization property.

Besides the properties of
d_{K}
we were able to prove,
the main contribution of the paper is to provide the necessary framework and tools
for finding more (interesting) properties of
d_{K}
in future,
and to state some open problems, which hopefully stirs interest.

One way to study the properties of metric space
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}:=(\mathbb{B}^{*},d_{K})
,
is to find isometric embeddings from or into other metric spaces or into itself.
This is the approach we take in this paper.
By showing that
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}
isometrically embeds into some
({\cal V}^{*},d_{\cal V})
,
it will inherit any “positive” properties of the latter.
For instance, if we could isometrically embed
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}
into a Hilbert space,
we would have shown that
d_{K}
is Euclidean.
Conversely, if we isometrically embed some
({\cal V}^{*},d_{\cal V})
into
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}
,
the latter would inherit any “negative” property of the former.
Indeed, we will embed a non-Euclidean space into
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}
,
showing that
d_{K}
is
not
Euclidean. On the other hand,
this does not preclude that
d_{K}
is Euclidean on some sub-spaces of
\mathbb{B}^{*}
.
Possibly
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}
is a universal
\langle
qualifier
\rangle
metric space,
where
\langle
qualifier
\rangle
has yet to be determined,
and could include/embed rich Euclidean and non-Euclidean spaces.

Definition 1

(Kernels)

A set

equipped with some function

The kernel usually possesses further properties, e.g. some of

(P)

Non-negative:

(Z)

Zero on diagonal:

(N)

Non-zero off-diagonal:

(S)

(TI)

(PD)

Positive Definite:
\forall m\in\mathbb{N}~\forall x_{1},...,x_{m}\in{\cal X}~\forall c_{1},...,c_{m}\in\mathbb{R}:\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}k(x_{i},x_{j})\geq 0\hskip-10.00002pt

\forall m\in\mathbb{N}~\forall x_{i}\in{\cal X}~\forall c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}

\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}=0\Rightarrow\sum_{i,j}c_{i}c_{j}k(x_{i},x_{j})\leq 0

(E)

\exists\Phi:{\cal X}\to H\mskip-3.0mu

Inner product: If

.

Most definitions require

If

We sometimes call a metric ‘proper’ to emphasize that it has none of the defects above.
The word ‘distance’ is used inconsistently in the literature,
so we will only use it generically if some of the first 5 properties hold.

Compression distance.

There are many different distances, suitable for different applications.
A natural quest is for

One such distance is based on Kolmogorov complexity,

(Complexity metric

\displaystyle d_{K}^{\vee}(x,z)~

\displaystyle\equiv~\max\{K(x|z),K(z|x)\}+c

\displaystyle\leq~\max\{K(x|y)+K(y|z),~K(z|y)+K(y|x)\}+c_{U}+c

Scale-Isometric Embeddings

Property-preserving (isomorphic) embeddings are important tools in mathematics,
e.g. to reduce problems in one domain to another,
especially isometric embeddings.
Consider, for instance,
the Frechet embedding
[

, p.162]
, a precursor of Kuratowski’s embedding:

Proposition 6

(Every separable metric space iso-embeds into
\ell_{\infty}
)

\ell_{p}:=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}:||v||_{p}<\infty\}
for some

is the vector space of infinite sequences with finite

.
More generally, with

for

with metric

.

Proof.
Let
({\cal X},d)
be a separable metric space. Let
{\cal X}^{\prime}:=\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},...\}
be a countable dense subset of
{\cal X}
.
Consider embedding
\phi:{\cal X}\to\mathbb{R}^{\infty}
with
\phi(x)_{i}:=d(x,x_{i})-d(x_{0},x_{i})
.
Let

and

a (sub)sequence converging to

for

,
which exists, since
{\cal X}^{\prime}
is dense in

.
Then

Random Scale-Embeddings

In this section we exploit the close relationship
between data compression and probabilistic modelling:
The optimal expected code-length of a data source
X\sim P

is its entropy
H(P)=H(X)
.
Even more, with high probability,
the optimal code length

.
We exploit this and define scale-embeddings into string-valued random variables.
We then show that every random scale-embedding can be derandomized,
and also prove a limited converse.
We also prove a product space theorem for random scale-embeddings for
||\cdot||_{1}
.
As an example application, combining all allows us
to extend the scale-embedding of
([0,1]^{m},||\cdot||_{1})
into
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}

from

to
m>1
.

MDL bound and randomness deficiency.
For any probability mass function
P:\mathbb{B}^{*}\to[0,1]
,
we can encode

[
HQC24

On the other hand, the set of strings that can be compressed significantly better than that
has small

be any string-valued random variable,
then the randomness deficiency

[
HQC24
, 2.7.29]

Together we have that with high

Then

for any collection of

Theorem 20

(Product scale-embeddings into

)

Theorem 22

(General randomization)

Let
x_{1},...,x_{m}\in\mathbb{B}^{*}
be a collection of strings,
and
x_{I}:=(x_{i})_{i\in I}
for
I\subseteq M:=\{1,...,m\}
be a sub-collection, and similarly
x_{J}
.
Let
{\cal I}\subseteq 2^{M}
be a collection of such
I
that includes
M
.
Then there exist random variables
Z_{1},...,Z_{m}

such that
|{\log}P[Z_{I}|Z_{J}]+K(x_{I}|x_{J})|\mathrel{\hbox{\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 6.02773pt\hbox to7.7778pt{\hfil$\scriptstyle+$\hfil}\crcr\lower 0.43057pt\hbox{$\leq$}\crcr}}}}}(2.2|{\cal I}|+5.5){\log}s+2.2K({\cal I})
for all
\omega\in\Omega
,
where
s:=K(x_{M})
, simultaneously for all
I,J
for which
I\cup J,J\in{\cal I}
.
If
J
is empty, then the condition
Z_{\emptyset}=\emptyset

and
x_{\emptyset}=\emptyset
can be dropped.
In particular
P[Z_{I}|Z_{J}]
is approximately uniform.

The basic idea of the proof is to construct a uniform distribution

P[Z_{M}=z_{M}]=1/|A|

\forall z_{M}\in A
and
0
otherwise,
where
A\subset(\mathbb{B}^{*})^{m}
is the set of all
z_{M}
such that

for the desired

and then show that
P(z_{I}|z_{J})\approx 2^{-K(x_{I}|x_{J})}
.

In
[
HRSV00
]
, a superficially related but actually quite different question is asked:
Do all Shannon-inequalities also hold for Kolmogorov complexity and vice vera.
The flavor is: If some statement is true for
all

is it then also true for all random variables.
As an example
K(x)+K(y)\gtrsim K(x,y)

holds for all strings and
H(X)+H(Y)\geq H(X,Y)
holds for all random variables.
On the other hand,
we
are asking whether for a particular set of strings

with
particular non-universal (metric

) properties,
there are corresponding random variables with the same (metric
D_{vv^{\prime}}^{s}
or

) property,
the reverse of
Theorem

19

Nevertheless many ideas carry over.

Theorem

22
strengthens/extends
[
SUV17
, Thm.211]

First, they use

More importantly, they only impose

,
which suffices to prove their results for set sizes (projections and

),
but not for showing that all slices have the same size, which we need for

.

Proof.

Let
x_{M}
be a fixed collections of strings.
Note that
2^{-O(1)}=2^{-K(\epsilon)}<\sum_{z\in\mathbb{B}^{*}}2^{-K(z)}<1

Let
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{=}}
denote “equal within
\pm c{\log}s\pm O(1)
” for complexities
and “equal within
\times 2^{\pm c{\log}s\pm O(1)}

may depend on further parameters like

.
With this notation,
K(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1.1}}{{=}}K(x|y)+K(y)
.

A_{b}
:

Let
{\cal I}\subset 2^{M}
be some collection of
I
that
includes
I\cup J
and
J
and

.
Define

\displaystyle A_{b}~

\displaystyle:=~\{z_{M}:K(z_{I})\leq K(x_{I})-b~\forall I\in{\cal I}\}~~~\text{for}~~~b\geq 0

\displaystyle|A_{b}|~

\displaystyle\leq~|\{z_{M}:K(z_{M})\leq K(x_{M})-b\}|~\leq~2^{K(x_{M})-b}

by Kraft inequality. On the other hand,

,
so we can encode

Ultimately we are interested in

(11)

7
Conclusion

We investigated properties of the complexity distance
d_{K}

over the space of binary strings
\mathbb{B}^{*}
,
defined as the symmetrized conditional Kolmogorov complexity
K(x|y)

(with some additive correction to make it a proper metric).
We did so by asking which other metric spaces can be scale-embedded into
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}
.
For instance, we showed that
([0,1]^{m},||\cdot||_{1})
does so for
m<\infty
but does not for
m=\infty
.
We also showed that
d_{K}
is not Euclidean,
but many questions have been left open, e.g. whether
d_{K}
raised to some small power is Euclidean.
We do not even know whether all finite metric spaces can be scale-embedded into
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}
.

Apart from solving the stated

12
,
15
,
21
,
29
,
37
and
39

in the text, especially the grand
12
,
there are many related questions we have not touched:
Natural is to consider the
Normalized
version of the Information/Compression Distance (NID/NCD),
which enjoyed great practical success
[
CV05
]
.
We could ask whether
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}
possesses any of the other properties some classical metrics possess,
for instance whether it is convex or geodesic,
provided these notions can be suitably adapted to make sense in
\mathbb{B}_{K}^{*}
.
Since every metric induces a topology, we could also ask about the weaker topological properties.
We could also relax the conditions for and hence easing scale-isometries: The weakest condition
that keeps the sprit of
\phi_{s}
being asymptotically exact would be to only require that

d_{K}(\phi_{s}(v),\phi_{s}(v^{\prime}))/s
converges to
d(v,v^{\prime})
for
s\to\infty
,
or any notion between these two extremes.
If convergence is non-uniform in
v,v^{\prime}
, this would imply that for each fixed
s
,
there are
v,v^{\prime}
for which the accuracy is arbitrarily bad, so this notion may be of limited interest.
There is a close connection between Shannon entropy and Kolmogorov complexity
[
HQC24
, Tab.2.22]
,
for instance, all linear inequalities that hold for one also hold for the other
[
HRSV00
