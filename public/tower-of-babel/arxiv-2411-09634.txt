Lowest-radiation environments in the Solar System: New opportunities for underground rare-event searches
Xilin Zhang, Jason Detwiler, Clint Wiseman

Abstract
We study neutrino, muon, and gamma-ray fluxes in extraterrestrial environments in our Solar System via semi-analytical estimates and Monte Carlo simulations. In sites with negligible atmosphere, we find a strong reduction in the cosmic-ray-induced neutrino and muon fluxes relative to their intensities on Earth. Neutrinos with energies between 50 MeV and 100 TeV show particularly strong suppression, by as much as 10 3 , even at shallow depths. The solar neutrino suppression increases as the square of the site’s distance from the Sun. Natural radiation due to nuclear decay is also expected to be lower in many of these locations and may be reduced to effectively negligible levels in liquid water environments. The sites satisfying these characteristics represent an opportunity for greatly extending the physics reach of underground searches in fundamental physics, such as searches for WIMP Dark Matter, neutrinoless double-beta decay, the diffuse supernova neutrinos, and neutrinos from nearby supernova. As a potential near-term target, we propose a measurement of muon and gamma-ray fluxes in an accessible underground lunar site such as the Mare Tranquillitatis Pit to perform a first measurement of the prompt component in cosmic-ray-induced particle production, and to constrain lunar evolution models. Although the focus of this work is on fundamental physics, the finding of this work – that the existence of an atmosphere in a planet enhances cosmic-ray-induced radiation in its underground – could be relevant for a broader range of sciences, such as radiobiology deep underground and searches for extraterrestrial life.

Neutrinos and energetic muons created by cosmic rays impinging on matter can penetrate deep underground and generate background in rare-event experiments.
The cosmic-ray induced neutrino flux is accompanied by fluxes from other terrestrial and astrophysical sources, including solar, reactor, and geological (anti)neutrinos and supernovae.
Most cosmic ray muons and neutrinos are produced far above the Earth’s surface, from the decays of short-lived particles generated in hadronic showers, seeded by interactions of primary cosmic rays (mostly protons) with gas molecules in the Earth’s upper atmosphere
[
39
]
.
Over most of the energy range that poses background for rare-event searches, the primary production mechanism is from decay-in-flight (DIF) of the charged
\pi
and
K
mesons.
At the highest energies, an additional “prompt” component from the rapid decay of ultra-short-lived particles such as charmed hadrons (collectively labeled as
\phi_{c}
here) is expected to dominate.
Production of these charmed hadrons by protons is not typically simulated by toolkits like Geant4, so we rely on our semi-analytic estimate of their contribution to the neutrino and muon fluxes.
Below 50 MeV, an additional mechanism is the decay-at-rest (DAR) of positive mesons and muons that are not captured on nuclei.

\phi_{c}
refers to charmed hadrons and similar ultra-short-lived particles that can contribute significantly at very high primary cosmic ray energies.

The presence of a significant contribution to the muon and neutrino fluxes from the meson DIF channel in the atmosphere arises from the fact that in a diffuse gas there is a wide energy range over which these particles’ decay lengths are much shorter than the interaction length or the stopping distance, as illustrated in Fig.
2
.
In environments with no atmosphere, one thus expects qualitatively different secondary cosmic ray production.
For primary cosmic rays impinging directly on a solid object with density on the order 1
\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
, the mesons produced in the hadronic showers have interaction lengths and stopping distances much shorter than the decay length.
The DIF muons and neutrinos are thus strongly suppressed in these environments.
At low energy, a strong component from the DAR of the positive mesons remains.
Muons and neutrinos generated by the prompt DIF of ultra-short-lived particles (
\phi_{c}
) are not expected to be suppressed in environments without atmosphere since the decay lengths of these particles are much smaller than their interaction lengths or stopping distances even in a solid.
As a result, the underground fluxes of the highest energy muons and neutrinos are expected to be largely independent of the atmospheric conditions above ground.

The physics of muon and neutrino production in gases versus solids is well established.
For example, the ability of dense materials to stop pions is the principle behind the pulsed DAR neutrino source that enabled the first detection of coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering by COHERENT
[

The MiniBooNE experiment used these same DIF and DAR channels to search for exotic Dark Matter

A recent paper

Since energetic

In the following sections, we present detailed calculations and Monte Carlo simulations of how these muon and neutrino fluxes differ in environments with differing amounts of atmosphere.
We consider three primary simulated environments; Earth, Moon, and the ice ball.
We also qualitatively discuss secondary environments.

III
Semi-analytic estimates for energetic particle production

The formalism for estimating secondary cosmic ray particle fluxes in gases and solids is well established.
In particular, the gaseous case is discussed in detail in Ref.
[
39
]
.
These energetic particles, including the secondary mesons and the leptons to which the mesons decay, move effectively collinearly.
The changes in their fluxes
N_{i}
(particles per unit area, per unit solid angle, per unit energy) along their shared trajectory can thus be described using the following set of coupled equations:

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{i}(E_{i},X)}{dX}=

\displaystyle-\frac{N_{i}(E_{i},X)}{\lambda_{i}}-\frac{N_{i}(E_{i},X)}{d_{i}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j}\int_{E}^{\infty}\,dE_{j}\frac{F_{ji}(E_{i},E_{j})}{E_{i}}\frac{N_{j}(E_{j},X)}{\lambda_{j}}\,.

(1)

This balances the production of new particles from all available channels (source terms), with their removal via decay or absorption (sink terms) as they traverse matter.
Here,
X
is the column depth (distance multiplied by the target mass density),
i
and
j
refer to particle types,
\lambda_{i}
is particle
i
’s absorption length, and
d_{i}
is its decay length.
The term
F_{ji}
represents the production distribution of species
i
from parent
j
.

In our semi-analytic calculations, we numerically solve these equation systems in various environments. A complete discussion of the formalism is given in Appendix
B
, including a list of the main symbols and numerical values used (Table
3
). We consider nucleons, charged pions, and kaons to estimate the muon and neutrino fluxes.
The neutral mesons mostly decay into photons that are absorbed in the targets and are thus neglected in the calculation. Leptons and their anti-particles are not differentiated unless specified otherwise. We compute only
\nu_{\mu}
flavor of neutrinos since the
\nu_{e}
flux is much smaller than the
\nu_{\mu}
at high energies in both gases
[
39
]
and solids. For the same reasons, the neutral kaons, in particular
K_{L}
, are neglected here, although they are important for estimating high energy
\nu_{e}
fluxes (but not for
\mu
and
\nu_{\mu}
).

Our semi-analytic calculation focuses on the neutrino energy range above 1 GeV.
At lower energies, processes like particle stopping, which are not included in the calculations, begin to play a major role.
Moreover, solar modulations and geomagnetic fields filter out the cosmic rays with energy below
\sim
10 GeV and thus reduce the secondary lepton fluxes around
\sim
1 GeV
[
39
]

We also assume the neutrino mean free path to be infinitely long in matter, which breaks down when the neutrino energy is high enough. For example, the mean free path length inside Earth could be reduced to Earth’s diameter when the energy is on the order of
10^{5}
GeV (see Fig. 8.5 in Ref.
[
39
]
).
Such energy thresholds are even higher for the Moon and other objects with smaller sizes.
In these situations, our results could overestimate the direction-integrated neutrino flux by a factor of two or less due to counting the neutrinos that get absorbed before they could emerge, if possible at all, from deep underground.

In addition to the production channels described above, there is also the prompt contribution to the lepton fluxes due to the decays of ultra-short-lived particles such as charmed hadrons. As briefly mentioned in Sec.
II

IV
Simulation of secondary neutrino and muon production

The semi-analytic calculations described in the previous section are accurate for modeling high-energy cosmic ray fluxes where DIF dominates.
At low energies where DAR dominates, various physics effects, including particles being scattered off into different directions, mesons’ energy loss, and their absorption by surrounding nuclei with nontrivial energy dependence, are highly non-negligible but either not included or not correctly represented in Eq. (
1
).
To account for these, and to compare against established results, we performed Monte Carlo simulations of primary cosmic rays impinging on celestial bodies using Geant4
[
65
,
66
,
67
]
.
We use Geant4 v4.10.08 with the NuBeam physics list for accurate neutrino production
[
68
,
69
]
.
NuBeam is based on the “FTFP_BERT” physics list, which models hadronic interactions below a few GeV with the Bertini intranuclear cascade model and higher-energy interactions using the Fritiof parton model, supplemented with a compound model to de-excite the final-state nucleus.
NuBeam uses slightly modified energy ranges over which the Bertini or Fritiof models are invoked, and for high-energy protons above 100 GeV, it switches to a quark-gluon string model.
We emphasize that this physics list is not expected to accurately model (if at all) the generation of very short-lived particles (represented by
\phi_{c}
in Fig.
1
) that generate the highest-energy muons and neutrinos in the underground flux.
Thus, while we expect the simulations to give a more accurate picture at low energies, we expect them to fall below the semi-analytic calculations at high energies.

Planetary geometries were implemented using the Geometry Description Markup Language (GDML)
[
70
]
, consisting of concentric spherical geometric shells with appropriately chosen composition and density.
The Earth, the Moon, and ice balls with radii 100 m, 300 m, and 1 km were simulated.
The ice ball geometry was modeled as a simple sphere of solid water ice of density 0.9 g/cm
3
.

The interior of the Earth was modeled following the Preliminary Reference Earth Model
[
71
]
, except that the average density of the upper crust was changed from 2.6 g/cm
3
to 2.83 g/cm
3
to account for the fact that physics experiments are performed within the continental crust.
The atmosphere was modeled following the U.S. Standard Atmosphere (1976)
[
72
]
.

For the lunar interior, we used Ref.
[
73
]
.
The crust is mostly plagioclase, modeled here as an even mixture of CaAl
2
Si
2
O
8
and NaAlSi
3
O
8
, with a
\sim
30% admixture of Al
2
O
3
. We include a 7.5-m-thick regolith on the surface of the moon composed of crust material at nearly half its density
[
74
]
.
The upper mantle comprises orthopyroxene, modeled here as an even mixture of MgSiO
3
and FeSiO
3
.
The olivine lower mantle is modeled as an even mixture of Mg
2
SiO
4
and Fe
2
SiO
4
. The middle mantle is taken to be an even mixture of orthopyroxene and olivine.
Like the Earth, the moon’s core is assumed to be 90% Fe and 10% Ni.

Primary event generation was performed starting from an incident primary cosmic ray proton.
The primary cosmic ray differential flux energy spectrum was taken from the so-called local interstellar spectra (LIS) in which the cosmic ray flux suppression at an energy below 10 GeV due to the solar magnetic field is neglected
[
75
]
.
At higher energy, the flux scales as
E^{-(\gamma+1)}
.
Although that spectrum falls steeply with energy, the neutrino and muon production mechanisms increase sharply with energy.
To achieve reasonable statistics for the most important processes, primary protons were generated with an energy spectrum flat in
\ln(E)
between 0.3 GeV and 30 TeV, and results were re-weighted to correspond to the primary differential flux spectrum.
The upper limit on the energy range was chosen to avoid excessive computation times and memory overruns in the simulations.
The results are essentially unchanged when the lower limit is pushed to lower energies.
Details on the weighting factors are given in Appendix
A
.

Since we are interested in both muons and neutrinos, it was important to simulate a flux of primary protons impinging isotropically on the body of interest.
Performing a simulation of cosmic rays impinging isotropically on a planet with a single detector location beneath its surface is highly inefficient since most cosmic rays will not reach the detector location.
So, as illustrated in Fig.
5
, we instead simulated a uniform flux of primary protons normal to a disk of radius larger than that of the planet (including its atmosphere) and used diagnostic spherical shells at different depths inside the planet to record the fluxes of secondary particles that traverse them.
The spherical symmetry of the planetary geometry then allows each traversal, after multiplication by an appropriate weighting factor, to contribute to the statistics for the corresponding depth.
This method is inaccurate at low statistics because the traversals in a particular simulated event are correlated.
However, such correlations are negligible at the high statistics required to capture the essential low-energy features of the cosmic ray secondaries while matching to the semi-analytic expressions at higher energy.

For each geometry,
10^{5}
primaries were generated, and double-different flux of each lepton flavor was computed as a function of depth.
At these statistics, tertiary production, e.g., of muons generated by atmospheric neutrinos, is negligible.
For neutrinos, 3-flavor neutrino oscillation probabilities were applied using the current best-fit parameters in the PDG
[
64
]
.

The simulations were benchmarked successfully against measurements and expressions derived for the Earth, found in Ref.
[
39
]
.
We find that the total cosmic ray muon fluxes at the surface of the Earth are reproduced to within 30–40%, a surprising level of agreement given the approximations used and the exponential showering and attenuation processes involved between the primary proton interactions in the upper atmosphere and the propagation of interacting daughters through the atmosphere.
The simulations also reproduce the expected energy and angular distributions well at the surface and depth.

In Fig.
3
, we plot the simulation results for neutrinos alongside the semi-analytic computations at different sites.
As expected, the Moon simulations reproduce the expected low-energy spectral features due to muon and pion decay that are not included in the semi-analytic calculations, but do not capture the prompt component that is prominent at high energy.
The simulations also fall below the semi-analytic calculations above 1 TeV due to the absence in the simulations of contributions from primary cosmic rays with energies above 30 TeV.
Nevertheless, between 1 GeV and 1 TeV the two computations show good agreement, so we use them to derive the results and conclusions below.
For the Earth’s (atmospheric) neutrino flux, the semi-analytic estimates and the result from Ref.
[
58
]
all agree with the simulations.
However, when the energy is below 1 GeV, the simulation result is above the result from Ref.
[
58
]
.
This is presumably because our simulations rely on the LIS primary spectrum
[
75
]
, which does not account for the suppression of the primary cosmic ray flux due to solar modulation and geomagnetic field.

In Fig.
4
, we compare the underground muon flux versus depth for the Earth, the Moon, and the ice ball.
We find the simulation reproduces shallow fluxes well but begin to fall below the semi-analytic calculations at greater depths.
The same over-attenuation may be responsible for the suppressed total muon flux at the surface of Earth.
This is not unexpected; simulating the exponential attenuation of penetrating cosmic rays is notoriously difficult, even when starting from the measured surface cosmic ray spectrum, and often ad-hoc renormalizations are required (see, for example,
[
76
]
).
Considering that we have initiated our simulations from primary protons in the upper atmosphere on Earth or from the surface of the Moon or ice ball, this level of agreement in the absolute flux—within a factor of
\sim
2 for depths of experimental interest—is relatively good.

V
Natural Gamma Radiation

To achieve the low backgrounds required for rare event searches, fundamental physics experiments typically must shield against natural radioactivity in the surrounding material.
Chief among these is the gamma emissions of the
238
U and
232
Th decay chain species, as well as
40
K.
Other contributors include
(\alpha,n)
reactions from these same decay chains and cosmogenic activation products with long half-lives.
We assume here that experimental shielding design will be driven, like it is on Earth, primarily by the need to reduce U/Th/K gamma radiation.
The investigation of the other components is left for future work.
Natural backgrounds can also take the form of neutrino emissions by U/Th/K species, however we assume here those levels to be negligible; in fact, observing them, along with the gamma ray fluxes themselves, would provide important inputs for planetary formation and composition science.

With half-lives much longer than the age of the Earth,
238
U,
232
Th, and
40
K were present in the primordial material from which the planets and other solar system objects were born.
Chondritic meteorites are thought to be formed by direct accretion of that primordial material.
Thus, the concentrations of these species can be taken as expected average levels throughout the solar system.
A meta-analysis in
[
77
]
gives chondritic concentrations of 7.4 ppb U, 29 ppb Th, and 550 ppm (elemental) K (the abundance of
40
K in K is 0.012%).

The different evolution histories of various celestial bodies will result in varying primitive concentrations of these species, but perhaps not by many orders of magnitude.
A sophisticated estimate of these and other elements in the primitive Earth, incorporating chemical and petrological modeling as well as seismic data, gives a bulk K concentration that is just half of that in chondritic meteorites, while U and Th concentrations are estimated to be around three times higher than their chondritic values
[
77
]
.
However, subsequent processes can lead to significant variation. For example, in the Earth, these elements are excluded from its liquid iron core; convection and geochemical processes result in migration from the mantle to the crust, where concentrations reach 3 ppm U, 10 ppm Th, and 3% K
[
78
]
.
Similar processes occurred on the moon and Mars, but presumably to a lesser extent.
Assays of lunar surface rocks retrieved by Apollo missions give concentrations that are lower than values on the Earth by a factor of 7 for U/Th, and by a factor of 50 for K
[
79
]
.
Likewise, investigations of heat-producing elements on Mars based on meteorite data, gamma-ray measurements, and seismic data estimate concentrations of 0.2-0.4 ppm U, 0.9-1.4 ppm Th, and 0.5-0.8%K in the Martian crust
[
80
]
.

Concentrations of radioisotopes in water require special consideration.
Levels of
238
U in seawater are on the order of 3 ppm, similar to levels in the crust.
Th, however, is not soluble in water and settles to the ocean bottom, leaving residual levels of
232
Th on the order of 100 ppt
[
81
]
.
And while the upper
238
U chain remains intact, its decay proceeds through other species, including Th, resulting in a strongly broken secular equilibrium.
Measurements of
226
Ra, which supports the energetic gamma decay isotope
214
Bi that is a ubiquitous background in rare event searches, indicate concentrations on the order of 0.04 ppq near the ocean surface,
and higher by a factor of 2-4 near the ocean floor
[
82
]
.
This is on the order of
10^{4}
times less than what would be expected from secular equilibrium with
238
U.
The lower
238
U chain (
210
Pb and below) could be out of equilibrium with the mid-chain isotopes.
However, the radiation in the lower
238
U chain is typically from alpha or low-energy beta decays, which can be stopped with modest shielding.
Finally, K has a natural concentration in seawater of 0.04%
[
83
]
, corresponding to 48 ppb
40
K.

Purification of water to ultrapure (18.2 M
\Omega
) levels (
<
0.1 ppt U/Th, 40 ppt K)
[
84
]
is possible via processes such as distillation, reverse osmosis, filtration, and degassing, and has been achieved for very large volumes of water for example by the Super-Kamiokande Collaboration
[
85
]
.
With a recirculation rate of 30 t/h, Super-Kamiokande measured residual Rn levels of just 0.2-2 mBq/m
3

[
86
]
, indicating that the purification process was achieved without significant injection of Rn or its supporting Ra parent.
Thus, in principle, radioactive backgrounds can be purified from a liquid water environment to negligible levels.

We discuss each site of interest in the following.

The moon represents a great opportunity for fundamental physics measurements.
Not only is it the closest celestial body with no atmosphere, as mentioned earlier, but it is also confirmed to have existing lava tubes with overburdens on the order of 100 m of rock.
An experiment could thus be fielded without significant excavation, a major advantage.
Atmospheric neutrino backgrounds would be much lower than those on Earth.
At low energy, a few 10s of MeV, the

Depending on the strength of the prompt contribution, the muon flux will be equivalent to or even lower than those of the deepest laboratories on Earth.

Simultaneously measuring gamma-ray fluxes in the environment would also provide an interesting cross-check of lunar surface rock radioassays.

Asteroids represent the nearest bodies that offer a significant reduction in solar neutrino fluxes by factors of 2-25 depending on their solar orbital radius.
Their density and size show a large range of variation, but muon and low/mid-energy neutrino flux suppression factors between those of the Moon and our ice ball geometry can be expected.
For gamma radiation, one anticipates levels similar to that of the primordial Earth, offering roughly two orders of magnitude reduction relative to the Earth’s crust.
We also note that the low gravity on asteroids makes excavation much less energy-intensive than on Earth.
A preliminary mission, perhaps piggybacking on a private-sector endeavor, to measure muon and gamma-ray fluxes would have substantial scientific value.

A surprise finding is that Europa potentially provides a significant reduction for all cosmic and gamma ray fluxes relative to the Earth, provided one can bore through its
\sim

Drilling into ice on scales beyond 1 km has at least already been achieved by the IceCube Collaboration

A detector submersed in the liquid water just below the crust would have suppressed cosmic ray neutrino fluxes similar to those on the moon.
Muon fluxes would be at Europa’s muon floor, roughly twice that of the Moon’s and much lower than levels achievable on Earth.
The gamma radiation from U and Th would be reduced by many orders, leaving a dominant contribution from
40
K that is still two orders of magnitude lower than that on Earth.
Solar neutrinos would be suppressed by a factor of 25.
As a distant body containing liquid water, there is a very exciting possibility that Europa harbors life, implying an opportunity for piggyback missions in astrobiology, but accompanied by stringent sterilization requirements so as not to contaminate the pristine Europa ecosystem.

Rhea, like Europa, is distant from the sun and has an icy composition.
While Rhea offers an additional factor of four lower solar neutrino background over Europa, it is most likely made of a mixture of rock and ice, and does not appear to harbor a large liquid water volume below its surface.
It is thus unlikely that boring can be achieved by simply melting the ice, and the gamma ray fluxes are likely to be similar to those of asteroids.

Comets are thought to be composed mostly of frozen ammonia, methane, carbon dioxide, and water mixed with rock, and thus would provide a composition perhaps similar to Rhea’s in terms of boring technique, but likely with improved natural radiation levels.
A comet with a highly elliptical orbit could potentially transport an experiment much further from the sun, achieving a much lower solar neutrino flux.
A comet-based science endeavor could piggyback on a mining mission.
The demonstration of the ability to harvest water from a comet could simultaneously achieve commercial goals while laying the groundwork for a future ice ball endeavor.
However, highly elliptical comets are naturally volatile environments, and one would need to deal with instabilities and coma generation while the comet is near the sun.

Should water harvesting prove viable, one can imagine subsequently purifying the water and using it to fill a giant balloon surrounding an experimental apparatus and then slingshotting the apparatus into a highly elliptical orbit.
A reflective balloon material would help minimize the impact of solar radiation, and the water would freeze on its own before the balloon reaches the frost line on its way to the edges of the solar system.
Such an ice ball would provide all of the advantages of Europa in terms of low cosmic neutrino fluxes, but with potentially greatly reduced solar neutrino and gamma ray fluxes, at the cost of a somewhat higher muon flux.
In addition to its scientific value, such an object would also serve as a water reservoir in outer space, a potential asset.

Finally, although this study is focused on implications for rare event search experiments, its counterintuitive central finding that the presence of an atmosphere enhances cosmic-ray-induced radiation underground, could be important to studies outside of physics, such as the search for extraterrestrial life
[
89
]
and underground radiobiology
[
90

91

92
]

For example, radiobiology studies are being performed in various terrestrial underground laboratories to take advantage of their low level of radiation; we therefore expect this work to be potentially relevant there.

VIII
Conclusions

We have investigated solar neutrino, cosmic neutrino, cosmic muon, and natural gamma-ray fluxes in various extraterrestrial sites of scientific interest.
Our results for suppression factors in these fluxes over those of underground laboratories on Earth are summarized in Table
2
.
A combination of semi-analytical calculations and Monte Carlo simulations were employed to understand the cosmic-ray-induced neutrino and muon fluxes.
We achieved good agreement between these calculations at medium neutrino energy ranges and shallow depths.
The Monte Carlo simulations helped us understand the low-energy neutrino and muon fluxes that can be dominated by decay-at-rest signatures in bodies without atmosphere.
The semi-analytical approach provided a handle on the prompt component contributions that are missing in the simulation physics.

Generally speaking, sites without atmosphere show a suppression of cosmic neutrino fluxes on the order of
10^{3}
–
10^{4}
in the mid-
E
range.
At the low-
E
region, the total neutrino flux is not substantially suppressed, but we expect
\bar{\nu}_{e}
flux to be suppressed by a factor of
\sim
10 after including neutrino flavor oscillation.
The underground muon fluxes in these environments are strongly suppressed as well.
For example, 100 m deep in the moon, the muon flux is similar to that at a depth of 1 km in the Earth.
At the deepest depths, the muon fluxes approach a neutrino-induced floor.
Again, this minimum is drastically reduced at sites without atmosphere, e.g., by a factor of
\sim
1000 if we compare the Moon to Earth.
The natural radiation at most extraterrestrial sites also shows promising potential reductions compared to that on the Earth. We found that it can be reduced to essentially negligible levels, particularly in water environments. Distant bodies or bodies in highly elliptical orbits can also provide greatly reduced solar neutrino fluxes simply due to the
1/r^{2}
scaling of that flux.

In these environments, significant sensitivity improvements can be achieved for rare-event searches such as Dark Matter direct detection and neutrinoless double-beta decay, for which those fluxes pose problematic backgrounds.
We also note that lower natural radioactivity implies greatly reduced gamma ray shielding requirements.
Of the sites considered, Jupiter’s moon Europa, along with a human-made ice ball in a highly elliptic orbit emerged as the two most potentially advantageous sites for scientific discovery.

Considering the obvious challenges of performing rare detection experiments in such sites, we propose a successive approach.
A near-term project could measure the muon and gamma-ray fluxes in Lunar lava tubes by piggybacking on current Moon exploration efforts.
Such a mission would discover and constrain the prompt component of cosmic-ray-induced particle production, which is important for particle physics and astrophysics, and could also measure gamma radiation to constrain planetary evolution models.
Science missions performed jointly with asteroid or comet mining expeditions could provide similar important measurements for those bodies, while simultaneously demonstrating drilling and water harvesting technologies that could enable future missions.

\displaystyle C

\nu_{\mu}

\mu

Then, we discuss

\nu_{\mu}

\mu

The

\mu

\nu_{\mu}
-induced
\mu
production provides the lowest
\mu
flux underground that cannot be reduced further by going deeper.
In the end, we discuss the neutrino and muon fluxes in the Moon and Mars.
We emphasize that the approach only applies to energetic particles, e.g.
\nu_{\mu}
and

The discussions for fluxes in the gas media mostly follow Chapters 5, 6, and 8 from Ref.

B.1
Charged pions and kaons

The master equation system (also see Eq. (
1
)) for computing particle fluxes
N_{i}(E_{i},X)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{i}(E_{i},X)}{dX}=

\displaystyle-\frac{N_{i}(E_{i},X)}{\lambda_{i}}-\frac{N_{i}(E_{i},X)}{d_{i}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j}\int_{E}^{\infty}\,dE_{j}\frac{F_{ji}(E_{i},E_{j})}{E_{i}}\frac{N_{j}(E_{j},X)}{\lambda_{j}}\,,

(12)

where
E
is energy,
X
is the particle’s track length multiplied by the medium mass density (see Table
1
for the values),
i,j
label particle type,
\lambda_{i}
is particle-
i
’s absorption length, and
d_{i}
is its decay length.
In the right side of the equation, the first two (sink) terms describes the absorption and decay of particle
i
.
The last (source) term is for the creation of particle
i
due to particle
j
’s interaction with medium, with
the
F_{ji}(E_{i},E_{j})
factor, which is proportional to the corresponding
j\to i
reaction cross section, characterizing the energy distribution of the source.

The flux
N_{i}
is a double-differential flux, so that

N_{i}(E_{i})dE_{i}d\Omega_{i}
gives the flux within
dE_{i}
of energy
E_{i}
and within solid angle
d\Omega_{i}
of direction
\hat{\Omega}_{i}
.
The energy loss of particles are not included here because in gas it is small, while in solid targets the hadronic interaction length is the smallest length scale among all the scales including decay length (for energetic particles) and stopping length (due to energy loss).
For high-energy particles, the
F_{ji}(E_{i},E_{j})
function of two variables can be simplified as a function of
x_{L}\equiv\frac{E_{i}}{E_{j}}
alone
[
39
]
.

To solve the equations, we need to assign an initial condition for particle fluxes at
X=0
. Here, only the nucleon flux (including the nucleons in cosmic ray nuclei) is nonzero:

N_{N}(E,X=0)=N_{0}(E)\equiv\mathcal{C}\,E^{-(\gamma+1)}\ ,

(13)

which is the primary cosmic ray nucleon flux at nucleon kinetic energy
E
. The values for
\gamma
and
\mathcal{C}
can be found in Table
3
.
In our estimation, we use
\gamma=1.7
for all the energies investigated to simplify the calculations, although for
E
above
1000\,\mathrm{TeV}

\gamma=2
is a better approximation.

We can solve Eq. (
1
) for particle fluxes when primary nucleons travel through a medium.
For nucleons, the equation is decoupled from other hadrons
[
39
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{N}(E_{N},X)}{dX}=

\displaystyle-\frac{N_{N}(E_{N},X)}{\lambda_{N}}

\displaystyle+\int_{E_{N}}^{\infty}dE_{N}^{\prime}\frac{F_{NN}(E_{N},E_{N}^{\prime})}{E_{N}}\frac{N_{N}(E_{N}^{\prime},X)}{\lambda_{N}}\,.

(14)

The nucleon flux is then

\displaystyle N_{N}(E_{N},X)=\,

\displaystyle N_{0}(E_{N})e^{-X/\Lambda_{N}}\ ,\text{with}

\displaystyle\Lambda_{N}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{N}}{1-Z_{NN}(\gamma)}\ ,

(15)

\displaystyle Z_{NN}(\gamma)\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}dx_{L}\,x_{L}^{\gamma-1}\,F_{NN}(x_{L})\ .

(16)

For the pion flux, we have

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\pi}(E_{\pi},X)}{dX}=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{\pi}}+\frac{1}{d_{\pi}}\right)N_{\pi}(E_{\pi},X)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}dx_{L}\frac{N_{\pi}(E/x_{L})F_{\pi\pi}(E_{\pi},E_{\pi}/x_{L})}{x_{L}^{2}\lambda_{\pi}}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}dx_{L}\frac{N_{N}(E/x_{L})F_{N\pi}(E_{\pi},E_{\pi}/x_{L})}{x_{L}^{2}\lambda_{N}}\ .

(17)

So far, the discussion has applied to both gaseous and solid media, but we will discuss these separately in the following sections because the pion’s behavior is quite different in each case.

Several length and energy scales are involved in the calculations, including the pion-stopping length (without absorption), decay length, and interaction length.
These are depicted in Fig.
2
.
There is a dependence on pion energy in gaseous and solid media, with representative densities at
10^{-3}\,\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
and
2.8\,\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
, respectively.
For gaseous media, pion propagation is dominated by decay and interaction, and thus there is no need to consider pion energy loss.
In solids, the dynamics are dictated by the interaction length.

To compute the stopping lengths for charged particles like pions and muons, we solve Eq. (
2
) (or (
42

45
).
Then, based on the fact that

\lambda_{\pi}=116\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{2}

We now examine Eq. (

[

\displaystyle P=gX_{v},

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d_{\pi}}=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{\pi}\frac{RT}{Mgc\tau_{\pi}}}{EX\cos\theta}\equiv\frac{\epsilon_{\pi}}{EX\cos\theta}\ ,

According to Sec. 5.8 in Ref.

\displaystyle N_{\pi}(E,X)=

\displaystyle\,N(E,0)\frac{Z_{N\pi}}{1-Z_{NN}}\frac{\Lambda_{\pi}}{\Lambda_{\pi}-\Lambda_{N}}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\left(e^{-X/\Lambda_{\pi}}-e^{-X/\Lambda_{N}}\right)\ ,

\displaystyle\text{with}\ \Lambda_{\pi}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{\lambda_{\pi}}{1-Z_{\pi\pi}(\gamma)}\ \text{and}

\displaystyle Z_{\pi\pi}(\gamma)\equiv

\displaystyle\,\int_{0}^{1}dx_{L}\,x_{L}^{\gamma-1}\,F_{\pi\pi}(x_{L})\ .

(20)

The

\displaystyle N_{\pi}(E,X)=N(E,0)\frac{Z_{N\pi}}{\lambda_{N}}e^{-X/\Lambda_{N}}\frac{XE\cos\theta}{\epsilon_{\pi}}\ .

(21)

Here, in the same way as

When computing the pion flux in solids, we do not need to consider energy loss and decay in the medium, as the interaction lengths are far shorter than the other length scales.

B.2
Neutrinos and muons from meson decays

In this section we compute the leptons created from meson two-body decay in both gas and solid.
Production from
\mu
decay and
K_{L}
and charged kaons’ three-body leptonic decays is not considered, although they are important for estimating the
\nu_{e}
flux in gas.
See Fig. 6.7 in Ref.
[
39
]
for a discussion of these neglected components.
Our calculation of
\nu_{\mu}
flux in gas is thus good for order-of-magnitude estimates for neutrinos with energies above 1 GeV.
Above 100 GeV, it is quite accurate because of the negligibility of
\mu
decay, see Fig. 6.7 in
[
39
]
.

For the
\mu
flux Earth’s atmosphere, we must consider muon decay.
We know that the hadronic activity peaks about
6.4
km above sea level.
In contrast, the decay lengths of muons with energies around or below 10 GeV are smaller or comparable to the traveled distance if observed at sea level (note that
c\tau_{\mu}=0.66
km).

In solids, we only consider
\nu_{\mu}
neutrinos with energies greater than 1 GeV and
\mu
with energies at the creation site that are even higher, as we mainly focus on the underground flux.
Since the detectors in our study are a few hundred or thousand meters below the surface (much shorter than the decay length), we will ignore the impact of
\mu
decay on both
\nu_{\mu}
and
\mu
fluxes in solids.
Most of the
\nu_{\mu}
-induced
\mu
fluxes, relevant deep underground, should come from energetic
\nu_{\mu}
because of their larger creation rates.
The neutrinos from
\mu
decay are again ignored when computing the
\nu_{\mu}
-induced
\mu

In short, our estimates of the
\mu
fluxes from both meson two-body decay and the
\nu_{\mu}
-induced component should be reliable.
The estimation of
\nu_{\mu}
fluxes at 1 GeV should be good to within an order of magnitude and becomes more accurate for energies at 10 GeV and beyond.

To get the fluxes of daughter leptons from pion and kaon decays, we can compute the production spectrum of leptons at location
X

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{i}(E,X)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=\pi,K}\int_{E_{min}}^{E_{max}}dE^{\prime}\,\frac{F_{ji}(E,E^{\prime})}{E}\frac{N_{j}(E^{\prime},X)}{d_{j}}\,,

(24)

with

Properly integrating
\mathcal{P}_{i}(E,X)
over
X

We first examine carefully the integral that, for example, involves
F_{\pi\mu}(E,E^{\prime})
for the pion-decay induced
\mu

[

\displaystyle F_{\pi\mu}=F_{\pi\nu}=\frac{1}{1-r_{\pi}}\ ,\text{with}\ r_{\pi}=\frac{m_{\mu}^{2}}{m_{\pi}^{2}}\,.

(25)

For kaons, the corresponding

.
The values of

and

and

respectively at high and low energies in the gas, and
E^{\prime-(\gamma+1)}
in the solid.
The final piece in the integral,
1/d_{\pi}
as a function of
E^{\prime}
, has a factor of
1/E^{\prime}
in both gas and solids based on Eqs. (

After combining all the

\mu

\displaystyle Z_{\pi\mu}(\gamma)=\int_{r_{\pi}}^{1}dx_{L}\frac{1}{1-r_{M}}x^{\gamma}=\frac{1-r_{\pi}^{\gamma+1}}{(1+\gamma)(1-r_{\pi})}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle Z_{\pi\mu}(\gamma+1)=\frac{1-r_{\pi}^{\gamma+2}}{(2+\gamma)(1-r_{\pi})}

(27)

\nu_{\mu}

\int_{0}^{1-r_{\pi}}

\displaystyle Z_{\pi\nu}(\gamma)=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-r_{\pi})^{\gamma}}{(1+\gamma)}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle Z_{\pi\nu}(\gamma+1)=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-r_{\pi})^{(\gamma+1)}}{(2+\gamma)}

(29)

for the low and high energy limits.

Z_{K\mu}
and

, can be defined for kaon decay.

) in the flux calculations.

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\nu}}{dE_{\nu}}=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{0}(E_{\nu})}{1-Z_{NN}}\bigg[\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\pi\nu}}{1+\mathcal{B}_{\pi\nu}\cos\theta\,E_{\nu}/\epsilon_{\pi}}

\displaystyle+0.635\frac{\mathcal{A}_{K\nu}}{1+\mathcal{B}_{K\nu}\cos\theta\,E_{\nu}/\epsilon_{K}}\bigg]\ ,\ \text{with}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\pi\nu}\equiv

\displaystyle Z_{N\pi}Z_{\pi\nu}(\gamma)\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\pi\nu}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{\pi\nu}(\gamma)}{Z_{\pi\nu}(\gamma+1)}\frac{\Lambda_{\pi}-\Lambda_{N}}{\Lambda_{\pi}\ln{\Lambda_{\pi}/\Lambda_{N}}}\,,

\displaystyle\text{and similar}\ \mathcal{A}_{K\nu},\mathcal{B}_{K\nu}\ \text{for charged kaons}\,.

Note
0.635
is the charged kaon’s branching ratio to the
\mu+\nu_{\mu}
decay.

N_{0}(E_{\nu})
is the cosmic ray spectrum.

For
\mu

must take into account of the
\mu

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\mu}}{dE_{\mu}}=

\displaystyle S_{\mu}(E_{\mu})\frac{N_{0}(E_{\mu})}{1-Z_{NN}}\bigg[\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\pi\mu}}{1+\mathcal{B}_{\pi\mu}\cos\theta\,E_{\mu}/\epsilon_{\pi}}

\displaystyle+0.635\frac{\mathcal{A}_{K\mu}}{1+\mathcal{B}_{K\mu}\cos\theta\,E_{\mu}/\epsilon_{K}}\bigg]\,,\ \text{with}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\pi\mu}=

\displaystyle Z_{N\pi}Z_{\pi\mu}(\gamma)\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\pi\mu}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{\pi\mu}(\gamma)}{Z_{\pi\mu}(\gamma+1)}\frac{\Lambda_{\pi}-\Lambda_{N}}{\Lambda_{\pi}\ln{\Lambda_{\pi}/\Lambda_{N}}}\,,

(31)

and similar
\mathcal{A}_{K\mu},\mathcal{B}_{K\mu}
for charged kaons.

\displaystyle S_{\mu}(E_{\mu})=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\Lambda_{N}\cos\theta}{X_{0}}\right)^{p_{1}}\left(\frac{E_{\mu}}{E_{\mu}+\alpha X_{0}/\cos\theta}\right)^{p_{1}+\gamma+1}

\displaystyle\times\Gamma(p_{1}+1)\,,

\displaystyle\text{with}\ p_{1}=\frac{\epsilon_{\mu}}{E_{\mu}\cos\theta+\alpha X_{0}}\,.

Here,

is the vertical length at sea level, and

\theta>\pi/2
, we take
\theta\to\pi-\theta
.
However, this rule doesn’t apply to the muon flux, due to
\mu
interaction with Earth and its decay.

For solids, we only use the high-energy limit pion’s flux and the
1/d_{\pi}
factor in Eq. (
23
). Note that this
1/d_{\pi}
has no
X
dependence, as opposed to a gaseous medium.
As a result, the lepton flux in solids and the corresponding high-energy flux in gases are different, although they share similar pion fluxes as their sources.

It should be added that although
X
is integrated from
0
to
\infty
, physically speaking, the integration is from the contributions within just a few meters below the surface of the solid medium.
This is because typical hadron track lengths are on the order of meters in solids, considering the hadronic absorption lengths
\lambda_{h}\sim 100~\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
.
After a few meters, all the hadronic activity fades away.
In this work, we consider detectors located a few kilometers or less below the surfaces of solid media. Since the decay lengths of GeV muons are mostly larger than a few kilometers (
c\tau_{\mu}=0.66
km), muon decay can be safely neglected here.

We then find for solids,

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\nu}}{dE_{\nu}}=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{0}(E_{\nu})}{1-Z_{NN}}\Bigg[\frac{\Lambda_{\pi}m_{\pi}}{\tilde{X}_{\pi}E_{\nu}}Z_{N\pi}Z_{\pi\nu}(\gamma+1)

\displaystyle+0.635\,\frac{\Lambda_{K}m_{K}}{\tilde{X}_{K}E_{\nu}}Z_{NK}Z_{K\nu}(\gamma+1)\Bigg]\ ,

(33)

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\mu}}{dE_{\mu}}=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{0}(E_{\mu})}{1-Z_{NN}}\Bigg[\frac{\Lambda_{\pi}m_{\pi}}{\tilde{X}_{\pi}E_{\mu}}Z_{N\pi}Z_{\pi\mu}(\gamma+1)

\displaystyle+0.635\,\frac{\Lambda_{K}m_{K}}{\tilde{X}_{K}E_{\mu}}Z_{NK}Z_{K\mu}(\gamma+1)\Bigg]\ .

(34)

Interestingly, in the high-energy limit, the ratio between the pion-induced flux in the solid and the flux in the atmosphere is

\displaystyle\frac{m_{\pi}\Lambda_{\pi}}{\epsilon_{\pi}\tilde{X}_{\pi}}=\frac{\Lambda_{\pi}}{h_{0}\rho_{\mathrm{solid}}}\sim\frac{1m}{10^{4}m}=10^{-4}\,.

(35)

Here,
h_{0}=\frac{RT}{Mg}
is a typical vertical scale of an atmosphere, about
6.5
km on Earth.
Importantly, the flux ratio for the kaon-induced flux has similar sizes, because
\Lambda_{\pi}\sim\Lambda_{K}
.
Therefore, the gas-to-solid ratio of the combined flux from both mesons is also about
10^{-4}
.
However, this ratio is significantly affected by the so-called prompt component that we now discuss.

This prompt component, originating from the decays of charmed mesons and potentially other mesons, dominates the lepton fluxes for energies on the order of 10 TeV and beyond.
The lifetimes of these mesons are so short that the decay lengths are the smallest among all the length scales in both gas and solid.
Therefore, the flux of these mesons (denoted as
\phi_{c}
) takes the form of the low-energy pion flux in a gas.
Ignoring meson energy loss and its absorption in solids, we get the
\phi_{c}
flux,

\displaystyle\Phi(E,X)=N(E,0)\frac{Z_{N\phi}}{\lambda_{N}}e^{-X/\Lambda_{n}}\frac{\tilde{X}_{\phi}E}{m_{\phi}}\,.

(36)

This formula can be derived by substitution in Eq. (
21
), inspired by the difference between Eq. (
23
) and (
19
).

In gas,

\displaystyle\Phi(E,X)=N(E,0)\frac{Z_{N\phi}}{\lambda_{N}}e^{-X/\Lambda_{n}}\frac{EX\cos\theta}{\epsilon_{\phi}}\,.

(37)

We plug into Eq. (
24
) the expressions for
\Phi(E,X)
and the corresponding
\frac{1}{d_{\phi}}
, which take the same forms as Eq. (
19
) (for gas) and (
23
) (for solid).
The lepton fluxes can then be parameterized as

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\nu}^{\mathrm{prompt}}}{dE_{\nu}}=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{0}(E_{\nu})}{1-Z_{NN}}\mathcal{A}_{\pi\nu}R_{\nu}\ ,

(38)

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\mu}^{\mathrm{prompt}}}{dE_{\nu}}=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{0}(E_{\mu})}{1-Z_{NN}}\mathcal{A}_{\pi\mu}R_{\mu}\ ,\ \text{with}

(39)

\displaystyle R_{\nu}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{N\phi}Z_{\phi\nu}(\gamma)}{Z_{N\pi}Z_{\pi\nu}(\gamma)}\,,

(40)

\displaystyle R_{\mu}\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{N\phi}Z_{\phi\mu}(\gamma)}{Z_{N\pi}Z_{\pi\mu}(\gamma)}\,.

(41)

These expressions apply to both solids and gases.
The ratios
R_{\nu}
and
R_{\mu}
determine the magnitude of the prompt component.

R_{\mu}
is believed to be smaller than
2\times 10^{-3}\equiv\overline{R}_{\mu}
, while
R_{\nu}
should be around
8\times 10^{-4}\equiv\overline{R}_{\nu}
(c.f. Sec. 8.4 in
[
39
]
).
Note that this flux component scales as
E^{-(\gamma+1)}
, which is the same as the low-energy behavior of the flux in the atmosphere, but the pion and kaon-induced fluxes at high energy in the atmosphere and in solids scale as
E^{-(\gamma+2)}
.
Therefore, the prompt component eventually dominates the lepton fluxes when the energy is high enough.

These were compared against a recent pQCD evaluation

R_{\nu}=0.5\,\overline{R}_{\nu}

\nu_{\mu}

\mu

B.3
Muon energy loss in solid media

The energy loss of muons through matter can be described by the following formula:

\displaystyle\frac{dE}{dX}=-\alpha-\frac{E}{\xi}\,,

(42)

with the first term describing the ionization energy loss and the second arising from other processes, including bremsstrahlung, pair production, etc.
\xi
has mild energy dependence.
As an example, for standard rock,
A=22
,
Z=11
, and
\rho=2.65\,\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}
,
\xi=2.55\times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{2}
at 1 TeV and
2.3\times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{2}
at 10 TeV.
This energy loss also introduces the so-called critical energy
\epsilon_{\xi}\equiv\alpha\times\xi
, beyond which the second term in the formula becomes dominant.
For muons with
\alpha\sim 2\,\mathrm{MeV}/(\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2})
,
\epsilon_{\xi}=500
GeV.

Based on this formula, we find the particle energy after traveling through length
X
,

\displaystyle E(X)=(E_{0}+\epsilon)e^{-\frac{X}{\xi}}-\epsilon_{\xi}\,,

(43)

with
E_{0}
as the initial energy.
If the muons have energy
E
as observed at a distance
X
from the creation point, their initial energy is

\displaystyle E_{0}(E,X)=e^{\frac{X}{\xi}}\left(E+\epsilon_{\xi}\right)-\epsilon_{\xi}\,.

(44)

For a given
E_{0}
, the range of the muon, i.e., its stopping length, is then given by

\displaystyle X^{\mathrm{max}}(E_{0})=\xi\log\left(1+\frac{E_{0}}{\epsilon_{\xi}}\right)\ .

(45)

The energy spectrum of muons at depth
X
is completely determined by the flux at
X=0
, as there is a one-to-one correspondence between the energies at different
X
(the muon’s decay can be safely neglected).
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\mu}(X)}{dE_{\mu}}=\frac{dN_{\mu}}{dE_{0}}\frac{dE_{0}}{dE_{\mu}}=\left.e^{\frac{X}{\xi}}\frac{dN_{\mu}}{dE_{0}}\right|_{E_{0}=e^{\frac{X}{\xi}}\left(E+\epsilon_{\xi}\right)-\epsilon_{\xi}}\,.

(46)

This formula predicts the muon flux underground from its flux at sea level on Earth or a few meters below the surface in an environment without atmosphere.
The discussion here applies to charged hadrons as well.
For example, Eq. (
45
) was used to compute the stopping lengths for the charged pions in Fig.
2
.

Note that
\alpha
has a mild dependence on muon energy.
When
E>10
GeV, a good approximation
[
39
]
is

\alpha(E)´\approx 1.9+0.08\ln\left(\frac{E}{m_{\mu}}\right)\ .

(47)

In our calculations, for simplicity, we apply this dependence for
E>1
GeV.
In this case
\alpha
increases slowly to
2.3
at
10
GeV, and
2.8
at
10^{4}
GeV.
Below
1
GeV, we set
\alpha=2
MeV
\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{2}
.

B.4
\nu_{\mu}
-induced muons underground

To compute this flux, we need to understand the probability of a
\nu_{\mu}
(with energy
E_{\nu}
) producing a
\mu
that has energy between
E_{\mu}
and
E_{\mu}+dE_{\mu}
when detected.
According to Eq. 8.24 in
[
39
]
, the probability density is

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{P}(E_{\nu})}{dE_{\mu}}=\frac{\xi N_{A}\sigma_{\nu}(\epsilon_{\xi})}{\epsilon_{\xi}}\frac{E_{\nu}-E_{\mu}}{\epsilon_{\xi}+E_{\mu}}\times\begin{cases}1\ \text{for $\nu_{\mu}$}\\
\frac{1+\frac{E_{\mu}}{E_{\nu}}+\left(\frac{E_{\mu}}{E_{\nu}}\right)^{3}}{3}\ \text{for $\overline{\nu}_{\mu}$}\end{cases}\

(48)

Here,
\sigma_{\nu}(\epsilon_{\xi})
is the cross section at energy
\epsilon_{\xi}
for
\nu_{\mu}
’s charged-current scattering off nucleons.
See Table
3
for the information about
\epsilon_{\xi}
,
\xi
and
N_{A}
.
The derivation depends on the Fermi theory for the weak-interaction cross-section, which holds for
E_{\nu}
below 3.6 TeV.
In addition, the integrated
X
between the source of particle production (near the surface) and the detector is required to be on the order of
\xi
or larger.
If the integrated
X
is far below this value, for example, just a few hundred meters below the surface in an ice ball, this formula should only be considered as an upper limit.
Note the factor
\xi N_{A}\sigma_{\nu}(\epsilon_{\xi})
is
6\times 10^{-7}
when evaluated at
\epsilon_{\xi}=500
GeV.

The muon flux can be computed via

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\mu}}{dE_{\mu}}=\int_{E_{\mu}}^{\infty}dE_{\nu}\frac{dN_{\nu}}{dE_{\nu}}\frac{d\mathcal{P}(E_{\nu})}{dE_{\mu}}\ .

(49)

Note that the probability density for
\overline{\nu}_{\mu}
differs from the
\nu_{\mu}
one by a factor ranging from
1/3
to
1
(see Eq. (
48
)).
Assuming the same weight for
\nu_{\mu}
and
\overline{\nu}_{\mu}
in the total neutrino flux, we can multiply the
\nu_{\mu}
-induced muon flux by a factor between
2/3
and
1
to get the full spectrum from neutrinos and antineutrinos.
Even without this factor, the neutrino-induced flux is a good estimation of the combined flux from neutrinos and antineutrinos.

B.5
Fluxes on Moon and Mars

Since atmospheric density is never truly zero, it is natural to ask whether the atmosphere on the Moon or Mars is dilute enough to be considered zero.
This question is related to whether meson production and their subsequent decays occur in the atmosphere.

The typical length scale of hadronic activity is about 100
\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{2}
.
For example, for high energy pions, the pion flux in (
20
) achieves its maximum at

\displaystyle X_{max}=\frac{\Lambda_{\pi}\Lambda_{N}\ln{\Lambda_{N}/\Lambda_{\pi}}}{\Lambda_{N}-\Lambda_{\pi}}\sim 140\,\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{2}

(50)

Kaons will have similar values. Note when
X\ll X_{max}
,

\displaystyle\frac{N_{\pi}(X\ll X_{max})}{N_{\pi}(X_{max})}=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda_{N}}-\frac{1}{\Lambda_{\pi}}\right)X}{e^{-\frac{X_{max}}{\Lambda_{\pi}}}-e^{-\frac{X_{max}}{\Lambda_{N}}}}\sim\frac{0.02}{\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{2}}X

(51)

Using Earth’s atmosphere as a reference, Eq. (
18
) gives the vertical column depths (
X_{X}
) for the other planets at their surfaces:

\displaystyle X_{\oplus}=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{\oplus}}{g_{\oplus}}\approx 10^{4}\,\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{2}\ ,

(52)

\displaystyle X_{X}=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{X}/g_{X}}{P_{\oplus}/g_{\oplus}}X_{\oplus}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&1.5\times 10^{2}\sim X_{max}\ \text{for Mars}\\
&10^{-10}\sim 10^{-7}\ll X_{max}\ \text{for Moon}\end{cases}\,.

(53)

We then see

\displaystyle\frac{N_{\pi}(X_{X})}{N_{\pi}(X_{max})}\approx

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&1\ \text{for Mars, since}\ X_{X}\sim X_{max}\\
&0.02X_{X}\approx 10^{-12}\sim 10^{-9}\end{cases}\,.

(54)

On the Moon, the pion fluxes on its surface is at most
10^{-9}
of the maximum flux achieved in Earth’s atmosphere.
The variation of the ratio for the Moon is caused by the variation of its pressure on the surface.
Therefore, the corresponding lepton fluxes are below
10^{-9}
of the atmospheric fluxes on Earth.

On the other hand, the pion flux at the surface of Mars is comparable with the maximum flux in Earth’s atmosphere, hinting that the lepton fluxes on Mars might be similar to those on Earth.
To proceed, we need to understand if pions have enough time to decay in the atmosphere before hitting the surface of Mars.
On Earth,
X_{max}
corresponds to about 14 km above sea level (see Eq. 5.59 in
[
39
]
).
Pions with energy below 100 GeV are more likely to decay when reaching the surface (note that
c\tau_{\pi}\approx 8
m).
Since
X_{Mars}\sim X_{max}
, the region where pions achieve maximum flux is around the surface by a physical-length scale defined by
h_{0}\equiv RT/Mg
(note
X_{v}=X_{0}e^{-h/h_{0}}
and thus
h_{0}
is a typical scale for
X_{v}
to vary).
On Earth,

\displaystyle\frac{RT_{\oplus}}{M_{\oplus}g_{\oplus}}\sim 6.5\ \mathrm{km}

(55)

Since the temperatures on Mars and Earth are on the same scale,
O(10^{2})
K, and gas molecule masses are also on the same scale, the ratio between
h_{0}
on other planets and
h_{0}
on Earth is proportional to
1/g
.
Mars’s
g_{Mars}=0.37g_{\oplus}
, meaning
h_{0}
on Mars is on the same scale as
h_{0}
on Earth.
This suggests that pions on Mars have a physical length of at least a km to decay before hitting the surface.
Therefore, we find the pion decay physics on Mars is similar to that on Earth, and so are the fluxes of the pion and kaon decay-induced leptons.
