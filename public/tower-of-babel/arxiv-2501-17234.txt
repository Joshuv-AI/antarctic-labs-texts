Freezing-in the Pure Dark Axion Portal


Abstract
A Z 2 Z_{2} symmetry under which “dark” and “visible” fields transform differently can further seclude already dark particles. In a dark sector consisting of an axion-like particle and a dark photon the dominant interaction can then be via a dark photon-axion portal to the ordinary photon. If the axion is lighter than the dark photon it naturally emerges as a viable dark matter candidate. We explore its freeze-in production both in a standard high-reheating temperature and weak coupling as well as a low-reheating temperature and strong coupling regime. Cosmological constraints are taken into account to identify viable regions in parameter space. For the strong freeze-in regime, we highlight the possibility of probing it at B-factories, beam dump and collider experiments. We also briefly discuss a potential misalignment contribution to the dark matter density and delineate under which conditions it can be neglected.

Preliminaries – Misalignment and the initial state

Before looking in detail at the thermal scattering processes that contribute to the freeze in production, let us briefly comment on the initial state.

For freeze-in production it is usually assumed that, after inflation, the universe contains a negligible abundance of the desired dark matter particles
[
33
]
.
However, for bosonic fields this is not automatically guaranteed as there might also be a contribution from the misalignment mechanism
[
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
, i.e. a contribution from a (spatially constant) displacement of the field from its minimum value that survives inflation. This can lead to a potentially sizable contribution to the dark matter density, e.g. for the case of a scalar it is
[
53
,
54
]

\frac{\Omega_{\phi}}{\Omega_{\rm DM}}\sim 5\left(\frac{\phi_{1}}{10^{11}\,{\rm GeV}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{m_{\phi}}{10\,{\rm keV}}\right)^{1/2}\sim 5\left(\frac{\phi_{1}}{10^{9.5}\,{\rm GeV}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{m_{\phi}}{10\,{\rm GeV}}\right)^{1/2}.

(5)

Notably, this grows with the mass.

In our case, we have two new dark sector fields: the ALP and the dark photon.
The latter is relatively harmless. The reason is that for vectors, and as long as we do not invoke non-trivial effects such as, e.g. a sizable extra coupling to the Ricci scalar
[
53
]
, dilution during inflation is effective
[
55
]
. After a suitable period of inflation, the contribution is then negligible.

For scalars this is not the case, as is clear from the above formula (
5
) for a scalar with standard couplings.
However, we can invoke that axion-like particles, being pseudo-Goldstone bosons have a typical field value that is constraint by the corresponding symmetry breaking scale
f
. Moreover, validity of the effective field theory also requires the field value not to be too large. Let us take the following naive estimate,

\phi\lesssim f\sim cg_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{-1},

(6)

where
c
is a combination of couplings in the underlying theory. For the model of
[
16
]
this is given by
c\sim g_{y}g_{d}/(\pi^{2})
(cf. Eq. (
3
)). Taking the dark coupling to be of the same order as the hypercharge coupling, this leads to

, which we take as an indicative value.
Using Eq. (
5
) a coupling of

\displaystyle g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\gtrsim 7\times 10^{-12}\,{\rm GeV}^{-1}\left(\frac{c}{10^{-2}}\right)\left(\frac{m_{\phi}}{10\,{\rm GeV}}\right)^{1/4},

(7)

ensures that the misalignment contribution is sub-dominant.

As we will see in the following this includes a significant part of the interesting parameter space.
However, there are also sizable regions of freeze-in parameter space where this is not guaranteed.
Importantly, however, Eq. (
5
) only applies if the Hubble scale during inflation is higher than the mass,
3H_{\rm inflation}\gtrsim m_{\phi}
.
If the inflation scale is lower, the scalar dilutes with the volume during inflation, and again, the abundance after inflation is negligible.
Taking reheating to be reasonably effective we have,

T_{\rm RH}\sim{\mathcal{O}(1)}\times 2\frac{\sqrt{H_{\rm inflation}M_{P}}}{g_{\star}^{1/4}},

(8)

where
g_{\star}
indicates the number of relativistic degrees of freedom and
M_{P}=2.4\times 10^{18}
GeV, the reduced Planck mass.
Using this we find the following condition ensures a small initial abundance,

T_{\rm RH}\lesssim 10^{6}\,{\rm GeV}\left(\frac{200}{{g_{\star}}}\right)^{1/4}\left(\frac{m_{\phi}}{10\,{\rm keV}}\right)^{1/2}.

(9)

As we shall see in the following (cf. Fig.
6
), this condition is fulfilled in a good fraction of our parameter space of interest.

If we do not fulfill this condition, we still may avoid the misalignment contribution. A simple, though admittedly not very pretty option is fine-tuning the initial value of the ALP. This should be possible as long as the required value to make the misalignment contribution subdominant is not smaller than the inflation scale. This requires,

\displaystyle T_{\rm RH}\ll 2\times 10^{14}\penalty\ {\rm{GeV}}\left(\frac{100}{g_{\star}}\right)^{1/4}\left(\frac{m_{\phi}}{10\,{\rm keV}}\right)^{-1/8}.

(10)

This is a rather mild condition, even for relatively high masses
m_{\phi}
. Therefore, in the following, we will simply assume that the misalignment contribution is small. If required, by tuning the initial field value.

Finally we note that an alternative way would be to invoke suitable extra couplings such as, again, e.g., a non trivial coupling to the Ricci-scalar
[
54
]
.
8
8

8

Another possible example might be a sizable quartic self-interaction. But this may also affect the thermal behavior.

But in these situations the above caveats on non-trivial model features should be kept in mind.

Thermalization in the strong freeze-in regime

Due to the exponentially increasing behavior of
g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
in strong FI scenarios, one may wonder about the possibility of thermalization between the visible and dark sectors, i.e. forward and backward particle reactions involving dark states equilibrate. To estimate whether this happens, we consider the extreme case in the strong FI regime where
\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi
dominates the production of relic abundance and the population of DP is negligible as a result of Boltzmann suppression. In this way, following a similar treatment as
[

\displaystyle\dot{n}_{\phi}+3Hn_{\phi}=\Gamma_{\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi}-\Gamma_{\phi\phi\rightarrow\gamma\gamma}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{\gamma,e}^{2}\braket{\sigma v}_{\gamma\gamma\rightarrow\phi\phi}=n_{\phi,e}^{2}\braket{\sigma v}_{\phi\phi\rightarrow\gamma\gamma}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi\phi\rightarrow\gamma\gamma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{\phi}^{2}\braket{\sigma v}_{\phi\phi\rightarrow\gamma\gamma}

(34)

4.3
Standard Freeze-In

We now take

The Boltzmann equation for the yield of the ALP and an identical for the DP can be written as (see Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{dY_{\phi}}{dT}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{45}{2\pi^{3}g_{*s}}\sqrt{\frac{90}{g_{*s}}}\frac{M_{P}}{T^{6}}\sum_{f}C(f\bar{f}\rightarrow\phi\gamma^{\prime}),

where

n_{f,e}=g_{f}\frac{\zeta(3)}{\pi^{2}}T^{3}

g_{f}
the internal degrees of freedom of the fermion. Eq. (
35
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{dY_{\phi}}{dT}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{45\zeta(3)^{2}g_{f}^{2}M_{P}}{2\pi^{7}g_{*s}}\sqrt{\frac{90}{g_{*s}}}\sum_{f}\braket{\sigma_{f\bar{f}\rightarrow\phi\gamma^{\prime}}v}.

(36)

Assuming that the effective degrees of freedom in energy and entropy remain constant, the yield can then be integrated to get
[

]

\displaystyle Y_{\phi}(T)=Y_{\phi}(T_{\rm RH})-\frac{45\zeta(3)^{2}g_{f}^{2}M_{P}}{2\pi^{7}g_{*s}}\sqrt{\frac{90}{g_{*s}}}\sum_{f}\int_{T_{\rm RH}}^{T}dT^{\prime}\braket{\sigma_{f\bar{f}\rightarrow\phi\gamma^{\prime}}v}(T^{\prime})\,.

(37)

At high energies, and similarly to the strong FI regime, we have different contributions to the average annihilation cross-section times velocity, depending on the flavor of the fermion (for details see App

\displaystyle\sum_{f}\braket{\sigma_{f\bar{f}\rightarrow\phi\gamma^{\prime}}v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\text{SM neutrinos}}\frac{e^{2}g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{2}}{24\pi c_{W}^{2}}+\sum_{\text{charged lep.}}\frac{5e^{2}g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{2}}{96\pi c_{W}^{2}}

(39)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\text{up-type quarks}}\frac{17e^{2}g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{2}}{2592\pi c_{W}^{2}}+\sum_{\text{down-type quarks}}\frac{5e^{2}g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{2}}{2592\pi c_{W}^{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{265e^{2}g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{2}}{864\pi c_{W}^{2}},

(40)

\displaystyle Y_{\phi}^{\infty}=\frac{1325\zeta(3)^{2}g_{f}^{2}e^{2}g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{2}M_{P}}{192\pi^{8}c_{W}^{2}g_{\star s}}\sqrt{\frac{90}{g_{\star}}}T_{\rm RH}\,.

Thermalization in the standard freeze-in regime

As in the strong freeze-in scenario, high couplings could lead to thermalization of the dark particles with the thermal bath and undergo a freeze-out process, instead of freeze-in. Let us quickly estimate when this would be the case. For simplicity, we consider only the processes with charged fermions. We define the interaction rate with charged fermions as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}=n_{q}\langle\sigma_{q\bar{q}\rightarrow\phi\gamma^{\prime}}v\rangle.

(42)

Here,
n_{q}=\sum_{i}Q^{2}_{i}n_{i}=(\zeta(3)/\pi^{2})g_{q}(T)T^{3}
and
Q_{i}
is the charge of the i-th particle and
g_{q}
the effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom of charge particles. We now compare the rate to the Hubble parameter, finding that the condition for thermalization is

\displaystyle g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}\gtrsim 8\times 10^{-16}\,\mbox{GeV}^{-1}\,\sqrt{\frac{10^{14}\,\mbox{GeV}}{g_{q}T_{RH}}}.

(43)

As we will see in the next section the standard freeze-in mechanism is safe from thermalization in all the relevant parameter space.

In summary, the FI production of both dark states is viable, for high and low
T_{\rm RH}
. These two cases differ strongly in the required values of
g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
to fulfill the correct relic abundance. This fact has profound consequences not only for the relevant constraints in each case, but also for the possible way to test each scenario. In the following section we explore these features in more detail.

Dark matter evolution in the early universe

The cosmological evolution of our model depends strongly on the lifetime of the dark photon and the maximum temperature in the universe which, for simplicity, we assume is the reheating temperature,
T_{\rm RH}

In this work we are interested in the regime where the DP is unstable, leaving the ALP as the only DM candidate. The decay condition is given by,

\displaystyle H(T_{\rm decay})=\Gamma_{\gamma^{\prime}\rightarrow\gamma\phi}.

The freeze-in production mechanism is sensitive to the hierarchy between dark particle masses and the reheating temperature, as discussed in the previous section. 5.1
Including the decay contribution

The dynamics of the decay are governed by the following Boltzmann equations

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{da}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Gamma_{\gamma^{\prime}\rightarrow\gamma\phi}\frac{N_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{Ha},

(47a)

\displaystyle\frac{dN_{\phi}}{da}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\gamma^{\prime}\rightarrow\gamma\phi}\frac{N_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{Ha},

(47b)

\displaystyle\frac{dT}{da}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{T}{a}+\frac{15}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{\Gamma_{\gamma^{\prime}\rightarrow\gamma\phi}}{Ha^{4}}\frac{N_{\gamma^{\prime}}m_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{T^{3}},

(47c)

with
a
the scale factor and
T
the photon temperature. Moreover, we have assumed a non-relativistic dark photon. We solve the set of equations using the Hubble parameter

\displaystyle H=\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{r}+\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{3M_{P}^{2}}}.

(48)

Here, the total radiation density is given by
\rho_{r}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{30}g_{\star}(T)T^{4}
and we have neglected the contribution from the ALP because we will require it to satisfy the observed abundance today, making it subdominant at early times. For the DP energy density, we use
\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}=\frac{N_{\gamma}}{a^{3}}m_{\gamma^{\prime}}
. The initial number of DPs and ALPs is obtained from the yield of Eq. (
41
), that is,
N^{i}_{\gamma^{\prime},\phi}=Y_{\phi}^{\infty}S_{i}
, with
S_{i}=S(T_{i})
the entropy evaluated at some initial temperature
T_{i}
where we start our integration. As a general case, we will consider the decay to occur while the DP is non-relativistic. This is certainly a good approach for most of the parameter space in which we are interested. Thus, the initial integration temperature is chosen to satisfy
T_{i}\lesssim m_{\gamma^{\prime}}
.

It is possible to find analytical solutions to the above equations assuming that DP never dominates the energy density (which is an excellent approximation in the allowed parameter space). They are

\displaystyle N_{\gamma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{i}\,e^{-a^{2}/2a_{\rm decay}^{2}},

(49)

\displaystyle N_{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{\phi}^{i}+{N_{\gamma}^{i}}(1-e^{-a^{2}/2a_{\rm decay}^{2}}),

(50)

where
a_{\rm decay}
is the scale factor at the decay. Note that, as expected, the final number of ALPs after decay is
N_{\phi}^{f}=N_{\phi}^{i}+N_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{i}=2N_{\phi}^{i}
, because the initial yield for DPs and ALPs is the same, see Eq. (
41

\frac{\Gamma_{\gamma^{\prime}\rightarrow\gamma+\phi}}{2H(a_{i})}

\displaystyle\frac{T(a)}{T_{i}}=\,\frac{a_{i}}{a}\left(1+\frac{\rho_{\gamma^{\prime},i}}{\rho_{\gamma,i}}\left(\frac{a}{a_{i}}\right)^{3}\frac{\Gamma}{6H(a_{i})}\right)^{1/4}.

(51)

Here,
\rho_{\gamma,i},\rho_{\gamma^{\prime},i}
are the energy densities of the photon and DP at
T=T_{i}

\displaystyle\frac{T(a)}{T_{\rm end}}=\frac{a_{\rm end}}{a},

(52)

with

a_{\rm end}
the temperature and scale factor once the decay has completely ended

\displaystyle\rho_{\gamma}(T_{i})\left(\frac{T_{\rm end}}{T_{i}}\right)^{4}=\rho_{\gamma}(T_{i})\left(\frac{T_{\rm decay}}{T_{i}}\right)^{4}+\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}(T_{i})\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\rm decay})}{g_{\star}(T_{i})}\left(\frac{T_{\rm decay}}{T_{i}}\right)^{3},

(53)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{T_{\rm end}}{T_{\rm decay}}\right)^{3}=\left[1+\frac{\rho_{\gamma^{\prime}}(T_{i})}{\rho_{\gamma}(T_{i})}\frac{g_{\star}(T_{\rm decay})}{g_{\star}(T_{i})}\frac{T_{i}}{T_{\rm decay}}\right]^{3/4}.

\displaystyle\Omega_{\phi,0}=Y_{\phi}^{f}s(T_{0})\frac{m_{\phi}}{\rho_{c,0}}.

{Y_{\phi}^{\infty}}S_{RH}={Y_{\phi}^{\infty}}S_{\rm decay}

\displaystyle\Omega_{\phi,0}=2{Y_{\phi}^{\infty}}\,s(T_{0})\,\frac{m_{\phi}}{\rho_{c,0}}\frac{S_{\rm decay}}{S_{\rm end}}.

5.2
Warmness of the DM

When the DP decays, the conservation of energy and momentum of the process fixes the magnitude of the momenta for the final states,

\displaystyle m_{\gamma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sqrt{k_{\phi}^{2}+m_{\phi}^{2}}+k_{\gamma},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,k_{\phi}\sim k_{\gamma}=k_{\rm decay},

(58)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow k_{\rm decay}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{2}-m_{\phi}^{2}}{2m_{\gamma^{\prime}}}.

(59)

Therefore, the ALPs from the decay are relativistic and their momentum redshifts according to
p_{\phi}=k_{\rm decay}\,(a_{\rm decay}/a)

\displaystyle p_{\phi,nr}\sim m_{\phi}\Rightarrow a_{nr}\approx\frac{m_{\gamma^{\prime}}}{2m_{\phi}}a_{\rm decay}\,,

(60)

where we have taken
k_{\rm decay}\approx m_{\gamma^{\prime}}/2
, for simplicity.
After the decay-originated ALPs are non-relativistic their average velocity is approximately given by

\langle v_{\phi}\rangle\sim(a_{nr}/a).

(61)

The ALP population shall be sufficiently cold before entering the matter dominated era and not interfere with structure formation,
[

67

\displaystyle\langle v\rangle\sim 10^{-3}c\,\,\,\,\,\mbox{ at}\,\,a=a_{\rm eq}.

We now analyze the situation where the cosmological evolution of our model could interfere with the process of BBN and the effective number of neutrinos. Leaving aside the decay of the DP for a moment, we will be in a scenario where the DP is heavier than 10 MeV, such that it is already non-relativistic when BBN starts. However, the population from the decay could lead to several potential issues:

a)

Appendix A
Unitarity

The EFT description for momentum transfer close to the EW scale must respect the gauge symmetry of the SM, otherwise annihilation of gauge bosons into dark states, relevant for freeze-in, could give rise to processes growing with the energy
[
75
]
. In particular, the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{F}
in Eq. (
2
) does not respect the full gauge symmetry. Therefore, the calculation of freeze-in at high reheating temperature must be carried out with
\mathcal{L}_{B}
. Let us comment in more detail on this fact.

Considering
\mathcal{L}_{F}
, the cross section for
W^{+}W^{-}\rightarrow\gamma^{\prime}\phi
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma(s)=\frac{e^{2}g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{2}s(12m_{W}^{2}+20m_{W}^{2}s+s^{2})}{864\pi m_{W}^{4}(s-4m_{W}^{2})}\left[\frac{(s-4m_{W}^{2})(m_{\phi}^{4}+(s-m_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{2})^{2}-2m_{\phi}^{2}(s+m_{\gamma^{\prime}}^{2}))}{s^{3}}\right]^{3/2}\,.

(74)

In the limit
s\gg 4m_{W}^{2}
, Eq. (
74
) reduces to
\sigma(s)\rightarrow\frac{e^{2}g^{2}s^{2}}{864\pi m_{W}^{4}}
, manifesting the growth in energy as
E_{\text{CM}}^{4}
.

Let us turn to the EFT that is
SU(2)\times U(1)
symmetric,
\mathcal{L}_{B}
. Now, the annihilation process
W^{+}W^{-}\rightarrow\phi\gamma^{\prime}
is mediated by both the SM photon and the
Z
boson (or equivalently by the
B_{\mu}
boson from
U(1)_{Y}
). The cross section in this case is given by,

\displaystyle\sigma(s)=\frac{c_{w}^{2}e^{2}g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}^{2}m_{Z}^{4}s(12m_{W}^{4}+20m_{W}^{2}s+s^{2})}{864m_{W}^{4}\pi(s-m_{Z}^{2})^{2}(s-4m_{W}^{2})}\left(\frac{s-4m_{W}^{2}}{s}\right)^{3/2}\xrightarrow{s\rightarrow\infty}\text{constant}\,,

(75)

and the problematic fast growth disappears.
16
16

16

We note that it does not decay as
\sim 1/s
. This is due to the nonrenormalizable coupling
g_{\phi\gamma\gamma^{\prime}}
.

In this way, using the symmetric gauge EFT description
