Complex slices on a real variety
Oleg Viro

Abstract




1
Transverse or not transverse

1.1
Transversality.

Remind that subspaces
V
and
W
of a vector space
U
are said to be
transverse
if
V\cup W
generates
U
, i.e.,
U=V+W
.
In a finite-dimensional
U
,
transversality is equivalent to a single condition in terms of dimensions:

V,W\text{ are transverse in }U\ \iff\ \dim U-(\dim V+\dim W)+\dim(V\cap W)=0

Two submanifolds of a smooth manifold are said to be
transverse
if
at each of their intersection points their tangent spaces are transverse.

Transversality of submanifolds is a condition of general position:
any two submanifolds can be made transverse by a suffitiently small
perturbation of any of them and if submanifold are transverse then a
sufficiently small perturbation cannot destroy transversality.

However, non-transverse intersections in Real Algebraic Geometry emerge
inevitably if one of the submanifolds is a complex variety and the other
is the real point set of the ambient real algebraic variety.

1.2
Example: a plane curve.

In
U=\mathbb{C}P^{2}
, the

complex

points set
V=B_{\mathbb{C}}
of a
real
algebraic curve
B
is
not
transverse

to
W=\mathbb{R}P^{2}
. The intersection
V\cap W=V\cap\mathbb{R}P^{2}
is the set
B_{\mathbb{R}}

of real points of
B
.

On the picture,
V
and
W
look transversal. However they lie not in

\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
but in the 4-dimensional
\mathbb{C}P^{2}
, where intersection of transverse
surfaces must be 0-dimensional.

The intersection of
V
and
W
can be
made transversal by an
arbitrarily small
perturbation of
V

under which
V
stay in the class of

complex
algebraic curves,
but may not be a
complexification of real
algebraic
curve.

1.3
Real algebraic varieties in a nutshell.

A real algebraic variety
X
has

•

the set
X_{\mathbb{C}}
of complex points,

•

anti-holomorphic involution
\operatorname{conj}:X_{\mathbb{C}}\to X_{\mathbb{C}}
, and

•

the set
X_{\mathbb{R}}=\operatorname{fix}(\operatorname{conj})
of real points.

If
X
is nonsingular, then

•

X_{\mathbb{C}}
is a smooth manifold with complex
structure,

•

\operatorname{conj}
is diffeomorphism and

•

X_{\mathbb{R}}
is a smooth submanifold of

X_{\mathbb{C}}
.

1.4
A complexification a real variety is never transverse to real
part of the ambient space.

Theorem 1
.

If
Y
is a real nonsingular subvariety of a real nonsingular variety
X
,
then both
Y_{\mathbb{C}}
and
X_{\mathbb{R}}
are smooth submanifolds of
X_{\mathbb{C}}
and

Y_{\mathbb{R}}=Y_{\mathbb{C}}\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
Y_{\mathbb{C}}
and
X_{\mathbb{R}}
are not
transverse in
X_{\mathbb{C}}
unless
Y_{\mathbb{R}}\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}=\varnothing
or
\dim Y=\dim X
.

Proof.

Assume that
Y_{\mathbb{C}}
and
X_{\mathbb{R}}
are transverse. Then by
the transversality condition

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X_{\mathbb{C}}-(\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X_{\mathbb{R}}+\dim_{\mathbb{R}}Y_{\mathbb{C}})+\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(Y_{\mathbb{C}}\cap X_{\mathbb{R}})=0.

Since

•

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X_{\mathbb{C}}=2\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X_{\mathbb{R}}
,

•

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}Y_{\mathbb{C}}=2\dim_{\mathbb{R}}Y_{\mathbb{R}}
and

•

Y_{\mathbb{C}}\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}=Y_{\mathbb{R}}
,

it follows

2\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X_{\mathbb{R}}-(\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X_{\mathbb{R}}+\dim_{\mathbb{R}}Y_{\mathbb{C}})+\dim_{\mathbb{R}}Y_{\mathbb{R}}\\
=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X_{\mathbb{R}}-\dim_{\mathbb{R}}Y_{\mathbb{R}}=0.

(1)

∎

In this paper we study how a
complex
subvariety
V

of
X_{\mathbb{C}}
can meet
X_{\mathbb{R}}
.

1.5
A complex cut of a real algebraic variety is real
algebraic.

Theorem 2
.

For any complex subvariety
V\subset X_{\mathbb{C}}
, the intersection
V\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}
is the
set of real points of a real algebraic subvariety of
X
.

Proof.

The complex conjugation involution

\operatorname{conj}:X_{\mathbb{C}}\to X_{\mathbb{C}}
maps
V
to a complex subvariety. The
intersection
V\cap\operatorname{conj}V
is a complex variety. Denote it by
Y
.
This is a real algebraic variety, because it is invariant under
\operatorname{conj}
.
Indeed,
\operatorname{conj}Y=\operatorname{conj}(V\cap\operatorname{conj}V)=\operatorname{conj}V\cap\operatorname{conj}^{2}V=(\operatorname{conj}V)\cap V=Y
.

Further,
Y_{\mathbb{R}}=Y\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}=(V\cap\operatorname{conj}V)\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}=(V\cap X_{\mathbb{R}})\cap(\operatorname{conj}V\cap X_{\mathbb{R}})=V\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}
.
Thus,
V\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}=Y\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}=Y_{\mathbb{R}}
.
∎

Corollary
.

For any complex subvariety
V\subset X_{\mathbb{C}}
transversal to
X_{\mathbb{R}}
,
there exists a real algebraic subvariety
Y
of
X
such that
Y_{\mathbb{C}}\subset V

and
Y_{\mathbb{R}}=V\cap X_{\mathbb{C}}
.

1.6
Complex transverse slices.

Let
X
be a nonsingular real algebraic variety.
Usually,
X_{\mathbb{C}}
contains lots of complex subvarieties.
They cut real algebraic varieties on
X_{\mathbb{R}}
.
Most of the complex varieties are transverse to
X_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Thus we have a large class of real algebraic varieties, which are
transverse intersections of complex varieties with
X_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Let
Y
be a real algebraic subvariety of a nonsingular real
algebraic variety
X
, let
V
be a complex subvariety
of
X_{\mathbb{C}}
transverse to
X_{\mathbb{R}}
. If
Y_{\mathbb{R}}=V\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}
,
then
Y
is called a
slice
of
X
.
We say also that
Y
is
sliced
in
X
by
V
.

Sliced subvarieties are quite special. In particular:

•

A sliced subvariety has
even codimension
.

•

The
transverse
(aka normal)
bundle
of a real sliced
subvariety admits a
complex
structure.

•

In particular, the transverse bundle is
orientable
.

•

A choice of the slicing complex subvariety determines a
complex
structure
and
orientation
of the transverse bundle.

•

With this orientation, a real
sliced

subvariety realizes in the ambient real variety an
integer cohomology class
of the complementary dimension.

•

The orientation and the cohomology class are involved in topological
restrictions on mutual position of a slice with other real algebraic
variaties.

•

An irreducible
sliced
subvariety of codimension two is a
base of a real
pencil
of hypersurfaces.

•

Conversely, a non-singular base of a real algebraic pencil of
projective hypersurfaces is a slice.

To the best of my knowledge, real sliced subvarieties have not been
discussed
in literature. Below we consider their basic properties and study some
topological theorems in which they are involved.

Our considerations are
restricted to non-singular varieties, although they admit generalizations
to singular varieties.

2
Codimension two slices and pencils of
hypersurfaces.

The simplest class of sliced varieties are bases of generic real pencils of
projective hypersurfaces.

Theorem 3
.

A real algebraic projective variety of codimension 2 is a slice if and only
if it is a base of a generic real pencil of projective hypersurfaces (and,
in particular, it is a regular complete intersection of two hypersurfaces
of the same degree).

2.1
Digression on projective hypersurfaces and pencils.

Recall that a hypersurface in an
n
-dimensional projective space
over a field
\mathbb{F}
is defined by a single equation
P(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=0

where
P
is nonzero homogeneous polynomial in
n+1
variables with
coefficients in
\mathbb{F}
. Thus the hypersurfaces are parametrized by
the polynomials considered up to a nonzero factor. In other words, the
hypersurfaces form an
n
-dimensional projective space over
\mathbb{F}
.

A projective line in this space is called a
pencil
of hypersurfaces
of degree
d
in
\mathbb{F}P^{n}
. As a projective line, a pencil is defined by
any two of its points, that is by any two projective hypersurfaces over

\mathbb{F}

of the same degree.

Let
Y
and
Z
be
real
algebraic hypersurfaces of degree
d
in the
projective space of dimension
n
.
As projective hypersurfaces,
Y
and
Z
are defined by a single polynomial
equation each. Let
Y
be defined by an equation
R=0
and
Z

be defined by an equation
S=0
, where
R
and
S
are real homogeneous
polynomials of degree
d
. The pencil containing
Y
and
Z
consists of
hypersurfaces defined by equations

\lambda R(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})+\mu S(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=0

with
(\lambda:\mu)\in\mathbb{R}P^{1}
.

For a generic pencil, the hypersurfaces can be chosen to be nonsingular and
transverse. Their intersection is nonsingular subvariety of a codimension
two in the projective space. The intersection is called the
base
of the pencil.
The pencil consists of all irreducible hypersurfaces containing the base.

2.2
Proof of Theorem
3
. Sufficiency.

Let
X
be a real projective variety of codimension two which is a base of
a generic real pencil of projective hypersurfaces of degree
d
.
Let us choose two generic hypersurfaces
Y
and
Z
belonging to the
pencil. Thus
Y
and
Z
are nonsingular real algebraic
hypersurfaces of degree
d
in the
n
-dimensional projective space. Let

Y_{\mathbb{R}}
and
Z_{\mathbb{R}}
intersect transversally. Then
X=Y\cap Z
is a variety
of codimension two. Notice that
X
is nonsingular.

As projective hypersurfaces,
Y
and
Z
are defined by a single real
polynomial
equation each. Let
Y
be defined by an equation
R=0
and
Z

be defined by an equation
S=0
, where
R
and
S
are real homogeneous
polynomials of degree
d
.

Then
X=Y\cap Z
is defined by the system

\begin{cases}R(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=0\\
S(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=0\end{cases}

and differential real 1-forms
d_{p}R
,
d_{p}S
are linear independent
at each point
p=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in Y_{\mathbb{R}}\cap Z_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Consider the complex projective hypersurface
V
defined by the equation

R(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})+iS(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=0.

It belongs to the pencil at
(\lambda,\mu)=(1,i)
. At each point

p=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in V\cap\mathbb{R}P^{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}P^{n}\mid R(x)=0,S(x)=0\}=Y_{\mathbb{R}}\cap Z_{\mathbb{R}}

the complex 1-form

d_{p}(R+iS)=d_{p}R+id_{p}S
does not vanish and annihilates
T_{p}V
.
Therefore
V
has complex codimension 1 and real codimension 2
in
\mathbb{C}P^{n}
at each
p\in X_{\mathbb{R}}
. On the other hand,
X_{\mathbb{R}}
has real codimension
2 in
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
. Hence
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}P^{n}-\dim_{\mathbb{R}}V=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}P^{n}-\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X_{\mathbb{R}}
. This
equality implies transversality of
V
and
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
. ∎

2.3
Proof of Theorem
3
. Necessity.

Let
X
be a sliced real algebraic variety of
codimension 2 in
\mathbb{R}P^{n}

and
V\subset\mathbb{C}P^{n}
be a non-singular complex variety transverse to
\mathbb{R}P^{n}

such that
X_{\mathbb{R}}=V\cap\mathbb{R}P^{n}
.

As a complex projective hypersurface,
V
is defined by a single equation

P(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=0
, where
P
is a complex homogeneous polynomial.
Since
X_{\mathbb{R}}=V\cap\mathbb{R}P^{n}
, we have
X_{\mathbb{R}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}P^{n}\mid P(x)=0\}
.

By Theorem
2
above, the polynomial
P

cannot be real
, because
the set
V
of its zeros intersects
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
transversally.
Thus
P=\operatorname{Re}P+i\operatorname{Im}P
, and both
\operatorname{Re}P
and
\operatorname{Im}P

are non-zero real homogeneous polynomials in
n+1
variables.
Put
\operatorname{Re}P=R
,
\operatorname{Im}P=S
and denote by
Y
and
Z
the hypersurfaces
of
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
defined by equations

R=0
and
S=0
, respectively. Obviously, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

P(x)=R(x)+iS(x)=0\iff R(x)=0\text{ and }S(x)=0.

Therefore

X_{\mathbb{R}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}P^{n}\mid P(x)=0\}\\
=\{x\in\mathbb{R}P^{n}\mid R(x)=0\}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{R}P^{n}\mid S(x)=0\}\\
=Y_{\mathbb{R}}\cap Z_{\mathbb{R}}.

The tangent space of
V
at
p\in X_{\mathbb{R}}
is the annihilator of the complex
1-form
d_{p}P=d_{p}R+id_{p}S
. Since
V
is a nonsingular complex hypersurface,
its tangent space
T_{p}V
at
p\in X_{\mathbb{R}}
has complex codimension one.

Since
V
is transverse to
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
at
p
,

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}T_{p}V+\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}P^{n}-\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(T_{p}V\cap T_{p}\mathbb{R}P^{n})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}P^{n}=2n.

From this, by substituting expressions for the known dimensions, we get

(2n-2)+n-\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(T_{p}V\cap T_{p}\mathbb{R}P^{n})=2n

and

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(T_{p}V\cap T_{p}\mathbb{R}P^{n})=n-2
.

On the other hand,
T_{p}V
is the annihilator of the complex
1-form
d_{p}P
which is equal to
d_{p}R+id_{p}S
. Therefore the real
vector space
T_{p}V\cap T_{p}\mathbb{R}P^{n}
(which as we have seen has real codimension
2) has annihilator generated by real 1-forms
d_{p}R
and
d_{p}S
. Therefore

d_{p}R
and
d_{p}S
are linear independent. Hence
Y_{\mathbb{R}}
and
Z_{\mathbb{R}}
are
nonsingular and transverse to each other at
p
.

Thus
Y
and
Z
are real hypersurfaces nonsingular and transverse to each
other at each real intersection point and
X=V\cap\mathbb{R}P^{n}
is a base of
pencil generated by them. ∎

2.4
Real hypersurfaces with real part having codimension two.

It’s worth mentioning that for a slice real projective variety
X

of degree
d
and codimension 2, there is a
real singular
hypersurface

Y
of degree
2d
such that
Y_{\mathbb{R}}=X_{\mathbb{R}}
. One can define such a
hypersurface by equation
R^{2}+S^{2}=0
, where
R=0
and
S=0
are
equations of any two real hypersurfaces from the pencil with base
X
.
The hypersurface defined by equation
R^{2}+S^{2}=0
is the union of two
conjugate imaginary hypersurfaces from the same pencil:

R\pm iS=0
.

3
Digression on orientations

3.1
Orientations of a real vector space.

Recall that an orientation of a finite-dimensional real vector space
X

is a function
O
on the
set of bases of
X
taking values in
\{\pm 1\}
which satisfies the
following relation:

O(b)O(b^{\prime})=\operatorname{sign}\det T,

where
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
and
b^{\prime}=(b^{\prime}_{1},\dots,b^{\prime}_{n})
are
bases of
X
, and
T
is the transition matrix:
b^{\prime}=bT
. Due to the
relation, an orientation is determined by its value on any basis and there are exactly two orientations on any
X
.
Usually an orientation
O
is identified with the set
O^{-1}(+1)

of bases, on which
O
takes value
+1
.

This approach is quite confusing for a zero-dimensional
X
, because in
this case there exists only one basis of
X
, the one that contains no
vectors.
Still, on the 0-dimensional space, there are two orientations: one that takes
value
+1
on this empty basis, and another, that takes value
-1
on it.
These orientations are identified with
+1
and
-1
, respectively.

On the contrary, for a vector space of dimension
>0
, there is no way to
tell the difference between the two orientations, because there are
linear automorphisms exchanging the orientations.

A linear isomorphism
f:X\to Y
acts contravariantly on orientations:
an orientation
O
of
Y
is sent to the orientation

f^{*}(O):(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\mapsto O(f(b_{1}),\dots,f(b_{n}))
of
X
.

A distinguished orientations appear if the space has an extra structure
(for example, if it has a complex structure, see Section
3.2
),
or if it is involved into a configuration of spaces in which the other spaces
are oriented, see Section
3.4
.

3.2
Natural orientations of a complex vector space.

Let
X
be a vector space over
\mathbb{C}
. It can be considered as a vector space
over
\mathbb{R}
(by forgetting multiplication of vectors by non-real complex
numbers). For each basis
b=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
of
X
over
\mathbb{C}
, one can cook
up a basis over
\mathbb{R}
. There are many prescriptions to accomplish this.
The most popular two of them give
b_{\mathbb{R}}=(e_{1},ie_{1},\dots,e_{n},ie_{n})
and

b^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n},ie_{1},\dots,ie_{n})
.

Observe, that for
X=\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
b=((1,0),(0,1))
the bases

b_{\mathbb{R}}=((1,0),(i,0),(0,1),(0,i))\text{ \ and \ }b^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}=((1,0),(0,1),(i,0),(0,i))

define different orientations. Nonetheless, application of the same
prescriptions to all complex bases of the same complex vector space give
bases belonging
to the same orientation of the real space. (If the transition matrix
between two bases over
\mathbb{C}
has determinant
D\in\mathbb{C}
, then the transition
matrix for the corresponding real bases has determinant
D\overline{D}=|D|^{2}>0
.)

For the purposes of this paper, we have to make a choice.
We prefer the first prescription, and call an orientation of a complex vector
space
X
the
complex orientation
if it takes value
+1
on

b_{\mathbb{R}}
.

3.3
Complex orientations and isomorphisms.

Of course, a complex linear isomorphism
X\to Y
between complex vector
spaces maps the complex orientation of
Y
to the complex orientation of

X
.

What about complex
semilinear
isomorphisms?

Recall that a
semilinear
map
f:X\to Y
between complex vector
spaces
X
and
Y
is an additive homomorphism (i.e.,
f(v+w)=f(v)+f(w)

for any
v,w\in X
) such that
f(zv)=\overline{z}f(v)
for any
v\in X
and

z\in\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
X,Y
be complex vector spaces. Then a semilinear
isomorphism
f:X\to Y
maps the complex orientation of
Y
to

(-1)^{\dim_{\mathbb{C}}Y}
times the complex orientation of
X
.

Proof.

A semilinear isomorphism
f:X\to Y
maps a basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}

of
X
over
\mathbb{C}
to a basis
f(e_{1}),\dots,f(e_{k})
of
Y
over
\mathbb{C}
, while
the corresponding basis
e_{1},ie_{1},\dots,e_{k},ie_{k}
over
\mathbb{R}
is mapped to

f(e_{1}),-if(e_{1}),\dots,f(e_{k}),-if(e_{k})
of
Y
. Let
O_{X}
and

O_{Y}
be the complex orientations of
X
and
Y
, respectively.
Then
O_{X}(e_{1},ie_{1},\dots,e_{k},ie_{k})=1
, while

f^{*}O_{Y}(e_{1},ie_{1},\dots,e_{k},ie_{k})\\
=O_{Y}(f(e_{1}),-if(e_{1}),\dots,f(e_{k}),-if(e_{k}))\\
=(-1)^{k}O_{Y}(f(e_{1}),if(e_{1}),\dots,f(e_{k}),if(e_{k}))=(-1)^{k}

∎

3.4
Orientations in a short exact sequence.

In a short exact sequence

0\to A\xrightarrow{\alpha}B\xrightarrow{\beta}C\to 0

(2)

of vector spaces
over
\mathbb{R}
, there are a number of ways to determine orientation of any
of the three spaces from the orientations of the other two.
Unfortunately, there are several ways. For our purposes, we
choose one.

The relation among the orientations comes from relation among bases.
Let
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
be a basis of
A
, let
b=(b_{1},\dots,b_{p+q})
be basis of
B

and
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{q})
be a basis of
C
. These bases are said to be
related
by sequence (
2
), if
\beta(b_{i})=c_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,q

and
\alpha(a_{j})=b_{j+q}
for
j=1,\dots,p
.
Orientations
O_{A}
,
O_{B}
,
O_{C}
will be
called
related
by sequence (
2
)
if

O(a)O(b)O(c)\\
=O_{A}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})O_{B}(b_{1},\dots,b_{q},\alpha(a_{1}),\dots,\alpha(a_{p}))O_{C}(\beta(b_{1}),\dots,\beta(b_{q}))\\
=1

for bases
a
,
b
and
c
related by sequence (
2
).

This rule may seem to be
counter-intuitive: why not to start the basis of
B
starting with
the images of
a_{i}
. Our choice is forced, in particular, by the commonly
accepted rule about the orientation of the boundary of an oriented smooth
manifold: according to this rule the basis of the tangent space
T_{p}M

formed by an outwards vector followed by positively
oriented basis of the boundary
T_{p}\partial M
is positively oriented. Here

A=T_{p}\partial M
,
B=T_{p}M
and
C={B/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$A$}}
.

4
The transverse bundle of a slice

4.1
Transverse bundle.

If
V
is a smooth submanifold of a smooth manifold
M
and
p\in S
, then
a
transverse space

N_{p}^{M}V
of
V
at
p
is defined as
the quotient space

{T_{p}M/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}V$}}
. The fiber bundle
formed by transverse spaces at all
p\in V
is called the
transverse
bundle
of
V
in
M
and denoted by
N^{M}V
.

If
T_{p}M
is equipped with an inner product (say, if
M
is a Riemannian
or Hermitian manifold), then there is a canonical subspace of
T_{p}M

isomorphic to
{T_{p}M/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}V$}}
(the orthogonal complement of

T_{p}V
). This subspace is called the
normal space
at
p
.
The fiber bundle formed by normal spaces at all
p\in V
is called
normal
bundle
of
V
in
M
. It is isomorphic to the transverse bundle.

If
M
and
V
are complex manifolds, then the tangent spaces
T_{p}M
and

T_{p}V
are complex vector spaces. Hence,
N_{p}^{M}V={T_{p}M/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}V$}}
is a
complex vector space, too.

4.2
Complex structure on the transverse bundle of a real slice.

Theorem 5
.

Let
X
be a non-singular real algebraic variety
and
Y
be a slice subvariety of
X
. Then the transverse bundle

N^{X_{\mathbb{R}}}Y_{\mathbb{R}}
of
Y_{\mathbb{R}}
in
X_{\mathbb{R}}

admits a complex structure. The complex
structure is defined by a choice complex subvariety
V
of
X_{\mathbb{C}}
,
wnich carves
Y_{\mathbb{R}}
on
X_{\mathbb{R}}
(that is
Y_{\mathbb{R}}=V\cap X_{\mathbb{R}}
).

Proof.

Let us fix a point
p\in Y_{\mathbb{R}}
.
Since
Y_{\mathbb{R}}
is a transverse intersection of
X_{\mathbb{R}}
and
V
,

T_{p}{Y_{\mathbb{R}}}=T_{p}{X_{\mathbb{R}}}\cap T_{p}V.

Therefore,

N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{R}}}{Y_{\mathbb{R}}}={T_{p}{X_{\mathbb{R}}}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}{Y_{\mathbb{R}}}$}}={T_{p}{X_{\mathbb{R}}}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}{X_{\mathbb{R}}}\cap T_{p}V$}}

There is a canonical isomorphism

{T_{p}{X_{\mathbb{R}}}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}V\cap T_{p}{X_{\mathbb{R}}}$}}\to{(T_{p}V+T_{p}{X_{\mathbb{R}}})/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}V$}}

Further, due to transversality of
V
and
X_{\mathbb{R}}
at
p
,

T_{p}V+T_{p}{X_{\mathbb{R}}}=T_{p}X_{\mathbb{C}}.

Thus, we have a canonical isomorphism

N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{R}}}Y_{\mathbb{R}}\to{T_{p}{X_{\mathbb{C}}}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}V$}}=N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{C}}}V

of the transverse space of
Y_{\mathbb{R}}
in
X_{\mathbb{R}}
onto the transverse space of
V
in

X_{\mathbb{C}}
. The latter is a complex vector space, as a transverse space of a
complex variety in a complex variety. The isomorphism
defines a complex structure on the former space
N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{R}}}{Y_{\mathbb{R}}}
(which a
priori has no a complex structure, because neither
X_{\mathbb{R}}
, nor
Y_{\mathbb{R}}
have).
∎

4.3
Coorientations of a slice variety.

A submanifold with oriented transverse bundle is called
cooriented
.
An orientation of the transverse bundle is called a
coorientation

of the submanifold.

Corollary of Theorem 5
.

Any slice is
coorientable
. The coorientation is
determined by a complex subvariety which carves out the slice.∎

4.4
Behavior of a slice coorientation under the complex
conjugation.

Coorientation of a slice depends on a complex variety which carves it.
In particular, the coorientation reverses if the codimension of slice is
not divisible by four and the complex variety is replaced by its image
under the complex conjugation.

Theorem 6
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5
, the
varieties
V
and
\operatorname{conj}V
define the same
orientation of
N^{X_{\mathbb{R}}}Y_{\mathbb{R}}
, if
\dim Y\equiv\dim X\pmod{4}
, and opposite
orientations, if

\dim Y\equiv\dim X+2\pmod{4}
.

Proof.

The isomorphisms which bring the complex structures
from
N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{C}}}V
and
N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{C}}}\operatorname{conj}V
to
N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{R}}}{Y_{\mathbb{R}}}
are included
into the following commutative diagram:

\begin{CD}N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{R}}}{Y_{\mathbb{R}}}={T_{p}X_{\mathbb{R}}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}X_{\mathbb{R}}\cap T_{p}V$}}@>{}>{}>{T_{p}X_{\mathbb{C}}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}V$}}=N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{C}}}V\\
\Big\|@V{}V{\operatorname{conj}_{*}}V\\
N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{R}}}{Y_{\mathbb{R}}}={T_{p}X_{\mathbb{R}}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}X_{\mathbb{R}}\cap T_{p}\operatorname{conj}V$}}@>{}>{}>{T_{p}X_{\mathbb{C}}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}\operatorname{conj}V$}}=N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{C}}}\operatorname{conj}V\end{CD}

The complex conjugation induces
semilinear
isomorphism

{T_{p}X_{\mathbb{C}}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}V$}}\to{T_{p}X_{\mathbb{C}}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}\operatorname{conj}V$}}
.

The dimension of
N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{R}}}{Y_{\mathbb{R}}}
over
\mathbb{C}
is
\frac{1}{2}(\dim X-\dim Y)
.
Therefore by Theorem
4
the orientations of
N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{R}}}{Y_{\mathbb{R}}}
,
which are defined by the
complex orientations of
N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{C}}}V
and
N_{p}^{X_{\mathbb{C}}}\operatorname{conj}V
, differ by
factor
(-1)^{(\dim X-\dim Y)/2}
.
∎

5
Coorientations and integer cohomology

5.1
A cooriented submanifold as an integer cocycle.

Cooriented submanifolds represent
integer cohomology classes in a very handy way. Let us start with two
similar statements:

\bullet
An
oriented
closed
k
-dimensional submanifold
Y\subset X

represents a
homology
class
\in H_{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})
of dimension
k

with integer coefficients.

\bullet
A
cooriented
closed submanifold
Y\subset X
of
codimension

k
represents a
cohomology
class
\in H^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})
of dimension
k
with integer coefficients.

The former is well known. The latter is known, but not that well, and
deserves to be explicated, see bellow
5.2
.

If the
ambient
manifold
X
is
oriented
, then
orientations
and

coorientations
of a submanifold
Y\subset X

determine

each other. Then orientability and coorientability are equivalent.
The homology and cohomology classes realized by the same submanifold with
the orientation and coorientation determined by each other are

Poincaré dual
.

In a
non-orientable
manifold, the classes of orientable and
coorientable submanifolds are
detached
from each other.

Many important real algebraic varieties (e.g., real projective
spaces of even dimensions) are non-orientable.
Under the assumptions of Theorem
5
, the manifolds
X_{\mathbb{R}}
and
Y_{\mathbb{R}}

may be orientable or not. In the simplest example,
when neither of them is orientable,
X_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}P^{4}
and
Y_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}P^{2}
.
In this example,
Y_{\mathbb{R}}
is
coorientable
, as being a
slice.

5.2
Formal construction of the cohomology class of a cooriented
closed submanifold.

Let
X
be a smooth closed manifold of dimension
n
and
Y
be its smooth
closed submanifold of codimension
k
. Let
T
be a tubular neighborhood of

Y
in
X
.

A coorientation of
Y
defines orientations on all fibers of
T
and
also the Thom class

u\in H^{k}(T,\partial T;\mathbb{Z})=H^{k}(X,X\smallsetminus\operatorname{Int}T;\mathbb{Z})
, which
takes value
+1
on the orientation class of each fiber of
T
.
The integer cohomology class realized by
Y
is the image of the Thom class

u

under the relativization homomorphism

H^{k}(X,X\smallsetminus\operatorname{Int}T;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z}).

5.3
Relative and compact support cohomology classes.

The construction of Section
5.2
admits natural extensions and
variations. First of all, like cochains in the simplicial theory, cooriented
submanifolds can be equipped with coefficients (say, rational numbers). This
turns a cooriented submanifold to a representative of a cohomology class
with the corresponding coefficient coefficient group.

Further, submaifolds may be non-compact or have boundary and represent
cohomology classes in manifolds with boundary or non-compact manifolds.

In a smooth compact manifold
X

with boundary
, an
absolute
cohomology
class with integer coefficients is realized by a cooriented
submanifold

Y

with boundary

\partial Y=Y\cap\partial X
. The construction is literally
the same as in Section
5.2
.

If a cooriented submanifold
Y
is
closed
, then the homomorphism

H^{k}(X,X\smallsetminus\operatorname{Int}T;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})
can be replaced by

H^{k}(X,X\smallsetminus\operatorname{Int}T;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{k}(X,\partial X;\mathbb{Z})
. This gives a realization of
a
relative cohomology
class. Namely,
Y
realizes the image of the
Thom class
u\in H^{k}(X,\partial X;\mathbb{Z})
under the inclusion homomorphism

H^{k}(X,X\smallsetminus\operatorname{Int}T;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{k}(X,\partial X;\mathbb{Z})
.

If the ambient manifold
X
is
non-compact
, then a cooriented

compact submanifold
would realize a cohomology class with

compact support
.
For realization of a cohomology class with (usual)
closed support
, one
may need to choose a
non-compact
cooriented submanifold, which is
closed as a subset of
X
.

5.4
Functoriality.

Let
M
and
X
be smooth manifolds and
f:M\to X
be a differentiable map.
Let
Y\subset X
be a cooriented submanifold of codimension
k
and

\eta\in H^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})
be a cohomology class realised by
Y
.

If
f
is transverse to
Y
, then
N=f^{-1}Y
is a smooth submanifold of

M
, which has the same codimension
k
, and
f
induces a fiberwise
isomorphism
N_{M}N\to N_{X}Y
. Then the cohomology class realised by

N
in
M
is the image of
\eta
under
f^{*}:H^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})\to H^{k}(M;\mathbb{Z})
.

5.5
Intersection number of oriented and cooriented
submanifolds in a non-oriented manifold.

Let
X
be a (
non-oriented
) smooth manifold, let
Y
be an

oriented
smooth
k
-dimensional
submanifold of
X
and let
Z
be a
cooriented
smooth
submanifold of
X
of codimension
k
,
transverse
to
Y
. Since
\dim Y=\operatorname{codim}Z
, the intersection
Y\cap Z
consists of isolated points.
Assume that the intersection is finite (this is automatically true if
Y

and
Z
are compact). Define the
intersection number

Y\circ Z

as the sum
\sum_{p\in Y\cap Z}Y\circ_{p}Z
of all

local intersection numbers

Y\circ_{p}Z
which are defined as follows.

At
p\in Y\cap Z
take

T_{p}Y\subset T_{p}X
and map it to
N_{p}^{X}Z={T_{p}X/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Z$}}
by the
restriction of the natural projection
T_{p}X\to{T_{p}X/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Z$}}
. The tangent
space
T_{p}Y
is oriented (by the orientation of
Y
), the transverse space

N_{p}^{X}Z
is oriented (by the coorientation of
Z
).
The map is an isomorphism due to transversality of
Y
and
Z
.
If this isomorphism maps the orientation of
N_{p}^{X}Z
to the orientation
of
T_{p}Y
, then
Y\circ_{p}Z
, the
local intersection
number at

p
, is defined to be
+1
, otherwise it is
defined to be
-1
.

Usually intersection number is defined for transverse
oriented

submanifolds of complementary dimensions in an
oriented
manifold.
The definition above
is more general. Indeed, if the ambient manifold is oriented,
then the orientation
of one of the submanifolds is canonically converted to a
coorientation and the
definition above gives the usual intersection number.

If the manifolds
X
,
Y
and
Z
are closed, then the intersection number of

Y
and
Z
equals the value of the cohomology class
z\in H^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})
, which
is realized by
Z
, on the homology class
y\in H_{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})
, which is
realized by
Y
.

5.6
Products.

Let
X
be a smooth (non-oriented) manifold.

Cup-product.
If classes
\eta\in H^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})
and
\zeta\in H^{l}(X;\mathbb{Z})

are realized by transverse cooriented closed submanifolds
Y
and
Z
, then

\eta\smallsmile\zeta\in H^{k+l}(X;\mathbb{Z})
is realized by
Y\cap Z
with
coorientation obtained via an isomorphism between the transverse bundle

N^{X}(Y\cap Z)
and the direct sum of the restrictions of the transverse
bundles of
Y
and
Z
to
Y\cap Z
. Over any
p\in Y\cap Z
the
isomorphism acts as follows:

N_{p}^{X}(Y\cap Z)={T_{p}X/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Y\cap T_{p}Z$}}={(T_{p}Y+T_{p}Z)/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Y\cap T_{p}Z$}}\\
={T_{p}Y/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Y\cap T_{p}Z$}}\oplus{T_{p}Z/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Y\cap T_{p}Z$}}\\
={(T_{p}Y+T_{p}Z)/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Z$}}\oplus{(T_{p}Y+T_{p}Z)/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Y$}}\\
={T_{p}X/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Z$}}\oplus{T_{p}X/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Y$}}=N_{p}^{X}(Z)\oplus N_{p}^{X}(Y)

Cap-product.
If classes
\eta\in H^{n}(X;\mathbb{Z})
and
\zeta\in H^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})

are realized by by transverse oriented and cooriented closed submanifolds

Y
and
Z
, then
\eta\smallfrown\zeta\in H^{n-k}(X;\mathbb{Z})
is realized by

Y\cap Z
oriented from

0\to T(Y\cap Z)\to TY|_{Y\cap Z}\to{(TY|_{Y\cap Z})/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T(Y\cap Z)$}}\to 0,

since for any
p\in Y\cap Z

{T_{p}Y/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Y\cap T_{p}Z$}}={(T_{p}Y+T_{p}Z)/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Z$}}={T_{p}X/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$T_{p}Z$}}=N_{p}^{X}Z.

Evaluation of an integer cohomology class on an
integer homology class of the same dimension, considered above in Section

5.5
, is a special case of the cap-product.

5.7
Would be good in a textbook.

Representation of an integer cohomology class by cooriented submanifolds
can hardly be found in Algebraic Topology textbooks. Though, it is mentioned
in Wikipedia’s article on cohomology.

Modern expositions of homology theory tend to expel
geometric objects, like cycles and cocycles, from the major
theorems and hide them under the hood of technical definitions and proofs.

I believe that the material of this section would be highly appropriate
in a textbook.
It shows a
geometric part of the story, demonstrates that cohomology classes are
not less geometric than homology classes and disclose a nice geometry
of operations with homology and cohomology.

As tools for illustrating homology theory,
oriented and cooriented submanifolds may compete with integer cycles and
cocycles. Submanifolds provide more transparent illustration
(thanks to usage of transversality and pullback construction).
However, simplicial cycles and cocycles have two well-known
advantages.

Firstly
, the class of simplicial spaces seems to be broader
than the class of smooth manifolds.

Secondly
, there exist homology and cohomology classes of a
smooth manifold,
which cannot be realized by smooth oriented and cooriented submanifolds.
This was discovered by Thom
[
6
]
.

The problem solved by Thom was posed as realizability of
homology
integer classes by oriented submanifolds. However,
technically Thom considered realization of a
cohomology

class Poincaré dual to the homology one. In particular, he
considered realization of integer homology classes only in
orientable
manifolds.

As long as we speak about slices, both issues are
irrelevant
: we study
non-singular real algebraic varieties, which are smooth manifolds, slices
are smooth cooriented submanifolds.

From the viewpoint of homotopy theory the class of compact
finite-dimensional simplicial spaces is not really broader than the class
of compact smooth manifolds. A compact
finite-dimensional simplicial space can be embedded to a smoothly
triangulated
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in such a way that its
regular neighborhood would be a smooth manifold homotopy
equivalent to the original space.

Furthermore, many homology and cohomology classes with integer
coefficients are realizable.
In particular, all integer cohomology classes in a closed smooth manifold
of dimension
\leq 9
are realizable. All integer cohomology classes of
dimensions 1 and 2 are realizable. For any integer cohomology class
u
,
some its non-zero multiple
Nu
is realizable.

The realizability problem disappears if
closed smooth submanifolds are replaced by closed
submanifolds with singularities of codimension
\geq 2
(i.e., by a passage
from submanifolds to pseudomanifolds), or by chains made of
smooth compact submanifolds with boundaries and corners.

However, writing a textbook is not the purpose of this paper. For what
follows, we restrict ourselves on one application to topology of real
algebraic varieties. It is based on the classical construction of linking
coefficients of a cycle and cocyle, see, e.g. Lefschetz
[
4
]
,
interpreted in terms of oriented and cooriented submanifolds. In the next
section a generalization of linking coefficients is sketched.
We skip routine verifications.

5.8
Cap-linking of oriented and cooriented
submanifolds.

Let
X
be a smooth closed manifold. Let
A
be its
smooth closed
oriented
submanifold of dimension
n
and
B
be a smooth
closed
cooriented
submanifold of
X
of codimension
k\leq n+1
.
Assume that
A\cap B=\varnothing
.

Assume that the homology class
\alpha\in H_{n}(X)
realized by
A
and the
integer cohomology class
\beta\in H^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})
realized by
B
are of finite
orders:
p\alpha=0
and
q\beta=0
for some integer
p
and
q
.

Under these assumptions,
A
and
B
define a
homology class
\operatorname{lk}_{\frown}(A,B)\in H_{n+1-k}(X;\mathbb{Q})
. We will call this
class
cap-linking
of
A
and
B
. It depends not
only of
\alpha
and
\beta
, but of
A
and
B
, or, rather, on the isotopy class
of
A\cup B\subset X
. When
k=n+1
and
X
is oriented, then

\operatorname{lk}_{\frown}(A,B)\in H_{0}(X;\mathbb{Q})
is the linking number of
A
and
B

equipped with orientation dual to the initial coorientation of
B
.

The cap-linking of
\operatorname{lk}_{\frown}(A,B)
can be obtained by two constructions, which give the same result.

In one of them, we choose a smooth compact
oriented
submanifold
A^{\prime}

transverse to
B
with
\partial A^{\prime}=pA
and take
\frac{1}{p}A^{\prime}\frown B
.

In the other one, we choose a smooth compact
cooriented
submanifold

B^{\prime}
transverse to
A
with
\partial B^{\prime}=qB
and take
A\frown\frac{1}{q}B^{\prime}
.

Although
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
have non-empty boundary,
both intersections,
A^{\prime}\cap B
and
A\cap B^{\prime}
, have empty
boundary and are oriented closed
n+1-k
dimensional submanifolds.
Indeed, since
A\cap B=\varnothing
,

•

\partial(A^{\prime}\cap B)=\partial A^{\prime}\cap B=pA\cap B=p(A\cap B)=\varnothing
and

•

\partial(A\cap B^{\prime})=A\cap\partial B^{\prime}=A\cap qB=q(A\cap B)=\varnothing
.

As rational multiples of intersections of oriented closed submanifolds and
cooriented closed submanifolds,
\frac{1}{p}A^{\prime}\cap B
and
A\cap\frac{1}{q}B^{\prime}

are rational multiples of oriented closed submanifolds which define
elements of
H_{n+1-k}(X;\mathbb{Q})
.

In fact, this is the same element.
In order to prove that, make
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}

transverse to each other. It can be done without changing them near their
boundaries
\partial A^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p}A
and
\partial B^{\prime}=\frac{1}{q}B
since
A\cap B=\varnothing
.

Consider the intersection
A^{\prime}\cap B^{\prime}
. This is a smooth manifold. By
Leibniz rule,

\partial(A^{\prime}\cap B^{\prime})=(\partial A^{\prime}\cap B^{\prime})\cup(A^{\prime}\cap\partial B^{\prime})=(pA\cap B^{\prime})\cup(A^{\prime}\cap qB)

Hence,

\partial\tfrac{1}{pq}(A^{\prime}\frown B^{\prime})=\tfrac{1}{pq}\left((pA\frown B^{\prime})\cup(A^{\prime}\frown qB)\right)=(A\frown\tfrac{1}{q}B^{\prime})\cup(\tfrac{1}{p}A^{\prime}\frown B).

In fact,
A\frown\tfrac{1}{q}B^{\prime}
and
\tfrac{1}{p}A^{\prime}\frown B

are involved in
\partial\tfrac{1}{pq}(A^{\prime}\frown B^{\prime})
with opposite orientations,
cf. the formula for the boundary of cap-product for
y\in C_{n}
and
z\in C^{k}

\partial(y\frown z)=(-1)^{k}((\partial y)\frown z-y\frown\partial z)

in the usual simplicial complexes.

5.9
Linking number of a point and a curve on
\mathbb{R}P^{2}

Consider the simplest example of the linking number. The ambient
space is the real projective plane
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
. Cf.
[
7
]
, where the
linking number was used in the statement of Rokhlin’s complex orientation
formula. There it appeared under the name of
index of a point with
respect to a curve
.

On the projective plane, a single oriented point does not bound, because

H_{0}(\mathbb{R}P^{2})=\mathbb{Z}
.

A double co-oriented point
bounds a projective line passing through it, with a co-orientation,
which flips at the point.

This allows to consider the linking number of a double co-oriented
point with any oriented closed curve.

\operatorname{lk}_{\frown}(C,2A)=2
,
\operatorname{lk}_{\frown}(C,A)=1
. By a similar calculation one
can show
that
\operatorname{lk}_{\frown}(\mathbb{R}P^{1},A)=\frac{1}{2}
.

6
Linking of real algebraic varieties

In this section we come back to real algebraic geometry.

6.1
Linking of dividing curves and codimension 2 slice varieties.

Let
n
be an integer
>1
, let
A
be a non-singular real algebraic projective curve of type I in

\mathbb{R}P^{n}
. Let
A_{\mathbb{C}}
realize
d\,[\mathbb{C}P^{1}]\in H_{2}(\mathbb{C}P^{n})
.

Let
B
be a slice real algebraic variety of dimension

n-2
of the same space
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
. Let
V\subset\mathbb{C}P^{n}
be a non-singular
complex hypersurface transverse to
\mathbb{R}P^{n}
with
B_{\mathbb{R}}=V\cap\mathbb{R}P^{n}
, let

[V]=D\,[\mathbb{C}P^{n-1}]\in H_{2n-2}(\mathbb{C}P^{n})
and
A_{\mathbb{R}}\cap B_{\mathbb{R}}=\varnothing
.

As a curve of type I,
A_{\mathbb{R}}
divides
A_{\mathbb{C}}
into two halves. Let
H
be one
of the halves. Orient
A_{\mathbb{R}}
as
\partial H
.

As a slice variety,
B_{\mathbb{R}}
is cooriented. Since
A_{\mathbb{R}}\cap B_{\mathbb{R}}=\varnothing
and

H_{1}(\mathbb{R}P^{n})={\mathbb{Z}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$2$}}
, there is a well defined cap-linking number

\operatorname{lk}_{\frown}(A_{\mathbb{R}},B_{\mathbb{R}})\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 7
.

Under the assumption above,
\frac{1}{2}Dd=H\circ V+\operatorname{lk}_{\frown}(A_{\mathbb{R}},B_{\mathbb{R}})
.

Proof.

Since
H_{1}(\mathbb{R}P^{n})={\mathbb{Z}/\lower 2.15277pt\hbox{\small$2$}}
, there exists a smooth oriented compact surface

A^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}P^{n}
with
\partial A^{\prime}\subset A_{\mathbb{R}}
and

[\partial A^{\prime}]=2[A_{\mathbb{R}}]\in H_{1}(A_{\mathbb{R}})

Assume that
A^{\prime}
is transverse to
B_{\mathbb{R}}
. Then

\operatorname{lk}_{\frown}(A_{\mathbb{R}},B_{\mathbb{R}})=\frac{1}{2}A^{\prime}\frown B_{\mathbb{R}}
, where
A^{\prime}\frown B_{\mathbb{R}}
is defined in
Section
5.6
, which is the same as the
intersection number of
A^{\prime}
and
B_{\mathbb{R}}
in Section
5.5
.

Lemma 1
.

Form an integer cycle
R=H\cup-\frac{1}{2}A^{\prime}
. The homology class
[R]
realized by
R
is

\frac{1}{2}d\,[\mathbb{C}P^{1}]
.

Proof.

The following arguments are borrowed from Rokhlin’s proof of
the complex orientation formula, see the end of section 2 in
[
5
]
.
Observe that
\operatorname{conj}[\mathbb{C}P^{1}]=-[\mathbb{C}P^{1}]
, cf. Theorem
4
.
The group
H_{2}(\mathbb{C}P^{n})
is generated by
[\mathbb{C}P^{1}]
.
Therefore
\operatorname{conj}_{*}:H_{2}(\mathbb{C}P^{n})\to H_{2}(\mathbb{C}P^{n})
acts as multiplication by

-1
. Hence
\operatorname{conj}_{*}[R]=-[R]
and
[R]-\operatorname{conj}[R]=2[R]
.

The cycle
\operatorname{conj}R
is
\operatorname{conj}H-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{conj}A^{\prime}=\operatorname{conj}H-\frac{1}{2}A^{\prime}
.
Therefore
[R]-\operatorname{conj}_{*}[R]=[H-\operatorname{conj}H]=[A_{\mathbb{C}}]=d\,[\mathbb{C}P^{1}]

6.3
On the projective plane.

On
\mathbb{R}P^{2}
, a slice variety
B
of codimension two is 0-dimensional.
It is a base for a real pencil
\{C_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}P^{0}}
of real curves.

How to perceive a coorientation of
B_{\mathbb{R}}
? The real curves
C_{t}
, which
belong to the pencil, pass through the points of
B_{\mathbb{R}}
. While moving
in the pencil,
C_{t}
rotates around the base points. The
coorientation at a point of
B_{\mathbb{R}}
is the direction of rotation.

Along a curve which belongs to the pencil, the coorientations alternate. On
the whole projective plane, it is impossible to compare coorientations of
different points, because the projective plane is non-orientable.

A choice of an oriented real line
A_{\mathbb{R}}
disjoint from
B_{\mathbb{R}}
turns
rpp

to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and makes a comparison of coorientations possible. Moreover,
a choice of orientation of
A_{\mathbb{R}}
determines the sign of a coorientation
at each point of
B_{\mathbb{R}}
. The total sum of the signs over the whole
B_{\mathbb{R}}

is
\operatorname{lk}_{\frown}(A_{\mathbb{R}},B_{\mathbb{R}})
. Corollary of Theorem
7
gives a strong
upper bound for the difference between the numbers of points in
B_{\mathbb{R}}
with
different coorientations.
