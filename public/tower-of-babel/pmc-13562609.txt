Impact of Dyslipidemia on Clinical Outcomes Among Cancer Patients Treated With Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors
Authors: Yao Liang, Osamu Maeda, Shohei Ishida, Kenji Zennami, Tomokazu Kimura, Satoshi Inoue, Tomoyasu Sano, Yuta Sano, Yushi Naito, Kazushi Miyata, Dai Shimizu, Kazuki Nishida, Basile Chrétien, Mitsuro Kanda, Shusuke Akamatsu, Yuichi Ando
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to investigate the influence of baseline blood lipid profiles on therapeutic efficacy and adverse events (AEs) among cancer patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). We retrospectively analyzed patients with solid tumors who received ICIs, with a focus on their blood lipid profiles at baseline. Associations between dyslipidemia and clinical outcomes following ICI therapy were assessed using both univariate and multivariable analyses. A total of 99 eligible patients were included: 52 with renal cell carcinoma, 23 with urothelial carcinoma, and 24 with esophageal carcinoma. Among patients with renal cell carcinoma, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol ≥ 140 mg/dL (p = 0.013) and high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol ≤ 40 mg/dL (p = 0.040) were significantly associated with shorter overall survival. Conversely, among patients with esophageal carcinoma, LDL cholesterol ≥ 120 mg/dL was associated with longer progression‐free survival (p = 0.035). Among all patients, HDL cholesterol < 40 mg/dL was identified as a risk factor for Grade 3 or higher AEs (p = 0.018), especially liver injury (p = 0.033). Prior statin use was associated with a trend toward an improved response to ICIs among patients with renal cell carcinoma (p = 0.057). Baseline blood lipid profiles—particularly LDL and HDL cholesterol levels—may influence clinical outcomes among cancer patients receiving ICI therapy. These findings suggest that lipid profiles might serve as predictive biomarkers for the efficacy and safety of ICIs.Keywords: dyslipidemias, esophageal neoplasms, immune checkpoint inhibitors, renal cell carcinoma, transitional cell carcinoma

Introduction

The treatment landscape for malignant solid tumors has undergone remarkable transformation due to the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Despite these advances, a substantial proportion of patients fail to respond to ICI therapy or suffer from immune‐related adverse events (irAEs) [1], thus underscoring the urgent need for reliable biomarkers to identify patients who are more likely to benefit from ICI therapy and for early detection and timely intervention to mitigate the influence of irAEs [2]. Recent evidence suggests that blood lipid profiles may influence clinical outcomes among cancer patients receiving ICIs. A multicenter cohort study of patients with advanced solid tumors demonstrated that a total cholesterol level ≥ 200 mg/dL was associated with longer overall survival (OS), whereas a triglyceride level ≥ 150 mg/dL was correlated with shorter progression‐free survival (PFS) and OS [3]. Interestingly, the same group reported that statin use was associated with a higher objective response rate (ORR) to ICI therapy, as well as prolonged PFS and OS in patients with thoracic malignancies [4]. Additional retrospective studies have explored the role of lipid parameters. In patients with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treated with ICIs, increased high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels (cutoff: 0.94 mmol/L, approximately 36 mg/dL) have been found to be associated with a higher ORR and disease control rate (DCR), whereas both low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) and high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels were identified as independent predictive factors for progression‐free survival (PFS) [5]. Another study among NSCLC patients revealed that higher LDL and HDL cholesterol levels were predictive of longer PFS and OS following nivolumab therapy, although no association with DCR was observed [6]. The findings across these studies are not always consistent and can be challenging to interpret owing to variations in patient demographics, disease characteristics, treatment regimens, and the types and cutoff values of lipid parameters assessed. Nonetheless, to clarify the potential of lipid profiles as predictive biomarkers for ICI therapy, it is essential to accumulate robust clinical data, including retrospective analyses. Herein, we investigated the associations between blood lipid profiles and clinical outcomes among patients with solid tumors treated with ICIs.

Patients and Methods

Patients

We retrospectively analyzed patients with solid tumors who received ICIs, with a focus on their baseline lipid profiles. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) received ICIs such as pembrolizumab, nivolumab, avelumab, or ipilimumab between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2023; (3) diagnosed with renal cell carcinoma, urothelial carcinoma, or esophageal carcinoma; and (4) at least one pretreatment measurement of total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, or triglycerides within 1 week prior to starting ICI therapy. Patients who received ICIs as preoperative or postoperative therapies were excluded. The inclusion and exclusion criteria did not specify the presence or absence of co‐morbidities. This study was conducted in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, Japan) and the Declaration of Helsinki. Additionally, this study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nagoya University Hospital (approval no. 2025‐0105).

Clinical Features and Outcomes

In accordance with the guidelines of the Japan Atherosclerosis Society, dyslipidemia in this study was classified into the following categories: hyper‐LDL (LDL ≥ 140 mg/dL), borderline hyper‐LDL (LDL 120–139 mg/dL), hypo‐HDL (HDL < 40 mg/dL), hypertriglyceridemia (fasting or non‐fasting triglycerides ≥ 175 mg/dL), hyper non‐HDL (non‐HDL ≥ 170 mg/dL), and borderline hyper‐non‐HDL (non‐HDL 150–169 mg/dL) during the pretreatment baseline period [7]. Non‐HDL cholesterol is calculated by subtracting the HDL cholesterol level from the total cholesterol level. Patients in one or more of these categories were considered to have dyslipidemia. The other baseline clinical features examined herein included age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS), body mass index (BMI), statin use at baseline, surgical resection of the primary lesion, treatment line, and regimen (ICI monotherapy or combination therapy). BMI (kg/m2) was calculated as weight (kilograms) divided by height (meters) squared, with a cutoff value of 25. The endpoints were the ORR to initial ICI therapy, PFS, OS, and AEs. The response was assessed on the basis of the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 [8]. PFS was defined as the time from the date of initiation of first ICI therapy to disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. OS was defined as the time from the date of initial ICI therapy to the date of death from any cause or the last date at which survival was confirmed. AEs were assessed in accordance with the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0. We evaluated the associations between these clinical features, including dyslipidemia status, and clinical outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics are summarized as medians and ranges (for continuous variables) and frequencies and percentages (for categorical variables). First, univariate analyses were conducted to assess the impacts of dyslipidemia on treatment response, survival, and AEs. Additional univariate analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between both target lipids and clinical features and treatment response or AEs via two‐sided Fisher's exact test. Variables that showed p < 0.1 in univariate analyses were included in multivariable logistic regression analyses (except those associated with contingency tables containing zero counts). For both univariate and multivariate analyses, the results are expressed as odds ratios (ORs) accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CIs). PFS and OS among patients with different dyslipidemia statuses were compared using the Kaplan–Meier method. Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% CIs for PFS and OS were estimated through univariate and multivariable analyses using the Cox proportional hazards model. Given the exploratory nature of this study, adjustments for multiple comparisons were not performed, thus minimizing the risk of missing potential associations. The threshold for statistical significance was p < 0.05. All the statistical analyses were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0 (IBM Japan Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Results

A total of 99 patients were included in the analysis, comprising 52 (53%) with renal cell carcinoma, 23 (23%) with urothelial carcinoma, and 24 (24%) with esophageal carcinoma. Regarding the clinical stage among the 99 patients, 83 (83.8%) had post‐operative recurrence and 14 (14.1%) had Stage IV disease. All the included patients had at least one recorded measurement of total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and triglycerides prior to the initiation of ICI therapy (Tables 1 and S1). The median interval between the baseline lipid measurement and the initiation of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy was 3 days. While patients with severe diabetes mellitus, severe obesity, severe metabolic syndrome, or severe hepatic dysfunction were not included in this cohort, patients receiving medication for dyslipidemia were included. Dyslipidemia was observed in 65 patients (66%); among these patients, 34% had LDL cholesterol levels ≥ 120 mg/dL, 29% had HDL cholesterol levels < 40 mg/dL, 28% had triglyceride levels ≥ 175 mg/dL, and 28% had non‐HDL cholesterol levels ≥ 150 mg/dL. Additionally, 24 patients (24%) had a history of statin use prior to ICI therapy. Of the 65 patients classified as having dyslipidemia, 16 were receiving active lipid‐lowering treatment at baseline, including statins.

Objective Response Rate (ORR)

The ORR was 35% among patients with renal cell carcinoma, 17% among those with urothelial carcinoma, and 58% among those with esophageal carcinoma. Univariate analyses were performed to evaluate the associations of lipid profiles and clinical factors with the ORR in each type of cancer (Table S2). No significant associations were observed between lipid parameters and treatment response across all groups. In patients with renal cell carcinoma, prior statin use was associated with a trend toward an improved response to ICI therapy (OR, 3.71; 95% CI, 1.07–12.90; p = 0.057). Because only one variable had a p < 0.1 in the univariate analysis for renal cell carcinoma, multivariable analysis was not conducted.

Progression‐Free Survival (PFS) and Overall Survival (OS)

In the univariate analysis of PFS, the variables that were associated with dyslipidemia or prior statin use with a p < 0.1 were hypertriglyceridemia in patients with urothelial carcinoma and borderline hyper‐LDL in those with esophageal carcinoma (Table S3). In the univariate analysis of OS, the variables that were associated with dyslipidemia or prior statin use with a p < 0.1 were hyper‐LDL, hypo‐HDL, and hyper non‐HDL in patients with renal cell carcinoma and hypertriglyceridemia in those with urothelial carcinoma (Table S4). These variables, along with other variables that were associated with PFS or OS at p < 0.1, were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 2).

Among patients with renal cell carcinoma, no significant association was found between lipid profiles and PFS. However, patients with hyper‐LDL (LDL ≥ 140 mg/dL) had a significantly shorter median OS of 18.3 months than did those with LDL cholesterol levels < 140 mg/dL, whose median OS was not reached (HR, 4.52; 95% CI: 1.22–16.79; p = 0.024; Figure 1A, Table S4). Additionally, patients with hypo‐HDL (HDL < 40 mg/dL) tended to have a shorter median OS of 42.2 months than did those with HDL levels ≥ 40 mg/dL, whose median OS was not reached (HR, 2.78; 95% CI: 0.93–8.31; p = 0.068; Figure 1B, Table S4). In the multivariable analysis, hyper‐LDL (HR, 6.85; 95% CI: 1.49–31.48; p = 0.013) and hypo‐HDL (HR, 3.71; 95% CI: 1.06–12.92; p = 0.040) were identified as independent negative prognostic factors for OS (Table 2).

Among patients with urothelial carcinoma, no statistically significant associations were identified between lipid profiles and PFS or OS. The patients with hypertriglyceridemia (fasting or non‐fasting triglycerides ≥ 175 mg/dL) tended to have a longer median PFS of 7.8 months than did those with lower triglyceride levels of 3.2 months (HR, 0.46; 95% CI: 0.19–1.15, p = 0.096; Figure 1C, Table S3). In the multivariable analysis, hypertriglyceridemia seemed to be associated with a longer PFS (HR, 0.38; 95% CI: 0.14–1.06; p = 0.064; Table 2), while an ECOG PS ≥ 1 was also identified as a significant risk factor for progression. Patients with hypertriglyceridemia also tended to have a longer median OS of 21.8 months than those with lower triglyceride levels (HR, 0.40; 95% CI: 0.15–1.10; p = 0.076; Figure 1D).

Among patients with esophageal carcinoma, dyslipidemia, defined as abnormal values of one or more lipid parameters (LDL, HDL, triglycerides, or non‐HDL), was significantly associated with a longer median PFS of 12.3 months than 3.4 months in patients without dyslipidemia (HR, 0.37; 95% CI: 0.14–0.96; p = 0.040; Figure 1E, Table S3). Additionally, patients with borderline or worse hyper‐LDL cholesterol (LDL ≥ 120 mg/dL) had a significantly longer PFS (21.8 months) than did those with lower LDL cholesterol levels (HR, 0.28; 95% CI: 0.09–0.86; p = 0.026; Figure 1F, Table S3). According to the multivariable model, borderline or worse hyper‐LDL cholesterol (HR, 0.30; 95% CI: 0.10–0.92; p = 0.035) and surgical resection of the primary lesion were independently associated with better PFS (Table 2). To avoid duplicate assessments in the evaluation of LDL cholesterol, dyslipidemia was excluded from the multivariable analysis. On the other hand, the median OS was not reached in patients with dyslipidemia, whereas it was 15.2 months in those without dyslipidemia (HR, 0.15; 95% CI: 0.03–0.76; p = 0.022; Figure 1G, Table S4). In the univariate analysis of OS in patients with esophageal carcinoma, no variables other than dyslipidemia had p < 0.1; therefore, multivariate analysis was not performed.

Adverse Events (AEs)

Among all the patients, 42 (42%) experienced Grade 3 or higher AEs. The most common grade ≥ 3 AEs were anemia (7%), hyponatremia (7%), hypoalbuminemia (6%), liver injury (5%), and diarrhea (4%). The associations between major grade ≥ 2/3 AEs and lipid profiles were analyzed, and only models with lipid parameters with p < 0.01 are presented.

Univariate analyses revealed that patients with hypo‐HDL (HDL < 40 mg/dL) were more likely to experience grade ≥ 3 AEs (OR, 2.55; 95% CI, 1.01–6.19; p = 0.045; Table S5), grade ≥ 3 hypoalbuminemia (OR, 14.38; 95% CI: 1.60–129.31; p = 0.008), and grade ≥ 3 liver injury (OR, 11.00; 95% CI: 1.18–103.55; p = 0.025). Hypertriglyceridemia (fasting or non‐fasting triglycerides ≥ 175 mg/dL) was associated with the occurrence of grade ≥ 2 diarrhea (OR, 7.56; 95% CI: 1.79–31.84; p = 0.005). Prior statin use was significantly associated with grade ≥ 2 endocrine AEs (OR, 2.89; 95% CI: 1.10–7.60; p = 0.038) and grade ≥ 2 diarrhea (OR, 5.92; 95% CI: 1.51–23.21; p = 0.012).

In the multivariable analyses, hypo‐HDL was independently associated with the risk of any grade ≥ 3 AEs (OR, 3.78; 95% CI: 1.26–11.35; p = 0.018; Table 3), as were age and regimen. Additionally, hypo‐HDL was an independent risk factor for grade ≥ 3 liver injury (OR, 12.26; 95% CI: 1.23–122.40; p = 0.033; Table 3). Prior statin use was positively associated with the occurrence of grade ≥ 2 endocrine AEs (OR, 3.44; 95% CI: 1.19–10.01; p = 0.023; Table 3), along with regimen and female sex. Surgical resection of the primary lesion was a protective factor against grade ≥ 3 hypoalbuminemia (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, blood lipid profiles were specifically associated with clinical outcomes, including PFS and OS, among patients treated with ICIs across renal cell carcinoma, urothelial carcinoma, and esophageal carcinoma. Higher levels of LDL cholesterol and lower levels of HDL cholesterol were independent predictors of poorer OS in renal cell carcinoma patients, whereas higher LDL cholesterol levels and dyslipidemia were linked to better PFS and OS in patients with esophageal carcinoma, respectively. Additionally, lower HDL cholesterol levels were associated with the risk of severe AEs.

Increased baseline HDL cholesterol levels have been shown to be linked to prolonged PFS and OS in patients with NSCLC [5, 6], which is consistent with our findings in those with renal cell carcinoma. HDL‐cholesterol can strengthen antitumor immunity through multiple mechanisms, including promoting cytotoxic T‐cell recruitment [9, 10]; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. On the other hand, we found positive associations between low HDL cholesterol levels and the risk of grade ≥ 3 AEs, especially liver injury. Our results are consistent with those of a previous study suggesting that lower HDL cholesterol levels may serve as a predictive biomarker for chemotherapy‐related hepatic dysfunction in patients with NSCLC treated with chemotherapy [11]. Decreased HDL cholesterol levels may contribute to a proinflammatory environment by impairing their ability to modulate immune cell activation and neutralize inflammatory mediators [12], thereby stimulating the development of AEs.

We found that higher LDL cholesterol levels at baseline were associated with shorter OS in patients with renal cell carcinoma. This finding is consistent with a previous report demonstrating shorter PFS; however, it is inconsistent with another study that reported prolonged PFS and OS [5, 6]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in cancer types or patient backgrounds. Although the mechanisms have not been clearly established, LDL cholesterol may exert both beneficial and detrimental effects by promoting cancer cell proliferation or activating intratumoral natural killer (NK) cells [13, 14]. Furthermore, these opposing clinical observations likely reflect the divergent metabolic dependencies of these malignancies. Renal cell carcinoma, particularly the clear cell subtype, is a prototypical metabolically reprogrammed tumor that accumulates cholesterol esters and lipids through constitutive signaling pathways that promote lipid storage to support rapid tumor growth [15]. Consequently, elevated circulating LDL cholesterol may provide a readily available substrate for this lipogenic phenotype, thereby contributing to disease progression and poorer outcomes. In contrast, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma is frequently associated with profound metabolic disturbances, and lower serum LDL cholesterol levels have been linked to advanced tumor stage and disease progression [16]. In this setting, reduced preoperative LDL cholesterol may reflect tumor‐induced nutrient depletion or systemic metabolic alterations associated with aggressive disease, rather than serving as a driver of tumor growth. These distinct metabolic characteristics may explain the opposite prognostic associations observed between these cancer types.

On the other hand, higher triglyceride levels were associated with a trend toward improved PFS and OS in urothelial carcinoma patients, which differs from the findings of a previous study involving various solid tumors [3] but aligns with the findings that elevated triglyceride levels are associated with prolonged OS in patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [17]. By serving as both energy sources and structural components for tumor‐infiltrating T cells, lipids enhance nonspecific antitumor immune responses [18]. Additionally, higher triglyceride levels were associated with a trend toward grade ≥ 2 diarrhea in this study. The enhanced cross‐presentation capacity and increased secretion of proinflammatory cytokines by dendritic cells, which tend to accumulate triglycerides, may help explain our findings [18].

In the present study, prior statin use was associated with a trend toward a higher ORR in patients with renal cell carcinoma. By shaping an inflamed tumor microenvironment, statins improve the efficacy of anti–PD‐1 immunotherapy [19]. Since statins are commonly prescribed to manage dyslipidemia [20], their use may confound the observed relationship between lipid levels and both clinical outcomes and AEs in this study. In some patients, statin therapy may have normalized lipid profiles, potentially masking the true influence of baseline lipid levels on those outcomes.

Despite providing valuable insights, this retrospective study has several limitations, and its findings should be interpreted as exploratory. First, the relatively small sample size from a single institute inherently limits the generalizability of the findings and statistical power. Especially, because severe liver injury occurred in only a small number of patients (5%), the high odds ratio observed for the association between hypo‐HDL cholesterol and severe liver injury should be interpreted with caution. Second, the inclusion of various types of ICIs and combination regimens introduces heterogeneity in treatment exposure. Third, variability in the timing of baseline lipid measurements may weaken the accuracy of these biomarkers. Fourth, as this was a retrospective analysis, we did not perform experimental validations, such as assessing antitumor immune functions (e.g., NK cells and T cells), to clarify the underlying mechanisms. Therefore, the mechanisms discussed remain speculative and rely on previous literature. Fifth, the presence of mild co‐morbidities and an imbalanced sex distribution (77 males vs. 22 females) may have introduced unmeasured confounding effects and sampling bias. Sixth, lipid‐lowering therapy may have masked the true physiological impact of untreated baseline lipid levels. Finally, no adjustment was made for multiple testing, increasing the risk of false‐positive findings in the exploratory analysis. In this study, baseline lipid profiles were found to be associated with specific clinical outcomes. However, compared with previous reports, the results are not entirely consistent. Recently, increasing attention has been devoted to the relationship between body composition and the efficacy of ICIs [21]. Additionally, cachexia has been suggested to attenuate the therapeutic effects of ICIs [22]. Therefore, baseline lipid profiles may merely reflect certain underlying factors that are more reliable predictors of treatment efficacy.

Our findings suggest that lipid profiles might influence ICI efficacy and toxicity, highlighting the potential utility of lipid biomarkers to optimize patient management. Further research is warranted to clarify these relationships and inform tailored immunotherapy strategies.
