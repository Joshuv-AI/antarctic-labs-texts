Who knows what good looks like in medicine?
Authors: Graham Walker
Published: 2025

The 23-year-old with right lower quadrant pain in the emergency room gets his laboratory results within minutes. The CT scanner is immediately available. Within the hour, the surgeon has removed his appendix laparoscopically. He is home the next day, and he has resumed normal life in a week.

Most clinicians can describe this ideal. But in daily practice, we operate three standard deviations away from it. It is this very divergence between aspiration and actuality that poses a serious risk for clinical generative artificial intelligence (GenAI).

If we train AI systems on the average, rather than the ideal—on documentation shaped by billing pressures, time constraints and systemic inertia—then we should not be surprised when AI learns to reproduce our mediocrity. While many discuss concerns around AI hallucinations, the larger issue is the systematic replication of flawed norms. Put simply: if AI learns from our notes, it will inherit our shortcuts; if it learns from our specifications, it can aim for our ideals.

What we document is not what we mean

Many suggest that GenAI should be trained on data from electronic health records (EHRs), and colleagues from New York University (NYU) even showed that training large language models (LLMs) on clinical notes can have significant predictive abilities.1 Similar challenges have been noted in the context of clinical decision support.2 But these models are learning to predict what happens in our current system, not what should happen in a better one. These sources capture what was recorded—not necessarily what was intended.

In practice, documentation is shaped by reimbursement, regulation, liability and workflow. Progress notes have increased 60% in length over a decade, with over half now copied or templated.3 Capturing nuanced clinical reasoning is increasingly rare. AI trained on these data will not learn how we think; it will learn how we bill and code.

This reality means AI often absorbs the ‘convenient’ version of medicine rather than the ‘good’ one. And while no one wants bad care, no one should settle for average care. Yet if AI continues to learn from systems optimised for throughput and box-checking, it will scale exactly that. Here is the catch: we cannot ask for richer notes to train AI and, in the same breath, demand shorter notes to ease burnout.

The answer is not more words—it is a different kind of artifact altogether: a clinical standard that sits alongside the chart, not inside it. AI scribes may help reduce the burden of documentation, but they do not solve the core problem: the absence of a dedicated space for clinical reasoning. That requires something separate from the chart—a shared standard clinicians agree on.

Automating bias and repeating errors

Training AI on documentation alone does not just reproduce workflows, it reinforces their worst tendencies. Overtesting, overdiagnosis and low-value care are already embedded in the record. Diagnosis: Viral Upper Respiratory Infection, Treatment: Azithromycin. The AI sees this pairing often enough that it assumes correctness—why else would physicians link them? Examples abound: the stable patient with atypical chest pain gets laboratory studies, stress tests and cardiology referral, not because of evidence but because that is what the record shows.

These patterns do not just persist, they become gospel. Without explicit definitions for good care, AI learns what is common, not what is correct. The result is mediocrity scaled to infinity, a regression toward an average that no one would choose but everyone will receive.

This is why we need explicit definitions that capture what ‘good’ looks like. Hoping AI will magically sort out the good from the garbage in our notes is wishful thinking. And no clinician wants to document their failures for a lawyer to find later, which is why these specifications should live outside the medicolegal record, in a separate lane where they guide the future rather than expose the past.

Even if AI could eventually extract signal from noise in our flawed documentation, why should we accept systems that learn despite our records rather than from them? Why train systems to work around our failures rather than fixing the failures themselves? The specification approach forces us to articulate our actual standards of care.

We need clinician specification writers

In software engineering, specifications define what systems should do and what success looks like—a concept medicine desperately needs. Our unspoken care pathways and discharge criteria—obvious to us but invisible in the data—are these same specifications, yet exactly what AI cannot learn.

Consider appendicitis: for our healthy 23-year-old, ‘good’ might mean ”operating room within the hour and discharge the next day.” For an 85-year-old with the same exact disease, ‘good’ might still require a prolonged recovery or even goals-of-care discussions. Our expectations are different, and so our specs are, too. While we clinicians make these calls intuitively, the EHR flattens them into the same diagnostic code—and the AI inherits our data’s blindness to context.

To prevent AI from replicating mediocrity, physicians must become spec-writers, explicitly defining ‘good’ care across different contexts. That does not mean padding our notes with more text. Specs could be lightweight order sets, shared registries or simple consensus lists—things that travel across settings and time.

This is not an argument to abandon real-world data, but to add context to it. Insights from the EHR are essential for showing how care actually happens—where it succeeds or strays. But those patterns alone cannot define ‘good’. AI can use specifications as a north star, and use the EHR data as the map of the terrain. The feedback loop between the two is where real progress lives: the specs define the goal, and the data show us how far off we may be from it.

And on the AI side, developers should be required to show their work. A ‘nutrition label’ that makes clear whether a model was trained on textbooks, guidelines or raw EHR data is the bare minimum. Without that level of transparency, clinicians have no way to judge whether a recommendation reflects best practices or just average ones.

Of course, defining ‘good’ care is not without risk. Once something is written down, it can be used against us. A clinician-authored spec that diverges from practice could look like deviation rather than evolution. The way around that is not more documentation—it is creating a separate, protected lane for specs that evolve over time. Specialty societies or health systems could host living registries that track ‘what good looks like’ without tying it to any individual chart. Think of it as ‘open-source code’ for medicine—transparent, versioned and continuously improved. Getting started does not need a task force or grant. Pick 10 common reasons for hospitalisation—pneumonia, heart failure, appendicitis, diabetic ketoacidosis and so on—and simply ask clinicians, online or in person: What does good look like? Gather, compare and iterate. Over time, those collective instincts become shared specifications. Any medical society, health system or residency programme could host that conversation tomorrow.

This is not about programming. It is about making the unspoken explicit: what ‘close followup’ means in a febrile infant, or what ‘success’ in heart failure care really is: context-dependent decisions that—without clinician-authored specs—AI will never grasp.

The note is for the record. The spec is for the future. And only we can write it. If we do not, others—algorithms, administrators or insurers—will gladly define ‘good’ for us. The note records what happened; the spec should define what should happen. One is the past. The other is the future. And it is on us to write it.
