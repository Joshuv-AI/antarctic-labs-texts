Outcomes of pull-through surgery among children with Hirschsprung’s disease in Tanzania: a comparative study
Authors: Ramadhani Hassani Khamisi, Tom Samora Edward, Mohamed Salim, Zaitun M Bokhary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical treatment of Hirschsprung disease (HD) has been performed through different techniques throughout the surgical history. All techniques evolve through a process of seeking optimal surgical outcomes. Our center has been using two main techniques to address the effects of Swenson and Rehbein’s procedure. Reports are inconclusive regarding which technique provides optimal outcomes.ObjectivesWe aimed to review and compare outcomes between Swenson’s and Rehbein’s procedures in a sub-Saharan African cohort.MethodsWe reviewed records of patients who underwent Pullthrough (PT) surgery at a tertiary hospital. The primary outcome of the study was bowel performance following surgery. Chi X2 and Fisher’s exact tests were used. Logistic regression analysis was carried out to detect any associations among the outcomes.ResultsData from 55 patients were reviewed, and 49 were analyzed. These patients underwent surgery between January 1, 2018, and July 31, 2020. Thirty-one patients underwent the Swenson procedure. A total of 75% of the patients who underwent Swenson’s procedure had good bowel performance after surgery, whereas only 33% of those who underwent the Rehbein’s procedure had good bowel performance (p = 0.01). The two procedures were comparable in terms of the amount of time needed. The complication rate was higher in Rehbein’s procedure (77%, 14/19) than in Swenson’s procedure (41%, 13/31). Constipation and residual aganglionotic segments were found mainly during Rehbein’s procedure.Conclusion and recommendationThe Swenson pull-through technique demonstrated superior outcomes compared to the Rehbein procedure, with fewer postoperative complications and improved bowel function. These findings support the Swenson approach as the preferred surgical option in our setting. Nonetheless, prospective studies are recommended to validate these results and explore long-term functional outcomes.Keywords: Hirschsprung’s disease, Swenson’s procedure vs. Rehbein’s procedure, Surgical outcome of pull through, Redo surgery in HD patients, Sepsis related to the PT procedure. Introduction

Hirschsprung’s disease (HD) is a congenital disorder of colonic motility caused by the failure of neural crest cells to migrate fully to the distal intestine [1–3]. Also known as congenital megacolon, HD is characterized by an aganglionic segment of the colon, resulting in functional intestinal obstruction [1, 3]. The condition affects approximately 1 in every 5,000 live births, with a male-to-female ratio of 4:1 [1–4].

Pull through (PT) surgery, the definitive treatment for HD, can be performed as a primary (one-stage) or multistage operation that occurs 3 to 9 months after colostomy creation [4–9]. Commonly employed staged techniques include Swenson, Soave, and Duhamel procedures, while Rehbein’s technique is used less frequently [3–5]. In sub-Saharan Africa, multistage PT remains the predominant approach due to delayed diagnosis [5, 10–13].

There is no clear evidence that a particular multistage PT technique (Swenson’s, Rehbein’s, Duhamel’s, or Soave’s) is superior to another [1, 10, 14, 15]. Consequently, the choice of procedure often depends on the surgeon’s training, experience, and personal preference [1, 10, 14, 15]. Among these, Swenson’s procedure is the most commonly performed staged technique [5, 10, 11]. Rehbein’s procedure has become infamous despite being the simplest procedure [3, 5, 16, 17].

Many centers avoid Rehbein’s procedure due to concerns that it leaves a substantial aganglionic rectal segment in situ, potentially leading to persistent obstructive symptoms [5, 17–19]. However, because it involves limited pelvic dissection, Rehbein’s technique may theoretically offer better functional outcomes by avoiding sphincter disruption and reducing the risk of adhesions and infection. Despite these potential advantages, its limited resection scope may increase the likelihood of incomplete excision and postoperative obstruction [19–21].

In contrast, Swenson’s procedure involves opening the pelvic peritoneal reflection to allow for complete resection of the affected rectum. This approach is expected to reduce postoperative obstructive symptoms, although it may carry a higher risk of adhesions and a potential trade-off in terms of soiling due to sphincter involvement.

We designed this study to review the clinical performance of Swenson’s and Rehbein’s techniques for HD patients at our national teaching hospital, where both procedures are routinely performed without institutional preference.

This study provides a rare comparative analysis of Swenson’s and Rehbein’s procedures in a sub-Saharan African context, addressing a persistent surgical dilemma in resource-limited environments. By assessing both functional and safety outcomes and comparing current results with historical data from the regional institutions, we aim to offer regionally relevant evidence to inform surgical decision-making.

Methods and materials

Study design

We conducted a retrospective review at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) to evaluate outcomes of PT surgery for HD performed from 1 st January 2018 to 31 st July 2020. Data collection occurred over a six-month period beginning July 1, 2023.

Study site

MNH is a national tertiary and referral hospital located in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. The pediatric surgery unit manages general pediatric surgical cases and has a capacity of 70 beds.

Study sample and surgical techniques

We identified all patients with HD who underwent PT surgery at MNH. Patients were excluded if they had no histological confirmation of HD or underwent procedures other than Swenson’s or Rehbein’s techniques. All patients had a colostomy prior to PT. Preoperative preparation included bowel irrigation with normal saline through the stoma 3 to 5 days before surgery, followed by a liquid diet. Perioperative prophylactic antibiotics were administered to all patients and included intravenous ceftriaxone (50–100 mg/kg/day) and metronidazole (7.5 mg/kg every 8 h), administered preoperatively and continued for 48 h postoperatively. Duration was extended in cases of suspected infection.

Swenson’s procedure was performed via abdominoperineal incisions, involving resection of the aganglionic colon with the distal margin 2 cm below the recto pelvic peritoneal reflection. A coloanal anastomosis was then completed. In the Rehbein procedure, the aganglionic colon and rectum are excised via a transabdominal approach. A coloanal anastomosis is then performed, positioned no more than 3 cm above the dentate line, in accordance with Rehbein’s original technique. This approach aims to preserve continence while minimizing residual aganglionosis. Intraoperative frozen section biopsies were not performed due to lack of pathology support.

Three pediatric surgeons performed the procedures—two with over five years of experience and one with over 35 years, all trained in multiple techniques. Procedure selection was based on individual surgeon preference, as no institutional protocol guided technique choice. Decisions were influenced by surgeon experience, diagnostic resources, and evolving practice patterns. Swenson’s procedure became more common in the latter half of the study period, reflecting increased access to pathology services and a shift toward trans-anal approaches.

HD diagnosis was confirmed via full-thickness rectal biopsy and clinical presentation. Frozen section biopsies were not performed due to limited availability of intraoperative pathology services at our institution. Instead, definitive diagnosis was based on full-thickness rectal biopsy and clinical presentation.

Postoperative care and follow-up

Postoperative care protocols were standardized across both groups. Nasogastric decompression was maintained for 24–48 h, and patients received intravenous fluids, analgesia, and prophylactic antibiotics per institutional guidelines. Oral feeding resumed upon return of bowel sounds. Bowel management included dietary counseling, stool softeners when indicated, and monitoring for signs of constipation, enterocolitis, or incontinence. Complication surveillance involved daily clinical assessment and imaging when warranted. Patients were discharged once they tolerated oral feeds, resumed bowel movements, and showed no signs of complications. Follow-up visits were scheduled at 2 weeks, 1 month, and every 3 months during the first year, with additional visits arranged based on clinical need. Functional outcomes and complications were documented at each visit.

Data collection

We collected data on demographics, age at diagnosis and surgery, sex, histological findings, length of aganglionosis, associated syndromes and enterocolitis, surgical technique, operation duration, postoperative complications, hospital stay, readmissions, cause of readmission, redo procedures and indications, mortality and cause, stooling pattern, nutritional status (weight and height), and HIV/AIDS status.

Follow-up data were obtained from outpatient records. Functional outcomes were assessed via clinician notes and caregiver reports. Five deaths occurred during the study period and were excluded from functional outcome analysis. No other patients were lost to follow-up. Due to the retrospective design, outcome assessments were not blinded.

Results

Characteristics of study population

A total of 55 patients with HD underwent Swenson’s and Rehbein surgeries from January 2018 to July 2020. 49 patients were confirmed to have HD histologically, reviewed and analyzed. There were 36 (73.5%) males. The mean age of the study population was 46.5 months. Swenson’s procedure was performed on 31 (63.3%) patients, whereas 18 (36.7%) patients had Rehbein’s procedure. The mean follow up duration between the two groups was comparable. Review of patient records showed no systematic differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups. Age, sex distribution, aganglionosis length, and associated anomalies were statistically comparable (Table 1).

Postoperative surgical interventions were required in 9 of 18 patients (50.0%) who initially underwent Rehbein’s procedure, compared to 6 of 31 patients (19.4%) following Swenson’s procedure (AOR = 4.9; 95% CI: 1.1–21.2; p = 0.038). Functional outcomes

Functional outcomes were assessed in 44 patients; five deaths were excluded. The mean follow-up duration was 18.1 months for the Rehbein group and 21.2 months for the Swenson group. Voluntary bowel movement was evaluated only in children who had reached the developmental age for toilet training, with most patients being ≥ 3 years at last follow-up. Among these, voluntary bowel movement was observed in 22 of 29 patients (75.8%) following Swenson’s procedure, compared to 5 of 15 patients (33.3%) after Rehbein’s procedure (AOR = 0.1; 95% CI: 0.02–0.7; p = 0.019).

Constipation was more common in the Rehbein group (8/15; 53.3%) than in the Swenson group (5/29; 17.2%) (AOR = 6.3; 95% CI: 1.2–33.7; p = 0.031). Soiling was assessed only in children aged ≥ 3 years, as indicated in Table 3. Rates of anastomotic stricture and residual aganglionosis are also presented in Tables 3 and 4.

Discussion

The definitive treatment for Hirschsprung disease (HD) has been established for over half a century, requiring complete resection of the aganglionic segment of the colon. Multiple surgical techniques have since been developed, each aiming to optimize functional outcomes while minimizing postoperative complications. Historically, transabdominal approaches such as Swenson’s procedure (1948), Rehbein’s procedure (1960), Duhamel’s (1960), and Soave’s (1964) laid the foundation for HD surgery. More recently, the Transanal Endorectal Pull-Through (TERPT), introduced by De la Torre-Mondragon and Ortega in 1998, has gained popularity for its minimally invasive profile.

Advancements in surgical technology have further expanded treatment options. Laparoscopic-assisted pull-through procedures offer improved visualization and reduced morbidity [22], while robotic-assisted surgery provides enhanced dexterity and precision, particularly in deep pelvic dissections [23]. Despite these innovations, open techniques such as Swenson’s and Rehbein’s remain widely used in resource-limited settings, including our center, where they continue to offer reliable outcomes in the absence of advanced platforms.

This study compared the outcomes of Swenson’s and Rehbein’s procedures for Hirschsprung’s disease (HD) at a national referral hospital over a three‑year period, with the aim of determining which approach more effectively alleviates functional bowel obstruction while minimizing complications. Both techniques have been in use for decades, but their relative performance in resource‑limited settings remains under‑reported.

Of the 55 patients assessed, 49 met the inclusion criteria, with baseline clinical characteristics comparable to those in the initial analysis. Postoperative bowel function was notably better among patients who underwent Swenson’s procedure, with 75% demonstrating satisfactory bowel movement, compared to only 33% in the Rehbein group. Moreover, Swenson’s procedure was associated with a reduced likelihood of requiring repeat surgery, offering definitive treatment for primary HD symptoms. In contrast, patients treated with Rehbein’s procedure exhibited a higher incidence of complications, including persistent constipation, poor bowel function, and elevated mortality. Despite comparable operative times, hospital stays, stricture rates, and readmission rates between groups, the surgical site infection rate in the Rehbein cohort was markedly higher, approaching 50%. Additionally, residual aganglionic segments were identified in 26.7% of Rehbein patients, versus only 3.4% in the Swenson group. The overall mortality rate exceeded 10%, underscoring the clinical implications of procedural choice.

The comparatively better postoperative bowel function observed in patients undergoing Swenson’s procedure suggests its potential superiority over Rehbein’s approach. This finding aligns with previous reports in the literature [17, 18, 24], likely attributable to the surgical technique employed in Swenson’s procedure, which ensures that the rectal resection line extends beyond the recto-pelvic peritoneal reflection. This anatomical precision facilitates optimal approximation of the dentate line, contributing to improved functional outcomes [25]. However, in the absence of intraoperative histological confirmation via frozen sections, complete resection of the aganglionic segment remains challenging. This limitation may explain the 25% incidence of poor bowel function even among Swenson patients. Interestingly, the rate of persistent aganglionic segment positivity postoperatively (3.4%) was lower than the observed rate of poor bowel movement, suggesting that other contributing factors may be at play. Nonetheless, the hypothesis that non-aganglionic causes are responsible appears weak, given the strong statistical association with the Rehbein technique (adjusted odds ratio: 0.01; 95% CI: 0.02–0.7; P = 0.019). These findings underscore the need for a rigorously designed prospective randomized trial with adequate sample size to validate these observations and inform surgical decision-making.

Postoperative constipation was significantly more prevalent following Rehbein’s procedure compared to Swenson’s (53.3% vs. 17.2%; P = 0.013). This disparity appears to be primarily attributable to residual or acquired aganglionosis. It is interesting to note that Aganglionosis was notably more common among Rehbein patients (4/15) than those treated with Swenson’s approach (1/29), yielding a crude odds ratio (COR) of 10.2 (95% CI: 1–101.5; P = 0.039). Although other contributing factors may exist, their influence seems limited, as evidenced by the adjusted odds ratio of 9.2 (95% CI: 0.7–114.7) in multivariate analysis, with a marginal P value of 0.085. These findings suggest a strong procedural association with postoperative constipation, which may be further substantiated through a prospective study incorporating additional clinical variables not captured in the current dataset. Notably, similar conclusions have been reported in prior studies [16–18, 24], reinforcing the need for more robust, controlled investigations.

Importantly, postoperative constipation should not be viewed as an automatic indication for redo surgery, as multiple factors may underlie this symptom [10]. Our clinical experience supports the efficacy of conservative management in many cases, with patients achieving satisfactory outcomes without the need for repeat pull-through procedures. This observation aligns with findings by Han and colleagues, who identified delayed ganglion cell maturation in the submucosa and myenteric plexus as a potential contributor to obstructive symptoms. Such maturation delays may manifest as chronic constipation, which often improves over time with supportive care. In these scenarios, a follow-up biopsy performed 12 to 18 months postoperatively may be prudent to reassess ganglion cell development before considering surgical intervention.

Nonetheless, the presence of residual aganglionic segments following initial surgery remains a significant clinical concern—often described as a “nightmare” for surgeons, patients, and families alike. In their 37-year review of HD surgeries, Han et al. [21] reported that 3 out of 57 patients required redo procedures due to remnant aganglionosis. This pattern was similarly observed in our cohort and has been corroborated by multiple studies [7, 8, 16, 17]. Notably, Arts and colleagues have questioned the continued use of Rehbein’s procedure, suggesting it may be best regarded as a historical technique rather than a contemporary standard. Despite these critiques, the procedure persists in many centers—including ours—likely due to its perceived technical simplicity. While Rehbein’s procedure in its original version is designed to achieve a low coloanal anastomosis and complete rectal excision via a transabdominal approach, its technical demands may limit consistent execution. In our cohort, cases of residual aganglionosis likely reflect challenges in achieving full resection rather than shortcomings of the original technique. This paradox highlights the tension between procedural accessibility and long-term clinical efficacy underscoring the importance of surgical experience, intraoperative pathology, and institutional support in ensuring procedural fidelity. While residual aganglionosis was modeled as a dependent outcome, we acknowledge that its occurrence is procedural in nature and intrinsic to the Rehbein technique. This distinction is critical to avoid misinterpreting it as a surgical failure or avoidable complication. Our inclusion reflects its clinical impact on long-term bowel function and the need for reintervention.

Our findings align with those of Visser et al. (2010), who reported significantly worse outcomes with the Rehbein procedure compared to TERPT, including higher rates of obstructive symptoms (84% vs. 48%) and longer hospital stays. While our study compares Rehbein with Swenson rather than TERPT, the observed difference in postoperative constipation (53.3% vs. 17.2%) supports the broader trend of declining favorability for Rehbein. The smaller difference in hospital stay in our cohort (10.1 vs. 8.9 days) may reflect institutional discharge practices rather than surgical technique alone. The findings of Yan et al. (2019), based on a meta-analysis of 724 patients, reinforce the limitations of traditional transabdominal techniques such as Swenson, Duhamel, and Rehbein. Their study found TERPT to be superior in terms of hospitalization time, postoperative incontinence, and constipation, with no significant differences in operative time or enterocolitis rates. Our results—particularly the higher constipation rate in Rehbein patients—are consistent with these conclusions. However, in resource-limited settings where TERPT may not be feasible, comparative data on traditional techniques remain essential for guiding clinical practice.

Redo surgery emerged as another critical variable in our analysis, with a notably higher frequency observed in the Rehbein group (7/18; 39%) compared to the Swenson group (3/31; 10%). Multivariate analysis revealed that patients undergoing Rehbein’s procedure had a six-fold increased likelihood of requiring repeat pull-through surgery, with an adjusted odds ratio of 0.025 (95% CI: 1.2–40.9; P = 0.028). While poor bowel function and remnant aganglionosis were the predominant contributors to reoperation, other causes—including sepsis, anastomotic leakage, stricture formation, and persistent constipation—were evenly distributed across both procedural groups. These findings contrast with prior literature, such as Han et al. [21], who reported ganglionic pathology as the leading indication for redo surgery in 43% of cases (21/49). In our cohort, residual aganglionic segments were more prevalent in the Rehbein group (4/15; 26.7%) than in the Swenson group (1/29; 3.4%), with an adjusted odds ratio of 9.2 (95% CI: 0.7–114.7; P = 0.085). Although the confidence interval is wide, these data suggest a meaningful trend that warrants further investigation through a randomized prospective controlled trial to account for potential confounders and strengthen causal inference.

The need for additional surgical interventions following the initial pull-through procedure represents another significant and undesirable outcome identified in our cohort. Multivariate analysis revealed that such procedures—including laparotomy and protective colostomy—were markedly more frequent among patients who underwent Rehbein’s procedure (9/18; 50%), compared to those treated with Swenson’s technique (6/31; 19.4%). This translates to an approximately threefold increased likelihood of requiring a secondary operation when Rehbein’s approach is employed, with an adjusted odds ratio of 4.9 (95% CI: 1.1–21.2; P = 0.038). These additional and redo procedures impose substantial burdens on both patients and healthcare systems, contributing to prolonged hospital stays, increased caregiver strain, and heightened technical complexity during reoperation. The cumulative impact underscores the importance of selecting surgical techniques that minimize the need for subsequent interventions.

Sepsis and anastomotic complications emerged as among the most frequent adverse events following HD surgery in both procedural groups, affecting nearly half of the study population (24/49). These included deep tissue sepsis (n = 14), anastomotic leakage (n = 7), abdominal wall dehiscence (n = 2), and postoperative enterocolitis (n = 2). Notably, statistical analysis indicated that these short-term complications were not significantly associated with the type of surgical procedure, a finding consistent with reports from other centers [10, 21]. While no preferential risk was identified between Swenson and Rehbein techniques, the overall incidence of sepsis-related events—particularly enterocolitis—was markedly higher than global benchmarks [7, 10]. This observation underscores the need for enhanced perioperative protocols and infection control strategies. Although the underlying causes of this elevated sepsis rate remain unclear, future studies are warranted to explore procedure-specific risk factors and institutional practices that may contribute to these outcomes. One limitation of this aspect in our retrospective cohort is the absence of bacterial culture data, which could have provided deeper insights into the etiology and management of postoperative sepsis. A prospective study to cover this aspect is warranted.

The observed low rate of postoperative Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis (HAEC) in our cohort likely reflects under documentation and the lack of standardized diagnostic criteria during the study period. This limitation may have led to underreporting of clinically relevant episodes, and future studies should incorporate uniform diagnostic protocols to ensure accurate incidence tracking and comparison.

Anastomotic leaks were managed conservatively in most cases, using bowel rest, intravenous antibiotics, and close monitoring. Surgical re-intervention was reserved for patients with persistent leakage or signs of generalized peritonitis. Some patients underwent redo anastomosis plus protective stomas after thorough intraoperative assessment of tension or blood supply, and protective drain insertion. While these management strategies reflect institutional practice, the outcomes of these interventions were not systematically analyzed and are beyond the scope of this study.

Hemorrhage is reported at high frequencies among patients who undergo HD surgery, occurring in relatively equivalent numbers between the two groups. When the ratio between the two groups is taken into account, Rehbein’s group seems to have more hemorrhage cases. Despite this statistically insignificant difference in the rate of hemorrhage between the two procedures, surgical violation to involve the sacral venous plexuses and retraction of mesenteric vessels or rectal vessels, as reported by Mabula [10] and team in their report, could occur with either technique. The suggestion that Rehbein’s procedure carries more risk of bleeding, as reported by Visser [17] and coworkers, could be due to the nature of the technique being more liable to deviation from the proper pelvic plane of dissection.

Fecal soiling was observed at a slightly higher rate in the Rehbein group (2/15; 13%) compared to the Swenson group (2/28; 7%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Interestingly, Rassouli et al. [24] reported lower rates of fecal incontinence following Rehbein’s procedure relative to Swenson and other techniques, suggesting potential variability in outcomes across centers. The underlying cause of the increased soiling rate in our Rehbein cohort remains unclear; however, prior literature has implicated mucosal erosion as a contributing factor [3, 20]. Notably, both procedures demonstrated comparable anastomotic quality in terms of stricture formation, indicating that technical difficulty in achieving a secure anastomosis is unlikely to explain the observed differences. These findings underscore the need for a robust prospective study focused on postoperative bowel function, which could elucidate the multifactorial contributors to fecal soiling and guide improvements in surgical technique and follow-up care.

Mortality and morbidity are other variables that were examined in this report, where the overall mortality was 10%. This rate is high, albeit relatively low compared with other reports ([4, 7, 10], and [12]). In the multivariate analysis, most patients died because of sepsis (6.1%), with an adjusted odds ratio of 13 (95% CI of 0.6–270.4), a P value of 0.094 and intra-abdominal hemorrhage (4.1%). The observed mortality rate of 10.2%, while lower than some previously reported rates in similar resource-limited settings, remains significantly higher than those reported in high-income countries. Most deaths occurred within 30 days postoperatively and were attributed to hemorrhage or sepsis. Notably, all hemorrhage-related deaths followed Rehbein’s procedure, raising concerns about intraoperative bleeding risk associated with this technique. Sepsis-related deaths were linked to anastomotic complications and wound infections. These outcomes reflect systemic challenges including delayed presentation, limited access to intensive care, and high rates of comorbidities such as malnutrition and immunosuppression. While some deaths may have been preventable with earlier intervention and enhanced perioperative support, these findings underscore the urgent need for improved infrastructure, early diagnosis, and standardized postoperative monitoring protocols in low-resource settings.

A meaningful comparison can be drawn with Mabula et al. (2014), who studied 110 patients from the same hospital system. Both studies share similar demographic profiles, with a predominance of male patients and Swenson’s procedure as the most commonly performed technique. However, our study demonstrates a notable reduction in mortality (10.2% vs. 21.8%), suggesting improvements in surgical expertise and perioperative management over time. While Mabula et al. compared multiple techniques; our focused analysis of Swenson versus Rehbein procedures provides a more detailed assessment of technique-specific outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of institutional experience and continuous quality improvement in pediatric surgical care.

This study also raises important ethical considerations. The continued use of the Rehbein procedure, despite its documented inferiority compared to the Swenson technique warrants critical reflection. In our context, this persistence has been driven by systemic challenges such as limited access to frozen section pathology, constrained laparoscopic infrastructure, and variability in surgical training. Nonetheless, the ethical imperative to provide the highest standard of care remains paramount. Our findings underscore the need for ongoing surgical audits, investment in capacity-building, and a commitment to transitioning toward evidence-based practices that optimize patient outcomes.”

Conclusion and recommendations

This analysis clearly revealed that Swenson’s procedure results in better overall clinical outcomes than does Rehbein’s technique in terms of functional bowel performance and has a lower incidence of short-term complications that are less likely to cause mortality in our setting. In contrast, Rehbein’s PT patients demonstrated a greater chance of having additional surgery, redo PT, constipation, and significantly higher rates of aganglionosis.

To improve, the center must now consider utilizing Swenson’s PT technique in preference to Rehbein’s technique; a prospective study is warranted to investigate the underlying contributors to sepsis and related complications, which remain a major cause of postoperative mortality. The routine use of intraoperative frozen section analysis should be prioritized to minimize the risk of remnant aganglionosis and reduce the need for reoperation. Our center is yet to commit to full abandon Rehbein’s procedure instead is preparing to conduct a prospective study that will address current limitations and guide a more evidence-based transition in practice.

This study offers a comparative evaluation of Swenson and Rehbein procedures within a sub-Saharan African context, where traditional techniques persist due to limited access to newer modalities such as TERPT. Our findings reinforce global concerns regarding Rehbein’s continued use and provide region-specific evidence to guide surgical decision-making. While Swenson’s superiority has been well established in high-resource settings, this study confirms its relevance and efficacy in low-resource environments.

Ultimately, these results underscore the importance of aligning clinical practice with both international evidence and local realities. They highlight the need for ongoing outcome monitoring, investment in perioperative infrastructure, and the development of context-sensitive surgical protocols. By embracing evidence-based approaches and strengthening institutional capacity, pediatric surgical care for HD can be significantly improved across underserved populations.
