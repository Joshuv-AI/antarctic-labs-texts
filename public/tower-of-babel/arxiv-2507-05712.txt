Abstract
. We establish interior Lipschitz regularity for solutions to anisotropic fully nonlinear equations with nonstandard growth, without imposing any restriction on the gap between the highest and lowest growth exponents. Our proof is based on an anisotropic variant of the seminal Ishii–Lions method. Our result furnishes a viscosity analogue of the divergence-form theory in [ 5 ] , adapted to the non-divergence setting.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study the interior Lipschitz regularity of viscosity solutions of the following degenerate anisotropic fully nonlinear elliptic equation:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle F(D^{2}u,Du,x)=f(x,Du)\quad\text{in }B_{1},

where
\displaystyle F=F(M,z,x)
satisfies

(1.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda\left\lVert\left[|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}\right]N\left[|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}\right]\right\rVert\leq F(M+N,z,x)-F(M,z,x)\leq\Lambda\left\lVert\left[|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}\right]N\left[|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}\right]\right\rVert

for any matrix
\displaystyle N\geq 0
and constants
\displaystyle 0\leq p_{1}\leq\cdots\leq p_{n}
, where
\displaystyle\left[|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}\right]
is the diagonal matrix with entries
\displaystyle|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}
on the diagonal.
The main point is the present paper is that there is no assumption about the closeness of
\displaystyle p_{1}
and
\displaystyle p_{n}
; for example, an upper bound of
\displaystyle p_{n}/p_{1}
or
\displaystyle p_{n}-p_{1}
which is the condition imposed in the earlier papers as in
[
21
]
.

The (degenerate) anisotropic Laplacian operator, which is defined as

(1.3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{(p_{i})}u=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}(|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}-2}\partial_{i}u)=\sum_{i}(p_{i}-1)|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}-2}\partial_{ii}u

for
\displaystyle p_{i}\geq 0
, has been a classical research topic in the area of PDEs and extensively studied by many authors.
It is, in the variational sense, Euler-Lagrange equation of the following orthotropic energy functional

(1.4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle w\mapsto\int\sum_{i}\frac{1}{p_{i}}|\partial_{i}w|^{p_{i}}dx.

Note that the anisotropic
\displaystyle(p_{i})
-Laplacian is more degenerate than the
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian, given by

\Delta_{p}u=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\partial_{i}u).

While the
\displaystyle p
-laplacian degenerates only when
\displaystyle|\nabla u|=0
, the anisotropic
\displaystyle(p_{i})
-Laplacian degenerates when
\displaystyle\partial_{i}u=0
for some
\displaystyle i
.
This makes the analysis more difficult and delicate since the equation can be degenerate where the gradient is large enough.
Thus, even for the pseudo
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian case when
\displaystyle p_{i}
are all the same, the Lipschitz regularity was not known until recently;
Bousquet, Brasco, Leone, and Verde
[
8
]
proved the Lipschitz regularity for the weak solution. See also
[
6
,
30
,
9
]
.
Furthermore, Demengel
[
20
]
proved the Lipschitz regularity for the viscosity solution, and also considered a more general class of the Pucci operators together with Birindelli
[
4
]
using the Ishii-Lions method.

Even if the anisotropic degeneracy is ignored, the equation still exhibits a nonstandard growth condition due to the variability of
\displaystyle(p_{i})
.
The nonstandard growth condition means that

(1.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle w\mapsto\int F(Dw)dx,\quad\text{ where }\quad|z|^{p_{1}}\lesssim F(z)\lesssim|z|^{p_{1}}+|z|^{p_{n}},

which was considered by Marcellini
[
32
,
33
]
.
If
\displaystyle p_{n}/p_{1}
is large, then there exist well-known unbounded counterexamples by Giaquinta
[
25
]
, Marcellini
[
32
,
33
]
and Hong
[
27
]
.
For the boundedness for weak solutions of (
1.3
), we require that the gap between
\displaystyle p_{1}
and
\displaystyle p_{n}
be small, and the known optimal condition, established by Fusco and Sbordone
[
23
]
, is
\displaystyle p_{n}\leq\overline{p}^{\star}
where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\overline{p}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{p_{i}},\quad\overline{p}^{\star}=\frac{n\overline{p}}{n-\overline{p}}.

Cupini, Marcellini and Mascolo
[
18
,
19
]
extended this result to more general functionals. See also
[
22
,
24
,
14
,
26
,
16
,
3
,
2
,
12
,
1
,
31
]
.
On the other hand, if we only consider bounded solutions, it is interesting to see what is the condition on
\displaystyle p_{1}
and
\displaystyle p_{n}
in order to get the regularity result.

Surprisingly, assuming the boundedness of the solution a priori, Bousquet and Brasco
[
5
]
recently proved that
the weak solution of (
1.3
) is Lipschitz continuous without any assumption on the closeness of
\displaystyle p_{1}
and
\displaystyle p_{n}
.
The method used in
[
5
]
is based on a Caccioppoli-type inequality, Moser-type iteration and higher integrability of each directional derivative with multiple iterative scheme, which cannot be applicable to viscosity solutions under consideration in this paper.
See also
[
7
]
for the singular case.

On the other hand, from the viewpoint of viscosity solutions, Demengel
[
21
]
proved the Lipschitz regularity of viscosity solutions of (
1.1
) under the anisotropic
\displaystyle(p_{i})
assumption (
1.2
) and some structural conditions by using the Ishii-Lions method. However, the condition

(1.6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle p_{n}-p_{1}<1

appears to be essential in their approach.
Since a viscosity solution is defined only for continuous functions and is therefore automatically bounded, we believe that the condition (
1.6
) can be removed, as in the case of bounded weak solutions.
We believe that condition (
1.6
) arises from the nonstandard growth condition (
1.5
) for bounded solutions, as discussed in
[
13
,
17
,
10
]
.
The main difficulty in dealing with nonstandard growth lies in the ellipticity ratio, defined by

(1.7)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{F}(z):=\frac{\text{highest eigenvalue of }\partial_{zz}F(z)}{\text{lowest eigenvalue of }\partial_{zz}F(z)}\lesssim 1+|z|^{p_{n}-p_{1}},

which can blow up when
\displaystyle|z|\rightarrow\infty
potentially leading to irregularity of the solution when
\displaystyle p_{n}-p_{1}
is large.
However, if we consider the ellipticity ratio of (
1.4
) in each direction, then

(1.8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{F}^{i}(z):=\frac{\max\{\partial_{z_{i}z_{i}}F(z)\}}{\min\{\partial_{z_{i}z_{i}}F(z)\}}\lesssim C

which remains bounded regardless of the values of
\displaystyle p_{1}
and
\displaystyle p_{n}
is, even though the growth of each directional derivative
\displaystyle\partial_{z_{i}z_{i}}F(z)
may differ. Therefore, by carefully treating each direction of the anisotropic equation separately, without mixing them, one may obtain regularity results without relying on the condition (
1.6
).
In fact, in
[
21
]
every function used in the proof via the Ishii-Lion method is isotropic.
As a result, without incorporating anisotropic information, the issue of the nonstandard growth ellipticity ratio (
1.7
) becomes unavoidable, and the assumption (
1.6
) remains necessary.
Therefore, in this work, we consider anisotropic test functions within the Ishii–Lions framework to take advantage of the directional ellipticity bound (
1.8
) and establish Lipschitz regularity without relying on the assumption (
1.6
).

We state the main assumptions on (
1.1
) as follows.
For any
\displaystyle x,y\in B_{1}
,
\displaystyle z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\displaystyle M,N\in S(n)
, we assume

(A1):

\displaystyle F
is anisotropic
\displaystyle(p_{i})
-growth with
\displaystyle 0\leq p_{1}\leq\cdots\leq p_{n}
in the sense that there holds

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{-}_{\lambda,\Lambda}(\left[|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}\right]N\left[|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}\right])\leq F(M+N,z,x)-F(M,z,x)\leq\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\lambda,\Lambda}(\left[|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}\right]N\left[|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}\right])

with some constants
\displaystyle 0<\lambda<\Lambda
.

(A2):

For any
\displaystyle h\in\mathbb{R}
,
\displaystyle F
satisfies

\displaystyle\displaystyle|F(M,z+he_{i},x)-F(M,z,x)|\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Lambda\left||z_{i}+h|^{p_{i}}-|z_{i}|^{p_{i}}\right||M_{ii}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Lambda\sum_{j\neq i}\left(\left||z_{i}+h|^{p_{i}/2}-|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}\right||z_{j}|^{p_{j}/2}|M_{ij}|\right).

(A3):

\displaystyle F
satisfies

\displaystyle\displaystyle|F(M,z,x)-F(M,z,y)|\leq\Lambda|x-y|^{\chi}\sum_{i,j}|z_{i}|^{p_{i}/2}|z_{j}|^{p_{j}/2}|M_{ij}|,

for some
\displaystyle 0<\chi\leq 1
.

(A4):

\displaystyle f\in C(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
with

\displaystyle\displaystyle|f(x,z)|\leq C_{f}(1+\sum_{i}|z_{i}|^{p_{i}+1}),

for some constant
\displaystyle C_{f}>0
.

Some examples of models satisfying the above assumptions are:

(1)

the anisotropic
\displaystyle(p_{i})
-Laplacian equation (
1.3
) with Hölder continuous coefficients:

(1.9)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i}a_{i}(x)|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}}\partial_{ii}u=f(x),

where
\displaystyle a_{i}\in C^{\chi}
for some
\displaystyle\chi>0
and
\displaystyle 1\leq a_{i}(x)\leq\Lambda
.

(2)

the anisotropic Pucci operator in
[
4
]
:

(1.10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\pm}_{\lambda,\Lambda}\left(\left[|D_{i}u|^{p_{i}/2}\right]D^{2}u\left[|D_{i}u|^{p_{i}/2}\right]\right)=f(x),

for some
\displaystyle 0<\lambda\leq\Lambda
.

(3)

More generally,

(1.11)

\displaystyle\displaystyle F(D^{2}u,Du,x):=G\left(\left[a_{i}(x)|D_{i}u|^{p_{i}/2}\right]D^{2}u\left[a_{i}(x)|D_{i}u|^{p_{i}/2}\right]\right)=f(x),

where
\displaystyle a_{i}\in C^{\chi}
with
\displaystyle\chi>0
,
\displaystyle 1\leq a_{i}(x)\leq\Lambda
and
\displaystyle G=G(M)
is uniformly elliptic in the sense that

\mathcal{M}^{-}_{\lambda,\Lambda}(N)\leq G(M+N)-G(M)\leq\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\lambda,\Lambda}(N)

for any
\displaystyle M,N\in S(n)
.

For
\displaystyle 0\leq p_{1}\leq\cdots\leq p_{m}\leq\cdots\leq p_{n}
, we denote by
\displaystyle p_{m}
to be the smallest number which is not
\displaystyle 0
.
We state the main theorem below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\displaystyle u\in C(B_{1})
be a viscosity solution of the anisotropic problem

\displaystyle\displaystyle F(D^{2}u,Du,x)=f(x,Du)\quad\text{in }\ B_{1}

under the assumptions
(A1):
-
(A4):
.
Then
\displaystyle u\in\operatorname{Lip}(B_{1/2})
with the estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\operatorname{Lip}(B_{1/2})}\leq C(n,\lambda,\Lambda,p_{m},p_{n},C_{f},\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}).

We gives some remarks of the theorem.

•

The assumption
(A1):
was first considered by Demengel in
[
21
]
.
Observe that (
1.2
) is equivalent to the assumption
(A1):
.
However, the assumption
(A2):
and
(A3):
differ from those in
[
21
]
.
In that work,
the following conditions

\displaystyle\displaystyle|F(M,z+he_{i},x)-F(M,z,x)|\leq\Lambda||z_{i}+h|^{p_{i}}-|z_{i}|^{p_{i}}|M|,

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle|F(M,z,x)-F(M,z,y)|\leq\Lambda|x-y|^{\chi}\sum_{i}|z_{i}|^{p_{i}}|M|.

were considered instead of
(A2):
and
(A3):
, respectively.
Observe that the two assumptions above involve
\displaystyle|M|
, which can be interpreted as information about the combined behavior across all directions. However, in the anisotropic setting where the growth may differ by direction, it is necessary to extract directional information. Therefore, we instead consider assumptions involving the individual components
\displaystyle|M_{ij}|
.

•

In fact, for weak solutions
[
5
]
, the authors consider only the case with homogeneous right-hand side,
\displaystyle f=0
.
It appears that, by employing the similar approach used in
[
5
]
, one could obtain Lipschitz regularity with
\displaystyle f\neq 0
, though at the cost of substantial additional computation.
On the other hand, our approach here allows for handling the nonhomogeneous case
\displaystyle f\neq 0
more easily, but only when
\displaystyle f\in L^{\infty}
, not
\displaystyle L^{\gamma}
for some
\displaystyle\gamma>n
.

We now introduce main ideas and novelties of our proof.
We intend to design a suitable anisotropic test function so that each direction exhibits even growth and compute each directional component separately without mixing them.
To this end, we employ the celebrated Ishii–Lions technique developed in
[
15
]
.
First, we prove that
\displaystyle u
is
\displaystyle C^{\gamma}
for any
\displaystyle\gamma<1
close to 1, and then prove the Lipschitz continuity in light of
\displaystyle C^{\gamma}
-continuity.
Recall that in the Ishii-Lions technique, the main goal is to show that, for sufficiently large constants
\displaystyle L,K>0

\displaystyle\displaystyle M=\max_{x,y\in\overline{B_{1}}}\left\{u(x)-u(y)-L\phi(|x-y|)-\frac{K}{2}|x-x_{0}|^{2}-\frac{K}{2}|y-x_{0}|^{2}\right\}\leq 0,

where
\displaystyle\phi(t)=t^{\gamma}
, in order to obtain
\displaystyle\gamma
-Hölder regularity.
This can be proved by contradiction.
Suppose
\displaystyle M>0
, and let
\displaystyle x,y
be the points at which the maximum is attained and set
\displaystyle a=x-y
.
We first consider
\displaystyle\phi
as a test function and substitute
\displaystyle D\phi(|a|),D^{2}\phi(|a|)
into the equation in place of
\displaystyle Du,D^{2}u
, respectively.
In doing so, we encounter terms of the form
\displaystyle|D_{i}\phi(|a|)|^{p_{i}}|D_{ii}\phi(|a|)|
, but due to the variation in the exponents
\displaystyle p_{i}
, these quantities are not balanced across directions.
To address this issue, we instead employ an anisotropic distance function with direction-dependent growth rates
\displaystyle\alpha=(\alpha_{i})
, defined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle|a|_{\alpha}^{2}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}|a_{1}|^{2\alpha_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}|a_{n}|^{2\alpha_{n}},\quad\alpha_{i}=\frac{1+p_{i}/2}{1+\gamma p_{i}/2}

instead of the standard isotropic distance function
\displaystyle|a|^{2}=|a_{1}|^{2}+\cdots|a_{n}|^{2}
.
Then, by direct computation, the terms
\displaystyle|D_{i}\phi(|a|_{\alpha})|^{p_{i}}|D_{ii}\phi(|a|_{\alpha})|
become balanced across directions, and therefore the closeness condition between
\displaystyle p_{1}
and
\displaystyle p_{n}
is no longer required.

Moreover, we need to find the the Hessians
\displaystyle X=D^{2}u(x)
and
\displaystyle Y=D^{2}u(y)
, which can be achieved by applying the Ishii–Lions lemma to transfer the estimates of
\displaystyle Z=D^{2}\phi(|x|_{\alpha})
to
\displaystyle X,Y
.
Explicitly, for any
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\left\lVert Z\right\rVert\right)\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\leq\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&-Y\end{pmatrix}\leq\begin{pmatrix}Z^{\epsilon}&-Z^{\epsilon}\\
-Z^{\epsilon}&Z^{\epsilon}\end{pmatrix},

where
\displaystyle Z^{\epsilon}=Z+2\epsilon Z^{2}
.
See
[
15
,
29
]
for the detailed explanation of the Ishii-Lion lemma.
By choosing
\displaystyle\epsilon\lesssim 1/\left\lVert Z\right\rVert
, we obtain
\displaystyle\left\lVert X\right\rVert,\left\lVert Y\right\rVert\lesssim\left\lVert Z\right\rVert
.
However, in the anisotropic setting, we require componentwise estimates for
\displaystyle X_{ij}
and
\displaystyle Y_{ij}
due to the assumption
(A2):
and
(A3):
.
In particular, we need estimates of the form
\displaystyle|X_{ii}|\lesssim|Z_{ii}|
so that the quantity
\displaystyle|D_{i}\phi(|a|_{\alpha})|^{p_{i}}|X_{ii}|
remains balanced across directions.
By applying
\displaystyle(e_{i},0)
to the above matrix inequalities, we obtain only
\displaystyle|X_{ii}|\lesssim\left\lVert Z\right\rVert
.
However, since
\displaystyle\left\lVert Z\right\rVert
combines effects from all directions while
\displaystyle Z
grows anisotropically, it fails to capture the precise behavior of the individual component
\displaystyle|Z_{ii}|
.
As a result, this approach fails to yield the desired estimate
\displaystyle|X_{ii}|\lesssim|Z_{ii}|
.

In order to overcome this obstacle, we instead consider the anisotropic Ishii-Lions Lemma (Lemma
2.1
).
Rather than using a scalar parameter
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, we introduce a direction-dependent vector
\displaystyle\epsilon=(\epsilon_{i})
where each
\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}>0
may vary with each direction.
Then we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle-(1+\left\lVert\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]Z\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]\right\rVert)\begin{pmatrix}\left[1/\epsilon_{i}\right]&0\\
0&\left[1/\epsilon_{i}\right]\end{pmatrix}\leq\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&-Y\end{pmatrix}\leq\begin{pmatrix}Z^{\epsilon}&-Z^{\epsilon}\\
-Z^{\epsilon}&Z^{\epsilon}\end{pmatrix},

where
\displaystyle Z^{\epsilon}=Z+2Z\left[\epsilon_{i}\right]Z
.
Then by choosing
\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}\lesssim 1/|Z_{ii}|
and applying
\displaystyle(e_{i},0)
or
\displaystyle(0,e_{i})
to the above inequalities, we get the desired componentwise estimates
\displaystyle|X_{ii}|,|Y_{ii}|\lesssim|Z_{ii}|
.

\displaystyle u(x)+v(y)-\phi(x,y)

\displaystyle\epsilon=(\epsilon_{i})

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}>0

\displaystyle X,Y\in S(n)

\displaystyle\displaystyle(D_{x}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y}),X)\in\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,+}_{\Omega}}u(\overline{x}),\quad(D_{y}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y}),Y)\in\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,-}_{\Omega}}v(\overline{y})

\displaystyle\displaystyle-(1+\left\lVert\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]Z\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]\right\rVert)\begin{pmatrix}\left[1/\epsilon_{i}\right]&0\\

\displaystyle Z=D^{2}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y})

\displaystyle 1-(1+p_{n}/2)\kappa-p_{n}(1-\gamma)>0
.
Combining all the estimates above with (
3.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle c_{3}\frac{L}{\delta^{1-(1+p_{n}/2)\kappa}}\leq-I_{1}\leq|I_{21}|+|I_{22}|+|I_{3}|+|I_{4}|\leq\frac{L}{C_{L}\delta^{1-(1+p_{n}/2)\kappa}}.

By choosing large enough
\displaystyle L>1
so that
\displaystyle 1/C_{L}<c_{3}
, we finally reach a contradiction.
Consequently, we conclude that
\displaystyle u
is Lipschitz continuous.

∎

5.
The anisotropic Ishii-Lion Lemma

In this section, we prove the anisotropic Ishii-Lion Lemma (Lemma
2.1
).
In fact, Lemma
2.1
can be proved by a simple scaling of the original Ishii-Lion Lemma (Lemma
5.1
), which is written below.
The proof of the Lemma
5.1
can be found in
[
15
,
29
]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

(The original Ishii-Lion Lemma)
Let
\displaystyle u,v\in C(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle\phi(x,y)\in C^{2}(\Omega\times\Omega)
.
Assume that
\displaystyle(\overline{x},\overline{y})\in\Omega\times\Omega
is a local maximum point of
\displaystyle u(x)+v(y)-\phi(x,y)
.
Then for any
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
, there exist
\displaystyle X,Y\in S(n)
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle(D_{x}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y}),X)\in\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,+}_{\Omega}}u(\overline{x}),\quad(D_{y}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y}),Y)\in\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,-}_{\Omega}}v(\overline{y})

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\left\lVert Z\right\rVert\right)I\leq\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&Y\end{pmatrix}\leq Z+\epsilon Z^{2},

where
\displaystyle Z=D^{2}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y})
.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

For any
\displaystyle\epsilon=(\epsilon_{i})
with
\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}>0
, we define
\displaystyle\tilde{u},\tilde{v}\in C
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}\in C^{2}
as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{u}(x)=u(\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]x),\quad\tilde{v}(x)=v(\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]x),\quad\tilde{\phi}(x,y)=\phi(\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]x,\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]y).

If
\displaystyle(\overline{x},\overline{y})\in\Omega\times\Omega
is a local maximum point of
\displaystyle u(x)+v(y)-\phi(x,y)
, then
\displaystyle(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=(\left[1/\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]\overline{x},\left[1/\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]\overline{y})
is a local maximum point of
\displaystyle\tilde{u}(x)+\tilde{v}(y)-\tilde{\phi}(x,y)
.
Therefore, we can apply the original Ishii-Lions (Lemma
5.1
with
\displaystyle\epsilon=1
) to
\displaystyle\tilde{u},\tilde{v}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}
.
We know that there exist
\displaystyle\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\in S(n)
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle(D_{x}\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}),\tilde{X})\in\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,+}}\tilde{u}(\tilde{x}),\quad(D_{y}\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}),\tilde{Y})\in\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,-}}\tilde{v}(\tilde{y})

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\left(1+\left\lVert\tilde{Z}\right\rVert\right)I\leq\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{X}&0\\
0&\tilde{Y}\end{pmatrix}\leq\tilde{Z}+\tilde{Z}^{2},

where
\displaystyle\tilde{Z}=D^{2}\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
.
Note that

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{x}\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]D_{x}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y}),\quad D_{y}\tilde{\phi}(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})=\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]D_{y}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y})

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{Z}=\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]Z\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]\quad\text{ with }\quad Z=D^{2}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y}).

Therefore, by scaling back, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle(D_{x}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y}),X)\in\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,+}_{\Omega}}u(\overline{x}),\quad(D_{y}\phi(\overline{x},\overline{y}),Y)\in\overline{\mathcal{J}^{2,-}_{\Omega}}v(\overline{y})

where
\displaystyle X=\left[1/\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]\tilde{X}\left[1/\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]
and
\displaystyle Y=\left[1/\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]\tilde{Y}\left[1/\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]
.
Note that

\tilde{Z}+\tilde{Z}^{2}=\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right](Z+Z\left[\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{i}\right]Z)\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right],

to discover that

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\left(1+\left\lVert\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]Z\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]\right\rVert\right)I\leq\begin{pmatrix}\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]X\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]&0\\
0&\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]Y\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]\end{pmatrix}\leq\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right](Z+Z\left[\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{i}\right]Z)\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right].

Finally, applying
\displaystyle\left[1/\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},1/\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]
to the above matrix inequality, we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\left(1+\left\lVert\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]Z\left[\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}},\sqrt{\epsilon_{i}}\right]\right\rVert\right)\left[1/\epsilon_{i},1/\epsilon_{i}\right]\leq\begin{pmatrix}X&0\\
0&Y\end{pmatrix}\leq Z+Z\left[\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{i}\right]Z,

which completes the proof.
∎
