Integrated genomic and mutagenesis analysis of Pseudomonas argentinensis SA190 reveals mechanisms of plant root colonization and stress resilience
Authors: Büsra Elkatmis, Sabiha Parween, Abdul Aziz Eida, Rewaa Jalal, Feras Lafi, Intikhab Alam, Stanislav Kopriva, Heribert Hirt, Maged M. Saad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPseudomonas argentinensis SA190 is a desert-adapted, plant-associated bacterium with demonstrated potential to enhance plant growth under abiotic stress. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive genomic and functional characterization of SA190 to uncover the molecular mechanisms underlying its biofilm formation, root colonization, and plant growth-promoting traits.ResultsThe SA190 genome consists of a single circular chromosome (5.07 Mb, 64% GC) encoding 4561 predicted ORFs. Functional annotation revealed genes related to phosphate solubilization (pqq operon), antifungal activity, and stress mitigation, including ACC deaminase. AntiSMASH analysis identified eight biosynthetic gene clusters linked to secondary metabolite production, including siderophores, terpenes, and β-lactones. Metabolic profiling demonstrated selective utilization of root exudate-associated sugars, consistent with rhizosphere adaptation. By a Tn5 transposon mutagenesis approach, we identified key regulators required for biofilm formation. Biofilm mutants deficient in wspC, rpoE, and fliD were differentially compromised in plant colonization and plant growth promotion under ambient and/or drought conditions.ConclusionOur study suggests that SA190 is a metabolically streamlined yet ecologically versatile PGPB and provides functional insights into its potential application as a bioinoculant for sustainable agriculture in arid environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04495-2.

Introduction

Climate change is a significant global challenge that poses a threat to both ecosystems and human populations by affecting food security, especially in regions predicted to experience extreme weather patterns such as droughts [1]. These environmental changes exert considerable stress on plants, which are inherently sessile organisms. Unlike animals, plants cannot relocate in response to stress, making them particularly vulnerable to both biotic stresses (e.g., pathogens) and abiotic stresses (e.g., drought, salinity, high temperatures) [2, 3]. This vulnerability is likely to lead to significant declines in food production, exacerbating the global food security crisis expected as the world’s population approaches 10 billion by 2050 [4, 5]. Therefore, understanding plant responses to these stress factors is crucial to develop solutions that ensure agricultural sustainability under changing environmental conditions.

Soil hosts a great microbial diversity [6–8]. This diversity diminishes from bulk soil to the rhizosphere [9, 10]. The plant creates a nutrient-rich environment in the rhizosphere by secreting a wide range of photosynthetically fixed carbon molecules such as amino acids, sugars, enzymes, vitamins, secondary metabolites, and organic acids [7, 8, 11, 12]. These exudates attract bacteria through chemotaxis, and in addition, flagella and type IV pili systems facilitate bacterial motility towards and colonization of the roots [6, 7, 13]. Once rhizosphere-associated bacteria reach the roots, they strengthen their attachment by secreting exopolysaccharides (EPS) and cell surface polysaccharides, converting weak interactions into irreversible binding [6]. This process often leads to the formation of biofilms, which protect bacterial cells from environmental stresses such as temperature fluctuations, pH shifts, drought, and pathogen attacks in the rhizosphere [6, 14]. While these microbial strategies are common among root-colonizing bacteria, plants also selectively recruit diverse beneficial microbes, including endophytes, to form a healthy root microbiome [15, 16]. Among the beneficial microbes, endophytic plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) play a crucial role in enhancing plant performance through various mechanisms [17]. For example, they improve plant growth by producing phytohormones and increasing nutrient acquisition [18]. Despite the abundance of nutrients in the soil, some may not be bioavailable for plants. PGPB help plants to utilize these unavailable nutrients through siderophore production, phosphate solubilization, and nitrogen fixation [19, 20]. Additionally, PGPB contribute significantly to enhancing plant biomass under stress conditions by releasing antioxidants and improving water use efficiency [21, 22]. Stressed plants produce more reactive oxygen species (ROS) than non-stressed ones [23]. Some PGPB bacteria produce antioxidants that reduce ROS accumulation in stressed plants [24]. For example, bacterial carotenoids contribute to the development of stress tolerance in plants, thereby promoting their growth [24]. Taken together, endophytic PGPB contribute significantly to plant growth and productivity.

Arid and semi-arid environments are ideal for identifying plant growth-promoting bacteria, as they harbour microbes naturally adapted to harsh conditions such as drought, salinity, and heat [25]. In line with this, an endophytic bacterium isolated from the nodule of Indigofera argentea in the Jizan region of Saudi Arabia was shown to enhance plant biomass under stress conditions [22, 26]. Draft genome sequence and 16 S rRNA analysis confirmed that SA190 belongs to the Pseudomonas genus, characterized by a gram-negative cell wall, rod-shaped morphology, aerobic metabolism, and polar flagellar motility [26, 27]. Biochemical analysis showed that SA190 was capable of phosphate solubilization, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and siderophore production [26]. SA190 has been shown to be an alternative biostimulant with its plant growth-promoting traits. The endophyte SA190 increased the biomass of Arabidopsis under drought stress [22]. The transcriptomic study revealed that under drought stress, the expression of aquaporin genes, specifically plasma membrane intrinsic proteins (PIPs) and tonoplast intrinsic proteins (TIPs), in Arabidopsis were induced through histone methylation triggered by SA190 [22]. Despite these findings, the specific genes and mechanisms by which SA190 enhances plant growth under stress remain largely unknown. In this study, we aimed to identify the genetic basis of biofilm formation and root colonization in Pseudomonas argentinensis SA190. Using integrated genomic and mutagenesis analyses, we focused on identifying regulators that contribute to its function as a rhizosphere-competent, stress-resilient bioinoculant. By characterizing these genetic components, this research provides a deeper understanding of how microbial interactions can be leveraged to improve crop resilience in the face of climate change.

Materials and methods

Growth condition of Pseudomonas argentinensis SA190 and mutants

Pseudomonas argentinensis SA190 was previously isolated from the root nodules of the Indigofera argentea, an indigenous desert plant in the Jizan region (16°56.475’N, 42°36.694’E) of Saudi Arabia [26]. SA190 was grown overnight at 28 °C on Luria-Bertani (LB, Invitrogen) supplemented with 0.9% agar (Sigma-Aldrich). For SA190 mutants, 50 µg/ml kanamycin (kan) was added to the LB medium.

Genomic DNA isolation

The genomic DNA of SA190 was extracted from the bacterial culture using a Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted DNA was treated with RNase A (Mo Bio Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, United States) and then purified using the Mo Bio PowerClean Pro DNA Clean-Up Kit (Mo Bio Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, United States), again following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quantity and quality of the DNA were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, United States) and a Qubit 2.0 (Life Technologies, Invitrogen Division, Darmstadt, Germany).

Whole genome sequencing and function annotation

Whole genome sequencing was performed by DNA Link Korea (Seoul, South Korea) using the PacBio RS II sequencing platform (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, United States). Large-insert libraries were sequenced in single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing cells using P6-C4 chemistry. PacBio reads were assembled into a single contig using the de novo Hierarchical Genome Assembly Process (HGAP.2) algorithm with default parameters. Genome annotation was conducted using the in-house Automatic Annotation of Microbial Genomes (AAMG) pipeline [28]. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using FragGeneScan. Functional analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [29]. AntiSMASH (version 8.0.4) was used to identify gene clusters associated with secondary metabolites [30].

Phylogenomic classification of SA190

The genome sequence of SA190 was uploaded to the Type Strain Genome Server (TYGS) (https://tygs.dsmz.de) to compare it with the genomes available in the TYGS database using the MASH algorithm for a fast approximation of intergenomic relatedness. Ten strains with the smallest MASH distances were selected. These closely related strains were identified based on the 16 S rRNA. The data were extracted using RNAmmer [31]. The sequences were then BLASTed against the 16 S rRNA gene sequences of the strains available in the database. This method was utilized to choose the most suitable strains based on their bitscore. The Genome BLAST Distance Phylogeny (GBDP) approach was used to calculate the precise distances using the “coverage” algorithm and distance formula d5 [31]. The closest strain genome to SA190 was determined based on the distance results. For the phylogenomic analysis, pairwise comparisons among the genomes were performed using GBDP. Intergenomic distances were accurately calculated using the “trimming” algorithm with distance formula d5, with 100 replicates generated for each distance measurement. The recommended settings of GGDC 3.0 were used to calculate the DDH values and confidence intervals. FASTME 2.1.6.1 and SPR post-processing were used for intergenomic distances in the phylogenetic tree [32]. The tree was visualized with PhyD3 [33].

Average Amino Acid Identity (AAI) calculation

Pairwise genomic similarity was assessed using Average Amino Acid Identity (AAI) analysis. Protein-coding sequences were extracted from each genome resulting from ANI analysis, and reciprocal best BLAST hits (RBH) were identified to determine orthologous gene pairs between genomes. For each ortholog pair, amino acid sequences were aligned using BLASTP, and pairwise identity was calculated. The AAI value for each genome pair was computed as the arithmetic mean of amino acid identity across all identified orthologous proteins. The EzAAI software (version 1.2.2) [34] was used with default parameters, employing a minimum alignment length threshold of 70% and a BLASTP e-value cutoff of 1e-5 to ensure robust ortholog identification.

Biochemical assays

Biochemical analyses were performed to evaluate the plant growth-promoting traits of SA190. Phosphate solubilization was tested on Pikovskaya’s (PVK) agar plates (M520, Himedia). Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production by SA190 was measured according to Bric et al., 1991 [35] Siderophore production by SA190 was evaluated using the method described by Louden et al., 2011 [36]. Zinc solubilization was tested on modified PVK agar plates supplemented with 3% ZnO and 0.1% ZnCO3, as described by Bapiri et al., 2012 [37]. Tolerance to abiotic stresses was assessed by growing SA190 in LB broth (Lennox L Broth Base, Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% or 20% (w/v) polyethylene glycol (PEG) 8000 for drought stress and 0.1 M, 0.5 M or 1 M NaCl for salt stress. The API 50 CH test kit (Biomerieux, France) was used to test the fermentation of 49 carbohydrates in SA190, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The antibiotic sensitivity of SA190 was tested using a clearing test. Antibiotic disks were placed on LB plates, and 100 µl of SA190 culture was spread onto the plates. Bacterial growth around the disks indicated antibiotic resistance (R), while no growth around the disks demonstrated antibiotic sensitivity (S).

Tn5 mutagenesis and insertion mapping workflow

Preparation of competent cells

A single colony of SA190 was cultured in LB broth to mid-log phase (OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.4). Cells were chilled, washed twice with ice-cold sterile water, and resuspended in 10% glycerol. Aliquots (100 µL) were stored at − 80 °C.

Electroporation with EZ-Tn5™ < KAN-2>

Competent cells were thawed and mixed with 0.5 µL of EZ-Tn5 transposon DNA (Epicentre). Electroporation was performed using the Bio-Rad MicroPulser (Ec1 program, 1.5 kV). SOC medium (900 µL) was added immediately post-electroporation, followed by 1.5 h recovery at 28 °C. Transformed cells were plated on LB agar supplemented with kanamycin (50 µg/mL). Control transformations (no transposon) were included.

Construction of mutant library

The library was constructed according to Elkatmis et al., 2025 [38]. In brief, individual kanamycin-resistant colonies were transferred to 96-well plates containing LBkan broth. Glycerol stocks were prepared (1:1 with 20% glycerol) and stored at − 80 °C. For screening, mutants were replicated onto LBkan agar using a 96-pin replicator.

RATE-PCR for insertion site mapping

Insertion sites were mapped using Random Amplification of Transposon Ends (RATE-PCR) following the insertation protocol in Elkatmis et al., 2025 [38]. Boiled cell lysates were used as PCR templates with primers Inv-1 or Inv-2 and Phusion polymerase. Amplification involved a three-phase cycling protocol. Products were purified with ExoSAP-IT and submitted to Sanger sequencing using KAN-2 specific primers. Detailed reagent composition, PCR conditions, and primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Sequence analysis validation

Insertion sites in the SA190 Tn5 mutants were validated by Sanger and HiSeq sequencing (Supplementary Table 2) [38]. Sequence data were analyzed using SnapGene (Version 6.2.1) and further examined with the NCBI BLASTn program to identify disrupted genes. This process allowed for creating a gene list for the identified SA190 mutants.

Biofilm formation assay

Biofilm formation was quantitatively assessed using a colorimetric assay [39]. Wild-type SA190 and mutants were grown overnight in LB and LBkan broth, respectively. The overnight cultures were diluted at 1:100 and transferred to a 96-well plate. This plate was incubated at 28 °C overnight. After incubation, the liquid was decanted, and the plate was rinsed with water to remove residual liquid. Next, 125 µl of 0.1% crystal violet solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The plate was then rinsed four times with water and allowed to dry for 4 h. To solubilize the crystal violet, 125 µl of 30% acetic acid was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 550 nm using a plate reader (TECAN Infinite).

Plant drought resistance assays

The plant drought resistance assay was performed as described by Alwutayd et al., 2023 [22], with minor modifications. Arabidopsis seeds were surface sterilized with 70% ethanol + 0.05% SDS for 10 min with shaking at 3000 rpm. The seeds were washed three times with 1 ml of 100% ethanol. SA190 and mutants were streaked onto LB and LBkan plates, respectively. Plates were incubated overnight at 28 °C. A single colony from the plates was transferred to LB broth medium. Fifty µg/ml kanamycin antibiotic was used in LB broth medium for mutants. Liquid cultures were incubated overnight at 28 °C, 220 rpm. 100 µl was taken from the overnight culture and transferred to fresh LB and LBkan broth medium. Incubation at 28 °C was followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min. The bacterial culture was washed with ½ MS solution (Murashige and Skoog basal salts, Sigma-Aldrich). The optical density of the bacterial suspension was set to OD600 0.21. 100 µl of bacterial suspension was added to 50 ml of ½ MS agar and poured into the square Petri plate (120 mm x 120 mm). 100 µl of ½ MS solution was added to 50 ml of ½ MS agar for the mock treatment. Sterilized seeds were distributed on the plates. The plates were covered with aluminium foil and kept in the dark at 4 °C for two days. They were transferred to the growth chamber under long-day photoperiod conditions (16 h light, 8 h dark). After five days of germination, seedlings with 1.0–1.5 cm root length were transferred to ½ MS and ½ MS + 25% PEG plates. Six seedlings were transferred for each plate. They were placed into the growth chamber under the same conditions. The fresh weight of the seedlings was measured 15 days after the seedlings were transferred to non-stress and stress plates. The colony-forming units (CFU) in the roots were determined by following M. Saad et al., 2018 [40] to compare the colonization efficiency between wild type SA190 and the mutants. To assess the CFU levels of endophytes, roots were surface sterilized with 70% ethanol for 30 s, followed by two rinses with sterile water.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.0). Biofilm data were analyzed using Welch’s and Brown–Forsythe one-way ANOVA. Post hoc multiple comparisons were performed with Dunnett’s T3 test. Results are presented as column graphs, and different letters above the bars indicate statistically significant differences between groups (p < 0.05). Colony-forming unit (CFU) data were generated from four independent biological replicates and log10-transformed prior to analysis. The plant assay was performed with three biological replicates and two technical replicates. Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. In the boxplot, whiskers represent the minimum and maximum values, while the horizontal line indicates the median. Different letters indicate statistically significant differences based on two-way ANOVA (p < 0.05).

Data deposition

The genome sequence of SA190 was deposited in NCBI/DDBJ/EMBL database under the accession number CP056087.1 with BioProject no. PRJNA312745.

Results and discussion

Genome sequence assembly and general features

To ensure the accuracy and completeness of the SA190 genome assembly, we re-sequenced the strain using PacBio long-read sequencing technology. This approach provided improved coverage of 100x and resolution, confirming the integrity of the genomic assembly and enabling more precise identification of genetic elements. The genome of SA190 consists of a single circular chromosome of 5,076,637 bp with no plasmids (Fig. 1). The GC content of the genome is 64%. A total of 4561 of ORFs (protein coding genes), 90 pseudogenes, 64 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), 12 ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), and 1 noncoding RNA (ncRNA) (Table 1). A clear GC skew transition was observed at positions 60,912 and 3,147,121 on the chromosome, corresponding to the origin of replication (oriC) and replication termination (terC) sites, respectively (Fig. 1a). The oriC in the SA190 chromosome is confirmed by the conservation of the gene operon SA190PBcda_000000664–669, which encodes gyrB-recF-dnaN-dnaA-rpmH-rnpA in that region [41]. In Pseudomonas species, the number of tRNAs varies between 60 and 80, while the number of rRNAs ranges from 4 to 7 [42]. High copy number of rRNAs showed the correlation of the high growth rate of the bacteria and adaptation to the environmental responses [43]. For example, Sphingomonas sp. strain RB2256 carries a single rRNA operon, exhibits a slow growth rate, and shows limited adaptability to changing environments [43, 44]. In contrast, Bacillus subtilis, which harbors 10 rRNA operons, grows rapidly and adapts more readily to environmental fluctuations [43, 45]. Similarly, Pseudomonas species with higher numbers of rRNA and tRNA genes tend to grow faster and adapt more effectively to environmental changes [42, 46]. Genomic Identification of Pseudomonas argentinensis SA190 Using ANI and AAI Analyses

To accurately determine the taxonomic position of strain SA190, we performed both Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) and Average Amino Acid Identity (AAI) analyses on whole genome level. Based on the TYGS genome-to-genome comparison, strain SA190 showed the highest relatedness to Pseudomonas argentinensis LMG 22,563, with a digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) value of 75% (confidence interval 71.0–78.6%), which is well above the accepted species threshold of 70%. The DDH of 75 corresponds to an ANI of about 96–97%, confirming the same species (Fig. 2A) [47, 48]. To further resolve species-level classification, we conducted AAI analysis, which confirmed that SA190 shares 93.5% amino acid identity with P. argentinensis LMG 22,563, firmly supporting species-level assignment (Fig. 2B) [49]. Other Pseudomonas species showed lower AAI values (84 − 88%), and non-Pseudomonas taxa fell below 76%, indicating clear genus- and species-level boundaries (Supplementary Table 4) [50, 51]. SA190’s genomic features including a genome size of ~ 5.06 Mb, GC content of ~ 64%, and ~ 4,642 protein-coding genes are consistent with P. argentinensis and typical of the Pseudomonas genus [52]. These multi-layered genomic comparisons (ANI, AAI, and genome metrics) confirm that SA190 is a bona fide strain of P. argentinensis, closely related to the type strain LMG 22,563.

Biochemical characteristics of SA190

Evaluation of the plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits revealed that P. argentinensis SA190 solubilized phosphate and produced indole acetic acid (IAA) and siderophores, but it could not solubilize zinc (Table 2). Siderophore production and phosphate solubilization of SA190 are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. These results indicate that SA190 exhibits most of the traits associated with PGPB, establishing a beneficial interaction with the host to enhance its biomass. Antibiogram results indicated that SA190 was resistant to penicillin G but sensitive to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, erythromycin, kanamycin, oxytetracycline, streptomycin, tetracycline, and rifampicin (Table 3). Abiotic stress resistance of SA190

The carbon substrate utilization profile of SA190 was assessed using the API 50 CH system, providing insights into the strain’s metabolic versatility and ecological adaptation. Out of 49 tested carbon sources, SA190 exhibited a selective pattern of substrate usage, utilizing a total of 11 compounds with a focus on simple sugars and a few derivatives (Supplementary Table 5). SA190 demonstrated a selective carbohydrate utilization pattern. Among monosaccharides, the strain was able to metabolize D-mannose, L-fucose, and L-rhamnose, while it did not utilize D-glucose, D-fructose, or D-galactose, which is consistent with the atypical carbon preferences reported in several Pseudomonas species [53, 54]. The ability to utilize methyl-α-D-mannopyranoside further supports the presence of enzymes capable of processing glycoside-linked sugars, a trait often linked with competitive colonization strategies in beneficial rhizobacteria [19]. Among disaccharides, SA190 metabolized D-lactose, sucrose, and D-trehalose, all of which are not only abundant in root exudates but also have been shown to enhance microbial survival and stress tolerance in the rhizosphere [55, 56]. However, the strain did not utilize D-maltose, D-melibiose, or D-cellobiose, suggesting substrate-specific transport or enzymatic limitations.

For glycosides, the strain was able to process salicin and esculin, indicating β-glucosidase activity. The bacterium also utilizes select polysaccharides, including inulin and amidon (starch), as well as certain polyols like glycerol, erythritol, and xylitol. Additionally, SA190 could metabolize potassium gluconate, suggesting active periplasmic oxidation pathways [57, 58]. The strain’s positive utilization of potassium gluconate is consistent with metabolic features commonly observed in plant-associated Pseudomonas species, where gluconate metabolism plays a central role in colonization and carbon flux through the Entner–Doudoroff pathway [2, 3]. These pathways are also implicated in biofilm formation and oxidative stress tolerance, reinforcing previous findings that link SA190’s genomic features and biofilm-related genes with plant growth promotion [59]. Collectively, these findings suggest that P. argentinensis SA190 employs a specialized metabolic strategy tailored to the rhizosphere environment, efficiently utilizing root-derived carbohydrates while lacking the machinery to process more complex or less abundant carbon sources. This specialization likely contributes to its ecological success and previously reported capabilities in promoting plant growth under abiotic stress conditions [25].

Genome mining and secondary metabolite biosynthetic potential of Pseudomonas argentinensis SA190

Genome mining revealed that SA190 carries stress-tolerance genes that may contribute to enhancing plant growth. A gene cluster responsible for phosphate solubilization, a crucial trait that ensures the plant’s access to bioavailable phosphate, was identified. The pqqEDCBAF operon consists of six genes involved in synthesizing pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ), which belongs to metabolites promoting plant growth [26, 60]. Additionally, genes encoding glucan endo-1,3-beta-D-glucosidase and chitinase, which exhibit antifungal activity, were discovered in SA190 [26]. In this way, SA190 can inhibit the growth of pathogenic fungi and protect plants from fungal infections [61]. The genome of SA190 also contains the acdS gene, which is important for plant growth. The acdS gene encodes the enzyme 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid deaminase (ACC deaminase), which enhances plant biomass and stress tolerance by lowering excessive ethylene production [62].

To assess the biosynthetic potential of P. argentinensis SA190, the genome was analysed using the antiSMASH (version 8.0.4). A total of eight biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) were identified, representing a diverse array of secondary metabolite classes, including RiPP-like compounds, redox cofactors, terpene precursors, non-ribosomal peptides (NRPs), β-lactones, terpenes, and NAGGN-type saccharide (Supplementary Fig. 2). Similar known clusters for eight biosynthetic clusters, identified by MIBiG comparison, are shown in Supplementary Figs. 3, 4, and 5. Region 1 (83,002–142,575 bp) was annotated as a hybrid cluster comprising RiPP-like, arylpolyene, and resorcinol biosynthetic elements. This cluster showed a low similarity to the APE Vf cluster, which is known for producing pigments that contribute to oxidative stress resistance in Gram-negative bacteria. Arylpolyenes serve as functional analogs to carotenoids, offering membrane protection and antioxidant functions in biofilms and host-associated environments [63]. Region 2 (870,281–892,428) was annotated as a redox – cofactor biosynthetic cluster. Region 3 (1,315,872–1,336,759) was annotated as a terpene-precursor biosynthetic cluster. Regions 4 and 5, spanning 2.48–2.79 Mb, encoded NRP-metallophore and NRPS hybrid clusters, known for Pyochelin, Coelibactin, Azotobactin D and Cupriachelin clusters, respectively. Coelibactin and Cupriachelin metabolites are typically involved in metal ion acquisition—notably iron and copper—critical for microbial survival in nutrient-limited environments like the rhizosphere. Siderophores and metallophores also contribute to microbial competitiveness and plant-beneficial traits [64]. Region 6 (2.81–2.84 Mb) was predicted to encode a β-lactone biosynthetic cluster. Although β-lactones are relatively underexplored compared to other classes, they are structurally related to β-lactam antibiotics and are increasingly recognized for their roles in antimicrobial defense and inter-microbial competition [65]. Region 7 (3.10–3.12 Mb) displayed a terpene biosynthetic cluster with high similarity to a carotenoid-producing cluster. Terpenes and carotenoids are associated with a broad range of functions, including light protection, membrane stabilization, and plant-microbe signalling, and are known to enhance rhizobacterial survival under abiotic stress conditions [66]. Region 8 (3.50–3.52 Mb) was identified as an NAGGN-type saccharide cluster, resembling O-antigen biosynthesis systems. These gene clusters are typically involved in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) assembly, which plays key roles in bacterial adhesion, immune modulation, and biofilm development, thus contributing to colonization efficiency and plant-microbe interaction dynamics.

The diversity of BGCs identified in SA190 indicates a robust capacity to synthesize structurally diverse and functionally significant secondary metabolites. These include compounds with potential roles in rhizosphere colonization, plant protection, and environmental resilience. The presence of siderophore-like NRPs, oxidative stress-related arylpolyenes, and LPS-modifying saccharides aligns with previously described phenotypes of PGPB bacteria, such as biofilm formation, stress tolerance, and plant growth promotion in arid environments [67]. The genomic potential observed here reinforces the ecological versatility and translational potential of P. argentinensis SA190 as a bioinoculant candidate for sustainable agriculture.

Chemotaxis and colonization

The chemotaxis ability of bacteria is crucial for their survival, adaptation, and colonization [68]. Bacteria detect environmental signals and initiate a response through a two-component regulatory system [69]. In SA190, 119 genes are associated with the two-component system. Key genes involved in the chemotaxis of SA190 include two copies of cheA (SA190PBcda_000004008, SA190PBcda_000000517), cheB (SA190PBcda_000001286, SA190PBcda_000000523), and cheY (SA190PBcda_000004010, SA190PBcda_000000515), as well as three copies of cheR (SA190PBcda_000001285, SA190PBcda_000002421, SA190PBcda_000000521) and cheV (SA190PBcda_000002420, SA190PBcda_000002652, SA190PBcda_000004099). These genes, functioning as histidine kinases and response regulators, play a significant role in processing environmental signals, thus facilitating bacterial movement [70]. SA190 also has four copies of the cheW gene (SA190PBcda_000000518, SA190PBcda_000000520, SA190PBcda_000003996, SA190PBcda_000003997), which is crucial for establishing the link between methyl-accepting chemotaxis proteins (MCPs) and cheA by synthesizing coupling proteins [71].

Flagella, essential for bacterial motility, directly contributes to the chemotaxis of bacteria [72]. The flagellar filament comprises the flagellin protein and a filament cap protein [72]. SA190 possesses the flagellin protein FliC (encoded by fliC, SA190PBcda_000002443) and a filament cap composed of the FliD protein (SA190PBcda_000002445). The fliG (SA190PBcda_000004032), fliM (SA190PBcda_000004022), and fliN (SA190PBcda_000004021) genes encode the cytoplasmic ring elements necessary for generating the torque required for flagellar movement, thereby significantly enhancing SA190’s motility [72]. Additionally, the genome of SA190 contains two flagellar motor proteins, MotA and MotB, which are integral to the operation of the flagella and, thus, to the overall chemotactic process [73].

Bacteria move into the rhizosphere through chemotaxis and produce exopolysaccharides (EPS) to adhere to plant roots [6]. At this stage, biofilm formation and quorum sensing are crucial for successful colonization. The ability of SA190 to colonize plant roots greatly contributes to its beneficial effects on plant growth [22]. In SA190, the Wsp regulatory system is hypothesized to govern biofilm formation and surface-related characteristics [74]. Key genes in this system, including wspA (SA190PBcda_000005030), wspB (SA190PBcda_000005029), wspD (SA190PBcda_000005027), wspR (SA190PBcda_000005024), wspF (SA190PBcda_000005025), and wspC (SA190PBcda_000005028), play essential roles in biofilm formation in response to environmental signals.

Quorum sensing, a bacterial communication mechanism, directly interacts with biofilm formation and regulates EPS production in response to varying environmental conditions [75]. This mechanism allows bacteria to form more robust biofilms, which protect them from abiotic stresses such as drought. The EPS matrix can absorb and retain water, thereby protecting the bacteria from the adverse effects of drought [76]. Consequently, the formation of biofilms on plant roots enhances the plant’s uptake and improves its resilience to drought stress, significantly contributing to plant-microbe interactions [76].

SA190 contains nine genes encoding LuxR family transcriptional regulators. Among these, luxR (SA190PBcda_000004790) represents the canonical quorum-sensing regulator. Two genes act as metabolic LuxR-like regulators, including SA190PBcda_000004434 (malT) and SA190PBcda_000003557 (amino acid transport). Five genes are annotated as two-component response regulators with LuxR domains (SA190PBcda_000003314, SA190PBcda_000003452, SA190PBcda_000005291, SA190PBcda_000005781, and SA190PBcda_000005817). Additionally, SA190PBcda_000000956 encodes a dual-function protein that contains both a LuxR family transcriptional regulator domain and a serine/threonine kinase domain. These LuxR family regulators may play a central role in coordinating quorum-sensing–mediated regulation of metabolism and signalling in SA190.

Characterization of biofilm genes in Pseudomonas argentinensis SA190 by Tn5 transposon library

To investigate the role of biofilm formation in plant-bacteria interaction in P. argentinensis SA190, we utilized a Tn5 transposon-induced mutation library. The Tn5 transposon system was employed to generate a transposon library containing 3,742 mutants of P. argentinensis SA190, resulting from 13 independent transformations. Since P. argentinensis SA190 contains only chromosomal DNA and lacks plasmids, all Tn5 insertions occurred within the chromosomal genes of SA190. To identify the insertion sites in the SA190 mutants, we used Random Amplification of Transposon Ends (RATE-PCR) followed by Sanger sequencing [32]. The distribution of Tn5 insertions across the genome of SA190 is illustrated in (Fig. 1h). Importantly, no significant accumulation of Tn5 insertions was observed in any specific region of the SA190 genome, indicating an even distribution of insertions. This even distribution of Tn5 insertions across the genome allows us to focus on bacterial traits that could play a role in successful root colonization. Biofilm formation is one of the crucial bacterial traits for root colonization. To investigate the role of biofilm formation in root colonization and plant growth promotion by SA190, we screened 1,000 transposon mutants using a crystal violet biofilm formation assay. Wild-type SA190 exhibited a biofilm formation value of approximately 1.5 (OD550), which was used as a reference (Fig. 3). Among the 1,000 mutants, 21 showed a noticeable reduction in biofilm formation compared to the wild type. From these, we selected three mutants—wspC::Tn5, rpoE::Tn5, and fliD::Tn5—with biofilm values below 0.25 for further analysis, applying this stringent threshold to identify mutants with severely impaired biofilm production (Fig. 3). This OD550 cut-off was chosen to classify mutants with low biofilm formation, ensuring that our analysis specifically addressed the impact of reduced biofilm capacity on root colonization and plant growth.

To investigate the functional role of wspC, rpoE and fliD in P. argentinensis SA190, we evaluated the colonization capacity of the mutants in Arabidopsis thaliana under non-stress and 25% PEG-induced drought stress conditions. After 15 days of plant growth, colonization levels of the mutants under non-stress conditions were similar to the wild-type SA190 (Fig. 4A). However, under 25% PEG-induced drought stress, the mutants exhibited a significant reduction in colonization compared to the wild type, indicating a stress-specific colonization defect. We next investigated the bacterial fitness in wspC::Tn5, rpoE::Tn5, and fliD::Tn5 mutants, they exhibited growth comparable to the wild-type SA190, indicating that the transposon insertions did not impair bacterial fitness (Supplementary Fig. 6). Then, we introduced a root surface sterilization step to remove epiphytic cells, allowing us to distinguish between loosely and stably associated bacteria. This approach enabled us to assess the effect of biofilm-related mutants on the stable colonization of SA190 on the root surface. Colonization of the mutants was significantly reduced under 25% PEG-induced drought stress after surface sterilization step (Fig. 4B). It suggests impaired stable root attachment in the mutants. This result supports the notion that wspC, rpoE, and fliD genes are particularly important for persistence on the root surface under drought stress conditions. To determine whether reduced colonization translates into a diminished growth-promoting effect, we assessed total plant fresh weight. Under non-stress conditions, plants colonized by the wspC::Tn5 mutant exhibited biomass levels comparable to those inoculated with wild-type SA190, whereas plants treated with the rpoE::Tn5 and fliD::Tn5 mutants showed a significant reduction in biomass (Fig. 4C). However, under 25% PEG-induced drought conditions, Arabidopsis plants inoculated with the mutants significantly decreased biomass compared to wild-type SA190 (Fig. 4C). This reduction in plant growth promotion results from the mutant’s compromised biofilm formation and root colonization capacity.

To contextualize our findings, we reviewed previous studies on the roles of wspC, rpoE, and fliD in biofilm formation and bacterial colonization. The wspC gene encodes a methyltransferase that modifies the WspA receptor, playing a central role in chemotaxis and regulation of the Wsp chemosensory pathway, which enhances both biofilm formation and motility [77, 78]. Given this regulatory function, wspC is important for bacterial colonization and adaptation to environmental stress. Chemotaxis and biofilm-related genes have been shown to play similar roles in other bacterial strains. For example, in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, the Wsp (wrinkly spreader phenotype) chemosensory system—which includes wspC—functions analogously to chemotaxis pathways and is involved in sensing environmental cues and envelope stress [77]. This system regulates intracellular levels of cyclic-di-GMP, a second messenger that governs the transition between motile and sessile (biofilm) lifestyles [77]. Activation of the Wsp pathway in response to surface contact or envelope perturbations leads to enhanced biofilm formation, enabling bacteria to adhere more effectively to surfaces, including plant roots [77]. Similarly, in Bacillus subtilis, disruption of the chemotaxis gene cheV resulted in a significant decline—over 15%—in the bacterium’s ability to colonize tomato roots [79]. The cheV gene was shown to play a crucial role in signal transduction during chemotaxis and biofilm, enabling bacteria to move toward favourable environments such as plant root exudates [79]. Loss of this function compromises the bacterium’s motility and capacity to establish stable associations with the host plant [79]. Consistently, the disruption of the wspC gene reduces biofilm formation in SA190, negatively affecting its stable attachment to the root. As a result, SA190 was unable to fully exert its beneficial effects on the plant. Moreover, our findings suggest that the wspC gene contributes to stable root colonization by SA190 and may play a role in establishing plant–microbe interactions.

The rpoE gene encodes an alternative sigma factor critical for the extracytoplasmic stress response in Gram-negative bacteria, playing a vital role in maintaining cell envelope integrity and responding to environmental stress [80]. Despite its recognized importance in bacterial physiology, the specific role of rpoE in biofilm formation and root colonization by SA190 has not been fully elucidated. In a study involving Crohn’s disease-associated adherent-invasive Escherichia coli (AIEC), inhibition of the RpoE sigma factor led to a significant reduction in biofilm formation and bacterial adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells, impairing colonization and persistence within the host [81]. Similarly, in Salmonella enterica, rpoE mutants exhibited increased sensitivity to extracytoplasmic stress, resulting in compromised biofilm development and a diminished ability to colonize host tissues [82]. In E. coli, the RpoE controls the expression of genes involved in maintaining the integrity of the outer membrane and the envelope stress response [83, 84]. Loss of RpoE function disrupts proper outer membrane protein folding, leading to defects in biofilm formation and reduced surface attachment [83, 84]. This stress response is crucial for bacterial survival under stressful conditions and for the maturation of biofilms on various surfaces [83, 84]. Similarly, the rpoE gene plays a crucial role in SA190’s biofilm formation, root colonization, and the resulting beneficial effects on plant growth under drought stress conditions.

fliD encodes a structural component of the bacterial flagellum essential for motility and many studies have emphasized the importance of the fliD gene in various bacterial systems [72]. For instance, in Helicobacter pylori, the fliD mutant showed delayed biofilm initiation and surface attachment [85]. Similarly, a study on Clostridioides difficile demonstrated that mutants lacking key flagellar components (fliC, fliD, and flgE) showed reduced colonization and adherence to the intestinal epithelium during infection [86]. These studies support the idea that flagellar components are essential for motility, surface attachment, and stable host interaction [86]. In parallel with the findings of these studies, our data demonstrate that the fliD gene in SA190 contributes to biofilm formation, stable root colonization, and the beneficial effects of SA190 on the plant.

Taken together, these results suggest that biofilm-related genes (wspC, rpoE, and fliD) contribute to stable root colonization and influence plant growth under 25% PEG-induced drought stress. Disruption of these genes reduces bacterial persistence on roots, limiting SA190’s beneficial effects and highlighting the role of biofilm formation in plant–microbe interactions. We note that complementation of the wspC, rpoE, and fliD mutants was not performed, which is a limitation of the Tn5 transposon library due to the potential disruption of large genomic regions. The mechanistic basis by which wspC, rpoE, and fliD influence biofilm formation remains to be elucidated. In Pseudomonas, biofilm development is regulated through the coordinated action of multiple pathways [87–89]. For example, the Gac/Rsm system controls biofilm matrix production [89], cyclic-di-GMP (c-di-GMP) metabolism promotes biofilm formation [90], quorum sensing (QS) regulates the population density of biofilm-forming cells [88], and exopolysaccharide (EPS) biosynthesis contributes directly to the establishment of the biofilm matrix [87]. The interplay among these systems ensures the initiation, maturation, and stability of biofilms [87]. The wsp operon has been associated with the modulation of c-di-GMP levels, thereby influencing surface attachment and biofilm stability [77, 78]. rpoE encodes an extracytoplasmic sigma factor involved in envelope stress responses, which can indirectly modulate biofilm-associated gene expression and EPS production [80, 82]. fliD encodes the flagellar cap protein, and mutations that impair flagellar function often reduce surface attachment and the initiation of biofilm formation [85]. Although our study identifies these genes as candidate contributors to biofilm formation in SA190, further studies are needed to determine how they integrate into the canonical regulatory networks described in other Pseudomonas species.

Conclusion

Pseudomonas argentinensis SA190 represents a metabolically streamlined yet functionally diverse PGPB with strong ecological adaptability. Its genetic potential for metabolite biosynthesis, stress resilience, and efficient root colonization underscores its value as a bioinoculant in sustainable agriculture. Notably, Tn5 transposon mutagenesis identified wspC, rpoE, and fliD as candidate genes potentially involved in biofilm formation and colonization. Disruption of these genes impaired biofilm integrity and reduced the bacterium’s ability to promote plant growth under drought stress, highlighting its role of biofilm-mediated attachment in rhizosphere competence. These insights demonstrate the power of functional genomics in unravelling the mechanisms underlying beneficial plant-microbe interactions. Collectively, this work provides a robust foundation for the translational deployment of SA190 to enhance plant resilience and productivity, particularly in arid and saline environments.
