On Ignorability of Preferential Sampling in Geostatistics
Changqing Lu

Abstract
Preferential sampling has attracted considerable attention in geostatistics since the pioneering work of Diggle et al. 2010 . A variety of likelihood-based approaches have been developed to correct estimation bias by explicitly modelling the sampling mechanism. While effective in many applications, these methods are often computationally expensive and can be susceptible to model misspecification. In this paper, we present a surprising finding: some existing non-likelihood-based methods that ignore preferential sampling can still produce unbiased and consistent estimators under the widely used framework of Diggle et al. 2010 and its extensions. We investigate the conditions under which preferential sampling can be ignored and develop estimators for both regression and covariance parameters without specifying the sampling mechanism parametrically. Simulation studies demonstrate clear advantages of our approach, including reduced estimation error, improved confidence interval coverage, and substantially lower computational cost. To show the practical utility, we further apply it to a tropical forest data set.



Keywords:
Covariance and cross-covariance; Geostatistical models; Marked point processes.

1
Introduction

Geostatistics models spatially continuous phenomena using data observed at discrete locations
\bm{s}_{1},\dots,\bm{s}_{n}
in a region of interest
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A commonly used formulation is
Z_{i}(\bm{s}_{i})=\bm{w}(\bm{s}_{i})^{\top}\bm{\beta}+Y(\bm{s}_{i})+e_{i}
, where
\bm{w}(\bm{s}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes spatial covariates with associated regression coefficients
\bm{\beta}
,
Y(\bm{s})
is a latent zero-mean Gaussian process, and
e_{i}
are independent Gaussian errors (nugget effects) with variance
\sigma_{e}^{2}
. The primary objectives are to consistently estimate the regression coefficients
\bm{\beta}
, the covariance function of
Y(\bm{s})
, and the nugget variance
\sigma_{e}^{2}
.

In classical geostatistical models, sampling locations are typically assumed to be deterministic or independent from the underlying spatial process, in which case the standard maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is generally preferred
(
Diggle & Giorgi 2019
)
. However,
Diggle et al. 2010
pointed out that, in many applications, the process
Z(\bm{s})
may depend on the locations at which it is observed. This phenomenon, termed as preferential sampling, can introduce substantial bias into the standard MLE, necessitating careful methodological adjustments. Recognizing its importance, a large body of research has focused on addressing this issue. For example,
Diggle et al. 2010
proposed a marked point process framework, where the observed locations are modelled as a realization of a log-Gaussian Cox process
(
Møller et al. 1998
, LGCP)
, and the corresponding spatial measurements
Z(\bm{s})
are treated as marks generated from a Gaussian process. Within this framework, the dependence between locations and marks is conveniently captured through a parametric relationship between their respective Gaussian random fields, facilitating likelihood-based estimation and inference for all model parameters
(
Dinsdale & Salibian-Barrera 2019a
)
.

To account for potential preferential sampling, the LGCP-based framework has been widely applied across various disciplines, such as ecology
(
Pennino et al. 2019
)
and physical oceanography
(
Dinsdale & Salibian-Barrera 2019b
)
. Methodologically,
Pati et al. 2011
developed a Bayesian approach for estimating the mean of the mark process, while
Zidek et al. 2014
extended the framework to a space–time context.
Ferreira & Gamerman 2015
employed it to guide the selection of new sampling locations, and
Amaral et al. 2024
examined spatially varying sampling degrees. Within the point process literature, further extensions have been proposed to accommodate specialized data structures, e.g. modelling the shared latent field via functional analysis when replicated marked point processes are available
(
Fok et al. 2012
,
Gervini & Baur 2020
,
Xu et al. 2020
,
Yin et al. 2021
,
Xu et al. 2024
)
. Recently,
Schliep et al. 2023
and
Hsiao & Waller 2025
adopted composite likelihood approaches with intensity-related weights to improve parameter estimation for the mark process. However, no theoretical guarantees have yet been established for these methods under preferential sampling.

A major limitation of the likelihood-based approaches is the need to specify the generating mechanism of the preferential sampling, which imposes a parametric formulation on the dependence between the point process and the marks. Consequently, these methods can be susceptible to model misspecification. In this work, we present a surprising finding: under the framework of
Diggle et al. 2010
and its extensions, some existing non-likelihood-based methods that ignore preferential sampling can still produce unbiased and consistent estimators. We carefully investigate the conditions under which preferential sampling can be ignored. Building on that, we develop estimators for both regression and covariance parameters without specifying a parametric sampling mechanism and establish statistical inference for the former. Our method is therefore applicable to a broader range of problems and, as shown in simulation studies, offers substantial computational gains over existing likelihood-based approaches.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the geostatistical model under preferential sampling and outlines the technical conditions for theoretical results. Section
3
examines the asymptotic behaviour of the least squares estimator for the regression coefficients and discusses the approximation of its asymptotic covariance matrix. In Section
4
, we develop unbiased estimators for the parametric spatial covariance function and establish their consistency. Section
5
presents numerical experiments that evaluate the proposed method and compare it with likelihood-based approaches. In Section
6
, we apply our method to a tropical forest data set to demonstrate its practical utility. Finally, the paper concludes with a discussion of the main findings and potential directions for future research.

2
Preliminaries and Technicalities

2.1
The Geostatistical Model under Preferential Sampling

Recall the classical geostatistical model introduced in Section
1

Z(\bm{s})=\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\bm{\beta}+Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s}),

(1)

where
Y(\bm{s})
is a zero-mean stationary Gaussian random field on
S
with variance
\sigma_{Y}^{2}
and covariance function
C_{Y}(\bm{s},\bm{t})=\mathrm{Cov}[Y(\bm{s}),Y(\bm{t})]
. Let
\|\cdot\|
denote the Euclidean distance. With slight abuse of notation, we assume
C_{Y}(\bm{s},\bm{t})=C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)
for some function
C_{Y}(r)
, implying that
Y(\bm{s})
is isotropic. A popular choice for
C_{Y}(r)
is the Matérn covariance function

C(r;\bm{\theta})=\sigma^{2}\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\sqrt{2\nu}\frac{r}{\phi}\right)^{\nu}B_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{2\nu}\frac{r}{\phi}\right),

(2)

where
\phi
and
\nu
are the range and smoothness parameters,
B_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order
\nu
, and
\Gamma
is the gamma function.

To generalize the preferential sampling framework of
Diggle et al. 2010
, we assume that the sampling locations are generated from an LGCP denoted by
N
and defined also on
S
with latent intensity

\lambda(\bm{s})=\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})\exp[X(\bm{s})],

where
\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})
is a baseline intensity and
X(\bm{s})
is a zero-mean Gaussian random field with covariance function
C_{X}(\bm{s},\bm{t})=\mathrm{Cov}[X(\bm{s}),X(\bm{t})]
. Consequently, the marginal first- and second-order intensity functions of
N
are given by

\rho(\bm{s})=\mathbb{E}[\lambda(\bm{s})]=\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})\exp(\sigma_{X}^{2}/2)

and

\rho_{2}(\bm{s},\bm{t})=\mathbb{E}[\lambda(\bm{s})\lambda(\bm{t})]=\rho(\bm{s})\rho(\bm{t})\exp[C_{X}(\bm{s},\bm{t})].

To introduce the dependence between
X(\bm{s})
and
Y(\bm{s})
,
Diggle et al. 2010
assumed a constant baseline
\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})=\lambda_{0}
and a proportional relationship
X(\bm{s})=\gamma Y(\bm{s})
for some
\lambda_{0}>0
and
\gamma\neq 0
. We extend this setting by allowing an arbitrary isotropic cross-covariance function
\mathrm{Cov}[X(\bm{s}),Y(\bm{t})]=C_{XY}(||\bm{s}-\bm{t}||)
, without imposing a specific parametric form.

2.2
Asymptotic Regime and Technical Conditions

Following
Diggle et al. 2010
, we analyze the model (
1
) within the framework of marked point process theory. Specifically, we adopt the standard increasing-domain regime (see, e.g.
Guan & Joh 2007
,
Xu et al. 2019
,
Xu et al. 2023
). Suppose that the observations of
Z(\bm{s})
and
N
are collected over a sequence of region
S_{n}
that expand to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as
n\to\infty
. Let
\partial S_{n}
denote the boundary of
S_{n}
with perimeter
|\partial S_{n}|
. We assume that, for every
n\geq 1
,

c_{1}n^{2}\leq|S_{n}|\leq c_{2}n^{2},\quad c_{1}n\leq|\partial S_{n}|\leq c_{2}n,\quad\mathrm{for}\ \mathrm{some}\ 0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}<\infty.

(C1)

This condition ensures that
S_{n}
grows in all directions and that
\partial S_{n}
is not too irregular.

To quantify the spatial dependence, we recall the definition of strong mixing coefficients
(
Rosenblatt 1956
)
. Define

\begin{split}\alpha(q;k)\equiv\sup&\left\{|P(S_{1}\cap S_{2})-P(S_{1})P(S_{2})|:S_{1}\in\mathcal{F}(T_{1}),S_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(T_{2}),\right.\\
&\left.T_{1},T_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},|T_{1}|=|T_{2}|\leq q,d(T_{1},T_{2})\geq k\right\},\end{split}

where
\mathcal{F(\cdot)}
denotes the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the random events of
N
that are in a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
d(T_{1},T_{2})
denotes the maximal distance between
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. We assume that

\sup_{q}\alpha(q;k)/q=O(k^{-\epsilon}),\quad\mathrm{for}\ \mathrm{some}\ \epsilon>2,

(C2)

which requires the dependence between any two fixed sets decaying to zero at a polynomial rate of the inter-set distance
k
, while the decay rate also depends on the size of the sets
q
. An LGCP, as assumed in Section
2.1
, satisfies this condition.

In addition to (C1)–(C2), we impose regularity conditions on the spatial covariates
\bm{w}(\bm{s})
and the baseline intensity
\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})
:

\sup_{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|\bm{w}(\bm{s})\|<\infty,\quad\sup_{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})\right|<\infty,

(C3)

on the covariance functions of the Gaussian random fields
X(\bm{s})
,
Y(\bm{s})
and
e(\bm{s})
:

\sup_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|C_{X}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)\right|<\infty,\quad\sup_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)\right|<\infty,\quad\sigma_{e}^{2}<\infty,

(C4)

and on the cross-covariance function between
X(\bm{s})
and
Y(\bm{s})
:

\sup_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|C_{XY}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)\right|<\infty.

(C5)

Moreover, we assume that
C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)
and
C_{XY}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)
are absolutely integrable everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\sup_{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)|\mathrm{d}\bm{t}<\infty,\quad\sup_{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|C_{XY}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)|\mathrm{d}\bm{t}<\infty.

(C6)

Finally, we require that the
p\times p
matrix

\int_{S_{n}}\rho(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}

(C7)

is invertible, a technical condition necessary for deriving the asymptotic covariance matrix in Theorem
1
. Furthermore, for Theorem
3
, we assume that the parametric semi-variogram function defined in Section
4
,

\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\theta)>0,

(C8)

has continuous partial derivatives with respect to
\bm{\theta}
. We also assume that the weight function
\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})
used in the objective functions
Q_{MC}(\bm{\theta})
and
Q_{CL}(\bm{\theta})
satisfies

\sup_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})|<\infty,

(C9)

and that the corresponding minimizers

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{MC}=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}}Q_{MC}(\bm{\theta}),\quad\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{CL}=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}}Q_{CL}(\bm{\theta})

(C10)

on
S_{n}
are unique.

It is worthy noting that, unlike most studies on marked point processes, we do not need to introduce an additional reference measure on the mark space. This is because, within our preferential sampling framework,
Z(\bm{s})
is modelled as a spatial Gaussian process, whose moments, conditional on N, can be characterized through the joint correlations, i.e. via the cross-covariance function.

3
Statistical Inference for the Regression Coefficients

A primary objective with the geostatistical model (
1
) is to estimate the regression coefficients
\bm{\beta}
for capturing spatial heterogeneity. Consider the least squares estimator

\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\left[\sum_{\bm{s}\in N}\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\right]^{-1}\sum_{\bm{s}\in N}\bm{w}(\bm{s})Z(\bm{s}).

(3)

In the absence of preferential sampling,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
is a consistent estimator with well-established asymptotic properties
(
Banerjee et al. 2014
)
. However, when the sampling locations are a realization of a point process that depends on
Z(\bm{s})
, the cross-correlation in between must be accounted for.

By applying the Campbell’s theorem and the Stein’s lemma, the expectations of the denominator and the numerator in (
3
) read

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\bm{s}\in N}\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\right]=\int_{S}\rho(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}

and

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\bm{s}\in N}\bm{w}(\bm{s})Z(\bm{s})\right]=\left[\int_{S}\rho(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\right]\bm{\beta}+C_{XY}(0)\int_{S}\rho(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})\mathrm{d}\bm{s}.

Assuming that the first element of
\bm{w}(\bm{s})
corresponds to the intercept term, we can use the results above to analyze the asymptotic behaviour of the least squares estimator (
3
) under the increasing-domain regime, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\bm{\beta}_{0}
and
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}
denote the true regression coefficients and the parameters estimated by (
3
) on
S_{n}
. Under conditions (C1)–(C7), as
n\to\infty
,

|S_{n}|^{1/2}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}-\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})\xrightarrow{d}\mathrm{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I}_{p}),

where
\bm{I}_{p}
is the
p\times p
identity matrix,
\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0}=\bm{\beta}_{0}+C_{XY}(0)\bm{\alpha}
with
\bm{\alpha}=(1,0,\dots,0)^{\top}
, and

\begin{split}\bm{\Sigma}_{n}&=|S_{n}|\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n}^{-1}(\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{n}+\bm{\mathrm{C}}_{n})\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n}^{-1},\quad\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n}=\int_{S_{n}}\rho(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s},\\
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{n}&=\int_{S_{n}}\rho(\bm{s})\left(\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}\right)\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s},\\
\bm{\mathrm{C}}_{n}&=\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\rho_{2}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\left[C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)+C_{XY}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)^{2}\right]\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{t})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}.\end{split}

Proof.

The proof is provided in the Supplementary Material.
∎

Theorem
1
shows that, despite preferential sampling, the least squares estimator (
3
) remains unbiased for all regression coefficients in
\bm{\beta}
, except for the intercept term. If a consistent estimator of
C_{XY}(0)
is available, the intercept bias can be readily corrected via
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}={\hat{\bm{\beta}}}-\hat{C}_{XY}(0)\bm{\alpha}
. Moreover, this theorem also enables valid statistical inference for all parameters in
\bm{\beta}
, provided that consistent estimators of the sill
\omega=\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}
, the spatial covariance function
C_{Y}(r)
and the cross-covariance function
C_{XY}(r)
are available.

Typically, we estimate the sill
\omega
via the moment-based estimator:

\hat{\omega}={\frac{1}{|N|}}\sum_{\bm{s}\in N}\left[Z(\bm{s})-\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}\right]^{2},

(4)

where
|N|
is the number of observations. Recalling the definition of the semi-variogram
(
Banerjee et al. 2014
)
,
V_{Y}(r)=\omega-C_{Y}(r)
, estimating
C_{Y}(r)
then reduces to estimating

V_{Y}(r)
, for which we use the kernel smoothing estimator:

\hat{V}_{Y}(r)=\frac{\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N}\left\{\left[Z(\bm{s})-\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}\right]-\left[Z(\bm{t})-\bm{w}(\bm{t})^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}\right]\right\}^{2}K_{h}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|-r)}{2\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N}K_{h}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|-r)},

(5)

where
K_{h}(\cdot)=h^{-1}K(\cdot/h)
with kernel function
K(\cdot)
and bandwidth
h
. The superscript
\neq
indicates that the sum is taken over pairs of distinct points in
N
. Similarly, the cross-covariance
C_{XY}(r)
can be estimated as

\hat{C}_{XY}(r)=\frac{\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N}\left[Z(\bm{s})-\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}\right]K_{h}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|-r)}{\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N}K_{h}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|-r)}.

(6)

Assuming that the semivariogram
V_{Y}(r)
and the cross-covariance
C_{XY}(r)
are smooth in a neighborhood of
r
, the following theorem establishes consistency of the three estimators (
4
)–(
6
) under preferential sampling.

Theorem 2
.

Under conditions (C1)–(C6), as
h_{n}\to 0
and
|S_{n}|h_{n}\to\infty
, it holds that, for every
r>0
,
|\hat{\omega}-\omega|=O_{p}(|S_{n}|^{-1/2})
,
|\hat{V}_{Y}(r)-V_{Y}(r)|=O_{p}[h_{n}+(|S_{n}|h_{n})^{-1/2}]
, and
|\hat{C}_{XY}-C_{XY}(r)|=O_{p}[h_{n}+(|S_{n}|h_{n})^{-1/2}].

Proof.

The proof is provided in the Supplementary Material.
∎

Theorem
2
reveals a surprising result: even in the presence of preferential sampling, the sill
\omega
, the semi-variogram function
V_{Y}(r)
and the cross-covariance function
C_{XY}(r)
can still be consistently estimated using classical moment-based estimator (
4
) and kernel-based estimators (
5
) and (
6
), without having to make parametric assumptions on
X(\bm{s})
nor the preferential sampling mechanism. Therefore, we can simply plug
\hat{\omega}
,
\hat{C}_{Y}(r)=\hat{\omega}-\hat{V}_{Y}(r)
and
\hat{C}_{XY}
back into the asymptotic covariance matrix derived in Theorem
1
to enable the inference for
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
. In the simulation study in Section
5.2
, we demonstrate that this approach yields valid confidence intervals.

4
Unbiased Estimation of a Parametric Spatial Covariance Function

Although the nonparametric estimator
\hat{V}_{Y}(r)
consistently estimates the semi-variogram
V_{Y}(r)
under the preferential sampling framework described in Section
2
, it is often desirable in geostatistics to fit a parametric model for
V_{Y}(r)
, and equivalently for the spatial covariance
C_{Y}(r)
. For instance, one may assume
V_{Y}(r)=\zeta(r;\bm{\theta})
with
\zeta(r;\bm{\theta})=\sigma_{e}^{2}+\sigma_{Y}^{2}[1-\exp(-r/\phi_{Y})]
, which corresponds to a special case of the Matérn class (
2
) with
\nu=0.5
and
\bm{\theta}=(\sigma_{Y}^{2},\phi_{Y},\sigma_{e}^{2})
.

To estimate
\bm{\theta}
, we propose to minimize the weighted minimum contrast objective function:

\int_{S}\int_{S}\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\rho_{2}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\left[V_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}||)-\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})\right]^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t},

(7)

where
\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})
is a nonnegative weight function. It is straightforward to show that, under preferential sampling, minimizing (
7
) is equivalent to minimizing

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N}\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left[Z^{*}(\bm{s})-Z^{*}(\bm{t})\right]^{2}-\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})\right\}^{2}\right],

where
{Z}^{*}(\bm{s})=Z(\bm{s})-\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}^{*}
with
\bm{\beta}_{0}^{*}
defined in Theorem
1
. Note that, by Theorem
1
,
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
is a consistent estimator for
\bm{\beta}_{0}^{*}
. We therefore propose to minimize the following function

Q_{MC}(\bm{\theta})=\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N}\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\left\{\left[\hat{Z}(\bm{s})-\hat{Z}(\bm{t})\right]^{2}-2\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})\right\}^{2},

(8)

where
\hat{Z}(\bm{s})=Z(\bm{s})-\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}
. Assuming that
\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})
is differentiable with respect to
\bm{\theta}
and denoting the partial derivative by
\bm{\zeta}^{(1)}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})
, minimizing (
8
) reduces to solving the estimating equation

\bm{Q}_{MC}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta})=\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N}\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\bm{\zeta}^{(1)}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})\left\{\left[\hat{Z}(\bm{s})-\hat{Z}(\bm{t})\right]^{2}-2\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})\right\}=\bm{0}.

(9)

Alternatively, note that, for any pair of distinct locations
(\bm{s},\bm{t})
, the difference
[Z^{*}(\bm{s})-Z^{*}(\bm{t})]\sim\mathrm{N}\left[0,2\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})\right]
. Hence, we can also minimize a weighted negative composite likelihood objective function:

Q_{CL}(\bm{\theta})=\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N}\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\left\{\frac{\left[\hat{Z}(\bm{s})-\hat{Z}(\bm{t})\right]^{2}}{2\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})}+\log\left[\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})\right]\right\},

(10)

which is equivalent to to solving the estimating equation

\bm{Q}_{CL}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta})=\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N}\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\frac{\bm{\zeta}^{(1)}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})}{\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})^{2}}\left\{\left[\hat{Z}(\bm{s})-\hat{Z}(\bm{t})\right]^{2}-2\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})\right\}=\bm{0}.

(11)

A good choice of
\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})
can improve the efficiency of the resulting estimators. In our simulation study, we adopt the following weight:

\mathrm{w}(\bm{s},\bm{t})=\frac{1(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|\leq R)}{2\pi\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\||S\cap(S-\bm{s}+\bm{t})|},

where
1(\cdot)
is the indicator function,
|S\cap(S-\bm{s}+\bm{t})|
is the overlap area between
S
and its translation by
\bm{s}-\bm{t}
, and
R
is a predefined constant of spatial dependence range.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\bm{\theta}_{0}
,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n,MC}
and
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n,CL}
denote the true parameters in
\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta})
and the estimators by minimizing (
8
) and (
10
) on
S_{n}
. Under conditions (C1)–(C6) and (C8)–(C10), as
n\to\infty
, it holds that
\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n,MC}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|=O_{p}(|S_{n}|^{-1/2})
and
\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n,CL}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|=O_{p}(|S_{n}|^{-1/2})
.

Proof.

The proof is provided in the Supplementary Material.
∎

Theorem
3
establishes consistency of the proposed estimators for the parameters
\bm{\theta}
under preferential sampling. This result holds because, when the sampling locations follow an LGCP and the spatial process
Y(\bm{s})
is Gaussian, the two estimating equations (
9
) and (
11
) remain unbiased, regardless of the strength of cross-correlation, by arguments in analogy to those for Theorem
2
.

5
Simulation Study

To evaluate the proposed approaches, we conduct simulations under two preferential sampling scenarios. Experiments are performed on
S=[0,n]\times[0,n]
with covariates
\bm{w}(\bm{s})=[1,w(\bm{s})]
, where
w(\bm{s})
is a fixed realization from a zero-mean Gaussian process with covariance function
\exp(-10r)
. The model is given by

Z(\bm{s})=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}w(\bm{s})+Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s})

with
\beta_{0}=\beta_{1}=1
and
\sigma_{e}^{2}=0.1

7
Conclusion

This paper revisited the estimation of the mean and covariance structures in geostatistical models under preferential sampling. We relaxed the restrictive linear dependence assumption between the point process and the marks in the preferential sampling framework of
Diggle et al. 2010
, allowing the cross-covariance function to take a general, isotropic form. Within this extended setting, we showed surprising findings that the least squares estimator for the regression coefficients and the kernel-based estimators for the spatial semi-variogram and cross-covariance remain consistent and unbiased, except for a bias in the intercept term of the regression coefficients. This bias can be corrected using the estimated cross-covariace at lag zero. Building on these results, we proposed unbiased estimators to infer the geostatistical model, without specifying a parametric sampling mechanism. Simulations under varying cross-correlation structures and an application to tropical rainforest data demonstrated that our method outperforms the likelihood-based approaches in estimation accuracy, computational efficiency and modelling flexibility.

For future work, the first direction would be to extend our proposed method to specialized geostatistical models under more complex preferential sampling mechanism, such that those with spatially varying regression coefficients or spatially varying sampling degrees. Second, it would be interesting to study the scenarios where the underlying point process moves beyond an LGCP, e.g. repulsive point processes that introduce inhibition among sampling locations.

Lemma 1 and Its Proof:

We will need the following lemma to prove Theorems 1–3.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
X
and
Y
are two Gaussian random variables with means
\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}
. Then,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}{[Y\exp(X)]}=\left\{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)+\mu_{Y}\right\}\mathbb{E}[\exp(X)],\\
&\mathbb{E}{\left[Y^{2}\exp(X)\right]}=\left\{\mathrm{Var}(Y)+\left[\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)+\mu_{Y}\right]^{2}\right\}\mathbb{E}{[\exp(X)]}.\end{split}

Proof.

Let
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
be zero-mean centred Gaussian random variables associated with
X
and
Y
. By the Stein’s Lemma,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[Y\exp(X)]&=\mathbb{E}[(Y^{\prime}+\mu_{Y})\exp(X^{\prime}+\mu_{X})]\\
&=\left\{\mathbb{E}[Y^{\prime}\exp(X^{\prime})]+\mu_{Y}\mathbb{E}[\exp(X^{\prime})]\right\}\exp(\mu_{X})\\
&=\left\{\mathrm{Cov}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\mathbb{E}[\exp(X^{\prime})]+\mu_{Y}\mathbb{E}[\exp(X^{\prime})]\right\}\exp(\mu_{X})\\
&=\left\{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)+\mu_{Y}\right\}\mathbb{E}[\exp(X)],\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}\exp(X)]&=\mathbb{E}[(Y^{\prime}+\mu_{Y})^{2}\exp(X^{\prime}+\mu_{X})]\\
&=\left\{\mathbb{E}[(Y^{\prime})^{2}\exp(X^{\prime})]+2\mu_{Y}\mathbb{E}[Y^{\prime}\exp(X^{\prime})]+\mu_{Y}^{2}\mathbb{E}[\exp(X^{\prime})]\right\}\exp(\mu_{X})\\
&=\left\{[\mathrm{Var}(Y^{\prime})+\mathrm{Cov}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})^{2}]+2\mu_{Y}\mathrm{Cov}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})+\mu_{Y}^{2}\right\}\\
&\quad\quad\mathbb{E}[\exp(X^{\prime})]\exp(\mu_{X})\\
&=\left\{\mathrm{Var}(Y)+[\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)+\mu_{Y}]^{2}\right\}\mathbb{E}[\exp(X)].\end{split}

∎

Proof of Theorem 1:

Proof.

Under the defined asymptotic regime, the estimator (3) becomes

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}=\left[\sum_{\bm{s}\in N\cap S_{n}}\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\right]^{-1}\sum_{\bm{s}\in N\cap S_{n}}\bm{w}(\bm{s})Z(\bm{s}).

It minimizes
\sum_{\bm{s}\in N\cap S_{n}}[Z(\bm{s})-\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\bm{\beta}]^{2}
, thus leads to the estimating equation:

\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta})=\sum_{\bm{s}\in N\cap S_{n}}\left[Z(\bm{s})-\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right]\bm{w}(\bm{s})=\bm{0}.

Using the Taylor expansion, we obtain

|S_{n}|^{1/2}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}-\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})=\left[-\frac{\nabla\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{n})}{|S_{n}|}\right]^{-1}\frac{\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})}{|S_{n}|^{1/2}},

where
\nabla\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta})
is the gradient of
\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta})
with respect to
\bm{\beta}
, and
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{n}
is a convex combination of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}
and
\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0}
.

First, by an application of the Campbell’s theorem, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[-\frac{\nabla\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})}{|S_{n}|}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\sum_{\bm{s}\in N\cap S_{n}}\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\right]=\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\int_{S_{n}}\rho(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s},

which, under conditions (C3)–(C4), is an
O(1)
. The variance of the
(i,j)
-th component in
\mathbb{E}[-\nabla\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})/|S_{n}|]
is

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{|S_{n}|^{2}}\int_{S_{n}}\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{s})^{2}\bm{w}_{j}(\bm{s})^{2}\rho(\bm{s})\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\\
&+\frac{1}{|S_{n}|^{2}}\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{s})\bm{w}_{j}(\bm{s})\bm{w}_{i}(\bm{t})\bm{w}_{j}(\bm{t})\left[\rho_{2}(\bm{s},\bm{t})-\rho(\bm{s})\rho(\bm{t})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t},\end{split}

which, under conditions (C3)–(C4), converges to zero as
n\to\infty
.

Second, we analyze

\frac{\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})}{|S_{n}|^{1/2}}=\frac{1}{|S_{n}|^{1/2}}\left[Z(\bm{s})-\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0}\right]\bm{w}(\bm{s}).

Applying the Campbell’s theorem, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})}{|S_{n}|^{1/2}}\right]=\frac{1}{|S_{n}|^{1/2}}\int_{S_{n}}&\mathbb{E}\{[Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s})-C_{XY}(0)]\exp[X(\bm{s})]\}\\
&\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})\mathrm{d}\bm{s}=0.\end{split}

(S1)

Moreover,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})^{2}}{|S_{n}|}\right]&=\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\int_{S_{n}}\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})\mathbb{E}\left\{[Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s})-C_{XY}(0)]^{2}\exp[X(\bm{s})]\right\}\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\\
&+\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left\{[Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s})-C_{XY}(0)][Y(\bm{t})+e(\bm{t})-C_{XY}(0)]\right.\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\ \left.\exp[X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})]\right\}\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})\lambda_{0}(\bm{t})\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{t})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}.\end{split}

By Lemma 1 and the Isserlis’ theorem,

\mathbb{E}\left\{[Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s})-C_{XY}(0)]^{2}\exp[X(\bm{s})]\right\}=\left(\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\{\exp[X(\bm{s})]\}

and

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left\{[Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s})-C_{XY}(0)][Y(\bm{t})+e(\bm{t})-C_{XY}(0)]\exp[X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})]\right\}\\
&=\left[C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)+C_{XY}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left\{\exp[X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})]\right\}.\end{split}

Hence, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})^{2}}{|S_{n}|}\right]=&\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\int_{S_{n}}\rho(\bm{s})\left(\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}\right)\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\\
+&\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\rho_{2}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\left[C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)+C_{XY}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)^{2}\right]\bm{w}(\bm{s})\bm{w}(\bm{t})^{\top}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t},\end{split}

(S2)

which is an
O(1)
under conditions (C3)–(C6).

Next, we divide
S_{n}
into
l_{n}
disjoint sub-blocks of equal volume, up to negligible boundary corrections, which are asymptotically independent due to strong mixing. Denote a sub-block by
B_{n,i}
, thus
S_{n}=\cup_{i=1}^{l_{n}}B_{n,i}
. Write

T_{n,i}=\sum_{\bm{s}\in N\cap B_{n,i}}\left[Z(\bm{s})-\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\bm{\beta}\right]\bm{w}(\bm{s}).

According to (S1)–(S2),
\mathrm{Var}(\sum_{i=1}^{l_{n}}T_{n,i})=O(|S_{n}|)
. Following Lemma 1 in
Guan & Joh 2007
,

|B_{n,i}|^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(T_{n,i}/|B_{n,i}|\right)^{4}\right]<\infty

under conditions (C2)–(C4). Then, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{l_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(T_{n,i}\right)^{4}\right]}{\left[\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l_{n}}T_{n,i}\right)\right]^{2}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{l_{n}}|B_{n,i}|^{2}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{l_{n}}|B_{n,i}|\right)^{2}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{l_{n}}=0.

By the Lyapunov’s central limit theorem,
\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})^{2}/|S_{n}|^{1/2}
converges in distribution to a normally distributed vector with mean zero and covariance matrix as (S2).

The derivations above show that

-\frac{\nabla\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})}{|S_{n}|}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n},\quad\frac{\bm{\mathrm{e}}_{n}(\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0})}{|S_{n}|^{1/2}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathrm{N(\bm{0},\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{n}+\bm{\mathrm{C}}_{n})}.

By the Slutsky’s theorem, the limiting covariance matrix of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}
is given by

\bm{\Sigma}_{n}=|S_{n}|\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n}^{-1}[\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{n}+\bm{\mathrm{C}}_{n}]\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{n}^{-1}.

The remainder of the proof follows arguments similar to those used in establishing the asymptotic normality of first-order estimating equations for point processes, e.g.
Schoenberg 2005
.
∎

Proof of Theorem 2:

Proof.

To establish the consistency of the estimator (5) to
V(r)
, we first show the convergence of the two sequences of random variables in the numerator and denominator, separately. For convenience, write

\begin{split}A_{1,n}(r)&=\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N\cap S_{n}}\left\{\left[Z(\bm{s})-\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}\right]-\left[Z(\bm{t})-\bm{w}(\bm{t})^{\top}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}\right]\right\}^{2}K_{h_{n}}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|-r),\\
A_{2,n}(r)&=\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N\cap S_{n}}K_{h_{n}}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|-r).\end{split}

Recall that
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}
denotes the estimator (3) for
\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0}
on
S_{n}
. We define
\Delta\bm{\beta}_{n}=\bm{\beta}^{*}_{0}-\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}
. Then,
A_{1,n}(r)

where

\begin{split}&\mathcal{A}_{n}(r)=\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N\cap S_{n}}\left[Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s})-Y(\bm{t})-e(\bm{t})\right]^{2}K_{h_{n}}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|-r),\\

&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\ K_{h}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|-r),\\
&\mathcal{C}_{n}(r)=\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\mathop{\sum\sum}^{\neq}_{\bm{s},\bm{t}\in N\cap S_{n}}\left\{\left[\bm{w}(\bm{s})^{\top}-\bm{w}(\bm{t})^{\top}\right]\Delta\bm{\beta}_{n}\right\}^{2}K_{h}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|-r).\end{split}

We now prove the convergence of the three sequences
\mathcal{A}_{n}(r),\mathcal{B}_{n}(r)
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}(r)
.

Consider
\mathcal{A}_{n}(r)
. By an application of the Campbell’s theorem, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}_{n}(r)]=&\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})\lambda_{0}(\bm{t})\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s})-Y(\bm{t})-e(\bm{t})\right]^{2}\exp[X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})]\right\}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\ K_{h_{n}}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|-r)\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}.\end{split}

Since

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Var}[Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s})-Y(\bm{t})-e(\bm{t})]=2\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}-C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)\right],\\
&\mathrm{Cov}\left[Y(\bm{s})+e(\bm{s})-Y(\bm{t})-e(\bm{t}),X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})\right]=0,\end{split}

it follows from Lemma 1 that

&\quad\ \ \mathbb{E}\left\{\exp[X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})+X(\bm{u})+X(\bm{v})]\right\}\end{split}

Then, we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[Z^{*}(\bm{s})-Z^{*}(\bm{t})\right]^{2}\left[Z^{*}(\bm{u})-Z^{*}(\bm{v})\right]^{2}\exp[X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})+X(\bm{u})+X(\bm{v})]\right\}\\
&=4\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}-C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)\right]\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}-C_{Y}(\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}\|)\right]\\
&+2\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}-C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)\right]\\
&\quad[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})+C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})]^{2}\\
&+2\left[C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})+C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]^{2}\\
&+4\left[C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})+C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]\\
&\quad\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})+C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]\\
&\quad\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})+C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]\\
&+2\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}-C_{Y}(\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}\|)\right]\\
&\quad\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})+C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]^{2}\\
&+\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})+C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]^{2}\\
&\quad\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})+C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]^{2}.\end{split}

Hence,

\begin{split}&\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})\lambda_{0}(\bm{t})\lambda_{0}(\bm{u})\lambda_{0}(\bm{v})\bm{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\bm{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})^{\top}\\
&\quad\mathbb{E}\left(\exp[X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})+X(\bm{u})+X(\bm{v})]\left\{\left[Z^{*}(\bm{s})-Z^{*}(\bm{t})\right]^{2}-2\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta}_{0})\right\}\right.\\
&\quad\quad\left\{\left.\left[Z^{*}(\bm{u})-Z^{*}(\bm{v})\right]^{2}-2\zeta(\|\bm{u}-\bm{v}\|;\bm{\theta}_{0})\right\}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{u}\mathrm{d}\bm{v}\\
&=\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\rho_{4}(\bm{s},\bm{t},\bm{u},\bm{v})\bm{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\bm{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{u},\bm{v})^{\top}\\
&\quad\ \ \left\{2\left[C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})+C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]^{2}\right.\\
&\quad\ \ +4\left[C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})+C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]\\
&\quad\quad\ \ \left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})+C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]\\
&\quad\quad\ \ \left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})+C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]\\
&\quad\ \ +\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})+C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})\right]^{2}\\
&\quad\quad\ \ \left.[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{v})+C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{v})]^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{u}\mathrm{d}\bm{v},\end{split}

(S7)

which, under conditions (C3)–(C6) and (C8)–(C9), is an
O(|S_{n}|^{3})
. Here,
\rho_{4}(\bm{s},\bm{t},\bm{u},\bm{v})
is the fourth-order factorial density function of
N
. Similarly, we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[Z^{*}(\bm{s})-Z^{*}(\bm{t})\right]^{2}\left[Z^{*}(\bm{s})-Z^{*}(\bm{u})\right]^{2}\exp[X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})+X(\bm{u})]\right\}\\
&=2\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}-C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|)\right]\\
&\quad\left\{2\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}-C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{u}\|)\right]+[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{t})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})]^{2}\right\}\\
&+2\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{t})-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})+C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})\right]^{2}\\
&+4\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{t})-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})+C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})\right]\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})\right]\\
&\quad\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{t})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})\right]\\
&+\left\{2\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}-C_{Y}(\|\bm{s}-\bm{u}\|)\right]+[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{t})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})]^{2}\right\}\\
&\quad\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})\right]^{2}.\end{split}

Hence,

\begin{split}&\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})\lambda_{0}(\bm{t})\lambda_{0}(\bm{u})\bm{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\bm{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{s},\bm{u})^{\top}\\
&\quad\mathbb{E}\left(\exp[X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})+X(\bm{u})]\left\{\left[Z^{*}(\bm{s})-Z^{*}(\bm{t})\right]^{2}-2\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta}_{0})\right\}\right.\\

&\quad\ \ \left\{2\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{t})-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})+C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})\right]^{2}\right.\\
&\quad\ \ +4\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{t})-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})+C_{Y}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})\right]\\
&\quad\quad\ \ \left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})\right]\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{t})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})\right]\\
&\quad\ \ +\left.\left[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{u})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})\right]^{2}[C_{XY}(\bm{s}-\bm{t})-C_{XY}(\bm{t}-\bm{u})]^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{u},\end{split}

(S8)

which, under conditions (C3)–(C6) and (C8)–(C9), is also an
O(|S_{n}|^{3})
. Here,
\rho_{3}(\bm{s},\bm{t},\bm{u})
is the third-order factorial density function of
N
. Furthermore,

\begin{split}&\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\lambda_{0}(\bm{s})\lambda_{0}(\bm{t})\bm{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\bm{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{s},\bm{t})^{\top}\\
&\quad\mathbb{E}\left(\left\{\left[Z^{*}(\bm{s})-Z^{*}(\bm{t})\right]^{2}-2\zeta(\|\bm{s}-\bm{t}\|;\bm{\theta}_{0})\right\}^{2}\exp[X(\bm{s})+X(\bm{t})]\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t}\\
&=8\int_{S_{n}}\int_{S_{n}}\rho_{2}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\bm{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{s},\bm{t})\bm{\mathrm{w}}(\bm{s},\bm{t})^{\top}\left[\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{e}^{2}-C_{Y}(\bm{s}-\bm{t})\right]\mathrm{d}\bm{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{t},\end{split}

(S9)

which, under conditions (C3)–(C4) and (C8)–(C9), is an
O(|S_{n}|^{2})
. Collecting the results of (S7)–(S9), we have
\mathrm{Var}[\bm{U}_{n}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{0})/|S_{n}|^{2}]=O(|S_{n}|^{-1})
, which converges to zero as
n\to\infty
.

Finally, to determine the convergence rate, under Theorem 1, we consider the Taylor expansion:

|S_{n}|^{1/2}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0})=\left[-\frac{\nabla\bm{U}_{n}^{*}(\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n})}{|S_{n}|^{2}}\right]^{-1}\frac{\bm{U}_{n}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{0})}{|S_{n}|^{3/2}},

where
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
represents
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n,MC}
and
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n,CL}
,
\nabla U_{n}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
is the gradient of
U_{n}^{*}(\bm{\theta})
with respect to
\bm{\theta}
, and
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
is a convex combination of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
and
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. Similar to the derivations above, both
\mathbb{E}[-\nabla\bm{U}_{n}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{0})/|S_{n}|^{2}]
and
\mathrm{Var}[U_{n}^{*}(\bm{\theta}_{0})/|S_{n}|^{3/2}]
converge to constants as
n\to\infty
. Hence, the rate is
O(|S_{n}|^{-1/2})
.
