Quasi-Monte Carlo confidence intervals using quantiles of randomized nets
Zexin Pan

Abstract
Recent advances in quasi-Monte Carlo integration have shown that for linearly scrambled digital net estimators, the convergence rate can be dramatically improved by taking the median rather than the mean of multiple independent replicates. In this work, we demonstrate that the quantiles of such estimators can be used to construct confidence intervals with asymptotically valid coverage for high-dimensional integrals. By analyzing the error distribution for a class of infinitely differentiable integrands, we prove that as the sample size increases, the integration error decomposes into an asymptotically symmetric component and a vanishing remainder. Consequently, the asymptotic error distribution is symmetric about zero, ensuring that a quantile-based interval constructed from independent replicates captures the true integral with probability converging to a nominal level determined by the binomial distribution.

Introduction

Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) methods have emerged as a powerful alternative to conventional Monte Carlo (MC) integration
[
3
]
.
Like MC, QMC approximates a high-dimensional integral
\mu=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}
by averaging
n
function evaluations
\hat{\mu}=(1/n)\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
.
Unlike MC, however, QMC replaces independent sampling with carefully constructed point sets
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n-1}\}
designed to efficiently explore the integration domain
[0,1]^{s}
, which corresponds to
s
uniform random seeds. This paper focuses on digital nets
[
4
]
, a class of point set constructions that enable QMC to mitigate the curse of dimensionality more effectively than classical quadrature rules while achieving convergence rates faster than MC under smoothness assumptions.

Despite their success, QMC estimators based on digital nets face challenges in error quantification because the function evaluations
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
within a QMC mean
\hat{\mu}
are dependent
[
22
]
. Conventional solutions employ randomization techniques to generate independent replicates of QMC means, from which
t
-confidence intervals are constructed. Common choices of randomization are Owen’s nested uniform scrambling
[
21
]
and Matoušek’s random linear scrambling
[
18
]
. Loh
[
16
]
establishes the asymptotic normality of Owen-scrambled QMC means under certain restrictions, thereby justifying the use of
t
-intervals. However, even for infinitely differentiable integrands, the variance of these estimators cannot decay faster than
O(n^{-3})
, compromising their computational efficiency.

Random linear scrambling, on the other hand, produces estimators with the same variance as Owen’s scrambling
[
32
]
, but exhibits markedly different error behavior. These estimators lack asymptotic normality and instead concentrate within a narrow interval whose length adapts to the smoothness of integrands. Notably, for one-dimensional analytic integrands, the median of linearly scrambled QMC means converges to the target integral at super-polynomial rates
O(n^{-c\log n})
for a constant
c>0

[
24
]
. This result was later generalized to multidimensional analytic integrands
[
25
]
. For
\alpha
-times differentiable integrands, Pan
[
28
]
demonstrates that the median attains nearly optimal convergence rates
O(n^{-\alpha-1/2+\varepsilon})
for every
\varepsilon>0
even without prior knowledge of
\alpha
.

The apparent paradox between fast median convergence and slow variance decay is explained by the presence of outliers
[
24
, Section 3]
. Owing to their outlier sensitivity,
t
-intervals constructed under linear scrambling often overestimate uncertainty and achieve coverage exceeding the nominal level, as observed empirically in
[
17
]
. Quantile-based intervals, while more robust and empirically accurate, currently lack theoretical guarantees on coverage—a critical gap that this work addresses.

Before presenting our results, we situate our contributions within the context of existing methods for error quantification with QMC. In
[
19
]
, asymptotically valid
t
-intervals are established by letting the number of independent QMC replicates
r
grow polynomially with the per-replicate sample size
n
. While theoretically sound, this approach requires a total sample size
N=nr=O(n^{1+c})
for
r=O(n^{c})
, resulting in suboptimal convergence rates. An alternative approach by
[
10
]
introduces robust estimation techniques to handle non-normal errors, but still requires reliable variance estimation and is ultimately constrained by the slow decay of the variance. Higher-order scrambled digital nets
[
5
]
attain optimal rates under explicit smoothness priors and enable empirically valid
t
-intervals, though rigorous coverage guarantees remain unproven. A recent method by
[
12
]
establishes valid finite-sample coverage based on empirical Bernstein bounds, but it requires explicit upper and lower bounds on the integrand and its variance decays no faster than
O(N^{-2})
given a total sample size
N
. For completely monotone integrands, point sets with non-negative (or non-positive) local discrepancy yield computable upper (or lower) error bounds
[
9
]
, but their convergence rates degrade with the dimension
s
and become unattractive for
s>4
. We refer to
[
22
]
for a comprehensive survey.

Together, these gaps motivate our focus on quantile-based intervals, which achieve both convergence rates that adapt to the smoothness of integrands and, as we will establish, asymptotically valid coverage for a class of infinitely differentiable integrands including
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp(\sum_{j=1}^{s}x_{j})
and
f(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}(1-x_{j}/2)^{-1}
. Furthermore, the number of replicates
r
need not scale with the per-replicate sample size
n
. Our main result, informally stated, is the following theorem. We refer to Theorem
7
and Theorem
10
for precise statements.

Theorem 1
.

If the integrand
f
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
6
, then the QMC estimator
\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}
with sample size
n=2^{m}
defined in Subsection
2.1
satisfies

\lim_{m\to\infty}\left(\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}<\mu)+\frac{1}{2}\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}=\mu)\right)=\frac{1}{2}.

As a consequence, given
r
independent replicates
\hat{\mu}^{(1)}_{m,\infty},\dots,\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{m,\infty}
ordered from smallest to largest,
we may choose indices
1\leqslant\ell<u\leqslant r
and construct the confidence interval
[\hat{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{m,\infty},\hat{\mu}^{(u)}_{m,\infty}]
for
\mu
. Its asymptotic coverage is guaranteed by Theorem
1
and follows directly from the binomial distribution. Precise expressions are given in Corollary
3
.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section
2
introduces foundational concepts and notation, including the Walsh decomposition framework and properties of Walsh coefficients that are critical to our analysis. Section
3
proves Theorem
1
for complete random designs, a simplified yet illustrative randomization scheme. After outlining the proof strategy, we systematically address each key component of the argument in Subsections
3.1
–
3.3
, and derive crucial corollaries in Subsection
3.4
. Section
4
extends these results to more general randomization schemes, with random linear scrambling as a central special case. Section
5
empirically validates our theory on two highly skewed integrands. Finally, Section
6
identifies challenges in extending these results to finitely differentiable integrands and concludes the paper with a discussion of open research questions.

Background and notation

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
denote the natural numbers,
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, and
\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}=\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
(excluding the zero vector). For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}=\{0,1,\dots,\ell-1\}
. When we wish to emphasize its field structure, we write
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
as
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
The dimension of the integration domain is
s\in\mathbb{N}
, with
1{:}s=\{1,2,\dots,s\}
. For a matrix
C
,
C(\ell,:)
denotes its
\ell
-th row. The indicator function
\boldsymbol{1}\{\mathcal{A}\}
equals 1 if event
\mathcal{A}
occurs and 0 otherwise. For a finite set
K
,
|K|
is its cardinality, and
\mathbb{U}(K)
represents the uniform distribution over
K
. Equality in distribution is written as
X\overset{d}{=}Y
. For asymptotics,
a_{m}\sim b_{m}
denotes
\lim_{m\to\infty}a_{m}/b_{m}=1
and
a_{m}\sim\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}b_{m,\ell}
recursively means
a_{m}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L^{\prime}-1}b_{m,\ell}\sim b_{m,L^{\prime}}
for
2\leqslant L^{\prime}\leqslant L
.

The integrand
f:[0,1]^{s}\to\mathbb{R}
has
L^{1}
-norm
\|f\|_{1}=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}|f(\boldsymbol{x})|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}
and
L^{\infty}
-norm
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{s}}|f(\boldsymbol{x})|
. Let
C([0,1]^{s})
and
C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})
denote the spaces of continuous and infinitely differentiable functions, respectively.

2.1
Digital nets and randomization

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}
, let the binary expansion
i=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}i_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}
be represented by the vector
\vec{i}=\vec{i}[m]=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
. Similarly, for
a\in[0,1)
and precision
E\in\mathbb{N}
, we truncate the binary expansion
a=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}a_{\ell}2^{-\ell}
to
E
digits, denoted
\vec{a}=\vec{a}[E]=(a_{1},\dots,a_{E})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
. For dyadic rationals (numbers with dual binary expansions), we select the representation terminating in zeros.

Let
s
matrices
C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
define a base-2 digital net over
[0,1]^{s}
. The unrandomized points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=(x_{i1},\dots,x_{is})
are generated by

\displaystyle\vec{x}_{ij}=C_{j}\vec{i}\ \ \mathsf{mod}\ 2\text{ for }i\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}},j\in 1{:}s,

(1)

where
\vec{x}_{ij}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
represents
x_{ij}\in[0,1)
truncated to
E
digits (trailing digits set to 0). Typically,
E\leqslant m
for unrandomized digital nets.

We introduce randomization via

\displaystyle\vec{x}_{ij}=C_{j}\vec{i}+\vec{D}_{j}\ \ \mathsf{mod}\ 2\text{ for }i\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}},j\in 1{:}s,

(2)

where
C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
and
\vec{D}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
are random with precision
E\geqslant m
. The vector
\vec{D}_{j}
is called the digital shift and consists of independent
\mathbb{U}(\{0,1\})
entries. A widely used method to randomize
C_{j}
is the
random linear scrambling

[
18
]
:

C_{j}=M_{j}\mathcal{C}_{j}\ \ \mathsf{mod}\ 2,

where
M_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
is a random lower-triangular matrix with ones on the diagonal and
\mathbb{U}(\{0,1\})
entries below, and
\mathcal{C}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
is a fixed generating matrix designed to avoid linear dependencies (see
[
4
, Chapter 4.4]
for details).

Despite the popularity of random linear scrambling, its dependence on
\mathcal{C}_{j}
causes technical difficulties, so we postpone its analysis until Section
4
. In Section
3
, we instead use
complete random designs

[
28
]
, where all entries of
C_{j}
are independently drawn from
\mathbb{U}(\{0,1\})
. This retains the asymptotic convergence rate of random linear scrambling without requiring pre-designed
\mathcal{C}_{j}
. Numerically, errors under complete random designs are typically larger than those under random linear scrambling, but the difference diminishes as
m
increases.

Let
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}[E],i\in\mathbb{Z}_{2^{m}}\}
denote points from equation (
2
) with precision
E
. Our QMC estimator for
\mu=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}
is

\hat{\mu}_{m,E}=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{i=0}^{2^{m}-1}f\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}[E]\right).

(3)

Notice that for random linear scrambling,
\hat{\mu}_{m,E}
implicitly depends on the generating matrices
\{\mathcal{C}_{j},j\in 1{:}s\}
. For most of our paper, we conveniently assume
E=\infty
and focus our analysis on
\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}
. Practical implementation uses finite
E
, often constrained by the floating point representation in use. Corollary
3
quantifies the required
E
to ensure the truncation error
|\hat{\mu}_{m,E}-\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}|
is negligible.

2.2
Fourier-Walsh decomposition

Walsh functions serve as the natural orthonormal basis for analyzing base-2 digital nets. For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x\in[0,1)
, the univariate
Walsh function
\mathrm{wal}_{k}(x)
is defined by

\mathrm{wal}_{k}(x)=(-1)^{\vec{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{x}},

where
\vec{k}\in\{0,1\}^{\infty}
and
\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{\infty}
are the binary expansions of
k
and
x
, respectively. Since
\vec{k}
contains a finite number of nonzero entries, a finite-precision truncation suffices for computation.

For multivariate functions, the
s
-dimensional Walsh function
\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}:[0,1)^{s}\to\{-1,1\}
is given by the tensor product

\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\mathrm{wal}_{k_{j}}(x_{j})=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{x}_{j}},

where
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
. These functions form a complete orthonormal basis for
L^{2}([0,1]^{s})

[
4
]
, enabling the Walsh decomposition:

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\text{where}

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.

Equality (
4
) holds in the
L^{2}
sense. A straightforward calculation using (
4
), such as that in
[
25
, Theorem 1]
, yields the following error decomposition for QMC estimators:

Lemma 1
.

For
f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})
, the error of
\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}
defined by equation (
3
) satisfies

\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}-\mu=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})S(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}),

(5)

where

Z(\boldsymbol{k})=\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}C_{j}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \mathsf{mod}\ 2\Big\}\quad\text{and}\quad S(\boldsymbol{k})=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{D}_{j}}.

We note that each
S(\boldsymbol{k})
with nonzero
\boldsymbol{k}
follows a
\mathbb{U}(\{-1,1\})
distribution given
\vec{D}_{j}
consists of independent
\mathbb{U}(\{0,1\})
entries. The distribution of
Z(\boldsymbol{k})
depends on
m,\boldsymbol{k}
and the choice of randomization for
C_{j}
. Under the complete random design assumption, each
Z(\boldsymbol{k})
with nonzero
\boldsymbol{k}
follows a Bernoulli distribution with success probability
2^{-m}
, and the collection
\{Z(\boldsymbol{k}),\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\}
is pairwise independent. Their distribution under more general randomization schemes is analyzed in Section
4
.

2.3
Notations involving
\boldsymbol{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}

For
k=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}a_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we define the set of nonzero bits
\kappa=\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\mid a_{\ell}=1\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. The bijection between
k
and
\kappa
allows interchangeable use of integer and set notation. In this framework, we can rewrite
Z(\boldsymbol{k})
as

Z(\boldsymbol{k})=\boldsymbol{1}\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\sum_{\ell\in\kappa_{j}}C_{j}(\ell,:)=\boldsymbol{0}\ \mathsf{mod}\ 2\Big\},

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})
and
\kappa_{j}
is the nonzero bits of
k_{j}
.

Next, we define some useful norms on
\boldsymbol{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
. For a finite subset
\kappa\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we denote the cardinality of
\kappa
as
|\kappa|
, the sum of elements in
\kappa
as
\|\kappa\|_{1}
and the largest element of
\kappa
as
\lceil\kappa\rceil
. When
\kappa=\varnothing
, we conventionally define
|\kappa|=\|\kappa\|_{1}=\lceil\kappa\rceil=0
. For
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{s})
and the corresponding
\boldsymbol{\kappa}=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{s})
, we define

\|\boldsymbol{k}\|_{0}=\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}|\kappa_{j}|,\ \|\boldsymbol{k}\|_{1}=\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\|\kappa_{j}\|_{1}\ \text{and}\ \lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil=\lceil\boldsymbol{\kappa}\rceil=\max_{j\in 1{:}s}\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil.

In our later analysis, it is helpful to view
\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
as a
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-vector space. For
\boldsymbol{k}_{1}=(k_{1,1},\dots,k_{s,1})
and
\boldsymbol{k}_{2}=(k_{1,2},\dots,k_{s,2})
, we define the sum of
\boldsymbol{k}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{k}_{2}
to be
\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\oplus\boldsymbol{k}_{2}=(k^{\oplus}_{1},\dots,k^{\oplus}_{s})
with
\vec{k}^{\oplus}_{j}=\vec{k}_{j,1}+\vec{k}_{j,2}\ \mathsf{mod}\ 2
for each
j\in 1{:}s
. In other words, each
\kappa^{\oplus}_{j}
is the symmetric difference of
\kappa_{j,1}
and
\kappa_{j,2}
. We also write
\oplus_{i=1}^{r}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}
for the sum of
\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{r}
. For a finite subset
V\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
, we define the rank of
V
as the size of its largest linearly independent subset. We say
V
has full rank if
\mathrm{rank}(V)=|V|
. One can verify that

S(\oplus_{i=1}^{r}\boldsymbol{k}_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{r}S(\boldsymbol{k}_{i})\text{ for }\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0},

and
\{S(\boldsymbol{k}),\boldsymbol{k}\in V\}
are jointly independent random variables if
V
has full rank.

2.4
Bounds on Walsh coefficients

The following lemma relates the Walsh coefficients
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})
to the partial derivatives of
f
. For
|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|=(|\kappa_{1}|,\dots,|\kappa_{s}|)\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
, let

\displaystyle f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{0}}f}{\partial x_{1}^{|\kappa_{1}|}\cdots\partial x_{s}^{|\kappa_{s}|}}.

Lemma 2
.

For
f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})
,

\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})=(-1)^{\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{0}}\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}(\boldsymbol{x})\prod_{j=1}^{s}W_{\kappa_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},

(7)

where
W_{\kappa}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
for
\kappa\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is defined recursively by
W_{\varnothing}(x)=1
and

W_{\kappa}(x)=\int_{[0,1]}(-1)^{\vec{x}(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor)}W_{\kappa\setminus\lfloor\kappa\rfloor}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

with
\vec{x}(\ell)
denoting the
\ell
-th bit of
x
and
\lfloor\kappa\rfloor
denoting the smallest element of
\kappa
. In particular,
W_{\kappa}(x)
for nonempty
\kappa
is continuous, nonnegative, periodic with period
2^{-\lfloor\kappa\rfloor+1}
and satisfies

\int_{[0,1]}W_{\kappa}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}2^{-\ell-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{x\in[0,1]}W_{\kappa}(x)=2\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}2^{-\ell-1}.

(8)

Proof.

Theorem 2.5 of
[
31
]
with
n_{j}=|\kappa_{j}|
implies equation (
7
). Properties of
W_{\kappa}(x)
are proven in Section 3 of
[
31
]
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

For
f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})
,

|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|\leqslant 2^{-\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}}\|f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}\|_{1}.

Proof.

By equation (
8
),

\displaystyle\Big\|\prod_{j=1}^{s}W_{\kappa_{j}}(x_{j})\Big\|_{\infty}\leqslant\prod_{j\in 1{:}s,\kappa_{j}\neq\varnothing}2\prod_{\ell\in\kappa_{j}}2^{-\ell-1}\leqslant\prod_{j\in 1{:}s}\prod_{\ell\in\kappa_{j}}2^{-\ell}=2^{-\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}}.

The result follows after applying Hölder’s inequality to equation (
7
).
∎

3
Proof of main results

In this section, we prove Theorem
1
for
\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}
under complete random designs. The proof strategy is as follows. Given a sequence of subsets
K_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}
, we use Lemma
1
to decompose the error
\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}-\mu
into two components

\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in K_{m}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})S(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus K_{m}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})S(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}).

(9)

We further define

\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in K_{m}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}),

(10)

where each
S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})
is independently drawn from
\mathbb{U}(\{-1,1\})
. We seek
K_{m}
small enough that
\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}\approx\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}
in distribution, yet large enough that
|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}/\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}|<1
holds with high probability, as made precise in the following lemma:

Lemma 3
.

For a subset
K_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}
, let the sums
\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}
,
\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}
and
\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}
be defined as above. We have the bound

\Big|\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}<\mu)+\frac{1}{2}\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}=\mu)-\frac{1}{2}\Big|\\
\leqslant d_{TV}(\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m},\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m})+\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}|\leqslant|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}|),

where
d_{TV}(X,Y)=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{B}(\real)}|\Pr(X\in A)-\Pr(Y\in A)|
is the total variation distance between the distribution of random variables
X
and
Y
(
\mathcal{B}(\real)
denotes the Borel sets of ).

The proof is given in Appendix A.

To apply Lemma
3
, we define

Q_{N}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\mid\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}\leqslant N\}

and set
K_{m}=Q_{N_{m}}
with

N_{m}=\sup\{N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\mid|Q_{N}|\leqslant c_{s}m2^{m}\},

(11)

where
c_{s}
is a constant to be specified in equation (
15
).
Notice that
\boldsymbol{0}\notin Q_{N}
and
Q_{0}=\varnothing
.

Our proof of Theorem
1
contains the following three steps:

•

Step 1
: prove
\lim_{m\to\infty}d_{TV}(\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m},\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m})=0.

•

Step 2
: prove
\lim_{m\to\infty}\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}|\geqslant T_{m})=0
for a sequence
T_{m}
to be specified in Corollary
2
.

•

Step 3
: prove
\lim_{m\to\infty}\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}|>T_{m})=1
.

From Steps 1 and 3 we obtain

\lim_{m\to\infty}\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}|>T_{m})=\lim_{m\to\infty}\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}|>T_{m})=1,

and together with Step 2 this yields

\lim_{m\to\infty}\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}|>T_{m}>|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}|)=1.

Consequently, both
d_{TV}(\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m},\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m})
and
\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}|\leqslant|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}|)
converge to
0
as
m\to\infty
, and Theorem
1
follows directly from Lemma
3
. The next three subsections are devoted to the proofs of Step 1-3.

Before presenting the proofs, we gather some useful facts about
Q_{N}
and
N_{m}
. Corollary 4 of
[
25
]
establishes the asymptotic relation

|Q_{N}|\sim\frac{D_{s}}{N^{1/4}}\exp\Bigl(\pi\sqrt{\frac{sN}{3}}\Bigr),

(12)

where
D_{s}
is a constant depending on
s
. From equation (
12
), it follows immediately that
\lim_{N\to\infty}|Q_{N+1}|/|Q_{N}|=1
. Together with the defining inequalities
|Q_{N_{m}}|\leqslant c_{s}m2^{m}<|Q_{N_{m}+1}|
, this yields

|Q_{N_{m}}|\sim c_{s}m2^{m}.

(13)

Equating the asymptotic expression (
12
) with
c_{s}m2^{m}
and solving for
N_{m}
gives

N_{m}\sim\lambda m^{2}/s+3\lambda m\log_{2}(m)/s\quad\text{for}\quad\lambda=3(\log 2)^{2}/\pi^{2}.

(14)

3.1
Proof of Step 1

We first show the number of summands in
\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}
is bounded by
2c_{s}m
with high probability.

Lemma 4
.

Under the complete random design assumption,

\Pr\Big(\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})\geqslant 2c_{s}m\Big)\leqslant\frac{1}{c_{s}m}.

Proof.

First recall that
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)=2^{-m}
and
\{Z(\boldsymbol{k}),\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}\}
are pairwise independent. By Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\left|\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})-\frac{|Q_{N_{m}}|}{2^{m}}\right|\geqslant c_{s}m\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}_{s}m^{2}}\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})\right)=\frac{1}{c^{2}_{s}m^{2}}2^{-m}(1-2^{-m})|Q_{N_{m}}|\leqslant\frac{1}{c^{2}_{s}m^{2}}2^{-m}|Q_{N_{m}}|.

Our conclusion then follows from
|Q_{N_{m}}|\leqslant c_{s}m2^{m}
.
∎

Next, we show
Q_{N}
contains few additive relations under the operation
\oplus
defined in Subsection
2.3
. The proof is given in Appendix B.

Lemma 5
.

Let
N\geqslant 1
and
\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{r}
be sampled independently from
\mathbb{U}(Q_{N})
. Then there exist positive constants
A_{s},B_{s}
depending on
s
such that for all
r\geqslant 2

\Pr\left(\oplus_{i=1}^{r}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\in Q_{N}\right)\leqslant A^{r}_{s}N^{r/4}r^{-B_{s}\sqrt{N}}.

As a consequence, we have the following bound on the cardinality of minimally rank-deficient subsets of
Q_{N}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let

\displaystyle I=\{V\subseteq Q_{N}\mid\mathrm{rank}(V)<|V|\},

\displaystyle I^{*}=\{V\in I\mid\text{every proper }W\subset V\text{ has full rank}\},

\displaystyle I^{*}_{r}=I^{*}\cap\{V\subseteq Q_{N}\mid|V|=r\}.

Then with
A_{s},B_{s}
from Lemma
5
, we have for
r\geqslant 2

|I^{*}_{r+1}|\leqslant\frac{|Q_{N}|^{r}}{(r+1)!}A^{r}_{s}N^{r/4}r^{-B_{s}\sqrt{N}}.

Proof.

Notice that
(r+1)!|I^{*}_{r+1}|/|Q_{N}|^{r+1}
is the probability that independent
\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{k}_{r+1}
sampled from
\mathbb{U}(Q_{N})
constitute a set
V\in I^{*}_{r+1}
, which is further bounded by the probability that
\oplus_{i=1}^{r+1}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}
since all proper subsets
W
of
V
have full rank. Because for any given
\boldsymbol{k}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{k}_{r}
, there is at most one
\boldsymbol{k}_{r+1}\in Q_{N}
for
\oplus_{i=1}^{r+1}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}
, we therefore have

\frac{(r+1)!|I^{*}_{r+1}|}{|Q_{N}|^{r+1}}\leqslant\frac{1}{|Q_{N}|}\Pr\left(\oplus_{i=1}^{r}\boldsymbol{k}_{i}\in Q_{N}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{|Q_{N}|}A^{r}_{s}N^{r/4}r^{-B_{s}\sqrt{N}}.

Rearranging yields the claimed bound.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Set
c_{s}
in equation (
11

c_{s}=\frac{1}{4}B_{s}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{s}},

(15)

with
\lambda=3(\log 2)^{2}/\pi^{2}
and

from Lemma

. Then under the complete random design assumption, there exist constants

Let
\mathcal{V}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}\mid Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\}

\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}=\sum_{V\subseteq Q_{N_{m}}}\boldsymbol{1}\{\mathcal{V}=V\}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in V}S(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}).

When
V=\varnothing

V
as
0

\{Z(\boldsymbol{k}),\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}\}
are independent of
\{S(\boldsymbol{k}),\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}\}

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in V}S(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in V}S(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})\overset{d}{=}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in V}S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}).

Let
I_{m}
be

from Lemma
6
with

. We have the bound

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m},\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{V\in I_{m}}\Pr(\mathcal{V}=V)=\Pr(\mathcal{V}\in I_{m}),

(16)

where we have used the fact that the total variation distance satisfies the triangular inequality and is bounded by
1
between any two distributions. By Lemma

, we further have

\Pr(\mathcal{V}\in I_{m})\leqslant\Pr(\mathcal{V}\in I_{m},|\mathcal{V}|\leqslant 2c_{s}m)+\frac{1}{c_{s}m}.

It remains to bound

. Let
I^{*}_{m}
,
I^{*}_{m,r}
be
I^{*},I^{*}_{r}
from Lemma
6
with
N=N_{m}
.
When
\mathcal{V}\in I_{m}
, we can find a subset

\mathcal{W}\in I^{*}_{m}

is at least
3

\Pr(\mathcal{V}\in I_{m},|\mathcal{V}|\leqslant 2c_{s}m)\leqslant\sum_{r=2}^{\lfloor 2c_{s}m\rfloor}\sum_{W\in I^{*}_{m,r+1}}\Pr(W\subseteq\mathcal{V}).

Because

has rank

Then by Lemma

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathcal{V}\in I_{m},|\mathcal{V}|\leqslant 2c_{s}m)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{r=2}^{\lfloor 2c_{s}m\rfloor}2^{-mr}|I^{*}_{m,r+1}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{r=2}^{\lfloor 2c_{s}m\rfloor}2^{-mr}\frac{|Q_{N_{m}}|^{r}}{(r+1)!}A^{r}_{s}N_{m}^{r/4}r^{-B_{s}\sqrt{N_{m}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{r=2}^{\lfloor 2c_{s}m\rfloor}\frac{(c_{s}mA_{s}N_{m}^{1/4})^{r}}{(r+1)!}r^{-B_{s}\sqrt{N_{m}}},

where we have used

\lambda m^{2}/s\leqslant N_{m}\leqslant 2\lambda m^{2}/s

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathcal{V}\in I_{m},|\mathcal{V}|\leqslant 2c_{s}m)\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{r=2}^{\lfloor 2c_{s}m\rfloor}\frac{(c_{s}A_{s}(2\lambda/s)^{1/4})^{r}}{(r+1)!}m^{(3/2)r}r^{-mB_{s}\sqrt{\lambda/s}}

Because

\max_{2\leqslant r\leqslant 2c_{s}m}m^{(3/2)r}r^{-mB_{s}\sqrt{\lambda/s}}=\max(m^{3}2^{-4c_{s}m},m^{-c_{s}m}(2c_{s})^{-4c_{s}m}).

The conclusion follows after we choose

\underline{m}_{s}
large enough.
∎

3.2
Proof of Step 2

We assume
f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})
throughout this subsection. Recall that

\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus Q_{N_{m}}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})S(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}).

Corollary
1
indicates that the magnitude of
\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}
is governed by the growth rate of
\|f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}\|_{1}
as

increases. We now present two results corresponding to different growth regimes. The first, simpler case occurs when
\|f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}\|_{1}
grows exponentially in

Theorem 3
.

Assume

\|f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}\|_{1}\leqslant K_{1}\alpha^{\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{0}}

for some positive constants
K_{1}
and
\alpha
. Then there exist a constant
D_{1}
and a threshold
\underline{m}_{1}
depending on
s
and
\alpha
such that for all

|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}|\leqslant\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus Q_{N_{m}}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|<K_{1}2^{-N_{m}+D_{1}\sqrt{N_{m}}}.

Proof.

We follow the strategy used in the proof of
[
25
, Theorem 2]
. Corollary
1
together with our assumption on
f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}
yields

|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|\leqslant K_{1}2^{-\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}}\alpha^{\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{0}}.

The constraint
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus Q_{N_{m}}
implies
\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}>N_{m}
. By
[
25
, Theorem 7]
,

|\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\mid\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}=N\}|\leqslant\frac{\pi\sqrt{s}}{2\sqrt{3N}}\exp\Bigl(\pi\sqrt{\frac{sN}{3}}\Bigr).

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\|\kappa_{j}\|_{1}\geqslant\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{|\kappa_{j}|^{2}}{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{2s}\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{s}|\kappa_{j}|\biggr)^{2}=\frac{1}{2s}\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{0}^{2}.

(18)

Therefore,
\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{0}\leqslant\sqrt{2s\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus Q_{N_{m}}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{N=N_{m}+1}^{\infty}K_{1}2^{-N}\max\Bigl(\alpha^{\sqrt{2sN}},1\Bigr)\frac{\pi\sqrt{s}}{2\sqrt{3N}}\exp\Bigl(\pi\sqrt{\frac{sN}{3}}\Bigr)

, we can find

. When
m

\sum_{N=N_{m}+1}^{\infty}2^{-N+D_{\alpha}\sqrt{sN}}\leqslant 2^{-N_{m}+D_{\alpha}\sqrt{sN_{m}}}\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}2^{(\rho-1)N}=2^{-N_{m}+D_{\alpha}\sqrt{sN_{m}}}\frac{2^{\rho-1}}{1-2^{\rho-1}}.

By choosing

, we obtain for large enough
m

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus Q_{N_{m}}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|\leqslant K_{1}\frac{\pi\sqrt{s}}{2\sqrt{3}(\sqrt{2}-1)}2^{-N_{m}+D_{\alpha}\sqrt{sN_{m}}}.

The conclusion follows once we choose a large enough
D_{1}
.
∎

for some

depending on
\boldsymbol{x}
and an open ball
V_{\boldsymbol{x}}
containing
\boldsymbol{x}
such that for all multi-indices
|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
,

\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in V_{\boldsymbol{x}}}|f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}(\boldsymbol{y})|\leqslant K_{\boldsymbol{x}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{0}}{\prod_{j=1}^{s}(|\kappa_{j}|)!}.

By the compactness of
[0,1]^{s}
, we can choose

independent of
\boldsymbol{x}
that satisfy (

) with
\mathcal{J}=\{1{:}s\}

3.3
Proof of Step 3

Recall that

\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}),

where each
S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})
is sampled independently from
\mathbb{U}(\{-1,1\})
. Our last step is to show
|\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}|
exceeds the threshold
T_{m}
from Corollary
2
with high probability. For this we require the following lemma from
[
7
]
:

Lemma 8
.

Let
\{c_{i},i\in 1{:}n\}
be a set of real numbers with
|c_{i}|\geqslant 1
for all
i\in 1{:}n
and
\{S^{\prime}_{i},i\in 1{:}n\}
be independent
\mathbb{U}(\{-1,1\})
random variables. Then

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Pr\Big(\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}S^{\prime}_{i}-t\Bigr|\leqslant 1\Big)\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{n}}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

Theorem 5
.

For
T\geqslant 0
, define

\Lambda(T)=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\mid|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|\geqslant T\}.

Suppose that
f
satisfies the assumptions of Corollary
2
and
T_{m}
in equation (
26
) satisfies

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\frac{|Q_{N_{m}}\cap\Lambda(T_{m})|}{|Q_{N_{m}}|}>0.

(28)

Then under the complete random design assumption,

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\sqrt{m}\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}|\leqslant T_{m})<\infty.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{W}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}\cap\Lambda(T_{m})\mid Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\}
. For large
m
, equations (
13
) and (
28
) imply

\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|=2^{-m}|Q_{N_{m}}\cap\Lambda(T_{m})|\geqslant c_{0}c_{s}m

(29)

for some constant
c_{0}>0
and
c_{s}
from equation (
15
).

Following an argument similar to that in Lemma
4
, Chebyshev’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\Pr\left(|\mathcal{W}|\leqslant\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|/2\right)\leqslant\frac{\mathrm{Var}(|\mathcal{W}|)}{\left(\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|-\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|/2\right)^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|}{(\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|)^{2}/4}\leqslant\frac{4}{\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|}.

On the event
|\mathcal{W}|\geqslant\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|/2
, we decompose

\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{W}}S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})+\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{W}}S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}).

Conditioned on the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{Z(\boldsymbol{k}),\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}\}
, we apply Lemma
8
to
\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}
by treating the sum over
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{W}
as a shift term, obtaining

\displaystyle\Pr\left(|\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}|\leqslant T_{m}|Z(\boldsymbol{k}),\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Pr\left(\left|\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{W}}S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})\frac{\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})}{T_{m}}-t\right|\leqslant 1\Bigg|Z(\boldsymbol{k}),\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}\right)\leqslant\frac{1}{2^{|\mathcal{W}|}}\binom{|\mathcal{W}|}{\lfloor|\mathcal{W}|/2\rfloor}\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{W}|}},

where the constant
C
is chosen large enough, as guaranteed by the asymptotic relation
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\sim 2^{n}(\pi n)^{-1/2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(|\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}|\leqslant T_{m}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\Pr\left(|\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}|\leqslant T_{m},|\mathcal{W}|>\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|/2\right)+\Pr\left(|\mathcal{W}|\leqslant\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|/2\right)\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|/2}}+\frac{4}{\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{W}|},

which, together with equation (
29
), yields our conclusion.
∎

The following theorem gives a sufficient condition for equation (
28
). Informally,

must be ”nondegenerate” in the sense that sufficiently many

have

comparable to their upper bounds in equation (

Theorem 6
.

For
\beta>0
and a collection
\mathcal{J}=\{J_{1},\dots,J_{L}\}
of subsets of

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{J}}=\Big\{f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})\Big|\sup_{c>0}\sup_{\beta>0}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{|Q_{N,c,\beta,\mathcal{J}}(f)|}{|Q_{N}|}>0\Big\}.

Then equation (
28
) holds if
f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})
satisfies equation (
25
) for some
K,\alpha>0
and some collection
\mathcal{J}=\{J_{1},\dots,J_{L}\}

.

The proof is given in Appendix C.

Remark 3
.

The definition of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{J}}
may appear nonstandard. Notably,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{J}}
is not convex and excludes the zero function. However, Clancy et al.
[

argue that conical input function spaces are preferred over convex ones in the context of adaptive confidence intervals, and

is by design conical. More generally, some nondegenerate assumption is needed to exclude constant integrands, for which

.
See also

for analogous nondegenerate conditions used to establish asymptotic normality of Owen-scrambled QMC means.

Remark 4
.

As an example of a function not belonging
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{J}}
, consider

satisfying

whenever
|\kappa_{1}|
(the number of differentiation with respect to

) exceeds a threshold
\underline{\kappa}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Lemma

then implies

whenever

and Lemma

shows only an exponentially small fraction of

have nonzero

, so

converges to

regardless of the choice of

and

.

In this case, however,

admits the representation

with

, so one can integrate

along the

direction and apply our algorithm to
\sum_{p=0}^{\underline{\kappa}}g_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}_{-1})/(p+1)
instead. This technique, known as pre-integration in the QMC literature, serves to regularize the integrand. See
[

]
for additional examples.

Another solution is to localize our calculation to
Q^{\prime}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\mid|\kappa_{1}|\leqslant\underline{\kappa}\}
. Specifically, we set
K_{m}=Q_{N_{m}}\cap Q^{\prime}
with

for a suitable

. Repeating the proof strategy of Steps 1–3, we can establish the corresponding results when
f
satisfies equation (
25
) with a collection
\mathcal{J}
of subsets of
2{:}s
, such that

\sup_{c>0}\sup_{\beta>0}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{|Q_{N,c,\beta,\mathcal{J}}(f)|}{|Q_{N}\cap Q^{\prime}|}>0.

These two arguments extend naturally to the case where, for a subset
u\subseteq 1{:}s
and thresholds
\{\underline{\kappa}_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0},j\in u\}
, we have

whenever

for some

. Whether our analysis can be extended to functions

Remark 5
.

It is straightforward to verify the assumptions of Theorem
6
when each derivative
f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}(\boldsymbol{x})
does not change sign over
[0,1]^{s}
. In this case, equations (
7
) and (
8
) imply

\displaystyle|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|\geqslant

\displaystyle\Big(\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{s}}|f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}(\boldsymbol{x})|\Big)\int_{[0,1]^{s}}\prod_{j=1}^{s}W_{\kappa_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{s}}|f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}(\boldsymbol{x})|\Big)\prod_{j\in 1{:}s,\kappa_{j}\neq\varnothing}\prod_{\ell\in\kappa_{j}}2^{-\ell-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{s}}|f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}(\boldsymbol{x})|\Big)2^{-\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}-\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{0}}.

For

to belong to
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{J}}
, it therefore suffices for

\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{s}}|f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}(\boldsymbol{x})|\geqslant c_{0}\beta^{\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{0}}\max_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\prod_{j\in J}(|\kappa_{j}|)!

(31)

to hold for some constants

. In particular, simple integrands such as

The above argument also suggests a regularization strategy in which we add a function with sufficiently large positive derivatives to

to make the sum meet the conditions of Theorem

. For instance, if

satisfies equation (

and some

satisfies equation (

and

. This regularization is not practically useful, however, because choosing suitable

Formulating easily verifiable conditions that allow

Main results

As outlined at the beginning of Section
3
, Steps 1-3 provide all the ingredients needed for the proof of Theorem
1
. Moreover, our analysis yields a quantitative guarantee on the rate at which the quantile of
\mu
converges to

.

Theorem 7
.

If
f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
6
, then under the complete random design assumption,

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\sqrt{m}\Big|\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}<\mu)+\frac{1}{2}\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}=\mu)-\frac{1}{2}\Big|<\infty.

Proof.

By
|\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}|\leqslant T_{m})-\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}|\leqslant T_{m})|\leqslant d_{TV}(\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m},\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m})
and Lemma

,

\displaystyle\Big|\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}<\mu)+\frac{1}{2}\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}=\mu)-\frac{1}{2}\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m},\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m})+\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}|\leqslant|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}|)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m},\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m})+\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}|\geqslant T_{m})+\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}|\leqslant T_{m})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2d_{TV}(\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m},\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m})+\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}|\geqslant T_{m})+\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}|\leqslant T_{m}).

Theorem
2
proves

\lim_{m\to\infty}\sqrt{m}d_{TV}(\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m},\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m})=0.

Corollary
2
proves

\lim_{m\to\infty}\sqrt{m}\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}|\geqslant T_{m})=0.

Theorem
5
together with Theorem
6
proves

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\sqrt{m}\Pr(|\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}|\leqslant T_{m})<\infty.

Our conclusion follows after combining the above results.
∎

The next corollary establishes that sample quantiles of
\hat{\mu}_{m,E}
yield asymptotically valid confidence intervals for
\mu
, provided the precision
E
grows with
m
at a suitable rate.

Corollary 3
.

Fix an integer
r\geqslant 1
and let
\hat{\mu}^{(1)}_{m,E_{m}},\dots,\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{m,E_{m}}
, ordered from smallest to largest, denote
r
independent estimates from equation (
3
) with precision
E_{m}
. If
f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
6
and
E_{m}\geqslant\lambda m^{2}/s
with
\lambda=3(\log 2)^{2}/\pi^{2}
, then under the complete random design assumption,

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\sqrt{m}\Big|\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}<\mu)+\frac{1}{2}\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}=\mu)-\frac{1}{2}\Big|<\infty.

(32)

Moreover, for any indices
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant u\leqslant r
,

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\Pr(\mu\in[\hat{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{m,E_{m}},\hat{\mu}^{(u)}_{m,E_{m}}])\geqslant F(u-1)-F(\ell-1),

(33)

where
F
denotes the cumulative distribution function of the binomial distribution
\operatorname{Bin}(r,1/2)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m,E_{m}}=\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m}+\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}-\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}
. Then

\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}-\mu=\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}-\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}+\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}-\mu=\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}+\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m,E_{m}}.

Lemma 1 of
[
25
]
provides the bound

|\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}-\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}|\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{s}}{2^{E_{m}}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{s}}||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})||_{2}.

(34)

Since
f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})
, its gradient
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})
is continuous over the compact domain
[0,1]^{s}
and therefore has a bounded norm
||\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})||_{2}
. With
E_{m}\geqslant\lambda m^{2}/s
and
N_{m}\sim\lambda m^{2}/s+3\lambda m\log_{2}(m)/s
, we obtain
|\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}-\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}|\leqslant T_{m}
for the sequence
T_{m}
defined in equation (
26
), given
m
is sufficiently large. Hence, under the conditions of Corollary
2
,

\Pr\Big(|\mathrm{SUM}_{2,m,E_{m}}|\geqslant 2T_{m}\Big)\leqslant m^{d_{s,\alpha}}\exp\left(-c^{\prime}_{s}\frac{m}{\log_{2}(m)^{2}}\right)

for large
m
.
Equation (
32
) then follows from a straightforward modification of the proof of Theorem
7
.

Next, using the standard formula for order statistics,

\Pr(\hat{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{m,E_{m}}>\mu)=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{r}{j}\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}\leqslant\mu)^{j}\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}>\mu)^{r-j},

which is monotonically decreasing in
\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}\leqslant\mu)
. From equation (
32
), we have

\liminf_{m\to\infty}\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}\leqslant\mu)\geqslant 1/2,

and therefore

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\Pr(\hat{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{m,E_{m}}>\mu)\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\binom{r}{j}\frac{1}{2^{r}}=F(\ell-1).

(35)

Similarly,

\limsup_{m\to\infty}\Pr(\hat{\mu}^{(u)}_{m,E_{m}}<\mu)\leqslant\sum_{j=u}^{r}\binom{r}{j}\frac{1}{2^{r}}=1-F(u-1).

(36)

Combining equations (
35
) and (
36
) gives the desired coverage bound (
33
).
∎

In addition to providing asymptotically valid coverage, the interval
[\hat{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{m,E_{m}},\hat{\mu}^{(u)}_{m,E_{m}}]
has length
\hat{\mu}^{(u)}_{m,E_{m}}-\hat{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{m,E_{m}}
that converges in probability to

at a super‑polynomial rate. To prove this, we first generalize
[
25
, Theorem 2]
to complete random designs.

Theorem 8
.

If
f\in C^{\infty}([0,1]^{s})
satisfies equation (

) for some

and

The proof is given in Appendix D.

Corollary 4
.

Under the assumptions of Corollary

, we can find for any
\gamma>0
a constant
\Gamma^{\prime}
depending on

such that

\limsup_{m\to\infty}m^{r^{*}\gamma}\Pr\Big(\hat{\mu}^{(u)}_{m,E_{m}}-\hat{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{m,E_{m}}>4K2^{-\lambda m^{2}/s+\Gamma^{\prime}m\log_{2}(m)}\Big)\leqslant\binom{r}{r^{*}},

Given

Remark 6

4
Generalization to other randomization

Up to this point, our discussion has focused on complete random designs. In applications, however, random linear scrambling is often preferred because the resulting digital nets typically have better low-dimensional projections. For example, the construction of
[
13
]
explicitly targets the quality of all two-dimensional projections in its optimization. The key difficulty in extending our results in Section
3
to this setting is that, under random linear scrambling, some rows of the matrices
\{C_{j},j\in 1{:}s\}
in equation (
2
) are not distributed as
\mathbb{U}(\{0,1\}^{m})
. In this section, we establish the additional assumptions required to generalize our main theorem to such broader randomization schemes.

Recall that in random linear scrambling, each matrix takes the form
C_{j}=M_{j}\mathcal{C}_{j}
, where
M_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
is a random lower-triangular matrix and
\mathcal{C}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
is a fixed generating matrix. Typically every
\mathcal{C}_{j}
is nonsingular, ensuring that no two points in the digital net overlap in their one-dimensional projections. A useful consequence of full-rank generating matrices is that, except for the first
m
rows of each
C_{j}
, the distribution of
C_{j}
under random linear scrambling coincides with that under complete random designs. This observation motivates the following notion:

Definition 1
.

The
marginal order
of a randomization scheme for the matrices
\{C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m},j\in 1{:}s\}
is the smallest integer
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that, for every
j\in 1{:}s
and
\ell>dm
, the row
C_{j}(\ell,:)
is independently drawn from
\mathbb{U}(\{0,1\}^{m})
.

The marginal order is
0
for complete random designs and
1
for random linear scrambling provided every generating matrix has full rank. Randomization with higher marginal order becomes relevant when randomizing higher‑order digital nets, such as those introduced in
[
5
]
.

Another key property of random linear scrambling is that for any nonzero
\boldsymbol{k}
,
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)\leqslant 2^{-m+R}
for some constant
R
depending on
s
and the choice of generating matrices
[
25
, Corollary 1]
. This motivates the following general definition:

Definition 2
.

For
r\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\mathbb{V}_{r}=\{V\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\mid|V|=\mathrm{rank}(V)=r\}.

The
r
-way rank deficiency

R_{m,r}
of a randomization scheme for matrices
\{C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m},j\in 1{:}s\}
is defined by

R_{m,r}=mr+\sup_{V\in\mathbb{V}_{r}}\log_{2}\Big(\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\text{ for all }\boldsymbol{k}\in V)\Big),

(37)

where
Z(\boldsymbol{k})
is given by equation (
6
).

In
[
28
]
, a randomization scheme is called asymptotically full-rank if
R_{m,1}
remains bounded as
m\to\infty
. This property holds for random linear scrambling based on common generating matrices, such as those from Sobol’
[
29
]
and Niederreiter
[
20
]
. Much less is known about
R_{m,r}
for
r\geqslant 2
. One might conjecture that
R_{m,r}\leqslant rR_{m,1}
, but this fails in general. For instance,
[
26
, Section 5]
exhibits a three-dimensional example where
R_{m,1}\leqslant 5
while
R_{m,2}\geqslant m/2+3
, and
m
can be taken as an arbitrarily large even number. Fortunately, for most generating matrices the corresponding
R_{m,r}
grows logarithmically in
m
, as made precise in the following theorem:

Theorem 9
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{m}
be the set of nonsingular
m\times m
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and let the generating matrices
\{\mathcal{C}_{j},j\in 1{:}s\}
be independently drawn from
\mathbb{U}(\mathcal{I}_{m})
.
Then for the random linear scrambling based on these generating matrices,

\Pr\Big(R_{m,1}\geqslant 3s\log_{2}(m+1)\Big)\leqslant\frac{\exp(2s)}{(m+1)^{2s}},

and for
r\geqslant 2
,

\Pr\Big(R_{m,r}\geqslant\max\big(R_{m,r-1},(2^{r}+2r-1)s\log_{2}(m+1)\big)\Big)\leqslant\frac{\exp(2sr)}{(m+1)^{2sr}}.

The proof is given in Appendix E.

Corollary 5
.

When
m\geqslant 3
, there exist generating matrices
\{\mathcal{C}_{j},j\in 1{:}s\}
for which random linear scrambling has marginal order
1
and satisfies
R_{m,r}\leqslant(2^{r}+2r-1)s\log_{2}(m+1)
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\{\mathcal{C}_{j},j\in 1{:}s\}
be independently sampled from
\mathbb{U}(\mathcal{I}_{m})
. The marginal order is
1
because every
\mathcal{C}_{j}
is nonsingular. Theorem
9
along with a union bound over all
r\in\mathbb{N}
yields

\displaystyle\Pr(R_{m,r}\leqslant(2^{r}+2r-1)s\log_{2}(m+1)\text{ for all }r\geqslant 1)\geqslant

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(2sr)}{(m+1)^{2sr}}.

For
m\geqslant 3
, we have
(m+1)^{-2s}\exp(2s)<2^{-s}
, so the infinite sum is strictly less than
1
. Hence the probability is positive, guaranteeing the existence of such generating matrices.
∎

Now we generalize Theorem
7
. Let

N_{m}=\sup\{N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\mid|Q_{N}|\leqslant(1/2)\log_{2}(m)2^{m}\}.

(38)

By a calculation similar to equations (

Q_{N_{m}}\sim(1/2)\log_{2}(m)2^{m}
and

We first present a generalization of Lemma

Lemma 9
.

Let
L_{m}\subseteq Q_{N_{m}}
and
\liminf_{m\to\infty}|L_{m}|/|Q_{N_{m}}|>0

Under a randomization scheme with marginal order

Let

6
Discussion

Our analysis has so far focused on infinitely differentiable integrands. A main obstacle in extending our results to finitely differentiable integrands is that the decay of Walsh coefficients is insufficient to decompose
\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}-\mu
in a manner compatible with Lemma
3
. Consider, for instance, an integrand
f
with square-integrable dominating mixed derivatives of order
1
(
f^{|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|}(\boldsymbol{x})
for
|\boldsymbol{\kappa}|\in\{0,1\}^{s}
). By
[
28
, Corollary 3]
with
\alpha=0,\lambda=1
, for any
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{s})\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}
,

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\boldsymbol{\ell},s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leqslant C_{f,s}4^{-\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}},

where
B_{\boldsymbol{\ell},s}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\mid\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil=\ell_{j}\text{ for }j\in 1{:}s\}
and
C_{f,s}
is a constant depending on
f
and
s
. Mimicking the proof in Section
3
, one could set
K_{m}=Q^{\prime}_{N_{m}}
with

Q^{\prime}_{N}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\Big|\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\boldsymbol{\ell},s}\text{ for }\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\text{ satisfying }\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}\leqslant N\Big\},

and attempt to tune
N_{m}
to satisfy Lemma
3
. Unlike
Q_{N}
, however, the set
Q^{\prime}_{N}
is rich in additive relations (it even forms a
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-vector space when
s=1
), which restricts the number of summands in
\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m}
if
d_{TV}(\mathrm{SUM}_{1,m},\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m})
is to remain small. Hence, our proof strategy cannot be directly applied to finitely differentiable integrands.

A natural follow-up question concerns the limiting distribution of
\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}-\mu
. By Theorem
2
and Corollary
2
, we can replace
\hat{\mu}_{m,\infty}-\mu
by
\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}
when studying its limiting distribution. The difficulty lies in the dependencies among
Z(\boldsymbol{k})
. For large
m
, we conjecture that
\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime}_{1,m}
can be approximated by

\mathrm{SUM}^{\prime\prime}_{1,m}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in Q_{N_{m}}}Z^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})S^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}),

where each
Z^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{k})
is sampled independently from a Bernoulli distribution with success probability
2^{-m}
. This approximation holds rigorously for polynomial integrands, where the support of non-zero Walsh coefficients is particularly sparse. How to extend this result to general integrands is another challenging question for future research.

A critical limitation of quantile-based confidence intervals lies in their finite-sample coverage guarantees. Assuming
\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}=\mu)=0
, the coverage probability of the interval
[\hat{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{m,E_{m}},\hat{\mu}^{(u)}_{m,E_{m}}]
from Corollary
3
, with
r
odd and a symmetric choice of indices
\ell=r+1-u
, is maximized when
\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}<\mu)=\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{m,E_{m}}>\mu)=1/2
. Only in this case does the interval reach the nominal level
F(u-1)-F(\ell-1)
. In applications where undercoverage poses significant risks, the slower but conservative
t
-interval may be preferable. If explicit bounds on the integrand are known, the empirical Bernstein interval from
[
12
]
yields finite-sample guarantees and faster-than-MC convergence. Designing intervals that simultaneously achieve robust finite-sample coverage and QMC convergence rates without such bounds remains an open problem.

First notice that

Appendix B
Proofs of Lemma
5
and Lemma
7

This section proves supporting lemmas concerning
Q_{N}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\mid\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{1}\leqslant N\}
. The proof strategy is inspired by
[
8
]
. To simplify the notation, we write
a_{N}=O(b_{N})
if
\limsup_{N\to\infty}|a_{N}|/|b_{N}|<C
for some constant
C>0
and
a_{N}=o(b_{N})
if
\lim_{N\to\infty}|a_{N}|/|b_{N}|=0
.

We first construct an importance sampling measure on
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}
. Recall that each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
corresponds to
\kappa\subseteq\mathbb{N}
through
k=\sum_{\ell\in\kappa}2^{\ell-1}
. Let
L_{N}(\boldsymbol{k})
be the likelihood function of
\boldsymbol{k}
under the importance sampling measure described by

L_{N}(\boldsymbol{k})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\frac{q_{N}^{\ell}}{1+q_{N}^{\ell}}\Bigr)^{\boldsymbol{1}\{\ell\in\kappa_{j}\}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{1+q_{N}^{\ell}}\Bigr)^{\boldsymbol{1}\{\ell\notin\kappa_{j}\}}=\frac{q_{N}^{\|\boldsymbol{k}\|_{1}}}{\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1+q_{N}^{\ell})^{s}}

with
q_{N}=\exp(-\pi\sqrt{s/12N})
. The value of
q_{N}
is chosen so that
L_{N}(\boldsymbol{k})
closely approximates
\mathbb{U}(Q_{N})
. Under
L_{N}(\boldsymbol{k})
, it is clear that
X_{j\ell}=\boldsymbol{1}\{\ell\in\kappa_{j}\}
equals
1
with probability
q_{N}^{\ell}/(1+q_{N}^{\ell})
and
\{X_{j\ell},j\in 1{:}s,\ell\in\mathbb{N}\}
are jointly independent. We use
{\textstyle\Pr},\mathbb{E},\mathrm{Var}
to denote the probability, expectation and variance when
\boldsymbol{k}
follows a
\mathbb{U}(Q_{N})
distribution and
{\textstyle\Pr}^{L},\mathbb{E}^{L},\mathrm{Var}^{L}
to denote those under the importance sampling measure
L_{N}(\boldsymbol{k})
.

Suppose we are interested in
\Pr(\boldsymbol{k}\in A)=|A|/|Q_{N}|
for a subset
A\subseteq Q_{N}
. We can compute it under the importance sampling measure by

\Pr(\boldsymbol{k}\in A)=\mathbb{E}^{L}\Big[\frac{\boldsymbol{1}(\boldsymbol{k}\in A)}{|Q_{N}|L(\boldsymbol{k})}\Big]=\mathbb{E}^{L}\Big[\frac{\boldsymbol{1}(\boldsymbol{k}\in A)}{|Q_{N}|q_{N}^{\|\boldsymbol{k}\|_{1}}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1+q_{N}^{\ell})^{s}\Big].

(41)

Since
\boldsymbol{k}\in A\subseteq Q_{N}
implies
\|\boldsymbol{k}\|_{1}\leqslant N
,

\Pr(\boldsymbol{k}\in A)\leqslant\mathbb{E}^{L}\Big[\frac{\boldsymbol{1}(\boldsymbol{k}\in A)}{|Q_{N}|q_{N}^{N}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1+q_{N}^{\ell})^{s}\Big]=\frac{{\textstyle\Pr}^{L}(\boldsymbol{k}\in A)}{|Q_{N}|q_{N}^{N}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1+q_{N}^{\ell})^{s}.

(42)

Hence, we can bound
\Pr(\boldsymbol{k}\in A)
by
{\textstyle\Pr}^{L}(\boldsymbol{k}\in A)
times a factor depending only on
N
and
s
, which is further bounded by the following lemma:

Lemma 10
.

When
N\geqslant 1
,

\frac{1}{|Q_{N}|q_{N}^{N}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1+q_{N}^{\ell})^{s}\leqslant A_{s}N^{1/4}

with
A_{s}
a constant depending on
s
.

Proof of Theorem

Recall that
\lceil\kappa\rceil
is the largest element of
\kappa\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
\lceil\boldsymbol{\kappa}\rceil=\max_{j\in 1{:}s}\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil
. For a matrix

denotes its
\ell
-th row.

We first prove the result for
R_{m,1}
. For any nonzero
\boldsymbol{k}
, if
\lceil\kappa_{j^{*}}\rceil>m
for a
j^{*}\in 1{:}s
, we can find
\ell\in\kappa_{j^{*}}
such that
\ell>m
and
M_{j^{*}}(\ell,:)
follows a
\mathbb{U}(\{0,1\}^{m})
distribution. Because
\mathcal{C}_{j^{*}}
is nonsingular,
C_{j^{*}}(\ell,:)=M_{j^{*}}(\ell,:)\mathcal{C}_{j^{*}}
also follows a
\mathbb{U}(\{0,1\}^{m})
distribution and
\sum_{j=1}^{s}\sum_{\ell\in\kappa_{j}}C_{j}(\ell,:)=\boldsymbol{0}\ \mathsf{mod}\ 2
occurs with probability
2^{-m}
. Hence, it suffices to consider the maximum of
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\mid\mathcal{C}_{j},j\in 1{:}s)
over all nonzero
\boldsymbol{k}
with
\lceil\boldsymbol{\kappa}\rceil\leqslant m
.

Instead of sampling
\mathcal{C}_{j}
directly from
\mathbb{U}(\mathcal{I}_{m})
, we first sample
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{j}
uniformly from all
m\times m
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and then view
\mathcal{C}_{j}
as
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{j}
conditioned on
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{m}
. For each

, the probability
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{m}
is given by
\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}(1-2^{-m+\ell-1})
, because there are
2^{m}-2^{\ell-1}
choices for the
\ell
-th row of
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{j}
that are linearly independent of previous rows. We notice this probability is monotonically decreasing in
m
and

\lim_{m\to\infty}\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}(1-2^{-m+\ell-1})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1-2^{-\ell})\geqslant\exp(-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{-\ell}}{1-2^{-\ell}})\geqslant\exp(-2),

where we have used
\log(1-x)\geqslant-x/(1-x)
for
x\in(0,1)
.

Now set
C^{*}_{j}=M_{j}\mathcal{C}^{*}_{j}

(55)

\kappa_{j}\neq\varnothing

(56)

Because
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)=\mathbb{E}[\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\mid\mathcal{C}_{j},j\in 1{:}s)]
, the Markov’s inequality shows for each nonzero
\boldsymbol{k}

\Pr\Big(\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\mid\mathcal{C}_{j},j\in 1{:}s)>2^{-m+R}\Big)\leqslant\exp(2s)2^{-R}

(57)

for

Next, observe that

\sum_{j=1}^{s}\sum_{\ell\in\kappa_{j}}C_{j}(\ell,:)=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\Big(\sum_{\ell\in\kappa_{j}}M_{j}(\ell,:)\Big)\mathcal{C}_{j},

\displaystyle\frac{\exp(2s)}{(m+1)^{2s}}.

By the definition of

R_{m,1}\leqslant R

. This completes the proof.
