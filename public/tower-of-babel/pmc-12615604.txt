Optimization of selective catalytic reduction for nox emission control: an experimental and CFD analysis of mixer designs
Authors: Swetha Bandaru, Jayaprabakar Jayaraman, K. Vijetha, Ashish Kumar, G. Hariharan, Man Mohan, Abhishek Sharma, Hemant Raj Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study focuses on optimizing the selective catalytic reduction (SCR) system to reduce urea deposits and enhance overall system performance. Both computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations and experimental analyses were conducted to evaluate an S-bend inlet SCR design and an improved configuration featuring multiple mixer setups. The enhanced design demonstrated superior performance, with the flap mixer reducing the accretion rate to 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ kg/m²s, representing a 60.9% reduction in urea deposition at an injection rate of 41.9 mg/s under acceleration conditions, compared to the no-mixer SCR setup. Furthermore, the thickness of the urea deposit was substantially reduced, with experimental measurements showing a decrease from 5.6 × 10⁻⁴ microns (no-mixer SCR setup) to 2.6 × 10⁻³ microns (flap mixer), marking a 53.6% reduction. The experimental and CFD results exhibited strong agreement, with deviations of 8% for the accretion rate and 7–9% for urea deposit thickness, reinforcing the reliability of the simulation model. The close alignment between experimental and numerical findings highlights the effectiveness of the optimized mixer in promoting uniform droplet breakup and reducing localized deposition. The implementation of optimized mixer geometries improved overall system performance, facilitated compliance with stringent emission regulations, and mitigated operational complexities related to urea accumulation. This study supports the ability of current SCR systems to meet future NOx emission limitations at reduced cost.

Introduction

Selective catalytic reduction (SCR) is a widely adopted strategy for lowering nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions, particularly in diesel engines after-treatment system, which are a major concern for air quality and human health in the automotive and industrial sectors. Among various mitigation strategies, SCR is widely implemented, especially in diesel engines, though problems like urea deposition and accretion pose significant operational hurdles. The inclusion of CuZSM5 catalysts enhances the hydrolysis of HNCO and oxidation of NH₃, contributing to higher NOx conversion efficiency while minimizing fewer unnecessary derivative1. Efficient interaction of urea-water spray with system components is vital for NH₃ creation, influencing droplet interactions with wall and the reduction of nitrogen oxides and hydrocarbon emissions2. The formation of solid urea contents such as biuret HN(CONH2)2 and cyanuric acid (CNOH)3, is often triggered by temperature fluctuations, leading to potential catalyst inactivation and increased pressure difference within SCR systems3. Optimized mixer models play an essential role in achieving uniform ammonia (NH3) distribution with improving velocity profiles, ultimately boosting NOx reduction performance4. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modelling serves as a key tool for predicting ammonia distribution and deposition by incorporating complex interactions such as drag, optimism buoyancy, and turbulence within the droplet flow5. Swirl-type mixers, especially when oriented at a 45º angle position, have demonstrated superior performance in marine SCR applications by improving flow mixing while maintaining minimal pressure drop6. The effectiveness of NH₃ distribution is closely linked to both mixer configurations and injection techniques, directly influencing urea accretion and complete system efficiency7. Moreover, temperature differences and strategies of urea injection significantly affect the decomposition process, NH₃ production rates, and NOx reduction performance8. Enhancements in mixer designs not only improve urea droplet dispersion but also contribute to higher NOx conversion rates while reducing ammonia slip and resisting crystallization under real-world conditions9. The right combination of mixers and injectors enhances NH₃ mixing, minimizes urea deposits on the wall, and ensures optimal SCR system performance10. The present study employs ANSYS Fluent for CFD simulations to analyze these factors in detail11. By examining urea droplet-wall interfaces and urea solid deposit creation, CFD simulations analysis offer valuable understandings for improving the efficiency of urea-SCR systems12. Advanced modelling approaches such as rapid mixing models and diffusion limit simulation models have been utilized to examine the vaporisation and thermal breakdown of urea CO(NH2)2 with water H2O solution droplets, enabling accurate predictions of urea conversion13. To refine predictions of droplet evaporation time, size variation, and deposit formation, a revised Abramzon-Sirignano vaporisation simulation has been introduced14. Further research into heavy-duty diesel engine applications has led to the design of dual baffle mixers, with CFD modelling aiding in the optimization of urea moves, droplet evaporate process, and deposit creation15. The Leidenfrost effect plays an essential role in determining UWS vaporisation dynamics and urea breaks down due to heat, significantly impacting deposit creation process and ammonia (NH3) circulation16. Additionally, studies on various mixer geometries highlight a strong correlation between urea deposition formation outlines, exhaust gas temperature, and urea injection rates, offering deeper insights into solid deposit development processes17. Incorporating static blade design mixers into SCR systems has shown promising results in enhancing ammonia reaction efficiency with gas, enhancing NOx reduction performance, and decreasing ammonia creep, thereby optimizing urea-to-ammonia conversion18. This study introduces an optimized SCR system designed to minimize urea deposition while maximizing NOx reduction efficiency. Using both CFD simulations and experimental validation, this research compares a conventional SCR system with an enhanced design featuring advanced mixer configurations. The findings demonstrate significant improvements in reagent distribution and NOx conversion, highlighting the potential of optimized SCR designs to meet stringent exhaust emission standards while addressing operational challenges. This design enables older SCR systems to comply with future emission limitations, allowing for easier maintenance by end-users with lower investment, without the need to replace the entire SCR system. However, the study has some limitations: the SCR system with the mixer is most effective in the three stages of the aftertreatment process i.e., DOC, DPF, and SCR.

Selective catalytic reduction: advanced insights

Functional principle and urea decomposition

SCR is a significant technology for reducing NOx emissions from engines with diesel exhaust gas. This SCR system injects a UWS into the hot exhaust stream, where it undergoes a series of chemical transformations to generate ammonia (NH₃). The ammonia bonds with nitrogen oxides over a catalyst material in SCR unit, converting into gas of nitrogen and water, thereby mitigating their environmental impact. To continue this study deep analysis on the effectiveness of SCR technology largely depends on the efficiency of urea decomposition, which involves two primary steps: thermolysis and hydrolysis. After injection, urea CO(NH2)2 thermally decomposes into ammonia NH3 and isocyanic acid (HNCO) at higher temperatures. The isocyanic acid further hydrolyses in the presence of aqueous vapor, producing additional ammonia and CO₂. This ammonia -NH₃ then engages in SCR unit reactions with NOx, leading to emissions reduction.

A controlled decomposition process is required to maximize ammonia availability for NOx reduction. However, this incomplete hydrolysis of isocyanic acid can lead to by-product formation, reducing the overall SCR system performance. To examine this process is highly effective, several operational challenges impact overall system efficiency, including non-uniform ammonia dispersion, urea deposition, and ammonia slip. Most primary concerns in SCR technology are the creation of solid deposits from urea, which can form when exhaust gas temperatures (EGT) are insufficient for complete decomposition. At lower exhaust gas temperatures, compounds such as cyanuric acid (CNOH)3, biuret HN (CONH2)2, and ammonium nitrate may form as solid deposits, leading to flow obstructions, reduced catalytic efficiency, and long-term system degradation. Additionally, non-uniform ammonia distribution can cause localized NOx reduction inefficiencies and increased NH₃ slip, which results in unreacted ammonia being released into the atmosphere. To mitigate these challenges, optimizing urea injection parameters and exhaust flow dynamics is crucial. Factors such as exhaust temperature, injector placement, spray atomization, and droplet evaporation rates directly influence urea breakdown and ammonia generation. As emission regulations continue to evolve, the innovation of high efficiency catalysts, improved urea injection strategies, and advanced exhaust flow management techniques remains a key focus in SCR research. Future innovations in catalyst surface modifications and active deposit-mitigation strategies are expected to further enhance the durability and effectiveness of SCR systems, ensuring compliance with stringent environmental standards. In addition to automotive and industrial applications, SCR technology is also widely utilized in marine systems, where efficient ammonia dispersion is crucial for optimal performance. Marine SCR systems have demonstrated superior performance when utilizing flapper and blade mixers, as these designs promote effective turbulence generation and uniform ammonia distribution19. Numerical simulations have confirmed that increased instability and optimized droplet dispersion play a crucial role in enhancing ammonia conversion in marine applications and industrial SCR systems20. A well-designed mixer ensures even NH₃ distribution, reducing the likelihood of urea deposition and wall wetting while improving overall SCR performance21. Advancements in mixer designs and urea injection strategies

To further optimize SCR system performance, researchers have explored various mixer designs and refined urea injection strategies. Static mixer configurations, including blade, swirl, and hybrid designs, have been shown to improve ammonia dispersion and enhance NOx reduction efficiency, particularly in high-load applications. Experiments studying urea breakdown with and without a catalyst highlight that catalytic surfaces speed up decomposition and help prevent solid deposits22. Additionally, the influence of SCR catalysts extends beyond NOx reduction, as they contribute to controlling nitrous oxide (N₂O) emissions, particularly in low-temperature operating environments23. In added to that, a three-dimensional CFD model for urea injection has offered important understanding of how ammonia spreads and how droplets evaporate under different exhaust conditions24. Building on these insights, high-pressure SCR (HP-SCR) systems, incorporating flow-guiding elements, have been optimized to minimize pressure losses while improving exhaust flow uniformity25. Injection parameters, including pressure and temperature, directly impact UWS spray characteristics, influencing droplet size, evaporation rate, and urea decomposition efficiency26. Enhancing urea transformation processes while ensuring optimal ammonia dispersion can significantly reduce NH₃ slip and deposit formation, contributing to improved SCR system longevity27. To complement these advancements, improvements in spray modelling techniques have refined the understanding for deep analysis of droplet interactions with surface wall, Leidenfrost effects, and catalyst material assisted for vaporization, resulting in better predictive accuracy for ammonia homogenization28. Recent studies investigate on optimized mixer configurations have demonstrated their effectiveness in reducing urea deposition, improving flow uniformity, and enhancing NOx conversion efficiency29. Among the investigated designs, hybrid mixers have emerged as the most effective in balancing flow uniformity and turbulence generation, ensuring improved NH₃ distribution while reducing urea deposit formation30. A field demonstration in mobile crane applications showcased a NOx conversion efficiency exceeding 93% by implementing an advanced urea dosing system while maintaining minimal NH₃ slip31. Comparative analyses of different urea injector designs, such as the I & L types, have revealed that the I-type excels in maximizing ammonia yield, while the L-type promotes more uniform ammonia dispersion32. In this case study involving the Hyundai D6CC engine, an optimized urea injector design successfully mitigated urea solidification issues, leading to improved NH₃ production and NOx conversion rates33. CFD-validated spray behaviour models have further refined predictions regarding droplet size, velocity, and evaporation dynamics, contributing to improved system design34. Insights into droplet evaporation mechanisms highlight the impact of on a refined urea aqueous solution (UWS) decomposition model has demonstrated that while urea has a negligible effect on water evaporation, its thermal decomposition is highly temperature-dependent35.

Optimization techniques for SCR system performance

Current ongoing research efforts are focused on refining SCR system performance through advanced modelling techniques and experimental validation. Analytical tools have been developed to quantify urea decomposition by-products, facilitating improved aftertreatment processes and reducing deposit accumulation36. Experimental studies on UWS droplet deposition under different exhaust conditions have deepened the understanding of spray-wall interactions, enabling better strategies to minimize solid deposit formation37. The integration of kinetic urea decomposition models with CFD-based UWS injection simulations has provided accurate predictions of spray behaviour and solid deposit tendencies38. To prevent droplet deposition and crystallization and thereby improve overall SCR system performance, better urea injection methods have been suggested in that research work39. Enhanced fuel distribution and NOx conversion rates have been achieved by using hydrocarbon lean (HC-LNT) NOx trap catalysts together with static type mixer40. Moreover, static mixers have been successfully used in marine application aftertreatment SCR systems to ensure uniform NH₃ circulation and meet strict emissions regulations41. A unique mixer configuration has been developed to improve velocity uniformity and ammonia dispersion while reducing crystallization risks, particularly at lower engine speeds in their research study42. Investigations into the role of pressure in deposit formation have indicated that higher pressures aid in minimizing residue accumulation, thereby improving system efficiency43. Numerical studies have highlighted that lower exhaust temperatures and increased injection rates contribute to urea deposition, emphasizing the need for optimized mixer designs to control deposition patterns44. The impact of spray impingement on wall temperatures has also been explored, revealing that maintaining higher exhaust temperatures reduces urea deposit accumulation45. Strategies are considered for that improve mixing caused by the spray and enhance the breakdown of urea into ammonia have been proven effective through both experiments and computer simulations46. Among the key factors influencing NH₃ distribution, a 75º injection angle combined with four-hole injectors has been found to produce the best outcomes47. Further studies on UWS spray-wall interactions have reinforced the importance of controlled wall temperatures in minimizing crystallization risks and ensuring effective droplet evaporation48. In marine SCR systems, detailed deposit composition analyses have identified sulphate and particulate matter as key contributors to urea deposit formation, emphasizing the need for innovative SCR designs to address these current working challenges49. These all-key findings highlight the continuous evolution of SCR technology to enhance NOx reduction efficiency while ensuring long-term operational stability.

NOx sensors

Two Siemens NOx sensors were deployed: one before the DOC to measure engine-out NOx emissions and another downstream of the SCR unit to monitor tailpipe NOx levels. These sensors play a crucial role in NOx conversion calculations and provide real-time feedback for NH₃ dosing control.

FTIR analysis

An FTIR analyzer (MKS Series 2000 Multi Gas Analyzer) was used to evaluate the exhaust gas composition at different points along the aftertreatment system. It also monitored NH₃ slip during driving cycles at a sampling frequency of 1 Hz.

Temperature measurements and insulation

A series of Pt200 sensors and K-type thermocouples were installed throughout the exhaust system. The DOC and CDPF components were insulated with aluminum foil and thermal bands, while the urea injection section and ammonia inlet were loosely wrapped in aluminum foil for heat management.

Results and discussion

Role of liquid wall film in solid deposit formation in SCR systems

In urea-SCR systems, it is essential to differentiate between wall film (liquid phase) and solid deposits, as misinterpretation can lead to incorrect conclusions. The wall film forms when urea-water droplets impact the SCR system walls, spreading as a thin liquid layer whose behaviour is defined by film height (hd, film), velocity (vd, film), and heat flux (hfd, film). The film formation and persistence are further influenced by the urea mass fraction in the film (yfilm, urea​), which determines the concentration of urea in the liquid phase. This parameter is critical because a higher yfilm, urea​ increases the likelihood of deposit formation as water evaporates, leaving behind solid by-products.

The total deposition risk is directly proportional to film formation, which is strongly governed by the hydrodynamic risk52. If the liquid film persists for longer durations due to inadequate evaporation or insufficient flow velocity, it increases the probability of transitioning into solid deposits. The hydrodynamic risk quantifies this persistence by considering film thickness, velocity, and heat flux, where inadequate heat transfer or stagnant flow conditions prevent effective liquid removal. Validation of CFD predictions with experimental data

The experimental validation on No mixer SCR system and Flap mixer SCR system in three different range of Injection rates: 19.6 mg/s (deacceleration), 31.5 mg/s (cruise) and 41.9 mg/s (acceleration) 52. For an injection rate at acceleration condition 41.9 mg/s, the comparison between CFD simulations and experimental data demonstrated reasonable agreement, reinforcing the reliability of the simulation model. In the No Mixer configuration, the CFD-predicted accretion rate was 8.5 × 10⁻⁵ kg/m²s52, whereas experimental measurements recorded 9.2 × 10⁻⁵ kg/m²s, indicating an 8% deviation. This discrepancy arises due to uneven droplet distribution in the absence of a mixer, localized high-deposition zones, and minor wall temperature variations during experiments.

The Flap Mixer setup showed improved agreement, with the CFD simulation predicting an accretion rate of 3.3 × 10⁻⁴ kg/m²s52 and experimental findings reporting 3.6 × 10⁻⁴ kg/m²s, resulting in a 9% deviation for injection rate at acceleration condition 41.9 mg/s. This study demonstrates the flap mixer’s (Model 1) effectiveness in enhancing droplet dispersion and promoting uniform urea distribution, thereby mitigating wall deposits. Conclusion and recommendations

Comparison of experimental and CFD results

A comparative analysis of experimental data and CFD simulations was conducted to validate the model’s precision in predicting accretion rates and urea deposit thickness across various mixer setups. The results demonstrated strong agreement, reinforcing the reliability of the CFD approach.

For the no mixer SCR setup model case in this study, the experimentally measured accretion rate was 0.00092 kg/m²s, closely aligning with the CFD-predicted 0.00085 kg/m²s, yielding a deviation of approximately 8%. This minor discrepancy stems from transient effects during system operation and slight inconsistencies in droplet distribution. In contrast, the flap mixer (Model 1) configuration exhibited even better alignment, with the experimental accretion rate at 0.00036 kg/m²s and the CFD value at 0.00033 kg/m²s, reflecting a 9% deviation. These findings confirm the SCR with flap mixer’s (Model 1) ability to enhance turbulence, promote finer droplet dispersion, and reduce wall urea deposits.

Similarly, urea deposit thickness comparisons reaffirmed the CFD model’s accuracy. In the no mixer SCR configuration, the experimental measurement recorded 0.56 microns, while CFD predicted 0.51 microns, with a 9% deviation. In this research for this Flap mixer setup (Model 1), experimental measurements indicated a thickness of 2.6 microns, aligning closely with the CFD predicted 2.4 microns, resulting in a 7% deviation. These results in this study highlight the model’s capability to simulate complex urea vaporisation and deposition mechanisms with high precision.

Impact of SCR with mixer designs

The assessment of different mixer setups through CFD and experimental tests revealed substantial improvements in minimizing accretion and controlling urea deposition. Among the assessed designs, the swirl mixer (Model 2) proved the very effective, accomplishing a 90.35% decrease in accretion rate at acceleration injection rate 41.9 mg/s when assessed to the no mixer SCR setup. While this configuration significantly improved flow uniformity, it resulted in a slight raise in to 4.7 microns deposit thickness, which remains within acceptable limits for operational efficiency.

On the other hand, the flap mixer (Model 1) demonstrated higher accretion and high deposit thickness comparative with the swirl mixer (Model 2). While it improved ammonia distribution, it was less efficient in controlling deposition due to uneven droplet dispersion. The combination of swirl and flap mixer setup (Model 3) showed moderate urea accretion reduction but enhanced mixing characteristics. The hybrid mixer setup (Model 4) further improved flow distribution but led to slightly improved deposition rates while judged with swirl mixer SCR setup alone. These analyses emphasize the importance of selecting an optimized mixer setup in SCR to balance urea distribution efficiency and deposition minimization.

Industrial significance

This research study’s outcomes offer valuable insights for industries aiming to enhance SCR system efficiency, particularly in reducing maintenance costs and improving system durability. The swirl mixer (Model 2) demonstrated superior performance in minimizing accretion and urea deposit thickness while maintaining optimal reagent distribution, making it a strong candidate for real-world applications. By reducing urea buildup, the risk of clogging is significantly lowered, leading to improved reliability, prolonged system lifespan, and reduced operational expenses. Optimized mixer designs also aid in meeting stringent emission regulations, ensuring compliance with evolving environmental standards while enhancing overall SCR performance.

Future research directions

Further research should explore advanced mixer geometries to extend mitigate urea deposition while maximizing reagent dispersion. Enhancements to the Swirl Mixer, such as refining vane angles and optimizing swirl intensity, may sustain its low deposition characteristics with higher injection rates. Furthermore, modifications to the hybrid mixer setup could balance the benefits of swirl blade design and flap blade design mixers while minimizing deposition challenges. Future research should also integrate practical life world SCR system testing under dynamic engine working environment to validate the optimized designs and ensure their adaptability to diverse industrial functions.
