HIV infection complicated with talaromyces marneffei, tuberculosis, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis and non-Hodgkin lymphoma: a complex case report
Authors: Wei Fu, Zi Wei Deng, Pei Wang, Zhen Wang Zhu, Zhi Bing Xie, Yong Zhong Li, Hui Qiang Zhang, Hong Ying Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to severe immunosuppression, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infected individuals are prone to multiple opportunistic infections and malignancies. This report presents a complex case of a 62-year-old male HIV patient who was diagnosed with co-infections of Talaromyces marneffei, tuberculosis, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), and ultimately non-Hodgkin B-cell lymphoma. This case provides valuable insights for clinical diagnosis and treatment.Case summaryThe patient was admitted with complaints of “poor appetite, weight loss, and diarrhea for two weeks, accompanied by fever for four days.” Upon admission, the patient was diagnosed with HIV infection, with a CD4 + T-cell count of only 25/µL. Pathological examination of cervical lymph node aspiration and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) confirmed infections with Talaromyces marneffei, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Aspergillus species. During treatment, the patient developed persistent high fever, pancytopenia, decreased fibrinogen, and significantly elevated ferritin levels. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed splenomegaly. Further bone marrow examination confirmed HLH, and symptoms improved after treatment with glucocorticoids and immunoglobulin. However, the patient’s condition further deteriorated, and positron emission tomography computed tomography (PET-CT) along with repeat lymph node biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of non-Hodgkin B-cell lymphoma. Under combined antifungal, anti-tuberculosis, antiretroviral therapy (ART), and chemotherapy, the patient showed clinical improvement.ConclusionThis case highlights the complexity and challenges in diagnosing and managing multiple opportunistic infections and malignancies in advanced HIV patients. Background

HIV-infected individuals with advanced disease are prone to severe immunosuppression, a condition commonly referred to as Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) [1], particularly a marked reduction in CD4 + T cell counts, which predisposes them to a wide range of opportunistic infections and malignancies [2]. Among these, co-infections with Talaromyces marneffei and Mycobacterium tuberculosis are relatively common in Southeast Asia and southern China [3, 4]. However, the simultaneous occurrence of multiple opportunistic infections, such as Talaromyces marneffei, tuberculosis, and Aspergillus fumigatus, in the same patient is extremely rare. Such cases often present overlapping clinical features, posing significant diagnostic challenges, especially when complicated by other immune dysregulation syndromes or malignancies.

HLH is one such rare but life-threatening immune dysregulation syndrome characterized by excessive inflammation and tissue damage due to abnormal immune activation [5]. It is often triggered by infections, malignancies, or autoimmune disorders [6]. In HIV/AIDS patients, HLH can result from various triggers, including direct viral effects, opportunistic infections, and malignancies [7]. The co-occurrence of HLH with multiple infections in immunocompromised patients is not only rare but also often underdiagnosed due to its nonspecific clinical manifestations and overlap with other systemic illnesses [7]. Early recognition and timely management of HLH are crucial for improving patient outcomes.

In addition to HLH, HIV/AIDS patients are at increased risk of developing malignancies due to chronic immune activation, persistent inflammation, and opportunistic infections [8]. Non-Hodgkin B-cell lymphoma, a common HIV-related malignancy, is highly aggressive and often progresses rapidly [9]. This adds another layer of complexity to the clinical management of advanced HIV/AIDS patients. In cases where infections, HLH, and malignancies coexist, the diagnostic process becomes exceptionally challenging due to overlapping symptoms and the dynamic progression of disease states.

This report presents a rare and highly complex case of an HIV/AIDS patient with concurrent Talaromyces marneffei, tuberculosis, and Aspergillus fumigatus infections, HLH, and subsequent diagnosis of high-grade B-cell lymphoma. Through this case, we aim to highlight the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in managing such patients, emphasizing the importance of a systematic and comprehensive diagnostic approach. This includes integrating traditional methods, such as pathology and blood cultures, with modern molecular techniques like mNGS. Furthermore, the case underscores the critical role of dynamic disease assessment, multidisciplinary collaboration, and individualized treatment strategies in improving outcomes for HIV/AIDS patients with complex disease presentations. By documenting this unique and challenging case, we hope to provide valuable insights for the clinical management of similar cases and contribute to the growing understanding of the interplay between infections, immune dysregulation, and malignancies in HIV/AIDS patients.

Case report

A 62-year-old male farmer presented to our department on June 29, 2024, with complaints of “poor appetite, weight loss, and diarrhea for half a month, along with fever for four days.” The patient’s medical history revealed a significant decrease in appetite with food intake reduced to one-third of his usual, accompanied by a weight loss of approximately 5 kg over the past two weeks. He also experienced watery yellow diarrhea about 10 times per day. For the last four days, he had recurrent high fever (maximum temperature 40 °C) with chills, but no abdominal pain, cough, sputum production, or dyspnea. He had been admitted to a local hospital for two days, where HIV antibody testing was positive, and was subsequently referred to our hospital for further evaluation and treatment. Notably, two months earlier, the patient had been diagnosed with left thigh herpes zoster, which had resolved.

Physical examination: On admission, his vital signs were as follows: temperature 39.0 °C, pulse 96 beats/min, respiratory rate 20 breaths/min, and blood pressure 144/77 mmHg. The patient appeared malnourished with an acute facial expression and oral thrush. Painless, enlarged lymph nodes of varying sizes were palpable in the right cervical region, with the largest being approximately the size of a quail egg and of slightly hard consistency. Imaging studies revealed nodules in the right hilum, mediastinal and retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly (Fig. 1A), and pelvic effusion. Cervical lymph node ultrasonography demonstrated multiple hypoechoic masses on both sides of the neck, with the largest measuring 15 × 6 mm on the left and 40 × 15 mm on the right (Fig. 1B). His chest CT demonstrated no characteristic findings of pulmonary tuberculosis or typical bacterial pneumonia, such as tree-in-bud opacities or lobar consolidation. These masses had well-defined borders, full shapes, significantly thickened cortices, loss of medullary structure, and heterogeneous echogenicity. To evaluate for disseminated infection, careful examination of solid organs was performed. Although CT imaging demonstrated splenomegaly and widespread lymphadenopathy involving cervical, mediastinal, and retroperitoneal regions, no focal lesions were identified in other solid organs including the liver and kidneys, and no definitive evidence of visceral organ dissemination beyond the lymphatic system was observed at this stage. Laboratory findings indicated pancytopenia, with markedly reduced white blood cell (WBC) and platelet (PLT) counts (2.6 × 10⁹/L and 81 × 10⁹/L, respectively) and mild anemia (hemoglobin (HGB) 104 g/L). Markers of infection and inflammation were significantly elevated, including a C-reactive protein level of 95.65 mg/L, procalcitonin (PCT) of 1.62 µg/L, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 523 U/L, and ferritin at an exceptionally high level of 15,725 ng/mL. HIV-related findings showed profound immunosuppression, with a CD4 + T-cell count of 25/µL and an HIV-1 RNA viral load of 2.08 × 10⁶ copies/mL. Testing for fungal infections revealed a positive β-D-glucan test (G-test) (581 pg/mL, ) with a negative Galactomannan (GM) test. Tuberculosis screening like tuberculin skin test and tuberculosis interferon-gamma release assay (TB-IGRA) was negative, given the absence of clinically significant cough or expectoration, mycobacteriological examinations including acid-fast bacilli smear microscopy, mycobacterial culture, and nucleic acid amplification testing were not indicated. While viral DNA levels showed elevated Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) DNA Load (22,700 copies/mL) and Cytomegalovirus (CMV) DNA Load (977 copies/mL). Given the strong suspicion of fungal infection, intravenous amphotericin B was initiated on the second day of admission. Two days later, cervical lymph node pathology (Fig. 1C) and fungal culture of whole blood confirmed Talaromyces marneffei. However, cervical lymph node fine-needle aspiration mNGS not only detected Talaromyces marneffei (262,731 reads) but also identified Mycobacterium tuberculosis (468,311 reads) and Aspergillus fumigatus (1,040 reads). Interpretation of these mNGS findings required careful clinical correlation. Given the patient’s severe immunosuppression, tuberculosis represented a critical opportunistic infection that, if left untreated, could lead to severe consequences. The high mNGS read count for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, combined with clinical findings of lymphadenopathy and systemic symptoms, strongly supported active tuberculous infection despite negative tuberculin skin test and TB-IGRA, which are known to have poor sensitivity in severely immunocompromised patients. Regarding Aspergillus fumigatus, while detected by mNGS, the low read count and negative GM test made it uncertain whether this represented true infection or mere detection of environmental presence. Importantly, the broad-spectrum amphotericin B initiated for Talaromyces marneffei would also provide coverage against potential Aspergillus infection. After comprehensive evaluation, we determined that Talaromyces marneffei and Mycobacterium tuberculosis were the most clinically significant infections requiring immediate targeted therapy. The cervical lymph node biopsy pathology demonstrated effacement of normal architecture by proliferating histiocytes and necrotic tissue replacing lymphoid structures. The stroma exhibited lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, with focal histiocytes containing abundant small, round fungal-like organisms in their cytoplasm. In conjunction with the clinical history, these findings were consistent with Talaromyces marneffei infection. Accordingly, anti-tuberculosis therapy consisting of ethambutol, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and levofloxacin was initiated. Rifampin was avoided due to the potential use of integrase inhibitors for ART.

Despite these interventions, the patient’s fever persisted. On July 4, follow-up investigations revealed pancytopenia (WBC 1.7 × 10⁹/L, HGB 90 g/L, PLT 16 × 10⁹/L), further elevated LDH (1,408 U/L) and PCT (37.77 µg/L), decreased fibrinogen (FIB 0.85 g/L), and a striking ferritin level of 53,515 ng/mL. Given the clinical suspicion of HLH based on these findings, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (20 g daily) and dexamethasone (10 mg daily) were promptly initiated without waiting for confirmatory results for five days. A bone marrow biopsy was subsequently performed (Fig. 2), which demonstrated hemophagocytosis, and further tests confirmed reduced Natural Killer cell activity (12.72%; normal: 15.11%-23.70%) and elevated Soluble CD25 (sCD25) levels (17,533 pg/mL; normal: 0–6,400 pg/mL). These findings solidified the diagnosis of HLH. Following treatment, the patient’s fever gradually subsided, enabling the initiation of ART (bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide). The patient’s condition improved under this regimen (Fig. 3). Due to the refractory nature of the infection, the patient completed a full 21-day course of amphotericin B as induction therapy prior to transitioning to itraconazole maintenance. Meanwhile, antitubercular therapy has been continued without interruption. Piperacillin-tazobactam served as the primary empiric antimicrobial regimen during the hospital stay, covering potential polymicrobial infections.

Trends in key laboratory parameters after admission. White blood cells, hemoglobin, platelets, and fibrinogen progressively decreased following admission, while procalcitonin, lactate dehydrogenase, and C-reactive protein showed a gradual increase. However, all parameters gradually returned toward normal levels following treatment

On July 16, the patient developed a pharyngeal mass (Fig. 4), along with progressive enlargement of the right cervical lymph nodes. While immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome was suspected, the possibility of malignancy could not be excluded. A PET-CT scan on July 19 revealed strong fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in the cervical and pharyngeal regions (Fig. 5), raising suspicion for lymphoma. A repeat cervical lymph node biopsy on July 22 confirmed high-grade B-cell lymphoma (Fig. 6), with immunohistochemistry results as follows: BCL-6 diffuse (+), Mum-1 (-), TdT (-), C-MYC weakly positive (20%-30%), CD20 weak (+), Ki-67 (> 95%), BCL-2 (-), CD5 (-), CD3 (-), CD10 (+). With the support of hematology and oncology specialists, the patient received Selinexor, Cyclophosphamide, Hydroxydaunorubicin, Oncovin, and Prednisone chemotherapy, followed by autologous bone marrow stem cell transplantation. The treatment outcomes are under continuous observation.

Discussion and conclusion

Timeliness and precision of early intervention

Invasive fungal diseases (IFDs) remain one of the most life-threatening complications in HIV/AIDS patients, particularly in those with severe immunosuppression [10]. Current guidelines emphasize the importance of early empirical antifungal therapy in high-risk populations, even before definitive diagnostic confirmation, as delays in treatment are often associated with increased mortality [11]. In this case, amphotericin B was initiated on the second day of admission based on clinical suspicion, reflecting adherence to best practices for high-risk patients. This aligns with previous studies highlighting that early initiation of antifungal therapy significantly improves survival rates in HIV patients with suspected IFDs [12].

HLH is a rare but severe hyperinflammatory syndrome commonly associated with infections, malignancies, or autoimmune diseases [13]. In the context of HIV/AIDS, HLH is often underdiagnosed due to overlapping clinical features, such as fever, cytopenia, and elevated inflammatory markers, which can mimic other conditions such as sepsis or systemic infections. This case demonstrates the importance of early suspicion and prompt treatment of HLH in HIV patients presenting with unexplained persistent fever, pancytopenia, and elevated ferritin. Previous studies have reported that untreated HLH in HIV/AIDS patients is associated with poor outcomes due to delayed diagnosis and irreversible organ damage. Although current guidelines do not recommend intravenous immunoglobulin as a primary treatment for HLH, multiple case reports suggest its potential therapeutic role. A meta-analysis reviewing 81 global cases of AIDS-associated HLH in recent years found that one-third of surviving patients received intravenous immunoglobulin, whereas glucocorticoids were administered to four-fifths of survivors [7]. In this case, the use of dexamethasone and intravenous immunoglobulin resulted in significant clinical improvement, demonstrating that glucocorticoids and immunoglobulin may be effective options for managing HLH in HIV-infected patients.

Unlike most reported cases of HLH triggered by a single factor, this patient’s HLH was likely multifactorial. While lymphoma is often the primary cause of secondary HLH in HIV-negative patients, the transient symptom improvement following antifungal and anti-tuberculosis treatments in this case suggests that the HLH was more likely secondary to infections.

However, this case also emphasizes the need for advanced diagnostic tools such as mNGS, which provided rapid and comprehensive pathogen identification [14], allowing for more targeted therapy. While mNGS has been shown to improve diagnostic accuracy in immunosuppressed patients, its cost and availability remain significant barriers in routine clinical practice. Compared to traditional diagnostic methods, mNGS was able to identify not only Talaromyces marneffei but also Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Aspergillus fumigatus, which might have been missed using conventional techniques alone.

Diagnostic limitations and interpretation of mNGS results

While this case demonstrates the value of mNGS in rapid pathogen identification, certain diagnostic limitations warrant acknowledgment. The diagnosis of Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Aspergillus fumigatus was based solely on mNGS detection without culture confirmation, raising uncertainty about whether these represented active infection or incidental detection. For Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the high read count, clinical presentation, and treatment response supported the diagnosis of active disease, particularly given the poor sensitivity of traditional diagnostics in severely immunosuppressed patients. For Aspergillus fumigatus, the low read count and negative GM test made definitive diagnosis challenging; however, the initiated amphotericin B provided adequate coverage. Regarding disseminated infection, systematic imaging evaluation revealed splenomegaly and multi-site lymphadenopathy involving cervical, mediastinal, and retroperitoneal regions. Although no discrete focal lesions were identified in the liver or kidneys, the diagnosis of disseminated Talaromyces marneffei infection was established based on positive blood cultures and widespread lymphatic system involvement. This case underscores that mNGS results require careful clinical correlation, considering read counts, traditional microbiological findings, and clinical context to distinguish true pathogens from colonizers or contaminants.

Dynamic assessment and avoidance of fixed thinking

This case illustrates the dynamic and evolving nature of disease in HIV/AIDS patients with severe immunosuppression. The cervical lymph nodes in this patient underwent repeated evaluations, yielding different diagnoses at various stages of disease progression: first confirming Talaromyces marneffei, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Aspergillus fumigatus, and later confirmed to have non-Hodgkin lymphoma. This finding is consistent with reports emphasizing that opportunistic infections and malignancies frequently coexist in HIV patients and may mimic each other clinically and radiologically [15]. The transition from infectious pathology to malignancy in this case underscores the importance of ongoing reassessment and the use of dynamic diagnostic tools, such as serial biopsies and PET-CT imaging. This approach is particularly critical in cases where initial treatment fails or new clinical features emerge. While recurrent lymphadenopathy in this patient warranted consideration of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome it appears to be less frequently reported in Talaromyces marneffei infections. After comprehensive evaluation, immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome was ultimately deemed unlikely in this case.

Compared to previous studies [7], this case highlights the dual challenges posed by opportunistic infections and malignancies in HIV/AIDS patients. While existing literature has documented cases of HLH caused by Talaromyces marneffei in HIV-infected patients [16], the coexistence of HLH with concurrent Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, Aspergillus fumigatus infection, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma is exceedingly rare. This case contributes to the growing body of evidence on the diagnostic complexity in such patients and emphasizes the need for a multidisciplinary and iterative diagnostic approach to avoid misdiagnosis or delayed treatment.

Clinical implications for management

The management of HIV/AIDS patients with coexisting infections and malignancies requires a highly individualized and multidisciplinary approach. Current guidelines recommend integrating antifungal, antibacterial, and antiretroviral therapies in patients with multiple infections, while balancing the risks of drug-drug interactions and overlapping toxicities [11]. This case illustrates the challenges of tailoring therapy in such complex scenarios. For instance, rifampin was avoided in this patient’s tuberculosis treatment due to potential interactions with integrase inhibitors used in ART [17]. Similarly, the use of amphotericin B was carefully managed to minimize its known side effects, including nephrotoxicity, hypokalemia, and bone marrow suppression. In this case, despite the patient’s severe fungal infection and coexisting hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, the adverse effects of amphotericin B were well-controlled, demonstrating that it remains a viable antifungal option for similar high-risk patients when closely monitored and appropriately managed. The addition of chemotherapy and stem cell transplantation for lymphoma further underscores the need for close collaboration between infectious disease, oncology, and hematology teams.

A meta-analysis of HIV-associated HLH cases revealed significant etiological heterogeneity, with 8% of patients having at least one non-HIV infectious trigger for HLH, most commonly EBV (26%), Human herpesvirus-8 (21%), and Histoplasma (17%). Notably, Talaromyces marneffei was not mentioned in this analysis, likely due to its geographically restricted endemicity (e.g., Southeast Asia and Southern China) [7]. A Spanish cohort study of 15 HIV-associated HLH cases identified viral infections, particularly Human herpesvirus-8, as the most frequent triggers. Other pathogens included CMV, mycobacteria, fungi (Blastomyces dermatitidis), and malignancies (e.g., Hodgkin lymphoma). All patients received HLH-directed therapy combining corticosteroids, etoposide, and intravenous immunoglobulin. The cohort exhibited a 30-day mortality rate of 73.3%, mean hospitalization duration of 34 days, and ICU admission rate of 46.6% [18]. Another reported case involved a 53-year-old male with newly diagnosed AIDS who concurrently developed EBV-associated HLH and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. His EBV viral load rose from 38,000 copies/mL at initial admission to 97,900 copies/mL upon HLH diagnosis. Despite combination therapy with corticosteroids, rituximab, tocilizumab, and chemotherapy, the patient initially improved but ultimately succumbed to refractory septic shock caused by multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae [19].

The relatively successful initial treatment laid the foundation for subsequent chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, while also revealing certain limitations and shortcomings in our approach. Despite initial clinical improvement achieved through antifungal therapy, anti-tuberculosis treatment, HLH immunomodulatory therapy, and the initiation of ART, it remains unclear whether HLH was secondary to infections or malignancy. Additionally, while elevated EBV-DNA and CMV-DNA levels were detected, the potential impact of these viral infections on the disease progression was not thoroughly considered or specifically addressed during treatment. The combined effects of severe immunosuppression, multiple opportunistic infections, viral reactivation, and the aggressive nature of non-Hodgkin lymphoma contributed to the poor overall prognosis for the patient. This underscores the need for a more comprehensive evaluation of co-infections, careful consideration of treatment risks, and the development of novel therapeutic approaches for immunocompromised HIV/AIDS patients. Future research should focus on strategies to reduce the risks associated with chemotherapy in immunosuppressed HIV/AIDS patients and explore innovative therapies to improve long-term outcomes.

In conclusion, this case underscores the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with advanced HIV/AIDS patients presenting with multiple coexisting conditions. While the integration of advanced diagnostic and individualized therapeutic strategies can improve short-term outcomes, the long-term prognosis for patients with advanced HIV/AIDS and coexisting malignancies remains poor. This case provides valuable insights into the complexities of managing such patients and emphasizes the necessity of continued efforts to address the unmet needs of this vulnerable population.
