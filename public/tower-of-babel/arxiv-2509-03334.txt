Creation of Lunar-Like Rims in Ilmenite using Synthetic Solar Wind
Roshan S.Trivedi, Advik D. Vira, Brant M. Jones, Katherine D. Burgess, Ziyu Huang, HonglinLiu, PranavRane, Mengkun Tian, MasatoshiHirabayashi, Thomas M. Orlando, Zhigang Jiang, Phillip N. First

Abstract
Space weathering of lunar minerals, due to bombardment from solar wind (SW) particles and micrometeoroid impacts, modifies the mineralogy within tens of nanometers of the surface, i.e., the rim. Via remote sensing, spectroscopic signatures of these modifications have long been used to gauge surface exposure times on the Moon. However, the relative contributions of the solar wind and micrometeroids in the creation of rim features are still debated, particularly for the few-nm clusters known as nanophase iron (np Fe 0 \text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{0}} ), which commonly form in ferrous minerals. We address this issue in the laboratory, using deuterium ions and low-energy electrons as a synthetic solar wind plasma to irradiate ilmenite ( FeTiO 3 \text{FeTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} ), a common lunar mineral. Characterization by high-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy and electron energy-loss spectroscopy shows that the solar wind alone creates rims with all the main characteristics of lunar samples. We conclusively identify np Fe 0 \text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{0}} and quantify its distribution as a function of depth and fluence, allowing us to estimate the SW exposure of Apollo soil 71501. Our results confirm that small np Fe 0 \text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{0}} ( < 10 nm <10\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m} in diameter) form due to the solar wind. Similar experiments could provide microscopic details of space weathering, improving the link between surface modification processes and macroscopic remote sensing data.



Introduction

Earth’s atmosphere and magnetosphere provide effective shielding against electromagnetic radiation, atomic particles, extraterrestrial dust and even man-made space debris. In the absence of such shielding, airless bodies, such as the Moon, are directly exposed to the harsh space environment that gradually alters their surface, a process known as space weathering. This process is broadly divided into two categories: (i) impacts of small dust particles from space (i.e., micrometeoroids) and (ii) cosmic and solar (i.e., solar energetic particles, solar wind (SW)) irradiation

The underlying mechanism driving the formation of small np
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{0}}
(
<10\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
) from SW irradiation is inherently linked to the generation of point defects. We investigate the role of SW ions in ilmenite to ascertain the potential defect-generating mechanisms leading to the formation of np

Mechanisms of Defect Production:

As ions traverse a material, the ions impart their energy into the host material,
eventually stopping within the material (or transmitting through). We consider three potential defect-forming mechanisms: (1) energy loss due to electronic processes (inelastic collisions), potentially leading to vacancies formed due to electron excitation induced Coulombic repulsion
Knotek_1979_auger
, (2) energy loss due to nuclear processes (elastic collisions) with nuclei, creating vacancies by knock-on events, and (3) the implantation depth for the incident
\text{D}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
ions, potentially resulting in a redox reaction between the implanted ion and

Implications for np
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{0}}
in Lunar Regolith

Our demonstration that SSW alone can form np
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{0}}
and replicate the characteristics commonly observed in returned lunar soils will be crucial to understanding space weathering on the Moon. Having the capability to create these characteristics in the lab, together with the ability to change exposure parameters, allows us to probe the formation mechanisms of the rim. Our results also show that small np
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{0}}
is formed as a result of SW irradiation, which will aid in the interpretation of remote sensing measurements of the lunar surface. We also present statistics on the np
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{0}}
distribution in SSW rims, creating an independent measure of the surface exposure age for lunar soils, which allows for intra-grain differences in exposure to be deduced. By bridging similar experiments (i.e., performing systematic laboratory irradiations of other lunar analogs) with macroscopic optical measurements, the solar wind exposure and the relative contributions of space weathering modification processes could potentially be mapped across the lunar surface. Identical methods would be applicable to airless bodies throughout the Solar System.

DFT Calculation

First-principles calculations for
\text{FeTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

(1)

where
A_{0}
is the exposed area. For the case of light ions, the formation of an amorphous area requires the overlap of multiple clusters. This effect can be accounted for by summing the exponential terms
Gibbons_1972
:

A_{A}=A_{0}\left[1-\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(A_{i}\phi)^{k}}{k!}e^{-A_{i}\phi}\right)\right],

(2)

where
n
is the number of overlapping clusters. First-order approximations of equation
2
can be described by power-law distributions, resulting in
A_{A}/A_{0}=\left(A_{i}\phi\right)^{n+1}
. For the direct impact amorphization model (
n=0
), this reduces to
A_{A}/A_{0}=\phi/\phi_{C}
, where
\phi_{C}=1/A_{i}
is the critical fluence. In our case, the np
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{0}}
distribution
\chi(z)
(Fig.
5
b) is one-dimensional, leading to:
\chi^{max}=\sqrt{A_{A}/A_{0}}=\left(\phi/\phi_{C}\right)^{1/2}
.
