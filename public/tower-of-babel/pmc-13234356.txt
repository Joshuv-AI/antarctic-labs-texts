Human motion recognition based on progressive neural architecture search for efficient and interpretable spatiotemporal learning
Authors: Feng Wang, Zhengchang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human motion recognition (HMR) is an important application area in smart surveillance, medical surveillance, and human-computer interfaces. However, human activities are extremely diverse, and the spatiotemporal modeling is a computationally resource-demanding concept; thus, proper and effective recognition is difficult. Traditional deep learning networks are usually not generated automatically; they are computationally expensive or cannot be used to model a wide variety of motion patterns. The current paper introduces a Progressive Neural Architecture Search (PNAS) neural architecture, PNAS-HMR, that is an automatic human movement recognition system that finds the most effective deep network models without compromising recognition accuracy, computation cost, or interpretability. The given framework comprises a progressive search strategy, where the lightweight network candidates are taken into account, and an upward search strategy is used to construct more complex structures with the help of multi-objective search. The search is directed by a surrogate predictor of performance based on a search using Pareto-optimal architectures that meet accuracy, latency, and performance energy requirements. Assessment was performed using motion data from standard datasets, including NTU RGB + D 120, Kinetics-400, and UCF101. The architectures found were further refined and studied using visualization tools and ablation experiments. Experimental tests show that PNAS-HMR achieves higher recognition accuracy (up to 4%) with lower FLOPs and latency than state-of-the-art methods, by more than 30%. The architectures searched exhibit strong generalization and cross-dataset transferability. PNAS-HMR is an effective combination of neural architecture search and spatiotemporal learning that provides an energy-efficient, interpretable, and scalable framework for real-time human motion recognition in various practical problems.

Introduction

Human motion recognition (HMR) is the automatic recognition and categorization of human body movements, ranging from the simplest, such as walking or waving, to more complex sequences of interactions among two or more agents or objects. Various applications are based on this capability: human-computer interaction, sports analytics, video surveillance, assistive robotics, healthcare monitoring, and rehabilitation1,2. As video and sensor data (RGB, depth, skeleton, inertial, and wearable modalities) are emerging, this demands robust, generalizable motion recognition systems as never before. As a case example, precise motion detection in smart surroundings and ambient assisted living can enable early fall (or abnormal behavior) detection3. In sports analytics, automatic identification of player movement can provide performance feedback, analyze strategy, and prevent injuries4. In police patrol and surveillance, identifying suspicious human behavior will assist in the effective implementation of proactive surveillance systems5. Recent surveys indicate that, although the HMR performance has been significantly enhanced during the last decade, there are still numerous challenges; hence, the current research on the progressive neural network architecture search adapted to the HMR tasks6,7. Despite significant achievements, several long-standing issues still hinder the creation of compelling, multi-purpose HMR systems. These obstacles are due to the inherent complexity of human motion, diverse sensing and acquisition conditions, and computing limitations. The following are the key issues. The same thing (e.g., running) could look significantly different in different subjects, perspectives, velocities, body types, garments, and situations. Meanwhile, dissimilar actions can appear visually similar (e.g., jogging vs. walking briskly), thereby enhancing inter-class confusion. This diversity makes it hard to learn the representation and classify8. Human motion is experienced in time, and its perception is significant only when it is modeled in space (body position, body shape) and in time (motional patterns). The expression of such spatio-temporal traits in a discriminative but compact form is not trivial, particularly under noise conditions, the presence of occlusion, or change of viewpoint9. Motion recorded in alternative camera angles or sensors exhibits significant appearance differences. The difference of scale also causes recognition problems, as does background clutter, camera movement, and changing illumination. These variations are likely to affect the generalization of models to unfamiliar environments10. Recent reports as well as surveys have indicated that even after a great advancement in human motion recognition (HMR) has been achieved over the last ten years, there are still a few underlying problems that have not been addressed. To begin with, there is a high degree of intra-class variability and slight inter-class consistency levels, which makes it hard to identify in a robust manner, especially in uncontrolled real-world conditions. Second, the difference in camera angles, display of the subject, speed of execution, and the presence of the background context usually leads to vast domain shifts, and such influence considerably reduces cross-dataset generalization performance. Third, it is still difficult to accurately model long-range temporal dependencies because discriminative motion cues can be sparse, and long video sequences8. Moreover, the problem of cross-modal alignment and the choice of fusion strategy, as well as a significant increase in the computational load, are also associated with the use of multimodal HMR systems that combine RGB, skeleton, and optical flow data9,10. Lastly, numerous modern state-of-the-art designs are based on large-scale designs with large computational and memory requirements, making them unsuitable for real-time and resource-constrained systems11,12. These issues underscore the importance of methods to be able to strike an effective balance between recognition accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency13. Although HMR has been optimized with the advancement in deep learning, network architecture development is cumbersome and sub-optimal. Automated Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has been shown to be promising in other areas. However, its implementation into spatio-temporal motion information is less investigated and has certain issues with search space, time modelling, efficiency, and interpretability14. Compared with prior progressive NAS and video NAS techniques, which primarily aim for accuracy in a fixed search configuration, PNAS HMR is specifically designed for human motion recognition by jointly incorporating three factors. It searches progressively in stages with explicit efficiency goals to be able to satisfy practical latency and computation constraints, employs surrogate guided evaluation to incur lower costs and minimally affect the ranking of candidates, and adopts an in-network multi-modal combination of RGB, optical flow, and skeleton representations so that complementary cues are used during architecture discovery and not added on top of the search. The present study aims to accomplish three practical tasks and to measure them using clear metrics and guidelines. To make the results interpretable, we conduct ablations and attention-based visualizations for each modality and fusion component, and report changes in Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy. To assess efficiency, we will measure parameter count and FLOPs, and we will also report end-to-end inference latency in milliseconds per frame under a fixed hardware configuration and input resolution. To provide flexibility, we measure cross-domain generalization via cross-dataset transfer by training a model on NTU RGB + D 120 and evaluating it on Kinetics 400 under a fully specified fine-tuning protocol, with standard validation. Collectively, the objectives of interpretability, efficiency, and adaptability are verifiable directly out of the reported experiments.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 reviews related work on human motion recognition and neural architecture search. Section 3 details the proposed PNAS-HMR framework and its methodology, the experimental setup and evaluation metrics, while Sect. 4 presents the results and analyses. Section 5 discusses the findings, limitations, and practical implications, and Sect. 6 concludes the paper with key insights and future research directions.

Related work

Human motion recognition: from handcrafted features to deep models

The initial human motion recognition (HMR) algorithms were based on handcrafted spatiotemporal features and features based on trajectories. The HOG3D and motion-boundary descriptors were also sensitive to viewpoint variations and background clutter and were used to encode local appearance-motion patterns. The dense trajectories and enhanced dense trajectories added camera-motion compensations and robust motion paths, which emerged as good pre-deep-learning baselines of action understanding15. With the introduction of deep learning, HMR changed its focus to no longer using engineered features, but learned representations. Two-Stream ConvNets directly split spatial appearance (RGB) and motion (optical flow), and allowed motion priors to be end-to-end trained16. Later, 3D convolutional networks built upon 2D counterparts, e.g., I3D, coupled spatial and temporal modeling and reached significant improvements with the large-scale pretraining on datasets, e.g., Kinetics17. Hybrid architectures also enhanced the efficiency of temporal modeling: SlowFast used two pathways by simultaneously decoupling semantics and motion by using dual paths at varying frame rates18, and X3D gradually expanded a light 2D backbone in time and space to obtain strong accuracy-efficiency trade-offs19.

Skeleton-based and transformer-based action recognition

By leveraging RGB videos, skeleton-based approaches use body structure as a powerful prior. ST-GCN proposed to directly learn the spatial-temporal relationship on the pose graphs by using joints and bones as its inputs20. Later, PoseConv3D represented skeleton sequences in the form of 3D heatmap volumes, which are more resistant to pose noise and can be merged by a multimodal fusion21. Video transformers have also become a viable alternative to convolutional architectures in parallel settings. TimeSformer separated spatial and temporal self-attention, showing competitive results and scaling to longer times22. Attention-based models can be, however, very computationally expensive, so factorized or sparse designs are encouraged to keep the computational costs down. Extensive studies also revealed that temporal operators, sampling strategies in clips, and pooling decisions have the potential to affect accuracy efficiency trade-offs to a similar extent as does backbone depth or width23,24. Rules like NTU RGB + D 120 remain a source of development because of the focus on generalization of subject, perspective, and type of interactions25.

Neural architecture search for vision

Neural architecture search (NAS) is an automated model design methodology that is defined in terms of search spaces, search algorithms, and performance prediction systems. ENAS minimized the cost of search through parameter sharing26, and DARTS enabled gradient-based optimization by reparametrizing discrete architectural decisions in continuous space27. Progressive NAS (PNAS) further scalable decreased the cost of computation by expanding the architectures and leveraging surrogate performance predictors during the search28. To overcome deployment limitations, hardware-aware NAS algorithms have used latency or energy as optimization objectives. MnasNet minimized errors with latency-specific device-specific constraints29, FBNet used differentiable NAS with latency look-up Table30, and ProxylessNAS directly optimized on device-specific hardware with no proxies31. EfficientNet took compound scaling rules together with NAS-discovered baselines to formidable accuracy-efficiency frontiers15. Various competitions summed up these changes and emphasized benchmarking traps and reproducibility problems in NAS research29,32,33. Recent research has further advanced human motion and action recognition by enhancing temporal and attention models and improving robustness to complex scenarios. Indicatively, in the example of ResLNet, the network architecture relies on the use of deep residual LUSST to improve the long-range dependency modeling of action recognition problems by using longer and extended temporal inputs34. Spatiotemporal modeling founded on attention has also proven to gain ground in the form of TASTA that combines textual clues with spatial-temporal attention to enhance video comprehension35. Probabilistic filtering and multisensory fusion have been used to achieve robustness against noisy as well as real-world conditions for human interaction recognition and multisensory locomotion analysis as demonstrated in the Extended Kalman Filter36,37. Later on, skeleton-based modeling developments have seen the use of graph convolutional networks alongside transformer models to improve the structural and temporal features of human behavior38. Such studies indicate current research trends and underscore the need for effective, scalable architectures capable of modeling complex spatiotemporal dynamics.

NAS for video and spatiotemporal modeling

The use of NAS for video understanding comes with further difficulties as spatiotemporal kernels, clip length, frame rate, and multi-stream fusion increase the search space by a significant factor. Practical video NAS (PV-NAS) was developed to deal with those issues and create spatiotemporal search spaces and gradient-based strategies, showing competitive results on Kinetics-400 and Something-Something-V2 at a lower computational cost39. Co-optimization of architectural hyperparameters like frame sampling rate, channel width, and per-stage depth to trade off accuracy and complexity was done in other methods40. Similar advances in manually-designed video architectures, e.g., SlowFast18, X3D19, and transformer-based architectures, e.g., TimeSformer22, give good backbones and guide the design of effective video NAS search spaces. Temporal receptive field and module selection are further explained by comparative analysis across CNNs, 3D CNNs, and attention-based modules in terms of their performance and efficiency24.

Limitations of existing work and motivation

In spite of the significant improvement, the literature on HMR and video NAS contains multiple gaps. To begin with, most search spaces are biased on the basis of a 2D image model, and video-native operators, attention patterns, and fusion primitives are not yet well explored. Second, proxy metrics, like FLOPs or look-up-table latency, are frequently optimized, whereas platform-specific aspects, such as throughput, memory footprint, sensitivity to batch size, and energy consumption, are seldom optimized together29–31. Third, multimodal integration (e.g., RGB, skeleton, depth, IMU) is often manually designed or confined to pre-defined fusion steps, and automatic fusion topology, stream rates, and cross-modal attention designs are rarely discovered39,40. Fourth, cross-dataset and cross-domain generalization have not been well tested in NAS-based methods25,32. Lastly, most NAS outcomes, e.g., preferred temporal receptive fields, tokenization strategies, or even graph motifs, are not often examined past simple ablations24,32. Such constraints drive our step-wise NAS architecture to HMR, which gradually increases the depth of time, the variety of operators, and the fusion graph, and optimizes the accuracy and latency directly and collaboratively, and allows us to study the architectures found systematically.

These are the gaps that drive our progressive NAS that is adapted to HMR: a progressive search that increases the temporal depth, fusion topology, operator diversity, and simultaneously trades off accuracy-latency and maintains interpretability of the discovered designs. Proposed methodology

Progressive NAS framework for HMR

The proposed PNAS-HMR architecture is designed to address the key problems identified in human motion recognition. To reduce reliance on large-scale annotated data, the progressive search algorithm analyzes lightweight candidates using partial training and a surrogate predictor, thereby reducing data and computational requirements. To enhance cross-domain generalization, architectures are selected using a multi-objective approach and evaluated on cross-dataset transfer tasks to identify transferable and robust spatiotemporal representations. The problem of successful multimodal integration has been solved by employing an attention-guided fusion process, which dynamically weights the RGB, optical flow, and skeleton signals. The search process imposes computational efficiency and real-time applicability requirements through a joint optimization of the accuracy, latency, and energy. Finally, model interpretability is promoted through modular design and visualization analyses, enabling transparent inspection of spatial–temporal attention and fusion behavior. The framework integrates four key stages: motion data representation, progressive search, surrogate-based performance prediction, and final model construction. At first, simple candidate cells having few operations are investigated. The search space is then refined in subsequent stages by incorporating additional temporal and spatial operators that enable it to find deeper, more useful structures. Performance predictor: an estimate of a candidate’s accuracy based on incomplete training, significantly reducing search time. Lastly, the best architecture is retrained on the entire dataset. The iterative search stages of developing candidate architectures are represented by arrows, and performance prediction and efficiency optimization are defined by feedback loops.

Motion data representation and preprocessing

PNAS-HMR employs an attention-guided cross-modal fusion mechanism to integrate RGB, optical flow, and skeleton features in a principled and interpretable manner. The fusion module is implemented at an intermediate stage of the network, where modality-specific features have sufficient semantic abstraction while still preserving motion and structural details. Specifically, features from each modality are first temporally aligned and projected into a shared embedding space using linear transformations. A cross-modal attention mechanism is then applied, in which queries are generated from one modality (e.g., RGB) and keys and values are derived from the remaining modalities (optical flow and skeleton). This design enables the model to adaptively focus on complementary motions and structural cues across modalities. Attention weights are calculated by scaled dot-product attention and normalized by a softmax operation, then giving modality-specific weights of importance that can dynamically change the contribution made by features during the fusion process. The fused representation is then generated by a weighted combination of modality features, which are then forwarded to subsequent spatiotemporal modeling layers. At this stage of attention-directed fusion, optimal modality-weighting rules are automatically learned by the network to enhance the robustness and flexibility to a wide range of motion patterns and datasets. Movement in human beings consists of space layouts and time progression. In order to do this, the framework employs one multimodal preprocessing pipeline. Regular sampling, upsampling, and downsampling of RGB frames generate fixed-length temporal clips, which increases the strength. The optical flow maps are calculated between the successive frames to emphasize short-term dynamics, and skeleton sequences are estimated with the help of pose estimators in order to encode the structural joint relations. Each of the modalities is time-synchronized and normalized, and then fused. The spatiotemporal tensors, which combine visual appearance, motion, and skeletal dynamics, provide a coherent representation that can be searched using a neural architecture. The consistency of temporal context, as well as the equal mixture of modalities, also relies on this preprocessing, which allows the progressive search mechanism to determine architectures that capture global motion patterns and fine-grained temporal interactions in the best possible way.

Progressive search strategy

The proposed PNAS-HMR algorithm is a stage-by-stage algorithm that explores the architecture space efficiently and regulates its computational cost. The lightweight candidate networks are sampled at the early stage on a constrained search space with shallow depth, diversity in operators, and the complexity of fusions. Partially training these candidates on a small fraction of the training data to get initial performance estimates is termed preliminary training. To prevent exhaustive analysis, a surrogate predictor of performance is trained to provide performance estimates of candidate architectures using structural features of the architectures and outcomes of partial training. Applicants are subsequently filtered by their estimated accuracy, together with measures of efficiency, and only the most successful architectures are kept. The retained candidates at later levels are then increasingly narrowed through network deepening, addition of new operators, and fusion connectivity. This refinement strategy allows a progressive refinement approach to be employed so that the search process narrows in on promising parts of the architecture space but then adds model capacity in a gradual and incremental fashion until a high-performing final architecture is obtained within the practical constraints of computational resources.

The PNAS-HMR framework Progressive Search Strategy targets the efficient exploration of an exponentially ample space of architectures while remaining computationally tractable. The strategy is done in stages, each one increasing the search space by a factor, rather than attempting to search every possible way of operation and connection. Search space definition

In PNAS-HMR, the computational architectures at the progressive stage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:s$$\end{document} are represented as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) consisting of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{s}$$\end{document} nodes. Nodes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{s}$$\end{document}, which are the number of nodes in the graph, are expanded over stages (e.g., shallow to deeper graphs) to ensure a manageable search complexity and allow refinement to be progressive. The gradual growth of the search also enables it to start with lightweight architectures and to add additional high-capacity architectures to promising candidates, as it advances. All nodes are not connected, but connectivity between nodes is restricted by a policy of sparse connectivity, where each node is only permitted to accept inputs from a limited number of previous nodes, as opposed to complete connectivity. The design has the effect of reducing redundancy, stabilizing the training, and reducing the cost of computation, without compromising enough representational flexibility. Every edge is linked to a candidate operator, chosen from a set of predetermined operators that include temporal convolutions, residual connections, and channel-attention modules such as Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) blocks. Their usefulness in improving channel-wise feature discrimination under low computational cost is enhanced with SE blocks. The last operator set is also selected to achieve a balance between expressiveness and efficiency, allowing the search to cover a variety of spatiotemporal transformations without over-inflating the search space.

PNAS-HMR search space consists of possible architectural building blocks and their connection topology, which is searched using progressive search. The candidate networks are represented as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), which are node feature maps and edge feature candidate operations. Formally, the search space at stage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:s$$\end{document} is defined as:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{S}}_{s}=({\mathcal{V}}_{s},{\mathcal{E}}_{s})$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{V}}_{s}=\{{v}_{1},{v}_{2},\dots\:,{v}_{{N}_{s}}\}$$\end{document} denotes the nodes (intermediate features), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{E}}_{s}=\left\{\right({v}_{i},{v}_{j},{O}_{ij})\mid\:i<j\}$$\end{document} represents the set of directed edges, each associated with a candidate operation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{O}_{ij}$$\end{document}. Each edge is a weighted mixture of possible operations:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\stackrel{\prime }{O}}_{ij}\left(x\right)=\sum\:_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\mathrm{exp}\left({\alpha\:}_{ij}^{k}\right)}{\sum\:_{m=1}^{K}\mathrm{exp}\left({\alpha\:}_{ij}^{m}\right)}{\hspace{0.17em}}{O}_{ij}^{k}\left(x\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:K$$\end{document} is the number of candidate operations, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{ij}^{k}$$\end{document} is the learnable architecture parameter, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{O}_{ij}^{k}$$\end{document} is one of the operation types such as 3 × 3 3D convolution, depthwise convolution, temporal pooling, or attention block. The search begins with a minimal set of operations:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{O}}_{0} = \left\{ {3 \times 3~Conv3D,~1 \times 1~Conv3D,MaxPool3D} \right\}$$\end{document}

and progressively expands by including more complex operators:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{O}}_{s+1}={\mathcal{O}}_{s}\cup\:\left\{\mathrm{Temporal\:Attention},\mathrm{Depthwise\:Conv},\mathrm{SE\:Block},\mathrm{Fusion\:Gate}\right\}$$\end{document}

Each candidate architecture is constructed by sampling a subset of edges and operators from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{S}}_{s}$$\end{document}, evaluated using the performance predictor, and refined via gradient-based optimization on architecture parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{ij}^{k}$$\end{document}. The optimal configuration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}^{*}$$\end{document}defines the final architecture topology:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}^{*}=arg\:\underset{A\in\:{\mathcal{S}}_{S}}{\mathrm{max}}\mathcal{F}\left(A\right)$$\end{document}

This formalized, progressively growing search space enables PNAS-HMR to effectively balance exploration and exploitation, thereby enabling accurate and robust recognition of human motion across a wide range of modalities and datasets.

Performance prediction and surrogate evaluation

The multi-objective optimization of PNAS-HMR is a combination of recognition accuracy, inference latency, and energy consumption, weighted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{1}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{2}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{3}$$\end{document} accordingly. These weights are not computed based on pre-established empirical values but instead are computed with the help of a hyperparameter search based on validation for every benchmark dataset. In particular, a discrete set of candidate weight combinations is fitted on a validation set held on to find a set of configurations that strike a good trade-off between accuracy and efficiency subject to data-dependent constraints. In PNAS-HMR, the search procedure needs to be efficient at estimating the performance of a large number of candidate architectures, without necessarily training each model. To solve this, a surrogate performance predictor approximates the validity and computational cost of each candidate using its structural encoding and sparse training measures. Each candidate’s architecture \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{s}$$\end{document} at the search stage \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:s$$\end{document} is represented by a feature vector:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{z}_{s}=\left[{e}_{\mathrm{op}},{e}_{\mathrm{conn}},{e}_{\mathrm{depth}},{e}_{\mathrm{width}},{e}_{\mathrm{mod}}\right]$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{e}_{\mathrm{op}}$$\end{document} encodes the operation types, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{e}_{\mathrm{conn}}$$\end{document} encodes connectivity patterns, and the remaining components represent depth, width, and modality configurations. A lightweight regression model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{P}}_{\varphi\:}$$\end{document} (typically a 3-layer MLP) is trained to predict the estimated accuracy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\left({A}_{s}\right)$$\end{document} from these encoded features:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\left({A}_{s}\right)={\mathcal{P}}_{\varphi\:}\left({\mathbf{z}}_{s}\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varphi\:$$\end{document} are the predictor’s learnable parameters. The predictor is trained on a small subset of fully evaluated architectures using the mean squared error loss:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{pred}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}{\left(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\left({A}_{s}^{\left(i\right)}\right)-\mathcal{A}\left({A}_{s}^{\left(i\right)}\right)\right)}^{2}$$\end{document}

Upon training, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{P}}_{\varphi\:}$$\end{document} is a fast evaluator and can be used to rank/filter thousands of candidate architectures in later phases without having to retrain it. Also, a confidence-based filtering system is added. In relation to every performance that is predicted, a score of confidence:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:c\left({A}_{s}\right)=1-{\sigma\:}^{2}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\left({A}_{s}\right)\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\sigma\:}^{2}(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} is the predictive variance due to dropout sampling is used to weight predictions which provide stability and robustness of surrogate evaluations. This surrogate evaluation method significantly saves computational cost (it is more than 90% cheaper than brute force NAS) and has high correlation with actual validation performance. Surrogate ranking identifies the most promising architectures, which are then subjected to multi-objective optimization to analyze trade-offs.

Multi-objective optimization for accuracy–efficiency trade-off

In PNAS-HMR, architecture search is formulated as a multi-objective optimization problem that jointly considers recognition accuracy, inference latency, and energy consumption. A surrogate performance predictor is trained to estimate the accuracy of candidate architectures based on their structural features and partial training results, while latency and energy are obtained using hardware-aware measurements or estimators. At each progressive search stage, candidate architectures are evaluated in this multi-objective space, and Pareto-optimal solutions are identified and retained. The surrogate-guided Pareto filtering enables efficient pruning of dominated candidates and steers the search toward architectures that achieve a favorable trade-off among accuracy, latency, and energy. In real-world systems for human motion recognition, high accuracy must be balanced with constraints on latency, energy, and parameter count. The PNAS-HMR multi-objective optimization step is based on a Pareto-optimization architecture search using the metrics of accuracy and efficiency. The optimization objective function is defined as:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\underset{{A}_{s}\in\:{\mathcal{S}}_{s}}{\mathrm{max}\mathcal{F}\left({A}_{s}\right)}=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\left({A}_{s}\right)-{\lambda\:}_{1}\frac{\mathrm{FLOPs}\left({A}_{s}\right)}{{\mathrm{FLOPs}}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}}}-{\lambda\:}_{2}\frac{\mathrm{Params}\left({A}_{s}\right)}{{\mathrm{Params}}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}}}-{\lambda\:}_{3}\frac{\mathrm{Latency}\left({A}_{s}\right)}{{\mathrm{Latency}}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{1},{\lambda\:}_{2},{\lambda\:}_{3}$$\end{document} are hyperparameters that control the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency. To guide the search, a Pareto frontier \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{P}}^{*}$$\end{document}is constructed as:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{P}}^{*} = \left\{ {A_{i} \left| {{\nexists }A_{j} :{\mathcal{F}}\left( {A_{j} } \right) \ge {\mathcal{F}}\left( {A_{i} } \right),} \right.|{\mathrm{with}}~{\mathrm{at}}~{\mathrm{least}}~{\mathrm{one}}~{\mathrm{strict}}~{\mathrm{inequality}}} \right\}$$\end{document}

Architectures on this frontier represent the optimal balance between accuracy and computational resources. During optimization, gradient-based updates are applied to the architecture parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document} following:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:\leftarrow\:\alpha\:+\eta\:{\nabla\:}_{\alpha\:}\mathcal{F}\left({A}_{s}\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\eta\:$$\end{document} is the learning rate. For non-differentiable objectives (e.g., latency), reinforcement learning-style reward updates are used:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:r\left({A}_{s}\right)=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\left({A}_{s}\right)-\beta\:\cdot\:\mathrm{Cost}\left({A}_{s}\right)$$\end{document}

and the architecture controller maximizes the expected reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathbb{E}\left[r\right({A}_{s}\left)\right]$$\end{document}. The optimization iterates until convergence on a stable Pareto frontier. The best architecture, denoted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}^{*}$$\end{document}, satisfies:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}^{*}=\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}\:\underset{A\in\:{\mathcal{P}}^{*}}{\mathrm{max}}\left(\widehat{\mathcal{A}}\left(A\right)-\lambda\:{\hspace{0.17em}Cost}\left(A\right)\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Cost}\left(A\right)$$\end{document} combines FLOPs, memory, and latency metrics.

The multi-objective optimization of this architecture guarantees not only high recognition accuracy but also computational efficiency and the ability to implement the final architecture on a real-time or embedded system.

Final architecture construction and fine-tuning

Once the progressive search process has ended, the final architecture \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}^{*}$$\end{document} is found as the result of the discretization of the architecture parameters as a continuous set of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document}. Precisely, each edge in the directed acyclic graph will get the operator associated with the largest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document} value, which will be an instantiated version of the architecture that is deterministic and reproducible. This maximum selection algorithm eliminates the ambiguity of heuristic thresholds and provides the same operators throughout search executions.

To explain this decision, we perform an ablation study comparing strategies such as max-alpha and threshold-based filtering, in which operators with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document} values greater than a predefined threshold are preserved. Empirical findings indicate that max-alpha discretization achieves comparable or superior accuracy with reduced FLOPs and latency, making it efficient and robust enough to inform the final architecture. Upon progressive search and optimization, the ultimate architecture \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}^{*}$$\end{document} is retrained to acquire the full-performance weights. The model parameters are re-initialized, and all operations are discretized, and their continuous relaxations are used. The selected operations are determined by:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{O}_{ij}^{*}=\mathrm{arg}\underset{k}{\mathrm{max}{\alpha\:}_{ij}^{k}}$$\end{document}

Finding ways of ensuring that only important connections make it to the final network topology.

The architecture usually has several spatiotemporal streams of feature extraction, lightweight attention modules, and modality-fusion layers. The end-to-end training of the model is done based on a cross-entropy loss function:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{train}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}\sum\:_{c=1}^{C}{y}_{i,c}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}\left({\widehat{y}}_{i,c}\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i,c}$$\end{document} is the ground-truth label and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{y}}_{i,c}$$\end{document} the predicted class probability for sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i$$\end{document}.

Temporal mixup and random frame dropout are used during training, and optimization is performed with AdamW and a cosine annealing learning rate. The final fine-tuned model is tested on standard benchmark datasets such as NTU RGB + D 120 and Kinetics-400 to ensure it is cross-domain robust.

Experimental setup

This section summarizes the datasets, evaluation protocols, implementation configurations, and performance measures used to evaluate the proposed Progressive Neural Architecture Search (PNAS-HMR) architecture for human motion recognition. The experiments were well-designed to ensure reproducibility and comparison with the previous state of the art. To ensure fair and reproducible energy efficiency evaluation, all models are tested under a unified hardware and software configuration. Energy consumption is measured at the identical NVIDIA A100 with a fixed power mode on. Every inference experiment is also done with a batch size of 1, FP16 precision, and with the same input resolutions and preprocessing pipelines to remove any variability with batch size and precision. After one of the models is measured, it is given a warm-up period to stabilize its use of the GPUs. Power consumption is measured with a consistent monitoring tool across the various inference runs and averaged to get consistent estimates. Under these controlled conditions, energy efficiency has been reported as GFLOps/W.

Datasets and evaluation protocols

Three popular benchmark datasets (NTU RGB + D 12042, Kinetics-40043 and UCF10144 are used to evaluate the PNAS-HMR framework. These datasets vary in the intricacy and diversity of their environments, providing a foundation for assessing overall accuracy and generalization. The NTU RGB + D 120 dataset comprises approximately 114,480 video samples across 120 action classes and 106 subjects. Three Kinect v2 sensors are used at various angles to capture all actions simultaneously in real time, delivering RGB, depth, and skeleton data in 3D. Evaluation is conducted in accordance with two official protocols, i.e., Cross-Subject (CS) and Cross-Setup (CSet), which ensure the model is tested on unseen participants and across different camera setups. In each dataset, training, validation, and testing subsets do not overlap. In NTU RGB + D 120, the official Cross-Subject (CS) and Cross-Setup (CSet) protocols are used, which are independent of users, and, therefore, subjects or capture setups in the test set cannot be viewed during training. In particular, the training and test partitions are made as the standard benchmarks, and 10% of the training data is also reserved to perform the validation. In the case of Kinetics-400, training and validation sets consist of videos separated by the official split, and the evaluation is independently conducted by design. In the case of UCF101, we use the official Split 1 protocol, where both training and test videos are piece-meal, and there is no overlap of subjects in a subset. Implementation details and training settings

All experiments are conducted in PyTorch 2.1 on an A100 with 80GB of memory. The four-stage search for a progressive neural architecture is conducted to control computational cost, and each stage is trained on 20% of the training set for 30 epochs. We find the optimal between AdamW with an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1\times\:{10}^{-3}$$\end{document}, weight decay \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:5\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}, and a cosine annealing learning rate schedule. Once the search is complete, architecture A will be trained in FP16 with a batch size of 64 on the entire dataset for 120 epochs using the selected architecture A1. The training is done with a dropout rate of 0.5 and batch normalization. For data augmentation, the RGB stream will use random cropping, horizontal flipping, color jittering, and temporal jittering, and the skeleton stream uses random rotation and scaling. In the process of inference, we use center-clipping and multi-clip averaging. The overall cost of computation of the NAS process is about 48 GPU hours. In order to examine the generalization properties of PNAS-HMR, we use a cross-dataset transfer learning protocol. To conduct the cross-domain analysis, we start with the pre-trained weights of the model to perform the NTU RGB + D 120 data. and then fine-tune the architecture using Kinetics-400. It is fine-tuned on 30 epochs with an AdamW optimizer and an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}. The learning rate is also reduced by a coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:=0.1$$\end{document} in a cosine annealing schedule, where the reduction occurs after every 10 epochs. The effective batch size at the fine-tuning stage is 32, where there is a small amount of memory on the GPU, which can be kept constant by taking 2 gradient accumulation steps. Otherwise, we use the same weight decay and regularization parameters as used in the initial training stage. We use the first 5 epochs to fine-tune the classifier in order to keep learned spatiotemporal features intact and change all layers during the rest of the training. Our final report on the performance of the Kinetics-400 validation set is based on the same multi-crop inference protocol as our main experiments. In order to confirm that it is not unique to AdamW that PNAS HMR performs well, we also trained the proposed model with AdaBoB, which is a dynamically constrained adaptive optimizer that uses a belief in observed gradients. In order to compare optimizers fairly, every optimizer experiment follows the same data splitting, epochs, batch size, mixed precision mode, augmentation pipeline, and learning rate schedule as the default setup45. We present Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy, and efficiency metrics, and find that the proposed strategy does not show significant performance variations and model ranking across optimizers, and proves that PNAS HMR remains stable even in the absence of AdamW.

Evaluation metrics

Standard quantitative metrics are used to evaluate the proposed PNAS-HMR model’s performance in terms of recognition accuracy and computational efficiency. Top-1 Accuracy is the percentage of test samples on which the prediction with the most considerable confidence is equal to the ground truth label and is calculated as:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Ac{c}_{Top1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}1\left({\widehat{y}}_{i}={y}_{i}\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N$$\end{document} is the number of test samples, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}$$\end{document} is the true label, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{y}}_{i}$$\end{document} is the model’s predicted label. Top-5 Accuracy is used to determine the presence of the correct label of the class in the top 5 most likely label predictions made by each test instance, which provides information on the class ambiguity. In addition to accuracy, various measures of efficiency are considered. FLOPs represent the computational cost per video inference, whereas Parameter Count (MParams) indicates the model’s compactness and memory usage. Inference Latency (per frame, in milliseconds) is used to measure the performance during actual deployment. Another parameter, Energy Efficiency (GFLOps/W), evaluates the use of hardware in real-time systems. The performance of the proposed model is tested against the baseline and state-of-the-art architectures under the same conditions. A model is said to be optimal if it lies on the Pareto frontier, that is, it is both the most accurate and minimizes computational cost and latency. All these evaluation metrics are essential to ensure that predictive quality and deployment feasibility are appropriately tested and to determine whether the proposed PNAS-HMR framework is credible and scalable.

An extensive ablation study was conducted to evaluate the contributions of the major blocks in the proposed Progressive Neural Architecture Search for Human Motion Recognition (PNAS-HMR) framework. Three key areas are considered in the experiments, namely (1) the effect of stages of progressive searching, (2) the effect of the surrogate performance predictor, and (3) the effect of multi-modal fusion. Table 6 summarizes the results, and Fig. 5 illustrates how the proposed architecture improves with each search and fusion configuration. The progressive search mechanism expands the candidate space and progressively narrows the network topology toward finer levels. At the initial stages (Stage 1–2), the search mainly investigates superficially convoluted structures that underlie spatial learning. At Stage 3, we use temporal attention and depthwise convolutions, enabling more detailed spatiotemporal feature modeling. Lastly, Stage 4 will combine multi-modal fusion and residual skip connections, yielding a fully grown and expressive network. The key component in predicting neural architecture search performance is the surrogate-based performance predictor, which estimates validation accuracy without completing training.

Table 6 indicates that predictor fidelity, as measured by the coefficient of determination R2, has a substantial impact on the quality of the architectures found. As surrogate accuracy increases from 0.80 to 0.97, the final model’s accuracy also increases to 95.3%, while the difference between predicted and actual performance decreases to 1.2%. These findings confirm that a high-fidelity predictor enables effective ranking of candidate architectures and significantly reduces overall search time (30 to 48 GPU-hours), thereby justifying the efficiency of the proposed surrogate-based progressive search.

Discussion

Structural patterns are among the informative findings of the architectures discovered in the PNAS-HMR. The development of shallow-to-deep candidate models suggests a natural architecture that emphasizes early feature abstraction by spatiotemporal convolutional modules, followed by fine-grained motion discrimination via attention-based fusion layers. Interestingly, the resulting architectures will always be hybridized: by using 3D convolutional backbones and lightweight temporal attention heads, space, time, and semantics are arguably best represented in unison, and the most efficient representations are optimized. This trend suggests the shift toward more balanced hybrid networks that dynamically allocate computational resources depending on the complexity of tasks to abandon either a convolutional paradigm or an attention-based paradigm. In addition, the adopted architectures are stagewise symmetrically modular in nature, and the lower levels handle joint-limb dynamics, and the upper levels address the semantics of contextual action. This type of structure demonstrates that the search space and surrogate predictor, when combined, support interpretable, task-oriented model formation that is compatible with human intuition but more effective in optimization.

Unlike traditional NAS models based on a single-shot supernet or reinforcement learning policies, PNAS-HMR uses a progressive, multi-objective framework that balances accuracy, latency, and energy consumption. Traditional NAS pipelines are typically resource-intensive and may overfit to a single dataset. Although designs can be interpreted, manual designs are not always scalable across multiple domains. PNAS-HMR seals these gaps by incorporating a surrogate performance predictor and optimizing the accuracy-efficiency trade-off, which is much less costly to search than the rigorous prediction model and still generalizes well. The searched PNAS-HMR variants are more efficient in both parameter costs (down to 35% reduction in FLOPs) and recognition accuracy (2–4% improvements) across various benchmarks than manually designed architectures (e.g., I3D, SlowFast, and CTR-GCN). The identified architectures reveal a clear Pareto-optimal trade-off that balances computational efficiency and interpretability better than most transformer- and GCN-based baselines, demonstrating the feasibility of automated neural design for complex spatiotemporal tasks. PNAS-HMR has an optimized structure that enables real-time inference on edge and embedded machines, with an average latency of less than 10 ms per frame on RTX-class GPUs and a high power efficiency (> 2 GFLOPs/W). This type of performance shows high adaptability for use in human-computer interaction, intelligent surveillance, sports analytics, and rehabilitation monitoring systems when accuracy and responsiveness are critical. The hierarchical feature extraction and attention-directed fusion in the models enable them to be resistant to motion blur, camera jitter, and occlusion, which are inherent to real-world conditions. PNAS-HMR can be used for context-sensitive motion reasoning in multi-modal pipelines with RGB, optical flow, and skeletal data, and can be generalized to diverse environmental conditions. These attributes make PNAS-HMR an exciting foundation for the vision-based AI systems of the future, which must achieve both low latency and high interpretability. Although, because of its excellent scalability on large-scale benchmarks, PNAS-HMR can be demonstrated in real-life situations, the input resolution, modality availability, and the problem of hardware may still impact the real-world implementation. The modular architecture is flexible in configuration as it can disable some modalities or lower the temporal depth in order to satisfy the most complex latency constraints. The trade-offs easily offer a viable route through which the model may be implemented in the limited resource or large-scale settings.

The observability of PNAS-HMR is conveyed into practice in the real world by way of the ability to determine which joints, motion phases, or modalities are the most informative about particular actions. The knowledge allows the specific model to be refined and select a modality and optimize the system to the domain-specific context, such as surveillance, human-computer interaction, and monitoring in health care. PNAS-HMR makes deployment more reliable and informed system design through revealing these internal decision mechanisms. Although PNAS-HMR is very effective, it might be limited in applications in the real-life context with varying movement patterns and domain-specific constraints, e.g., medical monitoring or surveillance applications. The quality of the data, sensor noise, camera perspective, and motion subtlety may influence recognition performance, especially when the domains of training and target are pretty different. More so, multimodal inputs (e.g., skeleton or optical flow) might not be available in specific settings, necessitating simplified settings. Although the modular nature of PNAS-HMR can be adapted by modifying the modality-use and time-depth, it can be further fine-tuned or data-adapted to ensure strong performance even in safety-critical or highly dynamic settings.

Conclusion

This paper introduces PNAS-HMR, a new Progressive Neural Architecture Search framework for human motion recognition that searches for network topology under multi-objective constraints. PNAS-HMR proved to be the best performer in terms of accuracy and lower latency compared to other manually designed and conventional NAS-based models, through a large number of experiments. The hierarchical structure and performance-predictability mechanism of the framework enable the automatic discovery of efficient, interpretable, and robust motion recognition architectures. The presence of visual analyses and ablation research also indicated that the models being searched have compact feature embeddings, low inter-class overlap, and high semantic alignment with human motion patterns.

Future studies are advised to investigate the expansion of PNAS-HMR to multi-agent motion perception, gesture-emotion perception, and cross-view 3D pose estimation, where spatiotemporal dependencies are more complex. Self-supervised representation learning, graph-conscious attention mechanisms, and hardware-conscious NAS objectives might be further used to achieve additional performance and deployability improvements. Besides, the use of foundation models and transformer-based search controllers could improve generalization to unseen tasks and datasets. Finally, the progressive search paradigm, as demonstrated by PNAS-HMR, provides a scalable framework for intelligent and efficient model discovery, which serves as a solid foundation for future generations of human-centered intelligent vision systems.
