The Influence of Fetal Hemoglobin on Hematological Parameters and Clinical Severity in Patients with Homozygous Sickle Cell Disease.
Authors: Shweta S Talhar, Bharat U Patil, Dipak Kumar Das, Prafulla Ambulkar, Jwalant E Waghmare, Jyoti Jain, Manish Jain, Nitin M Gangane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:In Sickle cell disease (SCD), fetal hemoglobin (HbF) influences clinical severity, resulting in variable disease phenotypes. This study evaluated HbF levels, hematological parameters, and clinical severity in homozygous SCD patients.Methodology:A cross-sectional observational study with the prospective recruitment of 40 patients with homozygous SCD was conducted. A standardized questionnaire collected demographic data. Clinical and laboratory assessments calculated the clinical severity scores. Analyses used Stata/MP version 17.0; significance was set at p < 0.05.Results:HbF levels were inversely correlated with age (ρ = -0.998) and clinical severity (ρ = -0.928) but positively correlated with Hb (ρ = 0.999), RBC count (ρ = 0.999), and Packed Cell Volume (PCV) (ρ = 0.965) (all p < 0.001). Clinical severity was positively associated with age (ρ = 0.929) but negatively associated with Hb (ρ = -0.930), RBC count (ρ = -0.930), and PCV (ρ = -0.916) (all p < 0.001). HbF was the only independent predictor of disease severity (β = -0.14, 95% CI: -0.22 to -0.05, p = 0.002).Conclusion:Increased HbF levels were associated with improved hematological parameters and reduced clinical manifestations and clinical severity in homozygous SCD patients, supporting the role of HbF as a biomarker for disease severity.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a common genetic hematological disorder characterized by homozygous hemoglobin S (HbS) or HbS combined with other abnormal hemoglobin variants, resulting in defective hemoglobin polymerization. Homozygous SCD (HbSS) arises from a homozygous mutation (p.Glu6Val) in the beta-globin gene (HBB). Compound heterozygous states, such as sickle beta-thalassemia (HbS/β+-thalassemia and HbS/β0-thalassemia), also produce SCD. [1] In sickle beta-thalassemia, beta-globin chain production is reduced or absent, often leading to compensatory increases in gamma-globin and fetal hemoglobin (HbF) levels. [2] Genetic polymorphisms affecting HbF regulation and the Arab-Indian haplotype further influence HbF expression in SCD. [1-3] Clinically, distinguishing HbSS from HbS/β-thalassemia is important; HbA2 levels below 3.6% typically indicate HbSS, while higher values suggest HbS/β-thalassemia. [4,5]

The clinical phenotype of SCD shows marked heterogeneity and is influenced by genetic and environmental factors. [1] HbF is the most important modulator, along with the α-thalassemia trait and β-globin gene cluster haplotypes. [3] Elevated HbF levels and their pan-cellular distribution are associated with milder disease by inhibiting HbS polymerization and preventing RBC sickling. In vitro studies have suggested that HbF can inhibit the polymerization of HbS, potentially preventing sickling of RBCs, and in vivo studies have shown that HbF and FS hybrid molecules interfere with the nucleation phase of polymerization. [6] At birth, HbF (α2γ2) constitutes 65–90% of total hemoglobin, declining to <2% by 6–12 months of age as the β-globin gene is activated and the γ-globin gene is suppressed. [7,8]

HbF levels in SCD patients vary widely across populations and correlate with disease severity and complications. While SCD is a major global health concern, data on the relationship between HbF levels and clinical severity remain limited, particularly in resource-constrained settings. [9] In addition to HbF, other hematological parameters, such as white blood cell count (WBC), packed cell volume (PCV), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), influence disease severity. [10]

The primary aim of this study was to identify parameters useful for predicting clinical severity in patients with homozygous SCD in resource-limited settings in India. Specifically, we assessed HbF levels in homozygous SCD patients at a tertiary care institution in rural central India and evaluated their relationship with hematological parameters and total clinical severity score.

Material and Methods

Patients' Selection

This cross-sectional observational study with prospective recruitment was conducted from study period of January 2022 to December 2022 at the Sickle Cell Clinic and Medicine/Pediatric Departments of Kasturba Hospital, Sevagram, Maharashtra, India. Forty patients with homozygous SCD (HbSS) in a steady state or with vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC) were included. Patients with sickle β-thalassemia or other hemoglobinopathies, those receiving hydroxyurea, or those with chronic illnesses, such as cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, gastrointestinal, rheumatoid arthritis, AIDS, peripheral vascular disease, and autoimmune diseases, were excluded. Other conditions that may affect hematological indices, such as pregnancy, renal failure, or recent blood transfusion (within 3 months), were excluded (Figure 1).

Homozygous SS status was confirmed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). HbA2 levels <3.6% were used to classify patients as having HbSS; those with HbA2 >3.6% (suggestive of sickle β-thalassemia) were excluded. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. In the pediatric group, informed consent was obtained from the parents. Assent was also obtained from children aged > 7 years. A pretested, validated questionnaire collected sociodemographic data, clinical profiles, and medical histories. Data were collected on the following: (1) current clinical status, including splenomegaly and hematological

parameters such as hemoglobin (Hb), red blood cell count (RBC), white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), packed cell volume (PCV), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and red cell distribution width (RDW); (2) number of VOC, hospitalizations, and blood transfusions in the previous 12 months; and (3) lifetime cumulative incidence of complications (acute chest syndrome [ACS], cerebrovascular disease [CVD], avascular necrosis [AVN]/bone necrosis, pneumococcal meningitis, gallstones, chronic leg ulcers, osteomyelitis, and priapism). Medical records were reviewed for accuracy.

Hematological Analysis:

Complete blood counts (Hb, RBC, WBC, PLT, PCV, MCV, and RDW) were measured using an XN-1000TM Hematology Analyzer (Sysmex). Hemoglobin fractions, including HbF, were quantified by HPLC using the VariantTM II Hemoglobin Testing System (Bio-Rad). Patients were categorized into three HbF groups: <10%, 10–20%, and >20%, for correlation analysis with hematological parameters.

Assessment of clinical severity:

Clinical severity was evaluated using six clinical and laboratory variables representing the current status and lifetime complications. [11-12] Each variable was scored on a 0–5 scale (Table 1), with higher weights for life-threatening events (e.g., CVD = 5). The total clinical severity score ranged from 0 to 31 and was categorized as mild (<8), moderate (8–17), or severe (>17). [11-12]

Statistical Analysis:

Data were analyzed using Stata/MP 17.0. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and summarized appropriately. Group comparisons used parametric or non-parametric tests, while Fisher’s exact test assessed categorical outcomes. Spearman correlation evaluated associations. Univariable and multivariable linear regression identified predictors, with multicollinearity assessed using correlation and VIF. Statistical significance was p < 0.05.

Ethics:

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Mahatma Gandhi Institute of Medical Sciences, Sevagram (MGIMS/IEC/ANAT/17/2022). All procedures followed the Helsinki Declaration (1964, revised 2013). Patient confidentiality was maintained.

Association of HbF with Hematological and Clinical Parameters:

There were significant differences in clinical and hematological parameters observed across the three HbF groups. Patients with higher HbF levels were significantly younger, with median (IQR) ages of 26 (24–34) years in the HbF <10% group, 18 (16–21) years in the HbF 10–20% group, and 13 (12–14) years in the HbF >20% group (Kruskal–Wallis χ2 = 31.28, p < 0.001). Hb concentration increased progressively across the HbF groups and differed significantly between all three groups (6.55 ± 0.64, 8.38 ± 0.60, and 9.80 ± 0.40 g/dL for HbF <10%, 10–20%, and >20%, respectively; ANOVA F = 72.09, η2 = 0.796, p < 0.001),

indicating a very large effect of the HbF group on Hb concentration. Similarly, the RBC count and PCV increased significantly across the HbF groups (RBC: F = 65.51, η2 = 0.780; PCV: F = 41.72, η2 = 0.693; both p < 0.001), with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc analysis demonstrating significant differences between all pairwise group comparisons. In contrast, the WBC count, PLT count, MCV, and RDW did not differ significantly among the HbF groups (all p > 0.05). Clinical Severity Score showed a significant inverse relationship with HbF level (Kruskal–Wallis χ2 = 24.03, p < 0.001), with significant pairwise differences observed between all HbF groups after Bonferroni correction [Table 3].

Association of Clinical Severity with Hematological and Clinical Parameters:

Patients with moderate clinical severity were significantly older than those with mild disease (median age: 23 [21–26] vs. 15 [14–17] years, Z = -5.25, r = 0.83, p < 0.001), with a large effect size. HbF levels significantly decreased with increased clinical severity (moderate severity, median: 10.5% [8.5–13.0] vs. mild severity, 18.9% [15.0–20.6], z = 5.23, r = 0.83, p < 0.001). Mean Hb levels were significantly higher in patients with mild clinical severity than in those with moderate clinical severity (9.22 ± 0.63 vs. 7.17 ± 0.77 g/dL, t = 9.15; Cohen's d = 2.99; p < 0.001). Similarly, RBC count (3.72 ± 0.44 vs. 2.52 ± 0.39 × 106/μL, t = 8.68, p < 0.001; Cohen's d = 2.84) and PCV (28.44 ± 4.61% vs. 19.51 ± 2.99%, t = 6.68, p < 0.001; Cohen's d = 2.18) were significantly increased in patients with mild clinical severity. No statistically significant differences were found between the mild and moderate severity groups in terms of WBC count, PLT count, MCV, and RDW [Table 5].

Correlation between HbF, clinical severity, and hematological parameters:

The clinical severity of patients with SCD showed a strong positive correlation with age (ρ = 0.929, p < 0.001), while strong negative correlations were observed with HbF levels (ρ = -0.928, p < 0.001), Hb concentration (ρ = -0.930, p < 0.001), RBC count (ρ = -0.930, p < 0.001), and PCV (ρ = -0.916, p < 0.001). However, no significant correlations were observed between clinical severity and WBC count, PLT count, MCV, or RDW (p > 0.001). There was a strong inverse correlation between HbF and age (ρ = -0.998, p < 0.001) and clinical severity score (ρ = -0.928, p < 0.001), whereas significant positive correlations were found between HbF and Hb concentration (ρ = 0.999, p < 0.001), RBC count (ρ = 0.999, p < 0.001), and PCV (ρ = 0.965, p < 0.001). However, a weak negative correlation was observed between HbF and PLT count (ρ = -0.330, p = 0.038). HbF levels did not show significant correlations with other hematological parameters, WBC count, MCV, or RDW (p > 0.001) [Table 7]. Scatter plots with locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) curves represented a progressive decrease in HbF levels as age increased, depicting a strong inverse relationship between HbF levels and age (Figure 2a). In contrast, HbF levels were positively correlated with Hb concentration, PCV, and RBC count (Figures 2b–d). The LOWESS curve also represented a positive association between age and clinical severity (Figure 2e), whereas clinical severity showed inverse relationships with RBC count, Hb concentration, PCV, and HbF level (Figures 2f–i).

Multivariable Linear Regression Analysis of Factors Associated with HbF and Clinical Severity:

In the multivariable linear regression analysis, age was independently and inversely associated with HbF levels (β = -0.62, 95% CI: -0.74 to -0.49, p < 0.001) in Model 1, whereas WBC count, PLT count, and RDW were not significant predictors of HbF levels. Model 1 explained 80% of the variability in HbF levels (adjusted R2 = 0.778, F = 35.07, p < 0.001). In Model 2, Hb was independently and positively associated with HbF levels (β = 3.72, 95% CI: 3.39 to 4.04, p < 0.001), whereas WBC count, PLT count, and RDW remained non-significant. This model explained 95.2% of the variability in HbF levels (adjusted R2 = 0.946, F = 171.50, p < 0.001). For clinical severity, HbF was the only independent predictor, showing a significant inverse association with disease severity (β = -0.14, 95% CI: -0.22 to -0.05; p = 0.002). Age and other hematological parameters were not significantly associated with clinical severity. The model explained 74.0% of the variability in clinical severity (adjusted R2 = 0.710, F = 24.83, p < 0.001) [Table 8].

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between HbF levels, hematological parameters, and clinical severity in Indian patients with homozygous SCD. The median HbF level in our cohort was 15.0%, similar to a previous Indian study [13] but higher than those reported from Tanzania [14-15], Nigeria [16], and Ghana [17]. This variability likely stems from polymorphisms in population-specific genetic modifiers, such as BCL11A, HBS1L-MYB, and KLF1, which regulate HbF production and play key roles in the developmental switch from fetal to adult hemoglobin. [18] The Arab-Indian β-globin haplotype, which

predominates in the Indian population, is associated with elevated HbF levels. [19] Collectively, these factors may be responsible for the relatively high HbF levels in our study group. However, these genetic modifiers were not investigated in the present study.

This study reported a strong inverse association between HbF levels and age in patients with homozygous SCD. This coincided with previous studies from Tanzania [20] and Nigeria. [21-22] The present study demonstrated a positive association between HbF and several hematological parameters, namely Hb concentration, RBC count, and PCV, which are well-known red cell indices, whereas the WBC count, PLT count, MCV, and RDW did not differ significantly across the HbF groups. Some studies have reported positive associations between HbF and red cell indices [23-24], with inverse relationships with PLT and WBC counts. [24] However, some past studies didn't show such associations. [25-26] The beneficial effects of HbF on hematological parameters can be explained by its inhibition of HbS polymerization, reduced hemolysis, improved RBC lifespan, and enhanced oxygen-carrying capacity. [27]

There was a significant inverse association between HbF levels and clinical severity. Multivariate regression analysis confirmed HbF as the only independent predictor of clinical severity. Similar findings have been reported in Nigerian and Iraqi studies. [12,23] Higher HbF levels are also associated with a reduced frequency of several clinical manifestations. We observed a progressive decline in VOC, bone necrosis, and gallstones with increasing HbF levels. Interestingly, splenomegaly was significantly more common in patients with HbF >20%, suggesting a protective role of HbF against autosplenectomy by reducing sickling, vaso-occlusion, and splenic infarction. [28-31]

The overall median clinical severity score was 7 (IQR 7–8), with 62.5% of patients classified as having mild disease and 37.5% moderate; none had severe disease. This relatively mild-to-moderate phenotype may be partly attributed to the higher HbF levels commonly observed in Indian patients owing to genetic modifiers such as BCL11A and XmnI variants, along with the Arab-Indian β-globin haplotype. [3,32]

Additionally, the relatively small sample size of the study might have reduced the chances of recruiting patients with severe disease during the study period. The severity scores in the present study matched those from The Gambia [33] and Yemen [34] but were lower than those in the Sudanese [11] and Nigerian populations. [12] Age was positively correlated with disease severity, consistent with Sudanese [11] and Nigerian findings [12], likely due to cumulative complications over time. Increasing clinical severity was associated with progressive deterioration in haematological parameters, namely Hb, PCV, and RBC count, consistent with previous studies. [10,33] In contrast, past studies in Nigeria [10,12] and Africa [35] reported a positive correlation between disease severity and WBC count, MCHC, and PLT count. The most frequently occurring clinical manifestation in the present study was VOC, consistent with past studies in Tanzania [20] and Gambia [33], and was significantly associated with increased disease severity [20, 33], which is consistent with the present study. In multivariable linear regression analysis, age was independently and inversely associated with HbF levels, whereas hemoglobin was positively associated. For clinical severity, HbF remained the only independent predictor (β = -0.14, 95% CI: -0.22 to -0.05; p = 0.002).

Limitations:

Limitations included small sample size, cross-sectional design, single-centre hospital-based recruitment, and absence of severe cases, limiting statistical power and generalizability. Recall bias and unassessed genetic determinants of HbF may have influenced findings. Larger longitudinal multicentre studies incorporating genetic analysis and all severity categories are recommended.

Conclusion

In the present study, higher HbF levels were associated with better hematological parameters, decreased frequency of clinical manifestations, and reduced clinical severity in patients with homozygous SCD. Regular evaluation of these parameters helps to identify high-risk patients requiring critical management and careful monitoring. The modified clinical severity score system used in this study is simple, feasible, and can be applied in areas with limited healthcare facilities. HbF was the only independent predictor of clinical severity and can be used as a biomarker for disease severity. However, prospective studies with recruitment of more study participants and longitudinal clinical data are needed to further assess the role of HbF in predicting disease progression and optimizing individualized management strategies for patients with SCD.
