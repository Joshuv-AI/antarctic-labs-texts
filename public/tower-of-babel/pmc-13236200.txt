The quiet cost of gender bias in the operating room
Authors: Javeria Akhter, Muhammad Nouman Javed, Waleed Ahmed, Muhammad Noshab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surgical specialties continue to be dominated by men, even though women make up more than 50% of medical school graduates. Female surgeons’ self-esteem, career paths, and well-being are destroyed by overt discrimination and microaggressions, which also have an impact on team dynamics and patient safety. Women in surgery report widespread bias, from being mistaken as nurses to receiving fewer highly complex cases, which contributes to emotional burnout, obstructed skill-learning, and premature career termination. The hidden “time taxes” of additional planning and self-monitoring, which go beyond the typical obstacles, reduce output and limit creativity. We support structured mentorship programs, clear criteria for case allocation and promotion, clear leadership commitment to gender parity in the operating room, and institution-wide implicit bias training as ways to break these long-standing trends.

Introduction

Gender bias in surgery often goes unacknowledged, yet its impact reverberates through every stage of training and practice. A national survey found that over 90% of female surgeons experience gender-related discrimination during their residency or practice, and nearly 90% encounter it in a clinical setting[1]. It is a culture where competence is constantly in question and is created by typical behaviors like assuming that a female resident is a nurse, undervaluing her clinical input during rounds, or failing to use her professional title in presentations. These patterns are far from isolated incidents; rather, they are a reflection of systemic stereotypes that undermine the professional identity and status of female surgeons by associating surgical authority with masculinity.

The landscape of gender representation in surgery

Even though women have steadily advanced into the field of medicine, making up over 50% of matriculants since 2017, their participation as practicing surgeons lags behind significantly. The 2023 CMS National Downloadable Database Analysis revealed that only 16.7% of active surgeons were women[2]. In orthopedics, which is the most male-dominated surgical subspecialty, women made up only 8% of practitioners in 2019, an increase from 6% in 2010, and at current rates, gender parity is projected to be reached only by the year 2036[3]. Other specialties, such as cardiothoracic and neurosurgery, also have low female representation. These stark numbers illustrate the persistent cultural obstacles that hinder women from either pursuing surgical careers or enduring temping in these fields and reinforce a cycle of underrepresentation in positions of influence.

Forms of gender bias in the operating room

Gender bias in the operating room (OR) manifests through both blatant and insidious behaviors. Female surgeons frequently report being interrupted more often than their male colleagues and receiving fewer high-complexity cases despite comparable skill levels. In grand rounds presentations, male introducers use formal titles for men over 95% of the time but for women only about half the time, subtly signaling lesser professional respect[4]. Multi-institutional data demonstrate that female general surgery residents receive significantly less operative autonomy than their male peers, even after adjusting for postgraduate year, case complexity, faculty sex, and program environment. Moreover, female physicians report a lack of mentoring tailored to their needs, low levels of institutional acceptance for flexible working to accommodate parental responsibilities, and perceived explicit gender biases[5]. These persistent slights not only diminish real-time authority but also erode long-term credibility and career advancement.

Consequences for surgeons and patient care

The toll of gender bias extends beyond individual discomfort to tangible career setbacks and patient outcomes. Among surgical trainees, female residents report higher rates of emotional exhaustion and burnout, often attributing this to work–home integration challenges and gendered expectations, highlighting the need for gender-sensitive wellness interventions[6]. Limited access to key operative experiences and leadership roles further constrains skill acquisition and promotion opportunities. Systemically, homogeneity in surgical teams stifles communication, situational awareness, and decision-making quality. Despite growing interest in board gender diversity and CSR since 2010, collaboration among researchers remains limited, pointing to a need for cross-disciplinary work on how diversity drives organizational outcomes, including team performance and patient safety[7].

The invisible price tag

Beyond these measurable outcomes lies an invisible “time tax” borne by female surgeons: the extra cognitive and emotional labor expended to anticipate and counteract bias. Whether meticulously preparing for presentations to forestall interruptions or diligently self-editing to avoid being labeled “abrasive,” women invest uncompensated effort that saps productivity and dampens innovation[8]. Over time, this hidden burden contributes to decreased research output, reluctance to speak up in hierarchical settings, and diminished career satisfaction.

Strategies for change

Addressing gender bias in surgery demands a multifaceted, sustained effort. A 2.5-h “bias-habit-changing” workshop significantly increased faculty self-efficacy to engage in gender-equity behaviors, boosted action to promote equity at 3 months, and improved departmental climate measures – demonstrating that a structured implicit-bias intervention can meaningfully reduce biased behaviors[9]. Second, structured mentorship and sponsorship programs pairing junior women with accomplished female surgeons bolster professional identity and networking opportunities. A recent scoping review of 24 randomized controlled trials across diverse workplace settings identified six categories of effective gender-equity interventions: quantifying gender impacts; behavioral or systemic changes; career flexibility; increasing visibility, recognition, and representation; creating development, mentorship, and sponsorship opportunities; and financial support – underscoring that multifaceted, system-level strategies can drive accountability and equity[10]. Finally, visible endorsement of gender equity by departmental and institutional leaders, coupled with clear metrics and progress reports, fosters a culture where merit, rather than gender, drives opportunity.

Conclusion

Gender bias in the OR remains a quiet but formidable barrier to surgeons’ well-being, professional advancement, and optimal patient care. Although progress has been made, enduring stereotypes and structural inequities continue to constrain women’s surgical careers. True equity requires unwavering commitment – from implicit bias training and transparent policies to mentorship and executive leadership – to dismantle old norms and build an OR culture where every surgeon’s skill and contribution are valued, regardless of gender.
