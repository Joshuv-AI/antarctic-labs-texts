Hippocampal subregions functional connectivity alterations in primary angle-closure glaucoma patients with cognitive dysfunction: a resting-state fMRI study
Authors: Shenghong Li, Yuanyuan Wang, Jian Li, Linglong Chen, Fengqin Cai, Lianjiang Lv, Zihe Xu, Chonggang Pei, Xianjun Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive dysfunction has been reported in patients with glaucoma, but the underlying neural mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to explore functional connectivity (FC) alterations in hippocampal subregions in primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) patients with cognitive impairment.MethodThis study included forty-four PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction, and forty-six healthy controls (HCs). Participants underwent 3D high-resolution T1 structural imaging and BOLD fMRI scanning. Seven hippocampal subregions were selected as seed regions to explore changes in FC between the bilateral hippocampal subregions (Cornu Ammonis 1, Cornu Ammonis 2, Cornu Ammonis 3, Dentate gyrus, Entorhinal cortex, HATA, Subiculu) and the whole brain in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction.ResultsCompared with the HCs group, the PACG group showed decreased FC between multiple hippocampal subregions and the cerebellum, precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, supplementary motor area, supramarginal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, opercular part, lenticular nucleus, pallidum, rolandic operculum, inferior parietal, supramarginal, and angular gyri. However, increased FC was found between the bilateral hippocampal subregions and the calcarine fissure and surrounding cortex, lingual gyrus, anterior cingulate, and paracingulate gyri. We also found that FC between hippocampal subregions and some brain regions were associated with visual acuity, average cup-to-disc ratio, and retinal nerve fibre layer thickness.ConclusionThese findings reveal widespread hippocampal FC alterations involving the cerebellum, sensorimotor, default mode, and visual network (VN) in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction, contributing to a better understanding of the underlying neural mechanisms.

Background

Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide [1, 2]. It is expected that 111.8 million people will develop glaucoma by 2040 with the ageing social population [3]. Glaucoma is characterised by optic nerve damage and progressive loss of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs). Primary glaucoma is common in clinical practice. Based on the opening and closing of the angle, glaucoma is divided into primary angle closure glaucoma (PACG) and primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG). PACG is mainly caused by a narrow or closed angle of the chamber, which prevents aqueous humour discharge and increases intraocular pressure (IOP). The intermittent or persistent elevation of the IOP in glaucoma patients exceeds the tolerance of the eyeball which causes damage to various parts of the eyeball and visual function, resulting in the progressive loss of RGCs, thinning of the retinal nerve fibre layer, atrophying of the optic nerve, narrowing of the visual field, and decreased vision. However, Gupta et al. found that glaucomatous damage was not limited to the optic nerve but also caused structural changes in the lateral geniculate body and visual cortex [4]. Subsequent studies have confirmed that glaucoma lesions are not only confined to the eyes but also cause structural and functional changes in the brain [5–7]. Recent studie has found that the white matter of the visual pathway in PACG patients has changed [8]. Moreover, many studies have confirmed that glaucoma is similar to neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [9–11]. Past studies have primarily focused on POAG, which is the most prevalent in western societies. However, the most common type in Asia and China is PACG. Compared with POAG, fewer retinal nerve fibre layer sectors have significant structure-function correlations in PACG [12], suggesting differences in the pathophysiology of optic nerve damage and even the whole visual pathway between PACG and POAG.

Recently, resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI), which reflects the spontaneous brain activity in the resting state, has been widely used. Huang et al. applied the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) of rs-fMRI [13], and Wang et al. applied fractional ALFF (fALFF) analysis [14], both revealing that glaucoma patients have abnormal spontaneous brain activity in multiple brain regions, including the visual cortex. Fu et al. found that patients with PACG showed abnormal regional homogeneity values in the cerebellum, visual cortex, and supplementary motor area (SMA) [15], which may reflect the neural mechanism of visual loss. The voxel-based degree centrality (DC) analysis technique previously used by our group and Chen et al. revealed the central dysfunction of a wide range of brain functional networks in PACG patients [16, 17]. Using voxel-mirrored homotopic connectivity analysis, our research group and Tong et al. found that PACG patients showed complex interhemispheric connectivity abnormalities [18, 19]. In addition, we previously used graph theory analysis to study the intrinsic functional brain network of PACG patients, and found that PACG patients had significant changes in the local characteristics of the visual and cognitive-emotional brain regions [20]. Our previous studies revealed different spatial distributions in functional connectivity density (FCD) mapping in PACG patients [21]. Jiang et al. used Granger causality analysis to show that PACG patients have abnormal effective connectivity between various brain regions [22]. In addition, our previous application of the functional connectivity (FC) method found that PACG patients had decreased integration of visual information in the primary-advanced visual cortex pathway [23]. We also found a wide range of abnormal resting-state FC between the thalamus, amygdala, and visual or extra-visual brain regions in PACG patients [24, 25].

Studies have shown that glaucoma patients have neurocognitive dysfunction and an increased risk of dementia, they exhibit cognitive impairments primarily in memory, executive function, language, calculation, orientation, and visuospatial abilities [26–29]. It indicates that glaucoma patients have impairments in multiple aspects of cognitive function. Therefore, this study mainly focuses on the overall cognitive impairment in PACG patients. However, previous rs-fMRI studies on PACG patients have paid little attention to their cognitive dysfunction, and the underlying neural mechanisms of cognitive dysfunction remain unclear. The hippocampus is a widely studied brain region that plays an important role in advanced cognitive functions such as learning, memory, and navigation [30–32]. It is one of the earliest structures affected by neurodegenerative diseases such as AD [33, 34]. Studies have shown that FC changes in the anterior and posterior hippocampal regions were associated with decreased subjective cognitive abilities [35]. This indicates that the hippocampus is a crucial structure in the brain that is closely related to cognitive functions. Therefore, this study used rs-fMRI to explore patterns of hippocampal FC in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction. The hippocampus is not a homogeneous structure but comprises distinct subregions such as Cornu Ammonis (CA1-CA3), Dentate gyrus (DG), each subregions have unique cytoarchitecture, connectivity, functional roles [36–39]. In the early stage of AD, neuron loss are more prominent in CA1 and subiculum (Subc) [40]. Therefore, in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, the structure and function of the hippocampal subregions gradually deteriorate, leading to symptoms such as memory decline and spatial cognitive impairment. Studying the functions of the hippocampal subregions is helpful for understanding the neural mechanisms of cognitive. Glaucoma, as a neurodegenerative disease, may exhibit differences in the functional changes of hippocampal subregions. Additionally, given that PACG patients report deficits in memory, executive function, language, calculation, orientation, and visuospatial abilities, we hypothesize that these specific functions are subserved by distinct subcircuits within the hippocampus. Therefore, a whole-hippocampus analysis would likely obscure these fine-grained, subfield-specific alterations in functional connectivity. By employing a subfield-level approach, our study aims to uncover precise and mechanistically informative neural correlates of cognitive impairment in PACG, moving beyond coarse-grained associations to identify which specific hippocampal circuits are most affected. However, the subregions of hippocampal functional changes in PACG patients and their association with cognitive impairment remain less clear. We used a probability map of the hippocampal cell structure divided into seven subregions: CA1-CA3, DG, Entorhinal cortex (EC), HATA, Subc to provide detailed hippocampal FC [41, 42]. We hypothesized that, compared to that in healthy controls (HCs), the FC pattern in the hippocampal subregions of PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction would be altered, and that these changes would be related to impaired visual and cognitive function.

Methods

Participants

Fifty PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction and 46 HCs were recruited for this study. All the participants were right-handed. PACG Patients were recruited from our hospital’s inpatient and outpatient departments.

The inclusion criteria for PACG patients were: (1) monocular or binocular anterior chamber angle stenosis; (2) glaucoma-related visual field defects; (3) optic disc cup-to-disc ratio > 0.6; (4) cognitive dysfunction; (5) willing to cooperate with relevant evaluations. Two glaucoma experts assessed the results without knowing the patients’ information. Participants were only included in the study when the diagnoses of the two experts were consistent. The exclusion criteria for PACG patients were: (1) diagnosed as other types of glaucoma; (2) combined with other eye diseases, such as cataract or high myopia; (3) history of brain trauma or epilepsy; (4) history of hypertension, diabetes, and other basic diseases; (5) history of glaucoma surgery; (6) age over than 65 years or difficulties to cooperate with the relevant evaluations; (7) during rs-fMRI exam, head movement with a maximum displacement greater than 2 mm in any direction of x, y, and z or a rotation angle greater than 2° on any coordinate axis; (8) conventional MRI revealed the presence of encephalomalacia; (9) unsuitable for or cannot tolerate MRI examinations. Finally, 44 patients with PACG (16 males and 28 females; age 39–63 years) were included (two patients were older than 65 years, one patient was found to have a large encephalomalacia during MRI, and three patients had head movement > 2 mm).

We recruited 46 right-handed, age- and sex-matched HCs from the community (18 males, 28 females. Age: 39–65 years). The exclusion criteria for HCs were: (1) diagnosis of eye diseases or other neurological diseases; (2) presence of diabetes, hypertension, liver disease, or other basic diseases; (3) high myopia; (4) MRI examination contraindications; (5) age over 65 years; (6) head movement with maximum displacement greater than 2 mm in any direction of x, y, or z or rotation angle greater than 2° on any axis.

At this stage, written informed consent was obtained, and clinical information was recorded. Participants underwent a cognitive assessment using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) before rs-fMRI examination, the MoCA scale score of less than 26 was defined as the presence of cognitive dysfunction [43].

Magnetic resonance data acquisition

The rs-fMRI data were collected using a 3 T MR scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with an 8-channel phased array head coil at the imaging centre of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. All participants were instructed to sit for 10 min before resting-state scanning. Appropriate sponge pads and earplugs were used to suppress head movement and noise, respectively, the participants were asked to remain relaxed and close their eyes but to not fall asleep and try not to think. The rs-fMRI data acquisition lasted 8 min, and 240 time points were obtained. The scanning parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR), 2000 ms; echo time (TE), 40 ms; flip angle, 90°; field of view (FOV), 240 mm × 240 mm; matrix, 64 × 64; layer thickness, 4 mm; layer spacing, 1 mm. High-resolution T1-weighted images of each participant were obtained using a 3D MRI sequence. The scanning parameters were as follows: TR, 1900 ms; TE, 2.26 ms; flip angle, 9°; FOV view, 240 mm × 240 mm; matrix, 256 × 256; number of sagittal planes, 176; layer thickness, 1 mm.

Data preprocessing

The DPARSF data processing module in the DPABI software (a toolbox for Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging) based on the MATLAB platform was used to preprocess the collected MRI data [44]. The main steps were as follows: (1) format conversion: the collected BOLD and 3D-T1 images were converted from DICOM file format to NIFTI format; (2) the first 10 time points of the functional image were removed to achieve magnetic field stability; (3) time correction: time layer correction was performed on the remaining 230 time point data of the functional image to check the different effects of different levels of scanning time; (4) head movement correction; (5) spatial standardisation: registration of functional images and high-resolution T1-weighted structural images, segmentation of structural data, spatial standardisation to standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, and re-sampling to 3 × 3 × 3 mm isotropic voxels; (6) smoothing: 6 mm full width half maximum Gaussian kernel smoothing; (7) de-linear drift: remove the linear trend of the time series; (8) filtering: filtering (0.01 ~ 0.08 Hz) to remove low-frequency physiological signals and high-frequency random noise; (9) regression covariates: head movement and white matter, cerebrospinal fluid and global signal as covariates.

Seed-based FC analyses

The DPARSF module was used for seed-based FC analysis. For the hippocampal subregions, we used the Anatomy Toolkit’s templates for the probabilistic graph of cellular structures [41, 42] and presented them using MRIcron software (MRIcron v1.0.20190902, https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mricron/) (Fig. 1). After normalizing the images and applying the probabilistic hippocampal subfield templates from the Anatomy Toolbox, we visually inspected the alignment between the region of interest (ROI) masks and each individual’s anatomical images using MRIcron. Two experienced researchers independently checked the registration quality, any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. No subjects were excluded due to misalignment, as all ROIs showed acceptable overlap with the corresponding hippocampal regions. Bilateral hippocampal subregions were selected as seed regions, including the CA1, CA2, CA3, DG, EC, HATA, Subc. The time series of each seed region was extracted for Pearson correlation FC analysis with whole-brain voxels, and an FC correlation coefficient map was obtained. Fisher’s Z transformation was performed to conform to the normal distribution for statistical analysis.

Clinical assessment

All participants underwent a detailed ophthalmic examination. The anterior chamber angle was determined using gonioscopy and slit-lamp examination. The average cup-to-disc ratio (A-C/D), vertical cup-to-disc ratio (V-C/D), and retinal nerve fibre layer thickness (RNFLT) were measured by optical coherence tomography (Cirrus HD-OCT). IOP was measured 1 h before the rs-fMRI examination using a tonometer. Visual acuity (VA) was measured using an international standard VA chart. The disease course was also recorded. Humphrey visual field instrument was used to conduct visual field tests on all the subjects. The inclusion criteria for the PACG group in this study include glaucoma-related visual field defects, such as tubular vision and nasal hemianopia. However, standard automated perimetry was not included in the primary analysis due to concerns regarding data reliability inherent to its subjective nature. Specifically, the test’s dependence on patient performance can lead to significant variability from factors such as fatigue, inattention, and fixation instability. Given the high proportion of unreliable tests in our cohort, potentially attributable to elderly patients with early cognitive impairment or patients from a population with low prior exposure to perimetry testing, we prioritized the use of more consistent parameters for assessing disease severity.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analysis of the general clinical data. Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and an independent sample t-test was used for measurement data that were normally distributed. If the measurement data were not normally distributed, the median (quartile) was used, and the Mann-Whitney test was used for analysis. Count data were analysed using χ2 tests. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

The statistical module in DPABI was used for rs-fMRI data analysis. A two-sample t-test was used to test differences in FC between PACG patients and HCs at the voxel level. Age and sex were used as covariates, and false discovery rate was used for multiple comparison correction (voxel-level, P < 0.05).

Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed for the PACG patients to assess the correlations between the clinical variables and the FC coefficient of the hippocampal subregions measured in each region, which showed differences between the groups. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Correlations between hippocampal FC and clinical indices in PACG patients

As shown in Table 3; Fig. 4, FC between the left hippocampal CA1 subregion and the right CAL was positively correlated with VA (r = 0.396, P = 0.008). The FC between the right hippocampal subregion and the left PreCG was positively correlated with A-C/D (r = 0.311, P = 0.04). The FC between the right hippocampal Subc subregion and the right PreCG was negatively correlated with RNFLT (r = −0.312, P = 0.039). The FC between the right hippocampal CA3 subregion and the left LING was negatively correlated with A-C/D (r = −0.358, P = 0.017). However, contrary to our hypothesis, we did not find a correlation between FC in the hippocampal subregions and the MoCA score.

Discussion

We used the FC method of rs-fMRI to analyse changes in the FC of bilateral hippocampal subregions in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction. As hypothesized, compared to HCs, PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction showed changes in hippocampal FC patterns. These changes were mainly observed in the cerebellum, PreCG, PoCG, SMG, SMA, and CAL. These findings provide new insights for understanding better central nervous system damage in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction.

Compared to that in HCs, we found that the FC between multiple subregions of the bilateral hippocampus and cerebellum was decreased in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction. In addition to motor coordination, the cerebellum is also involved in cognitive and emotional regulation [45–47]. Studies have shown that almost all acute cerebellar strokes occur after cerebellar cognitive–affective syndrome [48]. Using arterial spin labelling imaging, Wang et al. found that a decrease in cerebellar cerebral blood flow (CBF) in POAG patients confirmed cerebellar haemodynamic dysfunction in glaucoma patients [49]. Moreover, fMRI studies have confirmed the functional connection between the human cerebellum and hippocampus. A specific functional circuit between the cerebellum and hippocampus is mainly involved in the cognitive aspects of navigation [50]. Based on visual-motor integration, hippocampus-cerebellum interactions are involved in spatiotemporal movement prediction [51]. Studies have shown a specific relationship between the cerebellum and hippocampus during changes in visual-spatial information perception [52], highlighting the importance of the cerebellum and functional connections between the cerebellum and hippocampus in cognitive function. The decreased FC between multiple subregions of the bilateral hippocampus and cerebellum suggests that PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction have impaired cognitive function circuits between the hippocampus and cerebellum. We identified the bilateral CA1, CA2, DG, right CA3, Subc as the hippocampal subregions with decreased FC to the cerebellum. The CA1-CA3 subregions of the hippocampus collectively form the core circuitry of the hippocampus, and are involved in the storage, consolidation, and retrieval of memories [53, 54]. DG is mainly involved in the encoding of memories and the formation of new memories. Subc is an important hub for connecting the hippocampus with other areas of the brain, it plays a crucial role in the output of the hippocampal structure [55]. This indicates that in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction, the cognitive circuitry between the cerebellum and the hippocampus is dysfunctional, affecting multiple functional hippocampal subregions, which may reflect one of the mechanisms leading to cognitive dysfunction in PACG patients.

Compared to that in HCs, the FC between multiple subregions of the bilateral hippocampus and the PreCG, PoCG, SMA was decreased in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction. These brain regions are part of the sensorimotor network (SMN). As a ‘sensor’ in the brain, the SMN cooperates closely with other brain networks to perform important activities. Huang et al. found that spontaneous brain activity decreased in the left PreCG of PACG patients [13]. Wang et al. found that the CBF in the PoCG decreased in POAG patients [49]. In addition, patients with neovascular glaucoma showed abnormal PoCG function [56]. This result indicates that patients with different types of glaucoma have abnormal functions in some brain regions of the SMN. Dai et al. found that POAG patients showed decreased FC between the visual cortex and the PreCG [57], and our previous studies revealed that PACG patients showed decreased static and dynamic brain network connectivity between the VN and SMN [58]. These results suggest an abnormal visual sensorimotor processing integration function in PACG patients. Chen et al. found that PACG patients showed decreased FC between the amygdala and SMN brain regions [25], indicating that the amygdala-sensorimotor pathway was damaged in PACG patients. As a result of the visual field defects and progressive loss of RGCs in PACG patients, the visual information received by the VN is reduced, and so is the visual signal input received by the SMN. Consequently, the integration of visual-motor processing is affected. In this study, the FC between multiple subregions of the bilateral hippocampus and multiple brain regions of the SMN was decreased, these hippocampal subregions mainly include the bilateral CA1, DG, Subc regions. It indicates that the mechanism underlying the impaired hippocampus-sensorimotor pathway in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction appears complex, as it involves multiple hippocampal subregions, suggesting a need for more in-depth research. Furthermore, the hippocampus is part of the default mode network (DMN), suggesting that PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction may have a reduced interaction between the DMN and SMN.

In PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction, the FC between the left hippocampal Subc subregion, right hippocampal CA1 subregion and the SMG and that between the right hippocampal DG subregion and IPL were decreased. The SMG and the IPL are located in the inferior parietal lobule, which primarily involves visual attention, self-perception, nondirectional thinking, episodic memory, and social cognition [59]. Visual information is processed via the dorsal visual pathway to the inferior parietal lobules., The FC were reduced between inferior parietal lobule and multiple functional subregions of the hippocampus, including the encoding area, storage area, and output area of memory information. We speculate that in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction, reduced visual signal transmission leads to dysfunction of the inferior parietal lobule. This dysfunction thereby affects the visual input to the functional subregions of the hippocampus. Additionally, the inferior parietal lobule and hippocampus are parts of the DMN, indicating that the internal FC of the DMN is reduced in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction. Wang et al. found that the CBF of the DMN in POAG patients was reduced [49], and CBF-FC coupling was abnormal [60]. Our research group’s previous application of the DC analysis method also revealed that the DC value of the DMN brain regions was increased in PACG patients [16]. These results indicate that patients with glaucoma have abnormal DMN function. The DMN is involved in the body’s monitoring of the surrounding environment. We speculate that visual field defects and decreased vision inevitably lead to a decline in the ability of PACG patients to monitor their surrounding environment. Dai et al. found that FC was increased between the primary visual cortex and the DMN in POAG patients [57]. We previously found that the FC between the thalamus and DMN was abnormal in PACG patients [24]. In addition, Chen et al. found that PACG patients exhibited decreased FC between the amygdala and DMN [25]. These results indicate that patients with glaucoma have abnormal functional interactions between the DMN and other brain regions. FC analysis of human fMRI data revealed that the visual cortex region that selectively processes relevant information, is functionally connected to the frontoparietal network. In contrast, the visual cortex region, which processes irrelevant information, is simultaneously coupled with the DMN [61]. In addition, the DMN is a vision-oriented system [62]. Decreased VN function in patients with glaucoma may lead to abnormal DMN function [23, 63]. Several studies have shown that patients with AD have abnormal DMN function [64–68]. This study found that PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction had decreased FC within the DMN. Whether this was related to cognitive dysfunction requires further investigation.

Significantly increased FC between the bilateral hippocampal subregions and CAL, between the right hippocampal CA3 subregion and LING were found in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction. CAL and LING belong to the high-level visual cortex (V2). Dai et al. and our previous study reported that FC between the primary visual cortex (V1) and V2 region was decreased in patients with glaucoma [23, 57]. In contrast, FC between the hippocampus and V1 region was increased. Carvalho et al. found that visual cortex functioning was altered in glaucoma, which could be indicative of cortical plasticity, such local changes may be interpreted as attempts by the visual system to compensate for loss that results from the neural damage [69]. Demaria et al. demonstrated that no significant disruptions in FC were observed at the global brain level, while reduced centrality of LING was associated with impaired functional vision in POAG patients, which suggests the presence of local brain reorganization that has a bearing on functional visual abilities [70]. In this study, the increase of FC between some subregions of the hippocampus and the V2 region may manifest functional compensation. These hippocampal subregions mainly include left CA1, right CA3, right Subc regions, including the storage area for storing information and the output area. We speculate that local neural plasticity in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction may play a compensatory role in the occurrence and development of the disease. It should be noted that, this is just our speculation, further longitudinal studies are needed to verify it.

Additionally, compared to that in HCs, we found that the FC between the right hippocampal CA2 subregion and left IFGoperc, as well as between the right hippocampal CA3 subregion and right PAL, ROL were decreased in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction. Conversely, we observed increased FC between the right DG and ACG. The disconnection between the right CA2 and the left IFGoperc is particularly intriguing. This pathway may serve as a bridge for transferring contextually rich information from the hippocampus to the language and executive control systems of the frontal lobe [71]. Its disruption could directly contribute to the difficulties in verbal fluency, memory retrieval, and complex task execution observed in cognitively impaired patients. The decreased FC of the right CA3 with PAL and ROL suggests a broader disruption beyond pure memory circuits. The CA3-PAL link may be crucial for translating memory-based predictions into motivated behavior [72]. Its weakening could explain the apathy, reduced initiative, and impaired reward-based learning common in cognitive disorders. Meanwhile, the CA3-ROL disconnection might reflect a deficit in integrating internal memory representations with external sensory cues, potentially affecting navigation and environmental interaction. The observed hyperconnectivity between the right DG and the ACC is a critical finding. In the context of widespread network disconnection, this strengthened link might represent a compensatory mechanism. The brain may be attempting to heighten conflict monitoring (ACC) over the fine-grained discrimination of experiences (DG) to cope with increasing cognitive demands and internal confusion [73].

We found that the FC between the right hippocampal Subc subregion and the right PreCG was negatively correlated with the RNFLT. The RNFLT was correlated with the degree of visual impairment in PACG [74], indicating that the decreased FC between the right hippocampal Subc subregion and the right PreCG in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction may help alleviate their visual impairment. In addition, the FC between the left hippocampal CA1 subregion and the right CAL was positively correlated with VA, suggesting that functional compensation between the left hippocampal CA1 and V2 regions may improve VA. However, no significant correlations were found between the FC (between the hippocampal subregions and other brain regions) with global cognitive performance as measured by the MoCA. This dissociation suggests that certain FC alterations may be more closely tied to the core pathophysiology of PACG itself, whereas others might relate to specific cognitive domains not fully captured by a global screening tool. The lack of association with MoCA warrants careful consideration. Firstly, the MoCA, while an excellent and widely used instrument for screening general cognitive impairment, may lack the sensitivity to detect subtle, domain-specific cognitive deficits [43]. It provides a composite score that can mask deficits in specific areas such as visual memory, processing speed, or executive function, which are cognitive domains more likely to be linked to visual pathway and hippocampal dysfunction in PACG. Alternatively, our sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect a modest correlation. To conclusively uncover the neural correlates of cognitive deficits in PACG, future investigations should employ a more comprehensive neuropsychological battery targeting specific domains. Such as the Trail Making Test (Parts A & B) for executive function and processing speed, the Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test for visual memory and constructional ability, and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test for attention and psychomotor speed. Utilizing such domain-specific instruments will enhance the granularity of cognitive assessment and potentially reveal stronger, more meaningful links with the distinct FC profiles observed in PACG.

Furthermore, we found that the FC of hippocampal subregions with other brain regions exhibited a lateralized distribution. For instance, FC was increased only between the left CA1 and right CAL, and was decreased only between the cerebellum and the right CA3. This lateralized FC pattern is an important and ubiquitous feature in neuroscience. It likely evolved as an optimized strategy for the brain to achieve efficient, parallel, and specialized information processing [75–77]. This is exemplified by the scenario where the left hippocampal subregion (CA1) may need to send specific information to the right visual-associated cortex (CAL) for further integration, rather than processing it within the same hemisphere. Such “cross-hemispheric” connectivity may be vital for integrating visual-spatial information from the contralateral visual field.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, we only conducted a simple cognitive function assessment for PACG patients. Clinical studies have shown that patients with PACG not only suffer from cognitive dysfunction but may also exhibit mental symptoms such as depression, anxiety, insomnia, and mental disorders. The MoCA tool we used is a screening tool that only conducted a simple cognitive function assessment on PACG patients. This might lead to incorrect classification, meaning that the cognitive scores we obtained for the patients may not fully represent the severity of their cognitive impairment. This might explain why the FC changes are not related to the cognitive scores. Therefore, our next step is to conduct a detailed neuropsychological assessment on PACG patients. Secondly, this was a cross-sectional study, longitudinal studies are needed to track the progression of these alterations. Thirdly, the duration of the disease and the degree of optic nerve damage may have affected the FC of the hippocampal subregions. Further, in order to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio and reduce the influence of head movement, we regressed out the global signal to obtain the FC patterns, but it is unclear whether global signal regression has complex effects on functional connectivity [78]. Additionally, It should be noted that our analysis across the 14 hippocampal subfield seeds did not include an additional correction for multiple comparisons, which increases the potential for false positives (Type I error). Although this approach was chosen to maximize sensitivity for this exploratory investigation, we recommend that future studies adopt more stringent correction methods to confirm the robustness of these findings. Finally, in the current two-group design, it is not possible to determine whether the FC changes are specific to cognitive dysfunction or simply reflect PACG more broadly. Inclusion of a PACG group without cognitive impairment would be required to resolve this issue, this is the work we need to do in the future.

Conclusions

Extensive FC abnormalities exist between hippocampal subregions and the cerebellum, SMN, DMN, VN, and other brain regions in PACG patients with cognitive dysfunction, which provides a new perspective for understanding the neuropathological mechanisms in these patients and provides potential neuroimaging biomarkers for early diagnosis and intervention.
