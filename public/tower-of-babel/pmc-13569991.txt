Cellular transcriptomic signatures underpinning the heterogeneity of depression in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Michael W. Lutz, Zhaohui Man, Ornit Chiba‐Falek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONLate‐onset Alzheimer's disease (LOAD) and major depressive disorder (MDD) share genetic etiologies. Here, we investigated brain transcriptomic landscapes to gain insights into shared and divergent molecular and biological etiologies across LOAD and MDD.METHODSBrain single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq) datasets from cognitively normal older and young individuals and LOAD patients stratified by comorbid MDD were analyzed to identify differential expressed genes (DEGs). Using cell type–specific DEGs we performed biological pathway and intercellular‐communication networks analyses. We investigated shared DEGs across MDD and LOAD cohorts and sex‐specific DEGs. Results were validated by comparison with four transcriptomic and proteomic studies of MDD and depression.RESULTSMDD‐associated dysregulated genes and pathways were shared between LOAD and cognitive‐normal individuals, including JUNB and DUSP1 in glutamatergic neurons, and PRAM1 and SNX9 in microglia. DEGs shared between the MDD and LOAD cohorts included HSPA1A and NDUFB7 in glutamatergic neurons. Sex interaction analysis identified numerous new DEGs in the MDD cohorts, whereas there were ≈5 to 10 times more DEGs in female than in male individuals. LOAD and MDD common microglial pathways included neuronal injury, stress, peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR) signaling and interferon alpha/beta signaling.DISCUSSIONLOAD and MDD exhibited common molecular profiles, dysregulated pathways, and cellular communication changes. MDD develops earlier in life, thus, our findings provide a window into early molecular and biological processes preceding LOAD‐onset.

BACKGROUND

Late‐onset Alzheimer's Disease (LOAD) is a heterogeneous disease characterized by comorbid clinical symptoms including neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPSs), with depression being highly common. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Furthermore, depression has been found to be associated with an increased risk to develop LOAD. 1 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Patients with major depressive disorder (MDD), especially in late life, exhibit cognitive deficits and have an increased incidence of LOAD. 14 In addition, it was demonstrated that LOAD biomarkers, such as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) amyloid beta (Aβ)42 and CSF total and phosphorylated tau (t‐tau and p‐tau), are associated with depression trajectories in AD. 15 Noteworthy, other studies found higher depressive symptoms in women with a diagnosis of LOAD compared to men. 16 , 17 , 18 Collectively, the heterogenous and temporal interrelationship between LOAD and depression highlight the complex relationship between the onset of depression and cognitive decline, 19 as well as the differential comorbidity risk between sexes, 20 , 21 suggesting shared etiologies and intersecting biological pathways between LOAD and MDD.

Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) identified numerous loci associated with the risk of developing LOAD 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 and MDD, 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 and several studies have suggested a shared genetic architecture between LOAD and MDD. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 In addition, prior genetic studies have also identified LOAD‐MDD common genetic pathways, such as immune system, synaptic signaling and organization, myelination, development, and inflammatory pathways. 34 , 37 However, the genetic architecture underpinning the onset and heterogeneity of depression symptoms in LOAD has been understudied.

Differential gene expression has been reported widely in bulk brain tissues 38 and within different brain cell types 39 from LOAD compared to controls 38 , 39 , 40 ; moreover, using Religious Orders Study and Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP) bulk brain transcriptomic data, we discovered sex‐dependent differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in LOAD that were comorbid with depression compared to LOAD only. 41 Bulk brain tissue studies have also shown dysregulation of gene expression associated with MDD. 34 , 42 , 43 Collectively, this evidence suggests dysregulation of gene expression as a key genetic mechanism underlying LOAD and MDD pathogenesis and heterogeneity.

The current study analyzed existing single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq) datasets using a common single‐cell transcriptomic analytical pipeline (Figure 1) to examine and compare differential expression of genes associated with MDD in young and older individuals and in LOAD patients at a cell‐type and subtype level and with a potential sex interaction. The results identified DEGs associated with comorbid depression in LOAD, in sex‐independent and sex‐dependent analyses, at a cell‐type and subtype level of precision. Further work suggested that key biological pathways were affected in LOAD and MDD and altered intercellular communication.

METHODS

Study design and participants

We performed comparative transcriptomic analyses using two snRNA‐seq datasets: the Religious Orders Study and Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP) dataset comprising 424 subjects, 44 and a previously published MDD dataset. 45 For the ROSMAP cohort, the brain samples were generated from dorsolateral pre‐frontal cortex (DLFPC) tissue. Data were downloaded from the The Accelerating Medicines Partnership ‐ Alzheimer’s Disease (AMP‐AD) knowledge portal (Synapse ID: syn23650894). Details on sample preparation, sequencing, and processing have been published. 46 , 47 For the ROSMAP dataset, a clinical diagnosis of MDD was determined by an examining physician at each evaluation. The diagnosis is based on the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Third Edition, Revised (DSM‐III‐R), clinical interview with the participant, and review of responses to a series of questions adapted from the Diagnostic Interview Schedule. For this study, we considered diagnoses of highly probable, probable, and possible as indicative of MDD, in contrast to a diagnosis of not present.

The Maitra dataset for young individuals with and without MDD, also was generated from DLFPC. The dataset was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, accession number: GSE213982). Details on sample preparation, sequencing, and processing are in Maitra et al. 45 Highly probable or probable MDD cases were defined based on a clinical diagnosis by the examining physician according to criteria of the DSM‐III‐R, clinical interview with the participant, and review of responses to a series of questions adapted from the Diagnostic Interview Schedule.

Single‐cell RNA sequencing data processing and analysis

Cell‐type annotations were standardized using the scMAYOmap reference. 48 For both datasets, we employed a systematic differential expression analysis pipeline to identify cell type–specific transcriptional changes.

For the LOAD analysis, we utilized the ROSMAP cohort data, categorizing subjects as either LOAD (cognitive diagnosis scores of 4 or 5) or Normal (cognitive diagnosis score of 1). 49 To ensure data quality, we filtered out potential doublets by removing cells with DoubletFinder scores above 0.75. 50 We included demographic variables (sex, age), and postmortem interval (PMI) as covariates in our analysis.

For the MDD analysis, we processed the dataset from Maitra et al., 45 examining cell type–specific transcriptomic differences in depression. Cases and controls were analyzed following the original study's classification criteria, with sex included as a covariate in the statistical model.

Differential expression analysis

For both datasets, we implemented a consistent analytical approach using the NEBULA (NEgative Binomial mixed model Using LongitudinAl data) framework, 51 which accounts for the hierarchical structure of single‐cell data. Prior to differential expression testing, we applied quality control filters including removal of genes expressed in less than 10% of cells within each comparison group. 52 This filtering step was done separately by cell type and comparison group; for example, for the samples from individuals with MDD and for the samples from individuals without MDD. Genes with non‐zero counts were considered to be expressed.

The statistical model for LOAD and older cohorts MDD analyses included fixed effects for diagnosis, sex, age, and post‐mortem interval (PMI), whereas the young cohort MDD analysis incorporated diagnosis and sex as fixed effects; age and PMI were not available for the this data. Both models included subject‐level random effects to account for within‐subject correlation. A two‐stage process was used to select genes for comparative analysis. First, the DEG results were filtered to include only genes with an absolute value log2 fold change (FC) > 0.2; this was followed by filtering for false discovery rates (FDR) < 0.1. To account for multiple testing, p‐values were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure, these are annotated as p FDR.

Comparative analysis and cell–cell communication analysis

To identify shared transcriptional signatures between LOAD and MDD, we performed cell type–specific overlap analyses of DEGs. We separately analyzed upregulated and downregulated genes for each cell type annotated by scMAYOmap. 48 The intersection of DEGs identified for presence/absence of MDD for each of the three cohorts (ROSMAP older, cognitively normal; ROSMAP LOAD; and Maitra young) was determined for each cell type, and the results were visualized using Venn diagrams. For the ROSMAP data, we also identified LOAD DEGs (LOAD patients vs age‐matched controls without LOAD) to compare with the MDD results, examining genetic pleiotropy between the three cohorts and between LOAD and MDD. Common DEGs were then categorized by their direction of regulation (up or down) and subjected to pathway enrichment analysis using Metascape, 53 which integrated multiple ontology sources including Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Processes, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Pathways, and Reactome Gene Sets. The DEG analysis was also completed for sex‐stratified samples for all cohorts.

To investigate cell–cell communication patterns, we employed CellChat, 54 a tool for the systematic analysis of cell–cell communication at the molecular level. For both conditions, we analyzed the disease and control groups separately using the raw gene expression data. The CellChat analysis was also performed for each sex separately in addition to the full cohort.

For the LOAD analysis, due to the extensive size of the ROSMAP dataset exceeding computational matrix size limitations (greater than 2,147,483,647 elements), we implemented balanced sampling of cells according to the CellChat framework, setting a maximum of 5000 cells per cell type. 54 This approach not only addressed the computational constraints but also maintained representative cell type proportions for accurate signal inference. Balanced sampling is critical for cell–cell communication analysis to ensure reliable estimation of interaction strengths and to avoid biases introduced by overrepresented or underrepresented cell types. 55 Studies have shown that cell‐type imbalances in scRNA‐seq datasets can skew interaction networks and lead to erroneous predictions of signaling pathways. 56 This subsampling strategy is widely recognized as an effective method for enhancing the robustness of interaction inference while minimizing computational overhead. 57

We calculated differential interaction weights between disease and control conditions for each dataset separately, with weight differences computed as Disease − Control. Positive values indicated gained interactions in the disease state, whereas negative values represented lost interactions. Overlapping cell–cell communication patterns between LOAD and MDD were then identified, focusing on interactions that exhibited consistent directionality across both conditions.

Network visualization and differential interaction analyses were performed using the CellChat visualization toolkit. 54 Interaction weights were scaled to facilitate comparisons between conditions and datasets. This approach enabled the identification of conserved and unique signaling patterns in LOAD and MDD, advancing our understanding of their shared molecular mechanisms.

Biological pathway analysis

To further understand the biological significance of the gene sets derived from differential expression analyses, we used the Metascape program (Metascape.org). 53 For each cell type, we extracted upregulated genes (log2FC > 0.2, p FDR < 0.1) and downregulated genes (log2FC < –0.2, p FDR < 0.1) and used each as input target gene lists. All human genes were used for the background gene list. The minimum overlap of genes was 3, and a pFDR ‐value cutoff of 0.01 and minimum enrichment of 1.5 were used. For pathway analysis, 2026 versions of the GO Biological Processes, Reactome, KEGG, and WikiPathways databases were used.

Validation

We validated the results of this study by comparison to previous studies that examined MDD and depression endpoints. Four studies were used for validation. (1) A snRNA‐seq study using the same ROSMAP data as our study to examine the association with depressive symptoms. 46 This study integrated GWAS results for depression with transcriptome results followed by Mendelian randomization. Using this study for validation allowed investigation of the depressive phenotype along with MDD results from GWAS. (2) A study of large‐scale transcriptomic analysis of MDD. 42 This study was an independent dataset with a similar phenotype for MDD, the limitation was that the brain regions studied were amygdala and subgenual anterior cingulate cortex, not dorsolateral frontal cortex; amygdala would be most proximal. (3) A study of transcriptomic organization of the human brain in post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). 43 This study was for the pre‐frontal cortex and relevant for validation of our results, reported on an MDD sample of 45 MDD and 46 healthy controls. (4) A study of protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) and integration with GWASs of 21 neurological and psychiatric conditions. 58 This study was included for validation, since it mapped causal loci based on pQTLs for MDD.

MDD‐associated cell‐subtype differential gene expression within cognitively normal and LOAD cohorts

We investigated the differential gene expression associated with MDD within three cohorts, each consisting of individuals with compared to without MDD: (1) older cognitively normal individuals, (2) younger cognitively normal, and (3) LOAD (Table 1). First, we studied the changes in gene expression associated with MDD independent of cognitive symptoms. Toward this aim, we analyzed the dorsolateral frontal cortex from the old and young cohorts of cognitively normal individuals with compared to without MDD. In the older, cognitively normal cohort, we found the largest number of DEGs in the γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic and glutamatergic neurons (N = 230 and N = 191 DEGs, respectively) followed by the microglia (N = 100, Figure 2A). The younger cohort showed ≈10‐fold more DEGs in individuals with versus without MDD (Figure 2B), in contrast to the older cohorts across the major subtypes (Figure 2B,C). As in the older cohort, the largest number of DEGs were observed for GABAergic and glutamatergic neurons (Figure 2B).

Distributions of number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the dorsolateral frontal cortex by cell type for (A) older, cognitively normal individuals with and without major depressive disorder (MDD), (B) individuals with late‐onset Alzheimer's disease (LOAD) and with/without comorbid MDD, (C) younger individuals with and without MDD, and (D) LOAD versus cognitively unimpaired age‐matched controls.

We next analyzed individuals with LOAD and compared changes in gene expression in those with versus without comorbid MDD (Figure 2C). Although the number of GABAergic DEGs was similar (N ≈200), we found fewer DEGs for glutamatergic neurons and approximately the same amount in the microglia (N = 50, Figure 2C). There were relatively more DEGs within the LOAD cohort versus the cognitively healthy cohort (Figure 2D), with the largest number of DEGs within the glutamatergic (396) and GABAergic (337) neuronal clusters.

MDD‐associated transcriptomic overlap in glutamatergic neurons across the cohorts

Examination of the DEGs identified in glutamatergic neurons in the analyses of the older cognitively normal and LOAD cohorts demonstrated 14 common DEGs (Figure 3A). However, the directions of the MDD‐associated DEGs were opposite between the two cohorts. Comparison of the glutamatergic neurons DEGs between the young and LOAD cohort showed seven common DEGs, of which six showed same direction of the expression effects (Figure 3A and Table 2). There were 15 DEGs common between the young and older cognitively normal cohorts (Figure 3A), all with direction of the MDD‐associated DEGs being opposite. Two common MDD‐associated DEGs—DUSP1 and JUNB—were identified in the glutamatergic neurons for all three cohorts (Table 2). The same direction of effect was seen for the DEGs for the young and LOAD cohorts, whereas an opposite direction was observed for the DEGs for the older, cognitively normal group. DUSP1 is a gene that phosphorylates the MAP kinase MAPK1/ERK2, and plays an important role in the human cellular response to environmental stress as well as in the negative regulation of cellular proliferation. JUNB is activated in response to various stimuli like stress, inflammation, and neuronal injury. Numerous studies have reported an association between inflammation and depression. 59 , 60 , 61

MDD‐associated transcriptomic overlap in GABAergic neurons across the cohorts

Overlap analysis for the GABAergic neurons identified 6 common DEGs between the older cognitively normal and LOAD cohorts (Figure 3B and Table 3), 14 common DEGs between the young and LOAD cohorts, and 29 common DEGs between the young and older cohorts (Figure 3B). Notably, GABAR6 was identified as a DEG, but with opposite effect direction, in both older and LOAD cohorts, whereas GABABP1 was a common DEG with the same direction between the younger and LOAD cohorts. MDD was associated with a significant decrease in GABRA6 messenger RNA (mRNA) in GABAergic neurons in older cognitively normal individuals. This is consistent with the GABAergic deficit hypothesis of MDD. 62 , 63 , 64 Similarly, we found a relatively large number of DEGs in interneurons associated with MDD in young individuals, which is consistent with impaired function of GABA interneurons in MDD. 62 On the other end, in the LOAD cohort, MDD was associated with an increase in GABARA6 and GABABP1‐AS1 in GABAergic neurons. The opposite effect on the GABA subunit genes may indicate that specific isoforms of the GABAB receptor differentially regulate stress resilience in the context of depression on the background of cognitive decline compared to depression with no other preexisting conditions. 65

MDD‐associated transcriptomic overlap in microglia across the cohorts

Microglia are known to play key roles in the development of neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative diseases. 66 , 67 The young and the older cohorts showed 13 shared microglial DEGs (Figure 3C and Table 4), whereas there were 2 common DEGs (RBMS3 and UHRF1BP1L) between the LOAD and older cohorts and no DEGs shared between the young and AD cohorts (Figure 3C). The common DEGs for the young and older cohort included transcription factors, transporters, and scaffold/adapter proteins including PRAM1 with roles in myeloid differentiation and lipid binding, and SNX9, which is involved in endocytosis and intracellular vesicle trafficking. The two common DEGs for the LOAD and older cohort are involved in apoptosis, cell‐cycle regulation, and RNA metabolism (RBMS3) and lipid transfer (UHRF1BP1L).

Sex‐specific DEGs are associated with MDD across all cohorts

The sex‐stratified differential expression analysis identified multiple MDD‐associated sex‐dependent DEGs in each of the three cohorts (Table S1). There were far more MDD‐associate sex‐specific DEGs identified in each of the cohorts than DEGs shared between the sexes. Of the sex‐dependent DEGs in each cohort we found substantially more DEGs in females than males. Specifically, in the older, cognitively normal and LOAD cohorts the number of DEGs were ≈10 times greater and in the young cohort 5 times greater in female than male patients (Table S2).

In the older, cognitively normal cohort, only two genes were identified as overlapping between the sexes, CALCRL and IFIT3, both in glutamatergic neurons. Similarly, in the LOAD cohort, only two genes were identified as overlapping between the sexes, DBF4B in GABAergic neurons and SLC25A1 in interneurons.

Shared DEGs across MDD and LOAD cohorts

We identified 1914 DEGs for ROSMAP LOAD cases versus age‐matched controls (Figure 2D and Table S3). Overall, 321 DEGs were common between the LOAD case/control and MDD case/control cohorts. There were 264 LOAD DEGs that were common with the young cohort, 50 DEGs for the LOAD and cognitively normal older cohort, 5 for the cognitively normal young and older cohorts, and 7 DEGs shared between all three cohorts (Figure S1A); DEGs shared across the cohorts by cell subtype are shown for glutamatergic neurons (Figure S1B), GABAergic neurons (Figure S1C), and microglia (Figure S1D). Table S4 indicates the number of shared DEGs stratified by cell subtype between the cohorts within our study samples. For the LOAD case/control and cognitively normal older MDD case/control cohorts, among the shared DEGs, we identified RBMS3 in microglia (p FDR = 0.079) and ZBT88A in GABAergic neurons (p FDR = 0.0082). DEGs shared between the LOAD case/control cohort, the young MDD cohort and the older cognitively normal MDD cohort included HSPA1A (p FDR = 0.017) and NDUFB7 (p FDR = 0.017) in glutamatergic neurons. ((DUSP2 is a member of the Mitogen‐Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) phosphatase family like DUSP1 and was found as a significant DEG in the older MDD cohort (p FDR = 0.064) and in the LOAD cohort (p FDR = 0.0065). A summary of the complete comparative results was provided (Table S5). Relatively few overlapping (n = 321) DEGs were noted in this analysis relative to the larger number of LOAD case/control DEGs (n = 1914); it is possible that age may be a substantial contributing factor for the commonality in contrast to a biological commonality between LOAD and MDD.

Pathway analysis (Metascape) of DEGs common to LOAD/age‐matched controls and older MDD cases showed several pathways with neurobiological significance including trans‐synaptic signaling, solute carrier (SLC)‐mediated transmembrane stimulus, and MAPK signaling (Figure S2).

Biological pathway analysis

We sought to gain insights into the biological pathways involved in the risk for LOAD comorbid with MDD compared to LOAD only. In the glutamatergic neurons, pathway analysis using the MDD‐associated DEGs showed downregulation of the AP1 (activator protein 1) pathway that intersects with the MAPK pathway (Figure S3). Components of this pathway are transcription factors that regulate gene expression in response to stimuli including stress and cytokines. AP1 is involved in regulation of several biological and cellular processes relevant to LOAD and MDD including apoptosis and inflammation. In addition, the analysis identified two upregulated pathways broadly related to epigenetic regulation of gene expression and chromatin remodeling (Figure S3).

In the microglia, the analysis indicated enrichment for several pathways implicated in both LOAD and MDD pathophysiology including the PPAR signaling pathway, interferon alpha/beta signaling with an impact on neuroinflammation, and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFA/VEGF2) signaling (Figure S4).

The effect of MDD on alteration in cell–cell communication

Finally, we analyzed the effects of MDD‐associated gene dysregulation on the number and the strength of cell–cell communication (CCC) networks. Notably, the analyses suggested inverse effects of MDD diagnosis on the number versus the strength of the CCC networks across all three cohorts: cognitively normal old, young, and LOAD. The analysis of the older cognitively normal cohort revealed an overall significant increase in the number of CCC networks (Figure 4A) but with weaker interactions (Figure S5A) across all cell types in the MDD group compared to controls. In contrast, the young cohort exhibited an overall significant decrease in the number of CCC networks across all cell types (Figure 4B), except for pyramidal neurons, which showed significantly more CCC interactions with other neuronal subtypes including glutamatergic, GABAergic, interneurons, and chandelier neurons (Figure 4B). However, most interactions were significantly stronger, as opposed to the MDD effect in the older cohort (Figure S5B). The LOAD cohort showed even distributions of MDD decreased and increased numbers and strength of CCC networks (Figure 4C), whereas the most significant changes were observed for the glutamatergic neurons (Figures 4C and S5C). Specifically, the glutamatergic neurons showed more CCC networks with oligodendrocytes in LOAD comorbid depression compared to LOAD only (Figure 4C). However, the interactions across glutamatergic, GABAergic, and interneurons were significantly weaker (Figure S5C). Maitra et al. noted microglial–neuronal interactions based on CCC, notably a probable increase in SPP1 to integrin communication and decrease in GAS6‐MERTK communication from microglia to parvalbumin interneurons. 45 We also identified microglial to neuronal interactions based on the CCC analysis; one example is the interaction between NRXN1 and CLSTN2, observed between microglia and glutamatergic neurons in the young MDD cohort.

Sex‐specific changes in intercellular networks

The MDD‐associated CCC analysis was repeated for each of the three cohorts stratified by sex. Differences between the sexes were observed in the number and the strength of interactions for each cohort.

In female patients from the older, cognitively normal cohort, we observed more interactions between interneurons and both glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons and between interneurons and oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) (Figure S6A), in contrast to males patients (Figure S6D). Sex‐specific differences were also identified in the level of autocrine signaling (the cell subtype secretes ligands that bind to its own receptors); female patients showed relatively more autocrine interactions in interneurons and fewer interactions in glutamatergic neurons (Figure S6A), whereas male patients showed the opposite effect (Figure S6D). Differences in strength of CCC were more subtle, with relatively stronger interactions between glutamatergic neurons and interneurons and between GABAergic neurons and interneurons in female (Figure S7A) compared to male patients (Figure S7D).

In the young cohort, we detected substantial differences in CCC between the sexes. Overall the analysis showed fewer interactions between the cell types in female (Figure S6B) compared with male patients (Figure S6E), as well as more interactions between glutamatergic neurons and pyramidal cells in male (Figure S6E) compared to female patients (Figure S6B). There were fewer interactions between glutamatergic neurons and interneurons and between glutamateric neurons and astrocytes in male (Figure S6E) compared to female patients (Figure S6B). Furthermore, there were relatively stronger interactions between glutamatergic neurons and interneurons, glutamatergic neurons and GABAergic neurons, and a stronger autocrine signal for glutamatergic neurons in female (Figure S7B) compared with male patients (Figure S7E).

Analysis of the LOAD cohort revealed more interactions between glutamatergic neurons and interneurons, between GABAergic neurons and colecystokinin‐expressing (CCK) basket cells, between glutamatergic neurons and GABAergic neurons, and between interneurons and OPCs in female (Figure S6C) compared to male patients (Figure S6F). Notably, there were considerably stronger interactions between interneurons and glutamatergic neurons, considerably stronger autocrine reactions for glutamatergic neurons, and overall stronger interactions between cell types in the female group (Figure S7C) compared with the male group (Figure S7F).

Validation results

ROSMAP snRNA‐seq depressive symptoms study

Zeng et al. 46 integrated eQTL results from a snRNA‐seq database with results from a depression GWAS to perform a transcriptome‐wide association study (TWAS) of depression using the FUSION pipeline. Two genes from our MDD‐associated analysis were replicated in >1 cohort in the TWAS analysis for depressive symptoms: INSYN1 in GABAergic neurons (p FDR = 0.00015), which was significant in the LOAD and young cohorts, and CTNNA3 in microglia (p FDR = 0.00064) for the young and older cohorts. Overall, 52 genes that were significant in the TWAS (p FDR < 0.05) mapped to significant results in our dataset: 51 for the young cohort, 4 for the older cohort, and 2 for the AD cohort. Results for all comparisons are provided in Table S6.

For the TWAS analysis for MDD, 164 significant DEGs in the TWAS (p FDR < 0.05) mapped to our results: 157 for the young cohort, 8 for the older cohort, and none for the LOAD cohort (Table S7). In the older cohort, TWAS‐significant DEGs in glutamatergic neurons included PTPMT1 (p FDR = 0.00034) and ADAM12 (p FDR = 0.00053); and for GABAergic neurons, CUL9 (p FDR = 0.00016), MTERF1 (p FDR = 0.00066), and PANTR1 (p FDR = 0.000077).

Large‐scale transcriptomic analysis study of MDD

We compared our single‐cell DEG results to the bulk amygdala (the closest region to the dorsolateral pre‐frontal cortex) DEGs reported by Goes et al. 42 (Table S8). Two MDD‐associated DEGs in our dataset (all cohorts)—DUSP1 (p = 0.021, p FDR = 0.31) and JUNB (p = 0.021, p FDR = 0.31)—were identified in this study as well. Although these genes were nominally but not FDR significant, it is notable that they were downregulated in the young and LOAD cohorts, in addition as reported in Goes et al., 42 and upregulated in the older cohort.

Study of transcriptomic organization of the human brain in PTSD

The validation data from this study comprised the DEGs identified in samples from dorsolateral pre‐frontal cortex for 45 individuals with MDD and 46 healthy controls. Although the primary focus of this study was on PTSD, MDD cases were included as a comparator and DEG results were reported. For the dorsolateral cortex DEGs, for MDD in contrast to controls, four genes that were significant in at least two cohorts in our study had DEGs that were FDR significant at a level of 0.1: JUN in glutamatergic neurons (p FDR = 0.058), TNS1 in Bergmann glial cells (p FDR = 0.094), IRAK2 in microglia (p FDR = 0.065), and CSGALNACT1 in GABAergic neurons (p FDR = 0.091). For the subgenual prefrontal cortex, 43 genes were FDR significant for MDD in the validation cohort that were also significant for at least two cohorts in our study for MDD versus no MDD. Notably, DUSP1 was significant in all three cohorts in our study and significant in the replication cohort (p FDR = 0.050). For the older and LOAD cohorts, six genes were FDR significant including PTGS2 (p FDR = 0.01) in glutamatergic neurons and UHRF1BP1L (p FDR = 0.03) in microglia. Results for all comparisons are provided in Table S9.

Study of pQTL and integration with GWAS of 21 neurological and psychiatric conditions

This study was included as a validation cohort, as it presents results for MDD as one of the psychiatric conditions and identifies specific causal factors based on protein quantitative trait loci (or pQTL). Validated DEGs are summarized in Table S10. NCKIPSD was identified as a MDD‐associated DEG in interneurons from our LOAD and young cohorts. Consistently, this gene was reported as causal in the pQTL data (PWAS p FDR = 5.5·10−8). 58 The NTNG1 gene was identified in our study as a MDD‐DEG in the young and older cohorts in interneurons and was reported as causal in the validation study (PWAS p FDR = 5.3·10−5). 58

DISCUSSION

Previously we elucidated the shared genetic architecture between MDD and LOAD, 34 specifically, Mendelian randomization analysis showed a moderate influence of LOAD‐associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) on risk of MDD. Moreover, we reported common biological pathways, such as immune and inflammatory pathways, between MDD and LOAD. 34 A recent study of >400,000 individuals examined the shared genetic and biological mechanisms across psychiatric and neurodegenerative diseases and reported a significant correlation (p < 0.001) between LOAD and MDD based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) score regression. 68 In another study, we constructed a polygenic risk score (PRS) that predicted depression onset within LOAD patients, resulting in moderate predictability. 69 Furthermore, recently, using ROSMAP bulk brain transcriptomic data, we discovered sex‐dependent DEGs in LOAD that were comorbid with depression compared to LOAD only. 41 Altogether, our results suggested that genetic variability and transcriptomic alterations may underlie the risk and heterogeneity of comorbid depression symptoms in LOAD. Here we leveraged on these findings by interrogating the MDD‐associated DEGs at a single‐cell resolution in the brains of three cohorts including young and old cognitively normal subjects and LOAD patients. First, the outcomes pinpointed the cell subtypes driving the genetic and molecular pathogenic effects. Second, our strategy crossed a wide spectrum of ages from early middle age to late life, thus providing a window into the earliest molecular changes related to the development of LOAD later in life. The current study investigated differential gene expression associated with MDD across ages and in the context of Alzheimer's. In addition, comparative analysis of MDD‐associated DEGs across all the cohorts identified only few overlapping DEGs.

The analyses of the neuronal subtypes highlighted several biologically relevant genes. Genes involved in biological processes related to LOAD and MDD included ARC and CCN1. ARC is a master regulator of synaptic plasticity and CCN1 promotes cell proliferation, chemotaxis, angiogenesis, and cell adhesion. In addition, JUN and SERPINE2 were both MDD‐associated downregulated DEGs in both the young and LOAD cohorts with a similar magnitude fold‐change. SERPINE2 is a serine protease inhibitor with activity toward thrombin, trypsin, and urokinase that promotes neurite extension by inhibiting thrombin, and JUN is activated in response to stress.

Our results for the microglia also highlighted several pathways and genes involved in LOAD and MDD pathophysiology including neuronal injury, stress, the PPAR signaling pathway, interferon alpha/beta signaling with an impact on neuroinflammation, and VEGFA/VEGF2 signaling.

We and others reported epidemiological and molecular sex differences in LOAD. 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 In addition, MDD snRNA‐seq studies identified sex‐specific differences including a recent study that observed transcriptomic changes in different cell types in males versus females. 45 Recently, using bulk brain RNA‐seq data we studied sex‐dependent differences associated with comorbid MDD in LOAD, and identified 25 DEGs in males and only 3 DEGs in females. 41 Here we performed the analysis at a deeper resolution, at a single‐cell granularity. The distributions of DEGs by cell subtype were comparable to those of the combined‐sex analysis. However, there were very few common DEGs between the sexes for the two older cohorts. The MDD‐associated sex‐dependent DEGs should be interpreted with caution. More than 70% within the older cohorts were female, thus, potentially explaining the smaller effect sizes observed with male DEGs. In contrast to the older cohorts, the sex distribution was more balanced for the young cohort and yielded similar size effects across sexes. Thus, further sex‐stratified MDD‐association studies with larger sample sizes are warranted. IL1RAPL1, a gene that is involved in building synapses, was the only sex‐common microglial DEG identified in the young cohort. Of interest, a recent sex‐stratified MDD GWAS identified a SNP in IL1RAPL1 locus as significantly MDD‐associated in males. 75

The transcriptomic datasets from independent cohorts analyzed by the studies of Goes 42 and Girgenti 43 support MDD associations for several DEGs including DUSP1, JUN, and JUNB. NCKIPSD and NTNG1 were also identified in our study as interneuronal DEGs and glutamatergic neurons (only NCKIPSD). NTNG1 (Netrin G1) is a secreted guidance protein that directs axon growth and is critical for synapse formation and maintenance in glutamatergic synapse. Consistent with our findings, a pQTL study suggested a causal role for its protein in MDD. 58 Other studies demonstrated the broader involvement of NTNG1 in psychiatric diseases including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and MDD. 76 NTNG1 was also studied in the context of LOAD, specifically lower expression levels were reported in hippocampus of LOAD cases. 77 NCKIPSD encodes a protein involved in the building and maintenance of dendritic spines and modulates neuronal synaptic activity. Supportive of our results, a meta‐analysis GWAS reported the association of NCKIPSD with cognitive traits, body mass index (BMI), and various mental health traits including irritability and bipolar disorder. 78

Here we also explored the interaction between the different cell types in the context of MDD within the three studied cohorts. Prior studies have shown that the communications between excitatory and inhibitory neurons in LOAD were significantly increased, whereas the interactions between inhibitory neurons and all other non‐neuronal cell subtypes were globally decreased. 79 An MDD analysis of DLPFC snRNA‐seq data predicted alterations in lactate, which was corroborated by a sub‐study that showed decreased serum lactate levels in MDD patients compared to age‐matched healthy controls. 80 These results provided supportive evidence for abnormal communications between astrocytes and neurons in MDD. 80 In addition, more interacting ligand–receptor pairs and an overall increased communication strength were reported within and between microglia and parvalbumin (PV) interneurons in depression cases compared to controls. 45 Our study indicated the effect of MDD primarily on increased communications within glutamatergic neurons followed by interneurons and GABAergic neurons and across these neuronal cell types, whereas the effect was overall stronger in the LOAD cohort compared to age‐matched cognitively normal cohort. These results were in concordance with prior CCC network analyses in LOAD 79 and MDD, 80 the communications between glutamatergic (excitatory) and GABAergic (inhibitory) neurons and pyramidal cells and microglia. However, the opposite directions, weaker CCC in the older cohort versus stronger in the younger samples was not described previously. Of interest, there were inverse relationships for the number and strength of MDD‐associated CCC interactions for the younger and older (both cognitively normal and LOAD) cohorts.

The biochemical mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative and psychiatric diseases, such as LOAD and MDD, are complex and involve polygenic risk and environmental factors. Recently, an Integration of multimodal data types (i.e., GWAS, transcriptomic, single cell) across major psychiatric and neurodegenerative diseases, including MDD and LOAD, identified synaptic transmission, particularly the soluble N‐ethylmaleimide‐sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complex and soluble NSF attachment protein (SNAP) receptor, as a major shared mechanism among neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative diseases. 68 In addition, the authors showed that immune function and mitochondrial processes were common in the pathogenesis across these groups of brain diseases. 68 These results aligned with prior research, 19 , 81 as well as two additional studies investigating shared genetic etiologies between LOAD and PTSD, 82 and between LOAD and MDD. 34 Collectively, these studies indicate the existence of shared genetic underpinning and biological mechanisms between major psychiatric disorders and LOAD. Our study leveraged on this knowledge by providing comprehensive comparisons of cell‐type specific transcriptomic landscapes, biological pathways, and cellular communication networks across three case–control MDD cohorts.

Several limitations are noted. We used a clinical diagnosis of MDD as the phenotype; however, co‐morbidities with other neuropsychiatric symptoms including anxiety and apathy were often present and were potential confounders. The two cohorts used in the study represented a wide range of ages which allowed identification of common and distinct DEGs for MDD for the wider age range, however, there was also the potential for confounding of age with LOAD for the comparisons of the LOAD and young cohort data; the inclusion of the older, cognitively healthy group allowed a clearer consideration of age. Some overlap in DEGs across cell subtypes in MDD may be due to chance. An FDR threshold of 0.1 was used because DEGs were identified through overlap with independent cohorts and prioritizing moderate‐to‐large effect sizes. Few shared DEGs were found in older cohorts, whereas the young cohort showed a balance of sex‐specific and overlapping DEGs, consistent with Matira et al. 45 Greater confidence should be given to DEGs showing consistent effects across the four independent validation studies. Other study limitations include the use of postmortem brain tissue, potential confounding from medication use, substance abuse, acute stress, and comorbid pathologies such as cerebrovascular disease. RNA degradation and technical differences across cohorts may also affect results, although these were addressed through PMI adjustment, a unified preprocessing pipeline, and standardized cell‐type definitions based on scMayomap. 48 Finally, future studies focusing on depressive symptoms, multiple neuropsychiatric symptoms, and sex‐specific effects would further extend the knowledge in the field.

In conclusion, we identified transcriptomic profiles associated with MDD in young and older cognitively normal individuals as well as LOAD cases, across sexes and within each sex. The data showed shared and distinct genes, biological pathways, and altered cell–cell interactions involved in the etiologies of MDD and LOAD comorbid depression symptoms. The data provided a window into molecular events occurring decades prior to typical dementia onset and predisposing depression comorbidity. The shared transcriptomic outcomes will have important ramifications for future development of early treatment of depression symptoms comorbid with LOAD and for MDD.
