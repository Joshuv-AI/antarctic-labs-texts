Association between dietary inflammatory index and depression in individuals with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stage 0–3: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ying Li, Lixin Ye, Jingjing Yao, Wanru Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a newly recognized condition characterized by systemic inflammation, a process also implicated in depression. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) quantifies the inflammatory potential of diet, yet its association with depression among individuals with CKM syndrome remains underexplored.MethodsUtilizing data from 11,847 adults with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 enrolled in seven NHANES cycles (2005–2018), we examined the relationship between DII and depression. The DII was computed using 24-hour dietary recall data. Weighted logistic regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were applied to evaluate linear and nonlinear associations. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were also conducted to assess consistency and robustness.ResultsElevated DII values were consistently associated with a greater likelihood of depression across all models. Participants in the highest DII quartile (Q4) exhibited significantly higher odds of depression than those in the lowest quartile (Q1). RCS models revealed a positive, nonlinear dose–response relationship. Additionally, a significant interaction was observed between DII and diabetes mellitus status.ConclusionA pro-inflammatory dietary profile, reflected by higher DII scores, is positively associated with depression among individuals with CKM stages 0–3. These findings highlight the potential benefits of adopting anti-inflammatory dietary interventions to mitigate depression risk in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02171-7.

Introduction

Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome has recently emerged as an integrative concept encompassing the interconnected effects of obesity, metabolic dysfunction, and chronic kidney disease on cardiovascular health [1]. This framework recognizes that these disorders share overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms and act synergistically to elevate morbidity and mortality beyond the risk conferred by any single component [2]. The American Heart Association (AHA) defines CKM syndrome as a progressive continuum comprising five stages [3]. Notably, CKM stages 0–3 (CKMS03) encompass the majority of affected individuals—representing over 90% of the CKM population between 2011 and 2020—making these early stages an essential focus for prevention-oriented research and intervention [4]. Dietary behavior profoundly influences the onset and progression of CKM syndrome, primarily through modulation of chronic, low-grade inflammation. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII), a validated tool developed to evaluate the inflammatory potential of diet, provides an integrative measure of how dietary patterns affect systemic inflammation [5]. Elevated DII scores indicate a more pro-inflammatory diet, which has been linked to metabolic dysregulation, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular risk [6]. Growing evidence suggests that inflammation serves as a critical biological bridge between metabolic disorders and mental health conditions such as depression [7]. Depression is highly prevalent among individuals with metabolic and renal dysfunction and is independently associated with worse physical outcomes and increased mortality [8]. The DII thus provides a valuable lens through which to examine the dietary contribution to inflammation-related depression risk within the CKM context. Despite the growing recognition of inflammation’s role in both CKM and mental health, little is known about how diet-derived inflammatory potential influences depression among those in the early CKM stages. Prior research has largely focused on isolated disease states, leaving the integrated CKM framework underexplored. Because CKMS03 represents a window for lifestyle modification before the development of severe organ damage, clarifying this relationship is relevant from both clinical and public health perspectives. Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the association between DII and depression in individuals with CKMS03 using nationally representative NHANES data. We hypothesized that higher DII values would be positively associated with the presence of depression and that this relationship might exhibit a nonlinear pattern. Our findings are expected to inform dietary strategies for improving both physical and psychological outcomes among individuals with early-stage CKM syndrome.

Methods

Study design and data source

Data were derived from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), an ongoing program designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population through interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory testing. The survey uses a multistage, stratified probability sampling approach to ensure national representativeness across age, sex and ethnicity. All participants provided written informed consent, and the NHANES protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

For this cross-sectional analysis, we integrated data from seven NHANES cycles (2005–2018). After applying exclusion criteria (detailed in Fig. 1), 11,847 participants aged 20–85 years with CKMS03 were included in the final analytic sample. Because the dataset comprises publicly available, de-identified information, this secondary analysis was deemed exempt from further ethical review.

Definition of CKM stages

CKM syndrome is a multidimensional disorder involving interactions between cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic pathways. Following AHA criteria [9], CKM is categorized into five progressive stages. This study concentrated on stages 0–3, representing preclinical or subclinical phases:

Stage 0: Individuals without any CKM-related abnormalities.

Stage 1: Presence of adiposity or early metabolic impairment (e.g., overweight, obesity, or insulin resistance).

Stage 2: Established metabolic risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, or mild kidney disease.

Stage 3: Subclinical cardiovascular disease or equivalent high CVD risk.

This staging approach enables early identification of at-risk individuals prior to overt clinical manifestations.

Assessment of depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [10], a validated screening instrument that evaluates depressive symptom frequency over the prior two weeks. Total scores range from 0 to 27, with higher values reflecting greater symptom severity. A cutoff score of ≥ 10 was employed to identify participants with clinically significant depression, a threshold demonstrating high sensitivity and specificity for major depressive disorder [11, 12].

Assessment of DII

The DII is a literature-derived index developed to quantify the inflammatory potential of dietary intake based on specific nutrients, foods, and bioactive components associated with inflammatory biomarkers [4]. In this study, DII scores were calculated using 24-hour dietary recall data available in NHANES. Twenty-seven nutrient and food components, consistent with prior DII validation studies [13], were incorporated into the computation. Higher DII values indicate a more pro-inflammatory dietary profile, while lower (negative) scores denote anti-inflammatory diets.

Covariates

Participant demographic and clinical data were retrieved from the NHANES database. The dataset included information on age, measured in years, and sex, categorized as male or female. The educational attainment of the population was categorized into three levels: less than high school, high school graduate or equivalent, and more than high school education. Race/ethnicity was categorized as White, Mexican American, Black, and Other. Marital status was categorized into three groups: never married (Never), married or living with a partner (ML), and divorced, widowed, or separated (DWS). Lifestyle-related factors were also collected. The study’s participants were stratified according to their smoking history, which was categorized as follows: never smokers, former smokers, and current smokers. The classification of alcohol consumption followed a similar scheme, which included the categories of “never,” “former,” and “current” drinkers. Hypertension [14], diabetes mellitus(DM) [15], metabolic syndrome (MetS) [16], and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [17] were recorded as binary variables (yes/no). Self-reported physical and metabolic indicators were supplemented by measured clinical parameters. The aforementioned metrics encompassed body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), glycohemoglobin (HbA1c, %), fasting blood glucose (FBG, mg/dL), and serum insulin levels (µU/mL). The investigation further encompassed the evaluation of additional markers of renal function and cardiovascular risk, including the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR, mg/g), systolic blood pressure (SBP, mmHg), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP, mmHg). These variables were considered potential confounders and were controlled for in subsequent statistical modeling.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1) with appropriate weighting to account for NHANES’s complex survey design. DII was divided into quartiles (Q1–Q4) for group comparisons. Baseline differences across quartiles were evaluated using ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ² tests for categorical variables.Weighted logistic regression models were employed to assess the association between DII and depression. Three models were built sequentially:

Model 1: Adjusted for age, sex, race, education, marital status, and BMI.

Model 2: Additionally adjusted for FBG, HbA1c, insulin, UACR, SBP, and DBP.

Model 3: Further adjusted for smoking, alcohol use, hypertension, MetS, CKD, and DM.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions were used to explore potential nonlinear associations between DII and depression. Subgroup analyses evaluated effect modification across demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, BMI, and comorbid conditions. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents baseline characteristics of participants with CKMS03, according to quartiles of DII. A total of 11,847 participants were included (mean age: 45.78 ± 0.27 years; 49.25% male). The overall prevalence of depression was 6.56%, rising progressively from 4.47% in Q1 to 10.06% in Q4. Significant differences were observed across DII quartiles in sociodemographic characteristics. Higher DII scores were observed more frequently among participants with lower educational attainment and among women. The proportion of current smokers also increased with higher DII quartiles. Physiological and metabolic indicators demonstrated similar gradients. Participants in Q4 had elevated BMI and UACR, along with a greater prevalence of DM, MetS, and CKD compared with those in Q1.

Association between DII and depression

Table 2 summarizes the association between DII and depression among individuals with CKMS03. Three progressively adjusted, weighted logistic regression models were constructed. Across all models, higher DII scores were consistently linked with elevated odds of depression. In Model 3, after full adjustment, each one-unit increase in DII was associated with higher odds of depression (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.06–1.18; P < 0.001). When DII was categorized into quartiles, a dose-response trend was observed. Compared with participants in the lowest quartile (Q1), those in the highest quartile (Q4) had significantly higher odds of depression after full adjustment (OR = 1.56, 95%CI: 1.20–2.03; P = 0.001).

Nonlinear association between DII and depression

In the analysis, we performed RCS regression to examine the nonlinear relationship between DII and depression (Fig. 2). A significant nonlinear association was observed between DII and depression (P for nonlinearity = 0.01), with the overall relationship being highly statistically significant (P < 0.001). Specifically, the odds for depression increased with higher DII values, with a particularly notable increase observed at elevated DII levels.

Subgroup analyses

Results of the subgroup analyses are displayed in Fig. 3. The positive association between DII and depression was largely consistent across sex, age, BMI, and educational subgroups. However, diabetes status significantly modified the association (P for interaction = 0.02). The relationship persisted among participants without diabetes, hypertension, or obesity but was not statistically significant among those with these conditions. Other interactions—including age, sex, and BMI—were not statistically significant.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the association between the DII and depression among individuals with CKMS03. Our findings demonstrate a positive association between pro-inflammatory dietary patterns and depressive, even after extensive adjustment for metabolic and behavioral covariates. Specifically, participants in the highest DII quartile had more than 50% higher odds of depression than those with the lowest scores. A nonlinear relationship between DII and depression was also observed.

Our results align with prior research demonstrating associations between pro-inflammatory diets and mental health outcomes in the general population. Meta-analyses and cohort studies have consistently linked higher DII scores to greater depression risk [18, 19]. However, these earlier investigations did not account for the integrative CKM framework, where overlapping metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular abnormalities intensify systemic inflammation. By situating this relationship within the CKM continuum, our study extends current knowledge, showing that diet-related inflammation remains a significant determinant of depression even before advanced disease onset. Moreover, the nonlinear association observed in our study is broadly consistent with previous findings indicating that the adverse mental health effects of dietary inflammation may become more apparent beyond certain exposure levels [20].

Several biological mechanisms may help explain the observed association. Pro-inflammatory diets, characterized by high intakes of processed foods, refined sugars, and saturated fats, may contribute to systemic inflammation by influencing inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and C-reactive protein(CRP) [21].

These inflammatory mediators disrupt neuroendocrine pathways, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [22], and impair serotonin synthesis [23], both of which are implicated in the pathogenesis of depression. In the context of CKMS03, the presence of obesity, insulin resistance, and chronic kidney disease may exacerbate this inflammatory state [24, 25], creating a feedback loop that heightens depression risk. For example, visceral adipose tissue, prevalent in CKM syndrome, contributes to macrophage infiltration and cytokine release, further amplifying systemic inflammation. Our findings align with earlier reviews, such as that by Brain et al., which reported that elevated inflammatory markers such as CRP were associated with insulin resistance and an increased risk of metabolic and mental health disorders [26]. Recent umbrella reviews and meta-analyses have also shown that nutrition-related interventions, including curcumin and probiotic supplementation, can modulate inflammatory biomarkers in adults, further supporting the biological relevance of diet–inflammation pathways in chronic disease contexts [27–29].

The subgroup findings also merit attention. Overall, the positive association between DII and depression was relatively stable across most subgroups. Even if some subgroups did not reach statistical significance, most interaction tests were no significant. These results suggest that the relationship between DII and depression was generally consistent across age, sex, BMI, education, hypertension, CKD, and MetS. Therefore, it is important to explain the non-significant results, as they may be related to limited sample size, subgroup heterogeneity, or residual confounding, rather than a true absence of association. Notably, diabetes was the only factor showing a significant interaction (P = 0.02). The association remained significant in participants without diabetes, but not in those with diabetes. This may reflect the complex interplay of metabolic dysregulation in diabetic patients, where other factors, such as glycemic control, medication use, and disease burden, may overshadow the impact of dietary inflammation on depression. However, this finding should still be interpreted cautiously and needs to be confirmed in future longitudinal studies.

From a clinical perspective, our findings may have practical implications for the early management of CKM. Because CKMS03 represents a potentially modifiable phase before overt cardiovascular events or advanced kidney disease, identifying factors associated with depression during this period is clinically relevant. Assessment of dietary inflammatory potential may help identify individuals at higher risk of depressive symptoms and may complement existing metabolic and cardiovascular risk evaluation. More broadly, these findings support the integration of dietary assessment and nutritional counseling into multidisciplinary CKM care, not only to improve cardiometabolic health but also to potentially benefit mental health outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the study focused specifically on CKMS03, which is a clinically important yet relatively understudied segment of the CKM continuum. Second, a large, nationally representative NHANES sample provides robust generalizability of the findings to the adult U.S. population. Third, the use of weighted multivariable models, RCS analysis, and subgroup analyses allowed us to evaluate the association comprehensively.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the DII was calculated based on baseline dietary data from NHANES, which may not capture long-term dietary patterns or changes over time. Second, the NHANES dataset is limited to U.S. participants, potentially restricting the generalizability of our findings to other populations with different dietary habits or CKM prevalence. Finally, the observational nature of the study precludes causal inferences, and further longitudinal or interventional studies are needed to confirm the relationship between DII and depression in CKMS03.

Conclusion

In summary, higher DII scores were significantly associated with greater odds of depression among individuals with CKMS03, and this association appeared to be nonlinear. These findings suggest that reducing dietary inflammatory load through the adoption of anti-inflammatory eating patterns may offer a practical strategy to enhance psychological well-being and slow CKM progression. Further prospective and interventional studies are warranted to confirm causality and to elucidate mechanisms linking diet-induced inflammation with mood disorders in metabolically vulnerable populations.
