Experimental and numerical evaluation of a novel swirling reversed circular flow jet impingement for photovoltaic thermal module
Authors: Morad Ahmad Alzoubi, Adnan Ibrahim, Sharul Sham Dol, Mohammad Alkhedher, Hariam Luqman Azeez, Muhammad Amir Aziat Ishak, Banw Omer Ahmed, Ebraheim Alkaabi, Ahmad Fazlizan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heat accumulation on the surface of PV is considered a fundamental problem that reduces performance and degrades lifespan. Latest advancements in literature suggest employing the Reversed Circular Flow Jet Impediment (RCFJI) as a cooling mechanism. However, this technique needs to be modified to generate more turbulence and vortices in the flow while maintaining the same or lower friction penalty. Thus, this study introduces a Swirling Reversed Circular Flow Jet Impediment (SRCFJI). The study first meticulously optimizes the effect of geometrical parameters such as groove shapes (circular, cubic, and triangular), cup depth (20 mm to 50 mm), groove width (1–2 mm), and the groove tilt angles (30° or 45°). The optimized design is manufactured and experimentally tested under mass flow rates of 0.01 to 0.13 kg/s and solar irradiances of 500 to 900 W/m². The primary findings indicate that the best configuration includes a circular shape with a cup depth of 40 mm and a groove width of 1 mm at mass flow rates of 0.13 kg/s and solar irradiance of 900 W/m2, resulting 56% enhancement in Thermohydraulic Performance Factor (THPF). In addition, the energy efficiency analysis demonstrates that the newly proposed systems reach a maximum electrical energy efficiency of 12.42% at mass flow rates of 0.13 kg/s and solar irradiances of 500 W/m², while an optimal thermal energy efficiency of 73.68% was recorded at mass flow rates of 0.13 kg/s and solar irradiances of 900 W/m².

Introduction

Even though fossil fuels provide approximately 80% of the energy consumed by about 8 billion people, they have numerous disadvantages, including being exhaustible, contributing to climate change, driving the greenhouse effect, and posing many other ecological challenges1,2. Although the side effects are catastrophic, it is anticipated that reliance on fossil fuels as the main energy source will increase in the coming years, further exacerbating the aforementioned consequences3. Therefore, shifting from reliance on fossil fuels to more sustainable sources has become a fundamental need and a major challenge for governments, scientific communities, and international organizations worldwide4,5. Photovoltaic (PV) has been the focal point of this transition as it can produce electrical energy by converting sunlight with minimal environmental impact and at relatively lower cost6,7. Nevertheless, PV suffers from a fundamental problem that not only reduces its efficiency but also significantly shortens its lifespan8. The problem is that as the PV surface temperature increases, especially in hot areas, excessive heat accumulation on its surface reduces its efficiency by approximately 5% for a 10-degree rise9,10. However, this increase can vary slightly depending on the PV material and the technologies incorporated into the cell9,10.

To mitigate this drawback, which has a tremendous impact on the performance and lifespan of a PV module, cooling is considered essential. The literature has suggested incorporating passive and active cooling techniques into new PV system designs11. Passive cooling is preferred because it relies on no external energy sources. Yet, more recent studies have developed hybrid designs that optimize performance to its maximum12,13. In either a passive or an active setup, the choice of the best working medium is critical. Air is considered an effective cooling medium because it is abundant and relatively inexpensive. However, the main problem to be addressed is airflow manipulation, which should be performed to maximize turbulence and vortices while incurring minimal fractional penalty14. One of the common airflow manipulation techniques that has been used in the literature is Reversed Circular Jet Impingement RCFJI, and it has several applications, such as single-pass, double-pass, concentrated, and jet configurations, all of which aim to direct airflow toward the rear of the PV more effectively15.

The main difference between a double-pass and single-pass configuration is that in a single-pass, air immediately exits the system after entering the inlet, making the cooling system spontaneous16. Nevertheless, in a double-pass configuration, the air encounters a partition, causing it to circulate at the back of the PV. In double-pass collectors, the air stays longer in the collector, resulting in more robust cooling than in single-pass collectors17,18. Despite the effectiveness of the two aforementioned techniques, the literature suggests several modifications to further improve their performance. The modifications include integrating thermal storage components, turbulence- and vortex-generation techniques, and area-expansion strategies19. The study conducted by S.I.U. Din et al.20 can be considered one of the studies that modify a double-pass collector by incorporating staggered-sandwich, multi-functional NPCM cylinders that store heat and generate additional turbulence. The primary condition of the study included a mass flow rate range of (0.01 to 0.05) kg/s and solar irradiances of 400 to 1000 W/m2. The main conclusions show that the proposed design reduced surface temperature by 40◦C, resulting in a 31.45% increase in thermal energy efficiency. Such a modification proved much more powerful than a standalone PV or a standard double pass. Aside from the PCM storage component, another study conducted by Li et al.21 aimed to increase thermal energy efficiency by integrating a Hemispherical Dimple on the Absorber Plate, which is considered one of the most effective passive cooling techniques. The study was performed under Reynolds numbers ranging from 3000 to 11,000 and constant heat flux. The result indicates an 85% increase in THPF. The main reason for the enhancement was the turbulence induced by the dimples. Additionally, M.E. Hadi Attia et al.22 numerically demonstrated that a double-pass solar collector with finned photovoltaic/thermal outperformed the standard configuration when tested under solar irradiances of 600 to 1000 W/m2. The thermal efficiency increased from 40.37% to 60.01%, resulting in a 48.6% increase in PV module power output. Moreover, the application of ribs, in both single- and double-pass, is also significant. For example, Ravi et al.23 employed a double-pass solar air heater having discrete multi-V and staggered ribs. The study was numerical, and the primary condition involved solar irradiances ranging from 600 to 1000 W/m². The results indicated that the new design outperforms a standard configuration by 56.75% when the Reynolds number is 2000. Finally, twisted tapes as vortex generators are also common. H.L.Azeez et al.24 employed a coiled twisted tape under mass flow rates of (0.01 to 0.085) kg/s and solar irradiance levels (400 to 1000) W/m2. The study shows that the new design outperforms a bare PV by 32%, 21.2%, and 35.1% enhancement in electrical energy, thermal energy, and total exergy efficiency, respectively.

The use of compound parabolic concentrators and air-based mini-channel systems represents two additional avenues for enhancing thermal efficiency25,26. For instance, A. Gupta et al.27 compare the performance of a bare PV cell and a concentrated photovoltaic cell with confined-jet impingement at mass flow rates of 20–55 g/min. The main results demonstrate that the new design reduces surface temperature by 6.3 °C, increasing power output to 30.5 W. In another study, A. Djebara et al.28 evaluate the performance of a folded mini-channel heat sink for a solar cell against a bare PV under mass flow rates of 0.3, 0.6, 0.8, and 1 m/s and solar irradiances of 500 and 1000 W/ m2. Their results indicate improvements of 57.44% in surface-temperature reduction, 26.60% in electrical energy output, and 37.55% in thermal energy efficiency when the new design is compared with a bare PV module. Additionally, Z. Noui et al.29 proposed a new design featuring wavy mini-channel heat sinks, which were tested at Reynolds numbers of e (Re = 200–900) and a uniform heat flux of 100 kW/m2. The main outcome of the study indicates that the new design outperforms a standard design by 27% enhancement in the Thermohydraulic Performance Factor.

Aside from the studies discussed above focusing on manipulating airflow to improve thermal efficiency, two investigations are particularly relevant to the present research. The first one is conducted by W.E. Ewe et al.30 who used a jet impingement bifacial photovoltaic thermal JIBPVT solar air collector. The study encompassed mass flow rates of (0.012 to 0.036) kg/s and solar irradiances of 700 to 900 W/m2. The JIBPVT system achieves optimal performance, registering thermal, electrical, and thermo-electro-hydraulic efficiencies of 57.3%, 10.36%, and 83.93%, respectively. Later, another study conducted by M.A.A.B. Ishak et al.31 integrated a Reversed Circular Flow Jet Impingement RCFJI. The study determined the primary condition of the test to be mass flow rates ranging from 0.01 to 0.14 kg/s, solar cell packing densities of 0.22, 0.33, and 0.66, and solar irradiance of 700 to 900 W/m². After rigorously optimizing the effects of geometrical and operational parameters, the ideal design achieved the highest electrical and thermal energy efficiencies of 10.9% and 61.43%, respectively.

Finally, it should be noted that RCFJI has several limitations that future research should address. Among the most common issues is jet interference, which arises from poor jet-to-jet and jet-to-target optimization, leading to uneven flow distribution and reduced turbulence and vortex generation. Another reported problem is a high pressure drop, which significantly impacts heat transfer. All these problems together increase energy demand, ultimately leading to poor air mixing, reduced turbulence, poor flow reattachment, and a nonuniform flow distribution. These phenomena will ultimately result in poor cooling capacity for the system.

Thus, this study seeks to mitigate most of the aforementioned drawbacks by introducing a novel jet impingement. The new design will add swirling (imprinted grooves on the inner surface on the cup) effects to the design proposed by M.A.A.B. Ishak et al.31, which will create greater turbulence, promote better mixing and flow reattachment, and achieve the lowest possible pressure drop. The study will also optimize the geometrical parameters of the new design to achieve the best swirling effect. The design parameters studied are groove shapes (circular, cubic, and triangular), cup depth ranging from 20 mm to 50 mm, groove widths of 1 mm and 2 mm, and groove tilt angles of 30° and 45°. The resulting optimized configuration is then fabricated and experimentally evaluated at mass flow rates between 0.01 and 0.13 kg/s and solar irradiance levels between 500 and 900 W/m². The reason for the selected flow rates and solar irradiance is that they are consistent with references30,31, and the flow rates fall within laminar, mixed, and turbulence regions.

Methodology

An overview of the study

The study is an investigation that seeks to reveal the impact of a Swirling Reversed Circular Flow Jet Impingement-based PVT and is divided into two main parts:

Geometrical parameter optimization is conducted in ANSYS to examine the effects of groove shapes (circular, cubic, and triangular), cup depth (20 mm to 50 mm), groove width (1–2 mm), and groove tilt angles (30° or 45°).The best geometry is manufactured using a 3-D printer, and it is experimentally tested using an indoor solar simulator under mass flow rates of (0.01 to 0.13 kg/s) and solar irradiances of 500 to 900 W/m².

The numerical analysis

The 3D module

The study used SolidWorks to create 24 modules with varying groove shapes (circular, cubic, and triangular), cup depths (20 mm to 50 mm), groove widths (1–2 mm), and groove tilt angles (30° or 45°). Table 1 shows the details of the cases included in this study. The logic in the way Table 1 is constructed is to see first which groove shape is better when the depth of the cup gradually becomes bigger. Once the best cup depth and groove shape is known, then which groove width in the case of the circular and the cubic shape, and title angle in the case of the triangular shape is the best. As shown in Fig. 1, the 24 shapes were modelled ( using three groove shapes, 4 cup depth, two circular groove widths, two cubic groove widths, and two title groove angles in the case of the triangular shape) and attached to the back of the reflecting jet plate, coinciding with the plate hole. The PVT collector’s unchanging dimensions are L = 200 mm, W = 200 mm, and duct depth d = 25 mm.

Models and assumptions used in the numerical study

After the 24 modules were prepared, the study ran simulations using ANSYS Fluent on a Workstation equipped with an intel core i70 10,700 K processor at a frequency of 3.8 GHz and 32 GB RAM. Each one of the twenty-four cases needed a computational time ranging between 2 and 4 h to reach convergence, depending on the mesh size and flow metrics. Mesh details of the study

As shown in Fig. 2, before conducting the simulation cases determined for this study, it is deemed necessary to conduct a grid-independent study to ensure the right element size is selected. Table 2 presents the results of the grid-independent study on element size, and an element size of 806,368 is chosen. The medium mesh was selected because it provides a significant improvement in average element quality, increasing from 0.529 (coarse) to 0.632, while keeping the number of elements at a manageable 806,368. Further refinement to the fine and very fine meshes increases the element count to 1.31 million and 1.87 million, respectively, but improves the average quality by only 1.46% and 0.19%. These marginal gains do not justify the substantial increase in computational cost. Therefore, the medium mesh offers the best balance between mesh quality and efficiency.

Results and discussion

Thermohydraulic performance analysis of the study

The Nusselt number analysis

As previously elucidated, the study seeks to optimize the geometrical parameters of an SRCFJI. The main parameters examined are groove shape (circular, cubic, and triangular), cup depth (20 to 50 mm), and groove width (1 to 2 mm) for the circular and cubic shapes, and the tilt angles of 30 ° and 45 ° for the triangular shape. A total of 24 cases were generated, and simulations were conducted for all cases at three flow rates (0.01, 0.07, and 0.13 kg/s).

Nusselt number analysis shows that the circular shape outperformed the other two shapes examined under all tested mass flow rates. Taking the case of flow rates of 0.01 kg/s, cup width of 50 mm, and groove width and title angle of 30 °, Nusselt number values of 20.5, 21.6, and 24.4 were recorded for the triangular, cubic, and circular shapes. This enhancement is attributed to the circular groove, which induces a more coherent, intensified swirling motion in the flow. By stabilizing and directing the fluid’s rotational component, the groove improves overall flow organization and promotes more effective mixing than other shapes. Regarding the effect of cup depth, a 40 mm depth showed superior performance across all cases in this study, even at varying mass flow rates. For instance, at a flow rate of 0.01 kg/s and a circular groove with a width of 1 mm, the Nusselt numbers are 19.15, 20.89, 22.92, and 21.51 for cup depths of 20 mm, 30 mm, 40 mm, and 50 mm, respectively. As shown, the recorded Nusselt numbers increase up to a cup depth of 40 mm, after which they decline. The primary explanation is that increasing the cup depth provides a larger internal volume, allowing the flow to develop its swirling motion more fully and thereby enhancing heat transfer. However, when the cup depth reaches approximately 50 mm, recirculation zones and heat entrapment begin to emerge. Although swirling still occurs, it does so in a manner that retains heat and promotes recirculating flow patterns, ultimately generating localized hot spots and reducing overall heat transfer performance. Examining the effect of groove width, it is found that a groove width of 1 mm performs better.

For instance, with a mass flow rate of 0.13 kg/s and a cup depth of 20 mm, Nusselt numbers of 31.4 and 30.08 were recorded. This effect can be attributed to the fact that a sharper edge is more effective at disrupting the fluid’s thermal boundary layer, thereby facilitating the more rapid development of swirling flow. Finally, it should be noted that increasing the mass flow rate enhances the Nusselt number. For instance, for a triangular shape, a title angle of 45 °, and a cup depth of 40 mm, Nusselt numbers of 21.11, 25.64, and 30.12 are obtained. These results can be validated by comparing them with those reported in reference33. As shown in Fig. 5 (a to c), it should be noted that the enhancements recorded in Nusselt number values are not the only indicators of heat transfer enhancement. Therefore, friction factor analysis must be conducted.

The friction factor analysis

As explained above, although the increase in the Nusselt number is a good indicator of heat transfer enhancement, it is not the final deciding factor. Friction factor analysis must be conducted to minimize friction-induced heat loss.

Considering the effect of groove shapes, it is confirmed that a circular shape reduces friction more prominently than the other two examined shapes. For a mass flow rate of 0.13 kg/s, a cup depth of 40 mm, a groove width of 1 mm, and a tilt angle of 30o, the friction factor values are 0.042, 0.038, and 0.031 for triangular, cubic, and circular shapes, respectively, The primary explanation for this phenomenon is that a circular geometry provides a smooth contact interface without overlapping regions that could induce abrupt flow disturbances or create rough contact conditions. Once again, a cup depth of 40 mm yields the lowest friction factor among the investigated depths. For example, in the case of employing mass flow rates of 0.07 kg/s, a cubic shape, and a groove width of 1 mm, friction factor values of 0.056, 0.054, 0.051, and 0.052 are recorded for cup depths of 20 mm, 30 mm, 40 mm, and 50 mm, respectively. As previously elucidated, a cup with a depth of 50 mm leads to phenomena such as flow recirculation and heat trapping. Both phenomena facilitate more frequent and more intense interactions between the fluid and the cup surface, leading to deeper cups that generate greater friction. However, for depths between 20 mm and 40 mm, no recirculation is observed, and thus, rapid contact between the flow and the cup’s boundary layer does not occur.

Regarding the groove width, it is observed that a groove width of 1 mm causes less friction generation in both the circular and the cubic shapes. Similarly, a 30 ° title angle induces less friction than a 45 ° title angle in the case of the triangular shape. For instance, for a mass flow rate of 0.01 kg/s and a cup depth of 40 mm, friction factor values of 0.062 and 0.064 are recorded. The main reason is that the groove with a smaller contact area is less likely to be in direct contact with the flow, thereby significantly reducing friction. Finally, as mass flow rates increase, the friction factor decreases, as noted in many published articles, including those referenced in34. The thermohydraulic performance factor analysis

The Nusselt number and the friction factor are two opposite evaluation parameters. It is preferable to increase the Nusselt number and decrease the friction factor to enhance heat transfer. Since those two parameters are mutually exclusive, compensation should be made. THPF is a metric that considers both the increase in the Nusselt number and the decrease in the friction factor and can serve as a final indicator of whether a system is efficient at enhancing heat transfer. Therefore, this study will provide a detailed THPF analysis of the systems to determine which design best enhances heat transfer capacity.

As mentioned above, the circular shape yielded the highest THPF value, indicating its greater cooling effectiveness than other shapes. For example, when mass flow rates of 0.01 kg/s, a groove width of 1 mm, and a cup depth of 40 mm were selected, THPF enhancements of 21%, 31%, and 36% were achieved for triangular, cubic, and circular grooves, respectively. The main reasons behind such enhancement were discussed in the sections on the Nusselt number and the friction factor. Also, the results are consistent with those reported in reference35. Regarding cup depth, it was once again validated that 40 mm achieved the THPF value compared to all cases considered in this study. Determining mass flow rates of 0.07 kg/s, circular shape, and a groove of 2 mm as the primary testing condition, THPF enhancements of 45%, 46%, 48%, and 47% were attained for cup depths of 20 mm, 30 mm, 40 mm, and 50 mm. The reasons for the recorded enhancement have been previously explored, and the data can be validated by direct comparison against reference35. Similarly, the analysis of groove width shows that 1 mm performs better than 2 mm. For instance, when mass flow rates of 0.13 kg/s, a circular shape, and a cup depth of 40 mm are applied, the enhancement in THPF is 56% and 55% for 1 mm and 2 mm, respectively.

Therefore, it is decided to select a circular-shaped groove with a depth of 40 mm and a groove width of 1 mm for manufacturing, as it offers the highest enhancement of 56% compared to a smooth cup. It should be noted that increasing the mass flow rate positively impacts heat transfer in this design. Figure 7 (a-c) demonstrates all the recorded values and trends from the THPF analysis.

The surface temperature and thermal energy efficiency of the system

Once the final PVT design was constructed, it was experimentally tested over a range of mass flow rates from 0.01 kg/s to 0.13 kg/s and solar irradiances from 500 to 900 W/m2. The result shows a direct proportional relationship between the mass flow rate and the PV surface temperature. For instance, when employing solar irradiance of 900 W/m², surface temperatures of 67.7 °C, 66.8 °C, 65.9 °C, 64.8 °C, and 63.7 °C were recorded for mass flow rates ranging from 0.01 kg/s to 0.13 kg/s. The reason is that as mass flow rates increase, more air comes into contact with the PV rear, thereby facilitating cooling. The results obtained under varying mass flow rates can be validated by comparing them with the data reported in reference34. Also, increased solar irradiance intensifies surface temperatures. For instance, at a constant mass flow rate of 0.01 kg/s and varying solar irradiance from 500 to 900 W/m², the surface temperature increased from 40.1 °C to 48.5 °C, 60.2 °C, and 67.7 °C. Figure 10 (a) shows the surface temperature values and trends for the new design under varying conditions.

Additionally, the study recorded the thermal energy efficiency of the new design, which is primarily due to temperature differences between its inlet and outlet as mass flow rates increase from 0.01 kg/s to 0.13 kg/s, holding solar irradiance at 700 W/m2, 69.1%, 69.5%, 69.82%, 70.2%, and 70.5%. Even though the enhancement is small, the rationale for this enhancement has already been discussed, and the data’s validity can be confirmed using reference34. Similarly, as the irradiance increases from 500 to 900 W/m2 at a constant flow rate of 0.01 kg/s, thermal energy efficiencies of 66.1%, 67.8%, 69.1%, 70.5%, and 71.7% are recorded. The reason is that as more heat is available in the system, the likelihood of greater temperature differences between the inlet and outlet increases. References35 can be used to validate the obtained data. Figure 10 (b) shows the system’s surface temperature and thermal efficiency.

The electrical performance of the systems

A reduction in surface temperature and an increase in thermal energy efficiency are good signs that electrical energy efficiency and electrical power production will improve. As mass flow rates increase from 0.01 kg/s to 0.13 kg/s while holding solar irradiance at 500 W/m2, their electrical efficiencies are 11.96%, 11.98%, 12%, 12.03%, and 12.05%, respectively. The reason for the shown enhancement in electrical efficiency as mass flow rates increase is given in the previous section. This enhancement results in a power production of 1.46%. Nevertheless, intensifying solar irradiance negatively impacts electrical energy efficiency. As solar irradiance increases, power generation also rises. However, the additional power produced is insufficient to offset the thermal effects associated with higher irradiance. Therefore, it can be concluded that further design modifications are required to manage heat-related challenges effectively.
