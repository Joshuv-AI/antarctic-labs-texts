Temporo-mandibular joint alterations in juvenile idiopathic arthritis and young-onset inflammatory arthropathies: a magnetic resonance imaging study with ultrasound comparison
Authors: Adriano Lercara, Silvana Martino, Gloria Crepaldi, Stefano Cirillo, Marco Davico, Sarah Marouen, Enrica Vandelli, Claudia Lomater, Yves-Marie Pers, Francesco Licciardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo assess temporomandibular joint (TMJ) alterations using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in pediatric and young adult patients with juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) and non-JIA inflammatory arthropathies, and to evaluate the added value of ultrasound (US).MethodsThe study prospectively included three groups of patients: children with JIA; young adults under 35 years with JIA, and young adults under 35 years with non-JIA inflammatory arthropathies. All patients underwent joint count assessment, clinical examination of the TMJs, evaluation of global disease activity and TMJ MRI to identify inflammation or structural damage. TMJ US was additionally performed in the two young adult patient groups. Associations between clinical findings and MRI results were then analyzed using appropriate group comparison tests. Concordance between MRI and US findings was also calculated.ResultsA total of 41 patients were enrolled: 14 in the pediatric JIA group, 19 in the adult JIA group, and 8 in the adult non-JIA group. MRI analysis revealed significantly greater joint damage in adults compared to pediatric patients (p = 0.05). Tenderness on physical examination was significantly associated with MRI-detected joint damage (p = 0.01), whereas retrognathia was associated with MRI evidence of inflammation (p = 0.02) in all patients. The negative predictive value of clinical examination for detecting TMJ alterations was low (47.8%). US, performed in 18 out of the 27 adults, was compared with MRI findings and demonstrate poor concordance, with a sensitivity of 48% (95%CI 0.28–0.68), and a specificity of 73% (95%CI 0.47–0.99).ConclusionsTMJs alterations were common across all three patient groups. Clinical examination showed limited association with MRI-detected morphological lesions. MRI remains the gold standard for TMJ assessment, while the diagnostic performance of US requires optimization to enhance both sensitivity and specificity.Trail registration“Comité Local d’Ethique Recherche (IRB) du CHU de Montpellier” no. IRB-MTP_2022_06_202201149.Keywords: Juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Inflammatory arthropathies, MRI, Ultrasound, Temporomandibular joint

Background

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is the most common chronic inflammatory disorder in childhood, with an incidence ranging from 1 to 23 per 100,000 and a prevalence between 3.8 and 400 per 100,000, depending on geographic region [1–4]. JIA s defined as arthritis of unknown etiology with onset before the age of 16 years, persisting for more than six weeks [5]. The hallmark of JIA is joint swelling, resulting from synovial membrane thickening and joint effusion, which can progress to joint deformities and bone erosions [6]. Approximately half of affected patients experience persistently active disease or disease flares beyond adolescence— [7], sometimes after prolonged remission—which may prompt referral to an adult rheumatologist [8].

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ), historically referred to as “the forgotten joint” [9] is affected in approximately 50% of patients with JIA, with reported prevalence ranging from 11% to 87% [9–11], and bilateral involvement occurring in 53% to 83% of cases [12, 13]. Early disease onset (before 4 years of age) and long disease duration are recognized as independent risk factors for TMJ damage in JIA [14]. Clinical manifestations of TMJ may include pain, restricted mouth opening, crepitation, and joint swelling, while structural damage to the mandibular growth center in the condylar cartilage can result in micrognathia, retrognathia, or mandibular asymmetry [15, 16]. Although clinical examination demonstrates high specificity, its sensitivity for detecting TMJ inflammation or structural damage is low [17, 18]. Isolated TMJ arthritis can occur as the sole manifestation of JIA, yet up to 71% of cases are asymptomatic, contributing to diagnostic delays [13, 19, 20]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with gadolinium enhancement remains the gold standard for TMJ evaluation, offering high sensitivity and specificity and enabling the detection of both inflammatory and structural changes, including early asymptomatic lesions [21–25]. Early identification through MRI can facilitate timely therapeutic intervention, thereby reducing the risk of irreversible joint damage, which develops in approximately 69% of untreated cases, and preventing long-term complications such as physical disability, facial asymmetry, and deformities [26]. Consequently, routine TMJ screening is recommended in all children at the time of JIA diagnosis [27].

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), ankylosing spondylitis (AS) and psoriatic arthritis (PsA) are considered the primary non-JIA inflammatory rheumatic diseases in adults due to their clinical and pathophysiological similarities with JIA [28]. This relationship is supported by genetic, clinical, and immunopathological overlap [28]: rheumatoid factor (RF)-positive polyarticular JIA closely resembles adult seropositive rheumatoid arthritis [29]; enthesitis-related JIA is analogous to adult spondyloarthritis, including ankylosing spondylitis [30]; and juvenile psoriatic arthritis shares features with adult psoriatic arthritis [31]. In RA, the TMJ is involved in 8% to 70% of patients [32–34]. TMJ involvement in AS is seen in up to 38% of patients, while for PsA, the literature is scarce, finding that up to 80% of patients had TMJ disorders [32, 35, 36].

Our study aimed to assess the prevalence of MRI-detected TMJ alterations in pediatric patients with JIA and to compare these findings with those in young adults with JIA, as well as young adults with non-JIA inflammatory arthropathies. In addition, we sought to determine whether clinical examination findings reflect MRI-detected TMJ abnormalities, and, in the adult cohort, to evaluate the agreement between MRI and ultrasonography (US).

Methods

Study design

Participants were stratified into three cohorts: (1) pediatric patients with JIA (“pediatric JIA”); (2) young adults (< 35 years) diagnosed with JIA during childhood (“adult JIA”), both classified according to the 2001 ILAR criteria [37]; and (3) young adults (< 35 years) with non-JIA inflammatory arthropathy (“inflammatory rheumatic disease” [IRD] group).

Consecutive, prospective recruitment occurred between January and June 2018 across participating centers. All patients underwent standardized clinical examination of the TMJ. For adult patients, joint US was proposed concomitantly with MRI, both performed as part of routine follow-up. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the Comité Local d’Éthique Recherche (IRB) of the CHU de Montpellier (approval no. IRB-MTP_2022_06_202201149).

Patient selection and enrolment

Adult participants were recruited from the Rheumatology Centers of the Mauriziano Hospital of Turin (Italy) and the University Hospital Center (CHU) of Montpellier (France), while pediatric participants were recruited from the Immunorheumatology Division of the Regina Margherita Children’s Hospital of Turin (Italy).

Eligibility criteria required a disease duration ≥ 12 months; patients with shorter disease duration were also eligible if TMJ symptoms were present, so that recruitment was not symptom-driven. Both female and male participants fulfilling the classification criteria for JIA or for IRD, including rheumatoid arthritis, spondyloarthritis, or psoriatic arthritis, were eligible. Subjects were recruited consecutively and prospectively from the active patient lists of the three centers. TMJ imaging was proposed independently of TMJ symptoms, with the only requirement being agreement to undergo TMJ MRI for all participants and US examination for adults only. US in children could not be performed for organizational reasons.

Primary and secondary endpoints

The main objective of the study was to compare MRI-detected TMJ alterations in pediatric patients with JIA with those in young adults with JIA and young adults with non-JIA inflammatory arthropathies. Secondary objectives were to investigate the association between clinical examination and MRI findings in all participants, and, to evaluate the concordance between MRI and US in the adult cohort.

Outcomes measures

MRI of the TMJ was performed using a Philips Ingenia 1.5 T scanner for patients from Turin and a Skyra 3 T scanner for patients from Montpellier. Both TMJs were imaged in closed-mouth and open-mouth positions, without contrast enhancement, using the following sequences: axial T1 (centered), coronal proton density (PD) with closed mouth, sagittal PD with closed mouth, sagittal T2-STIR with closed mouth, sagittal PD with open mouth, and sagittal T2-STIR with open mouth. All MR images were interpreted by a single radiology specialist, using the MRI Scoring System developed by Tolend et al. [38]. The assessment included the inflammatory domain (bone marrow edema, effusion, synovial thickening) and the joint damage domain (condylar flattening, erosions, disc abnormalities). Inflammation was deemed present if at least one of the three inflammatory features was identified, and the same criterion applied for joint damage. MRIs showing either inflammation or joint damage were classified as “pathological.” The radiologist was blinded to the disease and the clinical evaluation findings for each patient.

Clinical examination assessed tenderness, swelling, joint crepitation, lateral deviation, retrognathia, and decreased mouth opening (DMO, defined as a maximum opening < 40 mm). The examiner was blinded to the MRI and US findings of the TMJ, and all assessments were performed by a single physician.

In adults, US examinations were performed using an Esaote MyLab™ Alpha7 ultrasound system equipped with high-frequency linear transducer (13–19 MHz) for patients from Turin and a MINDRAY TE7 equipped with high-frequency linear transducer (6–14 MHz) for patients from Montpellier. In both participating centers, machine settings were optimized for the assessment of superficial musculoskeletal structures, in accordance with EULAR recommendations for musculoskeletal US Grey-scale (GS) and power Doppler (PD) modalities were used for TMJ assessment. GS ultrasound was employed to evaluate synovial hypertrophy, joint effusion, and structural alterations, while PD was used to detect synovial vascularization as a marker of active inflammation. Doppler settings (including pulse repetition frequency, gain, and wall filters) were adjusted to maximize sensitivity for low-flow signals, as recommended for superficial joint evaluation [39]. The US examinations were performed and interpreted in real time by two experienced operators (> 5 years) familiar with TMJ ultrasonography, following the methodology described by Melchiorre et al. [40]. Prior to study initiation, the operators agreed on a shared scoring approach based on published EULAR concepts [39] and previously reported TMJ US methodology [40]. The assessment included evaluation of joint capsule inflammation, condylar profile (presence or absence of erosions), and joint effusion (capsule thickness > 1.5 mm). US findings were considered pathological if at least one inflammatory or damage parameter was present. Concordance between pathological US and pathological MRI was subsequently assessed. The operators were blinded to the clinical evaluation findings for each patient.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized as frequencies or as median and interquartile range (IQR), according to the distribution of each variable. Group comparisons and associations between clinical findings and MRI were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. MRI parameter distributions were compared across the three study groups; additionally, overall JIA patients were compared with the IRD group, and overall adult patients were compared with the pediatric JIA cohort.

Two analytical approaches were used to assess the predictive value of clinical features for MRI-detected abnormalities. For features with potential unilateral or bilateral involvement (tenderness, swelling, asymmetry, joint crepitation), each TMJ (two per patient) was analyzed separately. For features without side-specific information (retrognathia, DMO), analyses were performed at the patient level. For each clinical feature, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated with respect to the presence of inflammation and/or joint damage on MRI.

Concordance between MRI and US findings was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (k), interpreted as follows: <0.20, low; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, good; and 0.81–1.00, excellent. Sensitivity and specificity were also calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM Corp. released in 2011, IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp. GraphPad Prism Version 6.00 for Windows, GraphPad Software, La Jolla California USA.

A trend toward significance was noted for greater MRI-detected joint damage in adult JIA patients (68.4%) compared with pediatric JIA patients (46.4%, p = 0.08). Among the 27 adult patients, 18 underwent TMJ ultrasound (US). Of the 36 TMJs examined by US, 12 were pathological on both MRI and US, 8 were non-pathological on both modalities, 3 were pathological on US but non pathological on MRI, and 13 were pathological on MRI but non pathological on US, resulting in low concordance (κ = 0.17, 95% CI: 0–0.43). In the inflammation domain, only two TMJs demonstrated inflammation on US, with MRI confirming only one of these cases. In the damage domain, 12 TMJs showed damage on both MRI and US, 9 showed no damage on both imaging modalities, 2 had damage on US but not on MRI, and 13 showed damage on MRI but not on US. The concordance was fair (κ = 0.23, 95% CI: 0–0.48). For the detection of damage, US demonstrated a sensitivity of 48% (95% CI: 0.28–0.68) and a specificity of 73% (95% CI: 0.47–0.99).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study specifically designed to evaluate TMJ alterations in rheumatic patients through a comparative approach involving three distinct clinical groups, with the aim of determining the specific patterns of TMJ involvement in JIA from childhood to adulthood. A history of TMJ symptoms was reported by up to 47.4% of patients, although these were often not disclosed to the clinician. Consistent with previous reports [13, 26, 41–44], we observed that a substantial proportion of TMJ arthritis cases were not detected on clinical examination but were identified through imaging. Clinical examination identified TMJ alterations in 7.1–35.7% of cases, whereas pathological findings on MRI were present in up to 68.8% in our study. Moreover, MRI-detected TMJ alterations was bilateral in up to 63.2% of cases. Clinical examination alone demonstrated limited predictive value for TMJ alterations, as indicated by the low NPVs observed between clinical features and corresponding MRI findings. Among the assessed variables, only TMJ tenderness on clinical examination was a reliable predictor of MRI-detected structural damage, while retrognathia was specifically associated with MRI evidence of inflammation. Nonetheless, clinical evaluation retains clinical value, as self-reported TMJ pain and dysfunction in children with JIA have been shown to predict TMJ deformities on imaging [45].

TMJ alteration occurs in approximately 50% of children with JIA, warranting particular attention from pediatric rheumatologists [9–11]. Performing MRI prior to symptom onset or at the time of diagnosis, as recommended by others [13], could reveal subclinical damage and facilitate early therapeutic intervention to prevent progression. In contrast, TMJ receives comparatively less consideration in adult rheumatology, despite abnormalities being present in about half of rheumatoid arthritis patients—often bilaterally [46–48]—highlighting the need for improved management of TMJ arthritis in adult arthropathies. In spondyloarthritis, TMJ alterations are reported only in isolated case reports, with an estimated prevalence of 10–24% [49]. An observational study by Crincoli et al. reported TMJ disorders in 80% of patients with psoriatic arthritis [36]. In our cohort, erosions and disk abnormalities on MRI were more frequent in non-JIA adult patients than in pediatric patients (50% and 62.5% vs. 10.7% and 21.4%, respectively). It should be noted, however, that IRD patients in our study were relatively young and had a reduced median disease duration of six years, whereas in most of the cited studies, patients often had long-standing disease. Despite this, the prevalence of TMJ alterations in our IRD cohort aligns reasonably well with previous reports, suggesting that our findings are consistent with the broader literature [9–13].

Among JIA cases, MRI-detected TMJ damage was more prevalent in adults than in children (68.4% vs. 46.4%), most notably condylar flattening. This difference likely reflects longer disease duration, increasing the probability of inflammatory changes leading to structural damage. Conversely, pediatric JIA patients more often exhibited inflammatory changes—particularly moderate to severe joint effusion—reinforcing MRI as the optimal screening tool in this population [24, 50]. However, MRI in children poses challenges, including high cost, limited availability, need for sedation in younger patients, and intolerance in those with claustrophobia.

US is a relatively affordable and widely accessible imaging modality. However, there are currently no standardized protocols for the evaluation of the TMJ using US [51]. All previous studies aiming to establish a protocol for TMJ ultrasonography have been conducted in pediatric populations. This focus is due to the high prevalence of TMJ involvement in JIA and the necessity for early detection to prevent long-term craniofacial complications [52]. Conversely, in adult populations, TMJ involvement is less frequently assessed, as it is less symptomatic, less prioritized clinically, and more anatomically variable, which has delayed the development of standardized protocols [53]. In the present study, the detection of joint damage in adults demonstrated only fair concordance between US and MRI, indicating that US may yield a considerable number of false-negative results. This finding is consistent with previous reports [54, 55] and with the systematic review by Rotolo et al., which confirmed the limited correlation between US and MRI and, consequently, the current inability to completely replace MRI with US for TMJ assessment [24]. Although MRI remains the gold standard for detecting both acute and chronic TMJ changes in patients with JIA [24, 25, 50], as also emphasized by the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) guidelines on imaging in JIA [7], further research is warranted to clarify the role of US in TMJ monitoring and to develop an optimized protocol for detecting TMJ alterations in inflammatory arthropathies.

We must acknowledge several limitations in our study. First, the cohort size was relatively small; however, it remains sufficiently informative for our primary objectives, particularly in comparing TMJ involvement across pediatric JIA, adult JIA, and non-JIA IRD patients. Second, although a healthy control group was not included, our study design allowed robust comparisons with clinically relevant patient populations. In addition, the spectrum of TMJ abnormalities on MRI is already well established in both pediatric and adult populations [56, 57]. Third, gadolinium contrast was not used in our MRI protocol, leading to a potential sensitivity decrease for detecting synovial inflammation; nevertheless, the non-contrast MRI sequences employed are well validated for assessing key structural lesions such as erosions and disk abnormalities. Moreover, the decision to avoid gadolinium was consistent with a pragmatic and ethically sound approach, minimizing unnecessary exposure in a population undergoing multiple imaging exams. Fourth, US imaging of the TMJs was not performed in children with JIA. Pediatric patients were followed in a dedicated tertiary children’s hospital, where TMJ US was not routinely available and no operator with specific expertise in TMJ US was present at the time of the study, for a technique that is strongly operator-dependent. Performing examinations without appropriate expertise could have introduced significant variability and reduced the reliability of the findings. Moreover, requiring children and their families additional visit for US in a different facility would have represented a substantial logistical and psychological burden for children and their families and may have compromised adherence. Consequently, the analysis of MRI–US concordance and the evaluation of the role of US in TMJ assessment are limited to adult patients. Lastly, we acknowledge that no formal inter- or intra-observer reliability testing was performed for US reporting, which represents a further limitation of the study. Taken together, these factors underscore that our results should be interpreted cautiously. Future prospective studies specifically designed to include pediatric populations, with integrated MRI and US assessment are warranted to clarify the utility of US for TMJ monitoring in children with JIA. Finally, while optimal US assessment requires operators trained in standardized, reliability-tested protocols, a strength of our study is the blinded central review of MRI images, which minimizes observer bias and supports the reproducibility of our findings, providing a solid foundation for future screening programs of TMJ involvement in patients with articular rheumatologic conditions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the high prevalence of TMJ in patients with JIA and in young adults with inflammatory arthropathies, underscoring the limited value of clinical examination alone for detecting TMJ alterations. MRI remains the gold standard for evaluating both inflammatory activity and structural damage. In contrast, US requires further refinement and standardization and currently shows poor concordance with MRI findings, limiting its applicability as a reliable screening tool.
