New genomic techniques for sustainable agriculture and their prospects in Europe
Authors: George Gaskell, Agnes Allansdottir, Jürgen Hampel, Nicole Kronberger, Gema Revuelta de la Poza, Helge Torgersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Could societal debates over the future of new genomic techniques (NGTs) in sustainable agriculture be hampered by entrenched positions of environmental groups and/or populist political actors, as was the fate of genetically modified organisms (GMOs) in Europe? Apart from a few countries in Eastern Europe, current media coverage, the interests of environmental organizations, and public opinion point to a favourable reception of NGTs in the majority of European countries. Transparency and openness are argued to be the key to public support.

The promise of new genomic techniques in agriculture

Claims for biotechnology’s latest innovation, new genomic techniques (NGTs), include speeding up the precision breeding of plants to achieve drought and disease resistance, higher crop yields, improved nutrition, climate change mitigation, and contributing to the UN’s sustainable development goals. Genome editing is a group of technologies designed to change an organism’s DNA. A well-known example is the CRISPR-Cas9 system. In comparison to other techniques of gene editing, it is said to be faster, cheaper, more accurate, and more efficient.

Evolving European regulation

There has been a lengthy and sometimes heated debate in Europe on the regulation of NGTs. In December 2025, the European Council and Parliament reached a provisional agreement with the introduction of a two-tier system, designed to improve sustainability and competitiveness. Category 1 (NGT-1) plants with minor modifications within a defined range, no more than 20 genetic alterations (10.1111/pbi.70228), and that could occur naturally in crossbreeding, will be treated like conventional plants. Category 2 (NGT-2) plants with more complex modifications will be subject to the EU’s existing strict GMO legislation regarding authorization, traceability, and labelling. In addition, individual European Member States can restrict or prohibit the cultivation of NGT-2 plants, consistent with existing GMO regulations, Directive 2001/18/EC. In May 2026, the provisional agreement will be put before the European Parliament.

Europe’s problems with GMOs

However, given the controversy over genetically modified organisms (GMOs) in the years 1995–2005, the European Commission, plant breeders and the agricultural sector are worried that history may be repeated with NGTs. What is feared is a coalition of environmental and consumer groups, the organic food lobby and populist politicians, promoting dystopian scenarios to the public that there is no difference between NGTs and GMOs. This would entail forcing NGT products to undergo extensive and time-consuming authorization processes, and a labelling regime that would make them unattractive to plant breeders and retailers. Is this fear well-founded? A comparison of the political, scientific and societal contexts of the years of controversy with contemporary times is illuminating and largely reassuring for the future of NGTs in most European countries.

The years of controversy started in the early 1980s when environmental groups played a major role in motivating opposition to agricultural biotechnology. In Germany, the Öko-Institute added biotechnology to its opposition to nuclear power, and Greenpeace pressured retail chains to ban genetically modified (GM) foods. In 1996, Austria held a People’s Initiative seeking to ban GM foods and the release of GMOs. Environmental groups introduced the new concept of “unknown risks” and scientists playing God. Across Europe, a coalition emerged, involving producers, retailers, regulators, politicians, tabloid media and environmentalists, backed by an increasingly concerned public1. Reports such as Pusztai’s2 dubious claim that GM foods would affect human health; the arrival in Europe of Monsanto’s unlabelled Roundup Ready soybeans; changing risk assessments of previously approved food additives and pesticides, and the outbreak of mad cow disease, led to concerns about the slippery slope, what this technology might lead to, and to doubts about food safety regulation.

Against this background, governmental support followed claims for widespread applications bringing economic and societal benefits from the life sciences3. Meanwhile, the Eurobarometer survey in 1996 reported public disquiet in a number of countries4. The European Commission’s response was Directive 2001/18/EC replacing 90/220/EEC, which had been criticized for being too vague5. Citing the precautionary principle, it renewed the mandatory case-by-case assessment of the risks to the environment from GMO releases, and the requirement to label GM products6. This did little to alleviate public concerns, in part because the public could see no benefits from GMOs7. The differing positions on GMOs across the European Member States led to a constitutional impasse between the interests of national governments and the European single market. In 2015, the European Commission granted an opt-out provision allowing individual member states to exclude GMOs, de facto giving up any attempt at full harmonization.

Genetics in contemporary times

The last 25 years have seen dramatic changes in the media coverage of agricultural biotechnology. During the GMO controversy, environmental groups staged events designed for television news, such as uprooting field trials in hazchem suits. Today, while Greenpeace, Friends of the Earth and the Confédération Paysanne in France are officially opposed to the deregulation of genome editing, claiming risks to biodiversity, food safety and environmental health, the leading environmental groups have moved on to other issues. In many countries, scientific institutions have endorsed NGTs and are now joined by some farming associations and regional authorities. At the same time, the public perception of the gene has changed. In 1996, the Eurobarometer 46.1 survey showed that for the majority of the public, genes were an unknown quantity and genetic manipulation was frightening. Since then, the gene has become familiar, for example, in the ground-breaking medical applications of gene therapies treating debilitating genetic disorders. In 2017, a survey in 10 European countries and the USA showed consistent support for gene editing applied to adult therapies7.

Public opinion in Europe

In 2025, the European Food Standards Agency (EFSA) Eurobarometer survey on Food Safety in the EU8 asked respondents whether they were concerned about a range of issues related to food. Low on the list of concerns were “genetically modified organisms in food and drinks” and “the use of new biotechnology in food production, e.g. genome editing”.

As shown in Fig. 1, using data from EFSA’s 2025 food risk survey, the weighted average across the European member states of concern over genome editing in food production is 9%. In 16 countries, 10% or fewer expressed concern. In the remaining countries, between 11% and 20% expressed concern. Highlighting the fallout from the years of controversy over GMOs, the weighted average of concern about GMOs is 25%. This is at a time when the cultivation of GMO crops is extremely limited, with only one GM maize variety cultivated in a few countries. Public consultation and policy

A survey by the Norwegian Biotechnology Advisory Board in 2020 found that the public supports genome-edited crops (another label for NGTs), providing they have traits with a clear sustainability or societal benefit9. In 2021, a public consultation by the UK Food Standards Agency10 concluded by asking whether the participants, who had thought NGTs were GMOs by another name, would be willing to eat genome-edited food. Almost eight in ten said they would. In 2025, a detailed survey of the Swedish public11 found majority support for genome editing (NGTs) provided the purpose was clearly beneficial to society, such as reduced pesticide use, crops with lower impact on the environment or producing healthier food. In March this year, the German Länderministers of agriculture held a conference. While NGTs were discussed, the topic apparently did not merit a mention in the final Press Release.

After 20 years of silence, governments are beginning to make decisions supporting biotechnology. For example, the Italian government, with cross-party support, has introduced a new regulatory framework for gene technologies in agriculture, opening the way for NGT field trials. In the UK, a government working party is charged with developing procedures to support NGTs. Spain is actively committed to the European Commission’s proposed regulation, and a group chaired by the National Office for Scientific Advice recommended the use of CRISPR in agriculture. In December 2025, France shifted its position to support the European Commission’s proposals to deregulate many NGTs, with the approval of the compromise framework that treats certain gene-edited plants (NGT-1) like conventional crops, exempting them from strict GMO labelling.

Pockets of resistance may still exist nationally and in particular communities, not only in Eastern Europe. In France, in its defence of small-scale farming and opposition to industrial agricultural technologies, the Confédération Paysanne took the issue of NGT regulation to the European Court of Justice. In Austria, ‘anti-GM’ is mainstream across the political spectrum, with NGTs being seen as an example of industrialized agriculture running counter to the national identity, and a threat to organic interests built up over three decades. In 2012, the Hungarian parliament, led by a populist party, voted to keep the country GMO-free.

Regulatory challenges

The continued regulatory uncertainty in Europe on the future of NGTs in agriculture creates problems for major stakeholders. For the industry, the extent of red tape in the process of gaining approvals could become a disincentive to research and development. For retailers, labelling is not envisaged for NGT-1 products, but in some countries, labelling is planned to support consumer choice. At present, retailers in Germany and Austria will not stock GM products for fear of becoming the target of anti-GMO campaigners. Outstanding issues include the co-existence of NGT and organic farming, intellectual property rights and NGT patents. With the European Commission recognizing that EU-wide harmonization regarding NGTs will be difficult to achieve, the proposal to introduce an ‘opt out’ clause, replicating the 2015 policy of subsidiarity on GMOs, will allow Member States to chart their own paths with or without NGTs, abandoning the aim of full harmonization.

NGT's future, lessons from the past

A possible scenario for the prospects of NGTs in Europe might be illuminated by the early development of recombinant DNA and the famous ‘Berg’ letter to Nature12 and Science in 2008 on the potential biohazards of recombinant DNA. At the Asilomar conference in 1975, the social and ethical aspects of rDNA were sidelined, described as peripheral, a source of confusion, and with the potential to increase public concerns13. Fifty years later, the merits of a transparent and inclusive stance on innovation in science and technology are reflected in the conclusion of an article whose authors included Nobel prize winner J.A. Doudna. “(…) at the dawn of the recombinant DNA era, the most important lesson learned was that public trust in science ultimately begins with and requires ongoing transparency and open discussion”14. There is a lesson here for those applying NGTs to plants.

We consider that there are grounds for optimism regarding the future of NGTs if the following conditions are met. First, NGTs are introduced openly and transparently, accompanied by proactive, agenda-setting public information campaigns, avoiding the stealthy and misconceived introduction of GM soya and maize in the 1990s. And secondly, NGTs are seen to deliver benefits for farmers, the public, and contribute to sustainable agriculture in developed and developing countries.
