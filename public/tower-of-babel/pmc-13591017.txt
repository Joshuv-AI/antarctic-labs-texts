Traumatic Sinus Pericranii Managed Surgically: A Case Report
Authors: Saman Asadi, Arad Iranmehr, Mohamad Shirani, Amin Rohani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSinus pericranii is a rare venous anomaly characterized by abnormal communication between extracranial veins and intracranial dural venous sinuses. Traumatic sinus pericranii is uncommon. We report a 35‐year‐old man who presented with a progressively enlarging scalp mass 8 years after head trauma. The lesion demonstrated positional variation, enlarging in the supine position and collapsing when upright, which is characteristic of sinus pericranii. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated an enhancing extracranial lesion associated with focal calvarial thinning and dural enhancement, findings consistent with sinus pericranii. The patient underwent surgical treatment through a bicoronal approach with coagulation of the venous lesion and obliteration of the transosseous venous communication using bone wax. The postoperative course was uneventful, and no recurrence was observed during follow‐up. This case highlights the characteristic clinical and radiological features of traumatic sinus pericranii and demonstrates that surgical management can be safe and effective in selected nondominant lesions.

Introduction

Sinus pericranii is a rare venous malformation involving anomalous communication between extracranial veins and intracranial dural venous sinuses [1]. It typically presents as a compressible scalp mass with size variation related to changes in intracranial venous pressure [1, 2]. While most cases are congenital, acquired forms following trauma have been reported [3]. We present a case of traumatic sinus pericranii successfully managed surgically.

Case History/Examination

A 35‐year‐old man presented with a painless scalp mass in the right high parietal region near the sagittal suture. The lesion had developed following a head trauma 8 years earlier and had shown progressive enlargement over time. The mass increased in size when the patient was supine and collapsed upon sitting or standing, a characteristic feature of sinus pericranii [1, 2]. There were no associated headaches, seizures, or focal neurological deficits. Neurological examination was normal.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Computed tomography demonstrated a subgaleal lesion over the right parietal bone with focal thinning of the adjacent calvarium (Figure 1). Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a 66 × 45 × 12 mm extracranial scalp lesion with internal contrast enhancement, associated with focal thinning of the underlying parietal bone and adjacent dural thickening and enhancement, findings highly suggestive of sinus pericranii (Figure 2) [4, 5]. Given the characteristic positional behavior and cross‐sectional imaging findings, with no clinical or radiological features suggesting significant or dominant intracranial venous drainage, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was deferred. The decision was made with consideration of the potential risks and limited additional diagnostic benefit of invasive angiographic evaluation in this selected case.

Due to the progressive enlargement of the lesion, surgical intervention was planned. The patient underwent surgery under general anesthesia using a bicoronal skin incision. A supraperiosteal scalp flap was elevated, and the venous lesion was identified and coagulated using bipolar cautery. Multiple feeding vessels were carefully controlled. A small osseous defect was identified, through which a transosseous venous channel was supplying the lesion. The calvarium was preserved, and the transosseous communication was sealed using bone wax. No dominant venous drainage into the superior sagittal sinus was encountered [2, 6]. The differential diagnosis of SP includes several vascular and nonvascular scalp lesions. Chronic ossified cephalhematoma and intradiploic hematoma should also be considered in the differential diagnosis of scalp and calvarial masses following head trauma, particularly when imaging demonstrates calvarial remodeling or expansile skull lesions [7]. Conditions such as subgaleal hematoma, dermoid cyst, pseudomeningocele, growing skull fracture, vascular malformations, scalp cavernous hemangioma, subepicranial varix, and arteriovenous fistula may mimic SP clinically [8, 9]. Subepicranial varix is particularly important because it may present as a compressible scalp mass with positional variation but lacks communication with the intracranial venous sinuses [8]. Careful radiological assessment is therefore essential to establish the diagnosis and avoid inappropriate intervention.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient remained neurologically intact and was discharged without complications. At 5‐month follow‐up, there was no evidence of recurrence or new neurological symptoms.

Traumatic sinus pericranii is an uncommon but important differential diagnosis in patients presenting with position‐dependent scalp masses after head trauma. Careful clinical and radiological evaluation is essential for diagnosis. In selected nondominant lesions with progressive enlargement, surgical treatment can be performed safely with favorable results [2, 6, 10].

Discussion

Sinus pericranii (SP) is a rare venous anomaly characterized by abnormal communication between extracranial venous structures and intracranial dural venous sinuses through dilated diploic or emissary veins traversing the calvarium [1, 2]. The lesion most commonly involves the superior sagittal sinus and typically presents as a soft, compressible scalp mass with positional variation related to changes in intracranial venous pressure [1, 2]. Although congenital forms account for the majority of reported cases, traumatic SP represents an uncommon acquired subtype and remains poorly understood because of its rarity [3, 11].

The pathophysiology of traumatic sinus pericranii is thought to involve injury to diploic veins, emissary veins, or adjacent dural venous sinuses following cranial trauma [3, 11]. Over time, progressive venous dilation and remodeling may result in the development of abnormal transosseous venous channels connecting the extracranial venous system to the intracranial dural sinuses [3, 12]. In many reported traumatic cases, there is a prolonged interval between the initial trauma and clinical presentation, suggesting a gradual hemodynamic evolution rather than an acute process [3, 11]. In the present patient, the lesion developed after head trauma and progressively enlarged over an eight‐year period, supporting the hypothesis of delayed venous remodeling and chronic enlargement of the anomalous venous communication.

Clinically, SP typically presents as a nonpulsatile, soft, fluctuant scalp swelling that enlarges in the supine position or during Valsalva maneuver and collapses in the upright position due to alterations in intracranial venous pressure [1, 2, 5]. This positional dependency is considered one of the hallmark clinical features and was clearly observed in our patient. Neurological symptoms are uncommon, and most lesions are discovered because of cosmetic concerns or progressive enlargement [2]. However, symptomatic cases associated with headache, raised intracranial pressure, thrombosis, hemorrhage, or venous hypertension have been described [13, 14]. Rarely, dominant venous drainage through the lesion may occur, making treatment hazardous because interruption of venous outflow can result in venous infarction or catastrophic cerebral edema [5].

Radiological evaluation plays a crucial role in the diagnosis and surgical planning of SP. Computed tomography may demonstrate focal calvarial thinning, osseous erosion, diploic enlargement, or transosseous venous channels beneath the lesion [2, 8]. Three‐dimensional CT imaging has been shown to be particularly useful in delineating bony defects and multiple diploic channels associated with SP. [8] Magnetic resonance imaging and MR venography are highly valuable for evaluating the relationship between the lesion, the diploic veins, dura, and intracranial venous sinuses [4, 5]. Typical MRI findings include a strongly enhancing extracranial vascular lesion associated with adjacent calvarial thinning and enhancement of the underlying dura.3 In our patient, MRI demonstrated a contrast‐enhancing extracranial lesion with associated calvarial thinning and dural enhancement, findings highly suggestive of SP.

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) remains the gold standard for evaluating venous drainage patterns and identifying whether the lesion represents dominant or accessory venous outflow [5, 15]. Gandolfo et al. classified SP lesions into “dominant” and “accessory” types according to their role in cerebral venous drainage [5, 15]. In dominant lesions, the anomalous venous pathway constitutes a significant route of intracranial venous outflow, whereas accessory lesions contribute minimally to overall cerebral venous drainage [5]. This distinction has major therapeutic implications because treatment of dominant lesions may lead to severe venous complications [5]. In selected cases with characteristic clinical and MRI findings and no evidence suggesting dominant venous drainage, however, angiography may not be mandatory [4, 5]. In the present case, DSA was not performed because the patient had a characteristic position‐dependent scalp lesion, cross‐sectional imaging findings strongly suggestive of sinus pericranii, and no clinical or radiological evidence suggesting dominant intracranial venous drainage. The decision to defer invasive angiographic evaluation was therefore made on a case‐by‐case basis. Importantly, intraoperative inspection did not reveal a dominant venous drainage pathway to the superior sagittal sinus, supporting the safety of interruption of the abnormal transosseous communication. Nevertheless, DSA should be considered when the venous drainage pattern is uncertain or when imaging raises concern for dominant intracranial venous outflow.

Management strategies for SP remain controversial because of the rarity of the condition and the variable hemodynamic significance of individual lesions [2, 5]. Conservative observation may be appropriate for asymptomatic and stable lesions, particularly in pediatric patients or in lesions demonstrating dominant venous drainage [5, 16]. Some reports have documented spontaneous thrombosis or regression during conservative follow‐up [16]. However, surgical treatment is generally recommended for lesions associated with progressive enlargement, cosmetic deformity, pain, hemorrhage risk, thrombosis, or psychological distress [2, 6, 10]. In traumatic lesions, progressive enlargement over time may indicate persistent venous communication and increasing venous flow, supporting consideration of operative management.

Several surgical techniques have been described in the literature. Earlier reports advocated craniectomy with excision of the involved calvarium and subsequent cranioplasty to completely eliminate the abnormal venous communication [8]. More recent studies, however, suggest that limited surgery with interruption of emissary or diploic venous channels and obliteration of transosseous communications may be sufficient in carefully selected cases [6, 8]. Deshmukh et al. emphasized the importance of preserving normal venous drainage while disconnecting the abnormal extracranial venous pouch [6]. In our patient, the calvarium was preserved, and the transosseous venous communication was successfully sealed using bone wax after coagulation of the extracranial venous lesion. This less invasive approach resulted in excellent cosmetic and clinical outcomes without recurrence.

Intraoperative management of SP requires meticulous attention because massive venous hemorrhage and venous air embolism are recognized complications [6, 8]. The abnormal venous channels may communicate directly with major intracranial dural sinuses, particularly the superior sagittal sinus, and uncontrolled bleeding can occur during dissection [8]. Preoperative planning should therefore include availability of blood products and careful positioning strategies. Elevation of the head may reduce venous pressure and decrease intraoperative bleeding; however, excessive head elevation may increase the risk of venous air embolism because of negative venous pressure gradients [8]. David et al. reported intraoperative venous air embolism requiring aspiration from the right atrium in patients undergoing surgery for SP [8]. Consequently, careful anesthetic monitoring and gradual operative dissection are essential.

Histopathological examination of SP typically reveals dilated venous channels lined by flattened endothelium embedded within fibroconnective tissue [6, 11]. These findings are nonspecific but help exclude neoplastic or high‐flow vascular lesions. In the present case, histopathology demonstrated fibroconnective tissue with proliferation of venous structures consistent with a benign venous malformation.

The current case shares several similarities with previously reported traumatic SP cases. Amano et al. described a patient with delayed progressive frontal swelling years after childhood head trauma successfully treated surgically [11]. Similarly, Zhang et al. recently reported a frontal traumatic SP in a young adult managed with coronal incision and bone wax obliteration of the transosseous venous channel [12]. Kuyama et al. described a pediatric traumatic SP following head injury requiring surgical excision because of persistent enlargement and hemorrhagic risk [17]. These reports, together with the present case, support the concept that traumatic SP may exhibit delayed progression over years and can be effectively managed surgically in selected nondominant lesions. The present case further emphasizes several important practical considerations. First, traumatic SP should be considered in patients presenting with a slowly enlarging, position‐dependent scalp mass after head trauma, even many years after the initial injury. Second, MRI and CT findings may be sufficiently characteristic to establish the diagnosis in selected patients without mandatory angiography. Third, careful surgical interruption of the transosseous venous communication without extensive craniectomy may provide durable control with low morbidity in nondominant lesions.

An important limitation of the present report is the relatively short follow‐up period of 5 months. Although no clinical or radiological evidence of recurrence was observed during this interval, the available follow‐up is insufficient to determine the long‐term durability of treatment or to exclude late recurrence. Longer term follow‐up would be desirable in future reports of surgically treated traumatic sinus pericranii.

Because of the rarity of traumatic SP, there is currently no standardized management algorithm. Decisions regarding treatment should be individualized according to lesion size, symptoms, cosmetic impact, venous drainage pattern, and operative risk. Further accumulation of case reports and multicenter experiences will be necessary to better define the optimal management strategy and long‐term outcomes of this rare vascular anomaly.
