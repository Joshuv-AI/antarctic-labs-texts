Periodontitis and renal function decline in type 2 diabetes: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Risako Mikami, Koji Mizutani, Miho Ishimaru, Isao Minami, Daisuke Kido, Kohei Takeda, Hiromi Kominato, Natsumi Saito, Shu Takemura, Keita Nakagawa, Koichiro Matsuo, Yuichi Izumi, Yoshihiro Ogawa, Takanori Iwata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the effect of periodontitis on renal function in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) and to explore the potential underlying pathways. A cohort of 73 patients with poorly controlled T2D who received diabetes care was monitored for six months. The association between the changes in renal function and periodontal parameters at baseline was assessed using regression analysis. Causal mediation analysis was performed to examine the effect of periodontitis on renal function mediated by glycemic control. Of the participants, 57 (78.0%) had stage III or IV periodontitis. Hemoglobin A1c levels improved significantly, from 9.5 ± 1.9% to 7.6 ± 1.4% while estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) declined from 76.8 ± 23.7 to 71.8 ± 21.6 mL/min/1.73 m2 (p < 0.001). eGFR decline was significantly associated with periodontitis stage, periodontal epithelial surface area, and periodontal inflamed surface area. Glycemic control did not significantly mediate this association between periodontitis and eGFR decline, whereas the direct effect of periodontitis remained significant. These findings suggest that periodontitis may contribute to renal function decline in patients with T2D, partly through pathways independent of glycemic control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25309-5.Keywords: Periodontitis, Diabetes mellitus type 2, Diabetic nephropathies, Glomerular filtration rate, Causal mediation analysisSubject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Medical research, Nephrology

Introduction

Periodontitis has well-documented bidirectional relationship with diabetes1,2 and has been recognized as a possible contributor to the onset and worsening of microvascular complications among individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D)3,4. A meta-analysis reported that periodontitis significantly increases the risk of diabetic nephropathy, with an odds ratio of 1.75 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.07 − 2.85)4. However, these findings are based on cross-sectional studies which are inherently limited in establishing causal relationships. To date, only one longitudinal study has examined the association between periodontitis and renal function in patients with diabetes. This study, conducted in the Pima Indian Community in the United States, found that periodontitis is a risk factor for the development of overt nephropathy and for the incidence of end-stage renal disease in patients with T2D5. While this study provides valuable insights, it has limitations in explaining causal relationships and in the generalizability of its findings to other populations because of the specific characteristics of the study cohort.

Two potential pathways have been proposed through which periodontitis may influence renal function. The first is an indirect pathway, wherein periodontitis adversely affects glycemic control, thereby accelerating diabetic complications and contributing to renal function impairment. The second hypothesis suggests a more direct effect of periodontitis on the renal tissue, in which periodontopathic bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPS), inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress associated with periodontal disease may directly damage the kidneys. However, the precise mechanisms underlying the relationship between periodontal disease and renal dysfunction, particularly the relative contributions of the direct and indirect pathways, remain poorly understood.

Previously, both in vitro and in vivo studies have been conducted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms linking periodontal disease and renal dysfunction6–8. These studies have provided insights into the potential mechanisms of virulence factors associated with periodontopathic bacteria under diabetic conditions. However, such experimental models are limited in capturing the complexity of multifactorial chronic diseases such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD), which complicates the translations of findings to clinical settings. To address these limitations, we employed a causal mediation analysis, a methodological approach within the causal inference framework that allows for the disentanglement of the direct and indirect effects of periodontitis on renal function. By decomposing the total effect into direct and indirect effects, this approach enables identification and quantification of the mediating mechanisms underlying these associations. Based on the hypothesis that periodontitis contributes to renal function decline through both glycemic-mediated and direct pathways, we employed causal mediation analysis to investigate these effects and explore the mechanisms linking diabetic nephropathy and periodontal disease.

The objective of this prospective cohort study was to evaluate the association between periodontal disease and renal function decline in patients with T2D, with a focus on the mediating role of glycemic control.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This six-month follow-up cohort study was conducted at Tokyo Medical and Dental University Hospital (Tokyo, Japan). Patients with poorly controlled T2D scheduled for hospitalization were enrolled between October 2013 and April 2015.9,10 The participants underwent a comprehensive baseline examination, including dental assessments, followed by a two-week hospitalization for intensive diabetes management. Subsequently, they attended regular outpatient follow-up visits. At the six-month follow-up point, the participants underwent subsequent examinations. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of T2D, (2) age ≥ 20 years, and (3) completion of a six-month follow-up. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe renal impairment, defined by an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 or the implementation of renal replacement therapy; (2) the presence of severe infections or critical trauma; and (3) edentulism. Owing to the exploratory nature of this study, sample sizes were not calculated before the commencement of the investigation. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Tokyo Medical and Dental University (Medical Hospital #1573 and Dental Hospital #993) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. This trial was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network (https://www.umin.ac.jp/clinical trial number: UMIN000040218). All the participants provided written informed consent to participate in the study.

Clinical examination

Medical examinations were performed according to a previously described methodology9. Briefly, anthropometric measurements, blood tests, and urine tests were conducted at baseline and after six months. Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels were measured according to the National Glycohaemoglobin Standardization Program11.

Dental examinations were conducted at baseline as described in previous studies10,12. An experienced periodontist performed the following dental examinations: recording oral hygiene status using the O’Leary plaque control record (PCR)13, and examination of periodontal health, such as tooth mobility, probing depth (PD), and bleeding on probing (BOP), at six sites on all remaining teeth. Periodontitis is classified into five categories, namely periodontal health/gingivitis and periodontitis stages I, II, III, and IV, according to the 2018 American Academy of Periodontology and European Federation of Periodontology classification system14. The periodontal epithelial surface area (PESA) and periodontal inflamed surface area (PISA) were calculated based on the methodology described in a previous study15.

Medical interventions

Intensive diabetes care included a two-week hospitalization period, followed by regular outpatient follow-up visits9. All participants were instructed to make lifestyle modifications and prescribed outpatient medications. The components of lifestyle guidance varied according to the plans discussed among patients, dieticians, and physicians. Briefly, patients were advised to restrict their daily caloric intake to < 25–30 kcal/kg of ideal body weight, calculated as 22 x (height in meters)2, and to exercise for 30 min or more at least three times a week. After discharge, the participants continued to receive medical therapy (e.g., insulin, biguanides, and incretin-based agents) and lifestyle counselling in the outpatient setting.

Outcome variables

Changes in urine albumin levels and eGFR over the six-month follow-up period were used as the outcome variables. These parameters were selected because they are established markers of the diagnosis and progression of diabetic nephropathy16. Patients with diabetic nephropathy typically present with albuminuria and reduced glomerular filtration rate, both of which are consequences of hyperglycemia and impaired endothelial function. The eGFR was quantified using the following equation specific to the Japanese population, as proposed by the Japanese Society of Nephrology17: eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2) = 194 × SCr^-1.094 × age^-0.287 (× 0.739 if female), where SCr denotes serum creatinine in mg/dL, measured using an enzymatic method.

Exposure variable and mediator

The exposure variable in this study was periodontal disease status. Periodontal disease was assessed using three variables: periodontitis stage, PESA score at baseline, and PISA score at baseline. The stage classification of periodontitis was categorized into two groups: periodontal health/gingivitis/stage I/II and stage III/IV. As no universally accepted thresholds exist for PESA and PISA, these variables were categorized into tertiles, resulting in three groups: low, middle, and high.

Additionally, a causal mediation analysis was conducted to evaluate the effect of periodontitis on the decline in eGFR, with glycemic control serving as a mediating variable. The mediator variable was the HbA1c level at baseline.

Covariates

Possible confounders were included as covariates based on previous studies and clinical knowledge. These included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, hypertension, HbA1c level, and eGFR at baseline.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean values and standard deviations or as median, 25th, and 75th percentiles, whereas categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. The paired t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and Mann-Whitney U test were used to compare the two groups. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the association between the two variables. The association between periodontal status and the six-month change in eGFR was evaluated using linear regression analysis. To confirm the robustness of the model estimates, we performed bootstrap resampling (2000 replications) for the multivariable regression models presented in Table 2 and obtained 95% confidence intervals and p-values.

Figure 1 illustrates the hypothesized causal diagram investigated in this study. We conducted a causal mediation analysis to assess the effect of periodontitis on eGFR decline, with glycemic control serving as the mediating variable.18 This approach decomposed the total effect of periodontal disease on eGFR into two components: natural indirect effect (NIE) and natural direct effect (NDE). NIE represents the portion of the effect mediated through the glycemic control status indicated by the HbA1c level at baseline, whereas NDE reflects the effect of periodontitis on eGFR independent of glycemic control. We also calculated the mediated proportion, representing the extent to which NIE explained the proportion of the total effect. Linear regression models were fitted for both the outcome and mediator.

Directed acyclic graph illustrating the causal relationships in the present study. Based on the potential outcomes framework, we hypothesized that periodontitis contributes to poor glycemic control and subsequently increases the risk of renal function decline. Potential confounders affecting the relationships among the exposure, outcome, and mediators were also considered.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA software (version 18.0; Stata Corp LP, USA).

Results

Of the 86 recruited patients, 73 were included in the analysis (Fig. 2). The participant demographics are shown in Table 1. Of the 73 participants, 50 were male, and the mean age was 59.7 ± 12.9 years. The mean duration of T2D was 10.8 ± 8.5 years, and the mean HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose, and C-peptide index were 9.5 ± 1.9%, 155.7 ± 53.7 mg/dL, and 1.2 ± 0.9, respectively. The median urine albumin was 13.1 (7.0, 47.0), and the mean eGFR was 76.8 ± 23.7 ml/min/1.73 m2. Periodontal status was classified as periodontal health/gingivitis in four individuals and as stages I, II, III, and IV periodontitis in one, 11, 25, and 32 individuals, respectively. The mean periodontal epithelial surface area (PESA) was 929.3 ± 381.7 mm2 and the median of periodontal inflamed surface area (PISA) was 170.2 (51.6, 349.2) mm2. At baseline, 19 participants (26.0%) had an eGFR of < 60 ml/min/1.73m2. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, there were no significant differences in dental health status, including the number of teeth, periodontal stage distribution, PCR, BOP, PESA, and PISA, between participants with eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73m2 and those with eGFR ≥ 60 ml/min/1.73m2.

Insulin injections and biguanides were predominantly prescribed during the follow-up period (Supplementary Table 2). Supplementary Table 3 presents the six-month changes in diabetes-related parameters. After six months, HbA1c levels significantly decreased from 9.5 ± 1.9 to 7.6 ± 1.4% (p < 0.001) and urine albumin concentration significantly increased from 13.1 (7, 47) to 27.4 (7, 84) mg/dL (p = 0.009). The eGFR significantly decreased from 76.8 ± 23.7 to 71.8 ± 21.6 ml/min/1.73 m2 (p < 0.001; Supplementary Table 3).

Associations between the changes in renal markers and periodontal parameters were also examined. No significant association was observed between the six-month changes in urine albumin levels and the stages of periodontitis, PESA, or PISA at baseline (Fig. 3a, b, and c). Conversely, the six-month change in eGFR was significantly associated with the severity of periodontitis at baseline. Participants with stage III/IV periodontitis displayed significantly greater reductions in eGFR than those with periodontal health/gingivitis or stage I/II periodontitis (Fig. 3d; p = 0.035). Moreover, PESA and PISA at baseline exhibited significant negative associations with changes in the eGFR (Fig. 3e and f; Spearman’s rho = − 0.29 and − 0.25, p = 0.013 and 0.032, respectively). Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that the association between the stages of periodontitis and the six-month changes in eGFR was significant, even after adjusting for confounders such as age, sex, smoking status, body mass index, HbA1c, and eGFR at baseline (Table 2). The participants with stage III/IV periodontitis exhibited a significantly greater decrease in eGFR (6.0 ml/min/1.73 m2) after six months compared to those with periodontal health/gingivitis or stage I/II periodontitis (Table 2, Multivariate model 1, 95% CI: − 11.2, − 0.8, p = 0.025). This association remained robust in the bootstrap analyses (95%CI: -11.4, -0.6, p = 0.028). The participants with high tertile of PESA and PISA at baseline showed significantly higher decrease in eGFR by 6.9 (95% CI: − 13.2, − 0.6, p = 0.033) and 5.8 (95% CI: − 11.6, − 0.05, p = 0.048) ml/min/1.73 m2 after six-month follow-up compared to those with a low tertile of PESA and PISA (Table 2, Multivariate models 2 and 3). These associations also remained robust in bootstrap analyses (PESA: 95% CI: − 12.0, − 1.8, p = 0.008; PISA: 95% CI: − 11.4, − 0.2, p = 0.041). For sensitivity analysis, we treated PESA and PISA as continuous variables in the regression models. For PESA (per 100 mm2 increase), the coefficient for eGFR change was − 0.8 (95% CI: − 1.4 to − 0.2, p = 0.013, Supplementary Table 5, Multivariate model 1). For PISA (per 100 mm2 increase), the coefficient was − 1.0 (95% CI: − 1.9 to − 0.03, p = 0.043, Supplementary Table 5, Multivariate model 2). These results are consistent with those obtained using the tertile categories. In the additional analyses using conventional periodontal parameters (sites with PD ≥ 4 mm, mean full-mouth PD, and BOP), similar trends were observed, although these associations were not statistically significant (Supplementary Table 6). No significant association was observed between periodontal parameters and changes in urine albumin levels in the multivariate analysis, as in the univariate analysis (Supplementary Table 7).

The results of the causal mediation analysis are summarized in Table 3. For the exposure variables—stage III/IV periodontitis, high tertile of PISA, and high tertile of PESA—the total effects were − 6.61 (95% CI: -12.11, -1.12; p = 0.018), -7.04 (95% CI: -10.63, -3.47; p < 0.001), and − 4.34 (95% CI: -8.19, -0.49; p = 0.027), respectively. The NDEs for these variables were − 6.25 (95% CI: -11.59, -0.91; p = 0.022), -5.88 (95% CI: -10.23, -1.53; p = 0.008), and − 3.95 (95% CI: -7.88, -0.11; p = 0.049), respectively. However, the NIEs for all variables were not statistically significant. Their proportions are 5.4%, 16.6%, and 9.0%, respectively. In all the models, the total effect and NDE were statistically significant, whereas NIE was not. In an additional analysis using a six-month change in HbA1c level as a mediator, the indirect effect of periodontitis on eGFR decline through HbA1c changes was also negligible (Supplementary Table 8).

Discussion

In this study, periodontitis severity was significantly associated with a decline in eGFR among patients with T2D, even after adjusting for confounding factors. Additionally, the mediating effect of glycemic control on the association between periodontitis and renal function decline was small and not statistically significant. These findings suggest that periodontitis may be linked to renal function decline in patients with poorly controlled T2D, through mechanisms independent of glycemic control. This raises the possibility that periodontal disease may have a direct effect on renal function.

Previous large-scale clinical studies have demonstrated that albuminuria and low eGFR are key risk factors for the progression of cardiovascular disease and renal failure in patients with diabetes.19 Regular monitoring of these parameters and proper control of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and blood glucose levels are recommended to prevent the deterioration of renal function in patients with diabetes. In this study, intensive diabetes care was generally used to control blood pressure, lipid metabolism, and blood glucose levels9, but did not prevent the decline in renal function in some participants. This discrepancy suggests that non-glycemic factors, such as the chronic inflammatory burden associated with periodontitis, may contribute to renal deterioration.

There is accumulating evidence regarding the effects of periodontitis on kidney disease.20,21 Cohort studies in the general population have demonstrated that patients with periodontitis are at increased risk of renal function decline.22,23 A recent meta-analysis24 reported that individuals with periodontitis have a notably elevated likelihood of developing CKD. Despite this, limited research has focused on the effects of periodontitis on renal function in patients with diabetes. A prospective cohort study conducted in the Pima Indian population in the United States found that periodontitis was associated with an increased risk of both overt diabetic nephropathy and progression to end-stage renal disease in patients with T2D.5 Another study identified periodontitis as a significant predictor of diabetes-related mortality due to nephropathy.25 Although these findings are valuable, their generalizability may be limited, as factors involved in the development and progression of diabetes, such as glucose tolerance, vary widely across ethnic and racial groups.26 Therefore, investigating these associations in various ethnic and clinical contexts is crucial. Our study addresses this knowledge gap by providing evidence from a cohort of Japanese patients with poorly controlled T2D.

Notably, the severity of periodontitis was not associated with albuminuria, but was linked to a greater decline in eGFR. Traditionally, clinical diagnosis of diabetic nephropathy has relied on the presence of albuminuria. However, the prevalence of normoalbuminuric diabetic kidney disease (NADKD), a phenotype characterized by reduced GFR in the absence of albuminuria, has recently increased16. This form of nephropathy, which is often linked to nephrosclerotic changes rather than glomerular damage, is becoming more common, especially among older adults and those with well-controlled glycemia. Although the pathological mechanism of NADKD remains unclear, it involves not only renal dysfunction and renal hemodynamic abnormalities caused by persistent hyperglycemia but also a wide variety of risk factors, including obesity, smoking, stress, aging, and hypertension. In this study cohort, periodontitis rather than albuminuria was associated with a decline in eGFR. These findings suggest that periodontitis may be an important and underrecognized risk factor for NADKD.

One of the key strengths of this study was the application of causal mediation analysis, a statistical approach used to investigate the mechanisms by which an independent variable influences an outcome via a mediator. This method enabled us to decompose the total effect of periodontitis on eGFR into two components: NIE, which represents the effect mediated by glycemic control, and NDE, which reflects the effect independent of glycemic control. Using this analytical framework, we aimed to clarify the distinct pathways through which periodontitis influences renal function. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to use this method to explore the biological mechanisms linking periodontitis and diabetic nephropathy. Mediation analysis revealed that most of the effects of periodontitis on renal function decline occurred through a direct pathway rather than indirectly through glycemic control. This finding suggests that periodontitis directly affects renal tissues. Clinically, this implies that periodontitis should be considered as an independent risk factor for renal function decline, irrespective of glycemic control. Notably, this result diverged from our initial hypothesis that glycemic control partially mediates the relationship between periodontitis and renal function decline. Therefore, the absence of a significant mediating effect is unanticipated. Because periodontitis is considered to exert cumulative effects on systemic inflammation, baseline HbA1c was used as the mediator in the primary analysis. However, we also considered the possibility that periodontitis might indirectly influence renal function by affecting glycemic control during diabetes treatment. Therefore, we examined the six-month change in HbA1c levels as an alternative mediator. This additional analysis showed that changes in HbA1c levels during follow-up contributed little to mediating the association between periodontitis and eGFR decline. These findings support the main results and suggest that the effect of periodontitis on renal function is minimally mediated through glycemic control. A possible explanation for this is that the study population had a long duration of T2D and poor glycemic control, necessitating intensive diabetes management. These characteristics may have limited the variability in glycemic control, leading to a ceiling effect that obscured any potential mediating role. To gain further insights into the underlying mechanisms, future large-scale studies involving populations with varying degrees of diabetes control and disease duration are warranted.

Our findings provide new insights into the role of local chronic inflammation, as both PESA and PISA at baseline were significantly associated with eGFR decline. These indices, especially PISA, reflect the extent of inflamed periodontal tissue, and therefore represent a cumulative measure of the systemic inflammatory burden. This finding supports the hypothesis that periodontitis-related inflammation plays a key role in mediating renal impairment in patients with diabetes because inflammation contributes to kidney fibrosis and a reduction in eGFR. Periodontitis is known to elevate systemic levels of inflammation27, and it is possible that this inflammation contributes to nephrosclerotic changes. Previous basic research has also suggested that pathogenic factors derived from periodontopathic bacteria, such as Porphyromonas gingivalis (P.gingivalis), contribute to renal tissue fibrosis under diabetic conditions. In vivo studies using diabetic animal models have indicated that LPS derived from P. gingivalis can trigger periodontitis, resulting in glomerular changes and subsequent kidney dysfunction.6,7 Sakaniwa et al. further reported that stimulation with LPS derived from P. gingivalis triggers cellular transformation and the expression of fibrosis markers in mesangial cells, the primary structural cells of renal tissue, under hyperglycemic conditions8. Notably, the direct effects of periodontitis observed in this study may not be solely attributed to systemic inflammation. Additional mechanisms, including oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, may also be involved. Future studies should aim to achieve a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding by incorporating relevant biomarkers that reflect the pathophysiological pathways.

This study had several limitations. First, the number of participants in this single-center study was relatively small, which may have contributed to the wider confidence intervals in the analyses. However, bootstrap analyses confirmed consistent estimates, suggesting that the observed association between periodontitis and eGFR decline was robust, despite the limited sample size. Additionally, owing to the small sample size, prescribed medications could not be analyzed. Recently, the renoprotective effects of novel agents such as sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors28, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists29, and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors30 have been reported and warrant further large-scale investigations. Second, the effect of modifications in periodontal inflammation on renal function during the follow-up period was unknown. We previously demonstrated that the periodontal inflammation status improves with intensive diabetes care12. As periodontitis gradually progressed over the past decade, we examined the effect of periodontitis severity at baseline. It has been hypothesized that periodontitis before the baseline may have cumulative effects on the kidneys. Third, because we evaluated the change in eGFR after intensive diabetes care in participants with poorly controlled T2D, the apparent eGFR may have been lower owing to glomerular hyperfiltration, dehydration, or other effects, such as hypotensive drugs. Although we performed multivariate analysis with adjustments for possible confounders, a wide variety of factors affected renal function, and some confounders may not have been fully adjusted for. Fourth, the results of this study should be interpreted considering the characteristics of the study population. The participants did not have extremely severe periodontitis, and their renal function was moderately reduced, but not at the level of advanced kidney disease. Therefore, these findings may not be generalizable to populations with severe periodontal destruction or advanced renal impairment. Due to the limited sample size, stratified analyses according to baseline eGFR were not feasible in this study. However, future studies with larger populations should further investigate the association in participants with eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73m2, which corresponds to the diagnostic threshold for chronic kidney disease. Despite these limitations, our study underscores the clinical importance of periodontal assessment and management in patients with T2D. These findings suggest that maintaining periodontal health may contribute to renal protection independent of glycemic control. Further large-scale longitudinal and interventional studies are required to determine whether periodontal therapy slows the progression of renal dysfunction and reduces the burden of diabetic kidney disease.

Conclusions

Periodontitis may be associated with a decline in renal function in patients with T2D. These results suggest that the pathway through which periodontitis directly affects renal function might play a dominant role in patients with T2D, rather than the pathway mediated by glycemic control, although further studies are warranted to clarify the extent of any direct effects. These findings emphasize the importance of integrating periodontal care into the multidisciplinary management of diabetes to help prevent systemic complications such as CKD.
