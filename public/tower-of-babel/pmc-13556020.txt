Fathers’ attitudes toward breastfeeding, their participation, and influence: a cross-sectional study in Turkey
Authors: Emine Bayrak Aykan, Tutku Kırcı Temiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough breastfeeding is strongly recommended for optimal maternal and infant health, global rates remain suboptimal. Fathers play an important role in supporting breastfeeding through their attitudes and participation. However, evidence on how fathers’ attitudes, participation, and influence affect breastfeeding practices and maternal decision-making regarding breastfeeding continuation and cessation is limited. Understanding these relationships can inform family centered interventions and strategies to improve breastfeeding outcomes, highlighting the practical significance of this study beyond contributing to the literature. This study aimed to examine fathers’ attitudes toward breastfeeding, their level of participation in the breastfeeding process, and their influence on breastfeeding.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in Turkey and included 269 fathers who met the following inclusion criteria: agreeing to participate in the research, living in Turkey, being able to read and understand Turkish, and having a baby aged 0–24 months. Data were collected using the Sociodemographic Data Collection Form, the Fathers’ Breastfeeding Attitude and Participation Scale, and the Partner Breastfeeding Influence Scale. Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ characteristics and scale scores. Pearson correlation examined relationships between the scales. Simple linear regression examined the association between fathers’ attitudes and both participation and influence, while multiple linear regression assessed the associations between sociodemographic factors and fathers’ influence on breastfeeding. Significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsFathers’ breastfeeding attitudes were predominantly negative, and participation was generally low, whereas influence scores indicated a relatively higher level of influence on breastfeeding. Attitudes were moderately positively correlated with participation (r = 0.539) and weakly with influence (r = 0.177). Regression analysis showed that attitudes were significantly associated with both participation (β = 0.539, p < 0.001) and influence (β = 0.177, p = 0.004), with a stronger association for participation. Higher education was associated with greater influence, whereas participation in breastfeeding education was unexpectedly linked to lower influence scores. Fathers’ attitudes were associated with their participation in breastfeeding, while training attendance showed a lower than expected association with influence scores.ConclusionFathers’ attitudes toward breastfeeding were significantly associated with both breastfeeding participation and influence on breastfeeding. Introduction

Breast milk is an ideal food with high bioavailability that meets all the nutritional needs of infants during the first six months of life [1]. The World Health Organization recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, followed by the introduction of complementary foods while continuing breastfeeding up to two years of age or beyond [2]. Breastfeeding provides substantial health benefits for both infants and mothers. For infants, it reduces the risk of asthma, obesity, type 1 diabetes, and sudden infant death syndrome, while for mothers, it offers protection against various health conditions [3–5]. Despite these well documented benefits, global exclusive breastfeeding rates remain suboptimal; only 40% of infants under six months are exclusively breastfed worldwide [6].

In Turkey, breastfeeding initiation is nearly universal (98%); however, only 41% of infants under six months are exclusively breastfed, and the median duration of exclusive breastfeeding is 1.8 months [7]. Although initiation rates are high, sustaining exclusive breastfeeding for the recommended six months remains a significant public health concern [8, 9]. Several factors are associated with shorter breastfeeding duration, including maternal education, workplace support, and fathers’ participation in breastfeeding support. These factors may affect both breastfeeding duration and exclusivity, highlighting the need to better understand these relationships [10, 11]. Sociocultural factors play a crucial role in shaping breastfeeding practices in Turkey. In a predominantly family oriented social structure, extended family members, particularly grandmothers, may influence infant feeding decisions, sometimes contributing to early supplementation [8, 9].

In addition to the influence of extended family members, increasing evidence highlights the significant role of fathers in breastfeeding outcomes. Paternal attitudes and support are associated with breastfeeding initiation, duration, and maternal breastfeeding self-efficacy [12, 13]. Fathers serve as a primary source of social support for mothers, and their participation in breastfeeding education and counseling has been reported to be associated with longer exclusive breastfeeding duration [14, 15]. Furthermore, mothers who receive active spousal support experience fewer breastfeeding problems and manage breastfeeding challenges more effectively [16].

Breastfeeding behaviors are influenced by multiple factors, including early skin to skin contact, maternal employment, health conditions, and lifestyle factors [17]. However, social and interpersonal determinants, particularly paternal attitudes, are increasingly recognized as key contributors to maternal decision making and breastfeeding continuity [18, 19]. Accordingly, this study also examines the association between fathers’ sociodemographic characteristics (age, education level, employment status, and number of children) and their influence on breastfeeding. This study addresses a gap in the literature by simultaneously examining fathers’ attitudes, participation, and influence on breastfeeding within a single framework, while also exploring their associations with sociodemographic characteristics. The findings are expected to contribute to a better understanding of fathers’ participation in the breastfeeding process and inform the development of targeted breastfeeding interventions and policies. This study aimed to examine fathers’ attitudes toward breastfeeding, their participation in the breastfeeding process, and their influence on breastfeeding, as well as their associations with sociodemographic characteristics.

Theoretical framework

This study was conceptually informed by Social Support Theory, which emphasizes the role of interpersonal relationships in shaping health behaviors, including breastfeeding practices [20]. Social support is widely recognized as a multidimensional construct, encompassing emotional, informational, and instrumental components that may influence individuals’ health related decisions and behaviors [20, 21].

Social Support Theory posits that support received from significant others promotes positive health behaviors and facilitates coping with stress [20]. Within the breastfeeding context, fathers may represent a primary source of support for mothers, potentially influencing breastfeeding practices and experiences.

In this study, fathers’ attitudes toward breastfeeding, their participation in the breastfeeding process, and their influence on breastfeeding were examined. Although these variables were not directly developed based on Social Support Theory, they can be discussed in relation to social support theory, as they reflect different aspects of paternal support. Empirical evidence indicates that active paternal involvement is associated with longer breastfeeding duration, improved maternal breastfeeding self-efficacy, and more positive breastfeeding experiences [21–24]. Therefore, Social Support Theory provides a useful conceptual framework for interpreting the relationships among these variables.

Methods

Study design

This descriptive cross-sectional study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [25].

Study setting

The study was conducted in Turkey using an online survey design. Data were collected via a questionnaire administered through Google Forms. This format enabled the researchers to reach fathers across the country and allowed participants to complete the survey remotely via an online link distributed through social media platforms. This study was performed with fathers in Turkey between 30 April and 30 October 2023.

Sampling

Population

A combined convenience and snowball sampling approach was used. The target population consisted of fathers with infants aged 0–24 months in Turkey. As there is no publicly accessible national registry specifying the exact number of fathers within this group, the population size was considered unknown. Eligible fathers who voluntarily consented to participate in the online survey were included in the study. Fathers were initially recruited through social media platforms such as WhatsApp and Instagram, particularly via parenting related groups, and participants were encouraged to share the survey link within their networks.

Study participants

The study participants included fathers living in Turkey who were able to read and understand Turkish and had an infant aged 0–24 months. Fathers were eligible regardless of age, provided they met this criterion. Fathers who agreed to participate in the study were included. Fathers with incomplete responses to the data collection form were excluded.

Sample size calculation

The minimum required sample size was calculated using the formula for unknown populations (n = Z²pq/d²), assuming a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, resulting in a target sample of 384 participants. Data collection was conducted via social media platforms due to the voluntary nature of participation and the inherent limitations of online recruitment. Of the 272 participants initially recruited, 3 were excluded due to incomplete responses, resulting in a final sample of 269 fathers.

Data collection procedure

A combined convenience and snowball sampling approach was used. The study purpose and eligibility criteria were announced by the researchers via social media platforms, including WhatsApp and Instagram, through posts and messages shared in parenting related groups and accounts. Participants who met the inclusion criteria were invited to complete the survey and to share the survey link with other eligible fathers within their social networks.

The survey link was disseminated primarily through parenting focused WhatsApp groups and Instagram accounts targeting fathers of infants. Approximately 1,200 individuals, as indicated by platform analytics, were reached through the initial distribution. Participation was voluntary, and only individuals who provided informed consent were able to access and complete the questionnaire.

To minimize selection bias, recruitment was conducted through multiple social media platforms and diverse parenting related groups to reach fathers from different backgrounds. To reduce respondent bias, participation was voluntary, and no identifying personal data were collected other than email addresses used solely to prevent duplicate responses. The questionnaire was self administered and included neutral wording to avoid leading responses. It included demographic and health-related questions such as “What is your age?”, “What is your educational level?”, “Are you currently employed?”, “How would you describe your income level (higher than expenses / equal / lower)?”, “How many children do you have?”, and “Have you received breastfeeding education?”. These demographic and health-related variables were collected to characterize the study sample and to examine their associations with fathers’ attitudes, participation, and influence on breastfeeding. Duplicate entries were prevented by enabling the limit to one response option in Google Forms. Incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the final analysis.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using a Sociodemographic Data Collection Form and two previously validated and reliable instruments: the Fathers’ Breastfeeding Attitude and Participation Scale and the Partner’s Influence on Breastfeeding Scale. No new questionnaire items were developed for this study, apart from the sociodemographic form, and all variables were assessed using existing standardized scales. These validated and reliable instruments were used due to their relevance to the study objectives and their ability to measure fathers’ attitudes, participation, and influence on breastfeeding.

Sociodemographic data collection form

It is a form consisting of 11 questions about sociodemographic characteristics created by the researchers by utilizing the literature [18, 26–29].

Fathers’ breastfeeding attitude and participation scale (F-BAPS)

The “Fathers’ Breastfeeding Attitude and Participation Scale” [27], which was developed by Abu-Abbas et al. (2016) and aims to determine fathers’ attitudes towards breastfeeding and fathers’ participation in the breastfeeding process, was adapted into Turkish by Uyar Hazar and Gültekin (2023) and a validity and reliability study was conducted. F-BAP consists of two sub-dimensions: “Fathers’ Attitudes Towards Breastfeeding (F-BA)” and “Fathers’ Participation In The Breastfeeding Process (F-PB)”. Each sub-dimension consists of 14 items and the scale consists of 28 items in total. In this scale, responses are evaluated on a five-point Likert scale as “1 = Strongly disagree”, “2 = Disagree”, “3 = Unsure”, “4 = Agree” and “5 = Strongly agree”. The total score to be obtained from the F-BA sub-dimension varies between 14 and 70 and the cut-off point of the scale is 58, as determined in the original scale development and validation study. Those with a total score of ≥ 58 are considered as positive attitudes and those with < 58 are considered as negative attitudes. The total score to be obtained from the F-PB subscale ranges between 14 and 70 and the cut-off point of the scale is 58. Those with a total score of ≥ 58 are considered to have good participation, while those with a total score of < 58 are considered to have poor participation. The F-BAPS can also be used separately as F-BA and F-PB. In both the original and the Turkish version of the scale, The Cronbach’s alpha values of the total F-BA (0.80) and F-PB (0.82) parts and the sub-dimensions in both parts were found to be above 0.70 and it was stated that the scale had a high level of reliability and internal consistency [27, 30]. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients in the present study were 0.67 for the F-BA subscale and 0.842 for the F-PB subscale. The F-BA value was slightly below the commonly accepted threshold of 0.70. However, it is considered acceptable in exploratory social science research where constructs may be multidimensional.

The terms used in the F-BAPS are defined as follows

Participation in the breastfeeding process: Fathers’ active engagement in behaviors that support and facilitate breastfeeding, including emotional encouragement, practical assistance (e.g., infant care and household responsibilities), involvement in decision making, and responsiveness to the mother’s needs during the breastfeeding period.

Attitude towards breastfeeding process: Fathers’ perceptions and beliefs regarding breastfeeding, including perceived benefits and acceptance of breastfeeding as an infant feeding method, which may influence maternal decision making and breastfeeding continuity.

Partner Breastfeeding Influence Scale; The “Partner Breastfeeding Influence Scale (PBIS)” developed by Rempel and Rempel (2011), the Turkish validity and reliability study of which was conducted by Buldur and Akçay Didişen (2024), consists of 37 items [31]. The scale evaluates how often the spouses do the activity that the mother does while breastfeeding her child with a Likert-type rating system from 1 to 5. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 = Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Frequently and 5 = Very often. The scale has five sub-dimensions. These are understanding breastfeeding, help, valuing, readiness and sensitivity. The breastfeeding comprehension sub-dimension includes statements about learning and discussing breastfeeding information, the help sub-dimension includes items about direct or indirect support such as housework support, child care and caring for the spouse in this process, the valuing sub-dimension includes the mother’s encouragement and valuing behaviors for breastfeeding, the readiness sub-dimension includes the impact of the father’s support during breastfeeding, and the sensitivity sub-dimension includes items indicating the father’s sensitivity to the mother’s needs and respect for the mother’s decisions. A high total score on the whole scale indicates that fathers have a high influence on breastfeeding. The lowest score that can be obtained from the scale is 37 and the highest score is 185. In the Turkish version of the scale, Cronbach’s alpha reliability value was 0.95. Cronbach’s alpha reliability values of the five sub-dimensions were 0.82, 0.83, 0.83, 0.83, 0.83, 0.75 [29, 31]. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient in the present study was 0.96.

The term used in the PBIS is defined as follows

Influence on breastfeeding: Fathers’ supportive behaviors that facilitate breastfeeding through assistance, readiness, and valuing, contributing to the breastfeeding process.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from Ankara Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (No: E-81477236-604.01.01-1307 Date: 12.04.2023). In addition, informed consent was obtained electronically via Google Forms from the fathers, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data analysis

Participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and scale scores were summarized with descriptive statistics using mean, standard deviation, frequency and percentage values. The normal distribution of continuous variables was evaluated with skewness and kurtosis values accepted within ± 1.5 [32]. The relationships between the study scales were analyzed by Pearson correlation analysis. Simple linear regression analyses were conducted to assess the associations between fathers’ breastfeeding attitudes, breastfeeding participation, and breastfeeding influence. In addition, multiple linear regression analysis was performed to examine the contribution of sociodemographic variables (age, education level, employment status, and number of children) to fathers’ breastfeeding influence. Sociodemographic variables were included in the multivariable regression model only when they met the required statistical assumptions and inclusion criteria for regression analysis. Variables that did not meet these criteria were excluded from the model. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 22, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 based on the observed correlation (r = 0.177) between PBIS and F-BA, with a sample size of 269 and α = 0.05 (two-tailed). The achieved power was calculated as 0.83, indicating that the sample size was adequate. However, this result was interpreted cautiously, as post-hoc power may provide limited additional information beyond effect sizes and statistical significance.

Discussion

It is reported that fathers’ active role in the breastfeeding process is significantly associated with mothers’ breastfeeding initiation and continuation decisions, as well as their breastfeeding experiences. Fathers assume unique roles as members of the breastfeeding process and contribute to breastfeeding maintenance by supporting mothers and sharing domestic responsibilities [31, 33]. In the present study, fathers’ roles were conceptualized within the Social Support Theory. Fathers’ participation in the breastfeeding process was defined as instrumental support, reflecting practical assistance; attitudes were associated with emotional and informational support, reflecting beliefs, motivation, and understanding; and influence on breastfeeding was linked to evaluative and informational support, reflecting guidance and reinforcement of breastfeeding decisions. Each finding was explicitly interpreted in relation to the corresponding dimension of social support. Fathers’ positive attitudes toward breastfeeding were primarily associated with emotional support [20, 21], reflecting encouragement and positive appraisal of breastfeeding. Fathers’ participation in the breastfeeding process was linked to instrumental support [22, 23], including practical assistance such as sharing household responsibilities and facilitating maternal rest. In addition, fathers’ influence on breastfeeding was associated with informational support [24], reflecting guidance, awareness, and reinforcement of appropriate breastfeeding practices. Overall, these dimensions of social support helped to explain the observed relationships among fathers’ attitudes toward breastfeeding, participation in the breastfeeding process, and influence on breastfeeding. The findings indicated that fathers generally held negative attitudes toward breastfeeding, demonstrated weak participation in the breastfeeding process, and showed relatively higher influence on breastfeeding.

Findings showed that fathers’ attitudes toward breastfeeding were generally below the threshold for positive attitudes (55.64 ± 6.55). This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting similar paternal attitudes toward breastfeeding [27, 34]. In addition, fathers demonstrated relatively higher scores in the cognitive, experiential, and emotional dimensions, indicating that they were knowledgeable and emotionally supportive. Mothers have reported that fathers’ emotional support increases motivation and persistence in breastfeeding [18, 35]. However, lower scores in the social structure dimension suggest that cultural norms, social environment, and family roles may limit positive attitude development. Literature also indicates that sociocultural factors contribute to negative paternal attitudes toward breastfeeding [27, 33]. Overall, fathers’ attitudes toward breastfeeding were associated with their participation in the breastfeeding process and influence on breastfeeding.

Fathers’ participation in the breastfeeding process was at a low level (56.04 ± 8.23), with 55.4% of participants reporting low participation. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting similarly weak paternal participation in breastfeeding [27, 34]. In addition, studies conducted with healthcare professionals have indicated that fathers often lack awareness of their role in breastfeeding and require ongoing encouragement and support [36]. Although fathers showed relatively higher scores in the dimensions of help, motivation, and sensitivity, indicating both physical and emotional support, this does not necessarily reflect adequate knowledge. Indeed, supportive attitudes may not always correspond to sufficient theoretical understanding [37, 38]. Consistently, lower scores in the understanding breastfeeding dimension further support this gap between practical participation and knowledge. Overall, fathers’ participation reflected their level of engagement in the breastfeeding process.

In the study, fathers’ influence on the breastfeeding process was above the midpoint of the scale (137.06 ± 29.62), consistent with previous findings reporting comparable levels of paternal influence on breastfeeding [18]. These results suggest that fathers’ influence on breastfeeding reflected a supportive role in the breastfeeding process. Fathers scored higher in the dimensions of assistance, readiness, and valuing, indicating both emotional and practical involvement in breastfeeding support. However, lower scores in understanding breastfeeding and sensitivity suggest limited knowledge and reduced empathetic engagement with mother–infant needs. This highlights the need for improvement in fathers’ knowledge and supportive awareness. These gaps may be addressed through structured breastfeeding education programs, antenatal and postnatal counseling involving fathers, hospital-based family-centered care practices, and digital or community-based educational interventions [39, 40]. Similarly, Çiftçi and Sönmez (2022) reported that fathers showed stronger performance in practical support dimensions, particularly in help-related behaviors. Overall, fathers’ influence on breastfeeding reflected supportive participation in the breastfeeding process while indicating areas for improvement in knowledge and sensitivity. The findings showed that, fathers’ attitudes toward breastfeeding explained a small proportion of variance in breastfeeding influence (3.1%). Similarly, Çiftçi and Sönmez (2022) reported a comparable weak association (2.9%), indicating consistency across studies. These findings suggest that the relationship between fathers’ attitudes and their influence on breastfeeding is limited. This weak association may be explained by the multifactorial nature of breastfeeding, which is shaped by maternal factors, sociocultural context, previous experiences, and the level of paternal involvement [39, 41, 42]. Overall, fathers’ attitudes appear to have only a weak direct relationship with their influence on breastfeeding.

According to the findings, fathers’ attitudes toward breastfeeding (F-BA) were significantly associated with their participation in the breastfeeding process (F-PB), with the model explaining 29.1% of the variance (R²=0.291, β = 0.539, p < 0.001). Consistently, previous research has also reported that higher paternal attitude scores are associated with greater participation in breastfeeding [34]. These findings indicate that more positive attitudes were associated with greater participation in the breastfeeding process.

Educational status and participation in breastfeeding education were significantly associated with fathers’ influence on the breastfeeding process. Higher educational status was positively associated with fathers’ influence, whereas participation in breastfeeding education showed a negative association. Although the reasons for this finding remain unclear, possible explanations may include variations in the content, timing, or delivery of breastfeeding education programs, as well as potential mismatches between program expectations and fathers’ perceived roles [13, 29]. However, these explanations factors were not assessed in the present study and should be examined in future research. Previous studies have similarly reported that higher paternal education is associated with greater participation in breastfeeding [18], and that fathers who receive breastfeeding education during pregnancy demonstrate higher breastfeeding-related scores (p = 0.001). Overall, these findings highlight the importance of father-focused breastfeeding education programs that are practical, culturally appropriate, and aligned with fathers’ realistic roles and responsibilities.

These findings have important implications for clinical practice. In Turkey, breastfeeding support is primarily provided by nurses and midwives within maternal and child health services, with lactation counseling integrated into primary care. Pediatric nurses can encourage fathers’ participation in the breastfeeding process by providing breastfeeding education, addressing fathers’ concerns, and promoting supportive behaviors during the breastfeeding process [36, 38].

However, a multidisciplinary approach involving physicians, midwives, and other health professionals is also essential to strengthen family-centered care. Targeted father-focused education and counseling interventions may help support fathers’ participation in the breastfeeding process. These findings also have implications for undergraduate nursing education. Nursing curricula may benefit from incorporating learning experiences that emphasize father-centered breastfeeding support, effective communication with fathers, and family-centered care to better prepare future nurses for clinical practice [36, 38, 42]. Future intervention studies are needed to determine whether such approaches improve fathers’ attitudes and participation. Strengthening fathers’ capacity to provide emotional, informational, instrumental, and evaluative support may help reinforce their supportive role in the breastfeeding process. Future studies should evaluate whether such interventions improve breastfeeding and family health outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, a non-probability convenience sampling method combined with snowball sampling was used, which may limit the generalizability of the findings and increase the risk of selection bias. Future studies are recommended to employ probability-based sampling methods to improve representativeness. Second, although the initially calculated sample size was 384, the study was completed with 269 participants. This may have reduced the statistical power and affected the representativeness of the sample. Future research should aim to reach the required sample size or larger samples to strengthen generalizability. Third, data were collected via an online survey, meaning that only fathers with internet access and active social media use were included. This may have introduced a digital access bias and limited sample diversity. Future studies may consider mixed data collection strategies, including face-to-face recruitment, to include participants without digital access. Fourth, only married and cohabiting fathers were included in the study, which limits the generalizability of the findings to unmarried or non-cohabiting fathers. Finally, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Fathers’ Attitudes Toward Breastfeeding (F-BA) subscale was slightly below the conventional threshold (α = 0.67). Although this level of reliability may be considered acceptable in some research contexts, the relatively lower internal consistency may have attenuated the observed associations involving this subscale and should therefore be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

Fathers’ attitudes towards breastfeeding are associated with their participation and influence in the breastfeeding process. These findings highlight the potential value of father-focused educational programs, counseling, and targeted information activities to support fathers’ participation in the breastfeeding process and promote positive attitudes toward breastfeeding. Future intervention studies are needed to determine whether such approaches improve fathers’ attitudes, participation, and breastfeeding outcomes.
