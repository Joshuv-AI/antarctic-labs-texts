Study protocol for a randomized controlled trial of Adapt 2 Asthma (A2A), a culturally relevant coping skills and asthma management intervention for Latinx Families
Authors: Erin M Rodríguez, Spencer Westcott, María Paula Yávar Calderón, Sharon D Horner, Elizabeth C Matsui, Jendayi Dillard, Hadi Fareed, Jennifer Camacho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLatinx children in the United States experience disparities in asthma control and asthma-related functional outcomes compared to non-Latinx White children, including more school absences, emergency department visits, and hospitalizations for asthma. Stress appears to play a role in asthma control, but interventions designed to address the role of stress in asthma control for Latinx children are limited.MethodThe current randomized controlled trial tests the effects of Adapt 2 Asthma (A2A), a family-based coping skills and asthma management intervention tailored to the stressors, strengths, and cultural beliefs of Latinx families, compared to an asthma self-management control arm (the Asthma Plan for Kids; APK). Latinx families of children ages 8 to 14 years old with asthma (target N = 280) are identified and enrolled from primary care clinics and randomly assigned to either A2A or APK. The intervention is delivered by lay health workers trained and receiving ongoing consultation from the study team. The primary outcome is child- and parent-reported asthma control, while secondary outcomes are child’s quality of life, lung function, school absences, and emergency department visits, and child and parent coping and family asthma management behaviors. Participants will be assessed at baseline, post-intervention, and at 6- and 12-month follow-up timepoints.DiscussionThis study has the potential to provide new evidence regarding the effects of culturally relevant coping and asthma management intervention strategies for Latinx children with asthma.Trial RegistrationClinicaltrials.gov NCT05118282. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Asthma is the most common childhood chronic illness, affecting 8.3% of children in the U.S [1]. As a leading cause of missed school days and hospitalizations, asthma results in significant impairment for children and their parents [2]. Over half of children with asthma have activity limitations [3], and they miss 1.5 times as much school as those without asthma [4]. Poorly controlled asthma is particularly burdensome and is associated with more missed school [5], more missed work for parents [6], more frequent emergency department (ED) visits [7, 8], and poorer overall quality of life [9]. In one study of school-aged children with public insurance, asthma resulted in about 629,000 ED visits a year, costing $272 million [10].

Asthma disproportionately affects Latinx children and families. Asthma prevalence varies across different Latinx groups, with higher rates in Puerto Rican youth (12.9%) and lower rates in Mexican-origin youth (6.5%), compared to non-Latinx white youth (7.1%) [1]. Notably, however, asthma prevalence is twice as high in U.S.-born vs. foreign-born children of Mexican origin [11], and rates appear to be rising in Mexican-origin youth, from 5.1% in 2001 to 6.5% in 2016 [1]. In addition to disparities in prevalence rates, Latinx children experience disparities in asthma control. Compared to non-Latinx white youth, Latinx youth miss more school [12] and have higher rates of ED use due to asthma [13], and those from Spanish-speaking households face more barriers to asthma management [14, 15] and are more likely to be hospitalized for asthma [16]. Mexican-origin youth with asthma have higher rates of short-acting b-agonist use and hospitalizations than non-Latinx white youth with asthma [1].

Disparities in asthma control affecting Latinx children are associated with interrelated structural factors affecting Latinx families, including discrimination [17], exposure to immigration- and acculturation-related stressors [18–20], barriers to healthcare access [21, 22], and inequities in environmental exposures (e.g., pollution, poor housing conditions) [23, 24]. A significant and increasing number of Latinx children live in low-socioeconomic status (SES) contexts [25], which further exacerbates risk for poor asthma control [26]. All of these factors can result in increased experiences of stress, which is hypothesized to influence asthma control [27] through both psychobiological and behavioral pathways [28, 29].

Stress is also a risk factor for depressive symptoms and other mood and behavior problems [30]. Children with asthma are at greater risk for mood and behavior problems than healthy children [31], and Latinx children living in low-SES contexts are at particular risk for mood symptoms [32, 33]. Similarly, parents of children with asthma experience higher rates of depressive symptoms [34]. The Biobehavioral Family Model of Asthma [35] proposes that children’s mood and behavior problems, and parents’ depressive symptoms, are linked to poorer asthma control and symptom exacerbations through psychobiological channels. Further, children’s mood and behavior problems and parents’ depressive symptoms interfere with behaviors needed for asthma management [36, 37]. Thus, asthma control in Latinx children living in low-SES contexts may be mediated by their mood and behavior problems, and by their parents’ depressive symptoms, which in turn increases their risk for poor disease control [35].

Given the risks posed by stress, effective coping may promote improved asthma control. Primary control coping involves efforts to change a stressor or emotional response (e.g., problem-solving, emotion modulation), while secondary control coping involves efforts to accommodate/adapt oneself to a stressor (e.g., acceptance, cognitive restructuring) [38]. Across studies and populations, primary and secondary control have been linked to more positive mental health and disease outcomes in youth [39]. For Latinx youth with asthma living in low-SES contexts, primary control may be effective at supporting skills needed for asthma management, while secondary control may help families adapt in the face of stressful structural factors that are harder to control (e.g., environmental triggers, discrimination, and barriers to treatment) [40–42]. Depression intervention research suggests that primary and secondary control may mediate the effects of youth mental health interventions on mood and behavior symptoms. Thus, coping interventions for pediatric asthma may have a beneficial effect on asthma control via improvements in mood and behavioral symptoms.

In addition to stress, cultural and linguistic contexts may also shape asthma outcomes for Latinx youth. Latinx children experience more barriers to healthcare than non-Latinx white children, including a lack of services in Spanish [43], transportation problems [44], and lower parental health literacy [45]. Additionally, due to experiences of bias and a lack of culturally-relevant approaches to disease management in the medical system [46], Latinx parents may have more medication concerns than non-Latinx White parents, which are linked to lower adherence to controller medication [47]. Finally, Latinx cultural beliefs about the causes of and treatments for asthma vary based on nativity and location. For example, those outside the mainland U.S. may endorse more herbal remedies and temperature (e.g., heat or cold) as a cause [48, 49]. Cultural beliefs can coexist alongside, or predominate over, biomedical beliefs [49], which may affect use of controller medication [50]. Therefore, intervention approaches that are culturally congruent, reduce barriers to participation, and foster family-provider communication are more likely to show beneficial effects on asthma outcomes for Latinx youth.

Finally, Latinx families also bring specific strengths to asthma management and coping with asthma. For example, cultural values such as familismo (valuing high family cohesion) may lead to behaviors that support children’s asthma management [51], including parent, sibling, and extended family involvement in management strategies. High levels of religiosity or spirituality may support secondary control coping strategies such as acceptance and positive thinking [29, 52] and cultural receptiveness to complementary and alternative medicine may support families’ understanding of the role of stress in asthma control. Thus, a culturally tailored, family-based intervention that incorporates Latinx cultural values and involves parents and children learning skills together may build upon existing strengths and be a natural fit with families’ cultural values.

In summary, the current intervention is based on earlier research that the stressful context faced by Latinx youth and families may contribute to risk for poorer asthma control and functional outcomes. Primary and secondary control coping may improve asthma outcomes by positively altering child mediational processes (mood and behavior symptoms), parent mediational processes (depressive symptoms), and family asthma management behaviors (e.g., medication adherence). However, previous research has mostly been associational, and causal pathways from stress and coping to asthma control have not been rigorously tested through randomized controlled trials. Limited research has examined interventions that incorporate coping, and in these studies the intervention has been directed exclusively at parents [53] or children [54] and has not incorporated culturally-relevant content for Latinx families. Thus, the current study will test the effects of Adapt 2 Asthma (A2A), a culturally tailored coping skills + asthma management intervention for Latinx families. We will compare the effects of A2A on asthma control to an asthma self-management control group (the Asthma Plan for Kids; APK) in a randomized controlled trial. Testing A2A, and its mediators, in comparison with APK will provide insight into whether improvements in coping with stress, either directly or through asthma management behaviors, will improve asthma outcomes for Latinx youth.

Objectives {7}

Our primary study aim is to compare the effects of A2A and APK on asthma control and asthma-related functional outcomes in Latinx children ages 8–14 at post-intervention and 6- and 12-month follow up. We hypothesize that A2A will be more effective than APK at improving asthma control, quality of life, and lung function (FEV1), and more effective at reducing school absences and ED visits. Our secondary aim is to examine mediational pathways of the A2A intervention. We hypothesize that there will be a significant mediational pathway through child coping at post-intervention and child mood and behavior symptoms at 6 months which will serially mediate A2A’s effects at 12 months. We also hypothesize that parent coping at post-intervention and parent depressive symptoms at 6 months will serially mediate A2A’s effects at 12 months. Finally, we hypothesize that family asthma management behaviors (e.g., medication adherence) at 6 months will mediate A2A’s effects at 12 months.

Trial design {8}

This is a single-site, parallel arm randomized controlled trial of A2A compared to APK at post-intervention, 6-month follow up, and 12-month follow-up. This is a superiority trial with an allocation ratio of 1:1.

Method: participants, interventions, and outcomes

This manuscript uses the SPIRIT reporting guidelines [55].

Study setting {9}

This study is being conducted in partnership with community-based primary care clinics in and around Austin, Texas. These clinics serve a predominantly Latinx patient population that is low-income and publicly insured or underinsured. Study assessments and intervention sessions take place in participants’ homes, at their clinics, or at another community-based location (e.g., private room in public library) that is easily accessible to the participating families.

Eligibility criteria {10}

We will enroll 280 dyads of Latinx children with asthma and their parent, caregiver, or legal guardian (hereafter referred to as “parent”). Criteria for inclusion in the study are: (a) the child has a diagnosis of asthma based on parent report; (b) the child is between the ages of 8 and 14 years old; (c) the child identifies as Latino/a/x; and (d) the child and the parent are proficient in English or Spanish. The parent who participates is identified during eligibility screening by asking the parent during screening whether they or another parent/caregiver prefers to participate with the child. Beyond meeting the criteria for inclusion in the study, the only exclusion criteria are that the child has a disability beyond accommodations feasible in primary care that would interfere with their participation in the intervention. In the case of a child having a developmental disability, we will discuss the child’s developmental level (e.g., comprehension skills) with the parent to determine whether the child would be able to participate and complete activities in the intervention with accommodations. We will make every attempt to include families who can participate with accommodations (e.g., presenting all information verbally or with visual aids).

Who will take informed consent? {26a}

Research staff first obtain informed consent and permission for child participation from parent participants, and informed assent from child participants, at the beginning of the baseline assessment visit. Participants complete written consent/permission/assent forms that document all aspects of the study. A member of the research team uses a script to verbally summarize the important key details of the study within the consent/assent/permission forms during a conversation with the parent and child together. The purpose of the script is to integrate information across the parent and child consenting process, so that both parent and child hear the information together as a family. In addition to hearing the script, the participant has as much time as needed to read and review the form on their own during the visit. Visual aids are also used to emphasize information and check participant comprehension. Once participants complete their review of the forms and are given the opportunity to ask questions of the research staff, they are asked if they would still like to participate, and if so, they sign the forms. Participants are presented with an extra copy of the consent/assent/permission forms.

Additional consent provisions for collection and use of participant data and biological specimens {26b}

Participants are informed that all their data will be kept confidential, but if the research team observes or learns of child or elder abuse, we must report that abuse to the appropriate authorities. They are also informed that they may choose to stop participating and withdraw their consent at any time, but that the researchers will continue to use their data unless they ask the researchers to remove their data. We do not collect biological specimens in this study.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

Most behavioral interventions for pediatric asthma focus on asthma self-management skills [56, 57]. Overall, these interventions show good evidence of improving asthma symptoms, quality of life, and functional outcomes [56, 57], and teaching self-management is now an important standard of care for children with asthma [56]. However, gaps remain in interventions that target the disparities impacting Latinx children with asthma, and the mechanisms underlying these disparities such as stress. Therefore, we are comparing A2A (asthma self-management plus coping and culturally tailored content) with APK (asthma self-management) in order to test the hypothesis that the coping and culturally tailored content in A2A will improve asthma outcomes beyond asthma self-management alone for Latinx children.

A2A

Adapt 2 Asthma is a bilingual, family-based, combined coping skills and asthma management program designed to teach asthma knowledge, asthma management skills, and coping skills using interactive, culturally relevant activities for both the child and parent. The intervention is manualized and delivered in seven hour-long sessions, with homework assigned between sessions to practice skills and consolidate knowledge. Asthma management content includes education about asthma symptoms and medication, self-monitoring, inhaler skill, making an asthma action plan, and managing the environment. This content is identical to the asthma management content provided in APK, the control condition intervention which has been shown to improve children’s asthma management behaviors, symptom frequency, inhaler skill, hospitalizations, ED visits, and quality of life [58]. Coping content in A2A was adapted from previously tested primary and secondary control coping skills interventions to prevent depression in youth (PASCET and Act & Adapt) [59, 60]. Coping content in A2A includes psychoeducation about the relation between asthma, stress, and mood, mood monitoring, acceptance, distraction, cognitive restructuring, and selecting the best coping strategy for a given stressor, and content is introduced through video vignettes of racially/ethnically diverse young adolescents. Culturally relevant content in A2A includes facts and myths related to cultural beliefs about asthma, conducting a motivational interviewing activity (pros and cons) related to use of controller medication and complementary and alternative medicine strategies, and leveraging extended family and community support for managing the child’s asthma. This content was developed and refined through earlier pilot testing of A2A [61].

APK

Just as with A2A, children and their participating parent receiving APK attend seven sessions together. The curriculum provided to participants in the APK arm is the same number of sessions as A2A and covers identical asthma management content, including education about asthma symptoms and medication, self-monitoring, inhaler skill, making an asthma action plan, and managing the environment, consistent with standard of care recommendations for asthma management in primary care [56]. APK does not include coping content or culturally tailored asthma content, which are the essential elements of the intervention being tested in this study.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions {11b}

Participants may withdraw from the intervention or study at any time. If a child participant chooses to withdraw in the middle of their participation, their parent may continue participating; likewise, a child may continue to participate (with continued permission from the parent) if the parent decides to withdraw mid-study. Participants may receive any concomitant care, and any other intervention, during the trial {11d}. Once having been randomized, participants will be included in topline analyses following the intent-to-treat principle, employing missing data techniques as needed.

Intervention facilitators

Facilitators are lay health workers recruited from the community. Facilitators are fluent in both English and Spanish and have a history of involvement in the Latinx community (e.g., experience as a community health worker). Facilitators are randomly assigned to either the A2A or APK condition. There are no specific educational requirements for facilitators, but they must be able to complete the training described below as an indication that they can deliver the intervention with fidelity.

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention protocols {11c}

Facilitator training

Training for all facilitators includes research ethics, compliance, and safety training for working with human subjects offered through the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI Program), identifying and reporting abuse in accordance with the Texas Department of Family and Protective Services, and training in conducting home visits. Additional training content depends on condition assignment and is focused on the curriculum of the A2A or APK program. Training content is delivered in person and online through an interactive learning platform and involves opportunities to practice and roleplay session activities. Following this training, all facilitators must roleplay the delivery of the intervention with research staff, with the requirement that they adequately deliver 80% of target components for each session before being assigned a participant family. Finally, facilitators are accompanied by a research staff member the first time they deliver the program sessions to a participating family, with the research staff member offering support as needed to adhere to the program curriculum. Once they have successfully completed these training steps, facilitators proceed to work independently with families and receive ongoing consultation.

Facilitator consultation

Facilitators attend consultation for one hour per week with a consultant. Consultants are advanced doctoral students in school/clinical child psychology, postdoctoral fellows, and doctoral-level psychologists who are trained in the intervention. Consultation time is used to increase facilitator competence, including preparing for upcoming program sessions, providing feedback on completed program sessions, and managing challenges (e.g., participants’ engagement in the program). Consultants utilize evidence-based practices in consultation and supervision to support facilitators, including role-playing, modeling, and review of audiotaped sessions. Consultants also attend a biweekly “meta-consultation” meeting in which consultants discuss challenges and receive feedback and direction from the intervention developers.

Intervention fidelity

We assess fidelity to the intervention by coding facilitator adherence and competence from audio-recorded sessions. Session content is divided into elements which serve as the unit of coding. For example, a review of homework would be one element, and an interactive activity (e.g., inhaler skill) would be another element. Adherence coding quantifies the extent to which facilitators deliver all elements to the program protocol, while competence coding assesses the skill with which facilitators deliver the content.

Adherence

To assess program adherence, coders listen to session recordings and record whether or not session targets are present in a given session. We train coders on the intervention and control program curricula and Research Assistants (RAs) code a minimum of 4 recordings per session with feedback from a member of the research team. Once coders achieve an average agreement of Cohen’s Kappa = 0.80 with an expert coder (e.g., PI or postdoctoral consultant), coders are trained to code recordings independently. To ensure continuing reliability, coders meet weekly to discuss recordings and 10% of all session recordings are double coded with ongoing review of reliability statistics within the team of coders.

Competence

To assess facilitator competence, coders listen to session recordings and rate each element on the level of competence demonstrated. Competence coders are facilitator consultants who have a high degree of insight into the program content and underlying theory. The competence coding system was developed for the current study and is based on the Therapy Integrity in Evidence-Based Interventions (TIEBI) coding system used to code therapist competence in behavioral health interventions [62]. Coders group-code at least one recording of each session and discuss disagreements before beginning to code independently. Coders assign a score of 0 to 4, with 0 indicating the target was not administered and increasingly higher scores reflecting increasingly higher levels of skill in the target administered. Therapeutic skills include explaining content accurately, engaging bored or inattentive participants, modifying content for developmental level, personalizing content to the participants, checking for comprehension, building rapport, providing culturally relevant examples, and using English and Spanish flexibly based on participant language needs.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Participants may receive any concomitant care and any other intervention is permitted during the trial. We collect information regarding the child’s current asthma medications at each assessment timepoint.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

There are no provisions for post-trial care in the current trial. Participation does not affect participants’ ability to seek medical care as usual from their healthcare provider.

Outcomes, mediators, and co-variates/moderators by construct and timepoint

Primary outcome measure

Asthma control

The primary outcome of the study is child asthma control, as reported by both the child and parent. Children complete the Asthma Control Test (ACT; Nathan et al., 2004). The ACT assesses symptom control and impairment due to asthma (e.g., "how often have you had shortness of breath") and is responsive to clinical change [63]. For uniformity of administration across all ages, we use the ACT self-report version for children ages 12 years and older. The Spanish version of the ACT has been found to be a reliable and valid questionnaire for Spanish-speaking children [64]. Parents complete the symptom control subscale of the Asthma Therapy Assessment Questionnaire (cATAQ; Skinner et al., 2004) to assess symptom control (e.g., “wheezing or difficulty breathing”) in the past 4 weeks. Using the Spanish version of the cATAQ distributed by Merck & Company, Inc. as an initial version, the Spanish cATAQ was refined via forward and back-translation by our research team for use in this study.

Secondary outcome measures

Secondary outcomes of the study are child and parent coping, child quality of life, child lung function, and asthma-related missed school, ED visits, and hospitalizations.

Coping

The Response to Stress Questionnaire—Pediatric Asthma Version (RSQ) [42, 66] is a questionnaire with parallel forms for youth self-report, parent report of child, and parent self-report, and yields scores for Primary Control Coping and Secondary Control Coping. The RSQ has been validated cross-culturally, has strong psychometric properties, and shows sensitivity to change as a mediator of coping skills interventions [66, 67]. Children complete the child self-report form, and parents complete the parent report of child form, to assess the child’s primary and secondary control coping. Parents also complete the parent self-report form to assess their own coping. The Spanish version of the RSQ was developed by our research team through an iterative forward and backtranslation process, as recommended for translating research questionnaires [68].

Pediatric quality of life

Parents and children complete the Pediatric Quality of Life questionnaire (PedsQL) [69], a measure that assesses health-related quality of life for children with chronic health conditions such as asthma. Parents complete the parent report of the child version, while children complete the age-appropriate child self-report version for ages 8–12 years old or 13 years and older. The PedsQL includes the Generic Core Scales [69] that measure quality of life in physical, social, emotional and school domains (e.g., "it is hard for me to do chores around the house"; "it is hard to keep up when I play with other kids"), and the Asthma Module [69], which evaluates asthma-specific concerns related to symptoms and treatment (e.g., "I have trouble using my inhaler"). The PedsQL Generic Core Scales showed good validity for both the self-report and parent-report versions (α = 0.83 for child and α = 0.86 for parent) [69]. Furthermore, the PedsQL has response equivalence in English and Spanish in youth participants between the ages of 2 and 18, as well as parent participants [70].

Lung function

To measure pulmonary function, we collect forced expiratory volume at the first second (FEV1). Percent predicted values are calculated based on the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) reference [71]. Research staff collecting spirometry are trained through a National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH)-approved training course, and data is monitored and graded by trained RAs with oversight by the investigators.

School attendance, hospitalizations and emergency visits

We developed an Asthma Outcomes Questionnaire (AOQ) on which parents report the number of asthma-related school absences, ED visits, and hospitalizations for their children in the past 6 months. We show parents a calendar of the last 6 months and ask them to think about life events corresponding to dates in the last 6 months (e.g., birthdays, holidays, and the start of school) to anchor the timeline; this technique is drawn from clinical interviewing and is used to increase precision and reduce recall bias [72]. Once parents have a reference frame for the past 6 months, we then ask about the number of school absences, ED visits, and hospitalizations due to asthma each month.

Family asthma management

Children and parents jointly complete the Family Asthma Management Symptom Scale (FAMSS) [73], a semi-structured clinical interview to assess the family’s strengths and challenges in managing the child’s asthma. The FAMSS examines eight domains: asthma knowledge, symptoms assessment, family response to symptoms and exacerbations, child response to symptoms and exacerbations, environment control, medication adherence, collaboration with healthcare providers, and balanced integration of asthma and family life. These subscales assess asthma management at multiple levels such as evaluating developmentally appropriate responses of the child, family management of triggers and symptoms, and effective interactions with the healthcare provider. The FAMSS has high internal consistency among the scales (Cronbach’s α = 0.91) and concurrent validity with the Functional Severity of Asthma summary score, as well as high interrater reliability [73]. Additionally, the FAMSS has been validated in racially and culturally diverse families [74] and the interview guide was translated into Spanish by the developers for use with Spanish-speaking families [75]. Training on administering the FAMSS involves attending an orientation meeting to review the components of the measure, listening to two recorded interviews provided by the developers, and then administering a mock interview with other members of the research team and receiving feedback. Training on scoring the FAMSS requires achieving interrater reliability (intraclass correlations of 0.7 or greater) on each of the FAMSS subscales with previously coded recordings (coded by previously trained members of the research team). Ongoing consultation from measure developers and team consensus meetings help to support reliability and avoid coder drift throughout the study.

Mediators

In addition to being examined as outcome measures, parent and child coping and family asthma management will also be examined as mediators of the intervention (see information about coping and family asthma management measures above). Moreover, child mood and behavior symptoms and parent depressive symptoms will be examined as mediators of the intervention (see below). The mediator measures will be collected at baseline, post- and 6-month follow up timepoints.

Child mood and behavior symptoms

Children’s mood and behavior symptoms are assessed via youth and parent report on the Total Difficulties scale of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [76]. The Total Difficulties score captures emotional, conduct, attentional, and peer difficulties (e.g., “I worry a lot’’, ‘‘I get very angry and often lose my temper’’). The SDQ has been validated cross-culturally [77, 78] and is more acceptable to parents in primary care settings than comparable clinical measures [79]. The youth self-report SDQ, originally validated with children ages 11–17, has acceptable psychometric properties with children as young as seven [76].

Parent depressive symptoms

Parental depressive symptoms are measured with the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale-Short Form (CES-D Short Form), a 10-item, reliable and well-validated self-report of depressive symptoms [80]. Depressive symptoms in the last week (e.g., “I was bothered by things that don’t usually bother me”; “I had trouble keeping my mind on what I was doing”) are rated on a 4-point scale based on their frequency.

Co-variates and moderators

Co-variates and moderators are collected at baseline. We will control for baseline variables of child age and sex, season of assessment, asthma severity, and parent acculturation. We will also explore these variables as moderators of the effects of A2A.

Child age and sex

We collect child age and sex on a parent-completed demographic form.

Season of assessment

Because asthma symptoms and exacerbations vary seasonally [81], we collect the date of the baseline assessment and code as winter, spring, summer or fall to account for the potential effects of season.

Asthma severity

We assess asthma severity using the Composite Asthma Severity Index (CASI) [82] an empirically supported multidimensional measure developed to assess three components of severity: impairment, risk, and medication usage. Asthma severity reflects the level of treatment intensity needed to manage symptoms, while asthma control (our primary outcome) is defined as the extent to which asthma symptoms are reduced or controlled with treatment [83]. As such, scores on the CASI are distinct from asthma control scores [82] and will allow us to distinguish between these constructs, as recommended by the American Thoracic Society’s guidelines on the standardization of clinical trial outcomes [81].

Parent acculturation

We assess parent acculturation level using the Short Acculturation Scale for Hispanics (SAS) [84], a brief, well-validated measure that assesses acculturation in Latinx individuals related to language use, media consumption, and social relations (e.g., “In general, what language(s) do you read and speak?; What language(s) do you usually speak with your friends?”).

Participant timeline {13}:

Sample size {14}

We will enroll 280 children and 280 parents and aim to retain 80% of the sample (i.e., at least 224 children and 224 parents) through the 12-month follow-up. We used the Monte Carlo power analysis approach with 1,000 replications using maximum likelihood estimation in Mplus [85] to determine the power for Aims 1 and 2 with a sample size of N = 280 (140 participants per group) at baseline, including covariates. In conducting the power analyses, rates of missing data were modeled to increase to 20% missing (i.e., to reflect a retention rate of 80%) at 12-month follow up. Earlier research on the effects of A2A [52] guided our hypothesized effect sizes for the linear mixed models that will test the effects of A2A. Small, medium, and large effects (β’s) were included as estimates in the models using values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 (for categorical predictors) and 0.14, 0.36, and 0.51 (for continuous predictors), respectively [86]. For Aim 1, power for the treatment main effects at 12-month follow-up was 1.00 for the intercept and slope for continuous outcomes (asthma control, quality of life, FEV1, school absences) and power was ≥ 0.83 (for the intercept) and > 0.98 (for the slope) with the categorical outcome (ED visits). For Aim 2 (the mediational model), power was > 0.89 for the direct effect of treatment on outcomes, and > 0.99 for all indirect mediational effects.

Recruitment {15}

Participants are recruited from primary care partner clinics in and around Austin, Texas that serve predominantly Latinx families living in low-SES contexts. Partner clinics identify and provide the names and contact information of patients with asthma to the research team by screening electronic health records (EHR). Based on the clinic’s preference, clinic staff either (a) contact the patient’s parent first to introduce the study and ask permission for the research team to contact the parent, or (b) allow the research staff to make the first contact about the study. In the case that the first contact about the study comes from research staff, our team first mails an introductory letter to the family on behalf of the clinic describing the study and then contacts the parent by phone to invite the family to participate. If the family expresses interest after learning what the study involves, research staff conduct an eligibility screening over the phone and, if eligible, schedule the family for their baseline assessment visit.

While we anticipate that the majority of participants will be identified through primary care clinics, we will also employ additional recruitment strategies to reach our target sample size. For example, research staff attend events in the community (e.g., health fairs and back to school fairs) and distribute recruitment flyers at local organizations that serve the Latinx community. We also recruit through schools. School nurses, parent liaisons, and administrators help us to identify students with asthma and reach out to their parents to introduce the study. If the family is interested in learning more the school shares the parent’s contact information with the research team and research staff contact the parent. Additionally, we identify potential participants from other research studies conducted by our study investigators if those participants gave permission to be contacted about other studies.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation. {16a}

Families are randomly assigned to A2A or APK using stratified block randomization (SBR). Using SBR, families are assigned to strata to balance child age (> 11.5 years old and ≤ 11.5 years old), child sex (male and female), language preference (Spanish or English) and geographic location within central Texas (3 geographic zones); randomization occurs within each stratum (24 strata). Within each stratum, we use permuted block randomization to randomly assign participants to the different arms of the trial within blocks of random sizes. The randomly generated sequence is imported into a tracking document which lists the next assignment to be assigned in the block each time a family completes their baseline assessment.

Concealment mechanism. {16b}

Assignments are done immediately after the families complete the baseline assessment. A member of the team not involved in the outcomes assessments updates the tracking document to assign a new randomization assignment each time a new family completes their baseline assessment.

Implementation. {16c}

An independent member of the research team who is not involved in the assessment process generates the allocation sequence. The research team then uses the allocation sequence to assign families to a condition following their completion of the baseline assessment. Only research team members who are not involved in conducting outcomes assessments are unmasked to families’ condition assignment.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Outcomes assessors are masked to condition assignment. Condition assignments are stored in a password protected file accessed only by research team members who do not conduct outcomes assessments. Outcomes assessors will remain masked to participant condition assignment during the entire trial.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

Unmasking of research assistants who are outcome assessors will only occur if the research assistant’s role on the project changes and they will no longer conduct outcome assessments. For example, this may occur if a research assistant who is an advanced doctoral student changes tasks from conducting outcomes assessments to conducting consultation with facilitators. No research team members who continue to conduct outcomes assessments will be unmasked.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

Assessments are conducted by the research staff at four different timepoints: a baseline assessment immediately prior to the start of the program, a post-assessment immediately following completion of the 7 program sessions of A2A or APK, and two follow up assessments at 6 and 12 months after completing the assigned intervention (see Figs. 1 and 2). The research staff collect the assessment data electronically via REDCap [87, 88], which is a secure web platform for building and managing online databases and surveys. All research staff conducting assessments are bilingual in English/Spanish and have demonstrated fluency in oral and written communication in both languages. Assessors are trained using a standardized training checklist to ensure rehearsal and practice of all aspects of the assessment process with feedback from research staff. Training also involves shadowing one or more assessment visits with a participating family and completing a roleplay of the entire assessment with the principal investigator (PI) to demonstrate adherence to the assessment protocol. The PI monitors data collection by having assessors report each time assessments are completed and whether any protocol deviations or concerns occurred. Additionally, assessors complete checklists of the protocol at each assessment, which are reviewed by research staff to monitor for protocol deviations.

Participant timeline of study activities

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

We use several strategies to promote retention across follow-up assessments, including providing compensation at each follow up, obtaining updated contact information and alternate family contact information at each assessment visit, and maintaining engagement between assessments (e.g., through newsletters sent to participants). Finally, the researchers regularly solicit input from clinic partners to troubleshoot retention difficulties.

Data management {19}

Data is collected and stored on REDCap. Access to all data is limited to research staff, all of whom are IRB-approved and trained in data integrity and confidentiality procedures. All data is identified with a coded ID number rather than with participant names. The electronic file that matches participant names with ID numbers is stored in a separate file from the data. This file is password protected and saved on the research institution’s encrypted, secure cloud server. Hard copy data is stored in a locked filing cabinet in the researchers’ locked laboratory space. Audio recordings of FAMSS interviews and intervention sessions are transferred immediately from the recording device to the secure cloud server and removed from the recording device.

Confidentiality {27}

Consent/assent/permission forms are stored in locked file cabinets within the researchers’ locked lab space, or if completed electronically, in a separate REDCap database, and will be retained for the entire duration of the study. Other identifying information will also be retained for the duration of the study, while de-identified data will be stored indefinitely to allow for opportunities for future investigations using secondary data analyses. Electronic records are coded using a subject ID number and stored on the research institution’s encrypted, secure cloud system. Hard copy and electronic data and participant forms will be securely retained until no further research questions will be addressed, at which time the data will be destroyed. The data will be de-identified for analyses and dissemination of research results.

Statistical methods

Preliminary analyses

Prior to testing hypotheses, we will conduct preliminary analyses. We will present descriptive statistics on demographic and baseline variables by condition. We will also check whether data missingness varies as a function of demographic or study variables to determine whether data are missing at random (vs. missing completely at random), and use full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation to account for data missing at random.

Statistical methods for analysing primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Data analyses: aim 1

We will examine intervention outcomes using both a per-protocol analysis (families who have completed at least 4 sessions of A2A or APK, because both primary and secondary control coping are introduced in A2A by session 4) and an intent-to-treat analysis (including data from all families who enroll). Per-protocol analyses will provide information about A2A’s impact when a minimum dose is received (i.e., the intervention efficacy), while intent-to-treat analyses will provide information about A2A’s potential impact when implemented within a typical service delivery model, where not all families who start treatment are retained. We will use linear mixed modeling for continuous outcomes (asthma control, lung function, quality of life, school absences, coping, family asthma management), and generalized linear mixed modeling for the binary outcome (ED visits). Analyses will examine effects of Time, Treatment Group, and the Time x Treatment Group interaction. If the cross-level interaction is statistically significant, simple intercept and simple slope parameters of the conditional regression of the outcome on Time as a function of Treatment Group will be estimated to probe the Time x Treatment Group interaction [89]. This will also be done to probe other statistically significant interactions in the model.

Data analyses: aim 2

To examine the significance of the proposed mediational pathways, we will use Preacher & Hayes’ approach to test the bias-corrected bootstrapped confidence intervals with 10,000 bootstrapped samples [90] for each of the hypothesized indirect effects. Bias-corrected bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals associated with each of the indirect effect estimates that do not contain a value of zero will denote a statistically significant indirect effect. The specific indirect effects that will be tested will link treatment condition with each of the continuous outcomes from Aim 1 through each of the mediational pathways in the hypothesized model. Three indirect effects per outcome will be tested: a) the indirect effect of treatment to each outcome at 12 months (i.e., asthma control, quality of life, FEV1, school attendance, and ED visits) via child coping at post intervention and child mood behavior and symptoms at 6 months; b) the indirect effect of treatment to each outcome at 12 months (i.e., asthma control, quality of life, FEV1, school attendance, and ED visits) via parent coping at post intervention and parent depressive symptoms at 6 months; and c) the indirect effect of treatment to each outcome at 12 months (i.e., asthma control, quality of life, FEV1, school attendance, and ED visits) via family asthma management at 6 months.

Interim analyses {21b}

Because the study is low risk, interim analyses at the study midpoint will focus on examining adverse events and participant accrual. A data safety monitoring committee will review the results of the interim analyses and make recommendations regarding ways to support participant accrual.

Methods for any additional analyses {20b}

Exploratory analyses of moderators

Although we have excellent power to test main effects and mediational effects, we will not have sufficient power to conduct rigorous tests of moderators. Therefore, moderator analyses will be exploratory. We will explore child age, child sex, asthma severity, and parent acculturation as moderators of A2A’s effects. We will test these moderators one at a time by centering the variables and creating an interaction term between each centered moderator and the A2A variable. The main effect of each moderator, the main effect of group, and the interaction terms will be entered as predictors to predict post levels and slopes for each of the primary outcomes.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and missing data {20c}

As noted earlier, in addition to examining a per-protocol analyses, we will conduct an intent-to-treat analysis (including data from all families who enroll). In conducting the power analyses, rates of missing data were modeled to increase to 20% missing (i.e., to reflect a retention rate of 80%) at 12-month follow up. We will check whether missingness varies as a function of demographic or study variables to determine whether data is missing at random (versus missing completely at random), and use full information maximum likelihood estimation to account for data missing at random.

Plans, if any, for granting public access to the full protocol, participant-level dataset, and statistical code {31c}

In order to protect participants’ identities and sensitive personal and health information, final data from the study will be shared in a restricted capacity. As noted below in the availability of data and materials section {29}, access to the data will be restricted to certified researchers who complete a data-sharing contract to maintain participant confidentiality and have an IRB-approved protocol to secure the data and minimize risk of loss of privacy and confidentiality to participants. Data files transmitted electronically will be transferred using a secure cloud server.

Oversight and monitoring

Composition of the coordinating center and trial steering committee {5d}

The principal investigator provides oversight of all aspects of the trial. The PI supervises the project coordinator and research staff in conducting the day-to-day operations of the trial and will communicate with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and the Data Safety Monitoring Committee about any serious adverse events or changes in eligibility criteria or risks impacting the trial. The PI also monitors participant accrual, trains project staff on the data collection protocol and supervises research staff in monitoring intervention fidelity throughout the trial.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role, and reporting structure {21a}

A Data Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC) will independently monitor accrual and adverse events. The DSMC consists of independent clinicians and researchers who are not members of the research team and who have expertise in pediatrics, asthma, and working with Latinx families. The DSMC reviewed the protocol before the start of the study and provided recommendations as needed. The DSMC will also review the study on an annual basis, including adverse events, and will review any event when requested by the researchers or by the funder. The DSMC will also review the results of interim analyses examining participant accrual and make recommendations related to supporting participant accrual.

Adverse events and harms {22}

Adverse events are monitored throughout each family’s participation in the study, and specifically queried at post-intervention and at 6- and 12-month follow up assessments. Adverse events will be classified as non-serious or serious. For this study, serious adverse events are defined as the death of the child participant or hospitalization for more than 24 h due to asthma. ED visits and short hospital stays (< 24 h) occur periodically in the normal course of care for these children. Particularly for children living outside of urban centers (e.g., many patients treated at one of our partner clinics), short hospital stays are typical to monitor the effect of acute care. Therefore, short hospital stays and ED visits are considered adverse events but not serious adverse events. During these assessments, for any hospitalization that is reported, we will assess whether the hospitalization lasted longer than 24 h to identify adverse events that have occurred. For every hospitalization that exceeds 24 h, a research staff member will collect additional information about the hospitalization, including whether or not the child was in the critical care unit, if medications were required, and if the child received a secondary diagnosis (e.g. pneumonia), as well as recording any additional details about hospitalization that the family reports. In the event that any study participant experiences a serious adverse event during the course of the study, the PI will notify the research institution’s institutional review board, as well as the DSMC and appropriate personnel at the funding agency.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

Any adverse events are reported to the PI at the conclusion of each assessment visit. Additionally, the project coordinator produces a monthly report of adverse events in the dataset. Serious adverse events will be communicated to the institutional review board and study funder. Participant recruitment and accrual statistics are monitored through reports that are shared and discussed among the research team during weekly project meetings. Intervention fidelity is monitored through weekly coding meetings to examine facilitator adherence and competency during intervention sessions.

Plans for communicating important protocol modifications {25}

All changes to the protocol will be submitted for approval by the Institutional Review Board and must be approved before being implemented. Changes that significantly impact what participants are asked to do in the study (e.g., modifications that result in changes to informed consent) will require that participants who are currently enrolled complete an updated informed consent process. Any changes to eligibility criteria and outcomes will be communicated to the study funder and will be updated on the trial’s registration on ClinicalTrials.gov.

Dissemination plans {31a}

Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations. Results will also be communicated to community stakeholders and research participants through project newsletters and community presentations. Additionally, results will be submitted to ClinicalTrials.gov for public posting within one year after the study’s primary completion date.

Discussion

This trial aims to test the effectiveness and mediators of Adapt 2 Asthma, a novel coping skills and asthma management intervention developed specifically for Latinx children and their families living in low-SES contexts. There are few interventions for pediatric asthma with substantial psychosocial components, and none that target primary and secondary control coping, despite the evidence linking stress, child mood and behavior symptoms, and parent depressive symptoms to poorer asthma control. Therefore, the current study is innovative in its potential to understand the benefits of integrating culturally relevant asthma and coping content to improve asthma outcomes for Latinx youth.

Our design includes several strengths, including the application of an empirically supported model of coping to pediatric asthma intervention, the use of an asthma management comparison group, and the integration of culturally relevant asthma content for Latinx families. Our assessment measures are multi-method and psychometrically sound, and our comprehensive approach to identifying participants will increase the representativeness of the sample. Another strength is our examination of both child and parent mediational pathways of the intervention with sufficient statistical power to do so. While both child and parent processes play a role in children’s asthma control, the relative impact of each in the context of an intervention is unknown, particularly in school-aged and early adolescent youth. Our findings will speak to the importance of children’s, parents’, and their mutual involvement in intervention efforts for youth, which will guide the choice of intervention settings and structure of delivery.

One potential limitation of the study is that the generalizability of the parent findings may be limited to mothers, because of a low proportion of fathers in the sample. Similarly, families who decline to participate will limit generalizability for families less motivated to partake in a voluntary intervention. Our focus on “Latinx” families as a meaningful group could also be considered a limitation. Latinx individuals in the U.S. are ethnically diverse [91], and vary in their acculturation to U.S. culture. Therefore, when research focuses on Latinx families as a whole, there is a risk of ignoring important variability. On the other hand, research that excludes Latinx families from certain subgroups may increase the internal validity of the research but compromise external validity and perpetuate a lack of representation from less populous subgroups. Additionally, while between group variability across different Latinx ethnic subgroups does affect beliefs and behaviors, there are also shared commonalities in language, cultural values (e.g., familismo) and asthma management behaviors (e.g., use of home remedies) [49]. Finally, health disparities affecting Latinx families are historically rooted in U.S. policies towards Latin America and in the treatment of individuals of Latin American origin in the U.S., indicating the sociopolitical reality of being Latinx [92]. While this focus on Latinx families may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations, these families also represent the majority of patients seen in our partner clinics, which increases the local relevance of the study, the benefit of the findings to the community, and the potential for future research on the implementation of A2A. To address the potential pitfalls related to intergroup variability, we assess parent acculturation and will control for it in our analyses, and we also assess Latinx ethnic identity (e.g., Mexican, Puerto Rican, etc.) and will conduct exploratory analyses to examine differences between specific ethnic subgroups.

The challenge of childhood asthma control is a significant public health concern, but interventions designed to address the role of stress in asthma control are limited, and Latinx children and families are underserved by current approaches. The current study will examine evidence of the effects and mediators of Adapt 2 Asthma for Latinx children. Findings from this trial have the potential to advance knowledge not only of effective interventions but also why they work, which will guide cost-effective clinical decision-making.
