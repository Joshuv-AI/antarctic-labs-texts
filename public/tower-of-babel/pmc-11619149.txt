Application of artificial intelligence-based detection of furcation involvement in mandibular first molar using cone beam tomography images- a preliminary study
Authors: Shishir Shetty, Wael Talaat, Sausan AlKawas, Natheer Al-Rawi, Sesha Reddy, Zaid Hamdoon, Waad Kheder, Anirudh Acharya, Dilber Uzun Ozsahin, Leena R David
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiographs play a key role in diagnosis of periodontal diseases. Deep learning models have been explored for image analysis in periodontal diseases. However, there is lacuna of research in the deep learning model-based detection of furcation involvements [FI]. The objective of this study was to determine the accuracy of deep learning model in the detection of FI in axial CBCT images.MethodologyWe obtained initial dataset 285 axial CBCT images among which 143 were normal (without FI) and 142 were abnormal (with FI). Data augmentation technique was used to create 600(300 normal and 300 abnormal) images by using 200 images from the training dataset. Remaining 85(43 normal and 42 abnormal) images were kept for testing of model. ResNet101V2 with transfer learning was used employed for the analysis of images.ResultsTraining accuracy of model is 98%, valid accuracy is 97% and test accuracy is 91%. The precision and F1 score were 0.98 and 0.98 respectively. The Area under curve (AUC) was reported at 0.98. The test loss was reported at 0.2170.ConclusionThe deep learning model (ResNet101V2) can accurately detect the FI in axial CBCT images. However, since our study was preliminary in nature and carried out with relatively smaller dataset, a study with larger dataset will further confirm the accuracy of deep learning models.Keywords: Furcation defects, Cone beam computed tomography, Artificial intelligence, Diagnosis

Introduction

The progression of periodontal disease leads to bone loss in the furcation of multirooted teeth leading to furcation involvement (FI) [1, 2]. FI should be detected in the initial stage to prevent disease progression and avert future loss of attachment [3]. Conventional two-dimensional radiographs such as intraoral periapical radiographs and bitewing are used commonly used for diagnosis of periodontal disease since they are affordable and accessible [4]. However, the two-dimensional nature of these radiograph is a major limitation in detecting FI [4]. In one of the recently published studies, cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) findings showed the closest agreement with open flap surgery findings whereas panoramic and periapical radiographic findings showed modest agreement with findings of open flap surgery [5]. Another study by Padmanabhan et al. in 2017 showed that the CBCT measurements of height, width, and depth of the FI in mandibular molars had no significant difference with the clinical measurements [6]. Another study by Komšić et al. found that CBCT measurements of FI of molars showed significant correlation with intra-surgical measurements and periodontal probing [7].

The application of artificial intelligence (AI) has gained significant penetration into medicine and dentistry [8]. In dentistry the application of AI is focused mainly focused on cariology and periodontal diseases because these are the areas of major concern in dental health [9]. In the field of periodontology, the applications of AI include imaging of periodontium, disease prediction, prediction of treatment outcome and salivary marker classification [8]. Recently some research papers have described the accuracy of deep learning models in the detection of bone loss in periodontal diseases [10–12]. Most of the studies used intraoral periapical radiographs and panoramic radiographs [13–15]. However, so far as we know there are no studies exploring the accuracy of deep learning models in the detection of FI using CBCT images. The role of CBCT is further reinforced as axial CBCT slices provide a clear view of the FI. The objective of this study was to determine the accuracy of deep learning model in the detection of FI in axial CBCT images.

Methodology

A screening of 3000 scans obtained using Planmeca Viso G7 CBCT machine was carried out. The research ethics committee (University of Sharjah) provided the ethical approval for our study (REC-23-10-12-01-F). Only existing scans were screened, no new scans were made for the study. The scans were screened by a single examiner with 15 years of clinical experience in dental radiology. The scans belonging patients within the age group of 18 years to 60 years of both genders were included in the study. After screening the CBCT scan 285 scans were eligible for the study. Only the scans that were obtained using high resolution settings (150 μm voxel size, 100 kVp, and 12.5 mA, 5 s-exposure time) and the field of view (FOV) range of Ø3 × 3 – Ø6 × 6 (cms) were considered.

Scans not covering the region of interest (ROI) (mandibular first molar) were excluded from the research. Scan with caries, restoration, fracture and root canal treatment of mandibular first molar was not included in the study. The axial slice of the scans was examined and a screenshot of was made at the first slice below the furcation area where the two roots could be seen separately. To maintain uniformity the scans were cropped with uniform dimension (200 × 400-pixel square) (Fig. 1). The image was classified as normal and abnormal based on the absence and presence of FI respectively.

Study data pool

The initial custom dataset had total 285 CBCT images (143 normal and 142 abnormal) (Table 1). Among them 85 (43 normal and 42 abnormal) images were kept for testing of model. The remaining 200 images (further augmented 600 images) were used for training and validation (Fig. 2). A validation subset of 120 images was generated from the training data (20% of 600 augmented images = 120 images).

Data pre-processing

In pre-processing phase we resized the images (224 × 224-pixel square) according to input of ResNet101V2.

Data augmentation

Data augmentation technique was used to create 600 (300 normal and 300 abnormal) images by using 200 images from the training dataset (Fig. 3). For image augmentation “ImageDataGenerator” a tool in Keras deep learning library was used with following parameters: Rotation range = 5, width shift range = 0, height shift range = 0, zoom range = 0.1, horizontal flip = False, fill mode=’nearest’.

Pre-trained model selection

We analyzed three version of ResNet (50,101,101v2). The performance of all models was analyzed and ResNet101v2 was selected as a base model based on superior performance parameters. Table 2.

We employed the strategy to fine-tune the model in the present study. The process of training the model used in our study is diagrammatically represented in Fig. 4.

Flow Chart of Model training and fine training

The images were primarily pre-processed and augmented to develop input data. The base model was frozen at first followed by further addition of classification layers over it. Training and evaluation of base model was then carried out. This was followed by unfreezing of the top layers.

Retraining of the full model was then carried out but with a lower learning rate. After this another evaluation was carried out for improvement in metrics of performance. If improvement was noticed in performance metrics further fine tuning was carried out till no further improvement was noticed. Leveraging the existing architecture of ResNet101V2 with transfer learning was to improve the performance of the existing model [16–18].

The following hyperparameters were used to train the models: batch size 64, 30 epochs, Adam optimizer having learning rate of 1e-5, loss function (using Binary Cross Entropy), classification of upper layers (using squashing function).

Experiments were performed on local work station running on Ubuntu with Intel(R) Core(TM) M-5Y71 CPU (1.20 GHz) 1.40 GHz and 8GB RAM. Python was used to build the system using the deep learning frameworks Keras with TensorFlow as a back end.

Results

Training accuracy of model was 98%, valid accuracy was 97% (Fig. 5) and test accuracy was reported at 91% (Fig. 6). The formula used for calculating accuracy = (TN + TP)/(TP + FP + TN + FN) [TN = True negative, True Positive, FP = False Positive, FN = False negative]. The Kappa coefficients for training, validation and testing groups were 0.94 and 0.94 and 0.90 respectively.

Discussion

Recently, studies related to detection periodontal bone loss and disease progression using artificial intelligence (AI) have been published [16–19]. A majority of the studies have used panoramic radiograph for training and testing their AI models [20]. Few studies used intraoral periapical radiographs in their analysis [18, 20]. To our knowledge the present study is the first research using axial CBCT sections to train and test AI models. Recent studies have revealed that CBCT provided accurate measurements of the furcation defect up to 2 decimals in millimeters [21, 22].

A wide range of AI software models were used in the radiographic detection of periodontal bone loss. Uzun Saylan et al. used PyTorch-based YOLO-v5 model in their study [23]. Similarly, Amasya et al. used Cascade R-CNN architecture for detecting bone loss in panoramic radiograph [24]. Meanwhile Ryu et al. used Faster R-CNN for the detection of periodontally compromised teeth on panoramic images [25]. In the present study we used ResNet101V2 for detection of FI in axial CBCT images. We analyzed three version of ResNet (50,101,101v2). The performance of all models was analyzed and ResNet101v2 was selected as a base model.

The residual neural network (ResNet) which is a deep residual learning framework has been recently used for number of applications [26]. ResNet can reach a depth of 152 layers, can also tackle the issue of degradation problem and extract additional data from the original material [26]. ResNet 101 has been incorporated with modules like depth feature extraction and Denoised Deformable Convolution (DDC) module for performance enhancement [27].

We used the version 2.00 of ResNet101 (ResNet101V2) network which has better capacity to generalize compared to original form of ResNet [28]. This is possible due to pre-activation of weights in ResNet version 2 [28]. The output signal produced by the model is regularized and normalized due to the pre-activation of weights. This results in overall reduction of overfitting of the model [28].

The characteristics feature of transfer learning technique is that the model is primarily trained on base dataset. The features that are picked up during the initial training are readapted while being trained on separate database meant for separate task. The approach is beneficial when the dataset is lower in number [29]. Shin et al. found that transfer learning is also dependably useful in analysis of larger datasets which have undergone annotation (ImageNet) [30]. In the present study we carried out fine tuning which increases the computing efficiency and speed [29].

In the present study the test accuracy was 91% and AUC was 0.98. Kurt et al. found similar accuracy of (91%) when they used a Google Net Inception v3 CNN network which was pre-trained to detect alveolar bone loss in panoramic radiographs [31]. However lower precision values (77%,) were reported in study by Yavuz et al. in 2024 [32]. Yavuz et al. used bitewing radiographs in their study, whereas most of the studies were conducted on panoramic radiographs. Similarly, another study by Chen et al. using intraoral periapical radiographs showed lower accuracy (72.8%) [33]. Panoramic Studies by Liu et al. in 2023 (Axelnet) and Shon et al. in 2022 (U-Net and YOLOv5) reported an accuracy of 80% and 92.8% [34, 35].

In the present study the F1 score which is another accuracy metric was found to be 0.98. slightly lower F1 scores were reported in studies by Widyaningrum et al. 2022 (0.87 using Multi-Label U-Net and Mask R-CNN) and Ertas et al. (2022) (0.87 using ResNet 50) [36, 37].

One of the major limitations of our study was that we used two-dimensional axial slices of three-dimensional CBCT scans. Further, studies can be carried out using three dimensional CBCT scans using deep learning models. Another limitation was that we could not classify the FI based on the extent of bone loss in the axial slices. Future studies can be conducted on the classification of axial CBCT images based on the extent of FI.

Conclusion

The deep learning model (ResNet101V2) can accurately detect the FI in axial CBCT images. However, since our study was preliminary in nature and carried out with relatively smaller dataset, a study with larger dataset will further confirm the accuracy of deep learning models.
