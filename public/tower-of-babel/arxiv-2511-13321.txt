A Bi-fidelity based asymptotic-preserving neural network for the semiconductor Boltzmann equation and its inverse problem
Liu Liu, Xueyu Zhu, Zhenyi Zhu

Abstract
This paper introduces a Bi-fidelity Asymptotic-Preserving Neural Network (BI-APNNs) framework, designed to efficiently solve forward and inverse problems for the semiconductor Boltzmann equation. Our approach builds upon the Asymptotic-Preserving Neural Network (APNNs) methodology [ 14 ] , which employs a micro-macro decomposition to handle the model’s multiscale nature. We specifically address a key bottleneck in the original APNNs: the slow convergence of the macroscopic density ρ \rho in the near fluid-dynamic regime, i.e., for small Knudsen numbers ε \varepsilon . The core innovation of BI-APNNs is a novel bi-fidelity decomposition of the macroscopic quantity ρ \rho , which accurately approximates the true density at small ε \varepsilon , and can be efficiently pre-trained. A separate and more compact neural network is then tasked with learning only the minor correction term, ρ corr \rho_{\text{corr}} . This strategy not only significantly accelerates the training convergence but also improves the accuracy of the forward problem solution, particularly in the challenging fluid-dynamic limit. Meanwhile, we demonstrate through extensive numerical experiments that our new BI-APNNs yields substantially more accurate and robust results for inverse problems compared to the standard APNNs. Validated on both the semiconductor Boltzmann and the Boltzmann-Poisson systems, our work shows that the bi-fidelity formulation is a powerful enhancement for tackling multiscale kinetic equations, especially when dealing with inverse problems constrained by partial observation data.

Introduction

Kinetic equations form the basis for modeling a wide range of physical systems where non-equilibrium dynamics is dominant, spanning from rarefied gas flows and plasma physics to the behavior of electrons in semiconductor devices
[
28
]
. As a bridge between the microscopic world of individual particles and the macroscopic world of continuum mechanics, these equations, particularly the Boltzmann-type models, are indispensable
[
2
]
. These equations are essential for design, optimization, control, and inverse problems, including semiconductor device design, gas flow channel topology optimization, and risk management in quantitative finance
[
8
]
. Numerous applications necessitate the identification of unknown or optimal parameters in Boltzmann-type equations or mean-field models
[
1
,
4
,
5
,
7
,
23
]
.

Solving such kinetic models by using traditional numerical methods is challenging. The challenges are various, including the high dimensionality of the phase space and design of the method to capture dynamics across disparate spatial and temporal scales. It is more difficult to solve real-world problems and their parameter estimation, especially when there is only sparse data available. Data-driven approaches, including machine learning methods have gained significant popularity in recent decades
[
11
]
. Among these, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have emerged as a powerful framework. By embedding the governing partial differential equations (PDEs) directly into the neural network’s loss function, PINNs can effectively approximate solutions even with minimal data, and have been successfully applied to a variety of forward and inverse problems
[
6
,
26
,
27
,
33
]
.

Nevertheless, the intrinsic multiscale character of kinetic equations presents a subtle but significant hurdle for standard PINNs frameworks. The system’s behavior is often dictated by a small, dimensionless parameter—the Knudsen number,
\varepsilon
—which can lead to severe numerical stiffness. A naive application of PINNs often fails to preserve the correct physical behavior in the asymptotic limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, resulting in a loss of accuracy and robustness precisely in the regimes of most physical interest
[
18
]
. To address this challenge, the principles of Asymptotic-Preserving (AP) schemes, a well-established tool in classical numerical analysis
[
13
]
, were integrated into the neural network framework, leading to the Asymptotic-Preserving Neural Network (APNN)
[
3
,
14
,
18
,
25
]
. By employing a micro-macro decomposition, APNNs enforce the correct asymptotic structure at the level of the loss function, ensuring accuracy and efficiency across different physical regimes.

Despite its advantages, the APNNs methodology reveals a practical limitation: in the crucial fluid-dynamic regime where
\varepsilon
is very small, the neural network’s convergence for the macroscopic density,
\rho
, can be prohibitively slow. This paper introduces the so-called
Bi-fidelity Asymptotic-Preserving Neural Networks (BI-APNNs)
to address these challenges. Our core idea is to re-formulate the learning task by decomposing the density into a dominant, low-fidelity component and a minor, high-fidelity correction:
\rho=\rho_{\text{diff}}+\rho_{\text{corr}}
.
The
\rho_{\text{diff}}
term, which represents the dominant component of the density profile in the asymptotic limit, can be efficiently pre-trained or computed. A much smaller and more agile network is then dedicated to learning only the correction term,
\rho_{\text{corr}}
. This bi-fidelity strategy yields a twofold benefit: it substantially accelerates the training process and simultaneously improves the final accuracy of the forward problem solution. Crucially, we find that this enhanced fidelity provides a decisive advantage in solving inverse problems, leading to more reliable and accurate parameter estimations.

This paper focuses specifically on the Boltzmann semiconductor equation, a fundamental model in modern electronics
[
19
]
. We aim to address two fundamental challenges: first, the efficient and accurate simulation of the system’s evolution, known as the forward problem; and second, the inverse problem of estimating unknown physical parameters, such as the collision cross-section, given limited observational data of the particle density.

The main contribution of this work is to design a new and novel bi-fidelity based APNNs approach to significantly accelerate the training process in previously developed APNNs, and apply it to solve the semiconductor Boltzmann(-Poisson) equation and its parameter inference using limited data. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the semiconductor Boltzmann model and the micro-macro decomposition. In Section
3
, we present the formulation of our proposed BI-APNN framework, with convergence analysis provided in Section
4
. Extensive numerical experiments are shown in Section
5
to validate the robustness and efficiency of our method in solving both forward problem and parameters inference, followed by conclusion in the final section.

2
The models and Micro-macro decomposition method

The semiconductor-Boltzmann.

We consider the semiconductor Boltzmann equation
[
16
]
given by

\varepsilon\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\nabla_{x}\phi\,\cdot\nabla_{v}f=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{Q}(f),

(1)

where
f(t,x,v)
is the probability density distribution for particles located at
x\in\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
with velocity
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
. In our model,
\varepsilon
is the Knudsen number defined as the ratio of the mean free path and the typical length scale. In our application,
\varepsilon
varies from
O(1)
, the kinetic regime, to
\varepsilon\ll 1
, the diffusion regime. the
\phi(t,x)
is external electric potential.

The anisotropic collision operator
\mathcal{Q}
describes a linear approximation of the electron-phonon interaction, given by

\mathcal{Q}(f)(t,x,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}\sigma(v,w)\left(M(v)f(t,x,w)-M(w)f(t,x,v)\right)dw,

with
M
the normalized Maxwellian

M(v)=\frac{1}{\pi^{d/2}}e^{-|v|^{2}}
.
Here
\sigma(v,w)
denotes the scattering coefficient for the electron-phonon collisions. The collision frequency is defined as

\lambda(v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}\sigma(v,w)M(w)dw.

We refer the readers to
[
10
,
19
,
32
]
for more physical background.

Define
n_{x}
the unit outward normal vector on the spatial boundary

\partial\mathcal{D}
. Let
\gamma=\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega
, then the phase boundary can be split into an outgoing boundary
\gamma_{+}
, incoming boundary
\gamma_{-}
and a singular boundary
\gamma_{0}
, which are defined by

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}:=\left\{(x,v)\in\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega:v\cdot n_{x}>0\right\},

(2)

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}:=\left\{(x,v)\in\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega:v\cdot n_{x}<0\right\},

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}:=\left\{(x,v)\in\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega:v\cdot n_{x}=0\right\}.

The inflow boundary condition is given by

f(t,x,v)=f_{\text{BC}}(t,x,v),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }\,(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\gamma_{-}.

(3)

We assume the initial condition that

f(t=0,x,v)=f_{\text{IC}}(x,v).

The Boltzmann-Poisson system.

In this work, we also study the
nonlinear
Boltzmann-Poisson system, where the electric potential
\phi(t,x)
is solved by the Poisson equation as below:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\nabla_{x}\phi\,\cdot\nabla_{v}f=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{Q}(f),\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\beta\Delta_{x}\phi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}fdv-c(x),\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\phi(t,0)=0,\qquad\phi(t,1)=V,\end{array}\right.

(4)

with
\beta
the scaled Debye length,
V
the applied bias voltage and
c(x)
the doping profile.

2.1
The micro-macro decomposition method

We employ the micro-macro decomposition technique
[
24
]
and derive the micro-macro system for the semiconductor Boltzmann equation (
1
).
Assume the ansatz

f=\Pi f+\varepsilon g,

(5)

where
g:=g(t,x,v)
and the notation
\Pi f:=\langle f\rangle\,M(v)
is defined by

\langle f\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t,x,v)\,dv=\rho(t,x).

Inserting the ansatz (
5
) into (
1
), one gets

\varepsilon\partial_{t}(\Pi f)+\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g+v\cdot\nabla_{x}(\Pi f)+\varepsilon\nabla_{x}\cdot(vg)-2v\rho\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\,M(v)+\varepsilon\nabla_{x}\phi\ \cdot\nabla_{v}g=\mathcal{Q}(g),

(6)

where
\nabla_{v}(\Pi f)=\rho\,\nabla_{v}M(v)=-2\rho\,vM(v)
is used.

Take the projection operator
\Pi
on both sides of (
6
), then

\varepsilon\partial_{t}(\Pi f)+\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}(\Pi g)+\nabla_{x}\rho\left(\int vM(v)dv\right)\cdot M(v)+\varepsilon\Pi\nabla_{x}\cdot(vg)+\varepsilon\nabla_{x}\phi\,\cdot\Pi(\nabla_{v}g)=0,

which is equivalent to

\partial_{t}(\Pi f)+\Pi\nabla_{x}\cdot(vg)+\nabla_{x}\phi\,\cdot\Pi(\nabla_{v}g)=0,

(7)

where
\Pi g=0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}vM(v)dv=0
is used.
Now subtract (
6
) by (
7
), then

\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g+v\cdot\nabla_{x}(\Pi f)+\varepsilon(\mathbb{I}-\Pi)\nabla_{x}\cdot(vg)+\varepsilon\nabla_{x}\phi\,\cdot(\mathbb{I}-\Pi)\nabla_{v}g-2v\rho\,\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\,M(v)=\mathcal{Q}(g),

that is

\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\rho\,M(v)+\varepsilon(\mathbb{I}-\Pi)\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}g+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{v}g\right)-2v\rho\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\,M(v)=\mathcal{Q}(g).

(8)

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, equation (
7
) stays unchanged, and the microscopic equation (
8
) gives

g=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}\rho\,M(v)-2v\rho\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\,M(v)\right)=\left(\nabla_{x}\rho-2\rho\nabla_{x}\phi\right)\mathcal{Q}^{-1}(vM(v)).

Plug
g
into (
7
) and integrate over
v
, we derive the drift-diffusion limit
[
29
]
:

\partial_{t}\rho=\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(T\nabla_{x}\rho-2(\rho\,\nabla_{x}\phi)\right),

(9)

where the diffusion matrix
T
is defined by

T=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}\frac{v\bigotimes vM(v)}{\lambda(v)}dv
.

To summarize, the coupled equations (
7
)–(
8
) for
\rho
and
g
solve the following system in our micro-macro decomposition framework:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{x}\cdot\langle vg\rangle+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\langle\partial_{v}g\rangle=0,\\[6.0pt]
\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\rho\,M(v)+\varepsilon(\mathbb{I}-\Pi)\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}g+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{v}g\right)-2v\rho\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\,M(v)=\mathcal{Q}(g),\\[6.0pt]
\Pi g=0.\end{array}\right.

(10)

where the third equation is for the mass conservation for
g
, which is crucial to the training of neural networks
[
14
]
and will be mentioned in section
3.2
.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, according to (
10
), the limiting system for
\rho
and
g
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{x}\cdot\langle vg\rangle+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\langle\nabla_{v}g\rangle=0,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle v\cdot\nabla_{x}\rho\,M(v)-2v\rho\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\,M(v)=\mathcal{Q}(g),\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\Pi g=0,\end{array}\right.

(11)

which is
equivalent
to the diffusion limit given in (
9
).

3
Methodology

This section details the numerical methodology developed for efficiently solving the Boltzmann semiconductor system introduced in Section
2
. As previously discussed, this multiscale kinetic equation presents significant computational challenges for standard numerical and machine learning methods. To address these challenges, we propose a novel bi-fidelity framework built upon recent advances in physics-informed neural networks.

To logically construct our approach, this section is organized as follows. We begin by reviewing the foundational concepts of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs). We then discuss their extension, the Asymptotic-Preserving Neural Networks (APNNs), which are specifically designed for multiscale problems. Finally, we introduce our primary contribution: the Bi-fidelity Asymptotic-Preserving Neural Networks (BI-APNNs), and detail their formulation.

3.1
Physics Informed Neural Networks

The fundamental idea of Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
[
30
]
is to approximate the solution of a partial differential equation (PDE) using a neural network. The network is trained by minimizing a loss function that directly incorporates the underlying physics. This loss function is constructed by summing the mean squared errors of several components: (1) the PDE residual evaluated on a set of collocation points inside the domain, (2) the mismatch between the network’s prediction and the prescribed initial conditions, and (3) the mismatch at the boundary conditions. To find the optimal values for the network parameters
\theta
that are composed of all the weights
w_{ji}
and bias
b_{j}
, the neural network is trained by minimizing the following loss function

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{PINN}}^{\varepsilon}=Loss_{\text{GE}}+\lambda_{1}Loss_{\text{BC}}+\lambda_{2}Loss_{\text{IC}},

(12)

where the loss for the residual of the governing PDE is given by:

Loss_{\text{GE}}:=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\varepsilon\partial_{t}f_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+v\partial_{x}f_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\partial_{x}\phi\,\partial_{v}f_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{Q}(f_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}})|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{v}d\boldsymbol{x}dt.

(13)

The loss for the boundary condition is

Loss_{\text{BC}}:=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\gamma_{-}}\left|\mathcal{B}\left(\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(v)+\varepsilon g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right)-f_{\text{BC}}\right|^{2}\,dsdt,

(14)

and the loss for the initial condition:

Loss_{\text{IC}}:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathcal{I}\left(\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(v)+\varepsilon g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right)-f_{\text{IC}}\right|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{v}d\boldsymbol{x},

(15)

where the initial and boundary conditions are incorporated into the loss function as a regularization or penalty term,
with penalty parameters
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
chosen for optimal performance. The most popular method to minimize the loss function over the parameter space are stochastic gradient descent and advanced optimizers such as Adam
[
20
]
. After the training process and achieving the optimal set of parameter values
\theta^{\star}
by minimizing the PINNs loss
(
12
), i.e.,

\theta^{\star}=\text{argmin}\ \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{PINNs}}^{\varepsilon}(\theta),

the neural network surrogate
f^{NN}_{\theta^{\star}}(t,x,v)
can be evaluated at any given point in the temporal and phase space to obtain the solution of (
1
).

3.2
The APNNs framework

A key challenge arises from the multiscale nature of the model, characterized by the Knudsen number
\varepsilon
. Standard PINNs formulations often fail to respect the asymptotic properties of the kinetic equation, preventing them from capturing the correct macroscopic behavior uniformly across all ranges of
\varepsilon
. This can lead to significant inaccuracies, particularly in the fluid-dynamic limit. To overcome this limitation, Asymptotic-Preserving Neural Networks (APNNs) were introduced by Jin et al.
[
15
]
. The core strategy of the APNNs framework is to design a loss function that explicitly incorporates the model’s asymptotic behavior. By doing so, the network can accurately capture the macroscopic dynamics even when the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
is small, thereby possessing the crucial Asymptotic-Preserving (AP) property. The fundamental concept of the APNNs is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Illustration of APNNs.

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}
be a multiscale model that depends on the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
. As
\varepsilon\to 0
, it converges to a reduced order limit

\mathcal{F}^{0}
. The solution of
\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}
is approximated by the neural network through the imposition of residual term
\mathcal{R}_{NN}(\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon})
,
whose asymptotic limit is denoted by
\mathcal{R}_{NN}(\mathcal{F}^{0})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The neural network is called AP if
\mathcal{R}_{NN}(\mathcal{F}^{0})
is consistent
with the residual for the system
\mathcal{F}^{0}
.

In APNNs,
[
18
,
25
]
employ two deep neural networks to parameterize the two functions
\rho(t,\boldsymbol{x})
and
g(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})
, where the notations here are extended to the multi-dimensional spatial and velocity space. Since the density
\rho
is non-negative, we put an exponential function at the last output layer of the DNN for
\rho
and denote

\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x}):=\exp\left(-\tilde{\rho}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\right)\approx\rho(t,\boldsymbol{x})

(16)

We remark that the velocity discretization for
g(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})
, where the Hermite quadrature rule is used since
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
. This is standard and the same as traditional AP method studied in
[
16
]
. Let
g(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})=\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})M(\boldsymbol{v})
, with
M(\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{1}{\pi^{d_{v}/2}}e^{-|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}}
and

\psi(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\psi_{k}(t,\boldsymbol{x})H_{k}(\boldsymbol{v})

(17)

is the Hermite expansion with
N
being the order.
We then compute the collision operator
Q
in (
1
) as follows

Q(g)(\boldsymbol{v})=M(\boldsymbol{v})\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{v},v_{j})\,\psi(v_{j})\,w_{j}-\lambda(\boldsymbol{v})g(\boldsymbol{v}),

with
\lambda(\boldsymbol{v})=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\sigma(\boldsymbol{v},v_{j})\,w_{j}
.
The derivative in
\boldsymbol{v}
for
\psi
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}\psi=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{v}}\psi(v_{j})\,C_{j}(\boldsymbol{v}),

(18)

where
C_{j}(\boldsymbol{v})=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sqrt{2k}\,H_{k}(v_{j})H_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{v})w_{j}
.

In order to obtain approximation for
g
, we let
\tilde{\psi}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})
be the output of a fully-connected neural network with input
t
,
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
, then impose a post-processing step to guarantee the mass conservation in (
10
). Define

g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})=\psi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})M(\boldsymbol{v}):=\tilde{\psi}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})M(\boldsymbol{v})-\Pi\left(\tilde{\psi}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})M(\boldsymbol{v})\right),

(19)

as an approximation for
g
, now

\Pi g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}=0
is automatically satisfied. Moreover,
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}
is computed by

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\psi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},v_{j})C_{j}(\boldsymbol{v})M(\boldsymbol{v})-2\boldsymbol{v}M(\boldsymbol{v})\psi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})+2\boldsymbol{v}M(\boldsymbol{v})\langle\psi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{v})M(\boldsymbol{v})\rangle.

(20)

where
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}\psi_{\theta}^{NN}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\boldsymbol{v}}}\psi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},v_{j})C_{j}(\boldsymbol{v})
.

APNNs Loss.
For the APNNs method, the physics-informed loss function is constructed from the residuals of the coupled macro-micro system (
7
)–(
8
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNNs}}^{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left|\partial_{t}\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right\rangle+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\phi\cdot\left\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{x}dt

(21)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\varepsilon(I-\Pi)\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\phi\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}}\right)

\displaystyle-2\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\phi\,\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\,M(\boldsymbol{v})+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\boldsymbol{v})-\mathcal{Q}(g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}})\Big|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{v}d\boldsymbol{x}dt

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{|\mathcal{T}\times\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\gamma_{-}}\left|\mathcal{B}\left(\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\boldsymbol{v})+\varepsilon g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right)-f_{\text{BC}}\right|^{2}\,dsdt

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{|\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathcal{I}\left(\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\boldsymbol{v})+\varepsilon g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right)-f_{\text{IC}}\right|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{v}d\boldsymbol{x}.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the above first two terms arisen from the model equation lead to

\begin{split}&\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNNs}}\text{, residual}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left|\partial_{t}\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right\rangle+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\phi\cdot\left\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{x}dt\\[6.0pt]
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\left|-2\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\phi\,\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\,M(v)+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\,M(v)-\mathcal{Q}(g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}})\right|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{v}d\boldsymbol{x}dt,\end{split}

which is identical to the loss function associated with the limiting system (
11
). Consequently, the proposed loss function possesses the Asymptotic-Preserving (AP) property, a key feature that standard PINN formulations lack.

3.3
Bi-fidelity method and Bi-APNNs Method

The bi-fidelity method is a computational approach that leverages solutions from both low-fidelity and high-fidelity models to balance accuracy and computational efficiency. By combining a coarse, computationally inexpensive model with a corrective term derived from a more accurate but resource-intensive model, it aims to approximate high-fidelity solutions at a reduced cost. We consider two ways of decomposition for
\rho
based on the bi-fidelity idea, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\text{(i) Explicit formulation: }\hskip 10.00002pt\rho_{\text{bi}}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\rho_{\textit{diff}}(t,\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon\rho_{\textit{corr}}(t,\mathbf{x}),

(22)

\displaystyle\text{(ii) Implicit formulation: }\hskip 10.00002pt\rho_{\text{bi}}(t,\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\rho_{\textit{diff}}(t,\mathbf{x})+\rho_{\textit{corr}}(t,\mathbf{x}),

where the solution
\rho_{\textit{diff}}
from (
9
) is combined with a correction term. The explicit formulation is motivated by the asymptotic property of the semiconductor Boltzmann equation. When the Knudsen number
\varepsilon
tends to zero, the solution gradually approaches the diffusion limit, and the microscopic correction becomes negligible. By expressing the macroscopic variable as
\rho=\rho_{\textit{diff}}+\varepsilon\rho_{\textit{corr}}
, this scaling explicitly reflects the vanishing influence of the kinetic component in the diffusive regime, which means
\rho
naturally converges to
\rho_{\textit{diff}}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Forward solution approximation
. In the Bi-APNNs, based on (
22
), we design the following two formulations:

1.

Explicit formulation (E-bi-APNNs):

\rho_{\text{bi},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\mathbf{x}):=\rho_{\textit{diff}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon\rho_{\textit{corr}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\mathbf{x}),

(23)

2.

Implicit formulation (I-bi-APNNs):

\rho_{\text{bi},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\mathbf{x}):=\rho_{\textit{diff}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\mathbf{x})+\rho_{\textit{corr}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\mathbf{x}).

(24)

BI-APNNs Loss.
Similar to the APNNs, the physics-informed loss is the residual of the micro-macro system (
10
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\text{BI-APNNs}}^{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left|\partial_{t}\rho_{\textit{bi},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot\left\langle\boldsymbol{v}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right\rangle+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\phi\cdot\left\langle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{x}dt

(25)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\varepsilon(I-\Pi)\left(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\phi\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}}\right)

\displaystyle-2\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\phi\,\rho_{\textit{bi},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\,M(\boldsymbol{v})+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\rho_{\textit{bi},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\boldsymbol{v})-\mathcal{Q}(g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}})\Big|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{v}d\boldsymbol{x}dt

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{|\mathcal{T}\times\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\gamma_{-}}\left|\mathcal{B}\left(\rho_{\textit{bi},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\boldsymbol{v})+\varepsilon g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right)-f_{\text{BC}}\right|^{2}\,dsdt

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{|\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathcal{I}\left(\rho_{\textit{bi},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\boldsymbol{v})+\varepsilon g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right)-f_{\text{IC}}\right|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{v}d\boldsymbol{x}.

Note that the difference from the standard APNNs is that here we have two parts,
\rho^{\text{NN}}_{\textit{diff}}
and
\rho^{\text{NN}}_{\textit{corr}}
, both approximated from neural networks. In the forward problem, we first pre-train a neural network to approximate the solution of the diffusion equation (
9
) and obtain
\rho^{\text{NN}}_{\textit{diff}}
. In this step the loss function is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left|\partial_{t}\rho_{\textit{diff},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}-\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(T\nabla_{x}\rho_{\textit{diff},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}-2(\rho_{\textit{diff},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\,\nabla_{x}\phi)\right)\right|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{x}dt

(26)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{\text{diff}}}{|\mathcal{T}\times\partial\mathcal{D}|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\gamma_{-}}\left|\mathcal{B}\left(\rho_{\textit{diff},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\boldsymbol{v})\right)-f_{\text{BC}}\right|^{2}\,dsdt

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{2}^{\text{diff}}}{|\mathcal{D}|}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathcal{I}\left(\rho_{\textit{diff},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\boldsymbol{v})\right)-f_{\text{IC}}\right|^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{v}d\boldsymbol{x}.

This pretrained model captures the macroscopic behavior of the system when
\varepsilon
is small. In the subsequent training,
\rho^{\text{NN}}_{\textit{diff}}
is fixed and treated as a known function, and the neural network only learns the correction term
\rho^{\text{NN}}_{\textit{corr}}
. Incorporating the governing equation of the diffusion limit may accelerate the convergence of the training procedure and potentially improve the accuracy of approximating the solution. Since the macroscopic dynamics are already encoded in a pretrained network, the training process of our designed BI-APNNs is expected to be more efficient.

The BI-APNNs framework is applied to both the semiconductor Boltzmann equation (
1
) and the Boltzmann-Poisson system (
4
). A key distinction in the implementation of these two models lies in the treatment of the electric potential,
\phi(t,x)
. For the standard semiconductor Boltzmann equation, the potential is a known function. In contrast, for the Boltzmann-Poisson system, the potential is itself an unknown that depends on the particle density. To address this, we introduce a separate neural network,
\phi_{\theta}(t,x)
, dedicated to approximating this self-consistent potential. It is important to note that this architectural approach—employing an auxiliary network for
\phi
when necessary—is consistently used for both the forward and inverse problems. We summarize the framework of our method for the forward problem in Figure
2
.

For the inverse problem, we propose the physics-informed loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}
, which is composed of three components:

1.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{BI-APNNs}}
: This loss is the same as in (
27
).

2.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}}
: This loss is the same formulation as (
26
). However, different from the forward problem, where a pre-trained diffusion model can be used, in the inverse problem setting the key physical parameter (such as
\sigma
) is unknown. Therefore, the diffusion network should be
jointly
trained with the Bi-APNN loss, serving as the one regularizer in the total loss.

3.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}
: A data-misfit loss that penalizes discrepancies with observed data.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}=\frac{w_{d}^{\rho}}{N_{d_{1}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d_{1}}}\left|\rho_{\textit{bi},\theta}(t_{d}^{i},x_{d}^{i})-\rho(t_{d}^{i},x_{d}^{i})\right|^{2}.

(27)

Finally, the total loss is defined by

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{BI-APNNs}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{diffusion}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}.

(28)

Convergence of the Bi-APNNs solution

4.1
Preliminaries

We first review an important theorem on the existence of the approximated neural network solution, namely the Universal Approximation Theorem (UAT)
[
9
]
. We adapt it to our model (
1
) and use the notations introduced in subsection
3.1
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose the solution to (
1
) satisfies
f\in C^{1}([0,T])\cap C^{1}(\mathcal{D})\cap C^{1}(\Omega)
. Let the activation function
\bar{\sigma}
be any non-polynomial function in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then for any
\delta>0
, there exists a two-layer neural network

f^{NN}(t,x,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}w_{1i}^{(2)}\bar{\sigma}\left(\left(w_{i1}^{(1)},w_{i2}^{(1)},w_{i3}^{(1)}\right)\cdot(t,x,v)+b_{i}^{(1)}\right)+b_{1}^{(2)},

such that

\displaystyle\left\|f-f^{NN}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\delta,\hskip 20.00003pt\left\|\partial_{t}(f-f^{NN})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\delta,

(29)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{x}(f-f^{NN})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\delta,\hskip 10.00002pt\left\|\nabla_{v}(f-f^{NN})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\delta,

where the domain
K
denotes
[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}\times\Omega
.

We remark that the above result can be generalized to a neural network with several hidden layers
[
22
]
.

4.2
Convergence of the loss function

Recall the APNNs framework introduced in section
3.2
, the loss function is based on the residual of the macro-micro system (
7
)–(
8
), thus our neural network approximated solutions are
\rho
and
g
, instead of
f
if applying PINNs to the model (
1
).
In
[
25
]
, the authors show that a sequence of neural network solutions to
(
7
)–(
8
) exists such that the total loss function converges to zero, if assuming the analytic solution

\rho\in C^{1}([0,T])\cap C^{1}(\mathcal{D}),\hskip 20.00003ptg\in C^{1}([0,T])\cap C^{1}(\mathcal{D})\cap C^{1}(\Omega).

(30)

Below we show the convergence of our new APNN approach in the bi-fidelity framework, namely the Bi-APNN method.

Theorem 1

(Convergence of BI-APNNs)
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem 1 in
[
25
]
hold: consider the semiconductor Boltzmann equation (
1
), where the potential
\phi(t,x)
is given and satisfies

\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathcal{D})}
bounded. Let
(\rho,g)
be the analytic solution of the macro–micro system (
7
)–(
8
) which is sufficiently smooth.

Consider the BI-APNNs’ approximations of the macroscopic density by either the explicit formulation

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{bi},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,x)=\rho_{\mathrm{diff}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,x)+\varepsilon\,\rho_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,x),

(E-bi)

or the implicit formulation

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{bi},\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,x)=\rho_{\mathrm{diff}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,x)+\rho_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,x).

(I-bi)

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BI\text{-}APNNs}}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{\mathrm{bi}},g)
denote the BI-APNNs total loss function given in (
27
), then there exists a sequence of neural network parameters
\{\theta_{[j]}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
(where
\theta_{[j]}
encompasses parameters for both
\rho_{\mathrm{diff}}^{\mathrm{NN}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{corr}}^{\mathrm{NN}}
) and corresponding BI-APNNs approximations
\{(\rho_{\mathrm{bi},j},g_{j})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

(with
\rho_{\mathrm{bi},j}
given either by (E-bi) or (I-bi)) such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{Bi\text{-}APNNs}}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{\mathrm{bi},j},g_{j})\;\to\;0,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{as }j\to\infty.

(31)

Proof.

Expanding the analytic solution
\rho
in the following form:

\rho(t,x)=\rho^{(0)}(t,x)+\rho^{(1)}(t,x;\varepsilon),

(32)

where
\rho^{(0)}
satisfies the limiting drift-diffusion equation (
9
), and
\rho^{(1)}
is a higher-order correction term. The micro-part
g
is also smooth under the assumptions (
30
).

We construct the BI-APNNs solution sequence as follows. Let
\delta_{j}^{(1)},\delta_{j}^{(2)}>0
be a sequence such that they converge to zero as
j\to\infty
(e.g.,
\delta_{j}=1/j
). By UAT, there exist networks such that:

\displaystyle\|\rho_{\mathrm{diff},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}-\rho^{(0)}\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{j}^{(1)},

(33)

\displaystyle\|g_{j}^{\mathrm{NN}}-g\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{j}^{(2)},

(34)

where
K_{1}=[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}
and
K_{2}=[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}\times\Omega
. Due to smoothness of the solutions, similar bounds hold for derivatives up to first and second orders.

Let
\delta_{j}=\text{max}(\delta_{j}^{(1)},\delta_{j}^{(2)})
. We consider the two formulations:

1.

Explicit formulation (E-bi):
Assume

\|\varepsilon\rho_{\mathrm{corr},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}-\rho^{(1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}\leq O(\delta_{j})
,
the Bi-APNNs solution is
\rho_{\mathrm{bi},j}=\rho_{\mathrm{diff},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\varepsilon\rho_{\mathrm{corr},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}
, then we have:

\|\rho_{\mathrm{bi},j}-\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}\leq\|\rho_{\mathrm{diff},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}-\rho^{(0)}\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}+\|\varepsilon\rho_{\mathrm{corr},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}-\rho^{(1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}\leq O(\delta_{j}).

(35)

2.

Implicit formulation (I-bi):
Assume

\|\rho_{\mathrm{corr},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}-\rho^{(1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}\leq O(\delta_{j})
,
the BI-APNNs solution is
\rho_{\mathrm{bi},j}=\rho_{\mathrm{diff},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\rho_{\mathrm{corr},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}
, then we have:

\|\rho_{\mathrm{bi},j}-\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}\leq\|\rho_{\mathrm{diff},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}-\rho^{(0)}\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}+\|\rho_{\mathrm{corr},j}^{\mathrm{NN}}-\rho^{(1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}\leq O(\delta_{j}).

(36)

Similar to
[
25
, Theorem 1]
, there exists a constant
L>0
such that

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{BI-APNNs}}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{\mathrm{bi},j},g_{j})\leq L\left(\|\rho_{\mathrm{bi},j}-\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}+\|g_{j}-g\|_{L^{\infty}(K_{2})}\right)\leq O(\delta_{j}),

where
L
is independent of
\varepsilon
.

∎

Numerical examples

In our numerical experiments, we will show several examples to illustrate the robustness and accuracy of our designed BI-APNNs method. We compare the reference solution obtained by traditional numerical methods, APNNs method, PINNs method and BI-APNNs methods in different regimes, ranging from the kinetic regime (
\varepsilon\approx O(1)
) to the diffusive regime (
\varepsilon\ll 1
), and observe that the BI-APNNs can capture the multiscale nature of the model thanks to the design of the loss function based on the macro-micro decomposition.

For the velocity discretization, the integral in
v
is computed by using the Hermite quadrature rule with
N_{v}
quadrature points. We adopt the Hermite polynomials in velocity discretization to provide more accurate velocity derivatives, a technique similar to the moment method
[
17
,
31
]
. See details in the Appendix.

Networks Architecture.
In our experiments, we use the feed-forward neural network (FNN) with one input layer, one output layer and
4
hidden layers with
128
neurons in each layer, unless otherwise specified. The hyperbolic tangent function (Tanh) is chosen as our activation function. This setting is adopted from previous work
[
14
,
18
]
, as it has been validated to perform effectively on similar Boltzmann problems.

Training Settings.
We train the neural network by Adam
[
21
]
with Xavier initialization. We set epochs to be
20000
and the learning rate to be
10^{-4}
, and use full batch for most of the following experiments in the numerical experiments unless otherwise specified. All the hyper-parameters are chosen by trial and error.

Loss Design.
To compute the empirical risk losses for BI-APNNs in (
44
) and (
47
). For most of our experiments, we consider the spatial and temporal domains to be
[0,1]
and
[0,0.1]
respectively. We choose the collocation points
\{(t_{i},x_{i},v_{i})\}
for

in the following way. For spatial points
x_{i}

interior points evenly spaced in
[0,1]
. For temporal points

5.1.2
Inverse problem

In this section, we will examine the the proposed Bi-APNNs framework for an inverse problem: inferring the scattering coefficient from measurement data. To assess the robustness and practical utility of our method, we focus on a particularly challenging scenario where only partial observation data is available. The performance of the Bi-APNNs is evaluated across different physical regimes. For a direct comparison, we also solve the same inverse problem using the standard APNNs approach as a baseline, allowing for a clear assessment of the improvements offered by the bi-fidelity formulation.

In real-world applications, the microscopic term
g
is difficult to measure, we assume that measurement data can only be obtained for the macroscopic density,
\rho
. We generate a synthetic dataset, denoted as
\{\left(t_{d}^{i},x_{d}^{i}\right),\rho_{\text{obs}}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{d}}
, using the classical AP numerical scheme developed in
[
16
]
.

To solve this inverse problem using either the BI-APNNs or APNNs framework, we add a data misfit loss as below to the original empirical loss function (
44
) and (
47
). This term, which we denote as
\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}
, quantifies the misfit between the network’s prediction for the density and the observed data via the mean squared error:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}^{\rho}(\theta)^{=}\frac{w_{d}^{\rho}}{N_{d_{1}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{d_{1}}}\left|\rho^{\mathrm{NN}}(t_{d}^{i},x_{d}^{i};\theta)-\rho(t_{d}^{i},x_{d}^{i})\right|^{2}.

(38)

Since PINNs method can not infer
\sigma
when
\varepsilon
is small even using full observation data
[
25
]
, here we only test Bi-APNNs and APNNs’ performance on inferring the target parameter
\sigma

6
Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we have developed and analyzed a Bi-fidelity Asymptotic-Preserving Neural Network (BI-APNNs) framework for solving forward and inverse problems for the semiconductor Boltzmann equation. This work builds upon the standard APNNs methodology, specifically addressing a key practical bottleneck: the slow convergence. The slower convergence of the standard APNNs can be observed in both the training loss and the relative
\ell_{2}
error curves, particularly in the computationally challenging fluid-dynamic regime. In contrast, the BI-APNNs shows faster overall convergence of the macroscopic density,
\rho
, in the computationally challenging fluid-dynamic regime. The core of our approach is a novel bi-fidelity decomposition of the density. In the forward problem, it allows a pre-trained low-cost network to capture the dominant physical behavior, while a much smaller network learns the remaining high-fidelity correction. Through a series of numerical experiments, we have demonstrated the key advantages of this bi-fidelity formulation. The results confirm that, particularly in the small-
\varepsilon
regime, the BI-APNNs not only significantly accelerate training convergence but also improve the accuracy of the forward problem solution. Most importantly, we have shown that this enhanced accuracy translates directly into superior performance for the associated inverse problems when compared to the standard APNNs. This work underscores the potential of bi-fidelity methods to enhance physics-informed neural networks, making them a more efficient and reliable tool for complex multiscale kinetic problems.

Several promising directions for future research emerge from this work. On the theoretical front, our convergence analysis could be further refined by incorporating tools from Barron spaces
[
12
]
and developing posterior error estimates to more precisely quantify the network’s approximation capabilities. From a practical standpoint, extending the BI-APNNs framework to higher-dimensional problems is a key next step. Furthermore, the computational efficiency demonstrated by the BI-APNNs makes them an particularly attractive candidate for uncertainty quantification (UQ) problems, where the need for numerous forward solves often creates a computational bottleneck.
