Exploring the prevalence of gestational diabetes mellitus and associated factors among pregnant women attending tertiary antenatal hospitals in Bangladesh: a cross-sectional survey-based approach
Authors: Mahmuda Tasnim, Nazifa Tabassum Tasnim, Aklima Sultana, Nadia Sultana Deen, Fahim Kabir Monjurul Haque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a form of hyperglycemia which is first diagnosed during pregnancy. South Asian women show a higher prevalence of GDM, which has been associated with both genetic and lifestyle factors.ObjectivesThis survey-based cross-sectional study aimed to estimate the prevalence of GDM among pregnant women attending tertiary healthcare facilities for antenatal care and to identify factors independently associated with GDM.MethodsA total of 250 pregnant women at 18–40 weeks of gestation attending tertiary healthcare facilities in the Dhaka, Chattogram, and Mymensingh divisions were selected using a non-probability consecutive sampling approach. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire covering demographic, obstetric, clinical, and lifestyle factors after obtaining informed consent from all participants. GDM status was determined based on a one-step 75 g OGTT test in accordance with national and WHO criteria.ResultsThe prevalence of GDM was 14% in this study. Fisher’s exact test and Chi-square test were used for initial comparisons. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were performed to estimate crude odds ratios (cOR) and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for factors associated with GDM. In the adjusted analysis, obesity class II (aOR: 9.929, 95% CI: 1.174–83.983, p = 0.035) and obesity class III (aOR: 26.945, 95% CI: 1.101-659.624, p = 0.044) were significantly associated with increased odds of GDM. However, the wide confidence intervals suggest limited precision of these estimates. Parental history of diabetes (aOR: 3.079, 95% CI: 1.275–7.432, p = 0.012), betel quid chewing (aOR: 2.689, 95% CI: 1.010–7.160, p = 0.048), and sugar-sweetened beverage intake (aOR: 3.827, 95% CI: 1.649–8.880, p = 0.002) were also significantly associated with increased odds of GDM. In contrast, maternal age (aOR: 0.996, 95% CI: 0.911–1.088, p = 0.922), pre-obesity (aOR: 2.238, 95% CI: 0.461–10.864, p = 0.317), obesity class I (aOR: 4.515, 95% CI: 0.921–22.139, p = 0.063) and oral contraceptive use (aOR: 2.136, 95% CI: 0.871–5.240, p = 0.097) were not significantly associated with GDM after adjustment. The first pregnancy was associated with lower odds of GDM in the crude analysis; however, this association was no longer statistically significant after adjustment (aOR: 0.382, 95% CI: 0.118–1.241, p = 0.110).ConclusionThese findings highlight the multifactorial nature of GDM, with both genetic susceptibility and modifiable lifestyle behaviours contributing to its development. Targeted awareness initiatives focusing on dietary behaviours, particularly reducing the consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages, may be beneficial in similar populations, alongside early screening of women with a family history of diabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09358-w.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a type of glucose intolerance that develops during pregnancy and is often diagnosed between the 24th and 28th weeks of gestation [1]. Insulin resistance and pregnancy-induced hormonal shifts play central roles, which closely mirror the pathophysiology of type 2 diabetes [2]. Globally, systematic estimates indicate that approximately 14% of pregnancies are affected by GDM [3]. According to the International Diabetes Federation in 2017, one in seven births worldwide was affected by GDM [4]. However, prevalence varies substantially across regions. In Bangladesh, GDM is an emerging public health concern, with a steadily increasing prevalence [5].

The development of GDM is influenced by various predisposing factors, which can be broadly classified into lifestyle, sociodemographic, obstetric, and clinical categories [6]. Uncontrolled GDM can trigger a cascade of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, with maternal sequelae characterized by vaginal infection [7], polyhydramnios [8], macrosomia, preterm birth, increased rate of cesarean section, and obstructed labor [9]. Neonatal complications may include hypoglycemia, neonatal jaundice, hypocalcemia, respiratory distress syndrome, and birth trauma [10], while long-term outcomes in offspring include increased likelihood of obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, rapid weight gain, and cardiovascular diseases in later life [11]. From a maternal health perspective, a history of GDM is associated with higher odds of recurrence in subsequent pregnancies, as well as an increased likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease over time. These outcomes underscore the complexity of GDM and reinforce the need for comprehensive screening and interventions [12].

The study aimed to explore the factors associated with GDM, including sociodemographic, obstetric, clinical, and lifestyle determinants, to facilitate early identification of women at a higher likelihood of GDM. The findings may contribute to a better understanding of GDM prevalence and factors associated with GDM among participants attending tertiary antenatal hospitals and may help inform screening and prevention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 250 consecutively recruited pregnant women attending tertiary antenatal care facilities in the Dhaka, Chattogram, and Mymensingh divisions of Bangladesh between March and September 2024. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [13] (Table S.2).

Study population, eligibility, and recruitment

Pregnant women attending routine tertiary antenatal clinics were screened for eligibility. Informed written consent in both the local and English languages and institutional ethical approval were obtained before data collection. Inclusion criteria were gestational age between 18 and 40 weeks, and age ≥ 16 years. Participants were required to have undergone a one-step 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and had to provide written informed consent. Pregnant women who did not undergo the one-step 75 g OGTT or who refused to provide written informed consent were excluded from the study. All eligible participants during the study period were approached using a non-probability consecutive sampling method by trained research personnel. Enrollment continued until the required sample size was achieved. This approach aimed to reduce selection bias by ensuring systematic inclusion of all eligible participants.

The diagnostic criteria for GDM were based on a one-step 75 g OGTT with three plasma glucose measurements obtained at fasting, 1 h, and 2 h, in accordance with Bangladesh government guidelines adapted from World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations [14]. Although routine screening is recommended between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, early screening is performed for high-risk women [15]. All measurements were based on venous plasma glucose. GDM was diagnosed when any of these measured values met or exceeded the diagnostic threshold shown in Table 1.

Data collection

Questionnaire

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire with information verified from medical records where available. The components of the questionnaire aimed to evaluate sociodemographic factors, including age, educational qualifications, occupational status and marital status. The questionnaire also assessed the obstetric factors associated with GDM, including history of preeclampsia, pregnancy-related symptoms, and prior perinatal outcomes. Furthermore, the components were utilized to evaluate the clinical variables associated with GDM, including Body Mass Index (BMI), family history of diabetes and hypertension, and prior use of oral contraceptives. Lastly, the questionnaire evaluated lifestyle variables, including physical activity, chewing betel quid, and consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages and fast food.

Variables

Maternal age was analyzed as a continuous variable in the regression model, whereas all other sociodemographic, obstetric, clinical, and lifestyle variables were coded as categorical variables for statistical analysis.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric data, including weight and height, were collected to calculate Body Mass Index (BMI). Height was measured using a stadiometer at enrolment. Pre-pregnancy weight was obtained through self-report. BMI was calculated using the formula: weight (Kg) divided by height (m2), and categorized according to WHO cut-offs adapted for Asian populations: underweight (< 18.5 Kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 Kg/m2), pre-obesity (25-29.9 Kg/m2 ), obesity class I (30-34.9 Kg/m2), obesity class II (35-39.9 Kg/m2), and obesity class III (≥ 40 Kg/m2) [17–19].

Lifestyle factors

Lifestyle variables, including physical activity, betel quid chewing, sugar-sweetened beverage consumption, and fast-food intake, were self-reported and categorized as binary variables (Yes/No) according to regular engagement or consumption.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were organized in Microsoft Excel 2018, followed by statistical analysis using SPSS version 23.0 (Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 8 to generate comprehensive statistical results. The study variables were summarized using descriptive statistics, where categorical variables were shown as frequencies and percentages. A bar chart was used to graphically depict the prevalence of GDM.

Sociodemographic, obstetric, clinical, and lifestyle variables were first assessed using univariate logistic regression analysis to estimate crude odds ratios (cOR). Maternal age was modelled as a continuous variable (per 1-year increase). Variables with p-value < 0.05, along with those of established clinical relevance, were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% CI. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All variables included in the analysis had complete data, with no missing values. Estimates affected by sparse data or zero cell counts were reported as “not estimated” in the table.

Baseline characteristics of lifestyle factors

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 2. A total of 250 pregnant women were included in the final analysis. Nearly half (45.6%) were aged 16–25 years, while 4.4% were aged 36–40 years. Regarding educational attainment, 42 (16.8%) participants had primary-level education. In terms of occupational status, the majority were homemakers, while 22.8% were engaged in paid employment.

Regarding clinical history, 48.4% reported no family history of diabetes, while 13.6% reported both parental diabetes. In addition, 39.2% reported no family history of hypertension. In relation to contraceptive use, 63.2% reported no prior use, while barrier methods were the most common among users, followed by oral contraceptives (18%). Notably, the majority (96.8%) of the participants had no personal history of diabetes, and 92.0% had no history of hypertension (Table 2).

Obstetric characteristics of the participants

In the current pregnancy, based on a one-step 75 g OGTT, 14% were diagnosed with GDM, while 11.6% were diagnosed with preeclampsia. Additionally, 14.1% of the participants reported having GDM in the previous pregnancy. Further, among the study participants, 27.6% were in their first pregnancy, while the majority (72.4%) had experienced at least one pregnancy. Among those with prior deliveries, 39.2% underwent cesarean section, while 24.8% had vaginal delivery. Among participants with a previous history of GDM, 78.3% (18/23) reported having undergone a cesarean section in a prior pregnancy. Regarding pregnancy loss, 5.2% of the participants experienced induced abortion, 19.2% reported miscarriage, and 3.2% experienced intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD). In addition to that, 4% had a history of stillbirth (Table 2).

Lifestyle factors

In terms of lifestyle habits (Table 3), only 29.2% of the participants reported engaging in regular exercise. Notably, 85.5% did not chew betel quid. Moreover, 98.8% of participants reported consuming snacks between meals, while 96.4% reported regular consumption of fruits and vegetables. Additionally, 28% and 24.4% of participants reported regular consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages and fast food, respectively.

Discussion

Our findings indicate a GDM prevalence of 14% (35/250 participants), which closely aligns with the 13% prevalence reported in a recent systematic review from Bangladesh [20] and is consistent with the global estimate of approximately 14% reported by the IDF Diabetes Atlas [21]. This suggests that the burden of GDM in the current cohort is broadly comparable to pooled regional estimates despite differences in study design, setting, and population characteristics.

Maternal age was not significantly associated with GDM in the crude analysis (cOR: 1.036, 95% CI: 0.967–1.109, p = 0.320), and this finding remained non-significant after adjustment for key covariates (aOR: 0.996, 95% CI: 0.911–1.088, p = 0.922). The lack of a significant association may reflect the predominantly young age distribution of the sample, with nearly half of the participants (45.6%) aged 16–25 years, which may have limited the ability to detect an age-related effect. In contrast, the broader epidemiological literature consistently reports an association between advanced maternal age and GDM [22]. The discrepancy may therefore be attributable to the characteristics of the present study sample, particularly the limited representation of older maternal age groups.

Pre-pregnancy BMI was modelled as a categorical variable using established WHO clinical thresholds, with normal weight (18.5–24.9 Kg/m²) as the reference category. In the crude analysis, increasing BMI categories were associated with higher odds of GDM, particularly among women with obesity. After adjustment for relevant covariates, this increasing trend persisted. Although pre-obesity and obesity class I showed elevated estimates, these were not statistically significant, whereas obesity class II and class III were significantly associated with higher odds of GDM. These findings suggest a possible dose-response pattern between increasing maternal adiposity and GDM. Similar patterns have been reported in previous studies, where higher pre-pregnancy BMI has been consistently linked to higher odds of GDM [23]. However, the wide confidence intervals observed in the higher BMI categories indicate limited precision, likely reflecting small sample sizes within these groups. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

A parental history of diabetes (one or both parents) was strongly associated with GDM in both crude and adjusted analyses, indicating a possible contribution of genetic susceptibility and shared metabolic factors. This finding is consistent with previous studies highlighting parental diabetes as a factor associated with GDM [24, 25].

Women experiencing their first pregnancy (primigravida status) had significantly lower odds of GDM in the crude analysis; however, this association did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for potential confounders. Although prior studies have reported lower odds of GDM among primigravid women, this was not observed in the adjusted model. The initial difference may reflect underlying variations between primigravid and multiparous women, including younger maternal age and lower cumulative metabolic burden. In contrast, increasing parity has been associated with metabolic changes and higher insulin resistance [26].

Regarding hormonal exposure, oral contraceptive use was associated with increased odds of GDM in the unadjusted analysis; this relationship was attenuated after adjustment for potential confounders. This suggests that the initial association may reflect underlying metabolic or lifestyle characteristics rather than an independent effect. Previous studies have reported that oral contraceptives may influence glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity, which could partly explain the association observed in the unadjusted analysis [27].

Among lifestyle-related exposures, the intake of sugar-sweetened beverages was independently associated with GDM in the adjusted analysis. This association is supported by prior studies linking sugar-sweetened beverages to impaired glucose metabolism and insulin resistance [28, 29]. The relatively large effect size emphasizes the potential role of this exposure as a modifiable factor. Given the increasing consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages in Bangladesh, these findings may have important implications for antenatal dietary guidance and public health interventions.

Additionally, betel quid chewing was associated with higher odds of GDM in crude and adjusted analyses. Although direct evidence linking betel quid chewing specifically to GDM is limited, studies from Bangladesh have reported associations with type 2 diabetes, which may reflect underlying effects on glucose regulation [30, 31]. However, the relatively wide confidence interval observed in the adjusted analysis indicates limited precision; therefore, the estimates should be interpreted with caution.

An association between GDM and vaginal discharge was observed and should be interpreted with caution. Women with GDM reported a higher frequency of vaginal discharge compared to those without GDM. This may reflect alterations in the vaginal environment associated with hyperglycemia, which can disrupt vaginal pH and influence vaginal flora, potentially increasing susceptibility to infection [32]. This finding is presented in the supplementary file (Figure S.1).

Limitations and generalizability

The current study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Firstly, the hospital-based, cross-sectional study design, employing non-probability consecutive sampling in a tertiary antenatal setting, limits the generalizability of the prevalence estimate to the broader population of pregnant women in Bangladesh. Women attending tertiary hospitals may differ systematically from the general population, including exhibiting higher health-seeking behaviours and a greater burden of pregnancy-related complications, which may introduce selection bias and potentially overestimate the prevalence of GDM.

Secondly, several exposures, including lifestyle factors, contraceptive use, family history, and vaginal discharge, were self-reported and were therefore subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Thirdly, some subgroups, particularly women with a previous history of GDM, were small, resulting in imprecise estimates and wide confidence intervals. Despite efforts to minimize overfitting and sparse data bias in the multivariable analysis, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out due to the observational nature of the study.

In terms of generalizability, the findings are particularly relevant to pregnant women attending urban and peri-urban tertiary healthcare facilities in Bangladesh. Caution is needed when extrapolating these results to women in primary care settings, rural areas, or those with limited access to antenatal services.

In contrast to findings from a substantial body of international literature identifying advanced maternal age and previous obstetric history as important factors associated with GDM, some of these variables were not retained in the adjusted model. This discrepancy may be explained by the limited representation of these subgroups, which likely reduced the statistical power to detect independent effects. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as contradicting established epidemiological findings.

Overall, the present study highlights the multifactorial nature of GDM, with metabolic and lifestyle-related factors appearing particularly important in this population. These findings underscore the potential value of targeted health interventions, focusing on weight management, screening of family history and reducing sugar-sweetened beverage consumption.
