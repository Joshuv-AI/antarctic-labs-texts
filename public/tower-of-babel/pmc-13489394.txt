A novel prognostic model integrating the skeletal muscle index and systemic immune-inflammation index improves outcome prediction in unresectable esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Xinyue Wei, Dong Guo, Yang Li, Yuanyuan Cai, Bingyi Zhang, Shunjia Li, Daqing Sun, Jianwen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis investigation sought to assess whether integrating the Skeletal Muscle Index (SMI) and Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) could enhance prognostic stratification and improve survival prediction in patients with advanced, unresectable esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC).MethodsThe study included 200 individuals with confirmed unresectable ESCC, all of whom received chemoradiotherapy either as monotherapy or combined with immunotherapy. Kaplan-Meier methods were used to generate overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) curves, with survival differences between groups evaluated by the log-rank test. The prognostic value of the novel predictive scoring system, combining SMI and SII (SSG score), was analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression models.ResultsIn the overall cohort, the median OS and PFS were 18 months and 11 months, respectively. The prognostic significance of the SSG score was confirmed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, which yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.690 (P < .05), reflecting strong discriminative power for patient prognosis stratification. Multivariate Cox regression identified the SSG score as an independent prognostic factor for both OS (HR 2.955, 95% CI: 1.351-6.465, P = .007) and PFS (HR 2.028, 95% CI: 1.187-3.463, P = .010) in unresectable ESCC patients.ConclusionsWe developed and validated the SSG scoring system, integrating SMI and systemic SII, for prognostic assessment in unresectable ESCC. The SSG score demonstrated independent prognostic value for both OS and PFS. These findings support its clinical applicability in risk stratification and individualized treatment decision-making.Advances in knowledgeThe SSG score holds promise for improving treatment decisions and prognosis management in patients with unresectable ESCC.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer continues to be a major malignant tumor globally, accounting for over 450 000 new cases and more than 400 000 deaths each year.1,2 Although recent advancements in treatment have enhanced clinical outcomes, the overall prognosis remains unfavorable, with a 5-year survival rate ranging from 26.2% to 49.4%.3 Local recurrence or distant metastasis occurs in approximately half of the patients.4 Therefore, establishing a reliable prognostic assessment system is crucial for guiding the personalized treatment of patients with esophageal cancer, especially those with unresectable esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC).

Nutritional status is strongly linked to tumor growth, angiogenesis, and disease progression, with malnutrition acting as a key factor driving these malignant processes.5 The prognostic nutritional index (PNI), Glasgow prognostic score (GPS), and controlling nutritional status (CONUT) have emerged as valuable nutritional indicators for outcome assessment in cancer research.6 Existing studies indicate that although these nutritional indicators hold significant clinical value, their accuracy may be affected by certain confounding factors such as systemic inflammatory response and metabolic abnormalities. The skeletal muscle index (SMI) offers distinct advantages as a nutritional assessment parameter, providing an objective and reproducible quantification of whole-body muscle mass that directly reflects nutritional reserves.7 Skeletal muscle depletion represents a clinically significant prognostic indicator in patients with cancer, arising from multiple interrelated etiologies. The pathogenesis encompasses physical inactivity, endocrine abnormalities, chronic comorbidities, systemic inflammatory activation, and nutritional deficiencies.8,9 Currently, the SMI has become a key parameter for prognostic assessment of body composition. Calculated from CT-based skeletal muscle measurements at the third lumbar vertebra (L3), this index has robust clinical validation for assessing cancer prognosis.8,10

Emerging evidence demonstrates a strong pathophysiological association between chronic inflammatory processes and both tumorigenesis and cancer progression.11 Recent research has introduced the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) as a novel prognostic marker, calculated from peripheral neutrophil, platelet, and lymphocyte counts, offering a more comprehensive assessment of the host’s immune-inflammatory status.12 Accumulating evidence indicates that the prognosis of patients with diverse malignancies is closely contingent on their nutritional-immune status and the degree of systemic inflammation. Malnutrition and chronic inflammatory states promote tumor cell proliferation, migration, neovascularization, and resistance to antineoplastic therapies.13–15 Accordingly, reliance on single nutrition or inflammation-based markers remains suboptimal for prognostic assessment in ESCC.16 These considerations underscore an urgent and growing need for integrated prognostic models that concurrently incorporate inflammatory and nutritional parameters. Although prior studies have shown that the Naples Prognostic Score (NPS), which integrates nutritional and inflammatory markers, has prognostic value across various tumors, it does not account for body composition features, limiting its utility for therapeutic interventions. In contrast, the SSG score incorporates both systemic inflammation and body composition—the 2 core host factors—thereby constituting a more comprehensive and nuanced prognostic assessment. This model not only enhances predictive performance but, because its constituent indicators are potentially modifiable, also provides new clinical translation pathways for improving patient outcomes.

Notably, chronic inflammation induces muscle catabolism, resulting in a reduction of muscle mass and the development of sarcopenia.17,18 Pretreatment sarcopenia has been recognized as an important factor associated with poor prognosis in patients with cancer.19,20 Therefore, the interplay between inflammation and sarcopenia may affect clinical outcomes in these patients.21 In this study, we developed the SSG score-an integrated prognostic index that combines the SMI with the SII. Although inflammation and nutritional status have been established as effective tools for preoperative risk assessment and the prediction of postoperative outcomes in resectable esophageal cancer,22,23 their relevance in patients with unresectable ESCC remains to be elucidated; therefore, more rigorous investigation is warranted.

The prognostic significance of the SSG scoring system was evaluated in patients with unresectable ESCC in this study. Findings demonstrated that patients classified in the low-risk group (group 0) based on the SSG score exhibited significantly improved OS and PFS compared to other groups. Multivariate analysis further confirmed that the SSG score independently predicts both OS and PFS outcomes. These findings suggest that the SSG scoring system can provide reliable, evidence-based support for personalized treatment decisions in patients with unresectable ESCC.

Methods

Study population

The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent requirement. All patient data were anonymized in accordance with academic standards. This study retrospectively reviewed clinical data from patients with unresectable ESCC who received chemoradiotherapy between February 2020 and November 2024. Before receiving any chemoradiotherapy, all patients underwent routine blood tests and abdominal CT scans at our institution as part of their initial staging. Patients with a history of surgical treatment for unresectable ESCC, those with synchronous primary malignancies at other sites, and patients who did not complete the planned curative-intent chemoradiotherapy regimen were excluded from this study (Figure 1).

Flow chart of the patient inclusion and exclusion process.

Clinicopathological parameters

Under the premise of obtaining explicit informed consent from patients or compliance with applicable privacy protection regulations, study covariates were extracted from the electronic medical records system. These variables included demographic factors (age, sex, body mass index), lifestyle habits (history of smoking and alcohol consumption), Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), tumor location, TNM staging, tumor differentiation grade, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), SII, PNI, OS, and PFS. All data were systematically collected following standardized oncology research protocols to comprehensively evaluate clinical characteristics, therapeutic interventions, and prognostic outcomes.

CT image acquisition and assessment of SMI

All patients underwent imaging examinations before the treatment. All participants fasted for at least 4 hours prior to the examination, with only a small amount of clear water permitted. Chest and abdominal combined scans were performed in the supine position using a GE Revolution RT590 CT scanner (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) during end-inspiratory breath-hold. Scans covered the thoracic inlet to pubic symphysis, with raw data reconstructed using Varian Eclipse workstation (v16.1; Varian Inc., Palo Alto). Contours were delineated on the reconstructed axial venous‑phase image at the mid‑vertebral cross‑section of the L3. Two board-certified radiologists, each with more than 5 years of experience, independently performed double-blinded image analysis and measured the skeletal muscle area using specific Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholds. SMI was computed as skeletal muscle area (cm2) divided by height squared (m2) (Figure 2).

Muscle at the L3 level in patients over 60 years old (A), muscle at the L3 level in a 60-year-old patient (B). Red: Skeletal muscle mass.

Definition of inflammatory indicators

This retrospective study analyzed blood cell count data collected from patients within 1 week before treatment. Key parameters included neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts. The SII was computed as: SII = (platelets × neutrophils)/lymphocytes. Furthermore, the NLR and PLR were determined by dividing the absolute neutrophil count and absolute platelet count by the lymphocyte count, respectively. The LMR was calculated as the ratio of lymphocyte count to monocyte count. Additionally, the PNI, representing nutritional and immune status, was computed using the formula: serum albumin (g/dL) multiplied by 10 plus 5× the absolute lymphocyte count (×109/L). The NPS was developed to stratify patients based on a combined evaluation of serum albumin concentration, total cholesterol level, NLR, and LMR.24

Treatment characteristics and follow-up

In this study, radiotherapy was administered using intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT). Radiation was delivered at 1.8-2.0 Gy per fraction (5 fractions weekly) to a total dose of 50-60 Gy over 5.5-6 weeks (28-30 fractions total) Concurrent platinum-based chemotherapy regimens were administered. The prescribed radiation dose encompassed both the primary tumor and involved regional lymph nodes, including parapharyngeal and celiac drainage nodes. Sequential boost radiotherapy was considered based on tumor response evaluation. Patient staging was performed according to the updated AJCC 8th edition TNM classification system. This study employed a standardized efficacy evaluation protocol: contrast-enhanced CT scans were performed after the completion of the second and fourth chemotherapy cycles, followed by systematic imaging assessments every 3 months during subsequent follow-up, including but not limited to CT, MRI, or PET-CT. Following RECIST guidelines, 2 experienced radiation oncologists conducted blinded independent image assessments. A senior specialist resolved any disagreements.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.4.3 and R software version 4.5.0. The prognostic performance and distinctions of each scoring system were assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Associations between categorical variables and their relationship with the SSG score were evaluated using chi-square tests. All continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables that did not meet the normality assumption (P < .05) were compared between groups using the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test. For multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni correction was applied. Kaplan-Meier methods generated OS and PFS curves and group comparisons were made using the log-rank test. In survival analysis univariate analysis first screened clinical variables related to survival then significant variables were included in a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model to identify independent prognostic factors. A nomogram for risk assessment was constructed using R software. Calibration plots showed the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes using the Hosmer goodness-of-fit test. The concordance index measured nomogram accuracy and an auxiliary program corrected optimism bias. Decision curve analysis validated the prognostic performance of the nomogram. Variables with a Pvalue less than .05 were regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of patients

The study population consisted of 200 patients, with 182 males (91.0%) and 18 females (9.0%) (Table1). Among these, 140 patients (70.0%) were older than 60 years, while 60 patients (30.0%) were aged 60 years or younger. Tumor distribution analysis indicated that 131 cases (65.5%) involved the upper and middle esophagus, whereas 69 cases (34.5%) involved the lower esophagus. Disease staging revealed that 140 patients (70.0%) were classified as stage II-III, and 60 patients (30.0%) were at stage IV. During follow-up, 138 patients (69.0%) experienced disease recurrence or metastasis, and 106 patients (53.0%) died from the disease.

SSG score

We established the SSG score model based on 2 indicators (SMI and SII). The predictive performance of these 2 indicators was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. The optimal cutoff values were 37.87 cm2/m2 for SMI and 681.53 × 109/L for SII. Based on these cutoffs, patients were divided into 3 groups: group 0, comprising patients with higher SMI (>37.87 cm2/m2) and lower SII (<681.53 × 109/L); group 2, comprising patients with lower SMI (≤37.87 cm2/m2) and higher SII (≥681.53 × 109/L); and group 1, including all other patients not meeting the above criteria (Figure 3). The 200 patients were stratified into 3 groups by SSG score: group 0 (46 cases, 23.0%), group 1 (125 cases, 62.5%), and group 2 (29 cases, 14.5%). Comparison between groups showed that drinking status (P = .011) and TNM stage (P = .018) were statistically significant in baseline characteristics while age, gender, KPS, smoking, tumor differentiation and tumor location showed no significant differences (all P > .05) (Table 2).

Systemic nutritional and inflammatory marker analysis

This study measured SMI, systemic SII, NLR, PLR, and PNI. Median values were 42.835 (range 17.07-57.33) for SMI and 541.623 (range 166.045-1095.461) for SII. Complete data are presented in Table 3. ROC curve analysis for prognostic prediction demonstrated that the SSG score exhibited the highest discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.690 (95% CI: 0.611-0.769; P < .001). In contrast individual biomarkers showed limited predictive performance: SMI yielded an AUC of 0.567 (P = .120), systemic SII achieved 0.611 (P = .012), while the NPS displayed the lowest predictive value (AUC = 0.545; P = .312) (Figure 4).

Univariate survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that the SSG 0 group had a significantly better prognosis compared to the SSG 1 and 2 groups (P < .001) (Figure 5A). Systemic SII demonstrated significant association with OS (P = .005) while NPS (P = .760) and SMI (P = .820) showed no significant correlation with OS (Figure 5B-D). Regarding PFS the SSG 0 group exhibited significantly longer PFS than SSG 1 and 2 groups (P < .001) (Figure 6A). Similarly, SII was significantly associated with PFS (P = .012) whereas NPS (P = .124) and SMI (P = .293) showed no significant association with PFS (Figure 6B-D). Univariate analysis indicated that the SSG score (P = .002), TNM stage (P = .037), NLR (P = .002), PLR (P = .015), and SII (P = .006) were significantly associated with OS. And the SSG score (P = .002), NLR (P = .008), TNM stage (P = .019) and SII (P = .016) were significantly correlated with PFS (Table 4).

Multivariate survival analysis

Multivariable Cox regression analysis incorporating statistically significant variables from univariable analysis (including SSG score TNM stage, NLR, PLR, and SII) demonstrated that SSG score served as an independent prognostic factor for both OS (HR: 2.955, 95% CI: 1.351-6.465; P = .007) and PFS (HR: 2.028, 95% CI: 1.187-3.463; P = .010). Additionally, NLR (HR: 1.744, 95% CI: 1.042-2.921; P = .034) and PLR (HR: 1.518, 95% CI: 1.013-2.274; P = .043) were identified as independent prognostic factors for OS. However, TNM stage and SII failed to demonstrate independent prognostic value for either OS or PFS in multivariable analysis (all P > .05) (Table 5). The cumulative hazard curves showed that the high-risk group consistently exhibited higher hazard rates for both OS and PFS compared to the low-risk group, with an accelerating upward trend over time, indicating significantly worse prognosis. In contrast, the low-risk group displayed a gradual increase in the cumulative hazard curve, suggesting markedly superior clinical outcomes relative to the high-risk group (Figure 7A and B). Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of the model for OS and PFS. The results demonstrated that the model provided significant and consistent net benefits across a wide range of probability thresholds, confirming its reliable clinical utility (Figure 7C and D).

Discussion

Establishing a reliable prognostic model is of paramount importance for risk stratification and the selection of appropriate treatment strategies in patients with unresectable ESCC. Analyses of systemic inflammatory and nutritional status offer a means to interrogate the relationship between these host factors and clinical outcomes. The SSG score, a novel prognostic index that reflects patients’ inflammatory and nutritional states, is herein, to our knowledge, shown for the first time to be associated with prognosis; furthermore, compared with existing prognostic scoring systems, the SSG score demonstrates superior accuracy.

Nutritional status plays a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of malignant tumors.25,26 In recent years, new nutritional markers including GPS and PNI have been recognized as useful clinical tools for distinguishing the prognosis of esophageal cancer.27,28 Research has demonstrated that declines in skeletal muscle mass and function not only reflect the combined effects of surgery, inflammation, nutrition, physical activity, and treatment,29,30 but are also directly associated with adverse clinical outcomes in various patients with cancer-including deteriorated physical status, increased chemotherapy toxicity, and poorer survival prognosis. This pathological process highlights the critical predictive value of sarcopenia in cancer management.31 Numerous previous studies have shown that the SMI is significantly correlated with prognosis in patients with diverse malignancies. For instance, the study by Zhuang et al32 confirmed that low SMI was associated with a marked reduction in 5-year OS in gastric patients with cancer. Similarly, Kim et al33 observed significantly shortened OS in non-small cell lung cancer cohorts with low SMI. Further, research by Peng et al34 revealed that SMI serves as an independent prognostic factor in pancreatic cancer, indicating its potential clinical utility. Moreover, the prognostic significance of SMI has also been validated in malignancies such as rectal cancers,35 breast cancers,36 and ovarian cancers.37

Inflammation is a key factor in tumor progression by providing bioactive molecules to the tumor microenvironment, thereby rendering the byproducts of inflammatory processes as potential biomarkers.38,39 Neutrophils play a role in cancer development by facilitating tumorigenesis through the promotion of cancer cell invasion, proliferation, and metastasis.40,41 Meanwhile, tumor cell survival and dissemination are supported by interactions between platelets and tumor cells through multiple mechanisms.42,43 Moreover, lymphocytes are key regulators of the host cell-mediated immune response against malignant cells.44 The SII, which integrates these components, is regarded as a superior inflammatory marker reflecting this balance and has been validated as an independent prognostic indicator in a variety of solid tumors. Cho et al45 previously found that sarcopenia accompanied by a high NLR was associated with worse OS and PFS in patients with head and neck cancers. In the present study, we observed that patients in the low SSG score group had significantly better OS than those in the high SSG score group.

Skeletal muscle tissue secretes various cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-8, and IL-15, which participate in systemic inflammatory responses through autocrine, paracrine, and endocrine pathways, thereby influencing the tumor microenvironment.46 Chronic inflammatory conditions result in the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which induce protein carbonylation, leading to irreversible alterations in protein structure and, ultimately, muscle tissue damage.47,48 Notably, although some studies have explored the combined use of SMI and SII in tumor prognosis, related reports remain very limited, particularly in the field of unresectable ESCC. Our ROC analysis demonstrated that the SSG score achieved the best discriminative performance, with the highest AUC. Unlike unidimensional models such as GPS, PNI, and those based primarily on NLR or PLR that predominantly reflect systemic inflammation or nutritional status, the SSG score innovatively integrates SMI and SII, thereby jointly capturing the complementary dimensions of body composition and systemic inflammation. This multidimensional integration likely underlies the superior predictive performance of SSG, as it more comprehensively reflects the complex pathophysiology of patients with cancer. Based on these findings, we plan to pursue a stepwise roadmap-validation, integration, and intervention-by which the SSG score may evolve from a robust prognostic indicator into a practical clinical decision-making tool and, ultimately, contribute to improved outcomes for patients with cancer.

The SSG score, derived from routine CT and standard blood counts, should be used in concert with active lifestyle modification and multidisciplinary care, since tobacco and alcohol both promote ESCC and worsen treatment tolerance and recovery.49 AI-assisted endoscopy, deep learning imaging, and multiomics can improve diagnostic and prognostic precision, but their dependence on high-end equipment, large annotated datasets and substantial computational resources limits applicability in resource limited settings.50 By contrast, SSG is inexpensive, broadly accessible and easily integrated into clinical workflows, making it well suited for screening, risk stratification and bedside decision support; when resources allow, combining SSG with molecular or computational data can further enhance predictive performance.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces the possibility of selection bias and information bias; although we applied strict inclusion criteria and conducted careful data verification to mitigate these risks, unmeasured confounding-such as concomitant medications and specific lifestyle factors-may persist. Second, as a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size, the generalizability of our findings is constrained and external validation in larger, prospective, multicenter cohorts is required. Finally, we acknowledge that the present study lacks validation in randomized controlled trials. Randomized controlled trials represent the gold standard for determining whether a model can effectively guide treatment decisions and improve patient outcomes. Therefore, prior to routine clinical implementation, the utility of this model must be demonstrated in prospective randomized controlled trials.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates an association between the SSG score and prognosis in patients with unresectable ESCC, showing that the SSG score functions as an independent prognostic model in this population. We found that a low SSG score was associated with poorer OS and PFS. The combined use of SMI and SII within the SSG scoring system exhibited superior predictive performance compared with either indicator alone. The successful development and validation of this novel prognostic model provide more accurate survival estimates, improve prognostic assessment, and facilitate personalized risk stratification for these patients.
