Navigation-recorded plan execution in augmented total shoulder arthroplasty using the Advita GPS system and implant platform
Authors: Roy Romem, Tej Joshi, François Boux de Casson, Josie Elwell, Joseph D. Zuckerman, Mandeep S. Virk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComputer-assisted navigation (CAN) is increasingly utilized in total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA), yet data validating its intra-operative agreement with pre-operative plans in glenoid deformities remain limited. This study evaluated the navigation-recorded execution of the pre-operative plan for glenoid entry point, version, and inclination in TSA treated with augmented glenoid components.MethodsIn this retrospective database study, we analyzed pre-operative computed tomography and intra-operative navigation reports from 19,054 navigated TSAs performed by 871 surgeons using the Advita GPS system and implant platform. Cohorts were subdivided by glenoid retroversion (GRV) ≥15° or <15° and glenoid inclination ≥10° or <10°. Plan deviations were calculated from the navigation-recorded entry point and central drill trajectory, used as surrogates for the final implant position, and compared by deformity severity and augment sub-type.ResultsAfter excluding 189 cases (0.85%) with discontinued CAN, the cohort comprised 2,266 anatomic TSA (aTSA) and 16,788 reverse TSA (rTSA). In rTSA, GRV ≥15° showed marginally greater version deviation than GRV <15° although both groups remained approximately 0.3° from plan (P = .006). Entry-point deviations were statistically higher with GRV ≥15° and glenoid inclination ≥10° but remained approximately 1 mm across groups (P < .001 and P = .009, respectively). Posterior augments demonstrated higher version deviation than superior augments although both remained below 1° (P < .001). In aTSA, no sub-group differences were identified.ConclusionsCAN showed close intra-operative agreement between the pre-operative plan and the navigation-recorded surrogate for the final implant position across augmented aTSA and rTSA irrespective of glenoid deformity severity. Statistically significant differences remained below thresholds of clinical relevance.

Accurate glenoid component placement is important for long-term success in total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA). Implant malpositioning may result in eccentric joint loading, instability, early loosening, and sub-optimal clinical outcomes, potentially requiring a revision surgery.1,8,13,15,33 Achieving such precision is particularly challenging in the setting of glenoid bone loss. Posterior glenoid wear, commonly observed in Walch type B2, B3, and C,2,17,39,40 is characterized by glenoid retroversion (GRV), asymmetric erosion, and limited posterior glenoid bone stock, each posing a significant obstacle to ideal glenoid implant orientation.4,9,14,22,27,31 Similarly, superior glenoid erosion, as described in Favard types E2 through E3,34 increases glenoid inclination (GI) and compromises superior support.

To address these complex glenoid deformities, strategies like high-side glenoid reaming, bone grafting, and augmented glenoid components are being used in isolation or as a combined strategy.28 Augmented glenoid implants allow for targeted correction of the abnormal glenoid morphology while minimizing the need for excessive reaming, thereby preserving native bone and facilitating the restoration of anatomic alignment, and are being used in TSA.1,21 Correction of the morphometric glenoid anatomy to neutral in TSA may not necessarily be patient-specific in every case. Instead, glenoid wear correction during shoulder arthroplasty should aim to recreate the patient's premorbid glenoid anatomy. However, there is a limit to correction of glenoid deformity with augmented glenoid components, and a certain amount of glenoid deformity is considered acceptable.5 These limitations make it very challenging to execute the pre-operative plan intra-operatively using visual and tactile feedback alone, especially in the presence of a glenoid deformity.12 As a result, outcomes are influenced by surgical exposure, anatomic variability, and surgeons’ expertise.

Computer-assisted navigation (CAN) is a guidance technology that has emerged as a promising tool to enhance the precision and reproducibility of glenoid component placement in TSA.20,36,37 By integrating detailed pre-operative planning with real-time intra-operative feedback, CAN systems enable surgeons to execute implant positioning with greater fidelity to the surgical plan. Although prior studies have validated the accuracy and safety of CAN in broader TSA cohorts,10,18,24,30 data specifically examining its performance in the context of advanced glenoid deformities with the utilization of glenoid augments remain limited despite the growing clinical adoption of these techniques.42

This study aims to assess the navigation-recorded intra-operative agreement with the pre-operative plan and operative performance of CAN in both anatomic TSA (aTSA) and reverse TSA (rTSA) procedures using augmented glenoid components to address significant glenoid deformities.34 By quantifying the navigation-recorded deviations from the pre-operative plan and evaluating system reliability through intra-operative completion rates, this investigation seeks to define the clinical utility of CAN in complex glenoid re-construction. We hypothesize that regardless of glenoid deformity, CAN enables consistent, reproducible execution of pre-operative plans with minimal deviation in glenoid entry point and central drill trajectory.

Methods

Study design and patient cohort

This retrospective database study analyzed de-identified intra-operative data collected using the Advita GPS navigation system (Advita GPS, Blue-Ortho, Meylan, France). Data were sourced from the Advita global navigation database, which includes all TSAs performed worldwide using Advita's navigation system and implant platform (Advita Equinoxe, Advita, Gainesville, FL, USA) for which intra-operative CAN reports were uploaded. Eligible for inclusion were patients who underwent aTSA or rTSA using CAN with procedures performed between May 2020 and November 2025. Exclusion criteria included (1) missing pre-operative planning data, (2) procedures performed for fracture or revision procedures, (3) non-augmented, anteriorly augmented, and extended cage glenoid components, (4) incomplete CAN procedures where navigation was aborted prior to the completion of the last step, (5) outliers beyond the 1st and 99th percentile of native and navigation-recorded implant version and inclination. All eligible uploaded cases from the study period were included; no a priori sample-size calculation was performed. The remaining cases were divided into cohorts of aTSA with a posterior augment and rTSA with a posterior, superior, or postero-superior augment.

Pre-operative imaging and planning

All patients underwent a pre-operative computed tomography (CT) scan of the entire scapula, acquired with a ≤1.25-mm slice thickness and interval. CT data were uploaded into the Advita Equinoxe Planning Application (Advita Equinoxe Planning Application, Advita, Gainesville, FL, USA), which generated three-dimensional re-constructions of the scapula. The software provided detailed visualization of the native glenoid morphology, including GI and GRV, which guided the planning process. Based on these measurements, surgeons selected the appropriate glenoid component and augment configuration to correct the deformity per their individualized preferences. The software displayed the planned implant orientation and the estimated backside contact, which were then transferred to the CAN system for intra-operative execution.

Intra-operative navigation workflow

During surgery, an infrared tracker was secured to the coracoid process (G-tracker), and a second tracker (P-tracker) was used for registration. A third (T-tracker) tracker was attached to surgical instruments to enable real-time tracking of the entry point and glenoid preparation. The navigation system captured positional data related to the tool trajectory and guided the final implant placement. The final position and orientation of the central cage drill were used as a navigation-recorded surrogate for the final glenoid component orientation. Because planned and achieved values were both generated by the navigation system, these data reflect the internal plan execution rather than the independently validated implant accuracy. Reaming depth was not available in the extracted navigation reports. Completion of all navigation steps and execution of the pre-operative plan were documented in automated intra-operative navigation reports.

Data collection and outcome measures

Data were extracted from intra-operative navigation reports. A negative version indicated retroversion, and a positive inclination reflected superior tilt. For positional data, a positive value for anteroposterior (AP) indicated anterior, while a positive value for supero-inferior (SI) indicated superior. For data analysis purposes, glenoid deformity was classified as GRV ≥15° or <15° and GI ≥10° or <10°. The primary outcome was intra-operative deviation from the pre-operative plan in the starting point for the central cage and the recorded final glenoid component placement, with respect to version and inclination. Deviation was calculated as the difference between achieved and planned values. Global entry-point deviation was defined as the square root of the sum of squared AP and SI deviations: √(AP2+SI2).

Absolute deviations were used for all primary analyses to quantify the magnitude of difference between planned and achieved navigation-recorded values. For entry-point deviations, absolute differences were calculated in the AP and SI directions. To assess this outcome, the aTSA cohort with posterior augments was stratified by GRV severity, while the rTSA cohort (with posterior, superior, or postero-superior augments) was stratified by both GRV and GI.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Equality of variances was tested using Levene's test. If the data exhibited normality, continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD, otherwise they were summarized as median (first percentile; 99th percentile). In cases where the data did not meet normality assumptions, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare 2 groups. To compare 3 groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used. The post-hoc test used for the pairwise comparison of sub-groups was conducted using Dunn's test. Significance was set at P < .05 for all tests. Because analyses addressed pre-specified sub-group comparisons across distinct endpoints, no global multiplicity adjustment was applied. Statistical findings were interpreted in the context of effect magnitude and clinical relevance. Statistical analyses were performed using the Python SciPy v1.11.4 library.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 19,054 shoulder arthroplasties with augments, performed by 871 surgeons across 18 countries, were included in this study (Fig. 1). This cohort comprised 2,266 aTSA and 16,788 rTSA procedures with augments. Among the 2,266 aTSA cases with posterior augments, 1,096 (48%) had GRV ≥15°. Of the 16,788 rTSA cases, 5,220 (31%) had GRV ≥15°, and 1,758 (10%) had GI ≥10°. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty

Absolute version deviation remained low across all rTSA sub-groups. Cases with GRV ≥15° showed statistically higher version deviation than cases with GRV <15° although both groups remained approximately 0.3° from plan (P = .006). When stratified by GI, version deviation was not significantly different and remained near 0.3° in both groups (P = .096). Across augment types, posterior augments demonstrated statistically greater version deviation than both superior and postero-superior augments, while all groups remained below 1° from plan (P < .001).

Absolute inclination deviations remained low across all sub-groups for rTSA. Posterior augments showed a statistically higher inclination deviation than postero-superior augments although both remained approximately 0.3° from plan (P = .001).

Glenoid entry-point deviations differed statistically when stratified by GRV and GI but remained small and on the order of 1 mm across groups (P < .001 and P = .009, respectively). Discussion

This study evaluates the performance of CAN in shoulder arthroplasty with glenoid deformity and demonstrates close navigation-recorded execution of the pre-operative plan, even in challenging deformities. Deviations from the planned trajectory for version, inclination, and glenoid entry point remained consistently minimal across all measured parameters, typically amounting to mere fractions of a degree or a millimeter. These differences, while occasionally reaching statistical significance, likely due to the power of the study, fall well below thresholds of clinical relevance.16,41 Navigation-recorded plan execution was consistent across a wide range of augment designs and deformity types, demonstrating that even complex glenoid re-constructions can be performed with confidence.

Glenoid component malposition remains a leading contributor to failure in TSA, particularly in cases with complex deformities. Rojas et al32 identified glenoid bone loss and revision procedures as primary drivers of aseptic loosening in a meta-analysis of failed rTSA, suggesting an increased risk in anatomically challenging shoulders. Sperling et al35 reported that glenoid component malposition accounted for 93% of failed rTSA and 51% of failed aTSA cases in a revision cohort, emphasizing its widespread role in implant failure. Similarly, Iannotti et al16 found that moderate-to-severe deformities were associated with inconsistent glenoid implant placement, even in experienced hands. These findings highlight the challenge in achieving accurate glenoid alignment in deformed anatomy, and its critical role in maintaining implant longevity and minimizing revision risk, thereby supporting the need for guidance technology in these cases.

Several studies have previously demonstrated the accuracy of CAN in TSA. Larose et al24 reported on over 16,000 cases performed with CAN, showing consistent plan execution and a low complication rate (0.05%). However, their analysis did not stratify outcomes by glenoid deformity severity or augment type. Nashikkar et al29 compared CAN-guided TSA with conventional techniques and found reduced variability, more frequent attainment of neutral version and inclination, and increased use of augments in the navigated group. Similarly, Cunningham et al6 used post-operative CT-based measurements of the glenoid component position and found greater implant accuracy in navigated vs. conventional rTSA, particularly among patients with severe glenoid wear. A recent meta-analysis by Lee et al,25 which included 4 comparative studies, further confirmed that CAN significantly improves accuracy in glenoid component version and inclination. The findings of this study align with prior reports demonstrating the intra-operative accuracy of CAN. However, the current study specifically focused on cases with varying severity of glenoid deformities in version and inclination and found similar efficacy irrespective of glenoid deformity severity.

Although post-operative imaging was unavailable in this de-identified database, prior smaller studies have compared the pre-operative plan against CT-measured achieved component position in navigated shoulder arthroplasty.10,20,29,43 Nashikkar et al29 reported post-operative version and inclination within 5° of plan in 81.8% and 75.8% of cases, respectively, and Jones et al20 reported navigated placement within 1.9° and 2.4° of plan for version and inclination in a cadaveric model. Larose et al24 used navigation-recorded endpoints rather than post-operative imaging to evaluate intra-operative glenoid baseplate positioning across more than 16,000 shoulder arthroplasties. Therefore, navigation-recorded plan execution may be interpreted as a reasonable surrogate for achieved component position, although this correspondence was not directly measured in the present cohort.

Alternative technologies to CAN, such as patient-specific instrumentation (PSI) and mixed reality, have been investigated to improve glenoid component placement. Two meta-analyses by Cabarcas et al3 and Daher et al7 concluded that PSI did not offer superior accuracy over standard instrumentation. In contrast, Lee et al25 found that PSI yielded significantly lower mean deviations in version (1.9°) and inclination (2.0°) than in navigation (4.2° and 4.9°, respectively). Similarly, Kopriva et al23 reported that mixed reality significantly improved inclination accuracy in deformed glenoids, with a mean deviation of 2.5° vs. 4.7° using standard instrumentation. Yet, the present study demonstrated substantially lower median deviations, typically 0.3° to 0.35°, highlighting the small navigation-recorded deviations of contemporary CAN systems, even in challenging glenoids. Because the final implant position was not independently imaged, a direct comparison with PSI, mixed-reality, robotic, or navigation studies using post-operative imaging should be made cautiously.

Youderian et al44 evaluated outcomes of both aTSA and rTSA performed with CAN and compared them to conventional techniques over a 2-year follow-up period. They found similar or improved clinical scores in the CAN group, with a higher frequency of augment use and no increase in complication rate. A sub-group analysis further compared cases with greater vs. lesser GRV correction and found that patients with more extensive corrections had comparable, if not better, functional outcomes. Jones et al19 examined short-term outcomes of rTSA with and without navigation, reporting superior functional scores, greater external rotation, and lower complication and revision rates in the navigated cohort. Augments were more frequently used in the CAN group, underscoring its utility in managing complex glenoid deformities. Additional studies focusing on TSA in severely deformed glenoids requiring superior11,26 or posterior11,38 augmentation have also shown excellent short-term outcomes, suggesting that patients with augmented re-constructions can achieve functional results that are at least equivalent, if not superior, to those with non-augmented implants. Although long-term studies linking navigational precision of the glenoid component to improved outcomes and implant longevity are currently underway, the present data show that close execution of the pre-operative plan is consistently achievable, even in complex glenoid deformities.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective nature restricts control over confounding factors. Race, ethnicity, sex, and gender data were unavailable in this de-identified database. Therefore, we could not evaluate whether navigation execution differed across these groups, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Variability in surgeons, institutions, and patient populations may influence outcomes, yet such diversity strengthens the external validity of the results. Because this study evaluated the Advita GPS system and implant platform, findings may not be generalizable to other navigation systems or implant designs. Although no funding or grants were received, the database was provided by Advita, and disclosed relationships between several authors and Advita may represent potential sources of bias. Formal glenoid classification systems (e.g., Walch and Favard) were not employed, but objective documentation of deformity in coronal and axial planes was determined on CT scans. Post-operative CT or radiographs were not available. Therefore, this study measures navigation-recorded execution of the pre-operative plan as represented in the navigation report, not independent confirmation of registration accuracy, target transfer from the pre-operative CT plan, or the final implant position. Because the navigation system provides both the planned values and the navigation-recorded achieved values, this study lacks an independent external reference standard. Post-operative CT remains the preferred method to independently assess the final implant position. The central cage drill trajectory does not account for potential changes during definitive implant insertion, including incomplete seating, bone quality, sclerotic bone, screw purchase, compression, or implant-bone interface mechanics. Inclusion depended on the availability of an uploaded navigation report, so the cohort may preferentially represent completed CAN procedures. Cases in which navigation could not be completed or reported may be under-captured, which limits the interpretation of workflow-related limitations of CAN. Because the analysis relied on automated processing of a very large dataset, occasional outliers were generated, including instances in which instruments may have been recorded off the surgical field or log files were corrupted. To minimize distortion from these artifacts, the 1st and 99th percentiles were excluded, which allowed the resulting distributions to remain consistent with values reported in the literature. However, excluding extreme values may underestimate the frequency and magnitude of large deviations and could bias the results in favor of lower apparent navigation variability. Outlier reduction may be a key benefit of navigation. Future work should quantify deviations beyond clinically relevant thresholds and identify associated factors. The database lacked several potential predictors of outliers, including body mass index, bone quality, time required to achieve the planned drill trajectory, complete operative time, and post-operative imaging. Finally, this study focused exclusively on navigation-recorded execution of the pre-operative plan and did not assess post-operative outcomes. Despite these limitations, this study provides meaningful evidence that CAN enables execution of surgical plans across a broad range of anatomically complex cases, reinforcing its reliability, clinical relevance, and real-world utility in modern shoulder arthroplasty.

Conclusion

CAN showed close navigation-recorded execution of the pre-operative plan, as measured by glenoid entry point and central drill trajectory deviations, across augmented aTSA and rTSA cases, irrespective of glenoid deformity severity. In the absence of post-operative imaging, these findings provide a large-scale benchmark of navigation-recorded plan execution and should be interpreted within that methodological context. Although some deviations from the pre-operative plan reached statistical significance when stratified by deformity severity, their magnitude remained well below thresholds of clinical relevance.
