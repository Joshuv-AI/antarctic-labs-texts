Study protocol for the investigator-initiated Danish pragmatic randomised STENO INTEN-CT trial: does screening and intervention for subclinical coronary artery disease in type 2 diabetes reduce cardiovascular events?
Authors: Kristian Løkke Funck, Britt Borregaard, Kenneth Egstrup, Eskild Klausen Fredslund, Tine Willum Hansen, Per Kallestrup, Michael Hecht Olsen, Susanne Reventlow, Peter Rossing, Annelli Sandbæk, Jens Søndergaard, Janus Laust Thomsen, Peter Vestergaard, Per Løgstrup Poulsen, Axel Diederichsen

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCardiovascular disease (CVD) risk remains high but unevenly distributed in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Current risk stratification strategies are far from optimal, leading to both undertreatment and overtreatment of patients. The STENO INTEN-CT trial aims to evaluate a strategy of improved CVD risk management by using cardiac CT (coronary artery calcification (CAC)) for stratification and tailoring of multifactorial cardiovascular treatment based on CAC score. We hypothesise that (1) intensified medical treatment will lower CVD event rates in high-risk patients (CAC≥100), and (2) less intensive multifactorial treatment is safe in very low-risk patients (CAC=0).Methods and analysisThe Steno INTEN-CT trial is an investigator-initiated, pragmatic, open-label, event-driven randomised controlled trial including patients with T2DM without known CVD. All participants (expected n=7300) will be invited for a non-contrast coronary CT scan. After the scan, participants will be randomised to either standard treatment (blinded for CAC results) or CAC-based treatment. Participants in CAC-based treatment and their general practitioner (GP) will receive information on CAC and a recommendation of multifactorial treatment. High-risk participants in the interventional arm will be invited for one or more initial study visits to intensify treatment with a combination of sodium glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists, high-dose lipid-lowering, antihypertensive and antithrombotic treatment. Very low-risk patients in the interventional arm will be recommended less intensive treatment targets. After initial study-related activities, all participants will continue to be taken care of by their GP guided by specific treatment recommendations. The primary outcome in the primary hierarchical analysis (the rate of the combined CVD endpoint of cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke and hospitalisation for heart failure) will be monitored through national health registries. The trial is event-driven, but a median follow-up of 5 years is expected. Key secondary outcomes include patient-reported outcomes, quality-adjusted life years and healthcare costs.Ethics and disseminationThe protocol V.1.9 is approved by the Research Ethics Committee and the Danish Medicines Agency and the Danish Data Protection Agency. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) confers an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD).1 The risk is, however, unevenly distributed within the group of T2DM patients and current methods to estimate CVD risk are inaccurate.2,4 Recently, the Systematic COronary Risk Evaluation 2 (SCORE2-) Diabetes has shown better accuracy than previous models5; however, SCORE2-Diabetes is still based on the same traditional risk factors (age, sex, smoking, systolic blood pressure, total and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol) and diabetes-specific factors (age at diabetes diagnosis, haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)) as previous models. Inaccurate risk prediction challenges the clinical decision of primary CVD prophylaxis in T2DM as one approach does not fit all. New opportunities for primary CVD prophylaxis have become an option due to the emergence of two drug classes (sodium glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP1ra)), each with proven cardiovascular protection in patients with T2DM and manifest CVD, but the place in management of people with T2DM with presence of cardiovascular risk factors is less clear.6,12

We present the design and rationale for the randomised Steno INTEN-CT trial. The trial aims to evaluate a CVD prevention strategy with coronary artery calcification (CAC) score as a decisive tool to screen for CVD in patients with T2DM and based on the level of CAC to intensify or de-intensify CVD prophylaxis.

The overall objective is to evaluate the possible cardiovascular benefit of a multifactorial treatment strategy based on CAC score.

We hypothesise that intensified multifactorial treatment is superior to standard treatment in patients with CAC score ≥100.

Recruitment

Men and women with T2DM and no registration of CVD are identified by linking personal identification numbers to national health registries. On this basis, potential participants will receive an invitation to participate in the study through ‘e-Boks’—a secure platform used by the authorities and other public institutions (eg hospitals) to communicate with Danish citizens. To secure a broad inclusion, written information, a graphical presentation of the study and a link to a webpage with a short information video is included in the invitation. Furthermore, participants without ‘e-Boks’ are sent a physical letter instead. Potential participants can schedule a screening visit in which they will receive oral information about the study by trained study personnel. They may then choose to (1) decline, (2) to ask for time for consideration or (3) sign a consent form (online supplemental files 1; 2).

Study programme

After signing the informed consent form, all participants will be examined with anthropometric measures, blood pressure, blood samples and a non-contrast cardiac CT scan. Based on predefined standard operating procedures and according to international consensus guidelines,14 study personnel performing cardiac imaging will forward image files to a central imaging core lab without performing any analysis at the local study site. After the screening examination, the trial participants will be sent home and, within 1–2 weeks, receive information about randomisation and intervention in their e-Boks.

The core lab receives raw image files from study sites and performs image analysis including estimation of CAC based on the non-contrast cardiac CT scan. Image quality will be evaluated on a 5-point scale at the time of study site initiation and consecutively through the study, and in case of suboptimal image quality, the core lab will contact the study site to prospectively improve image acquisition. After the image work-up is completed, a message will be sent to the central secretariat that the participant has either signs of subclinical severe heart disease (exclusion criteria, and the participant will be referred to a clinical specialist in cardiology) or the patient is ready for randomisation. Participants are randomised to either (1) continue standard diabetes treatment blinded to CAC results or (2) receive CAC-based treatment. Randomisation is performed centrally in the electronic case report form. The result is sent to both the participant and the general practitioner (GP) of the participant. Standard electronic communication between hospitals and GPs ensures information concerning the patients is received in the GP’s electronic patient journal.

Depending on availability on the local study site, a contrast-enhanced coronary CT angiography and an echocardiography can be added, but these optional scan modalities will not impact the protocolised intervention unless signs of critical cardiac disease are detected.

Standard treatment

Participants randomised to standard treatment and their GP are not informed about the CAC score, but they are encouraged to follow contemporary diabetes guidelines. Thus, participants in standard treatment will appoint their GP as part of their usual diabetes control programme and, if necessary, adjust treatment. Participants in the standard treatment arm will not be offered further study visits. As per usual care, patients can be referred to a diabetes outpatient clinic, if clinically indicated as judged by their GP.

CAC-based treatment

Participants randomised to CAC-based treatment and their GPs will be informed about the CAC score category and the CAC-based multifactorial treatment. Participants are classified into one of four risk categories: (1) very low risk (CAC=0), (2) low risk (CAC=1–99), (3) high risk (CAC=100–399), and (4) very high risk (CAC>399). High-risk and very high-risk participants are invited for a visit at the study site and offered intensified multifactorial medical treatment. Patient-reported outcomes

The following patient-reported outcomes will be included: health-related quality of life measured using the EuroQol 5 Dimensions 5 Levels15 (EQ-5D-5L) with 5 items and visual analogue scale; patient activation with the 13-item Patient Activation Measure16 17; and self-reported depression and anxiety using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire18 and the 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder questionnaire,19 respectively.

The questionnaires will be sent to all participants at baseline (before randomisation) and then every second year during follow-up and as part of an End of Study questionnaire. High-risk and very high-risk participants in the CAC-based treatment arm will also receive the questionnaires at the first study visit after randomisation and 3 months after randomisation to assess patient-reported outcomes at the time when these participants are informed of their high cardiovascular risk.

Health economic evaluation

We will perform a health economic evaluation of the trial following the lines of Drummond et al20 and adhering to the reporting standards described in the CHEERS good practice guide form published by ISPOR.21 The health economic evaluation consists of a cost effectiveness study (CEA) and a cost utility analysis (CUA). In the CEA, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio will be the ratio between the incremental cost and the primary outcome as measured in the clinical effect analysis. In the CUA, the incremental cost will be related to the change in QALYs. The QALYs will be calculated based on the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire and the Danish QALY weights.22 The incremental cost will follow the approach described in the references cited previously by identifying the relevant cost components, measuring the cost in the relevant units and finally pricing the costs with the appropriate rates.

Costing will be based on a microcosting approach of the intervention based on observations, interviews and clinical time schedules and register data for subsequent treatment, and home care and labour market participation.

We choose the follow-up time in the trial as the follow-up time in the health economic evaluation but propose to do a health economic model (Markov model) to evaluate the long-term health economics consequences after the End of Study. The cost perspective is the societal cost; we use fixed prices and discount using the official discount rates.

Statistics

We have based our sample size calculations on our previous screening studies (DANCAVAS23 and pilot studies to Steno INTEN-CT)24 and expect the following distribution of patients in risk categories: very low risk (CAC=0): 17%; low risk (CAC=1–99): 33%; and high risk and very high risk (CAC≥100): 50%.

Based on data from Danish registries,25 we assume an expected 5-year event rate of the composite of cardiovascular endpoint of 1% in the very low-risk group and 17% in the combined high-risk and very high-risk groups.

Co-primary aim 1

For the primary analysis in the combined high-risk and very high-risk groups, we expect a benefit of 20% in the CAC-based treatment group compared with high-risk patients in the standard treatment group. With a power of 80%, and a probability of type I error of 0.05, the estimated sample size is 3482 patients (1741 in the intervention group and 1741 in the control group).

Co-primary aim 2

For the primary analysis in the very low-risk groups, we will perform a non-inferiority analysis on the primary outcome. The sample size calculation is based on the exact CI for the risk difference.26 Assuming a composite event rate of 1% within 5 years in each group, a significance level of 2.5%, and evaluating non-inferiority with a margin of 2% using a one-sided statistical test, 1078 patients (539 patients in each group) are needed to ensure 80% power.

With the above-mentioned expected distribution of low-risk and high-risk patients, and an expected dropout of 5%, we need to include a total of 7300 patients to identify a sufficient number of patients for the two co-primary analyses. Furthermore, after randomisation, we expect an equal distribution of the risk categories in the CAC-based treatment group and standard treatment group. Inclusion will continue until all 3482 high-risk patients are in the study. We expect inclusion to take 3 years. The trial of high-risk and very high-risk participants will end when 296 events in the standard arm have been accrued (event-driven) and is thus expected to continue approximately 4 years after inclusion has ended. In the trial of the very low-risk group, the number of events is expected to be low and thus this part of the trial ends after a median 5-year follow-up.

An outcome committee blinded for randomisation adjudicates all primary events in the electronic patient files after End of Study. During the study, a separate and external data manager will monitor when 296 primary events have occurred in the high-risk standard treatment group, as this will call for End of Study.

All patients will be analysed according to intention-to-treat principles, that is, according to planned allocation in all randomised patients. In the primary analysis in the high-risk and very high-risk groups, a Cox proportional hazards model will be used to compare hazard rates. A Kaplan-Meier curve will be provided for the primary composite outcome by randomisation group.

After the primary analysis, the subsequent comparisons follow a hierarchical approach in which all-cause mortality and individual events of the primary composite outcome will be tested consecutively. The primary analysis stops if a test exceeds a two-sided p value above 0.05, and further analysis will be considered hypothesis generating. The following order will be used:

Ethics and dissemination

The trial is to be conducted in full compliance with the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the articles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol has been approved by the Research Ethics Committee and the Danish Medicines Agency (EU CT number: 2022-500143-21-01) and the Danish Data Protection Agency. Important stakeholders representing the primary and secondary healthcare sector in Denmark have approved the study (Danish Regions and the Danish College of General Practitioners).

The safety of the participants is considered high. The participants will undergo a cardiac CT scan and an echocardiography at selected sites. In the presence of critical coronary artery disease (left main stenosis >50% or CAC>1000, if CT angiography is unavailable) or signs of systolic dysfunction (ejection fraction <40%), we will exclude and unblind the patient and offer further investigations and/or treatment. We will use a CT reconstruction technique in which minimal parts outside of the heart will be visible in the CT images; thus, image-based incidental findings unrelated to the heart will not be a part of the screening and this will be stated in the participant information.

High-risk and very high-risk participants in the CAC-based treatment arm will be enrolled in a low-intervention phase 4b drug trial with dapagliflozin and semaglutide. Open-label study drugs are used within their approved area of indication, and this means that safety monitoring is balanced in order not to pose an unreasonable extra burden to participants and study personnel during follow-up (eg, safety visits and phone calls). Safety monitoring consists of adverse event registration during the initial adjustment period at the study site.

Electronically reported source data will conform to good clinical practice standards by using the REDCap data collection. Data will be stored in Open Patient data Explorative Network (OPEN), a custom-designed study database secured against unauthorised access provided by the University of Southern Denmark. OPEN enables researchers to store research data in accordance with national legislation and requirements for data logging, password security and backup system. After the end of the study, the research data will be stored in a research biobank which will be made accessible to researchers outside of the research group on the condition that data access and use comply with national legislation.

The results of the study—positive, negative or neutral—will be published in peer-reviewed journals and through www.clinicaltrials.org.

Patient and public involvement

During the design phase of the study, meetings between the researchers and patient representatives were held (organised by the Danish Diabetes Association and Steno Diabetes Centre Aarhus). The meetings aimed to evaluate the objectives of the study, recruitment process and overall conduct. Based on these meetings and previous interview studies,27 we made specific changes to the study design (eg, prioritised patient-reported outcomes as part of the secondary analysis, re-evaluated the recruitment process, changed/modified the wording/illustrations of recruitment materials etc). Results will be published in scientific journals but also disseminated through the Danish Diabetes Association, the Danish Heart Foundation, the Danish Society of General Practitioners and pushed to national media.

Discussion

The Steno INTEN-CT trial is initiated by a consortium of endocrinologists, cardiologists, GPs and health economists with the aim to improve the treatment and quality of life of patients with T2DM without known CVD. Central design choices of the trial will be discussed below.

The research question of the Steno INTEN-CT concerns the effectiveness of a combined strategy of screening and intervention in a format that may be introduced to clinical practice if proven superior to a ‘real world’ usual care setting. Cardiovascular risk and risk stratification

Until recently, T2DM has been considered a ‘CVD equivalent’, implying that all T2DM patients have an elevated risk of future CVD comparable to non-diabetic patients with manifest CVD.29 30 However, mounting evidence suggests that T2DM is a heterogeneous disease and, importantly, patients harbour a highly uneven risk of future CVD.31 32 Conventional clinical risk factors (hyperglycaemia, hyperlipidaemia, smoking and hypertension) and risk models with these factors incorporated do not reliably estimate CVD risk in T2DM,2,433 although the recent SCORE2 model re-calibrated for T2DM (SCORE2-Diabetes) shows an improvement in performance.5 Novel CVD biomarkers (eg, blood, urine, vascular, genetic and environmental markers) have been proposed as means to improve CVD risk prediction; however, most markers do not improve prediction to an extent that is clinically meaningful34 and, generally, there is a lack of prospective studies evaluating the effect of novel biomarkers on CVD prophylaxis and subsequent CVD outcomes.35

CAC stands out as a biomarker that shows potential for CVD risk prediction beyond conventional CVD biomarkers, and it holds potential as a tool to guide CVD prophylaxis.36 It is highly specific of underlying subclinical atherosclerotic coronary artery disease37 and thus reflects pathological processes that are common for most types of CVD. Cohort studies including asymptomatic patients with diabetes have demonstrated that CAC improves the prediction of future CVD38 39 and all-cause mortality.40 41 In particular, a CAC of 0 (~20% of patients with T2DM) entails a very low risk of CVD regardless of T2DM for at least 5 years.31 41 42 Conversely, a large number of persons with T2DM suffer from silent, undiagnosed significant coronary artery disease (CAC score ≥100) with CVD and all-cause mortality rates 6–10 times higher than T2DM patients without the presence of CAC, and two-fold higher compared with non-diabetic individuals with similar CAC.38 40 43 44 In smaller studies, CAC has also shown value in the prediction of non-coronary CVD events (incident stroke45 and heart failure).46

In the Steno INTEN-CT trial, four risk categories with CAC thresholds at 0, ≥100 and ≥400 units have been chosen based on the predictive value and precision of CAC. However, we acknowledge that CAC has important limitations. First, in the very low-risk group, the predictive value of CAC=0 seems to be attenuated during long-term follow-up,40 and furthermore, a subpopulation of patients with CAC=0 will have the presence of non-calcified atherosclerosis and may represent a ‘hidden’ high-risk population within the very low-risk group (in a Korean study, 10% of asymptomatic patients with CAC=0 had signs of significant coronary atherosclerosis on CT angiography).47 Second, the threshold for initiation of intensified medical prophylaxis is a compromise between an increased average risk and an acceptable false-positive proportion of patients. Patients with diabetes and CAC=1–99 face a slightly elevated risk of future CVD events; however, we have chosen not to give specific advice on CVD prophylaxis for this group as the accuracy in this CAC range is only modest44 and the advice would likely not differ from standard recommendations. With increasing CAC, the false positive rate declines,44 and thus thresholds of CAC ≥100 and ≥400 were chosen. Finally, the presence and extent of CAC is dependent on age and sex, thus sex-specific age intervals were chosen as inclusion criteria.

In the Steno INTEN-CT trial, we will evaluate the above limitations of the CAC score. Importantly, a coronary CT angiography will be obtained in the majority of participants (~80%), and this will provide measures of calcified/non-calcified plaque volumes and vulnerable plaque characteristics that may reclassify patients stratified by CAC.48 Moreover, the authors will pursue the possibility of a re-scan at the time of study completion to evaluate CAC progression and risk re-classification after expectedly 5 years follow-up. Finally, in order to evaluate potential ‘legacy effects’ of the intervention, event rates will be assessed 2 and 5 years after End of Study.

Primary prophylaxis of CVD in type 2 diabetes

The concept of CVD risk equivalence has also influenced the recommendations for primary CVD prevention in T2DM in which targets for CVD risk factor control (blood glucose, blood pressure, plasma lipids and prophylactic antithrombotic drug use) have been largely the same for all patients (stratifying treatment according to the presence or absence of albuminuria and/or other CVD risk factors). Furthermore, the importance of multifactorial risk factor control was demonstrated in the Steno 2 trial49 and is now universally accepted as an important premise for the treatment of T2DM. Maybe somewhat neglected, the Steno 2 trial was based on T2DM patients with albuminuria, thus representing a high-risk subgroup of T2DM. Since then, several attempts have been made to target CVD risk factors in broad diabetes populations without known CVD, but these studies showed no effect on CVD events.50 51 A clinical trial with 8.5 years of multifactorial intervention found no overall decrease in CVD incidence despite a significant reduction in several CVD risk factors.52

In addition, the possibility for differentiated CVD prophylaxis in T2DM has increased with the emergence of new drugs. The introduction of SGLT2i and GLP1ra has gathered particular attention as large cardiovascular outcome trials have shown a reduction in CVD events in patients at high risk of or with manifest CVD.6,12 Importantly, these drugs lower CVD risk independently of HbA1c reductions. The clinical position of the drugs in primary CVD prophylaxis is, however, debated, and the effect of combining these two drug classes remains unsettled.53 54 Current European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD)/American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines recommend SGLT2i and/or GLP1ra for people with established CVD or at very high risk, although the guideline definitions of very high risk vary.55 56 Furthermore, combining SGLT2i and GLP1ra has not been tested in prospective studies, but subgroup analysis of cardiovascular outcome trials with the agents suggests the combination provides added benefit.53 54 Lipid-lowering therapy (primarily with statins) is also a cornerstone in primary CVD prophylaxis57 with increasingly tight LDL cholesterol targets introduced in guidelines in recent years58 but with the strongest evidence of effect among patients with atherosclerotic disease.59 Likewise, the role of aspirin in primary prophylaxis is debated and currently only recommended on an individual basis.58 60 ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers are recommended in the presence of hypertension and/or albuminuria as the drugs have been shown to lower the rate of cardiovascular outcomes in diabetes patients with risk factors or manifest CVD.61 62

In our previous cardiac screening studies (DANCAVAS23 and a pilot study to Steno INTEN-CT24), we have data on 1510 patients with diabetes but without prior CVD. Among these, statin treatment was used in comparable fractions of patients with CAC score of 0 (57%) and CAC score above 100 (68%). Likewise, aspirin was used by 14% patients with CAC score of 0 and by 37% with CAC score above 100. In addition, the use of GLP1ra and SGLT2i was similar irrespective of CAC. These results suggest that among Danish patients with T2DM, a significant number of low-risk patients may be treated with unnecessary medication, while others, despite having a high CVD risk, seem to be unprotected against CVD.

Screening and intervention for CVD in type 2 diabetes

The concept of screening for subclinical CVD and stratified treatment in the general population has been evaluated in large screening trials and holds promise for implementation in a real-life setting.23 Unfortunately, no such strategy in patients with T2DM has proven effective to date (summarised in63) and only the Factor64 study has used coronary CT angiography as a screening tool.64 Overall, these studies were designed to randomise patients to either screening or standard care without screening, leaving the risk status of the control group unknown. Consequently, direct comparisons between high-risk or low-risk patients in the interventional and control arm have not been possible. Furthermore, a general assumption in the above-mentioned screening trials has been that coronary bypass or percutaneous coronary intervention of subclinical coronary atherosclerosis would be the most important part of the intervention and in some studies leaving the medical intervention at the discretion of the clinician.63 In the Factor64 study, a mix of type 1 and 2 diabetes patients was included, and although an intensified treatment strategy was scheduled for the intervention group, only a modest proportion of participants achieved optimal medical treatment.64 Moreover, the Factor64 study was performed in the era before SLGT2i and GLP1ra and, finally, it was challenged by low inclusion rates and lower-than-expected event rates.64

The Steno INTEN-CT trial contrasts with previous T2DM screening-and-intervention trials in several important aspects. Notably, the (blinded) risk status of the standard treatment group is attained, which allows for comparison between similar risk categories in the interventional arm. Also, the intervention is primarily based on medical optimisation including cardioprotective drugs that were not available in previous trials, and the intended sample size will hopefully give firm signals on the effectiveness of the proposed treatment strategy.

Limitations

The participating study sites may have different scanner types; however, we find these differences to be negligible in respect to CAC score. Furthermore, the study will reflect a real-world situation and thus increase the external validity of the study results. We will, however, review the intra-reader and inter-reader variability of CAC-scoring in the core lab (online supplemental file 3).

With the widespread availability of coronary CT and the increasingly complicated landscape of medical treatment available for primary CVD prophylaxis, we believe that the Steno INTEN-CT trial addresses an urgent need to optimise and potentially simplify primary CVD prevention strategies in T2DM. Our ambition is to challenge the contemporary strategies and enable individualised imaging-based preventive treatment in T2DM patients without manifest CVD. We hypothesise that the management programme included in the Steno INTEN-CT study will be superior to the current guidelines not only in CVD protection but also in terms of patient-reported outcomes and cost-benefit. Thus, the STENO INTEN-CT study meets crucial parts of the WHO criteria for the assessment of a screening examination and will provide robust answers to evaluate the proposed treatment strategy in a clinical setting.
