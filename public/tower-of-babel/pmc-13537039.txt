Ceria support enhances activity of CoNi catalysts for ammonia synthesis under magnetic heating
Authors: Nina Križaj Kosi, Anja Sedminek, Janvit Teržan, Blaž Likozar, Gregor Dolanc, Jaka Peternel, Darko Makovec, Sašo Gyergyek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ammonia synthesis is an important target for electrified chemical production, but conventional processes rely on externally heated reactors and highly active catalysts that often contain noble metals. Here, we investigate noble-metal-free CoNi catalysts for ammonia synthesis under magnetic induction heating using a hierarchically structured CoNi/alumina platform. Additional CoNi deposition on alumina increased the number of CO-accessible metallic sites but produced only limited activity enhancement, while ceria incorporation without additional CoNi did not substantially increase the catalyst-mass-normalized NH3 formation rate. In contrast, CoNi/CN-A-C exhibited the highest turnover frequency, catalyst-mass-normalized NH3 formation rate, and outlet NH3 concentration, surpassing the Ru/CN-A benchmark at 5.5 MPa. The results associate the pronounced enhancement with the combination of deposited CoNi and a ceria-containing environment.

Introduction

Ammonia is an essential chemical feedstock and is increasingly discussed as a carbon-free energy carrier because it contains no carbon, has a high hydrogen content, and can be liquefied under comparatively mild conditions.1,2 In future energy systems based increasingly on renewable electricity, ammonia could serve both as a transportable hydrogen carrier and as a direct fuel or chemical energy-storage medium.1,2 However, conventional ammonia synthesis remains strongly associated with large, centralized Haber–Bosch plants, high operating pressures and temperatures, and fossil-derived hydrogen.2 This motivates the development of alternative ammonia-synthesis concepts that are more compatible with smaller-scale, electrified, and dynamically operated processes.2–4 Among emerging approaches is magnetic heating, in which magnetic nanoparticles embedded in or coupled to the catalyst convert energy from an alternating magnetic field into heat, thereby providing a direct route to electrified thermal catalysis.5 Compared with conventional external heating, magnetic heating can deliver energy directly within the catalyst bed, reduce thermal inertia, and enable rapid changes in catalyst temperature by switching the magnetic field on or off or adjusting its power.5,6 These features are particularly attractive for ammonia synthesis driven by intermittent renewable electricity, where operational flexibility may be as important as maximum steady-state productivity. The design of magnetically heated ammonia-synthesis catalysts requires the integration of two functions: efficient magnetic heating and catalytic activity. CoNi alloy nanoparticles are attractive magnetic susceptors because their magnetic properties can be tuned by composition, while their metallic character and stability under reducing conditions make them suitable for high-temperature catalytic environments.6,7 In our previous work, a hierarchically structured Co0.67Ni0.33/alumina nanocomposite, denoted CN-A, was used as a magnetically active platform for Ru-based ammonia synthesis.6 While this demonstrated the suitability of the CN-A architecture for magnetically heated operation, it remained unclear whether CoNi itself could function as an efficient noble-metal-free catalytic phase in this system. The embedded CoNi nanoparticles in CN-A are primarily designed for magnetic heating and are relatively large,6 whereas ammonia synthesis is a structure-sensitive reaction8–10 whose performance depends strongly on the nature, accessibility, and local environment of the active metal sites. Thus, the embedded CoNi phase in CN-A may not be optimized for catalytic performance, even if it is effective for magnetic heating. At the same time, particle size cannot be treated here as an isolated variable, because modifying the catalyst also changes the location of the metal and its interaction with the support. Moreover, Co- and Ni-based catalysts are generally less active than Ru for thermal ammonia synthesis,11 and simply increasing the amount of accessible CoNi may not be sufficient if the support environment is not favourable for ammonia-synthesis catalysis. A meaningful assessment of CoNi-based catalysts in this architecture therefore requires distinguishing between the effects of adding more CoNi and modifying the support environment around the CoNi nanoparticles. To address this issue, we use CN-A as the magnetically heatable catalyst platform and prepare three derived catalysts from it: Ru/CN-A, in which Ru nanoparticles are deposited on CN-A and used as a noble-metal reference; CoNi/CN-A, in which additional small Co0.67Ni0.33 alloy nanoparticles are deposited directly onto the alumina-containing CN-A support; and CoNi/CN-A-C, in which CN-A is first coated with nanocrystalline CeO2 and subsequently decorated with Co0.67Ni0.33 nanoparticles. This catalyst series allows the influence of Ru addition, additional CoNi deposition, and ceria incorporation to be compared within the same magnetically heated catalyst architecture. Our results show that neither simply increasing the amount of accessible CoNi nor incorporating ceria without additional CoNi is sufficient to produce the pronounced activity enhancement. In contrast, combining the deposited CoNi phase with a ceria-containing environment enables CoNi/CN-A-C to outperform the Ru/CN-A benchmark under the applied conditions.

The CN-A support used here consists of a hierarchically structured Co0.67Ni0.33/alumina nanocomposite in which CoNi alloy nanoparticles are embedded within a nanocrystalline alumina matrix.6 Under a 234 kHz alternating magnetic field, the embedded CoNi nanoparticles heat rapidly and selectively, enabling fast start-up of the catalyst bed.6 Previous catalytic tests showed that CN-A is an effective magnetically heatable platform, but that its activity without Ru remains limited. The CN-A support used here contains embedded Co0.67Ni0.33 nanoparticles with an average size of about 26 nm and a CoNi/alumina mass ratio of 1 : 1. STEM images of the support are shown in Fig. S1 in the SI. The specific surface area of CN-A was 109.7 m2 g−1, and CO pulse adsorption (CO-PA) gave a concentration of accessible metallic sites of 83.3 µmol g−1, corresponding to 0.76 µmol m−2.6 These data indicate that a fraction of the embedded CoNi phase remains accessible to the reactants, but also show that the embedded CoNi nanoparticles were designed primarily for magnetic heating rather than for maximizing catalytic surface area. To test whether the catalytic performance of the CN-A platform could be improved simply by increasing the amount of accessible CoNi, smaller Co0.67Ni0.33 nanoparticles were additionally deposited onto the alumina nanosheets of CN-A. Co2+ and Ni2+ ions were precipitated in an aqueous suspension of CN-A and reduced in flowing H2 at 600 °C, yielding the CoNi/CN-A catalyst (see SI for details). STEM analysis showed that the deposited CoNi nanoparticles were globular and typically 6–14 nm in size (Fig. S2). EDXS analysis performed in regions free of the larger embedded CoNi particles gave a Co/Ni atomic ratio of 2.4 ± 0.9 and indicated that 55 ± 17 wt% of additional CoNi nanoparticles had been deposited onto the alumina support. The BET surface area of CoNi/CN-A was 107.9 m2 g−1, while CO-PA showed an increased concentration of accessible metallic sites of 176.5 µmol g−1, corresponding to 1.6 µmol m−2. Thus, deposition of smaller CoNi nanoparticles substantially increased the number of CO-accessible metallic sites, enabling a direct comparison of catalytic activity resulting from increased accessible CoNi on alumina.

To modify the support environment around the deposited CoNi phase, CN-A was coated with nanocrystalline ceria prior to CoNi deposition. The ceria-containing support, denoted CN-A-C (CO-PA indicated a concentration of accessible metallic sites of 60.8 µmol g−1), was prepared by controlled precipitation of Ce3+ ions in the presence of hexamethylenetetramine in an aqueous suspension of CN-A (see SI for details).12 Small Co0.67Ni0.33 nanoparticles were then deposited onto CN-A-C by the same procedure used for CoNi/CN-A. EDXS elemental mapping showed a homogeneous distribution of Ce, Co, and Ni throughout the resulting CoNi/CN-A-C nanocomposite (Fig. 1b and c). STEM analysis indicated that ceria formed an approximately 5 nm thick shell on the alumina nanosheets, composed of 3–5 nm ceria nanocrystallites (Fig. S3). According to EDXS, the CN-A-C support contained 43 ± 5 wt% CeO2.

Due to the high atomic number of ceria in the support, the smaller deposited CoNi nanoparticles were not readily resolved in conventional STEM images. Their distribution was therefore evaluated by HAADF imaging combined with EDXS mapping (Fig. 1d–f). This analysis showed that, in addition to the larger embedded CoNi particles, the deposited CoNi nanoparticles were homogeneously distributed over the support and had sizes of 5–15 nm. Their Co/Ni atomic ratio was 1.9 ± 0.9, and their local EDXS-derived metal fraction on the ceria-containing support was 23 ± 8 wt%. The phase composition of CoNi/CN-A-C was confirmed by XRD (Fig. S4), which showed broad reflections attributable to γ-Al2O3, fcc CoNi alloy, and fluorite-type ceria. The BET surface area of CoNi/CN-A-C was 63.9 m2 g−1, markedly lower than that of CN-A and CoNi/CN-A, while CO-PA indicated a concentration of accessible metallic sites of 67.6 µmol g−1, corresponding to 1.06 µmol m−2. H2-TPR of the unreduced precursor of CoNi/CN-A-C (Fig. S5) revealed a complex low-temperature reduction feature centred slightly below 400 °C, with the signal approaching the baseline by approximately 500 °C. For nanocrystalline CeO2, reduction in this temperature range has been attributed to surface and near-surface Ce4+ species, whereas bulk ceria reduction generally occurs above 600 °C.13 The observed low-temperature feature is therefore consistent with surface reduction of the ceria component. However, reduction of the small deposited Co0.67Ni0.33 oxidic nanoparticles and oxidized species associated with the larger embedded CoNi nanoparticles occurs in the same temperature range,7 resulting in strongly overlapping contributions. Consequently, the measured H2 consumption (2.9 mmol H2 per gcat.) cannot be used to quantify the extent of Ce4+ reduction in ceria. The absence of a significant high-temperature reduction feature indicates that substantial bulk reduction of CeO2 does not occur.

Room-temperature magnetization curves of CoNi/CN-A and CoNi/CN-A-C confirmed that both materials retained soft ferromagnetic behaviour, with saturation magnetizations of 29.0 and 10.9 A m2 kg−1, respectively (Fig. S6). Thus, both catalysts preserved the magnetic function required for inductive heating, although incorporation of ceria reduced the overall magnetization because of dilution by the non-magnetic oxide phase.

The catalytic performance of the five materials was evaluated under magnetic heating at 5.5 MPa using an H2 : N2 molar feed ratio of 2 : 1. All temperatures reported for magnetically heated experiments refer to the thermocouple-measured catalyst bed temperature, Tbed. For comparison, the previously reported Ru/CN-A catalyst, in which 2–7 nm Ru nanoparticles were deposited on CN-A, was included as a noble-metal benchmark.6 This reference material had a BET surface area of 108.2 m2 g−1 and a Ru-specific CO-PA-derived concentration of accessible metallic sites of 43.3 µmol g−1, corresponding to 0.40 µmol m−2.6 Turnover frequency (TOF) values were calculated by normalizing the NH3 formation rate to the concentration of metallic sites estimated by CO-PA, assuming a CO : metal stoichiometry of 1 : 1. In the present study, TOF is used as an internally consistent comparative metric across the catalyst series, acknowledging that CO-PA may not reflect the absolute number of catalytically relevant sites.14–19 For the CoNi-containing catalysts, the measured CO uptake represents the total population of CO-accessible Co and Ni sites. For Ru/CN-A, the CN-A contribution was subtracted from the measured uptake, yielding a Ru-specific accessible-site concentration, as described previously.6 The support-derived CO uptake expected to be negligible under the sub-ambient CO-PA conditions employed here (−10 °C). The experimental rationale and supporting literature17–19 for this assignment are provided in the SI. Importantly, CoNi/CN-A-C remains the best-performing catalyst when evaluated by catalyst-mass-normalized NH3 productivity and NH3 yield (Fig. 2b and S7), demonstrating that the principal conclusion does not depend on the CO-chemisorption-based normalization.

As previously observed,6 CN-A showed low activity and only modest TOF values even at elevated temperature (Fig. 2a). Coating CN-A with ceria to form CN-A-C did not result in a substantial increase in catalyst-mass-normalized NH3 formation rate, which remained comparable to that of CN-A. The comparatively high TOF of CN-A-C at the highest temperatures reflects its relatively low concentration of CO-accessible metallic sites rather than an increase in overall NH3 productivity. Deposition of Ru on CN-A provided a clear performance increase, reaching a TOF of 82 h−1 at 500 °C. In contrast, CoNi/CN-A, despite exhibiting the highest concentration of CO-accessible metallic sites, remained much less active than Ru/CN-A over the entire investigated temperature range. This comparison shows that, within the present magnetically heated catalyst architecture, simply depositing additional CoNi nanoparticles onto alumina is not sufficient to achieve high ammonia-synthesis activity.

A markedly different behaviour was observed for CoNi/CN-A-C. This catalyst exhibited the highest TOF values in the series, reaching approximately 307 h−1 at 500 °C, and also showed the highest NH3 yield. Across all three performance metrics—TOF, catalyst-mass-normalized NH3 formation rate, and NH3 yield—CoNi/CN-A-C remained the best-performing catalyst. Because its superior performance is supported by multiple activity metrics, the principal conclusion cannot be attributed to TOF normalization alone. The concentration of CO-accessible metallic sites therefore does not correlate directly with ammonia-synthesis activity. CO-PA quantifies the total accessible metallic population but does not distinguish between the different CoNi populations or their local support environment. The control experiments illustrate this distinction. Incorporation of ceria into CN-A without deposition of additional CoNi nanoparticles did not substantially increase the catalyst-mass-normalized activity. Moreover, CoNi/CN-A-C contained only slightly more CO-accessible metallic sites than CN-A-C (approximately 68 and 60.8 µmol g−1, respectively), despite their markedly different catalytic activities. Conversely, deposition of small CoNi nanoparticles onto CN-A strongly increased the concentration of accessible metallic sites but produced only a limited activity enhancement. The pronounced activity enhancement observed for CoNi/CN-A-C is therefore associated with the combination of the deposited CoNi population and the ceria-containing environment, rather than simply with a larger number of CO-accessible metallic sites.

An additional factor that should be considered is that the deposited small CoNi nanoparticles are themselves magnetic and may therefore also contribute to heat generation under the alternating magnetic field. However, the present catalyst series shows that this effect alone cannot account for the observed performance enhancement. Since the deposited CoNi nanoparticles in CoNi/CN-A and CoNi/CN-A-C are of comparable size, the pronounced activity enhancement observed for CoNi/CN-A-C indicates that the local support environment of the deposited CoNi phase strongly influences its catalytic behaviour. At the same time, the local thermal behaviour of these particles may still differ between the alumina- and ceria-containing catalysts, because the surrounding support environment can affect heat dissipation. Ceria and alumina differ in heat capacity and thermal conductivity, and may therefore influence the local temperature reached under magnetic heating. The present results therefore identify the combination of the deposited CoNi phase and the ceria-containing environment as critical for the marked enhancement of ammonia-synthesis activity in CoNi/CN-A-C, while the detailed origin of this effect, including any influence on local magnetic heating, will require further kinetic, spectroscopic, calorimetric, and computational study.

Beyond the catalyst-level comparison presented here, the results are relevant in the broader context of magnetically heated fixed-bed reactors. Recent numerical modelling of induction-heated fixed-bed systems has shown that direct bed heating can improve start-up and dynamic response compared with conventional wall-heated reactor concepts.20 Although those results were obtained for methanation and reverse water–gas shift20 rather than ammonia synthesis, they support the broader relevance of magnetic heating for flexible electrified reactor operation. In this context, the present work addresses a complementary materials challenge by showing that a noble-metal-free CoNi/CeO2-based catalyst can achieve high ammonia-synthesis performance within a magnetically heated catalyst architecture.

The stability of the best-performing catalyst, CoNi/CN-A-C, was evaluated for 75 h of continuous operation under magnetic heating (Fig. 2c). During an initial transient period, the NH3 yield increased to a maximum of approximately 2.18%, followed by a slight decrease and stabilization at 2.13–2.15%, corresponding to an average yield of approximately 2.14% during the later stage of the experiment. No progressive decline in NH3 yield was observed during this steady operating period. The applied AC field amplitude remained nearly constant throughout the experiment, decreasing only slightly from approximately 57 to 56 mT after about 10 h. This small change may reflect minor variations in the thermal balance as steady operation was established. Overall, the results demonstrate stable operation of CoNi/CN-A-C under the applied reaction conditions following the initial transient period.

The comparison shows that Co/CeO2 and Ni/CeO2 catalysts have previously been investigated for thermocatalytic ammonia synthesis under conventional heating, demonstrating the relevance of ceria-containing environments for both metals. To the best of our knowledge, however, no directly comparable CoNi/CeO2 catalyst has been reported for thermocatalytic ammonia synthesis from N2 and H2. The present CoNi/CN-A-C catalyst therefore extends this chemistry to a bimetallic CoNi phase incorporated into a magnetically heatable catalyst architecture. The inclusion of previously reported magnetically heated ammonia-synthesis catalysts further places the present system within the emerging field of induction-heated ammonia synthesis. Conclusion

In this work, we examined whether the magnetically heatable CN-A platform can be extended from a Ru-based ammonia-synthesis catalyst to a noble-metal-free CoNi-based system. The comparison of CN-A, CN-A-C, Ru/CN-A, CoNi/CN-A, and CoNi/CN-A-C shows that neither incorporation of ceria into CN-A alone nor deposition of additional CoNi nanoparticles onto the alumina-containing architecture is sufficient to produce the pronounced activity enhancement. In contrast, CoNi/CN-A-C surpasses the Ru/CN-A benchmark in TOF, catalyst-mass-normalized NH3 formation rate, and NH3 yield under the investigated conditions. These results identify the combination of the deposited CoNi phase and the ceria-containing environment as critical for the enhanced catalytic performance. The present study does not establish the detailed microscopic origin of this effect, which may involve chemical metal–support interactions as well as differences in local thermal behaviour under magnetic heating.
