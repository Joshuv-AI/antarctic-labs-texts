Fairness In Real Estate Division: Rebuild and Divide
Noga Klein Elmalem, Rica Gonen, Erel Segal-Halevi

Abstract
Rebuild and Divide is an urban renewal process that involves the demolition of old buildings and the construction of new ones. Original homeowners are compensated with upgraded apartments, while surplus units are sold for profit, so theoretically it is a win-win project for all parties involved. However, many rebuild-and-divide projects withheld or delayed due to disagreements over the assignment of new units, claiming they are not ”fair”. The goal of this research is to develop algorithms for envy-free allocation of the new units. The main challenge is that, in contrast to previous work on envy-free allocation, the envy depends also on the value of the old units, as people with more valuable old units are entitled to more valuable new units. We introduce three models that capture different notions of fairness: (1) the Difference Model , where agents evaluate their gains relative to others; (2) the Envy Sum Model , which permits some envy as long as the total envy does not exceed that of the original allocation; and (3) the Ratio Model , where fairness is assessed based on the proportional value of old apartments. For each model, we establish an envy criterion and seek a payment vector and allocation that ensure envy-freeness. These models present both theoretical challenges and intriguing insights. Additionally, within the Envy Sum Model, we present a mechanism that computes an allocation and payment scheme that minimizes total envy. We also analyze the mechanism’s vulnerability to manipulation and identify conditions under which it is obviously manipulable.



1
Introduction

Urban renewal, such as
Rebuild and Divide
, aims to transform aging residential infrastructure into modern, safe, and more spacious housing while simultaneously increasing urban density. The process involves demolishing old buildings and constructing new ones, with original homeowners receiving upgraded apartments as compensation. While the primary goals include enhancing urban housing availability and improving disaster resilience, the success of these projects is often impeded by disputes among stakeholders, particularly concerning the assignment of new apartments.

These disagreements commonly stem from perceptions of unfairness, as homeowners compare the value of their newly assigned units to others. For example, differences in size, floor levels, or unique features of apartments can lead to envy among stakeholders. Such cases often get to court and lead to lengthy judicial processes, leading to delays or even project cancellations. Notably, current regulations require at least two-thirds of homeowners in a building to agree to participate in a renewal project. This highlights the urgent need for systematic, equitable solutions to apartment allocation. For instance, one homeowner might claim that their new apartment is only 19 square meters larger than their old one, while other homeowners with larger original apartments received upgrades of 23 square meters. Courts responding to these claims often justify the outcomes by stating that homeowners with larger original apartments belong to a different “class” and are therefore incomparable to others. These example illustrate the subjective nature of fairness and the challenges in resolving disputes in a way that satisfies all stakeholders.

Rental Harmony
defined by Su in
[
12
]
is a formal approach to resource allocation that seeks to assign
n
rooms to
n
tenants with a fixed total rent
R
, in such a way that the total prices equals
R
and no tenant envies the room and price of another tenant.
Inspired by this concept, we adapt and extend these principles to the context of
Rebuild and Divide
.
In a Rebuild and Redivide problem we have
n
old units,
n
new units and
n
agents, each with subjective valuations. Each agent owns exactly one old unit, and the goal is to assign one new unit to each agent, charging a positive or negative price to each agent such that the total sum of all prices is zero (balanced budget).

Additionally, the assignment should ensure that no agent envies the
improvement
of every other agent. We introduce three models to measure improvements:

1.

In the
Difference Model
, the improvement is the difference between the new apartment value and the old apartment value.
Envy-freeness in this model means that each agent values his own improvement at least as high as the improvement of any other agent.

2.

The
Envy Sum Model
still measures improvements as differences, but it permits agents to tolerate some envy toward the improvements of others, as long as the total envy they experience does not exceed the level present in the initial allocation.

3.

In the
Ratio Model
, the improvement is the ratio of the new apartment value and the old apartment value.
Envy-freeness, as in the Difference Model, means that each agent values his own improvement-ratio at least as high as the improvement-ratio of any other agent.

1.1
Our Results

In
Section

3
, we formally define the Difference
Model and provide a necessary and sufficient condition for an allocation to be Difference-EF-able (DEF-able). Additionally, we show that in certain scenarios, no DEF-able solution exists.

Since a DEF-able allocation does not always exist, in
Section

3.3
, we aim to identify, for a given allocation, a payment vector that minimizes the maximum envy. We prove, for the difference model, that this envy bound is equal to the maximum mean cycle cost in the graph corresponding to the allocation.
Hence, we can use existing algorithms to minimize the envy for a given allocation in strongly-polynomial time.
However, we do not know how to efficiently find the assignment that minimizes the maximum envy.

In
Section

4
, we introduce the envy sum model and provide a necessary and sufficient condition for an allocation to be Sum-EF-able (SEF-able). We show that it is possible to determine, in polynomial time, whether a SEF-able allocation and payment vector exist—and if so, to compute them. Moreover, we prove that one can efficiently compute an assignment that minimizes the maximum envy-sum. To achieve this, we define the
Minimum Envy Sum mechanism
, which outputs an allocation and payment vector that minimize the largest envy-sum across all agents.

As the envy-sum model is more computationally tractable, we focus on this model and discuss, in
Section

5
, its strategic aspects.
It is well-known that, even in the basic Rental Harmony setting, no fair and budget-balanced algorithm is truthful. But in the Rebuild-and-Redivide setting the situation is much worse, as each agent has a strong incentive to report the maximum possible value to his old apartment. In fact, this manipulation is a dominant strategy; it is both an
obvious manipulation
and a
safe manipulation
(see
Section

5
for definitions).
To alleviate this issue, we introduce a new way to elicit agents’ valuations: instead of asking them to directly report the values of old and new apartments, we ask them to report the values of
characteristics
, that appear both in old and in new apartments. We explore the conditions under which the Minimum Envy Sum mechanism has
no safe manipulations.

Lastly, we introduce the ratio model. Although this is the intuitive approach, we do not yet have significant results. This model is described in
, where we demonstrate that in some cases, no REF-able allocation exists.

1.2
Related Work

Equal entitlements.

Steinhaus
[
25
]
initiated fair allocation with the cake-cutting problem, followed by Foley’s
[
14
]
advocacy for envy-free resource allocation. Challenges with indivisible items were outlined by Schmeidler and Yaari
[
24
]
.

Subsidies.

The concept of compensating an indivisible resource allocation with money has been explored in the literature ever since
Demange, Gale, and Sotomayor
[
11
]
introduced an ascending auction for envy-free allocation using monetary payments for unit demand agents.

The subsidy minimization problem was first studied by
Halpern and Shah 2019
. They showed that, for any given allocation, there exists a permutation of the bundles that is
envy-freeable (EF-able)
, that is, can be made
envy-free
with subsidies. The total required subsidy is at most
(n-1)mV
, where
m
is the number of items,
n
the number of agents, and
V
the maximum item value for an agent, and this bound is tight in the worst case.
Brustle et al. 2020
considered the case in which the allocation is not given, but can be chosen. They presented an algorithm that finds an envy-freeable allocation through iterative maximum matching, requiring a total subsidy of at most
(n-1)V
, which is tight too.
Brustle et al.
[
9
]
also study the more general class of monotone valuations. They demonstrate that a total subsidy of
2(n-1)^{2}V
is sufficient to guarantee the existence of an envy-freeable allocation.
Kawase el al.
[
22
]
improved this bound to
\frac{n^{2}-n-1}{2}
.

Caragiannis and Ioannidis
[
10
]
developed an algorithm that approximates the minimum subsidies with any required accuracy for a constant number of agents, though with increased running time. However, for a super-constant number of agents, they showed that minimizing subsidies for envy-freeness is both hard to compute exactly and difficult to approximate.

Aziz
[
6
]
presented a sufficient condition and an algorithm to achieve envy-freeness and equitability (every agent should get the same utility) when monetary transfers are allowed for agents with quasi-linear utilities and superadditive valuations (positive or negative).

Barman et al.
[
7
]
studied agents with dichotomous valuations (agents whose marginal value for any good is either zero or one), without any additivity requirement.
They proved that, for
n
agents, there exists an allocation that achieves envy-freeness with total required subsidy of at most
n-1
, which is tight even for additive valuations.

Goko et al.
[
16
]
study an algorithm for an envy-free allocation with subsidy, that is also
truthful
, when agents have submodular
binary
valuations. The subsidy per agent is at most
V=1
.
Their algorithm works only for agents with equal entitlements.

House allocation.

The house allocation problem, also referred to as the assignment problem, involves allocating a set of
m
houses to a set of
n
agents based on their preferences, ensuring each agent receives exactly one house
[
20
,
31
]
.
The problem is often studied under the lens of economic efficiency, where Pareto optimality is a common goal—ensuring that no reassignment improves some agents’ outcomes without worsening others, as noted by Abraham et al.
[
3
]
in their study on Pareto optimality in house allocation problems, and Manlove in
[
23
]
.

Initial studies predominantly focused on achieving strategy-proofness and stability in house allocation mechanisms. For instance, Svensson
[
26
]
provided characterizations for strategy-proof allocations under strict preferences. Building on this foundation, Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez
[
2
]
explored mechanisms such as deferred acceptance and top trading cycles, emphasizing the trade-offs between fairness and efficiency.

Recent work has shifted towards fairness considerations. Gan et al.
[
15
]
present a polynomial-time algorithm that determines whether an envy-free assignment exists, and if so, computes one such assignment. Additionally, they show that an envy-free assignment exists with high probability if the number of houses exceeds the number of agents by a logarithmic factor.
Kamiyama et al.
[
21
]
study fairness in house allocation, showing that maximizing the number of envy-free agents is hard to approximate within a factor of

n^{1-\alpha}
for any constant
\alpha>0
. They prove that the exact problem is NP-hard, even for binary utilities, while deciding the existence of proportional allocations is computationally hard, and equitability can be efficiently determined.

In the context of binary preferences, where agents classify houses as acceptable or unacceptable, Aigner-Horev and Segal-Halevi
[
4
]
addressed the maximum envy-free partial matching problem, proposing efficient algorithms for special cases. In the context of dichotomous preferences, where agents classify houses as acceptable or unacceptable, Aziz
[
5
]
proposed strategy-proof and polynomial-time algorithms to maximize the number of agents receiving acceptable houses while ensuring individual rationality. Additionally, his work addressed scenarios with existing tenants, achieving core stability in these settings.

Hosseini et al.
[
19
]
extend the classical house allocation problem by introducing a graphical setting where agents are positioned at the vertices of a social network. In this model, an agent only experiences envy toward its neighbors. Their work focuses on minimizing the total pairwise envy across all edges in the social graph. They provide structural insights and computational results for various graph classes, including disjoint unions of paths, cycles, and more.

Truthfulness Relaxations.

Several studies have explored relaxations of truthfulness that restrict attention to specific types of manipulations deemed more plausible. These relaxations ensure that no agent benefits from any manipulation within the designated subset. The precise definition of ”likely” manipulations varies across different approaches.

Brams et al.
[
8
]
introduced maximin strategy-proofness, a concept in which an agent chooses to manipulate only when it is guaranteed to be beneficial in every scenario. Waxman et al.
[
28
]
were, to the best of our knowledge, the first to explicitly use the term ”safe manipulation”. Their work analyzed agents’ manipulation strategies under three different levels of knowledge in social networks.

In the context of cake-cutting, Bu et al.
[
30
]
defined a mechanism as risk-averse truthful (RAT) if no agent can manipulate in a way that is both safe and profitable. Hartman et al.
[
18
]
extended RAT beyond cake-cutting to general social choice settings and proposed a quantitative measure to evaluate a mechanism’s resilience to manipulation. They introduced the RAT-degree, which quantifies the minimum number of agents whose reports must be known to enable safe manipulation, thereby bridging the gap between classic truthfulness and RAT.

Another related concept, not-obvious manipulability (NOM), was introduced by Troyan and Morrill
[
27
]
. This framework assumes that agents consider only extreme outcomes—the best or worst cases—when deciding whether to manipulate. RAT and NOM are independent notions.

Preliminaries

Agents and valuations.

We denote by
[t]
the set
\{1,2,...,t\}
for any positive integer
t
.
We assume that the agents hold old apartments, denoted by
O
, where
o_{i}
is assigned to agent
i
.
We focus on the problem of allocating
M=\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
items (the new apartments) among
N=[n]
agents, where each agent receives exactly one item.

Each agent
i\in N
has a
valuation function

v_{i}:M\cup O\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, indicating how much they value different items.
Note that the classic model is equivalent to assuming that all agents value all old apartments at
0
.

An

, denoted as
A
, is a bijective mapping from agents to items. It consists of
n
different items
(A_{i})_{i\in N}
, where
\forall{i\in N}:A_{i}\in M
, and
A_{i}\neq A_{j}
for all
i\neq j\in N
. The item
A_{i}
is given to agent
i\in N
, and
v_{i}(A_{i})
represents agent
i
’s valuation of their bundle under allocation
A
.
We consider only allocations
A
that are
complete
, that is,
\cup_{i\in N}A_{i}=M
.

In addition to receiving item
A_{i}
, each agent
i\in N
is given a payment
p_{i}
,
where the vector of payments is denoted as
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},...,p_{n})
. These payments can be either positive or negative. A payment vector
\mathbf{p}
is said to be
budget-balanced
iff
\sum_{i\in N}p_{i}=0
.

We assume that agents are
quasilinear
, so that the utility of each agent is
u_{i}(A_{i},p_{i}):=v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}
.

Utilitarian social welfare.

The
utilitarian social welfare
of an allocation
A
is
\sum_{i\in N}v_{i}(A_{i})
. An allocation
A
is called
maximizing utilitarian social welfare
, if and only if
\sum_{i\in N}v_{i}(A_{i})\geq\sum_{i\in N}v_{i}(B_{i})
for any other allocation
B
.

Envy.

We recall here the standard notion of envy, when there are no old apartments. We later present several adaptations of this notion to the setting with old apartments.

The
envy
of agent
i
towards agent
j
under an allocation
A
and payment vector
\mathbf{p}
is defined as

u_{i}(A_{j})-u_{i}(A_{i})=(v_{i}(A_{j})+p_{j})-(v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i})
.
If for all
i,j\in N
the envy of
i
towards
j
is at most
0
, then
(A,\mathbf{p})
is called
envy-free (EF)
.

Let
\sum_{i,j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(A_{i})\right)
be
the total envy of allocation
A
.

Often, we are given only an allocation
A
, and want to find a payment vector with which the allocation will be EF.

An allocation
(A_{i})_{i\in N}
is called
envy-freeable (EF-able)
if there exists a payment vector
\mathbf{p}
such that
(A,\mathbf{p})

is EF.

Envy graph.

The
envy graph
of allocation
A
, denoted
G_{A}
, is a complete directed graph consisting of a set of vertices representing agents
N
.

For any pair of agents
i
and
j
in
N
, the cost assigned to the arc
(i,j)
in
G_{A}
is defined as the envy that agent
i
holds toward agent
j
under allocation
A
:

cost_{A}(i,j):=v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(A_{i})
.
We denote the cost of a path
(i_{1},...,i_{k})
as
cost_{A}(i_{1},...,i_{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}cost_{A}(i_{j},i_{j+1})
.

Envy-Freeable: Difference Model

In this model, we describe a situation where each agent assesses their individual improvement relative to the improvements of the other agents.

Now, we demonstrate how to construct a payment vector for a given allocation that minimizes the maximum envy, while also determining the value of this maximum envy. This is motivated by the fact that a DEF-able allocation does not always exist (as shown in

We can do this by solving a linear program, but there is also a combinatorial algorithm.

Definition 3.13
.

Given a directed graph
G
with edge costs,
The
mean cost
of a path is the total cost of the path divided by the number of edges in it.

The
maximum average cycle cost (MACC)
of
G
is the maximum, over all directed cycles
C
in
G
, of the mean cost of
C
.

In the literature, the MACC of
G
is also known as the
maximum mean cycle weight

[
29
]
.

Lemma 3.14
.

For a given allocation
A
, there exists a payment vector
\mathbf{p}
such that the envy between any two agents is bounded by the maximum mean cycle cost of
G_{A,O}
. Specifically, for all
i,j\in N
, the following holds:

(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})+p_{j})-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})+p_{i}\right)\leq c,

where
c:=MACC(G_{A,O})
.

Proof.

Let
C
be the maximum mean cycle in
G_{A,O}
.
meaning that for any other cycle
C^{\prime}
, it holds that
c=\frac{cost_{A}(C)}{|C|}\geq\frac{cost_{A}(C^{\prime})}{|C^{\prime}|}
.

Temporarily modify
G_{A,O}
to obtain
G^{\prime}_{A,O}
, where the cost of each edge is its original cost in
G_{A,O}
minus
c
. The cost of every cycle
C^{\prime}
in
G^{\prime}_{A,O}
is thus its cost in
G_{A,O}
minus
c|C^{\prime}|
, which is greater than
\frac{cost_{A}(C^{\prime})}{|C^{\prime}|}|C^{\prime}|=cost_{A}(C^{\prime})
, meaning there are no positive-cost cycles:

cost_{A}(C^{\prime})-c|C^{\prime}|\leq cost_{A}(C^{\prime})-\frac{cost_{A}(C^{\prime})}{|C^{\prime}|}|C^{\prime}|=cost_{A}(C^{\prime})-cost_{A}(C^{\prime})=0.

By
3.3
, there exists a payment vector
\mathbf{p}
, that eliminates envy in
G^{\prime}_{A,O}
. Using this payment vector, we have for each
i,j\in N
:

\displaystyle v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})+p_{j}\leq v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})+p_{i}+c\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})+p_{j}-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})+p_{i}\right)\leq c

∎

Note that when
c\leq 0
we said that the allocation is DEF-able.

Lemma 3.15
.

There is no payment vector that achieves an envy bound lower than
MACC(G_{A,O})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
be a payment vector, and let
C
be the cycle with the maximum mean cost.

Assume by contradiction that
v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})+p_{j}^{\prime}-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})+p_{i}^{\prime}\right)<\frac{cost_{A}(C)}{|C|}
for each
i,j\in N
. Then,

\displaystyle cost_{A}(C)=\sum_{(i,j)\in C}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})\right)\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in C}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})+p_{j}^{\prime}-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})+p_{i}^{\prime}\right)\right)<

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in C}\frac{cost_{A}(C)}{|C|}=cost_{A}(C).

Thus, there is at least one agent with an envy of at least the maximum mean cost.
∎

Lemma 3.16
.

The smallest maximum-envy attainable in a given allocation
A
equals
MACC(G_{A,O})
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a cycle in
G_{A,O}
, and suppose its mean cost is
c
.
By
3.5
,
the cost of a cycle remains the same under any payment vector, and the same holds for the mean cost.
By the pigeonhole principle, the cycle has at least one edge with cost at least
c
; this means that, for any payment vector, the maximum envy in
A
is at least
c
.

By
3.14
, there exists a payment vector for which the envy of all agents is at most
c
.
∎

In order to minimize the maximum envy, we aim to determine an allocation
A
that minimizes
MACC(G_{A,O})
. Given that there are
n!
possible allocations, no known polynomial-time algorithm guarantees finding the optimal allocation. The existence of such an algorithm remains an open question.

4
Envy-Freeable: Envy Sum Model

The Envy-Sum model relaxes the Envy-Difference model: it assumes that each agent aims to maintain their original envy sum.
This means that an agent may envy other agents,
as long as the sum of his envy levels over all the other agents is not larger than in the original allocation.

4.1
The Model

Let
v_{i}(o_{j})
be the value of the original apartment of agent
j
, by agent
i
.

Definition 4.1
.

For an allocation
A
, the
envy-sum
of an agent
i\in N
is defined by

ES(A)_{i}=\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})\right)-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})\right),

and the
total envy-sum
is given by

ES(A)=\sum_{i\in N}ES(A)_{i}.

Definition 4.2
.

For an allocation
A
and a payment vector
\mathbf{p}
, the
envy-sum
of an agent
i\in N
is defined by

ES(A,\mathbf{p})_{i}=\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})+p_{j}\right)-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})+p_{i}\right),

and the
total envy-sum
is given by

ES(A,\mathbf{p})=\sum_{i\in N}ES_{i}(A,\mathbf{p}).

Allocation
A
and payment vector
\mathbf{p}
are
Sum-Envy-Free (SEF)
if the envy-sum of each agent
i\in N
is at most 0.

In this model, the concept of the envy graph does not have significance because each agent does not directly compare the specific apartments received by other agents with their own. Instead, each agent is primarily concerned with bounding the total amount of envy they experience overall.

Observation 4.3
.

If an allocation is DEF, then it is SEF; but the opposite is not true.

To understand why this is true, recall the definition of DEF. An allocation
A
and a payment vector
\mathbf{p}
are DEF if for each agents
i,j\in N
, the following condition holds:
v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})+p_{i}\geq v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})+p_{j}
. Summing this inequality over all
j\in N
, we obtain for each
i\in N
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})+p_{i}\right)\geq\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})+p_{j}\right)\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in N}\left((v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})+p_{j})-(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})+p_{i})\right)=ES_{i}(A,\mathbf{p})\leq 0,

which is exactly the definition of SEF.

Proposition 4.4
.

There are original apartments
O
and a set of new apartments
M
such that, for every allocation of one apartment per agent, the resulting allocation is not SEF-able.

Proof.

Consider the following example with 2 agents.
The values of the original apartments are

\begin{bmatrix}&o_{1}&o_{2}\\
i_{1}&z+\epsilon&z\\
i_{2}&y&y+\epsilon\\
\end{bmatrix}

The values for the new apartments are

\begin{bmatrix}&a_{1}&a_{2}\\
i_{1}&V&V\\
i_{2}&V&V\\
\end{bmatrix}

when
x,y,\epsilon,V\in\mathbb{N}
.

For any allocation
A
such that each agent receives exactly one item: assuming there is a balanced payment vector
\mathbf{p}
, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}ES_{1}(A,\mathbf{p})\leq 0\\
ES_{2}(A,\mathbf{p})\leq 0\end{cases}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\begin{cases}v_{1}(A_{2})+p_{2}-v_{1}(A_{1})-p_{1}\leq v_{1}(o_{2})-v_{1}(o_{1})\\
v_{2}(A_{1})+p_{1}-v_{2}(A_{1})-p_{2}\leq v_{2}(o_{1})-v_{2}(o_{2})\end{cases}\Leftrightarrow^{p_{1}=-p_{2}}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}v_{1}(A_{2})+p_{2}-v_{1}(A_{1})+p_{2}\leq v_{1}(o_{2})-v_{1}(o_{1})\\
v_{2}(A_{1})-p_{2}-v_{2}(A_{1})-p_{2}\leq v_{2}(o_{1})-v_{2}(o_{2})\end{cases}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\begin{cases}V+p_{2}-V+p_{2}\leq z-z-\epsilon\\
V-p_{2}-V-p_{2}\leq y-y-\epsilon\end{cases}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\begin{cases}p_{2}\leq-\epsilon/2\\
\epsilon/2\leq p_{2}\end{cases}

Obviously, there is no solution.
∎

4.2
SEF allocation

Theorem 4.5
.

For
O
, the original apartments, and
A
, new apartments allocation, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

There exists a balanced payment vector
\mathbf{p}
such that
(A,\mathbf{p})
are SEF,

(b)

\sum_{i\in N}\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in N}v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})}{n}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in N}v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})

Proof.

(a)\Rightarrow(b)
: Suppose there exists a balanced payment vector
\mathbf{p}
such that
(A,\mathbf{p})
is SEF. That is, for all agents
i\in N
,

\displaystyle ES_{i}(A,\mathbf{p})\leq 0\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})+p_{j}-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}\right)\right)\leq\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(o_{j})-v_{i}(o_{i})\right)

(2)

Applying this inequality to all agents, we get:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\left(\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})+p_{j}-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}\right)\right)\right)\leq\sum_{i\in N}\left(\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(o_{j})-v_{i}(o_{i})\right)\right)\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\left(\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(A_{i})\right)\right)\leq\sum_{i\in N}\left(\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(o_{j})-v_{i}(o_{i})\right)\right)\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\left(\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})\right)\right)\leq\sum_{i\in N}\left(\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})\right)\right)\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\left(\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})\right)\right)\leq n\cdot\left(\sum_{i\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})\right)\right)\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in N}v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})}{n}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in N}v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i}).

(b)\Rightarrow(a)
: We will show how to find a balanced payment vector
\mathbf{p}
such that
(A,\mathbf{p})
is SEF. For each agent
i\in N
, we need to find such a vector that holds:

ES_{i}(A,\mathbf{p})\leq 0\Longleftrightarrow\\
\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})+p_{j}-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}\right)\right)\leq\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(o_{j})-v_{i}(o_{i})\right).

Since
\mathbf{p}
is balanced, it holds that
\sum_{j\in N}p_{j}=0
. Thus, we need to find such a vector that holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})+p_{i}\right)\right)\leq\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(o_{j})-v_{i}(o_{i})\right)\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})-v_{i}(A_{i})+v_{i}(o_{i})\right)\leq\sum_{j\in N}p_{i}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})-v_{i}(A_{i})+v_{i}(o_{i})\right)}{n}\leq p_{i}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{ES(A)_{i}}{n}\leq p_{i}

From
(a)
, we known that:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})\right)}{n}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})\right)\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})-v_{i}(A_{i})+v_{i}(o_{i})\right)}{n}\right)\leq 0\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\left(\frac{ES(A)_{i}}{n}\right)\leq 0

Therefore, we get the following constraints:

\displaystyle\frac{ES(A)_{i}}{n}\leq p_{i},\forall i\in N

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}p_{i}=0

Where
\sum_{i\in N}\frac{ES(A)_{i}}{n}\leq 0

Lemma 4.7

\sum_{i\in N}p_{i}=\sum_{i\in N}\frac{ES(A)_{i}-\frac{ES(A)}{n}}{n}=\frac{ES(A)-\frac{n}{n}ES(A)}{n}=0.

\displaystyle ES(A,\mathbf{p})_{i}=\sum_{j\in N}\left(v_{i}(A_{j})-v_{i}(o_{j})+p_{j}\right)-\left(v_{i}(A_{i})-v_{i}(o_{i})+p_{i}\right)=

5
Manipulations

In this section, we present an approach to gathering agents’ valuations. In order to reduce the incentives of agents to manipulate their valuations for old and new apartments,
rather than assessing the value of apartments based solely on unit values, we ask agents to assess the value of shared characteristics found in both old and new apartments — a common approach in real estate appraisal. Moreover, we analyze the conditions under which the Minimum Envy Sum mechanism (
4.10
) is free from
safe manipulation.

Truthfulness.

The classical concept of truthfulness requires that no agent can benefit from misreporting their valuations—meaning there is no
profitable manipulation
, an untruthful report that, under some circumstances, increases the agent’s utility. This definition assumes that a manipulating agent either knows the reports of all others or is willing to take the risk of acting as if they do.
To refine this notion, we use the definition of
safe manipulation
, as an untruthful report that never reduces the agent’s utility. A mechanism is
safely manipulable
if some agent has a manipulation that is both profitable and safe. If no such manipulation exists, the mechanism is said to be
Risk-Avoiding Truthful (RAT)
.

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}
takes reported valuations (which may differ from true valuations) as input and produces an outcome. Let
v_{i}(\mathcal{M}(v))
denote the valuation of agent
i
for their allocated outcome under
\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 5.1
.

A
manipulation
for a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
by an agent
i\in N
is an untruthful report
v_{i}^{\prime}
. It is
profitable
if there exists a set of reports
v_{-i}
from the other agents such that the agent gains a higher utility than by misreporting:

\exists v_{-i}:v_{i}(\mathcal{M}(v_{i}^{\prime},v_{-i}))>v_{i}(\mathcal{M}(v_{i},v_{-i})).

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is
manipulable
if some agent has a profitable manipulation; otherwise, it is
truthful
.

Definition 5.2
.

A manipulation is
safe
if it never results in a worse outcome for the manipulator—i.e., the agent weakly prefers it over truthfulness for any possible reports of the other agents:

\forall v_{-i}:v_{i}(\mathcal{M}(v_{i}^{\prime},v_{-i}))\geq v_{i}(\mathcal{M}(v_{i},v_{-i}))

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is
safely manipulable
if some agent has a manipulation that is both profitable and safe. Otherwise,
\mathcal{M}
is
Risk-Avoiding Truthful (RAT)
.

Definition 5.3
.

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is
not obviously manipulable
(or simply
NOM
) if for every agent
i\in N
with valuation function
v_{i}
and every possible report
v_{i}^{\prime}
of agent
i
, and reports of the other agents
v_{-i}
, the following two inequalities hold:

1.

\displaystyle\min_{v_{-i}}v_{i}\left(\mathcal{M}_{i}\left(v_{i},v_{-i}\right)\right)\geq\displaystyle\min_{v_{-i}}\left(\mathcal{M}_{i}\left(v_{i}^{\prime},v_{-i}\right)\right)
.

2.

\displaystyle\max_{v_{-i}}v_{i}\left(\mathcal{M}_{i}\left(v_{i},v_{-i}\right)\right)\geq\displaystyle\max_{v_{-i}}\left(\mathcal{M}_{i}\left(v_{i}^{\prime},v_{-i}\right)\right)
.

Intuitively, a mechanism is considered
NOM
if an agent cannot enhance her worst-case or best-case utility (as evaluated based on her true valuation) by misreporting her preferences. In other words, the best-case and worst-case utilities are maximized when the agent truthfully reports her valuation. If an agent can improve either her worst-case or best-case utility by misreporting, the mechanism is deemed
obviously manipulable
.

6
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we studied the practical problem of achieving envy-freeness in Rebuild-and-Redivide settings. We introduced three models for capturing envy—(1)
the difference model
, (2) the
envy-sum model
, and (3) the
ratio model
—and characterized when envy-free allocations exist in each. Since such allocations are not always guaranteed, we focused on minimizing envy through allocation and payment vectors.

In the
difference model
, we showed that the maximum attainable envy equals the maximum average mean cycle cost in the difference envy-graph. However, with
n!
possible allocations, no known polynomial-time algorithm can guarantee an optimal solution. Whether such an algorithm exists remains an open question.

For the
envy-sum model
, we proposed a mechanism that minimizes the maximum envy sum across agents. To reduce strategic manipulation in reporting, we introduced a novel elicitation method based on shared apartment characteristics and explored two aggregation methods for combining characteristic values. We also identified conditions under which the mechanism is resistant to obvious or safe manipulations. Extending these results to broader settings and identifying additional manipulation-resistant conditions remains an open direction.

Lastly, for the
ratio model
, while it is conceptually appealing, our current results are limited. Developing deeper theoretical insights in this model is a key area for future research.
