The association between frailty and quality of life in elderly population in Southern Iran: Findings from the Bushehr Elderly Health Program
Authors: Yasaman Zarinfar, Nekoo Panahi, Erfan Jannatalipour, Marjan Hosseinpour, Gita Shafiee, Mahnaz Sanjari, Iraj Nabipour, Noushin Fahimfar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is increasingly recognized as a common geriatric syndrome, particularly in rapidly ageing populations. Substantial evidence suggests that frailty is associated with poorer quality of life (QoL). However, in Iran, the relationship between frailty and both physical and mental QoL remains underexplored in community-dwelling older adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used data from 2,367 individuals aged 60 years or older (mean age: 69.3 ± 6.3 years) enrolled in the Bushehr Elderly Health Program, using a cluster random sampling approach. Frailty was assessed using the Fried frailty phenotype, which classified participants as non-frail, pre-frail, or frail. QoL was measured using the 12-item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12), which provides physical component summary (PCS) and mental component summary (MCS) scores. Covariates included age, sex, education, marital status, body mass index, number of physical and neuropsychiatric comorbidities, and living arrangements. Hierarchical linear regression and logistic regression were performed to assess the independent association between frailty status and QoL outcomes.ResultsIn this community-based sample, the prevalence of frailty was 8.0% (n = 191). Frail participants had lower mean PCS and MCS scores than non-frail individuals (PCS: 32.3 ± 7.85 vs. 45.8 ± 6.43; MCS: 42.1 ± 7.96 vs. 47.6 ± 7.49; both p < 0.001). In fully adjusted linear regression models, frailty status was independently associated with lower PCS scores (frail: β = -9.51, 95% CI: -10.70 to -8.31; pre-frail: β = -4.41, 95% CI: -5.66 to -3.16) compared with non-frail status. Pre-frailty was also associated with lower MCS scores (β = -1.15, 95% CI: -1.94 to -0.36), whereas the association between frailty and MCS did not reach statistical significance (β = -0.92, 95% CI: -2.44 to 0.60). In logistic regression, frail individuals had higher odds of being in the lowest PCS quartile (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 13.77, 95% CI: 8.81–21.52) and the lowest MCS quartile (aOR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.02–2.39).ConclusionFrailty is common among Iranian older adults and is independently associated with poorer physical QoL and, to a lesser extent, mental QoL. These findings support the need for targeted screening and multidomain interventions.

Introduction

Frailty is a common geriatric syndrome characterized by reduced physiological reserve, diminished physical strength, and increased vulnerability to stressors [1]. It typically manifests as muscle weakness, slow walking speed, unintentional weight loss, fatigue, and low physical activity [2]. Although frailty is not attributable to a single disease, it is clinically important because it is associated with higher risks of falls, hospitalization, disability, and mortality [3]. Despite its relevance in geriatric medicine, frailty remains difficult to define precisely because of its multidimensional nature and unclear onset [4]. Recent studies estimate that the prevalence of frailty among older adults is about 12% when based on physical criteria, with higher rates observed in women and in older age groups [5]. Prevalence is also higher in low- and middle-income countries than in high-income countries, although data across ethnic groups remain limited [6]. As the global population ages, healthcare professionals will increasingly encounter frail older adults in clinical practice [7].

Frailty may be associated with poorer QoL, a multidimensional construct that includes physical, psychological, emotional, and social domains [8, 9]. QoL reflects how well an individual functions and perceives overall well-being, including physical health, mental state, independence in daily activities, social relationships, and environmental support. Maintaining good QoL is especially important in older adults because it influences health, happiness, and the ability to function independently [9]. However, older adults often experience lower QoL because of accumulated health problems and environmental barriers that affect independence and well-being [10]. Compared with younger individuals, they are also more vulnerable to apprehension and anxiety triggered by adverse life events, which may contribute to depression, social isolation, reduced participation in leisure activities, and ultimately poorer QoL [11].

Frailty has been associated with adverse outcomes across multiple dimensions of QoL [12]. Physically, it can limit mobility, make routine tasks more challenging, and contribute to fatigue and chronic pain [13]. Psychologically, frailty may lead to feelings of dependence, helplessness, and reduced self-confidence [14]. Socially, it can restrict participation in family and community activities, often resulting in loneliness [15]. These factors can create a downward spiral that adversely affects both physical and mental health, ultimately reducing overall life satisfaction in older adults. Previous studies have shown that frailty is associated with a considerable negative impact on QoL [12, 16]. Frail individuals typically score lower on the physical component of QoL, and in some cases, reductions are also observed in the mental component [16, 17]. However, the strength and scope of this association vary across studies, potentially due to differences in age, sex, socioeconomic status, cultural context, and healthcare systems. In the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, research on this complex interplay is notably limited, with fewer studies simultaneously examining frailty in relation to multiple dimensions of QoL. For example, a recent cross-sectional study from Saudi Arabia reported a similar pattern, with a co-occurring prevalence of frailty and depression of 8.6% [18]. Their findings highlighted the co-occurrence of frailty and depression and their association with poorer well-being.

Given the rapid aging of the Iranian population and the substantial health and social costs associated with age-related conditions, assessing QoL among older adults while accounting for their comorbidities is particularly important. We have previously reported associations between several geriatric conditions, such as osteoporosis, diabetes, and polypharmacy, and QoL [19–21]. In the present study, we aimed to examine the relationship between frailty and QoL in a large, community-based sample of Iranian older adults, focusing on both the physical and mental components of QoL. By using standardized assessment tools and adjusting for key demographic and clinical variables, this study provides robust evidence on the association between frailty and QoL and offers policymakers and clinicians valuable insights for developing targeted geriatric care interventions.

Methods and materials

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted using data from the second phase of the Bushehr Elderly Health Program (BEHP), a large population-based cohort launched in Bushehr, Iran, in 2013. The BEHP was designed to enroll individuals aged 60 years and older to assess the prevalence of non-communicable diseases and associated risk factors. Participants were recruited using a multistage cluster random sampling strategy to ensure representativeness. The second phase of the program, conducted in 2015, focused specifically on musculoskeletal conditions and cognitive impairment among older adults [22]. Eligibility required permanent residence in Bushehr; individuals with severe health problems or those who declined participation were excluded. Details regarding the design and methodology of the BEHP have been previously reported [22, 23]. Enrollment was completed over 18 months. Participants with available data on QoL and frailty status were included in the current study, yielding a final sample of 2,367 individuals selected from the 2,426 participants in the second phase of the BEHP.

Measurements

Frailty assessment

Frailty was assessed using the Fried frailty phenotype criteria, a widely recognized definition that evaluates five domains indicative of frailty: weakness, exhaustion, slow gait speed, low physical activity, and unintentional weight loss. Weakness was assessed using handgrip strength measurements, with individuals in the lowest quintile for their gender and body mass index (BMI) classified as weak. Exhaustion was evaluated using self-report items from the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) [24, 25], where frequent reports of effortful exertion and inability to get going indicated exhaustion. Slow gait speed was determined by measuring walking time over 4.5 m, with the slowest quintile indicating frailty. Unintentional weight loss was defined as losing ≥ 4.5 kg or ≥ 5% of body weight over the past year without a deliberate attempt to lose weight. Low physical activity was defined using metabolic equivalent task (MET) values; participants in the lowest quintile were classified as having low physical activity [26]. Participants with no frailty features were categorized as non-frail (or robust), those with one or two features as pre-frail, and those with three or more features as frail. Based on these clinical criteria, participants were classified into three groups: non-frail, pre-frail, and frail. This assessment incorporated multiple domains relevant to geriatric health, including physical strength, chronic disease burden, mobility, and psychological resilience [27].

Quality of Life (QoL)

QoL was evaluated using the 12-Item Short Form Survey (SF-12), a widely validated instrument for measuring health-related QoL among older adults. The SF-12 yields two composite scores: the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS), each on a continuous scale. Both domains have demonstrated high reliability (Cronbach’s α > 0.89 for PCS and > 0.90 for MCS) and validity in distinguishing between demographic and clinical subgroups in elderly populations. Mean PCS and MCS scores, standard deviations, and inter-group comparisons were calculated [28, 29].

Comorbidity assessment

Neurological/psychiatric comorbidity was defined based on four conditions: depression, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and seizure disorder. Due to the low number of individuals with three (n = 7) and four (n = 0) concurrent neurological/psychiatric comorbidities, these categories were merged. Consequently, the neurological/psychiatric comorbidity variable was categorized into three groups: 0, 1, and ≥ 2 comorbidities. Physical comorbidity was derived from a composite list including anemia (defined by medical history or hemoglobin levels < 12 g/dL in women and < 13.5 g/dL in men) [30], chronic lung disease, cancer, rheumatic diseases, osteoporosis, kidney disease, thyroid disorders, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia. Dyslipidemia was defined as cholesterol levels ≥ 200 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, LDL cholesterol ≥ 130 mg/dL, or HDL cholesterol < 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women [31]. For analytical purposes, the total number of physical comorbid conditions was categorized as ≤ 1, 2, 3, 4, and ≥ 5.

Other variables

Other Variables: Additional information was collected using a validated self-report questionnaire, encompassing age, BMI, educational years, marital status, smoking habits (categorized as current smoker or non-smoker), and living arrangement.

Demographic and lifestyle characteristics were assessed through standardized interviewer-administered questionnaires. Age was recorded in years and subsequently categorized into two groups (60–69 and ≥ 70 years) to facilitate comparative analysis across early and advanced older adulthood. Sex was coded as male or female based on participant self-identification. Anthropometric measurements were obtained according to the WHO protocol: body weight was measured with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes using a calibrated digital scale, and height was recorded using a stadium-sized scale with the participant standing upright. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m²) and classified as < 25 (normal or underweight), 25–30 (overweight), and ≥ 30 (obese) [32].

Educational attainment was documented based on the highest level completed and grouped into five categories: uneducated, elementary, middle school, high school, and diploma or higher. Marital status was dichotomized as married versus single/widowed/divorced. Living arrangement was assessed by asking whether participants lived alone or with family members, recognizing this as a potential indicator of social support and vulnerability in aging. Smoking status was classified as current smoker versus never/past smoker, consistent with definitions used in previous epidemiological studies within the BEHP cohort. Medical histories were obtained to ascertain both neurological/psychiatric and physical comorbidities.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were calculated for all variables. Continuous variables with normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Inter-group differences by frailty status were assessed using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Hierarchical linear regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the independent association between frailty status and QoL domains, after controlling for demographic and health covariates, following procedures similar to those used in the Korean Frailty and Aging Cohort Study [33]. Model I included age, sex, BMI, educational level, marital status, living arrangement, smoking status, and neurological/psychiatric and physical comorbidity. Model II added frailty status to assess its incremental explanatory power (ΔR²). Unstandardized regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported. Additionally, multiple logistic regression models were fitted to examine the odds of scoring in the lowest quartile of PCS and MCS by frailty status, adjusting for all demographic and comorbidity variables. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR), 95% CIs, and p-values were calculated. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0. Assumptions of the regression models were checked using residual analysis, tests for multicollinearity, and goodness-of-fit statistics.

Results

A total of 2,367 older adults with a mean age of 69.3 ± 6.3 years were included in the study, comprising 1,159 non-frail (49.0%), 1,017 pre-frail (43.0%), and 191 frail (8.0%) participants. The mean age was 67.2 ± 4.6 years in the non-frail group, 70.3 ± 6.4 years in the pre-frail group, and 76.3 ± 8.3 years in the frail group. The distribution of participant characteristics by frailty status is shown in Table 1. Age ≥ 70 years was more common among frail individuals than among non-frail individuals (73.8% vs. 22.8%, p < 0.001). Women also constituted a larger proportion of the frail group than the non-frail group (61.3% vs. 44.0%, p < 0.001). Illiteracy was more frequent among frail participants than among non-frail participants (55.0% vs. 23.1%, p < 0.001). Regarding mental comorbidities, 44.5% of frail individuals had ≥ 2 mental conditions, compared with only 2.4% of non-frail participants (p < 0.001). In addition, 15.7% of frail individuals had ≥ 5 physical comorbidities, whereas only 2.3% of non-frail participants had ≥ 5 physical comorbidities (p < 0.001).

Quality of life and frailty

The mean QoL component scores by frailty status are shown in Fig. 1. Frail individuals had significantly lower physical and mental QoL scores than pre-frail and non-frail participants. The mean PCS was 32.32 ± 7.85 in frail individuals, 39.95 ± 8.84 in pre-frail individuals, and 45.79 ± 6.43 in non-frail individuals (p < 0.001). The corresponding mean MCS were 42.06 ± 12.67, 44.83 ± 10.61, and 47.58 ± 7.80, respectively (p < 0.001).

Hierarchical regression analysis

Based on the hierarchical regression model, adding frailty to the demographic model significantly improved the explanatory power of the model, with R2 increasing from 0.313 to 0.387 for the physical domain and from 0.211 to 0.213 for the mental domain, as shown in Table 2.

In the final model (Model II), frailty status was included to assess its incremental explanatory power. Frailty was the strongest predictor of lower PCS. Specifically, being frail was associated with a significant decrease in PCS (β = -9.51; 95% CI: -10.70 to -8.31), while being pre-frail was also associated with a significant decrease in PCS, although with a smaller effect size (β = -3.78; 95% CI: -4.41 to -3.16). In contrast, frailty was not significantly associated with MCS in the fully adjusted linear model (β = -0.92; 95% CI: -2.44 to 0.60), whereas pre-frailty was significantly associated with lower MCS scores (β = -1.15; 95% CI: -1.94 to -0.36). However, in the logistic regression analysis, both frail (aOR = 1.56; 95% CI: 1.02–2.39) and pre-frail (aOR = 1.71; 95% CI: 1.35–2.15) participants had higher odds of being in the lowest MCS quartile compared with non-frail individuals (Table 3). Female sex, older age, higher BMI, and physical and mental comorbidity were also associated with lower QoL scores (Table 2).

Logistic regression analysis

Our analysis revealed that frail individuals had significantly higher odds of being in the lowest quartile of QoL scores compared with non-frail individuals. Frail and pre-frail individuals had higher odds of being in the lowest PCS quartile (frail: aOR = 13.77; 95% CI: 8.81–21.52; pre-frail: aOR = 3.07; 95% CI: 2.37–3.98), as well as in the lowest MCS quartile (frail: aOR = 1.56; 95% CI: 1.02–2.39; pre-frail: aOR = 1.71; 95% CI: 1.35–2.15) (Table 3).

Discussion

In the present study, frailty status was strongly associated with poorer QoL among a large community-based sample of older adults in southern Iran, particularly in the physical domain. After adjusting for demographic and clinical confounders in hierarchical regression models, the association between frailty and physical QoL was substantial: frail individuals had physical QoL scores that were, on average, nearly 10 points lower than their non-frail counterparts (β = -9.51; 95% CI: -10.70 to -8.31). The association with mental QoL was not statistically significant in the fully adjusted linear model; however, a graded pattern was observed, with even pre-frailty associated with lower physical QoL scores (β = -3.78). These findings suggest that physical QoL may be particularly sensitive to the accumulation of frailty deficits, even after controlling for age, sex, and comorbidities. Overall, these results highlight the multidimensional nature of frailty and its association with physical well-being, and to a lesser extent, mental well-being and overall life satisfaction in older adults.

This pattern was further supported by the adjusted logistic regression analysis. When participants were categorized by QoL quartiles, frailty was strongly associated with poor physical QoL. Frail individuals had approximately 14 times higher odds of being in the lowest physical QoL quartile compared with the non-frail group (aOR = 13.77). Pre-frail individuals also faced a threefold increase in odds (aOR = 3.07), reinforcing the concept that functional vulnerability begins long before the full frailty syndrome is clinically diagnosed. Our findings align with a recent study that examined older inpatients in China and confirmed a strong association between frailty status and poorer physical health-related QoL (HR-QoL) using the SF-12 [34]. Their modeling underscored that sociodemographic factors exert direct and indirect effects on QoL, mostly mediated through increased frailty and comorbidity. As frailty progressed from pre-frail to frail among these hospitalized older adults, physical functioning scores showed marked declines, even after careful adjustment for clinical and demographic covariates. Similarly, Debnath et al. [35] investigated the association between frailty and HR-QoL among Indian patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Their cross-sectional analysis demonstrated that frail individuals with CKD had markedly lower levels of both physical and mental QoL compared with non-frail counterparts, independent of key clinical and demographic factors.

In contrast to the stronger association observed for physical QoL, the association between frailty status and MCS was weaker. Frailty was associated with lower MCS scores in unadjusted comparisons, but the adjusted association for frail participants was not statistically significant. In the fully adjusted linear regression model, frailty was associated with a small, non-significant decrease in MCS (β = -0.92), whereas the association was significant for pre-frailty (β = -1.15). However, logistic regression indicated significantly higher odds of being in the lowest MCS quartile for both frail and pre-frail individuals (aOR = 1.56 and aOR = 1.71, respectively). Interestingly, the odds ratio for poor mental health was slightly higher in the pre-frail group than in the frail group. This suggests that, while frailty may not substantially shift the average mental health score at the population level, it is associated with a higher likelihood of experiencing poor mental health outcomes. This pattern may reflect the heterogeneity of mental well-being among community-dwelling older adults, or the possibility that physical limitations are more strongly captured than mental health changes in this population. This aligns with a previous study [36], which found that frailty and multimorbidity are common and are associated with significant reductions in both physical and mental QoL, with the greatest effect observed in physical functioning and daily independence and lower effects on psychological and social domains. Recent findings [37] further highlight the negative impact of frailty on QoL in patients with chronic disease. In a study of older adults with diagnosed type 2 diabetes, nearly half were frail, and these individuals reported significantly lower QoL compared with robust counterparts. Notably, the study demonstrated that depressive symptoms were markedly more prevalent among those with frailty, with a substantial proportion of frail diabetic patients experiencing depressive states. This pattern indicates a notable association between pre-frailty and psychological vulnerability.

The difference between the larger physical effect size (β ≈ -9.5) and the smaller mental effect size (β ≈ -1.0) suggests that frailty may be more strongly associated with physical than mental aspects of QoL. Frailty is associated with reduced mobility and independence, consistent with findings from a study in India that reported similarly robust associations between frailty, chronic disease burden, and lower QoL among older adults with complex medical conditions [35]. In addition, multimorbidity and mental health conditions may contribute to the vulnerability associated with frailty and poorer QoL. These findings support the importance of early identification of pre-frailty and attention to modifiable factors that may help preserve physical and mental well-being in older adults.

Logistic regression analysis showed that frailty was strongly and independently associated with poorer physical QoL (aOR = 13.77 for the lowest PCS quartile), confirming its substantial impact on perceived health status and functional independence. With respect to the mental component of QoL, both frail and pre-frail groups showed higher odds of being in the lowest MCS quartile, although the association was weaker than that observed for physical functioning. This finding is consistent with prior research suggesting that psychological well-being may also be affected across the frailty spectrum [17]. Taken together, these findings suggest that both physical and psychosocial aspects of frailty should be considered when designing strategies to support QoL in older adults.

This study found that frailty was associated with poorer physical and mental QoL among older Iranian adults, even after adjustment for demographic and clinical variables. Overall, the magnitude of these odds ratios highlights the strong association between frailty and poor QoL, particularly in the physical domain. The increase in explained variance (ΔR²), particularly for the physical domain, further supports the relevance of frailty as an important clinical correlate of QoL. In addition, access to rehabilitation and community-based mental health support may be important to consider in efforts to maintain QoL in older age.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a large sample drawn from the established Bushehr Elderly Health Program (BEHP), the use of hierarchical regression models, and adjustment for a wide range of demographic and clinical covariates, including comorbidity burden. These features strengthen the precision and credibility of the findings. However, the study is limited by its cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference, and by reliance on self-reported data for some measures, which is susceptible to recall and reporting bias. Additionally, the sample is restricted to a single region in southern Iran, which may limit generalizability to other populations. To build on these findings, future research should utilize longitudinal designs to explore causality, incorporate objective physical performance measures, and conduct interventional trials examining multi-component programs to mitigate frailty and enhance QoL in Iran’s aging population.

Conclusion

Frailty was independently associated with poorer QoL, particularly in the physical domain, among older adults in this Iranian population. This association remained significant after adjustment for demographic and clinical characteristics. The association with mental QoL was weaker; however, frail and pre-frail participants showed higher odds of poorer mental QoL outcomes in the logistic regression analysis. Older age, higher BMI, lower educational level, and multimorbidity were also associated with reduced QoL, highlighting the multidimensional context in which frailty occurs. Taken together, these findings suggest that early identification of frailty and attention to both physical and psychosocial needs may be important in efforts to support QoL in later life. Because of the cross-sectional design, causal conclusions cannot be drawn. Longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the temporal relationship between frailty and QoL and to assess whether targeted interventions may help preserve well-being among older adults.
