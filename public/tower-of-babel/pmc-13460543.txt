Comparative Study of Tympanic Membrane Regeneration Therapy for Chronic Otitis Media
Authors: Shin‐ichi Kanemaru, Rie Kanai, Tomoya Yamaguchi, Shin‐ichiro Kita, Ryohei Yuki, Masayuki Kitano, Razumu Shirai, Hiroyuki Harada, Toshiki Maetani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo examine the effectiveness of tympanic membrane regeneration therapy (TMRT) for chronic otitis media (COM).MethodsOf 154 patients (166 ears) with COM and tympanic membrane perforation (TMP), 53 (54 ears) underwent conventional tympanoplasty (CTP), and 101 (112 ears) underwent TMRT. TMRT was performed using an endoscope to create a fresh wound around the TMP edge and remove most of the remaining TM. After high‐pressure saline irrigation of the tympanic cavity and removal of the lesion, a basic fibroblast growth factor‐infiltrated gelatin sponge was placed and covered with fibrin glue. After 4 ± 1 weeks, the eschar was removed and closure assessed. CTP was performed by tympanomeatal flap elevation with/without a postauricular skin incision using a microscope/endoscope. After cleaning the tympanic cavity, autologous tissue was collected and transplanted for TM reconstruction. TMP closure rate, hearing improvement, operative time, patient satisfaction, adverse events, and reperforation rate were compared between groups.ResultsThe main outcomes of TMRT versus CTP were: TMP closure rate 95.5% (107/112 ears) versus 88.9% (48/54 ears), average air conduction hearing (at 0.5/1.0/2.0 kHz) improvement 11.8 versus 7.7 dB, operative time 35.0 versus 112.0 min, patient satisfaction rate 94.1% (95/101 patients) versus 73.6% (39/53 patients), adverse event rate 9.8% (11/112 ears) versus 18.5% (11/54 ears), and reperforation rate 1.9% (2/105 ears) versus 8.3% (4/48 ears).ConclusionTMRT has many advantages over CTP as a surgical treatment for COM. In particular, significant differences were observed in operative time, hearing improvement, and patient satisfaction between the procedures.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: bFGF, chronic otitis media, gelatin sponge, tympanic membrane regeneration therapy, tympanoplasty

Introduction

Tympanic membrane (TM) regeneration therapy (TMRT) is a regenerative medical treatment for TM perforations (TMPs) that became eligible for health insurance coverage in Japan in 2019 [1, 2, 3]. Unlike conventional TM reconstruction procedures such as tympanoplasty, TMRT does not require harvesting and transplanting autologous tissue to close the TMP. This changes the nature of surgical intervention and reduces the risk of postoperative complications due to the size and location of the TMP. In other words, in conventional tympanoplasty (CTP), the difficulty of tissue transplantation varies depending on the size and location of the TMP, affecting postoperative hearing improvement and the likelihood of re‐perforation. However, with TMRT, TM regeneration occurs simply by placing a bFGF‐impregnated gelatin sponge, regardless of the size and location of the perforation, resulting in fewer postoperative problems. Furthermore, the short operative time and regeneration of a TM close to the original TM can significantly improve hearing.

However, TMRT generally avoids creating a tympanomeatal flap and preserves the integrity of the TM annulus, which can slightly limit visibility and access when removing intratympanic lesions. Therefore, TMRT has historically been limited to simple TMPs. Meanwhile, for more severe cases of chronic otitis media with TMP, tympanoplasty has been the only treatment available. In this study, to extend the application of TMRT to these severe cases, we preserved the TM annulus and, in principle, removed the remaining TM in all cases to maximize the surgical field. Using an endoscope, we removed lesions (infectious granulation tissue, edematous mucosa, polyps, sclerotic lesions, mucus, etc. in the middle ear associated with chronic otitis media) within the tympanic cavity, followed by high‐pressure irrigation of the tympanic cavity, including the epitympanic area. We then conducted a comparative analysis between patients treated with TMRT using this surgical method and those who underwent conventional tympanoplasty (CTP), and we report the findings here.

Materials and Methods

Patients

Since November 2019, when TMRT became covered by National Health Insurance in Japan, 864 patients have undergone TMRT at the two institutions listed below. Of these, 358 patients with chronic otitis media associated with otorrhea or intratympanic lesions, rather than simple TMP, required tympanoplasty. Of these, 101 patients (112 ears, 16–96 years old, 44 males, 57 females) who did not have cholesteatoma and did not require conductive reconstruction were followed up for at least 6 months after surgery and underwent all the prescribed evaluation items; these patients were designated as the TMRT group. In contrast, the control (CTP) group consisted of 53 patients (54 ears, 18–75 years old, 24 males, 29 females) with chronic otitis media and TMP who underwent conventional tympanoplasty at the same two facilities mentioned above between December 2018 and November 2019. Only patients who did not require mastoidectomy or ossicular reconstruction were included in the study. All patients were administered repeated antibiotic ear drops, oral medication, and tympanic cavity irrigation to maintain a tympanic cavity as dry as possible before surgery.

Details of the patients in each group are shown in Tables 1 and 2. Patients were older, disease duration was longer, and mastoid air cell development was slightly better in the TMRT group than in the CTP group. However, there were no significant differences between the two groups in factors potentially affecting disease progression, such as the TMP size or presence of soft tissue densities in the mastoid cavity.

This retrospective study was conducted at Kitano Hospital and Kanai Hospital in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the ethical committees of Kitano Hospital (P210600600) and Kanai Hospital (ECNo. 2001).

Drug Preparation for TMRT

The treatment used for TMRT was Retympa (Nobelpharma Co. Ltd. Tokyo, Japan), which comes in a kit containing a gelatin sponge, bFGF powder, and a dissolving solution. This agent was approved for coverage by the National Health Insurance in Japan in 2019. It is not approved in the United States but is currently undergoing phase II clinical trials to obtain FDA approval. The gelatin sponge, which was thoroughly soaked in the dissolved bFGF solution, was cut into several small pieces using scissors or tweezers.

Surgical Procedure

The surgical procedure used in the TMRT group is illustrated in Figure 1. TMRT was generally performed endoscopically under local anesthesia. If complete regeneration was not confirmed at 4 ± 1 weeks after surgery, the same treatment was repeated up to a maximum of four times.

TMRT procedures. (A) Cotton is infiltrated with 4% lidocaine to anesthetize not only the TM but also the external ear canal for approximately 20 min. In addition, anesthesia is administered by injecting 1% lidocaine containing epinephrine into the external ear canal. (B) An endoscope is used to create a fresh wound around the edge of the TMP. During this process, all calcifications are generally removed, and any sclerotic lesions connected to the tympanic cavity are also carefully removed until mobility of the ossicles is restored. Furthermore, the tympanic annulus and fibrous tissue near the umbilicus of the malleus are preserved as much as possible. In cases where preoperative CT reveals soft tissue densities or sclerotic lesions within the tympanic cavity, the remaining TM is removed, leaving the tympanic annulus intact. (C) A 16‐gauge flexible needle (Nippi Co. Ltd., Osaka, Japan) with a bent tip at a right angle and connected to an extension tube is attached to a 500 mL pack of saline attached to a pressure bag (Infuser bag 500 mL: Vyaire Medical Inc. USA). (D) The inside of the tympanic cavity is continuously irrigated at a pressure of approximately ≥ 300 mmHg. The amount of water varies depending on the case. During this process, the surgeon maneuvers the flexible needle tip to irrigate the superior tympanic cavity, posterior tympanic cavity, inferior tympanic cavity, and the area near the tympanic foramen of the Eustachian tube. (E) After irrigation, a small cotton ball is inserted into the tympanic cavity, and any remaining irrigation water is suctioned and removed. If mucus or other substances remain, they are removed as much as possible by repeatedly rubbing the cotton ball against the surface. (F) The trimmed bFGF‐impregnated gelatin sponge is packed tightly inside and outside the tympanic cavity and is left in place on the surface of the remaining TM. Fibrin glue is then dripped onto the gelatin sponge to cover the entire surface. After 4 ± 1 weeks, the crusts inside the external ear canal and on the TM surface are removed to check for TM regeneration. If regeneration is insufficient, the above procedure is repeated. This procedure can be performed up to four times.

In the CTP group, surgery was performed under general or local anesthesia using a microscope or endoscope. A tympanomeatal flap was created and elevated via a postauricular skin incision using a microscope, or via the ear canal using an endoscope, and the tympanic cavity was cleaned. In cases where lesions were found near the epitympanum (4 ears: 7.4%), the epitympanum was opened and the lesion removed, followed by scutum plasty using tragus cartilage as the reconstruction material. TM reconstruction was generally performed via the underlay or overlay technique using the temporal fascia or postauricular subcutaneous tissue. If TM closure was not complete by 16 weeks after surgery, up to four surgeries were performed according to the patient's request.

Evaluation Method

Regarding TM closure, in all cases, photographs of the TM were obtained using a flexible fiberscope before surgery, at 4 ± 1, 8, and 16 weeks after surgery, and at subsequent follow‐up intervals to confirm the presence or absence of a perforation. If closure of the perforation was unclear, tympanometry was performed at 16 weeks after surgery. Cases in which tympanometric readings suggested absence of pressure peak and large ear canal volume were considered to have a perforation. The following variables were compared statistically between the two groups: TMP size, mastoid air cell development, presence of soft tissue densities within the mastoid cavity, TM closure rate, reperforation rate, operative time, hearing improvement, improvement of the air–bone gap, adverse events, and patient satisfaction. Of these, the TM closure rate, hearing improvement, and air–bone gap improvement were investigated at 16 weeks after the final surgery, while the reperforation rate, adverse events, and patient satisfaction were investigated at subsequent follow‐up points. Moreover, to check for the possibility of cholesteatoma, photographs of the TM in each case were taken using a flexible fiberscope before and during the postoperative observation period. Furthermore, high‐resolution CT of the temporal bone was performed before surgery in all cases and during the postoperative observation period in cases with soft tissue density in the epitympanic cavity/tympanic cavity or depending on intraoperative/postoperative findings.

In addition, patient satisfaction was surveyed anonymously to prevent psychological bias among patients and was rated on the following 5‐point scale: “very satisfied,” “satisfied,” “neither satisfied nor dissatisfied,” “dissatisfied,” and “very dissatisfied.” The satisfaction level was compared between the two groups using the combined proportion of patients who responded “very satisfied” and “satisfied.”

Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.1.3. The chi‐squared test was used for comparisons of the presence of soft tissue densities in the mastoid cavity, TM closure rate, reperforation rate, and adverse events. The Welch two‐sample t‐test was used for comparisons of operative time, hearing improvement, and improvement in the air–bone gap. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons of TMP size, mastoid air cell development, and patient satisfaction. A 5% significance level was used for all comparisons.

TMP closure rate. In the TMRT group, the overall closure rate of the TMP was 95.5%, and the TM closure rates after one, two, three, and four treatments were 80.4% (90/112 ears), 11.6% (13/112), 2.7% (3/112), and 0.89% (1/112), respectively, demonstrating a closure rate of over 90% after two or fewer treatments. In the CTP group, the overall closure rate was 88.9%, with closure rates of 79.6% (43/54) and 9.3% (5/54) after the first and second treatments, respectively. The third and fourth treatments were not performed at the patient's request. There was no significant difference in the TMP closure rate between the two groups.

Hearing improvement at each frequency in the TMRT and CTP groups. According to this, the TMRT group showed significant improvement before and after surgery across all frequencies, while the CTP group showed significant improvement only at 0.25 kHz, 0.5 kHz, and 1.0 kHz, with no improvement in hearing at 2.0 kHz or 4.0 kHz.

Patient satisfaction. A total of 79.2% (80/101) of patients responded “very satisfied” in the TMRT group compared with 34.0% (18/53) in the CTP group, and the difference was significant. While there were no “dissatisfied” or “very dissatisfied” patients in the TMRT group, 9.4% (5/53) and 5.7% (3/53) of patients in the CTP group were “dissatisfied” and “very dissatisfied,” respectively.

Mastoid Air Cell Development and Soft Tissue Densities in the Mastoid Cavity

As shown in Table 2, mastoid air cell development was classified into three categories (good, fair, and poor) and compared between the two groups. Evaluation of mastoid air cell development was slightly worse in the CTP group. The presence of soft tissue densities in the mastoid cavity as detected by CT was compared between the two groups, but no statistically significant differences were found.

TMP Size and Closure Rate

The size of the TMP was classified into grade I (less than one‐third of the entire TM), grade II (between one‐third and two‐thirds), and grade III (more than two‐thirds). There was no significant difference in the perforation size between the two groups.

The overall closure rates in the TMRT and CTP groups were 95.5% and 88.9% respectively, and the difference was not significant. In four cases in the TMRT group, imaging diagnosis using a flexible fiberscope made it difficult to determine whether the TM was closed, so tympanometry was performed. Of the four cases, closure was confirmed in two because of pressure peaks obtained during tympanometry, while in the other two cases, an absent pressure peak and large ear canal volume indicated ongoing perforation and were classified as not closed.

Reperforation Rate

In both groups, reperforation occurred within one year post‐surgery. The reperforation rate was 1.9% (2/105) in the TMRT group and 8.3% (4/48) in the CTP group, with no significant difference between the groups. Both perforations in the TMRT group were small pinholes (diameter ≤ 1 mm). In the CTP group, there was one pinhole and one grade I, II, and III perforation; the grade II and III perforations were larger than the preoperative perforations.

Operative Time

The operative times in the TMRT and CTP groups, excluding anesthesia time, were 34.95 ± 9.17 and 112.04 ± 24.00 min, respectively. The operative time was significantly shorter in the TMRT group. (p‐value = 0.0000).

Hearing Improvement

Pure‐tone audiometry showed average hearing improvements from pre‐ to postoperatively of 11.84 ± 6.95 and 7.66 ± 6.91 dB in the TMRT and CTP groups, respectively, at 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz; this improvement was significantly greater in the TMRT group. (p‐value = 0.0004) As shown in Figure 3, the TMRT group demonstrated a significant hearing improvement at all frequencies before and after surgery, whereas the CTP group demonstrated significant postoperative improvement at 0.25, 0.5, and 1 kHz only, with no significant improvement at 2 or 4 kHz.

Air–Bone Gap Improvement

Improvement in Pneumo‐Bone Conduction Gap

At 0.5, 1, and 2 kH, the TMRT and the CTP groups showed postoperative average air‐bone conduction gaps of 5.38 ± 4.91 dB and 11.29 ± 8.95 dB, respectively, indicating improvement from preoperative levels. However, this improvement was significantly greater in the TMRT group. (p‐value = 0.0000) Furthermore, particularly at 4 kHz, the TMRT group showed a significant improvement after surgery, from 11.70 ± 7.93 dB preoperatively to 6.70 ± 9.41 dB postoperatively (p‐value = 0.0000), whereas the CTP group showed no improvement at all, from 10.19 ± 9.26 dB preoperatively to 10.74 ± 9.78 dB postoperatively (p‐value = 0.7625).

Adverse Events and Patient Satisfaction

In the TMRT group, TM cholesteatoma was the most common adverse event (4.4%; 5/112 ears). Of the five ears with cholesteatoma, two ears required removal alone, one ear additionally underwent TMRT for perforation after removal, and two ears showed spontaneous resolution of the TM cholesteatoma. TM retraction, which required myringotomy or tympanostomy tube placement, was the second most frequent adverse event (3.5%; 4/112 ears). Persistent otorrhea (1.9%; 2/112 ears) also occurred and was treated conservatively with ear canal irrigation and antibiotic ear drops. All adverse events were minor.

In contrast, in the CTP group, serious adverse events included a bone conduction decrease ≥ 10 dB on average at 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz in two patients (3.7%) and a taste disorder in one patient (1.9%). TM thickening occurred in 5.6% of ears (3/54), TM cholesteatoma and TM retraction in 3.7% (2/54) each, and TM thinning in 1.9% (1/54). However, there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of adverse events between the two groups.

As shown in Table 2 and Figure 4, patient satisfaction was significantly higher in the TMRT group than in the CTP group. (p‐value = 0.0000).

Discussion

Tympanoplasty is a surgical treatment used to remove lesions within the middle ear and reconstruct the conductive system. Most cases of chronic otitis media are accompanied by TMP. Removal of the middle ear lesion may require mastoidectomy depending on the extent of the lesion extending from the tympanic cavity to the mastoid cavity. However, if the lesion is limited to the tympanic cavity, tympanomeatal flap elevation is performed, followed by removal and cleaning of the lesion within the tympanic cavity. Conductive system reconstruction involves reconstruction of the ossicular chain and TM. In cases without ossicular chain abnormalities, the most important factor determining an improvement in postoperative hearing is the method of TM reconstruction.

TM reconstruction in CTP typically involves closure of the TMP using autologous tissue grafts. The difficulty of the surgery varies depending on the TMP size and position relative to the ossicles and tympanic annulus, as well as protrusion of the ear canal wall. Postoperative reperforation of the TM, thickening of the reconstructed TM, and TM retraction may occur. Depending on the skill of the surgeon, the hearing improvement is often insufficient [4, 5, 6].

Even when autologous tissue is used for TM reconstruction, the procedure still involves closing the TM with replacement tissue. Therefore, the newly formed TM will differ from a normal one and may not be able to reproduce the natural vibrational characteristics needed for normal sound transmission. Although basic tests such as pure‐tone audiometry may not always detect discrepancies, even individuals with the same hearing level may still experience variations from normal hearing [7, 8]. On the other hand, TMRT enables natural regeneration of the TM through proliferation of the patient's own TM tissue stem/progenitor cells, which are the cells responsible for TM regeneration; therefore, the regenerated TM is likely to closely resemble a normal TM in structure [9]. In fact, it has been reported that TMs regenerated by TMRT have a histology resembling that of a normal TM with a three‐layer structure [10], and may be able to restore their original vibration characteristics.

The differences in TM formation between the two treatments are significant. With TM reconstruction using CTP, autologous tissue is transplanted, and the epithelial and mucosal layers are stretched across this scaffold, resulting in immediate closure of the TMP during surgery. On the other hand, TMRT does not achieve closure during surgery; rather, it establishes an environment that allows the autologous cells to gradually regenerate the TM over the following 3–4 weeks. Therefore, with CTP, the final TM shape and degree of hearing improvement are highly dependent on the skill of the surgeon, whereas with TMRT, surgical skill has a much smaller impact. In other words, TMRT has the potential to overcome the limitations of existing autologous tissue transplantation treatments for TM reconstruction within the conductive system.

The TMRT and CTP groups showed significant differences in hearing and air–bone gap improvements, which were the outcomes of interest. TMRT demonstrated marked hearing improvements compared with TM reconstruction using autologous tissue transplantation. While hearing improvements may be insufficient due to factors other than the TM, such as Eustachian tube dysfunction, TMRT is likely to resolve hearing problems arising from the TM itself.

Regarding the closure rate of TMPs with TMRT or similar treatments, various results have been reported to date, showing considerable variability [11, 12, 13, 14]. On the other hand, in this study, a high closure rate of 95.5% was ultimately achieved in the TMRT group. The TM closure rate after one procedure was approximately 80% in both the TMRT and CTP groups. There was also no significant difference in the closure rate after the second procedure. However, in the TMRT group, the perforation was significantly smaller after the first procedure, and the second procedure was a very short outpatient procedure, making TMRT minimally invasive. In contrast, CTP required autologous tissue harvesting and transplantation for the second procedure, which are somewhat more invasive. For this reason, most patients in the CTP group who did not achieve closure after two procedures declined further surgery. There was also no significant difference in the rate of TM reperforation between the two groups. However, perforations were pinhole‐sized in the TMRT group, whereas larger perforations were more common in the CTP group; this could be due to the transplanted tissue shrinking over time.

Regarding the operative time, TMRT does not require tympanomeatal flap elevation, autologous tissue harvesting, or TM reconstruction by transplantation. Thus, the operative time is much shorter than that for CTP, making it a minimally invasive procedure for patients and surgeons.

One concern regarding adverse events is the potential for cholesteatoma formation due to the use of bFGF as a growth factor in TMRT [15, 16] However, in one case, an intratympanic epithelial pearl was observed and this was managed with office marsuplialization into the external ear canal without other serious adverse events. No mesotympanic cholesteatoma was observed in the TMRT cohort. While growth factors such as bFGF may act on the epithelium and promote cholesteatoma formation, the likelihood of middle ear cholesteatoma is low unless epithelial infiltration into the tympanic cavity is present. In particular, in the TMRT procedure, the three‐layer structure of the TM is maintained within the b‐FGF‐infused gelatin sponge scaffold during regeneration [10], and the risk of cholesteatoma formation due to epithelial infiltration is considered extremely low.

The adverse events in the CTP group included TM thickening and retraction, likely due to autologous tissue transplantation, as well as serious adverse events such as decreased bone conduction and taste disturbance. These adverse events in the CTP group depend on the surgeon's skill and experience, but they are also unavoidable factors associated with tympanomeatal flap elevation and TM reconstruction using autologous tissue. We also examined patient satisfaction after surgery and found clear differences between the groups. While there were no “dissatisfied” or “very dissatisfied” patients in the TMRT group, the most common complaint in the CTP group was insufficient hearing improvement. Other complaints included new onset of tinnitus and aural fullness after surgery, postoperative discomfort in the retro auricular region, taste disturbances, and frequent outpatient visits. Most of these adverse events were attributable to the TM reconstruction procedure, likely contributing to the high satisfaction with TMRT.

Although we compared the two treatments above, since TMRT was approved by the National Health Insurance in Japan in 2019, traditional tympanoplasty and some myringoplasties have gradually been replaced by TMRT [12, 13, 14].

Study Limitations

This was a retrospective chart review conducted at only two institutions. There were differences in the symptom durations and follow‐up periods between the two groups. The total number of patients (154 (166 ears)) was relatively small, so the results of this study need to be confirmed. We believe that a large‐scale, multi‐center prospective study is warranted in the future.

Conclusions

We compared TMRT, based on regenerative medicine, with CTP, based on reconstructive medicine, and found that TMRT was superior based on multiple factors. Significant differences were observed in operative time, hearing improvement, and patient satisfaction. TM regeneration therapy is considered an effective treatment not only for simple TMP but also for more severe chronic otitis media accompanied by perforation.
