Meet Me at the Arm: The Cooperative Multi-Armed Bandits Problem with Shareable Arms
Xinyi Hu, Aldo Pacchiano

Abstract
We study the decentralized multi-player multi-armed bandits (MMAB) problem under a no-sensing setting, where each player receives only their own reward and obtains no information about collisions. Each arm has an unknown capacity, and if the number of players pulling an arm exceeds its capacity, all players involved receive zero reward. This setting generalizes the classical unit-capacity model and introduces new challenges in coordination and capacity discovery under severe feedback limitations. We propose A-CAPELLA (Algorithm for Capacity-Aware Parallel Elimination for Learning and Allocation), a decentralized algorithm that achieves logarithmic regret in this generalized regime. Our main contribution is a collaborative hypothesis testing protocol that enables synchronized successive elimination and capacity estimation through carefully structured collision patterns. This represents a provably efficient learning result in decentralized no-sensing MMAB with unknown arm capacities.

Introduction

The multi-armed bandit (MAB) is a foundational framework in sequential decision-making that captures the trade-off between
exploration
and
exploitation
. In the classical setting, a learner is presented with
K
arms, each associated with an unknown reward distribution. The goal is to identify and exploit the best arm over a time horizon. At each round
t=1,\dots,T
, the learner selects an arm
a_{t}\in[K]
and receives a stochastic reward drawn from the corresponding distribution.

In this paper, we study the
collaborative multiplayer multi-armed bandit (MMAB)
problem, where multiple players interact with a shared set of arms. Unlike prior MMAB settings which assume unit capacity–i.e., each arm can support at most one player at a time, and any collision results in zero reward for all involved players
[
2
,
21
,
16
]
–we generalize the problem by allowing each arm to have an unknown capacity possibly greater than one.

We propose the Multi-Player Multi-Armed Bandits with Shareable Arms, local observations, and hard overload penalties (
MMAB-SAX
) framework, where
M
decentralized players interact with
K
arms. The instance is defined by a mean reward vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{K})\in[0,1]^{K}
, where each arm
\nu\in[K]
has an unknown mean reward
\mu_{\nu}
and an unknown capacity
C_{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}
. The capacity
C_{\nu}
represents the maximum number of players that can simultaneously pull arm
\nu
without incurring a collision penalty. At each time step
t=1,\dots,T
, each player
p\in[M]
independently selects an arm
a_{t}^{p}\in[K]
. If the number of players selecting arm
\nu
exceeds its capacity
C_{\nu}
, then all players on that arm receive zero reward–unlike in
[
23
]
, where only the overloaded players are penalized. Otherwise, each player pulling that arm receives an independent stochastic reward with expectation
\mu_{\nu}
.

Each player observes only their own reward and receives no information about the number of players on the same arm or whether a zero reward was caused by a stochastic realization or a collision. This strict no-sensing feedback regime poses significant challenges for coordination and capacity discovery.

Our model captures practical real-world applications in decentralized IoT network systems, such as unlicensed spectrum access in TV White Space and RF backscatter communication, where arms (communication channel) naturally have capacity greater than one, and devices operate independently under no sensing constraints, each observing only their own reward without information about collisions or other users
[
5
,
17
,
4
,
8
,
7
,
6
]
. Detailed discussions for applications are provided in Appendix
E
.

The goal is to design a decentralized learning algorithm that maximizes the cumulative reward over time. Due to the lack of communication, explicit coordination is infeasible. Our algorithm circumvents this by using structured collision patterns as implicit binary signals to enable coordination and synchronization.

We evaluate our algorithm using the following notion of pseudo-regret:

\mathcal{R}_{T}=T\cdot\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{a}\in[\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}]^{K}\\
\sum_{i}a(i)=M,\,a(i)\leq C_{i}\end{subarray}}\langle\mathbf{a},\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{p=1}^{M}\mu_{a_{t}^{p}}\cdot\mathds{1}\left(\sum_{q=1}^{M}\mathds{1}(a_{t}^{q}=a_{t}^{p})\leq C_{a_{t}^{p}}\right).

In our work, the following assumption about arm distributions hold:

Assumption 1.1.

The reward distributions of all arms are supported on
[0,1]
, and for any
i\neq j
, we have
\mu_{i}\neq\mu_{j}
.

We use the notation
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{K}
to denote the unknown permutation such that
\mu_{\sigma(1)}>\mu_{\sigma(2)}>\cdots>\mu_{\sigma(K)}
. For any
i,j\in[K]
, we denote the gap between the
i
-th and
j
-th best arms as:

\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}}=\mu_{\sigma_{i}}-\mu_{\sigma_{j}}
, where
i<j
.

Related Work

Early interest in multiplayer bandits grew out of
cognitive radio networks
, where radios must learn to share spectrum opportunistically
[
18
]
. Some early works applied multiplayer multi-armed bandit formulations to model how cognitive radios should select channels
[
11
,
10
,
14
,
15
,
1
]
. Most of the literature assumes that all players know the time horizon
T
; however, this assumption can sometimes be relaxed
[
3
]
. In this work we adopt the standard convention of a known horizon.

Decentralized multiplayer bandit models typically fall into two categories:
sensing
and
no-sensing
. In sensing models, players can detect collisions. Notably, the Musical Chairs routine introduced by
[
20
]
provides fast orthogonalization and has been used in several algorithms. Other approaches such as SIC-MMAB and DPE1 exploit collision feedback to transmit bits
[
2
,
21
,
16
]
. DPE1 achieved regret that matches the centralized optimal performance
[
21
]
.

In contrast, no-sensing models hide collision information: players only observe their own reward, which may be zero due to either collisions or stochasticity. Despite this challenge, several recent works have achieved logarithmic regret in this setting without requiring additional parameters
[
9
,
19
]
. Furthermore, it has been shown that
instance-dependent
regret scaling as
\tilde{O}(1/\Delta)
, where
\Delta
is the gap between the optimal arms and the remaining arms, is impossible without some form of communication
[
13
]
.

Recent work has extended multiplayer bandits to the setting of
shareable-capacity arms
(MMAB-SA), where each arm can support multiple players up to an unknown capacity.
Wang et al. 2022a
,
Wang et al. 2022b
introduced models in both centralized and decentralized settings where each arm has an unknown capacity. In their setting, if the number of players exceeds an arm’s capacity, only the overloaded players receive zero reward. Follow-up work by
Li et al. 2025
tightened the regret lower bound in the centralized setting originally established by
Wang et al. 2022a
, and proposed an algorithm that matches their minimax lower bound. Further work by
Xie et al. 2025
explored prioritized sharing in the centralized setting, where each player is assigned a priority weight that determines both the order in which capacity units are allocated and the magnitude of the reward received per unit.

In the decentralized setting,
Wang et al. 2022b
introduce two feedback mechanisms: Sharing Demand Awareness (SDA), where a player receives a binary indicator of whether the arm is shared, and Sharing Demand Information (SDI), where the exact number of co-players is observed. Their algorithms, DPE-SDI and SIC-SDA, strategically induce overloads to estimate arm capacities. However, the regret of these algorithms grows as
\mathcal{O}(\log(T)\mu_{K}^{-2})
, meaning when the worst arm has a small expected reward, the regret bound becomes extremely large. In contrast, our algorithm analysis delivers a sharper instance-dependent upper bound that depends only on
\min(\Delta_{\sigma_{V},\sigma_{V+1}},\Delta_{\sigma_{V-1},\sigma_{V}})^{-1}
, where
V
is the minimum number of top arms needed to accommodate all players. When there is a large separation between the top
V
arms and the remaining arms, our algorithm quickly identifies the optimal arm set.

Moreover, players in their setting observe the total reward collected on an arm. In contrast, our MMAB-SAX model operates under a strictly more challenging regime: all players pulling an overloaded arm receive zero reward, and each player observes only their own (possibly zero) reward, with no information about collisions or arm-sharing. The absence of both collision and sharing sensing makes coordination and capacity estimation more difficult. To overcome these difficulties, we introduce
A-CAPELLA

With slight modification, our algorithm can also accommodate the observed reward setting considered in
[

]
. The regret bound, depending solely on
\min(\Delta_{\sigma_{V},\sigma_{V+1}},\Delta_{\sigma_{V-1},\sigma_{V-1+1}})

We generalize prior work that assumes

We assume that all players and arms are indexed from 1 to

4
Algorithm Overview

Our algorithm operates in three sequential phases, with player 1 designated as the
coordinator
. The phases are designed to progressively identify and exploit the optimal set of arms while maintaining decentralized coordination through implicit signaling.

In Phase 1, the coordinator identifies a common communication arm, which serves as the coordination mechanism for subsequent phases. To inform the other players, known as
listeners
, of the selected arm, the coordinator deliberately disrupts the dynamics of a high-reward arm–one that is comparable to the best arm–thereby creating a detectable signal.
In Phase 2, the coordinator constructs a connectivity graph of arms by connecting arms whose empirical confidence intervals overlap, while concurrently executing a simple round robin exploration and updating the connectivity graph. This graph naturally partitions into two components: a top cluster comprising arms with higher estimated mean rewards, and a bottom cluster consisting of arms with lower estimated rewards. After the clustering is established, the coordinator signals the start of communication and informs the other players of the cluster containing more promising arms.
Phase 3 tests the capacities of arms in the high-reward cluster and marks those with known capacities.

The algorithm recursively executes Phase 2, conditionally skipping Phase 3 if the capacities of all arms in the high-reward cluster identified during Phase 2 already have known capacities. This process continues until the algorithm identifies the optimal
V
arms, where
V
is defined as the integer satisfying:

\sum_{i=1}^{V-1}C_{\sigma_{i}}<M\leq\sum_{i=1}^{V}C_{\sigma_{i}}.

(2)

Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1

There exists a decentralized algorithm which, with probability at least
1-\delta

O\left(\frac{\mathrm{poly}(M,K)}{\min(\Delta_{V-1,V},\Delta_{V,V+1})}\cdot\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)

4.1
Phase 1: Communication Arm Identification

In this section, we first explain the intuition behind Phase 1, then formally define the protocol for the coordinator in Section
4.1.1
and for listeners in Section
4.1.2
.

The phase begins with all players repeatedly executing full sessions of Grouped Round Robin, where the group size
\psi
ranges from
1
to
M
. This process is used to estimate mean rewards and explore the capacity
C_{\nu}
of some “good” arms with sufficient accuracy. Once the coordinator identifies an arm with a high mean reward that is suitable for communication (denoted
\hat{\nu}^{*}
), she begins signaling by repeatedly playing
\hat{\nu}^{*}
alone over several rounds, thereby disrupting its max capacity. Listeners remain in a passive state advancing the grouped round robin schedule, waiting to detect a signal. If a listener observes that an arm, which is believed to be a likely communication candidate, shows an obvious drop in its estimated maximum capacity
C_{\nu}
, they infer that this arm is
\hat{\nu}^{*}
. At the end of Phase 1, all players will agree on the communication arm
\hat{\nu}^{*}
, and the number of consecutive zero rewards required to send one bit.

A key challenge in this procedure is that players may identify different communication candidates at different times, causing them to begin signaling or listening at unsynchronized stages. For example, the coordinator might start signaling before others are ready to receive. To resolve this issue, all players will build a confidence interval using an
Inflated Confidence Radius
and use the collection of pre-specified, problem independent checkpoints
\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}
defined below to synchronize the start of the communication phase. This helps ensure that players begin communicating at coordinated times.

We define checkpoints
\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}
as time steps where the base-9 floor function value changes:

\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}=\left\{t\mid\left\lfloor\frac{2}{B(N_{t}(\cdot,1))^{2}}\right\rfloor_{9}>\left\lfloor\frac{2}{B(N_{t-1}(\cdot,1))^{2}}\right\rfloor_{9}\right\}

, where for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the base-9 floor function is defined as:

\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor_{9}=9^{\alpha}\text{ for the unique }\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\text{ satisfying }9^{\alpha}\leq x<9^{\alpha+1}
. Note that although the definition involves
N_{t}(\cdot,1)
, this value is identical for all arms at the end of each Grouped Round Robin session. As a result,
\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}
is a global checkpoint set shared across all arms.

Inflated Confidence Radius

Let
h\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
h>2

h\cdot B(N_{t}(\nu,1))
denotes an inflated confidence bound around the estimated mean reward
\hat{\mu}_{\nu,1}(t)
for arm

. The inflation factor

is chosen to ensure that the inequality

\hat{\mu}_{\nu,1}^{p}-5B(N_{t}(\nu,1))\geq 0

(3)

is satisfied at approximately the same time for all players. It can be shown that this event occurs when

\frac{\mu_{\nu}}{12}<B(N_{t}(\nu,1))\leq\frac{\mu_{\nu}}{4}
, and

2/B(N_{t}(\nu,1))^{2}
falls within an interval that aligns with a unique power of 9 (see Lemma

\frac{32}{\mu_{\nu}^{2}}\leq\frac{g(N_{t}(\nu,1))}{N_{t}(\nu,1)}<\frac{288}{\mu_{\nu}^{2}},

After identifying the communication arm

Regret Analysis

Under the event
\mathcal{E}_{\text{good}}
, the maximum number of Grouped Round Robin sessions in Phase 1 is

O(\frac{1}{\mu_{\sigma_{1}}}^{2}\cdot\log(1/\delta)),

since each session contributes at most

2(MK-M+1)M\cdot\mu_{\sigma_{1}}

to the total regret. Consequently, the total regret incurred in Phase 1 is bounded by

O(\frac{(MK-M+1)M\cdot\log(\log(T)/\delta)}{\mu_{\sigma_{1}}}).

In Phase 2, detecting disconnection in the connectivity graph via Simple Round Robin takes at most

O(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}^{2}})

rounds, and each round incurs at most

M(K-V)K\cdot\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}

in regret.
Communicating
K+1
bits to all players takes

O(\log(1/\delta)\cdot\frac{M^{2}K}{C_{\hat{\nu}^{*}}})

regret, where the term
C_{\hat{\nu}^{*}}
arises from grouping players into groups of size
C_{\hat{\nu}^{*}}
, which is necessary to enable collision-based signal detection.
Since the regret incurred from Simple Round Robin dominates the regret incurred from the
signal testing block
, the total regret in Phase 2 for the first partitioning is:

O(\log(\log(T)/\delta)\cdot(\frac{M(K-V)K}{\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}}+\frac{M^{2}K}{C_{\hat{\nu}^{*}}})).

In Phase 3,
we upper bound the duration number of Grouped Round Robin sessions
by

O(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}}),

so the total regret incurred in Phase 3 is simplified to

O(\frac{(MK-M+1)M\mu_{\sigma_{1}}}{\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}}\cdot\log(\log(T)/\delta)).

Since the recursion process repeats at most
K-1
times, the total regret including the regret in Phase 1 is bounded by the following Theorem
5.1
.
Theorem
A.1
in Appendix also provides a result with a bound of
O(\sqrt{T})
.

Let

Conclusion

We present the decentralized algorithm, A-CAPELLA, for the multiplayer multi-armed bandit problem with shareble arms and unkown capacity (MMAB-SAX). Our approach achieves logarithmic regret in the number of rounds, with instance-dependent scaling based on reward gaps. Unlike prior work, we allow arm sharing without direct collision feedback or arm sharing feedback, or assumptions on minimum reward values. Simulations shown in Figure
3
(Appendix) demonstrate sublinear cumulative regret that supports our theoretical guarantees. Overall, this work addresses the no-sensing multiplayer bandit problem under general, unknown capacity constraints.
However, our algorithm still faces limitations. The current implementation remains algorithmically complex, and the regret bounds scale with both the number of arms and players, which may be suboptimal in large-scale settings. We hope future research builds on our work to simplify the implementation.
Additionally, our method assumes that each player knows the total number of players
M
and their own unique index at the beginning of the game. Relaxing this assumption is not within the scope of the present work, and we leave it for future research and include a brief discussion in Appendix
E
.

Lemma A.5

(Regret of a Single Simple Round-Robin Round)
.

Let
M
players interact with
K
arms whose means are ordered

\mu_{\sigma_{1}}>\mu_{\sigma_{2}}>\dots>\mu_{\sigma_{K}}
.
Define
V
as the smallest integer satisfying

\sum_{i=1}^{V-1}C_{\sigma_{i}}<M\leq\sum_{i=1}^{V}C_{\sigma_{i}},

Denote one full Simple Round-Robin cycle by

R_{\mathrm{sr}}
.
Then

R_{\mathrm{sr}}\;=\;K\!\sum_{i=1}^{V}\mu_{\sigma_{i}}\tilde{C}_{\sigma_{i}}\;-\;M\!\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{\sigma_{j}}\;\;\leq\;\;M\,(K-V)\,(\mu_{\sigma_{1}}-\mu_{\sigma_{K}})\;\;\leq\;\;M\,(K-V)\,K\,\max_{i\in[K-1]}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}},

where
\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}=\mu_{\sigma_{i}}-\mu_{\sigma_{i+1}}
.

Proof.

During one round of simple Round-Robin, all
M
players collectively pull arms
MK
times. The expected reward can be written as

M\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{\sigma_{j}},

where
\mu_{\sigma_{j}}
is the expected reward of arm
\sigma_{j}
. The optimal reward is

K\sum_{i=1}^{V}\mu_{\sigma_{i}}\tilde{C}_{\sigma_{i}},

where
\tilde{C}_{\sigma_{i}}
represents the optimal number of players allocated to each arm, ensuring that exactly
\tilde{C}_{\sigma_{j}}
players pull arm
\sigma_{j}
. The value of
V
is determined as the smallest integer satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{V-1}C_{\sigma_{i}}<M\leq\sum_{i=1}^{V}C_{\sigma_{i}},

where
C_{\sigma_{i}}
is the maximum number of players that arm
\sigma_{i}
can sustain.

\tilde{C}_{\sigma_{i}}=\begin{cases}C_{\sigma_{i}},&\text{if }i\leq V-1\\
M-\sum_{j=1}^{V-1}C_{j},&\text{if }i=V.\end{cases}

Then, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{V}\tilde{C}_{\sigma_{i}}=M
.
The regret collected during one round of simple Round-Robin is given by:

R_{sr}=K\sum_{i=1}^{V}\mu_{\sigma_{i}}\tilde{C}_{\sigma_{i}}-M\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_{\sigma_{j}}.

Decomposing the regret into the sum of the difference between the reward collected from optimal allocation and reward collected from round-robin allocation on each arm, we have:

\displaystyle R_{sr}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{V}\left[(K\tilde{C}_{\sigma_{i}}-M)\mu_{\sigma_{i}}\right]-M\sum_{i=V+1}^{K}\mu_{\sigma_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{V}\left[(K\tilde{C}_{\sigma_{i}}-M)\mu_{\sigma_{1}}\right]-M\sum_{i=V+1}^{K}\mu_{\sigma_{k}}

\displaystyle=M(K-V)\mu_{\sigma_{1}}-M(K-V)\mu_{\sigma_{k}}

\displaystyle=M(K-V)(\mu_{\sigma_{1}}-\mu_{\sigma_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq M(K-V)K\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}.

B.1
Main Algorithm and Pseudocode for All Phases

Phase 1: Communication Arm Selection and Signaling

In Phase 1, the primary objectives are to employ the deterministic GroupedRoundRobin protocol, which synchronizes the actions of the players, to identify the communication arm and signal it through intentional collision patterns. Synchronization of player actions is achieved using inflated confidence bounds and predefined regular checkpoints, denoted as
\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}
.

\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}
is defined as a set of specific time points where the players check for synchronization and communication signals. These checkpoints are strategically chosen to ensure that all players are aligned in their actions and can detect the communication arm effectively.
We define checkpoints
\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}
as time steps where the base-9 floor function value changes:

\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}=\left\{t\mid\left\lfloor\frac{2}{B(N_{t}(\cdot,1))^{2}}\right\rfloor_{9}>\left\lfloor\frac{2}{B(N_{t-1}(\cdot,1))^{2}}\right\rfloor_{9}\right\}

, where for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, the base-9 floor function is defined as:

\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor_{9}=9^{\alpha}\text{ for the unique }\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\text{ satisfying }9^{\alpha}\leq x<9^{\alpha+1}
.

In order to create an intentional collision pattern for signaling, players must first independently select a subset of arms, denoted by
\mathcal{S}^{p}
, which contains potential communication arms. They then estimate the capacities of all arms in this candidate set. After identifying
\mathcal{S}^{p}
, the listeners wait for the subsequent checkpoints. If all players detect that an arm
\nu\in\mathcal{S}^{p}
exhibits an unusual sequence of consecutive zero rewards, despite the arm’s capacity not being exceeded, they infer that this arm is the communication arm selected by the coordinator. This is because, while the other players continue to follow a grouped round-robin schedule, the coordinator exclusively pulls the communication arm to reduce its available capacity by 1 in the following Grouped Round Robin Session.

Phase 3: Capacity Estimation

At the end of Phase 2, if the capacities of all arms transmitted by the coordinator are not yet known, the coordinator must first transmit the parameter

, which governs the duration

refers to the checkpoint corresponding to the first partition. As the recursive process continues, this will be updated to
t_{\text{second}}
, and so on.

It is important to note that since all players participate in the collision testing blocks to help disseminate the coordinator’s message, even fixed players are aware of which arms were selected in the latest partition. If any transmitted arm has not yet been processed in Phase 3, all players will proceed to Phase 3 to help the coordinator send information about
\gamma
to all the active players. Then, active players start finding capacities, while committed players play a fixed arm.

The main goal of transmitting
\gamma
is to ensure synchronized capacity estimation across all active players. Specifically, while all active players have estimated the mean rewards of the active arms up to the same level of accuracy, i.e.,

|\mu_{a}-\hat{\mu}_{a}^{p}|\leq B(N_{t_{\text{first}}}(a,1))\quad\text{and}\quad|\hat{\mu}_{a}^{p^{\prime}}-\hat{\mu}_{a}^{p}|\leq 2B(N_{t_{\text{first}}}(a,1)),

where
p
and
p^{\prime}
are two different active players, this allows all active players to construct a consistent lower bound for each transmitted arm using

\gamma\cdot B\left(N_{t_{\text{first}}}(a^{\prime},1)\right),\quad\text{where }a^{\prime}=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{\mu}_{a}^{p}

Each player
p
can compute the largest
\gamma_{p}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\gamma_{p}\cdot B(N_{t_{\text{first}}}(a^{\prime},1))<\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{\mu}_{a}^{p}-B(N_{t_{\text{first}}}(a,1)).

Given the bound
|\hat{\mu}_{a}^{p^{\prime}}-\hat{\mu}_{a}^{p}|\leq 2B(N_{t_{\text{first}}}(a,1))

|\gamma^{p}-\gamma^{p^{\prime}}|<10
. Therefore, the coordinator only needs to send the last digit of

Afterward, only the active players participate in the Capacity Estimation Protocol, while fixed players continue playing their assigned arms. It is straightforward to verify that the capacity estimation process concludes before the next checkpoint, ensuring all players are ready for the next collision testing block. Once Phase 3 ends, listener players are either assigned to a fixed arm or return to Phase 2 to continue the exploration process.

Appendix D
Modification for Wang et al. 2022a Setting

We now revise
A-CAPELLA
to handle the setting where each player only observes the
aggregate reward
on the arm they pull, rather than individual reward feedback, and where only players exceeding an arm’s capacity receive zero reward. This feedback model is studied by
Wang et al. 2022b
. While the overall algorithmic structure remains unchanged, both the capacity estimation procedure and the collision testing routine require adjustment under this model. We assume that the reward distributions for all arms are supported on the interval
[0,1]
.

Modified capacity estimation.

We replace the overload-detection method, previously based on observing consecutive zero rewards, with one based on detecting a
drop in per-player reward
. Let
r_{a,t}^{(\psi)}
be the total reward observed on arm
a
at time
t
when pulled by a group of size
\psi
. The per-capita reward is
r_{a,t}^{(\psi)}/\psi
, and we define the empirical average up to time
t
as:

\hat{\mu}_{a,\psi}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}\frac{r_{a,\ell}^{(\psi)}}{\psi}.

Let
\mu_{a,\psi}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{a,\psi}(t)]
, and suppose
C_{a}
is the capacity of arm
a
. Then:

\mu_{a,\psi}=\mu_{a,1}\cdot\frac{\min\{\psi,C_{a}\}}{\psi}.

Overloading occurs when
\psi>C_{a}
, leading to a drop in expected per-player reward. In particular,

\mu_{a,C_{a}+1}-\mu_{a,1}=\frac{\mu_{a,1}}{C_{a}+1}\geq\frac{\mu_{a,1}}{M},

since
C_{a}<M
for the communication arm. This guarantees that the overload-induced drop is at least
\mu_{a,1}/M
, a quantity that can be estimated once we obtain a reliable estimate of
\mu_{a,1}
.

Using concentration bounds such as Hoeffding or empirical Bernstein, this drop is detectable with probability at least
1-\delta
after:

O\left(\frac{M^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{\mu_{a,1}^{2}}\right)

samples. Accordingly, we set the inflation parameter
h=2M
, and redefine the signal-testing checkpoints as:

\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}=\left\{\left(\frac{4(2M+1)}{2M-1}\right)^{\alpha}:\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{+},\,\left(\frac{4(M+1)}{2M-1}\right)^{\alpha}<T\right\}.

Once the following condition is satisfied for any arm
\nu
:

h\cdot B(N_{t}(\nu,1))>\hat{\mu}_{\nu,1}(t),

(13)

Then, denote that time as
t_{0}^{1}
, the capacity of the communication arm
\nu
is known to all players.

Modified collision testing.

In this reward feedback model, the collision testing duration must also be adjusted to reflect the noisier observations. Each player listens during a collision testing block of duration:

\omega^{\prime}(t_{\text{comm}})=\left\lceil\frac{\log(\delta/4K^{2}M)}{1\cdot B^{2}(N_{t_{\text{comm}}}(\cdot,1))}\right\rceil.

During this period, players monitor the arms in the candidate set. If a player detects a significant unusual drop in per-player reward on any arm during the block when the arm capacity has not been exceeded, that arm is declared the communication arm. Furthemore, the number of collisions required to transmit a bit is thus given by
\omega^{\prime}(t_{\text{comm}})
.

Regret analysis

We now analyze the regret of
A-CAPELLA
under this revised setting.

Phase 1.

Under the event
\mathcal{E}_{\text{good}}
, detecting a capacity drop requires observing a gap of at least
\mu_{\sigma_{1},1}/M
, which can be reliably identified using empirical concentration bounds after:

O\left(\frac{M^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{\mu_{\sigma_{1},1}^{2}}\right)

Grouped Round Robin sessions.
Each session incurs
O(2M^{2}K(\mu_{\sigma_{1}}-\mu_{\sigma_{K}}))=O(2M^{2}K^{2}\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}})
regret. Thus, the total regret in Phase 1 is:

O\left(\frac{(M^{4}K\cdot\log\left(\log T/\delta\right)}{\mu_{\sigma_{1}}}\right).

Phase 2 (first partitioning).

Detecting a disconnection in the connectivity graph using Simple Round-Robin requires:

O\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}^{2}}\right)

rounds, and each round contributes at most
M(K-V)K\cdot\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}
regret by Lemma
A.5
. The resulting regret from this part is:

O\left(\frac{M(K-V)K\cdot\log(1/\delta)}{\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}}\right).

To transmit a bit via collision testing, players observe arms over a duration of

\omega^{\prime}(t_{\text{comm}})=O\left(\frac{M^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{\mu_{\sigma_{1}}^{2}}\right).

Each round may incur
O(M\cdot\mu_{\sigma_{1}})
regret. To transmit
K+1
bits to all
M
players, we need approximately
\lceil M/C_{\hat{\nu}^{*}}\rceil
groups per bit. Thus, the total communication regret is:

O\left(\frac{M^{3}K\cdot\log(1/\delta)}{C_{\hat{\nu}^{*}}\cdot\mu_{\sigma_{1}}}\right).

Combining both components, the total regret in Phase 2 is:

O\left(\log\left(\frac{\log T}{\delta}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{M(K-V)K}{\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}}+\frac{M^{3}K}{C_{\hat{\nu}^{*}}\cdot\mu_{\sigma_{1}}}\right)\right).

Phase 3 (first partitioning).

The dominant cost in Phase 3 arises from capacity estimation using Grouped Round-Robin.
Under the aggregate feedback model, players must detect a per-player reward gap of at least
\mu_{j}/M
, where
j=\arg\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}
, in order to distinguish the top cluster from others. To ensure this separation across all relevant arms, the number of Grouped Round-Robin sessions required is upper bounded by:

O\left(\frac{M^{2}}{\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}^{2}}\right).

Each session incurs regret up to
(MK-M+1)M\mu_{\sigma_{1}}
, resulting in a total regret for Phase 3 bounded by:

O\left(\frac{(MK-M+1)M^{3}\mu_{\sigma_{1}}\cdot\log\left(\log T/\delta\right)}{\max_{i}\Delta_{\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1}}^{2}}\right).

Since the recursive partitioning process repeats at most
K-1
times, and we set
\delta=\frac{1}{T^{2}MK^{2}}
, the total regret incurred across all recursive partitions is bounded by:

O\left(\log(TMK)\cdot\left[\frac{M(K-V)K^{2}}{\Delta}+\frac{(MK-M+1)M^{3}K\mu_{\sigma_{1}}}{\Delta^{2}}+\frac{M^{3}K^{2}}{C_{\hat{\nu}^{*}}\cdot\mu_{\sigma_{1}}}\right]\right),

where
\Delta=\min\{\Delta_{\sigma_{V},\sigma_{V+1}},\Delta_{\sigma_{V-1},\sigma_{V-1+1}}\}
. Suppose
\mu_{\sigma_{K}}<\Delta
, then
\mu_{\sigma_{1}}<K\Delta
. The total regret can be simplied to

O\left(\log(TMK)\cdot\left[\frac{M^{4}K^{3}}{\Delta}\right]\right),

We emphasize that this bound is conservative: it uses worst-case regret estimates from Simple and Grouped Round Robin under the assumption that
all players
pulling an overloaded arm receive zero reward. In the aggregate feedback model, however, only the
overloaded players
receive zero reward, while players within capacity still receive non-zero feedback. As a result, the actual regret in practice is significantly smaller than this upper bound.

Applications and Indexing Assumption

In this section, we first present two application examples of the MMAB-SAX framework in Subsection
E.1
. Then, in Subsection
E.2
, we discuss an alternative of the indexing assumption used in this paper.

E.1
Applications of the MMAB-SAX Framework

Our MMAB-SAX framework—modeling decentralized multi-player multi-armed bandits with unknown arm capacities and no sensing—is well-suited to address several key challenges in modern Internet of Things (IoT) networks. These networks consist of physical devices such as smartphones, smart vehicles, wearables, and sensors embedded with software, enabling them to connect and exchange data over the internet. In many IoT systems, devices operate in a decentralized manner, often without access to collision feedback or direct communication with other devices.

Unlicensed Spectrum Access in TV White Space (TVWS)

One prominent example is unlicensed spectrum access in TV White Space (TVWS). TVWS refers to underutilized low-frequency spectrum bands, originally reserved for TV broadcasting, which are now available for unlicensed broadband wireless devices. These white space channels can be tuned to operate as communication channels for multiple users simultaneously .

Each channel supports only a limited number of concurrent transmissions due to interference and power constraints . This channel capacity is unknown and varies depending on the environment. When the number of unlicensed users transmitting on the same channel exceeds this capacity, all transmissions may fail. Importantly, each device receives only its own feedback—it cannot observe collisions or how many other users accessed the same channel. Thus, transmission failure (due to collision or interference) results in zero reward or a noisy reward.

In practice, indexing devices and associating them with channels is feasible. Regulatory frameworks require unlicensed devices to query a white space database to obtain access credentials and operational parameters. This supports our assumption that players and arms are indexable.

RF Backscatter Communication for Passive IoT Devices

Another example is backscatter communication in passive IoT networks. In this setting, batteryless devices transmit data by backscattering ambient RF signals emitted by a reader or other infrastructure. This energy-efficient technique is commonly used in ultra-low-power IoT deployments.

However, when too many devices backscatter simultaneously, their reflected signals interfere destructively. In such cases, the combined signal strength can fall below the receiver’s noise floor, rendering all transmissions undecodable . Devices receive no information about the presence of other transmitters and only observe whether their own message was successfully received. This maps directly to our MMAB-SAX model, where each RF source is represented as an arm with unknown capacity and no sensing feedback.

This framework is therefore applicable to a wide range of decentralized resource allocation problems in IoT systems, especially those involving shared access to limited-capacity resources and minimal feedback.

E.2
Indexing Assumption

Instead of assuming that players know their own index and the total number of players at the start of the game,
Wang et al. 2022a
leverage
partial SDA/SDI feedback
to allow players to infer this information dynamically. In particular,
SDI
provides the exact number of players pulling the same arm, while
SDA
gives a 1-bit signal indicating whether sharing occurred on an arm.

Their algorithm begins with all players pulling a designated arm (e.g., arm 1) at the first time step. Based on the observed SDI feedback, players can directly determine the total number of players
M
. Next, to determine their own index, the algorithm enters an
orthogonalization phase
under SDA feedback. Each player initializes with a temporary rank of
M+1
and repeatedly selects a candidate rank
a_{p}\in[M]
at the beginning of each time block of length
M+1
. They pull arm
a_{p}
at the start of the block:

•

If they observe SDA = 0, they commit to rank
a_{p}
.

•

Otherwise, they retain the temporary rank of
M+1
and try again in the next block.

Once a player has a tentative rank
a_{p}
, they continue pulling arm
a_{p}
throughout each block, except at time slot
a_{p}+1
, where they briefly switch to arm
M+1
to test if any other player has not been assigned to a rank yet. This process continues until a full block passes without any observed sharing, confirming that all players have settled into unique ranks.

In the case of SDA feedback only, players can first determine an orthogonal arm assignment using a similar scheme, with the block length and initial rank range extended to
K
instead of
M+1
. Then, using a technique similar to SIC-MMAB
[
2
]
, they can infer the total number of players.

This approach demonstrates that the assumption of pre-configured player indices and knowledge of
M
can be relaxed and replaced with limited feedback models such as SDA or SDI. Even though the indexing assumption is plausible in some real word application, we hope future research can go further and relax the indexing assumption even
without
relying on such sensing feedback.
