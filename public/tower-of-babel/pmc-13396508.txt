High encoding-sensitivity vision sensor with complementary nonlinear neuromorphic computing
Authors: Quan Yang, Chuanqing Wang, Ziyang Shen, Yu Kang, Cheng Zhang, Yuanyuan Zhou, Zheng Bian, Xiangwei Su, Chunlian Qin, Jiabao Sun, Yanhua Liu, Bowen Zhu, Zhuangjun Fan, Mohamad Sawan, Yang Xu, Bin Yu, Yang Chai, Yuda Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBuilding a neuromorphic vision sensor capable of signal processing and spike generation is essential for developing hardware tailored to brain-inspired spiking neural networks. A critical challenge, however, is the constrained adaptive sensitivity when dealing with expanding ranges of light intensity. Here we show a neuromorphic vision sensor built on a one-transistor-one-memristor pixel structure, attaining high encoding-sensitivity over a broad intensity range by fusing complementary superlinear and sublinear encoding. Specifically, the superlinear intensity-to-spike firing is based on the plasmonic volatile Ag/hBN/Au memristor, which has the high time-to-first-spike (TTFS)- and rate- encoding sensitivity in high-brightness. And the sublinear firing behavior is based on the MoS2 synaptic photodetector and volatile Ag/hBN/Au memristor neuron, which has the high TTFS- and rate- encoding sensitivity in dim light. When deployed in polar environments (intense brightness to darkness), the complementary vision sensor achieves high-quality imaging, segmenting ice/land regions and predicting thickness—showcasing its robustness under challenging conditions.Subject terms: Photonic devices, Nanophotonics and plasmonics

Introduction

In the human visual system (HVS), the optical information is converted into trains of action potentials (spikes), and the communication between neurons is done by broadcasting these spike trains to downstream neurons1–5. These individual spikes are generated and processed in an efficient manner, containing high information content6–9. Spiking neural network (SNN), being bio-inspired computing paradigm, uses the idealized spike generation mechanisms and displays high energy efficiency and fast inference10–19. Inspired by the retina of human eyes with the ability of sensing, preprocessing, and coding, the neuromorphic vision sensor for SNN is expected to detect/preprocess the light information and convert the light into the sparse spike trains9,15. Several coding methods have been implemented in SNN vision sensors, including rate coding, time-to-first-spike (TTFS) coding, and rate-time fusion (RTF) coding14,20. However, the integration of preprocessing ability in the SNN vision sensor is imperative, which is still lacking.

Conventional image sensors output real-time electrical signals proportional to the intensity of the input stimuli, without adaptive ability to varying light intensity due to constant sensitivity (Fig. 1a, b)21–30. Neuromorphic vision sensors are expected to preprocess visual information, displaying high adaptability and robustness to the complex lighting environment31,32. The efficient strategy from the sensor node is to build a nonlinear relationship between the stimulus intensity and the output photocurrent. Specifically, a superlinear relationship can be used to implement noise suppression and object selectivity33–36. The low-intensity noise signals are suppressed, and high-intensity feature signals are enhanced. And a sublinear relationship can be used to implement a low-intensity optical feature enhancement. The sensitivity is much higher at low intensity and dramatically decreases at high intensity. In a complex lighting environment with high dynamic range, a single type of nonlinear relationship is unable to realize feature enhancement due to the limited range for high sensitivity37–40. Overall, the challenges to construct the neuromorphic vision sensor for high-accuracy SNN lie in the integration of sensing, encoding and nonlinear processing, aiming at high sensitivity over expanding ranges of light intensity.

In this article, we present a complementary SNN neuromorphic vision sensor that integrates a superlinear and sublinear RTF-based intensity-to-spike encoding scheme, enabling in-sensor adaption for high-quality imaging in complex lighting environments. The complementary SNN vision sensor employs a one-transistor-one-memristor (1T1R) pixel architecture, functioning as a visual sensory neuron. Specifically, the superlinear intensity-to-spike firing is based on the plasmonic volatile Ag/hBN/Au memristor in 1T1R pixel, which has the high TTFS- and rate-encoding sensitivity in high-brightness being of ~0.1 ms cm2 mW−1 and ~0.45 mHz cm2 mW−1, respectively. And the sublinear firing behavior is based on the MoS2 synaptic photodetector and volatile Ag/hBN/Au memristor neuron, which has the high TTFS- and rate-encoding sensitivity in dim light, being over ~0.2 ms cm2 mW−1 and ~0.44 mHz cm2 mW−1, respectively. By implementing a complementary working mode of a superlinear and sublinear neuromorphic vision sensor, the neuromorphic vision sensor displays high encoding sensitivity over varying light intensity. The complementary vision sensor has been used in polar exploration with high recognition accuracy for determining sea ice thickness. This neuromorphic vision sensor is compatible with SNN computing, resulting in the improvement of robustness, energy efficiency, and accuracy in vision information processing.

Results

Complementary vision sensor for spiking neural network

We design a neuromorphic vision sensor that integrates the HVS-inspired function of optical sensing and spike generation (Supplementary Fig. 1), following the complementary nonlinear relationship. The sensor pixel is composed of an optoelectronic transistor and an optoelectronic volatile memristor (Fig. 1c), namely a 1T1R pixel unit. The optical sensing can be implemented by the transistor or the memristor, while the spike generation is realized by the memristor. To fulfill the requirement of a complementary nonlinear relationship between optical intensity input and the spike firing, the optoelectronic transistor and the optoelectronic memristor should display the opposite behavior, e.g., the sublinear relation in the optoelectronic transistor and the superlinear relation in the optoelectronic memristor. In the superlinear working mode, the optical input is irradiated on the memristor, and the transistor works as the series resistor. The optoelectronic volatile memristor can detect the light intensity and convert the optical input into spikes based on the superlinear relation (Fig. 1d). In the sublinear working mode, the transistor acts as the synaptic photodetector irradiated by the optical input, and the memristor works as the spiking neuron. The pixel enables the sublinear relation between the optical input and the spike output (Fig. 1d).

Current neuromorphic photodetectors typically demonstrate only one form of nonlinear response, either superlinear or sublinear, displaying a high-sensitivity region and a low-sensitivity region. This phenomenon results in low imaging quality due to a limited dynamic range (unable to preserve high sensitivity over a wide range of light intensity). And the modification to the low-sensitivity region in the nonlinear relationship can slightly extend the effective dynamic range. However, it is still challenging to capture a high-quality image in a complex lighting environment (coexistence of dim light and high brightness, exemplified by entering and exiting the tunnel shown in Fig. 1b). Furthermore, the reported nonlinear photodetectors display diverse mechanisms and require designated modulation via external stimuli, which hinders the fusion of superlinear and sublinear responses. Our work proposes a complementary neuromorphic vision sensor, which can merge the superlinear and sublinear response, greatly extending the high encoding-sensitivity region (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, the photoresponse output of reported photodetectors is mostly in the form of continuous photocurrent, inevitably resulting in high energy consumption. In this work, the complementary neuromorphic 1T1R-based vision sensor has been designed with intensity-dependent spike firing, displaying high encoding sensitivity over a wide intensity range and obtaining high energy efficiency. In summary, this complementary computational neuromorphic vision sensor is promising to tackle the challenge in complex lighting environments for accurate image recognition.

Superlinear working mode

In the 1T1R pixel unit, we adopt the MoS2 transistor and hBN memristor. The optical image of the 1T1R pixel is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. In the superlinear mode, the hBN memristor plays the crucial role in the conversion of analog optical input into sparse electrical spikes, and the MoS2 transistor only works as the in-series load resistor. We will systematically discuss the characteristics of the MoS2 transistor in the following sublinear mode. The hBN memristor has a stacked structure of indium tin oxide (ITO)/Ag/hBN/Au. The top electrode is composed of transparent ITO and plasmonic Ag ultrathin film (Fig. 2a). The bottom electrode is inert Au, and the resistive switching layer is hBN. The memristor displays the crossbar structure with an active device area of 3 × 3 μm2 (Supplementary Fig. 3). The cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image with energy-dispersive spectroscopy mapping (Fig. 2b) and the atomic force microscopy (AFM) image (Supplementary Fig. 4) confirm the thickness of ITO, Ag, hBN, and Au as ~15, ~5, ~6, and ~20 nm, respectively. The TEM image also shows the high-quality Ag/hBN and the hBN/Au interface. The Ag layer is specially designed with a thin thickness (5 nm) and a post-annealing process to form discontinuous, island-like Ag nanoparticles, which can effectively exhibit the plasmonic effect (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Note 1). The plasmonic hBN memristor is optically sensitive and volatile, showing the potential to emulate the optoelectronic spiking neuron.

We first characterize the electrical properties of plasmonic hBN memristor under dark. During the forming process, the compliance current is limited to ~100 nA to form the weak and fragile filament. In the following set-reset cycles, the I–V curves show the typical volatile behavior (Fig. 2c, black line). When the applied voltage surpasses the threshold voltage (VTH, ~0.85 V), the memristor exhibits a transition from a high resistance state (HRS) to a low resistance state (LRS). When the applied voltage is smaller than the holding voltage (VHL) or is withdrawn, the memristor returns to its HRS, unable to maintain in the LRS. This phenomenon can be explained by the growth and spontaneous rupture of the Ag conductive filament. We statistically analyze ~230 I–V curves collected in nine devices (Supplementary Fig. 6), and quantified the cycle-to-cycle (C2C) and device-to-device (D2D) variability of VTH. The excellent C2C uniformity was evidenced by a low variation coefficient of 5%. The D2D mean (µ) and standard deviation (σ) of VTH from these devices have been obtained with the value of 0.62 and 0.13 V, respectively. Our device demonstrates a significant improvement in C2C variation and achieves a comparable D2D variation relative to reported works (Supplementary Table 1).

We further study the optoelectronic properties of the plasmonic hBN memristor. As shown in Fig. 2c, the light irradiation can increase both VTH and VHL, weakening the LRS holding ability. Increasing the light intensity from ~488 to ~1765 mW cm−2 can gradually enlarge the VTH from ~1.5 to ~2.8 V and the VHL from ~0.33 to ~0.66 V. These results demonstrate that light irradiation leads to the inhibition of filament growth and the promotion of filament rupture. Under laser irradiation of ~488 mW cm−2, the collected >390 optoelectronic I–V curves from nine devices have been statistically analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 7), and quantified the C2C and the D2D variability. The D2D µ and σ of VTH from these devices have been obtained with the value of 1.55 and 0.28 V, respectively. These results conclusively demonstrate that the plasmonic memristor shows the 1 V higher VTH under laser irradiation of ~488 mW cm−2 than that under dark, displaying the optoelectronic response behavior. Our plasmonic optoelectronic memristor exhibits narrowband operation with a responsive range from 400 to 532 nm (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Although the cyclic I–V sweep curves demonstrate the optoelectronic characteristics of the memristor, the LRS and HRS conductance are difficult to read. To verify the dependence of memristor conductance on light intensity, we perform over 300 cycles of pulsed voltage stress test under varied light intensities. One cycle of pulse voltage stress contains a small voltage pulse (amplitude 0.1 V, <VHL, width 1 s, interval 1 s) to read the HRS and a large voltage pulse (amplitude 3 V, >VTH, width 1 s, interval 1 s) to read the LRS. Figure 2d shows that the LRS conductance decreases from ~1.85 to ~0.25 μS with the increasing light intensity from ~292 to ~1765 mW cm−2. And the HRS conductance almost remains constant at ~2 nS. In the previously reported optoelectronic memristor devices41–45, the increasing light intensity normally decreases the VTH and enlarges the LRS conductance due to the increasing light-generated electrons and the promotion of filament growth, which is opposite to our device characteristics (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Note 2, and Supplementary Table 2). Therefore, the optoelectronic characteristics of our plasmonic memristor device contribute to the successful emulation of a superlinear relation.

The resistive switching in our Ag/hBN/Au device originates from the formation and rupture of Ag conductive filaments under an electric field. We believe that the light-induced VTH increase is due to the plasmonic-induced Ag filament fracture. Supplementary Fig. 5 shows the extinction spectra of the ultrathin Ag film (5 nm thickness) on the transparent quartz substrate with a full width at half maximum ranging from approximately 360–535 nm. The scanning electron microscope and AFM images of Ag ultrathin film demonstrate the discontinuous island-like nanoparticles with a mean diameter of ~25 nm and an average interparticle distance of ~12 nm (Supplementary Fig. 5). Under light illumination, the light-matter interaction from the nanostructured Ag surface leads to the localized surface plasmonic resonances (LSPR). The plasmonic effect of the Ag nanoparticles induces a strong local electric field enhancement, which is dissipated as heat via nonradiative decay processes (Supplementary Fig. 9). This photothermal effect increases the local temperature of the hBN layer. Given that hBN possesses a negative thermal expansion coefficient46,47, the interlayer distance decreases with increasing temperature, physically constricting the ion migration pathway. Consequently, the measured migration barrier (Eₐ) for Ag⁺ increases from 1.4 eV in the dark to 2.2 eV under illumination (Supplementary Fig. 10), leading to an increased VTH and reduced LRS conductance. Overall, light irradiation has the depression effect on the resistive switching of memristor.

To implement the conversion from light intensity to spiking behaviors by the 1T1R pixel unit, the MoS2 transistors is set to high conductance and the plasmonic hBN memristor is irradiated by the light. The electrical pulse with an appropriate voltage amplitude of 1.1 V (width 30 ms, interval 30 ms) is applied on the input terminal with a constant pulse number. And we measure the spike patterns generated by pixel unit as a function of light intensity, as shown in Fig. 2f. TTFS and rate of spikes have been summarized in Fig. 2g, h, respectively. We can observe that when the light intensity increases from 2.02 to 1836 mW cm−2, TTFS increases from 0.165 to 2.33 s, and frequency monotonically decreases from 10.6 to 1.832 Hz. The light intensity-dependent spike encoding result is consistent with the RTF coding method. And we use the exponential function to perform the curve fitting, with R2 being 0.993 and 0.999, respectively. The fitting curve displays the superlinear relation between light intensity and spiking behavior.

The delayed spiking behavior observed under light illumination (Fig. 2f) can be understood through the intrinsic accumulation dynamics of the Ag-filament-based volatile memristor, analogous to the integrate-and-fire mechanism in biological neurons. In the dark, when the applied voltage pulse amplitude is slightly below the threshold (e.g., 0.6 V), the device exhibits pulse number-dependent firing (Supplementary Fig. 11), where Ag⁺ gradually accumulates over multiple pulses until a conductive filament forms. This accumulative effect arises from the competition between field-driven Ag⁺ drift (integration) and concentration-driven diffusion (leakage). Under light illumination, the plasmonic photothermal effect increases the Eₐ of Ag⁺, effectively raising the threshold voltage. Consequently, a fixed super-threshold voltage pulse train (1.1 V) that would normally trigger firing in the dark now requires multiple pulses to accumulate sufficient Ag⁺, resulting in the observed light intensity-dependent delay in spike generation. Furthermore, we note that the integrate-and-fire behavior is facilitated by the parasitic capacitance (Cp) of the Ag/hBN/Au memristor, measured to be approximately 20–23 μF cm−2 in the 100–1000 Hz range (Supplementary Fig. 12). This capacitance mimics the membrane capacitance in a biological neuron, enabling current integration without the need for an external capacitor. Thus, the combination of Ag⁺ dynamics and intrinsic parasitic capacitance provides a complete circuit-level explanation for the light-modulated delayed spiking behavior in our plasmonic optoelectronic neuron. It is worth noting that a refractory period in conventional leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons arises from slow discharge48,49 (Supplementary Fig. 13). In our resistor-memristor configuration, the small parasitic capacitance shortens the discharging time, and we deliberately eliminate it to prioritize stable encoding of light intensity into spikes, resulting in one spike per voltage pulse after the first integration.

In traditional image sensors, the sensitivity is an important indicator to evaluate its performance of input–output signal efficiency relative to the dark current noise50–54. In our SNN vision sensors, sensitivity is used to evaluate the device performance of converting incident light into output spikes, which has not been defined (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Note 3). We define the TTFS- and rate- based encoding sensitivity as ST = dt/dI and SF = df/dI, respectively, where I is the light intensity, t and f are TTFS and rate-related light-induced spike firing parameters, respectively. In the definition of encoding sensitivity, dt (df) is the difference of tn (fn) and tn + 1 (fn + 1) between the adjacent light intensity In and In + 1, namely dt = (tn + 1 − tn) and df = (fn + 1 − fn). This encoding sensitivity indicates the electrical output magnitude generated by a unit optical power intensity input. Here, eliminating dark noise was implemented by obtaining dt=(tn+1light−tn+1dark)−(tnlight−tndark) and df=(fn+1light−fn+1dark)−(fnlight−fndark), which was complemented through experimentally applying large voltage to guarantee the definite firing of vision sensors under dark tn+1dark=tndark, fn+1dark=fndark. As shown in Fig. 2i, j, the high TTFS- and rate-sensitivity of ~0.1 ms cm2 mW−1 and ~0.45 mHz cm2 mW−1, have been obtained in the light intensity over 500 mW cm−2. We further demonstrate the imaging ability of our vision sensor by capturing the pattern of 0 under complex background illumination (Fig. 2k). The bottom half of the pattern in the bright background illumination can be accurately captured by the superlinear mode, and the contrast has been further enhanced (Fig. 2l, m).

The superlinear relationship between light intensity and spike encoding in our plasmonic optoelectronic memristor originates from the nonlinear photothermal effect mediated by LSPR. According to the classical electromagnetic enhancement mechanism55,56, the photothermal heat exhibits a superlinear dependence on the incident light intensity57. This nonlinear photoheating modulates the Ea of Ag⁺ —weakly at low intensities and strongly at high intensities—resulting in the observed superlinear intensity-to-spike encoding behavior. Detailed mechanism discussion can be found in (Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Note 4). While the superlinear response of our plasmonic memristor originates from the LSPR of Ag nanoparticles with a narrow characteristic wavelength range, this spectral response can be effectively tuned through control of film thickness and annealing conditions. Moreover, by integrating multiple sub-pixels with tailored LSPR responses, a pixelated architecture can achieve broadband spectral coverage without external filters, enabling practical application in everyday environments.

Sublinear working mode

In the sublinear mode, the MoS2 transistor in the 1T1R pixel unit is under the laser illumination with varying light intensity, acting as the optoelectronic synapse, and the memristor plays the role as the neurons to generate spike firing. The structure of the MoS2 optoelectronic synaptic transistor is illustrated in Fig. 3a, with the mechanically exfoliated 5-nm-thick MoS2 flakes as the channel (shown in AFM image Supplementary Fig. 15) and trap-state-rich SiO2 as the gate dielectric. Figure 3b depicts the transfer curves of the optoelectronic MoS2 transistor under 532 nm light illumination at varying light intensity, ranging from ~1.8 to ~728 mW cm−2. It is illustrated that significantly increased channel conductance from 0.08 to 0.9 μS (Vgs = −25 V) is due to the strong absorption of visible light by MoS2. With the intrinsic trap-state SiO2 to capture charge carriers, the MoS2 transistor can be feasibly applied as an optoelectronic synapse to memory the light-intensity-induced channel conductance excitatory. As shown in Fig. 3c, the device is illuminated under laser pulses (fixed numbers of 20) with varying light intensity from ~2 to ~140 mW cm−2, and the voltage bias (Vds = 1 V and Vgs = 0 V) has been applied to read the change in the drain current (Ids). Ids gradually increase and reach ≈13 μA under laser intensity of ~140 mW cm−2 due to the trapping of photogenerated carriers at the MoS2/SiO2 interface. Then Ids decays slowly over time when light input is ceased due to the gradual detrapping of photogenerated carriers, namely the persistent photoconductivity (PPC) effect. And Ids can decrease to the initial state of 10 nA (~107 Ω) after ~5 min. Both the increase and decay of the channel conductance depend on the light intensity. The higher light intensity generates a great number of charge carriers, causing the device to exhibit a larger photocurrent during or after laser irradiation. In addition to the light/gate excitatory modulation on channel conductance, the negative electrical gate voltage has an inhibitory effect on the channel conductance of the MoS2 phototransistor (shown in Supplementary Fig. 16), which can be used to reset the channel conductance to the initial state for a new cycle of spike firing.

Next, the sublinear coding of light intensity into an electrical spike is implemented. The light is illuminated on the MoS2 phototransistor. The varying light intensity (with the constant voltage bias Vds = 1 V and Vgs = 0 V) has been used to increase the channel conductance of the MoS2 phototransistor, reaching Ids value of tens of μA. The transfer curves under laser illumination collected from the other nine MoS2 phototransistor devices (Supplementary Fig. 17) demonstrate that the high channel conductance can be easily obtained with the high current value in the order of tens of μA. After light removal, the decay of MoS2 channel conductance can be kept in order of 0.1 μA (~106 Ω) for ~2 min because of the PPC effect. In this case, the MoS2 channel resistance is between the resistance of HRS and LRS in the memristor (Supplementary Fig. 18). The dynamic decay behavior of the MoS2 channel is determined by the light intensity, which can alter spike firing behavior of the memristor neuron based on the LIF model. After the removal of the light exposure on the phototransistor, the electrical voltage pulse (amplitude 1.2 V, width 30 ms, interval 30 ms) is applied to the drain electrode of the phototransistor and the memristor bottom electrode is grounded to enable current readout as spike output. During this short readout period, the conductance decay of the MoS2 phototransistor is negligible (Supplementary Fig. 19). After each spike encoding test, applying the negative gate voltage can decrease the conductance to the initial state, which can avoid the disturbing from previous tests (Supplementary Fig. 20).

We measured the spike patterns generated by the spiking neuron as a function of light intensity. Figure 3d shows the sublinear spiking behavior of an artificial visual neuron in response to varying light intensity. An exponential function has been used to perform the TTFS- and rate-curve fitting of light intensity dependent spiking behavior with good fitting results of R2 0.998 and 0.998, respectively. TTFS decreases from 2.475 to 0.78 s, and spiking frequency increases monotonically from 1.099 to 7.778 Hz as the varying light intensity increases from 0.0054 to 745 mW cm−2 (Fig. 3e, f). In the low light intensity range, the TTFS and rate-based photoconductance show a rapid decrease, and the spike saturation happens under high light intensity. For obtaining encoding sensitivity in low light intensity, eliminating dark noise was also implemented by obtaining dt=(tn+1light−tn+1dark)−(tnlight−tndark) and df=(fn+1light−fn+1dark)−(fnlight−fndark). In this case, appropriate voltage was experimentally applied to guarantee no firing under dark (tn+1dark=tndark=0, fn+1dark=fndark=0). As shown in Fig. 3g, h, the high TTFS- and rate-sensitivity being over ~0.2 ms cm2 mW−1 and ~0.44 mHz cm2 mW−1 have been obtained in the low light intensity region (smaller than 500 mW cm−2). We further demonstrate the imaging ability of our vision sensor by capturing the pattern of 0 under complex background illumination (Fig. 3i). The top half of the pattern in the dim background illumination can be accurately captured by the sublinear mode, and the contrast has been further enhanced (Fig. 3j, k).

This sublinear light-intensity-induced spiking behavior is mainly attributed to light-induced channel conductance saturation under high intensity in the MoS2 phototransistor. Under light illumination in the low-intensity range, MoS2 served as the photoactive layer, and the MoS2 channel conductance is highly dependent on the light intensity, leading to the high device sensitivity. Under light illumination in the high-intensity range, the photocurrent quickly becomes saturated, and the MoS2 channel conductance is less dependent on the light intensity, deteriorating the device sensitivity. This is the reason for sublinear light-dependent firing behaviors.

Benefiting from the complementary integration of sublinear and superlinear encoding modes, our neuromorphic vision sensor achieves a broad dynamic range of approximately 111 dB, spanning from 0.0054 to 1836 mW cm−2. This dual-mode operation enables high-sensitivity spike-based detection across both dim and bright light conditions—a distinct advantage over previously reported devices that typically offer only single-mode nonlinear response (Supplementary Table 4). Moreover, the spike output in the RTF coding scheme facilitates direct integration with neuromorphic computing architectures. The energy consumption per spike of our 1T1R pixel is calculated to be approximately 3.2 pJ for driving (charging/discharging) and 2.8 nJ for firing. Compared with other memristor-based neurons (e.g., Mott memristors), our Ag-filament-based device achieves significantly lower driving energy while maintaining competitive firing energy, highlighting its potential for low-power neuromorphic vision systems. Detailed discussion can be found in (Supplementary Fig. 21, Supplementary Tables 5, 6, and Supplementary Note 5).

In practical operation for high parallelism and high speed, each pixel comprises two 1T1R sub-pixels, providing superlinear and sublinear responses, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 22). Within an exposure cycle, the spike encoding process of both sub-pixels occurs in parallel. In the superlinear sub-pixel, the memristor is illuminated, and the pulsed voltage is applied to encode light information into spike output. In the sublinear sub-pixel, the transistor is illuminated to set its conductance. After removing the light, voltage pulses are applied to encode light information into the spike output. While C2C variability is relatively small (Supplementary Fig. 23), D2D variation in threshold voltage leads to moderate differences in spike timing and rate (Supplementary Fig. 24). Therefore, for array-level imaging applications, a one-time calibration using measured VTH values is recommended to ensure consistent encoding across devices.

Complementary vision sensor for polar exploration

Polar research vessels need to distinguish between sea ice and land, as well as sea ice thickness classification (which indicates relative thickness categories), to ensure safe navigation. However, in polar research environments, ice surfaces reflect sunlight during the day with high light intensity, while seawater appears dim at night with low light intensity. This wide range of light intensity easily causes overexposure or underexposure. Such complex scenarios pose challenges for existing neuromorphic sensors, which cannot effectively capture scene information, leading to catastrophic consequences. By offering high encoding sensitivity across both dim and bright light conditions, the complementary vision sensor proposed in this paper provides a near-field visual complement to multi-sensor navigation systems for polar exploration (Supplementary Note 6).

The proposed complementary coding scheme integrates two distinct phototransduction modalities—superlinear and sublinear—to encode the scene information into different spike sequences (Fig. 4a). Each modality is implemented using the RTF coding method, which encodes light intensity into spike trains by modulating firing TTFS and rate. In the superlinear modality, TTFS and rate coding exhibit high sensitivity across bright conditions, enabling detailed feature capture in high-intensity regions. The sublinear modality demonstrates enhanced responsiveness within dim environments, facilitating robust representation of low-intensity scene characteristics. This complementary coding approach can achieve full-intensity and all-weather sea ice scene information recording, providing high-quality encoded information for subsequent SNN processing tasks.

Based on the aforementioned complementary coding scheme, we propose a complementary processing framework composed of two key components: (1) two identical SNN models that extract corresponding scene features from superlinear or sublinear spike coding, and (2) a complementary network designed to integrate features extracted from the two models. Each SNN model adopts a lightweight spiking U-Net architecture, where the backbone network consists of two downsampling layers, two upsampling layers, and a residual block containing two convolutional layers (Supplementary Fig. 25). To further enhance the model’s feature extraction capability and compensate the representational limitations of 1-bit spike outputs from LIF neurons, we designed a parallel processing module and introduced membrane potential-assisted LIF (MP-LIF) neurons, enabling the model to retain membrane potential dynamics. By fusing high-precision membrane potential information with 1-bit spike features, the model’s feature extraction capability is significantly enhanced. The complementary network aggregates high-level features from both superlinear and sublinear SNN branches, adaptively combining complementary information from both branches through a weighted complementary strategy, ensuring optimal feature representation under varying illumination conditions. This complementary processing framework effectively handles prediction tasks, delivering precise sea ice and land segmentation with accurate sea ice coverage and thickness classification (Fig. 4b).

The complementary coding scheme achieves optimal prediction performance, correctly segmenting land and sea ice regions and classifying ice thickness. As shown in Fig. 4c, two sea ice regions are correctly classified as 70–90% ice and <10% ice. In comparison with individual superlinear or sublinear coding schemes, land regions are not correctly segmented and contain portions of fast ice areas. Additionally, for the sublinear coding scheme, sea ice is also incorrectly classified, where regions that should be 70–90% ice are misclassified as fast ice.

As shown in Fig. 4d, we employ two metrics, mean squared error (MSE) and structural similarity index measure (SSIM), to evaluate the task classification performance of different encoding approaches. MSE assesses pixel-wise differences, while SSIM compares overall structural differences between predicted images and ground truth. If MSE reaches 0 and SSIM reaches 1, it indicates a close resemblance between the predicted images and the ground truth. Comparing with the superlinear and sublinear encoding schemes, the complementary coding scheme exhibits lower MSE and higher SSIM, maintaining greater robustness and performance stability (Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27 display the results with Gaussian noise). Furthermore, this work analyzes the percentage distribution of prediction deviations, showing that complementary prediction errors of encoding are more concentrated around zero, demonstrating encoding and prediction performance (Fig. 4e). Therefore, based on the complementary coding capability, our neuromorphic vision sensors can compensate for the existing technology for the sea ice region segmentation and classification in polar exploration. A detailed discussion of the advantages and trade-offs of the complementary framework is in Supplementary Note 7.

Discussion

We have demonstrated a high encoding-sensitivity neuromorphic vision sensor, enabling the adaptability across expanding ranges of light intensity. The pixel structure of 1T1R merges plasmonic optoelectronic volatile memristor (superlinear) and synaptic MoS2 phototransistors (sublinear), contributing to the complementary vision sensor. The sensor converts the optical information into encoding spikes based on the RTF scheme, suitable for brain-inspired SNN computing. In a superlinear intensity-to-spike encoding scheme, the dramatic increase of light intensity contributes to the increased temperature and the enhanced rupture of conductive filament in plasmonic optoelectronic volatile memristor, obtaining high TTFS- and rate- encoding sensitivity in high-brightness being of ~0.1 ms cm2 mW−1 and ~0.45 mHz cm2 mW−1, respectively. In the sublinear intensity-to-spike encoding scheme, MoS2 channel conductance increases sharply in low intensity, exhibiting the high TTFS- and rate-encoding sensitivity in dim light, being of ~0.2 ms cm2 mW−1 and ~0.44 mHz cm2 mW−1, respectively. The application of the complementary working mode in polar exploration displays the high adaptability for dim-bright-coexisting varying-light-intensity environment and achieves high prediction accuracy for precise sea ice and land segmentation with ice thickness classification. Our complementary neuromorphic vision sensor shows the potential to realize low power consumption and high energy efficiency in a complex environment.

Methods

Materials

Bulk carbon-doped hBN crystals were grown at atmospheric pressure and high temperature using a metal alloy solvent. The hBN powder was mixed with the Ni–Cr alloy in a cylindrical crucible. The carbon powder was added to tune the vacancy concentration. The weight ratio of the alloy, hBN powder and carbon powder is 100:10:0.6.

Device fabrication and characterization

The hBN flakes were mechanically exfoliated by the scotch-tape method, transferred to polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and then transferred to the Au bottom electrode. The thickness of hBN flakes was monitored by an optical microscope and confirmed by AFM. Ag/hBN/Au memristor were fabricated by direct-write lithography. Au (20 nm)/Ti (5 nm) bottom electrode on the Si/SiO2 substrate, and Ag (5 nm)/ITO (15 nm) top electrodes, by sputtering evaporation at a base pressure of ~5 × 10−6 Torr and a deposition rate of 0.35 nm s−1 for Ag. The lift-off process was carried out in acetone. MoS2 is transferred onto a p++-Si substrate with 285 nm SiO2 as the channel, followed by the deposition of Au as the source and drain electrodes. All the devices were electrically characterized in an integrated photoelectric test platform, including a probe station, continuous adjustable laser source, optical power meter, and Keithley 2600. To avoid optical interference in the 1T1R pixel, the transistor and memristor are physically positioned with a sufficiently large separation distance on the test chip. In an exposure cycle using a 1T1R pixel, the operation would follow a sequential two-step process. In the superlinear mode sampling, the memristor is illuminated and pulsed simultaneously to encode light information into spike output. In the sublinear mode sampling, the transistor is illuminated (while the memristor is in the dark) to set its conductance, then read out with voltage pulses to encode light information. These two steps occur sequentially within one imaging frame.

Complementary spiking neural network for polar exploration

This study utilizes data from a publicly available dataset containing sea ice distribution and thickness information. The dataset comprises 3392 satellite images of the Hudson Bay region in the Canadian Arctic captured by Sentinel-2 satellites, along with their corresponding sea ice concentration masks. This study proposes an RTF neural coding scheme that employs a complementary coding strategy to encode satellite images. Specifically, the scheme utilizes two different encoding approaches—superlinear and sublinear coding—to encode images into spike trains, focusing on feature extraction from high-intensity and low-intensity regions, respectively. During the encoding process, each pixel in the image is encoded based on light intensity values into spike trains characterized by firing rate and first spike timing. To reflect realistic sensor characteristics, we incorporate Gaussian noise (±1 time-step perturbation) into this encoding stage to simulate the coding fluctuations inherent in proposed neuromorphic devices. The noise level is comparable with the measured coding fluctuations (Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29). For each RGB channel in the image, the RTF scheme encodes the channels individually and subsequently fuses them into 3-dimensional spike trains. The spike train length adopts 32 discrete time steps and serves as the final input for training the model.

The SNN model in the dual path SNN processing framework employs a spiking U-Net backbone enhanced with residual connections and LIF neuron connections, and a parallel processing module connected with membrane potential-assisted LIF (MP-LIF) neurons. The backbone adopts a lightweight structure consisting of two downsampling layers, two upsampling layers, and a residual block with two convolutional layers. The downsampling layers have input channel sizes of 128 and 512, while the upsampling layers have 1024 and 256 channels due to concatenation operations. Each convolutional layer uses a 3 × 3 kernel for feature extraction and a stride of 2 × 2 for pooling. LIF neurons are responsible for generating and propagating spike signals. To address the issue of gradient explosion in training SNN, we adopt the L1 loss function, which effectively stabilizes the training process. Additionally, a step-based learning rate scheduler is employed, with updates every 25 epochs to facilitate convergence towards an optimal solution. Group normalization is applied for all convolutional layers. The choice of group normalization over batch normalization is motivated by the following considerations: the high temporal resolution characteristics of SNN training lead to enormous GPU memory consumption, necessitating the use of smaller batch sizes during training. Under small batch conditions, batch normalization becomes ineffective as it cannot obtain sufficiently large samples to compute stable statistics. In contrast, group normalization operates by normalizing within channel groups, with operations independent of the batch dimension, thereby effectively maintaining model performance and training stability in small batch training environments.
