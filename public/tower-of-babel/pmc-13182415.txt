Trend and pattern of antenatal care utilisation and neonatal mortality in Ghana: analyses 2003–2022 demographic and health survey
Authors: Johnson Sam, Kwamena Sekyi Dickson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal mortality remains a significant public health issue in most of the developing countries including Ghana. This study examines the trends and patterns of antenatal care (ANC) utilisation and neonatal mortality in Ghana using the Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS) from 2003 to 2022. It also appraises the association between ANC visits and neonatal mortality while acknowledging key elements impelling the mortality rates over timeDesignThe Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) employed a cross-sectional study using nationally representative survey data.ParticipantsWomen in Ghana aged 15–49 years who had a live birth within 5 years preceding the 2003, 2008, 2014 and 2022 GDHS. The pooled weighted sample included 14 436 most recent live births.Measure of response variableNeonatal mortality, defined as death within the first 28 days of life. The primary exposure was ANC utilisation, measured by the number of visits and the timing of the first visit. Survey-weighted logistic regression and Joinpoint regression were used to assess trends and associations.ResultsNeonatal mortality declined significantly over the study period. Compared with 2003, the odds of neonatal death were significantly lower in 2014 (OR 0.59; 95% CI 0.43 to 0.81) and 2022 (OR 0.49; 95% CI 0.35 to 0.67). Joinpoint regression showed a significant average annual percentage decline of 3.7% (p<0.05). ANC utilisation improved substantially, with the proportion of women attending eight or more visits increasing from 22.8% in 2003 to 38.8% in 2022, and first-trimester initiation rising from 46.3% to 63.7% over the same period. Neonatal mortality was generally lower among women with higher numbers of ANC visits, although patterns were not strictly linear across all survey years.ConclusionNeonatal mortality in Ghana declined significantly between 2003 and 2022, coinciding with improvements in ANC utilisation. However, fluctuations in mortality across ANC categories suggest that increased coverage alone may not be sufficient; quality and timeliness of care remain critical. Strengthening comprehensive ANC services and ensuring equitable access may further accelerate progress towards Sustainable Development Goal target 3.2.

Background

Neonatal mortality (NM), defined as the death of infants within the first 28 days of life, remains a major global health challenge. Every day, approximately 7000 newborns die worldwide, amounting to 2.5 million deaths annually.1 This statistic underscores the critical need for effective intervention strategies, especially in low-resource settings. NM represents nearly half of all under-five mortality, indicating that it is a pivotal area of focus for global health policies.2 Alarmingly, 80% of neonatal deaths are attributed to preventable causes, emphasising the importance of timely and quality maternal and neonatal care.1 The statistics are particularly concerning for countries in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where neonatal mortality rates (NMR) remain disproportionately high. In SSA, the rates of neonatal deaths have been slow to decline over the past two decades, and the region continues to have the highest NMR globally, making it ten times more vulnerable to newborn mortality than developed nations.1 3 Although global progress has been made in reducing neonatal deaths, the NMR in many Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) has not decreased at the same rate as other child mortality indicators.4 The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2 seeks to reduce NM to 12 deaths per 1000 live births by 2030, a target that emphasises the urgency of addressing these disparities.4

In many LMICs, NM is highly linked to the quality and accessibility of maternal healthcare services, particularly antenatal care (ANC). ANC is widely recognised as a critical intervention in reducing both maternal and NM. Research has demonstrated that the more ANC visits a woman attends, especially if she receives comprehensive care as recommended by the WHO, the lower the risks of neonatal and maternal mortality.5 ANC offers significant benefits by providing medical care that can detect and prevent complications during pregnancy and childbirth.4 6 The provision of timely and quality ANC can prevent approximately 33% of stillbirths and 71% of neonatal deaths.7 Thus, ANC is not just a preventive measure for the mother but plays a direct role in improving neonatal health outcomes. In Ghana, the government has made strides to reduce NM, which remains a major contributor to overall childhood mortality. Ghana’s commitment to improving maternal and neonatal health includes participation in global initiatives such as the “Newborn Action Plan” and “Every Woman Every Child,” and the implementation of the National Newborn Care Strategy and Action Plan for 2019–2023.8 These initiatives are aimed at enhancing the quality and accessibility of maternal and newborn health services. Despite these efforts, the institutional NM rate in Ghana has seen a concerning rise from 3.8 per 1000 live births in 2014 to 8.4 per 1000 live births in 2017.8 This trend highlights a critical gap between the policy and its on-the-ground impact.

ANC utilisation is also found to be one of the most significant barriers to reducing NM in Ghana and other LMICs. Despite governmental efforts to increase access to maternal healthcare, studies have shown that a significant proportion of women in these regions still do not receive adequate ANC. Factors such as maternal age, education, place of delivery and socio-economic status play a pivotal role in determining ANC utilisation.9 10 Furthermore, research has shown that female neonates tend to have better survival rates than male neonates11, highlighting the complex interplay of biological and socio-economic factors that affect neonatal survival.11 In Ghana, although ANC utilisation has improved, the progress has been uneven across different regions and populations. According to the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS), the NMR was 17 per 1000 live births, down from 29 in 2014, yet it remains significantly higher than the target set by the SDGs.8 This pattern suggests that while access to ANC has increased, its impact on NM may be hindered by other factors such as the quality of care provided and disparities in healthcare access between urban and rural areas.

Problem statement

Over the past decade, while access to maternal and child health services has increased in many low-resource nations, including Ghana, this increase has not always led to improved health outcomes.8 Ghana has made notable commitments to reducing neonatal fatalities, yet the country continues to face significant challenges in addressing NM. For instance, in 2017, Ghana reported 25 neonatal deaths per 1000 live births, and in 2017–2018, this figure slightly rose to 27 per 1000 live births, with approximately 92% of these fatalities occurring during the early neonatal stage.12 This persistent challenge highlights that NM in Ghana remains a critical public health issue.

Additionally, data from the 2003, 2008, 2014, and 2022 GDHS reveal that NM consistently accounts for more than 50% of under-five mortalities in the country. This pattern of high NM is a significant concern, as it emphasises the disproportionate burden of early neonatal deaths within the broader context of child mortality in Ghana. Despite progress in some areas of child health, NM remains stubbornly high, suggesting that the underlying factors contributing to these deaths are still not being effectively addressed. These trends also suggest that while efforts to reduce child mortality in Ghana have made headway, the challenge of NM, particularly in the first days and weeks of life, remains persistent and complex. The data also points to the importance of understanding the factors contributing to these high rates, including the influence of maternal health services such as ANC and other interventions during pregnancy and childbirth. The relationship between ANC and neonatal outcomes, as well as the broader social, economic and healthcare-related factors that influence neonatal survival, continues to be a critical area for further research.

Methods

Ethics statement

Our study used publicly available DHS data and was exempt from ethical review. Ethical approval for the GDHS was obtained by the Ghana Health Service (GHS) Ethical Review Committee and ICF Institutional Review Board and no additional ethical approval was required for this study. Permission to use these datasets was granted by MEASURE DHS following a review of the submitted concept note. The datasets are accessible at: https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm

Setting

The study is based in Ghana, located in the SSA region, covering an area of 238 537 square kilometers. The country shares borders with three French-speaking nations: Burkina Faso to the north, Côte d'Ivoire to the west, and Togo to the east, while the Gulf of Guinea lies to the south. Positioned between 4° and 12° N latitude and 4° W and 2° E longitude, Ghana is intersected by the Greenwich Meridian, which passes through Tema, about 24 km from the capital, Accra. Ghana’s landscape is mainly low-lying, with a hilly range along its eastern border. The highest point is Mountain Afadjato in the Volta Region, standing at 883 meters above sea level. The country can be divided into three ecological zones: a coastal sandy area backed by a coastal plain, a forest region covering the Middle Belt and western areas, and the northern savannah. Ghana is administratively divided into 16 regions and 275 districts. According to the 2021 Population and Housing Census, the population of Ghana is 30,792,608, with an annual growth rate of 2.1%. This results in a population density of around 103 people per square kilometer. The sex ratio is estimated at 97 males for every 100 females (GSS, 2021).

Data source and method

The GDHS was conducted in 2003(dataset),13 2008(dataset),14 2014(dataset),15 and 2022(dataset)16 using the standard DHS model questionnaire developed by the Measure DHS programme.1317 The GDHS is a nationwide survey that covers all sixteen regions of Ghana and is conducted every 5 years. It is carried out by the Ghana Statistical Service (GSS) and the GHS, with technical support from ICF International through MEASURE DHS. The focus of the GDHS is on child and maternal health, providing essential data to monitor the population and health status in Ghana. The survey collects information on a range of demographic and health topics, including fertility, contraceptive use, child health, nutrition, malaria, HIV and AIDS, family planning, health insurance, and maternal health (such as ANC, delivery care, and post-natal care). For this study, women who had a birth history, were currently pregnant, or had given birth within 5 years before the survey and had answered questions on ANC attendance were included. In total, 5218 women participated in the 2022 survey, 4294 women in 2014, 2147 women in 2008, and 2777 women in 2003.131518 Permission to use the dataset was granted by MEASURE DHS after reviewing the concept note.

Sampling procedure

The Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) employs a two-staged stratified sampling design. In the first stage of the design, clusters known as Enumeration Areas (EAs) are selected using probabilities proportional to size. In the second stage of the design is a systematic sampling of households with the selected EAs. All women aged 15–49 years who are either permanent residents or visitors who spend the night in the sampled household were eligible participants. We restricted our analysis to women who had given birth within 5 years preceding each survey and who provided complete information on ANC utilisation and birth outcomes in our study. This study adhered to the STROBE guidelines for reporting cross-sectional studies.

Study variables and measurements

Outcome variable

The primary outcome variable for this study is NM, defined as the death of a live-born infant within the first 28 days of life. NM is a critical indicator of maternal and child health and has been identified as a key target for global health improvement, particularly under SDG 3.2. The study utilises data from the GDHS to assess NM trends and its relationship with ANC utilisation. NM was derived from the GDHS dataset using the “b6” variable, which captures the age at death. Specifically, neonates who died within the first 28 days of life were categorised as neonatal deaths, while those who survived past this period were classified as non-neonatal deaths. The data spans multiple years (2003, 2008, 2014, and 2022), providing a longitudinal perspective on trends in neonatal death.

Explanatory variables

The explanatory variables in this study are selected based on their potential to influence NM, with a focus on understanding the relationship between ANC utilisation and neonatal outcomes. These variables were chosen from existing literature and the available data from the GDHS. The key explanatory variables include:

Antenatal care visits

ANC visits categorised by the number of visits (no visits, 1–3 visits, 4–7 visits, and eight or more visits) and the timing of the first ANC visit (first, second, or third trimester), as the frequency and early initiation of ANC are known to impact neonatal health outcomes.

Tetanus toxoid injections

Tetanus toxoid injections received during pregnancy is also considered, as it is an important preventive measure against neonatal tetanus, which can contribute to NM.

Iron/folic acid supplementation

Iron/folic acid supplementation is examined, as the use of these supplements is crucial for preventing anaemia and related complications that may affect neonatal health.

Blood pressure monitoring

Blood pressure monitoring during pregnancy is another explanatory variable, as maternal hypertension is a known risk factor for both maternal and neonatal complications, and regular monitoring may help mitigate such risks. Finally, the

Place of residence

Place of residence (urban vs rural) is included, recognising that healthcare access and infrastructure often differ between these regions, which may contribute to variations in NMRs. These variables were selected for their potential impact on neonatal survival and provide a comprehensive framework for understanding the social, economic and healthcare-related factors that may influence neonatal outcomes in Ghana.

Data analysis

The main variable of focus in this study was NM, which refers to the probability of death within the first 28 days of life. In the 5 years prior to the survey, NMR was recorded at 20 deaths per 1000 live births, while the under-five mortality rate was 31 deaths per 1000 children. The neonatal death variable was derived from the “b6” variable, which captures the age at death. Children who passed away before reaching 1 month were categorised as neonatal deaths, whereas those who died after the first month were classified as non-neonatal deaths. This study specifically investigated all deaths among children aged 0 to 1 month for women who had their most recent birth within the 5 years preceding the survey. The statistical software STATA version 17 was used to process the data. Some variables were recoded and renamed so that they would be consistent across all the rounds, and all results were weighted. Univariate and bivariate analyses were carried out. The outcome variable (NM) was coded 0=No and 1=Yes. All analyses accounted for the complex survey design using sampling weights. We finally employed a Joinpoint regression analysis using the Joinpoint Regression Programme from the National Cancer Institute to assess temporal trends in NM and to estimate the annual percentage change (APC), identifying statistically significant changes in trend direction over time.

Patient and public involvement

The design, conduct, reporting and dissemination of this manuscript do not directly involve patient and the public as the study relied on secondary data from DHS.

Results

Online supplemental file 1 indicates that most children resided in rural areas across all survey years, although urban residence increased steadily from 2003 to 2022. The majority were from Christian households and belonged to the poorest wealth quintile, with limited improvement in access to health facilities over time. The Akan ethnic group was the most represented, consistent with national demographics. More than half of the children were male in most surveys. Higher birth orders were common in earlier years, though first births increased in 2022. Most births were single and of average size. Mosquito net use improved substantially over time, reflecting strengthened malaria prevention efforts. Maternal characteristics showed gradual improvement, with a shift from teenage motherhood toward ages 20–24 in 2022 and increasing levels of secondary education after 2003. Despite these gains, persistent socioeconomic disparities remain evident.

Table 1 presents various ANC factors identified by the WHO, which were included in the GDHS for analysis. Regarding ANC visits, most women with sampled children across all four GDHS surveys had between 4 and 7 visits. From 2003 to 2022, the majority of women attended their first ANC visit during the second trimester. However, from 2008 to 2022, more than 50% of women had their first visit in the first trimester, with the rates being 55.0%, 64.0% and 63.7%, respectively. A larger proportion of women in the surveyed years received at least two tetanus injections before childbirth. Furthermore, over 70% of women reported taking iron or folic acid tablets prior to delivery. Finally, nearly all women in each survey year had their blood pressure checked during pregnancy.

Trend of neonatal death in Ghana from 2003 to 2022

Table 2 presents the OR reflecting the trend of NM from the 2003 GDHS to the 2022 GDHS. The 2003 GDHS was established as the reference year, with an OR of 1. The 2008 GDHS had an OR of 0.75, suggesting a reduction in odds compared with 2003; however, the CI [0.52, 1.08] included 1, indicating that this result was not statistically significant. The 2014 GDHS showed an OR of 0.59, indicating a significant reduction in odds, supported by a CI of [0.43, 0.81] that did not include 1, marking this result as statistically significant (p<0.05). The 2022 GDHS, with an OR of 0.49 and a CI of [0.35, 0.67], also demonstrated a statistically significant decline in odds, further supporting the downward trend since 2003.

Pattern of neonatal mortality and ANC visit(S) in Ghana from 2003 to 2022

Online supplemental figure illustrates the variation in neonatal death percentages and the number of ANC visits across the four survey years with survey-design adjusted standard errors, showing an irregular pattern. The data revealed a consistent decline in the percentage of neonatal deaths for women with no ANC visits, from 5.3% (SE=0.019) in the 2003 GDHS to 2.5% (SE=0.008) in 2008 and 2.2% (SE=0.006) in 2014, before rising to 4.1% (SE=0.009) in the 2022 GDHS. For women who attended 1–3 ANC visits, there was no change between the 2003 and 2008 GDHS (both at 2.6%, SE=0.03), but a significant decline was observed in the 2014 GDHS (0.8%, SE=0.02), followed by an increase to 2.1% (0.019) in the 2022 GDHS. The percentage of neonatal deaths for women who attended 4–7 ANC visits dropped from 2.7% (SE=0.009) in 2003 to 1.4% (SE=0.005) in the 2022 GDHS. For women with eight or more ANC visits, neonatal mortality decreased from 3.7% (SE=0.016) in 2003 to 1.0% (SE=0.003) in 2022, despite a slight increase between the 2008 and 2014 GDHS. The chi-squared test of the pattern analysis revealed a statistically significant difference in the percentage of neonatal deaths and the number of ANC visits across all four survey points (X² = 8.903, p value = 0.001).

Discussions

The trend and Joinpoint regression analysis in tables2 3 revealed a significant downward trend in the odds of NM from 2003 to 2022, with the most notable reduction occurring between 2014 and 2022. The significant average annual decline of 3.7% in NM represents substantial progress in newborn survival over the two-decade study period. This trend aligns with broader patterns of NM across the continent, where the decline in neonatal deaths has been slower compared with mortality among children aged 1–59 months.19 Research by20 21 reported substantial reductions in under-five mortality globally and in SSA. As a result, the proportion of neonatal deaths among all under-five deaths increased from 40% in 1990 to 47% in 2018. Similarly,3 noted reductions in NMRs globally, though progress was uneven across regions. Both sources emphasised the ongoing challenges in achieving the SDGs, pointing out persistent disparities in child mortality rates. Furthermore, global NMR declined by 51% from 1990 to 2017, from 36.6 deaths per 1000 live births to 18.0 deaths per 1000 live births.3 The significant reductions in NM observed in the 2014 and 2022 GDHS suggest that favourable changes in factors affecting neonatal survival, such as public health interventions, policy changes or socio-economic improvements, may have contributed to this trend.

The pattern of neonatal deaths with no ANC visits from 2003 to 2022 was irregular, showing a steady decline from 5.3% in 2003 to 2.5% and 2.2% in the 2008 and 2014 GDHS, respectively. However, this percentage increased to 4.1% in the 2022 GDHS. The chi-squared test indicated a statistically significant change in the percentage of neonatal deaths and the number of ANC visits across all four survey periods (X² = 8.903, p value=0.001). The progressive narrowing of the standard errors from 2003 to 2022 GDHS, especially for those who had 4–7 and 8 and above ANC visits, indicates increased precision of the estimates over time. This result is in line with other studies on the continent. Oduse et al.22 found that ANC utilisation was strongly associated with reductions in NM rates (NMRs),1 highlighted that effective maternal healthcare before conception, during pregnancy and at childbirth could save 3 million newborns in high-burden countries. Additionally,12 found that ANC visits were linked to reduced NM, offering an opportunity for health workers to educate mothers on recognising warning signs and ensuring safe deliveries.4 suggested that 41% of neonatal deaths in SSA could be prevented with effective ANC utilisation. However, some studies in Eastern Africa, such as1 found no significant relationship between ANC visits and neonatal mortality.

Conclusion

The findings highlighted a meaningful downward trend in NMRs in Ghana from 2003 to 2022, with significant reductions evident in the later years. This trend was consistent with regional and global patterns, although NM continued to be a critical concern, reflecting disparities in health consequences. The results underscored the effectiveness of interventions implemented during this period, contributing to improved neonatal health. The findings also underscored a complex relationship between ANC utilisation and NM in Ghana. Despite general trends showing reduced neonatal deaths with increased ANC visits, the reversal in certain categories highlighted the need for continued focus on ANC services. This trend aligned with global studies emphasising the critical role of maternal healthcare in improving neonatal results.
