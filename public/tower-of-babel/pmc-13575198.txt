DHODH inhibition drives albumin-mediated drug resistance through TERF2IP lactylation-activated macropinosome-mitochondria contact
Authors: Junhu Yuan, Tan Wang, Yarui Ma, Guangyu Li, Chenxi Wang, Mei Liu, Zian Cai, Xiaoyue Wang, Xiaobing Wang, Hongying Wang, Yuchen Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMacropinocytosis enables cancer cells to scavenge extracellular nutrients and contributes to tumor progression, but its role in chemoresistance remains poorly characterized. Through CRISPR-Cas9 and small-molecule screens, we identify inhibition of dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), an enzyme in pyrimidine synthesis, as an inducer of macropinocytosis. Mechanistically, DHODH inhibition triggers metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis and lactate accumulation. This metabolic shift promotes lysine 208 lactylation of telomeric repeat-binding factor 2-interacting protein (TERF2IP), unveiling its moonlighting function in transcriptional activation of epiregulin, which activates EGFR signaling to promote macropinocytosis. Macropinosomes contact mitochondria, enabling albumin translocation into the mitochondrial intermembrane space where it interacts with DHODH, diminishing inhibitor binding and restoring DHODH activity. This adaptive response contributes to drug resistance in vitro and in vivo, and co-administration of DHODH inhibitors with macropinocytosis blockers or EGFR inhibitors enhances anti-tumor efficacy. Our findings reveal a previously unknown metabolic stress-induced macropinocytosis pathway and provide a rationale for combination therapy to enhance DHODH inhibitor efficacy in cancer treatment.Subject terms: Organelles, Cancer screening, Post-translational modifications

Introduction

Macropinocytosis, a form of non-selective endocytosis1,2, is a crucial survival mechanism that enables cancer cells to scavenge extracellular nutrients and maintain growth under nutrient-deprived environments3,4. This process plays a significant role in tumor development and drug resistance5–7. While external factors such as hypoxia8 and nutrient starvation9–12 are established inducers of macropinocytosis in cancer cells, the role of intrinsic cellular factors in activating this process remains largely unexplored, representing a critical gap in our understanding of cancer cell adaptation. Besides, macropinocytosis has also been shown to be a mechanism underlying the adaptation of cancer cells to stress or nutrient-depleted conditions induced by chemotherapy, thus rendering cells chemoresistant13. However, the mechanism by which macropinocytosis contributes to chemoresistance in tumor cells remains unclear.

Cancer cells exhibit hallmark metabolic reprogramming, with enhanced nucleotide synthesis being crucial for their uncontrolled proliferation14,15. The universal dependency on nucleotide metabolism presents a promising therapeutic target across diverse cancer types16,17. Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), a key enzyme in pyrimidine de novo synthesis, has emerged as a particularly attractive target18, particularly in colorectal cancer (CRC) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)19,20. However, DHODH inhibitors such as leflunomide (LEF) and brequinar (BRQ) have shown limited efficacy in clinical trials21–24, suggesting the existence of intrinsic resistance mechanisms and highlighting the need for innovative combination therapeutic strategies.

Protein lactylation, a recently identified post-translational modification at lysine residues, has been implicated in transcriptional regulation and cancer progression25,26. Histone lactylation contributes to gene expression control through epigenetic mechanisms27. Lactylation of non-histone proteins has been implicated in the progression of gastric cancer and the development of chemoresistance26,28,29. However, the role of non-histone protein lactylation in cancer cell adaptation, particularly in macropinocytosis regulation, remains unknown.

In this study, we demonstrate that endogenous pyrimidine deficiency, particularly caused by DHODH inhibition, activates macropinocytosis through telomeric TERF2IP K208 lactylation in cancer cells. This activation contributes to drug resistance through an albumin-dependent mechanism. Importantly, combining DHODH inhibitors with macropinocytosis inhibitors shows synergistic anti-tumor effects both in vivo and in vitro.

Results

CRISPR-based genetic screen and small-molecule screen converge on DHODH as a key regulator of macropinocytosis

To systematically identify regulators of macropinocytosis in cancer cells, we performed a CRISPR-Cas9-based genetic screen covering 19,112 genes and a small-molecule screen with a library of 2648 potential or FDA-approved drugs. Stringent filtering of the primary CRISPR hits (FDR ≤ 0.05) by requiring at least two independent high‑quality sgRNAs with consistent effects yielded a final set of 1667 genetic hits (Fig. 1a–c). In parallel, the drug screen identified 68 compounds that increased macropinocytosis by at least two-fold, 49 of which have well‑annotated protein targets (Fig. 1c–e). Both screening modalities successfully captured established positive regulators of macropinocytosis. In the CRISPR screen, these included mTOR30, LATS1 (whose inactivation promotes YAP/TAZ‑dependent nutrient scavenging via micropinocytosis)31, GLS1/2 (consistent with glutamine deprivation‑induced macropinocytosis), and ULK2 (linked to autophagy inhibition‑induced macropinocytosis). The drug screen likewise identified known inducers such as PAK1 activators, DNA‑damaging agents, Wnt pathway activators32, and autophagy inhibitors that act through NRF2. The convergence of these well‑characterized factors across the two orthogonal platforms strongly validated the sensitivity and specificity of our screening strategy. Pathway analysis of the combined screening hits revealed pyrimidine metabolism as the most significantly enriched pathway, and among the compounds targeting this pathway, DHODH inhibitors emerged as the most potent inducers of macropinocytosis (Fig. 1f, g). Intersecting the 1667 genetic hits with the 49 targeted genes from the drug screen identified a single overlapping gene, dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), the rate‑limiting enzyme in de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis (Fig. 1h, i). These complementary approaches thus pinpointed DHODH as a previously unrecognized key regulator of macropinocytosis and prompted us to investigate how DHODH inhibition controls this cellular process across different cancer models.

DHODH inhibition triggers macropinocytosis activation

To further elucidate the role of DHODH in macropinocytosis regulation, we performed comprehensive analyses using both pharmacological inhibitors and genetic approaches across multiple cancer models. Treatment with DHODH inhibitors leflunomide (LEF) or brequinar (BRQ) significantly activated macropinocytosis, as evidenced by increased TMR-dextran uptake in both tumor-derived xenografts and multiple cancer cell lines (Fig. 2a–e and Supplementary Movies 1, 2). This enhanced uptake was blocked by the macropinocytosis inhibitor EIPA, but not by the caveolin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor nystatin or the clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor CPZ (Supplementary Fig. 1a), confirming that DHODH inhibition specifically activates macropinocytosis. To validate the reliability of our assay, we also tested well-established inducers of macropinocytosis, including glutamine deprivation and FBS starvation33. Both conditions significantly increased TMR-dextran internalization, and this increase was reversed by EIPA pretreatment (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Together, these results demonstrate that DHODH inhibition triggers bona fide macropinocytosis in a specific and reproducible manner across multiple cancer models.

Mechanistically, DHODH inhibition induced prominent membrane ruffles (Fig. 2f, g) and markedly increased macropinosome formation (Fig. 2h). Genetic ablation of DHODH, achieved by either siRNA-mediated knockdown or CRISPR-mediated knockout, similarly enhanced macropinocytosis (Fig. 2i–l; see Supplementary Fig. 1c, d for validation data). The degree of DHODH reduction correlated with macropinocytosis induction (R² = 0.9), supporting a direct relationship. Supplementation with uridine or orotate (OA), the downstream products of DHODH18, reversed the increase in macropinocytosis induced by LEF or BRQ (Fig. 2m, n). Furthermore, depletion of phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate synthetase 1 (PRPS1), a key regulator of de novo pyrimidine metabolism34, also enhanced macropinocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f). Interestingly, depletion of exogenous nucleotide alone failed to induce macropinocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 1g). These results establish DHODH inhibition as a previously unrecognized trigger of macropinocytosis, revealing a new cellular nutrient-sensing mechanism.

DHODH inhibition promotes macropinocytosis through metabolic reprogramming and lactate accumulation

To investigate how DHODH inhibition stimulates macropinocytosis, we integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling (Fig. 3a). These analyses revealed that DHODH inhibition induces extensive metabolic rewiring, marked by pronounced accumulation of pyruvate and lactate and a concomitant reduction in acetyl-CoA levels in various cell lines. (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Fig. 2a–d). To validate our findings in a clinically relevant model, we employed the azoxymethane/dextran sodium sulfate (AOM-DSS) induced colorectal cancer model, which closely mimics human colorectal carcinogenesis. Mice received a single injection of AOM (12.5 mg/kg, i.p.) followed by 2.5% DSS in drinking water for 5 days to induce colitis-associated cancer. After tumor development (7 weeks), mice were treated with vehicle, LEF (20 mg/kg, every three days, i.p.), or BRQ (10 mg/kg, every three days, i.p.) for 5 weeks. Consistent with our xenograft results, both DHODH inhibitors significantly increased tumor lactate levels (2.6-fold for LEF, 6.9-fold for BRQ, p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 2d), confirming that DHODH inhibition induces metabolic reprogramming in endogenous tumors. Furthermore, the increased lactate levels in AOM-DSS tumors paralleled our observations in HT29 subcutaneous xenograft tumors (LEF, 20 mg/kg, i.p., every three days; BRQ, 10 mg/kg, i.p., every three days; 8 doses in total, Supplementary Fig. 2d) and cultured cells (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 2d), demonstrating that DHODH inhibition consistently induces lactate accumulation across multiple model systems. Importantly, the AOM-DSS model demonstrates that DHODH inhibition induces metabolic reprogramming not only in transplanted tumors but also in autochthonous tumors arising in their natural microenvironment. Notably, supplementation with uridine or OA reversed the DHODH inhibition-induced metabolic alterations, including the accumulation of pyruvate and lactate and the decrease in acetyl-CoA (Fig. 3d–f), supporting a direct connection between pyrimidine depletion and perturbed glucose metabolism35. We next asked whether DHODH inhibition drives pyruvate accumulation by enhancing glucose metabolism or modulating the activity of its key enzymes. We found that DHODH inhibition affected neither cellular glucose levels nor the activities of PK, HK, or PFK (Supplementary Fig. 2e–g). Although the protein expression levels of LDH and PDH remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 2h-i), DHODH inhibition significantly enhanced LDH activity and lactate production (Fig. 3g–i and Supplementary Fig. 2j, k), while suppressing both PDH activity and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 3j, k and Supplementary Fig. 2l, m). These results suggest that lactate accumulation stems from redirected pyruvate flux upon DHODH impairment. Given the mitochondrial localization of DHODH, we further assessed its role in mitochondrial integrity. DHODH inhibition resulted in aberrant mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 3l and Supplementary Fig. 2n), loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Supplementary Fig. 2o), and ROS accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 2o).

To test whether PDH inhibition alone is sufficient to drive this metabolic rewiring, we treated Huh7 cells with two structurally distinct PDH inhibitors, Pipcroside B (10 μM, 48 h) and AZD7545 (10 μM, 48 h)36. Both inhibitors effectively suppressed PDH enzymatic activity (Supplementary Fig. 2p) and, consistent with previous reports37,38, elevated LDH activity and intracellular lactate levels (Supplementary Fig. 2q, r). Importantly, PDH inhibition also stimulated macropinocytosis, as measured by TMR‑dextran uptake (Supplementary Fig. 2s). Taken together, these data demonstrate that DHODH inhibition compromises mitochondrial function, suppresses oxidative phosphorylation, and redirects pyruvate metabolism toward lactate production, thereby linking DHODH inhibition to the metabolic prerequisites for macropinocytosis induction.

Lactate accumulation triggers TERF2IP K208 lactylation to activate macropinocytosis

Given that lactate serves as a substrate for protein lactylation25, we examined whether DHODH inhibition affects protein lactylation patterns. Indeed, treatment with DHODH inhibitors significantly enhanced global protein lactylation, and this enhancement was abolished by oxamate or siLDHA-mediated knockdown of LDHA (more than 60% reduction, Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 3a). More importantly, inhibition of LDHA, the enzyme that converts pyruvate to lactate, abolished DHODH inhibitor-induced macropinocytosis (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 3b–d), establishing lactate accumulation and protein lactylation as critical mediators of this response. Concordantly, a marked increase in pan‑lactyllysine (pan‑lac) levels was observed in AOM/DSS‑induced colorectal tumors as well as in HT29 and Huh7 subcutaneous xenografts (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

To systematically identify relevant lactylation targets, we performed 4D label-free proteomics, which revealed 826 upregulated lysine lactylation sites on 532 proteins (fold change ≥ 1.2, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4c). To identify the functionally relevant lactylation targets for macropinocytosis, we performed two additional genome-wide CRISPR screens: one for suppressors of leflunomide-induced macropinocytosis and another for genes whose deletion synergizes with leflunomide to inhibit cell survival (Fig. 4d, e). Integration of these screens with the lactylome data yielded six common candidates, including TERF2IP (Fig. 4f). Among these six, individual knockdown experiments revealed that only depletion of TERF2IP significantly impaired DHODH inhibitor‑induced macropinocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b), confirming its unique functional relevance. Validation experiments demonstrated that DHODH inhibition markedly increased TERF2IP lactylation (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 3f).

Mass spectrometry and predictive analysis39 identified four potential lactylation sites on TERF2IP (Fig. 4h, i). Notably, in a TERF2IP‑null background, re‑expression of wild‑type TERF2IP restored macropinocytosis, whereas mutation of lysine 208 (K208R), but not the other candidate sites, failed to do so and simultaneously led to a substantial reduction in TERF2IP lactylation levels (Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 4c–e). The evolutionary conservation of the K208 site across species (Fig. 4k) further supports its functional importance. These findings reveal a mechanism by which metabolic stress can be translated into transcriptional responses through TERF2IP K208 lactylation (Fig. 4l).

To identify the writers and erasers responsible for TERF2IP lactylation, we systematically screened candidate enzymes in Huh7 cells under LEF treatment. For the writer screen, we knocked down twelve reported or putative lactyltransferase genes, including CBP, EP300, AARS1, ACAT1, ACSS2, GCN5, PCAF, KAT5, KAT8, KAT6A, KAT7, and GNAT13. Among these, only knockdown of EP300 reversed LEF‑induced TERF2IP lactylation (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), consistent with the established role of p300/EP300 as a lysine lactyltransferase40. For the eraser screen, we overexpressed six deacetylase or delactylase candidates, namely HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, SIRT1, SIRT2, and SIRT3. Only overexpression of HDAC1 reversed LEF‑induced TERF2IP lactylation (Supplementary Fig. 5, d), in line with recent reports identifying HDAC1 as a lysine delactylase41. Co‑immunoprecipitation experiments further demonstrated that the interaction between TERF2IP and EP300 increased upon LEF or BRQ treatment, whereas the interaction between TERF2IP and HDAC1 decreased under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5e). These results identify EP300 as the writer and HDAC1 as the eraser for stimulus‑induced TERF2IP lactylation. The molecular details of how EP300‑mediated TERF2IP lactylation regulates transcription warrant further investigation.

TERF2IP lactylation drives macropinocytosis through transcriptional activation of EREG-EGFR signaling

Given that protein lactylation can regulate gene expression25, we investigated downstream transcriptional changes induced by DHODH inhibition. Analysis of TCGA data revealed positive correlations between TERF2IP and several EGFR ligands (AREG, BTC, EREG, HBEGF) as well as PAK2 (Fig. 5a). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of transcriptomics revealed significant activation of the erythroblastic oncogene B (ErbB) signaling pathways upon DHODH inhibition (Fig. 5b). This finding was supported by our screen, which identified both ErbB pathway components and PAK1 activators as inducers of macropinocytosis (Fig. 1f, g). Since EGFR is known to promote macropinocytosis through PAK1-mediated cytoskeletal remodeling11, we examined the involvement of this pathway. Consistently, DHODH inhibition enhanced phosphorylation of both EGFR and PAK1 (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 6a). Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of EGFR or PAK1 blocked DHODH inhibitor-induced macropinocytosis (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Fig. 6b, c).

We next sought to identify which EGFR ligand plays a major role in activating macropinocytosis. Notably, DHODH inhibition upregulated multiple EGFR ligands (Fig. 5f, g and Supplementary Fig. 6d–f), among which EREG exhibited the most pronounced increase at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5f, g and Supplementary Fig. 6d–f). In contrast, exogenous nucleotide depletion did not affect the expression of these ligands (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Functional studies showed that recombinant EREG was sufficient to induce macropinocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 6i). Critically, EREG knockdown (more than 85% reduction) abolished DHODH inhibitor-induced macropinocytosis (Fig. 5h, i and Supplementary Fig. 6h), whereas BTC knockdown had no such effect (Supplementary Fig. 6j, k). Dual-luciferase reporter and ChIP-qPCR assays confirmed the direct binding of Lactylated TERF2IP to the EREG promoter (Fig. 5j, k and Supplementary Fig. 6l, m). Molecular docking simulations suggested that lactylation at K208 enhances the binding affinity of TERF2IP for the EREG promoter (Fig. 5l). Importantly, ectopic expression of TERF2IP significantly enhanced EREG promoter activity under DHODH inhibition, and this enhancement specifically depended on the K208 lactylation site (Fig. 5m, n and Supplementary Fig. 6n). Furthermore, PDH inhibition also recapitulated the downstream signaling cascade, including elevated TERF2IP lactylation, EREG induction, and EGFR/PAK1/2 phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 2t), reinforcing the link between metabolic stress and macropinocytosis. In conclusion, while DHODH inhibition affects multiple EGFR ligands through diverse mechanisms, our functional studies definitively identify EREG as the dominant ligand mediating macropinocytosis. The partial effect on total EGFR phosphorylation reflects the contribution of other ligands, but these do not significantly impact macropinocytosis induction, highlighting pathway-specific signaling downstream of different EGFR ligands.

Internalized albumin confers drug resistance through direct DHODH interaction

Given that DHODH inhibitors have shown limited therapeutic efficacy in cancer clinical trials despite promising preclinical results21–24, we investigated whether enhanced macropinocytosis contributes to drug resistance42,43. Since albumin is the most abundant plasma protein acquired through macropinocytosis, we examined its role in drug response. Indeed, DHODH inhibition significantly enhanced albumin internalization in cancer cells (Fig. 6a). In both Huh7 and HT29 cells, this enhanced albumin uptake was significantly reduced upon TERF2IP knockout (sgTERF2IP) compared to control cells. Reconstitution of sgTERF2IP cells with wild-type TERF2IP restored albumin internalization to levels comparable to those observed in parental cells, whereas expression of the K208R lactylation-resistant mutant failed to rescue the phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 7a). These results indicate that DHODH inhibitor–induced albumin uptake is mediated through TERF2IP lactylation at K208, establishing a direct link between this modification and the acquisition of a potential drug resistance mechanism.

Unlike other nucleotide-targeting agents that induce cell death and subsequent scavenging of nucleotides from cell debris, DHODH inhibitors did not trigger significant cell death; RSL3 was included as a positive control for ferroptosis induction to confirm the sensitivity of the cell death assay (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Macropinosome-mitochondria contact enables albumin-DHODH interaction

To understand how internalized albumin affects DHODH activity, we first examined the molecular interactions among DHODH inhibitors, DHODH, and albumin using molecular docking analyses and cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA). These analyses confirmed the binding of LEF and BRQ to DHODH (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Since DHODH is localized to mitochondria and albumin is primarily internalized via macropinocytosis (Fig. 6a), we asked whether macropinosomes come into contact with mitochondria. Confocal microscopy revealed close associations between macropinosomes and mitochondria (Fig. 7b). We next labeled macropinocytic vesicles in LEF‑treated cells with either TMR‑dextran or PE‑albumin and isolated GFP‑tagged mitochondria for flow cytometry. In both cases, LEF treatment significantly increased the fluorescence signal within the mitochondrial fraction (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Fig. 8c). Transmission electron microscopy further provided direct visual evidence of membrane contact between macropinosomes and mitochondria (Fig. 7d). We therefore investigated whether internalized albumin could access the mitochondrial compartment. Remarkably, in cells treated with DHODH inhibitors, we observed contact events between macropinosomes and mitochondria, enabling albumin translocation into mitochondria, where it colocalized with DHODH (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Movie 3). Consistent with this, Western blot analysis of isolated mitochondria revealed increased albumin levels following LEF treatment (Fig. 7f). Unexpectedly, molecular docking also predicted potential direct interaction between albumin and DHODH (Fig. 7g). Surface plasmon resonance analyses showed that albumin significantly reduced the binding affinity of DHODH inhibitors to their target (Fig. 7h–j and Supplementary Fig. 8d–f). Molecular dynamics simulations further demonstrated that albumin binding not only decreased inhibitor affinity for DHODH but also enhanced the binding of the native substrate dihydroorotate (Fig. 7k, l). To validate these predictions, we performed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis. Purified DHODH was immobilized on the sensor chip, and dihydroorotate (DHO) was injected as the analyte. In the absence of inhibitor or albumin, DHO bound to DHODH. The binding signal was virtually abolished in the presence of LEF, confirming that the inhibitor occupies the substrate‑binding site. Remarkably, when albumin was present in the running buffer, DHO binding to DHODH was restored to a level comparable to inhibitor-free conditions (Fig. 7m). These results directly demonstrate that albumin promotes substrate engagement by DHODH in the presence of the inhibitor, consistent with our molecular dynamics simulations. To further confirm that the protective effect of albumin depends on DHODH, we performed colony formation assays in Huh7 cells expressing control sgRNA (sgCtrl) or DHODH‑targeting sgRNA (sgDHODH). In sgCtrl cells, LEF significantly reduced colony formation, and albumin substantially restored clonogenic survival. In sgDHODH cells, baseline colony formation was decreased, LEF caused no further reduction, and albumin failed to rescue the phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 8g), demonstrating that the protective effect of albumin is strictly dependent on DHODH expression. Collectively, several independent lines of evidence support the functional significance of macropinosome-mitochondria contact in this resistance mechanism. Blocking macropinocytosis with EIPA abolished albumin accumulation in mitochondria and restored sensitivity to DHODH inhibitors (Fig. 6b, c). Co‑administration of albumin with LEF or BRQ, or in the context of sgDHODH, resulted in albumin uptake into the mitochondrial intermembrane space that correlated directly with the recovery of pyrimidine intermediates and oxygen consumption (Supplementary Fig. 8g). Furthermore, SPR and molecular dynamics simulations provide a mechanistic rationale for how albumin, once inside the intermembrane space, restores substrate binding to DHODH. While the molecular machinery mediating macropinosome–mitochondria tethering and fusion remains to be fully defined, these convergent functional, biochemical, and biophysical data provide strong support for the proposed model.

Validation of the DHODH-EGFR-PAK1 signaling axis in clinical samples

Supporting the clinical relevance of our findings, analysis of TCGA databases revealed negative correlations between DHODH and EGFR ligand expression (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Notably, we independently validated this relationship by analyzing fresh tumor specimens from 24 colorectal cancer patients, which showed significant negative correlations between DHODH/PRPS1 and key EGFR ligands (Supplementary Fig. 10b, c). To further strengthen this evidence at the protein level, we performed quantitative IRS (immunoreactive score) analysis and Spearman correlation for DHODH and p‑EGFR, as well as DHODH and p‑PAK in commercially purchased tissue microarrays. In colorectal cancer samples (n = 66), DHODH expression was negatively correlated with p‑EGFR (r = − 0.4381, p = 0.0002) and with p‑PAK (r = − 0.4352, p = 0.0003). In hepatocellular carcinoma samples (n = 40), the corresponding correlations were r = − 0.6057 (p < 0.0001) and r = − 0.4708 (p = 0.0022), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 10d, e). The representative IHC images shown in Supplementary Fig. S10d, e illustrate the overall inverse trends between DHODH and these phosphorylated signaling markers. Notably, the tissue microarrays contain only basic clinical information (age, sex, and tumor histological type) without treatment history, which may influence p‑EGFR levels. Correlating these findings with actual patient treatment responses or prognosis would be significantly more compelling and remains an important direction for future investigation. Together, these findings establish the inhibition of macropinocytosis as a candidate strategy that may overcome resistance to DHODH inhibitors, providing a strong rationale for combination therapy in cancer treatment.

Discussion

Our study reveals a previously unrecognized mechanism linking metabolic stress to macropinocytosis in cancer cells. While macropinocytosis has been traditionally viewed as a response to external nutrient deprivation44, we demonstrate that endogenous pyrimidine deficiency triggers a coordinated cellular response involving metabolic reprogramming, protein lactylation, and enhanced macropinocytosis. This adaptive response ultimately contributes to drug resistance through an albumin-dependent mechanism. Specifically, we show that: (1) DHODH inhibition induces metabolic rewiring toward enhanced glycolysis and lactate accumulation; (2) increased lactate triggers TERF2IP K208 lactylation; (3) lactylated TERF2IP transcriptionally upregulates EREG to activate EGFR-PAK1 signaling; and (4) the resulting macropinocytosis enables albumin delivery to mitochondria, where it modulates DHODH-inhibitor interactions. These findings establish an intrinsic metabolic stress-induced macropinocytosis pathway and identify a previously unknown mechanism of drug resistance.

Our findings establish a link between nucleotide metabolism and macropinocytosis, expanding the current understanding of cancer cell adaptation to metabolic stress. While previous studies have demonstrated that external nutrient limitation, such as amino acid or glucose deprivation, can trigger macropinocytosis9–12, our work reveals that internal metabolic perturbation, specifically pyrimidine deficiency- is sufficient to activate this process. This finding is particularly significant as it demonstrates how cancer cells can preemptively activate nutrient scavenging mechanisms before experiencing external nutrient stress. The metabolic rewiring we observed, characterized by enhanced glycolysis and lactate accumulation, serves as a molecular bridge connecting nucleotide deficiency to macropinocytosis activation. Lactate accumulation following DHODH inhibition is not a metabolic dysfunction to be corrected, but rather the initiating signal for an adaptive resistance mechanism. This paradigm shift has important therapeutic implications, suggesting that reducing lactate levels or blocking lactate signaling could enhance DHODH inhibitor efficacy. Furthermore, this represents a previously unrecognized role of lactate beyond its traditional functions in cancer metabolism - acting as a signaling molecule through protein lactylation to regulate adaptive responses. This mechanism may represent a broader paradigm for how cancer cells integrate information about their metabolic state to modify cellular behaviors.

Importantly, our findings extend to other forms of metabolic stress: glutamine deprivation, which acts upstream of pyrimidine biosynthesis, also induced TERF2IP lactylation and engaged the EREG/EGFR/PAK1 pathway. This suggests that the TERF2IP–lactate–EREG signaling circuit may function as a broader stress‑adaptive module that converges across diverse metabolic insults, although a complete dissection of glutamine deprivation‑induced macropinocytosis and its strict dependency on TERF2IP/EREG remains an important direction for future investigation. Despite this potential generality, the magnitude of the macropinocytic response varied considerably across tumor types: hepatocellular carcinoma (Huh7, HepG2, Hepa1‑6) and colorectal cancer (HT29, SW480, SW620) cells exhibited markedly stronger responses than other cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 6d). We speculate that this differential sensitivity reflects, at least in part, intrinsically high baseline activities of pyrimidine metabolism in HCC and CRC, which may prime these cells to engage the TERF2IP–EREG–EGFR–PAK1 axis more vigorously upon pyrimidine starvation. In contrast, tumor types lacking this metabolic and signaling infrastructure respond more weakly. Future studies that correlate baseline pyrimidine metabolic flux and EGFR ligand expression with macropinocytic responsiveness across larger tumor panels will be essential to test this hypothesis.

A particularly aspect of our findings is the discovery of an unexpected role for internalized albumin in drug resistance. Traditionally, albumin taken up through macropinocytosis has been viewed primarily as a nutrient source, undergoing lysosomal degradation to supply amino acids1,2. Our study reveals a fundamentally different mechanism, whereby macropinosomes containing albumin can contact with mitochondria, enabling direct albumin-DHODH interactions in the mitochondrial intermembrane space. This finding has several important implications. First, it demonstrates that cellular compartments traditionally considered separate can undergo dynamic contact events under metabolic stress. Second, it reveals that internalized proteins can directly modulate drug-target interactions without requiring degradation. Third, it suggests that the spatial organization of cellular compartments may play a previously unappreciated role in drug resistance. The ability of albumin to alter DHODH inhibitor binding while enhancing native substrate interactions represents a sophisticated mechanism of drug resistance that operates through protein-protein interactions rather than traditional resistance mechanisms such as drug efflux or target mutation.

The identification of TERF2IP K208 lactylation as a key regulatory mechanism represents another significant aspect of our study. TERF2IP, traditionally known as a component of the shelterin complex involved in telomere maintenance, has been previously shown to participate in DNA damage response45. Our findings reveal an unexpected metabolic regulatory role for this protein through lactylation-dependent transcriptional control. The specific lactylation of TERF2IP at K208, which is evolutionarily conserved across species, suggests this site’s functional importance in metabolic adaptation. While previous studies have demonstrated that post-translational modifications of TERF2IP, such as SUMOylation and phosphorylation, regulate its telomeric functions and cytoplasmic signaling46, our discovery of lactylation-dependent transcriptional regulation of EREG establishes a mechanism linking cellular metabolism to gene expression. This finding also expands our understanding of protein lactylation beyond its known roles in histone modification, suggesting that non-histone protein lactylation may represent a broader mechanism for cells to sense and respond to metabolic changes.

A recent study, employing genome-wide CRISPR screening combined with high-throughput drug susceptibility testing, similarly identified DHODH-mediated macropinocytosis as a key factor in PD-1 resistance in lung and breast cancers47. However, in contrast to our results, DHODH inhibition was found to reduce macropinocytosis in lung and breast cancer cells. To reconcile this discrepancy, we compared macropinocytosis in Huh7 and HT29 cells treated with a concentration gradient of BRQ or LEF for 36 h in the presence or absence of FBS. In the presence of serum, even the lowest concentrations tested (1 μM BRQ, 5 μM LEF) activated TMR‑dextran uptake, whereas serum starvation alone triggered macropinocytosis, and DHODH inhibition under these serum‑free conditions uniformly suppressed it. These findings indicate that serum availability is a major determinant of the divergent outcomes. While direct validation of the NRP1/O‑GlcNAcylation axis in our system was beyond the scope of this revision, future studies should systematically define the drivers that distinguish constitutive from stress‑induced macropinocytosis and clarify their respective contributions to therapy resistance.

Our findings have several important clinical implications, particularly for improving cancer therapy. First, we provide a mechanistic explanation for the limited efficacy of DHODH inhibitors observed in clinical trials. Despite promising preclinical results, DHODH inhibitors such as leflunomide and brequinar have shown disappointing outcomes in cancer patients21–24. Our discovery that macropinocytosis-mediated albumin uptake confers drug resistance may contribute to drug resistance under albumin-rich conditions, particularly given the abundance of albumin in human plasma. Second, by elucidating the role of EGFR signaling in this resistance mechanism, we identify a clinically actionable therapeutic strategy. The synergistic anti-tumor effects observed with combined DHODH and EGFR inhibition provide a strong rationale for this combination approach. Importantly, since EGFR inhibitors are already FDA-approved and widely used in cancer treatment, this combination strategy warrants further preclinical and clinical investigation. Third, our observation of negative correlations between DHODH and EGFR ligand expression in patient samples suggests the potential use of these markers for patient stratification. This could help identify patients most likely to benefit from combination therapy, advancing towards more personalized treatment approaches.

In conclusion, our work uncovers a previously unknown adaptive response linking DHODH inhibition to macropinocytosis through protein lactylation and organelle interaction. This finding challenges the traditional view of macropinocytosis as purely a nutrient-scavenging mechanism and establishes it as a sophisticated cellular response to metabolic stress. Beyond advancing our fundamental understanding of cancer cell adaptation, these insights provide a strong rationale for combination therapy approaches in cancer treatment. More broadly, our study demonstrates how cellular metabolic states can be integrated with membrane trafficking and transcriptional regulation to orchestrate adaptive responses, establishing a paradigm in cancer cell biology with important therapeutic implications.

Several important questions and therapeutic opportunities emerge from our study. At the molecular level, key questions include: what determines the specificity of TERF2IP K208 lactylation, and what molecular machinery mediates macropinosome-mitochondria contact. From a therapeutic perspective, our findings suggest several promising avenues. First, understanding the structural basis of albumin-DHODH interaction could guide the development of new DHODH inhibitors that maintain efficacy despite albumin binding. Second, given that internalized albumin can access mitochondrial compartments, it may similarly affect other mitochondria-targeted drugs, particularly those involved in pyrimidine synthesis and nucleotide metabolism. Understanding these potential interactions could reveal mechanisms of drug resistance and identify therapeutic targets. Third, the discovery of macropinosome-mitochondria contact as a drug resistance mechanism suggests that targeting membrane trafficking pathways might enhance the efficacy of various mitochondria-targeted therapies. Furthermore, while our study focused on DHODH inhibitors in liver and colorectal cancers, investigating similar mechanisms in other cancer types and with other mitochondria-targeted drugs could reveal broader therapeutic applications.

Plasmids EREG promoter constructs, HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, Sirt1, Sirt2, Sirt3 and TERF2IP expression vectors (wild-type and mutants) were synthesized by Mailgene Biosciences and verified by sequencing. CRISPR constructs were based on pX330-mCherry (Addgene).

Cell lines and culture

Cell lines were obtained from ATCC and authenticated by STR profiling. Huh7, HepG2, and Hepa1-6 cells were maintained in DMEM. SW620, SW480 and HT29 cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12. All media were supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination. For mechanistic studies, we primarily utilized Huh7 (hepatocellular) and HT29 (colorectal) carcinomas based on their technical advantages and representation of distinct cancer types. The mechanism was validated across additional lines, including SW620, which showed the strongest macropinocytosis response, confirming pathway universality across different cancer models. The cell line usage across all experiments are available in Supplementary Data 1.

Animal models

All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (approval no. NCC2024A256). For the AOM/DSS model, 6-8-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of AOM (12.5 mg/kg) at 7 weeks prior to the treatment period (designated as week -7). One week later (week -6), colitis was induced by administering 2.5% DSS in drinking water for 5 days. Following tumor development over the subsequent 5 weeks (weeks -5 to 0), the mice were treated for 5 weeks with vehicle, leflunomide (20 mg/kg, i.p., every three days), or brequinar (10 mg/kg, i.p., every three days).

For xenografts, 4-6-week-old male nude mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 10⁶ Huh7 or 2 × 10⁶ HT29 cells. Treatment began when tumors reached 50–100 mm³, using the following doses and routes: EIPA (20 mg/kg, i.p., every three days); Leflunomide (20 mg/kg for HT29, 40 mg/kg for Huh7, i.p., every three days); Gefitinib (20 mg/kg for HT29, 40 mg/kg for Huh7, i.g., every three days); and Brequinar (10 mg/kg, i.p., every three days). The maximal tumor burden permitted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee was set at 2000 mm³ per mouse, beyond which the animals would be humanely euthanized. Tumor volumes were measured using calipers and calculated as (length × width²)/2. Throughout the entire experimental period, we confirm that the maximal tumor burden (2000 mm³) was not exceeded in any of the xenograft-bearing mice across all treatment groups.

CRISPR-Based gene editing

Guide RNAs targeting DHODH (5′-CACCGCATCTTATAAAGTCCGTCCA-3′) or TERF2IP (5′-CACCGCTGTCGACGCTCATCCTGCA-3′) were cloned into pX330-mCherry. After transfection and puromycin selection, single-cell clones were generated and verified by Sanger sequencing.

RNA Interference

siRNAs (sequences provided in Supplementary Data 2) were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000. Final siRNA concentration was 100 nM, and cells were analyzed 36 h post-transfection.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol. cDNA synthesis used the Maxima First Strand kit. qPCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II on an ABI QuantStudio5 system. Relative expression was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method with 18S as internal control (sequences provided in Supplementary Data 3, 4).

RNA Sequencing

Total RNA was isolated with Trizol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing libraries were generated from 1 μg total RNA per sample using Hieff NGS Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Unique index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. The libraries were sequenced with the Illumina NovaSeq platform. The raw reads were further processed with a bioinformatic tool, the BMKCloud (www.biocloud.net) online platform.

Briefly, adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed, and the cleaned reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using Hisat2 soft. Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR, with a threshold of |log2FoldChange | ≥ 1 and adjusted P-value ≤ 0.05 for identifying significantly differentially expressed genes.

Protein analysis

Total protein was extracted from cell lines and tumor tissues using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The protein concentration was determined by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method. Equal amounts of protein lysates were then subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPFL00010, Millipore). After blocking with 5% skim milk in TBST (C520009, Sangon Biotech) for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by a 1 h incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. The protein bands were finally visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) on an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Healthcare).

Lactylation proteomics

Lactylproteomics analysis was performed by Jingjie PTM BioLabs using a 4D-DIA quantitative approach. A total of six samples were analyzed, comprising three biological replicates of Huh7 cells treated with leflunomide (100 μM, 48 h) and three biological replicates of DMSO-treated controls. No technical replicates were performed for mass spectrometry analysis (each biological replicate was injected once). Briefly, tryptic peptides were enriched for lactyl-modified peptides using anti-lactyllysine antibody-conjugated beads in NETN buffer (1 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0) at 4 °C overnight. The beads were washed four times with NETN buffer and twice with water. Enriched peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS using a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) coupled to a nanoElute UHPLC system. Peptides were separated on a reversed-phase C18 column (25 cm × 75 μm, 1.6 μm) at 50 °C with a flow rate of 300 nL/min, using a 115 min gradient of 2–37% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive-ion mode with PASEF acquisition; the DIA method consisted of 32 variable-width isolation windows covering 400–1200 m/z with collision energy ramping from 20 to 59 eV. The DIA data were processed using the MaxQuant search engine (v.1.6.6.0). Tandem mass spectra were searched against the Swiss-Prot human proteome database (version 20200509) with a reverse decoy database. Search parameters included trypsin/P as the cleavage enzyme, allowing up to 2 missed cleavages, a precursor ion mass tolerance of 20 ppm for the first search and 4.5 ppm for the main search, a fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.04 Da, carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification, and lactylation of lysine and oxidation of methionine as variable modifications. The false discovery rate was set to < 1% at both peptide-spectrum match and protein levels, using a target-decoy approach. For PTM localization, a minimum localization probability of ≥ 0.75 was required. Differentially lactylated proteins between leflunomide-treated and DMSO-treated groups were identified using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test with a P < 0.05 significance threshold and a fold-change cutoff of ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the FDR at < 0.05. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository under dataset identifier PXD055027. Comprehensive quality control information for all omics datasets is provided in Supplementary Fig. 11.

Metabolomics

Cells (2 × 10⁶) were seeded on culture dishes, and each experiment was repeated three times (n = 3 biologically independent experiments per group). A total of six samples were analyzed: three leflunomide-treated and three DMSO-treated control samples. No technical replicates were performed for LC-MS/MS analysis (each biological replicate was injected once). After leflunomide or DMSO treatment, cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and cell numbers were determined. Cells were then rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at – 80 °C until extraction. Metabolites were extracted with 1 mL of pre-chilled 80% (v/v) HPLC-grade methanol per pellet, followed by vortexing and incubation at – 20 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was transferred and dried using a vacuum centrifugal concentrator. Dried metabolites were reconstituted in 100 µL of 50% (v/v) methanol in water, proportionally adjusted to the initial cell count to normalize for cell number variations. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Metabolites were separated on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and a 20-min gradient of 95–50% acetonitrile (containing 10 mM ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid) in aqueous buffer (10 mM ammonium acetate, 0.1% formic acid, pH 9.0). The mass spectrometer was operated in both positive- and negative-ion modes in separate runs, with full-scan MS acquired at 70–1050 m/z at a resolution of 60,000 (at m/z 200). Data-dependent MS/MS was performed on the top 5 precursor ions using HCD with stepped collision energies of 20, 40, and 60 eV. Data were acquired using Xcalibur 4.0 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed using Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Metabolite identification was performed by searching MS/MS spectra against the mzCloud, HMDB (version 5.0), and METLIN databases, with confidence levels assigned according to MSI guidelines (Levels 1–4). Peak areas were normalized to the total ion count. Features with missing values in > 30% of samples were excluded; remaining missing values were imputed using half of the minimum positive value per feature. Principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were performed using SIMCA-P software (version 14.1, Umetrics). Differential metabolites were identified using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test with a fold-change threshold of ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67 and a P < 0.05, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing (FDR < 0.05). The metabolomics data have been deposited in the MetaboLights database under accession code MTBLS10913. Comprehensive quality control information for all omics datasets is provided in Supplementary Fig. 11.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were harvested and lysed in IP lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail and protein phosphatase inhibitors. The cell lysates were collected by scraping and then clarified by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein extracts were incubated with protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads, with the IgG control sample derived from the DMSO-treated group. The mixtures were incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Following five washes with lysis buffer, the beads were boiled in SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer and subjected to western blot analysis.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of human HBEGF, BTC, EREG, and AREG in cell culture supernatants were quantified using commercial ELISA kits, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, the supernatants were collected, appropriately diluted, and added to the 96-well plates pre-coated with specific capture antibodies. After incubation and thorough washing, enzyme conjugates and substrate solutions were added sequentially to develop a colorimetric reaction. The absorbance of each sample was subsequently measured at 450 nm.

Macropinocytosis assays

Cells were incubated with TMR-dextran (1 mg/mL) in serum-free medium for 1 h at 37 °C, Subsequently, they were washed three times with 1 × PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes. Following fixation, the cells were washed three more times with PBS, stained with DAPI, and prepared for analysis48. For the ex vivo analysis of macropinocytosis, 2 mm³ fragments were incubated with TMR-dextran for 30 min. After fixation, samples were analyzed by confocal microscopy. For the in vivo analysis of macropinocytosis, mice were injected intravenously with TMR-Dextran (1 mg/mL) via the tail vein. Following a 2 h period, the tissues were collected and processed for frozen sectioning. For quantitative analysis, a minimum of five randomly selected images (more than 50 cells counted) per condition were analyzed with ImageJ.

Phalloidin staining

For phalloidin staining, cells were seeded on glass coverslips. Briefly, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min. Following fixation, the cells were incubated with 0.05 μg/mL phalloidin at 4 °C overnight. Finally, the coverslips were mounted using Fluoroshield mounting medium and imaged under a fluorescence microscope.

Transmission electron microscopy

For transmission electron microscopy, cells were fixed overnight at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde (EM grade) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), rinsed three times in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, infiltrated and embedded in epoxy resin, and polymerized. Ultrathin sections (70–90 nm) were cut on an ultramicrotome, collected on copper grids, and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate.

Mitochondrial isolation

Cells were collected and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, followed by two washes with ice-cold PBS. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of pre-chilled Mitochondrial Isolation Buffer A per 20 million cells and homogenized on ice using a pre-cooled glass homogenizer. The homogenate was then carefully layered over an equal volume of Mitochondrial Isolation Buffer B in a centrifuge tube. For cultured cells, samples were centrifuged at 600 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the upper layer was collected. For tissue samples, centrifugation was performed at 700 × g for 10 min under the same conditions. The collected supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL tube and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet the mitochondria. A mitochondrial wash buffer was prepared freshly by mixing Mitochondrial Isolation Reagent C and D at a 17:3 ratio. The pellet was resuspended in 0.5 mL of the wash buffer per tube and centrifuged at 22,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was washed again with 1 mL of wash buffer, centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and collected as highly purified mitochondria. The mitochondrial fraction was subsequently subjected to flow cytometry or western blot analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Serial 5 μm tumor sections were subjected to deparaffinization, rehydration, and quenching of endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with 10% goat serum (G9023, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies. Immunoreactivity was detected using a MaxVision HRP-Polymer anti-Mouse/Rabbit IHC Kit (PV-9000, ZSGB-BIO) and diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (ZLI-9019, ZSGB-BIO). Finally, the sections were counterstained with Mosaic™ V2.1 (Tucsen Photonics) and mounted. Immunohistochemical scores were calculated as previously described49.

G0/G1 cell cycle arrest

Cells were seeded in 6-cm² dishes at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per dish and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were gently washed three times with pre-cooled PBS. The medium was then replaced with isoleucine-deficient complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS, containing 200 μM mimosine (HY-N0928, MCE) to synchronize the cell cycle. After 24 h of synchronization, the cells were treated with either BRQ (50 μM), LEF (100 μM), or 1% albumin (as a control). Following 48 h of treatment, the cell cycle distribution was analyzed.

Flow cytometry

For the cell cycle assay, Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. After trypsinization, cells were collected, rinsed with cold PBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol overnight at 4 °C. Fixed cells were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 3 min, rinsed three times with PBS, and then stained in the dark with propidium iodide (from kit C1052, Beyotime Biotechnology) for 15 min. Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using a BD LSR II flow cytometer.

For the macropinocytosis assay (TMR-dextran Uptake), cells were incubated with TMR-dextran (1 mg/mL) in serum-free medium for 1 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, followed by flow cytometric analysis.

For mitochondrial membrane potential assay (JC-1 Staining), cells were incubated with JC-1 (10 µM) in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37 °C, then immediately analyzed by flow cytometry.

For mitochondrial ROS assay, cells were incubated with MitoROS probe (5 µM) in serum-free medium for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by flow cytometric analysis.

Cell proliferation and viability assays

For the colony formation assay, 2 × 10³ cells were plated in 6-well plates and cultured for 12–14 days. The resulting colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and imaged after washing and drying.

For the 3D spheroid assay, a three-layer collagen I (2 mg/mL; 5074, Advanced BioMatrix) matrix was prepared in 12-well plates, with each layer polymerized at 37 °C for 1 h. A total of 1000 cells were embedded in the second layer, with treatments added in the top layer. The cultures were maintained for 2 weeks with medium changes every two days before imaging.

For the CCK-8 assay, 5000 cells/well were plated in 96-well plates, and viability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (C0037, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

TMR-Dextran/Albumin-mitochondria co-localization

Huh7 or HT29 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 2 × 10^5 cells per dish and treated with 100 μM LEF for 48 hours. Following treatment, the cells were incubated with either TMR-dextran (1 mg/mL for 1 h) or FITC-albumin (0.5 mg/mL for 6 h) in serum-free medium at 37 °C. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with 1 × PBS and subsequently stained with MitoTracker (50 nM) for 30 min. Following another three washes with PBS, the nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst (1 μg/mL) for 30 mins. After a final set of three washes with PBS, the cells were imaged. All Manders M2 analyses using the JACoP plugin (Just Another Colocalization Plugin) in Fiji (ImageJ2, version 2.14.0/1.54 f). Thresholds for each channel were determined automatically by the Costes regression method within JACoP, which iteratively identifies thresholds that minimize correlation between the two channels. After thresholding, M2 coefficients were calculated of interest per image across three independent experiments.

Albumin uptake Assay

To assess albumin uptake, cancer cells were seeded in confocal dishes and grown to 70% confluence. The cells were then incubated with FITC-BSA (0.5 mg/mL) in serum-free medium for 6 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, they were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, the coverslips were mounted using ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich) to visualize nuclei. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope as described in the “Confocal laser scanning microscopy” section. The fluorescence intensity of internalized albumin was quantified using ImageJ software.

Drug combination analysis

The inhibitory rate (IR, %) was calculated as: IR = [(OD_control - OD_experimental) / (OD_control - OD_blank)] × 100. The combined treatment index (Q) was determined using the equation: Q = E_ab / [(E_a + E_b) - (E_a × E_b)], where E_a and E_b represent the IR of Lef/BRQ and IPA3 alone, respectively, and E_ab represents the IR of the combination. A Q value between 0.85 and 1.15 indicates an additive effect; Q > 1.15 indicates synergy; and Q < 0.85 indicates antagonism.

Cell death assay

Cell death was quantified by real-time imaging. Cells seeded in 96-well plates were incubated with 50 nM SYTOX™ Green Dead Cell Stain (S34857, Invitrogen) and placed in an IncuCyte S3 Live-Cell Analysis System. Four images per well were captured every 4 h over 48 h, and SYTOX green-positive objects (dead cells) were quantified using the integrated Zoom image analysis software.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

A dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed to investigate TERF2IP-mediated transcriptional regulation of the EREG promoter. Cells were seeded in glass chamber slides overnight and subsequently co-transfected for 36 h with the following plasmids: the experimental reporters pGL3-basic-EREG or its mutant pGL3-basic-EREG△, an expression plasmid for the TERF2IP, and the internal control pRL-TK (11557ES03, Yeasen). After transfection, cells were treated with LEF (100 µM, 48 h) firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were sequentially measured using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (E1980, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s.

CRISPR Screen for macropinocytosis regulators

Huh7 cells were transduced with the genome-wide Brunello CRISPR/Cas9 knockout library at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) below 0.3 and 500-fold coverage. After 48 h of puromycin selection to remove non-transduced cells, the population was expanded and divided into treatment and control cohorts. The treatment group was subsequently exposed to leflunomide (LEF) for two weeks. Following this incubation, cells exhibiting either enhanced macropinocytosis in the control group or LEF-inhibited macropinocytosis in the treatment group were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Genomic DNA was then extracted, and sgRNA inserts were amplified via two rounds of PCR to construct sequencing libraries. These libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X10 platform, and the resulting data were analyzed using the MAGeCK algorithm to identify significantly enriched or depleted sgRNAs.

CRISPR Screen for synthetic lethality with leflunomide under albumin conditions

A parallel genome-wide CRISPR screen was conducted to identify genes that are synthetically lethal with leflunomide in the presence of albumin. Huh7 cells were transduced with the Brunello library under identical conditions (MOI < 0.3, 500x coverage) and selected with puromycin. The selected cells were then split and cultured for two weeks in medium containing leflunomide and supplemented with 1% albumin. Genomic DNA extraction, sgRNA library preparation, sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq X10 platform, and MAGeCK analysis were performed as described above.

Drug screens

Huh7 cells were seeded in 384-well plates and treated with compounds (provided by the Pharmaceutical Technology platform at Tsinghua University and the Biomedical Engineering Facility of National Infrastructures for Translational Medicine) for 48 h. Macropinocytosis was subsequently quantified by high-content analysis using the Opera Phenix system, based on TMR-dextran uptake. For imaging, 10 fields per well were captured using a 20 × objective and a Texas Red filter set, ensuring the analysis of over 500 cells per condition. Z-stack acquisition was performed to capture the entire cell volume. Following image acquisition, cells were segmented using CellProfiler 4.0. The integrated TMR intensity per cell was calculated, with background subtraction applied using cell-free regions. The intensity was then normalized to cell area to account for size variations, and plate positional effects were corrected using B-score normalization. Drug screening results are available in Source Data 1, and the screening summary is provided in Supplementary Data 5.

Target identification

We employed an integrated screening approach combining (i) quantitative lactylation proteomics to identify proteins with increased lactylation upon DHODH inhibition, (ii) genome-wide CRISPR screening to identify genes required for DHODH inhibitor-induced macropinocytosis, and (iii) focused CRISPR screening under albumin conditions to identify resistance factors. The intersection of these three independent screens identified TERF2IP as the top candidate, which was subsequently validated through site-directed mutagenesis and functional assays.

TERF2IP rescue experiments

TERF2IP knockout Huh7/HT29 cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding wild-type or K208R mutant TERF2IP. Viral titer was adjusted to achieve expression levels matching endogenous TERF2IP (0.8-1.45 × of parental cells). Single-cell clones were selected and validated by Western blot against parental Huh7/HT29 cells. Only clones with physiological expression levels were used for functional studies.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on the conformations that ranked first in each of the three regions were performed using GROMACS 2024.3 software. The AMBER99SB force field and SPC water model were sued, and the system temperature was 300 K, and the simulation time was 300 ns. The steepest descent method was applied for energy minimization, followed by equilibration to stabilize the system prior to conducting the MD simulation. The binding free energies were calculated using the MM/GBSA method.

Molecular docking

The SDF format ligand file of leflunomide and brequinar were obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and the three-dimensional structure models of the proteins DHODH and albumin were obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). AutoDockTools 1.5.6 was used to convert ligand and receptor files to pdbqt format and improve their structure by replacing water molecules with hydrogen atoms. Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock Vina 1.2.5, and PyMOL 2.4.0 was used for further analysis and visualization.

ECAR and OCR assays

Cellular metabolic function was assessed by measuring the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) using a Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent). Cells were seeded in a Seahorse XF96 cell culture plate at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated overnight. During the assay, the following compounds were sequentially injected to perturb metabolism: for the glycolytic stress test (ECAR), 10 mM glucose, 1.5 μM oligomycin, and 50 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG); for the mitochondrial stress test (OCR), 1.5 μM oligomycin, 2 μM FCCP, and 0.5 μM rotenone/antimycin A. All data were analyzed using Wave Desktop 2.6 software (Agilent).

Lactate, pyruvate, and glucose detection

The concentrations of lactate, pyruvate, and glucose were measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 50 μL of each sample was added to a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of the specific lactate, pyruvate, or glucose detection reagent. After incubation at room temperature for 1 h, the chemiluminescence signal was recorded.

Acetyl coenzyme A (Acetyl-CoA) detection

The concentration of acetyl-CoA was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 20 μL of each sample was added to a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of the detection reagent. After a 10 min incubation at room temperature, the fluorescence intensity of each well was measured using a microplate reader at excitation and emission wavelengths of 535 nm and 587 nm, respectively.

Metabolic enzyme activity assays

Enzyme activities were determined in 96-well plates by monitoring absorbance changes at 2 min intervals in kinetic mode, according to the respective manufacturer’s protocols.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

Cells were treated with sodium oxamate (10 mM) in complete medium for 48 h. For genetic inhibition, cells were transfected with siRNA targeting LDHA (100 nM) using Lipofectamine 2000. LDH activity was measured using the LDH Activity Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, A 25 μL sample was mixed with 25 μL of Total LDH Assay Buffer, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Pyruvate Dehydrogenase (PDH)

A 20 μL sample was mixed with 180 μL of Total PDH Assay Buffer, and absorbance was measured at 605 nm.

Hexokinase (HK)

A 10 μL sample was mixed with 190 μL of Total HK Assay Buffer, and absorbance was measured at 340 nm.

Phosphofructokinase (PFK)

A 10 μL sample was mixed with 90 μL of Total PFK Assay Buffer, and absorbance was measured at 340 nm.

Pyruvate Kinase (PK):

A 10 μL sample was mixed with 190 μL of Total PK Assay Buffer, and absorbance was measured at 340 nm.

Surface plasmon resonance

SPR analyses were performed on a Biacore T100 system (Cytiva) at 25 °C using PBS‑P⁺ supplemented with 5% DMSO as the running buffer. Recombinant DHODH protein (Cloud‑Clone Corp., Wuhan, China) was buffer‑exchanged into PB buffer (pH 8.0) by ultrafiltration (10 kDa cutoff) to remove Tris, and then pre‑concentrated on a CM5 chip using sodium acetate buffers at various pH values (5.5–4.0). The optimal condition (pH 4.5) was selected to immobilize DHODH (50 μg/mL) via amine coupling (EDC/NHS), yielding a surface density of approximately 8000 response units (RU). LEF or BRQ was first dissolved in DMSO at 5 mM, then diluted with PBS‑P, and subsequently serially diluted in running buffer. The prepared LEF/BRQ solutions were injected over the DHODH‑immobilized chip at a flow rate of 30 μL/min for 60 s (association) and allowed to dissociate for 60 s.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed with a dedicated kit (EMD Millipore, 17-295) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For data quantification, the enrichment of specific DNA regions was normalized to that of a normal IgG control. All ChIP-qPCR primer sequences utilized in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

Huh7 cells were treated with DMSO vehicle, LEF (100 μM), or BRQ (50 μM) for the indicated duration. Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and resuspended in lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After three freeze–thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen, cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were divided into equal aliquots and heated individually at 37 °C, 42 °C, 47 °C, 52 °C, 57 °C, or 63 °C for 3 min in a thermal cycler, followed by cooling on ice for 3 min. Heated lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to pellet the denatured protein aggregates. The soluble fractions were subjected to SDS-PAGE and western blotting with an anti‑DHODH antibody.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) software. Two‑sided unpaired Student’s t‑test, two-way ANOVA, two‑sided MAGeCK RRA test with FDR adjustment, and two‑sided permutation test (1000 permutations) with FDR adjustment were employed for two‑group comparisons, comparisons among multiple groups, CRISPR screen analysis, and GSEA, respectively. For metabolomics and lactylomics, a two‑sided unpaired Student’s t‑test without adjustment for multiple comparisons was used. Statistical significance was considered at P ≤ 0.05 or FDR q < 0.05. Results from at least three biologically independent experiments with consistent outcomes are presented, except for CRISPR screens performed once with 500‑fold coverage. Data are expressed as the mean ± s.d. as indicated in the figure legends.
