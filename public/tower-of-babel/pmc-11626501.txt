COVID‐19 and Oncofertility: No SARS‐CoV‐2 in Semen but Inflammation Seems to Affect Sperm Parameters
Authors: Chaput Laure, Pons‐Rejraji Hanae, Chabrolles Hélène, Fiot Mélanie, Lucas Cécily, Pereira Bruno, Bonnet Benjamin, Haj Hamid Rahaf, Rives‐Feraille Aurélie, Binois Olivier, Ferreux Lucile, Delepine Béatrice, Koscinski Isabelle, Lichtblau Isabelle, Giscard d'Estaing Sandrine, Bendayan Marion, Saias‐Magnan Jacqueline, Lousqui Johanna, Henquell Cécile, Brugnon Florence
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe COVID‐19 pandemic, driven by SARS‐CoV‐2, led authorities to recommend halting assisted reproductive technology programs, focusing instead on fertility preservation, for cancer patients. The presence of SARS‐CoV‐2 in semen remains controversial. This multicentric prospective cohort study, conducted across 12 university medical centers, aimed to determine if SARS‐CoV‐2 is present in spermatozoa/seminal plasma in cancer patients by RT‐PCR and to assess its impact on standard semen parameters. The levels of cytokines and TNF‐α were measured in seminal fluid by ELISA. We enrolled 129 men who underwent sperm cryopreservation between July 7, 2020, and June 30, 2021. The 63 were included and tested for COVID‐19 in nasal swab samples by RT‐PCR and/or by serology. All patients were asymptomatic on the day of semen collection: 50 were uninfected, 8 had a positive nasal swab (PCR+) and 5 were seropositive. SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA was not detected in the seminal fluid or spermatozoa. Ejaculate volume was significantly lower in the PCR+ group compared to the uninfected group (median [IQR]: 2.6 mL [1.6–3.4] vs. 4.6 mL [2.6–5.2] p < 0.05). Total and progressive motility were lower in the PCR+ group compared to the seropositive group (32.5% [25.0–45.0] vs. 50% [49.0–55.0] p < 0.05, and 22.5% [10.0; 32.5] vs. 44.5% [40–49] p < 0.05). Higher TNF‐α level was observed in the PCR+ group (1.9 pg/mL [0–3.9]) compared to the uninfected group (0 pg/mL [0–0.4]) p < 0.05. Although SARS‐CoV‐2 was not detected in the sperm samples of cancer patients who were PCR+, the infection appears to impact sperm parameters, likely due to inflammation.

Introduction

SARS‐CoV‐2 led to the large‐scale pandemic of COVID‐19, with severe acute respiratory syndrome and deaths, particularly in fragile patients who had altered immunity. Cancer patients are regarded as being highly vulnerable to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection due to their systemic immunosuppressive state caused directly by cancer invasion and indirectly by the effects of anticancer treatments [1]. Consequently, rapid upscaling of in vitro diagnostic assays not only to enable mass screening, but also to diagnose the population, were necessary to simultaneously ascertain robust data on SARS‐CoV‐2 transmission risks. As such, there has been accelerated development of both serological and molecular assays. It is also imperative to better understand the transmission modes of SARS‐CoV‐2 due to its rapid spread and possible damage to multiple organs. Although the most common manifestations are pneumonia, fever, cough, diarrhea, asthenia and anosmia, a possible impact of the virus on the male reproductive tract was also reported [2, 3, 4]. SARS‐CoV‐2 uses the angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor and the cell protease type II transmembrane serine protease (TMPRSS2) to bind to the cell and for virus–cell fusion [5]. Although there is evidence of a high level of angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor expression in spermatogonia, Leydig and Sertoli cells of the testis, and in the epididymis, the impact of SARS‐CoV‐2 in the male reproductive tract remains unclear [6, 7].

In the acute phase of severe COVID‐19, men may exhibit inflammation, vascular congestion, and extensive germ cell destruction in the testes [7]. The seminal plasma contains pro and anti‐inflammatory cytokines and TNF‐α produced by various immune competent cells in the male reproductive tract. High levels of cytokines and TNF‐α in the seminal plasma have been described to be associated with a decrease in sperm motility and/or infertility [8]. Therefore, cytokines and TNF‐α profiling in human semen could be used as a method for detecting inflammation in the reproductive tract due to COVID‐19. Indeed, the impact of high inflammation response due to COVID‐19 may explain the altered sperm parameters reported in some studies [9, 10].

In some patients with severe COVID‐19, SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA was detected in the semen or testes [11, 12]. However, only a few studies have reported its presence in the male reproductive tract [13] and at our knowledge, only one study reported molecular confirmation of the absence of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in seminal fluid from asymptomatic cancer patients [14]. For patients with mild COVID‐19, SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA is often absent from the semen [15]. Nevertheless, evaluation of the presence of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in seminal samples is important for semen cryopreservation because viruses may retain their pathogenic properties in liquid nitrogen [14]. At the beginning of the pandemic, concerned authorities worldwide took significant steps to address it and recommended stopping assisted reproductive technology (ART) programs and maintaining fertility preservation, particularly for cancer patients in whom fertility may be altered by the initiation of anticancer treatments [16, 17, 18].

The primary objective of this study was to determine whether SARS‐CoV‐2 is present in the spermatozoa and/or seminal plasma isolated by density gradient centrifugation of cancer patients, in the context of semen cryopreservation for future use in ART program. We analyzed sperm samples of men who performed fertility preservation during the first pandemic period. All these men provided nasal swabs to test for SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA by RT‐PCR. The secondary objectives were to evaluate the potential impact of COVID‐19 on standard semen parameters for these patients and on the inflammatory profile of their seminal plasma.

Participants and Method

Ethics

This study was approved by the French Research Ethics Committee (ID RCB: 2020‐A01409‐30) and conducted in compliance with the ethical committee of University of Clermont‐Ferrand and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. This study was registered in ClinicalTrials (NCT04487639).

Study Participants and Sampling Methods

This prospective multicentric study was conducted in 12 French CECOS (Centre Etude Conservation Oeufs et Sperme) centers (CECOS from Clermont Ferrand, Marseille, Nancy, Paris Tenon, Paris Cochin, Clamart Antoine Béclère, Poissy Saint‐Germain‐en‐Laye, Reims, Rouen, Bondy Jean Verdier, Lyon, Strasbourg) from July 2020 to June 2021. The inclusion criteria for the men in this study were: at least 18 years of age, needing oncofertility preservation before a putative gonadotoxic treatment, and either asymptomatic for COVID‐19 or having typical COVID‐19 symptoms (fever, cough, cephalalgia, myalgia, diarrhea, anosmia, pharyngodynia). During the counseling before fertility preservation, data were collected on cancer type, medical history, COVID‐19 related comorbidities and potential COVID‐19 symptoms. All patients recruited for this study completed a questionnaire regarding their potential COVID‐19 symptoms, risk factors for severe COVID‐19, and any exposures to factors that are potentially harmful to the male reproductive system. PCR of nasopharyngeal swab and serological tests for the detection of COVID‐19 were performed the day of the first sperm collection for fertility preservation. Finally, patients with a negative PCR or an absence of antibodies (IgM and IgG) were used to constitute the uninfected group whose samples underwent the same preparation steps as Covid‐19 positive patients. The inclusion of these patients made it possible to discriminate between cancer‐related and virus‐induced effects. The viral serologies were conducted for each patient undergoing fertility preservation (HIV, HBV, HCV and syphilis). All patients tested were negative for HIV, HCV and syphilis infection and were either negative or vaccinated for HBV. Standard semen bacteriological analysis was not routinely recommended for fertility preservation but when performed, the results were negative. Moreover, none of the semen samples showed leucospermia.

Semen collection was always performed before the start of chemotherapy. So, no potential effect of malignancy treatments was possible on semen samples characteristics. After measuring semen parameters, seminal fluid and spermatozoa were isolated by density gradient centrifugation and frozen. Seminal fluid and/or two straws of spermatozoa were used for SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA detection. Therefore, we planned to detect the presence of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in spermatozoa only if a sufficient number of straws were frozen to perform at least six ART attempts. All participants provided nasal swabs to test for SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA by reverse‐transcription (RT‐PCR, using Kit TaqPath COVID‐19 RT‐PCR, (ThermoFisher), SARS‐CoV‐2 ELITe MGB Kit (ELITech), or Allplex SARS‐CoV‐2 Assay (Seegene). Serology (IgG and/or IgM) was also performed using blood samples obtained on the day of semen collection. Qualitative detection antibodies to SARS‐CoV‐2 was performed in the different centers by two rapid chromatographic immunoassays (Biosynex COVID‐19 BSS IgG/IgM, Biosynex, and Standard Q COVID‐19 IgM/IgG Combo Test, SD Biosensor, Organtec) or four automated ELISA immunoassays (Abbott ARCHITECT SARS‐CoV‐2 IgG, Abbott laboratories; Elecsys Anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2, Roche Diagnostics; Anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 ELISA (IgG), Euroimmun and VIDAS SARS‐COV‐2 IgG, bioMérieux).

Sperm Cryopreservation and Analysis

Standard semen analyses and cryopreservation were performed at each CECOS center. Semen samples were obtained by masturbation after disinfecting the hands and glans penis. The men carried a mask to avoid possible SARS‐CoV‐2 contamination of the sperm collection. Conventional semen analyses were performed in accordance with WHO 2010 guidelines. Seminal fluid and spermatozoa fractions were isolated by two‐stage (90/45%) discontinuous density gradient centrifugation at 750 g for 20 min at room temperature (RT) as described previously. The seminal fluid in the top layer of the gradient medium was collected and frozen in cryotubes at −20℃. The high‐density fraction was washed by centrifugation and spermatozoa mixed at a 1:1 ratio (v/v) with CryoSperm medium (Origio, France) or at a 1:0.7 or 1:1 ratio (v/v) with SpermFreeze medium (FertiPro N.V., Belgium), according to the supplier's recommendations [19]. Next, spermatozoa samples were frozen in high‐security straws in a programmable freezer with a slow‐cooling protocol. In the final stage, spermatozoa straws and cryotubes of seminal fluid were stored in liquid nitrogen at −196℃. Two straws of spermatozoa and/or two cryotubes of seminal fluid were transported from the participating centers to the principal investigator's center (University Hospital, Clermont‐Ferrand) and stored in a liquid nitrogen tank before SARS‐CoV‐2 detection in the virology laboratory (Clermont‐Ferrand University Hospital).

Detection of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in Seminal Fluid and Spermatozoa By RT‐PCR

Seminal fluid and spermatozoa samples were thawed at 4℃ just before RNA extraction and detection using a previously validated process [19]. The internal control (a packaged phage RNA fragment) included in the extraction kit was spiked into each sample. RNA was extracted from 160 µL of each undiluted sample with the automated MGISP‐960 System and the MGIEasy Magnetic Beads Virus DNA/RNA Extraction Kit (MGI Tech), with the following steps: lysis buffer addition to samples, nucleic acid absorption on magnetic beads, washes and RNA elution. Each extraction run included negative and positive control samples, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Next, 10 µL of each RNA extract were reverse‐transcribed and cDNA were amplified in a real‐time RT‐PCR assay, performed with the one‐step TaqPath COVID‐19 CE‐IVD RT‐PCR Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific). The kit targeted the ORF1ab and the N (nucleocapsid) and S (spike) genomic regions of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA. Reactions were run on the QuantStudio 5 thermocycler (ThermoFisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The PCR thermal profile consisted of an incubation step at 25℃ for 2 min and a reverse transcription step at 53℃ for 10 min, followed by 2 min at 95℃, and 45 cycles of 3 s at 95℃ and 30 s at 60℃. The results were analysed with Applied Biosystems QuantStudio Design and Analysis Software 2.4.0. Analysis criteria were fulfilled when positive and negative controls were in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, and the internal control (IC) value was consistent with adequate efficiency of the extraction and amplification process (i.e., Ct‐IC (sample)‐Ct‐IC (negative extraction control) < |3|). To conclude on a positive detection of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA, at least two viral targets with Ct values ≤ 40 had to be detected. The sample was retested when one viral target was detected. A negative result was concluded when no viral target was detected.

Interleukin and TNF‐α Levels in Seminal Fluid

The levels of cytokines interleukin (IL)‐6, IL‐10, IL‐8, IL‐12p70, IL‐1β, and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF‐α) were measured simultaneously in seminal samples using the human pro‐inflammatory cytometric bead array kit (CBA, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions (LEGENDplex Human Inflammation Panel 1, BioLegend, #BLE740809). Samples (n = 6 for PCR+ and n = 11 from uninfected patients, control group) were acquired on the FACS Canto II cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and the generated FSC files analyzed by FCAP Array version 3.0 software.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were expressed according to their statistical distribution as means and standard deviation (SD) or medians and interquartile range (IQR). The assumption of normality was analyzed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. The comparisons between groups (uninfected, PCR+, and seropositive) were performed using the Kruskal‐Wallis test as the assumptions of analysis of variance were not met. When appropriate (omnibus p < 0.05), Dunn's post‐hoc test for two‐by‐two multiple comparisons was applied. Specific attention was given to the magnitude of differences. Thus, the results were expressed using Hedges' effect size (ES) with 95% confidence intervals and were interpreted according to the recommendations of Cohen, who defined the ES bounds as small (ES = 0.2), medium (ES = 0.5), and large (ES = 0.8). Concerning categorical data, the comparisons were performed using chi‐squared or Fisher's exact test followed by the Marascuilo procedure. Multivariable analyses were multiple linear regression to compare groups with the type of cancer as an adjustment covariate. The normality of residuals from these models was analyzed by the Shapiro‐Wilk test. When necessary, a log transformation was proposed to achieve the normality of the dependent variable. The statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 15, StataCorp, College Station, USA). All statistical tests were carried out for a two‐sided type I error at 5%, applying the appropriate method for two‐by‐two multiple comparisons.

Results

Patient Characteristics and SARS‐CoV‐2 Detection in Semen Samples

A total of 129 men were recruited for this study (Figure 1). Sixty‐six were excluded due to semen collection failure (n = 12) or missing serology and/or nasopharyngeal RT‐PCR (n = 54). Thus, 63 patients were eligible for inclusion: 8 had a positive nasopharyngeal swab detection for SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA (PCR+ group), 5 were PCR‐negative but positive for anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 antibodies (IgM+ and IgG+; seropositive group) and 50 were tested negative for SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA and antibodies (uninfected group). Patients had no known comorbidities or history of COVID‐19. The clinical characteristics of the uninfected group were not significantly different from the PCR+ and seropositive groups regarding age, BMI, tobacco consumption, profession, fecundity, COVID symptoms, and fever presence (Table 1). The PCR+ and seropositive patients were asymptomatic on the day of sperm collection for oncofertility preservation. Only 1 patient in the PCR+ group and ten in the uninfected group had a fever within 3 months of semen collection. Two patients in the uninfected group presented with respiratory symptoms. In the PCR+ group, five patients had Hodgkin's lymphoma and three had non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma. The seropositive group included patients with non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma (n = 2), testicular cancer (n = 2), and glioma (n = 1). Semen analysis by RT‐PCR revealed no detection of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in neither the seminal fluid nor the spermatozoa from any of the included patients.

Impact of SARS‐CoV‐2 on Standard Semen Parameters

Ejaculate volume was significantly lower in the PCR+ group compared with the uninfected group (median [IQR]: 2.6 mL [1.6–3.4] vs. 4.6 mL [2.6–5.2], p < 0.05, ES = 0.85 [0.09; 1.60]), though the values were in the normal range according to the WHO's criteria (Table 2). Spermatozoa concentration, total sperm number per ejaculate and vitality were not significantly different between the PCR+, seropositive and uninfected groups. Nevertheless, we observed significantly lower total and progressive motile spermatozoa in the PCR+ group (32.5% [25.0–45.0] and 22.5% [10.0–32.5]) compared with the seropositive group (50% [49.0–55.0], ES = 1.31 [0.11; 2.46] and 44.5% [40.0–49.0], ES = 1.56 [0.31; 2.75], p < 0.05 for both total and progressive motility). No statistical difference was observed for PCR+ versus uninfected group (45.0% [34.5–52.0], ES = 0.53 [−0.22; 1.27] and 35.5% [25.0–44.0], ES = 0.74 [−0.02; 1.48] for total and progressive sperm motility respectively).

Inflammatory Profiles of COVID‐19 Patients (PCR+ Group) With Hodgkin's and Non‐Hodgkin's Lymphoma

To discriminate the inflammatory effects induced by COVID‐19 and cancer, we measured the pro‐inflammatory cytokine profiles in seminal samples from patients with Hodgkin's (n = 5) and non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma (n = 1) in the PCR+ (total n = 6) and uninfected (total n = 11; n = 6 Hodgkin's lymphoma and n = 5 non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma) groups (Table 3). We performed this comparative study only for these cancer types because they were the only present in both the PCR+ and uninfected groups. We observed higher TNF‐α levels in the PCR+ group (1.9 pg/mL [0–3.9]) compared with the uninfected group (0 pg/mL [0–0.4], ES = 1.3 [0.2; 2.3]). No difference was observed for the other cytokines.

Discussion

In this multicenter study, SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA was not detected in the seminal fluid or spermatozoa of cancer patients when they performed sperm cryopreservation before the initiation of cancer treatment for fertility preservation. However, when the nasal swab was positive for SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA detection in a cancer patient without COVID‐19 symptoms, a deleterious impact on semen volume and sperm motility was observed. Moreover, a high level of TNF‐α was measured in the seminal plasma from PCR+ patients with Hodgkin's and non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma, probably due to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to report the measurement of SARS‐CoV‐2 detection in seminal fluid and spermatozoa of cancer patients, who had undergone a nasopharyngeal swab for SARS‐CoV‐2 testing at the time of their sperm collection for fertility preservation. Only Paoli et al., 2021 reported the absence of SARS‐CoV‐2 in seminal fluid from 10 asymptomatic cancer patients [14]. However, in this study cancer patients had not undergone a nasopharyngeal swab for SARS‐CoV‐2 testing. Indeed, only a questionnaire and a medical counseling were performed and all the patients have been identified as asymptomatic. In our study, all participants provided nasal swabs for testing for SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA by RT‐PCR, and the groups of cancer patients were clearly identified as SARS‐CoV‐2 positive by RT‐PCR or seropositive for COVID‐19. Moreover, in our study, we analysed the presence of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA by RT‐PCR not only in seminal plasma fraction but also in separated spermatozoa fraction which may be used for ART. This original approach ensures that these samples can be used safely for future ART program. The evaluation of the presence of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in sperm is particularly important because of their potential use in future ART. A recent meta‐analysis [7] showed that SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA is detected in semen mainly during the acute phase of COVID‐19 infection. Only a few studies have reported seminal viral RNA detection in a relevant number of patients [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. In our study, RT‐PCR analysis of semen revealed the absence of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in both the seminal fluid and spermatozoa from any of the patients positive for SARS‐CoV‐2 (PCR+ or seropositive). Only 8 patients had a positive RT‐PCR, three had a high viral load (cycle threshold values ranging from 17 to 28) while others had a low viral load (Ct range 30–38). These patients were asymptomatic the day of sperm collection. No patient with severe COVID‐19 was included because of the urgency of their condition and their health status being incompatible with fertility preservation. The absence of the virus in the seminal fluid or spermatozoa indicates a low possibility of sexual transmission of the infection to partners and offspring and are in accordance with the data provided from non‐cancer patients with asymptomatic COVID‐19 [25].

Only one positive patient had fever within the 3 months before sperm collection. Therefore, the virus was not present in the semen of cancer patients with mild COVID‐19 in the current study. These results were in accordance with published data with no cancer patients [10, 23, 26, 27]. The RT‐PCR method and sperm collection procedure applied in previous studies were not always clearly described and methods used were heterogenous. In our study, the real‐time RT‐PCR used targeted three genomic regions of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA, according to the microbiology guidelines (Methods for the detection and characterization of SARS‐CoV‐2 variants ‐ second update 2022) and was previously validated for semen specimens [20]. This ensures avoiding false negative results.

Since our study was performed in the beginning of the pandemic period, RT‐PCR of semen was also performed in the semen of uninfected patients. Indeed, during this period, a learning process was ongoing for nasopharyngeal swab collection. None patient was vaccinated at this time. Moreover the learned societies recommended to test all patients before starting sperm banking by RT‐PCR by nasopharyngeal swab and/or blood antibody testing and the use of safe devices for sample handling and cryostorage [28] to ensure safety. In the later part of the pandemic, different studies showed that the assessment of cellular immunity may only provide complementary information on whether a patient has been previously infected but did not indicate the precise timing of this infection [29]. Moreover, because masturbation is necessary for collection, it could cause contamination by hands, respiratory droplets, urine, and/or feces. In our study, sperm collection was performed after disinfection/washing of the hands and glans penis and the men carried a mask. The positive detection of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in semen in previous studies may be explained by patients having the severe form of COVID‐19 and collection occurring during the acute phase [9, 10]. During this phase, a high blood viral load may explain the possibility of SARS‐CoV‐2 reaching other organs and/or body fluids, including semen. Moreover, the presence of SARS‐CoV‐2 in sperm samples may be due to alteration of the blood‐testis barrier due to the inflammation related to COVID‐19 or the expression of SARS‐CoV‐2 in the seminal fluid. The physiopathology of COVID‐19 is strongly associated with expression of ACE2 and TMPPRS2 for cell entry and spread in the host. SARS‐CoV‐2 binds to ACE2, and this binding induces the recruitment of TMPPRS2. TMPPRS2 facilitates viral entry and spread into the host cell by cleaving ACE2 and S protein [30]. Their expression has been reported on spermatogonia and elongated spermatids in the testis and glandular cells of seminal vesicles and luminal epithelial cells of the prostate [31, 32, 33]. Low expression of ACE2 and no expression of TMPPRS2 have been reported for human spermatozoa. The physiopathology of the viral effects may also be explained by the impact of SARS‐CoV‐2 on the testis and/or accessory glands. Postmortem examination of the testes of one patient with gastric carcinoma found positive expression in the testis by RT‐PCR [34]. This patient had a high viral load and his lung, kidney, and spleen were also positive for virus detection by RT‐PCR. However, in this study, the expression of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA was only detected in the testes of this 1 patient out of 12 analyzed patients. Four other cancer patients (hepatocellular carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma of the face, lung carcinoma, gastric adenocarcinoma) in that study did not have SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA in their testes postmortem. Nevertheless, for all of these patients, there was significant injury to the Sertoli cells and seminiferous tubules, reduction of Leydig cells, and inflammatory infiltrate in the interstitium. This may explain the secondary hypogonadism, defined by decreased testosterone levels, associated with COVID‐19 in about half of patients during and after recovery from COVID‐19 [35, 36, 37, 38]. Unfortunately, the serum level of testosterone was not measured for all patients in our study since during the acute pandemic period, this analysis was not available in all the participating centers.

Most studies have reported alterations in the standard semen parameters during the course of COVID‐19 [10, 39, 40]. Whether this impairment is due to direct or indirect effects of viral infection is not clear. Specific pathogenic mechanisms of the virus, inflammation of the testes and/or accessory glands, impairment of spermatogenesis with endocrine disruption, and oxidative stress due to inflammation are different factors that may be involved [6]. A recent meta‐analysis showed that COVID‐19 with fever reduces sperm concentration and progressive motility of spermatozoa [4]. For the patients with COVID‐19 without fever ≥ 90 days, the total and progressive motility of spermatozoa decreased. Moreover, Donders et al. [10] showed that, although SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA was not detected in semen, altered standard parameters were observed. In our study, the analysis of sperm parameters showed that the total and progressive motility of spermatozoa was moderately reduced in cancer patients with COVID‐19 (PCR+ on nasopharyngeal swab) compared with uninfected or seropositive patients. As demonstrated in previous studies [41], we observed an impact of COVID‐19 on semen volume. Autopsy samples from COVID‐19 patients [12] showed alterations in the expression of different molecules, such as insulin‐like factor 3, which is usually expressed in Leydig cells [42]; E3 ubiquitin protein ligase, which is essential for spermatogenesis [9]; and the dynein regulatory complex subunit 7, which is necessary for spermatozoa motility [43]. In 7%–10% of severe COVID‐19 patients, symptoms of orchitis or orchiepididymidis were reported [41], which may explain the alterations in semen parameters. In mild to moderate cases, color Doppler ultrasound showed typical signs of epididymitis for approximately 40% of the men [44], though they did not always have symptoms. These deleterious effects on male fertility seem to also mainly be mediated by inflammation and immunological responses rather than viral infection and are described as being transient in non‐cancer patients [45, 46]. Further studies are required to determine the duration of the impact of COVID‐19 on male fertility in cancer patients not only by analyzing standard sperm parameters, but also sperm integrity, such as by sperm DNA fragmentation or epigenetic status.

SARS‐CoV‐2 infection modifies immunological characteristics and increases the systemic level of inflammatory mediators. The severity of COVID‐19 is associated with exaggerated immune responses described as a “cytokine storm”. This is followed by the excessive production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐6 and TNF‐α [47], to protect the host from infection. Nevertheless, this excessive relapse of cytokines could injure organ functions. Typically, cytokines are essential for maintenance of the structural and functional integrity of the testis [48], but they may impair spermatogenesis if they are present in excess and infiltrate the hematotesticular barrier. Moreover, an excess of cytokines induces inflammation, with oxidative stress that may alter sperm maturation in the epididymis [44]. Individuals with cancer have modified immunity due to cancer itself and after treatment [49]. In our study, we chose to compare the cytokine profiles in the seminal fluid of PCR+ and uninfected cancer patients. This comparative study was only possible for patients with lymphoma because of our recruitment process. Lymphomas are a heterogeneous group of neoplasms that affect the production and functions of lymphocytes. To the best of our knowledge, the analysis of cytokines profiles has never been performed in the seminal plasma of lymphoma patients. Additionally, measurement of IL and TNF‐alpha in patient serum, alongside the measurements in semen, could not be performed due to the absence of corresponding serum samples. Nevertheless, we showed by our study higher expression of TNF‐α in the seminal fluid of lymphoma patients infected by SARS‐CoV‐2 even though they were asymptomatic. TNF‐α has been shown to trigger the activation of NF‐kB and DAX‐1 to inhibit transactivation of the orphan nuclear receptors involved in steroidogenesis [50]. Moreover, pro‐inflammatory cytokines can dysregulate cld11 in Sertoli cells [51]. Studying a larger cohort of cancer patients could be interesting to better understand the pathophysiology.

In conclusion, it seems that SARS‐CoV‐2 is not present in the spermatozoa or seminal fluid of cancer patients who benefit from sperm cryopreservation before the initiation of cancer treatment when they are asymptomatic at the time of sperm collection. Nevertheless, larger multicenter studies are necessary to determine the long‐term consequences of COVID‐19 on male fertility, especially sperm quality function and possible deleterious effects of inflammation, especially in symptomatic COVID‐19 patients with positive nasal swabs.
