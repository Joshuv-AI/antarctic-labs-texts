Double-bundle anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) and anterolateral ligament reconstruction with suture tape augmentation offers satisfactory 2-year clinical functional scores between single-bundle ACLR and double-bundle ACLR with more lateral knee tightness
Authors: Lei Hsia, Poyu Chen, Cheng-Pang Yang, Chen-Heng Hsu, Chieh-An Chuang, Joe Chih-Hao Chiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSingle-bundle (SB) anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR), double-bundle (DB) ACLR, and DB ACLR combined with anterolateral ligament reconstruction (DB ACLR and ALLR) have been developed to address ACL tears. We hypothesized that these three techniques provide satisfactory results for patients with ACL tears, with the DB ACL and ALLR group having more lateral knee discomfort.Methods167 patients were retrospectively divided into three groups: SB ACLR, DB ACLR, and DB ACLR and ALLR. Postoperative evaluations were conducted via telephone interviews and in-person consultations using the Tegner and Lysholm scores.ResultsThe mean follow-up time was 27.7 ± 4.4 months in the SB ACLR group, 25.5 ± 3.2 months in the DB ACLR group, and 28.1 ± 3.6 months in the DB ACLR and ALLR group (P = 0.044). For Lysholm score, the DB ACLR and ALLR group (n = 55) was 92.3 ± 7.0, the DB ACLR group (n = 21) was 92.4 ± 6.6, and the SB ACLR group (n = 91) was 92.5 ± 10.2 (P = 0.995). For the Tegner score, the DB ACLR and ALLR group was 4.5 ± 2.2, the DB ACLR group was 5.0 ± 1.6, and the SB ACLR group was 4.4 ± 1.7 (P = 0.378). In the DB ACLR and ALLR group, 10 of the 55 (18.2%) patients exhibited symptoms in the lateral knee.ConclusionsSB ACLR, DB ACLR, and DB ACLR and ALLR with suture tape augmentation procedures yielded comparable clinical functional scores. While lateral knee symptoms were observed in patients receiving DB ACLR and ALLR with suture tape augmentation, these symptoms did not significantly influence the functional outcome.Keywords: ACL, ALL, Double-bundle, Single-bundle, Complications, Lateral knee tightness

Introduction

Both single-bundle (SB) anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) and double-bundle (DB) ACLR have evolved from isometric to anatomic reconstruction methods. This evolution aims to provide enhanced rotational stability and reduce the incidence of early knee osteoarthritis [1, 2]. Anatomic DB ACLR achieved better postoperative results in rotational stability than anatomic SB ACLR, but their clinical outcomes and risk of graft failure were still far from excellent [3]. Only 65% of athletes could reach a preinjury level after ACLR, and graft failure rate still remains 1.4% to 18% [4].

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of the anterolateral ligament (ALL). They demonstrate that reconstructing the ALL can control rotational laxity and share the load with the ACL graft [5]. As a result, the anatomic SB ACL combined with ALL reconstruction (ALLR) technique was developed. This combined approach has shown the potential to reduce the graft rupture rate and have greater odds of returning to preinjury levels of sport [3]. Furthermore, the DB ACLR combined with ALLR was developed to harness the benefits of both techniques: the potential superior knee function and stability offered by DB ACLR compared to SB ACLR, and the synergistic function of ALLR with the ACL in enhancing knee rotational stability [6]. However, there is still a paucity of reports comparing this technique between SB ACLR and DB ACLR.

The objective of the study is to report the clinical results following these three techniques for ACL tears. We hypothesized that these three techniques provide satisfactory results, while the DB ACLR and ALLR would have more lateral knee tightness due to the augmentation of the lateral part of the knee compared to other techniques.

Methods

Participants

This was a retrospective, questionnaire-based study. We enrolled patients receiving SB ACLR, DB ACLR and DB ACLR and ALLR between September 2011 and October 2021 from a single surgeon in one institute. All patients received an ipsilateral semitendinosus and gracilis autograft for the reconstruction and had at least 2-year follow-ups. The indications for ACLR with or without ALLR were patients who had a grade 2 pivot shift or greater, athletes participating in pivoting contact sports, a side-to-side difference in anterior tibial translation between the injured and uninjured knee > 10 mm, and the length of the ACL footprint on the femoral and tibial sides must be > 14 mm [7]. All patients who received DB ACLR and ALLR were after January 2019, after the technique [8] was developed. Before that, patients were assigned to SB and DB ACLR according to their demographic data. Patients with a history of prior knee surgery, anterior knee symptoms, neurologic injury or ligamentous injuries other than ACL were excluded.

Surgical techniques

SB ACLR

In patients in the SB ACLR group, the autologous semitendinosus and gracilis graft were fixed with an Endobutton CL (Smith & Nephew Endoscopy, Andover, MA) in the femoral side and interference screws (BioRCI-HA screws; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) in the tibia tunnel (Fig. 1A).

SB, single bundle; DB, double bundle; ACLR, anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction; ALLR, anterolateral ligament reconstruction; AMB, anteromedial bundle; PLB, posterolateral bundle

DB ACLR

Patients in the DB ACLR group had 2 femoral tunnels and 2 tibia tunnels, each tunnel was [9]fixed with an interference screw matched to their diameter (Fig. 1B).

DB ACLR and ALLR

The autologous semitendinosus was prepared in a triple folded fashion as an anteromedial bundle (AMB) and single gracilis in full length along with FiberTape (from one Biocomposite Swivelock C anchor, Arthrex, Naples, FL) as posterolateral bundle (PLB) intraarticularly and ALL extra-articularly. The femoral AMB tunnel was prepared by a Clancy curved drill guide (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) in the inside-out fashion, while PLB tunnel was drilled by an outside-in fashion. This technique ensured the femoral AM and PL tunnels to be positioned divergent from each other. One tibial ACL tunnel was then created in the center of ACL tibial insertion, and one tibial ALL tunnel was made posteriorly and proximally to the Gerdy’s tubercle. The AMB graft (triple semitendinosus) was fixed by a Endobutton CL on the femoral cortex and an interference screw at the tibial site. The PLB graft (single gracilis and FiberTape) was fixed by a 6 × 25 mm hydroxyapatite interference screws (BioRCI-HA) at the femoral side in an outside-in manner. Then another hydroxyapatite interference screw one millimeter larger than the drilled tibial ACL tunnel was used to fix the tibial end in the tibial ACL tunnel of both grafts and FiberTape at 60° of knee flexion with maximal tension on the sutures. At last, the single gracilis and FiberTape were shuttled from the lateral end of the femoral PLB tunnel, underneath the iliotibial band, to the lateral end of the tibial ALL tunnel as the ALLR. They were then shuttled through the tibial ALL tunnel from the lateral to the medial side of the tibia, making sure both gracilis and FiberTape were inside the ALL tibial tunnel. Then both limbs of No.2 Ethibond sutures of the gracillis and FiberTape were tied together at 0° of knee flexion and neutral knee rotation. Finally, all sutures were tied and fixed intraosseous by a 4.75 mm Biocomposite Swivelock C anchor (Swivelock C, Arthrex, Naples, FL) [8].

Patients in three groups received a brace-free mobilization and tolerated full weight-bearing and progressive range of motion (ROM) exercise immediately after the surgery. A gradual return to sports activities was allowed starting at 4 months for nonpivoting sports, 6 months for pivoting noncontact sports, and 8–9 months for pivoting contact sports [10], regardless of the meniscal treatment intraoperatively.

Outcome measures

Clinical evaluations were performed preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively during in-person consultation using Lysholm [11], and Tegner Activity Scale [12]. Included items were limping, using cranes or crutches, locking and giving away sensation in the knee, pain, swelling, function of climbing stairs and squatting, and activity grading evaluated based on work and sports activities. An additional question would be asked if they had femoral or tibial ALL insertion site tightness [9]. If yes, it would be classified into patients with lateral knee tightness. A telephone interview was conducted to assess saphenous nerve symptoms and lateral knee tightness in the SB ACLR and DB ACLR groups among patients who had undergone surgery earlier with a 2-year in-person consultation but were not able to attend in-person consultations at the last follow-up. Knee ROM data were collected preoperatively, 2 weeks, 9 months, and 2 years postoperatively by an independent orthopedic doctor blinded to the type of surgery and rehabilitation. Radiographs were obtained at 0-month and 2-year postoperatively to confirm the tunnel position by a radiologist with > 10 years of experience.

Adverse events

Since all patients used autologous hamstring tendons for the reconstruction, we also evaluated saphenous neuralgia [13], characterized by persistent neuropathic pain along the distribution of saphenous nerve following surgery. The post-operative saphenous syndrome was investigated by using items including pretibial area pain, numbness, or loss of sensory. Any other signs of clinical problems, such as wound or lateral knee tightness, were also recorded.

Statistics

All analyses were made with IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Values are shown as mean ± standard deviation for continuous data and number (%) for categorical variables. One-way student t-test was used for two-group comparisons, and one-way ANOVA was used for multiple-group comparisons. For all comparisons, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To determine the adequacy of our sample size for detecting a statistically significant effect, we conducted a power analysis for a two-sample t-test comparing the means of the ALLR and non-ALLR groups. The analysis was performed with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a desired power of 0.8. We assumed an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.65 based on prior research and the expected difference between the groups. Using the established parameters, the analysis indicated that a total sample size of approximately 35 participants is needed to detect the specified effect size with 80% power at the 0.05 significance level.

Results

Patient characteristics

Initially, 285 patients were enrolled, with 167 patients meeting the inclusion criteria, comprising 91 patients in the SB ACLR subgroup, 21 in the DB ACLR subgroup, and 55 in the DB ACLR and ALLR subgroup (Fig. 2). All patients in the DB ACLR and ALLR were operated on after 2019 since the technique [8] was developed.

CONSORT flow diagram for the study

The mean age for the study cohort was 30.4 ± 11.2 years (range, 15–61 years); 64.7% (n = 108) were male. The mean follow-up time for in-person consultation was 27.7 ± 4.4 months in the SB ACLR group, 25.5 ± 3.2 months in the DB ACLR group, and 28.1 ± 3.5 months in the DB ACLR and ALLR group. The mean telephone interview time for saphenous nerve symptoms and lateral knee tightness was 74.6 ± 5.3 months in the SB ACLR group, 113.1 ± 7.9 months in the DB ACLR group, and 28.1 ± 3.5 months in the DB ACLR and ALLR group. There was no significant difference between the groups with respect to the proportion of patients with meniscus repair (P = 0.087). However, there were significant differences between the groups regarding sex (P = 0.007) (male patients: SB ACLR: 60.4%, DB ACLR: 95.2%, DB ACLR and ALLR: 60.0%), age (P = 0.007), and type of sport (P = 0.015) (pivoting sports: SB ACLR: 28.6%, DB ACLR: 61.9%, DB ACLR and ALLR:38.2%). Discussion

This retrospective study confirmed our hypothesis that all three surgical techniques yielded satisfactory Tegner and Lysholm scores in patients with ACL tears, irrespective of meniscus repair. Another observation was that lateral knee tightness was exclusive to the DB ACLR and ALLR group, but this did not significantly impact the clinical outcome.

In recent years, the significance of the anterolateral complex has garnered increased attention [14]. Cheng et al. found that a combination of ACLR and ALLR led to significant post-operative improvements in functional outcomes, as assessed by subjective IKDC and Lysholm scores [15]. Our research found no significant difference in functional scores between the three groups. This suggests that all three surgical approaches yielded satisfactory outcomes for patients with ACL ruptures, aligning with our initial hypothesis.

There are two notable observations. Firstly, the mean age for both DB ACLR and ALLR (27.9 ± 10.3) and DB ACLR (29.0 ± 8.2) groups was significantly lower than that of the SB ACLR (32.2 ± 12.1) group (P = 0.007). This suggests younger patients with higher functional demands or engagement in competitive sports tend to have an additional ALLR [9]. Secondly, a larger percentage of patients engaged in pivoting sports underwent DB ACLR (61.9%) compared to those in the SB ACLR group (28.6%). This is consistent with findings that DB ACLR enhances knee rotational stability [16]. Interestingly, the DB ACLR and ALLR group featured participants from both rotational (38.2%) and non-rotational (61.8%) sports. This diversity suggests that the combined ACLR and ALLR approach is becoming a universal technique for treating ACL tears, especially given that it’s been linked to a significant decrease in ACL graft failure rates compared to isolated ACLR [3].

In our research, a notable observation was that 10 out of the 55 (18.2%) patients in the DB ACLR and ALLR group experienced lateral knee tightness post-surgery. We suspect this may be attributed to the use of FiberTape in the lateral part of the knee. This finding has parallels in existing literature. For instance, Panisset et al. observed specific pain at femoral and tibial sites in the initial months following lateral tenodesis reconstruction [9]. Subsequent check-ups revealed pain rates of 0.8% and 0.6% at 6 months and 0.2% for both sites at the one-year mark. Cumulatively, pain was experienced by 2.4% at the femoral site and 1.2% at the tibial site within the first year [17]. Imbert et al. pinpointed the source of this discomfort to impingement with the fixation hardware at the ALLR attachment site [18]. Notably, no existing studies have delved into the connection between lateral knee tightness and functional outcomes. In our analysis, no significant differences were found in the Lysholm and Tegner scores when comparing patients with and without lateral knee tightness. This suggests that such tightness does not critically impact clinical function.

In examining the specific variables of the Lysholm score, patients with lateral knee tightness registered a marginally lower score (9.2 ± 1.7) in stair-climbing activities compared to those without the pain (9.8 ± 0.8). However, the difference between the two groups wasn’t statistically significant. The pain has been attributed to impingement at the ALLR attachment site in existing literature. Some cases from past studies even underwent revision surgery to remove the lateral knee bioscrew [17]. In our cohort, only one patient (1.8%, 1/55) had a lateral knee bioscrew removed, but this was due to screw breakage. This finding is in line with the observations of Panisset et al., who reported a 1.7% surgical intervention rate specific to the ALLR, which included removal of both femoral and tibial screws [9]. In summary, while lateral knee tightness might arise as a post-operative concern following ACLR and ALLR, it doesn’t significantly impact the overall functional outcome.

In our study, a notable proportion of patients reported saphenous nerve symptoms post-operatively. Specifically, 73.3% (38/55) of the DB ACLR and ALLR group experienced these symptoms. This prevalence contrasts with the SB ACLR and DB ACLR groups, where the rates were 43.9% (40/91) and 38.1% (8/21), respectively. This sensory disturbance is a known post-operative concern, with previous studies reporting its occurrence in 55% to 88% of patients, lingering even 12 months post-surgery [19, 20]. A potential reason for the higher rate in the DB ACLR and ALLR group might be their shorter follow-up durations, as these patients underwent surgeries post-2019. Given that around 76% of patients can anticipate a reduction in hypoesthesia within the first year [20], and another 10% see improvement after a year [19], it’s plausible that extended follow-up would reveal diminishing saphenous nerve symptoms for the DB ACLR and ALLR group.

Limitations

First, this is a non-randomized, retrospective study. Second, the study’s sample size is limited, as only 167 out of 285 patients were included in the analysis. Additionally, the uneven distribution across groups may introduce selection bias. Third, the DB ACLR and ALLR group had a notably shorter mean follow-up time of 28.1 ± 3.5 months. In contrast, the SB ACLR and DB ACLR groups had longer follow-ups of 74.6 ± 5.3 months and 113.1 ± 7.9 months, respectively. A longer follow-up period is necessary to draw more precise conclusions when comparing the outcomes of these three surgical techniques. Fourth, double-bundle procedures were significantly more common in male patients participating in pivoting sports because the relatively smaller femoral ACL footprint in female patients discouraged surgeons from selecting DB ACLR. It was also a surgeon’s bias in patient selection.

Conclusion

SB ACLR, DB ACLR, and DB ACLR and ALLR with suture tape augmentation procedures yielded comparable clinical functional scores. While lateral knee symptoms were observed in patients receiving DB ACLR and ALLR with suture tape augmentation, these symptoms did not significantly influence the functional outcome.
