Congenital Melanocytic Nevus of the Nose: Clinical Experience and Reconstructive Approaches
Authors: Moris Topaz
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

I read with interest the recent article by Margulis et al, “Congenital Melanocytic Nevus of the Nose: Clinical Experience and Reconstructive Approaches.”1 The authors present insights into the challenges of managing congenital nevi in the nasal region. However, we are compelled to correct a factual inaccuracy in their interpretation of our previously published case report, which was referenced in their article.2

The authors state:

A novel skin-stretching device has been described for reconstruction after large forehead congenital nevus excision. Although this device did help close the postexcisional defect, the mechanical creep attained with this device apparently did not produce sufficient supple skin and ended up distorting key facial landmarks.

This characterization is inaccurate and does not reflect the methodology and outcomes reported in our case. The large forehead nevus was excised through staged simple excisions beginning at 3 months of age (Fig. 1A). In each stage, the TopClosure Tension-Relief System (TRS) was applied preoperatively for several days to achieve multivector, noninvasive skin expansion via mechanical creep, facilitating staged excision and primary closure. Intraoperatively, a stress relaxation technique was used under general anesthesia for rapid tissue expansion. Postoperatively, the TRS device secured the wound, maintaining tension relief and promoting optimal, tension-free healing. This approach eliminated the need for flaps, grafts, or conventional tissue expanders.

Representative clinical images illustrating the long-term outcome of staged excision and reconstruction of a large congenital forehead nevus using the TopClosure TRS. A, Preoperative appearance at 3 months of age, showing a large congenital nevus involving the central and lateral forehead as well as the glabellar region, before staged excisions. B, Long-term postoperative result at 10 years of age, 8 years after the completion of reconstruction. Note the excellent cosmetic outcome with preserved facial landmarks and minimal scarring.

Importantly, no substantial distortion of key facial landmarks was observed at any stage of the reconstructive process. Unlike conventional expanders, which cause temporary but pronounced facial disfigurement, our approach used an externally adhered skin-stretching device applied for several days to a week in the preoperative period, which did not result in significant distortion. The stretching technique is mainly noninvasive and associated with no discomfort or pain, as gradual expansion occurs without breaching the skin.

The cosmetic outcome was excellent, as shown in postoperative photographs taken at age 2, marking the completion of the reconstruction.2 This is further supported by an 8-year follow-up image at age 10 (Fig. 1B). Notably, the child has no recollection of the reconstructive process, which was completed entirely before age 2, offering both functional and psychological benefits.

Although all surgical interventions inherently carry some risk of altering facial contours and scarring, our technique demonstrated both clinical efficacy and long-term aesthetic success. We emphasize the importance of considering simple, minimally invasive, staged soft-tissue expansion at an early age as a valid and often preferable alternative to more complex procedures in the management of large congenital nevi and general pediatric procedures.

Furthermore, the efficacy of the TRS in pediatric patients has been independently corroborated. Olshinka et al3 reported successful application of the TRS in 21 pediatric patients with challenging scalp wounds, demonstrating its utility in facilitating primary closure without the need for more invasive procedures.

In light of the misrepresentation of our work, we respectfully request that this letter be published as a formal correction, in the interest of scientific accuracy and to properly inform your readership.
