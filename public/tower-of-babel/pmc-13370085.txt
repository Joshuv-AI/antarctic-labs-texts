An ordinary differential equation-based integrated pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic model for predicting anticoagulant responses to warfarin and diphacinone across rodent and bat species
Authors: Misaki FUKAMATSU, Moyu MIYAMAE, Ryo KAMATA, Kazuki TAKEDA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterspecies differences in sensitivity to anticoagulant compounds remain an important issue in veterinary toxicology and wildlife risk assessment. In the present study, we developed an integrated pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) model to quantitatively compare anticoagulant responses between rats and bats, linking hepatic exposure to vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR) inhibition and subsequent changes in prothrombin time–international normalized ratio (PT-INR). Species-specific pharmacokinetic parameters were estimated from experimental concentration–time data and incorporated into a mechanistic turnover model describing VKOR activity and clotting factor synthesis. The model reproduced dose-dependent PT-INR prolongation in rats and successfully described the delayed and attenuated anticoagulant responses observed in bats. Sensitivity analyses identified hepatic clearance (CLh), hepatic distribution volume (Vh), and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration for VKOR (IC50) as key determinants of PT-INR responses, with a smaller contribution from baseline coagulation turnover. In addition, structure-based binding affinity estimates were explored as surrogate indicators of inhibitory potency. Although computational affinity scores qualitatively reflected relative anticoagulant strength, substituting these scores for experimentally derived inhibitory parameters reduced predictive accuracy in some resistant species, highlighting the current limitations of structure-only approaches. These findings demonstrate that mechanistic PK/PD integration provides a quantitative framework for evaluating species differences in anticoagulant toxicity and may support veterinary risk assessment and wildlife protection strategies.Keywords: anticoagulant, cross-species, pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic modeling, vitamin K epoxide reductase

INTRODUCTION

Wild rodents act as vectors of numerous zoonotic diseases and are also major pests causing severe damage to crops and ecosystems [4, 6]. Consequently, rodent control has become an urgent global issue, and rodenticides have been widely employed as a chemical control measure. Among them, anticoagulant rodenticides (ARs), exemplified by warfarin, have served as a primary method for rodent management worldwide since the 1950s, being applied not only in urban and agricultural settings but also in island eradication programs targeting invasive rodents [9, 11]. ARs such as warfarin inhibit VKOR, thereby suppressing the production of vitamin K–dependent coagulation factors, leading to impaired blood coagulation and ultimately lethal hemorrhage [14]. Because AR toxicity requires depletion of coagulation factors synthesized prior to ingestion, a substantial time lag occurs between AR intake and mortality, with lethal outcomes delayed by approximately 6–9 days in rats (Rattus norvegicus) [8].

However, the extensive use of ARs has raised major concerns. The first is the emergence of resistance in target rodent populations. Resistance to warfarin was reported as early as the 1950s following its prolonged use [3, 20]. In response, second-generation anticoagulant rodenticides (SGARs), often referred to as “superwarfarins,” were developed and introduced in the 1970s to provide higher potency and persistence [5]. Although SGARs were initially effective even against resistant rodents with a single ingestion, resistance to SGARs has since been documented in several regions, and today a substantial proportion of wild rodent populations in Europe and North America harbor resistance-associated mutations [1, 7, 16, 18]. Resistance is primarily attributed to mutations in vkorc1, the gene encoding VKOR. Representative amino acid substitutions include Y139C and Y139F, with region-specific variants reported in Europe, China, and elsewhere [13]. In addition, metabolic resistance arising from upregulated cytochrome P450 (CYP) expression has been described, whereby enhanced drug metabolism contributes to resistance [14]. Notably, an L76P mutation has been identified in Japanese black rats (Rattus rattus), conferring significant resistance to warfarin while simultaneously reducing VKOR activity [24]. The second concern is the toxic impact on non-target wildlife and pets. Both primary poisoning (direct ingestion of AR-containing baits) and secondary poisoning (predation of AR-exposed rodents) have been reported to cause morbidity and mortality in raptors and other species, with AR residues being frequently detected in raptor populations worldwide [19]. Given the substantial interspecific and regional variability in AR efficacy and risk, it is essential to assess the sensitivity of both native wildlife and invasive rodent species prior to AR application in the environment.

Species differences in AR sensitivity have also been documented. For example, the Egyptian rousette bat (Rousettus aegyptiacus) exhibits relative resistance to warfarin but is highly susceptible to diphacinone, another first-generation AR [25]. The anticoagulant effect of ARs, manifested as prolongation of prothrombin time, can be quantitatively assessed by in vivo blood coagulation assays such as the PT-INR. Because first-generation ARs are generally metabolized and excreted more rapidly and possess shorter half-lives, multiple feedings are often required to reach lethal doses. Thus, combining single-dose experiments with PK/PD analyses provides a valuable approach to evaluating these pharmacological properties and interspecies sensitivities [2]. Indeed, Takeda et al. combined single-dose experiments with PK/PD modeling to compare warfarin-sensitive rats, warfarin-resistant rats, and wild rats from the Ogasawara Islands. Here, warfarin-resistant rats refer to a population originating from Tokyo that carries the Leu76Pro mutation in VKOR, whereas warfarin-sensitive rats denote a population lacking this mutation and exhibiting normal susceptibility to warfarin. As resistance to SGARs has not been confirmed in this population, the terms “FGAR-resistant” and “FGAR-sensitive” are used throughout this study to indicate resistance and susceptibility to first-generation anticoagulant rodenticides, respectively. They demonstrated marked differences among strains and species in recovery rates from coagulation impairment and in drug elimination kinetics [26]. Such single-dose experiments with PK/PD analyses represent a useful tool for quantitatively evaluating inter- and intra-species variation in AR sensitivity and resistance.

Nevertheless, direct administration of toxicants to rare or endangered wildlife species to test susceptibility is ethically and legally unfeasible. For instance, the Bonin flying fox (Pteropus pselaphon), an endemic species of the Ogasawara Islands with an estimated population of only 400 individuals [27], cannot ethically be subjected to harmful experimentation. Yet the sensitivity of such rare endemic species to ARs remains largely unknown, representing a major source of uncertainty in ecological risk assessments of AR deployment. Furthermore, conventional approaches have been insufficient to systematically and quantitatively compare or predict pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic responses to ARs between sensitive and resistant individuals within the same species. To address these gaps, the present study aimed to develop predictive PK/PD models by integrating in vitro data, computational simulations, and differential equation–based modeling. Specifically, we conducted PK/PD analyses of four animal groups—one bat species (the Egyptian rousette bat) and three rodent strains (Sprague–Dawley rats, FGAR-resistant black rats, and FGAR-sensitive black rats)—to evaluate interspecies differences in susceptibility to first-generation ARs. The model was constructed to simulate coagulation time dynamics from VKOR inhibition data, and its performance was evaluated against previously reported PT-INR time-course data. Our findings highlight that this modeling framework may serve as a data-sparing and reduced-animal-use approach for assessing AR effects in ecologically important wildlife, including rare and endangered species, while recognizing that some parameterization may still require experimental or surrogate-species data.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and analytical workflow

In this study, we performed PK/PD analyses of ARs and constructed an integrated PK/PD model to describe the temporal dynamics of PT-INR. The target compounds were the first-generation ARs diphacinone and warfarin, and the target species were Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, FGAR-sensitive black rats, FGAR-resistant black rats, and the Egyptian rousette bat. Baseline physiological parameters were defined as a common set across all species (Table 1), whereas species-specific differences were incorporated through IC50 values, body weight, dose, and optimized hepatic parameters. Initial PT-INR predictions were generated using the PK/PD model. We then performed global and local sensitivity analyses to identify influential parameters, followed by parameter optimization using a grid-search approach. Model fit to observed data was evaluated using goodness-of-fit metrics. In addition, as an in silico extension, predicted VKOR binding affinity values were introduced as surrogate parameters for IC50 to examine the feasibility of non-invasive estimation of inhibitory properties.

Source datasets and experimental conditions

The data used in this study were obtained from four previous studies (Studies I–IV) evaluating the sensitivity of rodents and wildlife to first-generation ARs [23,24,25,26]. The analyzed animal groups included SD rats, FGAR-sensitive black rats, FGAR-resistant black rats, and the Egyptian rousette bat. In Study I, SD rats, wild Ogasawara black rats, FGAR-sensitive black rats, and FGAR-resistant black rats were administered a single oral dose of diphacinone (0.5 mg/kg), and plasma diphacinone concentrations and PT-INR were measured [26]. The SD rats used were approximately 10 weeks old, whereas the FGAR-sensitive and FGAR-resistant black rats were adult males derived from established colonies. Body weights of FGAR-sensitive and FGAR-resistant black rats were 134.3 ± 4.0 g and 128.6 ± 11.7 g, respectively. In Study II, SD rats and Egyptian rousette bats were administered a single oral dose of diphacinone (4 mg/kg) or warfarin (2 mg/kg), and plasma drug concentrations and PT-INR were determined. In addition, hepatic microsomes were prepared for VKOR inhibition assays using diphacinone and warfarin [25]. All SD rats were female (approximately 10 weeks old, body weight ~195 ± 9.6 g for the warfarin administration study), and the Egyptian rousette bats were adult females with an average body weight of 124 ± 2.1 g. Animals were maintained under controlled temperature and light–dark cycles with ad libitum feeding prior to experimentation. In Study III, FGAR-sensitive and FGAR-resistant black rats were administered warfarin (10 mg/kg) either orally or intravenously. Plasma warfarin concentrations and PT-INR were measured, followed by PK/PD analyses [23]. The rats used in this study were male individuals, with a mean body weight of 112.9 ± 3.1 g and an estimated age of 208.3 ± 27.9 days. In Study IV, VKOR inhibition assays with warfarin were performed using hepatic microsomes from FGAR-sensitive and FGAR-resistant black rats [24]. The animals used for microsomal preparation were male rats, with a mean body weight of 120.4 ± 10.5 g and an estimated age of 180.5 ± 40.2 days, and were maintained under standard laboratory conditions prior to tissue collection.

Integrated ODE-based PK/PD model overview and assumptions

An integrated PK/PD model was constructed to describe PT-INR time courses after AR administration. The model (Fig. 1) was formulated as a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and consisted of three layers: (i) drug disposition (absorption, distribution, and metabolism), (ii) concentration-dependent inhibition of VKOR, and (iii) changes in coagulation factor dynamics leading to PT-INR responses. As shown in Fig. 1, the PK model consists of an absorption compartment (Ac), a central compartment (Cc), and a hepatic compartment (Ch). The PD model describes inhibition of hepatic VKOR activity (VKORa) by ARs using a Hill-type function, followed by changes in coagulation factor levels (Cf), and finally conversion of Cf to PT-INR using an Emax model. In addition, differences in dose levels were incorporated into the model as variations in the initial drug amount (Ac0), which was determined based on species-specific body weight and administered dose.

Structural diagram of the pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) model for warfarin and related anticoagulants. The compartments shown in blue represent the pharmacokinetic (PK) model, whereas those in red represent the pharmacodynamic (PD) model. The PK model consists of an absorption compartment (Ac), a central compartment (Cc), and a hepatic compartment (Ch). The drug is absorbed according to a first-order rate constant (Ka), followed by systemic distribution and elimination via hepatic blood flow (Qh), hepatic clearance (CLh), and total clearance (CL). In contrast, the PD model describes the inhibition of hepatic vitamin K epoxide reductase activity (VKORa) by drug concentration, represented as a Hill-type function defined by the half-maximal inhibitory concentration for VKOR (IC50) and Hill coefficient for VKOR inhibition (γ). VKORa is governed by the synthesis rate constant (ksyn) and degradation rate constant (kdeg), and coagulation factor levels (Cf) are regulated through VKORa-dependent synthesis and degradation. Furthermore, prothrombin time–international normalized ratio (PT-INR) is expressed as a function of Cf using a maximum-effect (Emax) model, with Emax, EC50, and γ as parameters.

PK submodel

The PK component was implemented as a two-compartment disposition model with a first-order oral absorption process. In this model, the drug is first introduced into Ac and transferred to Cc according to the first-order absorption rate constant (ka) (Equation 1). The drug in the central compartment is distributed to the liver (hepatic/peripheral compartment, Ch) via hepatic blood flow (Qh) and eliminated from the body through total clearance (CL) (Equation 2). The drug in the hepatic compartment is further metabolized via CLh (Equation 3). The temporal changes in drug concentration in each compartment were described by the following ODEs:

dAcdt=−kaAc (1)

dCcdt=kaAcVc−(QhVc+CLVc)Cc+QhVcCh (2)

dChdt=QhVhCc−(QhVh+CLhVh)Ch (3)

In these equations, Vc and Vh represent the volumes of distribution for the central and hepatic compartments, respectively.

PD submodel (VKOR and coagulation factor dynamics)

The PD component describes inhibition of VKOR activity in the liver as a concentration-dependent process using a Hill-type inhibition function. VKOR is synthesized at a zero-order rate constant (ksyn), and its activity is inhibited in a concentration-dependent manner in the presence of ARs. VKOR degradation follows a first-order rate constant (kdeg) and is described by Equation (4).

dVKORadt=ksyn(1−VKORa)−kdegVKORa(ChγIC50γ+Chγ) (4)

Here, Ch denotes the drug concentration in the liver (nM), IC50 is the drug concentration that inhibits 50% of VKOR activity, and γ is the Hill coefficient determining the slope of the inhibition curve.

Cf, which is synthesized in proportion to VKOR activity, changes over time according to Equation (5):

dCfdt=ksyn fVKORaCf0−kdeg fCf (5)

Here, ksyn_f is the synthesis rate constant of coagulation factors dependent on VKORa, and kdeg_f is the degradation rate constant of coagulation factors based on their half-life.

Observation model for PT-INR

The decrease in coagulation factor concentration was translated into an increase in PT-INR using a nonlinear Emax model (Equation 6):

PT_INR=PT_INRbase+Emax(1−CfγPTINREC50γPTINR+CfγPTINR) (6)

Here, Emax represents the maximum increase in PT-INR, EC50 is the coagulation factor concentration corresponding to 50% of the maximum effect, and γ is the Hill coefficient indicating the steepness of the response.

Parameterization and baseline parameter sets

Pharmacokinetic parameters (CL, Vc, Qh, and ka) were set as shown in Table 1. The synthesis (ksyn) and degradation (kdeg) rate constants for VKOR activity were assigned values that could reproduce the temporal changes in VKOR activity following AR administration. Parameters related to the hepatic compartment (Vh and CLh) were treated as adjustable parameters and optimized by grid search, as described below. The half-lives of vitamin K–dependent coagulation factors II, VII, IX, and X in rats have been reported to range from 2.6 to 9.0 hr [28]. Therefore, the synthesis and degradation rate constants of the coagulation factor pool (ksyn_f and kdeg_f) were assumed to approximate a physiological half-life of 8 hr and were set to ksyn_f=kdeg_f=ln2/8. Regulation of coagulation factor synthesis by VKOR inhibition was assumed to be proportional to VKORa and was represented using a Hill function of hepatic AR concentration.

IC50 values for diphacinone and warfarin were assigned with reference to previously reported data where available (Table 2). For diphacinone, IC50 values for FGAR-sensitive and FGAR-resistant black rats were not available from direct experimental measurements and were therefore determined by grid-search optimization. Specifically, IC50 values were varied over predefined ranges (FGAR-sensitive: 5–150 nM; FGAR-resistant: 60–2,500 nM), and model simulations were conducted in combination with grid-search optimization of Vh and CLh. Model performance was evaluated based on the goodness-of-fit to observed PT-INR data using the coefficient of determination (R2) and Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), and the IC50 values yielding the best combined performance metrics were selected as optimized parameters for subsequent simulations.

Tunable parameters and optimization targets

To model PT-INR responses, a nonlinear Emax model was used with maximum effect (Emax), the coagulation factor concentration at 50% of maximum effect (EC50), and a sensitivity coefficient (Hill coefficient; γ_PT-INR) as parameters. These values were treated as hypothetical estimates and were optimized in combination with grid-search optimization of hepatic Vh and CLh to improve agreement with observed PT-INR values.

Global sensitivity analysis (Sobol and Morris)

Global sensitivity analysis was performed for the PK/PD model using the variance-based Sobol’ method and the screening-based Morris method. The objective was to quantitatively evaluate the contribution of each parameter to variability in model outputs and to identify interaction effects and nonlinearities that cannot be captured by single-parameter perturbation. A total of 17 key parameters spanning the full model were evaluated, including pharmacokinetic parameters (CL, Vc, Qh, Vh, CLh, ka), VKOR-related parameters (ksyn, kdeg), coagulation-related parameters (Cf0, PT_INRbase, t_half_f, Emax, EC50, γ_PT-INR), and drug-specific properties (MW, IC50, γ).

In the Sobol’ analysis, random combinations of parameter sets were generated using the Saltelli sampling scheme implemented in the SALib package, and the first-order sensitivity index (S1,i), total sensitivity index (ST,i), and second-order interaction index (S2,i,j) were calculated for model outputs. These indices were defined as follows (Equations 7–9):

S1,i=VarXi(EX~i[Y|Xi])Var(Y) (7)

ST,i=1−VarX~i(EXi[Y|X~i])Var(Y) (8)

S2,i,j=VarXi,Xj(EX~i,j[Y|Xi,Xj])Var(Y)−(S1,i+S1,j) (9)

The first-order sensitivity index represents the contribution of each parameter alone to output variance. The total sensitivity index reflects both individual and interaction effects. The second-order interaction index quantifies the variance attributable to the interaction between two parameters.

In the Morris method, the elementary effect of each parameter was defined on a discretized parameter space (Equation 10):

EEi=Y(X1,…,Xi+Δ,…,Xk)−Y(X1,…,Xi,…,Xk)Δ (10)

For each parameter, multiple trajectories were sampled to calculate the mean of the absolute elementary effects (μ*), representing the main effect, and the standard deviation (σ), indicating nonlinearity and/or interactions with other parameters.

Local sensitivity analysis of PT-INR trajectories

To quantitatively evaluate the effects of parameter variation on PT-INR dynamics, local sensitivity analysis was conducted. Eight parameters were analyzed: CL, Vc, Qh, Vh, CLh, ka, γ, and IC50. For each parameter, five levels within ± 20% of the baseline value (80%, 90%, 100%, 110%, and 120%) were defined, and the PK/PD model was numerically solved to simulate PT-INR time courses under each condition. To quantify parameter sensitivity as a relative sensitivity coefficient, the relative sensitivity at time t was defined using a central-difference method with ± 10% perturbation around the baseline (Equation 11):

S(t;p)=pPT-INR(t;p)⋅∂PT-INR(t;p)∂p≈PT-INR(t;1.1p)−PT-INR(t;0.9p)0.2PT-INR(t;p) (11)

Here, p denotes the parameter under evaluation, and PT-INR (t) denotes the model output at time t when p is set to its baseline value. The right-hand term approximates the partial derivative of PT-INR with respect to p by the central difference between model outputs obtained after increasing and decreasing p by 10%.

To summarize time-dependent sensitivity as a single quantitative index, the time-averaged relative sensitivity was calculated as the mean absolute sensitivity over time (Equation 12). In discrete time, the average value across all simulation time points (0–96 hr in this study) was used. Parameter estimation and model evaluation

To improve reproducibility of PT-INR dynamics, parameter optimization was performed for Vh and CLh using a grid-search approach. The search space was defined as Vh=0.05–0.50 L in 0.05-L increments and CLh=0.01–0.10 L/hr in 0.01-L/hr increments, and model simulations were conducted for each parameter combination. Because the available datasets consisted of sparse time-course observations summarized from previously published studies, we did not divide the data into separate training and validation sets. In particular, individual-level raw data were not available for all source studies, and the reported PK/PD profiles were mainly available as group-level time-course data. A random split of such data would disrupt the temporal structure of the PK/PD profiles and further reduce the number of observations available for parameter estimation within each species–compound condition. Therefore, the model was evaluated by goodness-of-fit to the reported time-course data, global and local sensitivity analyses, and scenario-based assessments rather than by independent hold-out validation. Accordingly, the present model should be interpreted as a calibrated mechanistic simulation framework, rather than as an externally validated predictive model. Predicted PT-INR values were compared with observed data and evaluated using R2 and AIC. The optimal combination of Vh and CLh was identified based on these goodness-of-fit indices (i.e., maximizing R2 and minimizing AIC), and the selected parameter sets were used for subsequent species- and compound-specific simulations. Using the model and parameter settings described above, predictive analyses of PT-INR dynamics were conducted for diphacinone and warfarin in four animal groups (SD rats, FGAR-sensitive black rats, FGAR-resistant black rats, and Egyptian rousette bats). For each species, baseline parameters were first assigned based on physiological assumptions and literature values (Tables 1 and 2), followed by initial simulation, sensitivity analysis, and grid-search optimization.

In silico prediction of VKOR binding affinity and IC50 surrogate analysis

As an in silico extension, predicted binding affinities (µM) were calculated for diphacinone and warfarin using Boltz-2 [21]. Chemical structure information for both compounds was obtained from the PubChem database as SMILES representations. The analyzed species were SD rat, FGAR-sensitive black rat, FGAR-resistant black rat (L76P), and the Egyptian rousette bat. vkorc1 gene sequences for each species were retrieved from NCBI GenBank. Predicted binding affinities were calculated using Boltz-2 [21] implemented in ChimeraX [21]. The resulting values were treated as in silico inhibitory indices corresponding to species- and compound-specific VKOR–ligand interactions. The predicted affinity values were incorporated into the PK/PD model as exploratory surrogate parameters for IC50 to examine the potential and limitations of structure-based estimation of inhibitory properties. This analysis was not intended to replace experimentally derived IC50 or Ki values, but rather to evaluate whether sequence- and structure-based information could contribute to future data-sparing parameterization. Model performance after substitution was evaluated using the same prediction framework and goodness-of-fit metrics described above.

Computational environment and software

All computations in this study were performed using Python 3.11.9 in a PowerShell 7.5.3 environment on Windows 10. Numerical calculations were performed using NumPy 1.20.0 [10], and SciPy 1.6.0 [29] was used for solving ordinary differential equations and related computations. Data loading, preprocessing, organization of results, and output of grid-search results and predictions were conducted using pandas 1.2.0. Visualization was performed using matplotlib 3.3.4 [12], and model fit was evaluated by overlaying observed and simulated results. In addition, the grid search was implemented using the itertools module, correlation analysis was performed using scipy.stats.pearsonr [22], and global sensitivity analyses were conducted using the SALib package.

RESULTS

Model framework and analytical workflow

An integrated PK/PD model was constructed to predict PT-INR time courses after AR administration. The model was formulated as a system of ordinary differential equations and consisted of three coupled layers: (i) pharmacokinetics (absorption, distribution, and hepatic metabolism), (ii) concentration-dependent inhibition of VKOR activity, and (iii) coagulation factor dynamics linked to PT-INR through a nonlinear observation model (Fig. 1). The framework was implemented as a computational pipeline for sensitivity analysis, parameter optimization, and predictive simulation across species and compounds.

We then applied this workflow to four animal groups (SD rats, FGAR-sensitive black rats, FGAR-resistant black rats, and Egyptian rousette bats) and two first-generation ARs (diphacinone and warfarin), using sensitivity analyses to prioritize parameters, grid-search optimization to improve PT-INR fitting, and an in silico extension in which predicted VKOR binding affinity was substituted for IC50.

Global sensitivity analysis

Global sensitivity analysis (Sobol and Morris) identified CLh and IC50 as the principal contributors to model-output variability, with patterns differing by compound and species (Supplementary Tables 1–8). In the Sobol analysis, both AUC and Cmax were consistently influenced by CLh and IC50, and second-order interactions frequently involved CLh × IC50, indicating that hepatic metabolism and inhibitory potency jointly shaped model outputs. For diphacinone, IC50 contributed more strongly in FGAR-resistant black rats, whereas CLh was dominant in FGAR-sensitive black rats and Egyptian rousette bats. In contrast, for warfarin, CLh showed the highest contribution across species, while IC50 contributed less strongly than in diphacinone.

The Morris analysis was broadly consistent with the Sobol results: CLh showed among the largest mean absolute elementary effects (μ*) across drugs and animal groups, whereas IC50, γ, and Vh showed relatively large σ values, indicating nonlinearity and/or interaction effects. Together, these findings indicated that hepatic kinetic parameters dominate overall output variability, while inhibitory parameters contribute importantly to nonlinear and species-dependent responses.

Local sensitivity analysis

Local sensitivity analysis of PT-INR trajectories (Table 3) showed that the most influential parameters differed across species and compounds, but CLh and Vh were dominant under most conditions. In diphacinone models, CLh and Vh showed high sensitivity coefficients in SD rats (0.645 and 0.518, respectively) and FGAR-sensitive black rats (0.581 and 0.443), indicating that PT-INR responses in these groups were strongly governed by hepatic kinetic parameters. In FGAR-resistant black rats, sensitivities of CLh and Vh were lower (0.384 and 0.224), whereas IC50 and γ became more influential (0.495 and 0.758), suggesting a relative shift toward pharmacodynamic control. Egyptian rousette bats showed comparatively modest CLh and Vh sensitivities (0.330 and 0.237), implying a more distributed parameter contribution.

Baseline parameter optimization

Based on the sensitivity analyses, Vh and CLh were optimized by grid search, and model fit to observed PT-INR data was evaluated using R2 and AIC (Table 4). Targeted parameter readjustment for FGAR-resistant rats and Egyptian rousette bats

Because baseline optimization yielded low or moderate predictive accuracy in FGAR-resistant black rats and Egyptian rousette bats, targeted parameter readjustment was performed for these poorly fitted cases (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 9). This readjustment substantially improved model fit in both species. In FGAR-resistant black rats, targeted readjustment was performed for the warfarin model, in which baseline fitting remained suboptimal. After parameter readjustment, goodness-of-fit improved to R2=0.838 (AIC=−17.911) (Fig. 3A). The re-optimized parameters included not only Vh and CLh but also additional parameters related to hepatic kinetics and VKOR dynamics, including Qh and kdeg. For example, increasing Qh from 0.5 to 1.0 L/hr and adjusting kdeg to 0.07 1/hr improved the warfarin model fit in FGAR-resistant black rats.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to quantitatively understand interspecies differences in AR sensitivity and to establish a non-invasive predictive model applicable to risk assessment in non-target wildlife, including rare species. Traditionally, AR sensitivity has been evaluated primarily in model animals such as rats, but limited knowledge has been available for rare species or resistant populations for which experiments are difficult. In this study, we constructed an integrated PK/PD model based on ordinary differential equations using previously reported in vivo and in vitro data, with the objective of reproducing and predicting PT-INR responses following AR administration. The model consisted of three layers: pharmacokinetics, VKOR inhibition, and coagulation factor synthesis/degradation, and was used to investigate species- and drug-specific response characteristics through simulations and parameter analyses.

To this end, global sensitivity analyses were conducted using the Sobol and Morris methods. As a result, CLh and IC50 were consistently identified as the major contributing parameters for both AUC and Cmax (S1(CLh)=0.54–0.78; S1(IC50)=0.04–0.55). These parameters represent both pharmacokinetic and inhibitory components of the model and are considered key determinants underlying interspecies variation in PT-INR responses. In the case of diphacinone, CLh was dominant in sensitive species such as SD rats and FGAR-sensitive black rats (S1(CLh)=0.56–0.72), whereas IC50 contributed more strongly in resistant populations (S1(IC50)=0.34–0.44), indicating that the balance between metabolic rate and inhibitory potency governs AR sensitivity. In addition, the second-order interaction CLh ×IC50 showed a marked contribution in FGAR-resistant rats (S2=0.20–0.23), highlighting the combined effect of hepatic metabolism and VKOR inhibition. For warfarin, CLh exhibited the highest sensitivity in most species (S1(CLh)=0.26–0.78), being most pronounced in FGAR-resistant rats (S1(CLh)=0.78). This finding supports that warfarin is primarily eliminated through hepatic metabolism and that differences in metabolic capacity substantially contribute to interspecies variation in sensitivity. In contrast, diphacinone demonstrated more complex inhibitory kinetics, suggesting that fluctuations in its affinity for VKOR may be the primary driver of sensitivity differences. These trends were consistent with the results of the Morris analysis. The mean absolute elementary effect (μ*) of CLh was among the largest across species and compounds, and CLh ranked first in most conditions, confirming its major influence on model outputs. Conversely, the standard deviation (σ), which reflects nonlinearity and parameter interactions, showed that IC50 and CLh were frequently among the most influential parameters depending on the species–drug combination, whereas Vh and γ generally exhibited more moderate contributions. Collectively, these results indicate that metabolic capacity (CLh) is the principal determinant of output variability in most cases, while IC50 can become equally or more influential in particular species–drug combinations. Furthermore, to evaluate the time-dependent and quantitative effects of individual parameters on PT-INR dynamics, a local sensitivity analysis was performed. The results showed that CLh and Vh were consistently identified as the dominant parameters across most conditions (CLh=0.17–0.81; Vh=0.03–0.60), consistent with the findings of the global sensitivity analysis. This indicates that fluctuations in PT-INR dynamics are primarily governed by hepatic pharmacokinetic parameters. Meanwhile, under specific species–drug combinations, the sensitivities of pharmacodynamic parameters such as IC50 and γ increased (IC50=0.14–0.50; γ=0.03–0.76), suggesting that the relative contribution of the PD layer becomes greater as drug susceptibility decreases or inhibitory properties change.

In the diphacinone models, CLh and Vh were identified as key sensitivity parameters, showing particularly high values in SD rats (CLh=0.645; Vh=0.518) and FGAR-sensitive black rats (CLh=0.581; Vh=0.443), indicating that PT-INR responses in these species are mainly dependent on hepatic metabolism and tissue distribution. In contrast, in FGAR-resistant black rats, the sensitivities of CLh (0.384) and Vh (0.224) decreased, while those of IC50 (0.495) and γ (0.758) increased, suggesting that reduced drug susceptibility leads to a greater contribution of inhibitory dynamics. This observation is consistent with the global sensitivity analysis, in which IC50 showed higher S1 values in resistant populations (S1=0.34–0.44), supporting the concept that the balance between metabolic rate and inhibitory strength determines AR sensitivity. Similarly, in the warfarin models, CLh and Vh exhibited high sensitivities in susceptible species (FGAR-sensitive black rats: CLh=0.805; Vh=0.602), showing that PT-INR responses were strongly dependent on clearance variability. In contrast, their influence was reduced in FGAR-resistant rats and Egyptian rousette bats (FGAR-resistant rats: CLh=0.174; Vh=0.031; Egyptian rousette bat: CLh=0.311; Vh=0.172), while the relative impact of PD parameters such as IC50 and γ increased (IC50=0.138–0.252; γ=0.029–0.220). These patterns reflect interspecific differences in hepatic metabolic capacity and VKOR inhibitory characteristics, suggesting that in resistant populations and non-model species, variations in the PD layer may predominantly govern drug responses. Based on these findings, it was revealed that improving the predictive accuracy of the model requires not only PK parameters such as CLh and Vh but also PD parameters such as IC50. Because ARs act on VKOR, which is localized in the liver, and their major metabolic pathway involves hepatic microsomal CYP-mediated hydroxylation followed by glucuronidation catalyzed by UDP-glucuronosyltransferases, the result that hepatic-related parameters exert a strong influence on model predictability is pharmacologically reasonable.

In the present model, IC50 values were generally assigned based on previously reported VKOR inhibition data; however, experimentally determined IC50 values for diphacinone in FGAR-sensitive and FGAR-resistant black rats were not available. Therefore, these IC50 values were estimated by grid-search optimization based on the goodness-of-fit to observed PT-INR data. The optimized values were 65 nM for FGAR-sensitive black rats and 1,060 nM for FGAR-resistant black rats. These values should not be interpreted as directly measured biochemical IC50 values, but rather as model-derived apparent IC50 parameters that provided the best fit within the tested parameter ranges. Although the IC50 values estimated for diphacinone in FGAR-sensitive and FGAR-resistant black rats are not experimentally validated inhibition constants, their direction and magnitude are biologically plausible. In the warfarin model, experimentally reported IC50 values are substantially higher in FGAR-resistant black rats than in FGAR-sensitive black rats, indicating reduced VKOR sensitivity in the resistant population. Similarly, the present diphacinone model estimated a higher apparent IC50 in FGAR-resistant black rats than in FGAR-sensitive black rats, suggesting that reduced inhibitory sensitivity may also contribute to the diphacinone response in this strain. Furthermore, the estimated value for FGAR-resistant black rats was higher than the reported diphacinone IC50 for Egyptian rousette bats, a species known to be highly susceptible to diphacinone, which is consistent with the expected biological trend.

Nevertheless, parameter identifiability remains an important limitation. In the grid-search analysis, relatively good fits were also obtained around IC50 values of approximately 460–610 nM when accompanied by compensatory changes in Vh and CLh. This indicates that the optimized IC50 is not uniquely identifiable from the available PT-INR time-course data alone. Therefore, the estimated IC50 values should be regarded as best-fitting apparent parameters for model calibration, rather than definitive experimental IC50 values. Direct in vitro VKOR inhibition assays or kinetic measurements, such as Ki determination, will be required to validate these estimates and improve parameter identifiability in future studies.

Based on these considerations, parameter optimization was conducted for CLh and Vh by grid search to capture inter-group differences in metabolic capacity and distribution kinetics. As a result of this optimization, high goodness-of-fit was achieved in SD rats and FGAR-sensitive black rats, with R2 ranging from 0.849 to 0.982 and AIC values from −16.888 to 7.746, indicating good agreement between predicted and observed PT-INR responses. In contrast, in FGAR-resistant black rats and Egyptian rousette bats, the predictive performance of the initial model was low, except in some cases, and additional parameter adjustments were required. In FGAR-resistant black rats, the initial model showed a low R2 value for warfarin (R2=0.672); however, model fit was substantially improved by increasing hepatic blood flow (Qh) from 0.5 to 1.0 L/hr and further adjusting the VKOR degradation rate constant (kdeg) to 0.07 1/hr, reaching R2=0.838 (AIC=−17.911). This result is consistent with previously reported mechanisms in resistant populations, including enhanced hepatic metabolic capacity and reduced sensitivity to VKOR inhibition caused by vkorc1 mutations [24], and supports the contribution of both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic factors to resistance. In Egyptian rousette bats, the initial model showed poor predictive accuracy for both warfarin (R2=0.374) and diphacinone (R2=0.469). For diphacinone, adjustment of kdeg to 0.10 1/hr, Emax from 10 to 9, and γ_PT-INR from 15 to 8 improved model fit to R2=0.836 (AIC=1.234). For warfarin, adjustment of kdeg to 0.06 1/hr and γ_PT-INR to 12 improved model fit to R2=0.900 (AIC=−7.817). Moreover, the model successfully captured the species-specific response pattern characterized by low sensitivity to warfarin and high sensitivity to diphacinone. These tendencies were consistent with previous reports in the same species, describing high warfarin clearance and rapid recovery of coagulation, as well as delayed diphacinone elimination leading to prolonged tissue retention and strong VKOR binding affinity [25].

It should be noted that PT-INR values in the source studies were subject to an upper measurement limit of 8 when measured using CoaguChek XS. Therefore, data points reported at this upper limit should be interpreted as ceiling-limited observations indicating PT-INR ≥8, rather than exact PT-INR values of 8. This limitation may affect quantitative interpretation of model behavior in the high-INR range, particularly with respect to peak PT-INR values and goodness-of-fit metrics. In the present model, PT-INR dynamics above this upper limit were generated as continuous mechanistic simulations based on VKOR inhibition, coagulation factor depletion, and subsequent recovery. Such delayed rise-and-fall dynamics are physiologically plausible and consistent with the general concept of temporal dissociation between pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamic responses in PK/PD relationships [17]. However, they cannot be directly verified using the available source data once PT-INR exceeds the measurement limit. Therefore, the simulated high-INR portions of the curves should be interpreted cautiously as model-based extrapolations beyond the measurable range, rather than as directly validated quantitative predictions.

As an in silico extension, predicted binding affinities generated by Boltz-2 using target protein amino acid sequences and ligand SMILES representations were incorporated as alternative parameters for IC50. This approach improved predictive accuracy in SD rats and FGAR-sensitive black rats, particularly in the case of diphacinone, where the high predicted affinity was consistent with observed toxicity. However, in FGAR-resistant black rats, in silico predictions did not adequately reflect resistance, and model fit declined. Previous studies reported that the vkorc1 mutation (Leu76Pro) in FGAR-resistant black rats, although located outside the binding pocket, reduces protein flexibility and indirectly decreases warfarin binding affinity by 37.5-fold [24]. In addition, reduced VKOR catalytic efficiency (Vmax/Km) has been observed as a compensatory effect of resistance. The decreased model accuracy observed in this study is likely attributable to the limitation of static docking, which cannot adequately account for the flexibility of ligand-binding pockets or dynamic structural fluctuations. In contrast, molecular dynamics simulations, as used in prior work, can track time-dependent ligand–protein interactions and may therefore more accurately reproduce these resistance mechanisms, indicating that such approaches could improve future models. Importantly, in the Egyptian rousette bat, predicted affinity differences corresponded with its actual warfarin resistance and diphacinone sensitivity, suggesting potential utility for evaluating rare species. Therefore, the Boltz-2-based affinity analysis should be regarded as an exploratory, auxiliary approach for evaluating the future potential of low-invasive, structure-based estimation of inhibitory parameters, rather than as a substitute for experimentally determined IC50 or Ki values.

The biological characteristics of the source datasets should also be considered when interpreting the model. Because the present study relied on previously published datasets, the extent to which sex, age, body condition, and other individual-level covariates could be incorporated was limited by the information available in the original reports. In particular, individual-level raw data were not available for all datasets, and the Egyptian rousette bat data were used as group-level PK/PD profiles. Therefore, the simulated PT-INR profiles should be interpreted as average responses under the experimental conditions of the source studies, rather than as predictions for all individuals within each species. This limitation is relevant for anticoagulant compounds, because genetic variation in VKOR and drug-metabolizing enzymes, as well as physiological status, may influence anticoagulant sensitivity. Future models should incorporate sex, age, genotype, reproductive status, and physiological condition as covariates when sufficiently detailed datasets become available.

The Egyptian rousette bat was used in the present study because published in vivo pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic data and in vitro VKOR inhibition data were available for this species. Importantly, in the source study, the Egyptian rousette bat was used as a closely related surrogate species for assessing anticoagulant rodenticide sensitivity in the Bonin flying fox, for which direct toxicological experimentation is ethically and practically difficult. Therefore, the present model did not directly compare Egyptian rousette bats with Bonin flying foxes and did not directly estimate PT-INR responses in Bonin flying foxes. However, the source study included structure-based analyses of VKOR–anticoagulant interactions in both bat species, including molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. These previous analyses suggested that VKOR-related ligand recognition and interaction stability were broadly similar between Egyptian rousette bats and Bonin flying foxes, particularly when compared with the larger differences observed between bats and rodents. Therefore, extrapolation of VKOR-related pharmacodynamic parameters, such as IC50, from Egyptian rousette bats to Bonin flying foxes may be biologically plausible as an initial approximation. However, this extrapolation should be restricted to the pharmacodynamic component of the model. Pharmacokinetic parameters, particularly hepatic metabolic capacity involving cytochrome P450 enzymes and other xenobiotic-metabolizing systems, remain poorly characterized in Bonin flying foxes. This point is important because the present sensitivity analyses identified hepatic kinetic parameters, including hepatic clearance and distribution-related parameters, as major determinants of PT-INR responses. Thus, uncertainty in PK parameters could substantially affect quantitative prediction in Bonin flying foxes. The present framework should therefore be regarded as a data-sparing approach for generating risk hypotheses for Bonin flying foxes, rather than as a validated species-specific prediction model for this endangered species.

Raptors and other avian predators represent major non-target species affected by secondary exposure to anticoagulant rodenticides. Although the present study focused on mammalian species, the same conceptual PK/PD framework could, in principle, be extended to birds if avian-specific data are available. Indeed, Khidkhan et al. [15] evaluated interspecific differences in avian sensitivity to anticoagulant rodenticides by integrating in vivo warfarin pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamic effects, hepatic CYP expression involved in AR metabolism, and in vitro VKOR inhibition assays, with a particular focus on raptors. In addition, Watanabe et al. [30] examined CYP-mediated warfarin metabolic ability and warfarin sensitivity in avian species using in vitro assays in several birds and in vivo assays in chickens. These studies indicate that the types of data required for avian PK/PD model construction, including drug disposition, metabolic capacity, VKOR inhibition, and coagulation responses, are beginning to become available. Nevertheless, direct application of the present mammalian model to birds would not be appropriate without re-parameterization. Birds differ from mammals in several anatomical and physiological features relevant to PK/PBPK modeling, including renal organization and excretory physiology, hepatic xenobiotic metabolism, coagulation physiology, and species-specific VKOR properties. Therefore, avian application would require reconstruction of the PK component using bird-specific anatomical and physiological parameters, followed by integration of avian VKOR inhibition and coagulation-response data. For this reason, birds were not included in the present model. Future studies should extend the present framework to raptors and other avian species by incorporating published avian PK/PD datasets and species-specific metabolic parameters, because these species are among the most ecologically relevant non-target taxa for anticoagulant rodenticide risk assessment.

Overall, this study demonstrated the utility of an integrated PK/PD modeling approach for quantitatively interpreting interspecies differences in anticoagulant rodenticide sensitivity. The results highlighted hepatic clearance, distribution-related parameters, and VKOR inhibitory potency as important determinants of PT-INR responses. Rather than providing a fully non-invasive or externally validated prediction model for all non-model species, the present framework should be regarded as a data-sparing mechanistic modeling approach that integrates limited in vivo data, in vitro inhibition data, literature-derived parameters, and computational estimates. The analysis of the Egyptian rousette bat illustrates how available wildlife data can be incorporated into a quantitative framework for assessing anticoagulant sensitivity, while explicitly recognizing the limitations of extrapolation to closely related endangered species and to phylogenetically distant taxa such as birds. At the same time, this study highlighted challenges in replacing experimentally derived inhibitory parameters with structure-based predictions alone. For warfarin-resistant black rats, substitution of IC50 with predicted binding affinity alone failed to reproduce resistance-associated pharmacodynamic behavior. This is likely because resistance involves not only altered ligand binding but also changes in protein flexibility, enzymatic kinetics of VKOR, and possibly dissociation kinetics, which cannot be fully captured by static affinity estimates. Therefore, future improvements should include enzyme kinetics-based models incorporating Ki, Km, and Vmax, as well as dynamic structural information such as ligand residence time or dissociation rates derived from molecular dynamics simulations. Such extensions may improve the representation of resistant phenotypes while avoiding overinterpretation of static in silico affinity scores. Together, these findings suggest that the proposed framework can contribute to reduced-animal-use toxicological assessment by clarifying which parameters are most influential and which data are required for reliable extrapolation. Future applications to rare wildlife species should combine sequence-based or structure-based predictions with experimental data from closely related surrogate species, non-lethal sampling where feasible, and explicit uncertainty analysis for poorly constrained PK and PD parameters.
