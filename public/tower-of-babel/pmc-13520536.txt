Clinical outcomes of long head of the biceps tendon augmentation versus partial repair in massive rotator cuff tears
Authors: Ozgun Karakus, Ahmet Oztermeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the clinical outcomes of rotator cuff repair augmented with the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) and partial repair (PR) in patients with massive rotator cuff tears (RCTs).MethodsThis retrospective study included patients who underwent arthroscopic surgery for massive RCTs between January 2020 and May 2024. Forty-six patients were treated with rotator cuff repair augmented using the LHBT (LHBT group), and 49 patients underwent PR (PR group). Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Constant–Murley (CM), American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES), and University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) scores, visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, and shoulder range of motion (ROM). Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging was performed selectively in patients without satisfactory clinical improvement.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated statistically significant postoperative improvement in all clinical and functional parameters (p < 0.001). However, the LHBT group showed superior postoperative clinical outcomes compared with the PR group. Re-rupture requiring reoperation occurred in 3 patients in the LHBT group and in 11 patients in the PR group. Proximal LHBT rupture was observed in 1 patient. Increasing grades of fatty degeneration were associated with significantly worse postoperative clinical scores and ROM in both groups.ConclusionLHBT augmentation is a successful technique for the treatment of massive retracted RCTs and provides superior clinical outcomes compared with PR. PR remains a viable option in carefully selected patients when LHBT augmentation is not feasible.

Introduction

The definition and assessment of massive RCT remain an area of ongoing investigation, with recent studies proposing novel imaging-based parameters to improve diagnostic accuracy. [1, 2] In addition, the surgical management of massive tears remains challenging due to factors such as tendon retraction, poor tissue quality, and high re-tear rates. Various surgical techniques have been developed to reduce the risk of failure in the treatment of massive RCT. Historically, partial repair (PR) has been one of the most commonly used treatment strategies [3]. Over time, several augmentation techniques have been introduced for the management of massive tears, most notably superior capsular reconstruction (SCR) and various tendon transfer procedures [4]. In SCR, the fascia lata autograft has been the most frequently utilized graft material [5]. In subsequent studies, the use of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) as an alternative graft to the fascia lata has been proposed [6].

Multiple surgical techniques using the LHBT for augmentation purposes have been described; however, no clear consensus has yet been reached regarding the superiority of any specific method [7–10]. Pastor et al. compared rotator cuff repair with LHBT augmentation to rotator cuff repair performed after LHBT tenotomy in patients with massive tears and reported superior clinical outcomes in the augmentation group [11]. Similarly, McClatchy et al. reported favorable clinical results using their bioSCR technique incorporating the LHBT [12].

Several studies have demonstrated that satisfactory outcomes can also be achieved with PR alone in the treatment of massive rotator cuff tears [3]. However, Kawashima et al. compared patients with irreparable massive RCT treated with SCR using the LHBT to those treated with PR and reported significantly better outcomes in the augmentation group [13]. Despite these findings, the number of studies directly comparing rotator cuff repair with LHBT augmentation to PR in massive tears remains limited in the current literature.

The aim of the present study was to compare the clinical and radiological outcomes of patients with massive RCTs treated with LHBT-augmented rotator cuff repair using a reproducible and technically efficient approach and those treated with PR.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

Approval for this retrospective study was obtained from the local ethics committee (File no: 2025/06/61). All surgical procedures were performed by a single surgeon (OK) between January 2020 and May 2024. A total of 46 patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair augmented with the LHBT for massive RCT (≥ 5 cm) were included in the study LHBT group. Forty-nine patients with massive RCTs treated with PR were included as the PR group.

The exclusion criteria included patients with massive tears amenable to primary repair, insufficient follow-up duration (< 1 year), glenohumeral osteoarthritis, or comorbid conditions affecting the same extremity (including nerve injury, malignancy, or previous fracture). Patients with Goutallier grade IV fatty degeneration of the supraspinatus muscle and those who underwent revision arthroscopic repair due to re-rupture after primary repair were also excluded.

Surgical indications

In our study, the indication for LHBT augmentation was determined based on arthroscopic evaluation. LHBT augmentation was performed in patients with massive rotator cuff tears larger than 5 cm, particularly in cases where the tear apex was retracted to the level of the glenoid and a tension-free repair to the footprint could not be achieved despite adequate arthroscopic mobilization of the rotator cuff. Intraoperative evaluation of the integrity and quality of the LHBT was also considered when determining the surgical strategy. PR was preferred in patients in whom the LHBT could not be visualized during arthroscopic examination and was presumed to be previously ruptured, in those with severely degenerative biceps tendons considered unsuitable for augmentation, and in patients in whom the anterior rotator cuff tissue could not be adequately mobilized to reach and be incorporated into the LHBT due to excessive tendon retraction. This decision was made intraoperatively based on tendon mobility and tissue quality.

Clinical evaluation and follow-up

All patients were re-evaluated at a minimum of 12 months postoperatively. Clinical outcomes were assessed preoperatively and at the final follow-up visit. At the final follow-up, patient comorbidities, body mass index (BMI), Constant–Murley (CM) [14], American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) [15] and University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) [16] scores were recorded, along with shoulder range of motion (ROM). ROM measurements were performed using a goniometer for flexion, abduction, internal rotation, and external rotation. External rotation and internal rotation were measured with the patient in the supine position, with the shoulder at 90° of abduction and the elbow flexed to 90°. Fatty degeneration was evaluated using the Goutallier classification based on the supraspinatus muscle on preoperative MRI [17]. Postoperative MRI was not routinely performed in all patients and was obtained selectively, particularly in those with unsatisfactory clinical outcomes, to assess tendon integrity and the presence of re-rupture. All clinical evaluations at the final follow-up were performed by two orthopedic surgeons.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed with the patient in the beach-chair position. Arthroscopic evaluation of the glenohumeral joint was initially performed through a posterior portal. Concomitant subscapularis tears identified during intra-articular inspection were repaired using a single titanium anchor (Artroline, Artrotek, Adana, Türkiye). The arthroscope was then transitioned to the subacromial space, and the rotator cuff tear was carefully assessed. A massive, retracted rotator cuff tear was identified, involving at least the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons, with tendon retraction extending to the level of the glenoid or more medially. With the arm positioned in forward elevation, the LHBT was mobilized by releasing the transverse humeral ligament and freeing it from the intertubercular groove using radiofrequency, without performing tenotomy and while preserving its proximal continuity [18]. The LHBT was subsequently tenodesed at the entrance of the groove using a single 3.5-mm titanium anchor placed approximately 1 cm posteromedial to the groove. No additional groove preparation or deepening was performed.

After adequate mobilization of the anterior rotator cuff tissue, the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) was mobilized by releasing the transverse humeral ligament and freeing it from the intertubercular groove using radiofrequency, without performing tenotomy and while preserving its proximal continuity. The LHBT was subsequently tenodesed at the entrance of the groove using a single 3.5-mm titanium anchor placed approximately 1 cm posteromedial to the groove. No additional groove preparation or deepening was performed. Following tenodesis, the anterior rotator cuff tissue was incorporated into the LHBT using intratendinous sutures placed between the rotator cuff and the biceps tendon with a FirstPass device, allowing gradual reduction of the tear from medial to lateral. After achieving a crescent-shaped configuration, fixation at the footprint was performed using a 4.5-mm titanium anchor. The repair was then compressed using the Samsung Medical Center sliding knot technique, and a double-row construct was completed using a 5-mm knotless suture anchor (Artroline, Artrotek, Adana, Türkiye). Subacromial decompression was performed in all patients. Acromioplasty was selectively performed based on intraoperative findings, including the presence of acromial spurs or mechanical impingement. In cases treated with PR, the anterior limb of the rotator cuff was repaired to the footprint at the level it could be reached using a single titanium anchor. Subsequently, the posterior limb was released, mobilized to the footprint, and repaired using another titanium anchor. After repair of the anterior and posterior limbs, a central defect remained between these two structures.

Postoperative protocol

The same postoperative rehabilitation protocol was applied to all patients. Passive shoulder exercises were initiated one day after surgery. An abduction-supported shoulder brace was used for six weeks, and active shoulder motion was restricted during this period. After six weeks, the brace was discontinued, and all patients underwent a physiotherapy program including active shoulder exercises for an additional two months.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi software (version 2.6.x, The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages [n (%)].

For comparisons between groups, the independent samples t-test was used for continuous variables with normal distribution, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for variables without normal distribution. The distribution of categorical variables (acromion type, sex, fatty degeneration grade, and side) between groups was analyzed using the chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. Within-group comparisons of preoperative and postoperative values were performed using the paired samples t-test for normally distributed data and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed data.

For comparisons of postoperative clinical and functional outcomes among the three groups based on fatty degeneration grade, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used due to violation of the normality assumption. When a statistically significant difference was detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Dwass–Steel–Critchlow–Fligner (DSCF) post-hoc test, which incorporates adjustment for multiple testing therefore does not require additional correction.

To control for potential confounding factors, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed. Postoperative Constant–Murley, ASES, and UCLA scores were defined as dependent variables, while the surgical group, age, gender, fatty degeneration grade (Goutallier), acromion type, and preoperative scores were included as independent variables in the model.

A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Results

LHBT group

The mean age of patients in the LHBT group was 62.8 ± 8.5 years. Thirty three patients were female and 13 were male. Surgery was performed on the right shoulder in 25 patients and on the left shoulder in 21 patients. Acromion morphology was type I in 16 patients, type II in 19 patients, and type III in 11 patients. Fatty degeneration was graded as stage 1 in 7 patients, stage 2 in 21 patients, and stage 3 in 18 patients (Table 1).

Relationship between fatty degeneration and outcomes in the LHBT group

In patients treated with LHBT augmentation, increasing grades of fatty degeneration were associated with significant differences in postoperative clinical and functional outcomes. Patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration had significantly lower postoperative Constant–Murley scores compared with those with grade 1 and grade 2 fatty degeneration (p = 0.047 and p = 0.014, respectively). Similarly, postoperative ASES scores were significantly lower in patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration compared with grade 1 (p = 0.040) and grade 2 (p = 0.003). Postoperative UCLA scores were also significantly lower in patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration compared with those with grade 1 (p = 0.009) and grade 2 (p < 0.001). VAS scores were significantly higher in patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration compared with grade 2 (p = 0.013). Regarding ROM, postoperative flexion (p = 0.008), internal rotation (p = 0.017), and external rotation were significantly lower in patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration, particularly when compared with grade 2. In terms of external rotation, patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration also demonstrated significantly lower values compared with those with grade 1 fatty degeneration (p = 0.006). No significant differences were observed in postoperative abduction among the different grades of fatty degeneration. PR group

The mean age of patients in the PR group was 63.8 ± 9.15 years. Seventeen patients were male and 32 were female. Acromion morphology was type I in 14 patients, type II in 19 patients, and type III in 16 patients. Fatty degeneration was graded as stage 1 in 5 patients, stage 2 in 19 patients, and stage 3 in 25 patients. Surgery was performed on the right shoulder in 29 patients and on the left shoulder in 20 patients.

Re-rupture was detected in 11 patients. Statistically significant postoperative improvement was observed in all clinical and functional parameters. The Constant–Murley score increased from 39.86 ± 3.76 preoperatively to 61.73 ± 10.65 postoperatively (p < 0.001). Similarly, the ASES score improved from 24.10 ± 2.83 to 62.49 ± 12.91, and the UCLA score increased from 19.04 ± 2.12 to 24.43 ± 3.13 (all p < 0.001). The VAS score decreased from 7.41 ± 1.22 preoperatively to 3.98 ± 1.98 postoperatively (p < 0.001).

ROM analysis demonstrated significant postoperative improvements in flexion (from 94.08 ± 16.51° to 141.29 ± 21.97°), abduction (from 96.63 ± 17.33° to 139.51 ± 21.86°), internal rotation (from 40.92 ± 6.10° to 49.18 ± 6.14°), and external rotation (from 35.18 ± 5.29° to 48.94 ± 6.03°) (all p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Relationship between fatty degeneration and outcomes in the PR group

In patients treated with PR, increasing grades of fatty degeneration were associated with progressively poorer postoperative clinical and functional outcomes. Patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration demonstrated significantly lower postoperative Constant–Murley scores compared with those with grade 1 and grade 2 fatty degeneration (Group 1–3: p = 0.047; Group 2–3: p = 0.014). Similarly, postoperative ASES scores were significantly lower in patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration compared with grade 1 (p = 0.040) and grade 2 (p = 0.003), and postoperative UCLA scores were also lower compared with grade 1 (p = 0.009) and grade 2 (p < 0.001).

VAS scores were significantly higher in patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration compared with grade 2 (p = 0.013). Regarding ROM, postoperative flexion was significantly lower in patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration compared with grade 2 (p = 0.008). Postoperative internal rotation was also lower in patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration compared with grade 2 (p = 0.017). In terms of postoperative external rotation, patients with grade 3 fatty degeneration demonstrated significantly lower values compared with both grade 1 (p = 0.006) and grade 2 (p = 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed in postoperative abduction among the different grades of fatty degeneration (Table 4).

Comparison between the groups

When postoperative outcomes were compared between the two groups, the LHBT augmentation group demonstrated significantly better clinical and functional results than the PR group. Postoperative Constant–Murley, ASES, and UCLA scores were significantly higher in the LHBT group (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). Additionally, postoperative VAS scores were significantly lower in the LHBT group compared with the PR group (p < 0.001). Similarly, all postoperative range of motion parameters, including flexion, abduction, internal rotation, and external rotation, were significantly better in the LHBT group (p < 0.001 for all) (Table 2).

Factors Influencing Clinical Outcomes- Multivariable Analysis.

Multivariable linear regression analysis demonstrated that the LHBT augmentation was a strong independent predictor of higher postoperative scores compared to the PR group (Constant–Murley: beta = 16.82, p < 0.001; ASES: beta = 13.38, p < 0.001; UCLA: beta = 3.28, p < 0.001). Fatty degeneration was also identified as a significant independent negative predictor of clinical outcomes (p < 0.01 for all scores). Other factors such as age, gender, side, and acromion type did not significantly influence the postoperative clinical scores (p > 0.05) (Table 5).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that rotator cuff repair augmented with the LHBT, performed without tenotomy in massive, retracted tears, is associated with favorable clinical outcomes. Avoiding tenotomy preserves the proximal continuity of the LHBT, allowing its integration into the repair while maintaining its potential biomechanical function. Although partial repair was associated with comparatively less favorable outcomes in massive tears, it remains a viable option in cases where LHBT augmentation is not feasible.

Re-rupture rates remain high in massive retracted RCT, and various augmentation techniques have been developed to reduce this risk [19–21]. SCR using a fascia lata autograft has emerged as a contemporary surgical option for the management of massive and irreparable rotator cuff tears. SCR aims to restore superior glenohumeral stability by reconstructing the superior capsule, thereby preventing superior migration of the humeral head and improving shoulder biomechanics. Early clinical studies have demonstrated promising short-term outcomes with significant improvements in functional scores and range of motion; however, concerns remain regarding graft healing rates, donor site morbidity, and variability in surgical techniques [22]. In contrast, LHBT-based augmentation techniques utilize local autologous tissue and may offer a simpler, more cost-effective alternative while preserving native tendon continuity [4]. Nevertheless, direct comparative studies between superior capsule reconstruction and LHBT-based augmentation techniques are still limited, and further research is required to better define their respective roles in clinical practice.

The use of the LHBT offers several advantages, including lower cost, anatomical proximity, and the absence of additional incisions. In addition, arthroscopic LHBT tenodesis is a commonly performed procedure, making it a familiar and technically accessible technique for most shoulder surgeons [4, 23]. Numerous LHBT-based augmentation techniques have been described in the literature [7–10]. Mundakkal et al. tenotomized the LHBT at the glenohumeral joint, transferred it into the subacromial space, and fixed it to the footprint together with the massive tear using anchors [7]. Yoon et al. first performed tenodesis of the LHBT within the groove, then mobilized the proximal stump, performed proximal tenotomy, and fixed the stiff massive tear to the footprint together with the proximal LHBT [10]. Lin et al. sutured the LHBT to the anterior aspect of the supraspinatus and fixed it near the groove with an anchor; the tendon was tenotomized at two different levels—near the glenoid origin and near the groove—and the remaining LHBT was used to augment the rotator cuff repair [8]. Bonfatti et al. first performed tenodesis of the LHBT within the groove, followed by tenotomy immediately proximal to the fixation site, and incorporated the proximal segment into the anterior supraspinatus for augmentation; using their “Shoe Shop Lacing” technique, the LHBT and rotator cuff were fixed together to the footprint to provide augmentation [9]. A common feature of these techniques is that the LHBT undergoes tenotomy at the glenohumeral level, near the groove, or at both sites prior to augmentation, and the proximal segment is incorporated into the repair. In contrast, in the technique used in the present study, no tenotomy of the LHBT was performed; the tendon was tenodesed approximately 1 cm posterior to the groove. This approach is similar to the technique described by Terra et al., particularly in preserving proximal LHBT continuity. However, unlike their method, no new groove was created, and the tendon was incorporated into the repair without additional groove preparation [18]. Therefore, this technique can be considered a modification of previously described LHBT-based augmentation procedures rather than a completely novel approach. Incorporating the LHBT into the augmentation without complete release and while preserving proximal continuity may offer advantages such as fewer surgical steps, easier applicability, and potentially shorter operative time compared with other techniques. However, further comparative studies are required to validate these assumptions.

Pastor et al. compared rotator cuff repair with LHBT augmentation to repair performed after LHBT tenotomy and reported superior outcomes in the augmentation group [11]. However, they also reported a re-rupture rate of 16% in the augmentation group and 38.5% in tenotomy group, with all re-ruptures occurring in patients with advanced preoperative fatty degeneration. In our study, similarly favorable outcomes were observed with LHBT augmentation. Re-rupture was detected in 3 patients in the LHBT group and in 11 patients in the PR group. Consistent with the literature, all patients with re-rupture in our cohort had stage 3 fatty degeneration. Furthermore, increasing grades of fatty degeneration were associated with progressively worse outcomes in both groups. In patients with stage 3 fatty degeneration, both clinical scores and shoulder ROM were significantly inferior compared with those in lower grades. These findings suggest that fatty degeneration is an important factor influencing surgical outcomes in rotator cuff repair, regardless of the surgical technique employed.

In a study including 30 patients with massive RCTs, McClatchy et al. used the LHBT for biological SCR and reported successful outcomes, with proximal LHBT rupture observed in only one patient [12]. Kawashima et al. compared 12 patients treated with rotator cuff repair augmented with the LHBT with 10 patients treated with PR alone and reported superior clinical outcomes in the augmentation group, although the overall re-rupture rate was as high as 42% [13]. Notably, in the PR group of the Kawashima et al. study, a re-tear rate of up to 80% was reported. In our study, proximal LHBT rupture was observed in 1 patient in the LHBT group. Additionally, re-rupture requiring reoperation occurred in 3 patients in the LHBT group and in 11 patients in the PR group. The lower re-rupture rate observed compared with previous reports may be explained by several factors. First, patients with stage 4 fatty degeneration were excluded from the study, which may have favorably influenced structural healing outcomes. Second, unlike the study by Kawashima et al., in which postoperative MRI was routinely performed in all patients, postoperative MRI in our study was selectively obtained only in patients without satisfactory clinical improvement. Therefore, asymptomatic structural re-tears may have remained undetected. This selective imaging strategy was primarily adopted to reduce overall costs.

Patients treated with PR demonstrated statistically inferior outcomes across all evaluated scores compared with the LHBT group. Moreover, while re-rupture occurred in only 3 patients in the LHBT group, it was observed in 11 patients in the PR group. Nevertheless, when the PR group was evaluated independently, significant postoperative improvement was observed across all clinical scores compared with preoperative values. In a systematic review, Malahias et al. demonstrated that PR remains an acceptable treatment option for massive, retracted, irreparable RCTs Based on these findings, PR should still be considered a viable option in selected patients, particularly in cases where the LHBT is degenerated or already ruptured and therefore unsuitable for augmentation [24].

Some studies have incorporated proximal or distal tenotomy into LHBT augmentation techniques due to concerns that tenodesis-based augmentation may increase postoperative pain [7–10]. McClatchy et al. reported better outcomes in patients who underwent LHBT augmentation without tenotomy compared with those who underwent tenotomy [12]. Similarly, in our study, favorable outcomes were achieved without performing LHBT tenotomy. In line with our findings, Terra et al. also reported satisfactory clinical outcomes using a tenotomy-free LHBT-based augmentation technique [18]. Avoiding tenotomy may offer theoretical advantages. First, preservation of tendon continuity may allow improved vascularity and tendon healing. Second, the depressor effect of the LHBT on the humeral head may be maintained, potentially reducing superior migration and decreasing the load on the repaired rotator cuff. However, further studies directly comparing augmentation techniques with and without tenotomy are required to clarify these potential benefits.

The main limitations of the present study include its retrospective design and relatively limited sample size. In addition, tendon healing was not assessed using a standardized imaging protocol in all patients, as postoperative MRI was not routinely performed but obtained selectively, primarily to avoid additional healthcare costs, particularly in patients without clinical complaints. Therefore, asymptomatic structural re-tears may have remained undetected, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true re-tear rate. The follow-up duration of approximately one year may also be relatively short for evaluating long-term tendon healing and structural integrity, and longer-term studies are warranted to better assess the durability of the repair. Moreover, the intraoperative decision-making process may have introduced a degree of selection bias, and detailed preoperative tear characteristics, such as tear pattern and tendon quality, were not systematically recorded for all patients. Additionally, range of motion assessment did not include detailed evaluation of external and internal rotation at 0° of abduction, which may provide further insight into rotator cuff function and daily activity performance. Furthermore, patient-reported outcome measures such as satisfaction and quality-of-life scales were not included, which may limit the comprehensive evaluation of patient-centered outcomes. Finally, operative time, number of anchors used, and surgical cost were not systematically recorded or compared between the groups, limiting the assessment of the practical applicability and cost-effectiveness of the surgical techniques.

In conclusion, LHBT augmentation appears to be an effective method for the treatment of massive retracted RCTs. Satisfactory clinical outcomes can be achieved without performing LHBT tenotomy. Accurate preoperative assessment of fatty degeneration on MRI remains important for appropriate patient selection. Partial repair remains a viable treatment option in carefully selected patients. However, further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are needed to better establish the comparative effectiveness of these techniques.
