GPNMB promotes double-membrane vesicle accumulation and facilitates structural protein transport during PEDV infection
Authors: Qin Li, Qiang Wei, Aiping Wang, Yu Lu, Shenli Zhang, Hua Feng, Fangyu Wang, Xuefeng Sun, Guangxu Xing, Yunchao Liu, Yilin Bai, Wenqiang Jiao, Gaiping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPorcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) causes substantial economic losses in the swine industry globally. Host factors regulating the intracellular replication of PEDV, particularly early RNA synthesis and structural protein production, are not well understood, limiting antiviral strategies. We report that glycoprotein non‑metastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB) is a key host factor promoting PEDV infection. Initially identified as a PEDV S1-binding partner, GPNMB was confirmed to enhance infection via loss-and gain-of-function experiments in Vero and IPEC-J2 cells. Genetic knockout of GPNMB inhibited PEDV replication without affecting viral attachment or internalization. We show that GPNMB is necessary for the accumulation of double-membrane vesicles (DMVs) and promotes early viral RNA synthesis. Notably, GPNMB directly interacts with the PEDV spike (S) and nucleocapsid (N) proteins, increases their abundance, and facilitates their transport from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the Golgi apparatus, implicating it in structural protein maturation. Our work reveals a pivotal role for GPNMB in PEDV replication and nominates it as a target for host-directed antiviral intervention.KEYWORDS: PEDV, GPNMB, host factor, DMV, viral protein, ER-to-golgi transport

Introduction

Porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV), an enveloped virus with a diameter of 95–190 nm, belongs to the genus Alpha coronavirus within the family Coronaviridae. Its genome is approximately 28 kb in length and contains seven open reading frames (ORFs) that encode both nonstructural (nsp1-nsp16) and structural proteins, including the spike (S), envelope (E), membrane (M), and nucleocapsid (N) proteins, as well as the accessory protein ORF3 [1]. PED initially emerged in the United Kingdom during the 1970s, subsequently evolving into an endemic, sporadic disease across certain European nations and regions [2]. Commencing from 1990, isolated instances of PEDV infection have been documented in the Czech Republic, Belgium, Hungary, Thailand, as of present, PED has propagated throughout multiple countries [3]. In recent years, the frequent emergence of highly virulent mutant strains poses a sustained challenge to swine production, highlighting the need for new therapeutic interventions [4].

PEDV replication begins with viral entry, driven by the binding of the viral spike (S) protein to host receptors. The S1 subunit, comprising N-terminal and C-terminal domains (S1-NTD and S1-CTD), mediates attachment to glycans and protein receptors, respectively [5]. Following entry, the viral genome is translated and processed into nonstructural proteins that drive the formation of double-membrane vesicles (DMVs) [6,7]. The replication and transcription complex (RTC) assembles on DMVs to initiate RNA synthesis [8], producing both full-length genomic RNA and a nested set of subgenomic RNAs (sgRNAs) via discontinuous transcription. These sgRNAs direct the synthesis of a nested array of subgenomic mRNAs (sg mRNAs) [9,10], initiating structural protein production. Like host secretory proteins, viral structural proteins are processed through the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus before virion assembly and release [11,12]. While several host factors involved in PEDV entry have been identified, regulators of its intracellular replication, particularly those governing RNA synthesis and structural protein biogenesis, remain poorly understood.

GPNMB (Glycoprotein Non-Metastatic Melanoma Protein B) is a highly conserved type I transmembrane glycoprotein involved in diverse physiological and pathological processes, including immune regulation [13,14], tissue repair [15], and cancer progression [16–18]. In bacterial infection, GPNMB interferes with autophagosome-lysosome fusion by impairing the SNARE complex, thereby promoting intracellular survival of Mycobacterium leprae [19]. In virology, however, its role remains scarcely explored. To date, only two porcine viruses have been examined: in porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) infection [20], GPNMB inhibits autophagosome-lysosome fusion and attenuates replication; in porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2) infection [21], it binds the viral ORF5 protein, upregulates cyclin A, drives S-phase entry, and suppresses viral replication. Thus, while GPNMB appears to exert antiviral activity in these systems, its function during coronavirus infection, such as that caused by PEDV, remains to be elucidated, necessitating systematic investigation.

GPNMB, first identified as a host membrane protein interacting with PEDV S1, was functionally characterized in this study as a proviral factor essential for viral replication in Vero and IPEC-J2 cells. We demonstrate that GPNMB facilitates viral RNA synthesis and DMV accumulation, directly binds to the viral structural proteins S and N, and promotes their expression and ER-to-Golgi transport. These findings establish GPNMB as a novel host dependency factor for PEDV and highlight its potential as a target for host-directed antiviral strategies.

Materials and methods

Cells and virus

Vero cells (African green monkey kidney cells), IPEC-J2 cells (intestinal porcine epithelial cells, clone J2), and HEK-293T cells (human embryonic kidney cells) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; 12,100; Solarbio, Beijing, China). All media were supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; FS201-02, TransGen, Beijing, China) and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. Vero, IPEC-J2, and HEK-293T cells were stored in our laboratory.

The PEDV strains CH/Hubei/2016 (GenBank: KY928065.1) and DR13 (GenBank: JQ023162), both archived in our laboratory [22], were propagated in Vero cells. Viral titers were determined via the Karber method. The two strains were kept in our laboratory.

Antibodies

The following antibodies were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, United Kingdom): rabbit anti-GAPDH monoclonal antibody (MAb) (ab1811602), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse MAb (ab150113) and Alexa Fluor 647 donkey anti-rabbit pAb (ab150075). Rabbit anti-GPNMB MAb (84160–3-RR) was purchased from Proteintech (China) and used to detect Vero cells, and rabbit anti-GPNMB polyclonal antibody (pAb) (GB111475-100) was purchased from Servicebio (China) and used to detect IPEC-J2 cells. Mouse anti-PEDV S1 MAb (SD-3) and mouse anti-PEDV N MAb (SD-2) were obtained from Medgene Labs (SD, USA). Additionally, mouse anti-His MAb (66005–1-lg) and rabbit anti-HA tag MAb (7c9) were purchased from Proteintech (China). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse (115–035-003) and anti-rabbit (111–035-003) immunoglobulin G (IgG) pAbs were procured from Jackson Immuno Research (West Grove, PA). Finally, mouse anti-dsRNA (J2) MAb (10010200) was acquired from Scicons (Budapest, Hungary).

Reagents

FreeZol Reagent (R711), Lipomaster 3000 transfection reagent (TL301), HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect for qPCR(R333-C1) and Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix(Q712) were acquired from Vazyme (China). Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (13778150) was acquired from Invitrogen (USA). NP-40 lysis buffer (P0013F), Triton X-100 (P0096), and proteinase K (ST535) were acquired from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Minute ER enrichment kit (ER-036) and Minute GA enrichment kit (GO-037) were acquired from Invent Biotechnologies (Eden Prairie, MN).

The PEDV S plasmid (GenBank: JQ023161.1) conserved in our laboratory was cloned into the pFUSE-lgG1-Fc vector. Truncated versions of the PEDV S protein, namely S1, NTD, and CTD plasmids, were also cloned into the same pFUSE-lgG1-Fc vector. Full-length GPNMB cDNA, designed based on the green monkey GPNMB gene sequence (GenBank: XM_007981889.2), was synthesized by General Biol and subsequently inserted into the pcDNA 3.1 myc-His-A vector.

GPNMB-targeting siRNAs and a negative control siRNA (siNC) were commercially sourced from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). These siRNAs were designed for application in both green monkey (Vero) and porcine (Sus scrofa) cellular models. Fresh cells were transfected with the indicated siRNAs using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent according to the instructions for 30 h. Co-IP assay

For exogenous protein interactions, HEK-293T cells in 6-well plates were co‑transfected with the indicated tagged constructs (HA-Fc-NTD/CTD, Fc-S1, Myc-His-GPNMB). Lysates were incubated with HA-, Myc-, or protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress, Dallas, TX, USA). For endogenous interactions, Vero cells in 10-cm dishes were infected with PEDV (MOI = 1, 36 h). Lysates were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4°C with anti-GPNMB MAb, anti-PEDV S MAb, or corresponding IgG controls, followed by a 30-min incubation with protein A/G magnetic beads at room temperature.

The immune complexes adsorbed by the magnetic beads were collected using a magnetic rack, subsequently washed five times with PBS (or TBST) containing 0.5% Tween 20. An appropriate amount of PBST was added to resuspend the precipitate along with the magnetic beads, which were then combined with SDS-PAGE loading buffer and heated for 10 min at 100°C. The samples were separated via SDS-PAGE using an appropriate concentration, then transferred onto a 0.22/0.45 µm PVDF membrane. Membranes were blocked in 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 2 h, followed by incubation with specified primary antibodies either overnight at 4°C or for 1.5 h at room temperature, and further incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, immunoreactive bands were visualized using ECL reagent and imaged employing a chemiluminescence imaging system (Fusion FX7; VILBER, Paris, France).

IFA and confocal microscopy

The cells underwent washing with PBS, fixation using 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and permeabilization via immunostaining permeabilization buffer containing Triton X-100 (Beyotime, P0096) for 8 min, all at room temperature. Following three rinses with PBS, the cells were subjected to blocking with fresh 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 2 h, after which they were incubated with the respective primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to homologous secondary antibodies, followed by DAPI staining (8 min) to visualize nuclei. Fluorescence images were captured using a laser confocal scanning microscope (LSM700, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) with a 40× objective lens and a 63× oil immersion lens. Each sample was obtained from three independent experiments. The colocalization analyses were performed using the JaCoP plugin in ImageJ software according to previous research guidelines [24,25]. In our study, the Manders’ coefficient demonstrates excellent robustness to variations in fluorescent intensity, provides intuitive biological interpretability, and shows resilience against interference, rendering it a suitable choice for analyzing fluorescence co-localization [26].

TEM

WT and GPNMB-KO cell lines (6 cm plates) were infected with PEDV (MOI = 1) for 4 or 6 h. After washing once with PBS, the cells were harvested by trypsin digestion, followed by three additional washes with PBS. The cells were then fixed with 2 mL of 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Servicebio, G1102) at room temperature for 30 min. Fresh fixative was subsequently added, and the samples were left to stand overnight at 4°C to ensure complete fixation. Negative-stain electron microscopy was performed by Servicebio, and high-resolution images were captured using a transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, HT7700).

The Vero GPNMB-KO cell lines were constructed by UBIGENE, utilizing the gRNA-EZ-editor Cas9-EGFP plasmid detailed in Table 3. The establishment of the KO cell line was confirmed via Sanger sequencing, and endogenous GPNMB protein expression was verified by IB.

Viral attachment, internalization, and replication

Viral attachment

WT/KO cell lines and GPNMB-knockdown IPEC-J2 cells were plated in 24-well cell culture plates. At 90% confluence, cells were infected with PEDV (MOI: DR13 = 5; HB = 2.5) at 4°C for 2 h. Subsequently, the cells underwent three washes with cold PBS to remove unbound viruses. RT-qPCR was then employed to quantify cell-associated viral RNA. In parallel, attached virus was released by three freeze-thaw cycles for subsequent titration by TCID50 assay. Immunofluorescence microscopy was utilized to detect viral S protein on the cell surface, thereby confirming viral attachment.

Viral internalization

Following the attachment period, cells were washed three times with cold PBS to remove unbound virus. Pre-warmed DMEM was then added and the culture plates were transferred to a 37°C incubator for 1 h to allow internalization. External virus was removed using PBS containing proteinase K (10 µM). Internalized virus was quantified by RT-qPCR and virus titration assay. To visualize the colocalization of internalized virus with early endosomes, cells were immunostained with mouse anti-PEDV S MAb and rabbit anti-EEA1 MAb.

Viral replication

WT/KO cell lines and GPNMB-knockdown IPEC-J2 cells were seeded in 24-well culture plates. Upon reaching 90% confluence, cells were infected with PEDV at an MOI of 1. After 1 h, cells were washed with PBS to remove unbound virus, followed by the addition of fresh DMEM and incubation at 37°C for 4 or 6 h to allow viral replication. Cells were then washed three times with PBS. Intracellular viral genomic RNA (gRNA) and subgenomic RNA (sgRNA) levels were quantified by RT-qPCR to assess viral RNA synthesis. Additionally, colocalization of viral dsRNA with GPNMB was analyzed by immunofluorescence staining.

Enrichment of ER and GA proteins

KO cells were plated in 10-cm dishes (two dishes per sample) and allowed to adhere for 12 h. At 80% confluency, cells were co-transfected with S/N and GPNMB expression plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent for 30 h. Cells were then rinsed three times with ice-cold PBS, scraped, and subjected to ER and GA fractionation using the Minute ER/GA Enrichment Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions [27,28]. Enriched proteins were resuspended in 100 µL of denaturing lysis buffer (WA-009, Invent Biotechnologies), and protein concentrations were determined. Samples were mixed with SDS-PAGE loading buffer, heated at 70°C for 10 min, and analyzed by IB.

Statistical analysis

Experiments were repeated independently at least three times. Data are presented as means ± the standard errors of means (SEM) from three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was assessed by Student’s t-test, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using Prism 8.1 software (GraphPad, CA). Statistical significance is denoted by asterisks: not significant (ns), p > 0.05; *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001; ****, p < 0.0001.

RESULTS

GPNMB expression is dynamically dysregulated during PEDV infection

To identify host factors that facilitate PEDV infection, we performed mass spectrometry (MS) to profile proteins interacting with the PEDV S1 protein [29]. Among the candidates identified, GPNMB, which has a poorly defined role in viral infections, was prioritized for further mechanistic exploration in this study. To investigate the correlation between GPNMB and PEDV infection, we assessed its transcriptional and protein expression levels in Vero cells following infection. Samples from infected and mock-infected conditions, collected at designated time points (3, 6, 9, 12, 24, and 36 hpi), were analyzed by RT-qPCR and IB. The results showed that GPNMB mRNA levels were significantly upregulated from 24 hpi onward Figure 1(A,B), whereas its protein levels decreased markedly Figure 1(C). This divergent pattern suggests that PEDV infection may activate GPNMB gene expression, yet the subsequent reduction in protein could result from virus- or host-mediated regulation at the post-transcriptional or translational level.

(A and B) relative levels of PEDV RNA and GPNMB mRNA were measured by RT-qPCR. (C) Endogenous GPNMB protein was detected by IB. Grayscale values were quantified using ImageJ, normalized to GAPDH, and expressed relative to mock-infected controls (set as 1.0). Data are presented as means ± SEM from three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was assessed by Student’s t-test. **, p < 0.01; ****, p < 0.0001; ns, not significant.

GPNMB facilitates PEDV infection in Vero and IPEC-J2 cells

To define the role of GPNMB in PEDV infection, we first designed siRNA to knock down endogenous GPNMB in Vero cells Figure 2(A,E). Cells transfected with siRNA for 30 hours were infected with PEDV (MOI = 0.01) and harvested at 30 hours post-infection. Silencing GPNMB significantly reduced PEDV RNA abundance (~58%, Figure 2(B) and viral N protein levels to approximately 53% Figure 2(F). Conversely, transient overexpression of GPNMB from transfected plasmids Figure 2(C,G), prior to PEDV infection (MOI = 0.01), markedly increased viral RNA abundance 148%, Figure 2(D) and N protein level to approximately 170% Figure 2(H).

To further validate the proviral role of GPNMB in a pig-derived cell line, we designed and screened two siRNAs targeting endogenous GPNMB in IPEC-J2 cells Figure 2(I,J). Upon knockdown, both siRNAs significantly reduced PEDV RNA abundance (siRNA1: ~52%, siRNA2: ~37%, Figure 2(K) and viral N protein levels (siRNA1: ~87%, siRNA2: ~76%, Figure 2(L). Consistently, overexpression of exogenous GPNMB in IPEC-J2 cells Figure 2(M,O) enhanced PEDV RNA abundance ~196%, Figure 2(N) and viral N protein levels ~152%, Figure 2(O).

To genetically validate the proviral role of GPNMB, we generated a GPNMB-knockout (KO) Vero cell line using CRISPR-Cas9 Figure 3(A). Following PEDV infection, KO cells exhibited a significant reduction in viral N protein levels especially early in infection, progeny virus titers (~1.1 log10TCID50/mL), and infectivity (~83% detected by IFA), compared to wild-type (WT) controls Figure 3(B–D). To complement this, reintroduction of GPNMB into KO cells restored viral replication, increased N protein levels in a dose-dependent manner, virus titers (~1.2 log10TCID50/mL), and infectivity (~355% detected by IFA) to levels surpassing the baseline Figure 3(E,G). Taken together, our loss- and gain-of-function analyses establish GPNMB as a positive regulator of PEDV infection in both Vero and pig-derived IPEC-J2 cells.

GPNMB interacts with the C-terminal domain (CTD) of PEDV S1 subunit

GPNMB was initially identified as a protein that interacted with the PEDV S1 subunit by liquid-chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) in our prior pulldown assay. To validate this interaction endogenously, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) and confocal microscopy were performed in PEDV-infected Vero cells. Both assays confirmed that endogenous GPNMB interacts and colocalizes with the viral S protein during infection Figure 4(A,B). Consistent with this, the interaction between exogenous GPNMB and PEDV S protein was corroborated by confocal microscopy in HEK-293T cells Figure 4(B).

To directly verify the interaction between PEDV S1 subunit and GPNMB, we performed co-IP in HEK-293T cells co-transfected with plasmids encoding Myc-His-tagged GPNMB and Fc-tagged PEDV S1. The results confirmed that Fc-S1 efficiently captured Myc-His-GPNMB Figure 4(C). To map the interacting region within S1 subunit, we constructed two truncations (HA-Fc-NTD and HA-Fc-CTD) and performed reciprocal co-IP. The CTD, but not the NTD, readily precipitated GPNMB; conversely, GPNMB selectively captured the CTD Figure 4(D,E). These data indicate that GPNMB specifically interacts with the CTD of the PEDV S1 subunit. Confocal immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed that GPNMB exhibited stronger colocalization with HA-Fc-CTD than with HA-Fc-NTD (Figure S1).

GPNMB is dispensable for PEDV attachment and endocytic entry

To delineate the role of GPNMB in the PEDV life cycle, we first assessed its impact on viral attachment. GPNMB-KO and WT Vero cells were inoculated with PEDV (MOI = 5) at 4°C for 2 h. After removing unbound virions, quantitative analysis by RT-qPCR, TCID50 assay, and confocal microscopy revealed no significant difference in bound virus between the two Vero cell lines Figure 5(A–C). Similarly, knockdown of GPNMB in IPEC-J2 cells did not affect viral attachment Figure 5(D).

We then examined the internalization stage. Cells were incubated with virus at 4°C for 2 h, followed by a shift to 37°C for 1 h to allow entry. RT-qPCR and virus titration showed that GPNMB deficiency did not affect PEDV internalization in either Vero or IPEC-J2 cells Figure 5(E,G). Furthermore, since internalized PEDV is known to enter lysosomes via early endosomes, we examined colocalization with the early endosome marker early endosome antigen 1 (EEA1) at 30 min post-infection [29]. GPNMB knockout did not alter PEDV entry into EEA1-positive early endosomes Figure 5(H). Together, these findings rule out a requirement for GPNMB during PEDV attachment and endocytosis, thereby suggesting that it functions at a post-entry stage of the viral life cycle.

GPNMB is required for efficient PEDV replication in the early stages of infection

Since GPNMB knockout did not affect PEDV adsorption or internalization, we next asked whether it influences viral replication. Initially, we explored the dynamics of PEDV viral RNA replication and protein expression during infection. Viral RNA levels began to increase at 4 h, and N/S proteins were first detected by immunoblotting at 7 h Figure 6(A,B). Infectious progeny virions appeared in the supernatant at 7 h Figure 6(C), defining the duration of the first replication cycle. We therefore assessed the role of GPNMB at 7 h.

In Vero cells, GPNMB knockout significantly suppressed PEDV replication during a single cycle, reducing viral RNA by ~53%, progeny titers by ~1.34 log10 TCID50/mL, and N and S protein levels by ~69% and ~49%, respectively Figure 6(D–F). Consistent results were obtained in IPEC-J2 cells following GPNMB knockdown: viral RNA decreased by ~87% (siRNA1) and ~91% (siRNA2) Figure 6(G), while N protein decreased by ~78% (siRNA1) and ~75% (siRNA2), and S protein by ~67% (siRNA1) and ~54% (siRNA2) Figure 6(H). Notably, the inhibitory effect of GPNMB loss was more pronounced during the first replication cycle than over multiple cycles, suggesting that GPNMB exerts its primary effect early in infection. Together, these data demonstrate that GPNMB is required for efficient PEDV replication.

GPNMB promotes PEDV DMV accumulation to facilitate viral replication

Coronaviruses hijack the host endomembrane system to generate double-membrane vesicles (DMVs) as sites for viral RNA replication [30,31]. GPNMB, which we found essential for early PEDV RNA replication, has been reported to colocalize with LC3 [19], a protein implicated in coronavirus DMV biogenesis. Since PEDV RNA synthesis increases markedly from 4 hpi Figure 6(B) and DMVs typically appear by 6 hpi during coronavirus replication [32,33], we selected these time points for analysis. Immunofluorescence at 4 hpi revealed significant colocalization between dsRNA and GPNMB Figure 7(A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of infected cells (1 MOI) at 4 and 6 hpi showed a marked reduction in DMVs in GPNMB-KO cells compared to WT controls Figure 7(B,C). Consistent with impaired replication compartment accumulation, both genomic and PEDV N mRNA levels were significantly lower in KO cells at 4 hpi Figure 7(D). These data indicate that GPNMB is a key host factor regulating early PEDV RNA replication, likely through supporting DMV accumulation.

GPNMB facilitates the maturation of PEDV structural proteins S and N

Beyond its role in viral RNA synthesis, GPNMB knockout reduced the expression of PEDV structural proteins S and N Figure 6(F,H). Given its direct interaction with S protein (Figure 4), we hypothesized that GPNMB might enhance structural protein expression. Testing this in GPNMB-KO cells with increasing doses of GPNMB plasmid showed that both S and N mRNA and protein levels rose in a dose-dependent manner Figure 8(A–D). Notably, at lower doses (<0.4 µg), protein levels increased significantly while mRNA levels did not, prompting us to use 0.4 µg of GPNMB in subsequent experiments to focus on post-transcriptional effects. Furthermore, co-IP confirmed that GPNMB also directly interacts with the N protein (Figure S2), indicating that it interacts with and may regulate multiple PEDV structural proteins.

Given the importance of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus (GA) in viral protein maturation, we next asked whether GPNMB influences the localization of S and N proteins within these organelles. After co-transfection, ER and Golgi fractions were isolated using Calnexin and GM130 as markers, respectively. GPNMB expression reduced S protein levels in the ER while increasing them in the Golgi Figure 8(E,F). In contrast, there was minimal loss of N protein in the ER and an increase but slightly less than that of S protein in the Golgi Figure 8(G,H). To further validate this effect under infection conditions, GPNMB-KO cells were transfected with GPNMB plasmid and then infected with PEDV. GPNMB overexpression consistently promoted the ER to Golgi redistribution of endogenous S and N proteins Figure 8(I,J). These results suggest that GPNMB promotes the translocation of S and, to a lesser extent, N proteins from the ER to the Golgi apparatus, potentially facilitating their post-translational modification [34,35]. This previously unreported function of GPNMB warrants further investigation.

Discussion

Coronaviruses represent persistent threats to global health, driven by the need to uncover host pathways exploited for viral replication. PEDV, like other coronaviruses, hijacks host cellular machinery, making essential host factors prime targets for antiviral strategies. This study establishes the transmembrane glycoprotein GPNMB as a crucial host factor promoting PEDV replication, defining a role previously unknown in virology. Although GPNMB is broadly expressed and linked to cancer progression and Mycobacterium leprae pathogenesis, its involvement in viral infections was unclear. We found that GPNMB facilitates PEDV infection. Intriguingly, in the only viral systems studied to date, GPNMB has been reported to exert antiviral effects against PRRSV and PCV2, indicating starkly context-dependent roles. This functional divergence suggests that pathogens may differentially modulate GPNMB-related pathways. We propose that GPNMB could function as a pan-pathogenic modulator, a versatility potentially rooted in its conserved cellular functions.

We first examined the impact of PEDV infection on GPNMB expression over an extended time course. At 12 h post-infection, PEDV upregulated GPNMB transcription while simultaneously reducing its protein level, a seemingly paradoxical observation given that GPNMB overexpression subsequently enhanced viral replication. This discrepancy suggests that the decline in GPNMB protein may reflect a host post-translational regulatory response aimed at counteracting PEDV infection, rather than a direct pro-viral effect. Notably, analogous discordance between viral infection and host protein regulation has been documented for Sendai virus (SeV) [36] and various enteroviruses [37], implying that such regulatory mechanisms may be evolutionarily conserved across divergent viral families.

To delineate the functional role of GPNMB in PEDV infection, we systematically assessed its involvement across key stages of the viral life cycle. Initial experiments in Vero cells (infected with the DR13 strain) and IPEC-J2 cells (infected with the HB strain) revealed that GPNMB depletion did not affect viral attachment or internalization. Therefore, we focused on the early replication phase. Here, GPNMB depletion significantly suppressed PEDV replication, impairing both viral RNA synthesis and the expression of structural proteins in both susceptible cell lines. In addition, we observed that silencing of GPNMB exerted a greater effect on S and N protein expression in IPEC-J2 cells than in Vero cells during the early phase of viral replication (Figure 6(F,H). This discrepancy may be attributable to several factors, including differences in replication kinetics and infection efficiency between the two cell lines, as well as the use of distinct viral strains. Furthermore, the interference treatment itself may have influenced cell viability and viral proliferation, potentially contributing to the observed variations. These results define GPNMB as a host factor specifically required for PEDV replication post-entry.

A growing body of evidence has implicated a diverse array of host factors in the formation and accumulation of coronavirus-induced double-membrane vesicles (DMVs). These include clathrin adaptor complexes (AP1, AP2) and dynamin involved in endosomal sorting [38–40]; Rab GTPases such as RAB7A, RAB10, RAB14, and RAB6A, which regulate endosomal, exocytic, and Golgi trafficking [39,41–43]. Additionally, the endocytic sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) complex members have been consistently identified in multiple screens, predominantly as restriction factors [44]; Among autophagy-related proteins, LC3 and ATG family members have been extensively linked to DMV biogenesis, as coronaviruses are known to subvert autophagic machinery for replication organelle formation [45,46]. Given that GPNMB co-localizes with LC3 under physiological conditions [19] and that coronaviruses can co-opt LC3-I for DMV biogenesis [31], we hypothesized that GPNMB might facilitate DMV accumulation through interactions with LC3-I. Supporting this, our TEM analysis revealed dsRNA-like branched filamentous structures within DMVs=early during PEDV infection, consistent with cryo-EM observations of SARS-CoV-2 replication sites [47]. Furthermore, confocal immunofluorescence analysis revealed a marked colocalization of GPNMB with LC3 in PEDV-infected cells compared to mock-infected controls, further supporting our hypothesis (Figure S3). Importantly, GPNMB knockout significantly reduced DMV numbers, directly linking it to viral replication organelle accumulation. Consistently, immunoblotting showed that LC3 lipidation was also diminished in GPNMB-deficient cells (Figure S4) strengthening the functional connection between GPNMB and LC3. However, the precise relationship among GPNMB, LC3-I, autophagic flux and PEDV-induced DMVs remains unclear. Therefore, A key unresolved question is whether GPNMB modulates DMV accumulation by influencing how PEDV hijacks LC3-I and autophagic flux, or whether it participates more directly in the LC3-I-dependent membrane remodeling process itself. These hypotheses, however, remain to be rigorously tested in future studies.

During the rapid phase of PEDV RNA replication, we found that GPNMB significantly colocalizes with viral dsRNA, which is a replication intermediate typically confined within DMVs to evade host defenses [47]. Given the distinct biogenesis of genomic (gRNA) and subgenomic RNAs (sgRNAs, exemplified by N mRNA) [48], we tested the specificity of GPNMB’s effect. Loss of GPNMB reduced the levels of both gRNA and N mRNA, indicating a broad role in viral RNA synthesis. This broad effect aligns with the observed reduction in DMVs upon GPNMB knockout, thus providing a mechanistic basis for its essential role in viral replication.

In addition to supporting viral RNA synthesis, GPNMB promotes the expression of PEDV structural proteins S and N and facilitates their redistribution from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the Golgi apparatus, as confirmed by rescue experiments in GPNMB-KO cells under infection conditions. This effect was more pronounced for S than for N, a consistent pattern observed across both overexpression and infection datasets. We interpret this as a biologically meaningful difference rather than a technical limitation. Given that GPNMB directly interacts with both S and N proteins (Figure 3 and Figure S2), it is plausible that the strength or stability of these interactions differs, leading to distinct degrees of transport enhancement. Alternatively, the milder impact on N protein localization may reflect differences in its intrinsic ER retention or folding kinetics.

Several host proteins have been implicated in the ER-to-Golgi trafficking of viral components during coronavirus infection. For instance, components of the COPII vesicle machinery, such as SEC24C, are known to facilitate the transport of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein from the ER to the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) [49]. Similarly, the Rab GTPase RAB1B has been shown to remodel the early secretory pathway to benefit viral replication [50,51]. Compared to these well-characterized trafficking regulators, GPNMB appears to exert a broader effect, influencing not only the trafficking of multiple viral structural proteins (S and N) but also their expression levels. Given that rapid viral protein synthesis often leads to ER overload and accumulation of unfolded proteins, we hypothesize that GPNMB may facilitate the proper folding and/or ER-to-Golgi trafficking of viral structural proteins, thereby promoting their maturation and subsequent virion assembly [52,53]. The increased S/N protein levels in the Golgi further suggest a potential role for GPNMB in post-translational modification or quality control. Although our current data focus on S and N, it is plausible that GPNMB supports the trafficking of other viral structural proteins, a possibility that merits further exploration. Future studies aimed at dissecting the molecular interplay between GPNMB and the COPII or Rab machinery will be essential to fully elucidate the mechanistic basis of GPNMB-mediated viral protein trafficking. Of note, COPII vesicles have been shown to contribute membrane precursors for LC3 lipidation during autophagosome formation [54,55], suggesting a functional link between secretory trafficking and autophagy. Given that GPNMB influences both DMV accumulation and protein trafficking, it is plausible that GPNMB coordinates these processes through a shared COPII-dependent mechanism. This possibility, however, warrants further investigation.

A primary limitation of this study is its sole reliance on in vitro cell culture models; therefore, the physiological relevance of these findings necessitates in vivo validation. The absence of animal experiments in the current study was largely attributable to constraints in laboratory resources and available facilities at the time of this investigation. Notwithstanding these limitations, we have identified DMV accumulation and protein trafficking as key priorities for future research. We aim to extend these in vitro observations to in vivo models, for example, by employing GPNMB-knockout piglet models or performing challenge experiments in piglets treated with GPNMB inhibitors, to evaluate the role of GPNMB in PEDV replication, protein trafficking, and autophagic flux under physiological conditions. Such efforts will not only address the current limitations but also enhance the translational relevance of our findings.

In conclusion, we have identified GPNMB as a proviral host factor essential for PEDV replication. Our work reveals that GPNMB serves dual functions: it supports the accumulation of replication organelles (DMVs) to promote viral RNA synthesis and enhances the production of structural proteins S and N. This mechanistic insight advances our understanding of PEDV pathogenesis and provides a rationale for targeting GPNMB in antiviral strategies.
