Abstract
. This paper is concerned with the problem of prescribing Gaussian curvature K \displaystyle K and geodesic curvature h \displaystyle h in a compact surface with boundary Σ \displaystyle\Sigma with conical singularities { p 1 , … , p n } \displaystyle\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\} and corners { q 1 , … , q n } \displaystyle\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\} . This is equivalent to solving the Liouville-type equation: { − Δ ​ u + 2 ​ K 0 = 2 ​ K ​ e u − 4 ​ π ​ ∑ i = 1 n α i ​ ( δ p i − 1 | Σ | ) − 2 ​ π ​ ∑ j = 1 m β j ​ ( δ q j − 1 | Σ | ) in ​ Σ ∂ ν u + 2 ​ h 0 = 2 ​ h ​ e u / 2 on ​ ∂ Σ , \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+2K_{0}=2Ke^{u}-4\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\left(\delta_{p_{i}}-\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}\right)-2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}\left(\delta_{q_{j}}-\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}\right)&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\ \partial_{\nu}u+2h_{0}=2he^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\end{array}\right., where K 0 , h 0 \displaystyle K_{0},h_{0} are the pre-existing Gaussian curvature and the geodesic curvature, respectively, and α i , β j > − 1 \displaystyle\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}>-1 are given. Solutions are obtained using a new variational formulation, first introduced in [ 8 ] for the regular counterpart of the problem and extended here to the singular case. As far as we know, this is the first result for the problem of prescribed curvatures in surfaces with the two types of singularities. Key ingredients are a blow-up analysis around a sequence of points different from local maxima and Morse index estimates.

1.
Introduction

Let
\displaystyle(\Sigma,g_{0})
be a closed Riemannian surface. A classical problem in geometric analysis is given by the
prescribed Gaussian curvature problem
, that is: given a smooth function
\displaystyle K:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
, find another metric
\displaystyle g=e^{u}g_{0}
being conformally equivalent to
\displaystyle g_{0}
with Gaussian curvature equal to
\displaystyle K(x)
at each point
\displaystyle x\in\Sigma
. Such a problem is equivalent to solving the following Liouville-type equation

(1.1)

-\Delta u+2K_{0}=2Ke^{u}\qquad\mbox{in }\Sigma,

where
\displaystyle\Delta
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator associated to the metric
\displaystyle g_{0}
and
\displaystyle K_{0}
is the Gaussian curvature with respect to the metric
\displaystyle g_{0}
. The problem (
1.1
) has been widely studied for decades; we refer to Chapter 6 in
[
2
]
(and its references) for a survey.

A natural extension of problem (
1.1
) to compact surfaces with boundary is the
double curvature prescription problem
: given smooth functions
\displaystyle K:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle h:\partial\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
, we want to find a conformal metric
\displaystyle g=e^{u}g_{0}
such that the Gaussian curvature with respect to
\displaystyle g
equals
\displaystyle K
pointwise on
\displaystyle\Sigma
and its geodesic curvature coincides with
\displaystyle h
pointwise on
\displaystyle\partial\Sigma
. The equivalent formulation in terms of partial differential equations is given by the following nonlinear Neumann problem on
\displaystyle\Sigma
:

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+2K_{0}=2Ke^{u}&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}u+2h_{0}=2he^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\end{array}\right.,

with
\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}
being the outer normal derivative and
\displaystyle h_{0}
being the geodesic curvature with respect to
\displaystyle g_{0}
.

Despite its relevance, problem (
1.2
) has been significantly less explored compared to (
1.1
). Some results are available for particular cases: specifically, the case
\displaystyle h=0
has been treated in
[
14
]
, while the case
\displaystyle K=0
has been studied in
[
13
,
30
,
32
]
. Blow-up behavior of solutions has also been studied, see
[
3
,
18
]
. When
\displaystyle K
and
\displaystyle h
are constants, explicit solutions have been classified when the domain is a disc or an annulus; see
[
24
,
26
]
. In addition, classification results in the half-plane setting can be found in
[
23
,
29
,
42
]
.

However, not much is known about the general case where both curvatures are non-constant functions. A variational approach has been explored in
[
17
,
33
,
8
]
, while some asymptotic properties have been examined in
[
25
,
9
,
10
]
. Further progress in this direction remains an open challenge.

This paper is concerned with generalizing the problem (
1.2
) to a metric
\displaystyle g
with singularities such as finitely many given
conical points
in
\displaystyle\Sigma
or
corners
in
\displaystyle\partial\Sigma
. We say that
\displaystyle p
is a conical singularity of order
\displaystyle\alpha>-1
, or angle
\displaystyle 2\pi(1+\alpha)
, if there is a local coordinate system
\displaystyle\Xi:B_{\delta}(p)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\displaystyle\Xi(p)=0
and
\displaystyle g
has the form

(1.3)

g(x)=|\Xi(x)|^{2\alpha}e^{v(x)}g_{e}(x),

where
\displaystyle v
is a smooth function and
\displaystyle g_{e}
denotes the standard Euclidean metric. This implies that
\displaystyle(\Sigma,g)
is locally isometric around
\displaystyle p
to an Euclidean cone with angle
\displaystyle 2\pi(1+\alpha)
.

Similarly,
\displaystyle q\in\partial\Sigma
is a
corner
of order
\displaystyle\beta>-1
, or angle
\displaystyle\pi(1+\beta)
, if in a suitable local coordinate system
\displaystyle\Xi
around
\displaystyle q
the metric
\displaystyle g
has the form

(1.4)

g(x)=|\Xi(x)|^{2\beta}e^{v(x)}g_{e}(x);

in this case,
\displaystyle(\Sigma,g)
is locally isometric to the planar sector

\{(\rho\cos\theta,\rho\sin\theta):\,\rho\geq 0,\,0\leq\theta\leq\pi(1+\beta)\}.

Fixing some conical singularities
\displaystyle p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\in\Sigma
of orders
\displaystyle\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
, respectively, and some corners
\displaystyle q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\in\partial\Sigma
of orders
\displaystyle\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{m}
, respectively, the equation that must be satisfied by the logarithm of the conformal factor is now

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+2K_{0}=2Ke^{u}-4\pi\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\left(\delta_{p_{i}}-\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}\right)-2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}\left(\delta_{q_{j}}-\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}\right)&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}u+2h_{0}=2he^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\end{array}\right.,

where
\displaystyle\delta_{p}
denotes the Dirac delta function with pole at
\displaystyle p
, and
\displaystyle|\Sigma|
is the area of
\displaystyle\Sigma
.

The problem may be de-singularized by the substitution

u:=v+4\pi\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}G_{p_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\beta_{j}G_{q_{j}}\right),

where
\displaystyle G_{p}
is the Green’s function of
\displaystyle-\Delta
with pole at
\displaystyle p
, i.e. the solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta G_{p}=\delta_{p}-\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}G_{p}=0&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\\
\int_{\Sigma}G_{p}=0\end{array}\right.,

This leads to the following problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+2K_{0}=2\widetilde{K}e^{u}&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}u+2h_{0}=2\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\end{array}\right.,

where

(1.5)

\widetilde{K}=Ke^{-4\pi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}G_{p_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}G_{q_{j}}\right)},\,\,\,\,\widetilde{h}=he^{-2\pi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}G_{p_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}G_{q_{j}}\right)};

we point out that, in view of the logarithmic singularity of
\displaystyle G_{p}
, the local behavior of
\displaystyle\widetilde{K},\widetilde{h}
at each singular point is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widetilde{K}=d(\cdot,p_{i})^{2\alpha_{i}}\widehat{K}

around

\displaystyle\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widetilde{K}=d(\cdot,q_{j})^{2\beta_{j}}\widehat{K}

around

\displaystyle\displaystyle q_{j}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widetilde{h}=d(\cdot,q_{j})^{\beta_{j}}\widehat{h}

around

\displaystyle\displaystyle q_{j},

with
\displaystyle\widehat{K},\widehat{h}
smooth and bounded from below by positive constants, consistently with (
1.3
), (
1.4
).

By integrating both sides of the equation and applying the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, we obtain

(1.6)

\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}=2\pi\chi,

where
\displaystyle\chi
represents the singular Euler characteristic of
\displaystyle\left(\Sigma,\underline{\alpha},\underline{\beta}\right)
, first introduced by Troyanov in
[
41
]
, defined by

(1.7)

\chi:=\chi\left(\Sigma,\underline{\alpha},\underline{\beta}\right)=\chi(\Sigma)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j},

and
\displaystyle\chi(\Sigma)
is the classical Euler characteristic of
\displaystyle\Sigma
.

As a preliminary, let us remark that, without singularities, we can always prescribe zero geodesic curvature and constant Gaussian curvature (see for instance Proposition 3.1 in
[
33
]
). Due to this, we may assume that
\displaystyle h_{0}=0
and
\displaystyle K_{0}=\frac{2\pi\chi}{|\Sigma|}
, where
\displaystyle|\Sigma|
is the measure of the surface area of
\displaystyle\Sigma
, so that the problem simplifies to:

(1.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\frac{4\pi\chi}{|\Sigma|}=2\widetilde{K}e^{u}&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}u=2\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\end{array}\right..

As we will see in Section
2
, by re-writing this problem as a
mean-field equation
, solutions to (
1.8
) can be interpreted as critical points on the space

\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma):=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\Sigma):\int_{\Sigma}u=0\right\}

of the energy functional:

(1.9)

\mathcal{J}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-\mathcal{F}_{\chi}\left(\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u},\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right),

for some smooth
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\chi}(A,B)
.

This new approach is inspired by the work of
[
8
]
on prescribing Gaussian and geodesic curvature on smooth surfaces with boundary, without singularities. It extends the well-known mean field model for the prescribed Gaussian curvature problem on closed surfaces, a model that has been widely applied in this context, as seen in the seminal work
[
20
]
.

The introduction of this formulation, both for problem (
1.8
) and its counterpart on closed surfaces, is motivated by to its better adaptability to variational methods. Furthermore, it is invariant under the addition of constants. In fact, the mean field formulation allows us to find solutions as either absolute minima or min-max critical points of the energy functional through the use of Trudinger-Moser inequalities. This aspect will be explored in greater depth in Sections
3
and
5
.

In the study of the existence of solutions to the problem (
1.8
), a key role is played by the
Trudinger constant
of the singular surface, denoted by
\displaystyle\tau
. This constant is defined as

(1.10)

\tau:=\tau\left(\underline{\alpha},\underline{\beta}\right)=\min\left\{1,2+2\min_{i=1,\dots,n}\alpha_{i},1+\min_{j=1,\dots,m}\beta_{j}\right\},

and together with the
singular Euler characteristic

\displaystyle\chi
, heavily influences the study of this problem. This is basically due to Trudinger-Moser inequalities (see Proposition
3.1
) which allow us to establish whether the energy functional
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
is bounded from below and coercive, depending on which is larger between
\displaystyle\tau
and
\displaystyle\chi
, thus yielding minimizing solutions. Following
[
41
]
, we have the following definition:

Definition 1.1
.

The singular surface
\displaystyle\left(\Sigma,\underline{\alpha},\underline{\beta}\right)
is classified as:

•

subcritical
if
\displaystyle 0<\chi<\tau
;

•

critical
if
\displaystyle\chi=\tau
;

•

supercritical
if
\displaystyle\chi>\tau
.

In the
subcritical
case, Trudinger-Moser inequalities can be employed to show the existence of solutions, as the energy functional (
1.9
) is coercive. In the
critical
and
supercritical
cases, coercivity does not hold anymore in
\displaystyle\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
, but we can recovery coercivity under additional assumptions of symmetry on the curvatures
\displaystyle K
and
\displaystyle h
. Specifically, we assume that
\displaystyle K
and
\displaystyle h
are invariant under the action of an isometry group
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
of
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}
with no boundary fixed points, namely:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})\cap\partial\Sigma=\emptyset;\\
K(x)=K(gx)\qquad\mbox{for a.e. }x\in\Sigma,\,\forall g\in\mathcal{G};\\
h(x)=h(gx)\qquad\mbox{for a.e. }x\in\Sigma,\,\forall g\in\mathcal{G}.\end{array}

Symmetry allows us to search for solutions in the subspace of
\displaystyle\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
consisting on
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
-symmetric functions:

\widetilde{H}_{\mathcal{G}}:=\left\{u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma):u(gx)=u(x)\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\Sigma,\,\forall g\in\mathcal{G}\right\}.

On such a space, one can obtain improved Trudinger-Moser inequalities, which allow us to recover coercivity for
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
(see Proposition
3.8
). The role of symmetry was first highlighted in
[
37
]
and has been further explored in recent works on curvature prescription problems, such as
[
17
,
34
]
.

However, due to the presence of singularities, we must discuss whether
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
has fixed points and, if it does, whether they are conical singularities. We emphasize that this is a new aspect compared to the problem without singularities, originally considered in
[
8
]
, because in the regular case the only situation where
\displaystyle\chi>0
is critical, and it corresponds to simply connected surfaces (see (
1.7
)), where admissible symmetries have one fixed point.

Based on this, we got the following result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
\displaystyle\chi>0
and
\displaystyle K\geq 0,K\not\equiv 0
. Then, problem (
1.8
) has a solution if one of the following conditions holds:

•

\displaystyle\left(\Sigma,\underline{\alpha},\underline{\beta}\right)
is subcritical;

•

\displaystyle\left(\Sigma,\underline{\alpha},\underline{\beta}\right)
is critical, (
1
) holds and

either

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})\cap\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}=\emptyset,

or

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tau<2+2\min_{i:\,p_{i}\in\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})}\alpha_{i};

•

\displaystyle\left(\Sigma,\underline{\alpha},\underline{\beta}\right)
is supercritical, (
1
) holds and

either

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})=\emptyset\quad\mbox{and }\chi<k\tau,

or

\displaystyle\displaystyle\chi<\min\left\{2,2+2\min_{i:\,p_{i}\in\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})}\alpha_{i},k\tau\right\};

here,

(1.11)

k:=\min\{\#\mathcal{G}x:\,x\in\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})\}\geq 2.

We point out that we are making a sign assumption only on the prescribed interior curvature
\displaystyle K
and no assumptions at all on the prescribed boundary curvature
\displaystyle h
.

In the case
\displaystyle\chi=0
, we have two distinct scenarios, as discussed in
[
8
]
: when
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}
is positive,
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\chi}
is negative everywhere, making the energy functional coercive so that the problem has a minimizing solution. Conversely, when
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}
is negative, to deal with the nonlinear term and obtain minimizers we must impose conditions on the scale invariant function
\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}:\partial\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

(1.12)

\mathfrak{D}(x):=\frac{h(x)}{\sqrt{|K(x)|}}.

Thanks to this the following result can then be stated:

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume
\displaystyle\chi=0
. Then, problem (
1.8
) has a solution if one of the following conditions holds:

•

\displaystyle K(x)>0
for some
\displaystyle x\in\Sigma
and
\displaystyle h(y)\leq 0
for all
\displaystyle y\in\partial\Sigma
, with
\displaystyle\not\equiv 0
;

•

\displaystyle K(x)\geq 0
for all
\displaystyle x\in\Sigma
,
\displaystyle K\not\equiv 0
, and
\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}h<0
;

•

\displaystyle K(x)\leq 0
for all
\displaystyle x\in\Sigma
,
\displaystyle K\not\equiv 0
, and
\displaystyle h(y)\geq 0,\mathfrak{D}(y)<1
for all
\displaystyle y\in\partial\Sigma
,
\displaystyle h\not\equiv 0
.

In the case
\displaystyle\chi<0
, minimizing solutions can be obtained under a specific assumption involving (
1.12
), similar to Theorem
1.3
. This yields the following result, which extends
[
8
, Theorem 1.3]
to the singular setting:

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume
\displaystyle\chi<0
,
\displaystyle K\leq 0
for all
\displaystyle x\in\Sigma
,
\displaystyle K\not\equiv 0
,
\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\log|K|>-\infty
, and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(y)<1
for all
\displaystyle y\in\partial\Sigma
. Then, problem (
1.8
) has a solution.

Quite remarkably, singularities do not play any role in Theorem
1.3
nor in Theorem
1.4
. This is essentially due to the fact that the ratio (
1.12
) is invariant when
\displaystyle h
is replaced with
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}
and
\displaystyle K
is replaced with
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}
, in light of the definition in (
1.5
).

The next natural step is to remove the symmetry assumptions in Theorem
1.2
. This is addressed through a strategy that involves a topological analysis of the sublevel sets of (
1.9
):

\mathcal{J}^{a}=\left\{u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma):\,\mathcal{J}(u)\leq a\right\}.

This approach, originally introduced in
[
20
]
, has become a standard technique for handling mean-field equations. A key objective is to show that low sublevels are not contractible. In fact, when
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u)\ll 0
, the measure

\frac{\widetilde{K}e^{u}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}

tends to concentrate around a finite number of points, a phenomenon that can be rigorously established using localized versions of the Trudinger-Moser inequality.

A fundamental consequence of this concentration behavior is that the topology of low sublevels is inherited from the space of finitely supported measures, known as barycenters. This provides the framework for proving the existence of min-max type solutions. While this technique has been widely applied in closed surfaces, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first time it has been used to prescribe two non-vanishing curvatures on surfaces with boundary.

Given that the Palais-Smale condition is not known for mean-field type problems, we employ a variation of the
monotonicity trick
, first introduced by Struwe in
[
39
]
. In simple terms, we introduce an auxiliary parameter for which the Palais-Smale condition holds for almost every value, then we consider a sequence of such parameters satisfying the Palais-Smale and attempt to pass to the limit, for which we need the compactness of solutions to the corresponding auxiliary problems. Since this approach requires monotonicity with respect to the parameter, its choice is particularly delicate due to the new nonlinear term
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\chi}
, which involves both interior and boundary integrals.

This method allows us to bypass the Palais-Smale condition, provided that
\displaystyle 4\pi\chi
does not belong to the discrete set

(1.13)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Gamma=\Gamma\left(\underline{\alpha},\underline{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\{4\pi k+8\pi\sum_{i\in I}(1+\alpha_{i})+4\pi\sum_{j\in J}(1+\beta_{j}):\right.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left.\phantom{\sum_{j\in J}}k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,I\subset\{1,\dots,n\},\,J\subset\{1,\dots,m\}\right\}.

Solutions to (
1.8
) may have new and complex blow-up phenomena, compared to classical Liouville-type problems, especially in the case when
\displaystyle K
and
\displaystyle h
have opposite signs, or may change sign, since the
masses

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}
and
\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}
might both diverge to infinity with different signs; concentration phenomena have not been classified yet in full generality.

To address this issue, we restrict ourselves to sequences with finite Morse index; thanks to a result by Fang and Ghoussoub
[
21
]
, we suffice to consider the finite Morse index case because we are using a finite-dimensional min-max argument. In fact, one of the main novelties of this work is the identification of a relationship between the Morse indices of two different variational formulations of the problem. This link is used to exploit the fact that the direct one is particularly suitable for this specific analysis (see Subsection
2.2
).

Since our approach relies on a min-max argument, compactness properties are essential. A detailed blow-up analysis is provided in Section
4
.

In order to prove the existence of conformal metrics in the supercritical case, we assume that the singularities
\displaystyle\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}
satisfy the following inequalities:

(1.14)

\alpha_{i}\geq-\frac{1}{2},\,\forall i=1,\dots,n,\qquad\beta_{j}\geq 0,\,\forall j\in 1,\dots,m.

By (
1.10
), one can verify that this assumption is equivalent to assuming the Trudinger constant to be equal to
\displaystyle 1
, hence to get a Trudinger-Moser inequality with the same constant.

We also need
\displaystyle\Sigma
to be multiply connected, in order to apply a topological argument similar to
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

We therefore get the following result, which is proved in Subsection
5.2
:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\displaystyle\Sigma
be a supercritical singular surface with conical singularities and/or corners as in (
1.14
). Assume that:

•

\displaystyle K>0
on
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}
;

•

The surface’s boundary has at least two components:

\partial\Sigma=\bigcup_{k=1}^{l}\gamma_{k}\qquad l\geq 2;

•

There exists at least one boundary component without corners, w.l.o.g.

q_{j}\notin\gamma_{1}\quad\mbox{for all }j\in\{1,\dots,m\}.

If
\displaystyle 4\pi\chi\notin\Gamma
, then there exists a conformal metric with Gaussian curvature
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}
and geodesic curvature
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}
.

As for Theorem
1.2
, we emphasize that this result only needs a mild sign assumption on
\displaystyle K
and no assumption at all on
\displaystyle h
.

Notations

B^{+}_{r}(x):=\left\{(s,t)\in B_{r}(x)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}:t\geq 0\right\},

2.
Variational formulation

We will devote the first part of this section to introduce the mean-field formulation that we will use to study the problem. This is mostly an adaptation for the singular case of the one proposed in
[
8
]
for the study of regular case, so we will limit ourselves to collect the main results modifying the notation to the singular case; we recommend to the interested reader the Section 2 of
[
8
]
for proofs and comments.

The last part of this section is devoted to a useful comparison between the direct and the mean-field formulations.

2.1.
Mean field formulation

Following the approach of
[
8
, Proposition 2.1]
we can see that the problem (
1.8
) is equivalent to a mean-field type problem.

Proposition 2.1
.

Problem (
1.8
) is solvable if the following problem has a solution.

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\frac{4\pi\chi}{|\Sigma|}=2C^{2}(u)\widetilde{K}e^{u}&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}u=2C(u)\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\\
C(u)>0\end{array}\right.,

where
\displaystyle C(u)
is a root of

(2.2)

C^{2}(u)\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}+C(u)\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}=2\pi\chi

given by

C(u)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{4\pi\chi}{\sqrt{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}+8\pi\chi\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}&\chi>0\\
-\frac{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}&\chi=0\\
\frac{4\pi|\chi|}{\sqrt{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}-8\pi|\chi|\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}-\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}&\chi<0\end{array}\right..

In the cases when (
2.2
) has two positive solutions, depending on the values of the two integrals, two possible choices are allowed, getting to equivalent formulations. To be consistent with the global analysis, we choose the branch of solutions which extends the branch of unique solutions when multiplicity fails.

The main reason why we introduce this mean-field formulation is that it admits an energy functional whose geometry can be studied in a simpler way than if we used the one given by the direct formulation.

Proposition 2.2
.

Solutions to (
2.1
) are critical points of the energy functional
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
defined in (
1.9
) on the space

(2.3)

H_{\chi}=\left\{u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma):\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}>-\frac{1}{8\pi\chi}\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)_{+}^{2}&\chi>0\\
\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}<0&\chi=0\\
\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}<\frac{1}{8\pi|\chi|}\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)_{-}^{2}&\chi<0\end{array}\right\}\right\};

where

(2.4)

\mathcal{F}_{\chi}(A,B)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}8\pi\chi\left(\log{\left(\sqrt{B^{2}+8\pi\chi A}+B\right)}+\frac{B}{\sqrt{B^{2}+8\pi\chi A}+B}\right)&\chi>0\\
-2\frac{B^{2}}{A}&\chi=0\\
8\pi|\chi|\left(-\log{\left(\sqrt{B^{2}-8\pi|\chi|A}-B\right)}+\frac{B}{\sqrt{B^{2}-8\pi|\chi|A}-B}\right)&\chi<0\end{array}\right..

Proof.

It is a straightforward verification as in
[
8
, Proposition 2.4]
.
∎

Clearly, one has to make sure that the energy functional is defined on a nonempty space. To do so, certain considerations with
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}
and
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}
must be taken into account in order to obtain a solution, arguing as in
[
8
, Lemma 2.5]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The space
\displaystyle H_{\chi}
is non-empty if and only if:

(1)

if
\displaystyle\chi>0
,
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}(x)>0
for some
\displaystyle x\in\Sigma
or
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}(y)>0
for some
\displaystyle y\in\partial\Sigma
;

(2)

if
\displaystyle\chi=0
,
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}(x)\widetilde{h}(y)<0
for some
\displaystyle x\in\Sigma
,
\displaystyle y\in\partial\Sigma
;

(3)

if
\displaystyle\chi<0
,
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}(x)<0
for some
\displaystyle x\in\Sigma
or
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}(y)<0
for some
\displaystyle y\in\partial\Sigma
.

Moreover, if such conditions are not satisfied, then problem (
1.8
) is not solvable.

2.2.
Relation with the direct formulation

Solution to the problem (
1.8
) can be also seen as critical points to the following energy functional on the whole space
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Sigma)
:

(2.5)

\mathcal{I}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{4\pi\chi}{|\Sigma|}\int_{\Sigma}u-2\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}-4\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}.

This is the so-called
direct formulation
, which was the approach to regular problem in the papers
[
33
,
34
]
.

In order to stress the relation between the energy functionals (
2.5
) and (
1.9
), we introduce the following submanifold:

\mathcal{M}:=\left\{u\in\overline{H}^{1}\left(\Sigma\right):\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}=2\pi\chi\right\},

which is just the constraint given by the Gauss-Bonnet formula (
1.6
). One can easily check that the two functionals coincide, up to a constant, on this manifold:
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}|_{\mathcal{M}}=\mathcal{J}|_{\mathcal{M}}+c
.

The same argument works for non geometrical version of both problems, that is

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\frac{\lambda}{|\Sigma|}=2C^{2}(u)\widetilde{K}e^{u}&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}u=2C(u)\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\\
C(u)=\frac{4\pi\chi}{\sqrt{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}+8\pi\chi\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}>0\end{array}\right.,

and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\frac{\lambda}{|\Sigma|}=2\widetilde{K}e^{u}&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}u=2\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\\
\end{array}\right.,

whose energy functionals are given respectively by

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-\mathcal{F}_{\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}}\left(\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u},\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)

and

\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{|\Sigma|}\int_{\Sigma}u-2\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}-4\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}.

The following result highlights an important relation between the energy functionals
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}
related to the two formulation, as it shows that their Morse index on solutions may differ by only a finite number. This will be used in Section
4
, where finiteness of Morse index will be essential to classify concentration phenomena.

Proposition 2.4
.

There exists a constant
\displaystyle c_{\lambda}
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}
coincides with
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}+c_{\lambda}
on the submanifold

\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}:=\left\{u\in\overline{H}^{1}\left(\Sigma\right):\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\right\}.

Moreover,
\displaystyle|\mathrm{ind}(u)-\mathrm{ind^{*}}(u)|\leq 1
where
\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(u)
denotes the Morse index of
\displaystyle u
associated to
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}
:

(2.6)

\mathrm{ind}(u):=\sup\left\{\dim E:E\subset H^{1}(\Sigma),\,\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(u)[\phi,\phi]<0\,\forall\phi\in E\setminus\{0\}\right\};

and
\displaystyle\mathrm{ind^{*}}(u)
is the Morse index associated to the functional
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}
:

(2.7)

\mathrm{ind^{*}}(u):=\sup\left\{\dim E:E\subset H^{1}(\Sigma),\,\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(u)[\phi,\phi]<0\,\forall\phi\in E\setminus\{0\}\right\}.

Proof.

After simple computations one see that

\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}(u)|_{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-2\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}-\lambda,

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}(u)|_{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-2\lambda\left(\log\lambda+\frac{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-2\lambda\log\lambda-2\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2},

therefore
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}|_{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}=\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}|_{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}+2\lambda\log\lambda-\lambda
.

To deal with the Morse indices, at each point
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
we split
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Sigma)=T_{u}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}\oplus N_{u}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
, where
\displaystyle T_{u}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
is the tangent space of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
at
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle N_{u}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
is its normal space. Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}
coincide on
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
, up to the constant
\displaystyle c_{\lambda}
, the tangent spaces will coincide, therefore:

\mathcal{J}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(u)=\mathcal{J}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(u)|_{T_{u}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}+\mathcal{J}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(u)|_{N_{u}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}=\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(u)|_{T_{u}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}+\mathcal{J}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(u)|_{N_{u}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}.

On the other hand, since
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}
has co-dimension 2 in
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Sigma)
and co-dimension 1 in
\displaystyle\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
, then
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(u)|_{N_{u}\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}}
is a two-dimensional quadratic form, which furthermore vanishes in the direction of constants function, due to the invariance of
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
. Therefore the number of directions where the quadratic form associated to
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\lambda}
have different sign, that is the difference between the Morse indices, can be at most 1.
∎

3.
Energy-minimizing solutions in the case of positive singular Euler characteristic

In this section we study the case of
\displaystyle\chi>0
, where we will show that the energy functional (
1.9
) is coercive, hence we will get energy-minimizing solutions. We will divide our discussion into two parts, the first one devoted to the subcritical case and the second one to the critical and supercritical cases, for which we will need an additional symmetric hypothesis.

3.1.
Subcritical case

In the study of the geometry of the energy functional (
1.9
) the logarithmic term plays a crucial role, since the other term in the definition (
2.4
) of
\displaystyle F(A,B)
is easily seen to be bounded from above by
\displaystyle 1
, namely

\frac{B}{\sqrt{B^{2}+8\pi\chi A}+B}\leq 1.

This means that the analysis of the energy functional will be based on the study of the logarithmic term in detail. To this purpose we will follow the approach proposed in
[
8
]
, based on suitable Trudinger-Moser type inequalities. In our case, such inequalities will take account of the singularities in the problem, which are encoded in the Trudinger constant
\displaystyle\tau
defined in (
1.10
).

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists
\displaystyle C=C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
there holds:

\log{\left(\sqrt{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}+8\pi\chi\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{16\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C,

with
\displaystyle\tau
as in (
1.10
)

The following two lemmas are essential to get control on domains with conical singularities or corners. The proof of Proposition
3.1
, at the end of this sub-section, is an easy consequence of these lemmas.

The first lemma is based on a Euclidean singular Trudinger-Moser inequality from
[
1
]
and a covering argument.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
\displaystyle C\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(3.1)

\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{8\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C,

for all
\displaystyle u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

For any
\displaystyle x\in\overline{\Sigma}
we take
\displaystyle\delta(x)>0
such that one has isothermal coordinates
\displaystyle\Xi:B_{2\delta(x)}(x)\to B_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
if
\displaystyle x\in\Sigma
or
\displaystyle\Xi:B_{2\delta(x)}(x)\to B^{+}_{1}(0)
if
\displaystyle x\in\partial\Sigma
. In the former case, for any
\displaystyle u\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{2\delta(x)}(x))
we have
\displaystyle\widetilde{u}:=u\circ\Xi^{-1}\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{1}(0))
and

(3.2)

\int_{B_{2\delta(x)}(x)}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq C\int_{B_{1}(0)}\left(\widetilde{K}\circ\Xi^{-1}\right)e^{\widetilde{u}},\quad\int_{B_{2\delta(x)}(x)}|\nabla u|^{2}=\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\nabla\widetilde{u}|^{2},

latter being a consequence of conformal invariance. On the other hand, if
\displaystyle x\in\partial\Sigma
and
\displaystyle u\in H^{1}(B_{2\delta(x)}(x))
satisfies
\displaystyle u\equiv 0
on
\displaystyle\partial B_{2\delta(x)}(x)\cap\Sigma
, we set

\widetilde{u}(s,t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u\left(\Xi^{-1}(s,t)\right)&\mbox{if }(s,t)\in B^{+}_{1}(0)\\
u\left(\Xi^{-1}(s,-t)\right)&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.:

as before, we have
\displaystyle\widetilde{u}\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{1}(0))
, but (
3.2
) is replaced with

(3.3)

\int_{B_{2\delta(x)}(x)}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq C\int_{B_{1}(0)}\left(\widetilde{K}\circ\Xi^{-1}\right)e^{\widetilde{u}},\quad\int_{B_{2\delta(x)}(x)}|\nabla u|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\nabla\widetilde{u}|^{2}.

It is also not restrictive to assume
\displaystyle\delta(x)
so small that
\displaystyle B_{2\delta(x)}
does not include any singular point, except possibly
\displaystyle x
; this allows to apply the singular Trudinger-Moser inequality from Adimurthi and Sandeep (Theorem 2.1 in
[
1
]
) and get, if
\displaystyle u\not\equiv 0
:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\int_{B_{1}(0)}\left(\widetilde{K}\circ\Xi^{-1}\right)e^{\widetilde{u}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\int_{B_{1}(0)}\frac{e^{\widetilde{u}}}{|x|^{2\alpha(x)_{-}}}\mathrm{d}x+C

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\int_{B_{1}(0)}\frac{e^{\frac{4(1+\alpha(x)_{-}\pi)\widetilde{u}^{2}}{\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\nabla\widetilde{u}|^{2}}}}{|x|^{2\alpha(x)_{-}}}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{16(1+\alpha(x)_{-})\pi}\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\nabla\widetilde{u}|^{2}+C

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{16\tau\pi}\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\nabla\widetilde{u}|^{2}+C,

where

(3.5)

\alpha(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha_{i}&x=p_{i}\\
0&x\notin\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\end{array}\right..

We now cover
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}
with the open balls
\displaystyle\{B_{\delta(x)}(x)\}_{x\in\overline{\Sigma}}
and, thanks to compactness,
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B_{\delta(x_{i})}(x_{i})
. We then fix
\displaystyle u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
and, up to re-ordering the points, we will have

\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{1})}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\geq\frac{1}{N}\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u},\qquad\delta:=\delta(x_{1}).

We decompose
\displaystyle u
as a Fourier series with respect to an orthonormal frame of eigenfunctions
\displaystyle\varphi_{n}
for
\displaystyle-\Delta
on
\displaystyle\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
with associated eigenvalues
\displaystyle 0<\varsigma_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\nearrow}\infty
and we split

u=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_{n}\varphi_{n}=\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_{n}\varphi_{n}}_{=:v}+\underbrace{\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}u_{n}\varphi_{n}}_{=:w},

with
\displaystyle N=N_{\varepsilon,\delta}
to be chosen later. Since
\displaystyle v
belongs to an
\displaystyle N
-dimensional space, we get

(3.6)

\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma)}\leq C\sqrt{\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla v|^{2}}\leq\frac{C^{2}}{4\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla v|^{2}\leq\frac{C^{2}}{4\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2},

with
\displaystyle C
depending on
\displaystyle N
, hence only on
\displaystyle\varepsilon,\delta
; on the other hand, by the choice of
\displaystyle N
we get

\int_{\Sigma}w^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\varsigma_{N+1}}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla w|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\varsigma_{N+1}}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}.

Take now a smooth cut-off
\displaystyle\zeta
such that

(3.7)

\zeta\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(B_{2\delta}(x)),\qquad\zeta\equiv 1\mbox{ on }B_{\delta}(x),\qquad|\nabla\zeta|\leq C_{\delta},

so that
\displaystyle\zeta w
vanishes on
\displaystyle\partial B_{\delta}(x_{1})
and we can apply (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) to
\displaystyle\widetilde{\zeta w}\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{1}(0))
; we either get

\log\int_{B_{2\delta}(x_{1})}\widetilde{K}e^{\zeta w}\leq\frac{1}{16\tau\pi}\int_{B_{2\delta}(x_{1})}|\nabla(\zeta w)|^{2}+C

or

(3.8)

\log\int_{B_{2\delta}(x_{1})}\widetilde{K}e^{\zeta w}\leq\frac{1}{8\tau\pi}\int_{B_{2\delta}(x_{1})}|\nabla(\zeta w)|^{2}+C,

the latter clearly holding true in both cases.

Therefore, by choosing

N=N_{\varepsilon,\delta}:=\max\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\,\varsigma_{n}<\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon^{2}}C_{\delta}\right\}

with
\displaystyle C_{\delta}
as in (
3.7
), we get:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{B_{2\delta}(x_{1})}|\nabla(\zeta w)|^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\left(\zeta^{2}|\nabla w|^{2}+2\zeta w\nabla w\cdot\nabla\zeta+w^{2}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\left((1+\varepsilon)\zeta^{2}|\nabla w|^{2}+\left(1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)|\nabla\zeta|^{2}w^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla w|^{2}+\left(1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)C_{\delta}\int_{\Sigma}w^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla w|^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla w|^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1+2\varepsilon)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}.

Together with (
3.6
) and (
3.8
), this gets to the conclusion:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{1})}\widetilde{K}e^{u}+\log N

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\int_{B_{2\delta}(x_{1})}\widetilde{K}e^{\zeta w}+\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Sigma)}+\log N

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\tau\pi}\int_{B_{2\delta}(x_{1})}|\nabla(\zeta w)|^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1+2\varepsilon}{8\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1+\varepsilon^{\prime}}{8\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

∎

To deal with boundary integral term we have the following result, in the same spirit as
[
41
,
30
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
\displaystyle C=C_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\log\left|\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right|\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{16\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C,

for all
\displaystyle u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
.

Proof.

If
\displaystyle\tau=1
, then
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}
is bounded, therefore we just suffice to apply Corollary 2.6 from
[
17
]
, which says that

(3.9)

\log\int_{\partial\Sigma}e^{u/2}\leq\frac{1}{16\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

If
\displaystyle\tau<1
, then
\displaystyle h\in L^{p}(\partial\Sigma)
for
\displaystyle p=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon-\tau}<\frac{1}{1-\tau}
, therefore applying Hölder’s inequality and then (
3.9
) to
\displaystyle\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\tau}u
we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\left|\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1+\varepsilon-\tau}{1+\varepsilon}\log\int_{\partial\Sigma}\left|\widetilde{h}\right|^{\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon-\tau}}+\frac{\tau}{1+\varepsilon}\log\int_{\partial\Sigma}e^{(1+\varepsilon)/(2\tau)u}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1+\varepsilon}{16\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We consider the elementary inequality

(3.10)

\sqrt{B^{2}+8\pi A}+B\leq\max\left\{\sqrt{A},|B|\right\}\left(\sqrt{1+8\pi\chi}+1\right),\quad\mbox{for all }A>0,B\in\mathbb{R},

with

A=\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u},\qquad B=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}.

Therefore

\log{\left(\sqrt{B^{2}+8\pi\chi A}+B\right)}\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{2}\log A,\log|B|\right\}+C\leq\left(\frac{1}{16\tau\pi}+\varepsilon\right)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

∎

In the case when
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}
’s and
\displaystyle\beta_{j}
’s are all non-negative, that is when
\displaystyle\widetilde{h},\widetilde{K}\in L^{\infty}
and
\displaystyle\tau=1
, Lemmas
3.1
and

are well-known to hold true, even with
\displaystyle\varepsilon=0
, respectively from
[

]
(Corollary 2.5) and

3.2.
Critical and supercritical cases with symmetries

In the critical and supercritical cases, using suitable test functions one can see that the energy
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
is not coercive on
\displaystyle\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
. To bypass this issue we exploit the symmetry assumption (
1
), which gives and improved version of Proposition
3.1
.

We start by giving some
localized
versions of the Trudinger-Moser inequalities, see also
[
6
,
16
,
17
]
.

Proposition 3.4
.

For every
\displaystyle\delta,\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
\displaystyle C=C_{\varepsilon,\delta}>0
such that for any open
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\Sigma
and
\displaystyle u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
one has

8\tau\pi\log\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq\int_{\Omega^{\delta}}|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

If
\displaystyle\Omega^{\delta}\cap\partial\Sigma=\emptyset
for some
\displaystyle\delta>0
and
\displaystyle\Omega^{\delta}
contains no conical singularity, then

16\pi\log\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq\int_{\Omega^{\delta}}|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C:

if
\displaystyle\Omega^{\delta}\cap\partial\Sigma=\emptyset
and
\displaystyle\Omega^{\delta}\cap\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}=p_{i}
, then

16\pi\min\{1,1+\alpha_{i}\}\log\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq\int_{\Omega^{\delta}}|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

The details of the proof can be found in
[
35
, Proposition 2.2]
in a regular setting, but in the singular case a similar argument works. The main idea is to apply (
3.1
) to the function
\displaystyle u
multiplied by a cut-off function on
\displaystyle\Omega
. An immediate consequence of the above result is the following corollary, where a concentration of the conformal volume gives a global control of the volume in terms of the Dirichlet energy.

Corollary 3.5
.

Assume
\displaystyle\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{k}\subset\Sigma
are open domains satisfying, for some
\displaystyle\delta>0
,

\Omega_{i}^{\delta}\cap\Omega_{j}^{\delta}=\emptyset\quad\forall i,j=1,\dots,k,i\neq j.

(3.11)

\frac{\int_{\Omega_{i}}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}\geq\delta\quad\forall i=1,\dots,k.

Then, for every
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
\displaystyle C=C_{\varepsilon,\delta}>0
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
one has

\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{8k\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C,

Proof.

Applying Proposition
3.4
to each
\displaystyle\Omega_{i}
one has

8\tau\pi\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq 8\tau\pi\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\right)\leq\int_{\Omega_{i}^{\delta}}|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C_{\varepsilon,\delta}.

Since the
\displaystyle\Omega_{i}^{\delta}
’s are pairwise disjoint, then summing all of them we get:

8k\tau\pi\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\\
\leq\int_{\bigcup_{i=0}^{k-1}\Omega_{i}^{\delta}}|\nabla u|^{2}+k\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C_{\varepsilon,\delta}\leq(1+k\varepsilon)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

∎

The same ideas can be used to give a localised version of the Lemma
3.3
(see also Proposition 2.10 of
[
17
]
).

Proposition 3.6
.

For every
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
\displaystyle C=C_{\varepsilon}
such that for any
\displaystyle\Theta\subset\partial\Sigma
and
\displaystyle u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
one has

16\tau\pi\log\int_{\Theta}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\leq\int_{\Theta^{\delta}}|\nabla u|^{2}+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

Corollary 3.7
.

Assume
\displaystyle\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{k}\subset\partial\Sigma
are open domains satisfying, for some
\displaystyle\delta>0
,

\Theta_{i}^{\delta}\cap\Theta_{j}^{\delta}=\emptyset\qquad\forall i,j=1,\dots,k,i\neq j,

(3.12)

\frac{\int_{\Theta_{j}}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}\geq\delta\quad\forall j=1,\dots,k.

Then, for every
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
\displaystyle C=C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that, for any
\displaystyle u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
,

\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{16k\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

From now on, we assume that
\displaystyle K
and
\displaystyle h
are invariant under the action of an isometry group
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
with no fixed points on the boundary (as in (
1
)). This symmetry assumption allows us to improve the constant in the inequality stated in Proposition
3.1
by employing the refined Trudinger-Moser inequality from Corollaries
3.5
and (
3.7
), which in turn yield coercivity. The argument is analogous to that of Proposition 2.12 in
[
17
]
(see also
[
8
,
37
]
).

Moreover, it is easy to see that if
\displaystyle u\in\widetilde{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
does not vanish on some region of
\displaystyle\Sigma
, then the assumptions in (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) are satisfied. Consequently, the constant in the Trudinger-Moser inequality can be improved by

k=\min\left\{\#\mathcal{G}x:x\in\Sigma\setminus\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})\right\}.

In our case, the only additional issue to consider is whether there are fixed points in the interior of
\displaystyle\Sigma
or not.

Proposition 3.8
.

For any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
\displaystyle C=C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in\widetilde{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
, the following holds:

\log{\left(\sqrt{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}+8\pi\chi\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{16\pi\sigma}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C,

where

\sigma=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}k\tau&\mbox{if }\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})=\emptyset\\
\min\left\{2,2+2\min_{i:\,p_{i}\in\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})}\alpha_{i},k\tau\right\}&\mbox{if }\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})\neq\emptyset,\end{array}\right.,

and
\displaystyle k
is as in (
1.11
).

Proof.

We first establish an estimate for the boundary term and then for the interior term.

For any
\displaystyle x\in\partial\Sigma
, we choose elements
\displaystyle g_{1,x},\dots,g_{l,x}\in\mathcal{G}
that do not stabilize
\displaystyle x
, and select a small
\displaystyle\delta(x)>0
such that the points
\displaystyle g_{i,x}x
are at least
\displaystyle 2\delta(x)
apart, i.e.,

(3.13)

B_{2\delta(x)}(g_{i,x}x)\cap B_{2\delta(x)}(g_{j,x}x)=\emptyset,\quad\forall\,i\neq j.

We then cover
\displaystyle\partial\Sigma
with the open balls
\displaystyle\{B_{\delta(x)}(x)\}_{x\in\partial\Sigma}
so that, by compactness,

\partial\Sigma\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}B_{\delta(x_{i})}(x_{i}),

for some
\displaystyle x_{i},\dots,x_{N}
. Without loss of generality, assume that

\frac{\int_{B_{\delta(x_{1})}\cap\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}\geq\frac{1}{N},

where
\displaystyle\delta=\delta(x_{1})
. Since
\displaystyle u\in\widetilde{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
, for every
\displaystyle g_{i}=g_{i,x_{1}}
we have

\frac{\int_{B_{\delta}(g_{i}x_{1})\cap\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}=\frac{\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{1})\cap\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}\geq\frac{1}{N}.

Moreover, by our choice in (
3.13
) the balls
\displaystyle B_{\delta}(g_{i}x_{1})
are at a mutual distance of at least
\displaystyle 2\delta
. Thus, by applying Corollary
3.7
we obtain

(3.14)

\log\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{16k\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

Next, consider the interior estimate. We distinguish two cases.

Case 1:
\displaystyle\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})=\emptyset

Similarly to before, by a covering argument, up to choosing a smaller
\displaystyle\delta>0
there exist points
\displaystyle x_{1},g_{1}x_{1},\dots,g_{l}x_{1}
such that

B_{2\delta}(g_{i}x_{1})\cap B_{2\delta}(g_{j}x_{1})=\emptyset,\quad\forall\,i\neq j,

and

\frac{\int_{B_{\delta}(g_{i}x_{1})\cap\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}=\frac{\int_{B_{\delta}(x_{1})\cap\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}\geq\frac{1}{N},\quad\forall\,i=1,\dots,l.

Then, applying Corollary
3.5
, we deduce

\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{8k\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

Combining this with the boundary estimate and using (
3.10
), we obtain

(3.15)

\log\left(\sqrt{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}+8\pi\chi\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{16k\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

Case 2:
\displaystyle\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})\neq\emptyset

Since
\displaystyle\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})
is compact, we may fix a small
\displaystyle\delta>0
such that

d(\partial\Sigma,\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G}))\geq 2\delta

and, for any
\displaystyle x\in\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})
,
\displaystyle B_{\delta}(x)\subset\Sigma
and it contains no conical point except possibly
\displaystyle x
. Then, either

(3.16)

\frac{\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}(x)}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}\geq\frac{1}{2}

or

(3.17)

\frac{\int_{B_{\delta}(x)}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

In case (
3.16
), by a similar covering argument as in Case 1, we obtain

(3.18)

\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{8k\tau\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

Up to taking a smaller
\displaystyle\delta
, there exist
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N},x_{1},g_{1}x_{1},\dots,g_{l}x_{1}
such that

B_{2\delta}\left(g_{i}x_{1}\right)\cap B_{2\delta}\left(g_{j}x_{1}\right)=\emptyset\quad\forall\,i,j=1,\dots,l,\,i\neq j;

\frac{\int_{B_{\delta}(g_{i}x_{1})\cap\left(\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}(x)\right)}\widetilde{K}e^{u/2}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u/2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\int_{B_{\delta}(g_{i}x_{1})\cap\left(\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}(x)\right)}\widetilde{K}e^{u/2}}{\int_{\Sigma\setminus B_{\delta}(x)}\widetilde{K}e^{u/2}}\geq\frac{1}{2N},\,\forall\quad i=1,\dots,l.

From Corollary
3.5
one gets (
3.18
), hence, together with (
3.14
) and (
3.10
), we get again (
3.15
).

If instead (
3.17
) holds true, then by applying Proposition
3.4
, with the coefficient
\displaystyle\alpha(p)
defined in (
3.5
), we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle 16\pi\min\{1,1+\alpha(p)\}\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle 16\pi\min\{1,1+\alpha(p)\}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

In either case, we deduce

\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{8\pi\min\left\{2,2+2\min_{i:\,p_{i}\in\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})}\alpha_{i},k\tau\right\}}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

Finally, combining this with the boundary estimate and using (
3.10
) yields

\log\left(\sqrt{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}+8\pi\chi\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{16\pi\sigma}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C,

with

\sigma=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}k\tau&\mbox{if }\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})=\emptyset\\
\min\left\{2,2+2\min_{i:\,p_{i}\in\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})}\alpha_{i},k\tau\right\},&\mbox{if }\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})\neq\emptyset\end{array}\right..

This completes the proof.

∎

We are now in position to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

First of all, since
\displaystyle K\gneqq 0
, then
\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}>0
for any
\displaystyle u
, so the domain
\displaystyle H_{\chi}
of the energy functional
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
coincides with the whole
\displaystyle\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
(see (
2.3
)) and existence of minimizing solutions will follow by showing coercivity in
\displaystyle\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
in the subcritical case and coercivity in
\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
in the critical and subcritical case.

•

Subcritical case:

From Proposition
3.1
, for any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists
\displaystyle C>0
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
, we have:

\mathcal{F}_{\chi}\left(\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u},\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\chi}{\tau}+\varepsilon\right)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C,

implying:

\mathcal{J}(u)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\chi}{\tau}-\varepsilon\right)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-C.

Since we are in the subcritical case, we can take
\displaystyle\varepsilon<1-\frac{\chi}{\tau}
so that
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
is coercive, hence ensuring the existence of minimizers.

•

Critical and supercritical cases:

In the case
\displaystyle\mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal{G})=\emptyset
, Proposition
3.8
states that for any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
one has

\mathcal{F}_{\chi}\left(\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u},\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\chi}{k\tau}+\varepsilon\right)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C,

for any
\displaystyle u\in\widetilde{H}_{\mathcal{G}}
, hence

\mathcal{J}(u)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\chi}{k\tau}-\varepsilon\right)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-C.

Taking
\displaystyle\varepsilon<1-\frac{\chi}{k\tau}
, we ensure coercivity and existence of minimizers.

The cases when
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
has fixed points on
\displaystyle\Sigma
are dealt with similarly.

∎

4.
Blow-up analysis

This section will be focused on proving the following result concerning blow-up analysis of sequence of solutions to problem (
1.8
) and some other problems which approximate (
1.8
).

The main result of this Section, which will be proved in the end of the section, is the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle u_{n}
be a sequence of solutions to the problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u_{n}+\frac{\lambda_{n}}{|\Sigma|}=2\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u_{n}}&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}u_{n}=2\widetilde{h}_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\end{array}\right.,

where
\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\to\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{n}\to\widetilde{K}>0
, and
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{n}\to\widetilde{h}
in the
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{0,\gamma}
sense, with
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}
and
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}
as in (
1.5
). The singular set is defined as:

(4.1)

\mathcal{S}:=\left\{p\in\overline{\Sigma}:\exists x_{n}\to p\mbox{ as }n\to\infty\mbox{ such that }u_{n}(x_{n})\to\infty\right\}.

The following alternatives hold:

(1)

If
\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u_{n}}
is uniformly bounded, then, up to a subsequence, either:

(a)

\displaystyle u_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Sigma)
, or;

(b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
is finite and non-empty, such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u_{n}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightharpoonup}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}\cap\Sigma}8\pi(1+\alpha(p))\delta_{p}+\sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}\cap\partial\Sigma}\gamma(p)\delta_{p},

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\widetilde{h}_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightharpoonup}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}\cap\partial\Sigma}(4\pi(1+\beta(p))-\gamma(p))\delta_{p},

for some
\displaystyle\gamma(p)\in\mathbb{R}
,
\displaystyle\alpha(p)
as in (
3.5
) and

(4.3)

\beta(p):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\beta_{j}&p=q_{j}\\
0&p\notin\{q_{1},\dots,q_{m}\}\end{array}\right..

(2)

If
\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u_{n}}
is unbounded, then
\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(u_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\infty.

Here,
\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(u_{n})
represents the Morse index of the perturbed energy functional

\mathcal{J}_{n}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-\mathcal{F}_{\frac{\lambda_{n}}{4\pi\chi}}\left(\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u},\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}_{n}e^{u/2}\right)

on the functions
\displaystyle u_{n}
, defined as in (
2.6
).
This is essentially the number of negative eigenvalues of
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(u_{n})
, counted with multiplicity.

Blow-up with infinite mass is a new major issue with respect to classical mean field problems: in fact, Gauss-Bonnet theorem (
1.6
) gives a constraint on the sum of the two nonlinear integrals but, if
\displaystyle K,h
are sign-changing or have different sign, they may both diverge to
\displaystyle\infty
. The use of the Morse index allows to rule out this phenomenon, as in
[
33
]
, since solutions obtained with a finite-dimensional min-max scheme have uniformly bounded Morse index.

An easy consequence of Theorem
4.1
is that alternative (1.b) occurs only if the parameter
\displaystyle\lambda
belongs to a discrete set.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\Gamma
be as in (
1.13
). If
\displaystyle\lambda\notin\Gamma
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(u_{n})
is uniformly bounded, then
\displaystyle u_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Sigma)
.

To perform the blow-up, we first recall the key ideas from the classical works
[
12
,
28
]
, which were originally formulated for the regular case in closed surfaces and subsequently extended to the singular case in
[
40
]
. These works indicate that around any isolated concentration point
\displaystyle p\in\Sigma
, suitably rescaled solutions converge to solution to a limit problem on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
, whose solutions have been classified, provided the mass is bounded.
The limit problem is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v=2K_{0}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v}&\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v}<\infty\end{array}\right.,

where
\displaystyle K_{0}>0
and
\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha(p)
as in (
3.5
). Solutions have been classified by Chen and Li in
[
15
]
for
\displaystyle\alpha=0
and by Prajapat and Tarantello in
[
38
]
for
\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0
.

When
\displaystyle\partial\Sigma\neq\emptyset
and blow-up occurs at the boundary, the corresponding limiting problem has non-linear Neumann boundary condition in the half-space:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v=2K_{0}|x|^{2\beta}e^{v}&\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\\
\partial_{\nu}v=2h_{0}|x|^{\beta}e^{v/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|x|^{2\beta}e^{v}+\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|x|^{\beta}e^{v/2}<\infty\end{array}\right.,

where
\displaystyle K_{0}>0
and
\displaystyle\beta=\beta(p)
as in (
4.3
). The classification result for such problem has been obtained by Zhang in
[
42
]
for
\displaystyle\beta=0
and by Jost, Wang and Zhou in
[
27
]
.

In the study of problem (
1.8
) we may not have bounded mass, since both integrals in (
1.6
) may be infinite if
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}
and
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}
have different sign; in the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
this could happen if
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}
is sign-changing or everywhere negative. This is a new major issue, which affects massively the blow-up analysis, as in the works
[
33
,
34
]
which do not assume a bounded mass condition.

We tackle this issue by means of the Morse index, namely we show that any solution to limiting problems with infinite mass must have infinite Morse index. The first part of this section is devoted to the Morse index of entire functions, whereas in the second part we conclude the blow-up analysis and prove Theorem
4.1
.

4.1.
Solutions to the limit problems and their Morse Index

In this subsection, we study the Morse index of the solutions to two limit problems. One problem is defined on the whole space and the other on the half-space, that is, respectively,

(4.4)

-\Delta v=2K_{0}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v}\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{2},

and

(4.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta v=2K_{0}|x|^{2\beta}e^{v}&\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\\
\partial_{\nu}v=2h_{0}|x|^{\beta}e^{v/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\end{array}\right..

Solutions to (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) without mass assumptions have been classified, respectively, in
[
31
]
and
[
22
]
:

Theorem 4.3

(
[
31
]
)
.

Any solution
\displaystyle v
to (
4.4
) is in the form

v=\log\left(\frac{4|g^{\prime}|^{2}}{\left(1+K_{0}|g|^{2}\right)^{2}}\right)-2\alpha\log|x|,

where
\displaystyle g:\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}\to\overline{\mathbb{C}}
is a locally univalent meromorphic function.

Theorem 4.4

(Theorem 2,
[
22
]
)
.

Any solution
\displaystyle v
to (
4.5
) is in the form

v=\log\left(\frac{4|g^{\prime}|^{2}}{\left(1+K_{0}|g|^{2}\right)^{2}}\right)-2\beta\log|x|,

where
\displaystyle g:\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}\to\overline{\mathbb{C}}
is a locally univalent meromorphic function.

The definition of the Morse index of a solution
\displaystyle v
of problem (
4.4
) is as follows:

\mathrm{ind}(v):=\sup\left\{\dim E:E\subset\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right),\,\mathcal{Q}_{v}[\phi,\phi]<0\,\forall\phi\in E\setminus\{0\}\right\},

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{v}
is defined as:

\mathcal{Q}_{v}[\phi,\phi]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(|\nabla\phi|^{2}-2K_{0}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v}\phi^{2}\right).

Similarly, the Morse index of a solution
\displaystyle v
of problem (
4.5
) is defined as:

\mathrm{ind}(v):=\sup\left\{\dim E:E\subset\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\right),\,\mathcal{Q}_{v}[\phi,\phi]<0\,\forall\phi\in E\setminus\{0\}\right\},

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{v}
is dfined as:

\mathcal{Q}_{v}[\phi,\phi]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left(|\nabla\phi|^{2}-2K_{0}|x|^{2\beta}e^{v}\phi^{2}\right)-\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}h_{0}|x|^{\beta}e^{v/2}\phi^{2}.

The main result of this subsection is the following:

Theorem 4.5
.

(1)

The Morse index of any solution to (
4.4
) satisfies
\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(v)\geq 1
. Moreover,

\mbox{if}\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v}=\infty\qquad\mbox{then}\qquad\mathrm{ind}(v)=\infty.

(2)

The Morse index of any solution to (
4.5
) satisfies
\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(v)\geq 1
. Moreover,

\mbox{if}\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|x|^{2\beta}e^{v}=\infty\qquad\mbox{then}\qquad\mathrm{ind}(v)=\infty.

Proof.

(1)

For the first statement it suffices to find
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{v}[\phi,\phi]<0
. We fix
\displaystyle R>1
and define
\displaystyle\phi:=\eta\left(1-\frac{\log|x|}{\log R}\right)
, with
\displaystyle\eta\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\displaystyle\eta_{(-\infty,0]}\equiv 0,\eta_{[1,\infty)}\equiv 1
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\phi
verifies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\phi\equiv 1&\mbox{ in }B_{1}(0)\\
\phi\geq 0&\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\log R}&,\end{array}\right.

hence

\mathcal{Q}_{v}[\phi,\phi]\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla\phi|^{2}-\int_{B_{1}(0)}2K_{0}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\log R}-2K_{0}\int_{B_{1}(0)}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v},

which is negative if
\displaystyle R
is large enough.

To prove the second statement, we will show that
\displaystyle v
is unstable outside any compact set, that is: given
\displaystyle M_{0}>0
we will find
\displaystyle\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{M_{0}}(0)}\right)
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{v}[\psi,\psi]<0
. In this way, we can construct iteratively a sequence
\displaystyle\psi_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}\left(B_{M_{n+1}}(0)\setminus\overline{B_{M_{n}}(0)}\right)
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{v}[\psi_{n},\psi_{n}]<0
and clearly they are linearly independent.

For
\displaystyle M>2M_{0}>0
fixed we consider a smooth cut-off
\displaystyle\psi=\psi_{M,M_{0}}
such that

(4.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi\equiv 0&\mbox{in }B_{M_{0}}(0)\cup\left(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{2M}(0)\right)\\
\psi\equiv 1&\mbox{in }A_{2M_{0},M}(0)\\
0\leq\psi\leq 1&\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\leq C_{0}&\mbox{independently on }M_{0},M.\end{array}\right.

Since
\displaystyle v
is smooth, then

\int_{B_{2M_{0}}(0)}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v}<\infty=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{2M_{0}}(0)}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v},

therefore for
\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{0}-2K_{0}\int_{A_{2M_{0},M}(0)}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v}<0.

If
\displaystyle h_{0}\geq 0

as before with

2K_{0}\int_{B^{+}_{1}(0)}|x|^{2\beta}e^{v}>\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|\nabla\phi|^{2}

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{v}[\phi,\phi]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|\nabla\phi|^{2}-2K_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|x|^{2\beta}e^{v}\phi^{2}<0.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{v}[\psi,\psi]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-2K_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|x|^{2\beta}e^{v}\psi^{2}<0.

If
\displaystyle h_{0}<0

Z_{0}(s,t):=\log\left(\frac{2t}{1+K_{0}\left(s^{2}+\left(t+\frac{h_{0}}{K_{0}}\right)^{2}\right)}-\frac{1}{h_{0}}\right),

which solves

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left(z^{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}-2K_{0}|x|^{2\beta}e^{v}\phi^{2}z^{2}\right)-\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}h_{0}|x|^{\beta}e^{v/2}\phi^{2}z^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}z^{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}+2\left(h_{0}+\frac{K_{0}}{h_{0}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|x|^{2\beta}e^{v}\phi^{2}z

Similarly, we can show that

4.2.
Global blow-up analysis

The main objective of this sub-section is to provide a proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The initial step in this process is to appropriately rescale a blowing-up sequence
\displaystyle u_{n}
around a local maximum point
\displaystyle x_{n}
such that
\displaystyle u_{n}(x_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\infty
. If
\displaystyle x_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}p\in\Sigma\setminus\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
, then we restrict on a suitably small ball
\displaystyle B_{r}(x_{n})
and define

(4.7)

v_{n}(x):=u_{n}(\delta_{n}x+x_{n})+2\log\delta_{n},\qquad x\in B_{\frac{r}{\delta_{n}}}(0),\qquad\mbox{where }\delta_{n}:=e^{-\frac{u_{n}(x_{n})}{2}};

it is standard to see that
\displaystyle v_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}v
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}
to an entire solution to (
4.4
), with
\displaystyle K_{0}=K(p),\alpha=0
. On the other hand, if
\displaystyle p=p_{i}
, then we consider

(4.8)

v_{n}(x):=u_{n}(\delta_{n}x+x_{n})+2\log\max\{\delta_{n},(1+\alpha)d(x_{n},p)\}

if
\displaystyle\frac{d(x_{n},p)}{\delta_{n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\infty
, the rescalement does not see the conical point and the limiting profile is the same as in the previous case; otherwise, we have
\displaystyle v_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}v
with
\displaystyle v
solving (
4.4
) with
\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{p}
.

If the limiting point is
\displaystyle p\in\partial\Sigma
, then the limiting profiles could either be again a solution on the whole plane to (
4.4
), if
\displaystyle\frac{d(x_{n},\partial\Sigma)}{\delta_{n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\infty
, or a solution on the half-plane to (
4.5
).

We point out that, with respect to classical works, the local maximum points
\displaystyle x_{n}
of
\displaystyle u_{n}
may be not isolated, therefore
\displaystyle x_{n}
could converge to some point different than a given concentration point
\displaystyle p\in\mathcal{S}
. To overcome this issue, we use Ekeland’s variational principle (for details see
[
33
, Proposition 5.1]
).

We start by considering the case of finite mass, when the concentration points are only finitely many and we have no residual. The proof is similar to
[
33
, Lemma 7.4]
, therefore we will be sketchy.

Lemma 4.6
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
4.1
, if
\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u_{n}}
is uniformly bounded and the blow-up set
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
defined in (
4.1
), then
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
is finite and either
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\emptyset
or

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u_{n}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightharpoonup}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\gamma(p)\delta_{p}

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\widetilde{h}_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightharpoonup}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal{S}\cap\partial\Sigma}\theta(p)\delta_{p}

for some
\displaystyle\gamma(p),\theta(p)\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

If
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
, we fix
\displaystyle p\in\mathcal{S}
and construct a blow-up sequence as in either (
4.7
) or (
4.8
). Since
\displaystyle v_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}v
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}
to a solution
\displaystyle v
to either (
4.4
) or (
4.5
), then by Fatou’s lemma one has

\int_{B_{R_{n}\delta_{n}}(x_{n})}\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u_{n}}=\int_{B_{R_{n}}(0)}\widetilde{K}_{n}(\delta_{n}x+x_{n})e^{v_{n}}\geq\int K_{0}|x|^{2\alpha}e^{v}+o(1)\geq\varepsilon_{0}>0,

if
\displaystyle R_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\infty
slowly enough; therefore, if
\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u_{n}}
is uniformly bounded then
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
must be finite.

We now suffice to show that
\displaystyle u_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}-\infty
in
\displaystyle L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}\left(\overline{\Sigma}\setminus\mathcal{S}\right)
, which will be done as in
[
28
, Lemma 4]
. We consider, for small
\displaystyle r>0
,

either

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{R_{n}\delta_{n}\leq d(x,x_{n})\leq r}(u_{n}(x)+2\log d(x,x_{n}))

or

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{R_{n}\delta_{n}\leq d(x,x_{n})\leq r}(u_{n}(x)+2(1+\alpha)\log d(x,x_{n})),

depending on the blow-up profile we extracted: if the supremum is unbounded, then we can extract a new bubble around a point
\displaystyle x_{n,2}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}p
and repeat, which we can do only a finite number of times
\displaystyle x_{n,1},\dots,x_{n,m}
because each bubble has at least a fixed amount of mass and the total mass is finite.

Now, one can show that for any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists
\displaystyle C=C_{\varepsilon}
such that the radial average

\overline{u}_{n,i}(r):=\frac{1}{|\partial B_{r}(x_{n,i})|}\int_{\partial B_{r}(x_{n,i})}u

verifies, for any

\overline{u}_{n,i}(r)\leq\overline{u}_{n,i}(R_{n,i}\delta_{n,i})-(4-\varepsilon)\log\frac{r}{R_{n,i}\delta_{n,i}}+C.

Thanks to a Harnack-type inequality, a similar inequality holds for
\displaystyle u_{n}
, which implies in particular that the mass outside the balls
\displaystyle B_{R_{n,i}\delta_{n,i}}(x_{n,i})
vanishes and that
\displaystyle u_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}0
locally uniformly outside
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
.
∎

Quantization of the local blow-up masses follows by a Pohožaev identity, as in
[
7

and
[

.

Lemma 4.7
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
4.6
, we have:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma(p)=8\pi(1+\alpha(p))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{if }p\in\mathcal{S}\cap\Sigma,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma(p)+\theta(p)=4\pi(1+\beta(p))

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{if }p\in\mathcal{S}\cap\partial\Sigma,

where
\displaystyle\alpha(p),\beta(p)
are defined as in (
3.5
),(
4.3
).

Proof.

We fix
\displaystyle p\in\mathcal{S}
and
\displaystyle r<d(p,\mathcal{S}\setminus\{p\})
and apply a standard Pohožaev identity on
\displaystyle B_{r}(p)
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}(p)}\left(\frac{8\pi\chi}{|\Sigma|}\nabla u_{n}\cdot(x-p)+4\left(2\widetilde{K}_{n}+\nabla\widetilde{K}_{n}\cdot(x-p)\right)e^{u_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{r}(p)}\left(4K_{n}e^{u_{n}}((x-p)\cdot\nu)+2(\nabla u_{n}\cdot(x-p))(\nabla u_{n}\cdot\nu)-|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}(x-p\cdot\nu)\right).

By the choice of
\displaystyle r

\displaystyle B_{r}(p)

2\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u_{n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightharpoonup}\gamma(p),\qquad\left(\nabla\widetilde{K}_{n}\cdot(x-p)\right)e^{u_{n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightharpoonup}\alpha(p)\gamma(p);

moreover, since

\displaystyle\nabla u_{n}=-\frac{\gamma(p)}{2\pi}\frac{x-p}{d(x,p)^{2}}+O(1)

4(1+\alpha(p))\gamma(p)=\frac{\gamma(p)^{2}}{2\pi},

namely
\displaystyle\gamma(p)=8\pi(1+\alpha(p))
.
We similarly argue on
\displaystyle B^{+}_{r}(p)
if
\displaystyle p\in\partial\Sigma
.
∎

Finally, we consider the case of infinite mass blow-up, where we exploit the relation with the Morse index which was studied in Subsection

Lemma 4.8
.

Under the assumption of Theorem
4.1
,

\mbox{if}\qquad\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u_{n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\infty,\qquad\mbox{then}\qquad\mathrm{ind}(u_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\infty.

Proof.

We will prove that, if
\displaystyle\mathrm{ind}(u_{n})\leq m
for some

, then
\displaystyle\sup_{n}\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}_{n}e^{u_{n}}<\infty

We first show that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
has at most
\displaystyle m+1
elements. Otherwise, if
\displaystyle x_{1},\dots,x_{m+2}\in\mathcal{S}
, then we consider entire solutions
\displaystyle v_{1},\dots,v_{m+2}
to either (
4.4
) or (
4.5

4.7
) or (
4.8
) around
\displaystyle x_{i}
converges to
\displaystyle v_{i}
for
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,m+2

, we take
\displaystyle\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m+2}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{v_{i}}[\phi_{i},\phi_{i}]<0
and we define
\displaystyle\phi_{n,i}(x):=\phi\left(\frac{x-x_{n,i}}{\delta_{n,i}}\right)

are given by the rescalement around

therefore the index

5.
Min-max solutions in the supercritical case

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.5
, that is we will establish an existence result in the supercritical case without any symmetry assumptions. First, we will show that problem (
2.1
) can be viewed as a particular case of a general mean-field problem, consistent with the studies in
[
19

on closed surfaces.

For a fixed
\displaystyle\lambda>0
, we consider the following problem:

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u+\frac{\lambda}{|\Sigma|}=2C^{2}_{\lambda}(u)\widetilde{K}e^{u}&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}u=2C_{\lambda}(u)\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma,\end{array}\right.,

where

C_{\lambda}(u):=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}+2\lambda\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}+\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}.

Clearly, (
2.1
) is a particular case of (
5.1
) with the choice
\displaystyle\lambda=4\pi\chi
. Therefore, Theorem
1.5
will follow once we establish the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\Sigma
be a surface with conical singularities and/or corners as specified in (
1.14
), and at least two boundary components, one of which has no corners. If
\displaystyle K>0
in
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}
and
\displaystyle\lambda\notin\Gamma
, where
\displaystyle\Gamma
is as in (
1.13
), then there exists a solution to (
5.1
).

As in Proposition
2.2
, the solutions to (
5.1
) are critical points of the energy functional given by

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-\mathcal{F}_{\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\chi}}\left(\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u},\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right).

5.1.
Variational Structure

Inspired by
[
4
,
6
]
, our argument is based in studying the topology of the energy sublevels

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{a}:=\left\{u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma):\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}(u)\leq a\right\}.

The blow-up analysis developed in Section
4
is crucial to bypass the failure of the Palais-Smale condition, as illustrated in
[
33
,
34
]
.

Next, we will show that the low sublevels of
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}
are not contractible and one can construct a finite-dimensional min-max scheme based on that. To do so, we will prove that functions with low energy must be concentrated at a finite number of points. This will be done using the improved versions of the Trudinger-Moser inequalities introduced in Subsection
3.2
. We recall that, in view of the assumptions (
1.14
), the Trudinger constant
\displaystyle\tau
equals
\displaystyle 1
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\lambda<4(k+1)\pi
. Then, for any
\displaystyle\varepsilon,r>0
, there exists
\displaystyle L=L(\varepsilon,r)>0
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{-L}
, there exist
\displaystyle k
points
\displaystyle\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\subset\overline{\Sigma}
such that

\frac{\int_{\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}B_{r}(x_{i})}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Proof.

Assume the inequality does not hold. Then, by a standard covering argument (
[
5
, Lemma 4.4]
), there exist
\displaystyle\delta>0
and
\displaystyle\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\dots,\Omega_{k+1}\subset\overline{\Sigma}
such that

d(\Omega_{i},\Omega_{j})\geq 2\delta\quad\forall i\neq j,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{\int_{\Omega_{j}}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}\geq\delta.

For any
\displaystyle j=1,\dots,k+1
, we apply Corollary
3.5
and get

\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{8(k+1)\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

Moreover, since the ratio
\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{h}^{2}}{\left|\widetilde{K}\right|}
is uniformly bounded on
\displaystyle\partial\Sigma
, we have the following inequality (
[
8
, Proposition 4.1]
)

\frac{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}\leq C\left(\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+1\right),

from which we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\left|\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}+\log\left(\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+1\right)+C

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1+2\varepsilon}{16(k+1)\pi}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C.

Therefore, from (
3.10
) and the definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}
we get

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}(u)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{(1+2\varepsilon)\lambda}{8(k+1)\pi}\right)\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-C,

that is, if
\displaystyle\varepsilon
is chosen small enough,
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}(u)\geq-L
for some
\displaystyle L>0
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Lemma
5.2
implies that the unit measure
\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{K}e^{u_{n}}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u_{n}}}
resembles, in some sense, a finite linear combination of Dirac deltas with at most
\displaystyle k
elements. This allows us to describe the low sublevels of
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}
through the formal barycenters of
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}
of order
\displaystyle k
:

\overline{\Sigma}_{k}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}:\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}=1,x_{i}\in\overline{\Sigma},\forall i=1,\dots,k\right\}.

The topological properties of barycenters, which are essential to our purposes, are summed up in the following well-known result, whose proof can be found in
[
20
,
36
]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any compact
\displaystyle n
-dimensional manifold
\displaystyle M
, with or without boundary, and
\displaystyle k\geq 1
, the set
\displaystyle M_{k}
is a stratified set, namely a union of open manifolds of different dimensions, whose maximal dimension is
\displaystyle(n+1)k-1
. Furthermore,
\displaystyle M_{k}
is not contractible if
\displaystyle M
is not contractible.

Additionally, the following proposition can be derived from standard arguments based on Lemma
5.2
, (see
[
19
]
, Lemma 4.9).

Proposition 5.4
.

If
\displaystyle\lambda<8(k+1)\pi
, then there exists a projection
\displaystyle\Psi:\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{-L}\to\overline{\Sigma}_{k}
for
\displaystyle L>0
large enough.

However, barycenters on the entire
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}
may be too complicated to handle, therefore we will actually consider barycenter on a simpler manifold, such as a curve.

We now follow a strategy similar to
[
6
]
to construct barycenters on a boundary component of
\displaystyle\Sigma
. We assume that
\displaystyle\Sigma
has at least two boundary components, one of which has no corners, so that

\partial\Sigma=\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2}\cup\dots\cup\gamma_{l},

where
\displaystyle l\geq 2
,
\displaystyle\gamma_{i}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{1}
for
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,l
and
\displaystyle q_{j}\notin\gamma_{1}
for any
\displaystyle j=1,\dots,m
.

Lemma 5.5
.

If
\displaystyle\lambda<8(k+1)\pi
, then there exists a projection map
\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}:\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{-L}\to(\gamma_{1})_{k}
for
\displaystyle L>0
large enough.

Proof.

From Proposition
5.4
, we know that for
\displaystyle L\gg 0
, there exists a projection
\displaystyle\Psi:\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{-L}\to\Sigma_{k}
. To construct
\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}
, it is sufficient to define a retraction map
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}:\overline{\Sigma}\to\gamma_{1}
, and then set
\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}:=\Psi\circ\mathcal{R}_{*}
, where
\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}(u):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\delta_{\mathcal{R}(x_{i})}
if
\displaystyle\Psi(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}
.

Consider an open subset
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\Sigma
obtained by joining tubular neighborhoods of
\displaystyle\gamma_{1}
and
\displaystyle\gamma_{2}
together with tubular neighborhoods of a geodesic curve connecting these boundary components in
\displaystyle\Sigma
, as shown in Figure

.

Figure 1.
The open domain

Next, we define the surface
\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}
by identifying
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}\setminus(\Omega\cup\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})
to a single point. This identification is achieved via the quotient map
\displaystyle p:\overline{\Sigma}\to\widehat{\Sigma}
, which topologically collapses the region
\displaystyle\overline{\Sigma}\setminus(\Omega\cup\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2})
to a single point. The resulting surface is orientable of genus zero with two boundary components, hence it is topologically equivalent to a standard cylinder, i.e.
\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}\approx\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]
, with boundary
\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\cup\gamma_{2}\approx\left(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\{0\}\right)\cup\left(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\{1\}\right)
.

We define the retraction by quotienting
\displaystyle\Sigma
to the cylinder
\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}
and then mapping the latter on one boundary. More precisely, lef
\displaystyle f:\widehat{\Sigma}\leftrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]
be the above-mentioned homeomorphism and
\displaystyle\pi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\{0\}
the projection onto the first component,
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}:\overline{\Sigma}\to\gamma_{1}
is then defined as

\mathcal{R}:=f^{-1}\circ\pi\circ f\circ p.

∎

To further explore the topological properties of the low sublevels, we will construct a reverse map
\displaystyle\Phi:(\gamma_{1})_{k}\to\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{-L}
. To achieve this, we consider a family of standard
bubbles
centered on the boundary component
\displaystyle\gamma_{1}
and we define, for
\displaystyle\Lambda>0
,
\displaystyle\Phi:(\gamma_{1})_{k}\to\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
as

(5.2)

\Phi(\sigma):=\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}-\overline{\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}},

\mbox{where}\quad\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}(x)=\log\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\left(1+\Lambda^{2}d(x,x_{i})^{2}\right)^{2}}\qquad\mbox{for}\quad\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}.

In this context, the choice of
\displaystyle\gamma_{1}
is crucial, because we are working with bubbles centered on a boundary component without any conical points nor corners, hence we basically can neglect singularities. Therefore, we can argue as in
[
36
, Proposition 4.2]
to derive the following estimates.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\displaystyle\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}
be defined as in (
5.2
). Then, as
\displaystyle\Lambda\to\infty
, we have the following estimates, uniformly on
\displaystyle\sigma\in(\gamma_{1})_{k}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle 8k\pi(1+o(1))\log\Lambda,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}-\overline{\varphi}_{\Lambda,\sigma}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2(1+o(1))\log\Lambda,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\left|\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{(\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}-\overline{\varphi}_{\Lambda,\sigma})/2}\right|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1+o(1))\log\Lambda.

Moreover, we have

\frac{\widetilde{K}e^{\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}}}\underset{\Lambda\to\infty}{\rightharpoonup}\sigma,

in the sense of measures.

The above estimates indicate that the values of the functional can be made as negatively large as desired, leading to the following conclusion.

Proposition 5.7
.

If
\displaystyle\lambda>8k\pi
, then, for any
\displaystyle L>0
, there exists
\displaystyle\Lambda=\Lambda(L)\gg 0
such that
\displaystyle\Phi((\gamma_{1})_{k})\subset\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{-L}
, where
\displaystyle\Phi
is defined as in (
5.2
).

Proof.

By the previous lemma we get

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}\left(\Phi(\sigma)\right)\leq(8k\pi-\lambda+o(1))\log\Lambda

which, since
\displaystyle\lambda>8k\pi
, will have an arbitrarily large negative value by taking
\displaystyle\Lambda
large enough.
∎

5.2.
Proofs of the Theorems
5.1
and
1.5

In order to conclude, we will exploit the compactness results from Theorem
4.1
and Corollary
4.2
.

In particular, with respect to classical mean field problem, in order to prevent blow-up we will also need to take account of the boundedness of the Morse index. We get a crucial upper bound on the Morse index of solutions due to the fact that solutions come from a finite-dimensional min-max scheme, in the spirit of the work of Fang and Ghoussoub
[
21
]
.

We first construct a min-max scheme based on the analysis of the barycenters
\displaystyle\left(\gamma_{1}\right)_{k}
. Then, we introduce an auxiliary parameter
\displaystyle\mu
and apply the celebrated monotonicity trick by Struwe
[
39
]
; we get a solution to (
5.1
) for a dense sequence of values
\displaystyle\mu_{n}
with uniformly bounded Morse index. Finally, we pass to the limit for
\displaystyle\mu_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}1
using the compactness results we already got. The introduction of the extra parameter is a new ingredient for this type of problems; it is essential, since we need the energy functional to be monotone with respect to the parameter. Similar arguments have been used in other curvature prescription problems
[
20
,
33
,
34
]
.

In order to precisely define the min-max structure, we define the contractible cone based on the barycenter set
\displaystyle(\gamma_{1})_{k}
as

\widehat{(\gamma_{1})_{k}}:=\frac{(\gamma_{1})_{k}\times[0,1]}{(\gamma_{1})_{k}\times\{0\}}.

We then consider the family of maps on
\displaystyle\widehat{(\gamma_{1})_{k}}
which extend to the cone the bubbles defined on
\displaystyle(\gamma_{1})_{k}
as (
5.2
):

\Pi_{\Lambda}=\left\{\pi:\widehat{(\gamma_{1})_{k}}\to H^{1}(\Sigma):\mbox{ $\displaystyle\pi$ continuous and }\pi\left(\sigma\times\{1\}\right)=\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}\,\forall\sigma\in(\gamma_{1})_{k}\right\}.

Following the same strategy as
[
36
]
, we then obtain the next min-max structure.

Proposition 5.8
.

Assume
\displaystyle 8k\pi<\lambda<8(k+1)\pi
and
\displaystyle L\gg 0
so large that Lemma
5.5
holds true with
\displaystyle\frac{L}{4}
. Then, the set
\displaystyle\Pi_{\Lambda}
is non-empty and

\overline{\Pi}_{\Lambda}:=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\Lambda}}\sup_{(\sigma,t)\in\widehat{(\gamma_{1})_{k}}}\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}\left(\pi(z,t)\right)>-\frac{L}{2}.

Proof.

To show that
\displaystyle\Pi_{\Lambda}\neq\emptyset
, hence
\displaystyle\overline{\Pi}_{\Lambda}<\infty
, we observe that the map

\overline{\pi}(\sigma,t)=t\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma},\quad\mbox{for }(\sigma,t)\in\widehat{(\gamma_{1})_{k}},

is indeed an element of
\displaystyle\Pi_{\Lambda}
.

We assume, for the sake of contradiction, that
\displaystyle\overline{\Pi}_{\Lambda}\leq-\frac{L}{2}
. By the assumptions, Lemma
5.5
and Proposition
5.7
, there exist maps

\widetilde{\Psi}:\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{-L/4}\to(\gamma_{1})_{k},\qquad\Phi:(\gamma_{1})_{k}\to\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}^{-L/4},

whose composition
\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}\circ\Phi
is homotopically equivalent to the identity on
\displaystyle(\gamma_{1})_{k}
; the homotopical equivalence is obtained by letting
\displaystyle\Lambda\to\infty
, thanks to the last statement in Lemma
5.6
.

If
\displaystyle\overline{\Pi}_{\Lambda}\leq\frac{L}{2}
, then there exists
\displaystyle\pi\in\Pi_{\Lambda}
such that
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda}(\pi(\sigma,t))\leq-\frac{3}{8}L
for any
\displaystyle(\sigma,t)\in\widehat{(\gamma_{1})_{k}}
. Therefore, one may define the map
\displaystyle H(\sigma,t):\widehat{(\gamma_{1})_{k}}\to(\gamma_{1})_{k}
as
\displaystyle H(\sigma,t):=\widetilde{\Psi}(\pi(\sigma,t))
: this would be a homotopy between
\displaystyle\widetilde{\Psi}\circ\varphi_{\Lambda,\sigma}
and a constant map, but this is impossible, since the former map is homotopically equivalent to the identity and
\displaystyle(\gamma_{1})_{k}
is not contractible by Lemma
5.3
. We found a contradiction.
∎

In order to apply the Struwe’s monotonicity trick, we need to introduce the following perturbed problem:

(5.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\mu\Delta u+\frac{\lambda}{|\Sigma|}=2C^{2}_{\lambda}(u)\widetilde{K}e^{u}&\mbox{in }\Sigma,\\
\partial_{\nu}u=2C_{\lambda}(u)\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma.\end{array}\right.,

whose energy functional is given by

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,\mu}(u)=\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-\mathcal{F}_{\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}}\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2},\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\right).

It is easy to see that, if
\displaystyle\mu
is close enough to
\displaystyle 1
, then all estimates from Subsection
5.1
, as well as Proposition
5.8
, holds true also for
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,\mu}
uniformly in
\displaystyle\mu
. In particular, given any large number
\displaystyle L>0
, there exists a
\displaystyle\Lambda
so large that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{(\sigma,t)\in\partial\widehat{(\gamma_{1})_{k}}}\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,\mu}\left(\pi(\sigma,t)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\displaystyle-2L,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\mu}:=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\Lambda}}\sup_{(\sigma,t)\in\widehat{(\gamma_{1})_{k}}}\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,\mu}\left(\pi(\sigma,t)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{L}{2},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\mu}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{L},

for some
\displaystyle\overline{L}>0
.

Now, applying the Monotonicity trick of Struwe, we can argue as in
[
36
]
to obtain the following assertions.

Proposition 5.9
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.1
there exist
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for a dense set of values
\displaystyle\mu\in[1-\varepsilon_{0},1+\varepsilon_{0}]
, (
5.3
) has a solution
\displaystyle u_{\mu}
whose energy and Morse index satisfy

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,\mu}(u_{\mu})=\overline{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\mu},\qquad\mathrm{ind}(u_{\mu})\leq 2k.

Proof.

The existence of a solution for a dense set of parameters at the min-max energy level follow by arguing as in
[
39
]
. The upper bound on the Morse index is a consequence of Theorem 2 of
[
11
]
, since the min-max scheme is based on
\displaystyle\widehat{(\gamma_{1})_{k}}
, which has dimension
\displaystyle 2k
by Lemma
5.3
.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
5.1
and, as its particular case, Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

We take a sequence
\displaystyle\mu_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}1
such that the perturbed problem (
5.3
) has a solution
\displaystyle u_{n}
. Since
\displaystyle\lambda\notin\Gamma
and
\displaystyle\sup_{n}\mathrm{ind}(u_{n})<\infty
, by Corollary
4.2
we get that
\displaystyle u_{n}
is uniformly bounded in
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Sigma)
. By standard estimates,
\displaystyle u_{n}
converges in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{2,\gamma}\left(\overline{\Sigma}\right)
to some

which solves (

In the case
\displaystyle\lambda=4\pi\chi
we get a solution to (

).
∎

6.
The case of non-positive singular Euler characteristic

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
, that is the case
\displaystyle\chi\leq 0
.

Such results have counterparts in the regular case from the paper
[
8
]
and the proofs are rather similar. In contrast with the case
\displaystyle\chi>0
, here in the definition of the energy functional the logarithmic term does not play a significant role (see (
1.9
) and (
2.4
)), but it is crucial to control the rational term. A fundamental role is played by the function
\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}
, defined as in by (
1.12
), which interestingly is unaffected by the presence of conical singularities or corners.

In order to control the nonlinear terms by means of the quadratic term, we need the following result, see
[
8
, Proposition 4.1]
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Assume
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}(x)\lneqq 0
for any
\displaystyle x\in\Sigma
and let
\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}
be defined by (
1.12
). Then for any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists
\displaystyle C=C_{\varepsilon}
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in H^{1}(\Sigma)
one has:

\frac{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}}{\int_{\Sigma}|\widetilde{K}|e^{u}}\leq\frac{(\mathfrak{D}_{M}+\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+C\,\quad\mbox{where }\mathfrak{D}_{M}:=\max_{\partial\Sigma}|\mathfrak{D}|.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We recall that we study the energy functional (
1.9
) in the space

H_{0}=\left\{u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma):\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}<0\right\}.

We study separately each of the three cases:

(1)

Since
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}>0
somewhere and
\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}<0
for all
\displaystyle u
, then

H_{0}=\left\{u\in\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma):\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}>0\right\}\neq\emptyset.

Therefore,

\mathcal{J}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+2\frac{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}\underset{\|u\|\to\infty}{\to}\infty.

To conclude with the coercivity of
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
in
\displaystyle H_{0}
we need to verify that the energy also diverges on the boundary

\partial H_{0}=\left\{u\in H_{0}:\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}=0\right\}.

If
\displaystyle u_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightharpoonup}u_{0}\in\partial H_{0}
, then by weak continuity, one has

\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u_{n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u_{0}}=0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}_{n}e^{u_{n}/2}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}<0,

therefore

\mathcal{J}(u_{n})\geq\frac{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u_{n}/2}\right)^{2}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u_{n}}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\infty,

which shows coercivity, hence existence of minimizing solutions.

(2)

Since
\displaystyle\widetilde{K}\geq 0
, by the same argument as before we show coercivity on the whole
\displaystyle\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
and the existence of minimizers solving

(6.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u=2C^{2}(u)\widetilde{K}e^{u}&\mbox{in }\Sigma\\
\partial_{\nu}u=2C(u)\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\end{array}\right.,

without any information on the sign of
\displaystyle C(u)
(since we are on
\displaystyle\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
). To get a solution to (
2.1
), hence to (
1.8
), we need to show that
\displaystyle C(u)>0
.

If
\displaystyle C(u)=0
, then the only solution to (
6.1
) must be
\displaystyle u\equiv 0
, hence we get the following contradiction

0=C(u)=-\frac{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}=-\frac{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}}{\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}}>0.

Finally, if
\displaystyle C(u)<0
we get a solution to (
2.1
) with
\displaystyle-\widetilde{h}
instead of
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}
, since (
6.1
) is invariant by change of sign to
\displaystyle\widetilde{h}
; however, problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u=2\widetilde{K}e^{u}&\mbox{in }\Sigma,\\
\partial_{\nu}u=-2\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}&\mbox{on }\partial\Sigma\end{array}\right.

has no solutions because multiplying by
\displaystyle e^{-u/2}
and integrating by parts one gets again a contradiction:

-\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}<\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}-\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}=-\frac{1}{2}\int|\nabla u|^{2}e^{-u/2}<0.

Therefore, it must be
\displaystyle C(u)>0
, hence one has a true solution to (
2.1
).

(3)

Since
\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}<0<\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}
, therefore
\displaystyle H_{0}=\overline{H}^{1}(\Sigma)
, hence, in order to get minimizing solutions, we suffice to show coercivity at infinity. To this purpose, we take small
\displaystyle\varepsilon
such that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}(y)+\varepsilon<1
for any
\displaystyle y\in\partial\Sigma
and apply Proposition
6.1
:

\mathcal{J}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-2\frac{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)^{2}}{\int_{\Sigma}|\widetilde{K}|e^{u}}\geq\frac{1-(\mathfrak{D}_{M}+\varepsilon)^{2}}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-C_{\varepsilon}\underset{\|u\|\to\infty}{\to}\infty.

∎

In the case
\displaystyle\chi<0
we have an extra logarithmic term in the energy, which was not present when
\displaystyle\chi=0
, but it does not really affect the problem. This follows from the following elementary result.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
there exists
\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that the function

(6.2)

f(t):=F_{\chi}(-1,t)=8\pi|\chi|\left(-\log{\left(\sqrt{t^{2}+8\pi|\chi|}-t\right)}+\frac{t}{\sqrt{t^{2}+8\pi|\chi|}-t}\right)

verifies

f(t)\leq(2+\varepsilon)t_{+}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}.

In view of Lemma
6.2
, the case
\displaystyle\chi<0
can still be treated using Proposition
6.1
, similarly as Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We write the energy functional as

\mathcal{J}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+4\pi|\chi|\log{\left(-\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}\right)}+4\pi|\chi|\log{\left(8\pi\right)}-f\left(\frac{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}{\sqrt{-\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}}\right),

where
\displaystyle f
is as in (
6.2
).

The first nonlinear term is uniformly bounded from below on
\displaystyle H_{\chi}
because of the Jensen’s inequality

\int_{\Sigma}-\widetilde{K}e^{u}=\int_{\Sigma}e^{u+\log{|\widetilde{K}|}}\geq|\Sigma|\int_{\Sigma}e^{\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}\int_{\Sigma}(u+\log{|\widetilde{K}|})}=|\Sigma|e^{\frac{1}{|\Sigma|}\int_{\Sigma}\log{|\widetilde{K}|}}>-C.

Since
\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{+}_{M}:=\max_{\partial\Sigma}{\frac{h}{\sqrt{|K|}}}<1,
using Lemma
6.2
and Proposition
6.1
with
\displaystyle h_{+}
instead of
\displaystyle h
we get, for
\displaystyle\varepsilon
small enough:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(u)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}+f\left(\frac{\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}}{\sqrt{-\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}}\right)-C

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-(2+\varepsilon)\frac{\left(\int_{\partial\Sigma}\widetilde{h}e^{u/2}\right)_{+}^{2}}{-\int_{\Sigma}\widetilde{K}e^{u}}-C

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{2-(2+\varepsilon)(\mathfrak{D}_{M}^{+}+\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla u|^{2}-C\underset{\|u\|\to\infty}{\to}\infty,

hence
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
has minimizers which solve (
2.1
).
∎
