Barriers to physical activity among adolescents with asthma and obesity: a qualitative study of multiple stakeholders
Authors: Nan Lin, Xiaohui Chen, Fang Tang, Ye Sun, Peixin Liu, Hongzhen Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescents living with both asthma and obesity face distinctive challenges to physical activity. Evidence that integrates the perspectives of adolescents, families, schools, and healthcare providers remains limited.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the barriers to physical activity faced by adolescents with concurrent obesity and asthma, and to understand the perceptions of patients, families, PE teachers, and healthcare providers regarding these challenges.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted with semi-structured interviews of 15 adolescents (12 to 18 years) and focus group discussions with 36 stakeholders (12 parents, 8 physical education teachers, and 16 healthcare providers). Data were analyzed inductively using thematic analysis in NVivo.ResultsFour themes were identified: (1) psychological and social barriers to physical activity (self-efficacy and identity struggles; social acceptance barriers); (2) family and school barriers to physical activity (family support dilemmas; school policy limitations); (3) community and tech adaptation gaps (barriers to community resource access; digital tool personalization gaps); and (4) collaborative support solutions (personalized activity plans; stakeholder collaboration needs).ConclusionsBarriers operate at intrapersonal, interpersonal, institutional, and community or technology levels. Co-designed and individualized activity plans and coordinated communication among families, schools, and healthcare may enhance participation. Existing digital tools require health-sensitive personalization and safety features to support adolescents with asthma and obesity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25446-6.

Introduction

Obesity and asthma are two prevalent chronic conditions that significantly impact adolescent health, with their co-occurrence presenting unique challenges [1]. Recent data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention indicate that approximately 22.2% of U.S. adolescents are classified as obese [2], while 7.9–9.7% of adolescents are affected by asthma [3]. These two conditions are often linked in a bidirectional manner, where obesity exacerbates asthma symptoms and vice versa [4]. Obesity not only contributes to the development of asthma but is also associated with poorer asthma control due to mechanisms such as increased airway resistance and systemic inflammation, which impair lung function and treatment response [5, 6].

Both obesity and asthma independently act as significant barriers to physical activity, a critical component of managing these conditions. Obesity leads to a range of metabolic abnormalities and cardiovascular complications that restrict physical activity, thereby negatively affecting overall health and quality of life [7]. Asthma, with its hallmark symptoms of wheezing, coughing, and shortness of breath, presents additional obstacles to physical activity [1]. When these conditions coexist, the challenges become compounded: obesity exacerbates asthma symptoms through increased airway resistance and inflammation, while asthma-induced limitations in physical activity and negative self-perceptions create a cycle that impedes both exercise participation and weight management [1, 8, 9].

Given the profound impact of both obesity and asthma on adolescent health, physical activity emerges as a critical intervention to address these interconnected conditions. Physical activity is widely recognized for its multifaceted benefits, such as enhancing cardiopulmonary fitness, supporting weight control, and improving overall quality of life [10–12]. Moreover, engaging in regular physical activity has been shown to reduce the frequency and severity of asthma symptoms, making it an essential component of asthma management [13, 14]. Interventions promoting physical activity have been implemented to improve outcomes for adolescents with obesity and asthma. These interventions range from school-based programs to clinic-based initiatives, with many reporting short-term benefits for weight reduction and improved asthma control [15]. Systematic reviews confirm that structured exercise programs can be effective, but also emphasize that long-term adherence is low, dropout rates are high, and many adolescents fail to sustain behavior change beyond the intervention period [16]. Moreover, existing interventions are often not tailored to the unique psychosocial and environmental contexts of adolescents living with both obesity and asthma. For instance, issues such as limited access to suitable exercise facilities, lack of professional guidance, social stigma, and insufficient emotional or peer support are often overlooked in the design of these programs. As a result, the real-world impact of these interventions remains limited, despite the well-documented physiological benefits of exercise.

The limited success of interventions to promote physical activity and manage weight among adolescents with obesity and asthma raises important questions about the barriers these individuals face. Factors such as disease-related discomfort, social stigma, psychological barriers, and environmental constraints contribute to the reluctance of adolescents to participate in exercise programs. Overprotective family attitudes and a lack of suitable exercise opportunities further compound these challenges, making it difficult to implement and sustain successful interventions [17]. While quantitative research has provided insights into the physiological benefits of physical activity for this population, qualitative studies exploring adolescents’ perspectives on the barriers to physical activity are still scarce [18]. This gap in the literature limits our understanding of the complex interaction between physiological, psychological, and social factors that influence the willingness of adolescents to engage in exercise.

In response to these gaps, the present study adopts qualitative methods, including in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, to investigate the barriers to physical activity encountered by adolescents who experience the dual burden of obesity and asthma. By examining the interactions among biological, psychological, and social factors, this study seeks to deepen our understanding of the barriers confronting these adolescents. In doing so, it aims to guide the design of targeted, sustainable interventions that enhance both their health outcomes and quality of life.

Methods

Study design

This study utilized a qualitative research design underpinned by a constructivist paradigm and a social ecological framework [19, 20]. Guided by the assumption that knowledge is co-constructed through social and contextual interactions, this design allowed us to explore the multi-layered barriers and supports influencing physical activity participation among adolescents with obesity and asthma. A qualitative approach was chosen to capture the subjective experiences and contextualized understandings of participants, with particular attention to the dynamic interplay between individual, interpersonal, and environmental factors shaping their behaviors. Theoretical frameworks were not only used as guiding philosophies but also operationalized throughout the study: the social ecological framework structured both data collection and analysis into individual, interpersonal, institutional, and community levels, while the constructivist paradigm informed the dialogic and interpretive stance adopted during interviews and thematic interpretation.

Participants and recruitment

The study included adolescents aged 10 to 19 years and key stakeholders involved in their physical activity. Eligible adolescents had a clinician confirmed diagnosis of asthma and met criteria for obesity, defined as body mass index at or above the ninety-fifth percentile for age and sex. To examine barriers and supports within the study framework, parents, physical education (PE) teachers, and healthcare providers (HCPs) specializing in asthma or obesity management were also recruited. Stakeholders were required to have direct involvement in or influence over adolescents’ physical activity and health management. Exclusion criteria were adolescents with severe physical or cognitive impairments unrelated to asthma or obesity that limited participation, and adults without direct experience working with the target population.

Sample size was determined according to the principle of data saturation, where data collection was ceased after at least 10 interviews if no new themes or insights emerged from the last three consecutive interviews [21]. After each interview, two researchers independently reviewed the emergent codes to monitor whether new information was still arising. When three consecutive interviews yielded no novel codes or perspectives, the team collectively concluded that data saturation had been reached. The decision to stop data collection was made collaboratively by the research team after reviewing the data from each interview. Once no new themes or significant insights were identified after three consecutive interviews, the team concluded that data saturation had been reached. The study aimed to recruit adolescents for semi-structured in-depth interviews, utilizing maximum-variation sampling to capture a broad range of experiences. This approach was operationalized by purposively including participants across diverse age, sex, and asthma control levels, ensuring heterogeneity in clinical and demographic profiles. Additionally, parents, physical education teachers, and HCPs were invited to participate in 2–3 focus group discussions per stakeholder category, with each group comprising 4–6 participants.

Participants were recruited through healthcare institutions, schools, and online platforms. Pediatricians, serving as gatekeepers, referred eligible adolescents to the study. Study information sheets were distributed in clinics, schools, and online platforms to support recruitment efforts. Parents were contacted directly by the research team to explain the study’s purpose and obtain written consent. Physical education teachers were recruited through schools, with administrators also serving as gatekeepers. HCPs were recruited from large pediatric hospitals to ensure access to specialized expertise in asthma and obesity management.

Data collection

The data collection strategy was guided by a constructivist paradigm and a social ecological framework. Constructivism emphasizes the co-construction of meaning through dynamic interactions between researchers and participants [22]. This perspective influenced the use of dialogic and reflective methods, such as semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions, which allowed participants to actively share and co-create knowledge about their lived experiences. The social ecological framework, on the other hand, ensured that the data considered not only individual-level factors but also the influence of family, school, and community dynamics [19]. As a result, the data was both rich in experiential detail, reflecting participants’ lived experiences and perceptions, and structurally comprehensive, addressing the complex, interrelated factors that shape adolescent behavior. This integration provided a nuanced understanding of the interplay between individual, environmental, and societal influences on physical activity. To ensure clarity and relevance, the initial interview guide was developed based on existing literature and the social ecological framework. Three domain experts (two pediatricians and one exercise scientist) reviewed the guide for content validity, providing feedback on relevance, comprehensiveness, and wording. Their feedback informed refinement of question order and phrasing. A pilot test with three adolescents not included in the main study confirmed clarity, interpretability, and acceptability, with only minor adjustments required.

Semi-Structured interviews

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with adolescents diagnosed with both asthma and obesity. Each interview was led by a trained qualitative researcher, with a second researcher present to observe and record nonverbal cues. Consistent with the constructivist paradigm, the interviewer used open-ended and reflective probing to elicit participants’ lived experiences, perceptions, and interpretations related to physical activity. In this process, the interviewer acted as a facilitator in the co-construction of meaning, encouraging participants to share their personal experiences. Additionally, informed by the social ecological framework, the interview guide was designed to explore multiple levels of influence on behavior, including family dynamics, school-related factors, and community resources (see Supplementary Material 1). Participants were encouraged to reflect on personal motivations and constraints as well as broader environmental and structural conditions shaping engagement. Interviews lasted 30 to 60 min and were conducted in private settings to ensure comfort and confidentiality. Before the interviews, specific demographic and clinical data were collected from all adolescent participants, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI in kg/m²), age of asthma diagnosis, and physical activity frequency (in hours/week). Asthma control was assessed using the Asthma Control Test (ACT) [23], a five-item questionnaire (scores: 25 = well-controlled, 20–24 = partially-controlled, < 20 = poorly-controlled). Body mass index was measured with the InBody 270 body composition analyzer. The rationale for collecting this data was two-fold. Firstly, it facilitated maximum-variation sampling, ensuring diverse participant backgrounds crucial for rich qualitative inquiry. Secondly, these data provided essential contextual insights for interpreting narratives, allowing the research team to understand how variations in objective characteristics shaped participants’ subjective experiences.

Focus group discussions

To capture stakeholder perspectives, focus group discussions were conducted separately with parents, physical education teachers, and healthcare providers. Each session was moderated by an experienced facilitator and focused on perceived barriers, sources of support, and the effectiveness of current interventions related to adolescent physical activity (see Supplementary Material 2). Groups comprised 4 to 6 participants and lasted 60 to 90 min. With informed consent, discussions were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim, and a second researcher documented group dynamics and non-verbal behaviors to enhance contextual interpretation. Through shared dialogue and exchange of perspectives, participants collaboratively constructed a nuanced understanding of factors influencing adolescent exercise behavior. The focus group structure reflected the multilevel logic of the social ecological framework by grouping participants according to social roles, which brought role-specific perspectives and facilitated an in-depth exploration of how different social environments and relationships influence youth behavior.

Data analysis

The interviews and focus group recordings were transcribed within twenty-four hours. To ensure accuracy and cultural relevance, a two-step translation process was used. A bilingual researcher translated the Chinese transcripts into English with attention to cultural nuance, followed by back translation to Chinese by a second bilingual researcher. Discrepancies were reviewed and resolved by the research team, with special care for idioms and culturally specific terms. Data were managed in NVivo 12 and analyzed inductively using thematic analysis following the six phases outlined by Braun and Clarke [24]: familiarization, generating initial codes, constructing themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report. The social ecological framework informed the organization of themes across the four levels of influence, while the constructivist paradigm shaped interpretive attention to how participants constructed meaning through dialogue [25].

To enhance objectivity and consistency, three researchers independently coded the transcripts (investigator triangulation), followed by collaborative discussions to reconcile differences and develop a unified interpretation that captured the interplay across data sources. Methodological triangulation was also applied by integrating data from individual interviews and focus groups to cross-validate findings from multiple perspectives [26]. Credibility was strengthened through member checking with a subset of participants and expert review by domain specialists to assess relevance and rigor. Guided by the social ecological framework, themes were organized across levels of influence: individual for example self-perception, interpersonal for example family support and peer influence, institutional for example school policies, and community for example access to health resources. These analytic strategies collectively enhanced the trustworthiness and transparency of the findings.

Reflexivity

The lead researcher holds a PhD in medicine with extensive clinical and academic research experience, which could influence data collection, analysis, and interactions with participants, particularly in interpreting challenges faced by adolescents with asthma and obesity. Acknowledging and managing researcher positionality and its potential impact is fundamental to qualitative inquiry [27]. To mitigate potential bias, the research team included one to two experts in psychology and public health to provide complementary perspectives and to ensure attention to psychological and social contexts rather than an exclusive emphasis on physiological barriers. The researcher maintained ongoing reflexive practice throughout the study, including written notes after each interview that documented emotional reactions, assumptions, and researcher-participant dynamics, a key strategy for enhancing transparency and rigor in qualitative research [28]. During analysis, findings were regularly discussed with the team to keep interpretations grounded in participants’ perspectives and to minimize individual bias. These reflexive practices, team-based triangulation, and expert consultation collectively served as explicit strategies to mitigate researcher bias and enhance the overall trustworthiness and credibility of the study.

Ethical considerations

This study obtained official approval from the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, under the designated identifier 2024-IRB-0015-P-01. All participants were provided with detailed information about the study prior to data collection and signed written informed consent forms. For adolescent participants under the age of 16, informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians, along with the assent of the adolescents themselves. All recruitment procedures followed the principle of voluntary participation, and participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time without facing any negative consequences.

Results

A total of 15 adolescents participated in the semi-structured interviews (Table 1). The sample included 9 males (60%) and 6 females (40%), with ages ranging from 12 to 18 years (mean age = 14.9 years, SD = 1.6). All participants were diagnosed with both asthma and obesity. Their BMI values ranged from 24.8 to 29.7 kg/m² (mean BMI = 27.8 kg/m², SD = 1.72). Regarding asthma control, participants’ ACT scores ranged from 15 to 25 (mean ACT score = 21.2, SD = 3.20). Specifically, 2 participants were categorized as having well-controlled asthma, 8 participants as having partially controlled asthma, and 5 participants as having poorly-controlled asthma. In addition to the adolescents, 36 key stakeholders participated in the study, including 12 parents, 8 PE teachers, and 16 HCPs. Theme 1: psychological and social barriers to physical activity

Subtheme 1.1: Self-Efficacy and identity struggles

Insights from Semi-Structured interviews

The dual challenges of asthma and obesity significantly weakened adolescents’ self-efficacy, hindering their ability to develop a positive identity in physical activity. Participants commonly expressed that their health conditions led to a lack of confidence and self-doubt in their ability to engage in physical activities. This struggle was particularly pronounced among adolescents with partially or uncontrolled asthma and often compounded by their higher BMI, whose physical limitations were more immediate and unpredictable during activity. For instance, P2 (18-year-old female, BMI 29.4, poorly-controlled asthma) explained, “I always try to hide in the back row during gym class because I know that when the teacher calls my name, I’ll feel really embarrassed. I just never feel like I belong in sports.” Similarly, P4 (16-year-old female, BMI 28.8, partially controlled asthma) described feeling anxious before running: “I always worry I won’t be able to catch my breath, or that I’ll run too slowly and be laughed at. Sometimes, I even make excuses, such as saying I have a stomachache or that my period just started, to avoid running.”

Insights from focus group discussions

These adolescents’ experiences were echoed by their parents, who expressed similar concerns about their children’s self-image and reluctance to engage in physical activities. One mother shared, “My daughter refuses to participate in sports, and when I try to encourage her, she just says she doesn’t belong there. I can see how much her confidence is affected by her weight and asthma.”

These experiences were corroborated by HCPs. For example, one pediatrician noted that adolescents’ reluctance to engage in physical activities often stemmed from their negative self-assessment, particularly when asthma and obesity compounded their insecurities. The pediatrician emphasized, “These adolescents often internalize their health challenges, reducing their confidence and willingness to engage in physical activities.” Additionally, another healthcare provider shared, “When I speak to parents, I often hear how their children avoid physical activities because they don’t feel capable. It’s clear that these adolescents are dealing with a lot of internalized stigma, which holds them back from being active.”

Teachers also observed similar behaviors in the classroom. A PE teacher commented, “I’ve seen students with asthma or weight issues hesitate to participate, often standing aside, or sitting out. They are the ones who are most likely to avoid activities that make them feel vulnerable.” This observation is consistent with the self-reported experiences of P2 and P4, where both expressed an overwhelming fear of judgment from peers and teachers.

Taken together, the interviews revealed adolescents’ internal struggles with confidence and identity, whereas the focus groups highlighted how parents, teachers, and HCPs observed and sometimes reinforced these insecurities from the outside. These self-perceptions were profoundly shaped by both their asthma control status and the physical and social implications of obesity.

Subtheme 1.2: social acceptance barriers

Insights from Semi-Structured interviews

Adolescents with asthma and obesity often reported feeling “symbolic accepted” in social interactions during physical activities. While they were not completely excluded, they did not truly feel a sense of belonging in the athletic group. This experience was often shaped by their perceived physical limitations, which stemmed from both their asthma and obesity. P6 (13-year-old male, BMI 27, well-controlled asthma) shared, “In basketball, they always pick the best players first, and I’m always the last one left. I know they don’t really want me on their team; they just pick me to fill up the numbers.” Similarly, P9 (17-year-old male, BMI 27.7, poorly -controlled asthma) described his experience during soccer: “I couldn’t run fast enough, so they made me play as goalkeeper. When I saved a ball, they called me the goalkeeper, but I knew they only put me there because I couldn’t run or chase the ball. I felt like I was just a substitute.”

These instances were echoed by P12 (17-year-old female, BMI 28.7, partially-controlled asthma), who recalled, “My friend always picks me for badminton, saying it’s okay, I don’t need to jump and smash the birdie, and that we’ll take it slow. At first, I appreciated his support, but later, I realized he seemed to always go easy on me, as though he was afraid I’d lose too badly.” This “token acceptance” often left adolescents feeling isolated, reinforcing their reluctance to participate in physical activities.

Insights from focus group discussions

PE teachers have also observed similar phenomena. One PE teacher mentioned, “I’ve noticed that some kids are always marginalized during activities, especially those with health issues. When they participate in group activities, they are often assigned to safe positions, like substitutes. While these kids are allowed to participate, it’s often difficult for them to truly integrate into the activity.”

Overall, the interviews illustrated how adolescents felt marginalized in peer activities, while the focus groups showed that teachers also noticed these dynamics and recognized the difficulty of fostering real inclusion. The objective limitations imposed by asthma and the physical burden of obesity often served as visible markers that contributed to these feelings of marginalization and symbolic acceptance.

Theme 2: family and school barriers to physical activity

Subtheme 2.1 family support dilemmas

Insights from Semi-Structured interviews

Adolescents with asthma and obesity expressed mixed feelings toward their parents’ overprotective behaviors related to physical activity. While they acknowledged that exercise could improve their health and self-confidence, they often felt constrained by their parents’ constant concerns, which were often rooted in fears about asthma exacerbations and the broader health implications of obesity. For example, P5 (18-year-old male, BMI 28.1, poorly-controlled asthma) expressed his frustration, saying, “Every time I want to go play basketball, my dad always reminds me to bring my asthma inhaler and tells me not to push myself too hard. He even asks me to tell the coach that I can’t run. It makes me feel like I’m always on the edge of having an incident.”

Beyond direct overprotection, adolescents also noted limitations in the nature of parental support, particularly concerning active involvement and resource provision. P12 (17-year-old female, BMI 28.7, partially-controlled asthma), for instance, observed that while her mother frequently encouraged her to exercise, she never actively participated: “My mom always says that if I exercise more, I’ll lose weight and my asthma will get better, but she never exercises with me. When I suggest going to the gym or swimming, she says it’s too expensive and tells me to just run at home. We don’t even have a treadmill at home.”

Insights from focus group discussions

Parents of adolescents with asthma and obesity often demonstrated conflicting attitudes when supporting their children’s participation in physical activities. On one hand, they hoped exercise would improve their children’s health and self-confidence; on the other hand, concerns about potential health risks led them to adopt an overprotective approach. Several parents in the focus groups shared their struggles in balancing support with overprotection. One father of a 15-year-old explained, “I want my son to be more active, but I’m always afraid that he might have an asthma attack. It’s hard to know when to push him and when to hold back. I let him participate in sports, but I always stay close by just in case something happens.” Another mother shared, “I tell my daughter that she needs to exercise more, but I’m terrified she might overexert herself. I worry that the stress of trying to keep up with others could trigger an asthma flare-up. Even though I make sure she uses the inhaler before exercising, I still feel anxious. I’m always worried about something going wrong during physical activity. The fear doesn’t go away, even with the precautions. Sometimes, this anxiety makes me hold her back from certain activities, even though I know she needs to be more active.”

HCPs also observed that some parents, despite their good intentions, inadvertently discouraged physical activity due to their anxieties about their child’s health. Pediatrician explained, “Some parents use an avoidance strategy, asking doctors for asthma exemption notes so their children can skip physical education classes. While this may seem like protection, avoiding exercise can worsen long-term health issues.”

In contrast, the interviews emphasized adolescents’ mixed feelings of appreciation and frustration toward parental protection, whereas the focus groups placed these behaviors in a wider context, with HCPs warning how overprotection could unintentionally hinder activity.

Subtheme 2.2: school policy limitations

Insights from Semi-Structured interviews

Schools often failed to provide inclusive policies for adolescents with asthma and obesity, which exacerbated their challenges in participating in physical activity. This was particularly detrimental for adolescents with less controlled asthma and/or those significantly impacted by their obesity, whose needs for adapted activities were greater. P5 (18-year-old male, BMI 28.1, poorly-controlled asthma) explained, “The time requirement for the 800-meter run is the same for everyone, but with my physical condition, I can’t even meet the standard. I wish the teacher would allow me to fast walk or jog instead of evaluating everyone with the same criteria.” Similarly, P7 (14-year-old male, BMI 26.3, partially-controlled asthma) shared, “Gym class is the only time I get to exercise, but most of the time, the teacher just has me stand on the sidelines to avoid intense activity. I don’t think the teacher really cares whether I can fully integrate into the class.”

Insights from focus group discussions

Parents also expressed frustration with the one-size-fits-all policies. One father shared, “The school’s physical education classes don’t take my child’s health condition into account. For example, my son has asthma, and long-distance running is completely unrealistic for him. I had to apply for an exemption from the fitness test, but that resulted in him being excluded from all physical activity.”

The PE teachers also expressed concern about this situation. One teacher pointed out, “Due to large class sizes and limited resources, it is difficult for schools to provide appropriate exercise options for students with special needs.” However, some teachers suggested that, despite the resource limitations, schools could offer more support through flexible approaches. One teacher mentioned, “I usually arrange after-school sessions for students with special needs to help them engage in suitable exercises, but such flexible arrangements do not receive full support from the school policy.”

When viewed side by side, the interviews highlighted adolescents’ dissatisfaction with rigid school standards, while the focus groups drew attention to parents’ and teachers’ concerns about systemic barriers and limited resources.

Theme 3: community and tech adaptation gaps

Subtheme 3.1: community resource access barriers

Insights from Semi-Structured Interviews

Participants consistently reported facing significant barriers in accessing suitable fitness resources within their communities. These barriers included high costs, inconvenient distances to facilities, and a lack of infrastructure tailored to their health needs. Collectively, these factors hindered adolescents’ ability to engage in long-term physical activity. P7 (14-year-old male, BMI 26.3, partially-controlled asthma) shared, “I went to the gym once, but it was too expensive for us to keep up. My parents suggest skipping rope or push-ups at home, but I can’t stick with it.”

Insights from focus group discussions

Even when community facilities were available, the health suitability of the environment remained a significant issue. One father shared, “I wanted my child to play more racquet sports, but the local courts are overcrowded and not asthma-friendly. Now I regularly take him to play squash, where the environment is better.”

Schools also face practical challenges when attempting to collaborate with communities to expand students’access to fitness resources. The PE teacher noted, “We tried to collaborate with the community to provide more opportunities for students to exercise, like using community sports facilities or organizing joint activities, but these collaborations are hard to push forward. Community sports facilities tend to prioritize adults or high-paying programs, and our school can’t afford the rental fees.”

These perspectives combined show that adolescents focused on immediate challenges such as cost and access, whereas parents and teachers in focus groups pointed to broader structural issues including overcrowding and limited community-school collaboration.

Subtheme 3.2: digital tool personalization gaps

Insights from Semi-Structured interviews

Digital tools, such as fitness apps and gamified exercise platforms, were seen as potential ways to motivate adolescents with obesity and asthma to engage in physical activity. However, participants consistently mentioned the limitations of these tools, particularly their lack of adaptation for individuals with chronic conditions, which reduced their effectiveness. This lack of adaptation was acutely felt by adolescents whose physical capacities were challenged by their asthma. P9 (17-year-old male, BMI 27.7, poorly -controlled asthma) mentioned, “I tried a running app that encouraged me to run more, but I started struggling to breathe due to asthma, so I stopped using it. These apps work for healthy people, but they’re not user-friendly for those with health issues. It would be helpful if they could monitor asthma risk during exercise.” Compared to traditional fitness tools, adolescents preferred more gamified forms of exercise. P7 (14-year-old male, BMI 26.3, partially-controlled asthma) shared, “I like the fitness ring on the Switch because it’s more fun than traditional exercise, but sometimes the intensity is too high and I can’t catch my breath.”

Insights from focus group discussions

Parents also recognized the potential of digital tools but expressed concerns about their suitability for their children’s specific needs. One mother said, “We bought a smart bracelet, and my child thought it was fun. Watching the steps increase gave him a sense of achievement. But one time, his heart rate shot up to over 160, and he started to panic. After that, we didn’t use it anymore. There should be features that cater to their needs, like alerts for ‘safe zones,’ instead of letting the child worry.”

HCPs further emphasized the importance of integrating health data into these tools. One pediatrician explained, “If these tools could integrate health data, like asthma control and BMI, and dynamically adjust the exercise plan, parents would feel much more at ease. This would also allow children to engage in physical activities more safely.” A rehabilitation therapist added, “Some AR games are popular among adolescents, but they often don’t take into account the specific needs of children with health conditions. Sometimes the intensity is too high, which can lead to frustration. If these tools could adjust the intensity in real-time based on heart rate, it would be a significant improvement.”

Looking across both sources, the interviews demonstrated adolescents’ trial-and-error experiences with digital tools, while the focus groups revealed parental worries and professional calls for more adaptive, health-sensitive technology.

Theme 4: collaborative support solutions

Subtheme 4.1: personalized activity plans

Insights from Semi-Structured interviews

Participants widely stressed the importance of personalized exercise plans, pointing out the significant shortcomings of current plans in terms of adaptability. Their diverse physical capacities, shaped by individual asthma control levels and body weight, highlighted a clear need for tailored approaches. P5 (18-year-old male, BMI 28.1, poorly-controlled asthma) shared, “I have a classmate with asthma, but he’s very thin and doesn’t have issues in gym class. I, on the other hand, tire quickly due to my body size and struggle to breathe after one lap. It’s not that I don’t want to exercise, but nothing suits me, so I gradually stopped trying.”

Insights from focus group discussions

Parents also expressed confusion when helping their children choose suitable exercises. One mother said, “The doctor always tells us to get our child to exercise more, but he’s overweight and struggles with breathing. What kind of activities is he actually suited for? How much exercise is effective every day? Can he exercise safely and see results? These are the questions that confuse us.”

HCPs also noted the difficulty of providing precise exercise guidance within the limited time of a clinical consultation. One pediatrician explained, “Some overweight children with well-controlled asthma can exercise more, but others with poor asthma control can’t handle intense activity. Recommendations on duration and intensity require more resources and time than we have in a short visit.” A rehabilitation therapist further added, “These children need personalized plans, but it’s not just about their health conditions. It’s about their interests and schedules. Exercise needs to be part of their lifestyle, not a burden, but that’s difficult in reality.”

Together, the interviews highlighted adolescents’ difficulties in finding enjoyable and sustainable activities, whereas the focus groups illustrated the uncertainty of parents and the challenges professionals face in tailoring guidance.

Subtheme 4.2: stakeholder collaboration needs

Insights from Semi-Structured interviews

Adolescents expressed confusion and frustration when they received conflicting guidance from doctors, parents, and PE teachers. Several participants noted that these inconsistencies made them uncertain about what level or type of activity was safe. P8 (13-year-old male, BMI 25.5, partially-controlled asthma) illustrated this complex dilemma: “My doctor actually says it’s good for me to run more for my asthma. But then in PE, if I try to push myself, the teacher gets really worried and calls my mom to check if it’s okay. And when it comes to the fitness test, we all still have to run the same distance in the same time, no matter what. It’s like everyone tells me different things.” Some adolescents felt that the lack of coordinated communication placed the responsibility on them to negotiate between adults. This created additional stress and, in some cases, led them to avoid physical activity altogether. Others said they wished there was a shared plan or note from the doctor that teachers would actually follow, so they would not have to explain their condition repeatedly to different adults.

Insights from focus group discussions

Participants widely believed that the lack of collaboration between families, schools, and the healthcare system is a major barrier to adolescents’ physical activity participation and health management. The current communication mechanisms are insufficient, with each party operating in silos, leading to ineffective support for adolescents. One mother expressed her frustration with the lack of direct communication between schools and doctors: “We wish the doctor could communicate with the school to inform the PE teacher about suitable exercises for our child. If the school’s activities were adjusted by the doctor, we’d feel much more at ease. We follow the doctor’s advice, but the school hasn’t made any changes. My child is still expected to run and jump with the class, which is unrealistic.”

The PE teacher expressed frustration with the lack of collaboration, saying, “We wish there were a system to integrate doctors’ recommendations into our curriculum or at least a manual for special students. Right now, we don’t have that.”

The fragmentation of the existing system is at the core of the collaboration problem. One pediatrician concluded, “Each system works in isolation. Discussion

This study explored the intersection of obesity and asthma, conditions often addressed separately in the literature. The combined effects of these conditions create a particularly challenging landscape for adolescents, as obesity exacerbates asthma symptoms like shortness of breath and fatigue, already significant barriers to physical activity [9]. This compounded effect may trigger an adverse cycle. Limited physical activity can worsen both respiratory and weight related health, creating a complex barrier to improvement [29]. No new codes emerged in the final three interviews, which suggests code saturation within this sample. Maximum-variation sampling ensured key characteristics, such as age, gender, and asthma control, were well-represented, capturing a wide range of adolescent experiences. Additionally, 36 stakeholders, including parents, physical education teachers, and healthcare providers, were involved, ensuring that the study considered perspectives from those directly influencing adolescents’ engagement in physical activity. The diversity within these stakeholder groups provided a comprehensive understanding of the barriers and enhanced the validity of the findings.

This study contributes to the growing body of research on the dual impact of obesity and asthma on adolescents’ participation in physical activity. Previous research indicates that adolescents with obesity are more likely to experience lower self-esteem and reduced confidence, which significantly hinder their involvement in physical activity [30, 31]. These psychological barriers are compounded by a heightened sense of vulnerability in social settings, leading to reluctance in engaging in physical activities due to fear of judgment or exclusion. The present study corroborates these findings, identifying low self-efficacy, fear of embarrassment, and internalized stigma as major psychological barriers. Significantly, these psychological struggles were often amplified by the adolescents’ actual asthma control status. Those experiencing poorly-controlled asthma frequently reported physical limitations that were both immediate and unpredictable, further eroding their confidence to participate. This suggests that the severity of asthma directly contributed to their subjective feelings of inadequacy and fear in physical settings, demanding interventions that address both physical and psychological components. In addition, this study describes symbolic acceptance as a subtle social barrier to physical activity among adolescents with obesity and asthma. Symbolic acceptance refers to superficial inclusion in activities without genuine belonging, which differs from overt exclusion [32]. This phenomenon, distinct from the overt exclusion often discussed in the literature, creates a profound sense of “being on the outside” even in group settings, which reinforced adolescents’ reluctance to engage in physical activities. Our findings suggest that symbolic acceptance constitutes a subtle yet pervasive form of enacted stigma [33] or microaggression [34]. Rather than overt discrimination, this superficial inclusion communicates a tacit message of otherness, where adolescents with obesity and asthma are perceived as different or less desirable in the context of physical activities [35]. This subtle devaluation, instead of direct rejection, leads to a nuanced form of social exclusion, where individuals are physically present but emotionally marginalized [36]. This emotional marginalization, as a key tenet of stigma theory [37], aligns with Erving Goffman’s perspective on “deeply discrediting” attributes, where an individual is reduced from a whole person to a discounted one [38]. Consequently, the emotional isolation described by participants can be conceptualized as a manifestation of felt stigma, wherein individuals anticipate and internalize negative societal perceptions, leading to feelings of shame, anxiety, and a reduced sense of belonging [39]. This perceived lack of genuine belonging undermines confidence and reduces intrinsic motivation to participate, thereby forming a significant psychosocial barrier to physical activity engagement. Similar experiences are well-documented in youth with chronic conditions, where stigma can lead to impaired social functioning and increased loneliness [40]. Therefore, the observed social isolation is not merely an absence of social contact, but a deeply felt psychological response stemming from the subtle, devaluing interactions inherent in symbolic acceptance, as understood through the lens of stigma theory. These findings underscore the need for interventions that address not only physical participation but also genuine emotional and social inclusion to support adolescents’ engagement in physical activity.

In the family context, while parents generally aim to promote their children’s health through physical activity, health concerns often lead to overprotective behaviors, which paradoxically limit opportunities for active engagement. Overprotection undermines children’s autonomy and restricts their participation in physical activities [41, 42]. This overprotection was often more pronounced when parents perceived their child’s asthma to be less controlled, a perception that often aligned with an increased risk of exacerbations during physical exertion, driven by a genuine concern for their safety. Additionally, our study found that parental support was predominantly verbal, with adolescents desiring more direct involvement or tangible assistance, such as co-participation, logistical aid (e.g., transportation), or financial support for activities. This gap between verbal encouragement and concrete action often hindered their ability to overcome practical barriers to physical activity. This finding strongly aligns with existing evidence that active and enabling parental involvement significantly boosts children’s and adolescents’ motivation for physical activity and promotes sustained engagement [43–45]. At the school level, current physical education programs are found to inadequately support adolescents with obesity and asthma. Most curricula follow standardized protocols that fail to address the specific health needs of these students, further isolating them and hindering participation [46–48]. The rigidity of these school policies disproportionately affected adolescents, particularly those experiencing poorly-controlled asthma and higher BMI, whose diverse physical capacities and heightened risk of symptoms demanded more adaptable and inclusive approaches. Aligned with Theme 2, tiered evaluation criteria may better accommodate health variability. This is an implication for practice rather than a direct finding. Policies that support regular and secure information sharing between schools and healthcare and that encourage collaborative planning with families may help address fragmentation consistent with Theme 4. These points are offered as implications that derive from the themes identified.

This study discusses the potential and limitations of digital health tools for adolescents with chronic health conditions. While fitness apps and gamified tools are effective for healthy adolescents, their applicability to those with asthma and obesity remains underexplored [49, 50]. Findings indicate that, although digital tools motivate healthy adolescents, they generally lack the ability to personalize and dynamically adjust exercise plans for those with chronic conditions. Participants highlighted the absence of features such as integrated health data and safety zone alerts, which are crucial for ensuring safe and effective physical activity for adolescents managing chronic conditions. Our findings underscore that the utility of digital tools is significantly enhanced when they can integrate and respond to real-time asthma control data and symptom monitoring. This would not only improve safety by providing immediate, tailored guidance but also offer more informative insights for both adolescents and parents regarding appropriate activity levels, especially for those whose asthma control is less stable. Future research is needed to evaluate health-sensitive personalization and safety features for this population. Design-oriented work could explore real-time health data integration and safety zone alerts, consistent with Theme 3.

This study has several limitations. First, the study relied on self-reported accounts, which may be influenced by social desirability or recall biases and may not fully reflect participants’ actual behaviors or feelings. Second, as participation was voluntary, there may be selection bias, with adolescents who were more willing to talk about their experiences being overrepresented. In addition, interviewer interpretation bias during data collection and analysis could have shaped the way participants’ narratives were understood and categorized. Finally, the study did not include methodological triangulation (e.g., observational data or document analysis), nor did it formally integrate the collected demographic and clinical background data with the interview findings for a deeper, comparative analysis. This limits the ability to explore potential relationships between objective characteristics and subjective experiences more systematically, and could be considered a missed opportunity for a more comprehensive understanding. Future research could address these limitations by incorporating multiple data sources, such as observational or longitudinal data, or by adopting mixed-methods designs to formally integrate such objective and subjective information, to enrich and validate the findings.

Conclusions

This study highlights that barriers to physical activity for adolescents with co-occurring obesity and asthma are complex and multi-level, stemming from intrapersonal factors like stigma and low self-efficacy, interpersonal dynamics such as parental overprotection and insufficient tangible support, institutional challenges including inflexible school PE programs, and technological gaps in health-sensitive digital tools. These findings underscore the critical need for future research to develop and rigorously test tailored, multi-component interventions that integrate co-designed individualized plans and coordinated communication across families, schools, and healthcare. Clinically, a holistic approach is imperative, urging practitioners to actively address these multi-level barriers by advocating for tangible family support, fostering inclusive school environments, and strategically leveraging appropriate health-sensitive digital tools to enhance participation and emotional well-being in this vulnerable population.
