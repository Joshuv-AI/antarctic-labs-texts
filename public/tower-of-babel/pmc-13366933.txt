Factors associated with dying in hospital awaiting palliative care admission: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sally J Kang, Kalli Stilos, Lise Huynh, Jean Sugarbroad, Jennifer Bottoms, Lesia Wynnychuk, Sarah Torabi, Debbie Selby, Jillian Mead, Michael J Bonares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPalliative care facilities, including palliative care units (PCUs), remain underutilized with individuals dying in hospital despite having applied for admission. Historically, lower proportions of individuals with non-cancer diagnoses versus cancer diagnoses access these facilities. The study was conducted to determine the sociodemographic, functional, and clinical factors associated with dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study using routinely collected data from a clinical database supplemented with data from a chart review in a randomly selected subgroup. The cohort included individuals hospitalized at a single hospital in Toronto, Canada awaiting PCU admission between January and December 2023. Logistic regression was used to determine factors associated with dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission.ResultsIn the total cohort (N = 426; median age = 78 years, 52% female), a minority had a non-cancer diagnosis (32%) and an expected prognosis of hours to days (18%). Most (87%) were admitted to the PCU. The subgroup (N = 133) had similar characteristics to the total cohort. Regression analysis in the total cohort showed that dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission was associated with a prognosis of hours to days (OR = 7.42; 95% CI = 1.87–29.42) but not with having a non-cancer diagnosis, after adjusting for sociodemographic, functional, and clinical factors. Regression analysis in the subgroup showed no significant association between dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission and being followed by a palliative care specialist or having a documented goals of care discussion before the terminal admission.ConclusionsIn hospitalized individuals awaiting PCU admission, only a very short prognosis was associated with dying in hospital. Despite historical evidence of poor access, a non-cancer diagnosis was not associated with dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02154-5.

Background

Most individuals would prefer to die at home [1–7]. However, for some individuals, dying at home is unfeasible and they may require hospitalization at the end of life. Dying in hospital is common. For example, in Canada, 55.5% of deaths are in hospital [8]. High rates of in-hospital death have been observed in individuals with cancer [9] and non-cancer diagnoses [10, 11]. Nonetheless, dying in hospital should not reflexively be considered a negative outcome. That is, hospitalized individuals may receive high-quality end-of-life care [12]. However, previous work has demonstrated that end-of-life care delivered in acute care settings can be burdensome [13–17], costly [18], and avoidable [19–21].

Palliative care facilities are settings in which those with life-limiting illnesses could receive high-quality end-of-life care when in-home deaths are not feasible. In Canada, palliative care facilities include hospices and palliative care units (PCUs). Whereas hospices are residential and create a home-like environment, PCUs are often adjacent to or contained in acute care hospitals. Both facilities provide comprehensive end-of-life care and represent an alternative to dying in hospital when home may not be feasible. They give the feeling of being in a “home away from home” [22]. In addition, cost of end of life in a PCU is approximately one half that of a hospital death [23].

Despite the known benefits of these palliative care facilities, they remain underutilized [24]. Data from Hospice UK reports one in four people who find themselves at the end of life are not able to access the expert care they need, including hospice care [25]. Access to palliative care facilities remains inequitable, including along diagnostic lines; specifically, multiple studies have demonstrated that individuals with non-cancer diagnoses are less likely to access PCUs than those with cancer diagnoses [26–28].

Previous work demonstrated that some individuals who wish to die in a PCU may nonetheless die in hospital due to provider-, patient-, and system-level barriers [29–31]. However, these studies included small cohorts and did not conduct multivariable analyses accounting for the multiple factors that could influence whether one dies in hospital before PCU transfer. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to determine the sociodemographic, functional, and clinical factors associated with dying in hospital in individuals awaiting PCU admission. Specifically, we aimed to determine whether having a non-cancer diagnosis, receiving upstream specialist palliative care, or having a documented goals of care discussion were associated with dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission.

Methods

Design, setting, participants

This was a retrospective cohort study of hospitalized adults (≥ 18 years) followed by an inpatient specialist palliative care team at a single academic hospital in Toronto, Canada, Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre (SHSC), between January 1 and December 31, 2023. We included only individuals who ultimately died in hospital or were transferred to the PCU for end-of-life care, rendering the hospitalization a terminal one. We excluded individuals for whom an application to the PCU was not submitted. SHSC is a 638-bed tertiary care hospital with a geographically adjacent PCU, the largest in Toronto (56 beds).

The study was approved by the SHSC Research Ethics Committee (6293). Waiver of consent was approved as all patients died by the time of data collection, and it would have been impracticable to contact all bereaved caregivers to obtain consent. The study was reported in accordance with the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Data sources

Data were collected from two sources. First, we used data from the Palliative Care Database at our institution, which routinely captures sociodemographic (e.g., age, sex), functional (measured by the Palliative Performance Scale (PPS)), clinical (e.g., primary diagnosis, expected prognosis determined by the specialist palliative care provider), and admission/referral-specific information (e.g., referral service, reason for referral) for all hospitalized individuals followed by the inpatient specialist palliative care team. Second, a chart review was conducted in a third of individuals to extract additional variables. They were randomly selected based on their date of consultation to include individuals hospitalized throughout 2023. Additional variables included whether they were followed by clinic- or home-based specialist palliative care and whether they had a documented goals of care discussion before the terminal admission. A documented goals of care discussion was defined as any discussion pertaining to a patient or family’s values, hopes, fears, or medical preferences with respect to their medical care documented in the electronic medical record at SHSC [32–34]. We excluded isolated documentation of resuscitation status unless it was accompanied by documentation of the values that underlaid the decision [35].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the total cohort and subgroup. Specifically, continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) since they had non-normal distributions, and categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages. Standardized differences (SDs) were calculated to compare those who died in hospital and those who were transferred to the PCU. Standardized differences of ≥ 0.3 were considered potentially meaningful.

Two multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed to determine the factors associated with dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission. First, an analysis was performed in the total cohort to evaluate whether primary diagnosis (cancer versus non-cancer) was associated with dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission controlling for pre-specified covariates: sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex), functional status (PPS), clinical information (expected prognosis determined by the specialist palliative care provider), and admission/referral-specific information (days from admission to specialist palliative care referral). Second, an exploratory analysis was performed in the subgroup for whom a chart review was completed to evaluate whether being followed by clinic- or home-based specialist palliative care or whether having a documented goals of care discussion before the terminal admission was associated with dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission. This analysis controlled for the same covariates. In both analyses, PPS was categorized as 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40–100%; and prognosis was categorized as hours to days, days to weeks, < 3 months, and ≥ 3 months. No individuals had missing values for the variables included in the regression analyses, such that none were excluded from the analyses. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Studio (Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 597 individuals were followed by the inpatient specialist palliative care team during a terminal admission between January 1 and December 31, 2023. 171 were excluded because no PCU application was submitted. Therefore, the total cohort included 426 individuals awaiting PCU admission. A total of 133 individuals were randomly selected for the subgroup analysis, for whom additional data were collected through a chart review (Fig. 1). Table 1 outlines sociodemographic, functional, and clinical factors in the total cohort and subgroup. In the total cohort, median age was 78 years (IQR = 68–86), 52% (N = 221) were female, 63.8% (N = 272) had a PPS of ≤ 30%, and 18% (N = 78) had an expected prognosis of hours to days. Most had a primary cancer diagnosis (68%, N = 288) versus non-cancer diagnosis (32%, N = 138). The sociodemographic, functional, and clinical factors were similar for the subgroup. In the subgroup, 57 (43%) individuals were followed by clinic- or home-based specialist palliative care and 84 (63%) had a documented goals of care discussion before the terminal admission. In the total cohort, most were transferred to the PCU (87%, N = 372) versus died in hospital (13%, N = 54). Similarly, in the subgroup, most were transferred to the PCU (88%, N = 117) versus died in hospital (12%, N = 16).

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study found that an expected prognosis of hours to days was the only factor associated with dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission in the multivariable analysis. Having a non-cancer diagnosis was not associated with higher odds of doing so. Altogether, this suggests that proximity to death may be more influential than primary diagnosis on dying in hospital before PCU transfer.

Non-cancer diagnosis

A non-cancer diagnosis was not associated with dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission. This finding is striking given evidence of under-representation of individuals with non-cancer diagnoses in palliative care facilities and delayed access to palliative care facilities in these patient populations [28]. Reported barriers to timely palliative care access in individuals with non-cancer diagnoses include lack of awareness of referral criteria and the perspective that services prioritize individuals with cancer [36, 37]. In addition, survey studies showed that more than half of cardiologists [36] and respirologists [37] believe that their patients have negative perceptions of palliative care. However, our study specifically targeted individuals with non-cancer diagnoses who already submitted a PCU application and were awaiting admission. This suggests that patient- and provider-level barriers to palliative care access (e.g., fear of what palliative care represents) may have already been mitigated by the time a PCU application was submitted. That is, once a PCU application is submitted, individuals with a non-cancer diagnosis have a similar likelihood of PCU admission compared to those with a cancer diagnosis.

This study was done at a hospital with emerging non-cancer palliative care services, which include embedded, stand-alone, and virtual outpatient palliative care consultation for people with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [38], end-stage kidney disease, heart failure [39], chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases, and interstitial lung disease. These services may contribute to mitigating barriers to PCU access in people with non-cancer diagnoses. However, future studies are required to determine the impact of non-cancer palliative care services on the quality of end-of-life care in these patient populations.

Upstream specialist palliative care

The subgroup analysis demonstrated no statically significant association between dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission and being followed by a palliative care specialist or having a documented goals of care discussion before the terminal admission. This is in contrast to studies showing that both early palliative care and advance care planning interventions were associated with fewer in-hospital deaths and increased and earlier hospice use [40, 41]. This discrepancy may be explained by our cohort including only those who already submitted a PCU application. That is, upstream specialist palliative care and advance care planning may have more of an impact on whether a PCU application is submitted than on whether one is ultimately admitted to the PCU. Second, our exploratory analysis of 133 individuals may have lacked sufficient power to detect an association between dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission and pre-hospitalization palliative care involvement or goals of care discussions.

Although emerging evidence suggests that advance care planning may not improve end-of-life care [42], we cannot conclude that upstream specialist palliative care or goals of care discussions do not influence outcomes at the end of life based on our findings. In fact, we found that those who received upstream clinic-based specialist palliative care were more likely to be transferred to the PCU compared to those who did not receive this care (32% versus 0.75%; SD = 0.85), suggesting the potential value of upstream advance care planning in line with multiple international practice guidelines [43–45]. This is in keeping with studies that have shown that upstream advance care planning is a facilitator of end-of-life care [46, 47] and may be associated with goal-concordant end-of-life care [38, 48]. Our findings suggest that when hospitalized individuals are imminently dying, system-level barriers may outweigh the impact of individual-level planning.

Prognosis

The only factor found to be associated with dying in hospital awaiting PCU admission was a very short expected prognosis (hours to days) determined by the specialist palliative care provider at the time of consultation. In these cases, acute care providers may have considered a PCU transfer to be unsafe due to a high likelihood of death during or shortly after transfer. This is substantiated by a recent study in the Netherlands that identified “deteriorating health” as a common reason for cancelling transfer from hospital to hospice or home for end-of-life care [29]. Under these circumstances, in-hospital deaths may represent discordance with patient and family preferences but should not reflexively be interpreted as a failure. In these scenarios, the appropriate decision may be for the patient to stay in place. Nonetheless, the healthcare system may lack sufficient flexibility to support individuals who make later decisions about PCU transfer or who experience a rapid clinical deterioration.

This is in keeping with studies showing that hospice admissions often occur only a few days before death [49, 50]. Our findings demonstrate the need to manage patient and family expectations when a PCU application is submitted, preparing them for the possibility that death may occur before transfer. Collectively, our previous work on PCU transfers from the acute care setting [30, 31, 51] suggests that access is driven less by diagnostic inequity and more by process-related and prognostic factors. In an effort to enable goal-concordant end-of-life care, there is a need for institutions to identify and address barriers to timely and seamless transitions to palliative care facilities.

Strengths and limitations

Using routinely collected data supplemented with a chart review that spans 12 months, we systematically evaluated factors associated with dying in hospital awaiting admission to a palliative care facility. However, the study has multiple limitations. First, to our knowledge, there is no standardized definition of a goals of care discussion in quantitative research. We selected an inclusive definition, qualifying any discussion related to patient and family values, hopes, fears, or medical preferences as a goals of care discussion. Consequently, nearly two-thirds of the subgroup were found to have had a goals of care discussion before their terminal admission. This broad definition may have reduced our ability to identify the conversations that could have been most impactful on end-of-life care. Second, previously reported barriers to palliative care access, such as ethnicity, immigration status, socioeconomic status, and geographic location [52, 53] may have been confounding factors. They were not included in our analyses since they are not routinely collected in the Palliative Care Database and were not captured in the chart review. Third, this was a single-centre study conducted at a site with an adjacent PCU, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to settings that are more distant from a palliative care facility.

Conclusions

In hospitalized individuals who received specialist palliative care and were awaiting PCU admission, only a very short expected prognosis was associated with dying in hospital. Dying in hospital was not more common in individuals with non-cancer diagnoses, suggesting that barriers to PCU access in these patient populations do not apply once a PCU application has been submitted. The findings of this study should be supplemented by studies at other sites and would be enriched by qualitative studies evaluating the perspectives of bereaved caregivers of those who died in hospital awaiting PCU admission.
