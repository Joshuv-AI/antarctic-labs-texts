Improving fermionic variational quantum eigensolvers with Majorana swap networks
D. E. Fisher, S. A. Fldzhyan, D. V. Minaev, S. S. Straupe, M. Yu. Saygin

Abstract
Simulating computationally hard fermionic systems is a promising application of quantum computing. However, mapping nonlocal fermionic operators to qubits often produces deep circuits, rendering such simulations impractical on near-term hardware. We introduce two Majorana swap network techniques for variational quantum eigensolvers that reduce circuit depth and two-qubit gate count, thereby limiting error accumulation. First, we develop a cyclic compilation algorithm that localizes all two-particle interaction terms in a general fermionic Hamiltonian that contains 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 4 ) \displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{4}) such terms, using only 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 3 ) \displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{3}) auxiliary Majorana-swap gates, where n \displaystyle n is the number of fermionic modes. This algorithm targets all-to-all qubit connectivity (e.g., trapped-ion processors) and can be used to compactify UCCGSD circuits. Second, we design a Majorana swap network for the UpCCGSD variational ansatz, which is already more compact than UCCGSD. Our network achieves asymptotic reductions in circuit depth and gate count of approximately 50 \displaystyle 50 % and 20 \displaystyle 20 %, respectively, under all-to-all connectivity. For the more restricted 2 × N \displaystyle 2\times N connectivity, the reductions are even larger — about 55 \displaystyle 55 % (circuit depth) and 40 \displaystyle 40 % (gate count). These improvements translate directly into increased robustness to hardware noise, as demonstrated by numerical simulations on representative examples.



I
Introduction

Accurate simulation of correlated fermionic systems underpins progress in quantum chemistry, materials design, and condensed-matter physics
Georges et al. 1996
;
Aspuru-Guzik et al. 2005
;
James D. Whitfield and Aspuru-Guzik 2011
. While classical electronic-structure methods have achieved remarkable successes, their cost typically scales unfavorably with system size, reflecting the exponential growth of the underlying Hilbert space
Sherrill and Schaefer 1999
. Quantum computers promise polynomial-resource simulations in principle
Kitaev 1995
;
Bauer et al. 2020
;
Su et al. 2021
, but realizing this promise on noisy, connectivity-limited devices remains challenging.

A key obstacle is the encoding of fermionic operators into qubit Hilbert space. Standard mappings such as Jordan–Wigner (JW)
Jordan and Wigner 1928
and Bravyi–Kitaev (BK)
Bravyi and Kitaev 2002
produce non-local Pauli strings whose support grows with the number of fermionic modes, inflating circuit depth and number of entanglement gates, and exacerbating error accumulation on Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) hardware
Bharti et al. 2022
. Numerous strategies aim to mitigate this non-locality: alternative encodings (e.g., tree-based and task-based mappings)
Wang et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Miller et al. 2022
, ancilla-assisted compact encodings
Setia and Whitfield 2018
;
Derby et al. 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2019
;
Hastings et al. 2015
, and structured gate scheduling via swap Networks (SNs)
O’Gorman et al. 2019
. Yet, for widely used variational approaches such as the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE)
Peruzzo et al. 2013
with Unitary Coupled Cluster (UCC) ansatzes
Lee et al. 2019
;
Arrazola et al. 2022
, the cumulative overhead from non-local fermionic operators can still be significant.

Swap networks have been used to enforce effective locality under JW by bringing interacting modes next to each other by fermionic swap gates (FSWAP)
O’Gorman et al. 2019
;
Kivlichan et al. 2018
.
This work adopts the Majorana approach: each fermionic mode is represented by two involutive Majorana operators obeying simple anti-commutation relations, providing a natural way to represent them as elements of the Pauli group. We introduce
Majorana swap
gates (MSWAP) — operations that implement signed permutations of two Majoranas — and show how to assemble them into
Majorana swap Networks
(MSNs). Compared with FSWAPs, MSWAPs offer finer-grained control over operator support and also admit compact decompositions when mapped to qubits.

We focus on two widely used variants of UCC ansätze. The first is the generalized singles-and-doubles UCCGSD, which provides high expressivity at the expense of many parameters and gates. The second is
\displaystyle k
-UpCCGSD, which restricts double excitations to spatially paired orbitals and is commonly repeated in
\displaystyle k
layers to recover expressivity with modest overhead. We will show that the
\displaystyle k
-UpCCGSD structure aligns naturally with a
\displaystyle 2\times N
qubit connectivity layout consistent with existing topologies
Arute et al. 2019
;
Acharya et al. 2025
;
Langrock et al. 2023
, that places
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
orbitals in separate rows; in this geometry, MSNs can make all required single and paired-double excitations local with high parallelism. These improvements arise from the reduced number of swap operations and from embedding optimized single- and double-excitation rotations directly within the MSWAP layers.

Our main contributions are:

1.

Optimized single- and double-excitation decompositions.
We provide gate construction for double excitation rotation that reduce depth and the number of two-qubit gates number compared with existing implementations, and that integrate cleanly with planar connectivity to avoid redundant swaps.

2.

Cyclic permutation for UCCGSD with
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
overhead on all-to-all connectivity.
We present a permutation algorithm that preserves fermionic symmetry and guarantees local implementation of all double excitations rotations using only
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
swap operations—improving upon the
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
scaling of prior approaches
O’Gorman et al. 2019
.

3.

MSN circuits for UpCCGSD.
Inspired by the fermionic swap network (FSN)
O’Gorman et al. 2019
;
Kivlichan et al. 2018
, we design MSNs that localize the required excitation rotations while maximizing parallel execution. Compared to FSNs, the resulting circuits achieve a
\displaystyle 50
% reduction in depth and a
\displaystyle 20
% reduction in two-qubit gate count on all-to-all connectivity architectures. Since MSNs are explicitly tailored to
\displaystyle 2\times N
connectivity, they reduce circuit depth by
\displaystyle 55
% and the two-qubit gate count by
\displaystyle 40
% in this setting, further widening the gap to FSNs under connectivity constraints.

4.

Noise-aware evaluation.
Using representative depolarizing and correlated error channels, as well as a superconducting-device noise model, we quantify the sensitivity of different compilation strategies for UpCCGSD ansatz. MSNs consistently show lower energy susceptibility than considered alternatives.

II
Preliminary Details

In the formalism of second quantization, the quantum state of
\displaystyle n
fermionic modes is described by creation
\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{p}
and annihilation
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{p}
operators, where the index
\displaystyle p
(
\displaystyle 1\leq p\leq n
) labels the single-particle fermionic orthonormal states. These operators satisfy the canonical anti-commutation relations:

\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{p},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{q}\}=0,\quad\{\hat{a}_{p},\hat{a}_{q}\}=0,\quad\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{p},\hat{a}_{q}\}=\delta_{pq}.

(1)

These anti-commutation relations prevent a fully local encoding, since any two operators must act non-trivially on at least one common qubit. However, for systems with conserved particle number, only operator products containing equal numbers of creation and annihilation operators are allowed. In other words, only even-length products of creation and annihilation operators are physically allowed; we refer to such products as
physical operators
. The physical operators form the foundation for representing fermionic states and constructing fermionic Hamiltonians used in simulation.

II.1
Variational Quantum Eigensolver

We are interested in VQEs, which are being considered as a promising approach for leveraging NISQ-era quantum devices
Cerezo et al. 2021
;
Endo et al. 2021
;
Peruzzo et al. 2013
. The variational algorithms aim to approximate the ground state of a target Hamiltonian by preparing a parameterized quantum state and minimizing the expected energy. As in classical variational methods, VQEs restrict the optimization to a polynomially parameterized subspace of the exponentially large Hilbert space. This subspace is constructed by applying a parameterized unitary circuit
\displaystyle U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
to a fixed reference state
\displaystyle\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle
:

\left|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rangle=U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle.

(2)

The corresponding cost function is defined as

L(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left\langle\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right|\mathrm{\hat{H}}\left|\psi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rangle,

(3)

where
\displaystyle\mathrm{\hat{H}}
is the Hamiltonian of interest.

VQEs offer two main advantages. First, they can efficiently prepare highly entangled quantum states using native unitary dynamics. Second, they introduce an inductive bias: by tailoring the ansatz structure to salient features of the target state, they can make the optimization landscape more favorable.

Beyond quantum simulation, VQEs have been explored for diverse tasks, including integer factorization
Anschuetz et al. 2019
, solving nonlinear equations
Lubasch et al. 2020
, and quantum machine learning
Biamonte et al. 2016
;
Cerezo et al. 2022
. A wide range of VQE ansatz architectures has been proposed
Kandala et al. 2017
;
Wiersema et al. 2020
;
Lee et al. 2019
;
Grimsley et al. 2019
;
Tang et al. 2021
, trading off expressiveness, trainability, and hardware compatibility. In this work we focus on the
unitary coupled-cluster
(
UCC
) family
Lee et al. 2019
, which extends classical coupled cluster
Sherrill and Schaefer 1999
by exponentiating anti-Hermitian excitation operators. A general UCC ansatz takes the form:

\left|\psi(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{d})\right\rangle=\exp\bigg[\sum_{(p,q)\in S}\!\!\!s_{pq}\hat{T}_{pq}+\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{(p,q,r,s)\in D}\!\!\!\!\!\!d_{pqrs}\hat{T}_{pqrs}\bigg]\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle,

(4)

where
\displaystyle\mathbf{s}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{d}
are the sets of real-valued parameters
\displaystyle s_{pq}
and
\displaystyle d_{pqrs}
, and
\displaystyle S
and
\displaystyle D
are sets of index tuples corresponding to spin-preserving single and double excitations, respectively. In (
4
), the excitation operators are given by:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{T}_{pq}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{p}\hat{a}_{q}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{q}\hat{a}_{p},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{T}_{pqrs}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{p}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{q}\hat{a}_{r}\hat{a}_{s}-\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{s}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{r}\hat{a}_{q}\hat{a}_{p}.

(5)

To make the ansatz implementable on quantum hardware, (
4
) is typically Trotterized
Trotter 1959
. The resulting circuit, up to operator reordering, takes the form:

\left|\psi(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{d})\right\rangle=\!\!\!\prod_{(p,q)\in S}\!\!\!\hat{S}_{pq}(s_{pq})\!\!\!\prod_{(p,q,r,s)\in D}\!\!\!\hat{D}_{pqrs}(d_{pqrs})\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle,

(6)

where
\displaystyle\hat{S}_{pq}(s_{pq})
and
\displaystyle\hat{D}_{pqrs}(d_{pqrs})
denote the single and double excitation rotations, respectively:

\hat{S}_{pq}(s)=e^{s\hat{T}_{pq}},\quad\hat{D}_{pqrs}(d)=e^{d\hat{T}_{pqrs}}.

(7)

For the UCCGSD ansatz, the excitation index sets are given by:

\displaystyle\displaystyle S_{G}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\{(p_{l},q_{l})\mid p<q,\;l\in\{\alpha,\beta\}\},

(8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle D_{G}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\{(p_{l},q_{k},r_{l},s_{k})\mid p<r,\;q<s,\;l,k\in\{\alpha,\beta\}\},

(9)

where
\displaystyle 1\leq p,q,r,s\leq n/2
label spatial orbitals and
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta
denote two spin components.

For this ansatz, we introduce a
cyclic-permutation algorithm
that localizes all
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
double-excitation terms using only
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
auxiliary swap gates. This compilation strategy enables the full UCCGSD ansatz to be implemented with reduced circuit depth and a smaller two-qubit gate count.

Although UCCGSD is highly expressive and performs well in small-scale studies, its resource demands grow rapidly with system size. In addition, its optimization landscape can exhibit barren plateaus
Larocca et al. 2024
, making convergence challenging on near-term hardware.

To mitigate these issues, we also examine a more resource-efficient variant,
k-UpCCGSD
, which restricts double excitations to spatially paired orbitals and repeats them with independent parameters across k layers. This ansatz therefore includes only excitations of the form:

D_{\text{Up}}=\left\{(p_{\alpha},p_{\beta},q_{\alpha},q_{\beta})\mid 1\leq p<q\leq N\right\}.

(10)

And can be expressed as:

\!\!\!\!\!\!\left|\psi(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{d})\right\rangle=\prod_{l=1}^{k}\bigg[\prod_{(p,q)\in S_{G}}\hat{S}{p_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}}(s(l)_{p_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}})\hat{S}{p_{\beta}q_{\beta}}(s(l)_{p_{\beta}q_{\beta}})\\
\hat{D}{p_{\alpha}p_{\beta}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}(d(l)_{pq})\bigg]\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle.

(11)

It offers a favorable trade-off between expressiveness and circuit complexity, enabling chemically accurate results with significantly fewer parameters
Lee et al. 2019
.

II.2
Majorana Operators and the Pauli Group

To implement the unitary evolution described by (
7
) on a quantum computer, the second quantization operators must first be mapped onto qubit operators. This step is complicated by the anti-commutation relations of fermionic operators, which often lead to non-local qubit representations and deep quantum circuits.

Several fermion-to-qubit mappings has been developed to address this challenge
Derby et al. 2021
;
Bravyi and Kitaev 2002
;
Setia and Whitfield 2018
;
Verstraete and Cirac 2005
;
Whitfield et al. 2016
;
Jiang et al. 2019
. These mappings aim to reduce operator non-locality, often at the cost of introducing auxiliary qubits. In fully connected systems, these approaches may require up to twice as many qubits
Verstraete and Cirac 2005
;
Whitfield et al. 2016
;
Setia and Whitfield 2018
to achieve representations where physical operators (those composed of equal numbers of creation and annihilation operators) act non-trivially on only
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)
qubits. We refer to these operators, if they act on
\displaystyle n
qubits, as
\displaystyle n
-local
.

Two principal paradigms exist for constructing fermionic mappings
Bravyi et al. 2017
:

•

First quantization
, which encodes fermionic Fock states directly. This approach can reduce the number of required qubits using symmetries, but it often results in deeper and more intricate circuit constructions;

•

Second quantization
, which encodes creation and annihilation operators through Pauli operators. This method is more widely used in near-term quantum computing due to its compatibility with standard hardware and shallower circuit depth.

In this work, we adopt the second quantization framework and focus on fermion-to-qubit mappings based on
Majorana operators
, represented using
ternary tree (TT)
structures, which we explain below
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Vlasov 2019
. Majorana operators
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{j}
are defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{2p-1}=\hat{a}_{p}+\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{2p}=i(\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}-\hat{a}_{p}),

(12)

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{p}=\frac{\hat{\gamma}_{2p-1}-i\hat{\gamma}_{2p}}{2},

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}=\frac{\hat{\gamma}_{2p-1}+i\hat{\gamma}_{2p}}{2},

that obey the anticommutation relations:

\{\hat{\gamma}_{p},\hat{\gamma}_{q}\}=2\delta_{pq}.

(13)

The operators
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{j}
are Hermitian and unitary, and their algebra resembles that of Hermitian elements of the Pauli group
\displaystyle G_{n}=\{\iota X,\iota Y,\iota Z,\iota I\}^{\otimes n}
, where
\displaystyle\iota
is a fourth root of unity. The corresponding Pauli strings can be used to construct qubit-space representations of Majorana operators. In this representation, the number of fermionic modes
\displaystyle n
equals to the number of qubits in the system.

A valid mapping is obtained by associating each Majorana operator
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{j}
with a Pauli string
\displaystyle\gamma_{j}
such that the resulting set
\displaystyle\{\gamma_{j}\}
is mutually anti-commuting. Henceforth, we omit the hat symbol when referring to the Pauli representation. It has been shown that pairwise anti-commutation is a necessary and sufficient condition for a valid fermion-to-qubit mapping
Chiew et al. 2024
. It turns out that stricter conditions, such as algebraic independence, are not required
Miller et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2024
.

The TT formalism provides a compact and scalable way to construct such mutually anti-commuting sets, while preserving qubit locality, which is an essential requirement for low-depth circuit implementations.

In general, constructing mappings where all physical operators are strictly
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)
-local is not possible without ancillary qubits. However, this limitation can be circumvented by using
SNs
, which dynamically reorder fermionic modes
O’Gorman et al. 2019
;
Hashim et al. 2022
. SNs can be implemented efficiently in certain mappings, such as the JW mapping
O’Gorman et al. 2019
;
Kivlichan et al. 2018
, and allow locality to be maintained throughout the circuit.

In this work, we use SNs to support constant-locality implementations of physical operators without the need for ancilla qubits. These networks act as switching layers that transform between different representations of ladder or Majorana operators, enabling ansatz implementation under realistic connectivity constraints.

II.3
Ternary Tree Representation

By employing Pauli strings from the group
\displaystyle G_{n}
to represent
\displaystyle n
fermionic modes, one can construct an exponential number of valid fermion-to-qubit mappings — on the order of
\displaystyle N_{\text{all}}=\mathcal{O}(2^{n^{2}})
. A significant subset of these mappings can be captured using
TTs
— graphical structures in which each path from the root to a leaf encodes a Majorana operator
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Miller et al. 2022
;
Vlasov 2019
. Several well-known mappings, such as JW, Bravyi–Kitaev (BK)
Bravyi and Kitaev 2002
, and the parity mapping
Seeley et al. 2012
, admit TT representations
Vlasov 2019
.

In TT representation shown in Fig.
1
, square nodes represent qubits, while circular leaf nodes correspond to Majorana operators. To obtain the Pauli string associated with a given Majorana operator, one traverses the tree from the root to the corresponding leaf. The Pauli operator (
\displaystyle X
,
\displaystyle Y
, or
\displaystyle Z
) assigned to each qubit along the path is determined by the type of edge taken. The anti-commutation relations between Majorana operators (
13
) are guaranteed by construction, since any two distinct paths differ non-trivially at exactly one qubit — the node where the paths diverge —ensuring the correct algebraic structure.

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}=X_{1}X_{2}

\displaystyle\gamma_{5}=Y_{1}Z_{3}

\displaystyle\gamma_{5}=Z_{1}Z_{2}X_{3}

As illustrated in Fig.
1
, different mappings yield varying degrees of operator locality. Among them, the JW mapping exhibits the highest variability. In this encoding, Majorana operators are mapped to Pauli strings as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma_{2p-1}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}Z_{2}\cdots Z_{p-1}\,X_{p},

(14)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2p}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}Z_{2}\cdots Z_{p-1}\,Y_{p},

which generally results in highly non-local operators. However, products involving adjacent modes can remain local. For example,
\displaystyle\gamma_{5}\gamma_{6}=iZ_{3}
acts only on a single qubit. Throughout the text, operators written without the hat symbol
\displaystyle\hat{\cdot}
refer to their qubit-space representations, as determined by the current mapping that includes all previously applied permutations.

This degenerate locality is a key feature that enables the efficient use of swap Networks in the JW mapping, allowing for dynamic reordering of fermionic modes and substantially reducing of circuit depth and gate count.

Excitation operators are generally non-local unless all fermionic modes involved are adjacent in the JW mapping. In what follows, we consider this adjacency condition.

In the JW encoding, a single-excitation rotation corresponds to the sum of two two-qubit Pauli rotations:

(20)

This operator can be implemented with two
\displaystyle\mathrm{CX}

\!\!\!\!\!\!D_{p,p+1,p+2,p+3}(d)=\exp\Bigg[i\frac{d}{8}\big(\mathrm{XYXX}+\mathrm{YXXX}+\mathrm{YYYX}+\\
+\mathrm{YYXY}-\mathrm{XXYX}-\mathrm{XXXY}-\mathrm{YXYY}-\mathrm{XYYY}\big)\Bigg].

(21)

In this context, mode indices are omitted for clarity. This expression corresponds to the JW representation of a fermionic double-excitation acting on four consecutive modes.

full application of the algorithm enabling localization of all double excitation terms.

Here, we present a method to localize all two-particle interaction terms in a general fermionic Hamiltonian using only
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
auxiliary local swap gates under the assumption of all-to-all connectivity, despite the total number of such terms scaling as
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
. This connectivity requirement can be relaxed to linear at the cost of an additional
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
SWAP gates. This approach is particularly suited to variational quantum algorithms, where commonly used mapping techniques — such as fermionic Fourier transforms
Jiang et al. 2018
;
Babbush et al. 2018
or problem-based encodings
Wang et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2023
— are not directly applicable due to the presence of variational parameters.

We propose a cyclic permutation algorithm to efficiently transform each two-particle interaction term — that is, a product of two creation and two annihilation operators (such as double excitations in UCC ansatzes) — into a local operator. The idea of the algorithm is illustrated in Fig.
8
. We consider the JW-like encoding with reordered Majorana operators:

\displaystyle\gamma_{4p-3}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}\ldots Z_{2p-2}X_{2p-1},

(26)

\displaystyle\gamma_{4p-1}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}\ldots Z_{2p-2}Y_{2p-1},

\displaystyle\gamma_{4p-2}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}\ldots Z_{2p-1}X_{2p},

\displaystyle\gamma_{4p}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}\ldots Z_{2p-1}Y_{2p},

which can be obtained from the JW mapping after the layer of MSWAP gates (
22
). In this encoding, each fermionic mode is distributed throughout two qubits. As shown in Fig.
8
b, the modes are grouped into pairs (e.g.,
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{1}\hat{a}_{2}
,
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{3}\hat{a}_{4}
and
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{5}\hat{a}_{6}
with their Hermitian conjugates) which form 2-local qubit operators. Products of such pairs yield
\displaystyle 4
-local operators, aligning with the structure of the double-excitation rotations that we aim to localize, and can be implemented using the proposed circuit shown in Fig.
7
.

The cyclic permutation algorithm achieves localization through pairwise transpositions, rotating a selected mode through all others. For
\displaystyle 2n
modes, this requires
\displaystyle n(2n-1)
transpositions (Fig.
8
b, with each cycle enabling local implementation of all double excitations involving the selected mode (Appendix
A
). The corresponding swap gates required to implement this rotation are shown in Fig.
8
c and require no more than four
\displaystyle\mathrm{CX}
gates per transposition.

Full localization is achieved through application of the rotation procedure sequentially to different modes. The Fig.
8
d shows an example of this alternating rotation patterns: mode 1 rotates anticlockwise (
\displaystyle a_{1}\circlearrowleft 6
), mode 2 clockwise restoring the original encoding (
\displaystyle a_{2}\circlearrowright 6
), and subsequent modes follow this alternating scheme across disjoint subsets. This sequential application localizes all Hamiltonian terms without ancillary qubits by systematically processing each mode while compensating for ordering side-effects.

According to Theorem
A.1
of Appendix, this construction guarantees that all double excitation terms consisting of two fermionic pairs are encountered. The total number of transpositions required by the algorithm scales with the number of fermionic mode
\displaystyle 2n
as:

\sum_{k=3}^{n}2(2k^{2}-k)=\frac{4}{3}n^{3}+n^{2}-\frac{1}{3}n-14,

(27)

where we exclude subsets of fewer than four modes, for which a full cycle is unnecessary.

It is worth noting that an explicit SN for localizing all double excitation terms was previously proposed
O’Gorman et al. 2019
. That construction offers improved parallelization opportunities and requires only linear connectivity in the device architecture. However, it introduces significant overhead both in the number of swap layers and in the total number of swap gates, due to its recursive structure.

In contrast, the proposed algorithm – while assuming full qubit connectivity – can also be adapted to a linear architecture by inserting additional layers of SWAP gates. Specifically, after each transposition step, a set of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n)
double excitation rotations is applied, which can be implemented using an additional
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n)
SWAP gates without altering the original qubit layout at the end.

Our algorithm achieves a nearly optimal swap gate count. Any individual fermionic swap generates at most
\displaystyle 2n
new local double excitations. Since the total number of unique double excitation terms is
\displaystyle\binom{2n}{4}
, the minimal number of swaps is
\displaystyle\binom{2n}{4}/2n\approx\frac{n^{3}}{3}
. Hence, the total number of transpositions required by the cyclic permutation algorithm asymptotically matches the minimal possible count up to a constant factor of
\displaystyle 4
. While this comes at the expense of limited parallelism, it results in a more gate-efficient compilation strategy.

Although this algorithm substantially reduces the overall gate count in the UCCGSD circuit, the resulting sizes remain prohibitively large for our noisy simulations as the number of qubits increases. We next introduce a Majorana swap network designed to produce a more compact
\displaystyle k
-UpCCGSD ansatz, and evaluate numerically the benefits of integrating MSWAP gates with the optimized excitation-rotation decompositions.

\displaystyle k
-UpCCGSD

As outlined above, the
\displaystyle k
-UpCCGSD ansatz employs unitary evolution generated by a restricted subset of excitation operators as defined in (

).
Due to spin conservation, the set of orbitals can be partitioned into two groups according to spin projection. This partitioning naturally motivates a
\displaystyle 2\times N
lattice architecture, in which the upper and lower rows encode
\displaystyle\alpha
- and
\displaystyle\beta
-spin orbitals, respectively. We label the Majorana operators as
\displaystyle\gamma
for
\displaystyle\alpha
spins and
\displaystyle\zeta
for
\displaystyle\beta
spins. For instance,
\displaystyle\gamma_{1}
,
\displaystyle\gamma_{2}
and
\displaystyle\zeta_{1}
,
\displaystyle\zeta_{2}
correspond to spin components of the same spatial orbital (see Fig.

).

A key structural feature of the UpCCGSD ansatz is that each double-excitation operator acts on a pair of spin orbitals corresponding to the same spatial orbital. To preserve these pairings throughout the circuit, we apply synchronized permutations across both rows of the
\displaystyle 2\times N
lattice. These permutations are realized using a
2-complete swap network

O’Gorman et al. 2019
;
Kivlichan et al. 2018
—a structured sequence of swap layers that ensures every pair of fermionic modes becomes adjacent at least once during the circuit execution.

This property is illustrated in Figs.
9a
and
9b
, where each wire represents a fermionic mode and each crossing indicates a point where the corresponding modes are brought into adjacency. This enables the efficient implementation of all required two-mode fermionic operators as local operators after a certain layer.

Fig.
9a
depicts an MSN tailored for the 8-qubit
\displaystyle 1
-UpCCGSD ansatz. The circuit alternates between swap and excitation layers. In our construction, MSWAP gates are fused with double- and single-excitation rotations, as discussed in Sec.
IV.2
. As noted, this integration reduces circuit overhead by embedding excitation operations directly within the swap layers. Double excitations are realized as localized four-qubit gates. Since the corresponding qubits are not adjacent in the depicted layout, different parts of each double-excitation gate are color-coded for clarity.

By contrast, the FSN proposed in Ref.
O’Gorman et al. 2019
and shown in Fig.
9b
also realises a 2-complete network; however, it performs extra permutations between orbitals of opposite spin, which results in twice as many FSWAP operations. In FSN, single excitations require two CX gates, the same as FSWAPs. As a result, FSN exhibits reduced parallelism and increased circuit depth compared to MSNs.

VII.2
Specific noise models

In addition to these static resource metrics, we also examine the robustness of the synthesized
\displaystyle 1
-UpCCGSD circuits under realistic quantum noise. In particular, we simulate the behavior of the corresponding VQE algorithm under various noise models, allowing us to assess the practical viability of each construction beyond the ideal gate-based model.

To simulate the effect of noise, we adopt the general description of the evolution
\displaystyle\Lambda
of a noisy quantum system
\displaystyle\rho
, which uses Kraus operators
\displaystyle\{E_{i}\}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Lambda(\rho)=\sum E_{i}^{\dagger}\rho E_{i},

(29)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum E_{i}E_{i}^{\dagger}=I.

(30)

The presence of noise in gates was modeled as a combination of an ideal unitary transformations
\displaystyle U
of the gates followed by a noise channel
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{noise}}
, such that:

\mathcal{U}(\rho)=\Lambda_{\text{noise}}(U\rho U^{\dagger}).

(31)

In the following, we describe the noise processes considered in our classical simulations, focusing on two-qubit operations, with the corresponding subspace
\displaystyle A
, which constitute the dominant source of errors on most quantum hardware platforms:

•

depolarizing
\displaystyle 2
-qubit channel:

\Lambda_{D}^{A}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+\frac{p}{4}\mathrm{Tr}_{A}(\rho)\otimes I_{A},

(32)

with
\displaystyle A
being the two-qubit subspace.

•

correlated
\displaystyle 2
-qubit bit-flip channel:

\quad\quad\quad\Lambda_{X}^{A}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+p(X\otimes X)_{A}\rho(X\otimes X)_{A}.

(33)

The
\displaystyle Y
and
\displaystyle Z
2-qubit error channels are defined analogously.

Noise model for superconducting devices
. To partially assess the feasibility of applying VQEs on current superconducting quantum hardware, we employed a phenomenological noise model based on calibration data. The parameters were obtained as averages over the 156-qubit IBM device
ibm_kingston

IBM Quantum Platform 2025
. The native gate set for these qubits comprises
\displaystyle R_{zz}
,
\displaystyle R_{x}
, and
\displaystyle R_{z}
gates, and all circuits were transpiled to this basis using Qiskit’s
transpile
function.
The noise model consists of transverse (thermal) relaxation errors parameterized by the measured
\displaystyle T_{1}
and
\displaystyle T_{2}
times with zero population of the excited states, represented using Qiskit’s thermal relaxation channel (which consistently incorporates both amplitude and phase damping)
Naghiloo 2019
. In addition, depolarizing noise channels were included to match the reported single- and two-qubit gate infidelities from IBM benchmarking. The mapping from average gate infidelity to the depolarizing probability and details about transverse relaxation channel are summarized in Appendix
B
. For our superconducting-device simulations, the noise parameters are set to the mean across all qubits in the calibration data:

\quad\quad\quad\begin{aligned} &T_{1}=264\>\mu\text{s},\\

To explore different noise regimes, these parameters were scaled by a noise multiplier
\displaystyle\lambda

To quantify the sensitivity of different circuit constructions to noise, we introduce the
noise susceptibility parameter

\displaystyle\chi
, defined as the derivative of the estimated energy with respect to the erroneous gate infidelity
\displaystyle\varepsilon
evaluated at the ideal (noise-free) limit:

\chi=\left.\frac{dE}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0},

(36)

where
\displaystyle E
denotes the expectation value of the Hamiltonian. This parameter captures the leading-order degradation of circuit performance under weak noise and enables device-independent comparison of different implementations. A lower value of
\displaystyle\chi
implies that the circuit’s energy estimate is less sensitive to gate infidelity, making the ansatz more robust under the noise. All circuits were constructed to achieve chemical accuracy in the chosen basis, and all
\displaystyle\chi

VIII
Conclusion

We introduced
Majorana Swap Networks
(MSNs) — an architecture based on MSWAP gates that permute Majorana operators. This approach enables a substantial reduction in parametrized circuit depth and two-qubit gate count when compiling chemically motivated variational ansätze.

For the UCCGSD framework applied to the platforms with all-to-all connectivity, specifically, the ionic platform, we developed a cyclic-permutation strategy that localizes each double-excitation operator utilizing only
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
auxiliary swaps, which is nearly optimal when compared to previous
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
techniques. For the k-UpCCGSD method with
\displaystyle 2\times N
connectivity, we introduced a more streamlined swap network and incorporated excitation operators directly into the swap layers, further minimizing the circuit parameters. In both scenarios, MSN circuits consistently surpass existing methods in terms of compactness and
\displaystyle\mathrm{CX}
counts, achieving reductions of up to
\displaystyle 55
% in depth and
\displaystyle 40
% in two-qubit operations when compared to FSN-based circuits.

Numerical modeling clearly demonstrated that these structural advantages directly translate into improved noise resilience. In particular, for the superconducting and trapped-ion qubit platforms, MSN circuits systematically exhibited lower energy susceptibility
\displaystyle\chi
than traditional approaches. Moreover, the "short" excitation decomposition provided a more balanced robustness across different error channels compared to Yordanov’s decomposition. This indicates that robustness is influenced not only by the total number of gates but also by the selection of excitation decomposition.

Our findings underscore the potential of MSN as a fundamental component for deploying variational algorithms on NISQ devices. Looking ahead, combining MSNs with adaptive ansätze
Grimsley et al. 2019
;
Tang et al. 2021
, symmetry-tapering, and compiler-level selection among decomposition variants should further reduce error sensitivity while preserving locality — extending the reach of VQE on present-day hardware. Such directions could reduce error accumulation even further and bring practical quantum-chemical simulations closer to realization in the absence of error correction and limited connectivity of current devices.

Appendix B
Fidelity and parameters of noise channels

B.1
Depolarization

On a
\displaystyle d
-dimensional Hilbert space, the depolarizing channel with parameter
\displaystyle p\in[0,1]
is

\Lambda_{\text{d}}(\rho)=(1-p)\,\rho+p\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{d}}{d}.

(54)

The
average gate fidelity
of
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{d}}
to the identity is

F_{\mathrm{avg}}(\Lambda_{\text{d}},\mathbb{I})=\int\!\!d\psi\;\langle\psi|\Lambda_{\text{d}}(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)|\psi\rangle=(1-p)+p\,\frac{1}{d}=1-p\,\frac{d-1}{d}.

(55)

Hence the
average gate infidelity

\displaystyle\varepsilon:=1-F_{\mathrm{avg}}
and
\displaystyle p
are related by

\varepsilon=p\,\frac{d-1}{d}

(56)

Two common special cases are:

\displaystyle\displaystyle d=2\ \text{(1q):}\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{avg}}=1-\tfrac{p}{2},\qquad p=2\varepsilon,

(57)

\displaystyle\displaystyle d=4\ \text{(2q):}\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{avg}}=1-\tfrac{3p}{4},\qquad p=\tfrac{4}{3}\varepsilon.

(58)

B.2
Transverse relaxation

The reduced dynamics over a gate of duration
\displaystyle\tau
are well described on the Bloch sphere by an affine map
Krantz et al. 2019

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e^{-\tau/T_{2}}\,x,

(59)

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e^{-\tau/T_{2}}\,y,

\displaystyle z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=1+\bigl(z-1\bigr)\,e^{-\tau/T_{1}},

where
\displaystyle T_{1}
is the energy-relaxation time and
\displaystyle T_{2}
the transverse (coherence) time. The times satisfy the physical constraint

\frac{1}{T_{2}}=\frac{1}{2T_{1}}+\frac{1}{T_{\varphi}},\qquad T_{2}\leq 2T_{1},

(60)

with
\displaystyle T_{\varphi}
the
pure dephasing
time.

A convenient Kraus representation that realizes Eq. (
59
) (for
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{th}}=0
) is the composition of an
amplitude-damping
channel followed by a
pure-dephasing
channel which supplements the phase decay to match the measured
\displaystyle T_{2}
:

\Lambda_{\text{th}}(\tau)\;=\;\underbrace{\Lambda_{\text{P}}\!\bigl(\eta\bigr)}_{\text{pure dephasing}}\;\circ\;\underbrace{\Lambda_{\text{A}}\!\bigl(\gamma,p_{\mathrm{th}}\bigr)}_{\text{amplitude damping}},

(61)

with

\gamma=1-e^{-\tau/T_{1}},\qquad\eta=e^{-\tau/T_{\varphi}},\qquad\frac{1}{T_{\varphi}}=\frac{1}{T_{2}}-\frac{1}{2T_{1}}.

(62)

One convenient choice of Kraus operators for the amplitude-damping channel is

\displaystyle E_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{1-\gamma}&0\\[2.0pt]
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad E_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\[2.0pt]
\sqrt{\gamma}&0\end{pmatrix},

(63)

and a two-element Kraus representation for pure dephasing is, for example,

F_{0}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1+\eta}{2}}\,\mathbb{I},\qquad F_{1}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1-\eta}{2}}\,\sigma_{z}.

(64)

To prove the decomposition (
61
), note that the zero-temperature amplitude-damping channel with
\displaystyle\gamma=1-e^{-\tau/T_{1}}
acts on the Bloch vector as

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\gamma}\,x\;=\;e^{-\tau/(2T_{1})}\,x,

(65)

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\gamma}\,y\;=\;e^{-\tau/(2T_{1})}\,y,

\displaystyle z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\,z+\gamma\;=\;1+(z-1)\,e^{-\tau/T_{1}},

i.e., populations relax exponentially toward the ground state with time constant
\displaystyle T_{1}
while coherences are reduced by a factor
\displaystyle e^{-\tau/(2T_{1})}
.

The pure-dephasing (phase-damping) channel leaves populations unchanged and further attenuates coherences:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e^{-\tau/T_{\varphi}}\,x,

(66)

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=e^{-\tau/T_{\varphi}}\,y,

\displaystyle z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=z.

Combining these two effects (amplitude damping followed by pure dephasing) yields the Bloch map in Eq. (
59
) and the relation (
60
) between
\displaystyle T_{1}
,
\displaystyle T_{2}
, and
\displaystyle T_{\varphi}
.
