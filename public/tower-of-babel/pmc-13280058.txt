Atypical case of renovascular hypertension associated with median arcuate ligament syndrome in a paediatric patient
Authors: Ewelina Wojciechowska, Hanna Garnier, Łukasz Znaniecki, Iga Załuska-Leśniewska, Katarzyna Sinacka, Dominik Świętoń, Piotr Czauderna, Andrzej Gołębiewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedian Arcuate Ligament Syndrome (MALS) typically causes gastrointestinal symptoms due to celiac artery compression. Rarely, extrinsic compression of the renal artery may lead to renovascular hypertension. We report a 15-year-old boy with progressive renal asymmetry and poorly controlled hypertension without abdominal symptoms. Imaging demonstrated compression of the celiac trunk and left renal artery by the median arcuate ligament and diaphragmatic crura. Open surgical decompression restored renal artery flow and normalized blood pressure. This case highlights an unusual pediatric presentation of MALS and emphasizes that early recognition and timely surgical intervention can prevent irreversible renal vascular damage.Keywords: Hypertension, Renovascular, Celiac artery compression syndrome, Child

Introduction

Median Arcuate Ligament Syndrome (MALS) is a rare condition with uncertain true prevalence; anatomical celiac artery compression is reported in 3.4–24% of the general population.1,2 Typical symptoms include abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, and weight loss due to chronic ischemia from celiac trunk or adjacent vessel compression by fibrous median arcuate ligament bands. In children, diagnosis is mainly by exclusion of other causes of chronic abdominal pain, most often functional, psychosomatic, or organic.3 Although celiac artery compression is characteristic of MALS, rare cases describe diaphragmatic crura fibers compressing the renal arteries, potentially causing renovascular hypertension.

Case presentation

A 15-year-old boy was referred to the Pediatric Nephrology and Hypertension Clinic due to progressive renal asymmetry, which had worsened over the past two years. Elevated blood pressure had first been noted during school health check-ups. Despite ongoing antihypertensive therapy, which required increase in Amlodipine dose from 5 mg to 10 mg, 24-h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) still revealed an average blood pressure of 140/80 mmHg, indicating inadequate control. The patient presented no symptoms other than hypertension. Over the course of two years, renal asymmetry progressed markedly. Two years before the operation, ultrasound showed the right kidney measured 113 × 41 × 45 mm and the left 100 × 45 × 41 mm, corresponding to a 12% disparity in renal volume. In the preoperative ultrasound, the right kidney had grown to 123 × 58 × 49 mm, while the left had shrunk to 94 × 37 × 35 mm, resulting in an almost 200% difference in renal volume. The renal parenchyma demonstrated features of tubulopathy, and Doppler ultrasound of the renal arteries revealed marked asymmetry, with the left renal artery showing a peak systolic velocity of 50 cm/s and the right renal artery 160 cm/s [Fig. 1]. Renal function parameters and aldosterone levels were within normal limits, whereas plasma renin concentration was at the upper limit during Pediatric Test Reference Values 2009 (Renina 44.88uIU/ml). No proteinuria or albuminuria was detected.

Doppler ultrasound showing reduced flow velocity in the left renal artery (L) compared with the right renal artery (R).

The patient was diagnosed with hereditary spherocytosis and had been under hematology follow-up, undergoing laparoscopic splenectomy at 6 years of age. He remained stable, with follow-up imaging showing no residual splenic tissue or abdominal abnormalities, normal hematologic parameters and no evidence of immunodeficiency.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) was performed revealing compression of the proximal celiac trunk by the median arcuate ligament (MAL), with a long-segment stenosis resulting in 70% luminal narrowing at the most constricted point (3 mm). Imaging demonstrated a common level of origin of the superior mesenteric artery and both renal arteries from the abdominal aorta, with the superior mesenteric artery arising at an atypically low position relative to established anatomical landmarks. Additionally, the proximal segment of the left renal artery was compressed over a length of 14 mm by both the MAL and the left crus of the diaphragm. These findings were consistent with radiologic features of MALS involving both the celiac trunk and the left renal artery [Fig. 2].

(Y) Volume Rendering technique (VRT) reconstruction - level of origin of the visceral vessels. Arrow A (yellow) – stenosis of the celiac trunk. Arrow B (red) – stenosis and deformation of the proximal segment of the left renal artery.

Due to inadequately controlled hypertension, the patient underwent elective laparotomy. The left renal artery was meticulously dissected, and the median arcuate ligament compressing its proximal segment was completely released. The celiac plexus and all associated fibrous bands up to the abdominal aorta were fully transected [Fig. 3].

Intraoperative view illustrating anatomical relationships: left adrenal vein (A), left renal artery (B) and left renal vein (C).

Postoperatively, blood pressure was closely monitored, and at one month remained adequate on low-dose amlodipine (5 mg daily). At three months, Doppler ultrasound showed persistent left renal artery flow abnormalities (peak systolic velocity 40 cm/s) despite well-controlled blood pressure. At six and twelve months, blood pressure remained within pediatric targets with no progression of renal artery changes. The patient continues regular nephrology follow-up with planned Doppler monitoring.

Discussion

The median arcuate ligament syndrome has remained a subject of debate and controversy for over a century. The anatomical mechanism of celiac artery compression was first described by Lipshutz in 1917, while subsequent reports by Harjola and Dunbar in the 1960s highlighted its association with gastrointestinal symptoms (abdominal pain, nausea, and weight loss), which constitute the most common clinical manifestations of the syndrome.4 There is a limited correlation between anatomical findings and clinical symptomatology, as clinically manifested MALS is estimated to occur in fewer than 1% of cases.2 Diagnostic challenges in the pediatric population are further compounded by observations from Stiles-Shields (2021), who reported that approximately half of children undergoing surgical intervention for MALS met criteria for at least one psychiatric disorder, most commonly depression or anxiety.5 These comorbidities may intensify the subjective perception of pain and hinder accurate evaluation of clinical symptoms, thereby affecting disease interpretation and treatment outcomes. Their presence is likely driven by the persistent nature of pain, diagnostic complexity, and the considerable psychosocial burden associated with the syndrome. Although extremely rare, isolated cases have been reported in which hypertrophic fibers of the MAL compress the renal artery, resulting in reduced renal perfusion; this phenomenon may occur in the setting of abnormal anatomical relationships, such as a low-lying median arcuate ligament or a high-origin celiac or renal artery, leading to extrinsic compression of the arterial lumen.6 This may result in secondary renovascular hypertension and renal hypoplasia. In the study by Mirza et al., patients aged 4 to 65 years were included, but no separate analysis of the pediatric subgroup was performed.4 Our patient represents a rare pediatric case of MALS presenting solely with poorly controlled hypertension, without gastrointestinal symptoms or psychiatric comorbidities, highlighting an atypical clinical manifestation that is rarely documented in the literature. In clinical practice, Doppler ultrasonography is typically used as the first-line imaging technique, frequently revealing stenosis or compression of the celiac artery accompanied by elevated flow velocities.7 Computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can then be performed to confirm celiac artery compression, delineate precise anatomical relationships, and support the diagnosis of MALS. From a technical perspective, CTA generally provides superior spatial resolution and more precise visualization of small vascular structures, whereas MRI, although free from ionizing radiation, can be limited by motion artifacts and lower spatial detail.8 Due to anatomical variations, MALS is typically managed surgically using minimally invasive approaches, such as laparoscopic or robot-assisted laparoscopic decompression, or through conventional open surgery. Alternative interventions, including angioplasty or aorto-renal bypass, have been described in adults, but their use in pediatric patients is limited. This is primarily because children generally have smaller renal vessels, the arterial walls have not yet undergone chronic changes, and surgical decompression alone is often sufficient to restore normal blood flow, making complex vascular procedures rarely necessary.7,9 Patients who are most likely to benefit from surgical intervention should be carefully selected based on clinical presentation and imaging findings. Indications for surgery primarily include chronic abdominal complaints suggestive of visceral ischemia—such as postprandial pain, weight loss, nausea, or vomiting—confirmed by Doppler ultrasound or CTA/MRI evidence of celiac artery compression, and lack of improvement with conservative management. In a study by Mak et al., children undergoing laparoscopic release demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in quality of life,8 highlighting the efficacy of this approach in a properly selected patient population. Surgical decisions in MALS should be individualized, taking into account symptom severity, the degree of vascular wall changes, and the anticipated effectiveness of the intervention. A prospective study including 46 pediatric patients with symptomatic MALS (median age 16.6 years) reported that approximately 67% of patients experienced noticeable symptom relief following laparoscopic ligament decompression.8 These findings highlight that, although surgical intervention can be effective, clinical response is often variable, and complete resolution of symptoms is not always achieved, likely reflecting the incompletely understood pathophysiology of pain in MALS.3 In our patient, an open laparotomy was chosen due to anticipated anatomical challenges, including adhesions from the prior splenectomy and a high insertion of the left renal artery, which could limit exposure and increase the risk of vascular injury during minimally invasive approaches.8 Following surgical decompression of the median arcuate ligament, a marked improvement in blood pressure control was observed. Initially, the patient did not require antihypertensive therapy; however, from the third postoperative day, low-dose amlodipine (5 mg) was reintroduced, which effectively maintained blood pressure within the target range. During long-term follow-up, a gradual stabilization of blood pressure values was observed, and after 6 months, antihypertensive treatment was completely discontinued while maintaining normotensive measurements; follow-up ultrasonography confirmed stabilization of flow parameters in the left renal artery. Despite the noted clinical improvement, previous follow-up ultrasonographic examinations revealed persistent low flow in the left renal artery, likely resulting from chronic external compression leading to vascular wall changes such as intimal thickening, proliferation of elastic fibers, and adventitial disruption.9 This finding underscores the importance of early diagnosis and intervention to prevent irreversible vascular remodeling.

Conclusion

Median Arcuate Ligament Syndrome in children is rarely diagnosed. Its clinical presentation can be highly variable, which may complicate both diagnosis and treatment planning. Hypertension as the single presenting symptom is very rare. Surgical release of the median arcuate ligament can improve symptoms and hemodynamic parameters, but individual responses vary and require prolonged follow-up for full assessment of intervention efficacy.
