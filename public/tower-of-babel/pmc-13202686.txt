Association between the oral microbiome and clinical indicators of resectable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)
Authors: Vanessa DeClercq, Rowan E. Murphy, Nick Zinck, Morgan G. I. Langille, Alison Wallace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral microbial implications for lung cancer outcomes and association with clinical markers are largely unknown.ObjectiveThis work aimed to characterize the oral microbiome in relation to clinical indicators and identify oral microbial biomarkers associated with lung cancer outcomes in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).DesignThis is an observational prospective study of saliva samples from patients undergoing curative surgery for NSCLC. Taxonomic and composition profiles were generated using full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing (n=64). Differences in bacterial diversity and composition with cancer stage, histological subtype, overall survival (OS), and event-free survival (EFS) were examined.ResultsMost alpha diversity measures were lower with stage III cancer compared to stage I. All other clinical features were not statistically associated with alpha diversity (P>0.05). The relative abundance of Haemophilus and Solobacterium was detected as significantly differentially abundant in participants with stage III NSCLC by at least 2 differentially abundant tools and an FDR-corrected p-value<0.1.ConclusionThis study shows a significant association between cancer stage with oral bacterial diversity and the relative abundance of specific genera in relation to OS in participants with resectable NSCLC. This study is limited by sample size and needs to be confirmed in larger studies.

Background

Globally, lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer deaths [1]. In Canada, over 30,000 Canadians are predicted to be diagnosed with lung cancer each year [2,3]. The high rates of lung cancer are particularly concerning because more than 50% of cases are diagnosed at Stage IV, with a 3-year survival rate of only 5% [4]. Unfortunately, even with recent advancements in the treatment of patients with resectable stage IB-IIIA NSCLC, a large portion of those patients develop recurrence or metastases and eventually succumb to the disease 2 years post-surgery [5]. Presently, it is unclear why some people respond better than others, and this is an important area of investigation in the realm of precision medicine. Thus, to maximize treatment response, we need to gain a greater understanding of the factors that influence NSCLC outcomes and develop enhanced techniques and tools capable of effectively categorizing patients at early stages.

The prospect of harnessing the human microbiome as a prognostic instrument to anticipate treatment response in lung cancer holds promise to enhance long-term survival rates. The maintenance of a healthy microbiota may enhance overall immunity, promote postoperative recovery, and improve the response to immunotherapies. Previous research has demonstrated shifts in the gut microbiome with lung cancer [6–8] and established the importance of the gut microbiota in mediating responses to cancer therapies [9–14]. However, the gut microbiota may not be the only human microbiome that plays a role in lung cancer.

Recent research has demonstrated an enrichment of oral-related microbiota in faecal samples of individuals with lung cancer [15]. Microbes in the mouth support human health, both locally and systemically and shifts in the oral microbial community are observed in several health conditions, including lung cancer [16–23]. Hosgood et al. provided early evidence for a link between the oral microbiome and lung cancer [19]. Since then, a meta-analysis has demonstrated that higher observed species or Shannon diversity was associated with a lower risk of lung cancer, however, the evidence between the relative abundance of specific microbiota and lung cancer has been inconsistent [24]. The oral microbiome appears to be related to lung cancer, yet much uncertainty remains about the specific lung microbes that beneficially modulate treatment outcomes in NSCLC patients with resectable disease and those that may be useful biomarkers for predicting treatment response or potentially optimizing cancer outcomes.

Thus, this study aimed to analyze saliva samples from NSCLC to provide an understanding of the relationship between the oral microbiome and clinical features, as well as identify specific microbial biomarkers that can be used to predict lung cancer outcomes in resectable NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is an observational prospective clinical study that includes samples and data from patients undergoing curative resection for NSCLC at the Queen Elizabeth II Health Science Centre (QEII HSC) in Halifax, Nova Scotia, Canada. Ethics approval was granted by the Research Ethics Board at Nova Scotia Health Authority (REB# 1026340), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to data/sample collection. Inclusion criteria: adults over the age of 18 diagnosed with resectable NSCLC (clinical stage IA–IIIA, IASLC 8th edition) and who underwent curative-intent thoracic surgery at the QEII Health Sciences Centre and who provided consent for data and sample collection. Exclusion criteria: patients with locally advanced disease (stage IIIB–IIIC), patients treated with neoadjuvant systemic therapy (including chemo-immunotherapy), and patients who underwent surgery as part of treatment for advanced-stage disease were excluded from the current analysis. Based on these criteria, a total of 70 NSCLC patients who underwent curative resection between October 2022 and March 2025 were included, 65 who were willing/able to provide a saliva sample. The initial sample size estimation was estimated from previous literature available prior to the start of the study [12,19,25]. We estimated that the largest total sample size that was required to detect differences between groups with a statistical significance level of 5% was 55 and 74 participants to achieve a statistical power of 80 and 90%. Basic demographic and clinical information at the time of surgery was examined in relation to oral microbiome diversity and composition as described below. Patients were also followed over time to determine the association between microbial status at the time of surgery and survival outcomes at follow-up. Survival outcomes included overall survival (OS; defined as dead or alive) and event-free survival (EFS) as defined by a cancer event (progression/recurrence/death) or no event.

Biological samples & data capture

Saliva samples were collected preoperatively using a standardized in-hospital protocol. All patients were managed under an institutional ERAS (enhanced recovery after surgery) pathway. Approximately 2 h before sample collection, patients were permitted a sip of clear fluids, and approximately 1 h prior to collection, patients received oral acetaminophen with a sip of sterile water. Immediately prior to entry into the operating room, the operating surgeon collected unstimulated whole saliva. Patients were instructed to passively expectorate saliva into a sterile collection container (passive drool technique). The samples were then processed and stored at −80 °C until microbial analysis. Pre-hospital oral hygiene practices (including the timing of tooth brushing) were not formally recorded; however, in-hospital perioperative conditions and timing relative to surgery were standardized across all participants.

Demographic, clinical and pathological data were captured from electronic medical records. The variables included participants' sex, age, smoking status (never, former, current), antibiotic use ( ≤ 3 and ≤ 6 months prior to surgery), pathological stage, NSCLC subtype (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, other), PD-L1 status (<1%, 1–49% and ≥ 50% PD-L1 expression) and treatment/therapy (including neoadjuvant and/or adjuvant chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy ± radiation), survival data (OS and EFS) and tumour genetics. Tumour genetics included mutations in the following genes: anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), ALK fusion, B-rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (BRAF), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (ERBB2), fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1), KIT proto-oncogene, receptor tyrosine kinase (KIT), cell-cycle regulator cyclin D1 (CCND1), Kirsten rat sarcoma virus (KRAS), cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4), myelocytoma, (MYC), mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (MET), phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PIK3CA) and rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma-1 (RAF1).

Microbiome sequencing and bioinformatics

Samples were profiled using 16S rRNA gene sequencing at the Integrated Microbiome Resource (IMR) at Dalhousie University. DNA was extracted from biological samples using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (QIAGEN). DNA from saliva samples underwent PCR amplification for the full-length 16S rRNA gene using 27F (AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG) and 1492R (RGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT) primer set, then sequenced on a PacBio Sequel 2 or Vega. Sequencing data was processed using QIIME2 (version 2024.1) [26] as outlined in the Microbiome Helper Standard Operating Procedure v2 [27] to obtain estimates of alpha-diversity (e.g. richness, evenness, Shannon diversity, and Faith's phylogenetic diversity), beta-diversity (e.g. unweighted and weighted UniFrac distance, Bray‒Curtis dissimilarity and Jaccard distance) and the relative abundance of individual ASVs. During processing, data were denoised using DADA2 [28], and ASVs that were classified as mitochondria/chloroplasts, had an abundance of less than 0.1% of the mean sample depth, or were unclassified at the domain level were removed from further analyses. Taxonomy was assigned to the remaining ASVs using a scikit-learn classifier [29] trained on the Human Oral Microbiome Database (eHOMD; version 15.22) [30] and samples with <2000 reads were removed.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.0.0 and plotted with ggplot2. For demographic and clinical data, age is presented as the mean and range and categorical variables are presented as frequency (counts) and percentage (%). Chi-square analyses were used to determine significant associations between sex and categorical variables. For categorical data (demographic/clinical), alpha diversity metrics (observed ASVs, Pielou evenness, Shannon diversity and Faith's PD) on rarefied data at the ASV level were compared by the Kruskal‒Wallis test and, if necessary, followed by pairwise comparisons with Dunn's tests and Bonferroni. Pearson correlation was used to assess the relationship between alpha diversity and age, with correlation coefficients and p-values calculated for each metric. An additional statistical model, ordinal logistic regression, was applied to the alpha diversity data to further investigate the relationship with cancer stage and potential confounding variables (e.g. smoking status and antibiotic use). Beta diversity metrics (Bray‒Curtis dissimilarity, Jaccard distance and both weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances) were used to assess beta diversity at the ASV level (on rarefied data) using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA; the adonis2 function with version 2.6–6.1 of the vegan package [31]) with 1000 permutations. All diversity plots were generated using ggplot2 and an alpha value of 0.05 was chosen for determining significance.

Differential abundance analysis of bacterial taxa at the genus level was conducted using four differential abundance analysis tools: Corncob v0.4.1 was run using ‘differentialTest’ function with the Wald test [32]; ALDEx2 v1.36.0 was run using the ‘aldex.glm’ and ‘aldex.clr’ functions with a total of 128 Monte Carlo samplings [33]; MaAsLin3 v1.1.2 was run using the function ‘maaslin3’ with default parameters [34,35]; ANCOM-II abundance tables were first processed using the function ‘feature_table_pre_process’, then the main ‘ANCOM’ function, with a significance percentage cutoff of 60% for the w statistic [36]. All survival outcomes included follow-up time as a covariate in the DA models. The resulting p-values from the above DA tests were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini and Hochberg algorithm, and genera were considered statistically significant if the false discovery rate adjusted p-value was <0.1.

Taxa associated with baseline clinical variables that were identified by two or more of the differential abundance tools and had an unadjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 or FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.1 were considered noteworthy and further plotted with ggplot2 to visualize the relationships. A slightly more relaxed FDR threshold was used to allow for the detection of potentially novel biomarkers while acknowledging that further confirmatory studies will be needed.

A survival analysis was conducted to assess the association between alpha diversity or specific genera on survival outcomes (overall survival and EFS). The Cox proportional hazards model was used to quantify the effects of microbial diversity or specific genera on survival outcomes using the survival R package [37]. For the analysis, time was defined as the months between the date of surgery and the date of an event (recurrence/progression/death), and the last follow-up date was used to account for censored participants in the analysis. Both unadjusted and adjusted models were run; covariates included age, sex, smoking status, stage, antibiotic use and treatment. Forrest plots were used to visualize the significant (p-value ≤ 0.05) results of the Cox proportional hazard models. Specific genera that were identified by both the Cox proportional hazards models and the differential abundance analysis (p ≤ 0.05) were further assessed by dividing participants into presence or absence groups, then Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed to visualize the microbial features associated with survival probabilities using the survminer R package [38].

Results

Characteristics of participants with NSCLC

In total, 65 patients with resectable NSCLC provided saliva samples for microbiome analysis. After processing the data on sequence quality and potential contamination, 64 samples remained for subsequent analysis. Before assessing the relationship between the oral microbiome and lung cancer outcomes, the clinical information for the 64 participants was summarized (Table 1). There was a greater proportion of females than males, with an overall mean age of 68 years and most were former smokers. Overall, the majority of participants had stage I adenocarcinoma, and nearly half of the participants had <1% PD-L1 expression. The median follow-up time was 12.5 months, and the mortality rate was9% during follow-up, with 17% of participants experiencing a negative event (progression/recurrence/mortality).

Oral microbiome diversity

At the ASV level, multiple measures of alpha diversity (observed ASVs, Shannon diversity and Faith's PD) were significantly lower in participants with stage III NSCLC compared to those with stages I and II, but there was no difference in evenness (Figure 1 and Table 2). The relationship between cancer stage and Faith's PD and Shannon diversity remains significant in the regression models when adjusting for potential confounding variables such as smoking status and antibiotic use (observed ASVs: OR 0.993 (0.986–1.000), p = 0.084; Faiths PD: OR 0.737 (0.557–0.951), p = –0.024; Shannon OR 0.536 (0.288–0.945), p = 0.035; Pielou's evenness OR 0.002 (8.3–7–1.932), p = 0.082). None of the other demographic or clinical variables were associated with alpha diversity (Figure 2 and Table 2).

Differentially abundant genera

The relative abundance for each sample at the genus level is shown in Figure 4, with the top 10 genera being Streptococcus, Prevotella, Veillonella, Rothia, Haemophilus, Neisseria, Gemella, Granulicatella, Porphyromonas and Schaalia. The distribution of oral bacteria in the current study was similar to other studies [39–42]. Differential abundance tests at the genus level were carried out using four tools (MaAsLin3, ANCOM-II, ALDEx2 and corncob) and considered to be significantly differentially abundant (DA) if ≥ 2 tests had uncorrected p-values of <0.05 and at least 1 test with an FDR-corrected q < 0.1. Based on these criteria, only Haemophilus and Solobacterium were significantly DA with the cancer stage; Solobacterium was lower with stage III cancer compared to stage I, whereas Haemophilus was higher in those with stage III (Figure 5). With overall survival, the genera Haemophilus and Lancefieldella had an uncorrected p-value < 0.05 by multiple DA tools, and FDR q < 0.1, with at least 1 tool (Table 3).

Survival analysis

Hazards models and survival curves were used to further explore oral microbial features associated with survival outcomes. Cox proportional hazard models were used to quantify the effects of alpha diversity and specific general on OS and EFS. None of the alpha diversity measures at the time of surgery were significantly associated with OS or EFS (p > 0.05). On the other hand, 11 and 9 genera significantly increased the risk of death (OS) and a negative outcome (EFS), respectively, in the unadjusted model. Several genera were associated with both OS and EFS, including Megasphaera, Lancefieldella, Dialister, Mycoplasma and genera of the Peptostreptococcaceae family, the Bacteroidetes phylum, and the Bacilli and Clostridia classes (Figure 6A and B). None of the genera remained significant after adjusting for covariates (p > 0.05). Finally, Kaplan‒Meier survival curves were used to visualize the effects of the presence or absence of select microbial features on overall survival (Figure 6C‒F).

Discussion

This study adds to the understanding of the role of the oral microbiome in pathologic stage I‒III NSCLC. The findings show differences in microbial diversity among cancer stages as well as associations with specific microbial features. Findings showed that overall microbial diversity did not affect survival outcomes after resection, but the relative abundance of select taxa were associated with overall survival. The results from this study suggest a minimal influence of microbial diversity on NSCLC outcomes but highlight select bacterial taxa worthy of further investigation to evaluate the robustness and generalizability of oral microbial biomarkers.

The gut microbiome has been the most well-studied, with shifts in gut microbial diversity and changes in specific microbes observed in individuals with lung cancer vs those without [43], and in both treated [6,7] and treatment-naïve lung cancer patients [8]. Previous research has established the importance of the gut microbiota in mediating tumour responses to cancer therapies [10,12,44]. For example, Dora et al. used metagenomic sequencing to demonstrate differences in the gut microbial community composition and the abundance of specific taxa between long (>6 months) vs. short (≤6 months) progression-free survival in advanced-stage NSCLC patients treated with anti-PD1 immune checkpoint inhibitors [45]. Interestingly, this study showed that several Streptococcus species, including oral S. salivarius, were associated with short progression-free survival [45]. This study, along with previous works suggest possible oral-gut microbial transmission and interactions in the development and progression of cancers [46,47]. Although these findings about the gut microbiome are exciting, this is not the only human microbiome that has gained attention with respect to lung cancer over the past several years.

Early work by Nejman et al. showed that microbes may reside in many tumours, with each type having a unique microbiome profile [48]. More recently, differences in the microbial diversity and composition of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) have been documented between healthy controls and those with late-stage NSCLC, as well as between histological subtypes of NSCLC [49–51]. While microbial biomarkers may be present in tumours and BALF, these sample types require invasive techniques to acquire samples. In addition, we found that initial testing of tumour samples taken during this study either did not sequence successfully or provided consistent microbial profiles (data not shown). On the other hand, the collection of saliva samples is a quick, easy and non-invasive method with reliable microbiome profiles. Furthermore, there is an overlap of the lung microbiota (BALF and tumour) with bacterial communities from the oral cavity [41,52–54], suggesting that there may be a translocation of microbes from the mouth to the lungs.

Hosgood et al. provided early evidence for a link between the oral microbiome and lung cancer [19]. This work was followed by several case‒control studies comparing the oral microbiome of patients with lung cancer with that of healthy individuals; with several studies showing that some alpha diversity measures to be lower in individuals with lung cancer [15,25,55–57], but not all studies [58–60]. Some case‒control studies have also reported an association with beta diversity [15,25], suggesting a significant difference in community composition between healthy individuals and those with lung cancer. However, differential abundance results among case‒control studies are inconsistent [15,24]. Inconsistencies in the current literature may be due to heterogeneity in bioinformatic methods, differences in study design, or a lack of consideration for lung cancer stage or other known risk factors. For example, multiple alpha diversity measures were lower in individuals with stage III NSCLC compared to those with stage I in the current study, yet the cancer stage was not always reported in previous works.

Prospective studies on individuals who had no cancer at baseline showed that a greater alpha diversity and differential abundance of certain taxa (e.g. bacteria belonging to the Bacilli class) were associated with a higher risk of lung cancer [56,61]. Furthermore, a recent study profiled the salivary microbiome of 20 advanced-stage NSCLC patients with response to immunotherapy and reported higher levels of multiple species of Neisseria and Actinomyces in those who had complete/partial remission or stability of disease compared to those with disease progression [39]. But prospective studies that assess the relationship between the salivary microbiome and the treatment efficacy or outcomes of individuals with NSCLC, particularly in those with resectable disease, are lacking. The current study provides insights into the role of the oral microbiome of 64 participants with resectable NSCLC who were followed for up to a year post resection, showing specific genera, such as Lancefieldella, Haemophilus and Megasphaera, that might be worth following up in larger, more diverse populations. Patnaik et al. also examined the salivary microbiome of resected NSCLC patients for disease recurrence after resection and showed differences in Delftia and Bifidobacterium in individuals with recurrence compared to those without recurrence [54]. Unlike the current study, this study focused on stage I NSCLC only, and recurrence was assessed on average 2 years after resection. Interestingly, Patnaik et al. study also examined multiple sample types and noted 19 genera that were differentially abundant in BALF samples between recurrence and no-recurrence cases, as well as identified a recurrence-associated BALF microbiome signature that was associated with a shorter recurrence-free survival [54]. Additional studies that directly compare multiple sample types would be useful in determining the best body microbiome for predicting lung cancer response and outcomes.

BALF is not the only body site that has common taxa with saliva. Taxa that may translocate from the oral cavity to the gastrointestinal tract [62–64]. By studying both saliva and faecal samples, Shoji et al. demonstrated that the microbial community composition is distinct between healthy controls and participants with lung cancer in both sample types [15]. Furthermore, this study found six oral-related microbiotas in stool samples, which were enriched in participants with lung cancer compared to healthy controls [15]. These six oral microbiota comprised several of the most abundant genera in the current study, including Streptococcus, Prevotella, Veillonella, Rothia, and Granulicatella. Though none of these genera were associated with cancer stage or outcomes in the current study.

The current study observed a significant association between oral Solobacterium and cancer stage. Solobacterium has one known species (S. moorei) and has previously been shown to be predictive of pulmonary nodules [65] and associated with colorectal cancer [66]. Moreover, the levels of S. moorei in the stool were higher in patients with stage III/IV CRC compared to those with stage I/II CRC [66]. Whereas in the current study, the relative abundance of Solobacterium was lowest in those with stage III (Figure 5). Additionally, mice treated with S. moorei displayed increased tumour size and in culture, S. moorei induces cell proliferation and impedes apoptosis [66]. In the current study, the genera Lancefieldella and Haemophilus were detected as differentially abundant by at least 2 DA tools in participants who were alive compared to those who died. There is some literature to indicate an association between colorectal cancer and species of Haemophilus [67], and past studies in animal models suggest a link between Haemophilus and tumourgenesis in models of airway inflammation [68,69]. Interestingly, recent research in a large human study demonstrated the association between the abundance of certain bacterial taxa and an elevated risk of lung cancer, including several species of Haemophilus and Lancefieldella [70]. In the current study, the genera Lancefieldella and Megasphaera were predictive of both overall and event-free survival. Megasphaera is an anaerobic bacteria that has previously been shown to be increased in the BALF of patients with lung cancer [71]. These studies provide insight into the relationship between these bacteria and lung cancer risk, but the mechanistic link between these bacteria and cancer progression or recurrence remains to be determined.

Unlike most previous lung cancer microbiome research that has focused on advanced/unresectable disease, which only has a 10% survival rate, this project is unique in that it focuses on the role of the microbiome in resectable lung cancer patients, a critical stage associated with better treatment outcomes and longer survival. To our knowledge, this is also the largest oral microbiome study to date to examine survival outcomes after resection and examine the association with clinical features at the time of surgery. However, the current study does have some limitations. Previous research has shown that oral health is associated with microbial diversity and composition [72–74], but oral health information was not collected in the current study. Another limitation is the generalizability of our data due to the small sample size with lack of ethnic or cultural diversity. The participants were recruited from a region of the country that has a lower visible minority population, more individuals who identify as English, Irish or Scottish and a much lower population of immigrants compared to Canada overall [75]. Finally, although this was the largest oral microbiome study to examine oncological clinical measures, the sample size was not large enough to conduct sub-analyses (e.g. stratified by sex, frailty and additional health conditions) or apply more advanced computational approaches such as machine learning to investigate the clustering of microbiome profiles. Although the sample size limits the robustness and generalizability of these findings, the results provide initial data to guide future large-scale studies.

Conclusions

The findings of this study show that oral microbial alpha diversity and specific taxa are differentially abundant with NSCLC stage pre-resection. The abundance of specific oral bacteria pre-resection may be associated with the survival outcomes of NSCLC patients. But the generalizability of these findings is limited due to the small sample size, thus future studies with larger follow-up sample sizes that include diverse patient groups across different geographic regions are needed to confirm the robustness of microbial biomarkers. The oral microbiome appears to be linked to lung cancer, and the ease of salivary sample collection for studying the association with lung cancer makes these samples attractive targets for disease diagnostics or monitoring. However, other microbial body sites, such as the gut and lungs, have also been shown to have predictive capabilities for NSCLC prognosis. Thus, future research should include feasibility assessments and direct comparisons of the diagnostic/prognostic potential of several sample types from the same individuals, and microbial biomarkers will need to be confirmed by multiple microbial detection methods and validated in independent cohorts.
