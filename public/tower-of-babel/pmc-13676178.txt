The Paul Glaucoma Implant versus the Ahmed and Baerveldt Glaucoma Implants: A Systematic Review and Bayesian Network Meta-Analysis
Authors: Mohamed N Elshabrawi, Bara M Hammadeh, Ayodeji Arobo, Nader Elfar, Elsayed S Moubarak, Rami Al-Dwairi, Seren Al Beiruti, Hashem Abu Serhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare the efficacy and safety of the Paul Glaucoma Implant (PGI) with those of the two established glaucoma drainage devices, the Ahmed Glaucoma Valve (AGV) and the Baerveldt Glaucoma Implant (BGI).MethodsPubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane CENTRAL, ClinicalTrials.gov, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar were searched. Eligible studies were randomised or comparative observational studies reporting outcomes in patients who received the PGI alongside a concurrent AGV or BGI comparator. The primary outcomes were intraocular pressure (IOP) reduction, surgical success rate, and reduction in glaucoma medications. A Bayesian network meta-analysis was performed, with treatment ranking summarized by surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA). Quality assessment and evidence certainty was performed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool and GRADE framework respectively.ResultsFive comparative studies (238 patients) were included. Relative to the AGV, the PGI was associated with a mean difference in IOP at 12 months of -3.344 (95% CrI −7.743 to 1.058) mmHg, and relative to the BGI of 4.387 (95% CrI −0.288 to 9.074) mmHg. Corresponding differences in medication number at 12 months were]−0.186 (95% CrI −0.858 to 0.545) and 0.444 (95% CrI −0.358 to 1.272). Credible intervals crossed the null for 29 of the 30 primary contrasts. Ranking probabilities favoured the AGV for IOP reduction from 3 to 12 months (SUCRA 0.885 to 0.976) and the PGI for early medication reduction (SUCRA 0.769 at 1 month), but these rankings were derived from imprecise estimates. Safety estimates were based on small numbers of events and several were unstable. Certainty of evidence was low to very low for most comparisons.ConclusionWithin the limits of a small and predominantly non-randomised evidence base, the PGI achieved intraocular pressure and medication reduction broadly comparable to those of the AGV and BGI over the first postoperative year, with a distinct complication profile. The rankings reported here are hypothesis-generating and should not by themselves determine device selection. Adequately large randomised comparisons with follow-up beyond one year are required.Keywords: glaucoma drainage devices, Ahmed glaucoma valve, Baerveldt glaucoma implant, Paul glaucoma implant, intraocular pressure

Introduction

Glaucoma is the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide. Recent global estimates place the prevalence of open-angle glaucoma among adults aged 40 years and older at 2.8% (95% CI, 2.6 to 3.0) in 2024, corresponding to approximately 80.5 million affected individuals, and project a rise to 3.5% (95% CI, 3.2 to 3.8), or approximately 186.6 million individuals, by 2060, with the steepest increases anticipated in East Asia.1 These figures extend the earlier projection of 64.3 million affected individuals aged 40 to 80 years in 2013 rising to 111.8 million by 2040.2 Despite medical and laser therapy, a substantial proportion of patients ultimately require incisional surgery to reach target intraocular pressure (IOP).

Management follows a stepwise approach. Topical hypotensive medication and laser therapy are the usual first-line options; selective laser trabeculoplasty is now an established alternative to initial medical therapy, and cyclophotocoagulation retains a role in refractory disease and in eyes with limited visual potential. Incisional surgery is undertaken when these measures fail to achieve the target intraocular pressure or when adherence or tolerance to medication is inadequate.

Trabeculectomy remains the reference filtering procedure and continues to be widely performed, offering low target pressures at the cost of a recognised burden of bleb-related complications and the need for close postoperative management.3–5 Glaucoma drainage devices are not a general replacement for trabeculectomy; their established indications include refractory glaucoma, eyes with previous failed filtration surgery, secondary glaucomas such as neovascular, uveitic and post-keratoplasty disease, and eyes with extensive conjunctival scarring. Randomised comparisons of the two approaches have clarified their relative roles rather than establishing the primacy of either.5 In the late 1960s, Molteno introduced the first glaucoma drainage device (GDD) in New Zealand. Since then, several GDDs have been developed, offering comparable success rates with potentially fewer complications.4

Currently, a variety of glaucoma drainage implants are available, including the Ahmed Glaucoma Valve (AGV), the Baerveldt Glaucoma Implant (BGI), and, more recently, the Paul Glaucoma Implant (PGI).3,4 These devices differ in structural design, flow-regulation mechanism, surface area and implantation technique; the AGV incorporates a flow-restricting valve mechanism, whereas the BGI and the PGI are non-valved.6 Unlike trabeculectomy, GDDs provide a controlled, sustained outflow of the aqueous humor.5 Comparative evidence on the two established devices is well developed: the Ahmed Baerveldt Comparison and Ahmed vs Baerveldt studies provide randomised head-to-head data with follow-up to five years, and their findings have been synthesised in recent meta-analyses.7–9 Evidence on the more recently introduced Paul implant is at an earlier stage. The published literature consists largely of single-arm cohorts, and studies incorporating a concurrent Ahmed or Baerveldt comparator remain few, are predominantly retrospective, and are of modest size. Whether the design modifications of the Paul implant translate into outcomes comparable to those of the established devices is therefore not yet established, and it is this specific gap that the present review addresses.

The aim of this review was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the Paul Glaucoma Implant relative to the two established drainage devices, the Ahmed Glaucoma Valve and the Baerveldt Glaucoma Implant, over the first postoperative year, using a Bayesian network meta-analysis anchored on the Paul implant.

Materials and Methods

This systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis was registered with the National Institute for Health Research International Registry of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO ID: CRD420251154843). This study is a secondary analysis of previously published, aggregate, de-identified data. Institutional review board approval and informed consent were therefore not required for the present work. Ethical approval and informed consent for each of the included studies were obtained by the respective investigators and are reported in the original publications.

Search Strategy

We performed a comprehensive literature search from inception to September 2026, in the following databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane CENTRAL, ClinicalTrials.gov, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar, to identify contemporary studies reporting efficacy and safety outcomes among patients of any age with any type of glaucoma who received PGI vs AGV or BGI using the following MeSH keywords: (“Glaucoma”[Mesh] OR “Glaucoma”[tiab]) AND ((“Ahmed glaucoma valve”[Mesh] OR “Ahmed valve”[tiab]) OR (“Baerveldt implant”[Mesh] OR “Baerveldt”[tiab])) AND (“Paul glaucoma implant”[tiab] OR “Paul valve”[tiab]) AND (“Intraocular Pressure”[Mesh] OR “Hypotony”[tiab] OR “Choroidal detachment”[tiab] OR “Efficacy”[tiab] OR “Safety”[tiab]). The search strategy for each database is available in Supplementary Table S1.

The search strategy was deliberately anchored on the Paul Glaucoma Implant, because the review question concerns the performance of this recently introduced device relative to the two established comparators. Studies comparing only the AGV with the BGI were therefore not sought for inclusion, although they were retrieved by the search and are cited and discussed. To confirm that no eligible comparative study had been missed, the search was additionally re-run using a broader strategy anchored only on the Paul implant, without a mandatory Ahmed or Baerveldt term.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

The selection of studies was conducted in line with the recommendations from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) strategy.10 The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Supplementary File 1. After deduplication, records were screened by two independent reviewers [NF and SM] using Rayyan software.11 Any conflicts and discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer [MNS]. Titles and abstracts were reviewed against the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria; by implication, non-comparative studies, letters, commentaries, non-human studies, non-English papers, conference abstracts, case reports, studies with the wrong population or outcomes were all excluded. Following the first round of screening, the full texts were pooled from included studies for a second round of eligibility screening. Two authors [NF and SM] independently performed data extraction and accuracy was verified by a third author [MNS]. The extracted variables included studies characteristics as study ID, country, number of patients, study design and reported patients’ demographics. For binary outcomes, event counts and totals were extracted; for continuous outcomes, means, SDs, and sample sizes.12

Eligibility criteria were pre-specified as follows. Population: patients of any age with glaucoma of any type undergoing primary or repeat glaucoma drainage device surgery. Intervention: the Paul Glaucoma Implant. Comparator: the Ahmed Glaucoma Valve or the Baerveldt Glaucoma Implant, implanted concurrently within the same study. Outcomes: at least one of intraocular pressure, number of antiglaucoma medications, surgical success, or a pre-specified safety outcome, reported at a defined postoperative time point. Study design: randomised controlled trials and comparative observational studies with a concurrent comparator arm.

Studies were excluded if they were single-arm cohorts or case series; if they compared only the Ahmed with the Baerveldt device, since such studies do not inform the index comparison; if they were case reports, letters, commentaries, conference abstracts, non-human studies or non-English publications; or if outcome data could not be extracted. The requirement for a concurrent Paul implant arm reflects the review question, namely whether a recently introduced drainage device performs comparably to the established devices; the Paul implant is accordingly the anchor of the network, and the Ahmed and Baerveldt devices enter as reference comparators.

Quality Assessment

The quality of included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool for RCTs which evaluates five different domains of risk of bias: i) randomization process; ii) deviations from intended interventions; iii) missing outcome data; iv) measurement of the outcome; and v) selection of the reported result.13 Retrospective studies were evaluated independently by two authors [NF and SM] using the Newcastle-Ottawa. The NOS is a quality assessment tool constructed to evaluate the quality of nonrandomized studies, including case–control and cohort studies.14 It contains eight components: representativeness of the exposed cohort, selection of the non-exposed cohort, ascertainment of exposure, demonstration that outcome of interest was not present at start of study, comparability, assessment of outcome, was follow-up long enough for outcomes to occur, and adequacy of follow-up of cohorts. The highest possible score for a study is nine points, wherein studies with five points or more are considered to be of moderate to good quality according to the NOS assessment. Any conflict between the two authors was resolved by the consultation of a third author [MNS].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was efficacy, which was measured in terms of IOP reduction (mmHg), complete and qualified success rates, and reduction in the number of antiglaucoma medications across different time points. Complete success was defined as attainment of the target intraocular pressure without antiglaucoma medication, and qualified success as attainment of the target intraocular pressure with antiglaucoma medication. Because the target pressure thresholds and the criteria for failure were not identical across the included studies, success and failure were extracted as defined and reported in each study. The secondary outcomes included safety outcomes (measured in terms of failure rate, hypotony, reoperation, shallow anterior chamber, tube related complications, hyphema, choroidal effusion, endophthalmitis).

Statistical Analysis

A Bayesian network meta-analysis (NMA) was conducted to evaluate the comparative efficacy and ranking of various GDDs. This approach integrates both direct and indirect evidence across a network of studies, accounting for within- and between-study variability. A hierarchical Bayesian model was implemented using four Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains to ensure robust estimation and convergence. Each model was run for 20,000 iterations, with an initial burn-in (adaptation) phase of 5000 iterations to optimize sampling. No thinning was applied and iterations were kept for each digit. Uninformative priors were specified, including a uniform distribution (0–5) for the heterogeneity parameter. Treatment effects were summarized as odds ratios (ORs) for dichotomous outcomes and mean differences (MDs) for continuous outcomes, each with corresponding 95% credible intervals (CrIs) derived from the posterior distributions. The choice between fixed- and random-effects models was guided by the estimated between-study heterogeneity (τ), with the random-effects model used as the default starting point. Heterogeneity was quantified as the standard deviation of the random effects (τ) from the posterior distribution. All dichotomous outcomes were modelled with a binomial likelihood and a logit link within the arm-based hierarchical framework implemented in gemtc, which accommodates arms with zero events without the addition of a continuity correction; no continuity correction was applied at any point. Comparisons in which no events were observed in either arm of any contributing study provide no information about the treatment effect, and the corresponding posterior is determined largely by the prior; such estimates are reported as not estimable rather than as a point estimate.

Model convergence was assessed visually using trace plots. Treatment rankings were estimated using the Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA), with values ranging from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate superior treatment performance for efficacy outcomes and higher values indicating the worst treatment performance for safety outcomes. Network geometry was visualized using network plots, with nodes representing interventions and edge thickness proportional to the number of trials comparing those interventions. Node size reflected the total sample size receiving each treatment. As only three interventions were compared, and no direct comparisons between (BGI and AGV) were available, no closed loops were formed in the network. Consequently, inconsistency could not be statistically tested, and consistency was assumed under the transitivity assumption. All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.3 using the “gemtc” package.15–17 SUCRA summarises the posterior probability that a treatment ranks well; it does not constitute a test of superiority, it is not accompanied by a measure of uncertainty in the way an effect estimate is, and a treatment may rank highly on the basis of a very imprecise estimate. Rankings were therefore interpreted only in conjunction with the corresponding effect estimates and 95% credible intervals. Because fewer than ten studies contributed to any comparison, formal assessment of small-study effects and publication bias was not undertaken, in line with Cochrane guidance; the funnel plot is provided in the supplementary material for transparency only and is not interpretable.

Certainty of Evidence

The certainty of the evidence was assessed by [NF and SM] using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system. The quality of the evidence was categorized as (high), (moderate), (low), or (very low) certainty.18 Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third author was consulted when necessary (HAS). The assessment was extended to all primary efficacy outcomes and the principal safety outcomes, and the reasons for downgrading, comprising risk of bias, imprecision, indirectness, inconsistency where assessable, and publication bias, which was not assessable, are tabulated for each comparison in Supplementary Table S3.

Results

Search and Screening

As a result of the initial literature search, 118 records were identified from eight databases [PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane CENTRAL, ClinicalTrials.gov, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar]. After removing 59 duplicates, 59 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 36 were excluded for reasons such as unrelated research questions, study design, or population. A total of 23 full-text reports were sought for retrieval; one could not be retrieved. Thus, 22 reports were assessed for eligibility. After full-text screening, 17 reports were excluded (seven without comparators, five irrelevant study design, five missing outcomes). Finally, five studies comprising a total of 238 patients were included in the review. The search and screening process is shown in the PRISMA flowchart (Figure 1).

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for new systematic reviews which included searches of databases and registers only. *Records identified from the eight databases searched (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane CENTRAL, ClinicalTrials.gov, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar). **Records excluded by the two reviewers at title and abstract screening.

Studies Characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. In total, one RCT6 and four retrospective studies were included in the analysis.3,4,19,20 PGI was compared to AGV in three studies4,6,20 while it was compared to BGI in two studies.3,19 The mean age of adult patients was between 47.6 and 64.9 years, and the proportion of males ranged from 38.9% to 74%. The RCT focused on pediatric patients, with a mean age ranging from 7.5 to 8.8 years. The mean preoperative IOP was around 23.7 up to 40 mmHg. The number of IOP lowering medications at baseline was similar, averaging 3–4 agents. The best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), expressed in logMAR, mean values ranged from −1.06 to 1.1. Across the included studies, secondary open-angle glaucoma and primary open-angle glaucoma (OAG) were the most common type of glaucoma. Primary OAG constituted 43% of cases, while secondary OAG accounted for the remaining 57%. The majority of patients had undergone at least one prior glaucoma surgery as trabeculectomy, trabeculotomy, combined trabeculotomy-trabeculectomy, glaucoma drainage devices, bleb-forming or needling procedures, revision and minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) such as XEN stent, canaloplasty, iStent, Ex-PRESS shunt, and PreserFlo.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The quality assessment result varied across the included studies. Four retrospective studies (Oliver-Gutiérrez et al,19 Karapapak et al,20 Berteloot et al3 and Mendoza-Moreira et al)4 demonstrated good methodological standards with scores ranging from 8 to 9 points, indicating an overall low risk of bias. Common strengths across the studies included appropriate representativeness of the cohorts, reliable ascertainment of exposure (implant type), and objective measurement of outcomes. The main limitations were related minor limitation in comparability due to restricted adjustment for baseline confounders and insufficient clarity regarding adequacy of follow-up (Supplementary Table S4). Elhusseiny et al6 randomized clinical trial (RCT) raised some concerns overall due to lack of blinding of participants/surgeons Otherwise, the trial is well-conducted with low risk in other domains (Figure S1).

Outcomes

Intraocular Pressure Reduction (IOP)

The relative efficacy of the devices in reducing IOP varied over the first postoperative year. (Table 1 and Figure 2) At the one-month follow-up, the BGI had the highest probability of ranking first (SUCRA: 0.747, Rank 1), although credible intervals were wide and overlapping. (Figures S2 and S3) From three months onward, the AGV consistently had the highest ranking probability for IOP reduction (3M: SUCRA 0.885; 6M: SUCRA 0.938; 1Y: SUCRA 0.976, all Rank 1). The PGI consistently ranked second (SUCRA range: 0.506–0.729), while the BGI ranked third from three months to one year (SUCRA range: 0.018–0.058, Rank 3) (Figures S4–S9). The corresponding mean differences relative to the PGI at 12 months were −3.344 mmHg (95% CrI −7.743 to 1.058) relative to the AGV and 4.387 mmHg (95% CrI −0.288 to 9.074) relative to the BGI. These rankings are derived from imprecise estimates and do not demonstrate superiority.

Reduction in Medication Use

The results for the reduction in glaucoma medication burden presented a distinct pattern from IOP outcomes. (Figure 3) For medication reduction, the AGV was ranked as the most effective option at the 6-month and 1-year timepoints (6M: SUCRA 0.708, Rank 1; 1Y: SUCRA 0.854, Rank 1). (Figures S10–S13). In contrast, the PGI was most effective in reducing medication use in the early postoperative period (1M: SUCRA 0.769, Rank 1) (Figures S14 and S15), but its relative ranking decreased to second place by one year. The BGI ranked lowest for reducing medication dependence at one month and one year (Rank 3) and achieved an intermediate rank at three months. (Figures S16 and S17). The corresponding mean differences in medication number relative to the PGI at 12 months were −0.186 (95% CrI −0.858 to 0.545) relative to the AGV and 0.444 (95% CrI −0.358 to 1.272) relative to the BGI. Credible intervals were wide and overlapping at every time point, and the rankings should be interpreted alongside these estimates rather than in isolation.

Histogram of medication reduction at 1 month.

Surgical Success Rates

At one year, the rankings for qualified and complete surgical success rates were divergent. For complete success, the BGI was the most effective (SUCRA: 0.848, Rank 1), followed by the PGI (SUCRA: 0.462, Rank 2) and the AGV (SUCRA: 0.190, Rank 3). (Figures S18 and S19). Conversely, for qualified success, the PGI ranked first (SUCRA: 0.890, Rank 1), the BGI ranked second (SUCRA: 0.480, Rank 2), and the AGV ranked third (SUCRA: 0.230, Rank 3) (Figures S20–S21). The detailed analysis of all efficacy outcomes is summarized in Supplementary Appendix 1–4. The corresponding odds ratios relative to the PGI were 1.586 (95% CrI 0.342 to 7.827) relative to the AGV for the AGV and 0.552 (95% CrI 0.185 to 1.647) relative to the BGI for complete success, and 2.170 (95% CrI 0.721 to 6.708) and 1.567 (95% CrI 0.439 to 5.696) respectively for qualified success. The divergence between the two success definitions reflects the different roles of adjunctive medication in each, and the estimates are imprecise.

Safety Outcomes

Device Failure and Major Postoperative Complications

The analysis suggested a possible ordering in the risk of overall treatment failure and major complications requiring reoperation, although every estimate was imprecise Table 2. The AGV demonstrated the most favorable safety profile, ranking first (lowest SUCRA) for both treatment failure (SUCRA: 0.142, Rank 1) and reoperation (SUCRA: 0.017, Rank 1). Conversely, the BGI carried the highest probability of these serious adverse events, ranking worst for both failure (SUCRA: 0.797, Rank 3) and reoperation (SUCRA: 0.978, Rank 3). The PGI exhibited an intermediate risk profile for these outcomes (Failure: SUCRA 0.561, Rank 2; Reoperation: SUCRA 0.506, Rank 2). (Figures S22–S25).

Postoperative Hypotony and Related Anterior Segment Complications

The risk of postoperative hypotony and its sequelae varied notably between devices. The BGI was ranked as the safest option concerning hypotony (SUCRA: 0.069, Rank 1) and shallow anterior chamber (SUCRA: 0.295, Rank 1). The PGI presented the worst safety profile for these specific complications, ranking last for both hypotony (SUCRA: 0.824, Rank 3) and shallow anterior chamber (SUCRA: 0.697, Rank 3). The AGV’s risk for hypotony was intermediate (SUCRA: 0.607, Rank 2). (Figures S26–S29).

Tube-Related and Hemorrhagic Complications

The rankings for tube occlusion and hemorrhagic complications were distinct. The AGV was by far the safest device regarding tube occlusion (SUCRA: 0.002, Rank 1), whereas the BGI carried the highest risk (SUCRA: 0.981, Rank 3). (Figures S30and S31). For hyphema, the BGI was the safest option (SUCRA: 0.068, Rank 1), and the PGI was associated with the highest risk (SUCRA: 0.826, Rank 3). The AGV showed an intermediate risk for hyphema (SUCRA: 0.606, Rank 2). (Figures S32 and S33).

Serious Inflammatory and Posterior Segment Complications

For the most serious sight-threatening complications, the AGV and BGI were joint-safest for endophthalmitis (AGV SUCRA: 0.150, Rank 1; BGI SUCRA: 0.533, Rank 2), but the PGI had the highest risk (SUCRA: 0.818, Rank 3). (Figures S34 and S35). For choroidal effusion, the BGI was safest (SUCRA: 0.135, Rank 1), while the AGV posed the greatest risk (SUCRA: 0.932, Rank 3). (Figures S36 and S37).

All safety outcomes were based on small numbers of events, and several comparisons included arms in which no events occurred. The resulting odds ratios were correspondingly unstable, with very wide credible intervals. Estimates meeting the pre-specified instability criteria are flagged in Tables 3 and 4 and are not interpreted, and estimates whose posterior was determined largely by the prior are reported as not estimable. Event counts per arm for every safety outcome are given in Supplementary Appendices 5–7. The safety network is therefore too sparse to support a reliable hierarchy among the three devices, and the rankings reported above should be regarded as descriptive only.

Transitivity Analysis

Transitivity cannot be assessed statistically in a star network, and the assessment presented here is therefore clinical and methodological. Candidate effect modifiers were compared across the two comparison sets, namely studies comparing the PGI with the AGV and studies comparing the PGI with the BGI, with respect to age and paediatric vs adult population; glaucoma subtype, including secondary and silicone-oil-associated glaucoma; number and type of prior glaucoma procedures; baseline IOP and baseline number of medications; use of adjunctive antifibrotic agents; flow-control technique, including ligature, ripcord and fenestration; study design; duration of follow-up; and year of surgery in relation to the device learning curve. The comparison is presented in Supplementary Table S2. Several imbalances were identified. The only randomised study enrolled paediatric patients and compared the PGI with the AGV, whereas the PGI-versus-BGI evidence is exclusively adult and retrospective; one PGI-versus-AGV cohort was restricted to silicone-oil glaucoma; and flow-control practice necessarily differs between the non-valved comparators and the valved AGV. The difference in flow-regulation mechanism between the valved AGV and the non-valved BGI and PGI is itself a plausible effect modifier, particularly for early hypotony, shallow anterior chamber and early IOP. The transitivity assumption is therefore considered plausible for the PGI-versus-AGV and PGI-versus-BGI comparisons but cannot be regarded as established, and it is materially less secure for the indirect AGV-versus-BGI comparison, which is presented as exploratory. No included study compared the AGV directly with the BGI; the network is a star centred on the PGI, no closed loop is formed, and inconsistency could not therefore be evaluated statistically. Model fit statistics and the combined league tables of all device arms for all outcomes are presented in Tables 3 and 4.

Certainty of Evidence

Certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach for all primary efficacy outcomes and the principal safety outcomes. (Table S3) Downgrading decisions reflected risk of bias, since four of the five included studies were non-randomised; imprecision, owing to small samples, few events and wide credible intervals; indirectness, arising from the paediatric randomised trial, the silicone-oil cohort, and the absence of direct evidence for the AGV-versus-BGI comparison; and the inability to assess publication bias. Following reassessment, certainty was low for most comparisons and moderate for the indirect AGV-versus-BGI comparison of IOP. . The indirect AGV-versus-BGI estimates were downgraded for indirectness and should not be preferred to the direct randomised evidence available for that comparison.

Discussion

In this review of five comparative studies, we present a comprehensive evaluation of three commonly used GDDs: the AGV, BGI, and the more recently introduced PGI. The AGV had the highest probability of ranking first for IOP reduction from 3 to 12 months, whereas the BGI achieved the greatest IOP reduction at 1 month but declined thereafter. For antiglaucoma medication reduction, the PGI ranked first at 1 month, while the AGV showed the best long-term efficacy. The BGI achieved the highest complete success rate at 12 months, whereas the PGI ranked first in qualified success. In terms of safety, the AGV demonstrated the most favorable profile regarding device failure, reoperation, tube occlusion, and endophthalmitis. The BGI was least associated with hypotony, choroidal effusion, hyphema, and shallow anterior chamber. All contrasts were imprecise, and no comparison in this analysis reached a level of precision that would justify preferring one device over another on the basis of the ranking alone.

These outcomes can be attributed to the differing structural designs, materials, and flow-regulation mechanisms of the devices. Valved implants such as the AGV provide immediate flow restriction, reducing the risk of early hypotony and contributing to their favorable long-term safety profile and consistent IOP control.4 Non-valved implants with larger plate sizes, such as the BGI, may account for the greater IOP reduction observed at 1 month and the higher complete success rate.7 However, because the BGI relies on adjunctive flow-control strategies, including ligatures, fenestrations, and ripcords, it is associated with a higher risk of reoperation.7 The PGI, with its intermediate plate size and smaller internal lumen, demonstrates an intermediate safety profile and achieves a higher qualified success rate.21 The difference in flow-regulation mechanism between the valved AGV and the non-valved BGI and PGI is a clinically relevant distinction, and it is also a plausible effect modifier; this is considered further under Limitations.

The AGV demonstrated greater long-term efficacy in reducing medication use, whereas the PGI achieved superior early postoperative efficacy, an outcome not extensively reported in prior RCTs. Its role as a novel device with modified outflow dynamics may account for these early benefits.21–23 Our findings align with previous studies reporting superior complete success rates with the BGI and greater qualified success rates with the PGI, as demonstrated by Tan et al.8,21,23

The safety profile is a critical factor when selecting between glaucoma drainage devices. The AGV demonstrated the most favorable safety profile, with lower rates of device failure, reoperation, and tube-related complications. This finding is consistent with the results of Budenz et al, who reported a superior long-term safety profile for AGV compared to other devices.9 In contrast, the BGI was associated with higher risks in certain safety parameters, which may be attributable to its larger plate size and the reliance on flow-modulating strategies such as ligatures and fenestrations, both of which can introduce variability and complications. The PGI exhibited an intermediate safety profile but carried a notable risk of hypotony and hyphema, as highlighted in previous analyses.21,24 Interestingly, our findings showed that AGV and BGI were comparable in terms of hypotony, although prior studies have suggested that BGI generally carries only a small but significant risk.7,8 These differences underscore the importance of individual device design in shaping safety outcomes and should be considered when tailoring treatment to patient-specific risk profiles. These findings provide a practical framework for individualized device selection in refractory glaucoma. The AGV may be the preferred option for patients at higher surgical risk or those less able to tolerate reoperation, given its favorable safety profile. The BGI, while associated with a higher rate of tube-related complications, remains the device of choice when achieving medication-free complete success is prioritized, provided patients are closely monitored. The PGI emerges as a promising alternative, offering a balanced profile of efficacy and safety, particularly with its advantages in the early postoperative period. Collectively, this comparative evidence supports an evidence-based, personalized approach to glaucoma surgery, enabling surgeons to tailor device selection to each patient’s clinical context and long-term visual prognosis.

Strengths and Limitations

This network meta-analysis represents one of the few comprehensive comparative evaluations of the three most widely used GDDs (AGV, BGI, and PGI). By integrating data from both comparative cohorts and RCTs, it provides a dynamic ranking of devices over time for both efficacy and safety outcomes. The use of SUCRA-based ranking facilitates clearer interpretation of relative treatment effects, enabling clinicians to make evidence-based decisions across multiple endpoints.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The number of RCTs directly comparing the newer PGI with AGV or BGI remains limited, restricting statistical power and generalizability. Study populations were modest, and follow-up durations were relatively short. One study focused exclusively on pediatric patients, while others excluded this population, introducing variability. Moderate clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included studies further limits the comparability of the pooled estimates. Therefore, applying these findings in clinical practice requires careful consideration of success criteria, IOP reduction, medication use, and safety profiles for each device. The network formed by the eligible studies is a star centred on the PGI. No included study compared the AGV directly with the BGI, so that comparison is estimated indirectly only; these indirect estimates should not be preferred to the direct randomised evidence provided by the Ahmed Baerveldt Comparison and Ahmed vs Baerveldt studies, or to the meta-analyses that have synthesised them,7–9 and readers seeking that specific comparison should be guided by that direct evidence. The review was designed to evaluate the PGI against the established devices, and eligibility therefore required a concurrent PGI arm; studies comparing only the AGV with the BGI were not included. Because fewer than ten studies contributed to any comparison, publication bias could not be assessed and cannot be excluded. Success criteria and target pressure thresholds were not identical across studies, which constrains the comparability of the pooled success estimates. Finally, the difference in flow-regulation mechanism between the valved and non-valved devices may act as an effect modifier, and is a further reason to interpret the indirect AGV-versus-BGI estimates cautiously.

Despite these limitations, the analysis demonstrates important methodological strengths. The Bayesian network meta-analysis approach allows estimation of relative efficacy and safety even in the absence of head-to-head trials. The transitivity assumption was examined by comparing candidate effect modifiers across the two comparison sets, as described above; consistency could not be assessed statistically, because the network contains no closed loop. Overall, this integrated approach provides a clinically meaningful profile of each treatment option, supporting evidence-based selection of glaucoma drainage devices in refractory glaucoma.

Future Recommendations

Further multicenter RCTs are warranted to directly compare all three devices using standardized surgical protocols and long-term follow-up beyond three years. Future studies should incorporate patient-centered outcomes, including quality of life, visual field preservation, and cost-effectiveness. Research is also needed to elucidate which device-specific factors, such as plate size, shape, or composition, most significantly influence long-term success. Additionally, investigations into optimized perioperative strategies, including postoperative care and antifibrotic modulation, may help reduce complications and improve overall outcomes.

In this network meta-analysis of five comparative studies including 238 patients, the Paul Glaucoma Implant achieved reductions in intraocular pressure and in medication burden over the first postoperative year that were broadly comparable to those of the established Ahmed and Baerveldt implants, with a complication profile that differed in pattern rather than in overall magnitude. Credible intervals were wide for all contrasts, the safety network was too sparse to support a reliable hierarchy, and the certainty of evidence was low to very low. These findings support the continued evaluation of the Paul implant as an alternative to established drainage devices but do not establish the superiority of any device, and the rankings presented should be treated as hypothesis-generating. Adequately powered randomised trials with standardised success criteria and follow-up beyond one year are needed before comparative recommendations can be made.
