Curated set of tool compounds to probe PYK2 and FAK signaling in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Pathum M Weerawarna, Cynthia D Jesudason, Karen L Lobb, Emily R Mason, Xiaoping Gu, Shaoyou Chu, Timothy I Richardson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONProline‐rich tyrosine kinase 2 (PYK2) and focal adhesion kinase (FAK) are non‐receptor tyrosine kinases implicated in Alzheimer's disease (AD), but their functional role in microglia remains understudied. Selective pharmacological tools are required for preclinical studies leading to translational therapeutic development.METHODSWe evaluated potent and selective inhibitors described in publications and patents, synthesized or procured representative compounds, and profiled them in kinase assays. In vitro physicochemical and pharmacokinetic (PK) properties were assessed, and functional effects were studied in microglial phagocytosis assays using HMC3 and BV2 microglia cellular models.RESULTSBiochemical profiling confirmed potent and selective inhibition, consistent with reported data, though assay‐dependent differences in apparent selectivity were observed. Most compounds showed favorable physicochemical and PK properties. In HMC3 assay, the PYK2‐selective tool compounds showed that strong stimulation of phagocytosis and parallel cell counts declined at non‐toxic concentrations.DISCUSSIONThis curated set of well‐characterized inhibitors provides a validated toolkit to probe PYK2/FAK biology in AD‐relevant models.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, drug discovery, focal adhesion kinase, FAK, proline‐rich tyrosine kinase 2, PYK2, tool compounds

BACKGROUND

Proline‐rich tyrosine kinase 2 (PYK2), encoded by the gene PTK2B, and focal adhesion kinase (FAK) are closely related non‐receptor tyrosine kinases involved in adhesion, migration, and signal transduction. 1 , 2 , 3 Each shares a conserved domain structure – N‐terminal four‐point‐one, ezrin, radixin, moesin (FERM) , a central kinase, and C‐terminal focal adhesion targeting (FAT) – domains, with long linkers containing regulatory motifs. Despite this structural homology, PYK2 and FAK differ in expression, activation, and function. PYK2 is enriched in neurons and immune cells, including microglia, and is activated by Ca2+ influx and G protein‐coupled receptor signaling. 4 , 5 , 6 FAK is ubiquitously expressed, localized at focal adhesions within cells, and activated by integrin signaling. 3 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10

Dysregulation of PYK2 and FAK has been implicated in cancer, inflammation, and bone pathologies. 11 Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have identified PTK2B as a susceptibility locus for late‐onset Alzheimer's disease (AD). 12 The protein it encodes, PYK2, binds tau in vitro and colocalizes with hyperphosphorylated tau in transgenic mouse models and in human AD brain. 13 In addition, there are two main splice isoforms of PYK2, one with an exon encoding 42 amino acids between two proline‐rich sequences in the C‐terminal region and one without. 14 The variant that lacks this exon is predominantly expressed in peripheral hematopoietic cells, while the unspliced variant is highly expressed in brain. Recent studies have implicated high levels of PYK2 expression in microglia, the resident macrophages in the brain. 4 Giralt et al. reported that the overexpression of Pyk2 in the hippocampus of the 5XFAD amyloidosis mouse model improved behavioral deficits and synaptic markers, although not amyloid plaque levels. 15 The unexpected protection afforded by Pyk2 overexpression contrasted with prior findings. 16

PYK2 plays a key role in the proinflammatory functions of microglia, 17 and neuroinflammation is a recognized component of AD. 18 It has been shown that microglial state shifts upon exposure to amyloid beta (Aβ) are driven by tyrosine kinase‐based signaling cascades, such as Fyn, Lyn, and Syk, which leads to the activation of PYK2.17 Activated PYK2 promotes the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin 1β and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha, amplifying inflammatory responses. 19 These findings suggest that PYK2 inhibition might mitigate neuroinflammation and disrupt pathological connections between amyloid and tau. However, its impact on microglial phagocytic function remains incompletely understood and motivated us to assess pharmacological tools to further explore the role of Pyk2 in AD and as a potential therapeutic target.

Small‐molecule kinase inhibitors are essential for probing target biology and assessing pharmacological tractability. They complement genetic models by enabling acute, reversible modulation in cellular and in vivo contexts. Effective chemical probes must be potent and selective and possess favorable physicochemical and pharmacokinetic (PK) properties. For kinases, broad selectivity profiling is critical to differentiate on‐target effects from off‐target activities. Numerous PYK2/FAK inhibitors have been described, 20 , 21 targeting both catalytic and scaffolding functions. However, we focused on inhibitors of catalytic activity to evaluate phosphorylation‐driven signaling cascades governing microglial state transitions relevant to amyloid phagocytosis.

Although several reviews summarize known inhibitors, the diversity of chemotypes, variability in assay conditions used to characterize them, and limited data on target engagement and PK profiles make the selection of suitable tool compounds difficult. To address this, we performed a literature survey to identify potent and selective PYK2 and FAK inhibitors with appropriate activities for use as molecular probes. We also included selective SYK inhibitors for comparison, given the convergence of immune receptor signaling on SYK and PYK2 via immunoreceptor tyrosine‐based activation motifs (ITAMs). We validated reported potency and selectivity using the HotSpot Kinase assay and assessed in vitro physicochemical and PK properties, including microsomal clearance, Madin Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cell permeability, and solubility. Finally, as an example of the use of these tool compounds, we evaluated their effects in a high content imaging assay that assesses microglial activation and cellular health. 22 This work represents a Milestone 2 target‐enablement effort within the Indiana University School of Medicine (IUSM)–Purdue TREAT‐AD Center, aimed at evaluating existing ligands, assays, and pharmacological tools for AD‐relevant target validation and progression toward an enabled drug discovery program. 23

METHODS

Chemistry

The compounds referenced in this study were either purchased from commercial sources or synthesized, with a purity of ≥96%. Compounds 3, 4, 6, and 7 were purchased from ChemScene, and Compound 9 was purchased from DC Chemicals (full details, including purity and catalog/lot numbers, are provided in the Supporting Information). Compounds 1, 2, 5, and 8 were synthesized according to established literature procedures, and detailed purity assessments and characterizations of the synthesized compounds are included in the Supporting Information.

Hotspot kinase assay protocol

The assay was carried out at Reaction Biology Corporation according to a previously described protocol. 24 For details on the corresponding enzyme concentration, the K m ATP of the enzyme, and substrate information, please refer to the Supporting Information. The kinase substrate was first prepared in a freshly constituted base reaction buffer (20 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 0.01% Brij35, 0.02 mg/ml bovine serum albumin, 0.1 mM Na3VO4, 2 mM dithiothreitol, 1% dimethyl sulfoxide [DMSO]) and necessary cofactors were introduced to the substrate solution. After that, the kinase was added, ensuring gentle mixing for a homogeneous solution. Using the Echo550 acoustic technology, inhibitors dissolved in 100% DMSO (10 mM) were introduced into the kinase‐substrate‐cofactor mixture in nanoliter volumes to create a 10‐point, three‐fold dilution series starting from 100, 10, or 1 µM inhibitor concentrations. This mixture was subsequently incubated at room temperature for 20 min to facilitate optimal interactions. The kinase reaction was initiated by adding 33P‐ATP (10 µM) to the mixture. Following this, the reaction was allowed to proceed for 2 h at room temperature to ensure completion. After the incubation, the kinase activity was detected using the p81 filter‐binding method. The data were fitted to dose–response curves to estimate the IC50 values, which were then converted to K i values using the Cheng‐Prusoff equation. 25

Evaluation of in vitro physicochemical and PK properties

In vitro physicochemical and PK properties of selected compounds were determined at Q2 Solutions, as described below. Assays involving liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) analysis were performed using a LC‐MS/MS system equipped with an MDS SCIEX API 4000 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, unless otherwise noted. All reagents used were of high‐performance liquid chromatography grade, and mobile phases were adjusted using various aqueous and organic solvent mixtures to optimize analyte retention. All LC‐MS/MS methods were developed with a lower limit of quantification of 2.5 nM. The typical calibration curve ranged from 2.5 to 5000 nM. A detailed description of each assay is provided below.

Kinetics solubility assay

The assay was conducted to rank‐order the test compounds based on their apparent solubility in two aqueous buffer systems: Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) with 25 mM HEPES and 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Terfenadine and spironolactone (50 mM in DMSO) were included as internal standards. The assay was conducted in clear, flat‐bottom polystyrene 96‐well plates, where each well was preloaded with 198 µL of buffer and scanned using a NEPHELOStar Plus nephelometer to obtain background scatter (pre‐read). After that, 2 µL of compound stock solution (prepared in DMSO) was added to the wells, achieving a final DMSO concentration that was kept constant across all wells. The compound stock was serially diluted in DMSO to prepare a concentration range, and each dilution was tested individually by dispensing into buffer‐containing wells as described earlier. Plates were gently mixed and incubated for 2 h at room temperature, prior to repetition of the nephelometric measurements using the same scan mode (single scan measurement). Solubility was assessed by comparing final nephelometric signals to the pre‐read background, and the solubility cutoff was defined as five times the average background value.

MDCK permeability assay

The MDCK cell‐based permeability assay was performed to estimate the intestinal absorption potential of test compounds. Dexamethasone (10 mM in DMSO) was included as a control compound. The test compounds were first diluted to 200 µM by mixing 2 µL of the 10 mM stock solution with 98 µL of DMSO (1:50 dilution). MDCK cells were cultured on permeable supports, and prior to dosing, the apical chamber buffer was removed and rinsed three times with HBSS supplemented with HEPES (HBSSH). A final aliquot of 150 µL HBSSH containing cyclosporin A (CSA) was added to the apical chamber. The receiver plate was filled with 300 µL of HBSSH containing CSA, and the apical plate was placed atop the receiver plate and preincubated for 30 min at 37°C and 5% CO2. A pre‐dose reference plate was prepared by mixing 50 µL of stop reagent (containing internal standard) with 125 µL of HBSSH. A 25‐µL aliquot of the test compound solution was taken prior to dosing and added to this pre‐dose plate, mixed thoroughly, and submitted for LC‐MS/MS analysis. After that, 150 µL of the test compound solution was added to a fresh MilliCell receiver plate, and the post‐dose incubation was carried out at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 60 min, followed by preparation of post‐dose samples by adding 50 µL of stop reagent (with internal standard) and 125 µL of HBSSH to a new plate. A 25‐µL aliquot from the post‐dose solution was transferred to this new plate, mixed well, and submitted for LC‐MS/MS analysis. Simultaneously, 150 µL of the test compound solution was added to the apical chamber of the cell plate, and the cells were incubated for 60 min at 37°C. Following this, an apical sample plate was prepared by adding 50 µL of stop reagent (with internal standard) and 125 µL of HBSSH. A 25‐µL aliquot was collected from the apical chamber, added to the sample plate, mixed, and submitted for LC‐MS/MS analysis. Fluorescence measurements were performed using a plate reader with excitation at 590 nm and emission at 635 nm.

Mouse microsomal intrinsic clearance

The assay was conducted using pooled mouse liver microsomes (0.25 mg/mL final concentration) to estimate metabolic stability of the test compounds. The microsomal incubation buffer consisted of 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 0.4066 g/L MgCl2·6H2O, pre‐warmed to 37°C. The final protein concentration in the reaction was 0.28 mg/mL. Test compound stock solutions were prepared in DMSO. Internal standard controls included labetalol, imipramine, and diclofenac, each prepared at 10 mM in DMSO (16.42, 15.84, and 15.9 mg in 5 mL DMSO, respectively). Standards were stored sealed at ambient temperature or 4°C until use. Microsomal incubations were performed in 96‐well plates pre‐warmed to 37°C for approximately 15 min. To each well, 222.5 µL of microsomal solution was added and equilibrated for 10 min at 37°C with shaking. The reactions were initiated by adding 2.5 µL of compound stock solution followed by 25 µL of NADPH solution and mixed well. For negative controls, 25 µL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) was added in place of NADPH. Immediately after NADPH addition, a 20‐µL aliquot was withdrawn from the reaction mixture and quenched with 80 µL of metabolic stability stop reagent containing internal standard. After the designated incubation period, a second 20‐µL aliquot was withdrawn and similarly quenched. For the negative control plate, samples were collected and quenched after a 45‐min incubation under identical conditions. All quenched samples were centrifuged at ∼4000 rpm for at least 15 min at room temperature. The supernatants were analyzed using LC‐MS/MS.

Microglia phagocytosis high‐content imaging assay protocol

The pHrodo‐myelin phagocytosis/cell viability assay was conducted as described previously. 22 In brief, BV2 and HMC3 microglia cells were plated and treated with the compounds for a total of 48 h in 384‐well cell imaging plates (Corning Falcon 384‐well Optilux black/clear bottom). The final 20 h of this period included seeding with pHrodo‐myelin (5 ug/ml protein, a labeled phagocytosis ligand, isolated from mouse brain). Cells were then stained with Hoechst‐33342 (2 ug/mL, 30 min) prior to imaging. High‐content imaging was carried out using the ArrayScan automatic imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the imaging analysis was performed with the system's proprietary HCS Studio software. Three measurements were derived from this assay: (1) phagocytosis signal, measured as the mean phagocytosis spot total intensity per cell; (2) total cell counts per well, representing the primary measure of cell viability; and (3) mean average nuclear intensity per cell, used to profile cell health. It is noteworthy that apoptotic cells display an increase in nuclear intensity during early apoptosis and a decrease during later stages of apoptosis. The 10‐point dose–response experiments were conducted in duplicate. All phagocytosis experiments were performed using standardized culture conditions and uniform passage ranges for HMC3 and BV2 cells, ensuring consistent cell health and viability across experiments and minimizing variability due to passage number or survival conditions. Additional cell viability assays were performed in HMC3 cells using the same procedure as the phagocytosis assay, but without pHrodo‐myelin seeding. Cells were stained with a combination of Hoechst‐33342 and propidium iodide (PI; 2 µg/mL) added to the culture medium for 30 min prior to high‐content imaging. Cell viability was determined by counting total cells stained with Hoechst‐33342 and dead cells stained with PI and calculating percent viability as 100 minus the percentage of PI‐positive cells.

RESULTS

Compound selection

A careful evaluation of the literature revealed that most clinical candidates currently available as selective PYK2 and FAK inhibitors lack detailed reports describing the initial discovery efforts and structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies. Instead, information on these compounds is often limited to disclosures in patent applications. Conversely, literature reports that provide detailed SAR data on potent and selective PYK2 and FAK inhibitors often lack information regarding cellular target engagement, physicochemical properties, and PK characteristics, key attributes crucial for selecting tool compounds. To address this gap, we selected a subset of compounds derived from the 2,3‐diaminopyrimidine scaffold, which originates from the FAK‐selective clinical candidate PF‐431396, based on several criteria. The first criterion is that these compounds exhibit potent PYK2 and FAK inhibitory activities, and all except one are clinical candidates, ensuring that their physicochemical and PK properties are suitable for both in vitro and in vivo applications. Second, most of these compounds have undergone biophysical characterization using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and structural biology techniques, validating their inhibitory mode of action and supporting their reported activity and selectivity, making them preferable to other available clinical candidates. Notably, the availability of drug–target interaction off‐rates (k off) is particularly valuable, as it determines residence time, which influences pharmacological activity. Additionally, most of these compounds exhibit excellent kinome‐wide selectivity, a critical property for kinase inhibitor tool compounds. Figure 1 summarizes the chemical structures and reported inhibitory activities of the selected compounds, which include two potent PYK2‐selective inhibitors, one PYK2/FAK dual inhibitor, and three FAK‐selective inhibitors. This classification is based on their reported in vitro inhibitory activities. Although these compounds share a common scaffold, they incorporate sufficient structural diversity to serve as reliable tool compounds for probing PYK2 and FAK biology. Furthermore, we selected three SYK inhibitors for comparison, all of which are clinical candidates.

PYK2‐selective compounds (1 and 2)

Compound 1, bearing a diaminopyrimidine scaffold, was first described by Daniel et al. (Pfizer) in 2012 (Reference 35 example 2 g) as a highly selective PYK2 inhibitor (IC50 = 154 nM) over FAK (IC50 = 4500 nM), as determined by a fluorescence polarization (FP) assay that measured the phosphorylation of the corresponding peptide substrate relative to a vehicle control based on FP changes upon substrate phosphorylation. 26 In this study, they converted a FAK‐selective chemical series into highly potent, PYK2‐selective, and metabolically stable inhibitors through a series of structural changes, including the replacement of the electron‐rich fused lactam system of the lead inhibitor (PF‐431396) (Figure 2) with a sulfonamide functionality. This modification reduced bioactivation liability, as evidenced by a negative result in the reactive metabolite assay. Subsequently, two independent research groups conducted extensive X‐ray crystallography and SPR studies on PF‐431396 and Compound 1, along with several other inhibitors from this series, to elucidate the kinetic and structural basis of their potency and selectivity. 27 , 28 Interestingly, the X‐ray crystal structure of the FAK‐selective lead inhibitor PF‐431396 (PDB ID: 6YR9) and its derivatives bearing a sulfonamide functionality at the C‐4 amine of the diaminopyrimidine scaffold revealed an induced DFG‐helix conformation in FAK. In contrast, the corresponding structures in PYK2 exhibited a linear DFG conformation, whereas the DFG‐helix conformation in FAK was associated with slower off‐rates and longer residence times, as determined by SPR (Figure 2), accounting for the observed FAK selectivity. Notably, replacing the sulfonamide functionality at the C‐4 amine with cyclopentyl amine derivatives (regardless of the substitution at the C‐2 amine) reversed the selectivity from FAK to PYK2, as seen with Compound 1. Unlike PF‐431396, the X‐ray crystal structure of Compound 1 (Figure 3A) displayed a linear DFG conformation in both FAK (PDB ID: 6YVY) and PYK2 (PDB ID: 3H3C), accompanied by fast off‐rates according to SPR. Further structural analysis of PYK2 bound to Compound 1 revealed that the phenylalanine residue of the DFG motif shifts out of the distal DFG pocket, adopting an orientation reminiscent of a DFG out‐conformation (Figure 3A top panel). However, superimposition of this structure with an X‐ray crystal structure of a type II inhibitor (PF‐4618433) bound to PYK2 (PDB ID: 3FZT) indicated that, although the phenylalanine residue deviates from its typical DFG‐in position, the aspartate residue of the DFG motif remains oriented toward the ATP‐binding pocket, potentially preventing the binding of a true type II inhibitor. Furthermore, type II inhibitors are generally characterized by extremely slow on‐rates 29 (k on ∼102 M−1s−1), a feature that was not observed for Compound 1, which exhibited a k on in the range of 105 M−1s−1 according to SPR data (Figure 2). Although in vivo PYK2 inhibition data for Compound 1 has not been reported, its higher PYK2 selectivity and the availability of detailed SAR and X‐ray crystallographic data led us to select it as a PYK2‐selective tool compound in this study.

Compound 2 (PF‐719), a potent and selective PYK2 inhibitor, was first reported by Tse et al. in 2012 as part of a collaborative effort between Pfizer and the University of British Columbia. 30 This work was later disclosed in patent application as a method for inhibiting PYK2 to treat AD or to prevent synaptic loss associated with the condition. 31 The inhibitor was specifically designed as a novel PYK2‐selective probe to evaluate the role of PYK2 in B cells, making it an ideal tool compound for in vivo studies. This small‐molecule inhibitor exhibits high selectivity toward PYK2, with an IC50 of 17 nM, while displaying 469 nM IC50 against FAK in enzymatic assays (27‐fold selectivity). Structurally, Compound 2 shares a core scaffold with Compound 1, differing primarily in the substitution at the C‐2 amine position, where a cyclopropyl amide replaces the sulfonamide functionality (Figure 2). Although comprehensive SAR data remain unavailable, the preserved core architecture suggests a similar mechanism of selectivity to that of Compound 1. Kinome‐wide profiling revealed that Compound 2 had minimal off‐target effects on serine/threonine kinases involved in chemokine receptor signaling, including recombinant myosin light chain kinase 2, p38, AKT1, and ROCK1, all of which exhibit less than 1% inhibition at 1 µM. 30 Among Src family kinases (SFKs), 2 exhibits weak inhibition and moderate selectivity, with Lyn inhibition occurring at 6.7 µM and c‐Src, Fyn, Hck, and Lck, with IC50 values exceeding 10 µM. In cellular assays, it selectively inhibits PYK2 autophosphorylation at Tyr402 in A20 murine B cells, achieving complete inhibition at 1 µM, while showing no inhibitory effect on FAK autophosphorylation at Tyr397 at the same concentration. However, at an increased concentration of 2.5 µM, Compound 2 exhibits dual inhibition of both PYK2 and FAK, indicating a dose‐dependent expansion of target engagement. These findings highlight Compound 2 as a highly selective PYK2 inhibitor at low concentrations with minimal off‐target activity, though at elevated concentrations, it may exhibit dual PYK2/FAK inhibition.

FAK‐selective compounds (4, 5, and 6)

Compound 4 (PF‐562271), a clinical candidate, was first reported by Walter et al. (Pfizer, 2008) as a potent and selective FAK inhibitor with an IC50 of 1.5 nM in ELISA‐based assays, demonstrating nine‐fold selectivity over PYK2 (IC50 = 13 nM). 32 Similar to 1, Compound 4 was derived from the FAK‐selective PF‐431396, with a key modification in the aryl group attached to the methanesulfonamide moiety. In PF‐431396, the sulfonamide nitrogen was substituted with a 2‐phenyl (benzene) group, whereas in 4, this was replaced with a 2‐pyridinyl (pyridine) group (Figure 1 and 2). This replacement introduced a nitrogen heteroatom into the aryl ring, altering electronic properties, hydrogen bonding potential, and overall PK. Even though detailed SAR studies are not described, it can be hypothesized that the presence of pyridine in 4 may enhance solubility, modify metabolic stability, and influence binding interactions, thereby reinforcing its FAK selectivity. Extensive X‐ray crystallography and SPR studies revealed that 4 induced a DFG‐helix conformation in FAK (Figure 3B bottom panel), similar to PF‐431396, a structural rearrangement associated with slower off‐rates and prolonged target engagement confirmed by SPR data (Figure 2). 27 In contrast, the PYK2‐bound X‐ray crystal structure of 4 (Figure 3B top panel) shows a linear DFG conformation (associated with faster off‐rates), lacking the DFG‐helix induction observed in FAK. This conformational difference provides a mechanistic basis for the longer residence time in FAK compared to PYK2 and observed FAK selectivity over PYK2. Kinome‐wide profiling in vitro indicated that Compound 4 exhibited high selectivity, with off‐target activity detected only against a limited subset of kinases, including Aurora‐A (145 nM), CDK1/cyclinB (58 nM), Flt3 (97 nM), and Fyn (377 nM). 32 In addition, a chemical proteomic (kinobeads) coupled quantitative mass spectrometry approach found excellent kinome‐wide selectivity in a cell lysate mixture (K‐562, COLO 205, and MV‐4‐11), with an apparent dissociation constant (Kdapp) of 1 nM for FAK, followed by 79 nM for PYK2 and 89 nM for CDK17 (Figure 4A). 33 Moreover, only 11 other kinases exhibited Kdapp values between 100 nM and 1 µM, further confirming the excellent FAK selectivity in a cellular environment. Cellular studies further confirm strong in vivo FAK engagement, as evidenced by potent inhibition of FAK Tyr397 autophosphorylation in A431 cells (IC50 = 5 nM). 32 In contrast, PYK2 Tyr402 autophosphorylation inhibition is fourfold less potent, reinforcing the preference of 4 for FAK over PYK2 at physiologically relevant concentrations. Collectively, the DFG‐helix induction, slow dissociation kinetics, and preferential FAK inhibition establish 4 as a selective FAK‐targeting tool compound.

Compound 5 (PF‐573228), which shares structural characteristics similar to those of Compound 4, was first disclosed in a patent application (WO2005111016) and later described by Davis et al. as a potent FAK‐selective inhibitor, with an IC50 of 4 nM for FAK and >1 µM for PYK2 in vitro, as determined by an ELISA‐based method. 34 Similar to Compound 4, it has been explored as a clinical candidate, supporting its use as an in vivo probe. The same study that described the structural and biophysical characterization of Compounds 1 and 4 provided a detailed account of the potential origin of the higher potency and selectivity of this compound toward FAK. 27 The study revealed that Compound 5 induced a DFG‐helix conformation in FAK (Figure 3C), similar to PF‐431396 and Compound 4 (PF‐562271), leading to slower off‐rates and prolonged target engagement, as indicated by SPR data, which accounts for its in vitro potency and selectivity. Kinome‐wide profiling revealed that Compound 5 exhibited high selectivity, with measurable activity against only a limited subset of kinases, including CDK1/cyclinB (IC50 = 486 nM) and CDK7/cyclinH (IC50 = 197 nM), while showing minimal inhibition of GSK3β (>1000 nM), suggesting a favorable selectivity profile with reduced off‐target kinase interactions. 34 In cellular assays, Compound 5 effectively inhibited FAK autophosphorylation at Tyr397, displaying an IC50 of 11 nM in A431 cells, ∼100 nM in REF52 and PC3 cells, 50 nM in SKOV‐3 cells, 300 nM in L3‐6p1 cells, 30 nM in F‐G cells, and 500 nM in MDCK cells. 34 Notably, at 1 µM, complete inhibition of FAK autophosphorylation was observed in A20 murine B cells, with no detectable inhibition of PYK2 autophosphorylation, further reinforcing its selectivity for FAK over PYK2 in a cellular context.

Compound 6 (VS‐4718, also known as PND‐1186) was first described in a patent application (WO2008115369) as a potent FAK inhibitor with an IC50 of 1.5 nM, determined using an ELISA‐based assay. 35 Although no reported activity against PYK2 has been documented, and no detailed SAR studies are available, some insights into its activity can be inferred from the FAK‐bound X‐ray crystal structure of the structurally related clinical candidate TEA226 (Figure 2). 36 TEA226, which features a chloro substitution at the 6 position of the diaminopyridine core, differs from Compound 6, which instead contains a trifluoromethyl substitution at the same position. In the X‐ray crystal structure of TEA226 bound to FAK, it induces a DFG‐helix conformation, a feature associated with slow off‐rates, similar to previously described FAK‐selective inhibitors (Figure 3D). Compound 6 has also been widely employed as a FAK‐selective chemical probe in various in vivo studies, particularly in the context of cancer pathology. Kinome‐wide profiling at 1 µM indicates that 6 is highly selective for FAK (100% inhibition) but also exhibits moderate off‐target activity against ACK1 (84%), Aurora‐A (92%), CDK2/cyclinA (58%), IR (60%), Lck (68%), and TrkA (73%), while showing minimal inhibition of Flt3 (8%). 37 Despite these interactions, FAK remains the primary target, and the compound continues to be widely utilized as a FAK‐selective probe. Cellular studies confirm strong in vivo FAK engagement, as evidenced by complete inhibition of FAK Tyr397 autophosphorylation in murine 4T1 breast carcinoma cells at 100 nM. This robust inhibition suggests that Compound 6 effectively suppresses FAK‐driven signaling pathways at physiologically relevant concentrations.

PYK2/FAK dual targeting compound (3)

Compound 3 (Defactinib, PF‐0455487, and VS‐6063) is a highly potent inhibitor of both PYK2 and FAK, with an in vitro IC50 of 0.6 nM for both enzymes using ELISA‐based methods. 38 It was first disclosed by Pfizer in a patent application WO2008115369 and later a clinical candidate for the treatment of solid malignancies. 39 , 40 The same study that determined the kinome‐wide selectivity of Compound 4 using a chemical proteomic (Kinobeads) coupled quantitative approach also profiled Compound 3, indicating excellent FAK engagement in a cell lysate, with a Kdapp value of 0.5 nM (Figure 4A). 33 In contrast, a relatively lower affinity was reported for PYK2, with a Kdapp value of 423 nM, contradicting the equipotent in vitro inhibition of PYK2 and FAK reported using the ELISA‐based method. Only eight other kinases exhibited Kdapp values below 1 µM, including FLT3, AURKA, CDK12, STK16, MELK, MAP3K11, and NTRK1. The compound has been widely used in in vivo studies, demonstrating its suitability as an in vivo probe. These studies have shown that Compound 3 potently inhibits FAK and PYK2 autophosphorylation in a dose‐dependent manner. In A431 epidermoid carcinoma cells, Compound 3 effectively suppressed FAK Tyr397 phosphorylation with an IC50 of 3 nM, while exhibiting equivalent potency against PYK2 Tyr402 autophosphorylation (IC50 = 3 nM). 40 Similarly, in HeyA8 ovarian cancer cells, a dose‐dependent inhibition of both FAK and PYK2 phosphorylation was observed over a 0 to 1 µM concentration range. 41 These results indicate that Compound 3 exhibits dual‐target engagement with nanomolar potency, efficiently modulating both FAK and PYK2 signaling pathways across multiple cellular models.

SYK‐selective compounds (7, 8, and 9)

Compound 7 (Lanraplenib/GS‐9876) is a potent and selective SYK inhibitor first described by Peter et al. in 2020. 42 It was optimized starting from a clinical‐stage SYK inhibitor with the goal of improving metabolic stability and pH‐dependent aqueous solubility while maintaining the potency and selectivity of the original inhibitor. Compound 7 exhibits an in vitro SYK inhibitory activity IC50 of 6.2 nM and an in vivo BLNK phosphorylation IC50 of 24 nM, making it one of the most potent SYK inhibitors in both in vitro and in vivo studies. The in vitro SYK inhibition was measured using the KinEASE (Cisbio) time‐resolved fluorescence energy transfer (TR‐FRET) immune assay protocol, where phosphorylation of an XL665‐labeled peptide substrate (biotinylated peptide and streptavidin‐XL665 tracer) was monitored using a Europium‐conjugated phospho‐tyrosine‐specific antibody TR‐FRET assay. 42 In vivo target engagement was determined by measuring the inhibition of BLNK phosphorylation, a direct substrate of SYK in B cells, using phospho‐BLNK‐specific antibodies. The X‐ray crystal structure of Compound 7 bound to the SYK kinase domain (PDB ID: 6VOV) is available and confirms its type I inhibitor binding mode. Additionally, the compound exhibits excellent DMPK profiles in rats, dogs, monkeys, and humans, making it an attractive SYK inhibitor for in vivo studies.

Compound 8 was first described by Hoemann et al. in 2016 as a novel SYK‐selective inhibitor resulting from a structure‐based drug discovery effort that originated from the crystal structure of tamatinib (PDB ID: 3FQS), a potent and selective SYK inhibitor. 43 In this work, they exploited the presence of Pro455 in the SYK ATP‐binding pocket, a relatively rare feature observed in only eight other kinases. The key structural element of this inhibitor is the disubstituted furano[3,2‐d]pyrimidine moiety, which interacts with the hinge region, while the saturated ring system enhances lipophilic ligand efficiency (5.1), thereby improving SYK inhibitory activity (7 nM) and cellular potency (1.7 nM). The in vitro SYK inhibition assay was conducted using the C‐terminal His‐tagged catalytic domain in the presence of a biotin‐conjugated peptide substrate (200 µM) at an ATP concentration of 1 µM. Substrate phosphorylation was detected and quantified by fluorescence using an anti‐phosphotyrosine antibody and a Phycolink streptavidin‐allophycocyanin acceptor system. The in vivo SYK inhibition was assessed by monitoring calcium flux in Ramos cells. In this assay, a fluorescent calcium‐sensitive dye was used to detect calcium flux upon SYK activation, which was stimulated using an anti‐IgM antibody in the presence and absence of the SYK inhibitors. Even though the X‐ray crystal structure of this compound is not available, the X‐ray crystal structure of a related compound in this series (PDB ID: 5T68) is available, confirming its type I kinase inhibitor binding mode. Compound 8 also exhibited excellent kinome‐wide selectivity in an internal panel of 75 kinases assessed using a TR‐FRET format, where it inhibited only c‐Src and Fyn, with 30‐fold selectivity over its SYK IC50. Additionally, Compound 8 demonstrated acceptable in vitro PK properties, including an unbound clearance of < 4.9 L/h/kg in rat microsomes and 10.2 L/h/kg in human microsomes, making it a suitable in vitro and in vivo SYK tool compound with a distinct chemotype compared to Compound 7.

Compound 9 (SKI‐O‐592 or cevidoplenib, the orally bioavailable mesylate salt form) is a potent and selective SYK inhibitor developed by Oscotec Inc. through a structure‐based drug design approach. 44 It has a reported in vitro SYK inhibitor activity IC50 of 6.2 nM and in vivo SYK phosphorylation inhibition IC50 values of 282, 191, and 266 nM in Ramos cells, human CD14+ monocytes, and THP‐1 cells, respectively. The in vitro SYK inhibitor activity was measured by monitoring the tyrosine phosphorylation of the ULight‐labeled poly‐GT peptide substrate by SYK using time‐resolved FRET (TR‐FRET) between the phosphorylated peptide substrate and a Europium (Eu)‐chelate‐labeled anti‐phosphopeptide antibody. The in vivo SYK phosphorylation (Tyr526) inhibition in stimulated cell lines was measured by standard immunoblotting and ELISA using anti‐phospho‐SYK (Y526) antibodies. A recently disclosed X‐ray crystal structure of Compound 9 bound to SYK kinase (PDB ID: 8WYW) confirmed its type I inhibitor binding mode. Additionally, it demonstrated excellent kinome‐wide selectivity, with off‐target IC50 values ranging from 67‐ to 2753‐fold higher than its SYK inhibitor IC50. 44 It also exhibited excellent in vitro and in vivo PK profiles, making it a promising SYK tool compound.

Evaluation of inhibitory activities, PK, and physiochemical properties of selected tool compounds

Here we employed the HotSpot kinase assay platform to evaluate the kinase inhibitory activities of the selected tool compounds. The measured K i values are shown in Figure 4C. All compounds selected for this study demonstrated potent inhibitory activity against their intended targets and aligned with values reported in the literature. However, most failed to reproduce the magnitude of the reported in vitro selectivities. It should be noted that the HotSpot kinase assay determined K i values for Compounds 1, 4, and 5, which closely resembled the reported K d values derived from SPR studies rather than the literature‐reported IC50 values obtained using ELISA or FP‐based techniques. For example, the HotSpot kinase assay determined K i values of 173 and 44 nM for Compound 1, which aligned well with the SPR‐determined K d values of 150 and 25 nM for FAK and PYK2, respectively, but contrasted with the IC50 values of 4500 and 154 nM obtained using the FP technique for FAK and PYK2. For Compound 4, the HotSpot kinase assay determined a K i value of 2.7 nM for FAK, which closely matched the SPR‐derived K d value of 5.7 nM. However, for PYK2, the K i determined by the HotSpot kinase assay (2.8 nM) was somewhat higher than the SPR‐derived K d value (0.76 nM), making it appear to be a PYK2/FAK dual‐targeting inhibitor. Even though the compounds failed to reproduce the exact magnitude of selectivity reported in the literature, Compound 1 displayed a four‐fold selectivity toward PYK2 over FAK, while Compounds 4 and 6 exhibited approximately 90‐fold and 10‐fold selectivity toward FAK over PYK2, respectively, confirming their selectivity for the relevant target. All three SYK inhibitors selected in this study demonstrated potent and selective SYK inhibitory activity comparable to their literature values. Notably, Compound 7 (lanraplenib) exhibited significant inhibition of FAK (54 nM) compared to its SYK inhibitory activity (6.9 nM).

We also determined in vitro physicochemical properties and metabolic stability of some of the inhibitors selected for PYK2 and FAK, which demonstrated acceptable profiles, except for Compound 1, which displayed somewhat higher mouse liver microsome clearance, indicating its suitability for cellular applications rather than animal studies (Figure 5).

Evaluation of tool compounds in microglia phagocytosis high‐content imaging assay

As an example use of these tool compounds and a preliminary study of the impact of selective PYK2 and FAK inhibition on microglia, we tested these compounds in a high‐content imaging assay with simultaneous measurements of pHrodo‐myelin membrane debris uptake, cell number, and nuclear intensity to assess both cellular pharmacology and cell health. 22 Compounds 1 and 2 showed strong stimulation of phagocytosis in HMC3 cells up to 60 µM. At higher concentrations, phagocytosis decreased and nuclear intensity increased, suggesting the onset of toxic effects, while parallel dose‐dependent decreases in cell counts were observed (Figure 6). Within the <10‐µM dose range, cell counts remained stable at approximately 900 to 1000 cells per well, which is higher than the initial seeding density of >600 cells per well and coincided with sustained stimulation of phagocytic activity. To further assess whether reduced cell counts reflected overt cytotoxicity, HMC3 cells treated under the same conditions were evaluated using a PI exclusion imaging assay. The results showed that PI‐based viability remained >90% across all tested concentrations of Compounds 1 and 2, including conditions where cell counts were reduced (Figure S1). These findings indicate that, in HMC3 cells, Compounds 1 and 2 stimulate phagocytosis within a concentration range in which direct loss of viability is limited, while the accompanying reduction in cell number is consistent with the inhibition of proliferation. However, cytotoxic effects at the highest concentrations cannot be completely excluded. In BV2 cells, higher compound concentrations were also associated with marked reductions in cell number (Figure 6B,D). Because BV2 cells proliferate more rapidly than HMC3 cells over the 48‐h treatment period, these reductions may reflect inhibition of proliferation to a greater extent. PI‐based viability in BV2 cells remained near control levels across much of the tested concentration range and declined only at the highest concentrations (Figure S1), although cytotoxicity at the highest concentrations cannot be excluded. In contrast to HMC3 cells, a clear phagocytosis‐stimulatory effect was not observed in BV2 cells for Compounds 1 and 2 under the conditions tested. The selective phagocytosis phenotype observed in HMC3 cells with Compounds 1 and 2 remains consistent with on‐target PYK2 pathway modulation. Reference SYK tool Compound 9 also displayed a similar phagocytosis effect at higher concentrations in both BV2 and HMC3 cells, where the cell count significantly decreased in parallel with an increase in nuclear intensity (Figure 6).

DISCUSSION

This study exemplifies how the IUSM‐Purdue TREAT‐AD Center addresses translational gaps in AD research, particularly the limited availability of well‐characterized and selective pharmacological tools for genetically implicated targets such as PTK2B (PYK2). Although GWASs link PTK2B to late‐onset AD, the functional role of PYK2 in microglial and neuroinflammatory signaling remains poorly defined, in part due to inconsistent inhibitor characterization and assay variability. We therefore evaluated PYK2 as a potential therapeutic target based on the hypothesis that its inhibition may modulate AD‐associated neuroinflammatory pathways. Through coordinated efforts across technical cores, we established standardized biochemical and cellular assays, assessed potency and selectivity of PYK2 and FAK inhibitors, and identified experimentally suitable tool compounds for AD‐relevant models.

To enable future drug discovery efforts, we selected and validated representative inhibitors with appropriate potency, selectivity, and physicochemical and DMPK properties for use in cellular and animal studies. In addition to selective PYK2 and FAK inhibitors, we identified SYK‐selective compounds to facilitate comparative interrogation of immune signaling pathways. By applying well‐defined selection criteria, including biochemical potency, in vivo relevance, selectivity profiling, and biophysical characterization, we prioritized robust chemical probes intended to provide reliable tools for mechanistic and translational studies in AD.

A key challenge in interpreting prior studies of PYK2 and FAK inhibitors is the heterogeneity of assay platforms. Most compounds reported in the literature have been evaluated using antibody‐based ELISA methods (colorimetric) or FP assays, each with inherent detection biases and susceptibility to artifacts. In the antibody‐based colorimetric technique used for Compounds 2 to 5 (also referred as ELISA‐based), phosphorylation of the poly(Glu/Tyr) peptide substrate is detected using a general anti‐phosphotyrosine (PY20) antibody, which specifically recognizes phosphorylated tyrosine residues. 30 , 34 A horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibody is then introduced, enabling colorimetric detection through the addition of 3,3’,5,5’‐tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), a chromogenic HRP substrate. HRP catalyzes TMB oxidation, producing a blue‐colored product that turns yellow upon the addition of stop solution (2 M H2SO4). The optical density at 450 nm is measured, with the signal intensity correlating with the phosphorylation level of the substrate. For Compound 6, the K‐LISA™ PTK Screening Kit was used, which is also an antibody‐based colorimetric assay. 37 Unlike the method employed for Compounds 2 to 5, K‐LISA features an immobilized peptide substrate, allowing for more stringent washing steps and reduced background signal. In this approach, substrate phosphorylation is detected using a HRP‐conjugated anti‐phosphotyrosine monoclonal antibody, eliminating the need for a secondary antibody. The reaction is similarly developed using TMB and stopped with sulfuric acid, with optical density measured at 595 nm. A FP assay was used to test Compound 1, with the detailed protocol supposedly reported in Reference 44. 45 However, this reference, which the same group has cited multiple times as the original protocol they have continuously used, does not contain the expected details. 45 However, in general, the FP technique monitors molecular rotation and binding interactions by detecting changes in the polarization of emitted light. 46

In contrast to ELISA or FP‐based assays, we used the HotSpot kinase assay, a radiometric, filtration‐based assay that is widely regarded as the “gold standard” for detecting kinase activity due to its high sensitivity and reliability. 46 This assay utilizes γ−32P‐ATP as a phosphate donor, enabling the direct measurement of phosphorylated substrate formation. Following the kinase reaction, the phosphorylated product is captured on a filter membrane, while unincorporated radiolabeled ATP is removed through multiple washing steps. The retained radioactivity on the membrane is then quantified using a scintillation counter, providing a direct readout of kinase activity. Unlike fluorescence‐ or luminescence‐based assays, the HotSpot assay is not susceptible to interference from autofluorescent compounds or other assay components, making it highly suitable for profiling kinase inhibitors. Importantly, the inhibitory activities we measured were consistent with the literature in terms of target engagement, yet the magnitude of selectivity often differed from values reported using alternative assay formats. This observation underscore that apparent potency and selectivity can be strongly assay‐dependent, highlighting the importance of standardized methods for enabling head‐to‐head comparisons of tool compounds.

The in vitro physicochemical and PK profiles generated in house confirmed that most of the PYK2 and FAK tool compounds evaluated exhibited properties consistent with their use in cellular and translational studies, especially Compound 1, which displayed higher intrinsic clearance in mouse liver microsomes, possibly limiting its suitability for in vivo applications. Together, the biochemical validation and in vitro PK/physicochemical property characterization strengthen confidence in the selected inhibitors as high‐quality molecular probes. Compounds 3 and 4 (PF‐573228 and PF‐562271) represent our best FAK‐selective in vivo candidates based on potency, PK, and central nervous system (CNS) exposure. Compounds 5 and 6 (SYK inhibitors) exhibit demonstrated CNS exposure and target engagement suitable for in vivo studies. Compound 1, although selective and potent, is not recommended for in vivo studies due to suboptimal solubility and PK properties. These compounds will enable mechanistic interrogation of PYK2 and FAK signaling in disease‐relevant contexts, while highlighting the limitations of certain scaffolds, such as Compound 1, for in vivo studies.

A study by Lee et al. reported that inhibition of PYK2 with PF‐431396 (Figure 2), the parent compound of the subset of inhibitors we selected here, induced the formation of multinucleated microglia and enhanced phagocytosis of Aβ oligomers both in vitro and in an acute in vivo model. 47 They attributed this effect to abnormal cytokinesis leading to multinucleation and suggested that multinucleated microglia exhibit increased phagocytic and lysosomal activity without evidence of neuronal loss. Notably, their work also demonstrated that PYK2 inhibition dampened proinflammatory responses in human microglia, further supporting a potential role for PYK2 in modulating microglial phenotypes toward a more phagocytic and less inflammatory state. A key distinction between the two studies lies in the choice of inhibitors. While Lee et al. employed PF‐431396, which they used as a PYK2 inhibitor, this compound was originally developed and described as a potent and selective FAK inhibitor, with reported cross‐reactivity toward PYK2. Its activity profile therefore raises the possibility that the observed phagocytosis phenotype may stem from off‐target or dual‐inhibition effects rather than selective PYK2 inhibition. Nevertheless, we did not observe such phagocytosis induction with Compound 3, which is a PYK2/FAK dual inhibitor, ruling out the latter. Another major difference relates to cellular context, as Lee et al. reported multinucleated microglia formation following PF‐431396 treatment in murine MG6 microglial cells and validated this effect in vivo using an acute Aβ infusion mouse model. In the current study, we utilized BV2 and HMC3 cells in the preliminary assay, neither of which fully recapitulate the transcriptional and functional characteristics of disease‐associated microglia (DAMs) found in aging and AD brains. Recent RNA sequencing studies have shown that DAMs are characterized by TREM2 upregulation and exhibit both enhanced phagocytosis and an anti‐inflammatory transcriptional profile. 48 It is plausible that the ability of PYK2 inhibition to modulate microglial phagocytosis may be more pronounced in a DAM‐like state, which our current models do not fully capture. The strong stimulation of phagocytosis observed with PYK2 inhibition, together with reduced cell counts, requires careful interpretation. A lower final cell number after 48 h may reflect inhibition of proliferation, cell death, or both. The pHrodo signal in the multiplexed assay depends on intracellular acidification of phagocytic vesicles and therefore indicates the presence of metabolically active cells, but it does not by itself exclude cytotoxicity. In HMC3 cells, PI exclusion imaging showed that viability remained >90% across the tested concentration range for Compounds 1 and 2, indicating limited overt loss of viability. However, because HMC3 cells continue to proliferate during the 48‐h assay, the reduced cell number relative to control is most consistent with growth inhibition, although cytotoxicity at the highest concentrations cannot be excluded. In BV2 cells, the greater reduction in cell number likely reflects faster proliferation and greater sensitivity to growth suppression.

Taken together, these results highlight the importance of both compound selectivity and cellular context when interpreting the role of PYK2 and FAK signaling in microglial phagocytosis. Whereas Lee et al. observed enhanced phagocytosis using PF‐431396 in MG6 cells and acute in vivo models, our findings using validated, selective inhibitors in BV2 and HMC3 cells suggest that PYK2 and FAK inhibition alone is sufficient to drive phagocytic activity in our current model. Future studies in more disease‐relevant models, such as DAM‐like microglia derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), to rigorously test the hypothesis that PYK2 inhibition modulates microglial activation states in AD are needed. As emphasized by recent systematic analyses of chemical probe usage, employing multiple, chemically distinct tool compounds to interrogate a single target in cell‐based assays represents a best practice that minimizes off‐target confounds and strengthens mechanistic conclusions. 49

In conclusion, we provide a curated and experimentally validated set of PYK2, FAK, and SYK inhibitors as openly accessible molecular probes for AD‐related neuroinflammatory research. These compounds enable systematic interrogation of PYK2/FAK signaling, which should be further evaluated in MG6 cells, human iPSC‐derived microglia, and in vivo AD models to clarify their roles in disease progression.
