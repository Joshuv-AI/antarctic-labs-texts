Sidelobe suppression for cosine-sum window functions via chaotic particle swarm optimization
Authors: Zeyin Dong, Yuqi Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sidelobe suppression is a common and critical issue in radar pulse compression processing. However, the traditional window functions such as Hamming and Hanning windows are all limited to fixed weight parameters, and can only be applied to a specific application. Thus, we propose a flexible window function design algorithm based on chaotic particle swarm optimization (W-CPSO). First, a polynomial representation of the cosine-sum window functions is derived using Taylor series expansion. Then, the CPSO-based optimization algorithm is proposed to improve the sidelobe suppression performance of window functions by optimizing the polynomial coefficients. The proposed algorithm has been applied to optimize several widely used windows, including the Hamming, Hann, Blackman windows, etc. Analysis results demonstrate that the optimized window functions significantly improve sidelobe suppression while maintaining comparable mainlobe width. Furthermore, the simulation results of multi-target detection in radar systems also validate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm, showing that the optimized windows exhibit superior performance in detecting weak targets compared to the traditional windows.

Introduction

Window functions, as fundamental tools in key processes such as time-frequency analysis, pulse compression, and sidelobe suppression, play a crucial role in enhancing the performance indicators of radar systems, including the high precision of parameter estimation and target recognition1–3. Traditional window functions (e.g., Rectangular, Hann, Hamming, and Blackman windows) implement time domain weighting to strike a trade-off between mainlobe width and sidelobe attenuation, thereby offering a diverse set of options tailored to different application scenarios4,5. For instance, the Rectangular window, characterized by its minimal mainlobe width, is widely adopted in resolution prioritized tasks, while Hann and Hamming windows employ parameterized designs to balance sidelobe suppression and mainlobe broadening, making them suitable for interference resilient operations in complex electromagnetic environments6.

In recent years, research on window function optimization has exhibited two primary trends. The first focuses on customized designs targeting specific performance metrics, such as minimum-sidelobe-energy windows derived from convex optimization theory and adaptive windows tailored to time-frequency focusing7,8. The second trend emphasizes multi-objective collaborative optimization, exemplified by approaches leveraging multi-parameter tuning to balance the mainlobe broadening width and the relative sidelobe attenuation9,10. However, most existing algorithms concentrate on individual window function or local performance enhancements, lacking a unified mathematical representation of the traditional window functions. This limitation gives rise to several challenges: (I) the design of window functions becomes fragmented, often requiring task-specific reengineering or heuristic selection in practical applications; (II) traditional window functions rely on fixed weight parameters, limiting the flexibility to adapt or migrate performance characteristics across different members of the window function family; and (III) emerging radar waveforms, such as cognitive radar and joint communication sensing signals, demand higher degrees of dynamic reconfigurability, which existing discrete window function libraries are unable to provide in real-time applications11,12.

To address these challenges, we propose an improved algorithm based on chaotic particle swarm optimization (W-CPSO) to achieve a more flexible window function design. The main contributions are as follows: (I) by deconstructing the generation mechanisms of the traditional cosine-sum window functions, a unified polynomial representation is established that encompasses mainstream families for rectangular and cosine windows; (II) the W-CPSO algorithm is employed to optimize the polynomial coefficients to enhance sidelobe suppression performance. The simulation results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm can effectively improve the attenuation of the sidelobe while maintaining a certain width of the mainlobe. Moreover, simulation results of multi-target detection in radar systems also validates the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm, showing that the optimized windows exhibit superior performance in detecting weak targets compared to the traditional windows.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, the principle of the W-CPSO is presented. Section 3 provides a detailed verification of the improved cosine-sum window functions and the simulation of multi-target detection. Section 4 presents our discussion and the final is our conclusion.

The adaptive cosine-sum window functions

The principle of cosine-sum window functions

In radar signal processing, window functions such as the Hamming and Blackman windows are widely employed due to their ability to provide effective sidelobe suppression while preserving a reasonable mainlobe width9,13,14. The mathematical expression of the Hamming window is given by1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w(n) = 0.54 - 0.46\cos (2\pi \frac{n}{N}),0 \le n \le N, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}at the same time, the Blackman window is expressed as2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w(n) = 0.42 - 0.25\cos (\frac{{2\pi n}}{{N - 1}}) + 0.08\cos (\frac{{4\pi n}}{{N - 1}}),0 \le n \le N - 1. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

In addition, other members of the cosine-sum window functions are all derived from the cosine function, differing primarily in their weight coefficients. However, these traditional window functions possess fixed weights, which inherently limit their flexibility and adaptability in varying application scenarios15. Other well-established window functions, like Tukey window, even those with adjustable weight parameters, also exhibit limited functional flexibility. And their tunable parameters often fail to substantially enhance the desired spectral characteristics. Moreover, the development of such windows has traditionally relied on artificial empirical methods. Thus, it needs a more universal window function from which existing ones can be derived as special cases. To this end, inspired by the Taylor series expansion of the cosine function,3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \cos (x) = 1 - \frac{{{x^2}}}{{2!}} + \frac{{{x^4}}}{{4!}} + \frac{{{x^6}}}{{6!}} + \cdots , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}a more generalized formulation for cosine-sum window function family is proposed as follows:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w(t) = \sum \limits _{n = 0}^N {{a_{2n}}} {({\frac{t}{T})}^{2n}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}with that, the expression of traditional window function is represented uniformly by the Taylor polynomial expansion.

Moreover, the traditional window functions are typically symmetric about the center point N/2 (for data indices ranging from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i=1$$\end{document} to N), and are generally normalized at the center \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w[N/2]=1$$\end{document}. Therefore, by restricting the polynomial representation to the time interval \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-T/2$$\end{document} to T/2, the above Equation is modified as5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w(t) = {a_0} + \sum \limits _{n = 1}^N {{a_{2n}}} {(\frac{t}{T})^{2n}},\left| t \right| \le \frac{T}{2}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Furthermore, the considered polynomial is typically of the 10th order (i.e., N = 10), which is sufficient to accurately describe most traditional window functions16,17. With that, the sidelobe suppression performance of the window function can be improved by optimizing these coefficients.

As described in16, the polynomial coefficients are optimized in the frequency domain,6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W(w) = {a_0}{P_0} + \sum \limits _{n = 1}^N {{a_{2n}}{P_{2n}}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_0$$\end{document} is equated to unity, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_0$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{2n}$$\end{document} are obtained as7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{array}{l} {P_0} = \frac{2}{w}\sin ({{wT} /2})\\ \begin{aligned} {P_{2n}} & = \frac{1}{{{4^{n - 1}}{w^2}T}}[\frac{{wT}}{2}\sin ({{wT} / 2}) + 2n\cos ({{wT} / 2})] \\ & - \frac{{2n(2n - 1)}}{{{{(wT)}^2}}}{P_{2(n - 1)}}. \end{aligned} \end{array} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Thus, the polynomial coefficients are derived by solving N simultaneous equations in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{2n}$$\end{document} (n = 1 to N).

Another solution strategy is to directly solve for the weight parameters in the time domain by taking derivatives17. In other words, minimizing the window function derivative at its extremities to improve the relative sidelobe attenuation, or maximizing its second derivative at the center point, to decrease the mainlobe width, as described by8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{array}{l} \min \left\{ {{{\left| {dw(t)/dt} \right| }_{t = \pm {T / 2}}}} \right\} \\ \\ \max \left\{ {{{\left| {{d^2}w(t)/d{t^2}} \right| }_{t = 0}}} \right\} . \end{array} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

However, using either of the aforementioned algorithms to compute the polynomial coefficients would result in significant computational complexity. Moreover, the optimized coefficients may still suffer from insufficient optimization quality. To address these issues, we propose an improved window function design algorithm using W-CPSO algorithm.

The adaptive cosine-sum window functions using W-CPSO

In multi-target detection scenarios, pulse compression may cause weak targets to be masked by the sidelobes of strong targets. Therefore, windowing is required to suppress the sidelobe effectively. With a flexible functional form for the optimized window function, the challenge is now to find the appropriate polynomial coefficients that better suppress the sidelobe. For that, we take the relative sidelobe attenuation as the objective function and use the W-CPSO for optimization. The objective function of the W-CPSO algorithm is9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \hat{a}_{i} = \underset{a_i}{\text {arg max}} \{H_{db} = 20\log (|H_{side}(w(t))-H_{main}(w(t))|)\} , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{db}$$\end{document} represents the relative sidelobe attenuation amplitude, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{side}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{main}$$\end{document} represents the first sidelobe and main peak of the frequency response of window function, respectively.

In W-CPSO, to reduce computation, we initially perform a 10th-order polynomial fitting based on the traditional window functions, such as the Hamming window, to obtain the initial set of polynomial coefficients (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_2, a_4,\ldots , a_{2n}$$\end{document}). These coefficients are then optimized using the W-CPSO algorithm.

Particle swarm optimization is a swarm intelligence optimization algorithm inspired by the foraging behavior of birds or fish in nature. It was proposed by Kennedy and Eberhart in 1995 and is characterized by its simplicity, ease of implementation, few parameters, and fast convergence, especially in high-dimensional optimization problems18. In the algorithm, particles gradually approach the global optimal solution by tracking their individual historical best solution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{pbest}$$\end{document}) and the group’s historical best solution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{gbest}$$\end{document}).

As for the initialization of the particle swarm, the chaotic initialization strategy with the Logistic mapping is used. It can uniformly distribute the initial particles in the solution space and avoid the aggregation phenomenon that may be caused by random initialization, which is10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {x_{i + 1}} = \mu \cdot {x_i}(1 - {x_i}),\mu \in [3.57,4],{x_i} \in [0,1], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document} is set 3.70 in W-CPSO.

At the same time, the individual position changes to14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} x_{id}^{k + 1} = x_{id}^k + v_{id}^{k + 1}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Simulation experiments

The effect verification of the optimized window functions

As discussed above, the optimization process is initialized using the 10th order polynomial coefficients derived from fitting the traditional window functions. It facilitates efficient convergence and ensures the stability of the optimization outcome. Specifically, selecting an initial set of expansion coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_i$$\end{document} that approximate a well-known window function, such as those listed in Table 1, and then the W-CPSO iteratively optimizes these coefficients to minimize the defined objective function. Furthermore, to illustrate the effectiveness of our proposed algorithm, we also selected GAP algorithm in Ref.17 for comparison. And the window functions are all based on 64 sampling points in all simulation experiments.

Figures 1 and 2 exemplify the W-CPSO optimizations performed by analyzing the frequency domain response. Figure 1 shows the optimization results for the Nuttall window, the relative sidelobe of the optimized window function is significantly reduced while maintaining the mainlobe width, where the relative sidelobe is reduced from -93.8 to -100.3 dB (an improvement of 6.9%). And the optimized window by W-CPSO in orange has stronger sidelobe attenuation than that of GAP shown in blue. Figure 2 shows the optimization results for the Hamming window. It can be observed that the optimized window function has a slightly wider mainlobe, but its relative sidelobe attenuation improves from -42.5 to -50.8 dB, a decrease of 19.53%. In contrast, the mainlobe width only expanded by 0.003. Compared with GAP, the second sidelobe attenuation of W-CPSO is more obvious, indicating that our proposed algorithm is much effective. Table 2 summarizes the other window results of the mainlobe width (rad/samples), relative sidelobe attenuation (dB), and leakage factor of the traditional window functions. We can see that all relative sidelobe attenuation of window functions has been improved, and the main lobe width remains basically unchanged. It precisely meets the requirements of multi-target recognition for radar system. Moreover, Fig. 3 as a whole shows the comparison results, obviously indicating that W-CPSO is effective and robust.

Discussion

We constructed a unified polynomial representation framework for traditional cosine-sum window functions by introducing a Taylor series expansion. The performance improvement is attributed to the algorithm finding a better coefficient combination in the trade-off relationship of “mainlobe width—relative sidelobe attenuation” through intelligent search, breaking through the limitations of traditional empirical coefficient design.

The advantages of the W-CPSO algorithm are not only reflected in traditional active radar systems, but also in its good adaptability to emerging radar systems. Take the forward scattering radar (FSR) based on the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) as an example. This type of passive radar system uses satellite signals such as BeiDou as external illumination sources, and the signal power is relatively low. Therefore, it puts forward more stringent requirements for the sidelobe suppression capability of the receiver window function19,20. As shown in Fig. 5, when the optimized window function is applied to this type of passive radar model, its excellent sidelobe suppression performance is verified. The simulated illuminator of opportunity is a BeiDou Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO) satellite and the navigation signal broadcast in the B3I band whose carrier frequency is 1268.52MHz and pulse repetition frequency 1000 Hz. The result strongly demonstrates the potential of the W-CPSO algorithm in handling challenging scenarios such as low signal-to-noise ratio and weak target detection, and shows its application prospects in the demand for dynamic reconfigurable waveforms in future wireless systems such as cognitive radar and joint communication sensing.Fig. 5Results of sidelobe suppression when BeiDou signals are used as the transmission of passive radar systems.

Although the W-CPSO has achieved positive results, there are still some limitations that can serve as directions for future research. The current polynomial representation and optimization mainly focus on the sidelobe suppression index in the frequency domain. In the future, it can be considered to incorporate multiple objectives, such as mainlobe width characteristics and computational complexity, into the optimization function together for multi-objective collaborative optimization. Secondly, although the algorithm itself is reconfigurable, currently its optimization process relies on offline computing. To adapt to the dynamically changing electromagnetic environment, subsequent research can explore lightweight optimization algorithms or proxy models based on deep learning, to achieve online real-time optimization and adaptive adjustment of window functions.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a generalized explanation of the traditional cosine-sum window functions using Taylor series expansion through theoretical analysis. Based on this, a window function optimization algorithm is developed to enhance sidelobe suppression capability, employing chaotic particle swarm optimization. Compared to conventional window functions, the optimized windows demonstrate significant improvements in sidelobe suppression. The Hamming window achieves an enhancement of approximately 8.3 dB in relative sidelobe attenuation. Moreover, in radar multi-target detection simulations, the optimized window outperforms the traditional Triangular and Hamming windows, enabling clearer resolution of three closely spaced targets, thereby validating its superior performance. In summary, the proposed W-CPSO window optimization algorithm demonstrates strong practical effectiveness and broad applicability, extending beyond radar signal processing to fields such as chemical spectrum analysis and astronomical data processing.
