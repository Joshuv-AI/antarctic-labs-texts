Abstract
Cross-entropy method model predictive control (CEM–MPC) is a powerful gradient-free technique for nonlinear optimal control, but its performance is often limited by the reliance on random sampling. This conventional approach can lead to inefficient exploration of the solution space and non-smooth control inputs, requiring a large number of samples to achieve satisfactory results. To address these limitations, we propose deterministic sampling CEM, a novel framework that replaces the random sampling step with deterministic samples derived from localized cumulative distributions. Our approach introduces modular schemes to generate and adapt these sample sets, incorporating temporal correlations to ensure smooth control trajectories. This method can be used as a drop-in replacement for the sampling step in existing CEM-based controllers. Experimental evaluations on two nonlinear control tasks demonstrate that deterministic sampling CEM consistently outperforms state-of-the-art iCEM in terms of cumulative cost and control input smoothness, particularly in the critical low-sample regime.



I
INTRODUCTION

Optimal control plays a crucial role in achieving desired performance in many applications, such as autonomous driving and robotics.
One widely used method for solving these types of problems is model predictive control, which operates in a receding horizon fashion.
At each time step, model predictive control solves an optimization problem to determine the optimal control inputs over a finite horizon. It then applies only the first input to the system
[
1
]
.
However, this optimization can be challenging for nonlinear systems, non-differentiable dynamics, and non-convex cost functions.
In such cases, gradient-free optimization methods are often preferred because they can navigate complex cost landscapes and avoid local minima
[
2
]
.

The cross-entropy method
[
3
]
is a popular gradient-free technique that has been successfully applied to various optimal control problems
[
2
,
4
]
.
It iteratively refines a proposal distribution over control inputs by sampling, evaluating performance, and updating the distribution based on the best-performing samples.
cross-entropy method–model predictive control integrates this into the model predictive control framework, using a discrete-time stochastic process (e.g., a Gaussian process, see
fig.

1
) to generate control input sequences.
The parameters of this process are updated based on the cost of each sampled trajectory, which focuses the search on promising regions of the control space.

A key challenge in standard cross-entropy method–model predictive control is that control inputs are sampled independently at each time step, which can result in non-smooth control sequences and requires a large number of samples to adequately explore the control space.
The improved cross-entropy method (iCEM)
[
2
]
introduces smoothness by temporal correlations, allowing for more informed sampling strategies that lead to better performance.
However, reliance on random sampling, even with temporal correlations, can still produce non-smooth control trajectories.
This is often undesirable in practice because it can damage actuators and cause erratic system behavior.

To overcome these challenges, we propose replacing random sampling with a deterministic sampling strategy.
Our approach, which we term
deterministic sampling Cross-Entropy Method
(dsCEM), leverages precomputed optimal sample sets based on localized cumulative distributions
[
5
,
6
]
.
These deterministic samples are structured to cover the solution space more efficiently and with fewer discrepancies than random samples (see
fig.

1
).
Inspired by iCEM, we incorporate temporal correlations into the sampling process with the goal of generating control sequences that are both more effective and significantly smoother.
The proposed sampling schemes are designed as modular drop-in replacements for the sampling step in cross-entropy method-based controllers, making this approach broadly applicable.

OPTIMAL CONTROL PROBLEM

We consider discrete-time, finite-horizon deterministic optimal control problems with cumulative cost

\displaystyle J_{k}

\displaystyle=g_{N_{\mathrm{H}}}({\underline{x}}_{k+N_{\mathrm{H}}})+\sum_{n=k}^{k+N_{\mathrm{H}}-1}g_{n}({\underline{x}}_{n},{\underline{u}}_{n})\kern 5.0pt,

(2)

where
k
is the current time step,
N_{\mathrm{H}}
the prediction horizon,
{\underline{x}}_{k}\in\mathds{R}^{d_{x}}
the system state,
{\underline{u}}_{n}\in\mathds{R}^{d_{u}}
the control input,
g_{n}({\underline{x}}_{n},{\underline{u}}_{n})\colon\mathds{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathds{R}^{d_{u}}\to\mathds{R}
the stage cost at time step
n
, and
g_{N_{\mathrm{H}}}(x_{k+N_{\mathrm{H}}})\colon\mathds{R}^{d_{x}}\to\mathds{R}
the terminal cost.
The discrete-time system dynamics are given by

\displaystyle{\underline{x}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle={\underline{a}}_{n}({\underline{x}}_{n},{\underline{u}}_{n})\quad\text{for }n=k,\ldots,k+N_{\mathrm{H}}-1\kern 5.0pt,

(3)

with initial state
{\underline{x}}_{k}
at time step
k
and system function
{\underline{a}}_{n}({\underline{x}}_{n},{\underline{u}}_{n})\colon\mathds{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathds{R}^{d_{u}}\to\mathds{R}^{d_{x}}
.
The optimal control problem is to find the optimal control input sequence
{\underline{u}}_{k}^{*},\ldots,{\underline{u}}_{k+N_{\mathrm{H}}-1}^{*}
that minimizes the cumulative cost
J_{k}
while satisfying system dynamics and additional constraints on states and controls.
The optimal control problem can be solved in a receding horizon fashion using model predictive control, where at each time step
k
, the first control input
{\underline{u}}_{k}^{*}
of the optimal sequence is applied to the system, and the process is repeated at the next time step.

Typically, the optimal control problem is solved using gradient-based approaches such as direct single- or multiple-shooting methods
[
7
]
, differential dynamic programming
[
8
]
, iterative linear quadratic regulator
[
9
]
, or gradient-free methods such as model predictive path integrals
[
10
]
,
or the cross-entropy method
[
4
,
2
]
.
In this work, we focus on cross-entropy method–model predictive control, which is described in more detail below.

CROSS-ENTROPY METHOD MPC

Before introducing the cross-entropy method–model predictive control, we will briefly explain the concept of the cross-entropy method as an optimizer, based on
[
3
,
11
]
.
Note that, to prevent confusion with the control input
{\underline{u}}
and state
{\underline{x}}
, we denote the
d_{{\xi}}
-dimensional optimization variable for the cross-entropy method as
{\underline{\xi}}\in\mathds{R}^{d_{{\xi}}}

III-A

Cross-Entropy Method

The cross-entropy method aims to find a density
{\boldsymbol{\underline{\xi}}}\sim f({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}})
, parameterized by
{\underline{\theta}}
, whose samples
{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}
tend to achieve small costs
J({\underline{\xi}}^{(i)})
.
By setting a performance threshold
\gamma
and defining the event
A_{\gamma}=\{{\underline{\xi}}\mid J({\underline{\xi}})\leq\gamma\}
, i.e., the event of achieving low-cost solutions, the corresponding rare-event probability can be expressed as
[
3
]

\displaystyle l=\operatorname{P}_{f({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}})}\!\big(J({\boldsymbol{\underline{\xi}}})\leq\gamma\big)={\operatorfont{E}}_{f({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}})}\big\{\mathds{1}_{J({\boldsymbol{\underline{\xi}}})\leq\gamma}\big\}\kern 5.0pt,

(4)

where
\mathds{1}_{J({\underline{\xi}})\leq\gamma}
is the indicator function.
By multiplying
\nicefrac{{\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})}}{{\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})}}
within the expectation
eq.

4
, rearranging yields

\displaystyle l={\operatorfont{E}}_{f({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}})}\left\{\mathds{1}_{J({\boldsymbol{\underline{\xi}}})\leq\gamma}\frac{\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})}{\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})}\right\}={\operatorfont{E}}_{\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})}\left\{\mathds{1}_{J({\boldsymbol{\underline{\xi}}})\leq\gamma}\frac{f({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}})}{\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})}\right\}\kern 5.0pt,

(5)

where
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})
is an importance sampling probability density function.
Note that the expectation is now w.r.t. the importance probability density function
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})
.
According to importance sampling theory, the optimal choice of
\tilde{f}^{\ast}({\underline{\xi}})
that minimizes the variance of the estimator is
[
3
]

\displaystyle\tilde{f}^{*}({\underline{\xi}})=\frac{\mathds{1}_{J({\underline{\xi}})\leq\gamma}\,f({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}})}{l}\kern 5.0pt.

(6)

However, this is infeasible because it depends on
l
, which is the quantity we want to estimate using
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})
, and is therefore a-priori unknown.

To overcome this infeasibility, the central idea of cross-entropy method is to
iteratively approximate
the optimal probability density function
\tilde{f}^{*}({\underline{\xi}})
.
At each iteration
j
, we start with a known proposal distribution
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}_{j})
and aim to find a better set of parameters
{\underline{\theta}}_{j+1}
for the next iteration.
This is achieved by finding the parameters
{\underline{\theta}}_{j+1}
that minimize the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the
optimal probability density function derived from the current step,

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{j}^{*}({\underline{\xi}})=\frac{\mathds{1}_{J({\underline{\xi}})\leq\gamma_{j}}\,\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}_{j})}{l_{j}}\kern 5.0pt,

(7)

and the next proposal distribution
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}_{j+1})
.
Formally, the next parameters are given by

\displaystyle{\underline{\theta}}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{f}_{j}^{*}({\underline{\xi}})\|\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime})\right)

(8)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}}H\left(\tilde{f}_{j}^{*}({\underline{\xi}}),\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime})\right)-H\left(\tilde{f}_{j}^{*}({\underline{\xi}})\right)

(9)

where
H(\cdot,\cdot)
is the cross-entropy between both distributions, and
H(\tilde{f}_{j}^{*}({\underline{\xi}}))
is the entropy of
\tilde{f}_{j}^{*}({\underline{\xi}})
.
Since entropy
H(\cdot)
does not depend on
{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}
, minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence is equivalent to minimizing the cross-entropy

\displaystyle{\underline{\theta}}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}}-\int_{\mathds{R}^{d_{{\xi}}}}\tilde{f}_{j}^{\ast}({\underline{\xi}})\log\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime})\operatorname{d}\!{\underline{\xi}}

(10)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}}-\int_{\mathds{R}^{d_{{\xi}}}}\frac{\mathds{1}_{J({\underline{\xi}})\leq\gamma_{j}}\,\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}_{j})}{l_{j}}\log\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime})\operatorname{d}\!{\underline{\xi}}\kern 5.0pt,

(11)

where the unknown constant
l_{j}
can be dropped, as it does not depend on
{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}
.
By rewriting the integral as an expectation with respect to the
known
current probability density function
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}_{j})
, we get

\displaystyle{\underline{\theta}}_{j+1}=\argmin_{{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}}-{\operatorfont{E}}_{\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}_{j})}\big[\mathds{1}_{J({\boldsymbol{\underline{\xi}}})\leq\gamma_{j}}\log\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime})\big]\kern 5.0pt.

(12)

This expectation can be approximated by samples
\{{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}_{j})
.
This yields the objective for the next set of parameters

\displaystyle\hat{{\underline{\theta}}}_{j+1}=\argmin_{{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}_{J({\underline{\xi}}^{(i)})\leq\gamma_{j}}\log\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime})\kern 5.0pt.

(13)

Therefore, the new parameters
{\underline{\theta}}^{\prime}
are fitted to the subset of best samples

\mathcal{E}_{j}=\{{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}\mid J({\underline{\xi}}^{(i)})\leq\gamma_{j}\}
,
i.e., the
elite set
.

For common choices of
\tilde{f}
, the minimization has simple closed-form solutions, e.g., for a Gaussian
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{\theta}})=\mathcal{N}({\underline{\xi}};\hat{{\underline{\xi}}},{{\mathbf{C}}})
, where the solution is the maximum likelihood estimate on the elite set

\displaystyle\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{j}|}\sum_{{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}}{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}\kern 5.0pt,

(14)

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{C}}}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{j}|}\sum_{{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}}({\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}-\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j+1})({\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}-\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j+1})^{\top}\kern 5.0pt,

(15)

with
\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j+1}
and
{{\mathbf{C}}}_{j+1}
being the mean and covariance matrix of the Gaussian proposal summerized in parameter vector
{\underline{\theta}}_{j+1}=(\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j+1},{{\mathbf{C}}}_{j+1})
and the elite set size
|\mathcal{E}_{j}|
.

In each iteration,
\gamma_{j}
is reduced to focus on better-performing samples (i.e., more rare events).
In practice,
\gamma_{j}
is usually not specified directly but rather, the
N_{\mathrm{e}}
-th best cost among the samples is used, e.g., the best
10
samples out of
100
.
After the last iteration
j_{\mathrm{max}}
, the best sample from the final elite set
\mathcal{E}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}
is returned as the solution.
Note that in some variants, the mean
\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}
of the final proposal distribution is returned instead.

In summary, the cross-entropy method is an iterative method that refines the proposal distribution over the solution space, concentrating on regions with lower costs, and effectively guiding the search toward optimal or near-optimal solutions.

III-C

Practical Improvements and Related Work

A major challenge in this basic approach is the high dimensionality of the optimization problem.
While adapting a full covariance matrix can capture correlations, it is prone to estimation errors unless a large number of samples is used.
To address this, a common simplification is to assume a diagonal covariance matrix
{{\mathbf{C}}}_{j}
, e.g., as done in
[
12
]
, which reduces the number of parameters to be estimated and simplifies the sampling process, i.e.,
{{\mathbf{C}}}_{j}=\diag(\sigma_{j,1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{j,d_{u}\cdot N_{\mathrm{H}}}^{2})
, where
\sigma_{j,i}^{2}
is the variance of the
i
-th control input in the flattened sequence.
However, this assumption ignores temporal correlations between control inputs at different time steps, which can be crucial for generating smooth control sequences.

A standard technique to improve convergence smoothness is to introduce a momentum term in the parameter updates
[
11
]
, which smooths the updates across iterations. The momentum update is given by

\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j+1}=\alpha\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j}+(1-\alpha)\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j,\mathrm{e}}
, where
\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j}
is the mean from the previous iteration,
\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j,\mathrm{e}}
is the mean of the current elite set, and
\alpha\in[0,1)
is a momentum factor.
Analogously, the momentum can also be applied to the covariance matrix update
[
11
]
.

A further standard improvement, used in various model predictive control approaches
[
1
]
, is to warm-start the optimization at each time step by shifting the mean control sequence from the previous time step. This is achieved by setting the initial mean to
\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{0}=[{\underline{u}}_{k-1,1}^{\top},\ldots,{\underline{u}}_{k-1,N_{\mathrm{H}}-1}^{\top},{\underline{u}}_{\mathrm{init}}^{\top}]^{\top}
, where
{\underline{u}}_{k-1,i}
is the
i
-th control input from the optimized sequence at the previous time step and
{\underline{u}}_{\mathrm{init}}
is an initial guess for the last control input, often set to zero or the last optimized input.

A notable improvement is the iCEM algorithm
[
2
]
, which introduces temporal correlations in a structured way.
Instead of adopting a full covariance matrix, iCEM samples control actions from colored noise, imposing a smoothness prior on the control sequences.
This is achieved by generating samples from a stationary distribution whose power spectral density follows a power law,
PSD(f)\propto\nicefrac{{1}}{{f^{\beta}}}
, where
f
is frequency and
\beta
controls the noise color.
To further increase sample efficiency, iCEM retains a fraction of the elite set from the previous model predictive control time step, shifting the sequences in time and adding them to the current sample pool.
Other improvements in iCEM include clipping control samples to ensure feasibility and decaying the number of samples over iterations via
N_{\mathrm{CEM},j}=\max(\nicefrac{{N_{\mathrm{CEM}}}}{{\eta^{j}}},2N_{\mathrm{e}})
, where
\eta\geq 1
is a decay factor.
This reduces computational cost as the optimizer converges.
An overview of the iCEM algorithm is provided in
[
2
, Alg. 1]
.

Besides the improvements mentioned above, further enhancements focus on learning the sampling distribution itself, for instance, by using Gaussian processes
[
13
]
or normalizing flows
[
14
]
.

However, in this work, we focus on the sample generation process itself by introducing deterministic sampling to cross-entropy method–model predictive control. This approach is orthogonal to the learning-based improvements and can be combined with them.

IV
Cross-Entropy Method MPC Using Deterministic Samples

As an alternative to random sampling, we propose to use deterministic samples based on the localized cumulative distribution. In this section, we briefly summarize how to obtain such optimal samples and then describe how to integrate them into cross-entropy method–model predictive control.

IV-A

Deterministic Samples Using Localized Cumulative Distributions

The localized cumulative distribution is a multivariate generalization
[
5
]
of the univariate
cumulative density function. It is defined as

\displaystyle F({\underline{m}},b)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}^{d_{{\xi}}}}f({\underline{\xi}})K({\underline{\xi}},{\underline{m}},b)\operatorname{d}\!{\underline{\xi}}\kern 5.0pt,

(16)

where
f({\underline{\xi}})
is a probability density function over
\mathds{R}^{d_{{\xi}}}
, and
K(\cdot,{\underline{m}},b)
is a kernel function centered at
{\underline{m}}\in\mathds{R}^{d_{{\xi}}}
with bandwidth
b\in\mathds{R}_{>0}
. The kernel function is typically chosen as a Gaussian kernel
[
5
]

\displaystyle K({\underline{\xi}},{\underline{m}},b)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\|{\underline{\xi}}-{\underline{m}}\|_{2}^{2}}{2b^{2}}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

(17)

We then obtain a general distance measure between two probability density functions by comparing their respective localized cumulative distributions with a modified Cramér–von Mises distance
[
5
]

\displaystyle D_{\operatorfont CvM}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}w(b)\int_{\mathds{R}^{d_{{\xi}}}}\left(\tilde{F}({\underline{m}},b)-F({\underline{m}},b)\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}\!{\underline{m}}\operatorname{d}\!b\kern 5.0pt,

(18)

where
\tilde{F}({\underline{m}},b)
and
F({\underline{m}},b)
are the localized cumulative distributions of
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})
and
f({\underline{\xi}})
, respectively, and
w(b)
is a weighting function, typically
w(b)=b^{1-d_{{\xi}}}
.
Here we use this distance to compare a Gaussian probability density function
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})
with a Dirac mixture
{f}({\underline{\xi}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta({\underline{\xi}}-{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)})
. By minimizing
D_{\operatorfont CvM}
w.r.t. the sample locations
{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}
we obtain an optimal Dirac mixture approximation of the Gaussian
\tilde{f}({\underline{\xi}})

[
6
]
.

IV-B

Integration of Deterministic Samples into CEM–MPC

The core idea is to use deterministic samples rather than random ones in the cross-entropy method.
To speed up, we use pre-computed deterministic samples since online computation is prohibitive.
Optimal samples
\{\tilde{{\underline{\xi}}}^{(i)}\}
are generated offline for the isotropic standard Gaussian
\mathcal{N}({\underline{\xi}};{\underline{0}},{{\mathbf{I}}})

[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
These samples are then transformed at runtime (
algorithm

2
,
algorithm

2
) to match the current proposal distribution
\mathcal{N}({\underline{\xi}};\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j},{{\mathbf{C}}}_{j})
of the cross-entropy method optimizer via
[
17
]

\displaystyle{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}=\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j}+{{\mathbf{L}}}_{j}\tilde{{\underline{\xi}}}^{(i)}\kern 5.0pt,

(19)

where
{{\mathbf{L}}}_{j}
is the matrix square root of the Gaussian process covariance matrix, i.e.,
{{\mathbf{C}}}_{j}={{\mathbf{L}}}_{j}{{\mathbf{L}}}_{j}^{\top}
.
While this transformation does not preserve the samples’ optimality in the Cramér–von Mises sense
[
16
]
for a non-isotropic target distribution, it provides a practical and efficient way to generate structured samples with low discrepancy.

A naive implementation would use the same transformed set of deterministic samples in every iteration of the optimizer.
However, we found this limits exploration and can lead to premature convergence, especially with a small number of samples.
To address this, we propose three schemes for generating varied sample sets across iterations.

Sample Set Variability Schemes

To enhance exploration, we introduce variability into the sampling process either across iterations or across time steps.

(V1)
Random Rotation
:
In each iteration
j
of the cross-entropy method, the pre-computed isotropic standard Gaussian samples
\{\tilde{{\underline{\xi}}}^{(i)}\}
are rotated by a random rotation matrix
{{\mathbf{R}}}_{j}

before the transformation.
The matrix is drawn from the special orthogonal group
SO(d_{{\xi}})
, which consists of all
d_{{\xi}}\times d_{{\xi}}
orthogonal matrices with a determinant of
+1

[
18
]
.
Therefore the transformation in
eq.

19
changes to
{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}=\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j}+{{\mathbf{L}}}_{j}{{\mathbf{R}}}_{j}\tilde{{\underline{\xi}}}^{(i)}
.
Note that rotating the samples of an isotropic distribution results in another valid sample set without losing their optimality in the Cramér–von Mises sense. This introduces stochasticity, improving exploration at the cost of losing the fully deterministic nature of the algorithm.

(V2)
Deterministic Joint Density Sampling
:
To maintain a fully deterministic algorithm, we pre-compute optimal deterministic samples from a higher-dimensional random vector that encompasses all cross-entropy method iterations, i.e., we generate deterministic samples in dimension
d_{{\xi}}\cdot j_{\mathrm{max}}
.
Each high-dimensional sample
[\tilde{{\underline{\xi}}}^{(i)\top}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{{\underline{\xi}}}^{(i)\top}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}]^{\top}
is then partitioned into
j_{\mathrm{max}}
separate samples of dimension
d_{{\xi}}
, providing a unique, deterministic sample set for each iteration, which are then transformed using
eq.

19
.

(V3)
Combined Approach
: This hybrid scheme balances determinism within the optimization loop with stochastic exploration across time steps. A single set of high-dimensional samples is pre-computed as in (V2). At the beginning of each model predictive control time step
k
, this set is rotated by a single random matrix
{{\mathbf{R}}}_{k}\in SO(d_{{\xi}})
. The resulting samples are then partitioned and used deterministically for all subsequent cross-entropy method iterations within that time step.

Covariance Matrix Structures and Adaptation

Given the sample set by one of the above schemes, the samples are transformed to match the current proposal distribution of the cross-entropy method optimizer using
eq.

19
.
The use of deterministic samples is compatible with, e.g., maintaining a diagonal covariance matrix, or by adapting a full covariance matrix.
We focus on two approaches that incorporate temporal correlations, which are known to be beneficial for generating smooth control sequences
[
2
]
.
The initial correlation structure is derived from the power spectral density of colored noise (e.g., pink noise,
\beta=1
) via the Wiener–Khinchin theorem resulting in a Toeplitz structured correlation matrix
[
19
, pp. 576–578]
.

(M1)
Fixed Correlation with Adaptive Variance
: In this approach, the correlation structure of the proposal distribution is fixed throughout the optimization.
The covariance matrix is constructed as
{{\mathbf{C}}}_{j}=\diag({\underline{\sigma}}_{j}){{\mathbf{C}}}_{\rho}\diag({\underline{\sigma}}_{j})
, where
{{\mathbf{C}}}_{\rho}
is the fixed time-correlation matrix and
{\underline{\sigma}}_{j}
is the vector of marginal standard deviations.
In each iteration, only the marginal variances are updated based on the elite set, which simplifies
eq.

15
to

\displaystyle{\underline{\sigma}}^{2}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{j}|}\sum_{{\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}}({\underline{\xi}}^{(i)}-\hat{{\underline{\xi}}}_{j+1})^{2}\kern 5.0pt.

(20)

This reduces the number of parameters to be estimated and is less demanding on the number of elite samples; a minimum of two non-identical elite samples is sufficient.
The transformation in
eq.

19
then uses

{{\mathbf{L}}}_{j}=\diag({\underline{\sigma}}_{j}){{\mathbf{A}}}_{\rho}
,
where
{{\mathbf{A}}}_{\rho}
is the matrix square root of
{{\mathbf{C}}}_{\rho}
.

(M2)
Adaptive Full Covariance
:
This strategy allows for greater flexibility by adapting the full covariance matrix
{{\mathbf{C}}}_{j}
in each iteration.
The optimization is initialized with a structured covariance matrix based on colored noise as in the previous scheme.
Subsequently, the entire matrix is updated using the maximum likelihood estimate from the elite set.
This allows the optimizer to adapt the temporal correlations online.
However, estimating a full
d_{{\xi}}\times d_{{\xi}}
covariance matrix requires the elite set to contain at least
d_{{\xi}}+1
non-collinear samples
[
20
]
.

The sample set variation schemes (V1–V3) and covariance adaptation methods (M1–M2) can be combined arbitrarily.
For instance, random rotations (V1) can be used with either a fixed correlation structure with adaptive variances (M1) or a full adaptive covariance matrix (M2).
This modularity allows the proposed deterministic sampling strategies to serve as drop-in replacements for the random sampling step in various cross-entropy method–model predictive control algorithms.

V-C

Discussion

The experimental results demonstrate that replacing standard random sampling in cross-entropy method with our proposed deterministic sampling strategies, termed deterministic sampling CEM, leads to significant improvements in performance and sample efficiency.
Across both the mountain car and cart-pole swing-up tasks, the deterministic sampling CEM variants consistently outperform the baseline iCEM method, particularly when the number of samples is limited.

A key finding is the superior control input smoothness achieved by the deterministic sampling CEM methods.
This is especially pronounced for the fully deterministic variant deterministic sampling CEM-Var V2, which consistently yields the smoothest control trajectories.
This outcome is expected, as deterministic sample sets provide low-discrepancy coverage of the sampling space, mitigating the clustering and gaps inherent to random sampling.
Notably, in the cart-pole swing-up task, deterministic sampling CEM produces smoother control inputs than even the extensive iCEM baseline, despite using a fraction of the samples.
This highlights the ability of deterministic sampling to find high-quality, smooth solutions with remarkable efficiency.

In terms of cumulative cost, deterministic sampling CEM shows a clear advantage at lower sample sizes, indicating faster convergence to effective control strategies.
Although all methods tend to converge as sample sizes increase, the gains from deterministic sampling CEM in the low-sample regime are critical for practical applications.

The enhanced sample efficiency of deterministic sampling CEM has profound implications for the deployment of model predictive control.
Reducing the required number of samples lowers computational demand, a critical factor for real-time performance.
This reduction impacts four key stages of the cross-entropy method algorithm:
(i) the creation of random or deterministic samples;
(ii) the determination of the elite set;
(iii) the computationally expensive trajectory shooting, especially for complex system dynamics; and
(iv) the evaluation of cost functions for each trajectory and all its stages.
Furthermore, as hardware generally has limited parallelization capabilities, even a slight reduction in sample size can prevent substantial computational overhead.
For example, on such hardware, an algorithm requiring one more sample than the hardware’s capacity can
double the execution time
.
By achieving superior performance with fewer samples, deterministic sampling CEM promises to apply model predictive control to systems with more complex models and longer prediction horizons without sacrificing real-time capability.

VI
CONCLUSION

In this paper, we addressed the limitations of random sampling in cross-entropy method–model predictive control by introducing deterministic sampling CEM, a novel framework that leverages deterministic samples based on localized cumulative distributions.
We proposed several schemes for integrating these samples, demonstrating that this approach is a modular, drop-in replacement for the conventional sampling step.
Our experimental evaluation on two nonlinear control benchmarks showed that deterministic sampling CEM consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art iCEM method in terms of both cumulative cost and control input smoothness, especially in the critical low-sample regime.
These findings highlight the potential of deterministic sampling to significantly improve the efficiency of cross-entropy method–model predictive control, making it a promising option for real-time control on computationally constrained hardware.

As our approach is orthogonal to learning-based improvements, a viable option is to combine deterministic sampling CEM with these techniques, e.g., using a learned policy to warm-start the optimization process and apply it to higher-dimensional robotic systems.
