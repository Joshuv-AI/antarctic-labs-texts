Lower limb asymmetries in elite junior female vault gymnasts
Authors: Zhengyang Zhao, Xiaomei Wei, Yuanyan Ma, Zhenke Tan, Haowen Wang, Yiman Qin, Mengge Fu, Sijun Li, Xiaofeng Ren, Yingjie Chen, Yichuan Dai, Jing Guo, Yingjun Nie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDue to long-term repetitive training of specific techniques, competitive vaulting gymnasts are prone to develop lower limb functional asymmetry. The symptom of lower limb functional asymmetry can seriously impair vaulting performance and is a major contributing factor to sports-related injuries. This study aimed to analyze the effect of key factors, including functional asymmetry in explosive power and balance ability among elite junior female vaulters, on run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force. We conducted a series of assessments on twenty elite junior female vault specialists using the Noraxon MR3 wireless surface electromyography system, Kistler 3D force plates, and Tci-System infrared velocimetry. The assessments include countermovement jump (CMJ), Y-balance test (YBT), surface electromyography (sEMG), and vault trials. We employed paired-sample t-tests to analyze bilateral differences between dominant limb and non-dominant limb in jumping ground reaction force, balance, and muscle activation contributions. Pearson correlation analysis revealed the relationship between the bilateral asymmetry index (BAI) and vaulting run-up velocity or jumping ground reaction force. Segmented regression was employed to investigate the potential threshold effects of BAI on run-up velocity and jump ground reaction force. (1) We observed significant inter-limb differences in CMJ power production (P < 0.05), particularly in the vertical direction where the asymmetry index reached approximately 18.35%. (2) The YBT reach distance and center of pressure (COP) displacement both exhibited significant bilateral asymmetry (P < 0.05). The balance BAI were 3.25%, 3.04%, and 3.35% for the anterior (A), posteromedial (PM), and posterolateral (PL) directions. (3) The gluteus maximus showed significant inter-limb differences in muscle contribution rate (P < 0.05), while the rectus femoris, hamstrings, tibialis anterior, and gastrocnemius exhibited significant differences (P < 0.01). (4) The CMJ power asymmetry index exceeding 10.4% showed significant differences in jumping ground reaction force (P < 0.05), while exceeding 14.8% demonstrated significant differences in run-up velocity (P < 0.05). The YBT balance BAI showed no significant relationships with either jumping ground reaction force or run-up velocity (P > 0.05). Conclusion: Lower-limb power and balance asymmetries in elite junior female gymnasts exhibited measurable threshold ranges Significant disparities exist in muscle contribution rates between dominant limb and non-dominant limb. Introduction

Inter-limb asymmetry, which refers to performance differences between one limb and the other, has received considerable research attention in recent years1,2. Multiple classification systems have been established to quantify these inter-limb differences, including dominant limb vs. non-dominant limb3,4. Lower limb asymmetry encompasses three types: range-of-motion asymmetry, force asymmetry, and skill execution asymmetry5.

Lower limb asymmetry may lead to negative impacts on performance in sport-specific skills if exceeding certain thresholds6,7. Higher lower limb asymmetry may significantly impair run-up velocity, jumping ground reaction force, and movement execution quality in athletes2,8,9. A 10% threshold is widely adopted for defining clinically meaningful asymmetry in maximal strength and serves as a common criterion for return-to-play decisions in athletes10,11. However, studies have demonstrated that even small differences (as low as 5%) may impair physical performance in activities such as jumping and sprinting12,13.

Studies have demonstrated that lower limb power is a key determinant of run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force, and contributes significantly to the development of advanced skills14,15. The run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force is a compulsory element for female gymnasts aged 8–9 in Chinese youth competitions, and they serve as crucial foundational skills for developing advanced and difficult vault maneuvers among reserve competitive gymnastics talents16. However, due to repetitive sport-specific training that promotes limb dominance and asymmetric musculoskeletal development, adolescent female athletes may develop symptoms associated with lower-limb asymmetries8,16. There is a relationship between asymmetry and both run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force, then a potential threshold should be explored17,18. It remains unclear what critical value of lower limb asymmetry affects run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force during jumping for junior female vault gymnasts19.

The study hypothesized that (a) power and balance asymmetries may exist in elite junior female vault gymnasts, (b) power asymmetry within a certain threshold range exerts an effect on run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force, (c) balance asymmetry exerts an effect on run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force, and (d) there are differences in the muscle contribution rate between the lower limbs. To test this hypothesis, The study examined lower-limb power and balance in elite junior female vaulters to analyze asymmetry and its effects on run-up velocity or jumping ground reaction force, which provides scientific training insights for youth gymnastics.

Procedures

Countermovement jump test (CMJ)

The CMJ test was performed using a triaxial force platform (Kistler 9286BA, Switzerland; 1000 Hz) to quantitatively evaluate lower limb explosive power. Prior to testing, participants completed a 10–15 min warm-up protocol emphasizing lower extremity muscle activation. The CMJ test was performed with participants standing on bilateral force plates, maintaining a hands-on-hips position to isolate the lower extremity contribution20,21. Participants were instructed to jump to the maximum possible height, and verbal encouragement was provided before each trial22. Three bilateral CMJ testing were performed with a 60-second inter-trial rest interval to ensure sufficient neuromuscular recovery. The jumping ground reaction force during each trial were recorded using BioWare software (Kistler Group, Switzerland). The study quantified three-dimensional ground reaction forces (GRF) during the take-off phase, with the maximal value selected as the representative data for final statistical analysis. Specifically, FX represents the anterior-posterior horizontal force, FY denotes the mediolateral horizontal force, and FZ corresponds to the vertical force applied during take-off.

Y-Balance test (YBT)

The YBT kit (Functional Movement Screen, USA) was precisely positioned at the center of a three-dimensional force platform and synchronized with the motion capture system’s global coordinate frame. All tests were conducted in a dedicated laboratory with a consistent, non-slip surface. A standardized verbal instruction and demonstration protocol was used for all participants to ensure consistent understanding and effort. The same certified researcher supervised all testing sessions to guarantee procedural uniformity. The experimental protocol required participants to maintain a single-limb barefoot on the platform with hands placed on the iliac crests, as they performed maximal reach movements with the contralateral limb in three predetermined direction: anterior (A), posteromedial (PM), and posterolateral (PL) directions. The YBT quantitatively assesses dynamic postural stability in the lower limbs by analyzing both absolute and normalized reach distance data. The primary evaluation metrics consist of bilateral reach distance asymmetry where a difference >4 cm is considered clinically significant and COP displacement characteristics23. Three valid trials per direction were recorded with 60-second seated rest intervals between trials. The mean reach distance for each direction was calculated for subsequent statistical analysis (accurate to 0.5 cm).

Surface electromyography measurement (sEMG)

The vault test were assessed using a Noraxon system (USA, 1500 Hz). Prior to experimental testing, a standardized protocol was implemented for electrode placement on the target muscles (gluteus maximus, rectus femoris, hamstrings, tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius). The skin was shaved and cleansed with 75% alcohol to reduce impedance. Electrodes were positioned at standardized locations via anatomical palpation and measurements, with functional verification through submaximal contractions to confirm optimal placement and signal quality. Disposable pre-gelled electrodes were applied in a bipolar configuration, secured with waterproof tape, and their positions were marked on the skin for consistent placement. All testing sessions were conducted in a standardized environment. Prior to formal data collection, all participants completed a prescribed warm-up routine. We employed a standardized protocol to capture electromyographic (EMG) data from maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) and the entire vaulting event, with trials separated by 2-minute rest intervals to mitigate fatigue24. Raw data were processed in MATLAB, which included rectification, normalization, and band-pass filtering (20–400 Hz) to remove motion artifacts and noise. Processed data were expressed as integrated EMG (iEMG) and muscle contribution rate. Muscle contribution ratio refers to the proportion of the iEMG value of each individual muscle to the total iEMG value of all involved muscles, where the total iEMG value included bilateral gluteus maximus, rectus femoris, hamstrings, tibialis anterior, and gastrocnemius25. A higher value of this ratio indicates a greater influence of the corresponding muscle on the completion of the movement.

Vaulting run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force measurement

The vault (Figure Code 3.302) is a compulsory skill for Chinese junior female gymnasts in competitions. The run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force constitute the most critical components for successful execution. For measurement purposes, two force platforms were positioned on either side of the springboard, while Tci-System infrared velocimeter were installed at 0 m, 5 m, 10 m, and 15 m intervals26. Participants completed three vault trials under supervision by two professional coaches and one national judge, who concurrently validated the technical success of each attempt (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to assess data normality. Paired-samples t-tests were performed to analyze differences in CMJ results, YBT performance, and muscle activation contributions between dominant limb and non-dominant limb. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between the lower limb bilateral asymmetry index (BAI) and both run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force during vault performance. Segmented regression was performed to compare run-up velocity and jump ground reaction force across different BAI ranges in vaulting athletes. Statistical significance was determined with p-values less than 0.05. The Bonferroni correction was applied to control for Type I error in the context of multiple comparisons. Results

Determination of limb dominance

In this study, the dominant limb was operationally defined as the take-off preference, identified as the lower limb spontaneously and consistently used during the vaulting take-off phase following a high-speed run-up4. This determination was made through direct observation by a panel comprising experienced coaches, national-level gymnastics judges, and researchers, ensuring all participants exhibited a stable lateral preference for the task. We contend that for highly specialized skills such as vaulting, this task-functional definition more accurately reflects the neuromuscular control patterns directly relevant to performance than definitions based on generalized strength tests.

Countermovement jump test (CMJ)

Lower limb explosive power was assessed via CMJ test. Force platforms recorded triaxial peak ground reaction forces bilaterally, with net peak vertical ground reaction force calculated to quantify inter-limb muscle activation asymmetry27,28.

The CMJ demonstrated mean ground reaction force asymmetry index of 13.84% in the X-direction, 11.12% in the Y-direction, and 18.35% in the Z-direction (Table 2). The symbols represent sample sizes within different threshold ranges. Significant differences were observed in ground reaction force between dominant limb and non-dominant limb along the X- and Y-axes (P < 0.05), with more pronounced differences along the Z-axis (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2). Given that vaulting performance primarily relies on vertical force generation during board contact, the Z-axis asymmetry index was selected as the primary indicator of lower limb functional asymmetry in this study.

Y-Balance test (YBT)

The YBT test assessed balance asymmetry via bilateral comparisons of normalized raw distances (AT/PM/PL) and COP dynamics. Reach performance was normalized to limb length (%). COPx/y represented peak-to-peak displacements in mediolateral/anteroposterior axes.

The YBT test results demonstrated a consistent directional pattern in both dominant limb and non-dominant limb, with reach distances exhibiting the greatest extension in the PL direction, followed by PM and A reaches. Asymmetrical performance, defined as an inter-limb reach distance difference exceeding 4 cm, was observed in 7 participants (31.81%) for both A and PL directions, and 8 participants (36.36%) for the PM direction. The normalized BAI, calculated as the percentage difference in standardized reach distances between limbs, were quantified as 3.25% (A), 3.04% (PM), and 3.35% (PL) across the three movement planes.

Analysis of COP displacements revealed systematic differences between dominant limb and non-dominant limb during YBT test performance. In the COPx, the dominant limb exhibited displacements of 0.29 ± 0.08 m (A), 0.32 ± 0.07 m (PM), and 0.34 ± 0.08 m (PL), while the non-dominant limb showed greater variability with corresponding values of 0.33 ± 0.11 m, 0.33 ± 0.13 m, and 0.38 ± 0.15 m. Similarly, COPy displacements for the dominant limb measured 0.19 ± 0.06 m (A), 0.24 ± 0.06 m (PM), and 0.27 ± 0.07 m (PL), compared to 0.23 ± 0.09 m, 0.26 ± 0.12 m, and 0.31 ± 0.14 m for the non-dominant limb. Discussion

Analysis of lower limb power asymmetry

This study revealed prevalent lower-limb power asymmetry in vaulting athletes, with a mean asymmetry index of 18.35%. Available literature has shown that asymmetry may originate from neural factors (sensory information) and long-term technical training bias28. For instance, preferential reliance on the dominant limb during training leads to lateralization and imbalanced musculoskeletal development, resulting in power deficits on the non-dominant limb side15.

Extensive research indicates that CMJ asymmetry affects lower-limb power beyond specific thresholds. When exceeding these thresholds, power asymmetry directly impairs jump performance, with greater asymmetries correlating with reduced jump capacity and power output16,26. Bell demonstrated through CMJ testing that BAI exceeding 10% lead to decreased explosive power and jump height29. We provide empirical validation of this perspective by demonstrating that a BAI exceeding 10.4% significantly affects Jump Ground Reaction Force (JGRF) metrics. The non-significant relationship below 10.4% BAI indicates effective neuromuscular compensation during force production. This aligns with the concept of functional asymmetry, where minor inter-limb differences can be accommodated without performance degradation. However, the significant force reduction beyond this threshold suggests compromised force transmission and motor unit recruitment patterns. Vaulters’ jumping ground reaction force differs fundamentally from conventional vertical jumps, requiring both kinetic energy generation and horizontal-to-vertical velocity conversion. Jumping ground reaction force is modulated by board contact technique, skill difficulty, and body positioning angles. At lower CMJ asymmetry indices, athletes may compensate through self-regulation and takeoff technique to mitigate impacts on jumping ground reaction force. However, excessive asymmetry impairs force generation in the weaker limb, reducing total jumping ground reaction force. This indirectly compromises vaulting flight height and hang time, degrading overall performance quality, and increasing injury risks. Consequently, training regimens for elite junior female gymnasts should incorporate targeted power development of the non-dominant limb alongside conventional bilateral exercises to address these neuromuscular imbalances.

Furthermore, our results revealed a correlation between power BAI exceeding 14.8% and run-up velocity. Previous studies have demonstrated that lower limb power asymmetry below 15% shows no significant association with sprint performance over short distances18, whereas excessive CMJ asymmetry adversely affects sprinting capability18,24. Below the threshold of approximately 14.8%, lower-limb asymmetry appears to be well within the compensatory capacity of the neuromuscular system. The absence of a significant negative effect on velocity in this range suggests that minor asymmetries may be functionally irrelevant or effectively mitigated by inherent motor control strategies. However, exceeding this 14.8% threshold appears to overwhelm these compensatory mechanisms, leading to a significant degradation in performance, as quantified by vault run-up velocity. The substantial improvement in model fit strongly supports the threshold-based approach over traditional linear models. Vault run-up velocity fundamentally differs from linear sprinting by emphasizing optimized velocity distribution across critical intervals, requiring athletes to rhythmically modulate their 15-meter run-up. This emphasis on phased velocity modulation may mitigate the impact of moderate lower limb power asymmetry within certain thresholds, consistent with Raya-González et al.‘s findings that functional asymmetry below critical levels does not impair sprint performance30.

Analysis of lower limb balance asymmetry

The present study identified varying degrees of balance asymmetry in elite junior female gymnasts. The dominant limb showed greater reach distances in A, PM, and PL directions, along with superior COPx and COPy stability compared to the non-dominant limb. The balance asymmetry may associate with lower-limb power asymmetry and neuromuscular control deficits. Extensive evidence demonstrates that superior balance capacity enhances the execution of difficult vaulting maneuvers in gymnasts31,32.

This study innovatively examined the association between lower-limb asymmetry and run-up velocity or jumping ground reaction force. The YBT asymmetry was 3.25% (A), 3.04% (PM), and 3.35% (PL), showing no significant correlation with run-up velocity or jumping ground reaction force. This observation can be attributed to several factors: Firstly, the investigation focused exclusively on elite junior female vault gymnasts, whose long-term, specialized training develops highly advanced and uniform dynamic balance. This population characteristic led to restricted variability in balance asymmetry scores, which in turn diminished the likelihood of observing statistically significant associations with performance outcomes. Secondly, the absence of a significant correlation could stem from the low magnitude of asymmetry, which allows for effective compensation within a functional range. Compounding this, vaulting performance is primarily governed by the ability to generate maximal ground reaction forces during its explosive take-off phase. In this task-specific environment, the influence of dynamic balance can be masked, as robust lower-limb strength and joint stiffness can offset asymmetry without impairing run-up speed or take-off force. Thirdly, Elite junior vaulters not yet performing difficult vaults show less pronounced demands on lower limb balance during rhythmic run-up velocity and jumping ground reaction force generation. Fort-Vanmeerhaeghe found no correlation between lower limb balance asymmetry and run-up velocity. However, higher vault difficulty requires greater approach speeds. Excessive lower limb balance asymmetry alters gait mechanics, impairing dynamic stability and overall performance. This also increases lower-extremity joint loading and injury risk33. Furthermore, good dynamic balance facilitates explosive power development. Combined training integrating lower limb explosive power and balance capacity demonstrates more significant power gains34.

Muscle contribution rate analysis

Coordinated bilateral muscle activation is critical for maintaining symmetrical lower limb power and balance capacity28. This study found greater muscle contribution in the dominant limb compared to the non-dominant limb among elite junior female vaulters, particularly in the gluteus maximus, rectus femoris, hamstrings, tibialis anterior, and gastrocnemius. This disparity likely results from preferential dominant limb activation during skill execution, with reduced non-dominant limb muscle recruitment. Such differential muscle contribution patterns may constitute a key mechanism underlying lower limb asymmetry35. Furthermore, lower limb asymmetry is a recognized risk factor for sports-related injuries. These findings may be attributed to pronounced inter-athlete variability in individual characteristics, as well as divergent effects of asymmetries in power, balance, and sport-specific technical proficiency on injury mechanisms.

Differences in biological maturation were incorporated as a potential confounding factor. In cases of significant asymmetry, we considered rapid growth periods as a plausible cause, wherein neuromuscular control may lag behind structural growth. It is recommended that training interventions for elite adolescent female vaulters prioritize the improvement of neuromuscular function, focusing specifically on the activation and strength development of the non-dominant limb36. Incorporating strategies such as loaded plyometric exercises and a “joint-by-joint” training approach can effectively reduce bilateral asymmetry, thereby optimizing overall athletic performance37.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the increasing demands of vaulting difficulty, including higher run-up speeds and greater jump ground reaction forces, may alter the critical threshold at which lower-limb strength and balance asymmetries impact performance in elite junior female vault gymnasts. While the present findings are specifically applicable to elite adolescent gymnasts, future research should aim to develop a more universal interpretive framework for asymmetry. This necessitates studies that encompass athletes across skill levels, including sub-elite populations, and employ longitudinal designs to track the evolution of asymmetries in relation to athletic development. Secondly, while this study quantified asymmetries using CMJ, YBT, and sEMG and employed segmented regression to analyze their associations with run-up speed and take-off force, the testing and statistical methodologies were limited by standardization errors. Thirdly, the muscle contribution ratio is calculated as the proportion of an individual muscle’s iEMG value to the total iEMG value of all involved muscles (including the gluteus maximus, rectus femoris, hamstrings, tibialis anterior, and gastrocnemius). Notably, this ratio is highly dependent on the selected set of muscles and does not directly represent the overall muscle activation intensity of the limb. Therefore, future research warrants the validation and extension of these findings through the integration of more advanced biomechanical technologies and the implementation of more standardized analytical methods38.

Conclusion

Elite junior female vault gymnasts exhibit measurable bilateral asymmetry in lower limb explosive power and balance capacity. Dominant limb and non-dominant limb show significant differences in muscle activation contribution rates. Threshold effects were identified: explosive power asymmetry exceeding 10.4% correlates with reduced jumping ground reaction force, while asymmetry surpassing 14.8% associates with decreased run-up velocity. No significant impact of balance asymmetry on vaulting run-up velocity or jumping ground reaction force was observed. It is recommended that training interventions address functional lower-limb asymmetry through scientifically designed compensatory power training. A key component should be loaded plyometric exercises, such as unilateral and bilateral contrast drills, specifically targeting the non-dominant limb. This targeted approach can effectively mitigate bilateral asymmetries, leading to subsequent enhancements in run-up velocity and jump ground reaction force performance during vaulting.
