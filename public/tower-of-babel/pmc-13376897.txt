Atomistic insights into fluoride ion conduction in Ba1−xSnxF2 from 19F PFG-NMR studies
Authors: Arunkumar Dorai, Reiji Takekawa, Atsushi Mineshige, Miwa Murakami, Takahisa Omata, Junichi Kawamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Currently, a variety of solid F− conductors are being considered as potential electrolytes for the development of all-solid-state fluoride-ion batteries. Among those, PbSnF4 type solid F− conductors are promising due to their high ionic conductivity at ambient temperatures. Here we have developed a PbSnF4-type solid electrolyte Ba1−xSnxF2 (x = 0.54) (BSF), which exhibits high ionic conductivity of approximately 9 × 10−3 Scm−1 at 298 K. In this study, we investigated the origin of its high conductivity and the underlying ion conduction mechanism using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. Using 19F static and magic angle spinning (MAS)-NMR analyses it is revealed that the fluoride ions residing in Sn-rich environment of BSF are highly mobile, and they primarily contribute to the ionic conductivity. Also, the diffusion coefficient (DNMR) measured by pulsed field gradient (PFG)-NMR technique is estimated to be 6 × 10–12 m2 s−1 at 298 K which is higher than the diffusion coefficient of some well-known lithium ion conducting solid electrolytes. These findings indicate that by using NMR spectroscopic techniques atomistic insights into the ion conduction mechanism and the dynamics could be evaluated successfully in solid F− conductors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49832-1.

Introduction

All-solid-state batteries based on fluoride shuttles are promising candidates for commercial rechargeable batteries with higher energy densities compared to those of the currently available battery systems. The fluoride shuttle battery (FSB) functions through the reduction and oxidation of a metal as the anode and metal fluoride as the cathode, releasing electrons per metal atom. An energy density of up to 5000 Wh/L can be achieved by a suitable selection of anode and cathode materials, which is greater than that of any other battery system under consideration1. The first prototype FSB was proposed by Schoonman in 19762, and FSB research attracted attention following the work by Reddy et al. reported in 20113. Various fluoride-ion-conducting solid electrolytes with high ionic conductivities and good chemical stabilities have been considered for FSB development4–6. To date, most of the developed solid-state FSBs have operated at 423 K owing to the lower ionic conductivity of the solid electrolyte at ambient temperature3,7–9. Accordingly, further development of fluoride-ion-conducting solid electrolytes that can operate at lower temperatures is a critical requirement for realizing FSBs operating at ambient temperatures.

Several fluoride-ion-conducting solid electrolytes have been developed, which can be classified based on their crystal structure into fluorite-type, tysonite-type, perovskite-type, and PbSnF4-type materials10,11. Among these, PbSnF4 materials have been reported to show high ionic conductivity (~ 2.5 × 10–2 Scm−1) at room temperature12–15. Along with PbSnF4, BaSnF4-based compounds have been reported to be excellent ion conductors at room temperature (~ 1 × 10–4 Scm−1)16,17. Different approaches have been used to increase the ionic conductivity of BaSnF4, such as varying the Ba-Sn ratio in Ba1−xSnxF2 and modifications of synthesis procedures17–19. Further doping of the Ba site in BaSnF4 with isovalent and aliovalent atoms was considered to increase the ionic conductivity of Ba1−xMxSnF4 (M = Mn, Nd, Ce)20–22. Recently, Mori et al. have successfully achieved a high fluoride ion conductivity of 4 × 10–3 Scm−1 at room temperature by varying the Sn fraction in Ba1−xSnxF2 from 0.5 to 0.5323. The high ionic conductivity of Ba1−xSnxF2 and its increased chemical stability are advantageous for its use as an electrolyte in FSBs24.

Ionic diffusivity in solid electrolyte plays a key role in determining the battery operational performance and thus information about ionic diffusivity is necessary for selection of solid electrolytes in a battery system. Various experimental techniques can be used for the measurement of fluoride ion (F−) diffusion coefficients in solids25–33,33 and among those, pulsed-field gradient (PFG) NMR has been demonstrated to be an efficient technique for the evaluation of diffusion coefficients in solid electrolytes34–38. To date, diffusion coefficients of fluoride ion-conducting solid electrolytes evaluated using field gradient (FG)–NMR have been reported in only a few studies. Most fluoride ion diffusion coefficient measurements are performed at temperatures above 573 K25,26,28–32, primarily due to the low ionic conductivity of these materials at ambient temperatures (See Table 1). As stated earlier, among the fluoride ion conducting solid electrolytes, PbSnF4-type materials exhibit high ionic conductivity (~ 10–3 Scm–1) at ambient temperatures10,11. However, even for PbSnF4, the diffusion coefficient measured by PFG-NMR is reported at high temperatures only (3 × 10–10 m2 s–1 at 573 K)39. In addition, the atomistic details and the dynamic process governing high fluoride ion conduction in Ba1−xSnxF2, remain to be fully resolved. Previously, the fluoride ion conduction mechanism in Ba1−xSnxF2 (x = 0.5) was evaluated by Chaudari et al. using temperature dependent NMR spectral analysis and they have reported that the fluoride ions residing in the Ba-rich environment was immobile at ambient temperatures. Moreover from 2D exchange NMR analysis the mobility of the fluoride ions in the Ba-rich environment is reported to be nearly 10 orders of magnitude lower than the fluoride ions in the Sn-rich environment (μF(Sn) >> μF(Ba)) indicating that the fluoride ions in the Sn-rich environment to be the major contributor to the conductivity40. Apart from this using neutron diffraction (thermal factor) analysis on Ba1−xSnxF2 (x = 0.53), Mori et al., have identified that the fluoride ions residing in the Sn-rich environment (Ba–Sn layer) are more mobile and based on this they have proposed that the ion conduction in Ba1−xSnxF2 (x = 0.53) to take place through the zig–zag network motion of the fluoride ions in the Ba–Sn layers23. However, there are no reports that have directly investigated the ion dynamics and conduction mechanism in Ba1−xSnxF2. As mentioned earlier, the diffusion coefficient measurement by PFG-NMR is highly dependent on the spin–lattice (T1) and spin–spin (T2) relaxation time of the materials. Remarkably, the T1 and T2 relaxation time for Ba1−xSnxF2 (x = 0.54) (BSF) is long enough to measure the diffusion coefficient in BSF using 19F PFG-NMR. By combining the PFG-NMR and ionic conductivity measurement results, we aim to clarify the reason for the high conductivity and elucidate the underlying ion conduction mechanism in Ba1−xSnxF2.

Experimental methods

A Ba1−xSnxF2 (x = 0.54) (BSF) solid electrolyte was prepared by mechanical milling. The procedure for sample preparation was described in detail in our previous report23. Samples of the same batch were used for conductivity and NMR measurements. AC impedance measurements were conducted using Keysight E4990A impedance analyzer in the frequency range between 100 Hz and 100 MHz with the ac voltage set at 20 mV. A sample pellet with 91% relative density is prepared and platinum electrodes were sputtered on both sides. Then the sample is loaded to Qualtec ZF003 impedance cell in an argon-filled glove box and taken out for measurements. The impedance measurement was carried out in the temperature range 233–333 K.

The prepared polycrystalline BSF solid electrolyte sample was transferred to φ-5 mm NMR glass tubes (Shigemi, Japan) under Ar atmosphere inside a glove box. The measurements of the 19F NMR spectra and diffusion coefficients were performed using a Bruker Avance 400 NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) equipped with a Bruker Diff60 gradient probe operating at a resonance frequency of 376.5 MHz. 19F NMR spectra, spin–lattice (T1) and spin–spin (T2) relaxation times were measured using single-pulse, inversion-recovery and Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (cpmg) pulse sequences, respectively. A fluorobenzene (C6H5F) solution was used as an external standard for referencing the 19F chemical shift (δiso = − 113.15 ppm). NMR spectra were acquired with a delay time equivalent to five times the T1 relaxation time. The magic-angle spinning (MAS) NMR spectrum was observed by using a JEOL ECA600 NMR spectrometer operating at 14 T. The resonance frequency for 19F was 564.7 MHz. Results and discussion

Ionic conductivity

The ionic conductivity of BSF was investigated using ac impedance measurements over a wide temperature range (233–333 K). The impedance profile (Nyquist plot) of BSF at different temperatures is shown in Fig. 1a. The Nyquist plot at low temperatures (< 273 K) exhibits a distorted semicircle in the high frequency region and a spike in the low frequency region (Fig. 1b), which corresponds to contributions from the bulk/grain boundary and electrode, respectively. The bulk and grain boundary resistances were determined through equivalent‑circuit modeling of the experimental data (see Supplementary Fig. S1), which allowed us to distinguish their respective contributions based on the associated capacitance values (bulk ~ 10–12 F, grain boundary ~ 10–9 F). At temperatures > 283 K, the impedance which includes bulk and grain boundary resistances, was determined from the intercept of the electrode spike on the Z’-axis. The calculated bulk and the total ionic conductivities within the temperature range measured are presented in Fig. 1c. The total ionic conductivity of BSF at 298 K was found to be 9.2 × 10–3 Scm−1. Moreover, extrapolation of the bulk ionic conductivity data toward higher temperatures (> 283 K) indicates that the bulk ionic conductivity at room temperature is 16 mScm−1 (Fig. 1c) which is close to PbSnF4 (See Supplementary Fig. S2)12. Temperature-dependent 19F NMR

The XRD pattern (see Supplementary Fig. S4) of Ba1−xSnxF2 (x = 0.54) shows narrow peaks, which could be indexed to the crystalline tetragonal phase. Also, a slight secondary phase is also observed which can be indexed to cubic phase of Ba0.5Sn0.5F2. The structure of tetragonal BaSnF4 is shown in Fig. 2a where the Ba and Sn atoms are ordered in alternative double layers along [0 0 1] direction of the unit cell and the fluoride ions reside in two anion sub-lattices, one in the Ba–Ba layers and the other one in the Ba–Sn layers, respectively23. For clarity, we refer to fluoride ions residing in the Ba–Ba and Ba–Sn layers as “Ba‑rich” and “Sn‑rich,” respectively. The 19F MAS-NMR spectrum of BSF measured at room temperature (Fig. 2b) consists mainly of four peaks at 3, − 11, − 30, and − 41 ppm. The 19F NMR resonances observed at 3 and -41 ppm corresponds to the fluoride ions residing in the Ba-rich and Sn-rich environment respectively, which is consistent with those reported for BaSnF440,42. Further, in the present sample, Sn content is higher (0.54) compared to Ba (0.46). Sn is partially incorporated in the Ba-Ba layer, and the 19F resonance signal observed at − 30 ppm is attributed to the fluoride ions residing near the Sn incorporated into the Ba-Ba layer. The peak observed at − 11 ppm is attributable to fluoride ions in the cubic phase of BaSnF4 which is present as a minor secondary phase as confirmed from the XRD data (Supplementary Fig. S4)23. The 19F NMR spectrum of BSF measured at room temperature under static conditions is presented in Fig. 2c, where a single resonance peak is observed at ~ -43 ppm. The temperature dependence of the full width at half maxima (ΔF) of the 19F NMR spectra for BSF is shown in Fig. S3 together with the ΔF of PbSnF4. From Fig. S3, the ΔF for BSF shows a plateau with respect to temperature (extreme narrowing region) similar to ΔF of PbSnF4, indicating that the fast translational motion of the F- ions. Also, ΔF ~ 2 kHz observed at ambient temperature for BSF, indicates that the dipolar interaction between the fluoride ions to be partially averaged owing to the fast fluoride ion motion35,38. Additionally, the 19F spin–lattice relaxation time (T1) for BSF was measured between 298 and 333 K. The T1 values are observed to decrease with increasing temperature (Supplementary Table S1) indicating increase in mobility with respect to temperature. These results confirm that fast translational motion of the F- ions occur even at lower temperatures.

According to Chaudari et al., for BaSnF4, the fluoride ion residing in the Ba-rich environment is reported to be strongly confined to its site at ambient temperatures and the mobility of these fluoride ions is nearly 10 orders of magnitude lower than that of the fluoride ions in the Sn-rich environment40. Hence, appreciable 19F–19F dipolar broadening is envisaged for the three peaks observed under MAS condition (Fig. 2b) at 3, − 11, and − 30 ppm, leading to the absence of these signals under static condition. In other words, only the signal from the fluoride ions residing in the Sn rich environment (− 41 ppm under MAS condition) is appreciable under static condition.

Further, the total fluoride ion concentration for tetragonal Ba1−xSnxF2 (x = 0.54) can be estimated by summing up the peak intensities of the 19F resonances at (3, − 30 and − 41 ppm). From the peak-fitting analysis of 19F MAS-NMR spectra, the relative intensities of the peaks corresponding to fluoride ions in the Ba–Sn layer (− 41 ppm) and the Ba–Ba layer (3 and − 30 ppm) were determined to be 78% and 22%, respectively, consistent with the expected 3:1 ratio of fluoride ions residing in Sn‑rich versus Ba‑rich environments (Fig. 2a). Our 19F MAS-NMR results are in good agreement with neutron diffraction results on a similar composition of Ba1−xSnxF2 (x = 0.53), where it has been confirmed that the ratio of the fluoride ions residing in the Ba-Sn and Ba-Ba layers is 3:123.

Here, to calculate the number density of ions, we used the structural parameters for Ba1−xSnxF2 from Ref.23. The F– diffusion coefficient measured by PFG-NMR reflects the mobility of fluoride ions located in the Sn-rich environment, which corresponds to 3 out of the 4 fluoride ions present in the BSF unit cell as confirmed from our MAS-NMR results (Fig. 2c). Based on these observations, we have calculated the number density (n) with 3 fluoride ions (see supplementary note 1) and calculated the diffusion coefficient from ionic conductivity (Dσ,total) data using Eq. 5 and shown in Fig. 4 (green open circles), together with the values of the diffusion coefficient (DNMR) measured by PFG-NMR (red squares). Both DNMR and Dσ,total show Arrhenius-type thermally activated temperature-dependent behavior. Nevertheless, the diffusion coefficient (Dσ,total) calculated from the total ionic conductivity is lower than the diffusion coefficient (DNMR) measured using PFG-NMR (DNMR ~ 1.6 × Dσ,total). Moreover, from the ionic conductivity results (Fig. 1c), a contribution from the bulk of the electrolyte to the ionic conductivity is observed at temperatures below room temperature. Therefore, we calculated the diffusion coefficient corresponding to the bulk ionic conductivity (Dσ,Bulk) using Eq. 5 (Fig. 4 (blue open triangles)) and compared it with the diffusion coefficient measured by PFG-NMR (DNMR). It is clear from Fig. 4 that the diffusion coefficient calculated from bulk ionic conductivity data (Dσ,Bulk) to be greater (blue dashed line) than the diffusion coefficient measured by PFG-NMR (Dσ,Bulk > DNMR) when extrapolated to higher temperatures. This indicates that the fluoride-ion diffusion coefficient measured using PFG-NMR primarily reflects the contribution from the bulk (grain) of the material. The higher diffusion coefficient value for the bulk ionic conductivity compared with the measured diffusion coefficient indicates that fluoride ion conduction occurs through the correlated motion of the fluoride ions in BSF35,36,38. As shown in Fig. 2a, fluoride ions reside in 3 sites, one in the Ba-Ba (Ba-rich, F2) layer and the other two in the Ba-Sn (Sn-rich, F1 & F3) layer. Based on our results, we propose a fluoride‑ion migration mechanism in which an F1‑site fluoride hops to a neighboring F3 site, triggering the F3‑site ion to move into an adjacent F1 site. This sequence produces a non‑collinear, correlated (–F1–F3–F1–) motion involving multiple fluoride ions, giving rise to the two‑dimensional fast F⁻ diffusion in BSF23.

In this work, we succeeded in measuring the fluoride ion diffusion coefficient in BSF, a superionic conductor using 19F PFG-NMR technique at room temperature which is a first. Also, we have directly identified that the high conductivity observed for BSF is due to the correlated motion of the fluoride ions residing in the Sn-rich environment in BSF.

Conclusion

Ba1−xSnxF2 (x = 0.54) is found to exhibit very high fluoride ion conductivity of 9.2 × 10–3 S cm−1 at 298 K. The major ion conduction carrier was identified as the fluoride ions in the Sn-rich environment with a diffusion coefficient of 6 × 10−12 m2 s−1 at 298 K. In addition, MSD analysis indicated that PFG-NMR predominantly detected the diffusion of fluoride ions in the bulk material. Thus, the findings obtained in this study provide fundamental insight into controlling the ionic conductivity in BSF suitable for application in all solid-state batteries.
