DNAmBERT: a transformer-based model for non-invasive cancer diagnosis using DNA sequence and methylation data
Authors: Maryam Yassi, Mark Ezegbogu, Euan J Rodger, Peter Stockwell, Aniruddha Chatterjee, Matthew Parry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA methylation alterations are early and stable hallmarks of cancer and represent promising biomarkers for non-invasive detection using circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA). However, current computational approaches often model DNA sequence and methylation features separately and struggle to capture complex read-level methylation architecture in heterogeneous, low-signal liquid biopsy data. Here, we present DNAmBERT, a Transformer-based deep learning framework designed to jointly model DNA sequence context and read-level methylation haplotype structure from cfDNA methylation sequencing data. DNAmBERT integrates k-mer–encoded DNA sequences with methylation haplotype tokens using a unified representation and masked language modelling objective, enabling context-aware learning of sequence–epigenetic dependencies through self-attention. We evaluated DNAmBERT across multiple cfDNA methylation platforms (RRBS, cfRRBS, and cfMethyl-seq) and cancer types, including colorectal cancer, lung adenocarcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma. In binary classification tasks, the model achieved high performance across platforms (AUC up to 0.99–1.00) and outperformed conventional machine learning and existing deep learning approaches. Aggregation of read-level predictions enabled quantitative tumour probability estimation at the sample level. Beyond binary detection, DNAmBERT supported multi-cancer and stage-aware classification, including early-stage disease, with multiclass AUC values up to 0.99. The framework further demonstrated effective cross-cancer transfer learning, maintaining robust performance under limited data availability. These results indicate that integrated sequence–haplotype representation learning provides an accurate and scalable approach for cfDNA-based multi-cancer detection.Keywords: DNA methylation, circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA), transformer models, non-invasive cancer detection, multi-cancer classification, transfer learning

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with early detection playing a crucial role in improving patient outcomes [1]. Tumour development is driven by the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations that disrupt normal cellular processes, including growth, differentiation, and apoptosis. While genetic mutations have long been studied, it is now well established that epigenetic dysregulation, particularly DNA methylation, plays a fundamental role in cancer initiation and progression [2, 3].

DNA methylation is the most extensively studied epigenetic modification in cancer due to its stability, functional relevance, and tissue specificity [4]. Cancer-associated methylation changes typically involve promoter hypermethylation of tumour suppressor genes alongside widespread genomic hypomethylation, contributing to transcriptional silencing, genomic instability, and oncogene activation. Importantly, these alterations arise early during tumorigenesis and persist throughout disease progression, making DNA methylation an attractive biomarker for cancer detection, prognosis, and disease monitoring [2, 5].

Historically, cancer methylation landscapes have been characterised using tumour tissues obtained through surgical biopsy [6]. However, tissue biopsies are invasive, often unavailable for repeated sampling, and limited in their ability to capture tumour heterogeneity. Liquid biopsy, based on the analysis of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA), has therefore emerged as a minimally invasive alternative for cancer detection and surveillance [7]. cfDNA fragments originate from apoptotic and necrotic cells throughout the body and include a small fraction of circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) that retains the epigenetic signatures of the primary tumour and metastases [8, 9].

Despite its promise, cfDNA analysis presents substantial analytical challenges. Tumour-derived fragments are often present at very low abundance, particularly in early-stage disease, resulting in a low signal-to-noise ratio dominated by background cfDNA from healthy tissues [10]. Moreover, cfDNA methylation data are generated using heterogeneous sequencing platforms, such as EM-Seq, WGBS, cfRRBS, and cfMethyl-Seq, each with distinct coverage patterns, fragment lengths, and technical biases. Robust computational methods must therefore simultaneously address biological heterogeneity, technical variability, and limited sample sizes while retaining sensitivity to subtle cancer-specific methylation signals [11, 12].

A wide range of computational approaches has been developed for cfDNA methylation-based cancer detection, including conventional statistical and machine learning methods [13–17]. While effective in specific contexts, these approaches typically rely on aggregated regional methylation features and overlook read-level methylation architecture and local sequence context. As a result, they are limited in their ability to capture complex co-methylation patterns and sequence-dependent epigenetic structure that characterise tumour-derived cfDNA.

More recently, deep learning approaches have sought to jointly exploit DNA sequence and methylation information. DISMIR [18] models DNA sequence together with read-level methylation states within cancer-specific switching regions to classify individual cfDNA reads for cancer detection. MethylBERT [19] extends Transformer-based learning by integrating DNA sequence and methylation information to improve tumour-read classification and tumour deconvolution. More recently, DECIDIA [20] employs a pretrained Transformer together with multiple-instance learning (MIL) to analyse bisulfite sequencing reads for cancer diagnosis and tissue-of-origin prediction. Although these methods represent important advances, they differ substantially in their modelling strategies and downstream objectives. DISMIR primarily models read-level methylation states without Transformer-based contextual representation learning, whereas MethylBERT and DECIDIA learn sequence representations directly from sequencing reads but do not incorporate biologically informed methylation haplotype-guided DMR selection prior to representation learning. Furthermore, these methods primarily focus on tumour detection or deconvolution and provide limited support for unified downstream tasks such as tumour probability estimation, multicancer classification, stage-specific prediction, and transfer learning.

Transformer-based architectures offer a powerful alternative for modelling biological sequences due to their ability to capture long-range dependencies through self-attention mechanisms. In particular, Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) enable context-aware learning across entire sequences and are well suited for integrating heterogeneous biological signals [21]. These properties make Transformer models particularly well suited for cfDNA methylation analysis, where both local sequence context and long-range methylation haplotype structure convey biologically meaningful information.

To address these limitations, we developed DNAmBERT, a Transformer-based framework specifically designed for cfDNA methylation analysis. Unlike previous approaches, DNAmBERT first identifies biologically informative cancer-specific differentially methylated regions (DMRs) using methylation haplotype load (MHL), thereby restricting representation learning to regions enriched for tumour-associated methylation signals. Rather than using numerical MHL values as input, DNAmBERT encodes the underlying read-level methylation haplotype patterns together with DNA k-mer sequences in a unified Transformer architecture. Through self-supervised masked language modelling (MLM) followed by supervised fine-tuning, the model learns contextual relationships between local DNA sequence and methylation haplotype architecture. This unified framework supports binary cancer detection, patient-level tumour probability estimation through aggregation of fragment-level predictions, multicancer classification, stage-aware prediction, and cross-cancer transfer learning across heterogeneous cfDNA methylation sequencing platforms. Materials and methods

Methylation sequencing datasets

DNAmBERT was evaluated using DNA methylation sequencing datasets generated from three complementary platforms: reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS), cell-free reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (cfRRBS), and cfDNA methylome sequencing (cfMethyl-Seq). These platforms were selected to capture diverse CpG enrichment strategies, cfDNA fragment characteristics, and technical variability commonly encountered in liquid biopsy methylation studies.

For RRBS is a targeted bisulfite sequencing approach that enriches CpG-dense genomic regions using MspI-based restriction digestion followed by size selection of DNA fragments, typically ranging from 40 to 220 bp [22]. While cost-effective and CpG-enriched, RRBS provides limited genome coverage and exhibits reduced efficiency for cfDNA due to overlap with the native cfDNA fragment size distribution (~150–200 bp). To address these limitations, cfRRBS incorporates 5′-end dephosphorylation and selective adaptor ligation, enabling preferential enrichment of CpG-rich fragments from low-input cfDNA samples [23].

cfMethyl-Seq is a cfDNA-optimised methylome profiling method designed as a cost-effective alternative to whole-genome bisulfite sequencing. This approach enriches CpG-rich regions representing ~3% of the genome while capturing a large proportion of functionally relevant methylation sites. cfMethyl-Seq combines MspI-based digestion with unique molecular identifier (UMI)-based library preparation to improve molecule-level resolution and sensitivity, particularly for early cancer detection and tissue-of-origin inference, although challenges remain in low tumour fraction settings [24].

Across the three sequencing platforms, the study comprised tumour tissue samples, plasma cfDNA from healthy individuals, and plasma cfDNA from patients with colorectal cancer (CRC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), including both early- and late-stage disease. In total, 519 DNA methylomes generated using RRBS, cfRRBS, and cfMethyl-Seq were analysed (Supplementary Table S1). These datasets were used at different stages of the DNAmBERT framework. Cancer-specific DMRs were first identified independently for each cancer type using tumour tissue, tumour plasma cfDNA, and healthy plasma cfDNA samples. Subsequently, read-level fragments extracted from the identified DMRs were used for unsupervised masked language model pre-training. Supervised fine-tuning was then performed exclusively using plasma cfDNA samples, which were partitioned at the sample level into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets, ensuring that all fragments originating from the same sample remained within a single partition to prevent data leakage. Independent external plasma cfDNA cohorts, not used for DMR identification or model training, were reserved exclusively for tumour probability estimation and external validation. A detailed summary of dataset allocation for each analysis stage is provided in Supplementary Table S1 and Section 2.7.

Preprocessing pipeline

Read trimming, Bismark alignment, and UMI-based deduplication

All datasets were aligned to GRCh37/hg19 to maintain compatibility with publicly available cfDNA methylation datasets and previously reported DMR coordinates, many of which are annotated in hg19. Using a consistent reference genome avoided coordinate remapping biases and ensured comparability across sequencing platforms and benchmarking methods. All datasets were aligned to GRCh37/hg19 to ensure compatibility with publicly available methylation datasets, existing cfDNA analysis pipelines, and previously published DMR coordinates, thereby maintaining consistency across sequencing platforms and comparative benchmarking analyses.

For RRBS and cfRRBS datasets, preprocessing included initial quality assessment using FastQC [25], followed by adapter trimming with Trim Galore [26]. For cfMethyl-Seq datasets, preprocessing began with removal of UMIs and fixed sequence elements using UMI-tools, followed by Illumina adapter trimming with Trim Galore.

Given the use of multiple methylation sequencing platforms, distinct strategies were applied for PCR duplicate removal. PCR duplicates were removed exclusively from cfMethyl-Seq data, as this platform incorporates UMIs during library preparation. Duplicate removal was not applied to RRBS and cfRRBS datasets, as these protocols do not include UMI information. Consequently, sequencing depth and read-level methylation coverage differed across platforms, potentially introducing biases in downstream methylation haplotype representations.

To reduce the dependence of DNAmBERT on sequencing depth and platform-specific coverage, two complementary strategies were employed during model training. First, for RRBS-derived data, methylation haplotype sequences following the [SEP] token were split into shorter segments to approximate the read-depth distribution observed in cfMethyl-Seq data. Second, DNAmBERT was fine-tuned using read-level DNA methylation patterns, enabling the model to learn depth-invariant representations and improving robustness across sequencing platforms.

BAM file preprocessing

BAM files generated from bisulfite-converted sequencing were processed to extract read-level information required for methylation feature construction. These files contain alignment metadata including read identifiers, genomic coordinates, strand-specific alignments, bisulfite-converted read sequences, and corresponding methylation call strings.

All BAM file processing was performed using the Pysam library [27], which provides efficient programmatic access to aligned sequencing data. For each aligned read, relevant fields were extracted, including chromosome, start and end positions, read sequence, strand orientation, and methylation state annotations. Reads failing basic alignment or quality criteria were excluded from downstream analysis.

The extracted information was used to generate structured, read-level representations suitable for downstream methylation quantification, haplotype construction, and model input generation. A detailed description of the BAM parsing workflow, including pseudocode outlining data extraction and formatting steps, is provided in Supplementary Pseudocode 1.

Quantitative metrics: mean methylation and methylation haplotype load in cfDNA

Regional DNA methylation was quantified using two complementary metrics: mean methylation (MM) and MHL. MM summarises methylation as the average proportion of methylated CpG sites within a genomic region across all sequencing reads, providing a simple measure of overall methylation level.

To capture higher-order methylation structure, we additionally calculated MHL, which quantifies the extent of co-methylation among contiguous CpG sites within individual cfDNA fragments [28]. Unlike MM, which treats CpG sites independently, MHL incorporates both the frequency and length of fully methylated haplotypes, thereby reflecting local methylation continuity and read-level methylation architecture.

Together, MM and MHL provide complementary summaries of regional methylation status, with MHL offering increased sensitivity to tumour-specific methylation patterns that are not detectable using MM alone. Formal definitions, mathematical formulations, and illustrative examples comparing MM and MHL are provided in Supplementary Method and Supplementary Fig. S1. In DNAmBERT, the numerical MHL score is not used directly as an input feature to the Transformer. Instead, MHL is used during DMR identification to prioritise genomic regions exhibiting tumour-specific co-methylation patterns. The read-level methylation haplotype patterns from which MHL is calculated are subsequently encoded as symbolic methylation sequences and combined with DNA k-mer tokens to construct the model input. Consequently, DNAmBERT learns directly from the underlying methylation haplotype architecture rather than from a precomputed MHL summary statistic.

Identification of cancer-specific differentially methylated regions

Chromosome-level analysis

Cancer-specific DMRs, including both hypermethylated and hypomethylated loci, were identified using a chromosome-level analysis designed to accommodate cfDNA heterogeneity and platform-specific coverage differences. DMR identification was based on two complementary comparisons: tumour tissue versus normal plasma cfDNA, and tumour plasma cfDNA versus normal plasma cfDNA workflow shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.

To harmonise methylation profiles across sequencing platforms, the maximum observed length of each chromosome across all datasets was determined. Each chromosome was then partitioned into non-overlapping windows of 250 base pairs (bp). MHL values from regions overlapping each window were aggregated by computing a weighted average, generating a standardised regional methylation representation across platforms. Genomic coordinates were updated according to window positions along the reference genome. A schematic overview of the windowing and aggregation strategy is provided in Supplementary Fig. S3.

DMRs analysis process

Candidate DMRs were prioritised using a multi-criteria ranking strategy based on MHL. Three complementary feature-ranking methods were applied: Fisher ratio [29], between- versus within-class scatter ratio [30], and mutual information [31] summarised in Supplementary Table S2.

Analysis was restricted to genomic regions present in at least 75% of samples across all datasets. For each region, MHL-based scores were computed and ranked independently by each method. Rankings were then integrated using a voting-based aggregation strategy [32] and regions exceeding an empirically defined consensus threshold were retained as candidate DMRs. These candidates were further filtered using the Mann–Whitney U test, with regions exhibiting statistically significant differences between tumour and normal plasma cfDNA (P ≤ .05) retained for downstream analysis.

For each retained DMR, the corresponding DNA reference sequences and read-level methylation haplotype patterns were extracted and used for subsequent model construction and evaluation. Cancer- and tissue-specific DMRs were identified independently for each cancer type by comparison with healthy plasma cfDNA. Detailed DMR counts and fragment statistics are reported in the Results section.

The selected cancer- and tissue-specific DMRs were subsequently used to construct the input sequences for DNAmBERT. MHL value was used only during DMR identification to prioritise biologically informative regions enriched for tumour-specific co-methylation patterns. For each retained DMR, the genomic reference sequence was segmented into overlapping k-mer tokens, whereas the corresponding read-level methylation haplotype patterns were encoded as symbolic methylation sequences. These representations were concatenated using a special [SEP] token to form a unified sequence–epigenetic input. Thus, DNAmBERT learns directly from the underlying read-level methylation architecture rather than from numerical MHL values.

Input representation for DNAmBERT

Input sequences for DNAmBERT were constructed by integrating DNA sequence context and read-level methylation haplotype information using a unified tokenization strategy. For each genomic region, the DNA reference sequence was segmented into overlapping k-mers using a sliding window approach, preserving local sequence dependencies. In parallel, methylation haplotypes corresponding to contiguous CpG sites within individual cfDNA fragments were encoded as symbolic sequences. The DNA k-mer sequence and the corresponding methylation haplotype sequence were concatenated using a special separation token ([SEP]) to form a single composite input. Unlike approaches that represent methylation at individual CpG sites, DNAmBERT encodes read-level methylation haplotype patterns spanning contiguous CpG sites within each cfDNA fragment. This representation preserves local co-methylation architecture and enables the Transformer to learn sequence–epigenetic dependencies directly from the original methylation patterns instead of from aggregated methylation statistics.

We evaluated k-mer lengths of 3, 4, 5, and 6, training separate DNAmBERT models for each setting. Tokenized inputs were augmented with standard special tokens, including [CLS] for sequence-level representation, [PAD] for padding, [UNK] for unknown tokens, [SEP] for sequence separation, and [MASK] for MLM. The resulting vocabulary comprised all possible DNA k-mers together with observed methylation haplotype tokens, yielding vocabulary sizes of 3511 for CRCP, 2162 for LUADP, and 2198 for LIHCP.

This integrated input representation was used consistently for both pre-training and fine-tuning stages. A schematic overview of the input construction pipeline is provided in Supplementary Fig. S4. Alternative encoding strategies were explored as part of methodological benchmarking; however, these approaches did not yield improved performance and are therefore described in Supplementary Methods.

DNAmBERT model architecture

DNAmBERT is a Transformer-based framework derived from the BERT encoder architecture, designed to jointly model DNA sequence context and read-level methylation haplotype structure from cfDNA. Model inputs consist of k-mer–encoded DNA reference sequence tokens and symbolic methylation haplotype tokens representing co-methylation patterns across contiguous CpG sites within individual cfDNA fragments. These two token streams are concatenated into a single input sequence using a dedicated separator token ([SEP]). The overall DNAmBERT architecture and integrated input representation are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Architecture of DNAmBERT: DNAmBERT integrates DNA k-mer sequence tokens and read-level methylation haplotype tokens using a unified Transformer-based encoder. Input representations consist of DNA k-mers followed by methylation haplotypes, separated by a [SEP] token. Stacked Transformer encoder layers apply multi-head self-attention to jointly model sequence and methylation dependencies. Task-specific output heads support binary cancer detection and multiclass cancer classification.

Token embeddings are combined with positional embeddings and processed through stacked Transformer encoder layers comprising multi-head self-attention and position-wise feed-forward networks with residual connections and layer normalization. Self-attention is applied across the full token sequence, enabling the model to learn both intra-modality dependencies (within DNA sequence or methylation haplotypes) and cross-modal associations between sequence context and methylation architecture. This design preserves modality-specific structure while supporting integrated representation learning.

For downstream prediction tasks, DNAmBERT applies an attention-based pooling layer to aggregate token-level embeddings into a fixed-length sequence representation that prioritises informative features. Task-specific output heads are then applied according to the learning objective: a sigmoid-activated output layer for binary tumour versus normal classification, a softmax-activated layer for multiclass cancer type prediction and read-level probability outputs that are aggregated to estimate sample-level tumour burden. This unified architecture supports binary classification, multi-cancer detection, and tumour probability estimation within a single Transformer-based framework.

Pre-training and fine-tuning and tumour probability estimation

Pre-training with masked language modelling

DNAmBERT was pre-trained using a MLM objective to learn contextual representations of integrated DNA sequence and methylation haplotype information. Input sequences comprised k-mer–encoded DNA reference tokens concatenated with read-level methylation haplotype tokens using a [SEP] token.

Importantly, the pre-training stage was performed in a fully unsupervised manner, and no information about fragment origin (tumour or normal) was used at any point during this process. Although methylation metrics such as MHL or MM were used upstream to identify DMRs, their role was solely to capture the full variability of read-level methylation haplotype patterns for representation learning, rather than to introduce class-specific supervision. This design ensures that DNAmBERT learns generalizable sequence–methylation representations without any label leakage.

During pre-training, a subset of input tokens was masked, and the model was trained to predict the original tokens based on their surrounding context. Span-based masking was applied to contiguous DNA k-mer regions to prevent trivial inference from adjacent tokens, while methylation haplotype tokens following the [SEP] token were masked independently. This masking strategy enables DNAmBERT to capture both local sequence features and long-range sequence–epigenetic dependencies in an unsupervised manner.

Details of the masking strategy, loss formulation, optimization procedure, and hyperparameter settings are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Fine-tuning for cancer classification

The pre-trained DNAmBERT model was fine-tuned for supervised cancer classification using labelled cfDNA data. For this stage, the Transformer encoder was augmented with an attention-based pooling layer that aggregates token-level representations into a fixed-length sequence embedding. This mechanism enables the model to weight informative sequence–methylation features across the input sequence, rather than relying solely on the [CLS] token.

The pooled representation was passed to task-specific fully connected output layers for binary classification (cancer versus healthy) and multiclass cancer type prediction. Model optimization was performed using binary cross-entropy loss for binary classification and categorical cross-entropy loss for multiclass classification. Architectural variants and optimization parameters were systematically evaluated, and final configurations were selected based on validation performance.

Full details of fine-tuning architectures, hyperparameter ranges, and training procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Tumour probability estimation

During inference, DNAmBERT independently processes each cfDNA fragment extracted from the identified DMRs and produces a sigmoid probability representing the likelihood that the fragment originates from tumour-derived DNA. Thus, similar to read-level prediction approaches such as DISMIR, DNAmBERT can assign tumour probabilities to individual cfDNA fragments.

To estimate tumour probability at the patient level, the predicted probabilities of all cfDNA fragments originating from the same plasma sample were aggregated by calculating their arithmetic mean. This average fragment probability was used as the patient-level tumour probability, providing a quantitative measure of the overall tumour-associated methylation signal within the sample while reducing the influence of noisy predictions from individual fragments. Although a single fragment with a high tumour probability may indicate the presence of tumour-derived DNA, the mean probability provides a conservative, robust, and readily interpretable estimate that demonstrated excellent discrimination between cancer and healthy samples across all evaluated datasets.

Unlike binary classification, which assigns each patient to a tumour or healthy class, tumour probability estimation retains the aggregated probability as a continuous measure of tumour-associated signal. The mathematical formulation of the aggregation strategy and additional implementation details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Transfer learning framework

A transfer learning framework was implemented to improve generalisation across cancer types and to support model training in data-limited settings. DNAmBERT was first pre-trained on cfDNA methylation data from a source cancer using a MLM objective applied to integrated k-mer DNA sequence and methylation haplotype tokens. The pre-trained model was then fine-tuned on labelled cfDNA data from a target cancer by updating the Transformer encoder and task-specific classification layers.

This transfer learning strategy was applied to both binary (cancer versus healthy) and multiclass cancer classification tasks. Implementation details, including source–target cancer combinations and training configurations, are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Experimental design and evaluation metrics

All experiments were conducted using predefined training, validation, and test splits to ensure unbiased performance evaluation, with no overlap of samples between sets. Five-fold cross-validation was applied exclusively within the training data for model selection and hyperparameter optimization, while a fixed validation set was used for early stopping. An independent test set was held out and used only for final performance assessment. This experimental design was applied consistently across binary classification, multicancer classification, and transfer learning experiments.

To improve transparency and reproducibility, the allocation of datasets across each stage of the DNAmBERT workflow is summarised in Supplementary Table S1. The table details the datasets used for cancer-specific DMR discovery, self-supervised pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, and independent tumour probability estimation, together with the corresponding data types, partitioning strategies, and analytical objectives. DMR discovery was performed using tumour tissue and plasma cfDNA samples without dataset partitioning. Self-supervised pre-training used read-level fragments extracted from the identified DMRs, whereas supervised fine-tuning employed plasma cfDNA samples partitioned at the sample level into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets to prevent data leakage. Independent external plasma cfDNA cohorts were reserved exclusively for tumour probability estimation and external validation (Supplementary Table S1).

Model performance was evaluated using standard classification metrics, including accuracy, precision, sensitivity (recall), specificity, F1-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Definitions of evaluation metrics are provided in Supplementary Table S3. These metrics were selected to assess overall predictive performance, class-wise discrimination, and robustness under class imbalance.

For comparative analysis, DMR-based classification using MM and MHL was evaluated using conventional machine learning models, including Decision Tree, Bagging, Random Forest, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machines. In addition, DNAmBERT performance was benchmarked against existing deep learning approaches using identical data splits and evaluation criteria, as described in the Results section.

Results

Evaluating MM and MHL measurement for cancer and tissue-specific DMRs identification using machine learning models

We evaluated the discriminative performance of two regional methylation metrics—mean MM and MHL—using supervised machine learning models, including Decision Tree, Bagging, Random Forest, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machines with an RBF kernel. Models were trained to distinguish CRCP and LUADP from normal control plasma (NCP) and evaluated using accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and AUC, with full results in Supplementary Table S4.

Across both cancer types, models trained on MHL features consistently outperformed those trained on MM features. For CRCP, MHL-based Random Forest, Bagging, and XGBoost classifiers achieved accuracies of ~0.79–0.80 with AUC values of 0.90–0.91, whereas MM-based models showed reduced discrimination and lower specificity. Similar trends were observed for LUADP, where MHL-based Random Forest and Gradient Boosting models achieved accuracies of 0.88–0.89 and AUC values of 0.94–0.95. Support Vector Machine classifiers performed poorly across both metrics, particularly when trained on MM features (AUC = 0.65–0.73).

Together, these results demonstrate that MHL provides superior discriminative power compared with MM for cancer-specific DMR identification and supports the use of haplotype-level methylation structure for downstream cfDNA-based cancer detection.

Binary classification

Binary classification with DNAmBERT using haplotype methylation patterns

Methylation sequencing platform: RRBS and cfMethyl-seq

Using regional (MHL), cancer- and tissue-specific DMRs were identified and used as input features for DNAmBERT. In total, 890 DMRs (53 546 fragments) were identified for CRCP, 411 DMRs (28 481 fragments) for lung adenocarcinoma plasma LUADP, and 234 DMRs (15 729 fragments) for LIHCP. These regions were used as input features for downstream supervised learning analyses.

Models were pre-trained using k-mer tokenization with k = 3–6, where increasing k corresponded to progressively deeper Transformer architectures with larger hidden dimensions and increased attention capacity (Table 1). Specifically, k = 3–6 models ranged from 4 to 7 encoder layers, 16–28 attention heads, and hidden sizes from 128 to 224, enabling systematic evaluation of model capacity and representational complexity.

DNAmBERT models were pre-trained and fine-tuned using k-mer tokenization with k = 3–6. Pre-training performance across k-mer configurations is summarised in Table 1. Following fine-tuning, DNAmBERT achieved high classification performance for both cancer types in Supplementary Figs S8 and S9. For CRCP versus NCP, the k = 4 configuration yielded optimal performance, achieving an accuracy of 0.95, sensitivity of 0.99, specificity of 0.92, and an AUC of 1.00 (Table 1). Other k-mer configurations produced comparably strong results (accuracy = 0.93–0.94; AUC ≈ 0.99), indicating robust performance across model settings.

For LUADP versus NCP, the k = 6 configuration performed best, achieving an accuracy of 0.96, sensitivity of 0.99, specificity of 0.93, and an AUC of 0.99. Although k = 3–5 achieved competitive accuracy (0.92–0.93), k = 6 consistently provided improved sensitivity and specificity, highlighting the benefit of larger k-mers for capturing complex sequence–methylation dependencies in lung cancer cfDNA.

Tumour probability estimations

We next evaluated DNAmBERT for estimating tumour probability from cfDNA using the k = 6 configuration (Table 1) on independent test datasets containing both cancer and non-cancer plasma samples. For CRC, tumour probability estimates derived from RRBS data showed clear separation between CRC plasma (CRCP, n = 30) and normal control plasma (NCP, n = 22) in Supplementary Fig. S15. CRCP samples exhibited significantly higher tumour probability estimates than NCP samples (p = 1.97 × 10−9), with a narrow interquartile range indicating consistent classification confidence. In contrast, NCP samples displayed uniformly low tumour probability values (<0.3) with minimal variability.

Similarly, for LUAD, tumour probability estimates derived from cfRRBS and RRBS data demonstrated strong discrimination between LUAD plasma cfDNA (LUADP, n = 38) and healthy controls (n = 22) in Supplementary Fig. S16. LUADP samples clustered at high probability values, whereas NCP samples remained consistently low (<0.3), with a highly significant difference between groups (p = 2.81 × 10−10).

The robustness of tumour probability estimation was further validated using additional unseen cfDNA samples in Supplementary Figs S17 and S18. For CRC, DNAmBERT again achieved clear separation between tumour-derived and normal cfDNA (CRCP, n = 30; NCP, n = 22; p = 1.75 × 10−9). Consistent results were observed for LUAD, with tumour probability distributions for LUADP (n = 38) differing markedly from healthy controls (n = 22; p = 2.81 × 10−10). Across both cancer types, tumour-derived cfDNA exhibited narrow interquartile ranges and high probability estimates, while healthy samples showed low and tightly clustered values, indicating stable performance and a low false-positive tendency.

The patient-level tumour probability used throughout this analysis represents the average of the read-level tumour probabilities predicted by DNAmBERT for all cfDNA fragments within a plasma sample. Although this averaging strategy is conservative—since a single fragment with a high tumour probability may already indicate the presence of tumour-derived DNA—it proved highly effective in practise, achieving near-perfect discrimination between cancer and healthy samples (AUC ≈ 1). These findings demonstrate that simple aggregation of fragment-level predictions provides a robust and interpretable estimate of the tumour-associated methylation signal in cfDNA.

Stage-aware multiclass classification of cancer and healthy cfDNA

We further evaluated DNAmBERT for multiclass classification of early-stage (ES) and late-stage (LS) cancers versus NCP using integrated cfDNA sequence and methylation haplotype information. Transfer learning

Transfer learning for binary cancer classification

We evaluated the transfer learning capability of DNAmBERT by pre-training the model on one cancer type versus NCP and subsequently fine-tuning it on a different cancer type versus NCP, across k-mer sizes k = 3–6. Overall performance metrics for all transfer scenarios are summarised in Supplementary Figs S42–S47 and Supplementary Tables S9–S14.

When DNAmBERT was pre-trained on CRCP versus NCP and fine-tuned on LUADP versus NCP, the model achieved strong and consistent performance, with test accuracies up to 0.93, AUC values up to 0.98, and sensitivities reaching 0.99 across all k-mer configurations. Precision and recall remained well balanced, yielding F1-scores between 0.91 and 0.93. Among the evaluated settings, k = 5 provided the most favourable balance between sensitivity and specificity, indicating effective transfer of sequence–methylation representations between colorectal and lung cancer cfDNA.

In contrast, fine-tuning the CRCP-pretrained model on LIHCP versus NCP resulted in greater performance variability. Although AUC values remained high (up to 0.97), test precision and F1-scores were reduced (0.80–0.84), reflecting increased domain divergence. In this setting, the k = 6 configuration achieved the highest sensitivity and specificity (both 0.92), suggesting that longer k-mer representations partially mitigate cross-cancer differences for hepatocellular carcinoma.

Reciprocal transfer learning experiments revealed cancer-specific generalisation patterns. When DNAmBERT was pre-trained on LUADP versus NCP and fine-tuned on CRCP versus NCP, test accuracy ranged from 0.90 to 0.95, with AUC values of 0.98–0.99 and sensitivity consistently reaching 0.99 across all k-mer sizes. The k = 3 and k = 6 configurations provided the most balanced trade-offs between recall and specificity, yielding F1-scores up to 0.93. Fine-tuning the LUADP-pretrained model on LIHCP versus NCP produced stable but comparatively lower performance, with test accuracies of 0.90–0.92 and AUC values up to 0.96, while k = 6 again achieved relatively higher precision, sensitivity (0.90), and F1-score.

When DNAmBERT was pre-trained on LIHCP versus NCP, transfer to both CRCP versus NCP and LUADP versus NCP yielded consistently high performance. Fine-tuning on CRCP achieved test accuracies of 0.93–0.96 and AUC values up to 0.99, with the k = 6 configuration providing the highest precision (0.92), sensitivity (0.97), and F1-score (0.92). Fine-tuning on LUADP also resulted in strong performance, with test accuracies of 0.93–0.94 and stable AUC values of 0.98, although variability across k-mer sizes was slightly greater than for CRCP. In this setting, k = 4 and k = 6 offered the most balanced performance.

These findings show that DNAmBERT supports effective cross-cancer transfer learning for binary cfDNA-based cancer detection. The highest transfer performance was observed between CRCP and LUADP, while transfers involving LIHCP showed increased sensitivity to domain differences and benefited most from larger k-mer representations.

Discussion and conclusion

An important consideration for the practical application of DNAmBERT is the quality and reproducibility of upstream cfDNA preprocessing. Accurate preprocessing is particularly critical for early-stage cancer detection, where tumour-derived cfDNA constitutes only a small fraction of the total circulating DNA. Recent standardised workflows, such as cfDNApipe [33] and cfDNA-UniFlow [34], provide comprehensive and reproducible pipelines for quality control, read alignment, duplicate removal, methylation calling, and fragment generation from raw sequencing data. DNAmBERT is designed to complement, rather than replace, these preprocessing frameworks by operating on processed methylation sequencing data. Consequently, the proposed framework can be readily integrated with established preprocessing pipelines, enabling standardised upstream processing while leveraging Transformer-based modelling for downstream cancer detection, tumour probability estimation, multicancer classification, and transfer learning.

Binary classification and tumour probability estimation

This study demonstrates that DNAmBERT enables highly accurate and robust discrimination of tumour-derived versus normal cfDNA by jointly modelling DNA sequence context and whole-read methylation haplotype architecture within a unified Transformer framework. Across colorectal and lung cancer datasets, DNAmBERT achieved consistently high classification performance, with accuracies exceeding 90% and AUC values above 0.95 across multiple methylation sequencing platforms, including RRBS, cfRRBS, and cfMethyl-Seq. The preservation of performance across platforms with distinct coverage profiles, fragment length distributions, and technical biases highlights DNAmBERT’s robustness to sequencing heterogeneity, variable tumour fraction, and the low signal-to-noise conditions characteristic of cfDNAdata.

Systematic evaluation of sequence resolution revealed that larger k-mer representations, particularly k = 6, generally conferred superior performance across cancer types and platforms. This finding underscores the importance of capturing longer-range sequence dependencies and complex sequence–methylation interactions that characterise tumour-derived cfDNA fragments and are not readily accessible to region-level or handcrafted feature-based approaches.

Beyond binary classification, DNAmBERT provides a quantitative framework for tumour probability estimation by aggregating read-level predictions into sample-level scores. These continuous tumour probability estimates yielded clear separation between cancer and healthy plasma samples, including in independent test cohorts, while also resolving samples with intermediate signal. This probabilistic output offers improved interpretability over binary labels alone and supports potential applications in early cancer detection, disease monitoring, and longitudinal risk assessment.

Compared with conventional machine learning models based on predefined methylation features such as MM and MHL, DNAmBERT consistently achieved higher sensitivity and specificity, particularly in low-signal and early-stage settings. Together, these results establish that Transformer-based modelling of integrated sequence and methylation information enables a more expressive and biologically informed representation of cfDNA, advancing both the accuracy and interpretability of non-invasive cancer detection and supporting DNAmBERT as a scalable foundation for clinically relevant liquid biopsy diagnostics.

Multi-cancer classification

Extending beyond binary detection, DNAmBERT enables accurate and scalable multi-cancer classification from cfDNA by jointly modelling DNA sequence context and read-level methylation haplotype structure. When fine-tuned on datasets comprising colorectal, lung adenocarcinoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma plasma alongside healthy controls, DNAmBERT learned cancer-specific epigenetic representations that supported robust discrimination among multiple tumour types within a single unified framework.

Across diverse experimental configurations and sequencing platforms, DNAmBERT achieved consistently strong multiclass performance, with high accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-scores. Confusion matrix analyses revealed minimal inter-cancer misclassification, while latent-space representations—including 3D logit projections and t-SNE embeddings—demonstrated clear separation between cancer classes and healthy samples. These results indicate that integrated sequence–methylation modelling yields highly discriminative feature representations that generalise across both biological and technical variability.

Critically, DNAmBERT retained strong performance in early-stage disease, where tumour-derived cfDNA is present at low abundance and methylation signals are subtle. The model effectively distinguished early-stage colorectal, lung, and liver cancers from both late-stage disease and healthy controls, and maintained stability as classification complexity increased. Notably, DNAmBERT scaled successfully to multiclass classification of early-stage cancers across multiple tissue types without substantial loss of accuracy, underscoring its capacity to resolve fine-grained, tissue-specific epigenetic signatures in challenging low-signal settings.

Performance gains were most pronounced with higher k-mer resolution, with k = 6 consistently yielding optimal results, highlighting the importance of capturing longer-range sequence context when modelling sequence–methylation interactions. Compared with conventional machine learning approaches and the Transformer-based MethylBERT model, DNAmBERT demonstrated superior multiclass discrimination, particularly in early-stage and high class-diversity scenarios.

These findings establish DNAmBERT as a robust and scalable framework for multi-cancer detection and early-stage classification from cfDNA, supporting its potential utility as a unified liquid biopsy platform capable of resolving cancer presence, tissue of origin, and disease stage within a single deep learning architecture.

Transfer learning methodology

To improve classification performance and generalizability while reducing reliance on large, fully annotated cfDNA datasets, we implemented a transfer learning strategy within the DNAmBERT framework. The model was initially pre-trained on a heterogeneous, multi-cancer dataset integrating DNA sequence context and cfDNA methylation haplotype information from colorectal, lung, and liver cancers together with healthy controls. This pre-training stage enabled DNAmBERT to learn generalised representations of cancer-associated sequence–epigenetic patterns that are shared across tissue types.

When fine-tuned on new target cancer types or downstream classification tasks, including both binary and multiclass settings, DNAmBERT consistently retained and transferred this learned representation, resulting in robust discrimination between cancer-derived and normal cfDNA across different tissue origins. Compared with models trained from scratch, transfer learning led to systematic improvements in accuracy, AUC, precision, and recall, particularly in data-limited scenarios. In addition, pre-trained models converged more rapidly during fine-tuning and exhibited improved generalisation, indicating effective reuse of learned methylation and sequence dependencies.

Notably, the transfer learning framework demonstrated robustness across sequencing platforms and experimental conditions. Models pre-trained on cfMethyl-Seq and RRBS data maintained strong performance when fine-tuned on cfRRBS data or on cancer types not included during pre-training. The benefits of transfer learning were most pronounced in low-resource settings, where fine-tuning with reduced sample sizes preserved high classification performance, whereas models trained de novo showed substantial performance degradation.

Taken together, these findings indicate that DNAmBERT provides a flexible and scalable transfer learning framework capable of supporting accurate binary and multiclass cfDNA-based cancer diagnostics across heterogeneous clinical and technical contexts.

Methodological comparison with existing approaches

Current computational strategies for cfDNA methylation–based cancer detection can be broadly divided into feature-engineered machine learning methods and deep learning frameworks. Conventional machine learning approaches rely on predefined regional methylation summaries, such as MM or MHL, followed by classifiers including Random Forest and XGBoost. Although effective in selected applications, these approaches depend on manually engineered features and aggregated regional methylation statistics, limiting their ability to capture read-level epigenetic architecture.

Recent deep learning methods have addressed some of these limitations. DISMIR performs read-level cancer detection by combining DNA sequence and methylation states within predefined switching regions. However, it relies on manually defined genomic regions and does not exploit Transformer-based contextual representation learning. MethylBERT introduced Transformer-based modelling for DNA sequence and methylation information, improving tumour-read classification and tumour deconvolution through contextual sequence learning. More recently, DECIDIA combined a pretrained Transformer with MIL to analyse bisulfite sequencing reads for cancer diagnosis and tissue-of-origin prediction. Despite these advances, these methods differ substantially in their input representation, biological prior knowledge, and downstream analytical scope.

DNAmBERT extends these approaches by integrating biologically informed DMR selection with Transformer-based representation learning. Rather than using numerical MHL values as model inputs, DNAmBERT first identifies cancer-specific DMRs using MHL and subsequently encodes the underlying read-level methylation haplotype patterns together with DNA k-mer sequences within a unified Transformer architecture. Consequently, the model preserves co-methylation relationships across contiguous CpG sites while simultaneously learning sequence-dependent epigenetic context through self-attention. Unlike approaches that model CpG methylation independently or analyse genome-wide reads without biological prioritisation, DNAmBERT focuses representation learning on tumour-enriched genomic regions while retaining read-level methylation architecture.

Beyond methodological differences, DNAmBERT also provides a broader analytical framework. In addition to binary cancer detection, it supports patient-level tumour probability estimation through aggregation of fragment-level predictions, multicancer classification, stage-aware classification, and transfer learning across cancer types and sequencing platforms. These capabilities extend beyond the primary objectives of DISMIR, MethylBERT, and DECIDIA, which mainly focus on tumour-read classification, tumour deconvolution, or cancer diagnosis. A systematic comparison of these frameworks is presented in Supplementary Table S15.

Overall, the improved performance of DNAmBERT reflects not only advances in classification accuracy but also a conceptual shift toward integrated sequence–haplotype representation learning. By combining biologically informed DMR selection with contextual Transformer modelling of read-level methylation haplotypes, DNAmBERT provides a unified framework capable of supporting multiple downstream cfDNA analysis tasks while remaining applicable across heterogeneous methylation sequencing platforms.

Strengths and limitations of DNAmBERT

This study demonstrates the potential of deep learning for cfDNA methylation analysis by introducing DNAmBERT, a unified Transformer-based framework for non-invasive cancer detection and classification. The model combines biologically informed feature selection with contextual representation learning to jointly model DNA sequence and read-level methylation haplotypes. As summarised in Supplementary Table S16, this design provides several methodological advantages over existing approaches while also highlighting important limitations that should be addressed in futurework.

A principal strength of DNAmBERT is its integration of DNA sequence context with read-level methylation haplotype information within a single Transformer architecture. Unlike conventional machine learning approaches that rely on engineered regional methylation summaries, or deep learning methods that primarily model sequence or CpG-level methylation independently, DNAmBERT learns contextual relationships between local DNA sequence and contiguous methylation haplotypes through self-attention. This integrated representation enables the model to capture higher-order sequence–epigenetic dependencies that are likely to contribute to the improved classification performance observed throughout this study.

Another important advantage is the consistent performance of DNAmBERT across multiple analytical tasks and heterogeneous sequencing platforms. The framework achieved high accuracy, precision, recall, and AUC for binary cancer detection, multicancer classification, tumour probability estimation, and transfer learning using RRBS, cfRRBS, and cfMethyl-Seq datasets. The ability to generalise across different sequencing technologies and multiple cancer types demonstrates the robustness of the proposed representation-learning strategy and suggests that the framework may be applicable to a broad range of cfDNA methylation studies.

DNAmBERT also demonstrated encouraging sensitivity for detecting early-stage cancers, where ctDNA typically represents only a very small fraction of total cfDNA. By modelling methylation haplotypes at the read level, the framework is able to capture subtle tumour-associated methylation signals that may be diluted when regional methylation summaries are used. Furthermore, the successful application of transfer learning indicates that knowledge learned from relatively large datasets can be transferred to more limited datasets, providing a practical strategy for analysing rare cancer types or applications where only small numbers of training samples are available.

An additional strength is the introduction of patient-level tumour probability estimation through aggregation of fragment-level predictions. Rather than providing only a binary classification result, DNAmBERT generates continuous tumour probability estimates by averaging read-level tumour probabilities across all cfDNA fragments within a plasma sample. This quantitative output provides additional information regarding the strength of tumour-associated methylation signals and may support future clinical applications such as longitudinal disease monitoring or treatment response assessment.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, like most Transformer-based architectures, DNAmBERT requires substantial computational resources for model pre-training and fine-tuning, including high GPU memory and extended training times. Although inference is considerably less computationally demanding, optimisation of model efficiency will be important for large-scale clinical deployment.

Second, although DNAmBERT demonstrated robust performance across multiple datasets, extremely imbalanced datasets and ultra-low sequencing coverage remain challenging. Performance may decline when tumour-derived cfDNA is present at extremely low abundance or when certain cancer subtypes are represented by only a limited number of training samples. Future work should investigate more sophisticated strategies for handling data imbalance, improving robustness to sparse methylation signals, and learning from noisy low-coverage sequencingdata.

Third, while tumour probability estimation provides greater interpretability than conventional binary predictions, the internal representations learned by the Transformer remain difficult to interpret biologically. Additional work is needed to relate attention patterns and learned embeddings to known genomic regulatory elements, methylation mechanisms, and cancer-associated biological pathways. Such analyses could improve confidence in model predictions while providing additional biological insight.

The current framework is also influenced by methodological choices, including the selection of k-mer size for tokenization. Although the present study systematically evaluated several k-mer configurations and identified k = 6 as the optimal setting for the investigated datasets, the optimal tokenization strategy may vary across sequencing technologies and disease applications. Adaptive tokenization strategies may therefore represent an important direction for future research.

Finally, although DNAmBERT demonstrated excellent performance across retrospective public datasets spanning multiple sequencing platforms and cancer types, prospective validation in large multicentre clinical studies is still required before routine clinical implementation. Future extensions may also benefit from integrating additional molecular and clinical information, including fragmentomics, genomic variants, transcriptomics, proteomics, and patient metadata, to further improve diagnostic accuracy and biological interpretability.

Overall, the strengths and limitations summarised in Supplementary Table S16 place DNAmBERT within the broader landscape of cfDNA-based cancer diagnostics. While further methodological refinement and prospective validation are needed, the proposed framework provides a flexible and biologically informed foundation for Transformer-based analysis of cfDNA methylation and supports future development toward clinically deployable liquid biopsy applications.

Future work

Building on the strong performance of DNAmBERT in binary, multi-cancer, and transfer learning settings, several strategic directions are proposed to further enhance its accuracy, scalability, and clinical applicability. These future directions are motivated by the need to advance robust, generalizable, and clinically deployable AI-based frameworks for non-invasive cancer diagnostics and precision oncology. Key priorities include expanding the model to additional cancer types, integrating multi-omics data, increasing population diversity, advancing clinical validation, and addressing current methodological limitations to enable real-world deployment.

First, extending DNAmBERT to additional high-impact cancer types, such as breast, prostate, pancreatic, and ovarian cancers, represents a critical next step. This will require curating large-scale, high-quality cfDNA methylation datasets across diverse tumour types and adapting the model architecture to remain scalable despite increasing class complexity. Systematic investigation of shared and cancer-specific epigenetic signatures will further improve multi-class classification accuracy and tissue-of-origin resolution, strengthening DNAmBERT’s pan-cancer diagnostic capabilities.

Second, integrating multi-omics data offers a promising avenue to enhance diagnostic precision and biological interpretability. Incorporation of complementary modalities—including somatic mutations and copy number alterations, circulating RNA expression profiles, proteomic biomarkers, and relevant clinical and demographic variables—would allow DNAmBERT to capture a more comprehensive view of tumour biology. Such multi-modal integration could improve patient-specific risk stratification, support personalised diagnostics, and provide deeper mechanistic insights into cancer-associated epigenetic alterations.

Third, training and validating DNAmBERT on larger and more ethnically diverse populations is essential to ensure equitable performance and reduce bias. Expanding cohorts to include underrepresented populations, such as Māori and Pasifika communities in New Zealand, will improve robustness across demographic subgroups and promote fairness in precision medicine.

Finally, clinical translation of DNAmBERT will require prospective validation and methodological refinement. Longitudinal studies using serial plasma samples are needed to evaluate performance in early detection, treatment monitoring, and recurrence prediction. Integration into clinical decision-support systems will require collaboration with healthcare providers and development of secure, interoperable deployment platforms. Addressing current limitations—including computational complexity, sensitivity to data imbalance and low sequencing coverage, limited interpretability, and dependence on fixed k-mer tokenization—will be critical. Future work should focus on model optimization via compression and lightweight Transformer variants, adoption of explainable AI techniques to enhance transparency, robust noise-handling strategies, and adaptive tokenization methods to better capture variable genomic contexts. Collectively, these advancements will support DNAmBERT’s transition from a high-performing research framework to a clinically actionable tool for precision oncology.
