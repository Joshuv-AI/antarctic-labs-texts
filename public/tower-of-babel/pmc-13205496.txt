Environmental factors that regulate host–helminth interactions
Authors: Eric Y. Helm, Tighe Christopher, Alejandra Lopez Espinoza, Natalie Santillano, Elia D. Tait Wojno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soil-transmitted helminths (STHs) infect over a billion people worldwide. Individual outcomes vary, ranging from subclinical, mild infections to recurrent or severe disease. Emerging evidence reveals that susceptibility to STH infection reflects a multilayered integration of past and present environmental factors. At mucosal surfaces, STHs activate type 2 immune, epithelial, and neuronal circuits, while microbial- and diet-derived signals promote homeostasis. Environmental signals tune the transcriptional programs that control these events, shaping interleukin-13-driven effector functions required for worm expulsion. Concurrently, infection and inflammation history imprint immune and epithelial compartments, leading to durable epigenetic remodeling and altering responsiveness to STHs upon reexposure. Understanding how environmental factors impact host–helminth interactions offers new opportunities to predict susceptibility and design interventions that enhance anti-helminth immunity.

Expanding the scope of anti-helminth immunity

Soil-transmitted helminth (STH) (see Glossary) parasites have co-evolved with mammalian hosts under conditions of fluctuating nutrition, repeated exposure, and diverse environmental pressures. As a result, host–helminth interactions are shaped by genetic determinants of host immunity and parasite fitness, as well as by environmental and contextual factors, including diet, microbial metabolites, pollutant exposure, and prior inflammatory history. These variables influence whether STHs are expelled, tolerated, or establish chronic infection. Rather than acting in isolation, environmental cues are sensed and integrated across epithelial, immune, and neuronal compartments, where they shape the magnitude, durability, and tissue specificity of anti-helminth responses.

Type 2 immunity is the central axis of host defense against STHs. During these responses, epithelial and immune sensors detect the presence of STHs, prompting the release of alarmin cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-33, thymic stromal lymphopoietin, and tuft cell-derived IL-25. These cytokines act on innate immune cell types, including myeloid cells and group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s). ILC2s produce IL-5 and IL-13, and innate responses support and guide the emergence of polarized T helper type 2 (Th2) cells and class-switched B cells that produce immunoglobulin E (IgE). These activities culminate in the rich production of IL-4, IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13. IL-13, in particular, then acts back on the intestinal epithelium to alter the physiology of the barrier, prompting increased permeability, mucus production, and goblet and tuft cell hyperplasia. In conjunction with enhanced smooth muscle contractility, these responses constitute the ‘weep and sweep’ effect that drives STH expulsion and supports tissue repair [1]. However, these responses are metabolically demanding and must be tightly regulated to balance parasite control with tissue integrity. Accumulating evidence indicates that environmental inputs calibrate type 2 immunity, enabling hosts to dynamically tune protective responses according to environmental context.

In this review, we examine how environmental factors regulate host–helminth interactions through four interconnected lenses: (i) dietary and environmental exposures that shape susceptibility to infection; (ii) infection and inflammation history as determinants of immune imprinting; (iii) neuronal–immune–epithelial integration of environmental cues at barrier surfaces; and (iv) transcriptional and epigenetic programs that encode environmental information into durable immune and epithelial states.

Dietary factors and environmental exposures

Nutritional status and environmental exposures are major determinants of host immune function, yet how these factors shape host responses to STHs remains incompletely understood. However, some recent studies have highlighted negative associations between STH infection outcomes and micronutrient deficiencies, particularly low levels of serum iron and zinc [2,3], as well as the impact of environmental exposures such as pollutants [4]. In this section, we discuss examples illustrating how malnutrition, dietary perturbations, and environmental factors influence host susceptibility to helminth infection in murine models and humans.

Dietary factors

Many individuals living in helminth-endemic regions experience chronic malnutrition (https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/soil-transmitted-helminth-infections), which can negatively impact STH infection outcomes. For example, school-aged children in the Philippines who met recommended energy and nutrient intake had a lower prevalence of Schistosoma japonicum, whereas those deficient in iron, thiamine, or riboflavin had a higher Trichuris trichiura prevalence [5]. Evidence from murine models also highlights the essential role of nutritional adequacy in promoting resistance to STH infection. Supplementation of wild-captured mice with a high-quality, nutrient-dense diet significantly enhanced resistance to Heligmosomoides polygyrus bakeri, reducing worm burden, egg output, and reinfection after anthelmintic treatment [6]. Similarly, H. polygyrus-infected BALB/c mice fed a low-protein (6%) diet exhibited increased worm burden and heightened intestinal permeability at 28 days post-infection relative to mice receiving a high-protein (18.5%) diet [7].

Dietary fiber exerts a more complex influence on anti-helminth immunity. Fermentable inulin fiber intake was associated with increased large intestinal worm burdens and decreased expression of anti-helminth type 2 cytokines in infection with the murine parasite Trichuris muris [8,9]. While there was no effect of inulin fiber on resistance to the small intestinal parasite H. polygyrus [8], Arifuzzaman et al. found that an inulin-rich diet altered the microbiota, increasing enterohepatic circulatory bile acid levels, which promoted type 2 inflammation and enhanced resistance to the small intestinal parasite Nippostrongylus brasiliensis [10]. These data suggest that there are tissue- and context-specific effects of fermentable fiber, potentially dependent upon the microbiota [8–10]. Together, these findings illustrate the complexity of how macronutrient composition can profoundly influence host susceptibility to STH infection (Figure 1).

Micronutrients also impact host resistance and anti-helminth responses. In particular, retinoic acid (RA) and its dietary precursor, vitamin A, tune anti-helminth programs. Rather than acting as classical Th2-polarizing factors, RA and vitamin A are indicators of nutritional state that regulate the balance between adaptive immunity and epithelial and innate lymphoid responses. In mice, vitamin A deficiency induced a transcriptional shift toward innate type 2 immunity [11] and attenuated Th2 responses [12,13]. ILC2s activated IL-13-driven programs in vitamin A deficiency that enhanced goblet cell hyperplasia and protection against T. muris infection [11]. This RA-dependent adaptation was tightly linked to ILC2 metabolic programming, as ILC2s relied on fatty acid oxidation to sustain IL-5 and IL-13 production during helminth infection and vitamin A deficiency [14]. These studies indicate that vitamin A deficiency privileges ILC2-dominated, IL-13-driven programs promoting worm expulsion, while vitamin A sufficiency supports adaptive type 2 responses (Figure 1). Such reprogramming likely reflects an evolutionarily conserved strategy in which hosts prioritize immediate tissue protection during nutritional stress. Consistent with these findings, epidemiological studies in humans have reported associations between STH infection and vitamin A deficiency [15], although the strength and direction of these relationships vary across populations and contexts [16].

Essential mineral deficiencies are a major concern in low- and middle-income countries where STHs are endemic and may also be associated with increased severity of helminth infection. In murine models, zinc deficiency promotes faster H. polygyrus larval development into adults, which is associated with impaired type 2 immunity and reduced IgE, IgG1, and eosinophil responses [17]. In humans, Nepalese children aged 6–59 months with anemia and zinc deficiency had a higher likelihood of STH infection [15]; additionally, zinc and iron levels in helminth-infected individuals in Malaysia correlated negatively with infection status [18]. Furthermore, Nepalese women with severe anemia were 12-fold more likely to be infected with hookworms [19]. However, although iron deficiency has been consistently associated with STH infection in epidemiological studies [2], the mechanisms by which iron availability influences host resistance to STHs remain poorly defined.

Environmental factors

Extrinsic environmental factors such as temperature, elevation, soil composition, and sanitation shape the survival, development, and infectivity of helminth eggs and larvae in the environment, thereby modulating STH exposure risk and transmission efficiency. Epidemiological studies from Ghana [20] and Ethiopia [21] demonstrated that hookworm prevalence was higher in regions characterized by acidic soil pH and elevated ambient temperatures, which are conditions that favor egg viability and larval development. Seasonal variation further modulates transmission dynamics: in rural Indonesia, peak rainfall and increased humidity were associated with high hookworm prevalence among school-aged children [22]. In parallel, inadequate sanitation and limited waste management sustain transmission cycles in endemic settings. In addition, in murine models of rewilding, there is clear interaction between host genetics and environmental exposures in determining the outcome of STH infection, but the mechanisms that integrate these diverse cues remain to be fully dissected [81].

Other environmental factors, such as exposure to chemical pollutants and heavy metals, may also play a role in host–helminth interactions, though this is an emerging area of research. Perinatal exposure to the pollutant bisphenol A skewed offspring immunity toward production of T helper type 1 cells, reduced Th2 responses, and increased Toxocara canis burden [23]. In contrast, early-life bisphenol A exposure in BALB/c mice enhanced Th2 responses and eosinophil activation, reducing susceptibility to Taenia crassiceps [24]. Air pollutants and heavy metal exposure cause oxidative stress, disruption in growth hormone regulation, and altered nutrient metabolism, ultimately contributing to impaired growth and anemia in children [4]. These data suggest a complex interplay between environmental exposures and nutrition that modulates host responses to STH infection.

Despite growing evidence, the mechanisms by which nutritional and environmental variables converge to regulate host–helminth interactions remain incompletely defined. Key nutrients support effective immune responses, yet how deficiencies in these nutrients mechanistically impact the outcome of STH infection remains understudied. Defining how nutritional and environmental cues intersect with type 2 inflammatory pathways during STH infection may reveal new opportunities to enhance host resistance or mitigate pathology.

Role of infection and inflammation history in determining host–helminth interactions

Host–helminth interactions are also shaped by prior immunological history and context. While immunological memory is classically attributed to adaptive immunity, accumulating evidence describes the existence of durable, antigen-independent immune imprinting in innate and tissue-resident cells, often termed trained immunity, following infectious exposures [25–27]. Anti-helminth responses in barrier tissues rely heavily on cells susceptible to such imprinting, such as innate immune and epithelial cells. Thus, anti-helminth immunity reflects not only de novo cytokine production but also long-lived functional reprogramming from prior inflammatory events (Figure 2). In addition, while not a focus here, STH infections frequently coexist with other inflammatory diseases, including allergy, with substantial overlap between STH- and allergy-associated immune pathways that could amplify or dampen immune responses [28–30]. Together, these factors highlight how cumulative inflammatory history and context contribute to heterogeneity in STH infection outcomes.

‘Trained immunity’ in innate cell types

Epigenetic remodeling is considered a primary mechanism underlying trained immunity in innate immune cells, enabling long-term changes in cellular behavior without altering the DNA sequence [31] (Figure 2). STH-associated imprinting has been linked to persistent changes in chromatin accessibility and histone modifications in multiple immune cell types, biasing transcriptional responses toward tissue repair, type 2 cytokine production, or immune regulation [25,32–36].

Recent work demonstrates that ILC2s can acquire memory-like properties following type 2 inflammatory stimulation, characterized by durable activation and enhanced responsiveness upon reactivation in allergy [37] and STH infection [38]. IL-25 treatment and prior N. brasiliensis infection generated long-lived ILC2 populations with elevated Il5 and Il13 transcripts and open chromatin at type 2-associated loci that enabled heightened type 2 responses. In the case of IL-25 pre-treatment, these trained ILC2s spurred highly efficient STH expulsion in mice [38]. Mathä et al. recently described a CD127negative, CD45ROhigh ILC2 population in humans with an augmented ability to produce cytokines and proliferate, similar to memory-like ILC2s described in mice [39]. These findings position ‘trained’ ILC2s as key mediators that enhance future anti-helminth responses.

Myeloid populations, particularly macrophages, are similarly shaped by infection history. STH exposure promotes alternative macrophage polarization and tissue repair functions [40] and elicits macrophage epigenetic changes that impact the host–helminth relationship. For example, H. polygyrus-derived glutamate dehydrogenase elicited anti-inflammatory prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) production via p300-mediated histone acetylation to promote STH chronicity [34]. Prior exposure to STH products from a variety of species can also actively modulate M1-like macrophage function and dampen subsequent pro-inflammatory or autoimmune responses [32,33,35,36]. In addition, prior Strongyloides venezuelensis infection altered neuroimmune crosstalk, requiring eosinophils to prevent neuronal loss during subsequent Salmonella infection [41]. Thus, STH-programmed ILC2 and myeloid cell functional states can persist beyond pathogen clearance, potentially increasing response speed and altering the tone of the response to subsequent insults.

‘Trained immunity’ in non-hematopoietic cells

STH infections may also induce durable changes in non-hematopoietic cell populations that play key roles in barrier defense, including intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) and neurons (Figure 2). Recent studies demonstrate that inflammatory signals can imprint epithelial stem cells in graft-versus-host disease and allergy, altering differentiation trajectories and epithelial function long after the resolution of the inciting insult [42,43]. In STH infection, such imprinting could enhance mucus production, barrier repair, or alarmin release. Long-lived neural populations represent another emerging site of immune environmental memory. Enteric neurons respond dynamically to STH-induced inflammation [1]. Neuronal imprinting or ‘memory’ has recently been shown in a preprint to occur following papain exposure in the skin, which amplifies antigen-independent secondary responses [44]. However, epithelial or neuronal imprinting during helminth infection has not been described.

Heritability of trained immunity

Growing evidence indicates that immune imprinting can extend across generations. Maternal infection or inflammation during pregnancy can program offspring immune and tissue-resident cells, influencing immune responses and altering susceptibility to inflammatory diseases later in life [45,46]. For example, mice born to mothers that had been infected with H. polygyrus exhibited accelerated neural maturation, increased expression of neurogenesis-, glycogenesis-, and myelination-associated genes, and increased resistance to infection with H. polygyrus [47]. Maternal type I interferon-mediated prenatal inflammation provoked the accumulation of hyperactivated ILC2s in offspring and was associated with increased asthma susceptibility in adulthood [48], though whether similar processes occur during maternal STH infection remains unclear. Additionally, maternal Schistosoma mansoni infection resulted in impaired B cell responses to tetanus vaccination in male offspring [46]. Together, these findings support a model in which host–helminth interactions are shaped not by single encounters but by layered histories of infection and inflammation. Defining how these imprints are established, maintained, and potentially reversed remains an opportunity for future therapeutic intervention.

Integration of environmental cues by the neuronal–immune–epithelial axis

Neuronal–immune–epithelial (NIE) systems integrate at the mucosal barrier to maintain homeostasis and orchestrate host defense against a constantly shifting environment, including the commensal microbiota and extrinsic environmental factors. Environmental threats, such as pollution or helminths, act as inflammatory stimuli that override this homeostatic baseline. Upon threat detection in the gut, chemosensory tuft cells, dendritic cells, and neuroendocrine cells relay signals to adjacent neuromedin U (NMU)-producing and cholinergic sensory neurons through the release of epithelial alarmins, such as IL-25. These signals trigger the release of NMU, which directly acts on ILC2s and mast cells to amplify acetylcholine, IL-5, and IL-13 production, accelerating effector activities and promoting the ‘weep and sweep’ response that drives parasite expulsion [49–51] (Figure 3). In this context, precise integration of environmental signals across the NIE axis is essential for protective host defense.

Dietary and commensal microbial signals in the NIE axis at homeostasis

The maturation and function of the NIE triad are heavily dependent on signals derived from diet and the commensal microbiota. At homeostasis, epithelial sensors such as enterochromaffin cells and enteric glial cells detect microbiota-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and bacterial ligands. These signals tune the excitability of intrinsic primary afferent neurons (IPANs) and stimulate enteric glia to secrete neurotrophic factors, such as glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) [52]. To maintain tolerance, commensal bacteria and SCFAs derived from dietary fiber directly influenced serotonin biosynthesis in enterochromaffin cells and promoted Treg development [52]. In parallel, Wang et al. noted that enteric glial cells sensed bacterial products through Toll-like receptors, prompting secretion of GDNF; this ligand stimulated ILC3 production of IL-22 and epithelial barrier integrity [1]. Dietary intake further modulates this circuitry through feeding-dependent activation of vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)-ergic neurons, which released VIP to inhibit IL-22 production by CC chemokine receptor 6 (CCR6)+ ILC3s via VIP receptor 2, transiently reducing antimicrobial peptide expression to facilitate nutrient absorption during feeding cycles [53]. Together, these coordinated neuroimmune pathways link dietary and microbial inputs to epithelial barrier tone, ensuring mucosal homeostasis.

NIE responses to helminth infection and other stimuli

When mucosal barriers are exposed to noxious environmental stimuli, such as parasites and pollution, rapidly triggered NIE reflexes provoke type 2 inflammation (Figure 3). Under chronic inflammation, enteric glial networks are often associated with neuronal damage, loss of neural connections, and neuroinflammation [54]. Jean et al. found that helminths induced profound remodeling and hyperexcitability of sensory neurons that drive the ‘weep and sweep’ expulsion response. This response depended on specialized sensory neuron subsets, including NMU-high populations that detect parasite-induced leukotrienes and type 2 cytokines, such as IL-13. When type 2 inflammation is initiated, neuropeptides, including calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and neuromedin B, functioned as regulatory brakes, limiting ILC2 proliferation and cytokine production to restrain tissue damage [55]. In the respiratory tract, environmental irritants, such as cigarette smoke, activated nociceptors through transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 (TRPA1) and transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channels, inducing substance P release that signaled to CD301b+ dendritic cells to promote allergic immune responses (Figure 3), though the role of this pathway during STH infection remains unknown [56,57]. Chronic or repeated exposure to these stimuli may lead to maladaptive neuronal plasticity and heightened sensitivity, contributing to the persistence of allergic airway disease and post-infection dysfunction [58].

Thus, the NIE axis operates as a tightly integrated and highly plastic circuit that decodes diverse environmental cues. By linking epithelial sensing of luminal signals with rapid neuronal communication and robust immune effector programs, mucosal tissues can dynamically transition between homeostasis and type 2-driven defense. However, this same plasticity may render the system vulnerable to dysregulation: chronic exposure to STHs, environmental irritants, or repeated inflammatory insults can drive maladaptive neuronal remodeling and sustained type 2 activation. Understanding how environmental inputs are integrated across the NIE axis, therefore, represents a critical step toward identifying therapeutic strategies that restore circuit balance while preserving protective immunity.

Environmental regulation of transcriptional programs in anti-helminth immunity

Environmental exposures, including dietary micronutrients, microbial metabolites, and xenobiotics, shape anti-helminth immunity by reprogramming the transcriptional and epigenetic landscapes that govern immune and epithelial cell identity and function. Since mucosal surfaces are the primary interface between environmental signals and STH invasion, IEC subsets, including tuft and goblet cells and stem/progenitor populations, represent a critical transcriptionally responsive compartment that controls type 2 inflammation. Increasing evidence highlights the central role of environmental sensing pathways, such as aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) signaling, sensing of helminth-derived products, and bioactive lipid signaling, as regulators of an integrated transcriptional ecosystem that shapes host–helminth interactions [59–61].

AHR as a xenobiotic-responsive transcriptional hub

AHR is a ligand-activated transcription factor that integrates environmental signals to regulate transcriptional programs. During helminth infection, hosts are exposed to AHR ligands through several routes: dietary tryptophan- and indole-containing compounds that are converted to high-affinity AHR agonists [62], microbiota-derived tryptophan catabolites, such as indole derivatives [63], and environmental pollutants, including dioxin-like compounds and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, that activate AHR [64]. More broadly, infection-driven changes in metabolism and the microbiota can further modulate the local pool of endogenous AHR ligands [65]. Across these contexts, AHR acts as an environmental sensor that tunes mucosal immunity, providing the backdrop for its cell-intrinsic role in restraining type 2 effector programs.

Recent studies have shown that AHR directly modulates transcriptional and chromatin landscapes in ILC2s to constrain effector capacity. AHR was highly expressed in intestinal ILC2s, where it limited their activation through coordinated transcriptional repression and restriction of chromatin accessibility at key ILC2 effector loci. Loss of AHR in ILC2s resulted in enhanced type 2 cytokine production and resistance to H. polygyrus in the small intestine [66]. Beyond ILC2s, AHR also regulates eosinophil function. Work by Wang et al. [67] demonstrated that AHR promoted immunomodulatory Clec4A4+ eosinophils and limited eosinophil-driven type 2 inflammation. Loss of AHR in eosinophils drove enhanced ILC2 expansion and resistance to N. brasiliensis.

AHR also plays a regulatory role in the epithelium. In the airway, epithelial AHR deficiency resulted in exaggerated type 2 responses, increased goblet cell hyperplasia, and increased alarmin release [68]. Although this study examined allergens rather than helminths, the transcriptional consequences are directly relevant to the intestinal environment in STH infection: loss of epithelial AHR would be predicted to amplify the epithelial–ILC2 circuit and anti-helminth defense. These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for how environmental pollutants or high-xenobiotic diets, both potent activators of AHR, may suppress the host’s capacity to expel worms [69].

Together, these studies suggest that AHR activation imposes a cross-tissue transcriptional brake on type 2 immunity. These mechanisms may protect tissues from chronic type 2 inflammation but carry a significant trade-off by reducing readiness to mount robust IL-13-driven effector programs. In this way, AHR integrates environmental signals into a transcriptional program that biases mucosal tissues toward homeostasis at the cost of diminished anti-helminth immunity, though how AHR ligands influence human resistance to helminth infection remains unknown.

Transcriptional regulation of tuft cell identity and function

IECs serve as central environmental sensors, integrating nutrient availability, microbial metabolites, and STH-derived molecules into transcriptional programs that determine type 2 inflammation. Among IEC subsets, tuft cells offer a particularly clear model for dissecting how environmental inputs shape epithelial transcription. Their identity is defined by transcriptional drivers, including POU class 2 homeobox 3 (POU2F3), supported by atonal homolog 1 (ATOH1), growth factor-independent 1B (GFI1B), and the ion channel transient receptor potential melastatin 5 (TRPM5) [70–72]. As chemosensory sentinels, tuft cells initiate the IL-25 → ILC2 → IL-13 axis that drives goblet cell hyperplasia and helminth expulsion [73–75].

Environmental cues strongly influence intestinal epithelial stem cell (ISC) differentiation programs that feed into tuft cell production. Helminth-secreted molecules appear to be particularly important, counteracting the host type 2 cytokine-mediated IEC response. For example, H. polygyrus excretory/secretory (ES) products blocked both tuft and goblet cell differentiation in the presence of IL-13, potentially via Atoh1 suppression and upregulation of Hes1, indicative of Notch-mediated inhibition of secretory cell differentiation [76]. H. polygyrus ES also promoted a ‘fetal-like’ reversion of IEC transcriptional state that was not fully dependent on yes-associated protein (YAP), a transcriptional regulator of stem cell renewal, but was dependent on oxidative stress responses [77]. These data reveal direct helminth-mediated inhibition of epithelial effector lineage specification via transcriptional remodeling, though the complete underlying mechanisms remain to be fully defined.

Mature tuft cells detect environmental signals through a chemosensory system, including G protein-coupled receptors and TRPM5-dependent depolarization [78]. This machinery enables the sensing of metabolites such as succinate, which activates the succinate receptor 1 (SUCNR1) on tuft cells and induces IL-25 secretion [79]. Although the precise STH-derived ligand(s) remain unknown, STH colonization consistently expands tuft cells and enhances IL-25 transcription, implying that STHs release or induce metabolites that activate these cells. Tuft cells also produce prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), which can act back on IECs that express the PGD2 receptor chemoattractant receptor-homologous molecule expressed by Th2 cells (CRTH2) to limit STH-induced IEC responses [80], but it is unknown whether dietary lipid composition, local inflammatory lipid mediators, or helminth-derived lipids regulate CRTH2-dependent effects.

Despite these insights, major mechanistic questions remain unresolved. It is still unclear which AHR ligands, microbial tryptophan metabolites, or STH-derived factors modulate transcription factors or enhancer landscapes to link environmental sensing in IECs generally, and tuft cells specifically, to modulate type 2 inflammation. Resolving the transcriptional programs that link environmental sensing to epithelial effector output remains a key frontier for understanding how IECs orchestrate anti-helminth immunity.

Concluding remarks and future directions

Host–helminth interactions are shaped by more than immune recognition alone, emerging instead from the integration of environmental context, tissue history, and transcriptional state. Dietary nutrients, microbial metabolites, xenobiotics, and prior inflammatory exposures establish durable programs across epithelial, neuronal, and immune compartments. These influences converge to tune the magnitude, timing, and stability of type 2 immunity at the tissue level, dictating the outcome of infection. As a result, anti-helminth immunity reflects a context-dependent equilibrium rather than a binary outcome.

A central implication of this framework is that anti-helminth immunity is fundamentally a tissue property that stems from integrated molecular events. This coordination allows hosts to balance parasite expulsion with tissue protection but also introduces vulnerability: repeated or chronic activation can drive maladaptive remodeling and persistent inflammatory set points. At the molecular level, environmental cues function as transcriptional instructors rather than passive modifiers, reshaping chromatin landscapes and metabolic programs that determine effector potential and responsiveness to subsequent challenges. Importantly, these pathways intersect within the same cells. How signals such as RA availability, fatty acid metabolism, and AHR activation are integrated into unified transcriptional outputs remains poorly defined, particularly in tissues exposed to fluctuating environmental inputs.

Major limitations persist (see Outstanding questions). Most mechanistic insight derives from reductionist animal models that fail to capture the complexity of human exposure, where malnutrition, pollution, co-infection, and repeated STH encounters are the norm rather than the exception. The durability, reversibility, and developmental timing of environmentally imprinted programs, especially in epithelial and neuronal compartments, remain largely unknown in humans. Addressing these gaps will require longitudinal, tissue-resolved approaches that explicitly incorporate environmental variability, alongside multiomic strategies capable of linking metabolic state and chromatin architecture to functional outcomes.

This integrated perspective has important translational implications. STH control strategies remain dominated by parasite-targeted therapies, yet host susceptibility and pathology are clearly shaped by environmental and metabolic context. Targeting environmentally responsive regulatory nodes may offer new avenues to enhance protective immunity, mitigate tissue damage, or restore balance in settings of chronic infection. Conversely, pathways that restrain type 2 immunity in STH infection may be leveraged therapeutically in allergic diseases that share common tissue programs. In this way, STH biology provides a powerful lens through which to understand how environments write long-term instructions into tissue immunity, with relevance extending far beyond parasitic infection.
