AI-Generated Game Commentary: A Survey and a Datasheet Repository
Qirui Zheng, Xingbo Wang, Keyuan Cheng, Yunlong Lu, Wenxin Li, Peking University South China University of Technology zzzqr@stu.pku.edu.cn jacksimbol@stu.pku.edu.cn lwx@pku.edu.cn

Abstract
AI-Generated Game Commentary (AIGGC) has gained increasing attention due to its market potential and inherent technical challenges. As a comprehensive multimodal Natural Language Processing (NLP) task, AIGGC imposes substantial demands on language models, including factual accuracy, logical reasoning, expressive text generation, generation speed, and context management. In this paper, we introduce a general framework for AIGGC and present a comprehensive survey of 45 existing game commentary dataset and methods according to key challenges they aim to address in this domain. We further classify and compare various evaluation metrics commonly used in this domain. To support future research and benchmarking, we also provide a structured datasheet summarizing the essential attributes of these datasets in appendix, which is meanwhile publicly available in an open repository.

AI-Generated Game Commentary (AIGGC) refers to the task of automatically generating game commentary in natural language. Game commentary is a core component of game broadcasting, significantly enhancing viewer engagement

and contributing to commercial revenue
Deloitte. 2024

Silver et al. 2016

Tomlin et al. 2022

AIGGC is a highly challenging task. First, it requires AI commentator to handle complex multimodal inputs such as text

Yashwante et al. 2024

, audio

Kameko et al. 2015

To address the challenging tasks of AIGGC, numerous studies have emerged. However, there is still a lack of a comprehensive survey that systematically reviews existing AIGGC methods and datasets. Conducting a systematic review poses significant challenges: (i) the multimodal nature of game inputs—such as visual feeds, audio streams, and in-game telemetry data—varies significantly across different game genres; and (ii) commentary styles are highly diverse, ranging from emotionally charged play-by-play narration to tactical analysis and player background exposition. (iii) different datasets store games with widely varying styles and formats, making unified analysis difficult.

To address the above gap, we first propose a unified framework that spans all stages of the AIGGC pipeline, providing a systematic review of existing commentary generation methods. We also survey existing game commentary datasets by game category, analyzing their key attributes, target benchmarks, and data processing methods. 2
AIGGC Landscape

In this section, we give a common framework of AIGGC and compare three types of games based on the proposed framework.

2.1
Commentary Framework

There are multiple methods of division for game commentary.
Andrews et al. 2024a
introduced a basic distinction between play-by-play commentary and color commentary. While
Jhamtani et al. 2018
categorized chess game commentary into six categories: direct move description, quality of move, comparative, move rationale or planning, contextual game information and general information. This detailed classification, however, over-emphasizes move evaluation, making it less applicable to sports or esports commentary.

In this paper, we categorize game commentary based on the roles and purposes of comments as the following three categories:

Descriptive commentary
encompasses direct descriptions of the game’s current situations and players’ moves. Descriptive commentary is produced based on raw game data, which refers to raw data collected from real game plays, e.g. video recordings, live feeds, textual records, etc. The raw data are preprocessed to obtain decoupled (and sometimes aligned) multimodal data and basic representations of game states, which are then used to generate descriptive commentary.

Analytic commentary
involves evaluations of current states and recent moves, comparisons of alternative moves, analysis of tactic or strategy, and predictions or deductions about upcoming moves. Analytic commentary is generated by referring to domain knowledge (game rules, strategies and tips, etc) and strategic reasoning with decision models or algorithms according to current game states. External insights—such as statistics, win/loss probabilities, or move recommendations from models like AlphaGo
Branavan et al. 2012
—can also guide this process.

Background commentary

provides background information relevant to current games as supplementary contexts, including player or team details, related matches, etc. Background commentary keeps the audience engaged by enriching viewers’ understanding and enjoyment
Siu et al. 2023
, and can be produced by referring to a database of related information and retrieving contexts most relevant to the current game. Figure
1
illustrates a typical AIGGC framework.

Figure 1:
A typical AIGGC framework takes three input components: raw data, domain knowledge , and related information. The raw data undergoes pre-processing to extract timelines, text, audio, and structured data. The processed data is then utilized by a basic descriptor to generate the game-state representation and corresponding description. Based on game-state representations, analytic commentary is generated by an expert model incorporating domain knowledge and decision-making algorithms. The background retriever then retrieve related information according to its relevance to current game and produce background commentary. The three categories are combined to produce a comprehensive commentary.

2.2
Commentary Styles for Different Game Categories

This survey focuses on three categories of games: board games, sports games, and video games (esports). Notably, electronic board games like online Hold’em are classified as board games rather than video games in this discussion, and sports simulation games like Robocup
Chen et al. 2001
are classified as esports games rather than sports games.

Commentary generation involves raw data processing and text/audio generation within a limited reaction time. As a result, AI commentators adopt different strategies to produce comments with distinct styles for different types of games, which can be depicted by the contents and weights of three commentary components mentioned in
2.1
. For example, the raw data of board games consist of simple images or text-based game scores which are easy to process
Alrdahi and Batista-Navarro 2024
. Thus, AI commentary can allocate more resources to analytic commentary, and the turn-based nature of board games also allows for longer response times, making in-depth strategy deduction possible. On the other hand, for sports and video games, the raw data are usually videos containing multiple entities in motion, sometimes mixed with structured data (especially for video games)
Ishigaki et al. 2021
, which require considerable computation to process and understand within a more limited time. Therefore, accurate game state description is currently the leading problem of sports and esports commentary generation, while analytic commentary is temporarily limited to brief identifications and analysis of current tactics.

3
Datasets and Benchmarks

Datasets are essential for training, testing, and evaluating commentary generation models. This section starts from the composition of the datasets, and then we present the datasets in the order of game categories. In appendix
A
, we provide a datasheet summarizing essential attributes of datasets.

3.1
Dataset Composition and Modalities

AIGGC datasets usually contain the following three components:

Game states
. The game states refer to a record of the current situation in the game and the actions taken so far, often in diverse modalities such as video, image frames, and text. Among these, video is the most common in sports and esports, as matches are inherently presented in videos which offer the most comprehensive information. Image frames, typically extracted around key events, help filter out irrelevant content and highlight salient moments
Ghosh et al. 2024
. Text-based records naturally suit board games. In sports and esports, game states can also be logged in text through Event Spotting (ES)
Host and Ivasic-Kos 2022
. For example,
Giancola et al. 2018
labeled timestamps as textual events in soccer video with three predefined main classes, actually discretizing the action space. Some researchers manually annotate events
Faulkner and Dick 2017
;
Zhang and Eickhoff 2021
, while others train specific models to automatically identify events and related information from video
Kim and Choi 2020
;
Siu et al. 2023
.

Commentary
. Commentary data is typically recorded in text form, obtained either by scraping live commentaries from the web
Mkhallati et al. 2023
or by using automatic speech recognition to transcribe spoken commentary from videos
Zhang et al. 2024b
or audio
Ishigaki et al. 2023
. While most available commentary is in English, some datasets are also available in Chinese
Wu et al. 2024
, Japanese
Mori et al. 2016
, Korean
Mori et al. 2016
, and other languages.

With both game states and commentary introduced, we now turn to the alignment process that connects the two part. Alignment refers to the explicit linking of each game state with its corresponding commentary. This step is essential for providing a clear input–output correspondence that enables models to better learn the relationship between game representations and natural language. In most sports and esports games, where game states are presented as videos, alignment is typically achieved via timestamps or time intervals. In contrast, for turn-based board games, game states and commentary are naturally segmented by turns. In some studies, alignment is tailored to support specific research objectives, which will be discussed in Section
3.3
. Alignment also facilitates precise evaluation using metrics such as recall and precision
Marrese-Taylor et al. 2022
, and supports diverse research directions.

Background data
. Background data provides valuable information beyond game states and commentary. It includes external knowledge like game statistics
Chen et al. 2016
and related anecdotes
Lee et al. 2014
, supporting commentary generation either in conjunction with game states
Zhang et al. 2024a
or independently
Chen et al. 2016
.

3.2
Board Game Commentary Datasets

So far, commentary datasets for board games have mostly been created for chess, Go, Shogi(a Japanese chess), and GuanDan (a Chinese card game). In board game commentary datasets, the game state typically includes current board states and moves. In many cases, the moves can be inferred by comparing the previous and current board states given the turn-based nature of board games. As mentioned earlier, the turn-based nature of board games inherently ensures alignment between game states and commentary across rounds. Moreover, the slower pace allows commentators more time for reflection and reasoning, often leading to commentary with greater analytical depth.

Most board-game commentary datasets are structured as aligned pairs of game states and commentaries.
Jhamtani et al. 2018
pioneered a publicly available chess commentary dataset, comprising 298K move-by-move game states paired with human annotations from an online chess forum
1
. This dataset classifies commentaries into six types, establishing an essential benchmark for researches in automated chess commentary generation
Zang et al. 2019
;
Kim et al. 2024
. For Shogi ,
Kameko et al. 2015
curated a dataset featuring 4,327 games of varying ranks and 218,615 Japanese commentary, collected from an online resource
2
. Additionally, a comprehensive Go commentary corpus
Tomlin et al. 2022
consists of 9,524 games on standard 19×19 boards and 458,182 annotations obtained via digital redistribution
3
. They further converted board states and comments into feature vectors based on extracted patterns and keywords.

Other datasets are specifically annotated to support some research goals.
Kamlish et al. 2019
presented a chess dataset of 15,000 moves annotated with corresponding sentiment labels, serving as guidance for game-state understanding and logical reasoning.
Alrdahi and Batista-Navarro 2024
extracted structured and unstructured chess data from textbooks to construct player–predicate–move triples annotated with sentiment polarity.
The Shogi Commentary Corpus
Mori et al. 2016
contains 742,286 Japanese commentary sentences, each tied to a specific game state and annotated with domain-specific named entities defined therein.
Matsuyoshi et al. 2018
collected Shogi commentaries paired with board states and enriched the dataset through manual annotations of modality expressions, event classes, and factuality. The resulting dataset contains 1,622 modality expressions, 5,014 event class tags, and 3,092 factuality tags. This data set was then augmented by
Kameko and Mori 2020
by adding event appearance labels. Another Go-related dataset
Ikeda et al. 2016
includes moves annotated as "good" or "bad" by expert-level players based on matches played by intermediate players and AI opponent. Expert-level players annotated these moves based on matches played by intermediate players and AI opponent.

3.3
Sports Commentary Datasets

Sports commentary datasets cover multiple categories, including soccer, basketball, baseball, and cricket. As sports games are always presented as videos, commentary generation for sports games places a high demand on the multimodal processing capability of the models. In video-based input, multiple players may potentially act simultaneously, making basic state description a significant challenge for models. A common approach to address this complexity is ES, spotting predefined events from videos
Faulkner and Dick 2017
. While ES simplifies the game-state representation, it inevitably leads to information loss. SoccerNet
Giancola et al. 2018
introduced a representative dataset to support ES tasks by annotating soccer videos collected from the internet with actions categorized into three classes. Furthermore, they released a total of 506,137 commentaries to support AIGGC tasks.
Deliège et al. 2021
refined the dataset to SoccerNet-v2 by expanding the original three categories into 17 categories, enabling finer-grained classification and benchmarking using standard ES baselines. Subsequently,
Cioppa et al. 2022
further extended the dataset into SoccerNet-v3 by incorporating spatial annotations and cross-view correspondences.
Qi et al. 2023
selected 20 full-match videos from SoccerNet-v2 and transcribed the audio content into text using the Azure automatic speech recognition toolkit. A dataset named GOAL was obtained after manual proofreading, video-text alignment, and augmentation with heterogeneous knowledge sources.

Game-state representations extend beyond textual event logs to incorporate multimodal inputs such as video clips and audio streams. Handling multimodal inputs introduce additional challenges on AIGGC models.
Gautam et al. 2024
augmented the SoccerNet dataset into a new dataset, SoccerNet-Echoes, by automatically transcribing and translating audio commentaries in different languages. They tested several automatic speech recognition models based on this benchmark. The dataset
Ghosh et al. 2024
contains 2 hours of T20 cricket match footage from two different camera angles, processed into delivery-by-delivery video segments. Manual ball-by-ball commentary was collected from platforms, cleaned, and aligned with the corresponding video segments. To ensure robust generalization of the models,
Yashwante et al. 2024
employed a dataset including not only cricket game images and text commentaries, but also images and captions of various daily scenes and activities. In the basketball domain,
Chen et al. 2016
assembled a comprehensive NBA dataset integrating play-by-play textual data, action area annotations, box score statistics, and game video footage across entire NBA seasons.
Yu et al. 2018
first proposed a fine-grained multi-person sports narrative benchmark and introduced the Fine-grained Sports Narrative (FSN) dataset, comprising 2,000 selected video clips featuring detailed and diverse player motions, accompanied by 6,520 descriptive sentences; this dataset was later expanded to 5,903 video segments and 9,623 descriptions, with annotations refined by expert basketball analysts to produce the enhanced Sports Video Narrative (SVN) dataset
Yan et al. 2022
. The baseball commentary dataset proposed by
Kim and Choi 2020
not only contains event annotations but also incorporates baseball ontology knowledge to enrich contextual information for model training.

For sports commentary datasets, most alignments between game states and commentary are still performed manually, which is both time-consuming and labor-intensive. To mitigate this burden,
Rao et al. 2024
trained an automated commentary–timeline alignment pipeline on a small set of manually calibrated data. The pipeline integrated LLaMA-3 and contrastive learning, and was subsequently applied to correct and filter large-scale existing data. The result is a high-quality soccer commentary dataset named MatchTime to support downstream AIGGC tasks. In addition to automated pipelines, several alignment ways have been designed to support specific research objectives and benchmarking tasks. For example,
Mkhallati et al. 2023
developed a dataset by selecting data of 471 games from SoccerNet’s 500 games and aligning each commentary with a single timestamp. This dataset, SoccerNet-Caption, supports a novel task of generating captions anchored to a single timestamp in long, untrimmed soccer videos.
Zhang and Eickhoff 2021
presented a novel dataset from goal.com where there are more commentaries than events. The task with a sparse information distribution better reflects real-world scenarios and is more challenging, as proven by the empirical results.

In addition, game-related story recommendation can enhance audience engagement and emotional resonance during commentary. To support this,
Akshaya and Nisha 2015
introduced a vectorized story library for cricket, enabling dynamic story recommendation during commentary generation. In baseball, a story dataset was initially compiled from narrative content sourced from Wikipedia and two books on historic baseball stories
Lee et al. 2012
. This dataset was later expanded using structured MLB game statistics in XML format, collected from MLB’s official XML repository
Lee et al. 2014
.

3.4
Esports Commentary Datasets

Esports closely resembles traditional sports as both are based on video input and involve multiple players potentially acting simultaneously; consequently, esports commentary datasets share several characteristics with those of sports like the need for ES. Additionally, esports provides a distinctive advantage: the availability of official APIs
Wang and Yoshinaga 2021
. These APIs enable precise access to player actions, full game-state information, and other structured data for downstream analysis and text generation, including match metadata and audio streams
Ringer et al. 2019
. The diverse modalities of raw data imposes greater demands on models’ capability to process, understand, and generate multimodal content effectively.

Multiplayer Online Battle Arena (MOBA) games are among the most popular esports genres. For League of Legends (LoL), the LoL-V2T dataset
Tanaka and Simo-Serra 2021
includes video clips from LoL World Championships with gameplay-irrelevant segments removed by ResNeXt-50, each clip paired with multiple captions segmented via DeepSegment.
Wang and Yoshinaga 2021
introduced another dataset by extracting structured match events from official APIs of the 2019 LoL World Championship and aligning the events with auto-generated timestamped YouTube commentary. This dataset, named LoL19, was later expanded into LoL19-21 in the subsequent work
Wang and Yoshinaga 2024
, covering World Championships from 2019 to 2021. Additionally,
Zhang et al. 2024a
proposed Game-MUG, a multimodal dataset featuring match event logs, openSMILE-extracted audio features, and textual commentary from professionals and audiences. Game-MUG comprises 70,711 annotated clips and over 3.6 million audience chat messages, precisely aligned and enriched with commentary annotations generated by gpt-4.
Ringer et al. 2019
presented another multimodal dataset, containing 7,200 clips, each annotated for affect and game context. The emotional state is evaluated from audio and video modalities across two dimensions: valence and arousal. Besides LOL, the Dota2-Commentary dataset
Zhang et al. 2022
contains 234 matches with event-commentary pairs for Dota2, of which 70 matches were manually annotated by experienced game annotators, yielding 7,473 commentary entries, while the remaining matches were automatically annotated based on rules, generating an additional set of 175,627 commentaries.

The widely studied RoboCup simulator
Chen et al. 2001
provides detailed physical simulations of robot soccer matches along with structured data collected from visual, aural, body sensors, and log players.
Liang et al. 2009
employed a first attempt. They constructed a large candidate commentary database, then matched game scenes with candidate sentences to generate coherent commentary.
Chen and Mooney 2008
then introduced a RoboCup sportscasting dataset featuring human-annotated English commentary aligned with sensor-captured events. This resource was subsequently extended to incorporate Korean commentary
Chen et al. 2014
.
Ishigaki et al. 2021
built a Japanese commentary dataset consisting of 2,437 video recordings of the racing game Assetto Corsa captured from two viewpoints, which were aligned with 129,226 timestamped utterances transcribed from human comments and multimodal structured data from APIs. They later proposed an English version of this dataset
Ishigaki et al. 2023
.

4
Methodologies

This section surveys the technical methodologies adopted in prior AIGGC studies. We organize the discussion around the key challenges in AIGGC following our proposed framework in
2.1
, highlighting major obstacles and the techniques developed to address them.

4.1
Multimodal Input Processing

Processing and understanding complex multimodal data remains one of the core challenges in AIGGC. Currently, two main approaches are adopted to convert raw data into game-state representation: event-based representation and embedding-based representation.

Spotting predefined events from raw video offers a simplified solution to multimodal understanding. Once these events are spotted by trained classifiers, relevant video segments can be directly analyzed—e.g., with language models—without the need to process the entire video stream. Spotted events can either be used for template-based commentary generation
Kumano et al. 2019
;
Rauf et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2021
, or incorporated into prompts for language models
Tao et al. 2024
;
Ranella and Eger 2023
;
Taniguchi et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Andrews et al. 2024b
.

Embedding-based representation involves various encoders to directly transform video or other modalities of data into vector representations.
Jhamtani et al. 2018
first introduced the Game-Aware neural Commentary (GAC) model, capturing semantic and pragmatic features such as move threats and evaluation scores from chessboard states, and integrating these using a bidirectional LSTM encoder.
Zang et al. 2019
subsequently refined GAC into the Skilled Chess Commentator (SCC) model. The system integrates a neural chess engine with five specialized chessboard state encoders, each designed to produce unique commentary perspectives. This architecture enhances the semantic depth and diversity of the generated commentary.

Ul Abideen et al. 2021
adopted VGG-16 as the encoder to extract features directly from full video clips, while
Ghosh et al. 2024
applied the same encoder to process frames containing key information.
Zhang et al. 2024b
proposed a transformer-based multimodal encoder. They used the Video Swin Transformer (VST) to encode visual input and BERT to process simple textual descriptions. The two modalities are then via a multimodal transformer enhanced with prompts, enabling downstream commentary generation.

4.2
Content Selection

Content selection is a major challenge in AIGGC, especially for high-intensity sports and esports, where models must identify the most salient actions from numerous simultaneous events for commentary generation. So far, this problem has received limited attention.
Mkhallati et al. 2023
implemented a spotting head with a dense layer to predict whether to generate commentary within a time window, with non-maximum suppression reducing redundant outputs.
Mei et al. 2016
proposed a coarse-to-fine aligner that first filters irrelevant records by assigning each a selection probability and alignment likelihood. A fine-grained refiner then re-weights these scores to make the final selection decision.

4.3
Knowledge-Enhanced Analytic Commentary

In Section
4.1
we discussed language models’ capabilities to process multimodal input and produce descriptive commentary. Domain-specific expertise is required, however, to produce in-depth analytic commentary
Feng et al. 2023
. Language models mainly obtain the external expertise by incorporating domain knowledge or decision-making algorithms.

Domain Knowledge
. To incorporate domain-specific knowledge into generative models,
Kim and Choi 2020
converted Deep Learning (DL) recognition results into keywords and querying a domain ontology to retrieve relevant information for template-based generation of baseball analysis. For board games,
Kim et al. 2024
extracted prioritized chess-specific concept vectors from board states with a chess expert system and used the concept vectors to guide commentary generation.
Tao et al. 2024
integrated game rules into a state-commentary guider to produce clearer descriptions and employed a two-level Theory of Mind
Guo et al. 2023
to enable deeper reasoning in the context of GuanDan.

Decision-making Algorithms
. Decision making algorithms facilitate commentary generation by providing move evaluation and prediction, which can be used to improve the reasonability and depth of analytic commentary. The turn-based mechanics of board games, along with their extended action intervals and relatively straightforward game states, create favorable conditions for generating analytical commentary. At the same time, the inherent complexity of their gameplay presents unique challenges for in-depth analysis. To improve Chess Commentaries,
Lee et al. 2022
prompted BART with symbolic reasoning engines and fine-tuned it on manually annotated samples labeled with commentary categories.
Kameko et al. 2015
used the evaluation scores of a computer program to ground Shogi commentary to state spaces. For move analysis, they generated multiple candidate trees with rule-based methods and picked the commented tree with the least "bad moves" according to the evaluation scores by Shogi programs.
Decision-making algorithms are also applied to generate analytic commentary for sports and esports
Chen and Mooney 2008
first improved Word-Alignment-based Semantic Parsing (WASP)
Wong and Mooney 2007
, a method to translate Natural Language (NL) to Meaning Representations (MR), into WASPER-GEN by using expectation maximization-like retraining to learn from ambiguous supervision and selecting NL–MR pairs based on the NIST score of generation. Then
Chen et al. 2014
proposed WASPER-GEN-IGSL, an algorithm that extends WASPER-GEN by combining tactical and strategic generation scores to improve the disambiguation of NL–MR pairs. In a related line of work,
Rauf et al. 2020
used a rule-based method with regex matching on T20 cricket commentary to analyze batsman performance across bowling and fielding zones, demonstrating that AIGGC models can provide tactical and strategic insights beyond simple event descriptions.

4.4
Background Commentary

As introduced in
2.1
, background commentary includes contents such as general game information, player or team details, related matches, etc, serving as a complement to primary commentary discussed above. Background commentary can be implemented from various perspectives.
Lee et al. 2012
framed commentary recommendation as an information retrieval task, ranking feature similarity between game states and narratives with AdaRank
Xu and Li 2007
. A pointwise regression model filtered candidates as a evaluator, and a context controller ensured timely story insertion.
Andrews et al. 2024a
proposed a personalized color commentator with a retrieval module in addition to a play-by-play commentator, enabling viewers to customize team bias, enhancing emotional engagement.

4.5
Text Generation and Multimodal Rendering

Current text generation methods in AIGGC can be categorized into three types: template-based generation, traditional DL-based generation, and LLM-based generation. Template-based methods are popular in automated sports commentary
Kumano et al. 2019
;
Chan et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Xu et al. 2021
mainly due to the outstanding generating speed, with additional benefits like description correctness and accuracy, but often produce rigid and repetitive outputs
Gatt and Krahmer 2017
. Traditional DL-based approaches improve language diversity, flexibility, and contextual awareness at an acceptable generation speed
Dong et al. 2021
, commonly employing LSTM decoders
Ul Abideen et al. 2021
;
Yan et al. 2016
;
Yan et al. 2022
;
Mei et al. 2016
. Recently, LLM-generated commentary have shown greater fluency, contextual coherence, and strategic analytical depth
Shanahan 2022
. Multiple studies have utilized large language models (LLMs) as generative models
Andrews et al. 2024b
;
Wang and Yoshinaga 2024
;
Andrews et al. 2024a
;
Wu et al. 2024
with carefully-crafted prompts. However, they still suffer from issues like slow generation speed, high training cost, and hallucinations.

On top of text generation, multimodal rendering represents an emerging frontier in AIGGC. Currently, most non-textual commentaries are derived from generated texts, typically using Text-To-Speech (TTS) APIs
Andrews et al. 2024a
.
Xu et al. 2021
generated both textual commentary and volume, pitch for audio, enabling emotionally expressive audio commentary tailored to intense fighting game. Additionally,
Siu et al. 2023
employed TTS for both English and Cantonese, and used StyleGAN to create a virtual commentator with realistic facial expressions and lip-syncing, greatly enhancing viewer engagement.

5
Evaluation Metric

Evaluating automated commentary output is essential and challenging due to the open-ended nature of AIGGC. A major challenge stems from the lack of ground-truth reference, as the same game state can generate multiple equally valid but distinct commentary interpretations from different commentators. Furthermore, there is a lack of robust and quantifiable criteria to evaluate the quality of commentary, and human assessment is inherently subjective, as it depends heavily on individual preferences.

To provide a clearer overview, we group existing evaluation metrics into four main categories: human-centric metrics, untrained automatic metrics, ML-based metrics, and LLM-based metrics.

Human-Centric Metrics
. Human-centric evaluation methods are the most effective form of assessment for all kinds of commentary
Celikyilmaz et al. 2020
. They typically employ questionnaires, using Likert-scale ratings for relative quality or RankME-style evaluations for absolute quality
Novikova et al. 2018
. However, the high cost, time requirements, and reliance on domain expertise limit the practicality of human assessment methods in evaluating AIGGC. Moreover, these methods often suffer from subjectivity and low reproducibility, as evaluation criteria can vary significantly across individuals
Belz and Reiter 2006
.

Untrained Automatic Metric
. Untrained automatic metrics—particularly n-gram overlap metrics—are the most commonly used for evaluating automated commentary
Ishigaki et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Mkhallati et al. 2023
;
Yan et al. 2016
. These metrics assess the degree of overlap between generated and human-written commentary. Common examples include BLEU-4
Papineni et al. 2002
and its variants (BLEU-2, cBLEU, sBLEU
Angeli et al. 2010
), as well as ROUGE-1/2/3/4/L
Lin 2004
, METEOR
Denkowski and Lavie 2014
, CIDEr
Vedantam et al. 2015
, and F1-SCORE
Melamed et al. 2003
; less commonly used metrics include NIST
Reynolds et al. 2000
and SPICE
Anderson et al. 2016
. While these metrics offer fast, low-cost, and repeatable evaluation, they often correlate poorly with human judgment
Reiter and Belz 2009
and rely heavily on n-gram overlap—an assumption ill-suited for AIGGC, where multiple valid outputs may exist for the same input.

Sentiment scores provide an alternative perspective that better aligns with the open-ended nature of commentary. While surface expressions may vary, sentiment polarity often remains consistent—for example, strong moves always receive positive commentary. Thus, sentiment validation between generated and reference texts can serve as a meaningful evaluation signal. However, sentiment-based evaluation remains underexplored: only a few studies have incorporated sentiment into training
Kamlish et al. 2019
;

ML-based Metrics
. ML-based metrics typically involve neural network-based to assess semantic similarity via contextual embeddings
Celikyilmaz et al. 2020
. Among them, a RoBERTa

. ML-based metrics can approximate human evaluation and automate the assessment process, but they require large amounts of annotated data and suffer from the lack of interpretability and proneness to overfitting.

LLM-based Metrics
. The idea of using LLMs for evaluation is common beyond all kinds of similarity metrics. LLMs can provide comprehensive evaluation without reference texts assembling human evaluators
Zhong et al. 2022
, and can align with human preferences via prompting
Mendonça et al. 2023
or post-training

is a framework of using LLMs with Chain-of-Thought (CoT)

and a form-filling paradigm to evalueate NLG tasks requiring creativity and diversity, and
Kim et al. 2024
extended G-Eval into GCC-Eval with an expert chess model’s guidance to better evaluate chess commentary. Although few studies have adopted LLM-based metrics so far, they likely represent the key direction for evaluating AIGGC and probably other open-ended NLP tasks in the future
Li et al. 2025
.

6
Discussion

There are some research challenges and opportunities in the domain of AIGGC:

Datasets’ Scarcity and Limitations
.
At present, commentary is predominantly available for official competitions, thereby constraining the availability of commentary data sources. For sports and esports, processing commentary data is particularly labor-intensive, typically involving audio track extraction, transcription, and manual proofreading and annotation.
Efficient acquisition of high-quality AIGGC datasets necessitates proactive cooperation between researchers and tournament organizers, with an emphasis on the disclosure of massive raw match data on top of post-edited video materials.

The Limitations of LLMs
. LLMs are a highly suitable choice for AIGGC given their capabilities to maintain long-range context, handle multimodal input/output, and produce diverse content
Ray 2023
. However, LLM-based methods are struggling with factual hallucination and inference-related hallucination
Huang et al. 2025
. These issues may be mitigated through methods like GAC and CoT prompting, which enhance factual grounding and reasoning consistency. In addition, the generation speed of LLMs
Ray 2023
remains insufficient for AIGGC, particularly in fast-paced sports and esports scenarios. A hybrid approach that combines template-based and LLM-based methods may offer a practical solution.

Information Loss in Multimodal Processing
. AIGGC inherently requires multimodal capability, especially in sports and esports. To reduce processing complexity, current approaches often apply ES to extract actions from video inputs for commentary generation, leading to inevitable information loss. With advances in video-LLMs
Tang et al. 2023
, speech-LLMs
Arora et al. 2025
and even all-modality models
Ji et al. 2024
, directly interpreting raw video for commentary generation may soon become a feasible and more powerful alternative.

Personalized Preference Commentator
. The advances in LLM alignment technology
Ji et al. 2023
makes it possible for audiences to personalize AI commentators according to their preferences towards commentary styles. While such customization enhance audience engagement, a key challenge remains in the high data and training costs of personalization. Reinforcement learning from AI
Bai et al. 2022
;
Perez et al. 2023
feedback may help address this challenge.

Better Metrics
. Evaluation metrics for AIGGC remain underexplored. Although traditional automatic metrics such as BLEU and ROUGE are widely used, they often fail to capture the true quality of generated commentary due to the open-ended nature of the task
Dong et al. 2021
. A promising direction is to combine the strengths of existing metrics: untrained automatic metrics can effectively assess the accuracy of descriptive commentary, while LLM-based evaluations show strong potential for judging the depth and quality of analytical commentary, as well as the relevance and expressiveness of background commentary.

General Commentator
. Existing efforts have focused narrowly on subsets of commentary generation, with few studies simultaneously addressing the entire commentary pipeline. Achieving human-like commentary generation demands a general AI agent capable of integrates information and delivers multi-faceted output. This necessitates a joint commitment to AI commentary, involving collaboration among academia, players, and competitive gaming bodies.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a comprehensive survey and a common framework for AIGGC, and systematically review the datasets, technical methodologies, and evaluation metrics within this domain. We compile 45 commentary datasets, categorize them clearly, and summarize their key attributes into a datasheet provided in appendix and a public repository. Generation methods and datasets are organized according to the major challenges they address within this domain, while evaluation metrics are classified based on their characteristics, accompanied by comparative analyses of their strengths and limitations. Our survey aims to help researchers select suitable datasets, generation methods, and evaluation metrics tailored to their specific research goals.
