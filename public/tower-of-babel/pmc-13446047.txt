An explainable machine learning model for predicting in-hospital infection in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Jiabao Liu, Yong Wang, Zheyi Dong, Zhe Feng, Xiaomin Liu, Yue Niu, Jie Wu, Li Zhang, Pu Chen, Guangyan Cai, Sai Pan, Ping Li, Xiangmei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfection is a leading cause of mortality in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), yet effective tools for early identification of high-risk patients are lacking. This study aimed to develop an explainable machine learning (ML) model to predict in-hospital infection risk among SLE patients. We analyzed adult patients (≥18 years) with SLE (n = 7,833) from three departments using a population-based electronic medical record database (2000–2024). Among them, 3,157 (40.3%) patients developed an infection after 72 h of hospitalization. An initial comprehensive variable pool of 108 candidate predictors was included, encompassing demographics, comprehensive laboratory parameters, clinical features, disease activity, and treatment exposures. Ten machine learning models were applied. Model performance was evaluated using six metrics. Model interpretability was achieved using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Nine predictors were selected: daily prednisone equivalent dose, albumin, hydroxychloroquine use, C-reactive protein, D-dimer, glucose, cystatin C, hemoglobin, and alpha1-globulin. Among all models tested, the Gradient Boosting model demonstrated the best overall performance on the independent validation set, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.858, with its robustness confirmed by 5-fold and 10-fold cross-validation (mean AUCs of 0.855 ± 0.002 and 0.854 ± 0.008, respectively). SHAP analysis revealed that daily prednisone equivalent dose, albumin, and hydroxychloroquine use were the most influential factors. We developed and validated a high-performance, explainable ML model using nine routinely available clinical variables to accurately predict in-hospital infection risk in SLE patients. This tool provides transparent, individualized risk assessment and has the potential to guide personalized clinical stratification and early intervention, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Introduction

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) is a complex, heterogeneous chronic autoimmune disease that primarily affects women of childbearing age, with significant variations in global prevalence and incidence [1]. Despite substantial improvements in the survival rates of SLE patients over the past decades, infection remains one of the foremost causes of morbidity and mortality, often surpassing lupus activity itself as a primary driver of adverse outcomes [2,3]. Multiple meta-analyses have confirmed that, compared to the general population, SLE patients have a significantly higher risk of severe infection (RR 2.96), pneumonia (RR 2.58), and tuberculosis (RR 6.11) [4]. Infections account for a substantial proportion of hospitalizations in SLE patients (approximately 14%-37%) [5–7] and are the direct cause of death in one-third of cases [2,8,9]. The mortality risk in SLE patients exhibits a unique bimodal pattern, in the early phase, driven by high disease activity and intensive immunosuppression, infection is a predominant cause of death. As the disease progresses to the late phase, while the spectrum of mortality shifts toward long-term complications such as cardiovascular events, infection persists as a significant cause of death [10,11]. This phenomenon underscores that infection risk is not merely an acute complication, but a perpetual, lifelong threat to patients with SLE.

The heightened susceptibility to infection in SLE patients is multifactorial, stemming from intrinsic immune dysregulation, which involves dysfunction of T, B, and NK cells [12–14] alongside defective functioning of neutrophils and macrophages [15], combined with iatrogenic immunosuppression. Numerous studies and meta-analyses have identified key risk factors for infection in SLE. These include a high immunosuppressive burden (particularly heavy exposure to glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressants), elevated disease activity, cumulative organ damage (especially lupus nephritis or renal involvement), older age, male sex, metabolic and inflammatory comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus and elevated inflammatory markers), and immune cytopenias such as lymphopenia and hypocomplementemia [4,16,17]; conversely, hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) use is frequently associated with a lower risk of infection, including severe events, and is broadly recommended in contemporary management guidance [18–20].

Despite the broad recognition of these individual risk factors, a critical gap persists in clinical practice: the lack of a reliable, effective, and validated tool that can integrate these complex factors to provide a personalized, quantitative risk assessment for each patient. Systematic reviews have revealed that the few existing prediction models for infection in SLE suffer from methodological weaknesses, such as small sample sizes, a high risk of bias, and a propensity for overfitting, rendering them unsuitable for widespread clinical application [21]. This situation forces clinicians to rely more on personal experience and intuition when assessing infection risk, leading to a predominantly reactive rather than proactive management strategy. The lack of accurate risk stratification also impedes the timely implementation of targeted preventive measures, including enhanced surveillance, appropriate prophylactic antibiotics, and judicious tapering of immunosuppressive therapy.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful data analysis technique capable of effectively handling and modeling complex, non-linear relationships among variables, offering new avenues for addressing clinical challenges in heterogeneous diseases like SLE [22]. ML has shown great potential in areas such as SLE diagnosis, disease activity prediction, and prognostic analysis [23–25]. However, its application to the critical challenge of predicting infection risk in SLE remains notably underdeveloped.

Therefore, this study aims to leverage a large, real-world clinical dataset to develop and validate a robust, parsimonious, and fully explainable machine learning model for predicting the risk of in-hospital infection among patients with SLE, ultimately to guide timely clinical interventions aimed at reducing mortality and improving prognosis.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study utilized electronic medical record (EMR) data from three clinical departments at the Chinese PLA General Hospital: the Department of Nephrology, the Department of Dermatology, and the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology, covering admissions from 1 January 2000 to 31 July 2024. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (Ethics approval No. S2022-661-01). Given the retrospective design, the use of de-identified existing records, and the minimal risk to participants, the Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital granted a waiver of individual informed consent.

Study population

The study included adult patients hospitalized with a diagnosis of SLE from 1 January 2000 to 31 July 2024 at the First Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (Beijing, China). Missing data handling

For variables with missing values in the final analysis cohort (comprising patients with ≤10% missing baseline data), multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was employed to address the remaining missingness. This method generates multiple plausible imputed datasets, which are then analyzed separately, and the results are combined using Rubin’s rules to account for the uncertainty due to imputation. This approach helps to minimize bias and improve the precision of estimates compared to simpler methods like complete case analysis or single imputation.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was the first occurrence of a nosocomial infection, defined as an infection developing more than 72 h after hospital admission. This composite endpoint was rigorously defined based on the presence of one or more of the following in the EMR: (1) an explicit ICD-9 or ICD-10 diagnosis code for an infectious disease (e.g., pneumonia, sepsis, urinary tract infection); (2) a positive microbiological culture (e.g., from blood, urine, or sputum); or (3) the initiation of a new course of systemic antimicrobial therapy.

Candidate predictors and data extraction

A total of 108 candidate predictor variables were extracted from the EMR for model development. These variables encompassed five principal domains:

Demographics: Age, sex, and body mass index (BMI).

Laboratory Parameters: A comprehensive panel of laboratory tests conducted within the first 24 h of admission was included, categorized into: Hematological profile: Complete blood count and differentials (e.g., hemoglobin, platelet count, white blood cell (WBC) count, lymphocyte count, neutrophils). Serum biochemistry: Including assessments of renal function (e.g., creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, cystatin C), liver function (e.g., alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin), glucose and lipid metabolism, and electrolytes. Inflammatory markers: Such as C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), ferritin and interleukin-6 (IL-6). Coagulation status: Key indicators including D-dimer, fibrinogen, prothrombin time (PT), and international normalized ratio (INR). Immunological profile: Covering complement components (C3, C4), immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgE), and a wide array of SLE-related autoantibodies (e.g., anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, anti-SSA, anti-cardiolipin antibodies). Urinalysis: Parameters such as quantitative proteinuria, urinary pH, and microscopic examination for red blood cells, white blood cells, and casts.

Clinical Features: This domain included assessments of organ involvement at baseline, encompassing renal, neuropsychiatric, cardiac, and hematologic involvement, as well as serositis.

Disease Activity: The Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) score was used to quantify disease activity at admission.

Treatment Exposures: All relevant medication and treatment information was captured, including: daily glucocorticoid dosage (standardized to a prednisone-equivalent dose), use of immunosuppressants (e.g., HCQ, MMF, CTX, azathioprine), biologics (e.g., rituximab, belimumab), and other interventions like renal replacement therapy (RRT) or plasma exchange.

Feature selection

To identify a parsimonious yet robust set of variables for predicting in-hospital infection in SLE patients, a systematic feature selection strategy was implemented. This process aimed to optimize model performance while enhancing clinical interpretability and applicability.

All candidate predictors were first screened using two distinct methods simultaneously: the Boruta algorithm [29], a wrapper method based on Random Forest, and LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression [30]. The Boruta algorithm identifies all features relevant to the outcome by iteratively comparing the importance of real features against ‘shadow’ features, while LASSO regression performs feature selection by applying an L1 penalty that shrinks the coefficients of unimportant variables to zero. The intersection of variables deemed significant by both the Boruta algorithm and LASSO regression was then retained, forming a refined set of candidate variables for the subsequent stage. Following the initial broad screening, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) was employed to systematically identify the most parsimonious and predictive subset of variables from the candidate variables. RFE, a robust wrapper-type feature selection algorithm, operates by iteratively building a model and then removing the least important variables until the optimal subset is reached. This iterative process generates a ranking of all variables based on their contribution to the model’s predictive power. To determine the optimal number of variables, a performance curve was constructed, illustrating the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) of the model as a function of the number of included variables. The point on this curve representing the best balance between model performance and complexity was then selected, aiming to enhance the model’s generalizability and clinical utility by avoiding overfitting and reducing data collection burden.

Model development and validation

For model development and validation, the entire dataset (N = 7,833) was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%, n = 5,483) and an independent validation set (30%, n = 2,350). A comparison of the baseline clinical and demographic characteristics between the two sets revealed no statistically significant differences, confirming the balanced nature of the partition (details are provided in Supplementary Table 1). Within the training set, ten distinct machine learning algorithms were developed based on the nine selected predictors: Logistic Regression, Linear Support Vector Machine (Linear SVM), Support Vector Classification (SVC), K-Nearest Neighbors (K-Neighbors), Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Extremely Randomized Trees (Extra Trees), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), and Gradient Boosting. These algorithms were deliberately chosen to represent major methodological families commonly used for structured clinical data, including linear, kernel-based, tree-based, ensemble, instance-based, and probabilistic approaches. Logistic Regression was included as a conventional, interpretable baseline; linear and kernel-based SVMs capture different decision boundaries; tree-based and ensemble methods (Decision Tree, Random Forest, Extra Trees, AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting) can model complex, non-linear feature interactions; and K-Neighbors and Naive Bayes provide classical distance-based and probabilistic perspectives. This selection allows a comprehensive, diverse comparison of algorithm performance while balancing model complexity, interpretability, and suitability for tabular electronic medical record data. To ensure model robustness and prevent overfitting, a rigorous 10-fold cross-validation scheme was employed within the training set for each algorithm, with hyperparameter optimization performed using a grid search approach. The final performance of all models was comprehensively evaluated on the independent validation set using several metrics, including the confusion matrix, the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve (AUC), accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity, and specificity. Additionally, calibration was assessed by plotting calibration curves to compare the predicted probabilities against the observed infection frequencies. The robustness of the final model was further confirmed by performing 5-fold and 10-fold cross-validation on the entire dataset. For risk stratification analysis, patients were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the predicted probabilities generated by the final selected model. The optimal probability cutoff was determined using the Youden index from the ROC curve. Patients with predicted probabilities greater than or equal to this cutoff were classified as high-risk, whereas those with predicted probabilities below the cutoff were classified as low-risk.

Model interpretability

To ensure clinical transparency, the final selected model was interpreted using SHAP, a game theory-based method that assigns an importance value to each feature for each individual prediction [31]. For global interpretation, SHAP summary bar plots and beeswarm plots were generated to visualize the overall importance and impact of each predictor on the model’s output. To further investigate the effect of each feature, SHAP dependence plots were employed to illustrate how a feature’s value affects its corresponding SHAP value, thereby revealing the relationship between the feature and the prediction. For local interpretation, SHAP waterfall and force plots were used to deconstruct individual predictions, providing a clear visualization of how different features contribute to the final predicted risk of infection.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using Python version 3.13.5 (https://www.python.org) and R version 4.5.1 (https://www.r-project.org). Continuous variables with skewed distributions were presented as median with interquartile range and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers with percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Among the 7,833 hospitalized patients with SLE included in this study, 3,157 (40.3%) developed in-hospital infection ≥ 72 h after admission. A detailed comparison of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between the infection and non-infection groups is presented in Table 1. Baseline demographics (age, sex, and BMI) were comparable between the two cohorts (all p > 0.20). By contrast, at admission, patients who subsequently developed infection had higher disease activity and more organ involvement: SLEDAI 6.0 (3.0–11.0) vs 3.0 (1.0–6.0), renal involvement 59.2% vs 38.3%, and hematologic involvement 30.2% vs 13.6% (all p < 0.001), with more serositis (16.9% vs 2.5%, p < 0.001). Inflammatory and immunological profiles indicated pronounced systemic inflammation and hypocomplementemia in infected patients: levels of ESR, CRP, IL-6 and ferritin were all higher, while C3 65.6 (42.1–89.8) vs 85.5 (65.5–103.0) g/L and C4 11.8 (6.4–19.8) vs 16.8 (10.4–23.4) g/L were lower (all p < 0.001). Hematologic indices demonstrated more pronounced cytopenias (Platelet count 171 (108–233) vs 210 (162–259) ×109/L; Hemoglobin 106 (90–122) vs 122 (108–134) g/L; Lymphocyte count 0.2 (0.1–0.3) vs 0.3 (0.2–0.3) ×109/L; all p < 0.001). Infection was associated with heightened coagulation activation, evidenced by significantly elevated D-dimer levels (1.1 (0.4–2.6) vs 0.4 (0.2–0.9) mg/L) and prolonged Prothrombin Time (PT) and International Normalized Ratio (INR) (all p < 0.001). Regarding renal parameters, the infection group presented with higher cystatin C levels (1.3 (1.0–1.9) vs 1.0 (0.8–1.3) mg/L, p < 0.001) and urine WBC count (21.5 (6.9–65.8) vs 11.7 (4.4–35.6)/µL, p < 0.001); creatinine did not differ materially (p = 0.091). Nutritional and metabolic markers were less favorable in the infection group (albumin 32.5 (26.7–37.7) vs 41.5 (35.8–45.5) g/L; HDL-C lower and triglycerides higher; all p < 0.001), accompanied by higher fasting glucose (4.9 (4.2–6.0) vs 4.6 (4.2–5.0) mmol/L, p < 0.001). Patients with infection exhibited statistically significant alterations in their electrolyte profile, characterized by decreased concentrations of sodium and calcium, and increased chloride (all p < 0.001). Autoantibody profiles also differed: positivity for anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, anti-histone, and anti-nucleosome antibodies was more prevalent in infected patients (all p < 0.001), whereas ANA prevalence was similar (p = 0.506). Patients in the infection group received markedly different therapeutic regimens. Specifically, they were administered higher prednisone-equivalent doses (30.0 (0.0–50.0) vs 0.0 (0.0–5.0) mg/day), had lower rates of HCQ use (38.6% vs 71.2%), and were more frequently prescribed MMF, cyclophosphamide (CTX), and tacrolimus. Furthermore, RRT was more commonly required in this group (all p < 0.001).

Feature selection and identification of key variables

Following the multi-stage feature selection process, the intersection of the Boruta algorithm and LASSO regression yielded a refined pool of 39 candidate variables (Figure 2A–C). Subsequent Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) analysis demonstrated that model performance (AUC) consistently plateaued after the inclusion of approximately 10 variables (Figure 2D). Among these top 10 ranked variables, a high correlation was observed between hemoglobin and red blood cell count (RBC) (Pearson’s r = 0.88, p < 0.001). Given that hemoglobin is a more direct clinical indicator, RBC was removed to enhance model parsimony. A DeLong test was performed to compare the AUCs of the Gradient Boosting model with 10 versus 9 variables (excluding RBC), which yielded no statistically significant difference (p = 0.2432). This justified the adoption of the more concise 9-variable set. The final set of nine independent variables identified for model development included: daily prednisone equivalent dose, albumin, HCQ, CRP, D-dimer, glucose, cystatin C, hemoglobin, and alpha1-globulin.

Performance of the final prediction models

Ten ML models were trained and validated using the final nine predictors. The ROC curves for all models on the training (Figure 3A) and validation (Figure 3B) sets demonstrated good discriminative ability. As shown in Table 2, the Gradient Boosting model achieved the highest AUC (0.858) on the validation set and demonstrated a good balance between accuracy (76.4%), sensitivity (78.6%), specificity (75.0%), and F1-score (0.729) (Figure 3C–E). The confusion matrices for the top four performing models are displayed in Figure 3F–I. The confusion matrices for the remaining six models are provided in Supplementary Figure S1. The Gradient Boosting confusion matrix (Figure 3F) shows that out of 2,350 validation samples, the model correctly predicted 744 infection cases and 1,052 non-infection cases. The calibration curves (Figure 3J, 3K) indicated that the predicted probabilities of the Gradient Boosting model were well-aligned with the observed frequencies, demonstrating excellent calibration. To rigorously assess the stability and generalizability of the models, we performed 5-fold and 10-fold cross-validation on the entire dataset. In the 5-fold cross-validation, the Gradient Boosting model demonstrated robust performance with a mean AUC of 0.855 ± 0.002, a sensitivity of 0.796 ± 0.013, and a specificity of 0.734 ± 0.014. The ROC curves for the 5-fold cross-validation are shown in Figure 4A. Consistent results were observed in the 10-fold cross-validation, where the Gradient Boosting model maintained a leading position across all evaluation metrics, as detailed in Table 3. The corresponding ROC curves are presented in Figure 4B, visually confirming the model’s low variance and high robustness. Based on this comprehensive evaluation, the Gradient Boosting model was selected as the final model. Furthermore, to evaluate the clinical utility of the model for risk stratification, we categorized patients into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the predicted probabilities generated by the final Gradient Boosting model. The optimal cutoff value determined by the Youden index from the ROC curve was 0.412. Accordingly, patients with predicted probabilities ≥ 0.412 were assigned to the high-risk group, whereas those with predicted probabilities < 0.412 were assigned to the low-risk group. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was then performed to compare the probability of remaining infection-free between the two groups. As shown in Figure 4C–E, the high-risk group exhibited a significantly lower infection-free probability compared to the low-risk group (Log-rank p < 0.001). This significant distinction was consistent across different observation windows: within 15 days of admission (Figure 4C), within 1 month of admission (Figure 4D), and across the entire hospitalization period (Figure 4E). These results demonstrate the model’s capability to effectively identify patients at varying risks of infection over time, providing valuable information for dynamic clinical monitoring.

Model explanation

To demystify the inherent ‘black box’ nature of complex ML algorithms and foster clinical trust, we utilized the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method to interpret the output of the final Gradient Boosting model. This game-theoretic approach provides both global interpretability (at the feature level) and local interpretability (at the individual patient level) by quantifying the marginal contribution of each variable.

Global explanation described the overall functionality of the model. As shown in the SHAP summary plots (Figure 5A and 5B), the features were ranked in descending order based on their contribution to the model output, which was evaluated using mean absolute SHAP values. The results revealed that daily prednisone equivalent dose, albumin, and HCQ were the three most important factors influencing the prediction outcomes. Furthermore, the SHAP dependence plots can facilitate understanding how a single feature affects the output of the prediction model (Figure 5C–K). A SHAP value greater than zero corresponds to a positive class prediction, indicating a higher risk of infection. For instance, a daily prednisone equivalent dose > 50.0 mg/day, an albumin level < 39.6 g/L, and elevated levels of D-dimer, C-reactive protein (CRP), cystatin C, or glucose. All pushed the decision toward the ‘infection’ class. Patients not using HCQ (HCQ value of 0) had significantly higher SHAP values than users, indicating that HCQ use has a protective effect against infection. Furthermore, anemia (hemoglobin < 100.5 g/L) was associated with a higher infection risk, and an elevated alpha1-globulin level showed a similar trend.

Local explanation analyzed how a certain prediction was made for a specific individual by incorporating the individualized input data. Figure 6 illustrates the local explanations for both high- and low-risk patients. Figure 6A and 6E show a patient with SLE who did not develop an infection during hospitalization. For this patient, factors such as a low prednisone dose (0 mg/day) and a normal albumin level (49.7 g/L) were the main protective contributors, strongly pushing the prediction toward the ‘non-infection’ class. Figure 6C shows the case of another low-risk patient. Conversely, Figure 6B and 6F display the analysis for a patient who did develop an infection. A higher prednisone dose (50.0 mg/day) and a lower albumin level (22.9 g/L) were the key drivers that led the model to predict a high-risk ‘infection’ state. Figure 6D represents another high-risk case.

Web-based application for clinical translation

To facilitate the seamless integration of our model into routine clinical workflows, we developed a user-friendly, web-based clinical calculator (Figure 7, https://nephrology301-sle-infection.streamlit.app/). Upon inputting the actual values of the nine requisite clinical parameters (e.g., daily prednisone-equivalent dose, albumin, HCQ use), the application instantaneously computes the individualized probability of in-hospital infection. Additionally, the application displays an individualized force plot for the patient to indicate the features that contribute to the decision: the red features are those pushing the prediction toward the ‘infection’ class (higher risk), while the blue features are those pushing the prediction toward the ‘non-infection’ class (lower risk). The development of this tool greatly promotes the clinical translation of the model and has the potential to assist clinicians in rapidly and accurately stratifying infection risk for hospitalized SLE patients, thereby guiding early intervention.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted three sensitivity analyses by redefining the primary outcome (details in Supplementary Figures S2–S7). First, adjusting the definition to infections occurring >48 h after admission (n = 3,448) yielded an AUC of 0.871 for the Gradient Boosting model, identifying the exact same nine predictors as the primary analysis. Second, restricting the outcome to severe infections requiring intravenous antimicrobial therapy (n = 1,921) resulted in an AUC of 0.842. Third, focusing specifically on pulmonary infection (n = 926) achieved an AUC of 0.826. Across all scenarios, the Gradient Boosting model consistently demonstrated superior performance, and SHAP analyses reaffirmed the daily prednisone equivalent dose and albumin as the dominant risk factors. These results confirm the model’s stability across variations in infection timing, severity, and site.

Discussion

This study successfully developed and rigorously validated a high-performing, parsimonious, and fully interpretable Gradient Boosting model for predicting the risk of nosocomial infection in a large cohort of hospitalized SLE patients. The model, which utilizes only nine readily available clinical variables at admission, demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.858) and good calibration in the validation set. Crucially, the application of SHAP technology demystifies the model’s internal logic, transforming it from a ‘black box’ into a transparent clinical decision support tool, which is vital for enhancing clinical adoption and trust.

The key predictors identified by our model are strongly aligned with the known pathophysiology of infection in SLE. The daily prednisone equivalent dose, as the most important predictor, reaffirms the broad consensus that glucocorticoids are a core driver of infection risk in SLE [16,18,19,32,33]. SHAP dependence plots showed a strong positive collinearity with infection risk, with a notable increase when the daily dose exceeded 50 mg/day. Mechanistically, high-dose glucocorticoids broadly inhibit neutrophil and macrophage chemotaxis and phagocytosis, while suppressing the synthesis of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, effectively controlling lupus activity but simultaneously impairing the innate immune surveillance against pathogens [18,19]. The second most important predictor was albumin, with a risk threshold of 39.6 g/L. In SLE patients, hypoalbuminemia reflects multiple overlapping factors: proteinuria from lupus nephritis, systemic inflammation triggering hepatic acute-phase response (IL-6–mediated upregulation of positive acute-phase proteins such as CRP and α1-antitrypsin, and downregulation of negative acute-phase proteins such as albumin) [34–36], increased capillary permeability, altered distribution volume, enhanced protein catabolism, chronic disease consumption, and insufficient intake [37,38]. Low albumin indicates both nutritional deficiency and compromised immune and barrier reserves. Beyond maintaining colloid osmotic pressure, albumin has antioxidative functions, binds endotoxins and inflammatory mediators, and helps stabilize vascular endothelium [39,40]. When albumin is low or oxidatively modified, these protective functions are impaired, explaining the biological plausibility of its association with increased infection risk. Furthermore, hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) use demonstrated a protective effect against infection in the model. The 2023 EULAR guidelines recommend HCQ for all SLE patients without contraindications due to its potential to reduce disease relapse, mortality, and infection risk [18]. Prior studies and meta-analyses confirm that HCQ is an independent protective factor against severe infection in SLE [19,20]. The mechanisms may involve two complementary effects. First, the drug controls disease activity, thereby reducing the need for high-dose glucocorticoids and potent immunosuppressants. Second, it exhibits lysosomotropic properties that interfere with lysosomal acidification and autophagy, exerting antiviral and antibacterial effects [41–43]. Abnormal elevations in D-dimer, C-reactive protein, glucose, and cystatin C reflect synergistic contributions from coagulation dysfunction, acute inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and renal impairment in driving infection development. These findings support the biological plausibility of SHAP-identified risk factors.

Compared to the existing literature, this study significantly advances the field by addressing the long-standing issue of a scarcity of robust and high-quality prediction models for infection in SLE [21]. Previous studies were often limited by small sample sizes, retrospective biases, or methodological shortcomings. Using a large cohort of 7,833 patients and a rigorous, multi-stage feature-selection pipeline (Boruta, LASSO, and RFE), our study enhanced the model’s robustness and generalizability. Among the evaluated algorithms, the Gradient Boosting model yielded the highest AUC. This performance may be related to the algorithm’s iterative boosting mechanism and its capacity to handle non-linear relationships and feature interactions. As illustrated by the SHAP dependence plots, the relationships between key predictors (e.g., prednisone dose, albumin) and infection risk are non-linear. Gradient Boosting can capture these patterns without requiring pre-specified transformations, which provides a relative advantage over linear models in analyzing complex clinical data. However, complex ensemble models like Gradient Boosting carry an inherent risk of overfitting. To mitigate this, we implemented multiple layers of defensive strategies. First, the aforementioned feature selection strictly controlled model complexity (with LASSO inherently providing L1 regularization). Second, hyperparameter optimization via grid search was applied to constrain tree depth. Finally, model stability was confirmed by the low variance in our cross-validations, and the sustained predictive performance on a strictly independent 30% validation set provides objective evidence against overfitting. However, a major barrier to the clinical adoption of previous ML models has been their ‘black-box’ nature, as physicians are often reluctant to trust and adopt models whose decision-making processes are opaque [44]. The advent of eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques, particularly the SHAP method, provides a key solution to this problem [31]. SHAP not only offers global insights into the overall decision logic of a model but also provides individualized, precise local explanations for the predictions of single patients, thereby greatly enhancing model transparency and clinician trust [45]. Therefore, the core innovation of this research is the deep integration of XAI into the prediction of infection risk in SLE. While ML applications in SLE are increasingly common [25,46,47], few have focused on infection risk with a concurrent emphasis on interpretability. By providing clear, individualized attribution analysis for every prediction through SHAP, our study takes a critical step in translating predictive models from theoretical research to practical clinical application.

The strengths of this study are noteworthy. Firstly, the large sample size offers substantial statistical power for both model training and validation. Secondly, the rigorous multi-stage modeling approach ensures that the final model is both parsimonious and efficient. Thirdly, the model’s explainability stands out as its most significant feature, not only enhancing transparency but also offering actionable insights for clinicians. Finally, the development of an accompanying web application facilitates the clinical translation of the model, effectively completing the ‘data-to-decision’ loop.

However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective analysis, it is subject to unmeasured confounders and potential recording biases within the EMR data. For instance, highly granular epidemiological factors—such as recent contact with infected individuals—were not systematically documented in the historical EMR data and could not be evaluated as candidate predictors. Secondly, while the data are drawn from a single medical center with a large sample size, the model’s external generalizability requires validation in multicenter datasets from diverse geographical regions and healthcare systems. Furthermore, because our primary objective was intra-disease risk stratification, this study did not include a non-SLE disease control cohort. Consequently, the model cannot definitively determine whether its predictive patterns are strictly specific to SLE infections. Future studies introducing non-SLE inpatients or patients with other autoimmune diseases as control groups are warranted to further evaluate the cross-disease specificity of the model. Thirdly, the composite definition of infection, although a pragmatic choice for EMR-based research, may have inadvertently included cases where antibiotics were prescribed empirically for sterile inflammation, potentially leading to misclassification of outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the prediction tool developed in this study holds significant potential for clinical utility. By providing an objective, quantitative, and individualized risk score at the time of admission, this tool can assist clinicians in: (1) identifying high-risk patients for closer monitoring and timely diagnostic evaluation; (2) making more informed decisions when formulating immunosuppressive treatment plans, balancing efficacy with infection risk; and (3) establishing a foundation for exploring prophylactic antimicrobial strategies in select, very-high-risk patients. Future research should focus on several key areas: firstly, conducting prospective studies to validate the model’s predictive performance and clinical utility in real-world settings; secondly, performing external validation in diverse, multicenter, and multinational cohorts to assess its generalizability; and thirdly, designing clinical impact studies to determine whether using this model to guide clinical decisions can genuinely improve patient outcomes, such as reducing infection rates, shortening hospital stays, or lowering mortality.

Conclusion

In this large-scale cohort study, we developed and validated a robust, interpretable machine learning model that accurately predicts the risk of in-hospital infection among patients with SLE using only nine readily available clinical variables. By providing transparent, patient-specific risk stratification, this tool has the potential to enhance clinical decision-making and improve outcomes for this vulnerable patient population.
