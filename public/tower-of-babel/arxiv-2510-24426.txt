Unicoherence in Locales
Elena Caviglia, Luca Mesiti, Cerene Rathilal

Abstract
. In this paper, we generalize the concept of unicoherence to the context of frames. Unicoherence, originally introduced by Kuratowski, is a connectedness property that is well studied in classical topology and used to detect holes of a space. We extend the notion of unicoherence to locales and we then investigate its properties. In particular, we prove that many of the known characterizations of unicoherence for topological spaces extend to the setting of locales. Some of these characterizations interestingly involve separation properties for locales.

1.
Introduction

Unicoherence is a connectedness property which was first defined by Kuratowski
[
9
]
in 1926. Intuitively, a space is unicoherent if it is connected and does not contain any holes. So for example all euclidean spaces are unicoherent while the circle is not. Kuratowski introduced this topological notion to better understand and characterize the sphere
S^{2}
. Indeed, the sphere is unicoherent although the circle is not. Later, unicoherent topological spaces have been extensively studied, by (but not limited to) Stone
[
14
]
, Wallace
[
15
]
, Eilenberg
[
5
]
, Borsuk (
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
) and more recently Dickman and Rubin
[
6
]
. These investigations led to numerous interesting equivalent characterizations of unicoherence for topological spaces, many of which are collected in
[
12
]
. Among such characterizations, Brouwer property is of particular relevance, capturing unicoherence in terms of a separation condition for closed subsets. In this paper, we extend the notion of unicoherence and many of its known equivalent characterizations to the setting of locales.

The development of pointfree topology, or locale theory, offers an algebraic framework that broadens topological ideas without directly referring to points
[
13
]
. With this approach, one works with the lattice of open sets of a topological space rather than the points themselves. The objects of study in pointfree topology are called
frames
or
locales
. Many key topological concepts such as compactness, connectedness and separation properties have been successfully studied via locales (see Johnstone
[
7
]
, Pultr and Picado
[
13
]
). The study of connectedness in locales has created a rich parallel theory that reflects the classical setting while revealing constructive proofs.

Our work extends unicoherence to locales and studies its properties, generalizing classical results and developing new tools for pointfree topology. We present several equivalent characterizations of unicoherence for locales, exploring how unicoherence relates to sublocales, boundary operations, the components of open sublocales and separation conditions.

To present this work, we begin by recalling relevant background material on point-free topology. For details not provided here, we refer the reader to
[
1
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
13
]
.

A
frame or locale

L
is a complete lattice which satisfies the infinite distributive law:

x\wedge\bigvee S=\bigvee\{x\wedge s|s\in S\}
,

for all
x\in L,S\subseteq L.

The top element of
L
is denoted by
1_{L}
and the bottom by
0_{L}
.

If
X
is a topological space, then
\mathcal{O}X=\{U\subseteq X|\;U\mbox{ is open}\}
is a frame with partial order
\subseteq
and intersection as a binary meet. We call
\mathcal{O}X
the
frame of open sets
of
X
.

The
pseudocomplement
of
a\in L
is denoted
a^{*}
and is characterized by the following formula

a^{*}=\bigvee\{x\in L\;|\;a\wedge x=0\}.

An element
x
in a frame
L
is said to be connected if whenever
x=b\vee c
with
b\wedge c=0_{L}
we have either
b=0_{L}
or
c=0_{L}
. A frame
L
is connected if its top element
1_{L}
is connected and is locally connected provided each element in the frame can be written as the join of connected elements.

If
L
is a frame and
S\subseteq L
, then
S
is called a
sublocale
if
S
is closed under arbitrary meets and if for
x\in L
and
s\in S
,
(x\rightarrow s)\in S
. We denote the bottom element of
S
by
0_{S}
, where
0_{S}=\bigwedge S
. The
trivial
(also known as
void
) sublocale is
\{1_{L}\}
which we shall denote by
\emptyset.
An arbitrary intersection (or meet) of sublocales, is a sublocale. The collection of all sublocales of a frame
L
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}(L)
, is a complete lattice
(\mathcal{S}(L),\subseteq)
.
For
S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}(L)
for
i\in I
, we recall that the join of sublocales is defined as follows:

\bigvee_{i\in I}S_{i}=\{\bigwedge M\;|\;M\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}\}.

We note that the join of sublocales is indeed a sublocale. The
open sublocale
associated with any
a\in L
is :
\mathfrak{o}_{L}(a)=\{x\in L\;|\;a\rightarrow x=x\}
.
The
closed sublocale
associated with any
a\in L
is :
\mathfrak{c}_{L}(a)=\;\uparrow a=\{x\in L\;|\;a\leq x\}
. In the absence of ambiguity, we denote the open sublocale of
a
in
L
by
\mathfrak{o}(a)
and the closed sublocale of
a
in
L
by
\mathfrak{c}(a)
.
The
closure
of a sublocale
S
in
L
, denoted
\overline{S}_{L}
(or
\overline{S}
for simplicity), is defined as
\overline{S}=\;\uparrow\left(\bigwedge S\right).
We also note that if
S
is connected, then
\overline{S}
is connected. The
supplement
of
S
, denoted
\sup(S)
, is given by
\sup(S)=\bigwedge\{T\;|\;T\mbox{ is a sublocale of }L,\;T\vee S=L\}
, and
S
is called
complemented
sublocale in
L
if,
S\wedge\sup(S)=\{1_{L}\}(=\emptyset)
. The
interior
of
S
, denoted by
\operatorname{Int}\left(S\right)
, is
\operatorname{Int}\left(S\right)=\bigvee\{U\in\mathcal{S}(L)|\;U\mbox{ is open and }U\subseteq S\}
and correspondingly the
boundary
of a sublocale
S
, denoted
\operatorname{bd}\left(S\right)
, is
\operatorname{bd}\left(S\right)=\overline{S}\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(S\right).

2.
Properties of Sublocales

In this section, we recall and outline several properties of sublocales. These properties will be essential for our subsequent study of connectedness and unicoherence in locales. Amongst the various properties, we will investigate the behavior of open and closed sublocales, along with their interaction under closure, interior, and boundary operations.

Definition 2.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

A locale
L
is
normal
if for every
a,b\in L
such that
a\vee b=1
there exist
u,v\in L
such that
u\wedge v=0
,
a\vee u=1
and
b\vee v=1
.

Equivalently,
L
is normal if for every
\mathpzc{c}(a),\mathpzc{c}(b)\subseteq L
such that
\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(b)=\emptyset
there exist
\mathpzc{o}(u),\mathpzc{o}(v)\subseteq L
such that
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap\mathpzc{o}(v)=\emptyset
,
\mathpzc{c}(a)\subseteq\mathpzc{o}(u)
and
\mathpzc{c}(b)\subseteq\mathpzc{o}(v)
.

We now present proofs of well-known facts about sublocales that are not provided in the literature. These facts will be used in our later results.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
S\subseteq L
be a sublocale. A closed sublocale
\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)
of
S
is the same as
\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)\cap S
. An open sublocale
\mathpzc{o}_{S}(u)
of
S
is the same as
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap S
.

Let
T\subseteq S
be a sublocale. Then the closure
\overline{T}^{S}
of
T
in
S
is equal to
\overline{T}\cap S
.

Proof.

By definition, given
u\in S
,

\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)=\left\{{x\in S}\left.\right|\,{u\leq x}\right\}=\left\{{x\in L}\left.\right|\,{u\leq x}\right\}\cap S=\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap S.

Then
\mathpzc{o}_{S}(u)
is the unique complement in
S
of
\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)=\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap S
. But
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap S
is also a complement of
\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap S
in
S
. Whence
\mathpzc{o}_{S}(u)=\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap S
.

Consider now
T\subseteq S
. The closure
\overline{T}^{S}
is the smallest closed sublocale of
S
containing
T
. Since by the argument above
\overline{T}\cap S
is closed in
S
and contains
T
, we have
\overline{T}^{S}\subseteq\overline{T}\cap S
. Notice then that, by the argument above,
\overline{T}^{S}
needs to be of the form
\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap S
, with
T\subseteq\mathpzc{c}(u)
. But
\overline{T}
is the smallest closed sublocale of
L
containing
T
, so
\overline{T}\subseteq\mathpzc{c}(u)
. Whence
\overline{T}\cap S\subseteq\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap S=\overline{T}^{S}
.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
S\subseteq L
be a sublocale and let
\mathpzc{o}(u)\subseteq L
be an open sublocale. If
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap\overline{S}\neq\emptyset
then also
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap{S}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

We prove that if
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap S=\emptyset
then
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap\overline{S}=\emptyset
. If
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap S=\emptyset
, then
S\subseteq\mathpzc{c}(u)
and so
S\subseteq\overline{S}\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)
. By definition of closure, this implies
\overline{S}=\overline{S}\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)
and hence
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap\overline{S}=\emptyset
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
S\subseteq L
be a sublocale and let
x\in L
with
x\neq 1
. Then
x\in\operatorname{Int}\left(S\right)
if and only if there exists an open sublocale
\mathpzc{o}(u)
such that
x\in\mathpzc{o}(u)
and
\mathpzc{o}(u)\subseteq S
.

Proof.

If
x\in\operatorname{Int}\left(S\right)
, then
\operatorname{Int}\left(S\right)
is an open sublocale contained in
S
that contains
x
. If there exists an open sublocale
\mathpzc{o}(u)
such that
x\in\mathpzc{o}(u)
and
\mathpzc{o}(u)\subseteq S
, then
x\in\operatorname{Int}\left(S\right)
since
\operatorname{Int}\left(S\right)
is the biggest open sublocale contained in
S
.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
S\subseteq L
be a complemented sublocale and let
x\in\operatorname{bd}\left(S\right)
with
x\neq 1
. Then every open sublocale
\mathpzc{o}(u)
such that
x\in\mathpzc{o}(u)
is such that
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap S\neq\emptyset
and
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap(L\setminus S)\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let
\mathpzc{o}(u)
be an open sublocale of
L
such that
x\in\mathpzc{o}(u)
. Since
x\in\operatorname{bd}\left(S\right)\subseteq\overline{S}
, by Proposition
2.3
, we have
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap S\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, since
x\notin\operatorname{Int}\left(S\right)
, by Lemma
2.4
,
\mathpzc{o}(u)\cap(L\setminus S)\neq\emptyset
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
C\subseteq L
be a complemented sublocale of
L
. Then
\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)=\operatorname{bd}\left(L\setminus C\right)
.

Proof.

To prove that
\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\subseteq\operatorname{bd}\left(L\setminus C\right)
, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap(L\setminus\operatorname{bd}\left(L\setminus C\right))=\emptyset
. Suppose
\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap(L\setminus\operatorname{bd}\left(L\setminus C\right))\neq\emptyset
. Since
\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap(L\setminus\operatorname{bd}\left(L\setminus C\right))=\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap((L\setminus(\overline{L\setminus C}))\vee\operatorname{Int}\left(L\setminus C\right))=(\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap L\setminus(\overline{L\setminus C}))\vee(\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap\operatorname{Int}\left(L\setminus C\right))
it must be either
(\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap L\setminus(\overline{L\setminus C}))\neq\emptyset
or
(\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap\operatorname{Int}\left(L\setminus C\right))\neq\emptyset
. But
(\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap L\setminus(\overline{L\setminus C}))=\emptyset
because otherwise, by Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
2.4
, there would exist
x\in(\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap L\setminus(\overline{L\setminus C}))
and
U_{x}\subseteq L\setminus(\overline{L\setminus C})
open such that
U_{x}\cap L\setminus C\neq\emptyset
and this is a contradiction. Moreover,
(\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap\operatorname{Int}\left(L\setminus C\right))=\emptyset
since otherwise, by Lemma
2.5
and Lemma
2.4
, there would exist
x\in(\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap\operatorname{Int}\left(L\setminus C\right))
and
U_{x}\subseteq L\setminus C
such that
U_{x}\cap C\neq\emptyset
and this is a contradiction. Hence it must be
\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\cap(L\setminus\operatorname{bd}\left(L\setminus C\right))=\emptyset
, that implies
\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)\subseteq\operatorname{bd}\left(L\setminus C\right)
. The same argument for
L\setminus C
in place of
C
shows that
\operatorname{bd}\left(L\setminus C\right)\subseteq\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
A,B\subseteq L
be sublocales of
L
. Then

\operatorname{bd}\left(A\cap B\right)\subseteq\operatorname{bd}\left(A\right)\vee\operatorname{bd}\left(B\right)

\operatorname{bd}\left(A\vee B\right)\subseteq\operatorname{bd}\left(A\right)\vee\operatorname{bd}\left(B\right)

Proof.

We notice that
\operatorname{bd}\left(A\right)\vee\operatorname{bd}\left(B\right)=(\overline{A}\vee\overline{B})\cap(\overline{A}\vee L\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(B\right))\cap(L\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(A\right)\vee\overline{B})\cap(L\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(A\right)\vee L\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(B\right))
. Moreover,
\operatorname{bd}\left(A\cap B\right)\subseteq\overline{A\cap B}
and so
\operatorname{bd}\left(A\cap B\right)\subseteq\overline{A}
and
\operatorname{bd}\left(A\cap B\right)\subseteq\overline{B}
. In addition to this, since
\operatorname{Int}\left(A\right)\cap\operatorname{Int}\left(B\right)\subseteq\operatorname{Int}\left(A\cap B\right)
, we have that
L\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(A\cap B\right)\subseteq(L\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(A\right))\vee(L\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(B\right))
. Hence
\operatorname{bd}\left(A\cap B\right)
is contained in
\operatorname{bd}\left(A\right)\vee\operatorname{bd}\left(B\right)
.

Notice then that
\operatorname{bd}\left(A\vee B\right)\subseteq\overline{A\vee B}\subseteq\overline{A}\vee\overline{B}
. Moreover,
\operatorname{Int}\left(A\right)\subseteq\operatorname{Int}\left(A\vee B\right)
and
\operatorname{Int}\left(B\right)\subseteq\operatorname{Int}\left(A\vee B\right)
, whence
L\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(A\vee B\right)\subseteq(L\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(A\right))\cap(L\setminus\operatorname{Int}\left(B\right))
. We thus conclude that
\operatorname{bd}\left(A\vee B\right)\subseteq\operatorname{bd}\left(A\right)\vee\operatorname{bd}\left(B\right)
.
∎

3.
On Connectedness

We now explore connected sublocales and understand their properties with respect to
separated
sublocales,
complemented
sublocales and their
components
. We introduce the notion of
strongly locally connected
locale, which in the context of locales differs from local connectedness.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
P,Q\subseteq L
be sublocales of
L
.
P
and
Q
are
separated
if
\overline{P}\cap Q=\emptyset=P\cap\overline{Q}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
S\subseteq L
be a sublocale. The following are equivalent:

(
i
)

S
is connected;

(
ii
)

The only clopen sublocales of
S
are
\emptyset
and
S
itself;

(
iii
)

If
S=P\vee Q
with
P
and
Q
separated, then
P=\emptyset
or
Q=\emptyset
.

Proof.

We prove (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) and (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii).

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). If
T
is a clopen sublocale of
S
such that
T\neq S
and
T\neq\emptyset
, then
T
and
S\setminus T
exhibit a separation of
S
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). If
S
is not connected then there exists open sublocales
U
and
V
such that
S\subseteq U\vee V
,
S\cap U\cap V\neq\emptyset
,
S\cap U\neq\emptyset
and
S\cap V\neq\emptyset
. Then
S\cap U
and
S\cap V
are non-void clopens of
S
that are not the entire
S
, contradicting (ii).

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Let
S=P\vee Q
with
P
and
Q
separated. Then
S\subseteq\overline{P}\vee\overline{Q}
and
S\cap\overline{P}\cap\overline{Q}=\emptyset
. Then, since
S
is connected, it must be either
S\cap\overline{P}=\emptyset
or
S\cap\overline{Q}=\emptyset
and hence
P=\emptyset
or
Q=\emptyset
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i). Let
S\subseteq F_{1}\vee F_{2}
with
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
closed sublocales. Then
F_{1}\cap A
and
F_{2}\cap A
are separated and
S=(F_{1}\cap A)\vee(F_{2}\cap A)
. Then by (iii) we have that
F_{1}\cap A=\emptyset
or
F_{2}\cap A=\emptyset
and hence
S
is connected.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
L
be a connected locale and
C\subseteq L
a connected and complemented sublocale of
L
. If
L\setminus C=P\vee Q
with
P
and
Q
separated, then both
C\vee P
and
C\vee Q
are connected.

Proof.

Let
A
and
B
be separated sublocales of
L
such that
C\vee P=A\vee B
. Since
C\subseteq A\vee B
is connected, we have
C\cap A=\emptyset
or
C\cap B=\emptyset
. Without loss of generality we suppose
C\cap A=\emptyset
. Then
A\subseteq P
and so
A
and
Q
are separated. Moreover
L=C\vee P\vee Q=A\vee(B\vee Q)
and
A
and
B\vee Q
are separated because
A
is separated from both
B
and
Q
. Since
L
is connected this implies
A=\emptyset
or
B\vee Q=\emptyset
and hence
B=\emptyset
. This shows that
C\vee P
is connected. The same argument can be used to show that
C\vee Q
is connected as well.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
E\subseteq S
be sublocales of
L
. Then
E
is connected as a sublocale of
S
precisely when it is connected as a sublocale of
L
.

Proof.

We first prove that if
E
is connected in
L
then it is connected in
S
. Recall that, by Proposition
2.2
, the closed sublocales of
S
are of the form
\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)=\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap S
. Suppose now
E\subseteq\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)\vee\mathpzc{c}_{S}(v)
with
E\cap\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)\cap\mathpzc{c}_{S}(v)=\emptyset
. Then
E\subseteq\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap\mathpzc{c}(v)
and we have
E\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap\mathpzc{c}(v)=E\cap\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)\cap\mathpzc{c}_{S}(v)=\emptyset
. Since
E
is connected in
L
this implies either
E\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)=\emptyset
or
E\cap\mathpzc{c}(v)=\emptyset
and hence either
E\cap\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)=\emptyset
or
E\cap\mathpzc{c}_{S}(v)=\emptyset
.

We now prove that if
E
is connected in
S
it is connected in
L
as well. Let
E\subseteq\mathpzc{c}(u)\vee\mathpzc{c}(v)
with
E\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap\mathpzc{c}(v)=\emptyset
. Then
E\subseteq\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)\vee\mathpzc{c}_{S}(v)
and
E\cap\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)\cap\mathpzc{c}_{S}(v)=E\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap\mathpzc{c}(v)=\emptyset
. Since
E
is connected in
S
this implies either
E\cap\mathpzc{c}_{S}(u)=\emptyset
or
E\cap\mathpzc{c}_{S}(v)=\emptyset
and hence, since
E\cap S=E
, either
E\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)=\emptyset
or
E\cap\mathpzc{c}(v)=\emptyset
.

∎

In the next result, we present an intuitive characterisation of closed sublocales in terms of closed sublocales.

Lemma 3.5
.

For a closed sublocale
\mathpzc{c}(u)\subseteq L
the following are equivalent:

(
i
)

\mathpzc{c}(u)
is connected;

(
ii
)

Whenever
\mathpzc{c}(u)=\mathpzc{c}(a)\vee\mathpzc{c}(b)
for some

with
\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(b)=\emptyset
, we have that
\mathpzc{c}(a)=\emptyset
or
\mathpzc{c}(b)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

(i)
{\mathrel{\to}}
(ii). Trivial using the definition of connected sublocale.

(ii)
{\mathrel{\to}}
(i). Let
\mathpzc{c}(u)\subseteq\mathpzc{c}(a)\vee\mathpzc{c}(b)
with
\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(b)=\emptyset
. Consider then the disjoint closed sublocales
\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)
and
\mathpzc{c}(b)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)
. We have
\mathpzc{c}(u)=(\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u))\vee(\mathpzc{c}(b)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u))
and
(\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u))\cap(\mathpzc{c}(b)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u))=\emptyset
. Hence, by (ii), we conclude that either and hence
\mathpzc{c}(u)
is connected.
(ii)
{\mathrel{\to}}
(i). Let
\mathpzc{c}(u)\subseteq\mathpzc{c}(a)\vee\mathpzc{c}(b)
with
\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(b)=\emptyset
. Consider then the disjoint closed sublocales
\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)
and
\mathpzc{c}(b)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)
. We have
\mathpzc{c}(u)=(\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u))\vee(\mathpzc{c}(b)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u))
and
(\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u))\cap(\mathpzc{c}(b)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u))=\emptyset
. Hence, by (ii), we conclude that either
\mathpzc{c}(a)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)=\emptyset
or
\mathpzc{c}(b)\cap\mathpzc{c}(u)=\emptyset
and so
\mathpzc{c}(u)
is connected.
∎

Definition 3.6

(
[
11
]
)
.

A
component
of a sublocale
T\subseteq L
is a non-void connected sublocale
D\subseteq T
that is maximal among connected sublocales of
T
in the following sense: given any connected sublocale
S\subseteq T
such that
S\cap D\neq\emptyset
, we have
S\subseteq D
.

Lemma 3.7

(

Remark 3.13

For topological spaces, strongly local connectedness is equivalent to local connectedness. But for locales one of the two properties is stronger than the other one.

4.
Unicoherent Locales

In this section, we extend the notion of unicoherence to the setting of locales. We prove that the locale of opens of a topological space is unicoherent precisely when the topological space is, immediately obtaining many examples of this theory. We also introduce
open unicoherence
, which for topological spaces is an equivalent characterization of unicoherence of notable relevance. We investigate some results on unicoherent locales and prove some lemmas that will be useful in the following section.

Definition 4.1
.

A
continuum
in a locale
L
is a non-void closed connected sublocale
\mathpzc{c}(u)\subseteq L
.

A
region
in a locale
L
is a non-void open connected sublocale
\mathpzc{o}(u)\subseteq L
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
X
be a topological space. The closed sublocale
\mathpzc{c}(X\setminus F)
of
\mathcal{O}(X)
associated to a closed subspace
F
of
X
is a continuum precisely when
F
is a continuum (in the classical topological sense).

Proof.

The closed sublocale
\mathpzc{c}(X\setminus F)
is non-void precisely when
F\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, it is connected as a sublocale if and only if whenever
F=G\cup H
with
G,H
closed disjoint subsets of
X
then either
G=F
or
H=F
, that is precisely when
F
is connected as a subspace of
X
.
∎

We now extend the notion of unicoherence from topological spaces to locales.

Definition 4.3
.

A locale
L
is
unicoherent
if whenever
L=H\vee K
with
H,K\subseteq L
continua, we have that
H\cap K
is a continuum.

L
is
open unicoherent
if whenever
L=A\vee B
with
A,B\subseteq L
regions, we have that
A\cap B
is a region.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
X
be a topological space. The locale
\mathcal{O}X
is unicoherent precisely when
X
is unicoherent.

Proof.

Straightforward using the definition of unicoherent locale and Proposition
4.2
.
∎

Example 4.5
.

Proposition
4.4
gives many examples of unicoherent locales. For instance, the locale of reals (which coincides with the locale of opens of the euclidean space
\mathbb{R}
under mild conditions) is unicoherent, thanks to Proposition
4.4
. While the locale of opens of a circle is not unicoherent, again thanks to Proposition
4.4
.

B\subseteq A
,
A\cap B=\emptyset
or
A\vee B=L
.

Proof.

Let

be a simple sublocale and let

be an open sublocale such that

. Consider the following sublocale:

is open and
\operatorname{bd}\left(A\right)\subseteq B\subseteq U
. Moreover,
B
is connected. Indeed, notice that

. Both

and

are connected because for every component

and

. In addition to this,

and

are open sublocales. The fact that

is open unicoherent implies then that

is connected. And so, we have shown that condition (ii) of Proposition

holds for

is normally connected.

We now prove the second part of the statement. Let

and

be simple sublocales of

such that

. If

and if

then

. If

is not void and not the entire

cannot be clopen because

is connected. This implies that
\operatorname{bd}\left(B\right)
is not void. But then
\operatorname{bd}\left(B\right)
cannot be contained in

. If
\operatorname{bd}\left(B\right)
is not contained in

then
\operatorname{bd}\left(B\right)\cap(L\setminus A)\neq\emptyset
. Since
\operatorname{bd}\left(B\right)
is normally connected (by the first part of this Lemma) and

, by (iv) of Proposition

and since

is connected this implies either

or

and thus

. Analogously, if

is not contained in

, since

is connected we can apply the same argument to

and conclude that either

.

Lemma 4.13

be strongly locally connected and

5.
Main Results

We now present several characterizations of unicoherence for a connected, locally connected locale. The results presented here are obtained through generalizations of properties that for topological spaces were known to be equivalent to unicoherence. Many of such properties are recorded in the survey paper
[
12
]
. The properties for locales that we obtain here, together with the detailed proofs of their equivalence, will form the foundation for further study of unicoherent locales in future.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
L
be a connected and locally connected locale. The following properties are equivalent:

(I)

Whenever

with
X\neq\emptyset\neq Y
and

are such that
\mathpzc{c}(u)\cap\mathpzc{c}(v)=\emptyset
and neither
\mathpzc{c}(u)
nor
\mathpzc{c}(v)
separate any element of

(different from 1) from any element of

(different from 1) in

, we have that
\mathpzc{c}(u)\vee\mathpzc{c}(v)
does not separate
X
and
Y
in
L
;

(II)

(Brouwer Property) If
\mathpzc{c}(u)\subseteq L
is a continuum, then every component
D
of

has as boundary
\operatorname{bd}\left(D\right)
a continuum;

(III)

(Unicoherence)
L
is unicoherent;

(IV)

If
\mathpzc{c}(u)\subseteq L

(VI)

\operatorname{bd}\left(C\right)
is connected;

(VII)

(VIII)
