Employment type and non-compliance with SARS-CoV-2 diagnostic testing: insights from a nationwide seroprevalence study in South Korea
Authors: Youngook Jang, Kyuhyun Yoon, Suyoung Jo, Jina Han, Jung Ae Kim, Hye Jin Baek, Jaehun Jung, Hyejin Joo, Yumi Jang, June-Woo Lee, Soon Young Lee, Dong-Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo assess variations in compliance with SARS-CoV-2 diagnostic testing by examining the prevalence of unreported infections according to socioeconomic factors including occupation and employment type. We hypothesised that individuals in more precarious employment conditions would be less likely to undergo COVID-19 testing.DesignNationwide follow-up survey linked to a prior seroprevalence study (K-SEROSMART).SettingGeneral population-based study conducted across 258 counties in South Korea.ParticipantsThe final analysis included 4502 individuals aged 19 years and older who completed both the socioeconomic survey and SARS-CoV-2 antibody testing and were reactive for anti-nucleocapsid (N) antibodies.Primary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was non-compliance with COVID-19 diagnostic testing, defined as the absence of a self-reported confirmed diagnosis among individuals who tested reactive for anti-SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) antibodies, indicating a history of natural infection.ResultsNon-compliance with diagnostic testing was significantly associated with socioeconomic characteristics. Self-employed individuals (adjusted OR (aOR) 1.97, 95% CI 1.47 to 2.65) and non-regular workers (aOR 1.45, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.93) had higher odds of unreported infection compared with regular workers. In the analysis by occupational group, certain occupations showed significant associations in the univariate analysis; however, these associations did not retain statistical significance in the multivariable analysis.ConclusionsSocioeconomic disparities play an important role in compliance with COVID-19 diagnostic testing. Individuals in precarious employment may face barriers to testing, contributing to undetected infections. Policies that improve access to testing and reduce employment-related constraints, such as paid sick leave, may help mitigate these disparities. Future research should investigate the specific reasons underlying non-compliance with testing and assess its contribution to community transmission.Keywords: COVID-19 diagnosis, Compliance, Socioeconomic factors, COVID-19 serological testing

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has profoundly impacted various sectors, including health, the economy, society, culture and education.1 Previous studies have shown that certain groups—such as women, small business owners, temporary workers, youth, immigrants and ethnic minorities—have been disproportionately affected.2 Socioeconomic characteristics significantly influence individuals’ ability to cope with the pandemic, including their compliance with interventions such as stay-at-home orders, remote work, sick leave, distance learning, testing, screening, quarantine and vaccination.3 4 For instance, recent research indicates that lower income groups exhibit smaller reductions in travel during travel restrictions,5 while compliance with distancing measures is lower among individuals with less education or income.6 Additionally, accessibility to and familiarity with internet use—often shaped by socioeconomic factors such as education and income—are associated with higher compliance with social distancing guidelines.7

Testing and contact-tracing policies reveal similar disparities. Pre-emptive testing of suspected COVID-19 cases has been an effective tool in preventing disease spread, enabling early detection and timely treatment.8 9 However, testing accessibility, willingness and ability often vary by individual characteristics. Studies on inequality in testing accessibility have reported that testing sites are disproportionately distributed by race and region.10 11 Even when low-cost testing is available, significant differences in individuals’ willingness and ability to get tested remain. While some research has investigated disparities in testing behaviours, limited data availability has hindered further investigation.12 13

The ‘Test, Trace, Isolate and Quarantine’ strategy has been a cornerstone of South Korea’s relatively successful management of the COVID-19 pandemic. Nevertheless, infection rates, severity and mortality have varied by region and age group.14 Furthermore, disparities related to socioeconomic conditions remain underexplored. Given that the impact of the pandemic can differ based on socioeconomic factors, these disparities may reflect non-compliance with prevention and control measures influenced by individual socioeconomic characteristics. South Korea’s pandemic prevention policy provided free COVID-19 diagnostic tests to contacts, symptomatic patients and high-risk groups until August 2023, with all test results recorded in the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) database.

Citizens who tested positive for COVID-19 antibodies through serological analysis but lacked a record of diagnostic testing in the KDCA database were categorised as ‘non-reported hidden infections’. These individuals may have contributed to the uncontrolled spread of COVID-19, escaping the oversight of quarantine authorities. The failure to detect and control these hidden cases could lead to increased infections within affected population groups, resulting in significant socioeconomic losses, as well as higher severity and mortality rates.15

This study aimed to assess individual compliance with infection preventive measures by combining data from antibody and diagnostic test results. It also evaluated the socioeconomic factors associated with non-compliance with infectious disease prevention and control measures. To achieve this, we utilised data from a large, community-based seroprevalence survey for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies (K-SEROSMART) conducted in South Korea.16 17

Methods

Data and study population

We analysed data from the K-SEROSMART survey, conducted at approximately 4-month intervals since August 2022. The first survey targeted individuals aged 5 years and older, residing in households selected through a complex, multistage stratified cluster sampling design proportional to the population size of 258 counties (si/gun/gu)—the basic administrative units in South Korea.

To ensure the quality of research data, we established rigorous internal and external quality control measures, including standardised surveys, specimen management manuals, systematic training and interviewer evaluations. Interviews were conducted from August 11 to 30, and blood samples were collected from August 12 to September 5.

A total of 9945 individuals from 5041 households participated in the first survey. Interview questionnaires were used to collect demographic, health and socioeconomic information, including education and household income. Medical histories, including details of COVID-19 infections and vaccination status, were also collected. Blood samples were tested for antibodies against SARS-CoV-2. Han et al provide a detailed description of the protocol and initial results of the first survey.18

A follow-up survey was conducted in December 2022 among participants from the initial survey who consented to participate again. Of the 9945 individuals who completed the first survey, 7528 (4158 women; 55.2%) participated in the follow-up, achieving a participation rate of 75.7%. The second survey included additional items related to economic activities, such as occupation and employment type, while demographic and education-related information for adults was retained from the initial survey. The follow-up survey was conducted from 6 December to 27 December 2022 and included blood sampling.

Participants received an online self-administered survey link via a URL. For non-respondents, telephone surveys were conducted first, followed by in-person household visits for those who could not be reached by phone, to ensure comprehensive data collection. The analysis focused on individuals aged 19 years and older to examine the influence of economic factors, such as occupation and employment type. Variables

This study evaluated the distribution of unreported infections based on socioeconomic characteristics to assess how these factors influenced compliance with COVID-19 diagnostic testing and quarantine guidelines. Key socioeconomic factors included education, income, occupation, employment type and the availability of sick leave. Health-related factors, such as the presence of COVID-like symptoms, rest availability and self-rated health, were also included in the analysis model.

The term ‘unreported infection’ was defined as cases where an individual tested positive for anti-nucleocapsid antibodies (anti-N) against SARS-CoV-2 but was not recorded as having COVID-19 in the KDCA diagnostic test results database. Anti-N antibodies serve as biomarkers of natural infection, as they are induced by infection but not by vaccination. Testing was performed on a Cobas e801 analyser using the Elecsys Anti-SARS-CoV-2 assay (Roche Diagnostics), which detects total antibodies against the nucleocapsid (N) protein. A cut-off index of 1.0 or higher was classified as reactive. The clinical sensitivity and specificity of this assay were 99.5% and 99.8%, respectively.19

To confirm diagnoses, personal identification data were cross-referenced with the KDCA database. The KDCA operates free screening clinics in public health centres and major public venues across 258 counties, providing testing for symptomatic individuals, contacts of confirmed cases and high-risk groups, including older adults and those with underlying conditions. The database includes diagnostic and vaccination histories for individuals tested at both free screening clinics and paid medical facilities until August 2023.

Among the explanatory variables, income data were collected at the household level and used as an indicator of individual economic status by calculating equivalised income, which accounts for the number of household members.20 However, as the survey collected information on household generation types rather than the exact number of members, the household size was imputed as follows: a single-person household was assigned a value of 1; a one-generation household (eg, a couple or siblings) was assigned 2; a two-generation household (eg, parents and children) was assigned 3 and a three-generation household, typically including grandparents, was assigned 4. Equivalised income was then calculated by dividing total household income by the square root of the household size. The resulting values were divided into quintiles to serve as an indicator of economic status.

Education level was classified into three categories: (1) middle school or below, (2) high school and (3) college or higher. Occupations were grouped into 10 categories based on the International Classification of Occupations (ISCO-08) by the International Labor Organization: (1) managers, (2) professionals, (3) technical, (4) clerical, (5) craft and related, (6) care service, (7) sales, (8) plant and machine operators, (9) elementary workers and (10) agricultural workers.[1] For the analysis, the categories ‘managerial’, ‘professional’ and ‘technical’ were combined into a single group labelled ‘managerial and professional’, reflecting the Korean occupational classification and sample size. Employment type was classified into three categories: (1) self-employed, (2) regular workers and (3) non-regular workers.

Statistical analysis

Unweighted summary statistics were calculated, and χ2 analyses were conducted to evaluate differences in the proportions of unreported infections across subgroups of various exploratory variables. A simple logistic regression analysis was performed, adjusting for gender and age. Finally, multiple logistic regression analyses were conducted, adjusting for all covariates, to estimate OR and CIs for the explanatory variables. Unweighted regression was conducted as it performs better than weighted regression in the data derived from respondent-driven sampling.21 All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (V.9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

Patient and public involvement

It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Unreported infections were particularly prevalent among certain groups, such as women, middle-aged individuals, those with a high school education, individuals in the third income quintile, sales workers, self-employed individuals and those without COVID-like symptoms. Significant differences were observed between subgroups based on gender, age, education, income, occupation, employment type and presence of COVID-like symptoms.

Employment type and occupation highlighted disparities in reporting status most conspicuously. Among self-employed and non-regular individuals, unreported infections accounted for 44.2% and 33.9%, respectively, whereas those among regular workers accounted for only 21.1%. Occupational group analysis showed that agricultural and sales workers had the highest proportions of unreported infections (46.2%, 37.5%, respectively), whereas clerical workers had the lowest (15.5%).

COVID-like symptoms also had a pronounced effect on reporting status, as expected. 14.5% of infections were not reported for those with COVID-like symptoms, whereas 66.1% were not reported for those who were asymptomatic. In contrast, respondents with good self-rated health had a similar proportion of unreported infections (29.1%) compared with others (31.5%), where the difference is not statistically significant.

Table 2 also highlights disparities in the risk of unreported infections by employment type and occupation. Among employment types, self-employed and non-regular workers showed a higher likelihood of unreported infections compared with regular workers. After adjusting for potential covariates, including COVID-like symptoms, self-employed individuals (aOR 1.97, 95% CI 1.47 to 2.65) and non-regular workers (aOR 1.45, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.93) were more likely to have unreported infections than regular workers.

Occupational differences in the likelihood of unreported infection were observed in the unadjusted analyses. After adjustment for gender and age, these patterns remained largely similar, with agricultural, sales, elementary and craft-related occupations continuing to show significantly elevated odds. However, in the fully adjusted multivariable model, none of the occupational groups retained statistical significance, although point estimates for several groups remained elevated relative to the reference category.

The analysis also underscored the significant impact of COVID-like symptoms on reporting status. Individuals without symptoms were far more likely to have unreported infections than those with symptoms (aOR 10.83, 95% CI 9.26 to 12.66).

Table 3 presents the results of the stratified analysis, which evaluates the confounding effects of gender, age and COVID-like symptoms on the relationship between employment type and the risk of unreported infection. Among men, self-employed individuals were 1.76 times more likely to have unreported infections (95% CI 1.20 to 2.60) compared with regular workers, while non-regular male workers had a 1.83-fold increased risk (95% CI 1.19 to 2.81). Among women, self-employment was associated with a 2.34-fold higher risk (95% CI 1.47 to 3.74) of unreported infections compared with regular female workers. Although non-regular female workers also showed an increased risk, the result was not statistically significant.

Age-stratified analysis revealed that self-employed individuals under 45 years of age had a 2.97-fold higher risk of unreported infections (95% CI 1.67 to 5.26) compared with regular workers, while non-regular workers in this age group had a 1.98-fold increased risk (95% CI 1.20 to 3.24). For middle-aged adults (45–64 years), self-employment was associated with a 1.84-fold increase in risk (95% CI 1.26 to 2.70), but non-regular employment was not significantly associated with unreported infections. Among individuals aged 65 years or older, employment type had no significant effect on the risk of unreported infections.

When stratified by COVID-like symptoms, which showed the strongest effect in the regression analysis (table 2), self-employment increased the risk of unreported infections, 2.46-fold (95% CI 1.71 to 3.55), while non-regular employment increased the risk 1.65-fold (95% CI 1.14 to 2.39) among those with symptoms. However, among those without COVID-like symptoms, employment type did not have a significant effect.

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that unreported infections were more prevalent among self-employed and non-regular workers compared with regular workers, with the disparity being particularly pronounced among women, younger age groups and those with COVID-like symptoms. The implications of these findings become more meaningful when situated within the Korean context. In South Korea, testing facilities were widely available at any time and from any location, and the government implemented pre-emptive testing as a key strategy to contain the spread of the virus.17 22 Individuals who had close contact with an infected person were required to undergo testing and isolate for 10–14 days.16 These services, including testing, were provided free of charge and were accessible 24 hours a day across all cities and provinces.22

Disparities in testing compliance by employment type persisted despite the widespread and free availability of testing facilities. While previous studies have highlighted the importance of socioeconomic factors in shaping SARS-CoV-2 testing capacity, site availability and testing decisions, our findings, conducted in the Korean context, further suggest that employment type and financial losses may serve as key channels through which individuals become reluctant to undergo testing.12 23 24 Our results show that individuals unable to take one to 2 weeks off work likely encountered significant challenges in adhering to testing and isolation protocols, even when exhibiting COVID-like symptoms. The self-employed, in particular, were often forced to immediately close their workplaces—their primary source of income—on receiving a positive test result, as required by quarantine measures for diagnosis and isolation. In addition, jobs requiring in-person interaction and offering precarious employment expose workers to greater personal losses when adhering to preventive measures, testing and treatment protocols. A study using data from Korea’s COVID-19 Behavior Tracker (April 2020–April 2021), found that regular workers were more likely to voluntarily avoid social interactions and take steps to reduce infection risks at work. In contrast, economic constraints made it more challenging for non-regular workers and self-employed individuals to limit outings and social engagements.25

Our study highlights the critical importance of ensuring equitable access to COVID-19 testing for all individuals, regardless of employment status, as a key strategy in effectively combating the pandemic. Providing adequate support and resources to individuals who face financial or job-related consequences from testing can promote early detection and timely treatment, ultimately aiding in the containment of the disease. Previous studies have identified the failure to detect and isolate asymptomatic infections as a major driver of community transmission.26 27 Policy recommendations to prevent this would include enacting legislation to expand paid sick leave, introducing sickness benefits and enhancing employment insurance, especially for workers in small and medium-sized enterprises. According to Lee et al, four countries, including Korea, of the 37 OECD countries do not have statutory sick leave, and eight countries do not provide a subscription to the self-employed.28 Addressing barriers to testing for marginalised groups, therefore, offers societal benefits by mitigating the spread of infectious diseases. As such, a comparative study of Germany, Sweden and the UK further emphasises that institutionalised welfare systems were instrumental in managing socioeconomic inequalities during the pandemic.29

Although our study reveals important findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, key variables such as income, education level and COVID-19–like symptoms are based on self-reported survey responses. This may cause measurement error, as respondents could misreport information due to recall bias or misunderstanding of survey questions. For socioeconomic variables, such errors are likely non-differential with respect to diagnostic status. COVID-19-like symptoms, recall bias may have led to misclassification in either direction; however, given the already strong association observed (aOR 10.83), such bias is unlikely to alter the direction of our findings. Second, because the survey was administered using three modes, online, telephone and in-person, there is potential30 for bias if responses differ systematically across modes. The online sample exhibited a lower proportion of unreported infections compared with the other survey modes, suggesting potential differences in outcome distribution across modes. However, the effect of survey mode on unreported infection became non-significant after adjusting for age (data not shown), indicating that the observed differences were largely attributable to age distribution rather than survey mode itself. As our main analyses controlled for age and other sociodemographic characteristics, any bias arising from differences in survey mode is unlikely to have substantially affected the findings. Third, serological measures may be affected by the waning of antibodies over time. Individuals who were previously infected with SARS-CoV-2 may test negative if antibody levels decline below detectable thresholds, potentially leading to misclassification of prior infections. However, antibody testing in our second survey was conducted in December 2022, within approximately 10 months of the Omicron wave, during which most infections in South Korea occurred. Evidence suggests that anti-nucleocapsid antibody responses remain detectable for extended periods following infection, with sustained levels observed for several months and, in many cases, beyond 200 days postinfection.30 Therefore, the likelihood that the antibody waning substantially biases our results is limited.

Despite these limitations, the findings offer valuable evidence to inform policy recommendations aimed at mitigating the disproportionate impacts of future disease outbreaks. Screening vulnerable populations based on socioeconomic characteristics and providing targeted education and information should be prioritised. Additionally, compensation mechanisms should be developed to enhance compliance by addressing how employment status impacts adherence to preventive measures. Future research could investigate the specific reasons why participants with unreported cases were not tested for COVID-19, and whether non-compliance with testing contributes to the extent of community transmission. In addition, data from the K-SEROSMART study could provide insights into the determinants of testing behaviours, drawing on previous studies that explored the association between epidemic dynamics and testing.31 In conclusion, addressing the socioeconomic barriers to testing and preventive measures is essential to mitigating the unequal burden of infectious diseases and strengthening societal resilience against future public health crises.
