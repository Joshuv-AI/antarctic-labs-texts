Positive Genus Pairs from Amplituhedra
Joris Koefler, Dmitrii Pavlov, Rainer Sinn

Abstract
A main conjecture in the field of Positive Geometry states that amplituhedra are positive geometries. It is motivated by examples showing that the canonical forms of certain amplituhedra compute scattering amplitudes in particle physics. In recent work, Brown and Dupont introduced a new framework, based on mixed Hodge theory, connecting canonical forms and de Rham cohomology. In this paper, we show that this framework is consistent with the known results for amplituhedra but does not apply beyond those families. We provide an explicit example showing that the central assumption of the Brown-Dupont framework (namely to have a pair of genus zero) is not a necessary condition to be a positive geometry in the original sense of Arkani-Hamed, Bai, and Lam. This underscores the fact that our results do not immediately disqualify the amplituhedron from being a positive geometry.



1
Introduction

The
amplituhedron

\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}(Z)
, introduced by Arkani-Hamed and Trnka in
[
3
]
, is a geometric object of great importance in particle physics, where it is used to calculate scattering amplitudes in
\mathcal{N}=4
super Yang–Mills theory.
It is defined to be the image of the totally nonnegative Grassmannian
\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}

[
26
]
under the linear map
\tilde{Z}:\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)\dashrightarrow\mathrm{Gr}(k,k+m)
induced by a
n\times(k+m)
matrix
Z
all of whose minors are positive.
Here
n,k,m
are positive integers such that
k+m\leq n
.
The case of immediate relevance for physics is
m=4
where
m
indicates the dimension of space-time; the case
m=2
serves as an important toy model.

The discovery of the amplituhedron stimulated active search for further geometric objects that describe scattering amplitudes in different quantum field theories.
Certain physical properties that an amplitude satisfies can be phrased mathematically in terms of poles and residues of differential forms.
This observation lead to the introduction of the framework of
positive geometries
in
[
1
]
.
A central conjecture of
[
1
]
is that the amplituhedron
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}(Z)
is a positive geometry for all values of
n,k,m
and generic choices of
Z
.
Despite a vast amount of mathematical work on the amplituhedron that appeared in the recent years (see e.g.
[
23
,
35
,
22
,
20
]
for
m=2
and
[
9
,
8
]
for
m=4
), this conjecture is still wide open.
The only settled cases are (1)
k=1
: the amplituhedron is a cyclic polytope, see
[
31
]
,
[
19
, Theorem 1]
; (2)
k+m=n
: the amplituhedron is isomorphic to
\mathrm{Gr}_{\geq 0}(k,n)

[
19
, Theorem 9]
; (3)
k=m=2
: see
[
27
, Theorem 1.3]
.

In recent work, Brown and Dupont
[
4
]
recast the notion of positive geometries in the language of Deligne’s mixed Hodge theory
[
5
]
. In this context, positive geometries come from pairs of algebraic varieties
(X,Y)
that have genus zero.
The genus of the pair is defined as the sum of outer Hodge numbers
h^{p,0}
for
p>0
, of the mixed Hodge structure on the relative cohomology group
H^{n}(X,Y)
with
\mathbb{Q}
-coefficients, where
n=\dim X
. A possible definition of positive geometries within this framework is given in
[
32
, Definition 1.13]
.

In the framework of
[
1
]
a positive geometry is a pair
(X,X_{\geq 0})
, where
X
is a complex variety defined over
\mathbb{R}
and
X_{\geq 0}
is a semi-algebraic set inside its real points. In the setup of
[
4
]
one works with pairs
(X,Y)
of complex varieties such that
Y\subset X
. The way to go between these two approaches is to set
Y=\partial_{a}X_{\geq 0}
, where
\partial_{a}
denotes the algebraic boundary operator (see Section
2.1
for a definition).

The notions of positive geometries in the sense of
[
1
]
and of
[
4
]
are not equivalent. For instance, a positive geometry in the sense of
[
1
]
is a real algebraic object, while the real numbers play no role in the framework of
[
4
]
.
It has been an open question if there exist positive geometries in the sense of
[
1
]
that do not give rise to genus zero pairs of
[
4
]
.
In Section
6
we answer this question by giving an example of a positive geometry which gives rise to a pair of genus one inspired by our results for amplituhedra.

The guiding question of the present paper asks for the relation between the framework in
[
4
]
and the amplituhedron
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}(Z)
for
m=2
and
m=4
. We now describe our main results.
On the positive side, for the cases in which the amplituhedron is known to be a positive geometry, we verify in Section
3
, that it gives rise to a genus zero pair
(\mathrm{Gr}(k,k+m),\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}(Z))
(Theorem
3.3
).
Here
\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}(Z)
denotes the algebraic boundary of the amplituhedron.
On the negative side, for
k\geq 3
and large enough
n
we discover that this pair has strictly positive genus (Theorems
4.1
and
5.0
), meaning that the amplituhedron is not, in general, a positive geometry in the sense of
[
4
]
.
However, in view of the example constructed in Section
6
, we note that positivity of the genus does not immediately disqualify amplituhedra from being positive geometries in the sense of
[
1
]
. This raises the question of whether the framework of
[
4
]
can be adapted to match that of
[
1
]
. We intend to pursue this question in future work.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we give necessary definitions and review the main concepts that we will use in the proofs of later sections.
This includes basic notions of positive geometry and mixed Hodge theory.
In Section
3
we show that for the cases
k=1
,
k+m=n
and
k=m=2
the pair
(\gr(k,k+m),\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}(Z))
has genus zero for a generic choice of the matrix
Z
.
In Section
4
we prove that for
m=2
and
n\geq 2(2k-1)
the pair above has strictly positive genus. Section
5
is devoted to an analogous statement for
m=4
.
Finally, in Section
6
we construct an example of a semi-algebraic set
P\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
which is a positive geometry in the sense of
[
1
]
, but such that the pair
(\mathbb{P}^{3},\partial_{a}P)
has genus one.

2
Preliminaries

The
Grassmannian

\gr(k,n)
is the variety of
k
-dimensional vector spaces contained in a fixed
n
-dimensional complex vector space. It is an irreducible smooth complex projective algebraic variety of dimension
k(n-k)
.
We usually consider the Grassmannian in its Plücker embedding into
\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{k}-1}
.
This embedding is given by taking a
k\times n
full rank matrix
\Lambda
, representing a point
[\Lambda]\in\gr(k,n)
and defining the coordinates on
\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{k}-1}
to be the
k\times k
minors of
\Lambda
.
If it is clear from context, we will often write
\Lambda
both for the point in the Grassmannian and a matrix representative of it.
It is well known, see e.g.
[
29
, Corollary 2.33]
, that the degree of the Grassmannian
\gr(k,n)
in this embedding is

\displaystyle\deg\gr(k,n)=(k(n-k))!\cdot\frac{0!\cdot 1!\cdot\ldots\cdot(k-1)!}{(n-k)!\cdot(n-k+1)!\cdot\ldots\cdot(n-1)!}.

(2.1)

In the case of
k=2
, that is in the case of the Grassmannian of lines, this simplifies to

\displaystyle\deg\gr(2,n)=C_{n-2},

(2.2)

where
C_{n-2}
is the
(n-2)
-th Catalan number.
The
non-negative Grassmannian

\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}

[
26
]
is the semi-algebraic subset of
\gr(k,n)
, defined as the intersection of
\gr(k,n)
in its Plücker embedding into
\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{k}-1}
with the non-negative orthant
\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{k}-1}_{\geq 0}
.
Equivalently,
\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}
consists of all linear spaces represented by real matrices
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
of rank
k
whose
k\times k
minors are all non-negative, up to a global change of sign.
A (tree-level) amplituhedron depends on the choice of positive integer parameters
k,m
, and
n
, with
n\geq k+m
, and on the choice of a
n\times(k+m)
matrix
Z
with positive maximal minors. In this paper we are going to fix
m=2
or
m=4
. The matrix
Z
induces a rational map

\widetilde{Z}:\gr(k,n)\dashrightarrow\gr(k,k+m),\quad V\mapsto VZ.

By the assumption on
Z
, this map is well-defined on
\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}
. The image
\widetilde{Z}(\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}
of the non-negative Grassmannian under this map is called the
(tree-level) amplituhedron

\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}(Z)
, first defined in
[
3
]
.
Henceforth, we will often suppress writing the dependence on
Z
, that is
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}=\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}(Z)
.
The amplituhedron is a regular semi-algebraic set in
\gr(k,k+m)
, i.e. it is the Euclidean closure of its interior. In particular, its Euclidean boundary is
\partial\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}=\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}\setminus\operatorname{int}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}
.
Then, we define the
algebraic boundary

\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}\subset\gr(k,k+m)
as the Zariski closure of the Euclidean boundary
\partial\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}
.

The amplituhedron, or rather its algebraic boundary, is the central object of this article.
Therefore, we recall the descriptions of the algebraic boundary of the amplituhedron
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}
for
m=2
and
m=4
.
These commonly use the notion of
twistor coordinates
, see e.g.
[
16
]
or
[
34
, Definition 4.5]
. The twistor coordinate
\langle Yi_{1}\dots i_{m}\rangle
(or
\langle YZ_{i_{1}}\dots Z_{i_{m}}\rangle
) is defined as the determinant of the
(k+m)\times(k+m)
matrix obtained by stacking a matrix representing
Y\in\gr(k,k+m)
together with the rows of
Z
labeled by
i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\in[n]
.
A twistor coorindate can be regarded as a function on
\gr(k,k+m)
and its vanishing locus is a hyperplane section of
\gr(k,k+m)
.
It will be convenient for an index
i>n
, to take it modulo
n
, such that
i\mod n\in[n]
, in which case we say that the indices are
read cyclically
.
We now record the algebraic boundary of
\partial_{a}A_{n,k,2}
as in
[
27
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
m=2
the algebraic boundary
\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}
of
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}
, with
n\geq k+2
,
is the union of the following hyperplane sections of
\gr(k,k+2)
:

\langle YZ_{i}Z_{i+1}\rangle=0,

where
Y\in\gr(k,k+2)
and
i\in[n]
, with indices read cyclically.

We will now give a simple interpretation of the boundary components. For this, we need one more definition. Let
\Lambda
be a linear subspace in
\mathbb{P}^{k+1}
of dimension
k-d
and
X
an irreducible projective variety of dimension
d
. Then, the
Chow hypersurface

\operatorname{Ch}(X)
of
X
is

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ch}(X)=\overline{\{\Lambda\mid X\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing\}}\subset\gr(k-d+1,k+2).

Remark 2.2
.

One way of thinking about the algebraic boundary of
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}
is that it is characterized by the cycle of lines
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}(Z_{i},Z_{i+1})\subset\mathbb{P}^{k+1}
for
i\in[n]
, with indices read cyclically.
That is,
\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}
consists of the Chow hypersurfaces of these lines. This will be important later in the proofs of Section
4
.

Similarly, the algebraic boundary for
m=4
was conjectured in
[
2
, Section 5]
and eventually proven in
[
9
, Corollary 8.8]
to be the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

For
m=4
the algebraic boundary
\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,4}
of
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,4}
, with
n\geq k+4
, is the union of the following
hyperplane sections of
\gr(k,k+4)
:

\langle YZ_{i}Z_{i+1}Z_{j}Z_{j+1}\rangle=0,

where
Y\in\gr(k,k+4)
and
\{i,i+1\}\cap\{j,j+1\}=\varnothing
, with
i,j\in[n]
read cyclically.

2.1
A pinch of Positive Geometry

We recall the definition of
positive geometries
in the sense of
[
1
]
, see also
[
19
]
.
Let
X
be a
d
-dimensional irreducible complex projective variety defined over
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
X_{\geq 0}
be a
d
-dimensional semi-algebraic subset of the real points
X(\mathbb{R})
of
X
.
We require that the interior
X_{>0}
of
X_{\geq 0}
(with respect to the analytic topology on
X(\mathbb{R})
)
is an oriented manifold, such that its (analytic) closure is
X_{\geq 0}
.
The algebraic boundary
\partial_{a}X_{\geq 0}
is the Zariski closure in
X
of
X_{\geq 0}\setminus X_{>0}
.
The irreducible components of
\partial_{a}X_{\geq 0}
are prime divisors
D_{1},\ldots,D_{r}
on
X
. We define
(D_{i})_{\geq 0}
as the closure in
D_{i}(\mathbb{R})
of the interior of
D_{i}\cap(X_{\geq 0}\setminus X_{>0})
.

Definition 2.4
.

The pair
(X,X_{\geq 0})
is called a
positive geometry
if there exists a unique rational
d
-form
\Omega(X_{\geq 0})
on
X
, called the
canonical form
, satisfying the following axioms.

•

If
d>0
, the form
\Omega(X_{\geq 0})
has poles only along
D_{1},\ldots,D_{r}
.

•

For each component
D_{i}
of the algebraic boundary, the pair
(D_{i},(D_{i})_{\geq 0})
is a positive geometry;
\Omega(X_{\geq 0})
has a simple pole along
D_{i}

and the Poincaré residue
\operatorname{Res}_{D_{i}}\Omega(X_{\geq 0})
equals the canonical form of

(D_{i},(D_{i})_{\geq 0})
. Here
D_{i,>0}
is an oriented manifold,
with orientation induced by that of
X_{>0}
.

•

If
d=0
,
X=X_{\geq 0}
is a point and
\Omega(X_{\geq 0})=\pm 1
.
The sign determines the orientation on this 0-dimensional manifold.

Remark 2.5
.

Note that if the variety
X
has non-zero global holomorphic top-forms, the pair
(X,X_{\geq 0})
cannot be a positive geometry: adding such a form to a canonical form would produce another canonical form, contradicting the uniqueness requirement.
Moreover, if the canonical form is not unique, then the difference between any two canonical forms is a global holomorphic top-form on
X
: both canonical forms are holomorphic on
X\setminus\partial_{a}X_{\geq 0}
, and their difference has zero residues along each component of
\partial_{a}X_{\geq 0}
.

Lastly, the
residual arrangement

\mathcal{R}(X_{\geq 0})\subset X
of a semi-algebraic set
X_{\geq 0}\subset X
is defined as the union of the irreducible components of the singular locus of its algebraic boundary
\partial_{a}X_{\geq 0}
which do not intersect the Euclidean boundary
\partial X_{\geq 0}
.
If there is a unique polynomial of lowest degree interpolating
\mathcal{R}(X_{\geq 0})
, then we call it the
adjoint
of
X_{\geq 0}
, denoted
\operatorname{adj}(X_{\geq 0})
.

2.2
A hint of Hodge Theory

In the recent paper
[
4
]
an alternative framework for positive geometries, based on mixed Hodge theory, was suggested. We briefly recall the essential notions.
Let
X
be a smooth complex variety of dimension
n
.
In this work we exclusively work with singular cohomology groups of
X
with rational coefficients, denoted by
H^{k}(X)
.
There is a natural decomposition of the cohomology groups on
X
, first identified by Hodge in
[
15
]
:

\displaystyle H^{k}(X)=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}H^{k}(X)^{p,q}=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}H^{q}(X,\Omega_{X}^{p}),

(2.3)

where
\Omega_{X}^{p}
is the
sheaf of algebraic
p
-forms
on
X
, which is defined by taking the
p
-th exterior power of the sheaf of regular functions
\mathcal{O}_{X}
on
X
.
This is the
Hodge decomposition
, and it gives
X
, or rather its cohomology groups, a
pure Hodge structure of weight
k
.

Singular varieties can be endowed with a
mixed Hodge structure
, which roughly speaking allows for pure Hodge structures of different weights to be combined. We use the definition as in
[
4
, Definition 1.5]
.

Definition 2.6
.

A
mixed Hodge structure
is the data of a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
H
together with

•

an increasing filtration
W
on
H
, called the
weight filtration
,

•

a decreasing filtration
F
on the complexification
H_{\mathbb{C}}
, called the
Hodge filtration
,

such that for every integer
w
, the filtration induced by
F
on

\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{w}H:=W_{w}H/W_{w-1}H

defines a pure Hodge structure of weight
w
.
For
w=p+q
record its Hodge filtrated parts by

(\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{w}H)^{p,q}=\operatorname{gr}_{F}^{p}(\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{w}H)=\frac{F^{p}(\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{w}H)}{F^{p+1}(\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{w}H)}=F^{p}(\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{w}H)\cap\overline{F^{q}(\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{w}H)}.

The corresponding
Hodge numbers
are the complex dimensions

h^{p,q}=\dim H^{p,q}=h^{q,p},\quad\text{where }H^{p,q}:=\bigl(\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{p+q}H\bigr)^{p,q}.

Remark 2.7
.

In our setting, we will exclusively deal with mixed Hodge structures on the
k
-th cohomology group of a complex algebraic variety
X
. Then, for the Hodge numbers we explicitly write

\displaystyle h^{p,q}(H^{k}(X))=\dim\left(\operatorname{gr}_{F}^{p}\bigl(\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{p+q}H^{k}(X)\bigr)\right).

(2.4)

Now, let
Y
be a closed subset of an
n
-dimensional complex algebraic variety
X
, and let
H^{k}(X,Y)
denote the
k
-th relative cohomology group.
The first central notion that allows us to use this framework to study positive geometries is the genus of the pair
(X,Y)
.
We define the
genus

g(X,Y)
of the pair to be

g(X,Y)=\sum_{p>0}h^{p,0}(H^{n}(X,Y)).

We write
g(X)=g(X,\varnothing)
for the trivial pair
(X,\varnothing)
.
This definition is equivalent to writing
g(X,Y)=\sum_{p>0}h^{-p,0}(H_{n}(X,Y))
essentially by Poincaré duality, see
[
4
, Equation (16)]
.
Secondly, denote by
\Omega_{\operatorname{log}}^{n}(X\setminus Y)
the space of global holomorphic differential
n
-forms on
X\setminus Y
with logarithmic poles along
Y
.
If
X\setminus Y
is smooth and
g(X,Y)=0
we have an surjective morphism of
\mathbb{Q}
-vector spaces

\displaystyle\operatorname{\Omega}:H_{n}(X,Y)\twoheadrightarrow\Omega_{\operatorname{log}}^{n}(X\setminus Y),\quad\sigma\mapsto\Omega(\sigma)=\omega_{\sigma},

(2.5)

which associates to a
n
-cycle
\sigma
in
X
with boundary in
Y
, a logarithmic differential
n
-form
\Omega(\sigma)
on
X\setminus Y
.
In this sense, one way to define a positive geometry is as follows, see
[
32
]
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
X
be an
n
-dimensional complex algebraic variety, and
Y
a divisor, such that the complement
X\setminus Y
is smooth.
Further, let the pair
(X,Y)
have vanishing genus, i.e.
g(X,Y)=0
, and non-vanishing combinatorial rank (see
[
4
, Definition 2.2]
)
\operatorname{cr}(X,Y)>0
.
Then, a
positive geometry
is a relative homology class
\sigma\in H_{n}(X,Y)
. The
canonical form
of
\sigma
is the image
\Omega(\sigma)\in\Omega_{\operatorname{log}}^{n}(X\setminus Y)
under the map in (
2.5
).

Remark 2.9
.

In several examples, such as hyperplane arrangements, see
[
4
, Section 6]
, this notion of canonical form coincides with that of Definition
2.4
.
However, this definition is certainly more broad, as it does not have any recursive conditions, see
[
4
, Remark 2.4.3]
.
On the other hand, it is not immediately clear whether being a positive geometry in the sense of Definition
2.4
implies being a positive geometry in the sense of Definition
2.8
.
In Section
6
we show that this is not the case.

If a mixed Hodge structure
H
satisfies
H^{p,q}=0
for all
p\neq q
, then we say
H
is of
Tate type
.
Let
a
be an integer, then denote the
twisted Hodge structure
by
H(-a)
, where
H(-a)^{p,q}:=H^{p+a,q+a}
.
Additionally, we say a map
f:H_{1}\rightarrow H_{2}
between two Hodge structures is a
morphism
of Hodge structures if
f(F_{p}(H_{1}))\subset F_{p}H_{2}
for all
p
or equivalently
f(H_{1}^{p,q})\subset H_{2}^{p,q}
for all
p
and
q
.

Remark 2.10
.

Some other sources choose to introduce morphisms between Hodge structures of type
(a,a)
for an integer
a
, such that
f(F_{p}(H_{1}))\subset F_{p+a}H_{2}
or equivalently
f(H_{1}^{p,q})\subset H_{2}^{p+a,q+a}
for all
p
and
q
.
We choose to work only with morphisms of Hodge structures of type
(0,0)
, and add Tate twists to the Hodge structures if necessary.
For example, for the morphism of type
(a,a)
from above, we write
f(H_{1}^{p,q})\subset H_{2}(-a)^{p,q}
for all
p
and
q
and simply say
f
is a morphism of Hodge structures.

Throughout the rest of the article,
Y
will usually be a reducible subvariety of
X
.
Then, in order to understand the Hodge structure on the relative (co-)homology groups of the pair
(X,Y)
, it will be important to understand how intersections of the irreducible components of
Y
behave homologically.
To that end, we recall a few standard tools from algebraic topology.
Firstly, let
A,B\subset X
be subvarieties of the complex algebraic variety
X
such that
X=A\cup B
, then we have the
Mayer–Vietoris
long exact sequence in cohomology:

\displaystyle\cdots\to H^{k-1}(A)\oplus H^{k-1}(B)\to H^{k-1}(A\cap B)\to H^{k}(X)\to H^{k}(A)\oplus H^{k}(B)\to\cdots.

(2.6)

Additionally, let
Y
be a closed subset of
X
of codimension one. Then we get the long exact sequence in relative cohomology for the pair
(X,Y)
:

\displaystyle\cdots\to H^{k-1}(X)\to H^{k-1}(Y)\to H^{k}(X,Y)\to H^{k}(X)\to\cdots.

(2.7)

Both sequences are long exact sequences of mixed Hodge structures, and all of the maps appearing in them are morphisms of Hodge structures, see
[
6
, Appendix C.23/30]
and
[
25
, Proposition 5.46]
, respectively.
Lastly, we recall the Lefschetz hypersurface theorem.

Theorem 2.11

(
[
33
, Theorem 1.23]
)
.

Let
X
be a complex algebriac variety of dimension
n
, and let
Y
be a hypersurface in
X
, such that the complement
X\setminus Y
is smooth. Then, we get an isomorphism

\displaystyle H^{k}(X)\cong H^{k}(Y)\quad\text{ for all }k\leq n-2,

(2.8)

and an injection
H^{n-1}(X)\hookrightarrow H^{n-1}(Y)
.

3
Amplituhedra that make genus zero pairs

In this section we consider specific choices of the parameters
n
,
k
, and
m
for which the amplituhedron
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}
is known to be a positive geometry in the sense of
[
1
]
. We show that for these choices the pair
(\mathrm{Gr}(k,k+m),\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m})
has genus zero, and thus fits nicely into the framework proposed in
[
4
]
.
We collect the results in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
Z
be a generic
n\times(k+m)
matrix with positive maximal minors.
Then, for any choice of integers
k
,
m
, and
n\geq k+m
, satisfying either
k=1
,
k+m=n
, or
k=m=2
, the boundary divisor
\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m}\subset\gr(k,k+m)
of the amplituhedron makes a genus zero pair with the ambient Grassmannian.
That is,

g(\gr(k,k+m),\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,m})=0

for the choices of
k,n
and
m
as above.

We cover the cases of the theorem above individually.
The first case is the non-negative Grassmannian, corresponding to the choice
k+m=n
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The non-negative Grassmannian
\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}
gives rise to a genus zero pair. That is,
g(\gr(k,n),\partial_{a}\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0})=0
.

Proof.

By
[
11
, Corollary 4.13]
the cohomology groups of open positroid varieties in
\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}
are pure of Tate type.
In particular, this is true for the top-dimensional open positroid variety
\mathring{\Pi}
. Its intersection with the non-negative Grassmannian
\mathring{\Pi}\cap\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}
, has an algebraic boundary coinciding with
\partial_{a}\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}
.
Therefore,
\mathring{\Pi}=\gr(k,n)\setminus\partial_{a}\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}
.
Then, since
\mathring{\Pi}
is smooth, it follows from Poincaré duality in
[
4
, Equation (16)]
that

H_{n}(\gr(k,n),\partial_{a}\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0})\cong H^{n}(\gr(k,n)\setminus\partial_{a}\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0})(n)=H^{n}(\mathring{\Pi})(n).

Since
H^{n}(\mathring{\Pi})
has pure Hodge structure of Tate type and weight
2n
, the relative homology group
H_{n}(\gr(k,n),\partial_{a}\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0})
must have a pure Hodge structure of weight
0
and Tate type, in particular implying
g(\gr(k,n)\partial_{a}\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0})=0
.
∎

The second case is the amplituhedron
\mathcal{A}_{n,2,2}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

The amplituhedron
\mathcal{A}_{n,2,2}\subset\gr(2,4)
gives rise to a genus zero pair, i.e.
g(\gr(2,4),\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,2,2})=0
.

Proof.

The Grassmannian
\gr(2,4)
is a smooth quadratic hypersurface in its Plücker embedding into
\mathbb{P}^{5}
.
Moreover, the irreducible components
\mathcal{D}_{i}
for
i\in[n]
of the algebraic boundary
\mathcal{D}=\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,2,2}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{D}_{i}\subset\gr(2,4)
can be extended to hyperplanes
D_{i}
in the ambient
\mathbb{P}^{5}
.
We also set
D=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}
to be the union of these hyperplanes.
Now, since the complement
\mathbb{P}^{5}\setminus D
is smooth and affine, we get

\displaystyle g(\gr(2,4),\mathcal{D})=g(\mathbb{P}^{5},D\cup\gr(2,4))-g(\mathbb{P}^{5},D),

(3.1)

by
[
4
, Corollary 3.12]
.
Moreover, since
H^{4}(\mathbb{P}^{5})^{p,0}=0
for all
p\geq 1
,
[
4
, Corollary 3.11]
implies that

\displaystyle g(\mathbb{P}^{5},D)=g(\mathbb{P}^{5})+g(D),

(3.2)

and

\displaystyle g(\mathbb{P}^{5},D\cup\gr(2,4))=g(\mathbb{P}^{5})+g(D\cup\gr(2,4)).

(3.3)

Using
[
4
, Theorem 3.26]
for
g(D)
and
g(D\cup\gr(2,4))
, as well as the fact that
h^{p,0}(H^{5}(\mathbb{P}^{5}))=0
, we see that both genera vanish in the right-hand sides of (
3.2
) and (
3.3
).
Therefore, we obtain
g(\gr(2,4),\mathcal{D})=0
from (
3.1
), as had to be shown.
∎

We collect these statments to prove the main theorem of this section.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

For
k=1
, the amplituhedron
\mathcal{A}_{n,1,m}\subset\gr(1,k+m)\cong\mathbb{P}^{k+m-1}
is a cyclic polytope, see
[
19
, Theorem 1]
. In this case the claim follows from
[
4
, Section 6]
.
For the case
k+m=n
, the map
\tilde{Z}:\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\gr(k,n)
is a linear isomorphism and thus the amplituhedron is isomorphic to the non-negative Grassmannian
\gr(k,n)_{\geq 0}
.
Therefore, the claim is Proposition
3.2
.
Lastly, the case of
k=m=2
is covered in Theorem
3.3
.
∎

4
The
m=2
amplituhedra
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}
make positive genus pairs

Let
m=2
,
k\geq 3
and
n\geq 2(2k-1)
.
The main goal of this section is to prove that the algebraic boundary
\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}
and the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)
form a positive genus pair.
To that end, let the
n\times(k+2)
matrix
Z
with positive maximal minors, which defines the amplituhedron
\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}=\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}(Z)
, be generic.
Here, generic means we pick
Z
in the open subset of matrices of this size, such that Kleiman’s transversality theorem
[
7
, Theorem 1.7]
applies in the situations appearing later in this section, see Lemmas
4.7
and
4.8
.
The main result of this section is then the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
n
and
k
be positive integers such that
n\geq 2(2k-1)
. Further let
Z
be generic and have positive maximal minors,
and
\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}(Z)\subset\gr(k,k+2)
be the algebraic boundary of the amplituhedron.
Then, the genus of the pair
(\gr(k,k+2),\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}(Z))
is at least
1+\frac{k-3}{2}C_{k}
, where
C_{k}
is the
k
-th Catalan number. In particular, for
k\geq 3
it is positive.

Remark 4.2
.

The lower bound from Theorem
4.1
is not tight. The number
1+\frac{k-3}{2}C_{k}
is the genus of a single curve in the residual arrangement of the amplituhedron whose cohomology injects into the relative cohomology of the pair
(\gr(k,k+2),\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}(Z))
. The residual arrangement in general contains multiple curves of positive genus. Moreover, one could in principle have non-zero contributions to the genus from higher-dimensional components of the residual arrangement. However, since our primary goal is to show that the genus of the pair is non-zero rather than to compute it exactly, we do not aim to improve this bound.

First, let us make a note of the strategy we are going to employ in this section.
Since the Grassmannian is a smooth projective variety, we have
g(\gr(k,k+2),\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2})=g(\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2})
.
Therefore, in order to compute the genus of the pair, we need to understand the algebraic boundary of the amplituhedron.
Moreover, the boundary is reducible and hence its cohomology is essentially determined by multiple intersections of its components.
Therefore, we start by establishing a series of results that will help us compute the cohomology groups of intersections of boundary divisors of the amplituhedron.
Firstly, we show that dense subsets of global complete intersections inside the Grassmannian are local complete intersections.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
H
be a (not necessarily smooth) global complete intersection in
\gr(k,n)
.
Then, any open subvariety
Y\subset H
obtained as the complement of a divisor on
H
is a local complete intersection.

Proof.

By assumption the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[H]
, as a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-algebra, is a (global) complete intersection. Any localization
\mathbb{C}[H]_{g}
for
g\in C[H]
is a complete intersection by
[
30
, Lemma 10.135.2]
, so in particular a local complete intersection.
By assumption
Y
is the complement of a divisor in
H
, therefore we can find
f\in\mathbb{C}[\gr(k,n)]
such that
f
is not identically zero on
H
and
\mathbb{V}(f)\cap H=Y
.
Then,
\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[H]_{f})_{0}\cong Y
is a local complete intersection.
∎

The following is a direct consequence of the Lefschetz type theorem
[
24
, Theorem B]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X
be a (not necessarily smooth) global complete intersection of hyperplane sections in the Grassmannian
\gr(k,n)
.
Then, the
\ell
-th cohomology group
H^{\ell}(X)
is isomorphic to
H^{\ell}(\gr(k,n))
for all
\ell\leq\dim X-1
. In particular, the Hodge structure on
H^{\ell}(X)
is pure of Tate type of weight
2\ell
for all
\ell\leq\dim X-1
.

Proof.

Since
X
is a global complete intersection of linear spaces in
\gr(k,n)
, say of codimension
r
, we can find hyperplane sections
H_{1},\ldots,H_{r}\subset\gr(k,n)
such that
X=H_{1}\cap\ldots\cap H_{r}
.
We also write
H_{[j]}=\bigcap_{i\in[j]}D_{i}
for any
j\in[r]
.
We prove the claim by induction on the codimension
r
of
X
.
The case
r=0
is tautological.
If
r=1
, we know that
\gr(k,n)\setminus X
is smooth, and the claim follows by the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem, see Theorem
2.11
.

Now let
r
be arbitrary, and
X=H_{[r]}
.
So suppose
H^{\ell}(H_{[r-1]})\cong H^{\ell}(\gr(k,n))
for
\ell<n-r+1
.
Next, note that
X\subset H_{[r-1]}\subset\gr(k,n)
is a very ample effective Cartier divisor, since the Grassmannian
\gr(k,n)
has an embedding into projective space – the usual Plücker embedding.
Moreover, since
H_{[r-1]}
is a global complete intersection by definition, it follows from Lemma
4.3
that the complement
U=H_{[r-1]}\setminus X
is a local complete intersection, in particular
U
has vanishing local cohomology defect by
[
24
, Example 2.5]
.
Then,
[
24
, Theorem B]
applies, showing that
H^{\ell}(X)\cong H^{\ell}(H_{[r-1]})\cong H^{\ell}(\gr(k,n))
for all
\ell<\dim X=n-r
, finishing the proof.
∎

As a corollary, we obtain a result about Hodge structures on divisors in complete intersections inside the Grassmannian.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
X
be a (not necessarily smooth)
d
-dimensional global complete intersection of hyperplane sections in the Grassmannian
\gr(k,n)
.
Further, let
r
be a positive integer and
D=D_{1}\cup\ldots\cup D_{r}
be a union of
r
Schubert hyperplane sections
D_{i}
in
\gr(k,n)
.
Then, the
\ell
-th cohomology group
H^{\ell}(X\cap D)
is isomorphic to
H^{\ell}(\gr(k,n))
for all
\ell\leq d-2
, in particular its Hodge structure is pure of weight
2\ell
and Tate type.

Proof.

By assumption
X
is a global complete intersection, hence it follows from Lemma
4.3
, that
U=X\setminus(D\cap X)
is a local complete intersection.
In particular, its local cohomology defect vanishes by
[
24
, Example 2.5]
.
Next, the line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)}(r)|_{X}
is ample.
Then,
X\cap D
is a section of this line bundle and as such ample itself; therefore
X\cap D
is an ample effective Cartier divisor in
X
.
Then,
H^{\ell}(X\cap D)\cong H^{\ell}(X)
for all
\ell\leq d-2
by
[
24
, Theorem B]
.
The cohomology of
H^{\ell}(X)
is isomorphic to
H^{\ell}(\gr(k,n))
, as computed in Lemma
4.4
, finishing the proof.
∎

Next, we show that, in the same setting, we get a very useful lower bound for some Hodge numbers in the Hodge filtrated parts of cohomology groups in the Mayer–Vietoris sequence.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
X
be a (not necessarily smooth)
d
-dimensional global complete intersection of hyperplane sections in the Grassmannian
\gr(k,n)
.
Further, let
D_{1},\ldots,D_{r}
be Schubert hyperplane sections in
\gr(k,n)
, we let
D=X\cap D_{1}
and
R=X\cap(D_{2}\cup\ldots\cup D_{r})
be the intersection of
X
with such a Schubert divisor, and a union of Schubert divisors, respectively.
Then, we get an injection in the Mayer–Vietoris long exact sequence in cohomology

H^{\ell-1}(D\cap R)^{p,0}\hookrightarrow H^{\ell}(D\cup R)^{p,0}

for all
\ell\leq d-1
and
p>0
. In particular,
h^{p,0}(H^{d-2}(D\cap R))\leq h^{p,0}(H^{d-1}(D\cup R))
.

Proof.

We consider the Mayer–Vietoris long exact sequence in cohomology for the union
D\cup R
, see Equation (
2.6
):

\cdots\to H^{\ell-1}(D)\oplus H^{\ell-1}(R)\to H^{\ell-1}(D\cap R)\to H^{\ell}(D\cup R)\to H^{\ell}(D)\oplus H^{\ell}(R)\to\cdots.

The term
H^{\ell-1}(D)\oplus H^{\ell-1}(R)
, with
D=X\cap D_{1}
and
R=X\cap(D_{2}\cap\ldots\cap D_{r})
, admits a pure Hodge structure of Tate type for all
\ell\leq d-1
by Corollary
4.5
.
In particular, the Hodge numbers
h^{p,0}
vanish for all
p>0
.
Therefore, the claim follows since the Mayer–Vietoris sequence is a long exact sequence of mixed Hodge structures, see
[
6
, C.30]
.
∎

We fix some notation for the remainder of this section.
For
i\in[n]
, let
D_{i}
be the irreducible components of the algebraic boundary
\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}
. In particular,
D_{i}
denotes the Chow hypersurface
\operatorname{Ch}(L_{i})
of the line
L_{i}\subset\mathbb{C}^{k+2}
spanned by the rows
Z_{i}
and

of the matrix

.
Further, for

we write

for the intersection
\bigcap_{i\in I}D_{i}
. Lastly, for any odd

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
I
be a subset of
[n]
such that
i\in I\Rightarrow i+1\notin I
.
Then, the intersection
D_{I}=\bigcap_{i\in I}D_{i}
is a global complete transversal intersection in
\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)
.
Geometrically speaking, for a set of pairwise skew lines
L_{i}
the intersection of the corresponding Schubert divisors
D_{i}
is transversal and a global complete intersection.
In particular,
D_{([j])}
is a transversal and global complete intersection for any odd
j\in[n]
.

Proof.

For the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)
the general linear group
\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbb{C}}(k+2)
acts transversely on it as an algebraic group.
By our choice of
I
, and since
Z
is generic, the lines
L_{i}
are in general position.
Therefore, Kleiman’s theorem,
[
7
, Theorem 1.3]
, applies, showing that the intersection
\bigcap_{i\in I}D_{i}
is generically transversal. This shows the first part of the statement.
Lastly, notice that if we consider
D_{([j])}
, then in particular the lines
L_{i}
for
i\in([j])
are skew, and thus in general position, so this is a special case of the above.
This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
n\geq 2(2k-1)
. Take
I\subset[n]
, with
|I|=2k-1
, and such that the lines
L_{i}=\operatorname{span}(Z_{i},Z_{i+1})
for
i\in I
are pairwise skew.
Then, the intersection
E=\bigcap_{i\in I}\operatorname{Ch}(L_{i})
in
\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)
is a smooth, irreducible and reduced curve.

Proof.

By assumption the lines
L_{i}
are skew and since
Z
is generic the lines are in general position, so Lemma
4.7
applies, stating that the dimension of
E
is indeed one.
Moreover, it also follows from the same lemma that for all
J\subset I
the intersections
\bigcap_{j\in J}D_{j}
are global complete intersections, and in particular are transversal.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Sing}(E)=\bigcup_{i\in I}\left(\operatorname{Sing}D_{i}\cap\bigcap_{j\neq i}D_{j}\right),

(4.1)

since points that are smooth on each component will be smooth points of the intersection by the transversality of the intersection.
We want to show that the union in (
4.1
) is empty, and in order to do so we consider the following incidence variety:

\Sigma_{i}=\{(p,[\Lambda])\mid p\in\Lambda\}\subset L_{i}\times\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2).

Since this incidence variety
\Sigma_{i}
is smooth, see e.g.
[
18
, Proposition 3.4]
, it follows from
[
13
, Corollary 14.10]
, that a point
[\Lambda]\in D_{i}
is smooth if and only if
[\Lambda]\cap L_{i}=\{p\}
.
Conversely, the singular locus
\operatorname{Sing}D_{i}
is given by the subset of
D_{i}\subset\gr(k,k+2)
of all
k
-planes
[\Lambda]
that contain the line
L_{i}
.
In particular, the codimension of the singular locus
\operatorname{Sing}D_{i}
inside
D_{i}
is
3
, that is
\dim\operatorname{Sing}D_{i}=2k-4
.
By the genericity assumption on
Z
, the intersection
\operatorname{Sing}D_{i}\cap\bigcap_{j\neq i\in I}D_{j}
is also transversal.
So the dimension count

\dim\operatorname{Sing}D_{i}-\operatorname{codim}\bigcap_{j\neq i}D_{i}=2k-4-2k+2=-2

shows that this intersection is empty.
Since
D_{i}
was arbitrary, by Equation (
4.1
) the curve

must be smooth. And since
E
is smooth it must be reduced as a scheme, else the Jacobian criterion for smoothness fails.

It remains to show that the curve is irreducible. For a smooth curve it suffices to show that
E
is connected.
This follows from
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
applied to the Plücker embedding
\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\binom{k+2}{2}-1}
.
∎

We compute the (geometric) genus of the curve

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
E
be a curve obtained from the
(2k-1)
-fold transversal intersection of
D_{i}=\operatorname{Ch}(L_{i})
, where the lines
L_{i}\subset\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{k+2})
spanned by
Z_{i}
and
Z_{i+1}
are pairwise disjoint.
Then, for

the (geometric) genus of

can be computed as

g(E)=1+\frac{k-3}{2}C_{k},

where

is the

-th Catalan number. For

the genus of the curve

is zero.

Proof 4.3
.

Let
k=1
. Then, we are in
\gr(1,3)\cong\mathbb{P}^{2}
, and the divisors
D_{i}
are lines in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. In particular,

is such a line and thus has genus

.
For

we get a linear section of

, which is a conic and thus has genus

.
Now, let

.
By construction we can apply Lemmas

and

to

.
Therefore, it follows from
[

\displaystyle N_{E}\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{2k-1}N_{D_{i}}\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)|_{E},

(4.7)

where

are the normal bundles of the divisors

.
Since all of the divisors

(4.8)

\deg_{E}\omega_{E}=(k-3)[H][E]=2g-2,

where

\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)
, respectively.
Therefore, we get
g=1+\frac{(k-3)}{2}[H]^{2k}
since
[E]=[H]^{2k-1}
, as

is a complete intersection by Lemma
4.7
.
Finally, recall from Equation (
2.2
) that the degree of the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)
in its Plücker embedding is precisely the
k
-th Catalan number
C_{k}=[H]^{2k}
, and the claim follows.

We are now finally in a position to prove the main result of this section.

Proof 4.4

(Proof of Theorem
4.1
)
.

Let
\Delta=\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}
be the decomposition of the algebraic boundary of the amplituhedron into irreducible components
D_{i}\subset\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)
.
For
I\subset[n]
we write
D_{I}
for the intersection
\bigcap_{i\in I}D_{i}
.
We start by considering the following long exact sequence in relative cohomology.

\cdots\to H^{2k-1}(\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2))\to H^{2k-1}(\Delta)\to H^{2k}(\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2),\Delta)\to H^{2k}(\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2))\to\cdots.

By
[
25
, Proposition 5.46]
this is also a long exact sequence of mixed Hodge structures where the appearing maps are morphisms of such structures.
Since the Hodge structure on the cohomology of the Grassmannian is pure of Tate type, it follows that the
(p,0)
part of the terms
H^{2k-1}(\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2))
and
H^{2k}(\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2))
in the above exact sequence are zero, in turn implying that
h^{p,0}(H^{2k}(\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2),\Delta)=h^{p,0}(H^{2k-1}(\Delta))
for all
p>0
.
Now, we proceed by iteratively applying the Mayer–Vietoris long exact sequence to the union and intersections of the
D_{i}
.
We consider
D_{([j])}
for some odd
j\in[n]
, and let
d
denote its dimension.
By convention we set
D_{([j])}=\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2)
for
j<0
.
Note the
D_{([j])}
are global complete intersections in the Grassmannian by Lemma
4.7
.
Then, let
R_{j}
be the union of all
D_{i}
with
i\notin([j])
intersected with
D_{([j-2])}
, that is
R_{j}=D_{([j-2])}\cap\bigcup_{i\notin([j])}D_{i}
, and
\dim R_{j}=d
.
The union
D_{([j])}\cup R_{j}
fits into the Mayer–Vietoris long exact sequence (
2.6
) as

H^{d-1}(D_{([j])})\oplus H^{d-1}(R_{j})\to H^{d-1}(D_{([j])}\cap R)\to H^{d}(D_{([j])}\cup R_{j})\to H^{d}(D_{([j])})\oplus H^{d}(R_{j}).

(4.9)

Since
D_{([j])}
and
R_{j}
as divisors in the complete intersection
D_{([j-2])}
satisfy the assumptions in Corollary
4.6
, we get an injection
H^{d-1}(D_{([j])}\cap R)^{p,0}\hookrightarrow H^{d}(D_{([j])}\cup R_{j})^{p,0}
and thus the inequality

\displaystyle h^{p,0}(H^{d-1}(D_{([j])}\cap R_{j}))\leq h^{p,0}(H^{d}(D_{([j])}\cup R_{j})).

(4.10)

By noting that
D_{([j])}\cap R_{j}=D_{([j+2])}\cup R_{j+2}
, which are divisors in
D_{([j])}
, we can apply Equation (
4.9
) iteratively until
D_{([j])}
and
R_{j}
become varieties of dimension one.
Then,
D_{([mk-1])}
becomes a smooth curve

by Lemma
4.8
.
We get the following Mayer–Vietoris sequence for

:

\ldots\to H^{0}(E\cap R_{2k-1})\to H^{1}(E\cup R_{2k-1})\to H^{1}(E)\oplus H^{1}(R_{2k-1})\to H^{1}(E\cap R_{2k-1})\to\ldots.

Note that zeroth-cohomology groups always have pure Hodge structure of weight zero, and the first cohomology group on a union of points vanishes completely, i.e.
H^{1}(E\cap R_{2k-1})=0
.
Therefore, we get an isomorphism of vector spaces
H^{1}(E\cup R_{2k-1})^{p,0}\cong H^{1}(E)\oplus H^{1}(R_{2k-1})^{p,0}
. In particular,

h^{1,0}(H^{1}(E\cup R_{2k-1}))\geq h^{1,0}(H^{1}(E))=g(E)=1+\frac{k-3}{2}C_{k},

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
4.2
.
Then, since
E\cup R_{2k-1}=D_{([2k-3])}\cap R_{2k-3}
, where
D_{([2k-3])}
is of dimension two, we get a sequence of inequalities for the Hodge numbers we are interested in:

\displaystyle h^{p,0}(H^{2k-1}(\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}))\geq\ldots\geq h^{1,0}(H^{2}(D_{([mk-3])}\cup R_{2k-3}))\geq h^{1,0}(H^{1}(E\cup R_{2k-1}))\geq g(E)

(4.11)

by Equation (
4.10
), and with
g(E)=1+(k-3)C_{k}/2
.
Lastly,
h^{p,0}(H^{2k}(\operatorname{Gr}(k,k+2),\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}))=h^{p,0}(H^{2k-1}(\partial_{a}\mathcal{A}_{n,k,2}))
as asserted above. This finishes the proof.

To conclude the section, we show that curves

stemming from generic intersections of Schubert hyperplane sections, as in Lemma

, are entirely residual, that is to say, they are contained in the residual arrangement

of the amplituhedron

where the scaling of
\operatorname{adj}(P)
is chosen so that all its residues at the vertices of
P
evaluate to
\pm 1
. In particular, taking the residues of
\omega_{P}
along the facets of
P
recovers their canonical forms. Our code verifying this is available at
[
17
]
. By Remark
2.5
, the uniqueness of the canonical form follows from the fact that there are no non-zero global holomorphic top-degree differential forms on
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

Next, let us show that the genus of the pair
(\mathbb{P}^{3},\partial_{a}P)
equals one.
First, notice that we constructed the example in such a way that
\partial_{a}P
is a simple normal crossing divisor in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.
Therefore, by
[
4
, Corollary 3.14]
the genus of the pair
(\mathbb{P}^{3},\partial_{a}P)
is bounded from above by the sum of the genera of the intersections of the irreducible components of
\partial_{a}P
.
Since all boundary divisors are smooth projective varieties (which have vanishing genus), the only non-zero genus contribution stems from two-fold intersections.
As described above, we have a single elliptic curve
S\cap L_{0}
of genus one, and a collection of reducible cubic curves and lines, all of which have vanishing genus.
Therefore,
g(\mathbb{P}^{3},\partial_{a}P)\leq 1
.
It remains to show that this bound is tight.
Let
D=\bigcup_{i=0}^{5}D_{i}=\partial_{a}P
, with
D_{5}=S
and
D_{i}=L_{i}
for
i=0,1,2,3,4
.
Consider the spectral sequence converging to relative cohomology, see e.g.
[
4
, Equation (12)]
:

E^{p,q}_{1}=\bigoplus_{|I|=p}H^{q}(D_{I})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad H^{p+q}(\mathbb{P}^{3},D),

where
I\subset\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}
.
Since
D
is a normal crossing divisor, this sequence degenerates on the second page, that is all higher differentials between the pages vanish, i.e.
\partial_{i}=0
for
i\geq 2
.
For
\ell\notin I
, the obvious inclusion maps
D_{I\cup\ell}\rightarrow D_{I}
induce morphisms in cohomology
\delta^{j}_{i}:\bigoplus_{|I|=i}H^{j}(D_{I})\rightarrow\bigoplus_{|J|=i+1}H^{j}(D_{J})
.
Therefore, the weight
j
part of
H^{i+j}(\mathbb{P}^{3},D)
is computed by
\ker\delta^{j}_{i}/\operatorname{im}\delta^{j}_{i-1}
.
We now show that the weight one part of
H^{3}(\mathbb{P}^{3},D)
is at least one-dimensional. So let
j=1
, and thus
i=2
.
Note the
\ker\delta_{1}^{2}=H^{1}(S\cap L_{0})\oplus\bigoplus_{1\leq i<j\leq 4}H^{1}(L_{i}\cap L_{j})
, as the codomain of
\delta_{1}^{2}
is the first cohomology group of a collection of points, which vanishes trivially.
Similarly, the domain of
\delta^{1}_{1}
is the direct sum of the first cohomology groups of the Del Pezzo surface
S
and the five planes
L_{i}
.
Since all of these are smooth varieties, their first cohomology vanishes.
We have
\operatorname{im}\delta^{1}_{1}=0
.
Using the upper bound for the genus of
(\mathbb{P}^{3},D)
from above, we get that

\displaystyle\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{1}(H^{3}(\mathbb{P}^{3},D))\cong\frac{\ker\delta^{1}_{2}}{\operatorname{im}\delta^{1}_{1}}\cong H^{1}(E),

(6.11)

where
E
is the elliptic curve obtained obtained by intersecting
S\cap L_{0}
.
This shows that
g(\mathbb{P}^{3},D)=g(E)=1
.

Moreover, the argument above implies that the the map
R:\Omega^{3}_{\log}(\mathbb{P}^{3}\setminus\partial_{a}P)\rightarrow\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{0}H_{3}(\mathbb{P}^{3},\partial_{a}P)
, see
[
4
, Definition 2.4]
, has a one-dimensional kernel.
We can see that the kernel comes from the genus one curve
E=S\cap L_{0}
in the residual arrangement of
P
as follows.
Consider the
3
-form
\kappa\in\Omega^{3}_{\log}(\mathbb{P}^{3}\setminus\partial_{a}P)
given as

\kappa=\frac{1}{SL_{0}}du_{0}\wedge du_{1}\wedge du_{2},

with a local system of parameters on
\mathbb{P}^{3}\setminus\partial_{a}P
given by
u_{0},u_{1}
and
u_{2}
.
By
[
4
, Equation (21)]
we identify
\Omega^{3}_{\log}(\mathbb{P}^{3}\setminus\partial_{a}P)\cong F^{3}H^{3}(\mathbb{P}^{3}\setminus\partial_{a}P)
, whose weight one part, see Equation (
6.11
), is then given by
\kappa
under this identification.
Then,
R(\kappa)=0
, as the codomain of
R
is
\operatorname{gr}^{W}_{0}H_{3}(P,\partial_{a}P)
, and since the kernel of
R
is one-dimensional,
\kappa
is a generator of it.

In
[
4
, Section 2.7]
the authors suggest to consider the one-dimensional space
\omega_{P}+\lambda\kappa
with
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
as the space of candidate canonical forms for
P
.
However, for any
\lambda\neq 0
the residue of the form
\omega_{P}+\lambda\kappa
along the linear divisor
L_{0}
has a pole along the residual elliptic curve
E=S\cap L_{0}
and thus does not recover the canonical form of the facet of
P
contained in
L_{0}
.
Therefore, we must have
\lambda=0
, and thus recover the unique canonical form
\omega_{P}
.
