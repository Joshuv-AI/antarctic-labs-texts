Complexity of Abduction in Łukasiewicz Logic
Katsumi Inoue, Daniil Kozhemiachenko

Abstract
We explore the problem of explaining observations in contexts involving statements with truth degrees such as ‘the lift is loaded’, ‘the symptoms are severe’, etc. To formalise these contexts, we consider infinitely-valued Łukasiewicz fuzzy logic Ł . We define and motivate the notions of abduction problems and explanations in the language of Ł expanded with ‘interval literals’ of the form p ≥ 𝐜 p\geq\mathbf{c} , p ≤ 𝐜 p\leq\mathbf{c} , and their negations that express the set of values a variable can have. We analyse the complexity of standard abductive reasoning tasks (solution recognition, solution existence, and relevance / necessity of hypotheses) in Ł for the case of the full language and for the case of theories containing only disjunctive clauses and show that in contrast to classical propositional logic, the abduction in the clausal fragment has lower complexity than in the general case.



1
Introduction

Abduction, deduction, and induction are three main forms of reasoning
[
\citeauthoryearFlach and Kakas2000
]
. Abduction (finding explanations) has multiple applications in artificial intelligence, such as diagnosis
[
\citeauthoryearEl Ayeb, Marquis, and Rusinowitch1993
,
\citeauthoryearJosephson and Josephson2009
,
\citeauthoryearKoitz-Hristov and Wotawa2018
]
, commonsense reasoning
[
\citeauthoryearPaul1993
,
\citeauthoryearBhagavatula et al.2020
]
, formalisation of scientific reasoning
[
\citeauthoryearMagnani2011
]
, and machine learning
[
\citeauthoryearDai et al.2019
]
. In
logic-based abduction

[
\citeauthoryearEiter and Gottlob1995
]
, the reasoning task is to find an explanation for an observation
\chi
from a theory
\Gamma
, i.e., a formula
\phi
s.t.
\Gamma,\phi\models\chi
but
\Gamma,\phi\not\models\bot
(i.e.,
\Gamma
and
\phi
should
consistently entail

\chi
).

Observe, however, that in many applications, one needs not only to state whether a formula is true but also to specify
to which degree
it holds. E.g., a lift may be safe to use only when loaded to at least
5\%
of its maximal capacity (otherwise, its software will register it as empty) and to no more than
90\%
of the capacity. Or the cruising speed of a car can be defined as
60\ldots 90~\mathrm{kmh}
(with the maximal speed being
150~\mathrm{kmh}
). As classical logic has only two values, it is not well-suited to reason about contexts involving truth degrees. On the other hand,
fuzzy logics
evaluate formulas in the real-valued interval
[0,1]
and thus are much better suitable to formalising such contexts than classical logic. In particular, to formalise the examples above, we can set
v(l)\geq 0.05
and
v(s)\in[0.4,0.6]
where the truth degrees of
l
and
s
denote, respectively, the load of the lift and the speed of the car w.r.t. its maximal speed.

Fuzzy Logic

Originally
[
\citeauthoryearZadeh1965
,
\citeauthoryearZadeh1975
]
fuzzy logics were introduced to reason about imprecise statements such as ‘it is cold outside’, ‘the symptoms are severe’, etc. The values between
[0,1]
are interpreted as degrees of truth from
0
(absolutely false) to
1
(absolutely true). Fuzzy logic has also been applied to reasoning about uncertainty (cf., e.g.,
[
\citeauthoryearHájek and Tulipani2001
]
and
[
\citeauthoryearBaldi, Cintula, and Noguera2020
]
) and beliefs
[
\citeauthoryearRodriguez et al.2022
]
. In knowledge representation and reasoning, fuzzy logics have found multiple applications in representing graded and fuzzy ontologies
[
\citeauthoryearStraccia2016
]
. In such ontologies, concept assertions and terminological axioms have degrees of truth. Moreover, fuzzy versions of description logics and their computational properties have been extensively investigated
[
\citeauthoryearBorgwardt and Peñaloza2012
,
\citeauthoryearBorgwardt2014
,
\citeauthoryearBorgwardt, Distel, and Peñaloza2014
,
\citeauthoryearBorgwardt and Peñaloza2017
]
.

In addition to that, fuzzy logic has found multiple applications in artificial intelligence. Recent work on machine learning tries to combine perception by deep learning and symbolic knowledge representation. Neurosymbolic frameworks such as
[
\citeauthoryearDiligenti, Gori, and Saccà2017
,
\citeauthoryearBadreddine et al.2022
]
adopt semantics of fuzzy logic to support learning and reasoning in real-world domains.
[
\citeauthoryearKrieken, Acar, and Harmelen2022
]
analyse how different fuzzy logic semantics affect the behaviour of learning. Fuzzy logic has also been used in reasoning problems with knowledge graphs
[
\citeauthoryearChen, Hu, and Sun2022
]
and MaxSAT
[
\citeauthoryearHaniková, Manyà, and Vidal2023
]
. Furthermore, t-norms (functions used to interpret conjunctions in fuzzy logic) are applied for autonomous driving with requirements
[
\citeauthoryearStoian, Giunchiglia, and Lukasiewicz2023
]
.

Abduction in Fuzzy Logic

Abduction in different systems of fuzzy logic has long attracted attention. To the best of our knowledge, it was first presented by
[
\citeauthoryearYamada and Mukaidono1995
]
. There, the authors formalised abduction problems in the infinitely-valued Łukasiewicz fuzzy logic
Ł
and proposed explanations in the form of
fuzzy sets
, i.e., assignments of values from
[0,1]
to propositional variables. This approach was further expanded by
[
\citeauthoryearVojtáš1999
]
to Gödel and Product fuzzy logics. Solutions to abduction problems in multiple fuzzy logics were further systematised by
[
\citeauthoryeard’Allonnes, Akdag, and Bouchon-Meunier2007
]
and
[
\citeauthoryearChakraborty et al.2013
]
. Abduction in fuzzy logic has found multiple applications, in particular, diagnosis
[
\citeauthoryearMiyata, Furuhashi, and Uchikawa1998
]
, machine learning
[
\citeauthoryearBergadano, Cutello, and Gunetti2000
]
, fuzzy logic programming
[
\citeauthoryearVojtáš2001
,
\citeauthoryearEbrahim2001
]
, decision-making and learning in the presence of incomplete information
[
\citeauthoryearMellouli and Bouchon-Meunier2003
,
\citeauthoryearTsypyschev2017
]
, and robot perception
[
\citeauthoryearShanahan2005
]
.

Contributions

Still, the complexity of fuzzy abduction largely remains unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, the only discussion is given by
[
\citeauthoryearVojtáš1999
]
. There, the author explores fuzzy abduction in definite logic programmes (i.e., sets of rules of the form
\langle B\leftarrow A_{1},\ldots,A_{n},x\rangle
where each
A_{i}
is an atom and
x
belongs to the real-valued interval
[0,1]
). In particular, the author claims (cf.
[
\citeauthoryearVojtáš1999
, §4.4]
for details) that ‘[a]s linear programming is lying in
\mathsf{NP}
complexity class (even much lower) as prolog does, to find minimal solutions for a definite [fuzzy logic programming abduction problem] …does not increase the complexity and remains in
\mathsf{NP}
’. Thus, there seems to be no formal study of the complexity of abduction in fuzzy logic.

In this paper, we make a step towards a study of the complexity of abduction in fuzzy logic. We concentrate on the Łukasiewicz logic as it is one of the most expressive ones. In particular, it can express rational numbers and continuous linear functions over
[0,1]
(cf.
[
\citeauthoryearMcNaughton1951
]
for details). In addition, it still retains many classical relations between its conjunction, disjunction, implication, and negation. We formalise abduction in
Ł
and explore its computational properties. Our contribution is twofold. First, we propose and motivate a new form of solutions —
interval terms
— that allow to express intervals of values a variable is permitted to have. Second, we establish an (almost) complete characterisation of the complexity of standard reasoning problems (solution recognition, solution existence, relevance and necessity of hypotheses) for the case of theories containing arbitrary formulas and those comprised of disjunctive clauses.

Plan of the Paper

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we present the Łukasiewicz logic. In Section
3
, we propose and motivate interval terms that we will use as solutions to Łukasiewicz abduction problems. Sections
4
and
5
are dedicated to the study of the complexity of abductive reasoning in the Łukasiewicz logic and its clausal fragment. Finally, we summarise our results and provide a plan for future work in Section
6
. Due to limited space, some proofs are put into the appendix of the full version
[
\citeauthoryearInoue and Kozhemiachenko2025
]
.

2
Łukasiewicz Logic

We begin with the language of Łukasiewicz logic (
Ł
). We fix a countable set
\mathsf{Pr}
of propositional variables and define
\mathcal{L}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
via the following grammar.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{{\textsf{\L}}}\ni\phi

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\in\mathsf{Pr}\mid\neg\phi\mid(\phi\odot\phi)\mid(\phi\oplus\phi)\mid(\phi\!\rightarrow\!\phi)

Convention 1

(Notation)
.

We use the following shorthands:

\displaystyle\top

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\oplus\neg p

\displaystyle\bot

\displaystyle\coloneqq p\odot\neg p

\displaystyle\phi\!\leftrightarrow\!\chi

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\phi\!\rightarrow\!\chi)\!\odot\!(\chi\!\rightarrow\!\phi)

For a set of formulas
\Gamma
and a formula
\phi
, we write
\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)
and
\mathsf{Pr}[\Gamma]
to denote the set of all variables occurring in
\phi
and
\Gamma
, respectively.

We use
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{Q}
to denote the sets of real and rational numbers, respectively. When dealing with intervals, square brackets mean that the endpoint is included in the interval, and round brackets that it is excluded. Lower index
Q
means that the interval contains rational numbers only. E.g.,

\displaystyle[\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}]

\displaystyle=\{x\mid x\in\mathbb{R},x\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},x\leq\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}\}

\displaystyle(\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}]_{\mathbb{Q}}

\displaystyle=\{x\mid x\in\mathbb{Q},x>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},x\leq\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}\}

The semantics of
Ł
is given in the next definition.

Definition 1

(Semantics of Łukasiewicz logic)
.

An
Ł
-valuation
is a function
v:\mathsf{Pr}\rightarrow[0,1]
extended to the complex formulas as follows:

\displaystyle v(\neg\phi)

\displaystyle=1-v(\phi)

\displaystyle v(\phi\odot\chi)

\displaystyle=\max(0,v(\phi)+v(\chi)-1)

\displaystyle v(\phi\oplus\chi)

\displaystyle=\min(1,v(\phi)+v(\chi))

\displaystyle v(\phi\!\rightarrow\!\chi)

\displaystyle=\min(1,1-v(\phi)+v(\chi))

We say that
\phi\in\mathcal{L}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
is
Ł
-valid (
{{\textsf{\L}}}\models\phi
)
if
v(\phi)=1
for every
Ł
-valuation
v
;
\phi
is
Ł
-satisfiable
if
v(\phi)=1
for some
Ł
-valuation
v
.

We define two notions of equivalence —
strong equivalence (
\phi\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi
)
and
weak equivalence (
\phi\simeq_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi
)
:

\displaystyle\phi\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\forall v:v(\phi)=v(\chi)

\displaystyle\phi\simeq_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\forall v:v(\phi)=1\leftrightharpoons v(\chi)=1

Given a finite
\Gamma\!\subset\!\mathcal{L}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
,
\Gamma
entails
\chi
in
Ł
(
\Gamma\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi
)
iff
v(\chi)\!=\!1
in every
v
s.t.
v(\phi)\!=\!1
for all
\phi\!\in\!\Gamma
,
and that
\Gamma
consistently entails
\chi
in
Ł
(
\Gamma\!\models^{\mathsf{cons}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}\!\chi
)
iff
\Gamma\not\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\bot
and
\Gamma\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi
.

We note some important semantical properties of
Ł
. First, every connective
behaves classically on
\{0,1\}
: in particular,
\oplus
behaves like disjunction and
\odot
like conjunction. Thus, we will call
\oplus

strong disjunction
and
\odot

strong conjunction
.

Second,
deduction theorem does not hold for
\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}
: indeed, while
p\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}p\odot p
, it is easy to show that
{{\textsf{\L}}}\not\models p\rightarrow(p\odot p)
by setting
v(p)=\frac{1}{2}
. Third,
\odot
and
\oplus

are not idempotent
: setting
v(p)\!=\!\frac{1}{2}
, we have that
v(p\!\odot\!p)\!=\!0
and
v(p\!\oplus\!p)\!=\!1
.

Still,
\neg
,
\odot
,
\oplus
, and
\rightarrow
interact in an expected manner. In particular, it is easy to check that
v(\top)=1
and
v(\bot)=0
for every valuation and that the following pairs of formulas are indeed
strongly equivalent
:

\displaystyle\neg(\phi\odot\chi)

\displaystyle\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\neg\phi\oplus\neg\chi

\displaystyle\neg(\phi\oplus\chi)

\displaystyle\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\neg\phi\odot\neg\chi

\displaystyle\neg(\phi\!\rightarrow\!\chi)

\displaystyle\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\phi\odot\neg\chi

\displaystyle\neg\phi\oplus\chi

\displaystyle\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\phi\rightarrow\chi

(1)

\displaystyle\neg\neg\phi

\displaystyle\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\phi

\displaystyle(\phi\odot\chi)\!\rightarrow\!\psi

\displaystyle\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\phi\!\rightarrow\!(\chi\!\rightarrow\!\psi)

It is also important to observe that
weak
conjunction (
\wedge
) and disjunction (
\vee
) are definable as follows:

\displaystyle\phi\vee\chi

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\phi\rightarrow\chi)\rightarrow\chi

\displaystyle\phi\wedge\chi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\neg(\neg\phi\vee\neg\chi)

(2)

Using Definition
1
, one can recover semantics of
\wedge
and
\vee
:

\displaystyle v(\phi\!\wedge\!\chi)

\displaystyle=\min(v(\phi),v(\chi))

\displaystyle v(\phi\!\vee\!\chi)

\displaystyle=\max(v(\phi),v(\chi))

In what follows, we will write
\Gamma,\phi\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi
as a shorthand for
\Gamma\cup\{\phi\}\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi
. Similarly, if the set of premises is given explicitly, we omit brackets. E.g.,
\phi,\chi\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\psi
stands for
\{\phi,\chi\}\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\psi
. Note, furthermore, that the comma in the set of premises can be equivalently interpreted as
\wedge
and
\odot
as
(\phi\wedge\chi)\simeq_{{\textsf{\L}}}(\phi\odot\chi)
. Hence, for
\Gamma=\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\Gamma\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\phi_{i}\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi\text{ iff }\bigwedge^{n}_{i=1}\phi_{i}\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi

We finish the section by recalling the complexity of
Ł
. It is known
[
\citeauthoryearMundici1987
, Theorem 3.4]
that satisfiability of arbitrary formulas in
Ł
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete while validity and entailment are
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete
[
\citeauthoryearHaniková2011
, Corollary 4.1.3]
just as in classical propositional logic (
\mathsf{CPL}
).

Proposition 1
.

1.

Ł
-satisfiability is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

2.

Entailment in
Ł
is
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete.

3
Interval Terms

Before proceeding to the formal presentation of abduction, let us introduce the type of terms that we will be using in solutions. Traditionally, the form of solutions is restricted to conjunctions of literals (
terms
). In this case, a solution corresponds to a statement of facts. Moreover, in this case, the logically weakest solutions are the subset-minimal ones. We begin with Łukasiewicz counterparts of classical terms and clauses.

Definition 2

(Simple literals, clauses, and terms)
.

•

A
simple literal
is a propositonal variable or its negation.

•

A
simple clause
is a
strong disjunction
of simple literals, i.e., a formula of the form
\bigoplus^{n}_{i=1}l_{i}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

A
simple term
is a
strong conjunction
of simple literals, i.e., a formula of the form
\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}l_{i}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

One can observe, however, that
simple terms
from Definition
2
are too restrictive if we want to use them as solutions for abduction problems. Indeed, a simple term
\tau
has value
1
iff all its literals have value
1
. But in a context with fuzzy propositions, we might need to express statements such as ‘
p
has value
\frac{2}{3}
’ or ‘
q
has value at least
\frac{1}{4}
’. The following example illustrates this situation.

Example 1
.

Assume that we have a lift with a weight sensor that controls two indicators: green and blue. The green indicator is on when the weight sensor detects the load of
at least
\frac{1}{4}
of its maximal capacity. Blue light is on when the lift is loaded to
at most
\frac{2}{3}
of its maximal capacity. We see that both indicators are lit.

Let us now formalise this problem in
Ł
. We interpret the value of
c
as the percentage of capacity to which the lift is loaded and translate the condition ‘the lift is loaded to at least
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
of its capacity’ as
c\oplus c\oplus c\oplus c
. Observe that
v(c\oplus c\oplus c\oplus c)=1
iff
v(c)\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
. To represent the other condition ‘the lift is loaded to at most
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
of its capacity’, we write
\neg c\oplus\neg c\oplus\neg c
.
We have that
v(\neg c\oplus\neg c\oplus\neg c)=1
iff
v(\neg c)\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}
, i.e., iff
v(c)\leq\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
. Finally, we use
g
and
b
to represent that the green and blue lights are on. We obtain the following theory
\Gamma_{\mathsf{lift}}
and observation
\chi_{\mathsf{lift}}
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\mathsf{lift}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{matrix}(c\oplus c\oplus c\oplus c)\leftrightarrow g,(\neg c\oplus\neg c\oplus\neg c)\leftrightarrow b\end{matrix}\right\}

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathsf{lift}}

\displaystyle=g\odot b

To explain why both indicators are on, we need to present a formula
\phi
s.t.
\Gamma_{\mathsf{lift}},\phi\models^{\mathsf{cons}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}g\odot b
. For this, we need that
v(\neg c\oplus\neg c\oplus\neg c)=1
and
v(c\oplus c\oplus c\oplus c)=1
in every valuation that makes
\Gamma_{\mathsf{lift}}
true. As we noted above, this requires that
v(c)\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{2}{3}]
.
On the other hand, a simple term
\tau
has value
1
iff all its literals have value
1
(i.e., all variables in
\tau
should have value
0
or
1
). Thus, there is no simple term
\tau
containing
c
or
\neg c
s.t.
\Gamma_{\mathsf{lift}},\!\tau\!\models^{\mathsf{cons}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}\!\chi
. Hence
\phi
cannot be a simple term.

One way to circumvent this problem is to adopt the proposal of
[
\citeauthoryearYamada and Mukaidono1995
]
and
[
\citeauthoryearVojtáš1999
]
and define solutions to fuzzy abduction problems as sets of assignments of values to propositional variables. This approach, however, has a drawback. In this setting, one can only express exact values of variables but not
intervals
of their values. Now, observe from Example
1
that any assignment of a value from
[\frac{1}{4},\frac{2}{3}]
to
c
solves
\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{lift}},\chi_{\mathsf{lift}}\rangle
. Thus, in the general case, it is impossible to generate the set of all solutions as there are infinitely many of them. Moreover, it may be problematic to choose between different values.

In this section, we propose an alternative. For that, we define terms that allow us to express both exact values of variables and their intervals and compare different solutions w.r.t. entailment in
Ł
. Moreover, as we will see in Section
4
, every abduction problem will have only finitely many solutions in our setting.

Definition 3

(Rational interval literals, terms, and clauses)
.

Let
p\!\in\!\mathsf{Pr}
,
\lozenge\!\in\!\{\leq,\geq,<,>\}
, and
\mathbf{c}\in[0,1]_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

•

A
rational interval literal
has the form
p\lozenge\mathbf{c}
or
\neg(p\lozenge\mathbf{c})
. The semantics of rational interval literals is as follows:

\displaystyle v(p\lozenge\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }v(p)\lozenge\mathbf{c}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle v(\neg(p\lozenge\mathbf{c}))

\displaystyle=1-v(p\lozenge\mathbf{c})

For a rational interval literal
p\lozenge\mathbf{c}
, we call
\mathbf{c}
its
boundary value
and call the set
\{v(p)\mid v(p\lozenge\mathbf{c})=1\}
its
permitted values
.

•

A
rational interval term
has the form
\bigodot^{n}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}
with each
\lambda_{i}
being an interval literal.

•

A
rational interval clause
has the form
\bigoplus^{n}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}
with each
\lambda_{i}
being an interval literal.

Convention 2
.

We use
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
to denote the language obtained from
\mathcal{L}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
by expanding it with rational interval literals. We will mostly write ‘interval literals (terms, clauses)’ instead of ‘rational interval literals (terms, clauses)’. The notions of
Ł
-validity, satisfiability and entailment are preserved from Definition
1
. We will also utilise notation from Convention
1
for
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
. Additionally, given an interval literal
p\lozenge\mathbf{c}
, we use
p\blacklozenge\mathbf{c}
to denote
\neg(p\lozenge\mathbf{c})
. Finally, given a literal
\lambda
and a term or clause
\varrho
, we write
\lambda\in\varrho
to designate that
\lambda
occurs in
\varrho
.

We observe that it follows from (
1
) that interval clauses and terms are dual in the following sense:

\displaystyle\neg\bigodot\limits^{n}_{i=1}(p_{i}\lozenge\mathbf{c}_{i})\!\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\!\bigoplus\limits^{n}_{i=1}(p_{i}\blacklozenge\mathbf{c}_{i})

\displaystyle\neg\bigoplus\limits^{n}_{i=1}(p_{i}\lozenge\mathbf{c}_{i})\!\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\!\bigodot\limits^{n}_{i=1}(p_{i}\blacklozenge\mathbf{c}_{i})

(3)

Remark 1
.

From Definition
3
, it is clear that interval terms generalise simple terms in the following sense: for every simple term
\tau
, there is an interval term
\tau^{\lozenge}
s.t.
\tau\simeq_{{\textsf{\L}}}\tau^{\lozenge}
. Indeed, given
\tau=\bigodot^{m}_{i=1}p_{i}\odot\bigodot^{n}_{j=1}\neg q_{j}
, we can define
\tau^{\lozenge}\!=\!\bigodot^{m}_{i=1}(p_{i}\!\geq\!\mathbf{1})\!\odot\!\bigodot^{n}_{j=1}(q_{j}\!\leq\!\mathbf{0})
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle p\leq\mathbf{c}\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}p\leq\mathbf{c}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{c}\leq\mathbf{c}^{\prime}

\displaystyle p\geq\mathbf{c}\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}p\geq\mathbf{c}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{c}\geq\mathbf{c}^{\prime}

and similarly for the literals of the form
p<\mathbf{c}
and
p>\mathbf{c}
.

The idea of interval terms comes from a logic first introduced by
[
\citeauthoryearPavelka1979a
,
\citeauthoryearPavelka1979b
,
\citeauthoryearPavelka1979c
]
as an extension of
Ł
with constants for every
real number
. It turns out, however, (cf.
[
\citeauthoryearHájek1998
, §3.3]
for details) that if one adds constants only for
rational
numbers, the resulting logic (‘Rational Pavelka logic’ or
\mathsf{RPL}
) will have the same expressivity as the original one. To simulate the two-valued behaviour of interval terms, one also needs to introduce the ‘Delta operator’
\triangle
proposed by
[
\citeauthoryearBaaz1996
]
with the following semantics:
v(\triangle\phi)=1
if
v(\phi)=1
and
v(\triangle\phi)=0
, otherwise. Now, using the following equivalences

\displaystyle p\leq\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\triangle(p\rightarrow\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle p\geq\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\triangle(\mathbf{c}\rightarrow p)

\displaystyle p<\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\neg\triangle(\mathbf{c}\rightarrow p)

\displaystyle p>\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle\equiv_{{\textsf{\L}}}\neg\triangle(p\rightarrow\mathbf{c})

and expanding the constraint tableaux calculus of
[
\citeauthoryearHähnle1999
]
with rules for
\triangle
and rational constants, we have that the satisfiability and validity of
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
-formulas have the same complexity as those of
Ł
.

Proposition 2
.

1.

Ł
-satisfiability of
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
-formulas is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

2.

Entailment in
Ł
of
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
-formulas is
\mathsf{coNP}
-complete.

We note briefly that interval terms allow us to express not only the values of variables but of arbitrary formulas as well. Given
\phi\!\in\!\mathcal{L}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
and
p\!\in\!\mathsf{Pr}
s.t.
p\!\notin\!\mathsf{Pr}(\phi)
,

4
Abduction in Łukasiewicz Logic

Let us now present abduction in
Ł
. Our idea is to use interval terms as solutions to problems
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\chi,{\mathsf{H}}\rangle
. Here,
{\mathsf{H}}
is the set of hypotheses (interval literals) that one can use to build solutions. One can restrict it in two ways. First, one may allow
arbitrary
interval literals over a given
finite
set of variables. Second, one can explicitly define a finite set of interval literals. We choose the second option, as the first one leads to
infinite
sets of solutions (cf. Example

Definition 4

(
Ł
-abduction problems and solutions)
.

•

An
Ł
-abduction problem
is a tuple
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\chi,{\mathsf{H}}\rangle

\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}

,
\chi

observation
, and members of

•

An
Ł
-solution
of
\mathbb{P}
is an
interval term

\tau

\Gamma,\tau\models^{\mathsf{cons}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi
.

•

A solution is
proper

\tau\not\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\chi
.

•

A proper solution
\tau

\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}
-minimal (entailment-minimal)
if there is no proper solution
\sigma

\tau\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\sigma

A proper solution

Convention 3
.

Given an abduction problem
\mathbb{P}
, we will use
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{P})
,
\mathcal{S}^{\mathsf{p}}(\mathbb{P})
,
\mathcal{S}^{\min}(\mathbb{P})

to denote the sets of all solutions, all proper solutions, all
\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}
-minimal solutions, and all theory-minimal solutions of
\mathbb{P}
, respectively.

In the definition above, it is evident that there are finitely many (at most exponentially many in the size of
{\mathsf{H}}
) solutions for each abduction problem. We also present two notions of minimal solutions. Entailment-minimality corresponds to
subset-minimality
by
[
\citeauthoryearEiter and Gottlob1995
]
in the setting of Łukasiewicz logic.
Theory-minimal solutions correspond to

[
\citeauthoryearStickel1990

\citeauthoryearSakama and Inoue1995

[
\citeauthoryearPoole1989

by
[
\citeauthoryearMarquis1995
]
.

In addition, it is easy to see that even though a theory-minimal solution is entailment-minimal, the converse is not always the case. Indeed, let
\mathbb{P}=\langle\{p\vee q,r\},q\wedge r\rangle

Let us now see how we can solve abduction problems using interval terms. Recall Example

Example 2

Conversely, let

5.1
Simple Clause Fragment

Let us now consider the complexity of abductive reasoning for the simple clause fragment of
Ł
. We begin with the definition of simple clause abduction problems.

Definition 7

(Simple clause abduction)
.

A
simple clause abduction problem
(SCA problem) is an
Ł
-abduction problem
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\chi,{\mathsf{H}}\rangle
s.t.
\Gamma
is a set of simple clauses and interval terms and
\chi
is an interval clause, simple clause, interval term, or a simple term.

In the definition above, note that
\Gamma
can contain interval terms. This makes it possible to express constraints on the values of variables. Similarly, different observations correspond to constraints that we explain based on the theory.

First, we show that recognition of arbitrary, proper, and
\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}
-minimal solutions is
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,\chi,{\mathsf{H}}\rangle
be an SCA problem, and
\sigma
an interval term. Then it is
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete to decide whether
\sigma
is an arbitrary, proper, or
\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}
-minimal solution.

Proof sketch.

For the membership, we provide a sketch of the proof for the case of
\chi
being an interval literal. Other cases can be dealt with in a similar manner. Observe from Definitions
1
and
3
that if
\kappa\!=\!\bigoplus^{m}_{i=1}p_{i}\oplus\bigoplus^{n}_{j=1}\!\!\neg q_{j}
is an interval clause and
\tau\!=\!\bigodot^{r}_{i=1}(s_{i}\lozenge\mathbf{c}_{i})
an interval term, then
v(\kappa)=1
iff
\sum^{m}_{i=1}v(p_{i})+\sum^{n}_{j=1}(1-v(q_{j}))\!\geq\!1
and
v(\tau)\!=\!1
iff
v(s_{i})\lozenge c_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
.
It can now be easily shown that the satisfiability of sets of clauses and interval terms can be reduced to solving systems of linear inequalities over
[0,1]
. Similarly, entailment of an interval literal from a set
\Gamma
containing simple clauses and interval terms can be reduced to verifying that a system of linear inequalities
does not
have a solution over
[0,1]
. As both tasks can be done in polynomial time, arbitrary and proper solutions can be recognised in polynomial time. Finally, consider determining whether
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}^{\min}(\mathbb{P})
. From Theorem
3
, we have that given
\sigma
, we only need to check polynomially many solution candidates, each of which requires polynomial time.

Let us now tackle the hardness. We provide a logspace reduction from (proper, entailment-minimal)
solution recognition for classical Horn theories
, which is
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete. Let
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma,p,{\mathsf{H}}\rangle
be a classical Horn abduction problem and
p\notin{\mathsf{H}}
, clauses in
\Gamma
be finite sets of literals, and
\tau\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{P})
. I.e.,
\Gamma\cup\{\tau\}
is
\mathsf{CPL}
-satisfiable, and there is a unit resolution inference of
\{p\}
from
\Gamma\cup\{\tau\}
(i.e., in each application of the resolution rule, at least one premise is a unit clause). Let
\Gamma^{\oplus}
be the result of replacing each clause
\{l_{1},\ldots,l_{k}\}
in
\Gamma
with
l_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus l_{k}
;
\tau^{\lozenge{{\textsf{\L}}}}
be the result of replacing
\wedge
with
\odot
, positive literals
r
in
\tau
with
r\!\geq\!\mathbf{1}
, and negative literals
\neg q
with
q\leq\mathbf{0}
; and
{\mathsf{H}}^{{\textsf{\L}}}
be the result of replacing
r
in
{\mathsf{H}}
with
r\!\geq\mathbf{1}
, and
\neg q
with
q\!\leq\!\mathbf{0}
. The size of
\mathbb{P}^{\oplus}=\langle\Gamma^{\oplus},p,{\mathsf{H}}^{{\textsf{\L}}}\rangle
is linear in the size of
\mathbb{P}
, and
\Gamma^{\oplus}\cup\{\tau^{\lozenge{{\textsf{\L}}}}\}
is
Ł
-satisfiable because
\Gamma\cup\{\tau\}
is
\mathsf{CPL}
-satisfiable. Furthermore, unit resolution is
Ł
-sound: if
v(l_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus l_{k})=1
and
v(l_{1})=0
, then
v(l_{2}\oplus\ldots\oplus l_{k})=1
. Hence, reusing the inference in
Ł
, we have
\Gamma^{\oplus}\cup\{\tau^{\lozenge{{\textsf{\L}}}}\}\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}p
. Thus,
\tau^{\lozenge{{\textsf{\L}}}}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{P}^{\oplus})
. Conversely, let
\tau
solve
\mathbb{P}^{\oplus}
, i.e.,
\Gamma^{\oplus}\cup\{\tau^{\lozenge{{\textsf{\L}}}}\}\models^{\mathsf{cons}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}p
. Let
\tau_{\mathsf{cl}}
be obtained from
\tau
by replacing
\odot
with
\wedge
,
r\!\geq\!\mathbf{1}
with
r
, and
q\!\leq\!\mathbf{0}
with
\neg q
. Clearly,
\Gamma,\tau_{\mathsf{cl}}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}p
. Assume for contradiction that
\Gamma,\tau_{\mathsf{cl}}\models_{\mathsf{CPL}}\bot
. Then there is a classical unit resolution inference of the empty clause from
\Gamma\cup\{\tau_{\mathsf{cl}}\}
. As it is sound in
Ł
,
\Gamma,\tau_{\mathsf{cl}}\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}\bot
. Contradiction.

Observe that all solutions of
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\oplus}

are proper
because
p
does not occur in them. Thus, our reduction can be used to show that determining the existence of proper solutions is
{\mathsf{P}}
-hard as well. For the
{\mathsf{P}}
-hardness of
\models_{{\textsf{\L}}}
-minimal solution recognition, one can check that if
\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\min}(\mathbb{P})
, then
\tau^{\lozenge{{\textsf{\L}}}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\min}(\mathbb{P}^{\oplus})
. Conversely, if
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}^{\min}(\mathbb{P}^{\oplus})

\sigma_{\mathsf{cl}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\min}(\mathbb{P})
.

.

Proof.

The membership is immediate from Theorem

. For the hardness, we can reuse the reduction from classical Horn abduction given in Theorem

∎

It is

We finish the section with two remarks. First, the complexity of simple clause abduction coincides with that of
classical Horn abduction

[
\citeauthoryearEiter and Gottlob1995
,
\citeauthoryearCreignou and Zanuttini2006
]
. Thus, Łukasiewicz abduction with clausal theories is simpler than classical abduction over clausal theories. Second, one can easily see that simple clause abduction problems can be straightforwardly generalised to problems whose theories are
Łukasiewicz fuzzy logic programmes
as presented by
[
\citeauthoryearVojtáš1999
,
\citeauthoryearVojtáš2001
]

while preserving the complexity
.

Definition 8
.

A
Łukasiewicz fuzzy logic programme
(
Ł
-FLP) is a finite set
\Gamma_{\mathsf{P}}=\{\langle\kappa_{i},x_{i}\rangle\mid 1\!\leq\!i\!\leq\!n,x_{i}\!\in\!(0,1]_{\mathbb{Q}}\}
with
\kappa_{i}
’s being simple clauses written as
\bigodot^{m}_{i=1}l_{i}\rightarrow l
. Pairs

are called

. An
Ł
-valuation

satisfies
\Gamma_{\mathsf{P}}
if
v(\kappa_{i})=x_{i}
for every
\langle\kappa_{i},x_{i}\rangle\in\Gamma_{\mathsf{P}}
.

An
Ł
-FLP abduction problem
is a tuple
\mathbb{P}=\langle\Gamma_{\mathsf{P}},\chi,{\mathsf{H}}\rangle
with
\Gamma_{\mathsf{P}}
being an
Ł
-FLP,
\chi
a fuzzy rule or interval term, and
{\mathsf{H}}
a set of interval literals. A solution to
\mathbb{P}
is defined as in Definition

.

One can observe from Definition
7
that simple clause abduction problems are a particular case of
Ł
-FLP abduction problems (namely, when

for every

). Thus, the hardness results are preserved. For the membership, note that
Ł
-FLP abduction problems can be reduced to solving systems of linear inequalities in the same way as simple clause abduction problems.

5.2
Interval Clause Fragment

Results in Section
4
show that the complexity of
Ł
-abduction in the general case was not affected by whether we allow the use of interval literals
in theories or observations
(recall from Definition
4
that theories and observations are defined over
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\textsf{\L}}}
). Interval literals can also reduce the size and facilitate the understanding (for a human) of an abduction problem while preserving its solutions. E.g., the theory
\Gamma_{\mathsf{lift}}
of the problem
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{lift}}
from Examples
1
and
2
can be reformulated as follows:
\Gamma^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathsf{lift}}=\{(c\geq\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{4}})\leftrightarrow g,(c\leq\mathbf{\tfrac{2}{3}})\leftrightarrow b\}
. Observe that
(c\!\geq\!\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{4}})\!\leftrightarrow\!g
is weakly equivalent to
(c\!\oplus\!c\!\oplus\!c\!\oplus\!c)\!\leftrightarrow\!g
and
(c\!\leq\!\mathbf{\tfrac{2}{3}})\!\leftrightarrow\!b
to
(\neg c\!\oplus\!\neg c\!\oplus\!\neg c)\!\leftrightarrow\!b
.
So, for
\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathsf{lift}}=\langle\Gamma^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathsf{lift}},g\odot b,{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathsf{lift}}\rangle
, we have
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{lift}})=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{P}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mathsf{lift}})
(and likewise for other types of solutions).
Moreover, using interval literals in
\oplus
-clauses simplifies the presentation of relations between values. E.g., ‘if
v(p)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
, then
v(q)\!\leq\!\tfrac{3}{4}
’ can be put as
(p\!\geq\!\mathbf{\tfrac{1}{2}})\!\rightarrow\!(q\!\leq\!\mathbf{\tfrac{3}{4}})
.

Thus, one might wonder whether we can permit theories built from

(recall Definition
3
) and preserve the complexity bounds from Section

. As the next statements show, this is not generally the case.

.

Ł

\mathbb{P}
can be obtained from a solution for the

-abduction problem by a reverse replacement.
∎

6
Conclusion and Discussion

We studied abduction in Łukasiewicz logic and its clausal fragments. Our analysis gives an almost complete outline of the complexity of the main decision problems related to abduction (Tables
1
and
2
). We established that the complexity of abductive reasoning is never higher than that in
\mathsf{CPL}

[
\citeauthoryearEiter and Gottlob1995
]
,
[
\citeauthoryearCreignou and Zanuttini2006
]
,
[
\citeauthoryearPichler and Woltran2010
]
,
[
\citeauthoryearPfandler, Pichler, and Woltran2015
]
. Moreover, the complexity of
Ł
-abduction in clausal fragment is
lower
than that of the classical abductive reasoning as long as clauses do not contain interval literals.

Several questions remain open. First, we do not know the exact complexity of theory-minimal solution recognition and relevance in the full language nor its clausal fragments. One way to approach this would be to establish the complexity of the closely related notion of theory prime implicants in
\mathsf{CPL}
and its fragments
[
\citeauthoryearMarquis1995
]
.

It would also be instructive to consider abductive reasoning in fuzzy logics when the entailment is defined via the preservation of the
truth degree
from the premise to the conclusion as proposed by
[
\citeauthoryearBou et al.2009
,
\citeauthoryearErtola et al.2015
]
. Indeed, one might not be guaranteed that the statements in the theory are
absolutely true
. Still, in this case, it is reasonable to expect that the observation should be
at least as true
as the theory and the explanation.

Furthermore,
[
\citeauthoryearDubois and Prade1992
]
,
[
\citeauthoryearPoole1993
]
,
[
\citeauthoryearDubois, Gilio, and Kern-Isberner2008
]
, and
[
\citeauthoryearSato, Ishihata, and Inoue2011
]
have considered abduction in possibilistic and probabilistic contexts. It is also known from
[
\citeauthoryearHájek and Tulipani2001
,
\citeauthoryearBaldi, Cintula, and Noguera2020
]
that so-called ‘two-layered’ fuzzy logics can be adapted to reasoning about uncertainty. Thus, it makes sense to explore probabilistic abduction using two-layered logics.

We also note that the context from Example
1
can be represented in abductive constraint logic programming (ACLP) studied by
[
\citeauthoryearKakas, Michael, and Mourlas2000
]
. It thus makes sense to explore the connection between abduction in fuzzy logic and ACLP.

It is also known that machine learning for visual perception problems is related to abduction
[
\citeauthoryearShanahan2005
,
\citeauthoryearLiang et al.2022
]
. As fuzzy logic has also found numerous applications in visual perception, it makes sense to explore the applications of abduction in fuzzy logic in this field.

Finally, we recall that there are algorithms for solving fuzzy logic abduction problems with fuzzy sets or by specifying
exact
values of hypotheses. In our framework, however, a solution is an interval term corresponding to a set of
intervals
of permitted values of hypotheses. Thus, it would be instructive to devise algorithms for the generation of solutions in the form of interval terms. As satisfiability and validity in
Ł
are reducible to mixed-integer programming (MIP)
[
\citeauthoryearHähnle1992
,
\citeauthoryearHähnle1994
,
\citeauthoryearHähnle1999
]
, it would make sense to apply MIP solvers. Moreover, as the semantics of
Ł
can be given over
rational numbers

[
\citeauthoryearEsteva et al.2002
]
, one may try to apply
rational
MIP solvers such as the one by
[
\citeauthoryearCook et al.2013
]
as they produce
exact
solutions to mixed-integer problems.
