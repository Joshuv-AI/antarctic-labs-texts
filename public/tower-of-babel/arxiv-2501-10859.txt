Which price to pay? Auto-tuning building MPC controller for optimal economic cost
Jiarui Yu, Jicheng Shi, Wenjie Xu, Colin N. Jones

Abstract
Increasing types of price-based demand-side management (DSM) programs offered in the residential household market presents a challenge for consumers to select the most cost-effective option. In this paper, we aim to offer guidance to the consumers based on simulation statistics which considers the optimal costs of possible programs in the market and requirements from the consumers. Model predictive control (MPC) controller was considered for temperature management in buildings but its performance heavily depends on hyperparameters. Consequently, MPC necessitates meticulous hyperparameter tuning to attain optimal performance under diverse contracts. However, conventional building controller design is an open-loop process without critical hyperparameter optimization, often leading to suboptimal performance due to unexpected environmental disturbances and modeling errors. Furthermore, these hyperparameters are not adapted to different pricing schemes and may lead to non-economic operations. To address these issues, we propose an efficient performance-oriented building MPC controller tuning method based on a cutting-edge efficient constrained Bayesian optimization algorithm, CONFIG, with global optimality guarantees. We demonstrate that this technique can be applied to efficiently deal with real-world DSM program selection problems under customized black-box constraints and objectives. To substantiate the efficacy of the proposed approach, a case study was conducted on the building simulation platform, BOPTEST. In this study, a simple MPC controller, which offers the advantages of reduced commissioning costs, enhanced computational efficiency, was optimized to perform on a comparable level to a delicately designed and computationally expensive MPC controller. The results also indicate that with an optimized simple MPC, the monthly electricity cost of a household can be reduced by up to 26.90% compared with the cost when controlled by a basic rule-based controller under the same constraints. Then we compared 12 real electricity contracts in Belgium for a household family with customized black-box occupant comfort constraints. The results indicate a monthly electricity bill saving up to 20.18% when the most economic contract is compared with the worst one, which again illustrates the significance of choosing a proper electricity contract.

Methodology

3.1
Overview

To address the aforementioned performance disparity and guidance challenges in the contract selection, we propose a performance-oriented constrained black box optimization framework for tuning MPC hyperparameters in building control, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

First, historical data from a building controlled by a local controller is collected to identify a parametric building model. Notably, the local controller may be arbitrary and the collected data is used solely to model building dynamics, not to predict electricity usage patterns. Second, an MPC controller is formulated with an initially specified cost function (objective) and constraint functions, and then integrated into the building simulator. In this study, offline simulations are considered, with the online deployment reserved for future work. Third, using offline simulation data, the closed-loop performance of the MPC controller is assessed. Key metrics, such as total electricity bills and indoor thermal comfort, are fed into the CONFIG BO algorithm, which then determines new parameters to be applied to the offline simulation. This process is repeated, forming a closed-loop optimization cycle. Our offline simulation-based optimization approach can leverage digital twin technology
Grieves2017DigitalTM
, enabling detailed simulations and modeling that provide a reliable representation of real building behavior without disrupting or altering the physical system.

With this framework, the optimal hyperparameter set and the accordingly electricity cost of a certain billing contract can be obtained efficiently. Finally, by comparing the optimal results of various given contracts, we guide consumers in selecting the most economical contract and offer control recommendations tailored to each option.

3.2
CONFIG algorithm

Prior to controller deployment in buildings, the relationship between the hyperparameters and the objective along with constraints is not well understood in the context of optimizing hyperparameters for building control. Consequently, the optimization of parameters for building control presents a black box optimization challenge, which is commonly addressed through Bayesian Optimization (BO). BO is a method that considers the objective of an optimization problem and utilizes probabilistic models, i.e. Gaussian processes, as a surrogate function to efficiently search for the optimal solution within a complex and often expensive-to-evaluate parameter space. By intelligently selecting the subsequent set of parameters based on previous observations and specific acquisition functions, BO can substantially enhance performance with a few evaluations.

Under the framework of BO, CONFIG
xu2023constrained
outperforms other BO methods by optimistically balancing the exploration, exploitation, and violation. Consequently, it has proved a theoretical global convergence guarantee. The ability to model black box constraints contributes to satisfying various complex requirements from consumers that cannot be easily integrated into the controllers. And the global optimality ensures us to find the globally optimal combination of parameters and the least cost for a specific billing contract. In this way, CONFIG was chosen to be the optimization algorithm in this work.

4.2
Building modeling

To enhance the MPC controller performance, it is important that the building model is capable of accurately capturing the building dynamics. While there are various advanced building modeling techniques available, as discussed in the introduction section, we implemented a linear AutoRegressive model with eXogenous inputs (ARX) model for two primary reasons.

The first reason is that linear models show good expressive ability for buildings controlled by heat pumps
10089206
which is exactly what has been employed in this case study. In addition, considering the thermodynamics and the testing results of the BOPTEST
bestest_hydronic_heat_pump
test case, there indeed exists a linear relationship between the modulated heat pump input signal and the actual power used for heating. Furthermore, another study
Bunning2022-io
compared a physics-informed linear regression method with machine learning models on a real building application. The results show that the linear ARMAX model actually outperforms the ML methods. Besides the merit of good expressive ability, implementing a linear model also requires less amount of data, which would lead to lower commissioning costs as well, compared with a nonlinear model obtained by some advanced approaches. Indeed, with modern buildings becoming increasingly complex, there are limitations on model flexibility and accuracy in terms of linear models. However, it is time-consuming and relatively computationally expensive to build a nonlinear model in practice and this explains why linear models are the most frequently used nominal models for MPC in the building sector
DRGONA2020190
.

The second reason is that solving non-linear MPC problems requires either considerable computational resources for real-time optimization or simplification techniques such as decoupling nonlinearity in building models from linear dynamics and representing it by Hammerstein-Wiener models which may also introduce mismatches between the models and the real building dynamics
DRGONA2020190
. Even for an approximate MPC that employs machine learning methods to mimic an MPC controller, a large training set and an original implicit MPC are necessary. We have also conducted experiments comparing the computational time in Section
4.3
which again illustrates the demanding computational resources required by solving nonlinear MPC problems, which is not practical for daily household building management.

Consequently, considering the fact that it is barely worthwhile to employ expensive devices for controllers in typical households and that a linear model normally has a good enough expressive ability, a linear ARX model is selected for the MPC prediction model, also to demonstrate the superior effect of the proposed performance-oriented building control design.

Mask MPC
MIQP problem is computationally expensive and is challenging to be solved by free solvers like SCIP
BolusaniEtal2024OO
, especially on a cheap commercial chip. In order to generalize controllers to wide-spread households, in this case study, we have proposed a novel Mask MPC, as illustrated in Fig.
4
. In order to circumvent the issue of start up costs, we have applied a mask to the MPC and named it Mask MPC. The lower bound of the mask contributes to solving the start up cost issue and the upper bound of the mask can limit the peak power used to decrease the peak power penalty in certain DSM programs as well as to reduce the overall energy used. Both bounds are critical to the performance of the Mask MPC and are to be tuned by our optimization framework.
Besides, it is important to note that the upper bound of the mask is defined within the MPC as shown in Eq.(
5

We introduce the modulated heat pump input computed by the MPC,

Parameters

Constants
Generally in building control, consumers care most about the final costs and the individual comfort level inside building. Given that the MPC cost function only has penalties for electricity-cost-like term and slack variables for indoor temperature, to avoid the large violations of the indoor temperature, we set penalty coefficients of both terms to be constant and the penalty for slack variable is much heavier. Besides,to accurately represent the building with an ARX model, we are using a relatively high-order structure. This ensures that the model captures the necessary dynamics and interactions of the system, even though it results in a larger number of coefficients. We also set indoor temperature upper bounds to constants as the case study is in Winter heating seasons and the nature of the mentioned MPC will decrease the indoor temperature as much as possible to reduce costs.

Constraint Function
The optimal control of building systems is a constrained problem. Therefore in this work, in addition to the direct temperature constraints in the controllers, an additional constraint of Predicted Mean Vote (PMV) is introduced into the optimization methods, which is also the actual performance evaluation for the controller implementation. The PMV is a comfort index developed by P.O. Fanger
Fanger1970-ye
for the assessment of human thermal sensation in indoor environments and is a widely used method in the field of thermal comfort to evaluate the perceived thermal conditions experienced by individuals. The PMV index ranges from -3 to +3, with negative values indicating cold conditions, zero representing thermal neutrality and positive values indicating warm conditions.

In this project, a relatively relaxed but more abstract constraint on the PMV is implemented based on guidance
noauthor_2007-xq
, as in practice, a few violations are allowed, decided by building designers and clients. It is considered that we are operating in a new building and therefore the PMV value has to be between -0.5 and 0.5. For the constraint implemented in the CONFIG algorithm, we have introduced a cumulative distribution function (CDF) integrated with the PMV values. For each time step, the PMV value is calculated and after the offline simulation, the PMV values are transmitted to the CONFIG algorithm. A CDF is implemented to fit the PMV series and it is expected that within the occupied hours, the absolute PMV value at 80% is smaller than 0.5, as shown in Fig.
5
. We introduce
PMV_{CDF}
to be the absolute PMV value at 80% during the occupied hours and then the constraint function can be defined as Eq.(
10
).

h_{1,k}=g_{config,k}=PMV_{CDF}-0.5

In this research, only one constraint is implemented, however, both the CONFIG algorithm and the PMV integrated CDF constraint can deal with multiple constraints problems, e.g. adding a constraint demanding that the PMV value at 50% is smaller than 0.2.

5.1
Simulation settings

5.1.1
Baseline controllers

This case study employs three baseline controllers: a basic local loop rule-based controller (Baseline), a delicately designed MIQP MPC controller (MIQP MPC) and a simple MLP that does not consider start up cost (QP MPC). The rule-based controller has already been integrated into the BOPTEST simulation platform and is operated by set points with a higher setpoint (
21.2^{\circ}C
) during occupied hours and a lower setpoint (
20.5^{\circ}C
) during unoccupied hours. This approach is employed to prevent the necessity of abrupt changes in the indoor temperature, which may not be achievable due to the limitations of the building structure.

Another baseline controller is a MIQP MPC controller as shown in Eq.(
7

There is no constraint on the modulated heat pump input signal, as the mix integer nature could capture the relationship between the modulated signal and actual electricity power. The lower bound of the indoor temperature for the occupied hours is set to be
21.5^{\circ}C
and
16^{\circ}C
for the unoccupied hours. These hyperparameters values are not precise, as they are not tuned according to closed loop performances, however, they can still be considered to satisfy the PMV constraint while reducing the electricity bills to a reasonable extent.

The last baseline controller is a simple QP MPC without the knowledge of start up cost issue. The formation of the QP MPC follows Eq.(
5

5.2.1
Details of optimization

Firstly, we have conducted a comparative analysis of the final electricity costs of the baseline controller, the QP MPC, the delicately designed MIQP MPC controller, and the tuned QP Mask MPC under the same pricing case (ddd as shown in Table.

In fact, the hyperparameter tuning contributes to as much cost reducing as the predictive ability of MPC which illustrates the vitality to perform hyperparameter on MPC controllers in building control and the superior effects of our optimization framework.

Besides, the Mask MPC, in particular, has the potential to reduce these costs by up to 26.90%. Moreover, for all months, the costs of the Mask MPC are again smaller than those of the MIQP MPC. It can be demonstrated that the proposed Mask MPC can achieve greater performance after tuning in our proposed hyperparameter optimizing framework than the MIQP MPC controller.

Moreover, although the indoor temperature trajectories of Mask MPC and MIQP MPC are quite similar, Mask MPC has more accurate temperature constraints after optimization, which again allows it to achieve certain cost savings while maintaining comfort constraints.

It should be noted that it seems straightforward to tune these hyperparameters manually, i.e. simply lowering the temperature and limiting the heat pump input. However, it is not possible to do so by trial and error, as the PMV comfort constraint is a black-box one and is difficult to satisfy precisely.
Overall, with much lower commissioning costs in terms of requirements for solvers and hardware compared with the MIQP MPC, Mask MPC achieves comparable or even superior performance.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we addressed the challenge of choosing the most economic electricity contract from a wide range and the suboptimal performance in conventional building MPC controller design by introducing a performance oriented optimization framework that leverages the advanced constrained black box optimization technique, CONFIG. On a digital twin such as BOPTEST simulation platform, we can do the offline optimization, which enables us to identify the optimal set of hyperparameters that would result in the smallest objective value while still satisfying the black-box comfort constraints.

Through a case study of a household building in Belgium, we demonstrated that a simple MPC controller can be tuned to match the performance of a delicately designed, complex MPC controller, which requires high commissioning cost. Furthermore, we showed in simulation of hardware and solver that the optimized MPC controllers are capable of reducing the monthly household electricity bill by up to 26.90% during a heating month, while still satisfying the given black-box constraint that is hard to achieve manually. Finally, we have demonstrated that this proposed framework is efficient in dealing with the economic electricity contract selection problem. In a case study of this problem, the result indicates a 20.18% saving with the most economic contract compared with the worst one.

The framework is capable of addressing real-world issues, although only offline optimization experiments have been conducted at this stage. Further adaptation for online optimization represents an intriguing direction for future work, as performance metrics such as the monthly electricity bill can be readily obtained in offline simulations, whereas they remain unknown in real-time online problems.
