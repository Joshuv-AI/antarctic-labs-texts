Negative Online Experiences and Reporting Rates in Youth With Mental Health Conditions
Authors: Mirelle Kass, Lindsay M. Alexander, Tatum Connell, Sara Bagheri Hamaneh, Najuma Abdullah, Michelle Freund, Michael P. Milham, Aki Nikolaidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveGrowing engagement with digital spaces among children and adolescents increases the risk of negative online experiences (NOE). This study examined the prevalence, risk factors, and reporting barriers for NOE among youth with mental health and neurodevelopmental conditions.MethodA cross-sectional, mixed-methods study between January and July 2023 was conducted using a community-based sample from the Child Mind Institute’s Healthy Brain Network. A total of 1,009 youth aged 9 to 15 years (58.4% male; mean age = 11.79 years, SD = 1.71 years) completed a quantitative survey. A subsample (n = 109) participated in a qualitative follow-up involving a 3-day moderated online bulletin board.ResultsOf the 1,009 participants, 26.6% experienced NOE in the previous year, with 68.7% reporting multiple incidents; however, 80% did not report them. Factor analyses identified 3 key reporting barriers: reporting process, reporting policy, and emotional barriers. Social aptitude, mental health symptoms, and parenting style predicted youths’ likelihood of encountering NOE and key reporting barriers. Qualitative findings indicated that reporting decisions involved assessing malice, perpetrator intent, and NOE type; ambiguity in these areas contributed to reporting uncertainty.ConclusionThis study highlights the gap between the high prevalence and low reporting rates of NOE among youth with mental health and neurodevelopmental conditions. Identified demographic, clinical, and family factors were associated with increased risk of experiencing NOE and the 3 reporting barriers. Findings underscore the need for targeted, developmentally appropriate strategies involving policymakers, technology developers, clinicians, and educators to support safer online environments for at-risk youth.Clinical guidance• Educate children and families about online reporting processes using developmentally appropriate, clear, and accessible language.• Encourage parents to adopt consistent, supportive involvement in their children’s online activities, as positive parenting was associated with lower risk and reporting barriers.• Address emotional barriers by normalizing help-seeking, validating fears of embarrassment or retaliation, and connecting youth with safe peer or adult support.• Collaborate with schools and digital platforms to advocate for user-friendly reporting tools and resources tailored to younger or more vulnerable users.

The Internet has become deeply integrated into our daily lives, particularly for youth. Although childhood Internet use has been associated with improvements in academic achievement,1 learning development,2 and digital literacy,3 digital spaces also introduce significant risks to online harassment (eg, cyberstalking, sexual harassment), especially for younger users.4 One report of 1,000 children and adolescents, 9 to 17 years of age, found that 48% experienced at least one negative or potentially harmful online incident in their lifetime that made them feel uncomfortable.5

In the past year, one in 4 youths (9-16 years of age) reported having a “negative online experience” (NOE), defined as “any unwanted or uncomfortable negative experience while online.”6 These incidents have well-documented psychological and physical effects7 and exist in many forms, including impersonation,8 doxing/outing,9 trickery,9 exclusion,9 flaming,9 cyberstalking,9 sexual harassment,10 and masquerading11 (Supplement 1, available online), all of which have been linked to poorer mental health outcomes.7 Victims of NOE report higher rates of depression,7,12, 13, 14 anxiety,7,15 self-harm,12 post-traumatic stress disorder,14 and suicidal ideation.7,12,13 Although negative long-term effects (eg, worsening depression) have been noted by longitudinal studies,16 few have explored variances among different forms of NOE.

Despite the growing awareness of NOE, there is still limited understanding of how children and adolescents respond to these encounters. The most common advice provided to a victim of NOE is to disclose the incident,17 as sharing the encounter has been shown to reduce the likelihood of future harassment.16 Nonetheless, rates of reporting NOE in children and adolescents remain quite low,5,18 with one study noting that over one in 4 youths who experienced NOE did not seek help from the platform, an adult, or a peer.5 Potential reporting barriers that have been noted include anonymity concerns,5 fear of escalation,19 feelings of embarrassment,5,18 and perceived consequences or retaliation from reporting.18 In addition, youths’ comfort levels with disclosing NOE have been found to vary by report type, with a preference for peers over parents and caregivers15,18 and great discomfort with the platform directly.18

Some studies have explored how parent–child relationships may influence a youth’s likelihood of experiencing or reporting NOE.14,20 They propose that more inconsistent and distant parenting may be a risk factor for experiencing NOE,20 whereas greater positive attachment may be a protective factor against the negative impacts of NOE.14 In addition, increased risk for experiencing NOE has been observed in individuals with preexisting mental health symptoms16,21 and poorer health14; however, little is known about how at-risk youth experience different motivators or barriers to reporting.12,13

The present study explored NOE and reporting behaviors in 1,009 children and adolescents with mental health and neurodevelopmental conditions from the Healthy Brain Network,22 a large-scale, community research initiative. This study focused on youth 9 to 15 years of age to capture the transition from early to mid-adolescence—a window defined by heightened vulnerability to risky behaviors. We leveraged this range to enable balanced subgrouping (9-12.9 years vs 13-15.9 years), allowing for the analysis of age-specific developmental differences while extending the prior findings on unwanted online interactions.5 A qualitative follow-up study (n = 109) provided deeper insights into youths’ decision-making processes and mental models regarding NOE and reporting.

Method

Participant Recruitment

Eligible participants included current and past participants of the Child Mind Institute’s Healthy Brain Network (HBN)22 who were between the ages of 9 and 15 years at the time of the study. HBN is a transdiagnostic research initiative; enrollment requires that parents have mental health–related concerns and/or observe mental health symptoms in their child, ensuring that all participants had clinically relevant presentations.22 Parents and/or caregivers of eligible participants were contacted via recruitment emails and phone calls. Appropriate informed consent and assent processes were conducted remotely with the parent and/or caregiver and the participant, respectively. During the assent process, participants were also able to opt-in to be considered for the qualitative follow-up study and participate if they were to be randomly selected. All study data collection occurred between January and July 2023.

Participants were compensated with an electronic gift card up to $185 ($75 for the quantitative survey and $110 for the qualitative follow-up study). Participants were able to withdraw from the study at any time. This study was approved by the Advarra Institutional Review Board (https://www.advarra.com/) and followed the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) reporting guidelines.23

Procedure

We used a mixed-methods approach to bridge the gap between standardized quantitative measurement and the rapidly evolving nature of youth digital experiences. Because traditional quantitative instruments may not fully capture the nuance of contemporary online threats or emerging technological platforms, we included a qualitative follow-up component to the study using an online bulletin board.24,25 This design allowed for a discovery-oriented analysis to identify novel attitudes and experiences beyond our a priori hypotheses, ensuring a more comprehensive understanding of the current digital safety landscape.

Quantitative Survey

The quantitative survey, administered via REDCap,26 incorporated existing survey questions (Table S1, available online) and project developed questions designed by mental health experts. Data collected included background and demographic information, parenting styles, general online behavior, prevalence of NOE, reporting behaviors, and desired features to promote online safety (Supplement 1, Table S1, available online)

Qualitative Follow-up

Cascade Strategies (https://cascadestrategies.com/) hosted the online bulletin board under a confidentiality agreement. A subset of the sample were randomly selected to participate in one of the 4 moderated online bulletin boards to explore how youth experience and respond to different forms of NOE (N = 109; 10.80%) Selected participants were asked to answer questions for 30 minutes per day for 3 consecutive days.

Measures

The present study involved data previously collected via HBN, a 310-item quantitative survey, and a 3-day qualitative follow-up study (30 minutes per day). Data from the self-report quantitative survey included background and demographic information, parenting styles, general online behavior, prevalence of NOE, reporting behaviors, and desired features to promote online safety (Supplement 1, Table S1, available online). The qualitative follow-up study included hypothetical scenarios of different harassment types (Table S2, available online), perceptions of NOE and reporting behaviors, and factors that influence comfort levels with reporting.

To develop a deeper profile of the participants, a subset of data from HBN that included demographic, cognitive, behavioral, and clinical evaluations was incorporated. For the present study, clinical diagnoses were categorized into 7 HBN Diagnosis Categories (Table S3, available online). Although all participants met HBN enrollment criteria (ie. requiring parental mental health concerns and/or symptoms), some participants either had not yet received their formal diagnosis at the time of their present study participation and could not be assigned an HBN Diagnosis Category, or had received a mental health diagnosis that fell outside of these 7 primary categories.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative Survey

All statistical analyses (July 2023 to December 2023; December 2024 to February 2025; hiatus because of personnel transitions and resource limitations) were conducted using R. For all analyses, a 2-sided statistical significance cutoff of p < .05 was applied. Odds ratios were calculated to evaluate how sample characteristics influenced the likelihood of NOE (Table 2). Covariates included mental health status (Child Behavior Checklist [CBCL], Pediatric Symptom Checklist [PSC]) and social aptitude (Social Aptitude Scale [SAS]); adjustments were made for age, gender identity, sexual orientation, racial/ethnic identity (>2% of participants), socioeconomic status (SES), and HBN diagnosis category. We split our sample into 2 demographically matched halves (ie, age, gender identity, racial/ethnic identity matched). We conducted an exploratory factor analysis on the first half of the sample and performed a confirmatory factor analysis on the second split sample to evaluate model fit. Parallel analysis revealed 3 significant factors emerging that summarized barriers to reporting (Figure 1). Multivariate regression models were conducted to explore predictors of barriers to reporting. Covariates included in the models were mental health status (PSC), social aptitude (SAS), and Internet behaviors (Internet Addiction Test [IAT], prior online behaviors). Qualitative Follow-up

Researchers from Cascade Strategies performed qualitative text analysis and prepared a summary of key points and trends of the data. We conducted in-depth thematic analyses and content analyses using the frameworks outlined by Braun and Clarke27 and Hsieh and Shannon,28 respectively.

Results

Sample Characteristics

General Online Behavior

Mean total time online per day was 5.94 hours (SD = 4.14 hours). Participants reported spending more time online for personal/social activities (mean = 3.60 hours, SD = 2.77 hours) than for school-related activities (mean = 2.33 hours, SD = 2.56 hours). Older participants (13-15 years) reported spending more time online (mean = 7.52 hours, SD = 4.46 hours) than younger participants (9-12 years) (mean = 4.97 hours, SD = 3.60 hours). Of the online activities assessed, watching online videos (n = 801, 79.85%) and playing video games (n = 621, 61.85%) were endorsed most frequently (Figure S1, available online).

Negative Online Experiences

Slightly more than one-fourth of participants (n = 265, 26.26%) reported experiencing at least one NOE in the past year, with more than one-third (n = 182, 68.68%) noting multiple incidents, including on a weekly (n = 11, 4.15%) or daily (n = 12, 4.53%) basis. Of the 265 participants who experienced NOE, most experienced negative emotions during the incident, such as anger (n = 131, 49.43%), sadness (n = 119, 44.91%), and fear (n = 115, 43.40%). One-fifth of those who experienced NOE reported the incident to the platform (n = 53, 20.00%); others chose to close the platform (n = 98, 36.98%) or to ignore it (n = 79, 29.81%).

Barriers to Reporting

Figure 1 depicts the 3-factor solution that was identified through parallel analysis as the best fit for the reporting barrier questions. Confirmatory factor analysis revealed a well-fitting factor structure (comparative fit index [CFI] ≥ 0.94; Tucker–Lewis Index [TLI] ≥ 0.92). The 3 key reporting barriers were as follows: (1) reporting process barriers; (2) reporting policy barriers; and (3) emotional barriers. (Table S4 available online) depicts the 2 reporting barrier questions that loaded separately and were explored independently.

Mental Models and Decision-Making Processes

Qualitative analyses revealed that participants used 3 components to assess NOE when deciding whether to make a report: (1) degree of malice toward the victim; (2) indicators of the perpetrator’s intent; and (3) number and severity of the harassment form(s) present. Increased ambiguity in any of these components resulted in elevated levels of uncertainty in the participants when deciding whether to report the presented scenario.

Harassment severity scores were calculated for each hypothetical scenario based on the number and severity of the harassment form(s) present (Table S3, available online). Higher scores were significantly correlated with increased decision to report (r = 0.308, p <.05). There was a slight preference for reporting to an adult (mean = 71.74%, SD = 20.03%) over a platform (mean = 62.42%, SD = 24.31%). Although younger participants (9-12 years; mean = 57.57%, SD = 30.97%) tended to report more often than older participants (13-15 years; mean = 51.99%, SD = 31.68%), they also expressed greater uncertainties about reporting.

Desired Features to Create Safer Online Spaces

Slightly less than half of the participants (mean = 47, SD = 47.00%) were comfortable reporting NOE on a platform that they frequently used. Increased comfort levels were associated with greater ease in locating the reporting button (n = 66, 66.0%) and whether platforms provided more support to users during (n = 60, 60.0%) and after (n = 54, 54.0%) report investigations. Participants desired that platforms provide more information on how users can protect themselves better online (n = 794, 78.69%). They also expressed a desire for learning more about how to use the platform’s features, such as blocking (n = 746, 76.35%) and reporting (n = 722, 73.59%), in general, and through tutorial videos (n = 605, 61.73%) (Figures S2 and S3, available online).

Discussion

This study examined negative online experiences (NOE) and reporting barriers in children and adolescents with mental health and neurodevelopmental conditions to provide a deeper understanding of youths’ experiences with NOE and what prevents them from reporting these incidents. Over one in 4 participants experienced NOE in the past year, with 68.7% reporting multiple incidents. Aligning with previous research,14,20,21,29 inconsistent parenting styles and elevated mental health symptoms increased their likelihood of experiencing NOE. Despite its high prevalence, reporting rates were modest and greatly affected by 3 key barriers: reporting process barriers; reporting policy barriers; and emotional barriers. Insights from the qualitative follow-up study revealed that younger participants chose to report more frequently than older participants, but also experienced more uncertainty in deciding to report. Participants’ decision-making process was heavily based on the degree of malice toward the victim, perceptions about the perpetrator’s intent, and number and severity of the harassment form(s) present. Overwhelmingly, participants voiced a desire for greater clarity and guidance from platforms about reporting and how to practice greater online safety behaviors.

Appropriate recognition of the different reporting barriers that emerge across developmental stages is crucial for promoting greater online safety. Younger participants (9-12 years) were less likely to report NOE because of being hindered by a lack of understanding about reporting processes and policies. Although it is not uncommon for an online user to be in this age group,5,6,18,30 most digital platforms implement minimum age restrictions of 13 years to comply with privacy laws (eg, Children's Online Privacy Protection Act31). As a result, many platforms’ technical, social, and linguistic components may be too complex or mature for younger users to understand,32,33 which may exacerbate existing uncertainties and hesitations stemming from a lack of experience or familiarity with the platform or device itself. These 2 barriers were also significant obstacles for youths with lower social aptitude, regardless of age, suggesting that confusion and hesitation about reporting may be associated with one’s ability to decode social cues, to manage emotions, and to understand platform language. Although it may be challenging to implement age verification processes with full accuracy, there seems to be significant value for platforms to strengthen these processes to protect their users. Our findings support prior work34 that suggests that it may be beneficial for platforms to increase clarity and accessibility around the reporting process. Enhancing the visibility and usability of reporting tools can reduce confusion,35 especially for younger users and those with lower social aptitude. Educational resources, like interactive tutorials and user-friendly guides, may further de-mystify the process and policies,36 encourage reporting, and foster safer online spaces for at-risk youth.

Emotional responses, such as embarrassment, fear, and worry, are commonly reported among individuals who experience NOE5,18; however, these reactions appear to disproportionately serve as a reporting barrier for youth with internalizing symptoms (eg, anxiety, mood dysregulation). Potential solutions for platforms to reduce emotional barriers could include strengthening anonymity protections to prevent retaliation37 and tailoring messaging to be developmentally and emotionally appropriate to suit a variety of users.34 In addition, because youth frequently turn to their peers for emotional support and practical advice during moments of distress and uncertainty,38 integrating peer-led reporting guidance and support networks may help normalize help seeking and reduce levels of uncertainty and hesitation.

Although prior studies propose an association between parenting styles and increased risk of encountering NOE,20 our findings suggest they may also reduce youths’ likelihood of seeking help. Our findings found that parenting styles characterized by higher levels of inconsistent discipline and poor supervision strongly predicted increased likelihood of NOE, suggesting that a lack of parental involvement and oversight may leave youth more exposed to online harm. Importantly, this relationship is likely multidimensional, as elevated risks of NOE were also predicted by the youth’s psychiatric profile, such as externalizing symptoms. It is worth noting that youths’ psychopathology may also shape their parents’ parenting style and the subsequent success of their behaviors. As a result, parenting styles and youth psychopathology may not only shape one another but also may jointly contribute to youths’ risk of experiencing NOE and their responses to these incidents. Children from families with more positive parenting behaviors were less likely to experience any of the 3 reporting barriers. In conjunction with youths’ preference for reporting to adults over the platform, these findings point to parents and families as a possible avenue for interventions addressing NOE. Current efforts to ameliorate NOE and to increase reporting that are either native to online platforms or exist as separate programs tend not to incorporate parents and families,14 despite their importance as a safety net for youth to keep themselves safe online. Thus, developing new approaches to support parents and caregivers in navigating both youths’ mental health needs and online safety may represent a particularly impactful strategy for reducing youth exposure to NOE and strengthening their support system.

Comparing our quantitative survey to our in-depth qualitative analysis revealed a key insight—namely, that youth show clarity and a willingness to report NOE when discussing hypothetical scenarios, but they show significant hesitation when they are personally victimized in real life. This discrepancy may suggest that although youth recognize certain behaviors as unacceptable or “report-worthy” in theory, real-life reporting is hindered by emotional, social, developmental, or other barriers. In moments of personal distress, a number of factors may prevent them from acting—including fears of retaliation, uncertainty about the process, the perception that reporting is perfunctory (eg, no consequences or meaningful follow-through), or lack of confidence may prevent them from taking action—even when they recognize that reporting is the “right” response to the situation. Future research should explore this intention–behavior gap in greater depth. Platforms might consider tools that not only educate and help youth learn about acceptable and unacceptable online behavior but also provide them with real-time emotional and procedural support to bridge the gap between recognizing NOE and taking action.

Limitations of the study include the sample’s predominantly White and high–socioeconomic status composition. Preliminary studies have noted differences in prevalence of and experience with NOE by users’ racial and/or ethnic identities.39 Reporting behaviors may be shaped by differential trust in institutional platforms and perceptions of safety, potentially creating barriers for marginalized youth. Youth from marginalized backgrounds may face unique barriers, including prior negative experiences with authority figures, concerns about being believed, or cultural factors, that decrease their willingness to report NOE even when they recognize a situation as harmful. As a result, these findings may underrepresent both the prevalence and impact of such experiences and the specific reporting barriers faced by youth from underrepresented groups, highlighting the need for future research on how systemic inequities and demographic factors (eg, racial and/or ethnic identity, gender, and socioeconomic status) influence youths’ experiences with and responses to NOE incidents, as well as their engagement with digital safety tools.

The clinical composition of the sample warrants consideration, as 63.23% of participants met criteria for an attentional disorder. Although this enrichment offers a unique opportunity to study a population prone to impulsivity, a potential risk factor for NOE, it may also limit generalizability to the broader population. Although we anticipated that this enrichment would heighten sensitivity for detecting associations with NOEs, we observed no specific effects related to impulsivity in either our categorical or dimensional analyses. Our aggregation of specific diagnoses into broader categories (particularly for attentional disorders) may have obscured granular, symptom-level associations between impulsivity and online risk. Nevertheless, the high prevalence of these phenotypic traits may have enhanced our statistical power to detect associations across other domains (eg, behavioral disorders). Together, these findings suggest that clinical diagnosis alone does not fully account for the variance in adolescent online behavior.

Although prevalence rates in the present study align with prior literature,6 many participants reported uncertainty when asked if they had encountered NOE in the previous year. When combined with the finding from the qualitative online bulletin board that youths sometimes have a difficult time discerning what constitutes online harassment, it is possible that rates of NOE are greater than reported here. In addition, the present study did not require that participants disclose any details related to their online harassment incident besides following events (eg, reporting), which may have provided greater clarity on the low reporting rates.

Importantly, the present study used a cross-sectional design and subjective, self-report measurements. As a result, potential limitations, such as sampling bias and recall bias, respectively, may be introduced. Future work would benefit from corroboration via objective measures (eg, passive sensing) or third-party informants (eg, parents). Finally, it is important to note that no formal language or terminology (eg, “cyberstalking” or “sexual harassment”) was shown to participants, in order to minimize labeling bias. Thus, although our application of the general term “NOE” and scenario-based approach in the qualitative follow-up allowed for a broad spectrum of NOE, the present study did not examine the protracted longitudinal processes associated with sexualized grooming, trafficking, or radicalization. As a result, this approach may have limitations due to a lack of alignment with specific legal or clinical definitions.

In conclusion, children and adolescents with mental health and neurodevelopmental conditions frequently encounter negative online experiences but do not report them because of developmental, cognitive, and emotional barriers. Findings highlight the need for improved clarity, accessibility, and emotional support to help protect at-risk youth and to foster safer digital spaces.
