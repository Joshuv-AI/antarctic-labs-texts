Normality and the Turing Test
Alexandre Kabbach

Abstract
This paper proposes to revisit the Turing test through the concept of normality . Its core argument is that the statistical interpretation of the normal—understood as the average both in the normative and mathematical sense of the term—proves useful for understanding the Turing test in at least two ways. First, in the sense that the Turing test targets normal/average rather than exceptional human intelligence, so that successfully passing the test requires building machines that “make mistakes” and display imperfect behavior just like normal/average humans. Second, in the sense that the Turing test is a statistical test where judgments of intelligence are never carried out by a single “average” judge (understood as non-expert) but always by a full jury. As such, the notion of “average human interrogator” that Turing talks about in his original paper should be understood primarily as referring to a mathematical abstraction made of the normalized aggregate of individual judgments of multiple judges. In short, this paper argues that the Turing test is a test of normal intelligence as assessed by a normal judge characterizing the average judgment of a pool of human interrogators. Its conclusions are twofold. First, it argues that large language models such as ChatGPT are unlikely to pass the Turing test as those models precisely target exceptional rather than normal/average human intelligence. As such, they constitute models of what it proposes to call artificial smartness rather than artificial intelligence per se , insofar as they deviate from the original goal of Turing for the modeling or artificial minds. Second, it argues that the core question of whether the Turing test can contribute anything to the understanding of human cognition is that of whether the human mind is really reducible to the normal/average mind—a question which largely extends beyond the Turing test itself and questions the conceptual underpinnings of the normalist paradigm it belongs to.



keywords
Turing test, normality, average intelligence, epistemology of artificial intelligence, artificial smartness, history of statistics, large language models, ChatGPT

1
Introduction

When
Turing \APACyear1950
introduces his seminal paper in
Mind
on
Computing machinery and intelligence
, he sets the goal of the field of artificial intelligence straight: the aim is to build a machine capable of passing his “Imitation Game”.
The Imitation Game—now better know as the “Turing test”—is an indistinguishibility test.
It consists in having a human interrogator sit alone in a room and communicate with two separate interlocutors through a text-only channel while having to decide, based solely on the content of their textual interactions, which one of the two participants is the human and which one is the machine.
If, on a repeated number of occasions, the human interrogator cannot distinguish the machine from the other human participants, then the machine can be said to have successfully passed the test.

Turing intended his test to substitute the question “Are there imaginable digital computers which would do well in the imitation game?” for the question “Can machines think?”—a latter question which he himself considered “too meaningless to deserve discussion”
(
Turing \APACyear1950
, p.442)
.
The Turing test thus crucially rests on the assumption that intelligence should be approached as a human
faculty
—a fundamental ability to “think” that one possesses by virtue of being human—which is precisely why some have argued that it constitutes a test of “humanity” rather than intelligence
per se

(
Fostel \APACyear1993
, e.g.,)
.
However, if the Turing test treats “being intelligent” as being more or less synonymous with “being human”, does that mean that it equates
intelligent behavior
with
human behavior
, in turn?
Not exactly, and this is where the fundamental contribution of this paper lies.
In this paper indeed, I argue that the Turing test equates intelligent behavior with
normal
human behavior rather than human behavior strictly speaking.

There are at least two ways to make sense of this concept of “normal human behavior” for the present purpose.
The first one is through the normal/exceptional dichotomy and by appealing to a normative interpretation of the normal that evokes the average as
mediocre
.
What is crucial to understand about the Turing test indeed is that it targets
normal/average
rather than
exceptional
human intelligence—the average intelligence of most people rather than the exceptional intelligence of a happy few geniuses
(see
Hodges \APACyear1983/2014
, p.316)
.
The normal/average brain, however, is necessarily imperfect and bound to make mistakes at some point, which is precisely why the Turing test requires machines to make mistakes so as to be truly indistinguishable from normal people
(
Turing \APACyear1950
, pp.447–449)
.
This explains why, in turn, large language models such as ChatGPT are unlikely to pass the Turing test: who needs machines that make mistakes?
For the practical purpose those language models are usually being put to use, normal intelligence is just not enough: they need to be
smart
.
They need to conform to a normative ideal of correct and exceptional human behavior that no human being actually abides to in practice.
In practice indeed, real people make mistakes—they always deviate from whichever normative ideal of correct human behavior they live by—and that is precisely what makes them human.

The second way to make sense of this concept of “normal human behavior” is through the
statistical
interpretation of the normal this time, which evokes the average understood both as the
mean
and the (statistically)
typical
.
A crucial aspect of the Turing test to bear in mind is that it is a statistical test where judgments of intelligent behavior are never produced by a single human interrogator but always by a full jury.
The notion of “average interrogator” that
Turing \APACyear1950
talks about in his original paper should thus be understood primarily as an
idealization
referring to a mathematical abstraction consisting in aggregating judgments of individual human interrogators and computing their mean value—a mean value considered to be “truer” and more objective than those individual judgments alone.
The methodology of the Turing test is thereby consistent with the standard objectivization practice of the behavioral sciences and with its foundational epistemological assumption: that human variations follow a normal distribution and that individual variability should be treated as
error
around a mean value characterizing a statistical type. What lies at the core of the Turing test is thus a quest for the human type—or at least
a
human type since the type objectivized by the Turing test will necessarily be both socio-culturally and socio-historically situated, as some have fundamentally stressed in the past
(
French \APACyear1990
, e.g.)
.
Ultimately, intelligent behavior for Turing is thus to be understood as referring to a (sample of) normal/typical human behavior.

In sum, this paper argues that the Turing test has often been (and keeps being) critically misunderstood.
Turing never intended indeed to target what we commonly refer to as “intelligence” in everyday language and which actually refers to
exceptional
intelligence most of the time.
What he wanted to investigate was
normal
intelligence understood as
average/typical
human intelligence—in accordance with the then and now dominant normalist paradigm taking normality as its “central organizing concept”
(
Hacking \APACyear1990
;
Hacking \APACyear1996
)
.
By shifting the target of inquiry from the exceptional to the normal, Turing brings back intelligence into normalism in full but also reduces the study of the human mind to that of the normal/average/typical mind.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows.
Section
2
begins by introducing the core concept of normality alongside Turing’s conception of intelligence.
Section
2.1
introduces the concept of normality from a historical perspective, distinguishing in particular its biomedical interpretation understood as the
functional
or the
healthy
from its statistical interpretation understood as the
average
or (statistically)
typical
—a statistical interpretation particularly relevant to the understanding of the modern conception of intelligence.
After detailing the epistemological contribution of the concept of statistical normality and notably the crucial role that it has played in the foundation of the behavioral sciences and of modern psychology, the section shifts to the introduction of two distinct normative interpretations of statistical normality: the normal/average as
good
on one side and the normal/average as only
mediocre
on the other.
The latter normative interpretation paves the way for the normal/exceptional dichotomy and, through it, to the core argument of this section: that the Turing test actually targets normal/average/mediocre rather than exceptional human intelligence.

Section
2.2
introduces Turing’s conception of intelligence in more details, tracing back its origins to the notions of
intellect
,
understanding
and the cartesian concept of
reason
.
It proposes to revisit the distinction between
intelligence
and
intelligent behavior
through the fundamental contribution of
development
, emphasizing that Turing’s conception of intelligence as a faculty implies that intelligence is always but a potential that can only become behaviorally manifest through gradual exposure to environmental stimuli.
After comparing Turing’s approach to the faculty of intelligence to Chomsky’s approach to the faculty of language, it concludes that the Turing test remains fundamentally normalist in that it does not aim to characterize the boundaries of human cognition—determining all that can and cannot be human—but only
one
of its possible manifestation: the intelligent behavior associated with a particular community of human beings. Such considerations explain, in turn, why the Turing test cannot be taken to provide an operational characterization of intelligence—i.e., providing both a
necessary
and
sufficient
condition for the possession of human intelligence. Considering otherwise would be like saying that manifesting a particular type of developmentally-situated linguistic behavior—say, speaking English—provides both a necessary and sufficient condition for the possession of the faculty of language, which everyone will agree is absurd (at least for the necessary part).

Section
3
revisits the Turing test through the normal/exceptional dichotomy.
Section
3.1
introduces the fundamental distinction between
intelligence
and
smartness
focusing on the opposition between intelligent behavior understood as normal/average human behavior on one side and smart behavior understood as exceptional human behavior on the other.
The notion of smart behavior is further refined as a
normative ideal
of
correct
in addition to
exceptional
human behavior, which enables two things.
First, framing smart behavior as a
normative ideal
of human behavior explains how smartness characterizes an
ideal
type of human behavior from which most if not all real/normal people deviate.
Second, framing smart behavior as a normative ideal of
correct
human behavior explains why the Turing test, as a test of normal/average human behavior, specifically requires machines to
make mistakes
and deviate from the normative ideal of smart behavior in the way normal people would.
The intelligence/smartness dichotomy is then illustrated through the example of
spelling
as perfect spelling is taken—in English at least—to constitute a prototypical example of a normative ideal on human behavior that normal people ought to abide to but never quite manage to in practice.
The example provides a concrete way to illustrate the fundamental difference between the two scientific projects that constitute
artificial smartness
on one side and
artificial intelligence
on the other: the former being dedicated to the modeling of correct and exceptional human behavior in general and perfect/correct spelling in particular while the latter is dedicated—for Turing at least—to the modeling of normal/average human behavior in general and imperfect/normal/average spelling in particular.

With those distinctions in mind, Section
3.2
introduces the core implications of the normalist reading of the Turing test for large language models such as ChatGPT.
Its main argument is that such models are unlikely to pass the Turing test as they are models of artificial smartness rather than artificial intelligence—targeting exceptional rather than normal human intelligence.
The argument however, is analytical and not ontological.
That is, its point is not to claim that such models could
never
be made so as to successfully pass the Turing test but only that, for the practical applications those models are usually being put to use, it makes more sense for them to model smart/exceptional/correct human behavior rather than intelligent/normal/incorrect human behavior.
The argument is plain:
who needs machines that make mistakes?

That being said, if nothing prevents us from training those very same models on new data so as to learn how to “make mistakes”, what is crucial to understand is that
not any mistakes goes
as far as the Turing test is concerned: to successfully pass the Turing test, those models would need to produce
normal/average mistakes
as normal people would.
And determining the nature of those normal mistakes—and creating the corresponding datasets along with it—is quite a scientific challenge.
It is, in fact, the very challenge that
artificial intelligence
as originally conceived by Turing was supposed to tackle.

Section
4
moves on to the understanding of normality as a statistical type.
Section
4.1
reminds us that the Turing test is a statistical test where success is never assessed by a single judge by always by a full jury.
Understanding that the Turing test characterizes a test of
normality
rather than
humanity
strictly speaking then enables two things.
First, it explains how judgments of “humanity” can vary significantly from one judge to the next: human interrogators are simply expressing what feels normal
to them
.
Second, it provides a deeper understanding of the nature of the objectivization process in the Turing test, made possible by the use of statistics: the point is to move past the normal as
familiar
to reach the
statistically
normal—moving away from a form of subjective normality so as to characterize a purportedly objective statistical
type
.
Such considerations then beg the question of
which type
will be objectivized by the Turing test, to which the joint analyses of the specifications of the test, the methodological biases of experimental psychology and the historical roots of statistical normality suggests that it is unlikely to be a universal or even a representative human type.

Section
4.2
finishes on some important limitations of the Turing test, starting with the “game” aspect of the test which puts participants in a configuration where they compete to appear
as normal as possible
from the perspective of the average human interrogator.
The problem with that aspect of the test is that it leads human participants to behave not as they would normally do but as they think they
ought
to so as to appear normal in that context—ultimately leading to the objectivization not of normal human behavior
per se
but of a
normative ideal
of normal human behavior.
The second limitation of the test pertains to the implicit prerequisite of “cultural alignment” between human interrogators and participants—requiring them both to share roughly the same conception of what it means to be “normal”—and which conditions the meaningfulness of the test results themselves.
The problem with that prerequisite is that it is impossible to enforce in practice given the fundamentally open-ended nature of the test, so that it is ultimately impossible to guarantee
both
a completely open-ended
and
meaningful Turing test.
The next limitations pertain less to the Turing test itself than to its overarching normalist paradigm.
The third limitation points at the descriptive/prescriptive tension behind the concept of normality—constantly navigating between what
is
and what
ought to be
—and questions whether what is objectivized by the Turing test can be considered “real” in any meaningful sense of the term or whether it is not simply a constructed fiction.
This descriptive/prescriptive tension, in turn, is what makes normality a
constantly moving target
which questions, in the case of artificial intelligence, whether we are not actually modifying our target of inquiry (intelligence) as we attempt to model it. The final limitation of the test is that of the concept of statistical normality itself, which proves of little help when the goal is to determine the nature of the human faculty of intelligence.
The point is that human mind is simply not reducible to the normal/average/typical mind, as there is simply more to being human than to being normal.

2
Normality and intelligence

2.1
What is normal?

Normality is
[\ldots]

both timeless and dated, an idea that in some sense has been with us always, but which can in a moment adopt a completely new form of life.

[\ldots]

As a word, ‘normal’
[\ldots]

acquired its present most common meaning only in the 1820s.

[\ldots]

The normal was one of a pair.
Its opposite was the pathological and for a short time its domain was chiefly medical.
Then it moved into the sphere of—almost everything.
People, behaviour, states of affairs, diplomatic relations, molecules: all these may be normal or abnormal.
The word became indispensable because it created a way to be ‘objective’ about human beings.
The word is also like a faithful retainer, a voice from the past. It uses a power as old as Aristotle to bridge the fact/value distinction, whispering in your ear that what is normal is also all right.
(
Hacking \APACyear1990
, p.160)

There are at least two ways to understand the concept of normality.
The first one, as the quote from
(
Hacking \APACyear1990
, p.160)
above suggests, is through its
biomedical
interpretation where the normal evokes the functional or the healthy by opposition to the dysfunctional or the unhealthy—as in the famous “normal/pathological” dichotomy of
Canguilhem \APACyear1966/1991
.
In this context, normality is typically associated with the concept of (biological)
function
: the normal,
King \APACyear1945
tells us, is “that which functions in accordance with its design”.
The
normal heart
, for instance, is that which can adequately satisfy its function to
pump blood
.

As far as intelligence is concerned, however, it is not so much this biomedical interpretation than the
statistical
interpretation of normality that will interest us, where the normal evokes the standard, the typical, the frequent, the usual, the common and of course, the
average
.
Those two interpretations may occasionally overlap, but they are also crucially distinct: “The average may be, and very often is, abnormal”
King \APACyear1945
emphasizes and indeed, an average heart may not always be a functional heart and
vice-versa

(see
Wachbroit \APACyear1994
, p.580)
.

Historically, the concept of statistical normality has been associated with two distinct normative interpretations
(see
Hacking \APACyear1990
, ch.19–21, for details)
.

The
standard
normal curve, centered on the mean/average (dashed line).

Figure 2:

cumulative
normal curve, centered on the mean/average (dashed line).

The first one—where the average evokes the correct, the good, the right or the ideal—is the one typically associated with the standard representation of the normal curve in Figure
2

normal
distribution”, the “
normal
law” or the “
normal
curve”.
It is the normative interpretation most often associated with the Belgian astronomer turned statistician Adolphe Quetelet and with his theory of the “Average Man” in particular—corresponding to an ideal human being made of the aggregate of all average human attributes or characteristics, both physical and moral
(
Quetelet \APACyear1835/2013
)
.

Under this normative interpretation,
every
deviation from the normal/average is considered undesirable—as with the typical example of the “Body Mass Index” (BMI) originally called the “Quetelet Index”
(
Eknoyan \APACyear2008
, see)
.
As far as the BMI is concerned indeed, you are either “underweight” if deviating leftwards from the average, or “overweight” if deviating rightwards.
Only in the average dashed line Figure

.

Quetelet had a tremendous influence on modern psychology and on the behavioral sciences at large
(see
Jahoda \APACyear2015
;
Tafreshi \APACyear2022
, for overviews)
.
Many of his contributions have now made their way into modern Science as simply “good practice”—such as the removal of statistical outliers or the averaging of individual responses to experimental conditions
(see
Danziger \APACyear1990
, especially ch.5 on
The triumph of the aggregate
, for an overview)
.
Such methodological practices, however, remain grounded in a fundamental epistemological assumption, namely, that the normal/average ought to become to proper target of scientific inquiry, for only it characterizes something
objective
as far as human being is concerned.
This epistemological assumption is motivated by a fundamental empirical observation, in turn: that many human attributes (e.g. height, weight) follow a normal distribution across populations.
Those considerations constitute the theoretical underpinnings of Quetelet’s theory of the “Average Man”.

Quetelet’s scientific practice was crucially inspired by the metrological realism of the natural sciences of his time and of astronomy in particular—remember that he was an astronomer himself—which lead him to argue that individual variations could be treated as “error” around a “true” mean or average
(
Porter \APACyear1985
)
.
Astronomers had indeed long considered that individual measures of celestial objects necessarily contained some amount of error, but that such error could be mitigated by retaining only the
average
measure of their aggregates—an average measure supposed to capture the
true
measure of an otherwise
real
entity
(see also
Stigler \APACyear1986
, for a comprehensive overview)
.
Quetelet’s fundamental contribution to the Science of his time was to borrow astronomy’s method of averages but to apply it to
people
this time
(
Rose \APACyear2016
, ch.1)
. This gave us the now dominant paradigm for the study of human being, a paradigm dedicated to the study of normal responses, normal behaviors, normal brains and normal people in general and which I propose to call the
paradigm of normalism
.

2

2

Though historically associated with psychology and the behavioral sciences, normalism has since spread into other fields such as neuroscience.

Rose \APACyear2016
details indeed how the field of neuroscience remains fundamentally grounded in Quetelet’s epistemological assumptions, insofar as it is dedicated to the study of an “Average Brain”—paralleling the “Average Man” of Quetelet—quite literally made of an aggregate of individual brains, in the sense that every reported brain activity is always constituted of an averaging of individual brain imaging.
That Average Brain, in turn, is presumed to represent “the normal, typical brain, while each individual brain represents a variant of this normal brain”
(
Rose \APACyear2016
, p.20)
.

Normalism marked a radical turning point in the study of human being in the nineteenth century, as it “displaced the Enlightenment idea of human nature as a central organizing concept” to focus on “a model of normal people with laws of dispersion”
(
Hacking \APACyear1990
, pp.vii;xi)
.

Under this normalist paradigm, the concept of normality became a “metaconcept” that “provides a way of thinking that is applied to human beings—their actions and their societies—in every possible aspect” through “a completely unifying theme and even methodology”
(
Hacking \APACyear1996
, p.64)
.
A central argument of this paper then, and as we will see throughout the following sections, is that Turing’s study of human intelligence places itself directly under this normalist paradigm.

The second normative interpretation of the average then is when the normal is only the
mediocre
potentially in need of improvement.
It is the interpretation typically associated with the British eugenist Francis Galton this time and with his
Hereditary Genius
in particular
(
Galton \APACyear1869/1892
)
.
Under this normative interpretation—and unlike with that of Quetelet—
some
(though not all) deviations from the normal/average can prove desirable and be explicitly valued and praised
(see
MacKenzie \APACyear1981
, especially ch.3, for an introduction to Galton’s philosophy)
.
4
4

4

“Genius” in the words of Galton implies two things:
exceptionality
and
innateness
.
The word expresses “an ability that [is] exceptionally high, and at the same time inborn”
(
Galton \APACyear1869/1892
, p.viii)
.
The genius for Galton is still abnormal—as it characterizes a specific deviation from the (statistical) norm—but it is abnormal “good” this time as it constitutes a “desirable” atypicality.

It is the normative interpretation that we find behind the modern conception of intelligence underlying psychometrics tests such as IQ tests, which remain fundamentally grounded in the Galtonian figure of the genius
(see
Brody \APACyear2000
, for an overview)
.
Galton ultimately came up with a new representation of the normal curve to better represent the idea that the extreme right hand side of the normal curve—precisely deviating from the mediocre average—was actually in some circumstances the desired target: the
cumulative
normal curve.
At the far right of the cumulative normal curve in Figure
2
, you precisely find yourself “on top” of everybody else while at the average dashed line at the center of the curve, you are “just
average
”, so to speak, since
50\%
of the data points will be below you and
50\%
above you.

Distinct as they may be, those two normative interpretations of the normal/average—the good and the mediocre—remain nonetheless fundamentally intertwined, as the exceptional is always defined in relation to the (statistically) normal and as a specific deviation from it.
What is more, even when the normal/average does not constitute a normative
ideal
—as with the Galtonian interpretation of the average as mediocre—it remains at the very least an
idealization
as nobody is ever
perfectly
normal.
Nobody measures
exactly
the “average height” for instance, especially not when that average height is computed with infinite precision.
The normal/average thus often remains but a mathematical abstraction and “being normal” under the statistical interpretation of normality is more often than not a matter of remaining under an arbitrary threshold and of being “close enough” from an idealized value so as to be considered falling within its scope.

Within the field of artificial intelligence, this idea of “normative ideal” takes on a particular meaning for it specifically questions which type of human behavior will be modeled through the development of artificial minds: the normal/average or the exceptional?
The normative, as we have seen, specifies what is “correct” in a sense and so the specific question at stake will be that of what constitutes “correct behavior” as far as machines are concerned.
Will machines target normal/average or exceptional human intelligence?
From those two distinct normative ideals will arise two distinct scientific projects for the development of artificial minds:
artificial intelligence
on one side and what I propose to call
artificial smartness
on the other (see Table

My motivation for using the term “artificial intelligence” to refer to the project of modeling normal intelligence is rooted in the fact that Turing ihmself explicitly told us that he aimed at modeling the normal/average/mediocre mind of normal people rather than the exceptional mind of geniuses.
As he is reported to have said to his colleagues at the Bell Laboratories during his 1943 stay indeed:

I’m not interested in developing a
powerful
brain. All I’m after is just a
mediocre
brain, something like the President of the American Telephone and Telegraph Company.
(Alan Turing, as quoted in
Hodges \APACyear1983/2014
, p.316)

In the above quote, Turing makes clear that he places himself directly under the Galtonian normative interpretation of the normal as mediocre while at the same time shifting the target of inquiry from the exceptional to the normal.
In doing so, he brings back intelligence into normalism in full to focus on its standard object of study: the normal/average/typical intelligence of a normal/average/typical human mind.

2.2
From intelligence to normal human behavior

The first thing to bare in mind about intelligence as currently and conventionally understood by psychologists is that it is “a brashly modern notion”
(
Daston \APACyear1992
, p.21)
.
Before Galton operated a radical shift in the conception of intelligence leading to our modern and everyday understanding of the term, intelligence was primarily conceived as a universal faculty and as something that one either
has
or
does not
have rather than something that one can have
more or less
of
(see
Gonzalez \APACyear1979
;
Malabou \APACyear2019
;
Peterson \APACyear1925
, pp.44–46; ch.1; and ch.III, respectively, for overviews)
.
5
5

5

See also
(
Cave \APACyear2020
)
for an overview in the context of artificial intelligence.

As
Gonzalez \APACyear1979
makes clear: “In the eighteenth and in most of the nineteenth centuries it was thought that humans were endowed by nature with similar, if not identical, powers of the mind”.
Galton thus shifted the notion of intelligence from the universal to the singular and from an essential characteristic of our human nature to a singular attribute belonging only (in its highest form at least) to a “happy few”
(
Malabou \APACyear2019
, p.20)
.
He turned what was so far considered a shared attribute of all human beings into a mechanism that could be used to
compare
—and ultimately
rank
—each and every one of us with respect to some specific normative ideal or normative point of reference.
This gave us the conception of intelligence that we have today—a conception that is fundamentally articulated around (individual) variability rather than universality, as is typically illustrated by the 1996 report of the American Psychological Association (APA) on
Intelligence: Knowns and Unknowns
, which begins by the following statement:

Individuals differ from one another in their ability to understand complex ideas, to adapt effectively to the environment, to learn from experience, to engage in various forms of reasoning, to overcome obstacles by taking thought.
(
Neisser \BOthers. \APACyear1996
, p.77)

The conception of intelligence understood as a universal endowment of the human species has a long history in North Atlantic philosophy, tracing back to the notions of
intellect
,
understanding
and the Greek concept of
Noûs

(see
Malabou \APACyear2019
, ch.1)
.

Goldstein \APACyear2015
, for instance, provides a concise etymological overview of the term as follows:

The word intelligence derives from the Latin verb
intelligere
. This verb finds its roots in
inter-legere
, meaning to “pick out” or discern [
(
Traupman \APACyear2007
)
]. A form of this verb, intellectus, was the medieval technical term for someone with a good understanding as well as a translation for the Greek philosophical term nous. Nous, however, was strongly linked to the metaphysical, cosmological theories of teleological scholasticism, including theories of the immortality of the soul and the concept of the active intellect. Its entire approach to the study of nature, however, was rejected by modern philosophers, including Francis Bacon, Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and David Hume, all of whom preferred the word “understanding” in their English philosophical works. Hobbes, in his work
Latin De Corpore
, used the term “intellectus intelligit” or “the understanding understandeth” as a typical example of a logical absurdity. The term intelligence, therefore, has become less common in the English language philosophy but has been widely adopted in contemporary psychology, absent the scholastic theories which it once implied.
(
Goldstein \APACyear2015
, p.3)

Turing, as it so appears, places himself directly within this longstanding tradition.
In his seminal
Turing \APACyear1950
paper indeed, he introduces the concept of machine intelligence by asking the fundamental question “Can machines think?”, showing thereby that he uses the two terms
intelligence
and
thinking
interchangeably.
Such a conception of intelligence, however, remains fundamentally at odds with our everyday use of the term, as
Shieber \APACyear2004\APACexlab\BCnt1
rightly stresses in his introduction to the Turing test:

Turing used the terms “think” and “be intelligent” as if they were synonyms, as one can tell by a simple comparison of his article’s title and first sentence. In common usage, the two often mean quite distinct things. When I say that my son is intelligent, I usually mean something beyond the fact that he is capable of thought. However, I and many authors follow Turing’s practice, taking the notion of “being intelligent” under which it means “being capable of thought”, rather than “being smart”.
(
Shieber \APACyear2004\APACexlab\BCnt1
, footnote 2, p.6)

Block \APACyear1981
argues along similar lines when discussing Turing’s original question:

Note that the sense of “intelligent” deployed here—and generally in discussion of the Turing Test [footnote omitted]—is
not
the sense in which we speak of one person being more intelligent than another. “Intelligence” in the sense deployed here means something like the possession of thought or reason.
(
Block \APACyear1981
, p.8)

Intelligence, under the framing of the Turing test, is primarily approached as a human
faculty
—a species-level (maybe even species-
specific
) ability to “think” that one possesses by virtue of being human—and this “faculty of thinking” appears to directly echo the “faculty of understanding” which characterizes the historical (i.e. pre-Galtonian) conception of intelligence
(see
Malabou \APACyear2019
, p.3)
.

Examples illustrating such considerations do not stop at Turing’s framing of the problem, however.
Several authors have also pointed out that Turing’s conception of the mind
(underlying notably his concept of “child machine”, see
Turing \APACyear1950
, pp.455–460, for details)
bears strong resemblance to Locke’s (
Locke \APACyear1690/1975
)
tabula rasa
theory which implies a conception of the mind as a “blank slate” upon which environmental experiences (i.e. external stimuli) impress themselves
(
Mays \APACyear2001
, e.g.)
.
But as
Gonzalez \APACyear1979
fundamentally stresses, such a conception of the mind as a blank slate entails the general equality of the mind between individuals (thought not the equality of experiences) and echoes once again the concept of “faculty” as a universal property of human being.
Others, such as
Shieber \APACyear2004\APACexlab\BCnt2
,
6
6

6

See also
(
Abramson \APACyear2011
)
.

typically trace back the origins of the Turing test to Descartes and notably to the Chapter V of his
Discourse on the Method
where he tells us that:

[\ldots]
if any such machines resembled us in body and imitated our actions insofar as this was practically possible, we should still have two very certain means of recognizing that they were not, for all that, real human beings. [footnote omitted]
The first is that they would never be able to use words or other signs by composing them as we do to declare our thoughts to others. For we can well conceive of a machine made in such a way that it emits words,

[\ldots]

but it is not conceivable that it should put these words in different orders to correspond to the meaning of things said in its presence, as even the most dull-witted of men can do.
(
Descartes \APACyear1637/2006
, p.46)

And indeed, in the above passage, Descartes points at what constitutes two core assumptions of the Turing test: first, that linguistic behavior is a fundamental expression of our human nature and second, that this human nature is actually distinct from what we would today characterize as “intelligence”, as it is manifested even by “the most dull-witted of men”. As he further insists:

[\ldots]
it is a very remarkable fact that there are no men so dull-witted and stupid, not even madmen, that they are incapable of stringing together different words, and composing them into utterances, through which they let their thoughts be known; and, conversely, there is no other animal, no matter how perfect and well endowed by birth it may be, that can do anything similar.
(
Descartes \APACyear1637/2006
, p.47)

Central to the reflection of Descartes above is the question of what constitutes the distinctive feature that separates humans from non-human animals.
To him, that distinctive feature is
reason
(understood as a
rational soul
) and it is in fact not surprising to see the origins of the Turing test be traced back to Descartes since, as
Malabou \APACyear2019
tells us, the concept of intelligence as a faculty “played the same role as did reason during the Enlightenment”.

Turing’s conception of intelligence as a universal property of all human beings thus places itself directly in the footsteps of this cartesian tradition.
7
7

7

Which is not to say that Turing adheres to Descartes’ argument that “thinking” is impossible without reason, however, as he makes clear in his answer to “The Theological Objection”
(see
Turing \APACyear1950
, p.443)
.

Which is precisely why some authors such as
Fostel \APACyear1993
have been able to argue that the Turing test “is testing humanity, not intelligence”.
Intelligence for Turing is indeed a fundamental human ability, a property of our universally shared human nature and
not
, contrary to what our modern conception of the term suggests, a normative point of reference that serves to rank us with respect to one-another.

Now of course, approaching intelligence as a faculty and a universal endowment of the human species does not mean that it is necessarily readily available to every human being from birth.
Like other biological faculties—such as
language
or
vision
—it can be conceived as an ‘‘organ’’ that must grow and develop, notably in interaction with its environment and through exposure to external stimuli, before it can turn into an effective ability manifesting itself through observable behavior.
8
8

8

See notably
(
Anderson \BBA Lightfoot \APACyear2000
)
on approaching the language faculty as an organ, and the work of Noam Chomsky in general for the concept of
language faculty
.

Chomsky \APACyear2017
provides a concise and reader-friendly introduction to his work while
Chomsky \APACyear2005
explicitly refers to the language faculty as an “organ of the body”.

Until then, it would remain but a mere “potential”, as Turing himself makes clear in his earlier writings:

[\ldots]
the potentialities of the human intelligence can only be realised if suitable education is provided.
(
Turing \APACyear1948/2004
, pp.431–432)

Between
intelligence
and
intelligent behavior
thus lies the fundamental contribution of
development
, which is why the two cannot really be considered perfectly equivalent, even for Turing.

However, if Turing renews with a longstanding tradition in the conceptualization of intelligence, he also distances himself from it in a significant way.
For what constitutes the proper target of his scientific inquiry indeed is not this faculty of intelligence
per se
but only its overt manifestation: intelligent behavior.
To better understand this point, it may be useful here to compare the work of Turing to that of Chomsky—itself dedicated to another fundamental human faculty: the faculty of
language

(see
Chomsky \APACyear2017
, for a brief overview)
.
To characterize the content of the language faculty is, for Chomsky, to delineate the boundaries of human cognition—to understand, in some sense, what
can
and
cannot be
human.
The ultimate goal of linguistics under the Chomskyan approach is thus “To define the class of possible human languages”
(
Moro \APACyear2016
, p.1)
.
The concept of
possible
here opposes another fundamental epistemological concept: the
probable
and, through it, the concept of statistical normality.
Within the Chomskyan approach to linguistics indeed, statistically prevalent linguistic behaviors play no particular role in investigating the boundaries of human cognition: nothing makes “English” or “Mandarin” more valid objects of study than “Quechua” or “Breton”, for instance, as exhibiting a higher number of speakers does not make a particular language a more relevant object of study when the ultimate goal is to determine the boundaries of human cognition.
The statistical prevalence of a particular language or sets of linguistic behaviors is thus irrelevant to the Chomskyan approach to linguistics, as its paradigm is fundamentally dedicated to what
can
be human and not to what is
likely
to be human.
9
9

9

If anything, the Chomskyan paradigm actually requires us to pay particular attention to the atypical and the (statistically) abnormal since, “unlikely” as it may be, it remains nonetheless a possible manifestation of what can be human.

It is in that sense that we can say that the Chomskyan approach to language is ‘‘anti-normalist’’: the concept of statistical normality plays little to no role in its epistemology and does not constitute its target of scientific inquiry.
10
10

10

Which is why, in passing, Chomsky and colleagues have been so critical of the use of probabilistic models in linguistics
(
Chomsky \APACyear1957/2002
;
Chomsky \APACyear1968
;
Chomsky \BOthers. \APACyear2023
;
Moro \BOthers. \APACyear2023
;
Bolhuis \BOthers. \APACyear2024
, see)
.
When Chomsky tells us that “probabilistic models give no particular insight into some of the basic problems of syntactic structure”
(
Chomsky \APACyear1957/2002
, p.17)
or that “it must be recognized that the notion of ‘probability of a sentence’ is an entirely useless one, under any known interpretation of this term”
(
Chomsky \APACyear1968
, p.57)
, his argument is not—contrary to what some computational linguistics may have interpreted
(
Pereira \APACyear2000
;
Norvig \APACyear2012
, e.g.)
—a mere technical point regarding the capabilities of statistical language models to capture nuances of grammaticality (such as the difference between the grammaticality of the sentence
colorless green ideas sleep furiously
and the agrammaticality of the sentence
furiously sleep ideals green colorless
).
What is at stake is a central epistemological argument regarding the role that the probable (and statistical normality in general) is supposed to play within the language sciences.
As far as Chomsky’s approach is concerned, the matter is clear: the critical concept is that of the
possible
and not the probable.
Which is precisely why his recent critique of large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT focuses on the fact that “they are incapable of distinguishing the possible from the impossible”
(
Chomsky \BOthers. \APACyear2023
)
.
The argument is taken on by
Moro \BOthers. \APACyear2023
who tell us that “the distinction between possible versus impossible languages cannot be formulated by definition for LLM” as well as by
Bolhuis \BOthers. \APACyear2024
who argue that “LLMs can produce ‘impossible’ languages, not generated by the principles governing all known human languages, just as well as (if not better than) natural language output, and cannot distinguish between them”.
Else, the fact that Chomskyan linguistics constitutes a fundamental divorce from the normalist paradigm—as it attempts to bring back human nature in place of normality as its central organizing concept—is made explicit by
Hacking \APACyear1996
and by Chomsky himself (though somewhat less explicitly) in his
Cartesian Linguistics

(
Chomsky \APACyear1966/2009
)
.

In comparison, the Turing test does not aim at delineating the boundaries of human cognition or at characterizing
all
forms of intelligent behavior for that matter.
It does not actually provide an “operational” definition of intelligence as there is no equivalence relationship between possessing intelligence and successfully passing the Turing test
(
Moor \APACyear1976
;
Moor \APACyear2001
;
Copeland \APACyear2000
)
.
Turing makes that clear in his original paper when he tells us that:

May not machines carry out something which ought to be described as thinking but which is very different from what a man does? This objection is a very strong one, but at least we can say that if, nevertheless, a machine can be constructed to play the imitation game satisfactorily, we need not be troubled by this objection.
(
Turing \APACyear1950
, p.435)

Failing to pass the Turing test thus does
not
rule out something (or someone) as being intelligent.
At most, what we can is that the Turing test aims at providing a
sufficient
condition for the possession of intelligence, but certainly not a
necessary
one.
11
11

11

Just as displaying English linguistic behavior could in some sense be considered sufficient to attest of the possession of the language faculty (though even that is actually debatable) but certainly not necessary.

What makes Turing’s approach fundamentally normalist in comparison to Chomsky’s is that it does not target the boundaries of the faculty of intelligence but only its typical manifestation (or at least
one
of its typical manifestation)—not intelligence proper but only intelligent behavior understood as normal human behavior, or at least
a
normal human behavior.
For indeed, note how, in the above quote,
Turing \APACyear1948/2004
employs the term ‘‘education’’ rather than ‘‘development’’ to discuss the transition from intelligence to intelligent behavior—using a term that evokes a rather socio-culturally and socio-historically situated practice in comparison, insofar as it seems much less straightforward to talk about ‘‘human education’’ than to talk about ‘‘human development’’.
Turing’s terminological choice suggests, even if only implicitly, that what we take to be ‘‘intelligent behavior’’ may depend on the ‘‘education’’ we have received and so, ultimately, on the particular group of humans we belong to.
12
12

12

And indeed, many have argued that the normative point of reference we use to rank intelligent behaviors—or even what constitutes the
set
of intelligent behaviors to begin with—is very much socio-culturally (and socio-historically) situated
(see
Vernon \APACyear1965
;
Vernon \APACyear1969/2013
;
Berry \APACyear1972
;
Berry \APACyear1980
;
Sternberg \APACyear1984
;
Sternberg \APACyear1985
;
Sternberg \APACyear2004
, for overviews)
.

It also explains why the Turing test can be considered a test of “culturally-oriented human intelligence” that “could be passed only by things that have experienced the world as we have experienced it”
(
French \APACyear1990
, p.53)
.
The argument is not restricted to intelligence, however, as we also develop all sorts of group-specific developmental features depending on our environment.
For instance, we all acquire sets of group-specific linguistic behaviors depending on the particular linguistic environment we grow up in and the linguistic stimuli we are exposed to.
Thus, we all end up speaking or signing a particular ‘‘language’’, be it English, Turkish, Swahili, etc., that is always both socio-culturally and socio-historically situated, despite our purportedly sharing a common language faculty.
13
13

13

The argument extends to other fundamental human faculties such as
bipedalism
, for instance, for which upright posture and bipedal gait have been considered “outstanding features of human nature”
(
Hewes \APACyear1961
, p.687)
.
Anthropologists have indeed long shown that the human bipedal striding gait is affected by socio-cultural factors so that “The striding gait would be better viewed as a biologically based cultural trait that predominates as a locomotor form in our own society but one that should not be applied as such to other groups in an ethnocentric way”
(
Devine \APACyear1985
, p.551)
.
As
Devine \APACyear1985
elaborates: “Everyone is aware of subtle differences in gait that exist between individuals. One of the first experiences of early visitors to non-Western societies was an immediate realization that the manner of walking of the people they were encountering was quite dissimilar from their own”.

The ambition of the Turing test is therefore quite modest, all things considered, as it does not aim to model
all
possible normal human behaviors or even
the
normal human behavior (in the sense of the most statistically prevalent behavior within the human population as a whole) but only
a
possible normal human behavior—the average/typical behavior of a particular community of human beings.

3
Normality against exceptionality

3.1
Intelligence against smartness

As we understand from the previous section, it is not so much Turing’s conception of intelligence as a faculty that makes it fundamentally at odds with our everyday use of the term—as it does not really constitute the target of his scientific inquiry—but his conception of intelligent behavior as normal rather than exceptional human behavior.
To make the matter clearer, what I propose in this section is to reframe the opposition between normal and exceptional human behavior as the opposition between intelligent and smart behavior so as to better introduce the critical distinction I wish to make between artificial intelligence and what I call “artificial smartness”.
My core contribution in this section is that artificial intelligence and artificial smartness correspond to two different scientific projects for the construction of artificial minds, each targeting a different normative ideal of human behavior: the normal on one side and the exceptional on the other (see Table
2
).

The tension between (artificial) intelligence and (artificial) smartness is actually present throughout the literature on artificial minds, albeit under different forms and using different terminologies.
Harnad, for instance, approaches the normal/exceptional dichotomy through the real/ideal one—emphasizing thereby how smart behavior characterizes a normative
ideal
of human behavior.
His point is that, to successfully pass the Turing test, a machine needs to be able to do “many (perhaps most, perhaps all) of the things a real person can do”
(
Harnad \APACyear1991
, p.43)
.
He thus opposes “real” people who display normal/typical/average human behavior to geniuses like Einstein who, if no less “real” than normal people, still constitute significant deviations from the normal/average and, as such, cannot constitute the target of machines aiming at passing a Turing test which requires them to be indistinguishable from normal people:

As to Einstein well, it seems a rather high standard to hold computers to, considering that almost every one of us would probably likewise fail resoundingly to meet it.
(
Harnad \APACyear1991
, p.43)

Others, such as Russell and Norvig, mobilize a concept of
reason
or
rationality
, loosely defined as the ability to “do the right thing”, and which emphasizes this time the
normative
aspect of an ideal of smart behavior from which most people deviate:

We are not suggesting that humans are “irrational” in the dictionary sense of “deprived of normal mental clarity.”
We are merely conceding that human decisions are not always mathematically perfect.
(
Russell \BBA Norvig \APACyear2020
, p.31)

In practice indeed, real people ‘‘make mistakes’’, and that is precisely what makes them humans.
14
14

14

Pinsky \APACyear1951
notably writes that humans are “unique [in the universe] by virtue of the ability to
mis
use the faculty of reason”.

Such considerations underline two things.
First, that smart behavior constitutes a normative ideal of
correct
(if not
perfect
) human behavior, in addition to being a normative ideal of exceptional human behavior from which normal/average humans deviate.
Second, that intelligent behavior for Turing, as a particular deviation from this normative ideal of correct and exceptional human behavior, specifically targets the imperfections of our human nature.
Turing makes clear indeed in earlier writings that “If a machine is expected to be infallible, it cannot also be intelligent”
(
Turing \APACyear1947/1992
, p.105)
.
In his original
Mind
paper, he tells us explicitly that a machine would have to “deliberately introduce mistakes in a manner calculated to confuse the interrogator” so as to be able to pass his test
(
Turing \APACyear1950
, p.448)
.
To successfully pass the Turing test, a machine thus needs to be able to “make mistakes” so as to behave in an “incorrect” or “imperfect” way—just like normal people would.

The best way to understand the dichotomy between intelligent and smart behavior is through the example of
spelling
which constitutes (in English at least) a typical example of a normative ideal of correct behavior from which most if not all people deviate (see Table

).

Even the best speller indeed is bound to make a spelling mistake at some point, whether it is because of temporary fatigue, distraction, memory lapse, etc., and even though they may otherwise know perfectly well the correct form of the expression at hand.

15

15

“Mistakes are a fact of life” write Lunsford and Lunsford in their review of first year college students writing in the USA, for which they report a typical error rate of 2 to 3 mistakes per 100 word
(see
Lunsford \BBA Lunsford \APACyear2008
, Table 8)
.
Note that their concept of “error” includes notions such as “vague pronoun reference” or “unnecessary comma” which probably extends much beyond what is relevant to our present discussion, but that at the same time their concept of “spelling error” excludes notions such as “missing word/comma/hyphen/capitalization” which I would definitely include in our concept of “spelling mistake”
(see
Lunsford \BBA Lunsford \APACyear2008
, and notably Table 4 and Table 7, for details)
.
Flor and colleagues otherwise report that only 10.7% of essays from native speakers of English in the TOEFL or the GRE exams contain no spelling mistake
(see
Flor \BOthers. \APACyear2015
, Table 3)
.

In fact, Turing explicitly says in a 1952 BBC interview that, in order to successfully pass his test, “the machine would be permitted all sorts of tricks so as to appear more man-like, such as waiting a bit before giving the answer, or making spelling mistakes”
(
Turing \BOthers. \APACyear1952/2004
, p.495)
.
And indeed, practical Turing tests carried out in 2008 at the University of Reading
(
Shah \BBA Warwick \APACyear2010\APACexlab\BCnt1
;
Shah \BBA Warwick \APACyear2010\APACexlab\BCnt2
)
, or in 2012 at Bletchley Park
(
Warwick \BBA Shah \APACyear2014
;
Warwick \BBA Shah \APACyear2016\APACexlab\BCnt2
)
show that the production of perfect spelling by machines calibrated on smart behavior is often precisely what makes them detectable by human judges.
Warwick and Shah note, for instance, that, on one occasion, “the judge did correctly identify the human entity as there were a lot of spelling mistakes in their discourse and the conversation was quite stilted” and conclude from their review of practical Turing tests that “the occasional spelling mistake seems to add human credibility”
(see
Warwick \BBA Shah \APACyear2016\APACexlab\BCnt1
, pp.1001 and 1003, respectively)
.

Acknowledged as it may be by the literature, the distinction between artificial intelligence and artificial smartness is nonetheless usually framed as a distinction between “useless” and “useful” artificial models, emphasizing thereby the potential limitations of a Turing test dedicated to “incorrect” behavior of some sort.
Hayes and Ford, for instance, argue in their paper
Turing Test Considered Harmful
that “Our most useful computer applications (including Al programs) are often valuable exactly by virtue of their lack of humanity” and that “A truly human-like program would be nearly useless”
(
Hayes \BBA Ford \APACyear1995
, p.975)
.
They conclude accordingly that the Turing test “is no longer a useful idea” and even that “it is now a burden to the field, damaging its public reputation and its own intellectual coherence”
(
Hayes \BBA Ford \APACyear1995
, p.972)
.
The argument rejoins that of French who would later argue that:

[\ldots]
we need to put aside the attempt to build a machine that can flawlessly imitate humans; for example, do we really need to build computers that make spelling mistakes or occasionally add numbers incorrectly, as in Turing’s original article [footnote omitted] in order to fool people into thinking they are human?
(
French \APACyear2012
, pp.74–75)

The argument is not new: “who needs a machine that can’t type?” asked
The Economist
already in a
The Economist \APACyear1992
article on
Artificial Stupidity
dedicated to the results of the first edition of the
Loebner Prize Competition
held at the Computer Museum in Boston on November 8 1991
(see
Epstein \APACyear1992
, for details)
.
16
16

16

Note that it is precisely the results of those experiments that lead Fostel to introduce his humanity/intelligence dichotomy:

The recent Loebner trial [
(
The Economist \APACyear1992
)
], in which a computer program achieved considerable success, perhaps passing [the Turing test] as construed for that trial, demonstrates further that [the Turing test] tests for humanity.
The computer program used carefully calculated typing errors to fool [the human judge] into believing it to be human.
The capacity to make statistically human-like typing errors is not a convincing step towards intelligence yet it seems to be a major step towards passing [the Turing test]. It made the program more human.
(
Fostel \APACyear1993
, p.8)

The vocabulary associated with artificial smartness models is typically that of
useful
and
profitable
artifacts dedicated to
realistic
tasks and
practical
problems—by opposition to artificial intelligence models aiming at successfully passing the Turing test and which, as such, are considered (even if only implicitly) useless and unprofitable models dedicated to unrealistic and abstract tasks and problems.
Such is the case of
Bringsjord \BBA Govindarajulu \APACyear2022
, for instance, who, in their encyclopedic entry on
Artificial Intelligence
, tell us that “most AI researchers and developers, in point of fact, are simply concerned with building useful, profitable artifacts, and don’t spend much time reflecting upon the kinds of abstract definitions of intelligence explored in this entry (e.g., What Exactly
is
AI?)”. Or of
Russell \BBA Norvig \APACyear2020
who argue that “Few AI researchers pay attention to the Turing test, preferring to concentrate on their systems’ performance on practical tasks, rather than the ability to imitate humans”. In fact, Turing himself wrote, in a letter to the cyberneticist W. Ross Ashby, that “I am more interested in the possibility of producing models of the action of the brain than in the practical applications to computing”
(see
Turing \APACyear1947/2004
, p.374)
.
However, framing the distinction between artificial intelligence and artificial smartness in terms of absolute usefulness is somewhat misleading, as nothing is ever intrinsically “useful” in and of itself.
In effect, there is nothing about artificial smartness that makes it
inherently
more “useful” or “profitable” than artificial intelligence—everything depends on the target application and on its context of use.
I thus propose to introduce the two notions as simply referring to two different scientific projects dedicated to the modeling of two different normative ideals of human behavior (see Figure
4
and Figure
4
).

Turing, here again, provides us with the means to synthesize our discussion so far.
In his original paper, he warns us against a possible confusion between “rules of conduct” and “laws of behavior”
(
Turing \APACyear1950
, p.452)
which allows us to recast the intelligence/smartness dichotomy as the opposition between
social
and
biological
norms—or between
norms
and
regularities
of behavior.
Smart behavior, as a normative ideal of correct and exceptional human behavior, corresponds to those “rules of conduct” which specify how people
ought
to behave by opposition to an intelligent behavior which, understood as normal/typical/average human behavior, specifies how they
do
behave in practice.
What Turing reminds us here is that his whole enterprise is to be understood as belonging to the biological sciences and as a project dedicated to the understanding of the functioning of the human mind—a way, as Harnard would later put it, to “reverse-engineer” human cognition
(see
Turing \APACyear1950/2009
, p.24)
.
Under the normalist paradigm, however, to understand something about the human mind is to understand something about the normal/typical/average mind.
So is Turing, as a scientist of its time, ultimately focusing on the normal mind rather than the human mind strictly speaking.

3.2
Why ChatGPT is unlikely to pass the Turing test

With those distinction in mind, we can now better make sense of recent results which argue that ChatGPT falls short of passing the Turing test
(
Restrepo Echavarría \APACyear2025
, e.g.)
.
They stand in the long tradition of studies which have consistently argued that machines exhibiting exceptional human behavior would not be capable of passing the Turing test.
Back in 1991, for instance, and after a Loebner Prize Competition which saw a human participant being repeatedly misclassified as a computer due to her extensive knowledge of Shakespeare
(
Epstein \APACyear1992
, p.88)
,
17
17

17

See also
(
Shieber \APACyear1994
, p.72)
.

Fostel would argue that:

Suppose [an alien] robot had scanned and absorbed all of Earth’s libraries, media, daily conversations and so on. Would the robot be able to pass [the Turing test]? I think not.

[\ldots]

the Robot will be so different from any available human
[\ldots]
that this failure in discrimination by [a human judge] would be unlikely.
A nearly omniscient agent
[\ldots]
would dearly not be like any human
[\ldots]
Even if the alien robot is extremely capable, and superior to any human in intellectual endeavors, it will fail [the Turing test].
(
Fostel \APACyear1993
, p.8)

Fastforwarding to 2025 and Restrepo Echavarría tells us specifically that ChatGPT (in its GPT-4 version at least) fails to pass Turing test because “its intellectual abilities surpass those of individual humans” and because it simply proves “too smart” to be human
(see
Restrepo Echavarría \APACyear2025
, pp.1 and 5, respectively)
.
In short, ChatGPT fails to pass the Turing test because it is a model of artificial smartness rather than artificial intelligence
per se

(
Turing \APACyear1950
, that is, in the original sense of)
.

The fact that, even after significant tweaking
(see
Restrepo Echavarría \APACyear2025
, §3, for details)
, ChatGPT still struggles to exhibit near-normal intelligence tells us precisely that ChatGPT remains, at its core, a model of exceptional rather than normal human intelligence.
18
18

18

Which is precisely how we should interpret the fact that it is reported to score higher than 99.9% of the population of Verbal IQ tests
(

)
or that it outperforms most average performance on academic and professional exams
(see

.
As a model of artificial smartness, ChatGPT targets exceptional human intelligence located at the far right of the normal curve as far as those exam performance are concerned.

And in some sense we can understand why: take the example of spelling once again.
Why would anyone design a language model that deliberately makes spelling mistakes when you know that spelling mistakes can cost you millions in online sales or advertising revenue
(
Everard \BBA Galletta \APACyear2005
;
Coughlan \APACyear2011
;
Mozafari \BOthers. \APACyear2019
)
, affect your online credibility
(
Stiff \APACyear2012
;
Cooper \BOthers. \APACyear2020
)
or the perception of the quality of your writing
(
Kreiner \BOthers. \APACyear2002
;

, or that even a single spelling mistake can earn you a million pounds lawsuit in the UK

See

Yet again, it is important to remember here that the fact that ChatGPT targets exceptional rather than normal intelligence is contingent and has nothing to do with a purportedly intrinsic usefulness of artificial smartness over artificial intelligence.
The fact that ChatGPT—in its default behavior at least—exhibits a behavior calibrated on perfect spelling rather than normal/average spelling has probably more to do with a socio-economic reality and with the fact that, for most of the applications the system is currently being put to use, it makes more sense for it to exhibit perfect or near-perfect spelling rather than normal/average spelling.
In a different context and for specific applications—e.g., create a chatbot that should ‘‘go undercover’’ and appear as normal as possible in an online forum or social medium for instance—it may very well prove more beneficial to target normal rather than exceptional human behavior—in which case the Turing test would prove more relevant than ever.
20
20

20

Note that the distinction between intelligence and smartness is not strictly binary and admits infinite nuance, in the sense that there could exist many use-cases which would require imperfect behavior that is not exactly normal/average (so something in between intelligent/normal and smart/exceptional human behavior).
Think, for instance, of the typical use-case of trying to model the behavior of a “good” student, precisely
not
average but not “perfect” either as a perfect student would just be too good to be true and therefore too easily detectable by the teacher.

In fact, this is precisely the point made by Fostel in his original paper who, upon acknowledging that the Turing test targets “humanity” rather than “intelligence”—that is, a form of normal rather than exceptional human behavior—tells us that:

This does not mean the [Turing test] is useless.
It might be a useful marketing tool for 21st century firms producing domestic robots.
No one is going to want a vacuum cleaner that speaks better English than they do.
Talking alarms in cars have already earned considerable ire by noting minor errors their human occupants make in closing doors.
Harnad’s [Total Turing test, see
(

] is an even better candidate for that marketing function: who wants a baby-sitting robot that makes mom or dad look bad?
(

That being said, what are we to make of those other results which claim that “ChatGPT broke the Turing test”
(
Biever \APACyear2023
)
or that “People cannot distinguish GPT-4 from a human in a Turing test”
(

)
?
Glossing over experimental considerations as to whether such work strictly align with the original spirit and specifications of the Turing test
(see
Restrepo Echavarría \APACyear2025
, §1, for a discussion)
there are at least two things that we could say.
First of all, the Turing test is fundamentally
flexible
.
That is, we do not know in advance what will be the topic of conversation (which remains free and unconstrained) and so which aspect of human behavior will be targeted for evaluation.
As
Shieber \APACyear2004\APACexlab\BCnt1
puts it, “The
open-ended
nature of the interaction is crucial because it allows any possible area of human experience to be used as criterial in the decision’’.
That point is important precisely because not every aspect of human behavior is necessarily characterized by a normative ideal of correct and exceptional behavior deviating from the normal/average.
This is in fact where the example of spelling I have used so far may show its limit: not every aspect of human behavior behaves like English spelling does with respect to normal behavior, insofar as not every human behavior is characterized by a significant gap between normal/average and correct behavior.
Not every spelling system even behaves like English in the first place, so the example may prove even more restricted in scope.

21

English spelling is indeed notorious for exhibiting a many-to-many mapping between phonemes and graphemes.
This particular feature is often blamed for making English spelling very hard to (perfectly) acquire, especially in comparison to more straightforward spelling systems which exhibit a one-to-one mapping between phonemes and graphemes, such as Serbo-Croatian

.
This may explain, in turn, why English spelling is characterized by a significant gap between normal/average and correct spelling.
See

For the limits of artificial smartness models to become salient as far as the Turing test is concerned, those models need to be confronted to types of human behavior which precisely show a significant gap between intelligent and smart behavior—like English spelling typically does with respect to normal/average and correct/perfect spelling.
Otherwise—and especially in the case where the normal equates the correct—a machine trained on what is “correct” will appear normal and so will seem capable of passing the Turing test.

Second, those results may not actually prove robust through time.
Let us not forget indeed that artificial smartness models are often still evolving models which have not quite yet reached “perfect smartness”.
And as we see from Figure

That stage, however, can only be temporary at best if such models keep progressing towards perfectly smart behavior, and we should thus observe that such models become increasingly unlikely to pass the Turing test as they become progressively more and more capable of successfully modeling smart behavior.
The purpose of the present analysis is therefore not to negate that a certain “progress” has been achieved in the field, but rather to question
what
is driving this progress and more specifically
which normative ideal
of human behavior.

To conclude, the purpose of this section is not to argue that language models such as ChatGPT could
never
be made so as to robustly pass the Turing test, for the argument carried out here is not an ontological one but an analytic one.
That is, the the point is not to argue about the intrinsic capabilities of language models but rather to discuss their modeling targets as far as human behavior in concerned. If anything, the observed limitations of ChatGPT with respect to the Turing test have more to do with the
data
it has been trained on than its
architecture
, for instance.

22

I am implicitly assuming here that two (statistical) language models can be considered the “same models” if they share an identical architecture or overall design, even if the two rely on different datasets.
This somewhat contradicts standard usage, especially within the machine-learning community, which usually talks about “different models” the moment those are (pre-)trained or fine-tuned (in the case of machine learning models) on different datasets.
My motivations for doing so is to emphasize that the core of the problem does not lie with the nature of those language models and especially not with their being statistical/machine-learning models, which would probably be considered a deeper problem from an engineering perspective.

The point is that, given their current applications and overall context of use, it makes more sense for such models to be trained on “smart” rather than “intelligent” data.
If nothing prevents us from training those same language models on data containing mistakes so as to model intelligent behavior, however, what is crucial to understand is that
not any mistake goes
as far as the Turing test is concerned.
Those models would have to be trained on

human mistakes indeed if they intend to successfully pass the Turing test.
And let us not underestimate the challenge that represents determining every aspect of normal/average human behavior and refactoring our training datasets accordingly.

23

23

Just imagine what it would mean to refactor our entire training datasets on normal/average rather than correct/perfect English spelling, for instance.

What
Turing \APACyear1950
offered us with his test was thus much more than just a simple game. It was a genuine scientific project.

4
Normality and the statistical type

4.1
The Turing test is a statistical test

There is, no doubt, an unsophisticated usage according to which what is normal is what is familiar, and the unfamiliar is feared or condemned as abnormal. But since we are all sophisticated this need not detain us.
(
Dupré \APACyear1998
, p.221)

In her paper deconstructing Turing’s conception of intelligence,
Proudfoot \APACyear2017
argues against the standard “behaviorist” interpretation of the Turing test by claiming that the test does not actually evaluate machine behavior but only “the observer’s reaction to the machine”
(
Proudfoot \APACyear2013
;
Proudfoot \APACyear2020
, see also)
.
Her argument rejoins that of
Watt \APACyear1996
which claims that “The ascription of intelligence [in the Turing test] depends on the observer as well as the behaviour of the system” and that intelligence for Turing may, like beauty, “truly be in the eye—or the mind—of the beholder”.
The underlying argument suggests—even if only implicitly—that the Turing test suffers from a major shortcoming in that it fundamentally relies on the subjectivity of the human interrogator to settle on machine intelligence.
Such considerations are what make Hayes and Ford, for instance, argue that:

The imitation game conditions say nothing about the judge, but the success of the game depends crucially on how clever, knowledgeable, and insightful the judge is.
(
Hayes \BBA Ford \APACyear1995
, p.973)

However, the argument neglects a fundamental aspect of the Turing test, namely, that it is a
statistical
test where success is never assessed by a single judge but always by a full
jury

(see notably
Guccione \BBA Tamburrini \APACyear1988
, pp.38–39)
.
Turing himself makes that clear in his original paper when he discusses the possibility to carry out his Imitation Game already:

There are already a number of digital computers in working order, and it may be asked, ‘Why not try the experiment straight away? It would be easy to satisfy the conditions of the game. A number of interrogators could be used, and statistics compiled to show how often the right identification was given.’
(
Turing \APACyear1950
, p.436)

Remember that the Turing test involves a tripartite configuration where a human interrogator converses with both a human participant and a machine.
In its minimal interpretation, a “single occurrence” of the test can thus be understood as a few minutes long conversation resulting in the production of a classification judgment by a single human interrogator.
The statistical aspect of the test then requires multiple conversations to be carried out between the same participants so as to be able to produce robust classification judgments. The underlying idea being that the open-ended nature of the test makes it difficult for a single conversation to avoid over-emphasizing idiosyncratic aspects of human behavior potentially irrelevant to the assessment of machine intelligence.
Shieber, for instance, typically tells us that:

The
statistical
aspect of the decision is fortuitous since on any given running of a Test, even between two people, one of the two will be selected out. Failure on a single test therefore cannot be taken to be indicative of anything at all; the statistical approach moves the test in the direction of testing a disposition or capacity, rather than a singleton behavior.
(
Shieber \APACyear2004\APACexlab\BCnt1
, p.8)

Yet, even when Turing emphasizes this necessity to carry out repeated occurrences of the test across the same participants, he also stresses that those different occurrences must involve a whole
jury
rather than a single human judge:
24
24

24

Note also that in his above quote he mentions that “A number of interrogators could be used”
(
Turing \APACyear1950
, p.436)
making explicit that the jury involves multiple interrogators.

We had better suppose that each jury has to judge quite a number of times, and that sometimes they really are dealing with a man and not a machine. That will prevent them saying ‘It must be a machine’ every time without proper consideration.
(
Turing \BOthers. \APACyear1952/2004
, p.495)

In other words, the statistical aspect of the test involves
both
the aggregation of different judgments carried out by the same judge across multiple conversations with the same participants (and in particular the same machine)
and
the aggregation of multiple judgments carried out by
different
judges for the same participants.

The best way to understand this critical aspect of the test is to return to Turing’s original paper once again and to his famous prediction where he tells us that:

I believe that in about fifty years’ time it will be possible to programme computers, with a storage capacity of about
10^{9}
, to make them play the imitation game so well that an average interrogator will not have more than 70 per cent, chance of making the right identification after five minutes of questioning.
(
Turing \APACyear1950
, p.442)

The statistical aspect of the test becomes clear from the probabilistic framing of success as a matter of (70%)
chance
of making the right guess.
Incidentally, it also stresses how the Turing test is fundamentally an
indistinguishibility
test and not a
misidentification
test, since this 70% chance implies that a machine does not have to be systematically misidentified as humans to successfully pass the test—though strictly statistically speaking, true indistinguishibility involves a 50% chance of being identified either as human either as machine.
25
25

25

The fact that Turing suggests a 70% chance rather than 50% chance in his original paper can probably be put—alongside the choice of considering only 5min of questioning—on the account of trying to provide a reasonably challenging goal for a task that was back then considered hard enough already.
In effect, a baseline of 70% makes the test slightly “easier” than a baseline of 50%, since with the former the average human interrogator is precisely given “more chance” to make the right guess than with the latter.
In what follows, I will only consider true indistinguishibility and a 50% baseline.

But the statistical aspect of the test also conditions how we ought to interpret this notion of “average interrogator” that
Turing \APACyear1950
talks about in his above quote.
Most of the time, this notion is understood through the normal/exceptional dichotomy and as referring to a normal/average interrogator opposing an exceptional/expert one.
Copeland, for instance, tells us that:

The qualification ‘average’ presumably indicates that the interrogators are not to be computer scientists, psychologists, or others whose knowledge or training is likely to render them more skilled at the task than an average member of the population.
(
Copeland \APACyear2000
, p.527)

This is not surprising given that Turing himself says in his later BBC interview that:

The idea of the test is that the machine has to try and pretend to be a man, by answering questions put to it, and it will only pass if the pretence is reasonably convincing.
A considerable proportion of a jury, who should not be expert about machines, must be taken in by the pretence.
(
Turing \BOthers. \APACyear1952/2004
, p.495)

Note, however, that
Turing \APACyear1950
talks about “
an
average interrogator” (singular) in his original paper rather than “average interrogator
s
” (plural), suggesting thereby that this notion of “average” applies to the
set
of interrogators as a whole rather than to every human interrogator taken individually.
And indeed, it is also possible to interpret this notion of “average interrogator” as referring to an
abstraction
made of the aggregate of individual interrogators’ judgments—an abstraction corresponding to the standard objectivization practice of the behavioral sciences consisting in averaging individual judgments so as to smooth out intrinsic inter-and intra-subject variability.
26
26

26

The two interpretations are not incompatible with one-another, of course, as it is perfectly possible to consider
both
the average interrogator to characterize an aggregate of multiple interrogators
and
all of them to be individually average (in the sense of non-expert).

So when Moor reframes the question underlying the Turing test as follows:

On the average after
n
minutes or
m
questions is an interrogator’s probability of correctly identifying which respondent is a machine significantly greater than 50 percent?
(
Moor \APACyear1976
, p.249)

His reference to the “average” can be seen as an explicit connection to the standard methodological practices of experimental psychology since, as
Tafreshi \APACyear2022
tells us, “when psychologists make inferences based on aggregate-level statistics, they make claims regarding what is true
on average
[
(
Bakan \APACyear1967
;
Lamiell \APACyear2003
)
]”.
The underlying assumption—which
Gigerenzer \APACyear1987
refers to as the “nomothetic ideal”—is that individual variability should be treated as “error” around a true mean which alone characterizes an objective reality.
The statistical aspect of the Turing test thus rests on an ideal of
objectivity
that lies at the core of the normalist paradigm which grounds most if not all of the behavioral sciences today
(see
Krüger \BOthers. \APACyear1987
, Part I, for a comprehensive overview)
. Under this normalist paradigm, statistics constitutes an objectivization tool that enables the elimination of subjectivity in both subjects and experimenters
(see
Gigerenzer \APACyear1987
, for details)
.
In the case of the Turing test, those ‘‘experimenters’’ are human interrogators which are all presumed to have different (and potentially erroneous) appreciations of what it means to be ‘‘human’’ at the individual level. And indeed, Warwick and Shah observe from their practical Turing test that:
27
27

27

The unreliability of human judgment as to what should be considered human becomes even clearer in the so-called “viva-voce” configuration of the Turing test which involves only a single participant this time (human or machine) being evaluated for humanness by a human interrogator
(see
Turing \APACyear1950
, p.446, for the original mention)
.
The phenomenon of human misidentification (humans being misidentified as machines) is what
Shah \BBA Henry \APACyear2005
refer to as the “confederate effect” and which is analyzed more extensively in
(
Warwick \BBA Shah \APACyear2015
)
.

[\ldots]
subjectivity plays a big part in attributing ‘humanness’ to another on the basis of responses to chosen questions.
(
Warwick \BBA Shah \APACyear2015
, p.11)

Hence the necessity to aggregate judgments from a pool of human interrogators so as to smoothen out those individual variations.

Be that as it may, the use of statistics in the Turing test produces an apparent paradox, one that makes Hayes and Ford argue that:

[\ldots]
whatever that quality is [that the Turing test is testing] it cannot be characteristic of humanity, since many humans would fail a Turing Test.
Since one of the players must be judged to be a machine, half the human population would fail the species test.
(
Hayes \BBA Ford \APACyear1995
, p.974)

The apparent paradox disappears, however if we acknowledge that the Turing test is not a test of “humanity” strictly speaking but a test of
normality
.
That is, human interrogators are not actually required to discriminate between human and machine participants based on what they take to be “human behavior” but on what they take to be
normal
human behavior—which makes all the difference.
For indeed, as
Dupré \APACyear1998
tells us at the beginning of this section, the normal is also very often the
familiar
, which explains how judgments of “humanity” can vary significantly from one judge to the next: individual human interrogators are just expressing what feels normal
to them
.
The singularity of their individual appreciations of normality is intrinsically rooted in the singularity of their own respective experience of the world which justifies, in turn, this necessity to aggregate multiple interrogators’ judgments so as to assess success in the Turing test.
The best way to illustrate this argument is to make a quick detour by the
software engineering stack exchange
and its thread dedicated to the Turing test, titled ‘‘If you could pose a question to a Turing test candidate, what would it be?’’.
28
28

28

See
https://softwareengineering.stackexchange.com/q/64248

User TZHX suggests the following answer:

“That September 11th thing was amazing, wasn’t it?” – A human would get the reference, a machine is much less likely to. (TZHX, comment posted on April 1st 2011 at 9:27)

To which user Job immediately comments:

I believe that 70% of Earth population
[\ldots]

would not get that reference, and 30% would not get it even if you say it in their native language. We are not being fair to the machines. (Job, comment posted on April 1st 2011 at 15.39)

The example clearly illustrates how we often have limited appreciations of the narrowness of our own respective subjectivities, especially when dealing with what we take to be “normal”.
The use of statistics in the Turing test thus provides a mean to move past the subjectivity of the normal as familiar—rooted in the singularity and potential idiosyncracies of our respective developmental experiences—to rely on a purportedly more objective characterization of the normal: the statistically typical.

With those considerations in mind, we can now look back at previous claims such as that of Shah and colleagues who argue that:

One feature of ‘humanness’ that Turing did not factor into his
imitation game
for machine thinking and intelligence is that mistakes will be made by some of the human interrogators, and others are easily fooled.
(
Shah \BOthers. \APACyear2012
, p.1)

For as we can understand now—and contrary to what is claimed—“mistakes” made by human interrogators are an integral part of the Turing test.
First, in the sense that for machines to be truly indistinguishable from human participants, human interrogators precisely
need
to ‘‘make mistakes’’ and misidentify them as humans half of the time on average (with a baseline of 50% at least, less with a baseline of 70%).
Second, in the sense that what motivates abstracting away from the subjectivities of individual interrogators through statistical averaging is precisely that those subjectivities are presumed to be noisy and so ‘‘mistaken’’ in the first place.
29
29

29

It is interesting to note that in later work, Warwick and Shah repeatedly misquote
(
Turing \APACyear1950
)
by mentioning “average interrogator
s
” plural rather than “average interrogator” singular, as in
(
Warwick \BBA Shah \APACyear2015
, p.2)
or
(
Warwick \BBA Shah \APACyear2016\APACexlab\BCnt1
, p.1005)
.
At the same time, this is not completely surprising given that their interpretation of
(
Turing \APACyear1950
)
derives from their considering this notion of “average” to apply only to human interrogators taken individually rather than (also) to the
set
of human interrogators as a whole.

Having said that, it is true that Turing tells us nothing about human interrogators outside of the fact that they should not be experts about machines.
More generally, he tells us nothing about the populations of human interrogators and participants and how they should be constituted.
The question is important because it specifically conditions which (statistical) type will be objectivized by the Turing test.
Will it be a
universal
human type or, if not, at least a
representative
type—representative of the most statistically prevalent features within the human population as a whole?
Critical psychology today warns us that if we do not pay attention to our experimental conditions and to our population samples in particular, we may end up equating what is normal with what is WEIRD
(
Henrich \BOthers. \APACyear2010\APACexlab\BCnt1
;
Henrich \BOthers. \APACyear2010\APACexlab\BCnt2
, that is, with what is specific to Western, Educated, Industrial, Rich and Democratic societies, to borrow the expression of)
—especially given the prevalence of the WEIRD category within experimental psychology in general
(
Arnett \APACyear2008
;
Rad \BOthers. \APACyear2018
, see also)
.
Consequently, if we do not pay attention to our population samples in the Turing test, we may end up equating
intelligent behavior
with the behavior of a
particular
community of human beings—and most likely to equate normal human intelligence with average WEIRD intelligence. At its core, the argument points at the fact that human development is highly dependent on environmental contingencies—often both socio-culturally and socio-historially situated—so that there may exist a plurality of statistical types that may be objectivized within the human population as a whole
(
Forbes \BOthers. \APACyear2022
)
.
So when
French \APACyear1990
tells us that the Turing test is a test of
culturally-oriented intelligence
that can only be passed “by things that have experienced the world as we have experienced it”, we understand that the critical question that unfolds is that of who this “we” might actually refer to—and that it is unlikely to be a universal we or even a representative we.
The argument also points at the historical lack of representativeness of the concept of normality itself—particularly problematic when the normal also defines what is “correct” and what is “good”:

Normality is a very slippery notion.
It also has had a long history of abuse.
Responsible authorities in the past have argued in all sincerity that other races are degenerate forms of the Caucasian race, that women are just incompletely formed men, and that homosexuals are merely deviant forms of heterosexuals.
The normal state for human beings is to be white, male heterosexuals.
All others do not participate fully in human nature.
That white, male heterosexuals make-up only a small minority of the human race did not give these authorities pause.
(
Hull \APACyear1986
, p.7)

Be that as it may, it is important to remember here that the concept of statistical normality was never meant to characterize a representative let alone universal human type.
As
Hacking \APACyear1990
explains indeed, the Average Man of Quetelet—intrinsically tied to the concept of statistical normality itself—was never intended to characterize an average of the human species as a whole but rather an average of a people or a nation.
The lack of representativeness of statistical normality thus points at the conceptual origins of a normalist paradigm which remains historically rooted in a eugenist tradition which began with the creation of different “types” or “kinds” of human beings—among which, of course, we find (biological) “races”—so as to ultimately rank them
(
Hacking \APACyear1990
, pp.107–108)
.
Some of the critical problems of the Turing test thus run deep, and may be more related to its overarching normalist paradigm than to the specifications of the test themselves.

4.2
Limits of the Turing test, limits of normalism

As I have argued in Section
4.1
, the methodology of the Turing test fundamentally rests on the nomothetic ideal of the normalist paradigm which consists in approaching individual variability as error around a mean value considered to capture a shared and objective reality only partially manifested (at best) by each individual variation.
For the Turing test, this theoretically implies two things.
First, that the judgment of each human interrogator is presumed to characterize a partial and potentially partially erroneous appreciation of a shared and objective experience of what it means to be normal/average/typically human.
Second, that the behavior of every individual human participant
itself
is presumed to characterize a variation around this same normal type—each behavior being, in turn, a partial expression and potential deviation from this supra-individual entity that characterizes the normal type.

The first crucial point to understand about the Turing test, however, is that it does not exactly objectivize a regularity of normal behavior among human participants but only a
normative ideal
of normal behavior. For indeed, the “game” aspect of the test pushes human participants to behave not as they would “normally” do but rather as they think they
ought
to in order to win the game and be correctly identified as humans.
Human participants thus strive to align with the normative ideal of normal behavior of the human interrogators as much as possible—or at least with what they
think
this normative ideal is—so as not to be misidentified as machines.
If they know a lot about Shakespeare, for instance—to use the historical example of
(
Epstein \APACyear1992
, p.88)
—or if they know that their behavior on a certain topic is likely to deviate from the average norm, they might be tempted to alter their behavior so as to align with that norm.
30
30

30

This point is also discussed by Sterret regarding the original configuration of the test involving woman impersonation
(see
Sterrett \APACyear2000
, p.547, for details)
.
The “game” aspect of the test is indeed often approached in the literature through the question of
deception
and the argument that the Turing test essentially requires machines to impersonate human beings and deceit human judges.

Pinar Saygin \BOthers. \APACyear2000
, for instance, explicitly tell us that “the game is inherently about deception”.
See also
(
Pantsar \APACyear2025
)
for a more recent account on the matter and
(
Natale \APACyear2021
)
for a more general overview of deception in AI and technology from and beyond the Turing test.
The discussion on deception stems from the original framing of the Turing test which, before actually involving machines, requires a male participant to convincingly (i.e. indistinguishably) impersonate a woman.
This crucial aspect of the Turing test has been the object of much discussion
(
Genova \APACyear1994
;
Hayes \BBA Ford \APACyear1995
;
Sterrett \APACyear2000
;
Sterrett \APACyear2020
;
Traiger \APACyear2000
;
Kind \APACyear2022
, e.g.)
which I have to leave aside in the present paper for reasons of space.

At the individual level, the Turing test puts human participants in a configuration where they have to compete with machines so as to appear
as normal as possible
from the perspective of human interrogators.
This is precisely why Warwick and Shah recommend the following to human participants for them not to be misidentified as machines:

Do not show that you know a lot of things—the judge may conclude that you are too clever to be human.

[\ldots]

Do not add new material of a different nature even if you feel this is helpful, otherwise the judge may feel that you know too much to be human.
(
Warwick \BBA Shah \APACyear2015
, p.11)

What is ultimately objectivized by the Turing test is thus not the normal/typical human behavior corresponding to the average behavior of the population of human participants, but the
expectations
of normal/average/typical human behavior of that very same population.
Which is, in turn, what can make
Gonçalves \APACyear2023
argue that the Turing test is ultimately a test that requires machines to imitate
stereotypes
of humans.
The attempt to objectivize laws of behavior grounding normal human behavior collapses against the experimental setup of the test as normality becomes a normative ideal under the conditions of the game, which is probably what constitutes the first important limitation of the Turing test.

The second and no less crucial point to understand about the Turing test is that it presupposes a relative alignment of normative ideals of normal behavior between interrogators and participants.
For the test to function properly indeed, both human interrogators and participants must share roughly the same conception of what it means to be “normal”.
In the literature, this argument usually takes the form of a prerequisite of shared language and shared culture among interrogators and participants in the Turing test
(see
Traiger \APACyear2000
, pp.570–571)
.

Traiger \APACyear2000
explicitly tells us that the Turing test “is unlikely to work
[\ldots]
if the players come from very different cultures”—and rightly so, because this prerequisite of shared language and shared culture actually conditions the meaningfulness of the test results themselves.
To better make sense of this argument, let us imagine a case where human interrogators and participants in the Turing test would
not
share a common language.
Consider, for instance, a situation where a set of human interrogators having no experience or knowledge of English whatsoever would have to converse with human participants and machines expressing themselves exclusively in English.
31
31

31

Glossing over the question of who might initiate the interaction between interrogators and participants and what the content of that interaction might be without a common language.
To push the example to an extreme, imagine human participants and machines only having knowledge, experience and/or access to (in the case of machines) English, and human interrogators expressing themselves exclusively in a language using a non-latin writing system, such as Russian, Mandarin, Japanese, etc.

How would such human interrogators discriminate between human and machine participants?
A reasonable assumption, consistent with the statistical interpretation of the test, would be that they would base their judgment on pure
chance
.
If so, then whatever the machine says—and irrespective of whether that would even be considered meaningful English from the perspective of a native speaker—its behavior will be deemed indistinguishable from that of the average human participant by the average human interrogator. In other words, whatever the machine says, it will successfully pass the Turing test.
32
32

32

This is precisely what is at stake behind Hayes and Ford’s argument that the Turing test relies on a flawed experimental setup insofar as it involves having to “detect nothing”—as they say: “a difference between two behaviors is what isn’t being detected”—and in fact ultimately having to confirm the “null hypothesis” which, they argue, goes against the very basis of sound experimental design in psychology
(see
Hayes \BBA Ford \APACyear1995
, §3, for details)
.

Here again, the normalist interpretation of the Turing test allows us to make better sense of that example.
The point is that, as a tripartite configuration, the Turing test requires machines to behave in a way that is
as normal as
the average human participant from the perspective of the average human interrogator—no more, no less.
Remember that the Turing test is an indistinguishibility test so that if the machine systematically appears
more
normal than the average human participant it will not be able to successfully pass the Turing test as it will be systematically misclassified as a human and will not match the 50% chance of (mis)identification target.
Ultimately, the behavior of the machine in the Turing test is constrained in two ways: by the normative ideal of normal human behavior of the average human interrogator
and
by the normative ideal of normal human behavior of the average human participant.
However, the test can only function properly if those normative ideals are relatively aligned.
If the normative ideal of the average human participant deviates too much from that of the average human interrogator—as is typically the case when they do not share a common language—the machine will find itself caught in an irreconcilable tension, as it will have to align with two diverging normative ideals at once (see Figure
6
).

Figure 6:
Normative ideals of normal human behavior between human interrogators (dashed red) and human participants (continuous blue) in the Turing test.
The center/mean of each normal curve denotes a normative ideal of
perfectly
normal human behavior while each normal curve denotes variations around that mean.
Left-hand side figure illustrates the relative alignment between those normative ideals as required by the Turing test.
Right-hand side figure illustrates a configuration where those normative ideals are completely at odds, as is typically the case when human interrogators and participants do not share a common language.
Only the left-hand side configuration would lead to meaningful Turing test results.
Shapes of the normal curves are completely arbitrary and chosen for illustrative purposes only.

If the machine decides to align with the normative ideal of the average human interrogator, it will be misclassified as a human beyond chance and will thus fail the Turing test.
If it decides to align with the normative ideal of the average human participant, its behavior will become indistinguishable from the average human participant from the perspective of the average human interrogator and it will pass the Turing test no matter what.
However, in this latter case, the results of the test will not be truly meaningful as they will not really attest of the performance of the machine but only of the limitations of the experimental setup—characterizing in this case a lack of shared experience grounding a common normative ideal of normality between all interacting parties.
In the literature, this tension is usually framed as an opposition between meaningful Turing tests assessing the performance of machines and meaningless tests assessing only (and being limited by) the capabilities of human interrogators
(see, e.g.,
Sterrett \APACyear2000
, p.545)
.
33
33

33

See also
(
Hayes \BBA Ford \APACyear1995
, p.973)
on the cleverness or insightfulness of the judge conditioning success in the Turing test and
(
Shah \BOthers. \APACyear2012
, p.1)
on the fact that some human interrogators are supposedly more easily fooled than others.

However, blaming the (in)capabilities of individual human interrogators somewhat misses the point here, as the soundness of the experimental setup of the Turing test remains a matter of
alignment
between all interacting parties—as
Traiger \APACyear2000
rightly stresses—and so misalignment between them can certainly not be put on the account of a single party alone.
Why blame human interrogators for
not
knowing English, for instance, and not blame human participants for knowing
only
English?
What is often framed as a limitation of the interrogator can also be framed as a limitation of the participant depending on how one looks at the problem.
34
34

34

Though in fact, “failure”, (cognitive) “limitation”, “lack of” (knowledge) etc.—and so ultimately “blame”—are irrelevant concepts when the core of the problem is a matter of (cultural/linguistic) alignment between people having just
different
though equally rich experiences of the world.

Be that as it may, the point here is that the meaningfulness of the Turing test results
presupposes
the alignment of normative ideals of normality between interacting parties, which cannot in fact be guaranteed under the experimental conditions of the test, and not so much because there are no specified control conditions over the populations being sampled but rather because the topic of conversation remains essentially free.
At every occurrence of the test, it is always possible indeed that judgments of intelligence are clouded by the fact that individual human interrogators and participants actually belong to different “(sub)cultures” and simply hold different normative ideals of normality—different conceptions of what it means to be normal/familiar for the case at hand.
What constitutes an essential feature of the Turing test—its open-ended nature and the complete freedom in the content of interactions—is thus also what renders its results potentially meaningless, as it is impossible in practice to guarantee (cultural) alignment between interacting parties on every possible topic of conversation.
Consequently, it is impossible to maintain
both
a completely open-ended
and
systematically meaningful Turing test, which constitutes another possible limitation of the test.

The concept of
normative ideals
of normality makes apparent the underlying tension between the
prescriptive
and
descriptive
aspects of normality—remember
Hacking \APACyear1990
at the beginning of Section
2.1
telling us how normality bridges the fact/value distinction between what
is
and what
ought to be
.
What is interesting about those normative ideals of normal human behavior objectivized by the Turing test is not so much that they may deviate from the “real” normal behavior of human participants but that they actually
condition
the behavior of those same participants—and thus potentially interfere with what is being purportedly objectivized.
Ultimately, what such considerations fundamentally question is whether what is objectivized by the Turing really exists or whether it is not in fact
constructed
in part or in full by the objectivization process.
In the case of intelligence, the question takes particular relevance as psychometric measures of intelligence such as IQ tests are famous theoretical constructs, notorious for having been designed precisely
so as to follow the normal curve
.
The distribution of IQ results alongside a normal distribution was indeed “not an empirical discovery”,
Hacking \APACyear1996
tells us, but the output of a normalization process which precisely intended such results to have a normal distribution.
Colman’s (
Colman \APACyear2015
) dictionary of psychology tells us more or less the same thing when it says that IQ is “an index of intelligence having a normal distribution with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15 (usually)”.
Moreover, and inasmuch as we can trace back our contemporary conception of intelligence underlying IQ tests to Galton
(remember
Brody \APACyear2000
, from Section
2.1
)
, we might as well go directly to the source as Galton explicitly tells us in his
Hereditary Genius
that:
35
35

35

The keyword here being “must” which suggests not so much a natural necessity than a conceptual (if not a political) one, and implies for Galton that his conception of intelligence is designed specifically
so as to
follow a normal distribution among populations
(see
MacKenzie \APACyear1981
, ch.3, for a more general discussion on the political underpinnings of Galton’s scientific project)
.

This is what I am driving at—that analogy clearly shows there must be a fairly constant average mental capacity in the inhabitants of the British Isles, and that deviations from that average—upwards towards genius, and downwards towards stupidity must follow the law that governs deviations from all true averages.
(
Galton \APACyear1869/1892
, p.32)

In what sense, then, can the
normal intelligence
or
normal human behavior
objectivized by the Turing test be said to capture something “real” in any meaningful sense of the term?
Is it a truth that we uncover and a reality that we describe or merely a fiction that we construct?
And if so can it carry any scientific value?
From the answers to such questions may arise additional important limitations to the Turing test.

The descriptive/prescriptive tension behind the concept of normality also underlies a critical aspect of the concept, namely, that it is a constantly moving target.
As
Hacking \APACyear1996
frames it indeed: “As soon as you’ve said what’s normal, it isn’t” for “by saying what
is
, it at once sets going change in the status quo”.
Once again, such considerations take on particular relevance when discussing intelligence since as
Hacking \APACyear1996
explains, for instance, participants to Standard Achievement Tests (SATs) or Graduate Record Examinations (GREs) in the United States typically displace the target of normal/average performance as they collectively attempt to outperform it.
But such considerations also take on particular relevance when discussing the development of artificial minds this time, as machines themselves constantly displace and refactor our concept of intelligence as we develop them.

Sternberg \APACyear1984
, for instance, citing
(
Horn \APACyear1979
)
, typically explains how the development of calculating machines in the twentieth century contributed to the evolution of the concept of intelligence, leading notably to the downgrading of numerical calculation from intelligence tests where they were so far prevalent—as in Thurstone’s (
Thurstone \APACyear1938
) Primary Mental Abilities tests.
36
36

36

Sternberg’s (
Sternberg \APACyear1984
) considerations are all the more relevant to us here than Turing himself referred to flawless arithmetics as a typical example of a behavior that could potentially give machines away in his test, and explicitly told us that machines would have to make
arithmetics
mistakes so as to pass his test
(see
Turing \APACyear1950
, p.448, for details)
.

And we can understand why: since our modern conception of intelligence reduces intelligence to
exceptional
intelligence and since the exceptional is what is
good
but also what is fundamentally
rare
, the moment a certain behavior becomes accessible to most if not all it ceases to be considered exceptional and so to be part of ‘‘intelligence’’ altogether.
We can thus understand how the development of calculating machines that made correct/perfect arithmetics more accessible to the general population also pushed the associated behavior outside of the scope of intelligence and made it less and less prevalent within intelligence tests.
37
37

37

Note that the fact that a behavior ceases to be considered part of intelligence does not mean that it necessarily ceases to be valued altogether.
It may indeed retain its normative value but loose its rarity, and since both are needed to be considered exceptional, it just ceases to be considered intelligent.
As far as arithmetics skills are concerned, for instance, the point is not to say that perfect arithmetics is no longer valued in itself, of course.
But only that it has become less and less valued for individuals to demonstrate perfect arithmetics
by themselves
since it has become possible (or at least easier) for people to calculate quickly and accurately with the help of a calculator.

This analysis can potentially explain, in turn, how the development of spellchecks and various machine-based spelling assistants may lead us to progressively divest from teaching spelling
(see
Pan \BOthers. \APACyear2021
, for an overview)
or how Wikipedia and search engines may be progressively relegating encyclopedic knowledge outside of the scope of intelligence—why consider someone “smart” for knowing something you can access in five minutes through your favorite search engine indeed?
If the present analysis is correct, we should therefore expect the recent development of large language models such as ChatGPT to lead to further refactoring of our conception of intelligence, as they make previously exceptional behaviors more accessible to the general population.
The point being that machines constantly displace our normative ideal of exceptional behavior, which explains why it often feels like what is “intelligent” is whatever machines
cannot
(yet) do.
By trying to approach human intelligence through the modeling of artificial minds, we thus essentially modify its content and constantly displace our target of inquiry which, if not a limitation of the Turing test itself, probably constitutes an important limitation of its encompassing field of artificial intelligence.

One of the core argument of this paper has been that the Turing test is the product of a specific paradigm—the paradigm of normalism—and I would like to finish this section by showing how the most important limitation of the Turing test stems not from the formalization of the test itself but from its overarching normalist paradigm and from the concept of statistical normality in particular.
In the previous Section
4.1
, I discussed how the concept of statistical normality has historically suffered from a lack of representativeness as far as human beings are concerned—even though the statistical type was never actually meant to be a human type—and how this representativeness problem finds itself today questioning the relevance of experimental results in psychology
(
Arnett \APACyear2008
;
Henrich \BOthers. \APACyear2010\APACexlab\BCnt1
;
Henrich \BOthers. \APACyear2010\APACexlab\BCnt2
;
Rad \BOthers. \APACyear2018
, see)
.
However, it would be a mistake to think that all the problems with the concept of statistical normality reduce to a question of representativeness—and in particular, to a lack of typicality of the statistically normal.
The real problem with that concept indeed—and with the Turing test by extension—is whether it can really contribute anything to our understanding of what is human.
Remember the original conception of intelligence at the root of the Turing test: a
faculty
conceived as a universal property of all human beings and as a
potential
to display not just the most
typical
(i.e. statistically prevalent) human behavior but
any
kind of human behavior in fact (see Section
2.2
).
Human intelligence for Turing is thus to be understood primarily as what sets the boundaries of human cognition.
In that context, how could the understanding or modeling of
the
typical human behavior help us better understand the nature of those boundaries exactly?
In what sense would having a population of human subjects speaking English or Mandarin rather than any other language, for instance, get us any closer to the understanding of what can be human?
Having a more representative—in this case typical (as far as linguistic behavior is concerned)—sample of human subjects is not really the issue here, for the real problem with statistical normality is a conceptual one, not a methodological one.
38
38

38

Some may argue here that representativeness and statistical typicality are two different concepts that should not be confused
(though see
Chasalow \BBA Levy \APACyear2021
, and references therein for a discussion on that point)
.
However, I would argue that the alignment constraints put on the Turing test forces us to equate the two concepts and to approach the question of representativeness through the question of typicality rather than that of the diversity of human behaviors.
In other words, the question of representativeness in the Turing test is that of modeling
the
normal human behavior rather than just
a
normal human behavior—as it is impossible to model
all possible
human behaviors within the configuration of the test anyway.

The point is that statistically prevalent traits among the human population are often conditioned by purely socio-historical contingencies which may contribute little to our understanding of the biological constraints that condition the diversity of human traits in general.
To put it slightly more straightforwardly, we may learn little about what
can
be human by focusing solely on what is
likely
to be human.
And this problem cannot be overcome by demultiplying norms of normality and diversifying populations of human subjects either, as what
can
be human is sometimes attested in just a single individual.
The problem lies with the very nomothetic ideals which reduces human variability to
error
around an idealization—if not an ideal—when even the “abnormal” always remains fundamentally human.
In short, we won’t understand what it means to be human by understanding all the ways one can be normal, as there is simply more to being human than to being normal.

5
Conclusion

The main goal of this paper was to show that the concept of (statistical) normality can provide the conceptual backbone to make sense of the Turing test and weave together many of the arguments that have been carried out about the Turing test throughout the years.
Its core argument is that the Turing test is a test of
normal intelligence
understood as (a form of) normal/typical/average human behavior as assessed by a
normal judge
which is itself an abstraction made of the aggregate of multiple interrogators’ judgments.
From a general perspective, this paper attempts to replace the Turing test in its historical context—and more specifically in its
scientific
context—to show how it can be considered a direct product of a normalist paradigm taking normality as its central organizing concept and which remains very much predominant today in the field of psychology and the behavioral sciences at large.
Doing so, it proposes to shift our standard approach to Turing himself and to move away from the historical figure of the “genius”
(see
Feigenbaum \APACyear1996
;
Copeland \BBA Proudfoot \APACyear1999
;
Hilton \APACyear2017
, for explicit references)
and from all the discoveries he may have anticipated or pioneered—such as
neural networks
or
connectionism
, to name a few
(see
Copeland \BBA Proudfoot \APACyear1996
;
Copeland \BBA Proudfoot \APACyear1999
, for details)
—to uncover all the theoretical or methodological assumptions that came before him and that he may have carried over in his practice (willingly or not).
The point is to look
backwards
this time rather than
forwards
, and to insist not so much on what singularizes Turing than on all the things that make him a regular scientist of his time.
Far from negating or downplaying his scientific contribution, the point is simply to show that Turing can
also
in many ways be considered a “normal” scientist after all.
