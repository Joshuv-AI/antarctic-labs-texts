Value of different nutritional and inflammatory markers for diagnosis of peritonitis in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis
Authors: Xing-Yu Chen, Yu-Lu Fan, Jian Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis retrospective study evaluated hematological, inflammatory, and nutritional indicators of peritonitis in 132 peritoneal dialysis (PD) patients, including 83 with confirmed peritonitis and 49 without peritonitis. The peritonitis group had a higher platelet-to-albumin ratio (PAR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SIRI), but lower levels of hemoglobin, cholesterol, hematocrit, albumin, lymphocytes, triglycerides, and uric acid (all p < 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression identified hemoglobin (OR = 0.944, p < 0.05) and albumin (OR = 0.719, p < 0.01) as protective factors, and C-reactive protein (CRP; OR = 1.053, p < 0.01) as an independent risk factor. ROC analysis revealed moderate diagnostic performance for PAR (AUC = 0.700), NLR (AUC = 0.718), MLR (AUC = 0.717), and SIRI (AUC = 0.688), but the neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio (NMR) and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) performed poorly. A combined PAR/NLR/MLR/SIRI model achieved an AUC of 0.798. These findings link poor nutrition and heightened inflammation to peritonitis in PD patients, suggesting blood-derived indices, such as PAR, NLR, MLR, and SIRI, may serve as adjunctive markers to assist the established diagnostic methods.Keywords: Peritoneal dialysis, peritonitis, inflammation, neutrophils, lymphocytes

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major public health problem [1,2] and peritoneal dialysis (PD) is an important renal replacement therapy for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [3]. The main modalities of PD are continuous ambulatory PD (CAPD), automated PD (APD), and continuous cycling PD (CCPD). CAPD consists of manually instilling dialysis fluid into the abdominal cavity, followed by drainage after a dwell period of several hours [4]. CAPD has become a convenient, cost-effective, and well-established treatment option for ESRD patients, particularly for those living in rural areas [5]. However, several complications may lead to PD failure, among which peritonitis is the most common and serious. PD-related infections may present as peritonitis, exit-site infection, or tunnel infection [6].

Neutrophils are white blood cells (WBCs) that play a central role in the innate immune response through mechanisms such as chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and the generation of reactive oxygen species. Lymphocytes are the key components of the adaptive immune system, and they mediate antigen-specific immune responses [7]. The neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio (NLR) reflects the balance between innate and adaptive immunity. An elevated NLR is generally considered to indicate an enhanced inflammatory response with immune suppression. Many previous studies have examined the use of NLR as a prognostic or diagnostic marker for several conditions, including malignancies, sepsis, COVID-19, and cardiovascular diseases [8].

The systemic immune-inflammation index (SIRI, calculated as monocyte count × [neutrophil count/lymphocyte count]) is another composite indicator of the immune and inflammatory status. Compared with NLR, SIRI places greater emphasis on activation of the mononuclear phagocyte system, and an elevated level is considered to indicate immune-inflammatory dysregulation [9]. Previous studies have mainly investigated SIRI in patients with digestive system tumors, urinary system tumors, cervical cancer, breast cancer, and certain other malignancies [10–18]. In cervical cancer, for example, an elevated SIRI (generally above 2.3 × 109) is associated with tumor grade, stage, and poor differentiation [15]. In PD populations, recent evidence indicates that SIRI may be associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [9]. Other studies reported that an elevated NLR (>6.53) was as a risk factor for failure of PD and that the ascites NLR may help differentiate tuberculous from non-tuberculous infection [19]. In addition, a higher platelet-to-albumin ratio (PAR) is linked to increased risks of dialysis failure and mortality among PD patients [20].

The current diagnostic criteria for PD-associated peritonitis require at least two of the following: (i) clinical manifestations consistent with peritonitis (such as abdominal pain and/or cloudy dialysis effluent); (ii) dialysis effluent with WBCs count above 100/µL after a dwell time of at least 2 h with more than 50% polymorphonuclear cells (PMNs); and (iii) positive culture from dialysis effluent [21]. However, the leukocyte count in peritoneal fluid may be influenced by dwell time, and dialysate culture results are often delayed. The positivity rate of dialysate cultures at the onset of peritonitis is approximately 80%, and the definitive results may require 1 to 3 days of incubation [22]. Therefore, additional biomarkers that could assist in the early recognition of peritonitis may be clinically valuable.

This retrospective study aimed to determine whether systemic inflammatory indices (NLR, MLR, PAR, and SIRI), in combination with clinical and nutritional indicators, could help identify peritonitis in patients undergoing PD. An additional aim was to elucidate the potential clinical utility of these readily available hematological markers as adjunctive tools for assessing PD-related peritonitis.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study examined the records of 83 patients undergoing PD who were hospitalized for confirmed peritonitis at the Department of Nephrology, Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University (Hohhot, China) from January 2017 to December 2023. (Supplementary Figure S1). The control group consisted of 49 PD patients regularly followed in the outpatient department of the same institution without evidence of peritonitis during the study period.

Diagnostic criteria for peritonitis

All peritonitis cases met the diagnostic criteria of the 2022 International Society for PD (ISPD) guidelines, which require at least two of the following conditions: (i) clinical manifestations consistent with peritonitis (e.g., abdominal pain and/or cloudy dialysis effluent); (ii) dialysis effluent with WBCs count above 100/µL after a dwell time of at least 2 h, with more than 50% PMNs; and (iii) positive culture from dialysis effluent.

Data collection and laboratory measurements

Medical and laboratory records were reviewed to extract data on the counts of WBCs, neutrophils (N), lymphocytes (L), monocytes (M), and platelets (PLT); and levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin (ALB), cholesterol, uric acid, hemoglobin (Hb), and triglycerides.

The following inflammatory indices were calculated: N/M ratio (NMR), L/M ratio (LMR), M/L ratio (MLR), PLT/ALB ratio (PAR), and SIRI (N × M/L). All hematological and biochemical parameters were obtained within 24 h after the diagnosis of peritonitis using blood test kits (Mindray Nanjing Biotechnology Co., Ltd). The reagents for cholesterol, uric acid, and triglyceride were from Beijing Strong Biotechnologies, Inc, and the CRP assay kit from Beijing Simes Sikma Biotechnology Co.,Ltd.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University (approval No. S2021035) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA) and R software version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) as appropriate, and values were compared using the independent t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the independent risk factors for peritonitis, and variables with P values below 0.10 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of different biomarkers, and the area under the curve (AUC) and Youden index were calculated. In accordance with commonly accepted criteria, an AUC value of 0.5 to 0.6 indicated poor diagnostic performance, a value of 0.7 to 0.9 indicated acceptable diagnostic performance, and a value above 0.9 indicated excellent diagnostic performance. A two-sided P value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

This study was a retrospective analysis of the records of 132 patients (83 with peritonitis, 49 without peritonitis) who were undergoing PD at the study institution (Table 1). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, sex, history of diabetes, and hypertension. However, the duration of PD was significantly longer in the peritonitis group (p < 0.001).

Screening for key diagnostic biomarkers using multivariate logistic regression

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed with the presence of peritonitis as the dependent variable. The independent variables included dialysis duration and laboratory indicators associated with inflammation and nutritional status. Model 1, which adjusted for dialysis duration, indicated longer dialysis duration was as a significant risk factor for peritonitis (OR = 1.016, p < 0.01). Model 2, which adjusted for dialysis duration and laboratory variables, indicated that hemoglobin (OR = 0.944, p < 0.05) and albumin (OR = 0.719, p < 0.01) were the protective factors, but CRP was a significant risk factor for peritonitis (OR = 1.053, p < 0.01). These findings suggest that prolonged dialysis duration, high inflammatory activity (elevated CRP), and impaired nutritional status (low levels of Hb and albumin) were associated with an increased risk of peritonitis (Tables 3 and 4).

ROC analysis of different biomarkers

The discriminatory capacity of individual biomarkers was evaluated using ROC curve analysis with calculation of the AUC (Figure 2). Among individual markers, CRP had the best diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.854, Youden index = 0.607). Several inflammation-related ratios also had potential diagnostic value, including NLR (AUC = 0.718, Youden index = 0.406), MLR (AUC = 0.717, Youden index = 0.362), PAR (AUC = 0.700, Youden index = 0.354), and SIRI (AUC = 0.688, Youden index = 0.391). In contrast, LMR (AUC = 0.282) and NMR had poor diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.533). A combined model incorporating four markers that indicate nutritional and inflammatory status (NLR, MLR, PAR, and SIRI) had an AUC of 0.798 (Youden index = 0.520), indicating improved diagnostic performance compared with individual ratio-based markers.

Discussion

This retrospective study compared groups of patients undergoing PD who did or did not have confirmed peritonitis. The peritonitis group had significantly higher levels of PAR, NLR, MLR, and SIRI, but significantly lower levels of Hb, cholesterol, hematocrit, ALB, lymphocytes, triglycerides, and uric acid (p = 0.033). Multivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that when dialysis duration and other baseline indicators were used as the independent variables, then dialysis duration was a primary risk factor for peritonitis. However, when dialysis duration, nutritional indicators, and inflammatory indicators were analyzed together, and then, dialysis duration was not a primary risk factor; instead, nutritional indicators (Hb and ALB) and the inflammatory indicator CRP were associated with peritonitis.

The diagnostic performance of the combined model, which considered PAR, NLR, MLR, and SIRI, had an AUC of 0.798, slightly lower than CRP alone (AUC = 0.854). The combined model had higher sensitivity but lower specificity than CRP. These findings suggest that, with increasing dialysis duration, an adverse nutritional and inflammatory status may contribute to the development of peritonitis. The combined use of these markers may therefore assist in the early identification of peritonitis, particularly before culture results become available or when culture results are inconclusive.

Patients undergoing PD often experience impaired gastrointestinal function due to the presence of large volumes of dialysis fluid in the abdominal cavity. Consequently, patients receiving long-term PD may develop compromised gastrointestinal function, and this can contribute to poor nutritional status [23], with manifestations such as reduced serum levels of total cholesterol and ALB. These findings suggest that PD may contribute to the development of malnutrition. Previous studies have identified advanced age, female sex, severe obesity, diabetes, hypoalbuminemia, and hypokalemia as the risk factors for PD-associated peritonitis [3,24,25]. In addition, more than 30% of patients undergoing PD were reported to exhibit chronic systemic inflammatory activity [26], and this may be attributable to long-term malnutrition [27]. Thus, the interaction between poor nutritional status and increased inflammation may increase the risk of peritonitis in patients undergoing PD. The accumulation of uremic toxins in these patients may amplify the inflammatory response to lipopolysaccharides, and a high glucose load reduces adiponectin levels by increasing insulin resistance. Furthermore, persistent inflammation may exacerbate renal dysfunction and disrupt adipocyte differentiation [28]. Together, these interactions may constitute a positive feedback loop, in which inflammation promotes metabolic disturbances while metabolic disturbances aggravate inflammatory activity. This interaction may accelerate the progression of PD failure and contribute to the development of peritonitis.

Patients with PD-associated peritonitis often present with dyslipidemia, characterized by hypertriglyceridemia and reduced levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol. This pattern is consistent with the lipid metabolic profiles in patients with chronic renal insufficiency, systemic inflammatory response syndrome, and sepsis [29,30]. Inflammation- and nutrition-related markers may have relevance beyond diagnosis of infection in dialysis populations. Central sensitization can occur in hemodialysis patients and may contribute to symptom burden. Because pain and central sensitization were not assessed in the present study, this potential relationship remains speculative [31]. Similar to many inflammatory conditions, patients with PD-associated peritonitis typically exhibit elevated levels of leukocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, and CRP. Concurrently, malnutrition manifests as hypoalbuminemia, accompanied by anemia, and reduced counts of platelets and lymphocytes. The hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte (HALP) score (H [g/L] × A [g/L] × L [/L]/P [/L]) is a novel composite index that reflects systemic inflammation and nutritional status. Consistent with the present findings, previous research reported that a high HALP score was an independent risk factor for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in PD patients [26]. Based on changes in the complete blood cell count, the ratios of different types of cells can effectively differentiate PD patients with and without peritonitis. More specifically, PD patients with peritonitis had significantly higher levels of PAR, NLR, MLR, and SIRI and lower levels of NMR and LMR. However, ROC curve analysis indicated that NMR had limited predictive value (AUC = 0.541, p = 0.495).

These findings are consistent with those reported by Zahorec [32], who found that the NLR was effective as a real-time monitor of stress and inflammation in diverse diseases. Zahorec first proposed the NLR in 2001 [32] based on observations of postoperative cancer patients in the ICU, who had simultaneous elevations of neutrophils and reductions of lymphocytes. The NLR quantifies the balance between innate immune activation and adaptive immune suppression, and functions as a real-time monitor for stress and inflammation. Neutrophils develop during the sequential process of granulopoiesis in the bone marrow from promyelocytes, myelocytes, metamyelocytes, band cells, and finally PMN cells. Neutrophils exert antimicrobial effects through degranulation, robust generation of reactive oxygen species, phagocytosis, and formation of neutrophil extracellular traps. As pivotal inflammatory cells, they function in innate and adaptive immunity [33]. The impact of inflammation on lymphocytes depends on its duration: Acute inflammation promotes naïve T cell activation, enhances effector T cell function, and supports memory T cell formation; chronic inflammation drives T cell exhaustion, accelerates T cell senescence, and disrupts regulatory T cell function [34]. Zahorec et al. demonstrated that a higher inflammatory burden correlated with an elevated NLR. An increased level of neutrophils is associated with increased demargination, release of bone marrow, apoptosis, inflammation, and cellular stress; a decreased level of lymphocytes is associated with increased marginalization, redistribution, and apoptosis [32]. The NLR can also differentiate between infection by tuberculous and non-tuberculous mycobacteria in patients with bacterial PD-associated peritonitis [19].

The SIRI (a function of neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes) provides a more comprehensive characterization of the complex systemic inflammatory network. In contrast, the PAR reflects the coexistence of a systemic inflammatory state and deteriorating nutritional status [35]. The MLR captures the dual imbalance between pro-inflammatory processes (increased monocytes) and immunoregulatory processes (decreased lymphocytes). Although application of the MLR is generally more limited than other inflammatory markers, it has prognostic value in the prediction of coronary artery disease and diabetic foot ulcers [36,37]. The results indicate it also has value in the diagnosis of PD-associated peritonitis (AUC = 0.744). In contrast, the results of this study showed that the LMR had poor predictive value, likely because the lymphocyte and monocyte counts are affected by non-inflammatory factors, such as nutritional status and concomitant medication use, resulting in lower specificity.

Dyslipidemia occurs in patients with CKD and in those undergoing hemodialysis or PD. This condition is characterized by decreased levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and HDL cholesterol, and elevated levels of triglycerides, very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), and intermediate-density lipoprotein. This pattern is primarily attributed to inhibitors of lipoprotein lipase in uremic plasma, which impedes VLDL hydrolysis, and to the reduced activity of lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase, which impairs the maturation of nascent HDL [38–41].

The present study demonstrated that patients with PD-associated peritonitis had lower levels of triglycerides, inconsistent with the findings of some previous studies [23]. It is possible that the findings of the present study are unreliable estimates due to the small sample size. In addition, elevated triglyceride levels often occur during the initial onset of PD-associated peritonitis [5]. The discrepant results in this study may therefore be related to the high proportion of patients receiving long-term PD, a group that commonly exhibits malnutrition. Malnutrition may mask the acute inflammation-induced dysregulation of lipid metabolism.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design and relatively modest sample size may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, although multivariate analyses were performed, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as medication history or variations in dialysis regimens, cannot be completely excluded. Third, laboratory parameters were collected within 24 h after the diagnosis of peritonitis; therefore, causal relationships between inflammatory biomarkers and the development of peritonitis cannot be determined. In addition, the combined inflammatory-ratio model demonstrated lower specificity than CRP, which may increase the likelihood of false-positive results when used as a screening indicator. Finally, the present study indicated a lower triglyceride level in patients with peritonitis (which differs from some previous reports) highlights the complex interaction between inflammation and nutritional status in patients undergoing PD.

Conclusion

The present findings suggest that poor nutritional status may be associated with an increased risk of peritonitis in patients undergoing PD. In addition, several blood-derived inflammatory indices, including PAR, NLR, MLR, and SIRI, may provide additional information for the identification of peritonitis. Although these indices demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance, their combined use may serve as a complementary approach to the established diagnostic methods, rather than simply replacing conventional indicators.
