On Distributed Colouring of Hyperbolic Random Graphs
Yannic Maus, Janosch Ruff

Abstract
We analyse the performance of simple distributed colouring algorithms under the assumption that the input graph is a hyperbolic random graph (HRG), a generative model capturing key properties of real-world networks such as power-law degree distributions and large clustering coefficients. Motivated by the shift from worst-case analysis to more realistic network models, we study the number of rounds and size of the colour space required to colour HRGs in the distributed setting. In the conceivable simplest algorithm each vertex selects a colour uniformly at random and keeps it permanently if no neighbour tries the same colour; otherwise it discards the candidate colour choice and tries a fresh random colour in the next round. • We first show that this randomised algorithm terminates in exactly two rounds a.a.s. when given a colour space of ε ⋅ Δ \varepsilon\cdot\Delta , for any constant ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , while failing w.h.p. if the colour space is only χ ⋅ n δ \chi\cdot n^{\delta} for some small constant δ > 0 \delta>0 . • We then consider another classic variation of that random colour trial algorithm that resolves conflicts by prioritising nodes with smaller IDs. While it also colours with colour space ε ⋅ Δ \varepsilon\cdot\Delta in two rounds, it w.h.p. fails to do so with Δ / log Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) ⁡ Δ ≫ χ \Delta/\log^{\Omega(1)}\Delta\gg\chi colours. • Lastly, inspired by the structure of HRGs, we consider a variant of the random colour trial algorithm that prioritises high-degree vertices. It achieves a valid colouring in two rounds a.a.s. using only Δ 1 − δ \Delta^{1-\delta} colours for some constant δ > 0 \delta>0 . We also show that our bound on the colour space is asymptotically tight (up to polylogarithmic factors) for certain values of the power-law exponent. All three randomised algorithms are extremely simple and run in the bandwidth-restricted CONGEST model. These positive results demonstrate that constant-time algorithms can outperform the classical Ω ⁡ ( log ∗ ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log^{*}n) lower bound for the Δ + 1 \Delta+1 -vertex colouring problem in worst-case graphs, established by [Linial; FOCS ’87] and [Naor; SIAM Journal Disc. Math. ’91]. Our results rely on several new structural insights into HRGs, which may be of independent interest. More generally, our results contribute to the line of research of simple algorithms beating the general lower bounds on HRGs like Ω ⁡ ( n ) \Omega(n) for computing the shortest path [Bläsius, Freiberger, Friedrich, Katzmann, Montenegro-Retana and Thieffry; Transactions of Algorithms ’24].

In this paper, we aim to get the best of all worlds, i.e., a extremely simple and efficient algorithms that colour with few colours significantly outperforming all existing results.

We use the simplest conceivable randomised distributed colouring algorithm as our baseline approach:

Random Colour Trial (RCT):

Each node randomly picks a uniformly random candidate colour from its palette of available colours, i.e., the whole colour palette without the colours used by its already permanently coloured neighbours, and sends this colour to its neighbours while receiving their colours. If no neighbour tries to get coloured with the same candidate colour, the node permanently adopts the colour, otherwise it discards the candidate colour and retries in the next round with a new random candidate colour from an updated palette.

This process repeats until all nodes have a valid colour.

It is simple and local—when using
\Delta+1
colours, where
\Delta

\mathcal{O}(\log n)
rounds with high probability on any graph
[
15
,
50

. As messages only contain colours, it also runs in the CONGEST model, where communication is restricted to
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
-bit messages per edge per round.

In distributed settings, it is known that already colouring with a single colour less changes the entire nature of the problem. In order to make fast progress distributed colouring algorithms colour large parts of the network need in parallel, but doing so carelessly, may result in an uncoloured subgraph on which we can even existentially not complete the colouring. As a result there is an

Background on Hyperbolic random graphs

Power-law degree and clustering coefficient.

Many real-world networks like the internet or social networks share common properties like a power-law degree distribution
[
34
,
79
]
and a non-vanishing clustering coefficient
[
67
,
76
,
80
]
. Krioukov, Papadopoulos, Kitsak, Vahdat, and Boguñá
[
56
]
introduced
hyperbolic random graph
(HRG) where they use hyperbolic geometry for random graphs to incorporate both of these properties
[
43
]
. In contrast to a random geometric graph (RGG)
[
71
]
that lives in the Euclidean space, a hyperbolic random graph is generated in the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. One restricts the plane to a disk of radius
R
and then
n
vertices are sampled into the disk where its radius
R
scales with
n
. As opposed to the Euclidean plane where space expands polynomially, hyperbolic space expands exponentially, and as a result, most area of the disk is close to the boundary. This leads to most vertices being located close to the boundary of the disk while only a few vertices are close to the centre of the disk. The hyperbolic geometry also implies that vertices at the boundary of the disk have smaller (expected) degrees which gives rise to a heterogenous degree distribution. This is in contrast to the homogeneous degree distribution of RGGs while preserving large clustering coefficients of models based on Euclidean space.

Algorithmic properties.

The resulting graph has further properties one finds in many complex networks like a small diameter
[
38
,
52
,
65
]
and a giant component of linear size
[
25
,
36
]
. From the algorithmic side, many problems like minimum vertex cover
[
17
,
20
]
, maximum independent set
[
24
]
, the largest clique
[
23
]
, the shortest path
[
18
]
or colouring
[
8
]
have been studied in the centralised setting. Moreover, the two core properties of a non-vanishing clustering coefficient and a power law degree distribution inherited by HRGs were also proposed for
propositional satisfiability
by Bläsius, Friedrich, Göbel, Levy and Rothenberger
[
19
]
as a possible explanation for the tractability of industrial SAT instances.

A Bird’s Eye View on our Analysis

One of the major challenges of analysing randomised algorithms on hyperbolic random graphs arises from the fact that we need to deal with two different sources of randomness: the randomness of the graph and the probabilistic nature of a randomised algorithm. To circumvent the problems that arise from dealing with two different probability spaces at once, we make use of structural graph properties that emerge asymptotically almost surely on HRGs. We call a HRG with these properties a
typical
HRG. We identify properties of typical HRGs and condition on those to ensure the desired fast performance of our randomised algorithms. To the best of our knowlegde, we are the first to analyse a randomised algorithm on HRGs. However, Kiwi, Schepers and Sylvester have analysed the behaviour of random walks on HRGs
[
55
]
.

In any case, the main difficulty then lies in identifying the aforementioned properties, i.e., properties that (1) hold with a sufficiently large probability over the generation of the random graph, and that (2) are useful for designing a fast algorithm for those typical graphs, or in our case to show that the given
\mathrm{RCT}
-based algorithms perform well on HRGs. Examples of properties that emerge with high probability in HRGs and are algorithmically exploited in other works are for example the
dominance rule
for vertex cover
[
17
,
20
]
, the
degeneracy
for colouring
[
8
]
or a mixture of
locality
and

heterogeneity
for the study of shortest path
[
18
]
. Bläsius and Fischbeck
[
16
]
provide evidence that these structural properties may also appear in real-world networks, possibly explaining why simple algorithms outperform their worst-case bounds.

In the classic runtime proof of

one shows that each vertex gets coloured with a constant probability in each round of the algorithm, regardless of what has happened in the past, yielding a logarithmic runtime; one can also show that this is tight for worst-case input instances, see

similar to

; the main advantage is that vertices in the same layer
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
have similar degrees of roughly

. See

for an illustration of the layers and the neighbourhood of a vertex following the geometry of the hyperbolic disk.

In the sequel, we use different arguments and measures of progress for different layers. The probability of a small degree vertex to be coloured is much larger than constant, in fact, the probability to be unsuccessful with colouring in the first round is upper bounded by
\deg(v)/\mathsf{|ColourSpace|}
, as

\deg(v)
colours with their candidate colours. Ignoring dependencies, this shows that in the layers with constant degrees only
\mathcal{O}(n/\mathsf{|ColourSpace|})=\mathcal{O}(n/\Delta)=o(\sqrt{n})
vertices remain uncoloured after the first iteration, and heuristically, only
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}/\Delta)\ll 1
nodes after the second iteration.

The main challenge is to make sufficient progress in layers with large degree nodes. Imagine that we want to prove
Theorem

1
with
\varepsilon=1/100
, that is, we aim to colour the graph with
\Delta/100
colours. For a node

\gg\Delta/100
this is troublesome, as we cannot complete the colouring for
v
(not even existentially) if
v
’s neighbours use the whole colour space resulting in no available colours left for
v
. First, we show that the randomness of
\mathrm{RCT}
implies that the individual palette of available colours of each node remains of size
\Omega(\Delta)
after the first round. While large degree vertices initially may have up to
\Delta
neighbours, we show that after the first round their uncoloured degree drops to
o(\Delta)
; the latter statement heavily exploits the structure of a typical HRG as most neighbours of a large degree node have a much better than constant success probability. We show that in subsequent rounds, the palette remains large, but due to the smaller uncoloured degree the probability to remain uncoloured decreases drastically.

2.2

Upper bound for
\mathrm{RCTID}

In order to show a smaller error probability than the one we show for
\mathrm{RCT}
, we exploit that whether a vertex discards its candidate colour or not, does not necessarily depend on all its neighbours. Following the lines drawn by Barenboim, Elkin, Pettie and Schneider
[
15
]
,
we use that the ID’s give us a natural order in which we reveal the randomness of the candidate colours (only for the purpose of analysis), such that when we reveal the candidate colour of some vertex
v
, the probability that
v
discards its colour only depends on the already revealed randomness. This allows us to apply an Azuma-Hoeffding type concentration bound. To obtain the desired overall result, we need to carefully apply this idea to the nodes in different layers separately exploiting the respective structural properties to obtain strong concentration for the drop in the number of uncoloured vertices per layers and down the line the uncoloured degree of vertices after the first round. This leads to the smaller
1/\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}n
error guarantees of
\mathrm{RCTID}
as compared to the a.a.s. guarantee of
\mathrm{RCT}
. We remark that a seemingly easier approach for
\mathrm{RCT}
employed by prior work is actually incorrect
[
44
]
. As many neighbours have individual small probabilities to remain uncoloured, one may be tempted to show that the uncoloured degree of a vertex
v
is stochastically dominated by a process of independent binomial random variables indicating whether
v
’s neighbours are coloured or not. If this was true, one could apply a simple Chernoff bound as done in
[
44
]
to show that the uncoloured degree of vertices drops significantly with high probability. See
Appendix

A
for a detailed discussion and an explanation of why this analysis in
[
44
]
is incorrect and more care is needed.

\mathrm{RCTID}

The remaining uncoloured vertices in the second round (see

This implies that, given the first round was successful at colouring all high degree vertices, the algorithm will produce a valid colouring eventually; we show that such a colouring is found quickly as well. For this we make use of the fact that the uncoloured vertices with a relatively large degree, have most of their larger degree neighbours already coloured.

Why does this result in colouring with
o(\Delta)
colours?

A key advantage of
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
is the successful colouring of the
\approx\sqrt{|\mathsf{ColourSpace}|}
highest degree vertices in the first round: in contrast to
\mathrm{RCT}
, the rainbow candidate colours among high degree vertices directly ensure that they are also coloured when using
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
. Now, exploiting the power-law structure of a typical HRG, with a colour space of size
o(\Delta)
, after the first round all non-coloured vertices have a degree that is smaller than the colour space. Thus, it is also ensured that a legal colouring for the yet to be coloured sub-graph exist. Indeed, we show that such a colouring for the remaining uncoloured sub-graph is found quickly as well.

Behaviour of
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
for different power-law exponents.

In order to fully grasp our results for
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
, recall that the degree distribution of a hyperbolic random graph follows a power law, where the power law exponent
2\alpha+1
is controlled by the model parameter
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
. We refer to
Theorem

5
in
Section

\mathrm{RCTDEG}
for different values of the parameter

2.4

Limitations of
\mathrm{RCT}
,
\mathrm{RCTID}
and
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
.

As any partial colouring can always be extended to a legal colouring at low-degree vertices regardless of how the rest of the graph has been coloured, any non-termination result should investigate the behaviour of large-degree vertices. For example, if some palette becomes empty during the algorithm’s execution, it won’t terminate.
Palettes change over time and it is notoriously difficult to keep track of the probability distribution of vertices’ palettes over multiple rounds of the algorithm. Thus, the key ingredient in proving our nearly matching lower bounds on the performance of these graph colouring algorithms is a structural result on HRGs. This result allows us to show that if the colour space is too small, a non-completable partial colouring is likely to emerge already after the first round. This structural result may be of independent interest: We show that most vertices in most of the layers
\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{\lfloor R\rfloor-1}
have many leaves, i.e., degree

be the set of its neighbours that are leaves and define

as the set of vertices for which a constant fraction of their neighbours are leaves. We prove the following lemma.

We show that after the first round many vertices of a relatively large clique are colour locked on a uniform at random colour. When the number of colours available to
\mathrm{RCT}
is not too much larger than the number of vertices in this clique of colour locked vertices, the "birthday paradox" enforces that w.h.p. there exists a pair in the clique that is colour locked on the same colour. Then not only the algorithm does not terminate anymore but even existentially the computed partial colouring cannot be completed.

In
Section

\mathrm{RCTID}

\mathrm{RCTID}

\Delta/\log^{\Omega(1)}n

u

u
has no possible candidate colour for round two and beyond and the algorithm will never terminate.

In
Section

8.3
, we use a similar argument as for
\mathrm{RCT}
on large-degree vertices being colour-locked for
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
. In principal, this is also the reason why a
with high probability guarantee

\mathrm{RCTDEG}
is not possible if we use a colour space that is
\Delta^{1-\delta}
: using that few colours, with probability
n^{-c}
for a constant
c<1
there exists a pair of high degree vertices which are neighbours picking the same colour while their respective colour spaces are consumed by their leaves. This birthday paradox can only be omitted with probability
1-n^{-c}
which implies that with probability at least
n^{-c}
the algorithm never finishes.

3

Conclusion

In this paper we investigated how the performance of simple randomised distributed colouring algorithms on hyperbolic random graphs depend on the colour space. We showed that the most classic and simple algorithms undergo an abrupt transition from using
2
rounds when using
\varepsilon\cdot\Delta
colours, over constant rounds for fewer colours to never terminating when the colour space is further restricted. Interestingly, our positive results demonstrate that constant-time algorithms can break the fundamental
\Omega(\log^{*}n)
worst-case runtime lower bound for the
\Delta+1
-vertex colouring problem proven by Linial and Naor for deterministic and randomised algorithms respectively
[
60
,
66
]
.

All our results are based on several structural insights on the structure of hyperbolic random graphs that may be of independent interest. For upper bounds on the colour space required, all three randomised algorithms we analysed require significantly less colours than the maximum degree
\Delta
and for any
\varepsilon>0
, it is sufficient to use
\varepsilon\cdot\Delta
. We showed that when we use the degrees of vertices as a heuristic for which vertices should be prioritised, the algorithm is highly likely to be successful even if the colour space is
\Delta^{1-\delta}
for some
\delta>0
. Moreover, our upper bounds for this algorithm match our established lower bounds by a multiplicative polylogarithmic factor for certain regimes of the power law degree distribution inherited by hyperbolic random graphs.

Future directions.

There are several intriguing directions to go from here.

•

First, there remains the question whether
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
is truly superior to
\mathrm{RCT}
. In the area where
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
colours with
o(\Delta)
colours, the performance of
\mathrm{RCT}
remains open. Surprisingly,
\mathrm{RCTID}
cannot close this gap and does not terminate with
\mathcal{O}(\Delta/\polylog n)
colours (see also
Figure

1
).

•

Second, for
\alpha>3/4
, it is unclear if our lower bound on the colour space for
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
is tight for all regimes of
\alpha
or if our upper bound can be improved upon (see grey area in
Figure

1
c).

•

Third, it would be interesting to further investigate the existence of efficient deterministic CONGEST colouring algorithms for hyperbolic random graphs.

•

Fourth, it remains open whether another simple CONGEST algorithm can colour with
(1+o(1))\chi
colours; as shown
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
uses few colours but still significantly more than the optimal number of colours. Alternatively, it remains open to design such algorithms whose runtime bounds hold with high probability.

•

Fifth, can other typical problems in the field like maximal independent sets be solved faster on hyperbolic random graphs than on worst-case instances
[
9
]
?

In general, we hope to spark further research on distributed algorithms and more generally sublinear algorithms on real-world graphs.

and
\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\right]
for probabilities and expectations; in contrast, we use
\mathbf{P}\left(\cdot\right)
and
\mathbf{E}\left[\cdot\right]
when referring to randomness over the graph distribution
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,C)
.

5

Typical HRGs: Layers, Leaves & Larger Degree Neighbourhood

In this section we introduce structural properties of hyperbolic random graphs that hold at least a.a.s. and thus, also hold for typical hyperbolic random graphs. In
Section

5.1
we introduce the discretisation of the hyperbolic disk into layers with the for us relevant properties in
Section

5.1
. In
Section

5.2
we present the leaves lemma that we use throughout the paper for the lower bounds on the colour space. At last, we establish to notion of larger degree neighbourhood in
Section

5.3
which we use to analyse
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
.

5.1

Discretisation into Layers

Through out the paper, we use the following discretisation of the disk
\mathcal{D}_{R}
: a
layer

\mathcal{A}_{\ell}\subset\mathcal{D}_{R}
is the sub-space with distance
[\ell,\ell+1)
to the boundary (see
Figure

2
b for a sketch). More formally, for
\ell\in[\lfloor R\rfloor]
the layer of
level

\ell
is defined by
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}:=\mathcal{B}_{0}(R-\ell)\setminus\mathcal{B}_{0}(R-\ell-1)
. A similar layering has been introduced by Friedrich and Krohmer
[
38
]
in order to bound the diameter of HRGs. We use a different property here, namely that vertices that share the same layer have roughly the same degree. Using
Section

4
the measure of a layer is given by

\displaystyle\mu(\mathcal{A}_{\ell})=\Theta(1)e^{-\alpha\ell}.

(3)

We also refer to the set of vertices
V\cap\mathcal{A}_{\ell}=:V_{\ell}
as the vertices of layer
\ell
. The expected degree of a vertex
v\in V_{\ell}
is given via
Section

4
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\deg(v)\right]=\Theta(1)e^{\ell/2}.

(4)

Finally, we write
\ell^{*}:=\max{(\{\ell:V_{\ell}\neq\emptyset\})}
, that is, the largest level for a layer that still contains at least one vertex. The following lemma is a simple application of mostly
Equation

3
and
Equation

4
. It sets us up with concentration bounds we make use of throughout our arguments. It provides us with (1) how many vertices are located in a layer, (2) what the degree of vertex in a layer is, and (3) what the largest level of a layer is.

{lemma}

[Layer lemma] Let
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,C)
be a threshold hyperbolic random graph and let
\ell\in[\lfloor R\rfloor]
. Then it holds for
V_{\ell}:=V\cap\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
,
v\in V_{\ell}
and the largest layer level
\ell^{*}
that

1.

w.e.h.p.
|V_{\ell}|\in\begin{cases}\Theta\left(ne^{-\alpha\ell}\right),&\text{if $\ell\leq\lceil\alpha^{-1}(\log(n)-2\log(\log(n)))\rceil$ }\\
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}(n)),&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

2.

and
\deg(v)\in{\mathcal{O}}\left(e^{\ell/2}+\log(n)\right)
w.e.h.p.

3.

Moreover,
\ell^{*}\leq\lceil\alpha^{-1}\cdot(\log(n)+\log(\log(n))\rceil
a.a.s.

Proof.

Fix any layer
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
and consider the random variable
|V_{\ell}|
, i.e., the number of vertices that are located in this layer. Notice that the number of vertices located in area
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
is a Poisson random variable. This allows us to apply Chernoff bound. Using
Equation

3
we have for
\ell\leq\lceil\alpha^{-1}(\log(n)-2\log(\log(n)))\rceil
that
\mathbf{E}\left[|V_{\ell}|\right]=\Theta(1)ne^{-\alpha\ell}\in\Omega(\log^{2}(n))
since
\mathbf{E}\left[|V_{\ell}|\right]
is decreasing in
\ell
. Thus, using a Chernoff bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(|V_{\ell}|\not\in\Theta\left(ne^{-\alpha\ell}\right)\right)\in n^{-\omega(1)}.

(5)

For the case
\ell\geq\lceil\alpha^{-1}(\log(n)-2\log(\log(n)))\rceil
, we use again that
\mathbf{E}\left[|V_{\ell}|\right]
is decreasing in
\ell
and thus,
\mathbf{E}\left[|V_{\ell}|\right]\in\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}(n))
which proves
Item

1
using another Chernoff bound.

Next, consider any vertex
v\in V_{\ell}
. Recall that the neighbourhood of a vertex
v
lies within the ball
\mathcal{B}_{v}(R)\cap\mathcal{D}_{R}
. Thus, the size of the neighbourhood is again a Poisson random variable and another Chernoff bound gives us for the degree of a vertex
v
via
Equation

4

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(\deg(v)\not\in{\mathcal{O}}\left(e^{\ell/2}+\log(n)\right)\right)\in\mathcal{O}(1/n^{c}),

(6)

where
c
is an arbitrarily chosen constant. This concludes the argument for
Item

2
.

Finally, set
r_{0}:=R-(\lceil\alpha^{-1}\cdot(\log(n)+\log(\log(n))\rceil)
. Note that in order to ensure
Item

3
, it suffices to show that
V\cap\mathcal{B}_{0}(r_{0})=\emptyset
. To this end we use
Section

4
and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[|V\cap B_{0}(r_{0})|\right]=n\cdot\mu(\mathcal{B}_{0}(r))=n\cdot(1-o(1))e^{-\alpha(R-r)}=\Theta(1)(1/\log(n))\in o(1).

Markov’s inequality
Appendix

E
with
a=\sqrt{\log(n))}\mathbf{E}\left[|V\cap B_{0}(r_{0})|\right]
finishes the proof.
∎

5.2

Leaves Lemma

In this section we introduce a property of HRGs we will make use of in particular for the lower bounds on the number of rounds (
Section

6.2
) and for the probability that
\mathrm{RCT}
does not terminate with a too small colour space (
Section

6.3
). We will also us it later in
Section

7
when we analyse
\mathrm{RCTID}
. We show a lot of vertices with many neighbours of degree one to which we refer as
leaves
. This is in particular useful in the context of randomised colouring since leaves have only one neighbour and thus, there are no dependencies for the candidate colour an uncoloured leaf tries in each round. The following will be useful to show our desired properties of leaves.

{lemma}

[Layer-leaves]
Let
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,C)
be a threshold hyperbolic random graph and let
u\in V(G)
be a vertex in
G
with radius
r\in R-\omega(1)
. Then with constant probability
\Omega(\deg(u))
neighbours of
u
are leaves in layer
\mathcal{A}_{0}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathcal{B}_{0}(R)\setminus\mathcal{B}_{0}(R-1)
. For vertex
u
let us write
N_{0}(u):=N(u)\cap\mathcal{A}_{0}
, i.e., the subset of the neighbourhood of
u
located in layer
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. Then, by
Equation

2
, all the vertices with angle distance
\theta_{R}(r,R)
to
u
are neighbours of
u
since
\theta_{R}(r,x)
decreases monotonically in
x
. Then the expected size of
|N_{0}(u)|
is

\mathbf{E}\left[|N_{0}(u)|\right]\geq n\cdot\mu(\mathcal{B}_{0}(R)\setminus\mathcal{B}_{0}(R-1))\cdot 2\theta_{R}(r,R).

Using
Section

4
and
Equation

3
for the angle and the measure of the layer we get

\mathbf{E}\left[|N_{0}(u)|\right]\geq\Theta(1)e^{(R-r)/2}=\Theta(1)\mathbf{E}\left[\deg(u)\right]\in\omega(1),

(7)

where for the equation we used
Section

4
and in the last step we used that
r(u)\in\omega(1)
. Next, consider any vertex
v\in N_{0}(u)
and let
Z_{v}
be the indicator random variable that is
1
if
\deg(v)=1
and
0
otherwise and we write
Z=\sum_{v\in N_{0}(u)}Z_{v}
for the number of leaves of
u
that have radius at least
R-1
. Since the degree of
v
follows a Poisson distribution and the expected degree of a vertex with radius at most
R-1
is constant using
Section

4
, we obtain that
\mathbf{P}\left(Z_{v}=1\right)\in\Omega(1)
.

Moreover, applying a Chernoff bound in conjunction with
Equation

7
we obtain
|N_{0}(u)|\in\Omega(\deg(v))
with constant probability. Thus, by linearity of expectation and law of total expectation we get
\mathbf{E}\left[Z\right]\in\Omega(\deg(u))\in\omega(1)
. Let us now write
Y=|N_{0}(u)|-Z
for the number of "none-leaves" for which we get by linearity of expectation that there exists a constant
0<\delta<1
such that
\mathbf{E}\left[Y\right]-\mathbf{E}\left[|N_{0}(u)|\right]-\mathbf{E}\left[Z\right]\leq\delta\mathbf{E}\left[|N_{0}(u)|\right]
. Finally let
\varepsilon<1-\delta
while being non-vanishing by which we get by an application of Markov’s inequality for the random variable
Z
that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(Z\in\Omega(\deg(u))\right)\geq 1-\mathbf{P}\left(Y\geq\underbrace{(\delta+\varepsilon)}_{<1}|N_{0}(u)|\right)\geq 1-\delta/(\varepsilon+\delta),

which is non-vanishing by our choice of
\varepsilon>0
. This proves our claim.
∎

We now present the
leaves lemma
. Recall that
V_{\ell}
is the set of vertices located in layer
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
, i.e.,
V_{\ell}=V\cap\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
and we write
L(u):=\{v\in N(u):\deg(v)=1\}
for the leaves of
u
. The proof is deferred to
Appendix

D

{lemma}

[Leaves lemma]
Let
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,C)
be a threshold hyperbolic random graph and let
c>0
be constant large enough. Then, for all integers
\bar{\ell}
such that
\lceil c\log(\log(n))\rceil\leq\bar{\ell}\leq\lfloor\frac{\log(n)-\log(\log(n))-c}{\alpha}\rfloor
, a.a.s. there exists a set
U_{\bar{\ell}}\subseteq V_{\bar{\ell}}
where

1.

for any
u\in U_{\bar{\ell}}
, a constant fraction of
u
’s neighbours are leaves
|L(u)|\in\Theta(\deg(u))
and

2.

the size of the set
U_{\bar{\ell}}
is
|U_{\bar{\ell}}|\in\Theta(|V_{\bar{\ell}}|)
.

Proof sketch.

We fix a layer
\bar{\ell}
and by
Section

5.2
the lemma statement holds in expectation, i.e., in expectation a constant fraction of the vertices in layer
\mathcal{A}_{\bar{\ell}}
have a constant fraction of its neighbours as leaves that are located in
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. To obtain the desired concentration, we consider a subset of vertices
U_{\bar{\ell}}\subseteq{V}_{\bar{\ell}}
that fulfils the following: for any pair of vertices
u,v\in U_{\bar{\ell}}
, let
\mathcal{B}_{u}
and
\mathcal{B}_{v}
in the disk
\mathcal{D}_{R}
be the areas in the disk
\mathcal{D}_{R}
that we need to reveal for the events that
u
has
\Theta(\deg(u))
leaves in
\mathcal{A}_{0}
and
v
has
\Theta(\deg(v))
leaves in
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. We ensure that these two areas are disjoint, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}_{u}\cap\mathcal{B}_{v}=\emptyset
. We accomplish this by having an angle distance for
u,v\in U_{\bar{\ell}}
large enough such that for any point
x\in\mathcal{A}_{0}\cap\mathcal{B}_{u}(R)
we have
B_{x}(R)\cap(\mathcal{B}_{v}(R)\setminus\mathcal{B}_{0}(R-\bar{\ell}-1))=\emptyset
. Moreover, for potential leaves we only consider a sub area
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{0}
such that for any point
a\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{0}
and any vertex
w\in V\cap\mathcal{B}_{0}(R-\bar{\ell}-1)
, the distance is
\dist(a,w)>R
and consequently, the probability that a vertex in the area
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{0}
is a leaf-neighbour of
u\in U_{\bar{\ell}}
is independent of the set of vertices
V\cap\mathcal{B}_{0}(R-\bar{\ell}-1)
since this set of vertices has no neighbours in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{0}
. Thus, for any pair of vertices
u,v\in U_{\bar{\ell}}
, the area on the disk
\mathcal{D}_{R}
that we reveal for the events that
u
and
v
have a constant fraction of their neighbours as leaves are disjoint. Then, for each vertex
u\in U_{\bar{\ell}}
it is an independent coin flip if
u
has
\Theta(\deg(u))
many leaves and the concentration follows from Chernoff bound with
|U_{\bar{\ell}}|\in\omega(\log(n))
coin flips and a union bound over all layers yields the desired result.
∎

5.3

Larger Degree Neighbourhood

For the analysis of
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
we are interested in the neighbourhood of
u
that has a larger degree than
u
. The following lemma tells us up to which radius
r^{\prime}
there might be vertices with a larger degree than than vertex
u
with radius
r(u)=r
. For an illustration we refer to
Figure

3
a.

{lemma}

[Larger degree radius]
Let
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,C)
be a threshold hyperbolic random graph and let
c^{\prime}=7
. Moreover, let
u\in V
with radius
r\leq R-\frac{2\log(\log(n))}{(1-\alpha)}
and degree
\deg(u)
. Then, w.e.h.p. all vertices with radius
r^{\prime}\geq r+c^{\prime}
have a smaller degree than
\deg(u)
.

Proof.

Consider the expected degree of
u
, which given via
Section

4
is at least

\mathbf{E}\left[\deg(u)\right]\geq(1-o(1))n\cdot\frac{\alpha e^{-r/2}}{\pi(\alpha-1/2)}\in\Omega(\log^{2}(n)),

where the second step followed from the assumption of our case that
r\geq R-2/(1-\alpha)\log(\log(n))
and
R=2\log(n)+C
. From a Chernoff bound it then follows that
\deg(u)\geq\mathbf{E}\left[\deg(u)\right]/2\geq(1-o(1))n\cdot\frac{\alpha e^{-(R-\ell)/2}}{2\pi(\alpha-1/2)}=:k
w.e.h.p. using that
\mathbf{E}\left[\deg(u)\right]\in\Omega(\log^{2}(n))
.

In contrast, consider any vertex
v
with radius
r^{\prime}\geq r+c^{\prime}
. By
Section

4
we get for the expected degree of vertex
v

\mathbf{E}\left[\deg(v)\right]\leq(1+o(1))n\cdot\frac{\alpha e^{-(r+c^{\prime})/2}}{\alpha-1/2}\leq e^{-3/2}(1+o(1))n\cdot\frac{\alpha e^{-(R-\ell)/2}}{2\pi(\alpha-1/2)},

Another Chernoff bound then yields that
\deg(v)\leq e^{3/2}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\deg(v)\right]<k
w.e.h.p. Though the degrees
\deg(u)
and
\deg(v)
might be non-independent, a union bound over the complementary events for both degrees gives
\deg(u)>\deg(v)
w.e.h.p. A union bound over all vertices with radius
r^{\prime}\geq r+c
finishes the proof.
∎

Let
N^{+}(u):=\{v\in N(u)|\deg(v)\geq\deg(u)\}
be the neighbourhood of
u
with larger degree and
\deg^{+}{(u)}=|N^{+}(u)|
. We bound
\deg^{+}{(u)}
parameterised by layer level
\ell
(see also
Figure

3
b)

{lemma}

[Larger degree neighbourhood]
Let
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,C)
be a threshold hyperbolic random graph and let
u\in V_{\ell}
. Then, w.e.h.p.,

\deg^{+}{(u)}\in\begin{cases}{\mathcal{O}}(e^{\ell/2}+\log(n))\text{ for $0\leq\ell\leq\lfloor 2/(1-\alpha)\log(\log(n))\rfloor$},\\
\mathcal{O}(e^{\ell(1-\alpha)})\text{ for $\lceil 2/(1-\alpha)\log(\log(n))\rceil\leq\ell\leq\lceil R/2\rceil$ },\\
\mathcal{O}(ne^{-\alpha\ell})\text{ for $\lceil R/2\rceil\leq\ell\leq\lceil\alpha^{-1}(\log(n)-2\log(\log(n)))\rceil$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Case 1
[
0\leq\ell\leq\lfloor 2/(1-\alpha)\log(\log(n))\rfloor
]: This follows directly from the upper bound on the degree by
Section

5.1
.

Case 2
[
\lceil 2/(1-\alpha)\log(\log(n))\rceil\leq\ell\leq\lceil R/2\rceil
]:

Fix a vertex
u\in V_{\ell}
and recall that w.e.h.p. all vertices with radius
r^{\prime}\geq r+7
have a smaller degree than
\deg(u)
due to
Section

5.3
. Let
A
be the event that no neighbour of
u
with radius larger than
r^{\prime}
has a larger degree than
\deg(u)
. Since
u\in V_{\ell}
has maximal radius
R-\ell
, we obtain by using law of total expectation and
[
57
, Lemma 3.3]
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\deg^{+}{(u)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\deg^{+}{(u)}|A\right]\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\deg^{+}{(u)}|A^{C}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(A^{C}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Theta(1)e^{\ell(1-\alpha)}+o(1)\in\omega(\log(n)).

since
\mathbb{P}\left(A^{C}\right)\in n^{-\omega(1)}
and we used that by our case that
\ell\geq 2/(1-\alpha)\log(\log(n))
in the last step. A Chernoff bound concludes the case.

Case 3
[
\lceil R/2\rceil\leq\ell\leq\lceil\alpha^{-1}(\log(n)-2\log(\log(n)))\rceil
]: Fix a vertex
u
with radius of at most
r\leq R/2
and by
Section

5.3
all vertices with radius
r^{\prime}\geq r+7
have a smaller degree than
u\in V\cap\mathcal{B}_{0}(R/2)
w.e.h.p. Via event
A
similar to case 2, we obtain by
Section

4
and law of total expectation that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\deg^{+}{(u)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\deg^{+}{(u)}|A\right]\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\deg^{+}{(u)}|A^{C}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(A^{C}\right)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\mu(\mathcal{B}_{0}(R/2+c^{\prime})+o(1)

\displaystyle\leq\Theta(1)ne^{-\alpha\ell}.

Then we use
\ell\geq\lceil\lceil\alpha^{-1}(\log(n)-2\log(\log(n)))\rceil
, and a Chernoff yields the desired result.

∎

6.1

First round: Maintaining Palette size while reducing Uncoloured Degree

In this section we analyse the first round of
\mathrm{RCT}
. In order to show that the algorithm terminates, we need to ensure that there are enough colours available to every vertex. To make this more formal, let
X_{t}(u)
be the random variable that counts the number of colours that are not assigned to any neighbour of
u
, before round
t
starts and we define

\displaystyle X_{t}(u):=|\Psi_{t}\left(u\right)|=\sum_{\psi\in\Psi}\prod_{v\in N(u)}\left(1-\mathbf{1}_{\{\psi_{t-1}\left(v\right)=\psi\}}\right).

(8)

That is
X_{t}(u)
tells us how large the colour space of
u
at round
t
is.

{lemma}

[Slack round 1
\mathrm{RCT}
]
Let
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,C)
be a typical hyperbolic random graph and consider round
t=2
of
\mathrm{RCT}
with colour space
|\Psi|\in\omega(\log(n))
. Then, for any vertex
u\in V
with degree
\deg{(u)}\in\mathcal{O}({|\Psi|})
, the colour space of
u
is at the second round is
X_{2}(u)\in\Omega(|\Psi|)
w.e.h.p.

Proof.

First consider a vertex
u\in V
that has degree
\deg(u)\in o(|\Psi|)
. Then the statement immediately follows deterministically as
X_{t}(u)\geq|\Psi|-\deg(u)
since at most every neighbour of
u
can have one different colour assigned and it follows for any
t

\displaystyle X_{t}(u)\geq|\Psi|-\deg(u)\in\Omega(|\Psi|).

Thus, to finish the proof, it is left to show that our statement holds for any vertex
u
where the degree is
\deg(u)\in\Theta(|\Psi|)
. To this end, fix a vertex
u
with degree
\deg(u)\in\Theta(|\Psi|)
and consider the first round of our colouring algorithm
\mathrm{RCT}
. Since every vertex picks a colour uniform at random from
[|\Psi|]
, we get for any
v\in N(u)
that the probability that it tries a specific candidate colour
\psi
is
p_{v}:=\mathbb{P}\left(v\text{ tries }\psi\text{ as its candidate in round 1.}\right)=|\Psi|^{-1}
. Next, we count the number of colours that were not tried by any neighbour of
u
. To this end, fix a colour
\psi
and let
Z_{\psi}
be the number of times that
\psi
was tried by a vertex
v\in N(u)
. By a binomial distribution we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\psi}=0\right)=(1-p_{v})^{\deg(u)}=(1-|\Psi|^{-1})^{\Theta(1)|\Psi|}\in\Omega(1).

(9)

This shows that with constant probability
p>0
, a colour
\psi\in\Psi
is not picked by any neighbour of
u
. Modelling the amount of colours picked by neighbours of
u
by a balls into bins experiments, where
|\Psi|
many bins represent the colours and
\deg(u)
many balls represent the neighbours of
u
, let
Z^{\prime}_{\psi}
the indicator random variable that is
1
if there is at least one ball in bin
\psi
. Moreover, we "Poissonise" the experiment, by throwing
N\sim\text{Po}(\mathbb{E}\left[\deg(u)\right])
balls into
|\Psi|
bins. Let
Z^{\prime}=\sum_{\psi\in\Psi}Z^{\prime}_{\psi}
be the number of bins with at least one ball. Then the expected number of balls in bin
\psi
is
\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{\prime}_{\psi}\right]<1-p
by
Equation

9
and we obtain by linearity of expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{\prime}\right]\leq(1-p)|\Psi|
. Let
Z
be the number of bins without a ball and by linearity of expectation we have
\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[|\Psi|-Z^{\prime}\right]\geq p\cdot|\Psi|
. Since
Z
follows a Poisson distribution and
|\Psi|\in\omega(\log(n))
we obtain via Chernoff bound that
Z\in\Theta(|\Psi|)
w.e.h.p. Then for the balls into bins experiment under binomial distribution with exactly
\deg(u)
balls it follows that there are also w.e.h.p.
Z\in\Theta(|\Psi|)
empty bins, i.e., non-used colours by any neighbour of
u
which follows from "de-Poissonising" the experiment
[
64
, Corollary 5.9]
, which says that if an event holds w.e.h.p. in the Poisson case, then it also holds w.e.h.p. in the binomial case. A union bound over all vertices with degree
\Theta(|\Psi|)
finishes the proof.
∎

Recall that
V_{\ell}=V\cap\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
are the vertices contained in a layer (see
Section

5.1
). In the next lemma we give a bound on the random variable
Z_{\ell}=|\{v\in V_{\ell}|{\psi_{1}\left(v\right)=\emptyset}\}|
, with which we count how many vertices are uncoloured in any layer
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
after round 1 has terminated.

{lemma}

[
\mathrm{RCT}
uncoloured layer vertices]
Let
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,C)
by a typical hyperbolic random graph and, let
Z_{\ell}=\sum_{v\in V_{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\psi_{1}\left(v\right)=\emptyset\}}
. Then, for
\mathrm{RCT}
with colour space
|\Psi|\in\Omega(\Delta)
, for any
c>0
it holds
Z_{\ell}\in\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(e^{-\ell(\alpha-1/2)}\cdot n^{\frac{2\alpha-1}{2\alpha}}+1)
with probability
1-\mathcal{O}(\log^{-c}(n))
.

Proof.

Fix a vertex
u\in V_{\ell}
and let
Z_{u}
be the indicator random variable that is
1
if
u
is uncoloured after the first round of
\mathrm{RCT}
. Reveal the candidate colour of
u
first such that before revealing the candidate colours of
u
’s neighbour we have
\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{u}\right)=(1-|\Psi|^{-1})^{\deg(u)}
where
|\Psi|
is the colour space of
\mathrm{RCT}
. By the assumption of the lemma that
|\Psi|\in\Omega(\Delta)

{proposition}

that exists w.e.h.p. by

where we have for both vertices

many leaves. We show for

that tries any colour

in the first round w.e.h.p. Using a union bound this also holds for

and
v

and

are colour locked on the same colour after the first round and the algorithm never terminates as both

and

Now, fix

6.3
has
|L(u)|\in\Omega(n^{\zeta_{min}})

\psi
in the first round. For this we use

for the indicator random variable that is

picked the fixed candidate colour in the first round. Since in the first round a vertex tries a candidate colour u.a.r. from

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\psi}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{w\in L(u)}Z_{w}\right]\geq\Theta(1)|L(u)|\cdot|\Psi|^{-1}\geq\Theta(1)\varepsilon^{-1}\log(n),

(17)

where we used that

u
and

Analysis of Random Colour Trial with Degree Priority (Proof of Theorem
3
)

In this section we analyse another variant of
\mathrm{RCT}
we refer to as
random colour trial with degree priority
(
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
). Similar to
\mathrm{RCT}
, at the beginning of each round all uncoloured vertices try a candidate colour uniform at random from their respective colour space. A vertex assigns its candidate colour if and only if no vertex with higher degree has the same candidate colour; breaking ties of vertices with same degree by giving priority to the vertex with smaller ID. In a certain sense,
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
is a special case of
\mathrm{RCTID}
where the smallest ID’s are given to the largest degree vertices. We first state the formal results.

5
Random colour trial with degree priority.

Let
\zeta_{1}=\frac{1}{2\alpha+1/2}
and let
\zeta_{2}=\frac{1}{4\alpha-1}
.

1.

On a typical hyperbolic random graph
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
terminates in 2 rounds a.a.s.

(a)

if
\alpha\leq 3/4
and the colour space is at least
\log^{4}(n)\cdot\chi^{2}
with chromatic number
\chi
;

(b)

if
\alpha>3/4
and the colour space is at least
\log^{4}(n)\cdot\sqrt{n}
.

2.

On a typical hyperbolic random graph
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
terminates in
\mathcal{O}(1)
rounds a.a.s.

(a)

if
\alpha\leq 3/4
and the colour space is at least
\log(n)\cdot n^{\zeta_{1}}
;

(b)

if
\alpha>3/4
and the colour space is at least
\log(n)\cdot n^{\zeta_{2}}
.

3.

There exists a constant
\delta>0
such that
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
on a typical HRG never terminates w.e.h.p.

(a)

if
\alpha\leq 3/4
and the colour space is at most
\frac{\delta}{\log(n)}\cdot{n^{\zeta_{1}}}

(b)

if
\alpha>3/4
and the colour space is at most
\frac{\delta}{\log(n)}\cdot{\chi^{2}}

Theorem
5

In this section we translate the results of

from
\mathrm{RCT}
to
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
. Note that, given we use the same colour space for
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
as for
\mathrm{RCT}

6.3
holds also true for
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
as a candidate colour is choosen uniform at random.

{corollary}

[
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
birthday paradox]
Let
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,C)
be a typical hyperbolic random graph, let
\zeta_{min}=\min((2\alpha+1/2)^{-1},2(1-\alpha))
and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be constant small enough. Then in the first round of
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
with colour space at most
|\Psi|\leq\epsilon/\log(n)\cdot n^{\zeta_{min}}
, there exists a pair of neighbours
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
such that
u
and
v
try the same candidate colour and
|L(u)|,|L(v)|\in\Omega(n^{\zeta_{min}})
.

From this we get in similar fashion to
Section

6.3
that at least one of the two vertices
u,v\in E(G)
as given in
Section

8.3
will never be coloured w.e.h.p.

{proposition}

[
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
lower bound]
Let
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,\alpha,C)
be a typical hyperbolic random graph, let
\zeta_{min}=\min((2\alpha+1/2)^{-1},2(1-\alpha))
and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be constant small enough. Then
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
with colour space at most
|\Psi|\leq\varepsilon/\log(n)\cdot n^{\zeta_{min}}
will never terminate on
G
w.e.h.p.

Proof.

Consider the pair of neighbours
u,v\in E(G)
given by
Section

8.3
w.e.h.p., that share the same candidate colour and which have both
|L(u)|,|L(v)|\in\Omega(n^{\zeta_{min}})
many leaves and fix
u
. Then, fix a colour
\psi\in\Psi
and let
Z_{\psi}
be the random variable that counts the number of leaves of
u
, that try candidate colour
\psi
in the first round. Moreover, let
Z_{w}
be the indicator random variable that is
1
if
w\in L(u)
tries the fixed candidate colour in the first round. Since in the first round a vertex tries a candidate colour u.a.r. from
\Psi
, we get by linearity of expectation and
u
having
|L(u)|\in\Theta(n^{\zeta_{min}})
leaves that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\psi}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{w\in L(u)}Z_{w}\right]\geq\Theta(1)|L(u)|\cdot|\Psi|^{-1}\geq\Theta(1)\varepsilon^{-1}\log(n),

(22)

since
|\Psi|\leq\varepsilon/\log(n)\cdot n^{\zeta_{min}}
. A Chernoff bound for
Z_{\psi}
gives that there exists at least one leaf
w\in L(u)
that tries
\psi
with probability
\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\psi}\geq 1\right)\geq 1-n^{-\Theta(1)/\varepsilon}
. Thus, given that
\varepsilon>0
is small enough, a union bound over all colours yields that any colour is tried at least once by a leaf w.e.h.p. Since leaves only have an edge to
u
, this implies that none but the colour pick of
u
is discarded after the first round finishes w.e.h.p.

Via union bound this holds also true for
v
. Without loss of generality let
\deg(u)\geq\deg(v)
. Then, since
u
and
v
have the same candidate colour and
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
gives priority in the colour choice to the vertex with larger degree,
v
does not assign its candidate colour (in case of
\deg(u)=\deg(v)
consider without less of generality that
u
has the smaller ID among the two). Since after the first round all colours except the candidate colour of
v
are assigned to its leaves w.e.h.p., after round 1 vertex
v
has an empty colour space
X_{2}(v)=0
while being uncoloured after the first round. Thus,
\mathrm{RCTDEG}
never terminates w.e.h.p. and the proof is complete.
∎

Appendix A
Stochastic Domination for Randomised Colouring

In this section, we briefly explain the issue with applying stochastic dominance as done in
[
44
, Lemma 5.2]
. There, at its core, the authors aim to apply
[
33
, Lemma 1.8.7]
stated by Doerr, which says that a sequence of arbitrary binary random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
is stochastically dominated by independent random variables
X^{*}_{1},\ldots,X^{*}_{n}
if

\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i}=1|X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1}=x_{i-1}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(X^{*}_{i}=1\right)

for all
i\in[1..n]
and all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1}\in\{0,1\}
with
\mathbb{P}\left(X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1}=x_{i-1}\right)>0
. The upshot of using stochastic domination lies in the applicability of Chernoff bounds for the random variable
X=X_{1}+\ldots+X_{n}
.

In
[
44
, Lemma 5.2]
, the authors define
Z_{u}
to be the indicator random variable that is
1
if vertex
u
is uncoloured after iteration
i
and then basically sate that
\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{u}=1\right)\leq\deg(u)/|\mathsf{ColourSpace}(u)|
, and that this holds
irrespective
of other
Z_{u^{\prime}}
. While this is true if we condition on the candidate colour of
u^{\prime}
, observe that in order to apply
[
33
, Lemma 1.8.7]
, the upper bound on
\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{u}|Z_{u^{\prime}}=z_{u^{\prime}}\right)
needs to hold true for
all

z_{u^{\prime}}\in\{0,1\}
with
\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{u^{\prime}}=z_{u^{\prime}}\right)>0
.

Now, consider as an example a graph that consists of two vertices
V=\{\{u\},\{u^{\prime}\}\}
with edge
e=\{u,u^{\prime}\}
and at iteration
i
, an uncoloured vertex
v\in V
picks its candidate colour u.a.r. from all available candidate colours. Let
Z_{u}
be the indicator random variable that is
1
if
Z_{u}
is uncoloured after iteration
i
. Fixing a vertex
u
we have indeed
\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{u}\right]\leq\deg(u)/|\mathsf{ColourSpace}(u)|
. However, when we condition on the event
Z_{u^{\prime}}=1
which has probability
\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{u^{\prime}}=1\right)>0
, then
u
must have surely tried the same candidate colour as
u^{\prime}
giving
\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{u}|Z_{u^{\prime}}=1\right)=1\not\leq\deg(u)/|\mathsf{ColourSpace}(u)|
and thus, the stochastic domination does not apply.
