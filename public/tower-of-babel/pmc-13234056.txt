Engineering quantum dot surfaces to preserve protein-DNA interactions for single-molecule visualization
Authors: Youngseo Kim, Hyerim Kim, Munryul Choi, Hyun Suk Kim, Soyeong An, Orlando D Schärer, Jongnam Park, Ja Yil Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantum dots (QDs) are fluorescent nanoparticles widely used for single-molecule imaging because of their exceptional brightness and photostability. However, the impact of QD surface chemistry on biomolecular interactions has not been systematically investigated. Here, we report that commercial QDs unexpectedly destabilize protein-DNA complexes by inducing protein dissociation from DNA. Using the human nucleotide excision repair protein, xeroderma pigmentosum complementation group A (XPA) as a model system, we demonstrate that antibody-conjugated QDs promote dissociation of XPA from DNA substrates, independently of sizes and surface modification of QDs, antibody types, epitope tags, buffer conditions, or DNA structures. We find that polyethylene glycol (PEG), a common polymer coating on QD surfaces, is the primary factor responsible for this effect. To tackle this problem, we engineered QDs with precisely controlled surface polymer compositions. By systematically changing the ratio of anchoring, hydrophilic, and PEG-based functional groups, we find that reducing PEG density below a critical threshold effectively suppresses protein dissociation while maintaining excellent colloidal stability and brightness. Furthermore, antibodies conjugated via click chemistry between azide groups and DBCO enabled specific labeling of XPA without perturbing the DNA binding activity. Using these optimized QDs, we conducted single-molecule DNA curtain assays to visualize XPA-DNA interactions. QD-labeled XPA exhibits one-dimensional diffusion with frequent pausing on undamaged DNA. DNA curtain assays revealed that XPA preferentially binds DNA bubbles and searches for bubble structures through both one-dimensional diffusion and three-dimensional collision. Quantitative analysis showed that three-dimensional collision is the dominant pathway for bubble recognition. Taken together, our results uncover a previously unrecognized limitation of PEG-coated QDs in single-molecule studies and provide an improved surface-engineering strategy to preserve native protein-DNA interactions. Introduction

Semiconductor fluorescent nanoparticles, quantum dots (QDs), have been widely applied in physics, chemistry, and display technology due to their high brightness and photostability [1–7]. In addition, QDs have been employed for life sciences. Functionalized QDs conjugated with antibodies and nucleic acid probes have been employed for sensitive detection of biomarkers associated with cancers, infectious diseases, and genetic disorders (Fig. 1A) [8–11]. In addition, they have been explored as nano-carriers for drug delivery and gene therapy [12–15]. QDs can also serve as theragnostic platforms by combining therapeutic delivery with real-time imaging, allowing simultaneous monitoring of treatment efficacy. Moreover, QDs have been extensively applied for molecular imaging as fluorescent probes [16–18]. Their broad excitation spectra enable simultaneous imaging of multiple targets with minimal spectral overlap [19, 20]. Furthermore, remarkable resistance to photobleaching compared to conventional organic dyes makes them particularly attractive for monitoring biomolecular interactions over extended time scales.

QDs are also actively adopted for single-molecule imaging [21–27]. Single-molecule imaging has emerged as a powerful tool for elucidating the dynamic behaviors and heterogeneity of biomolecules that are often veiled by ensemble-average effects in traditional biochemical assays [28]. By directly visualizing the activities of individual proteins, nucleic acids, and their complexes in real time, the technique has provided unprecedented insights into molecular mechanisms underlying diverse biomolecular reactions. However, the reliability of such observations critically depends on the fluorescence labeling of target molecules. In particular, highly photostable and bright fluorescent probes such as QDs are central to success of single-molecule studies as they enable high-resolution and long-term visualization of molecular events. Despite the broad usefulness of QDs, we report here the surprising discovery that commercial QDs destabilize the protein-DNA complexes and accelerate dissociation of proteins from DNA. We systematically investigated components of QD that interfere with binding of proteins to DNA and developed optimized surface modification and bioconjugation strategies to prevent interference with biochemical processes. We validate our engineered QDs using single-molecule DNA curtain experiments. We establish a quantitative framework for evaluating and minimizing QD-induced perturbation in biochemical and single-molecule protein-DNA assays.

Methods/Experimental

DNA preparation

All DNA oligomers were synthesized from Bioneer (South Korea) and listed in Table S1. All DNA constructs (three-way junction, Y-shape, CPD DNA, and duplex) were prepared by annealing oligomers, for which the oligomer mixture was heated at 95 °C for 10 min and slowly cooled down to 25 °C. For three-way junction DNA, YFA, YFB, and FAM_YFC were mixed at 2:2:1 molar ratio. For duplex, CY5_Dup and Dup_comp were mixed at 1.2:1 molar ratio. For Y-shape DNA, Cy5-Yshape and Yshape-comp were mixed at 1.2:1 molar ratio. For UV-DDB experiment, CPD_temp and CPD_comp_FAM oligomer were mixed at 1.5:1 molar ratio to make CPD-containing DNA substrates.

Commercial quantum dots (QDs)

Anti-FLAG 705 QDs were prepared by labeling ANTI-FLAG M2 antibody (F3165, Sigma-Aldrich) using the SiteClick™ 705 QD™ Antibody Labeling Kit (S10454, Invitrogen). Anti-FLAG 605 QDs were generated by labeling the same ANTI-FLAG M2 antibody with the SiteClick™ 605 QD™ Antibody Labeling Kit (S10469, Invitrogen). Anti-His 705 QDs were prepared by labeling a 6 × His Tag Monoclonal Antibody (MA1-135, Invitrogen) using the SiteClick™ 705 QD™ Antibody Labeling Kit (S10454, Invitrogen). All antibody–QD conjugation reactions were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Streptavidin QDs were obtained commercially (QD™ 705 Streptavidin Conjugate, Q10163MP, Invitrogen) and used as received. For UV-DDB experiments, 605 Carboxyl QD (Qdot™ 605 ITK™ Carboxyl Quantum Dots, Invitrogen, Q21301MP) were purchased.

Protein purification

Wild-type full length XPA with 6 × His only, 3 × FLAG and 6 × His, or no epitope tag at the amino-terminus were cloned into pBG100 plasmid vector (Fig. 1B). A deletion mutant of XPA (XPA_ΔIDR) lacking the intrinsically disordered region (1 to 97 amino acids), which had 3 × FLAG and 6 × His at the amino terminus, was also cloned into pBG100 plasmid vector (Fig. S11A). All wild-type XPA proteins and XPA_ΔIDR were purified by the following procedure. The plasmid containing the XPA gene was transformed into Rosetta (DE3) E. coli strain. Two liters of E. coli cells were grown at 37 °C in LB media supplemented with 50 μg/mL Kanamycin until OD600 reached ~ 0.6. Temperature was then cooled down to 18 °C and cells were further grown until OD600 reached ~ 1.2. The protein was expressed with 1 mM IPTG (Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside) at 18 °C overnight. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6700 g and 4 °C for 30 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 45 mL of lysis buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl [8.0], 500 mM NaCl, 10% Glycerol, 20 mM imidazole, 5 mM βME (2-mercaptoethanol), 10 μM ZnCl2, 200 μg/mL lysozyme, 0.5 mM PMSF (phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride), 1 mM benzamidine, and 1 × halt protease inhibitor (Thermo scientific, 78438). Then the cells were lysed by 20 strokes of Dounce homogenizer (Duran, 357546) and sonication (Qsonica, Q700) at 30% amplitude with total 2 min pulse-on (10 s pulse-on and 10 s pulse-off). Cell lysates were clarified by ultracentrifugation at 46500 g and 4 °C for 40 min. The clarified lysates were then incubated for 1.5 h with 8 mL of Ni–NTA resin (HisPur™ Ni–NTA Resin, Thermo scientific) pre-equilibrated with Ni–NTA bind buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl [8.0], 500 mM NaCl, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 10 mM imidazole, 5 mM βME, and 10 μM ZnCl2). The resins were gently poured into an empty column with gravity flow and washed twice with 20 mL of Ni–NTA wash buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl [8.0], 500 mM NaCl, 10% (v/v) Glycerol, 30 mM imidazole, 5 mM βME, and 10 μM ZnCl2). XPA protein was eluted with 3-h incubation in 10 mL of Ni–NTA elution buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl [8.0], 300 mM NaCl, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 250 mM imidazole, 5 mM βME, and 10 μM ZnCl2). The eluants were subsequently loaded onto a heparin column (HiTrap Q HP, GE Healthcare), which was pre-equilibrated in Heparin buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl [8.0], 50 mM NaCl, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 5 mM βME, and 10 μM ZnCl2). The proteins were eluted around 0.5 M during salt gradient from 0.1 M to 1.5 M of NaCl in Heparin buffer. The eluted XPA was further purified by gel filtration (HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75 pg, Cytiva), which was pre-equilibrated in GF buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [8.0], 150 mM NaCl, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 1 mM DTT, and 10 μM ZnCl2). The final eluants were concentrated with Amicon (MWCO: 3 kDa) and were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for -80 °C storage until use.

UV-DDB was purified as previously described with minor modifications [27]. Human DDB1, fused at its N-terminus to a 1 × FLAG tag, a PreScission protease cleavage site, and a 3 × HA tag, together with DDB2, was cloned into the pFastBacDual vector. Recombinant bacmid DNA was generated in MAX Efficiency DH10Bac competent cells (Thermo scientific, 10361012). Sf9 cells were transfected with purified bacmid DNA to produce P1 virus, which was amplified in High-Five cells to generate P2 virus. High-Five cells were then infected with P2 virus for UV-DDB overexpression. Infected cells from a 0.4 L culture were harvested and resuspended in 80 mL of lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl [8.0], 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 M NaCl, 0.25 mM TCEP, 10% (v/v) glycerol, and cOmplete™ EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, 5056489001). Cells were lysed by sonication, and the lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 148,000g for 30 min at 2 °C. The clarified lysate was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 mL of anti-FLAG M2 agarose beads (Millipore, A2220). The beads were washed three times with buffer PET containing 0.3 M NaCl, where buffer PET consisted of 20 mM sodium phosphate [7.8], 10% glycerol, 1 mM EDTA, 0.01% Triton X-100, 1 mM DTT, and 0.25 mM PMSF. The beads were packed into a disposable column and washed with buffer PET containing 0.1 M NaCl. UV-DDB was eluted with buffer PET containing 0.1 M NaCl and 0.1 mg/mL 1 × FLAG peptide. The eluted fractions were incubated overnight at 4 °C with PreScission protease to remove the N-terminal 1 × FLAG tag. The cleaved sample was loaded onto a HiTrap Heparin HP column equilibrated with buffer PET containing 0.1 M NaCl. UV-DDB was eluted with buffer PET containing 1 M NaCl. The heparin eluate was desalted using a HiTrap desalting column equilibrated with buffer PET containing 0.1 M NaCl and further purified on a Capto™ HiRes Q 5/50 (Cytiva, 29275878) using a linear NaCl gradient from 0.1 to 0.6 M. UV-DDB was eluted at approximately 0.3 M NaCl. Purified UV-DDB was stored at − 80 °C until use.

In vitro NER assay

In vitro NER assay was perfomed as described previously [29]. 5 nM of XPC-RAD23B, 10 nM of TFIIH, 20 nM of XPA, 41.6 nM of RPA, 27 nM of XPG, and 13.3 nM of ERCC1-XPF were used. All proteins were > 95% pure and produced as previously described for XPC-RAD23B [25], TFIIH [30], RPA [31], XPG [32], and ERCC1-XPF [33]. The reactions were conducted in repair buffer containing 45 mM HEPES–KOH at pH 7.8, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.3 mM EDTA, 40 mM phosphocreatine (di-Tris salt, Sigma), 2 mM ATP, 1 mM DTT, 2.5 μg BSA, 0.5 μg creatine phosphokinase (Sigma), and NaCl (to a final concentration of 70 mM) in a volume of 10 uL. The mixtures were pre-warmed at 30 °C for 10 min and then 25 ng of plasmid DNA containing GTG 1,3-cisplatin was added and incubated at 30 °C for different incubation times (0, 15, 45, and 90 min). 0.5 μL of 1 μM complementary oligonucleotide (5’-GGG GGA AGA GTG CAC AGA AGA AGA CCT GGT CGA CCp-3’) for product labeling was added, and the mixture was heated at 95 °C for 5 min. The sample was allowed to cool down to room temperature for 15 min, followed by adding 1 μL of a Sequenase/[α-32P]-dCTP mix (0.25 units of Sequenase and 2.5 μCi of [α-32P]-dCTP per reaction) and incubating at 37 °C for 3 min. Then 1.2 μL of dNTP mix (100 μM of each dATP, dTTP, dGTP; 50 μM dCTP) was added and incubated for another 12 min. The reactions were stopped by adding 12 μL of loading dye (80% formamide and 10 mM EDTA) and heating at 95 °C for 5 min. Sample was loaded on 14% sequencing gel (8 M urea in 0.5 × TBE) and run at 45 W for 2.5 h. The reaction products were visualized using PhosphorImager (Typhoon RGB, Cytiva).

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

To test the activity of purified XPA, we performed electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs). 5 nM of three-way junction or duplex DNA was incubated with different concentrations of XPA (0, 10, 20, 40, 80, 120, and 200 nM) in XPA reaction buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl [8.0], 60 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mg/mL BSA, 5% glycerol and 1 mM EDTA) with or without 0.1 mM ZnCl2 at 25 °C for 30 min in a dark room to prevent the photobleaching of fluorophore. The reactants were loaded onto 8% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel and run in 0.5 × TBE buffer at 4 °C. The gel was then scanned by Amersham typhoon RGB (GE Healthcare). The band intensity was analyzed by ImageJ (NIH). The bound fraction was calculated by dividing the intensity of bound band by the sum of bound and free DNA band intensities.

QD-mediated protein dissociation test

5 nM of three-way junction DNA and Y-shape DNA were incubated with 30 nM and 60 nM XPA in XPA reaction buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl [8.0], 60 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mg/mL BSA, 5% glycerol and 1 mM EDTA) at 25 °C for 30 min in a dark room, respectively. Then commercial or synthesized QDs at different concentrations (0, 3, 10, 30, and 100 nM) were added, followed by 30-min incubation at 25 °C in a dark room. These samples were loaded onto 8% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel and run in 0.5 × TBE buffer at 4 °C.

For UV-DDB, 5 nM of CPD-containing DNA was incubated with 15 nM UV-DDB in DDB reaction buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl [8.0] and 1 mM EDTA) at 25 °C for 30 min in a dark room. Then commercial or synthesized QDs at different concentrations (0, 10, 30, 100, and 300 nM) were added, followed by 30-min incubation at 25 °C in a dark room. The reactants were loaded onto a 6% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel and run in 0.5 × TBE buffer at 4 °C.

All gels were then scanned by Typhoon RGB (Cytiva). The band intensity was analyzed by ImageJ (NIH). The bound fraction was calculated by dividing the intensity of bound band by the sum of bound and free DNA band intensities. Then each bound fraction was normalized by the bound fraction at 0 nM.

Polymer-mediated protein dissociation test

To examine the effect of macromolecular crowding agents on XPA–DNA complex stability, 5 nM three-way junction DNA was pre-incubated with 30 nM XPA in XPA reaction buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl [8.0], 60 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mg/mL BSA, 5% glycerol, and 1 mM EDTA) at 25 °C for 30 min in a dark room. Following the complex formation, PEG 200, 400, or 1000, or Dextran 35,000 was introduced at varying concentrations (2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 30% [w/v]), and the mixture was further incubated at 25 °C for 30 min in the dark. The resulting samples were resolved by electrophoresis on a 6% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel in 0.5xTBE buffer at 4 °C.

For UV-DDB, 5 nM CPD-containing DNA was pre-incubated with 15 nM UV-DDB in DDB reaction buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl [8.0] and 1 mM EDTA) at 25 °C for 30 min in the dark. PEG 1000 was subsequently added at different concentrations (2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 30% [w/v]), and the samples were resolved on a 6% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel in 0.5 × TBE buffer at 4 °C.

Gels were imaged using Typhoon RGB (Cytiva), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (NIH). The bound fraction was calculated by dividing the intensity of bound band by the sum of bound and free DNA band intensities. Then each bound fraction was normalized by the bound fraction at 0 nM.

QD synthesis

Materials

Cadmium oxide (CdO, 99.99%), oleic acid (90%), zinc acetate (99.99%), 1-octadecene (90%), selenium (99.9%), sulfur (99.5%), trioctylphosphine (TOP, 90%), 1-dodecanethiol (DDT, 98%), 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (TFE), [3-(methacryloylamino)propyl]dimethyl(3-sulfopropyl)ammonium hydroxide inner salt (96%), 4-cyano-4-(phenylcarbonothioylthio)pentanoic acid, 4,4′-azobis(4-cyanovaleric acid) (≥ 98%), triethylamine (TEA), trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), 2-(2-aminoethoxy)ethanol (AEE, 98%), bovine serum albumin (BSA) (98%), DBCO-TFP ester, ANTI-FLAG® M2 antibody, DBCO-Cy5, Tween-20 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. PBS buffer was purchased from Biosesang (Korea). All aqueous solutions were prepared with DIW, which was purified by a Milli-Q Purification System (Millipore, United States).

QD Characterization

The UV–Vis spectra were measured by UV-1800 (Shimadzu, Japan) and by NanoDrop instrument, BioSpec-nano (Shimadzu, Japan). Fluorescence spectra were measured by a VARIZN. Photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) was measured by QE-2000 (Otsuka, Japan). BRUKER AVANCE III HD spectrometer (400 MHz) was used to record the 1H Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectra. All NMR analysis were conducted in D2O solvent. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on an IRTracer-100 (Shimadzu, Japan). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging was measured by JEM-2100 (JEOL), using an acceleration voltage of 200 kV using Cu grids (Ted Pella, USA). X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted using a monochromated Al–Kα radiation source and He I (21.2 eV) discharge lamp (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted with a SmartLab instrument (Rigaku, Japan) operating at 45 kV/200 mA using a Cu Kα₁ radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å). The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of the nanoparticles were obtained using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments). Measurements were carried out at 25 °C using disposable cuvettes with a 4 mW, 633 nm laser and a fixed scattering angle of 90°, and all samples were diluted with De-Ionized Water (DIW). FPLC analyses were performed on NGC Chromatography System (Bio-Rad, USA) equipped with multi detector for protein and nanoparticle measurement and a FPLC column packed with Superose 6 10/300 resin (Cytiva, 17-0489-01) with 50 mM phosphate buffer [7.4] as the eluent.

Availability of azide groups of QD surface

DBCO-Cy5 standard curve was generated by measuring the absorbance of known DBCO-Cy5 concentrations at 646 nm. For each reaction, azide-functionalized QD (0.01 nmol) was incubated with DBCO-Cy5 at designated molar equivalents in a final volume of 200 µL. After the reaction, unbound DBCO-Cy5 was removed by purification using centrifugal membrane filtration (Amicon Ultra-0.5, 50 kDa) for 3 times, and the absorbance of the purified QD–DBCO-Cy5 conjugates was measured at 646 nm. The absorbance of QD-only samples was subtracted as background, and the concentration of QD-bound DBCO-Cy5 was calculated from the standard curve. The amount of bound DBCO-Cy5 was then divided by the amount of QD used in the reaction to determine the average number of DBCO-Cy5 molecules per QD.

Synthesis of polymer ligands

Red-emitting alloy-typed CdZnSeS/ZnS QDs were synthesized as described [34, 35] with a few modifications. The polymer was synthesized via RAFT polymerization following a previously reported protocol [36], and the monomers corresponding to the anchor and functional groups were synthesized according to the literature with modification [8, 37]. Briefly, the original solvent of anchor and functional monomers was degassed to remove solvent, and monomers were dispersed with a certain ratio in the mixed TFE and methanol (2:1, v/v) solvent. RAFT agent and initiator were added at the ratio of 1:1. The mixture was dispersed and then transferred to an ampule. After 4 repeats of freeze–pump–thaw, the ampule was sealed by a torch under vacuum and put in an oil bath (70 ºC, 12 h) for polymerization reaction. After polymerization, the crude polymer was washed with excess ethyl ether and hexane for 2 times. To activate the anchoring functionality, the polymer was treated with an acidic solvent mixture (TFE:TFA = 1:1) for 1 h to remove the BOC protecting group. The resulting deprotected by-products were removed by two washing steps using an anti-solvent mixture using excess ether and hexane. The pH was then adjusted back to neutrality using triethylamine (TEA), and the polymer was thoroughly dried.

Antibody conjugation with surface-modified QDs

For antibody functionalization, DBCO-TFP ester (3.5 mM in DMSO) was first conjugated to the ANTI-FLAG M2 antibody. The antibody (50 μg) was purified prior to reaction to remove residual sodium azide using centrifugal membrane filtration (Amicon Ultra-0.5, 50 kDa) twice with 1 × PBS (pH 7.4), followed by an additional wash with pH 9 NaHCO₃ buffer. The purified antibody was then mixed with DBCO-TFP ester solution with 25 ~ 30 molar equivalents excess in pH 9 NaHCO₃ buffer and reacted at 30 °C for 2 h. The resulting DBCO-modified antibody (DBCO-Ab) was purified by centrifugal filtration (three cycles), and the antibody concentration at each purification step was monitored by UV spectroscopy using a NanoDrop. Separately, Azide-functionalized QDs (QD-N3) was purified by FPLC and directly used for conjugation. The DBCO-Ab and QD-N3 were mixed at the desired molar ratio and incubated overnight at 4 °C to complete the strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) click chemistry. The final QD–antibody conjugates (QD-Ab) were purified by FPLC and concentrated using centrifugal membrane filtration (Amicon Ultra-0.5, 50 kDa).

Nonspecific binding (NSB) test of BSA protein

BSA solutions of various concentrations were added to water-dispersed QDs (0.2 μM). After incubation for 1 h, the mixtures were subjected either to agarose gel electrophoresis or loaded onto an FPLC sample loop to evaluate the extent of nonspecific adsorption of BSA onto the QD surface. And 0.5 wt% agarose gel (Conda) was prepared and mounted on an electrophoresis system (Mupid-exU, Mupid). The chamber was filled with 300 mL of 1 × TAE buffer (Biosesang), and the prepared QD samples were carefully loaded into the wells. Electrophoretic separation was performed at 100 mV for 30 min.

DNA curtain assays

Lambda phage DNA preparation

For DNA curtain assays, Biotin-cos-R and multi-Dig-cos-L oligomers were added to lambda phage DNA (New England Biolabs, N3011) at a 500-fold molar excess. The mixture was incubated at 65 °C for 20 min and slowly cooled to 23 °C. Subsequently, 20 U/μL of T4 DNA ligase (Enzynomics, M001) was added for ligation. Excessive free oligonucleotides were removed by gel filtration spin column (Illustra MicroSpin S-400, GE Healthcare).

On the other hand, λ-DNA containing 57-nt bubble was prepared as in the previous studies (Fig. S8) [23, 38]. 5 nM I3 λ-DNA containing seven serial Nt.BspQI nickase recognition sites was incubated with 0.8 U/μL of Nt.BspQI (New England Biolabs, R0644) in a reaction buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl [7.9], 1 mM ATP, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl₂, and 100 μg/mL recombinant albumin. The reaction was carried out at 50 °C for 2.5 h to create a single-stranded gap at a specific location within the λ-DNA. Following nickase digestion, the Nt.BspQI was inactivated by treatment with 0.5 mg/mL proteinase K. To facilitate gap hybridization and labeling, a 500-fold molar excess of Biotin-cos-R, multi-Dig-cos-L, and a λ-I3-bubble oligonucleotide was added to the reaction. The mixture was incubated at 80 °C for 20 min and then slowly cooled to 23 °C to allow oligonucleotide hybridization and inactivation of proteinase K. Subsequently, 20 U/μL of T4 DNA ligase (Enzynomics, M001) was added, and the reaction was incubated overnight at 23 °C to promote ligation. The ligase was inactivated by heating the mixture at 65 °C for 20 min. Unbound oligonucleotides were removed using a gel filtration spin column (Illustra MicroSpin S-400, GE Healthcare).

Flow cell preparation

For single-tethered DNA curtain assays, nano-trench patterns were engraved on fused-silica slides [39]. For double-tethered DNA curtain assays, nanometer-high diffusion barriers and pentagon-shaped pedestals made of chromium were used [40]. Both types of slides were cleaned and assembled into flow cells as described previously [39, 40]. Flow cells were washed with lipid buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl [8.0] and 100 mM NaCl) and then incubated with 0.5% biotinylated-DOPE (1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(cap biotinyl) (sodium salt)) (Avanti Polar Lipids, 870273), 8% mPEG 2000-DOPE (1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[methoxy(polyethylene glycol)-2000]) (Avanti Polar Lipids, 880130), and DOPC (1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine) (Avanti Polar Lipids, 850375) in lipid buffer for 10 min. After free liposomes were washed with lipid buffer, lipid bilayer was promoted on the slide surface for 20 min. For double-tethered DNA curtain experiments, 200 μL of 0.1 mg/mL anti-digoxigenin Fab fragment (Roche, 11214667001) was subsequently introduced into the flow cell and incubated for 40 min. The surface was passivated by BSA buffer (40 mM Tris-HCl [8.0], 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 0.2 mg/mL BSA). Successively, 25 μg/mL streptavidin in BSA buffer was incubated for 10 min. After unbound streptavidin was removed with BSA buffer, 40 pM biotinylated λ-DNA was anchored on the biotinylated lipid bilayer through streptavidin. The flow cell was placed on a custom-built total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscope and connected with a syringe pump fluidic system. All DNA curtain experiments were conducted at 23 °C. DNA curtains were formed in XPA imaging buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl [8.0], 60 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.1 mg/mL BSA).

Single-tether DNA curtain assays

Single-tethered DNA curtains were built up with multi-biotinylated λ-I3 containing a 57-nt bubble structure between 33,544 bp and 33,600 bp (Fig. S8). 50 nM 3 × FLAG tagged XPA was pre-incubated with 50 nM αFLAG-QD on ice for 10 min. Then, 1 nM XPA-QDs were injected into a flow cell through a 100 μL injection loop at 0.2 mL/min flow rate, followed by 5 min incubation without buffer flow. Then the flow resumed to wash out unbound XPA-QDs. Fluorescence images were collected using NIS-Elements software (Nikon) with 0.1 s exposure time.

Double-tethered DNA curtain assays

To investigate the motion of XPA on undamaged DNA, double-tethered DNA curtains with undamaged λ-DNA were used. To observe diffusion of XPA, 1 nM XPA-Qdots were injected into the flow cell through a 100 μL injection loop in XPA buffer at different NaCl concentrations (0, 25, and 50 mM) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min, followed by a 5-min incubation without buffer flow. Unbound XPA–QDs were then washed out, and QDs fluorescence weremeasured. To examine how XPA recognizes a bubble, 1 nM XPA-QDs were injected into double-tethered DNA curtains with bubble-containing λ-DNA in XPA imaging buffer. Images were taken immediately after the injection was stopped when the proteins maximally arrived at the DNA curtains. All DNA curtain images were collected for 10 min using NIS-Elements software (Nikon) in the absence of buffer flow with 0.1 s exposure time.

Data analysis for DNA curtains

Binding distribution of XPA

The binding distribution of XPA was analyzed using single-tethered DNA curtain assays using 57-nt bubble-containing λ-DNA. Each QD fluorescence signal was fitted with a two-dimensional Gaussian function, and the QD bound position was estimated from the distance between the center position and the nano-barrier. The distribution histogram of QDs was generated with an 1 kbp bin size using the Origin software (Origin Labs). The distribution was fitted with a Gaussian function, and the center position of the fitted Gaussian was defined as the main binding position.

Immobile and mobile analysis of XPA

For individual DNA molecules from the double-tethered DNA curtain assays, kymographs were generated using ImageJ. In kymographs, molecules that remained at a fixed position for longer than 5 s were classified as immobile, whereas molecules showing movement for longer than 5 s were classified as diffusive or mobile. The relative fractions of diffusive and immobile molecules were estimated by the number of each state divided by the total number of both states.

To test the sequence dependency of immobile states, a distribution histogram for the positions at which XPA was immobile was generated with a bin size of 1 kbp. In addition, to check whether immobile states repeatedly occurred at the same positions of individual DNA molecules, pairwise relative displacements between immobile positions were calculated within the same DNA molecule. A distribution histogram for these relative displacements was built up with a bin size of 1 kbp.

Relative displacement of XPA molecule

Time-resolved trajectories of XPA molecules were obtained through particle tracking using the MOSAIC plug-in in ImageJ (NIH). To quantify the relative displacement of XPA along DNA, the positional change within defined time intervals was calculated from these trajectories. Specifically, the displacement over one frame (0.1 s) was defined as the 1-step value, the displacement over two frames (0.2 s) was defined as the 2-step value, and the displacement over three frames (0.3 s) was defined as the 3-step value. These displacement distributions were then fitted with a Gaussian function. Results

Commercial QDs dissociate XPA protein from DNA

Human XPA with 6 × His and 3 × FLAG at the amino terminus was purified using a Ni–NTA column (Fig. 1B and S1A). The activity of purified tagged XPA was tested using in vitro NER assays. A plasmid containing a site-specific 1,3-cisplatin interstrand lesion was incubated with purified XPA along with the NER core proteins (XPC-RAD23B, TFIIH, RPA, XPG, ERCC1-XPF), and the excision of the lesion containing oligonucleotide was monitored in a time-dependent reaction. Product formation was observed in the expected manner, showing that the purified XPA was active (Fig. S1B) [29]. We next conducted electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) to examine the binding affinity of the purified XPA to DNA. As previously reported, the XPA preferentially bound to 3-way junction DNA compared with homoduplex (Fig. 1C and D) [29, 41]. A supershift of the XPA–DNA complex was observed in EMSA upon addition of anti-FLAG or anti-His antibodies, confirming antibody binding to purified XPA (Fig. S1C and S1D).

To fluorescently label the purified XPA, we prepared anti-FLAG antibody-conjugated QDs with an emission peak at 705 nm using the commercial QD-antibody conjugation kit. We freshly conjugated XPA and QD for each experiment as QDs must be stored at 4 °C whereas purified XPA is stored at -80 °C for a long term. In addition, antibody-conjugated QDs allow orthogonal labeling of proteins having different epitope tags. We tested the binding of anti-FLAG antibody-conjugated QD (αFLAG-QD) to XPA by supershift in EMSA. Unexpectedly, with increasing concentration of αFLAG-QDs, a free DNA band emerged while XPA-bound DNA band disappeared (Fig. 1E). Given that XPA binds to DNA in the presence of an anti-FLAG antibody, the data suggested that QDs added induce the dissociation of XPA from the 3-way junction DNA (Fig. S1C).

XPA dissociation is not affected by QD type, antibodies, buffer composition, epitope tags, and DNA substrates

We examined what induced the XPA dissociation from DNA in the presence of antibody-conjugated QDs. We first tested a different QD with a 605 nm emission wavelength, which also caused the dissociation of XPA from DNA (Fig. 1F and S2A). Changing the conjugated antibody from anti-FLAG to anti-His (αHis-QD) also dissociated XPA from DNA (Fig. 1F and S2A). Moreover, a commercial streptavidin-conjugated QD also caused XPA dissociation (Fig. 1F and S2A). These results demonstrated that the XPA dissociation is independent of the type, size, and conjugated antibodies of QDs.

Since XPA has a zinc-finger domain, Zn2+ might affect the DNA binding (Fig. 1B). To examine the Zn2+ effect on XPA dissociation in the presence of QDs, we removed Zn2+ from the buffer and added a divalent cation chelator EDTA. In the absence of Zn2+, QDs still dissociated XPA from the 3-way junction DNAs (Fig. S2B). We also suspected that the epitope tags at the amino terminus might influence DNA binding. Hence, we tested 6 × His-XPA and XPA without a tag. Regardless of the presence of epitope tags, αFLAG-conjugated QD led to the XPA dissociation (Fig. S2C). Finally, we changed a DNA substrate from 3-way junction DNA to Y-shape DNA (fork structure), which is also a good substrate of XPA[41]. XPA also dissociated from Y-shape DNA in the presence of QDs (Fig. S2D). Taken together, our data demonstrated that the XPA dissociation from DNA substrates does not result from the intrinsic interaction between XPA and DNA but results from QDs themselves.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG) coated on the QD surface causes XPA dissociation from DNA

Given that the XPA dissociation could not be attributed to types of commercial QDs, we suspected that the polymers that are coated on the QD surface might disrupt the binding between XPA and DNA. Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is commonly used to coat the surface of commercial QDs to stabilize the QDs. To test if PEG on the QD surface is indeed related to the XPA dissociation, we synthesized QDs that are covered with 500 Da PEG (PEG500) or 1000 Da PEG (PEG1000) (Fig. 2A and S3A). Both PEG-coated QDs induced XPA dissociation (Fig. 2A, C, and S3A). We also tested QDs, in which PEG was partially modified with azide- or carboxyl-groups. The partial modification similarly led to the dissociation of XPA, showing that PEG is a dominant factor for the XPA dissociation from DNA (Fig. 2B, C, and S3B). We finally tested the effect of PEG itself on the XPA-DNA complex. With increasing concentrations of PEG of different molecular weights (200, 400, and 1000 Da), the band for free DNA increased, suggesting that PEG itself induces the XPA dissociation from DNA, independently of the molecular weight of the PEG (Fig. 2D, E, S3C, and S3D). High concentrations of polymers can influence binding between proteins and nucleic acids due to the molecular crowding effect.[42–44] To test if the XPA dissociation from DNA in the presence of concentrated PEG 1000 would be due to the molecular crowding effect, we titrated the unrelated polymers 35000 Da dextran (Dextran35000) to the XPA-DNA binding reactions. Even at a high concentration (30%) of Dextran35000, XPA remained bound to DNA (Fig. 2D and E). These results demonstrated that the destabilization of the XPA-DNA interaction does not result from the molecular crowding effect but from the interaction with PEG.

Surface modification of QDs

Given that PEG on the QD surface induces XPA dissociation from DNA, we engineered the QD surface to prevent interference with biomolecular interactions. To this end, CdZnSeS/ZnS alloy-typed QDs were synthesized according to published protocols with a few modifications (Fig. S4A) [34, 35]. Synthesized QDs offer stable and superior features for subsequent aqueous ligand-exchange. These QDs exhibited a uniform particle size (9.20 ± 0.55 nm) (Fig. S4B). UV–vis absorption and photoluminescence (PL) emission spectra exhibited well-defined first excitonic absorption and narrow emission, with high quantum yield (QY) reaching ~ 100% (Fig. S4C). In addition, oleic-acid–based surface ligands were confirmed by FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. S4D). For water dispersion of QDs, polymer ligands were designed, consisting of three key components: an anchor group (A-group), a hydrophilic group (H-group), and a functional group (F-group) (Fig. 3A and S5A). A-group binds strongly to the QD surface, while the H group enables water dispersion; together, these two components play a central role in determining the colloidal stability of QDs. On the other hand, the F-group, based on PEG, is responsible for specific targeting of cells or biomolecules. The reason for introduction of PEG chain into F-group is increasing the conjugation efficiency by exposing it to the outside because the hydration layer of zwitterion in H-group can hinder the chemical reaction of functional moieties with biomolecules. The polymer ligand was synthesized via reversible addition–fragmentation chain-transfer (RAFT) polymerization. The three monomer components, corresponding to A-, H-, and F-groups, form a random copolymer, producing the final polymer ligand. We ensured the incorporation of each component into the polymer backbone with 1H NMR measurement (Fig. 3B). Using this polymer, we did surface modification with a two-step ligand-exchange strategy to obtain compact and water-dispersible QDs (Fig. 3A) [36].

To confirm successful surface modification, a series of spectroscopic and colloidal analyses were performed. After ligand exchange, the 1H NMR spectrum of purified QDs showed a pronounced reduction in imidazole-related proton signals from the A-group, indicating strong coordination of the anchor units to the QD surface, while signals from the zwitterionic H-group and PEG-based F-group remained, confirming the presence of the polymer ligand (Fig. S5B). FT-IR (Fourier Transform Intra-Red spectroscopy) analysis showed that the characteristic azide stretching band at 2100 cm− 1 was retained after ligand exchange, indicating the availability of azide groups for subsequent bioconjugation (Fig. S5C). XPS (X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy) analysis confirmed that the characteristic Cd, Zn, Se, and S signals were preserved after polymer coating, while increased C 1 s and O 1 s signals and the appearance of an N 1 s peak verified successful polymer ligand introduction without disruption of the core/shell structure (Fig. S5D). Furthermore, the polymer-coated QDs remained well dispersed over a wide pH range (5–13) and high salt concentrations (0.5–2 M NaCl), with unchanged hydrodynamic size confirmed by DLS (Dynamic Light Scattering) (Fig. S5E). UV–vis absorption and PL emission spectra showed that high emission efficiency was largely preserved after aqueous phase transfer, with the PLQY (Photo-Luminescence Quantum Yield) reaching approximately 94.8% (Fig. S5F). XRD (X-Ray Diffraction) analysis further confirmed that the characteristic (111), (220), and (311) diffraction peaks were maintained without peak broadening, indicating preserved crystalline phase and structural integrity after ligand exchange (Fig. S5G).

Although the analyses above confirmed the presence of azide groups on the QD surface, those do not provide quantitative information on whether the introduced azide groups remain accessible and reactive, or on how many azide groups per QD are available for subsequent conjugation. To address this problem, DBCO-Cy5 labeling was performed as a functional assay for surface-accessible azide groups. Since DBCO reacts with azide groups through strain-promoted azide–alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) with a 1:1 stoichiometry, the amount of DBCO-Cy5 retained on the QDs after purification was used to estimate the number of azide groups that were accessible and capable of participating in the reaction. To examine whether the labeling reflected the azide content on the QD surface, two different QDs having 20% PEG-N₃ and 5% PEG-N₃ were analyzed. After purification of excess DBCO-Cy5, QD-bound DBCO-Cy5 was quantified using a DBCO-Cy5 standard curve. Based on this analysis, 20% PEG-N₃ QDs contained approximately 9 ~ 10 accessible azide groups per QD, whereas 5% PEG-N₃ QDs contained approximately 4 accessible azide groups per QD (Fig. S5H). These results indicate that the azide groups detected by FT-IR were not only introduced onto the QD surface, but were also retained as reactive, surface-accessible functional groups. Therefore, the azide-functionalized QDs used in this study possessed sufficient surface azide groups available for DBCO-mediated conjugation.

Reducing PEG density may generally weaken the steric shielding effect on the particle surface and thereby increase the likelihood of nonspecific binding with proteins. Therefore, we further examined whether the low-PEG QDs used in this study induce nonspecific protein adsorption or colloidal instability. To this end, a nonspecific binding (NSB) test was performed using BSA, a widely used model globular protein for assessing nonspecific interactions. Water-dispersed QDs (0.2 μM) were incubated with BSA solutions at various concentrations, and the resulting mixtures were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis and FPLC to evaluate nonspecific adsorption of BSA onto the QD surface [45, 46]. In the agarose gel electrophoresis analysis, the migration position of the QD band remained nearly unchanged regardless of the BSA concentration (Fig. S5I). These results indicate that BSA adsorption did not appreciably alter the surface charge or hydrodynamic behavior of the QDs. For a more quantitative assessment, FPLC analysis was performed (Fig. S5J). The elution peak corresponding to the QDs remained essentially unchanged at approximately 29.1 min across all BSA concentrations, whereas only the peak corresponding to free BSA, observed at approximately 34.8 min, increased progressively with increasing BSA concentration. If nonspecific adsorption of BSA onto the QD surface had occurred, a shift or broadening of the QD peak, or the appearance of an additional QD–BSA complex peak, would be expected. However, no such changes were observed. These results demonstrate that the polymer-coated low-PEG QDs exhibit minimal nonspecific interaction with BSA, even in the presence of excess protein. This resistance to nonspecific binding can be attributed not only to PEG-mediated steric shielding, but also to the hydration layer formed by the zwitterionic polymer coating on the QD surface, which effectively suppresses protein adsorption [47]. In addition, the unchanged migration behavior in agarose gel electrophoresis and the stable FPLC elution profile support that the QDs retained colloidal stability under the tested low-PEG conditions. Taken together, these results clarify that reducing PEG density in our QD system did not lead to appreciable nonspecific BSA adsorption or colloidal instability under the biological experimental conditions used in this study.

QDs with reduced PEG content on the surface do not induce XPA dissociation from DNA

To avoid XPA dissociation by QDs, we systematically changed the relative fraction of polymers on the QD surface. We changed the relative fraction of A-group and F-group with roughly similar fraction of H-group. QDs with a lower amount of F-group relative to the A-group did not detach XPA from DNA (Fig. 3C and E). By contrast, QDs with a relatively high amount of F-group compared with that of A-group caused dissociation of XPA from DNA as we had observed with the commercial QDs (Fig. 3D and E). These results supported our hypothesis that PEG is the major factor interfering with the XPA-DNA interaction. When we tested a number of different ratios between A-, H-, and F- groups, A-group and F-group showed anti-correlation for the XPA dissociation (Fig. 3F and S6). Quantitatively, we found that QDs did not dissociate the protein from DNA when the F-group content was less than 7% (Fig. 3F and Table S2).

Antibody is conjugated with QDs via SPAAC chemistry

Azide-functionalized QDs (QD-N₃) were conjugated with DBCO-modified antibodies (Ab-DBCO) via strain-promoted alkyne-azide cycloaddition (SPAAC) click chemistry to generate QD and Ab conjugates (QD-Ab) (Fig. 4A)[48]. To conjugate XPA containing a FLAG tag, an anti-FLAG antibody was used. In Step 1, TFP ester group of DBCO-TFP ester was reacted with primary amines on the antibody to introduce the DBCO moiety onto the Ab. After purification, successful DBCO modification was confirmed by UV-vis spectroscopy, showing the characteristic antibody absorption at 280 nm together with an additional absorption feature at ~ 309 nm corresponding to the DBCO group (Fig. 4A and B). The concentration of Ab-DBCO was determined based on these absorption characteristics. In Step 2, the DBCO-Ab was reacted with QD-N₃, yielding a triazole-linked QD-Ab conjugate through SPAAC chemistry. After conjugation, subtracting the QD-N₃ baseline from the QD-Ab spectrum revealed the distinct 280 nm Ab peak, confirming that the antibody was successfully coupled to the QD surface (Fig. 4A and C). The conjugation was further validated by DLS analysis. The hydrodynamic diameter increased from that of QD-N₃ (15.1 nm), indicating a larger size upon antibody conjugation (18.5 nm) (Fig. 4D). Finally, the number of antibodies conjugated per QD was evaluated by increasing the ratio of antibody and monitoring the peak intensity of fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) elution profile of Ab–DBCO at 33.8 min. As the antibody-to-QD ratio increased, the elution peak gradually shifted toward shorter retention times, confirming successful antibody conjugation (Fig. 4E). Based on these chromatographic changes, an antibody-to-QD ratio of 3 was selected for subsequent experiments to maintain a compact particle size suitable for single-QD imaging. These results confirmed that the antibody is successfully and specifically conjugated to the QD surface.

Single-molecule imaging shows that low-PEG-density QDs do not dissociate XPA from DNA

We finally tested if antibody-conjugated QDs with a reduced amount of PEG would be able to monitor the association of XPA with DNA and avoid the induction of dissociation. In nucleotide excision repair, XPA is engaged in a bubble that is formed by TFIIH at a damage site and serves as a scaffold protein for the pre-incision complex[26, 49, 50]. Since XPA preferentially binds a bubble, a single 57-nt bubble construct was inserted into lambda DNA at a specific location (33,498 ~ 33,655 bp) (Fig. S8). DNA curtains were built up with the bubble-containing lambda DNA (Fig. 5A). We used single-tethered DNA curtain assays, in which only one end of lambda DNA is modified with biotin and anchored on a lipid bilayer, while the other end is free (Fig. 5A). Continuous buffer flow is required to align and stretch DNA molecules at a diffusion barrier (Fig. 5B, top). When QD-labeled XPA was incubated with the lambda DNA, a majority of QDs were well aligned at the bubble location on the DNA curtain (Fig. 5B, 2nd panel). When the buffer flow was turned off, the QDs disappeared, indicating that QD-labeled XPA bound to DNA (Fig. 5B, 3rd panel). When QDs not conjugated to XPA were incubated, no QDs bound to DNA were observed (Fig. 5B, bottom). The binding distribution of QDs exhibited one distinct peak at 33.7 ± 2.1 kbp, corresponding to the bubble location (Fig. 5C). Taken together, these data demonstrated that QD-labeled XPA preferentially binds to a bubble in DNA curtains.

We next tested how XPA searches for a bubble using the double-tethered DNA curtain assay, in which the other end of lambda DNA is tethered to pedestals via antigen–antibody linkage (Fig. S9A). In the absence of a bubble on lambda DNA, we observed that XPA diffused along DNA with frequent immobile states (pauses) (Fig. 5D). The immobile fraction was approximately 60% regardless of ionic strength, which was consistent with the previous report (Fig. 5E) [26]. We analyzed the diffusive motion of XPA using particle tracking (Fig. S9B and S9C). The relative displacements exhibited Gaussian distributions with their peaks at zero, indicating Brownian motion (Fig. S9D). We analyzed the positions where XPA was in the immobile state. We found them to be sequence-independent, suggesting that the immobile states occur at random sequences (Fig. 5F). However, the relative distance between positions of immobile states was mostly zero, showing that XPA repeatedly showed immobile states at the same locations in a single DNA molecule (Fig. 5G). Next, diffusion coefficients were estimated from the linear fitting of mean square displacement and estimated to be approximately 0.27 μm2/sec (Fig. 5H and S9E). The diffusion coefficients were independent of ionic strength, suggesting that XPA diffuses along DNA not via hopping but via sliding (Fig. 5H) [25, 27]. We then investigated how XPA searches for a DNA bubble using the double-tethered DNA curtain assays, which allow observing XPA behaviors on DNA in the absence of hydrodynamic force (Fig. 5I). Molecules that were initially bound to duplex DNA and subsequently moved to the bubble structure were classified as one-dimensional (1D) diffusion, whereas those directly bound to the bubble position without diffusion were classified as three-dimensional (3D) collision. XPA recognized the bubble through both 3D collision and 1D diffusion (Fig. 5J and K). However, 3D collision prevailed over 1D diffusion for XPA’s searching for ss/dsDNA junctions (Fig. 5L).

PEG-induced protein dissociation is not specific to XPA

We next examined whether PEG-induced protein dissociation is specific to XPA or can also occur with other DNA-binding proteins. To address this question, we examined UV-DDB, a damage-sensing factor in NER that preferentially recognizes cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) [27] (Fig. S10A and S10B).

In EMSA using CPD-containing DNA, UV-DDB produced two shifted bands: a lower-mobility complex corresponding to CPD binding and another corresponding to nonspecific binding to the undamaged duplex region (Fig. S10C). Similar to XPA, UV-DDB dissociated from DNA as PEG concentration increased. Notably, the nonspecifically bound UV-DDB complex was more sensitive to PEG and dissociated before the CPD-bound complex (Fig. S10C and S10D). These results indicate that PEG-induced dissociation is not limited to XPA but can also affect other protein-DNA complexes.

We further investigated whether QDs could induce UV-DDB dissociation from DNA. A commercial QD promoted dissociation of UV-DDB from the undamaged region, whereas our low-PEG (5%) QD did not appreciably perturb the UV-DDB-DNA complex (Fig. S10E–S10G). Taken together, these results demonstrate that PEG and PEG-coated QD-induced protein dissociation is not specific to XPA.

Discussion

We found that commercial QDs promote the dissociation of XPA from its nucleic acid substrates. We were able to attribute this dissociation to the PEG polymer coated on the QD surface. Initially, we considered the molecular crowding of PEG as a cause. This effect has been shown to affect protein conformation, dynamics, and function via the excluded volume effect by forming large clusters that physically block the active site [51]. However, this hypothesis was challenged by the observation that high concentrations of dextran did not induce XPA dissociation (Fig. 2D and E), suggesting that molecular crowding is not the primary mechanism. Next, we considered the role of XPA’s intrinsically disordered domains (IDRs). IDRs may function in protein-nucleic acids or protein–protein interactions [26, 52, 53]. We hypothesized that PEG might restrict this conformational flexibility, thereby impairing XPA’s ability to adopt a stable DNA-bound conformation and reducing the binding stability. To test this hypothesis, we purified a deletion mutant lacking the N-terminal IDR (1 ~ 97 amino acids) of XPA (XPA_ΔIDR) (Fig. S11A and S11B). XPA_ΔIDR showed similar binding affinity to that of wild-type XPA (Fig. S11C and S11D). Addition of anti-FLAG antibody-conjugated commercial QDs also induced the dissociation of XPA_ΔIDR from DNA, which excluded this possibility (Fig. S11E). Instead, we focused on the nature of the XPA-DNA interaction itself. Since the interaction is not sequence-specific but structure-dependent, it relies heavily on water-mediated hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with the DNA backbone [29, 54]. PEG is a highly hydrophilic polymer that strongly competes for water molecules, likely disrupting the essential hydration shell at the protein-DNA interface. We speculate that this PEG-induced dehydration destabilizes the XPA-DNA complex and increases its dissociation from DNA.

Given these findings, we speculated that the high local density of PEG on the QD surface is the critical factor. The densely packed PEG on the QD surface likely interacts directly with the existing XPA-DNA complex, inducing the XPA detachment through localized dehydration and potential steric interference. To test this, we synthesized QDs having different densities of PEG on their surface. The lower PEG density suppressed the XPA dissociation (Fig. 3E).

This PEG-induced protein dissociation is not specific to XPA. Another DNA damage repair protein, UV-DDB, also dissociated from DNA in a PEG- or commercial QD concentration-dependent manner (Fig. S10C–S10G). Of note, PEG or commercial QDs preferentially disrupted the nonspecific UV-DDB complex bound to undamaged DNA, whereas the CPD-bound UV-DDB complex was relatively more resistant. This difference is likely explained by the lower binding affinity of UV-DDB to undamaged DNA compared with CPD-containing DNA [27]. On the other hand, in a previous single-molecule study, commercial QDs were successfully used to label UV-DDB because the QD concentration used in that study was substantially lower than the concentrations at which QD-induced dissociation becomes apparent [27].

We then conjugated antibodies to the newly synthesized QDs via click chemistry between azide and DBCO for fluorescent imaging of XPA using the single-molecule DNA curtain technique (Fig. 5). We found that QD-labeled XPA diffuses along DNA with frequent pauses as reported in the previous literature [26]. We analyzed diffusion coefficients only for the diffusive motion, which was approximately 0.27 μm2/sec regardless of ionic strength, suggesting that XPA slides along DNA during 1D diffusion. Furthermore, diffusion coefficients were all greater than the theoretical maximum (0.062 μm2/sec) for helical rotation around DNA duplex, suggesting that XPA does not rotate along DNA helix during 1D diffusion. Our diffusion coefficients are order-of-magnitude higher than those (~ 0.037 μm2/sec) in the previous study [26]. This discrepancy results from the difference in the analysis. In the previous analysis, the immobile portion seemed to be included for the estimation of diffusion coefficients, which led to small diffusion coefficients [26].

Using DNA curtain assays with QD-labeled XPA, we revealed that XPA searches for ss/dsDNA junctions via both 1D diffusion and 3D collision. Moreover, we showed that 3D collision prevails over 1D diffusion. 1D diffusion facilitates target search when protein concentration is low [55]. The number of XPA in a human cell is approximately 150,000 ~ 200,000, while the number of XPC-RAD23B, which is known to diffuse along DNA, is 25,000 ~ 80,000 [56]. The intracellular number of XPA is up to 8 times higher than that of XPC-RAD23B. At this high XPA concentration, 3D collision is sufficient to rapidly recognize ss/dsDNA junctions.

Conclusion

In this study, we uncover an unexpected limitation of commercially available QDs, demonstrating that PEG-coated QDs can actively destabilize protein–DNA interactions by inducing protein dissociation. Using XPA as a model system, we show that this effect is independent of antibody type, epitope tags, buffer conditions, and DNA substrates, and instead arises primarily from PEG on the QD surface. By systematically engineering the surface polymer composition, we identify a critical PEG density threshold below which protein–DNA interactions are preserved without compromising colloidal stability or fluorescence performance. Furthermore, the use of SPAAC-based click chemistry enables efficient and minimally perturbative antibody conjugation. The optimized QDs allow accurate single-molecule visualization of XPA dynamics, revealing that XPA locates DNA bubble structures predominantly via three-dimensional collisions rather than one-dimensional diffusion. Collectively, our findings provide a general strategy for designing nanoparticle probes that maintain native biomolecular interactions and establish a robust platform for high-fidelity single-molecule imaging studies.
