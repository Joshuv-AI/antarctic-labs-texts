Micro- and nanoplastics in human biological materials: a systematic review of detection methods and methodological challenges
Authors: Aleksandra Dmitrowicz, Mariusz Sacharczuk, Dominik Skiba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Micro- and nanoplastics are an increasing focus in biomedical research, yet their definition, classification, and detection methods remain largely unstandardised. The lack of consistent nomenclature, agreed size criteria, and uniform reporting methods significantly hinders comparison of available data. Therefore, a systematic review of studies on analytical methods for detecting micro- and nanoplastics ex vivo in human biological samples was conducted. Most included studies (63.89%) were published between 2024 and 2025, highlighting the topicality of this research area. Study populations varied widely in age and health status, and sample sizes were often small. The most frequently analysed samples included placenta, kidneys, liver, lungs, heart, and urine, while other tissues were reported sporadically. Detection methods primarily involved microscopic techniques, chromatographic approaches, and Raman and FTIR spectroscopy. Considerable variability was observed in sample preparation procedures, including storage, digestion, and filtration, which may affect analytical sensitivity and specificity. Differences were also noted in quality control strategies and contamination minimisation approaches. Reported particle sizes and concentration units varied substantially, and many studies did not distinguish between microplastics and nanoplastics, treating them as a single category. This further hinders data comparison and the accurate assessment of human exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00204-026-04392-1.

Introduction

Micro- and nanoplastics are small synthetic polymer particles primarily formed from the degradation of larger plastic fragments and, to a lesser extent, intentionally produced on a small scale (Andrady 2011; Cole and Galloway 2015). When assessing environmental pollution or health risks, the characteristics of polymer particles, such as chemical composition, origin, and shape, are determined. However, the basic classification parameter is their size (Hartmann et al. 2019). There is no consensus in the scientific literature regarding the precise size thresholds that differentiate microplastics from nanoplastics, making it difficult to compare results and interpret data (Koelmans et al. 2015). Many publications have attempted to define these particles. The term microlitter was used to describe plastic particles ranging in size from 67 to 500 μm, even before the concept of microplastic was introduced (Gregory and Andrady 2003; Thompson et al. 2004). In subsequent years, numerous discrepancies appeared in the literature regarding both the lower and upper size limits of these particles (Fig. 1). The lower limit of microplastics is defined as 0.1 μm (EFSA 2016), 1 μm (Browne et al. 2007; Hartmann et al. 2019), or 5 μm (Rochman et al. 2015), while the upper limit can be 1 mm (Browne et al. 2007, Hartmann et al. 2016, ISO, 2020), 2 mm (Ryan et al. 2009), or 5 mm (Arthur et al. 2008; State Water Resources Control Board 2020; EFSA 2016). In the case of nanoplastics, the discrepancies are even greater, with the lower limit rarely specified and the upper limit ranging from 100 nm (Arthur et al. 2008; EU Commission 2011; Klaine et al. 2012) to 1 μm (Gigault et al. 2018; ISO/CEN ISO/TR 21960 2020).Fig. 1Examples of differences in the categorization of plastic debris by size, as applied and/or defined in the scientific literature and institutional reports for microplastics and nanoplastics. Changes over the years in the proposed definitions and size limits may result from the development of analytical techniques and equipment. It is now possible to detect increasingly smaller particles, which has led to a redefinition of the lower size limits. In early studies, such as Browne et al. (2007), the lower limit for microplastics was 1 μm, and smaller particles were classified as nanoplastics or no lower limit was specified for them. However, more recent publications take a more precise approach, defining nanoplastics as particles ranging in size from 1 nm to 1 μm (Hartmann et al. 2016; Gigault et al. 2018). The lack of a consistent approach to distinguishing between microplastics and nanoplastics means that the same particles can be classified in both categories depending on the definition used. For example, Hartmann et al. (2016) include particles up to 1 μm in the nanoplastics category, while EFSA (2016) still includes this size in the microplastics category. This ambiguity makes it difficult to compare research results and develop a consistent environmental or health risk assessment, which is why it is necessary to standardize the definitions.

A similar issue concerns the analytical methods used to detect microplastics and nanoplastics in biological materials. Numerous measurement techniques exist, each with different sensitivity, resolution, selectivity, and sample preparation requirements. The absence of standardised protocols for extraction, purification, and quality verification further increases result variability, making it difficult to accurately assess human exposure to these particles.

In recent years, the number of studies analysing the presence of microplastics and nanoplastics directly in human tissues and other ex vivo biological samples has increased rapidly. This growing interest arises from the need to understand the bioavailability of these particles, their potential routes of entry into the body, and possible health consequences. However, the available publications display considerable methodological heterogeneity, making it difficult to draw consistent conclusions.

Therefore, conducting a systematic appraisal of the existing studies investigating the detection of micro- and nanoplastics in human biological materials appears justified. The aim of this study is to assess the extent to which the diversity of definitions, analytical methods, and sample preparation protocols affects the results obtained and the comparability of data. This review allows us to identify key research gaps, indicate areas requiring standardization, and assess which techniques are currently most commonly used for the detection of plastic particles in human tissues.

Methods

An article search was conducted in the electronic databases PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, accessed on 14.11.2025) and Science Direct (https://www.sciencedirect.com/, accessed on 14.11.2025). Articles were searched by title and abstract, and the following keywords were used (“microplastic*” OR “nanoplastic*”) and (“human tissue*” OR “human-derived”). Total of 221 publications from the PubMed and ScienceDirect databases were included in the systematic literature review. Based on titles and abstracts, 148 publications were excluded, including 64 duplicate records. Among the excluded studies, 24 were in vitro experiments conducted solely on cell lines, 6 were environmental or ecological investigations without analysis of human biological material, and 54 were review or theoretical articles. The remaining 73 publications were assessed in full text. During this stage, an additional 40 publications were excluded. 5 studies were not accessible in full or contained incomplete data, 3 involved special cases such as milk or placentas intentionally spiked with microplastics, and 3 were non-human studies conducted in animal models. The remaining publications were excluded due to methodological limitations, including the absence of appropriate microplastic detection techniques or lack of results regarding the presence of microplastics in human biological samples. To ensure comprehensive coverage of the existing literature, the reference lists of retrieved articles were examined, which led to the identification of additional studies eligible for inclusion. Ultimately, the analysis was limited to ex vivo studies in which micro- and nanoplastics were detected directly in human tissues or biological fluids.

The aim of this review is to critically evaluate the analytical methods used in in vivo studies of human biological materials for the presence of micro- or nanoplastics, with particular emphasis on their capabilities, limitations, and methodological issues related to detection.

Results

This review includes 36 ex vivo studies, with the largest proportion coming from 2025 (41.67%, n2025 = 15), followed by 2024 (22.22%, n2024 = 8,). More than half of the publications were published in the last two years, which emphasizes that the review collects the latest available data and reflects the current state of knowledge.

Characteristics of in vivo studies on the presence of micro- and nanoplastics in human biological material

Characteristics of the studied populations

The studies included in the review involved populations with diverse characteristics, both in terms of demographic and clinical aspects (Table S1, Supplementary Material). Some studies examined only specific, narrowly defined populations, such as women of childbearing age (Oliveira et al. 2025; Ni et al. 2025; Yun et al. 2024; Montano et al. 2023), while others included people from different age groups. The age range of the subjects was very wide, from newborns (Braun et al. 2021) and children (Zhang et al. 2025a, b) to older people over 70 years of age (Wang et al. 2025b; Zhang et al. 2025a, b; Zhao et al. 2024; Rotchell et al. 2023; Marfella et al. 2024; Ao et al. 2023), and selected studies were based on postmortem samples (Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Amato-Lourenço et al. 2024; Nihart et al. 2025).

The sample size in some studies was relatively small, ranging from single cases (Dzierżyński et al. 2025) to a few or several participants (Oliveira et al. 2025; Erdely et al. 2025; Ni et al. 2025; Amato-Lourenço et al. 2024; Rotchell et al. 2023; Horvatits et al. 2022; Ragusa et al. 2022a, 2021; Braun et al. 2021; Montano et al. 2023; Ao et al. 2023), which limited the generalizability of the findings. Overall, 41.67% of the studies included participants of both sexes, whereas the remaining studies focused on a single sex, with females being considerably more frequently studied (36.11% of all publications). This distribution reflected the nature of the biological material analyzed, such as placenta (Oliveira et al. 2025; Yun et al. 2024; Garcia et al. 2024; Ragusa et al. 2022a, 2021; Weingrill et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025b), follicular fluid (Kong et al. 2025; Ni et al. 2025), breast milk (Ragusa et al. 2022b), and, in the case of males, semen (Kong et al. 2025; Montano et al. 2023). The limitations associated with the characteristics of the study population were repeatedly emphasized by the authors. Oliveira et al. (2025) noted that the small number of participants was a significant limitation, although they argued that a similar or even smaller sample size had been used in previous studies on microplastic accumulation in human tissues. However, it should be emphasized that referring to previous studies of lower methodological quality does not eliminate the problem, but rather perpetuates it. Yun et al. (2024) pointed out that the small size of the study group limits the possibility of generalizing the results to a wider population. A larger and more diverse group of participants would increase statistical power and allow for more reliable conclusions about the relationship between microplastic exposure and health outcomes. In one of the largest studies to date, involving 51 samples of follicular fluid and 51 samples of seminal fluid, it was shown that although the number of samples provides sufficient statistical power to analyze the number of oocytes, it remains insufficient to assess sperm quality and pregnancy rates in the context of in vitro fertilization (Kong et al. 2025). Another limitation is the heterogeneity of the populations studied. As noted by Ni et al. (2025), participants originated from diverse regions, performed various occupations, and exhibited different lifestyle habits. This resulted in heterogeneous exposure to different types and doses of plastic particles, which complicates the comparability of results and limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions.

The studies included both healthy individuals and patients with specific diseases. The cases analyzed included digestive system diseases such as stomach and colon cancer (Zhao et al. 2024; Cetin et al. 2023), and respiratory diseases, including lung cancer and various types of adenocarcinoma (Ao et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2024). In some studies, the health status of the participants was directly related to the nature of the biological material analyzed, and the samples were collected during diagnostic or surgical procedures (Massardo et al. 2024). Some studies used postmortem material (Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Amato-Lourenço et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025a, b; Cao et al. 2025).

In some cases, limitations related to the representativeness of the study groups were noted. Marfella et al. (2024) pointed out that their results apply only to asymptomatic patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy, which limits the possibility of generalizing the conclusions to the general population. In addition, several studies lacked complete data on participant characteristics, which made it difficult to fully interpret the results (Erdely et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2024; Braun et al. 2021; Ragusa et al. 2022a, b; Leslie et al. 2022; Weingrill et al. 2023; Wright et al. 2025).

An analysis of the geographical distribution of in vivo studies on the presence of micro- and nanoplastics in human biological material (Fig. 3) showed their concentration in countries such as China (n = 10) and Italy (n = 7), as well as in Mexico (n = 3), the United Kingdom (n = 3), Brazil (n = 2), USA (n = 2) and Single studies came from Poland, Germany, the Netherlands, Iran, Hawaii (USA), the Czech Republic, and Canada. Three publications lacked information about the country of origin of the samples or the location of the analysis, which makes it difficult to assess the potential impact of environmental factors on the results obtained. A relatively large number of studies have been conducted in China and European countries, which may be due to the high level of domestic plastic production and consumption, as well as increasing scientific interest in the health consequences of population exposure to plastic particles (Fok et al. 2020). Figure 3 also highlights significant gaps, with data missing from many regions of the world, including Africa, Australia, and most Asian countries. Scope of studied human organs and biological fluids

Thus far, in vivo investigations addressing the presence of micro- and nanoplastics in human biological material have covered a broad spectrum of tissues and organs (Fig. 4.). The most frequently analyzed materials were the placenta (Oliveira et al. 2025; Yun et al. 2024; Garcia et al. 2024; Ragusa et al. 2022a; Braun et al. 2021; Ragusa et al. 2021; Weingrill et al. 2023; Zurub et al. 2024; Halfar et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025b) and the kidneys (Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Nihart et al. 2025; Erdely et al. 2025; Horvatits et al. 2022; Massardo et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025a, b; Cao et al. 2025), which is partly attributable to the accessibility of samples obtained during clinical contexts such as childbirth or diagnostic procedures. The selection of the analyzed material is dictated by the possibility of obtaining it, e.g. through surgical procedures (Massardo et al. 2024) or autopsies (Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Amato-Lourenço et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025a, b; Cao et al. 2025). However, it should be emphasized that the presence of micro- and nanoplastics in tissues obtained in this way may only partially reflect the circulation of particles in the bloodstream and not their actual accumulation in the organ, which complicates the unambiguous interpretation of the results (Leslie et al. 2022).

Numerous tissues and fluids, including the umbilical cord (Oliveira et al. 2025), thyroid gland (Dzierżyński et al. 2025), pancreas (Zhao et al. 2024), adrenal glands (Wang et al. 2025a, b), and stomach (Zhao et al. 2024), have been analyzed in only a few studies. Although these are potentially interesting objects for studying microplastic accumulation, the limited number of analyses may be due to the difficulty in obtaining samples and the fact that previous studies have focused on organs that are more easily accessible clinically. At the same time, such isolated observations point to a significant gap in the literature that requires systematic exploration in future studies.

Methods of detecting micro- and nanoplastics

The reliability of research findings depends on appropriate sample preparation, ensuring that the analyzed material is representative of the entire area under investigation. Preparing samples for analysis is a multi-step process that involves collection, drying, separation of micro- and nanoplastics, and filtration. To date, no standardized preparation or detection technique has been established that would allow for comprehensive characterization of micro- and nanoplastics in human biological material. Each method has certain limitations, related, for example, to the size of the analyzed particles or their destructive nature. A review of available in vivo studies indicates that authors use different analytical approaches, often combining several techniques, which highlights the need for a critical assessment of the choice of methods in the context of the specificity of the sample under study.

Collection and preparation of samples for analysis

The samples were mainly obtained from clinical procedures (childbirth, surgical procedures, biopsies) or autopsies. Some were collected during routine outpatient procedures (e.g., urine (Rotchell et al. 2024; Pironti et al. 2022), semen (Montano et al. 2023), or tear fluid (Wang et al. 2025b). The biological material in the studies included in this review was collected using rigorous procedures to minimize the risk of contamination of samples with micro- or nanoplastics from the environment, and the collection method was adapted to the specific characteristics of the material. After collection, most samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis (Kong et al. 2025; Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Ni et al. 2025; Garcia et al. 2024; Weingrill et al.. 2023; Massardo et al. 2024; Cui et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025b; Wright et al. 2025; Massie et al. 2025); in some cases, a temperature of − 20 °C was used (Zhao et al. 2024; Amato-Lourenço et al. 2024; Horvatits et al. 2022; Ragusa. et al. 2022b; Ragusa et al. 2021; Leslie et al. 2022; Zurub et al. 2024) or general freezing conditions (Rotchell et al. 2024, 2023; Ragusa et al. 2022a). Some of the materials were fixed in a 10% formalin solution (Nihart et al. 2025; Erdely et al. 2025; Marfella et al. 2024; Cetin et al. 2023; Massie et al. 2025), in a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution (Marfella et al. 2024), or embedded in paraffin (Wright et al. 2025). In several studies, a drying step was incorporated prior to subsequent sample-processing stages. Wright et al. (2025) reported preliminary dehydration of the material, while specific drying conditions were described in other works. Kong et al. (2025) dried their samples at 60 °C using a laboratory drying oven. Braun et al. (2021) applied drying at 100 °C for 2 h, whereas Montano et al. (2023) used a higher temperature of 120 °C with a prolonged drying time of 4 h.

Almost all researchers included the matrix digestion stage, which involved removing organic and inorganic contaminants present in the collected sample in order to isolate plastic particles. In general, various digestion methods such as acid, alkaline, oxidative, and enzymatic are used depending on the type of matrix (Adhikari et al. 2022). The most commonly used reagent was a 10% KOH solution (Oliveira et al. 2025; Nihart et al. 2025; Erdely et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2024; Garcia et al. 2024; Ragusa et al. 2022b, 2021; Montano et al. 2023; Weingrill et al. 2023; Massardo et al. 2024; Halfar et al. 2023; Pironti et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2025b; Massie et al. 2025), with digestion times varying depending on the protocol and type of biological material. This process was carried out for 48 h (Ragusa et al. 2022b; Montano et al. 2023; Pironti et al. 2022), for 3–7 days (Oliveira et al. 2025; Nihart et al. 2025; Erdely et al. 2025; Garcia et al. 2024; Ragusa et al. 2021; Weingrill et al. 2023; Massardo et al. 2024) to 14 days (Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2024) at room temperature or at 40 °C (Nihart et al. 2025; Ragusa et al. 2022b) or 60 °C (Massardo et al. 2024). The second most commonly used reagent was a 30% H2O2 solution (Rotchell et al. 2024, 2023; Braun et al. 2021; Leslie et al. 2022; Zurub et al. 2024). Rotchell et al. (2023), who used H2O2 to digest samples, pointed out that the presence of non-microplastic particles suggests that the digestion process is incomplete. They indicated that in the case of relatively fatty human tissues, the use of an additional enzymatic digestion step could increase the effectiveness of matrix removal and reduce the risk of false positive results. Concentrated nitric acid (HNO3), often assisted by heating, was used much less frequently (Ni et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025b; Wang et al. 2025a, b), probably due to the risk of micro- or nanoplastics degradation. Horvatits et al. (2022) used multi-step digestion, initially in a 10% KOH solution, followed by a 30% H2O2 solution. Amato-Lourenço et al. (2024) prepared samples using enzymatic digestion with a mixture of Corolase 7089 enzymes, which allowed for gentler removal of organic matter. Cui et al. (2025) justified the use of enzymatic digestion of samples to remove organic material while preserving microplastic particles. Wang et al. (2025a, b) employed ultrasonic treatment to facilitate the dispersion and release of microplastic particles from tissues, aiming to enhance extraction efficiency. A single study additionally incorporated a density separation step using NaCl, exploiting differences in density between non-organic material present in the samples and microplastic particles (Cetin et al. 2023). In contrast to most studies, which subjected samples to intensive digestion, Arslan et al. (2025) applied a simplified methodological approach, in which samples were merely collected and diluted. The final preparation step in the majority of studies was filtration, typically performed using vacuum-assisted systems (Pironti et al. 2022). Depending on the nature of the sample, different types of filters were used, ranging from glass fiber membranes with varying pore diameters (Oliveira et al. 2025, Zhao et al. 2024, Ao et al. 2023; Weingrill et al. 2023; Zurub et al. 2024; Halfar et al. 2023; Cui et al. 2025), PTFE membranes (Massardo et al. 2024), microfiber membranes (Montano et al. 2023), aluminum oxide membranes (Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Rotchell et al. 2024, 2023), stainless steel (Wang et al. 2025a, b; Ni et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025b) to silver filters (Amato-Lourenço et al. 2024; Horvatits et al. 2022). The need to use filters that are not made of plastic has been emphasized in order to avoid contaminating the samples.

It is worth noting that regardless of the type of reagents used, the chemical digestion process has limitations in that it does not allow for the accurate and actual location of micro- or nanoplastics particles in tissues to be determined. Horvatits et al. (2022) emphasized that the method used did not allow for the identification of polymer particle accumulation sites within the liver, nor did it allow for determining whether the particles were deposited intracellularly. A similar limitation was described by Massardo et al. (2024) described a similar limitation, pointing out that the sample preparation protocol used did not allow for the spatial localization of particles in the kidney to be determined. The need to develop available preparation methods that allow for the precise determination of micro- and nanoplastic deposition in biological material is emphasized.

Analytical protocols used differ significantly in terms of both storage and preparation of biological material. Some research teams use classic chemical or enzymatic digestion methods, others use ultrasound or multi-stage purification procedures, and some limit themselves to diluting the sample before analysis. Such a wide divergence in approaches indicates a clear lack of standardization in research on micro- and nanoplastics in biological material.

Analytical techniques

Micro- and nanoplastics can be analyzed in terms of their chemical and physical properties. Chemical analysis involves identifying the chemical composition, including the type of polymers and additives, while physical analysis involves determining the morphological characteristics of the particles, including their size, shape, and color (Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Ni et al. 2025; Rotchell et al. 2024). Comprehensive analysis of plastic particles requires the use of various, often different research methods that work complementarily. Quality control and contamination minimization

Quality assurance and reliability control of procedures are fundamental elements of properly planned and conducted scientific research. In the context of micro- and nanoplastic analyses, where potential sources of contamination are ubiquitous and often difficult to identify unequivocally, the role of quality standards becomes particularly critical. It is practically impossible to completely eliminate the risk of contamination, as polymer particles are present in the air and on work surfaces, as well as on materials and equipment used for sampling and processing, regardless of their declared composition. Therefore, transparency regarding the control measures implemented is a key component in assessing the reliability of research. Figure 6 shows the number of publications that have applied selected contamination control procedures, both those described directly in the methodology and those highlighted in separate subsections on quality assurance. The list includes only information explicitly declared by the authors, which highlights a certain structural weakness in the field: some procedures may have been used but were not described in detail (Table S2, Supplementary Material). This lack of standardization in reporting makes it difficult to compare studies and assess the reliability of results, but it should not be interpreted as a failure to apply appropriate precautions, but rather as a reflection of diverse documentation practices.Fig. 6Number of studies implementing each procedure for controlling micro- or nanoplastics contamination. The Y-axis shows the type of procedure, and the X-axis indicates the number of studies

Almost all studies included a description of using blind controls, such as exposing filters to air or performing a full analytical protocol under conditions analogous to those of biological samples (Figure S1, Supplementary Material). Such controls are the minimum standard in micro- and nanoplastic analyses, but their effectiveness depends on whether they accurately reflect the entire sample preparation cycle. Selected publications used procedural blank tests, which included all stages of tissue processing except the biological sample itself (Rotchell et al. 2023). Kong et al. (2025) used a saline solution passed through a needle and incubated under conditions analogous to the analysed samples to simulate the process of collecting alveolar fluid, allowing for the assessment of contamination specifically originating from the material collection process.

Many studies were conducted using personal protective equipment without plastic components, such as cotton aprons or latex or nitrile gloves (Kong et al. 2025; Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025b; Zhao et al. 2024; Amato-Lourenço et al. 2024; Rotchell et al. 2024; Horvatits et al. 2022; Ragusa et al. 2022a; Ragusa et al. 2022b; Braun et al. 2021; Ragusa et al. 2021; Montano et al. 2023; Ao et al. 2023; Halfar et al. 2023; Zurub et al. 2024; Pironti et al. 2022; Cui et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, b; Arslan et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025b; Wright et al. 2025; Cao et al. 2025). Similarly, almost half of the studies included in this review stated that analyses were performed in dedicated micro- and nanoplastic research rooms. Working under laminar flow cabinets or in laboratory fume hoods significantly reduces the risk of polymer aerosol contamination (Zhao et al. 2024; Rotchell et al. 2023; Ragusa et al. 2022a; Ragusa et al. 2022b; Braun et al. 2021; Weingrill et al. 2023; Massardo et al. 2024; Halfar et al. 2023; Zurub et al. 2024; Pironti et al. 2022; Cui et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, b; Arslan et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025b; Wright et al. 2025; Cao et al. 2025). However, access to this type of infrastructure varies, and not all laboratories have the capacity to implement such solutions. The lack of standardized guidelines for environmental parameters remains a research gap and makes it difficult to replicate results in other laboratories.

Attention was drawn to the need to use glassware and metal utensils to reduce the use of plastic equivalents (Kong et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025b; Ni et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2024; Rotchell et al. 2024, 2023; Horvatits et al. 2022; Ragusa et al. 2022a, 2022b; Braun et al. 2021; Montano et al. 2023; Ao et al. 2023; Weingrill et al. 2023; Halfar et al. 2023; Pironti et al. 2022; Cui et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, b; Arslan et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025b; Wright et al. 2025; Massie et al. 2025). However, it is impossible to eliminate the risk of microparticle migration from components such as silicone seals, stoppers, or membranes, which are often not described in materials and methods. Filtration of solutions through filters with an appropriate pore diameter, as well as washing glassware, equipment, and work surfaces with a 70% ethanol solution before starting analytical procedures (Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025b; Zhao et al. 2024; Rotchell et al. 2024, 2023; Horvatits et al. 2022; Ragusa et al. 2021; Montano et al. 2023; Ao et al. 2023; Massardo et al. 2024; Halfar et al. 2023; Pironti et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2025a, b; Arslan et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025b; Wright et al. 2025; Cao et al. 2025), are also procedures whose declared implementation significantly increases data reliability.

Recovery tests, which involve adding a known amount of microplastics and calculating the recovery rate (Kong et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, b; Zhang et al. 2025a, b; Leslie et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2025b), were used only in selected studies. This is one of the few procedures that enables a genuine assessment of the protocol’s effectiveness in the analysis of micro- and nanoplastics, as well as its ability to identify particle loss during digestion or filtration.

It is important to emphasise that these activities should be an additional aspect, not the primary responsibility of those conducting micro- or nanoplastic analysis. Despite the increasing use of quality control procedures, the lack of a uniform reporting standard significantly hinders the assessment of result comparability between studies. The standardisation of methodological practices and transparent description of implemented control measures should become key directions for the development of this rapidly growing field.

Differences in particle sizes detected in the placenta (Table 2) reflect the limitations of the analytical methods rather than the actual diversity of polymers present in the tissues. Studies using FTIR or LDIR mainly identified particles larger than 10 µm (Halfar et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025b), while protocols based on µRaman microscopy allowed detection of particles as small as 1 µm (Yun et al. 2024). The most frequently detected polymers in placental tissues were PE and PP, followed by PS, PVC, PET, PA, and PMMA, which are commonly found in the environment, suggesting that some detected particles may have originated from laboratory contamination.

Similar limitations are observed in kidney studies, where the lack of standardised reporting also prevents direct comparison of results. When analysing particle sizes, most microplastics detected in the kidneys were in the range of several dozen micrometres, while smaller ones (< 10 µm) were less common, which may reflect detection limitations. After standardising the data, it can be assumed that micro- or nanoplastics in the kidneys occur in small amounts, most often in the range of several to several dozen µg/g of tissue. Considering the types of polymers, most studies reported the same materials (PE, PP, and PS, less frequently PET, PVC, and PA), reflecting environmental exposure.

Smaller particles (10–50 µm) predominate in the liver, except in the study by Dzierżyński et al. (2025), which detected significantly larger fragments. The polymers identified included PE, PP, PS, PET, and PVC. The concentrations of microplastics and nanoplastics in the liver were generally low but detectable in all studies. Microplastics predominated in quantitative studies (particles/g), while nanoplastics predominated in mass-based studies. Average values ranged from a few to several hundred µg/g or from 3 to 65 particles/g, indicating high variability depending on the detection method and sample preparation.

The range of particle sizes in the kidneys reported in individual studies spans almost three orders of magnitude, from nanoplastics (< 0.4 µm; Nihart et al. 2025) to microplastics (250–1504 µm; Dzierżyński et al. 2025). The detected fractions are not mutually exclusive but reflect different aspects of tissue contamination. Two studies reported results in particles per gram (Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Cao et al. 2025), three in µg/g (Nihart et al. 2025; Erdely et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, b), and one study (Massardo et al. 2024) presented the total number of particles detected in 10 kidneys, making direct comparisons between studies impossible. All studies identified numerous polymers, including PET, PE, and PP, while some also detected less common plastics such as CE, PUPC, and ABS (Nihart et al. 2025; Dzierżyński et al. 2025).

The brain can accumulate both microplastics and nanoplastics, with nanoplastics again appearing to dominate in mass-based studies (Nihart et al. 2025) and microplastics in quantitative studies (Dzierżyński et al. 2025). Dzierżyński et al. (2025) and Cao et al. (2025) report particle counts in particles per gram (24.4 and 48.5 particles/g, respectively), while Nihart et al. (2025) and Erdely et al. (2025) present mass concentrations in µg/g, reaching several thousand µg/g, indicating significant amounts of nanoplastics in brain tissue. The lack of standardisation of measurement units makes direct comparison of results difficult, as is the case with other organs.

The collected data confirm the presence of micro- and nanoplastics in the lungs, but there are differences in the sizes and types of polymers detected. The presence of larger particles (Dzierżyński et al. 2025) in the lungs compared to other organs suggests that they originate from inhalation from the environment. Thus, the lungs serve as a deposition site for a wide range of plastics, from large microparticles deposited in the respiratory tract to nanoplastics that can reach the alveoli (Ao et al. 2023; Cao et al. 2025).

Analysis of urine studies indicates the possibility of eliminating micro- and nanoplastics from the body through the urinary system. The data show high variability in terms of particle size, polymer types, and particle abundance. Studies by Pironti et al. (2022) and Massardo et al. (2024) showed similar particle sizes (4–15 µm and 3–13 µm, respectively). Particle abundance was low in all studies, suggesting that urine is not the main site of plastic accumulation, but only a route for the elimination of small amounts of microplastics. The lack of quantitative data in the study by Pironti et al. (2022) prevents its results from being included in the comparative analysis.

Challenges in the classification of micro- and nanoplastics

One of the significant limitations in the analysis of available studies is the absence of a standardised classification for particle size, which directly impedes the comparison of results. In some studies (Dzierżyński et al. 2025; Nihart et al. 2025; Ni et al. 2025; Garcia et al. 2024; Marfella et al. 2024), the combined abbreviation MNP (micro- and nanoplastics) was introduced to cover both microplastics and nanoplastics, while other studies used the terms inconsistently. Wang et al. (2025b) defined the abbreviation MP as microplastics in the introduction but then used the term “MP nanoparticles” in the results, introducing semantic ambiguity. Another limitation is the lack of information on particle size in selected studies (Zhang et al. 2025a, b), making it impossible to determine whether the detected particles should be classified as microplastics or nanoplastics. Some studies present a more consistent approach. Erdely et al. (2025) introduced definitions for microplastics (≤ 5 mm) and nanoplastics (≤ 1 µm), and reported results on a nanometric scale for individual organs. Rotchell et al. (2024) adopted the classic size range for microplastics (1 µm–5 mm), and all particles they detected were between 10 µm and 5 mm, thus falling within this range. Braun et al. (2021) deliberately limited their analysis to particles > 50 µm, arguing that smaller fragments pose a greater risk of contamination and artefacts. This allowed them to classify all analysed particles as microplastics without specifying their exact sizes. Erdely et al. (2025) detected nanoplastics in human tissues, with all identified particles ranging from 13 to 654 nm, corresponding to the nanoplastic size range reported by Browne et al. (2007), Hartmann et al. (2016), and Gigault et al. (2018). In turn, Kong et al. (2025) identified all detected particles as nanoplastics, but did not specify their actual dimensions.

The varied approach to classification indicates an urgent need to standardise definitions, as ambiguous size criteria make it difficult to compare studies and reliably assess human exposure to microplastics and nanoplastics.

Conclusion

The review highlights considerable diversity among available studies on the detection of micro- and nanoplastics ex vivo in human biological samples. The articles feature ambiguous definitions and classification criteria, substantial variation in the patient populations studied, and significant differences in material preparation and analytical techniques. The most frequently analysed tissues (placenta, kidneys, liver, lungs, heart, and urine) provide the first consistent evidence of micro- or nanoplastics in human material. However, many other tissues, which have only been evaluated sporadically, do not permit broad interpretation of exposure. Significant differences in sample storage, digestion, filtration, and isolation methods, as well as in the extent of quality control implementation, indicate a lack of standardised protocols. This hinders the assessment of result reliability and the comparison of data between studies.

The characteristics of the detected particles also remain inconsistent. The units used, size ranges, and methods of reporting concentrations vary considerably, and many studies do not distinguish between microplastics and nanoplastics, treating them as a single category, even though differences in their physicochemical properties may be crucial for health risk assessment. Despite the use of advanced techniques such as microscopy, Raman and FTIR spectroscopy, and chromatography, significant methodological limitations remain, including the sensitivity and specificity of detection and the risk of secondary contamination.

Overall, these results indicate that the field of micro- and nanoplastic detection in human biological material is undergoing dynamic development, but the lack of standardization of methods, analytical validation, and consistent reporting ranges poses a serious barrier to further progress. Systematic research using standardized protocols is needed to enable a reliable assessment of human exposure to micro- and nanoplastics and to estimate the potential health consequences of their presence in tissues.
