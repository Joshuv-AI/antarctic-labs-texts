Refined Strichartz Estimates for sub-Laplacians in Heisenberg and H H -type groups
Davide Barilari ∗, Steven Flynn ∗

Abstract
. We obtain refined Strichartz estimates for the sub-Riemannian Schrödinger equation on H H -type Carnot groups using Fourier restriction techniques. In particular, we extend the previously known Strichartz estimates obtained in [ 3 ] for the Heisenberg group also to non radial initial data. The same arguments permits to obtain refined Strichartz estimates for the wave equation on H H -type groups. Our proof is based on estimates for the spectral projectors for sub-Laplacians and reinterprets Strichartz estimates as Fourier restriction theorems for nilpotent groups in the context of trace-class operator valued measures.



1.
Introduction

The Schrödinger equation in the Euclidean space is one the main prototype of dispersive equations. Indeed it is well-known that the solution of

(1.1)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=0\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}\end{cases}

where
\Delta
denotes the classical Euclidean Laplace operator, satisfies the following a priori estimate for every
t\neq 0

(1.2)

\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{C}{|t|^{n/2}}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}.

The inequality (
1.2
) is at the core of the proof of the following Strichartz estimates, which are space-time estimates of the following type

(1.3)

\|u\|_{L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{x}}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\sigma}}

where the pair
(p,q)
satisfies the relation

(1.4)

\sigma:=\frac{n}{2}-\frac{2}{q}-\frac{n}{p}\geq 0.

Estimates (
1.3
) are fundamental for applications to the non-linear Schrödinger equation and passing from (
1.2
) to (
1.3
) is nowadays formalized in a classical abstract argument known as
TT^{*}
.

More recently, the Schrödinger equation in the sub-Riemannian setting started to attract some attention due to the following key observation contained in
[
5
]
: in the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{d}
endowed with its sub-Laplacian
\Delta_{H}
there exist a choice of the initial condition
u_{0}
for which the solution

(1.5)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{H}u=0\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}\end{cases}

behaves like a transport, thus a dispersive estimate like (
1.2
) cannot hold. Nevertheless, it has been proved in
[
3
]
that for radial initial data
u_{0}
(the terminology actually stands for a cylindrical symmetry with respect to the last coordinate), we have the following Strichartz type estimate

(1.6)

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{v}}L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\sigma}}

for pairs
(p,q)
satisfying the relation

(1.7)

\sigma:=\frac{2d+2}{2}-\frac{2}{q}-\frac{2d}{p}\geq 0,

where
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{v}
denotes the splitting of the Lie algebra of Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{d}
into the 1-dim vertical and the
2d
-dim horizontal directions.

We stress that with respect to (
1.4
), in formula (
1.7
) the topological dimension
n
is replaced in the two occurrencies by
Q=2d+2
the homogeneous dimension (which corresponds to the rescaling of the Lebesgue measure with respect to the non isotropic dilations) and by
2d
the dimension of the horizontal distribution.

The inequality (
1.6
) for radial initial conditions in the Heisenberg group has been proved inspired by the original approach of Strichartz, which is based on a Fourier restriction theorem. Indeed, even in the Euclidean case, the solution to (
1.5
)
can be interpreted, setting
y=(t,x)
and
z=(\tau,\xi)
,
as an inverse Fourier transform

(1.8)

u(t,x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{i(x\cdot\xi+t|\xi|^{2})}\widehat{u}_{0}(\xi)d\xi=\int_{\Sigma}e^{iy\cdot z}g(z)d\Sigma(z)

of the function
g(z)=g(|\xi|^{2},\xi)=\widehat{u}_{0}(\xi)
defined on the paraboloid

(1.9)

\Sigma=\{z=(\tau,\xi)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\tau=|\xi|^{2}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}={\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

where
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is understood in the frequency space. Here
d\Sigma
is the non-intrinsic measure on
\Sigma
defined by the pull-back of the Lebesgue measure by projection onto the first
n
variables. The result then can be reduced to a restriction estimate for the Fourier transform on the paraboloid
\Sigma
.

Performing a similar analysis in the Heisenberg group is less straightforward: the Heisenberg group is a non-commutative group so the ordinary Fourier transform should be replaced by the non-commutative one; moreover, the surface
\Sigma
is actually an hypersurface of the space of frequencies, which in the case of a non-commutative group is parametrized by unitary irreducible and infinite dimensional representations.

Such difficulties have been considered in
[
3
]
in which Strichartz estimate for the Schrödinger and wave equation are proved in the Heisenberg group, when the initial condition is radial.

1.1.
Heisenberg group, no radial assumption

In this paper we remove the radial assumption thus extending the result of
[
3
]
to every initial condition. The estimates are stated in terms of Sobolev spaces.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given
(p,q)
belonging to the admissible set

\mathcal{A}=\Big\{(p,q)\in{(2,\infty]^{2}}\,\mid\,p\leq q\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{2}{q}+\frac{2d}{p}\leq\frac{Q}{2}\Big\}

the solution to the Schrödinger equation in the Heisenberg group

(1.10)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{H}u=f\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}\end{cases}

satisfies

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{v}}L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\sigma}}+\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{\sigma}}\,.

where
\sigma=\frac{Q}{2}-\frac{2}{q}-\frac{2d}{p}
, recalling that
Q=2d+2
.

It is crucial that the vertical variable has a
L^{\infty}
norm due to the counterexample of Muller in
[
16
]
. We stress that
\sigma=0
is not admitted here, since together with
p\leq q
forces
p=2
. The endpoint case
p=2
is excluded in our statement since our proof does not work for
(m,p)=(1,2)
, where
m
is the vertical dimension. Notice that in
[
3
]
the endpoint case has been proved for radial initial data with a separate ad hoc argument.

1.2.
H
-type groups

Consider now a more general Carnot group of step 2 with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{v}
of dimension
n=2d+m
where
\dim\mathfrak{h}=2d
,
\dim\mathfrak{v}=m
and

(1.11)

[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{v}]=\mathfrak{v},\quad[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{v}]=0.

Fix a scalar product
g
on
\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathfrak{h}\perp\mathfrak{v}
. For any
\mu\in\mathfrak{v}^{*}
, consider the skew symmetric operator
J_{\mu}:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{h}
defined by

(1.12)

\langle\mu,[X,Y]\rangle=g(X,J_{\mu}Y).

A step 2 Carnot group is
H
-type
if for all
\mu\in\mathfrak{v}^{*}
we have

J_{\mu}^{2}=-\|\mu\|_{g}^{2}I
,
with
I
the identity map on
\mathfrak{h}
. The sub-Laplacian
\Delta_{H}
on the
H
-type group
G
is given by

(1.13)

\Delta_{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{2d}X_{j}^{2}

where
\{X_{j}\}_{j=1}^{2d}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathfrak{h}
. We consider the Schrödinger equation

(1.14)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{H}u=f\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}\end{cases}

We obtain the following result.
(Recall that
H
-type groups of dimension
n=2d+m
have homogeneous dimension
Q=2d+2m
.)

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be an
H
-type group with
\dim\mathfrak{h}=2d
and
\dim\mathfrak{v}=m
. Assume
(m,p)\neq(1,2)
. Given
(r,p,q)
belonging to the admissible set

\mathcal{A}=\Big\{(r,p,q)\in[2,\infty]^{3}\,/\,p\leq r,q;\ r\geq 2+\frac{4}{m-1},\ \frac{2}{q}+\frac{2d}{p}+\frac{2m}{r}\leq\frac{Q}{2}\Big\}

the solution to the Schrödinger equation (
1.14
) on an
H
-type Carnot group satisfies

\|u\|_{L^{r}_{\mathfrak{v}}L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\sigma}}+\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{\sigma}}\,.

where
\sigma=\frac{Q}{2}-\frac{2}{q}-\frac{2d}{p}-\frac{2m}{r}
and
Q=2d+2m
.

Notice that in this case, when the codimension
m>1
, we also get the endpoint case
p=2
. Also,
m=1
forces
r=\infty
and
p>2
, which is exactly the Heisenberg case. We stress that for
m>1
we can remove the
L^{\infty}
restriction in the vertical variable.

1.3.
On the wave equation

The same approach permits to obtain Strichartz estimates on the wave equation associated with the sub-Laplacian on
H
-type groups

(1.15)

\begin{cases}\partial^{2}_{t}u-\Delta_{H}u=f\\
(u,\partial_{t}u)|_{t=0}=(u_{0},v_{0})\end{cases}

Also in this case in the Heisenberg group we remove the radial assumption in the initial data, and we can generalize to
H
-type groups.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
G
be an
H
-type group with
\dim\mathfrak{h}=2d
and
\dim\mathfrak{v}=m
. Assume
(m,p)\neq(1,2)
. Given
(r,p,q)
belonging to the admissible set

\mathcal{A}=\Big\{(r,p,q)\in[2,\infty]^{3}\,/\,p\leq r,q;\ r\geq 2+\frac{4}{m-1},\ \frac{1}{q}+\frac{2d}{p}+\frac{2m}{r}\leq\frac{Q-2}{2}\Big\}

the solution to the wave equation (
1.15
) on an
H
-type Carnot group satisfies

\|u\|_{L^{r}_{\mathfrak{v}}L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}}\leq C(\|\nabla_{H}u_{0}\|_{H^{\sigma}}+\|v_{0}\|_{H^{\sigma}}+\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t}H^{\sigma}})\,.

where
\sigma=\frac{Q-2}{2}-\frac{1}{q}-\frac{2d}{p}-\frac{2m}{r}
and
Q=2d+2m
.

Again, we highlight that
m=1
forces
r=\infty
and
p>2
, recovering the Heisenberg case, for which the corresponding estimate has been proved for radial data in
[
3
]
.

1.4.
Link with the Fourier restriction

In the Euclidean case one can observe that the equation (
1.8
) can be rewritten as

u=R^{*}_{\Sigma}g

where
R^{*}_{\Sigma}
is the formal adjoint of the Fourier restriction operator

R_{\Sigma}f=\mathcal{F}(f)|_{\Sigma},

where
\Sigma
is the paraboloid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
defined in (
1.9
). It is not difficult to prove that the continuity of
R^{*}_{\Sigma}
from
L^{2}(\Sigma)
to
L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{p^{\prime}}_{x}(G)
is equivalent to the continuity of the operator

R^{*}_{\Sigma}R_{\Sigma}f=f*\kappa_{\Sigma}

from the space
L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{x}(G)
to
L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{p^{\prime}}_{x}(G)
, that is an estimate of the form.

(1.16)

\|f*\kappa_{\Sigma}\|_{L_{t}^{q^{\prime}}L_{x}^{p^{\prime}}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{x}},

where
\kappa_{\Sigma}
denotes the inverse Fourier transform of the pullback measure
d\Sigma
induced on
\Sigma
by the projection onto the first
n
variables.

Remark 1.4
.

It is important here to observe that
\Sigma
is a surface in the space of frequencies, which in the Euclidean case is isomoprhic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
itself. On general nilpotent groups
G
the space of frequencies is replaced by the dual of the group
\widehat{G}
which is the set of (equivalence classes of) strongly continuous unitary representations of
G
. For more details we refer to Sections
3
and
4
.

A crucial point in the Euclidean case is to observe that one can write, at least formally,

(1.17)

f*\kappa_{\Sigma}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\mu t}\mathcal{P}_{\mu}(f^{\mu})(x)d\mu,

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}=\mathcal{P}^{-\Delta}_{\mu}
is the spectral projector of the Euclidean Laplacian
-\Delta
and
f^{\mu}
denotes the Euclidean Fourier transform of
f
with respect to the last variable and evaluated at
\mu
. One can then link the estimate (
1.16
) to the properties of the spectral projectors.

Actually to prove such an estimates, and to guarantee the convergence of the integral (
1.17
), one needs compactness either of the surface or of the measure. For this reason one works with a truncation
\psi
in the
\mu
variable in the integral (
1.17
) and we denote
\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}
the inverse Fourier transform of the corresponding compactly supported measure.

The proof of our results is obtained by showing that the solution to the Schrödinger Equation on an
H
-type group
G
:

(1.18)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{H}u=0\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}\end{cases}

can be written as a inverse Fourier transform as in (
1.8
)

(1.19)

\displaystyle u_{t}(x,z)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{-1}\{\mathcal{F}_{G}(u_{0})d\Sigma\}(t,x,z).

where
\mathbb{G}={\mathbb{R}}\times G
is the extended space and
\Sigma
is a suitable subset of the dual space endowed with the relevant measure
d\Sigma
.
Considering

(1.20)

\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{-1}(\psi d\Sigma)

(one actually need to multiply by a bump function
\psi
to ensure compact support) the proof is then based on the analogue estimates of (
1.16
) in our setting. We refer to Section
3.3
and Section
8
for all details.

To state these estimates we need to introduce a few more objects. The twisted Laplacian
\Delta_{H}^{\lambda}
is defined by the action of the sub-Laplacian on the
z
-fourier modes of a function
f=f(x,z)
on the Heisenberg group:

(\Delta_{H}f)^{\lambda}=\Delta_{H}^{\lambda}f^{\lambda}

Precisely, letting
y_{i}:=x_{i+d}
for
i=1,\ldots d
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{H}^{\lambda}:=\Delta_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}}+i\lambda\sum_{j=d}^{n}\left(y_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}-x_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}\right)+\frac{1}{4}|\lambda|^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(x_{j}^{2}+y_{j}^{2}).

The twisted Laplacian
\Delta^{\lambda}_{H}
is an essentially self-adjoint elliptic operator on the set
S(\mathbb{C}^{d})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
with a pure point spectrum consisting of eigenvalues
\{-|\lambda|(2k+d)\mid k\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
.
The following estimate involves the norm of the spectral projector
\Lambda_{k}
for the twisted Laplacian, i.e., the projector on the
k
-th eigenspace of the operator
\Delta^{\lambda}_{H}
when
\lambda=1
.

For the readers convenience we state the results separately for the Heisenberg group and the
H
-type case, to highlight the differencies when the codimension
m
is equal to one and when it is greater.

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
\mathbb{G}={\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{H}^{d}
, and
\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}
be as defined in (
1.20
). For any
p,q\in[1,2)
satisfying
q\leq p
there exists
C>0
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{G})

(1.21)

\|f*_{\mathbb{G}}\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}\|_{L_{\mathfrak{v}}^{\infty}L_{t}^{q^{\prime}}L_{\mathfrak{h}}^{p^{\prime}}}\leq C\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\|\Lambda_{k}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p^{\prime}}}}{(2k+d)^{1+d(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}}\right)\|f\|_{L^{1}_{\mathfrak{v}}L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}},

and the series in the right hand side is convergent.

Notice that the endpoint case
p=2
is excluded. Concerning
H
-type groups we prove the following result. Recall that
m=\dim\mathfrak{v}
.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
\mathbb{G}={\mathbb{R}}\times G
, where
G
is an
H
-type with
\dim\mathfrak{h}=2d
and
\dim\mathfrak{v}=m
. Let
\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}
be defined in (
1.20
). For any
p,q\in[1,2]
and
1\leq r\leq 2(m+1)/(m+3)
satisfying
q\leq p
and
r\leq p
with
(m,p)\neq(1,2)
, there exists
C>0
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{G})

(1.22)

\|f*_{\mathbb{G}}\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}\|_{L^{r^{\prime}}_{\mathfrak{v}}L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{p^{\prime}}_{\mathfrak{h}}}\leq C\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\|\Lambda_{k}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p^{\prime}}}}{(2k+d)^{m(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{r^{\prime}})+d(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}}\right)\|f\|_{L^{r}_{\mathfrak{v}}L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}},

and the series in the right hand side is convergent.

Given this result, proved in Section
4
, the proof of Strichartz estimates is then reduced to the following steps: understand the solution as the Fourier extension of a measure defined on a suitable subset, prove boundedness of the Fourier restriction and extensions, prove the equivalence between the Fourier restriction/extension estimates with estimates on the spectral projectors. This is the object of Sections
5
–
8
.

1.5.
Link with the literature

Strichartz estimates for the Schrödinger equation in
H
-type groups have been considered in
[
8
]
. These Strichartz estimates are consequence of dispersive estimates available in this setting when
m>1
, cf. also
[
4
]
for dispersive inequalities on more general step 2 Carnot groups. The result obtained here are refined estimates for two reasons: our results are available even in the case there is no dispersion, morever we obtain estimates in mixed Lebesgue norms in space.

We mention also similar Strichartz estimates in the Grushin space
[
13
]
and more recent works on smoothing effect for the sub-Riemannian Schrödinger equation in Heisenberg group
[
9
,
10
]
.

Sharp
L^{p}\to L^{2}
estimates for the spectral projector
\Lambda_{k}
for the twisted Laplacian are given in
[
15
]
. Recently Jeong, Lee and Ryu
[
14
]
proved sharp
L^{p}\to L^{q}
estimates for
\Lambda_{k}
. This result is an improvement on
[
15
]
, since it includes
L^{p}\to L^{q}
estimates the case when
p\neq 2
and
q\neq 2
.

In the main results of this article, the sets of admissible indices always require
p\leq q
and
2<p\leq r
. These requirements comes from technical points of the proof and the constraint
p\leq q
was already present in the previous work
[
3
]
. The authors of this article do not have an example which shows these requirements are necessary.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
G
be a step 2 Carnot group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{v}
of rank
2d
and dimension
2d+m
. In particular,
\dim\mathfrak{h}=2d
and
\dim\mathfrak{v}=m
, and

(2.1)

[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]=\mathfrak{v},\quad[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{v}]=[\mathfrak{v},\mathfrak{v}]=0.

Note that the previous condition implies that
\mathfrak{v}
is the center of
\mathfrak{g}
. For any
\mu\in\mathfrak{v}^{*}
, there is a natural skew symmetric form
\omega_{\mu}:\mathfrak{h}\times\mathfrak{h}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

(2.2)

\omega_{\mu}(X,Y)=\langle\mu,[X,Y]\rangle.

Fix a scalar product
g
on
\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathfrak{h}\perp\mathfrak{v}.
The skew symmetric operator
J_{\mu}:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{h}
is defined by

(2.3)

\omega_{\mu}(X,Y)=g(X,J_{\mu}Y).

To the Riemannian metric
g
on
\mathfrak{g}
there is a cometric
\widetilde{g}
on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. We will use the cometric implicitly in the sequel.

Definition 2.1
.

A step 2 Carnot group is
H
-type
if for all
\mu\in\mathfrak{v}^{*}
,

(2.4)

J_{\mu}^{2}=-\|\mu\|_{g}^{2}I,

where
I:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{h}
is the identity map and
\|\cdot\|_{g}
is the norm on
\mathfrak{v}^{*}
induced from
g
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the sequel, we will write
|\mu|
for the norm of
\mu\in\mathfrak{v}^{*}
instead of
\|\mu\|_{g}
.

The choice of an orthonormal basis
e_{1},\ldots,e_{2d}
of
\mathfrak{g}
and
f_{1},\ldots f_{m}
of
\mathfrak{v}
yields, through the group exponential map
\exp_{G}:\mathfrak{g}\to G
, coordinates
(x,z)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
on
G
such that
p=(x,z)
if and only if

p=\exp_{G}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2d}x_{i}e_{i}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}z_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}\right)

We will identify the coordinates
(x,z)
with elements of the group
G
or the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}

Since
[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]=\mathfrak{v}
there exists a linearly independent family of skew-symmetric
d\times d
matrices
\{L^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{m}
such that the Lie bracket reads

(2.5)

[e_{i},e_{j}]=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}L_{ij}^{\alpha}f_{\alpha},\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,2d.

In these coordinates, the left-invariant vector field
X_{i}
corresponding to the Lie algebra element
e_{i}
is given by

(2.6)

X_{i}=\partial_{x_{i}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{2d}L^{\alpha}_{ij}x_{j}\partial_{z_{\alpha}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,2d.

Furthermore,
Z_{\alpha}=\partial_{z_{\alpha}}
for
\alpha=1,\ldots,m
, are the left invariant vector fields corresponding to the basis elements
f_{\alpha}
of
\mathfrak{v}
.

The sub-Laplacian
\Delta_{H}
on the
H
-type group
G
is defined by

(2.7)

\Delta_{H}=\mathrm{div}(\nabla_{H}u)=\sum_{j=1}^{2d}X_{j}^{2}.

where
\nabla_{H}u=\sum_{j=1}^{2d}(X_{j}u)X_{j}
is the horizontal gradient and the divergence is computed with respect to the Haar measure.

2.1.
The Heisenberg group

The main prototype of
H
-type group is
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, that is the Lie group
{\mathbb{R}}^{2d+1}
endowed with the group law (here we let
y_{i}:=x_{i+d}
for
i=1,\ldots d
)

\displaystyle(x,y,z)\cdot(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\left(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(x_{j}y_{j}^{\prime}-x_{j}^{\prime}y_{j})\right).

Its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is spanned by the family of left invariant vector fields

\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{n},Y_{1},\cdots Y_{n},Z\}

satisfying the commutation relations

\displaystyle[X_{i},Y_{i}]=Z,\qquad\forall\,i=1,\ldots,n.

In particular the Lie algebra can be written
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{v}
with
\dim\mathfrak{h}=2d
and
\dim\mathfrak{v}=1
where

\mathfrak{h}=\mathrm{span}\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{n},Y_{1},\cdots Y_{n}\},\qquad\mathfrak{v}=\mathrm{span}\{Z\}.

We refer to
[
1
]
for more details on Carnot groups.

3.
Fourier Analysis and the dual space

In this section
G
is a step 2 Carnot group of
H
-type. We state some results about the Fourier Analysis on nilpotent groups. For more details we refer to
[
7
]
.

3.1.
Dual Space of
G

By
\widehat{G}
we mean the set of (unitary equivalence classes of) strongly continuous unitary representations of
G
. It is given as a set by
\widehat{G}=\widehat{G}_{\infty}\coprod\widehat{G}_{1}
where

(3.1)

3.3.

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\|\Lambda_{k}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p^{\prime}}}}{(2k+d)^{m(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{r^{\prime}})+d(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{{p^{\prime}}})}}.

Proof.

Applying Corollary
B.4
, the series (
4.7
) is bounded by a constant multiple of

(4.8)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2k+d)^{\varrho(p^{\prime})}}{(2k+d)^{m(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{r^{\prime}})+d(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}}.

where
\varrho(p^{\prime})
is defined in (
B.11
). To prove convergence in (
4.8
), we preliminary observe that given the assumption on

we have

(4.9)

m\left(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{r^{\prime}}\right)\geq\frac{2m}{m+1}\geq 1.

with equality if and only if
m=1
. Indeed
r\leq 2(m+1)/(m+3)
implies

\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{r^{\prime}}=\frac{2}{r}-1\geq\frac{m+3}{m+1}-1=\frac{2}{m+1}.

We now split the proof into two cases. Denote
p_{*}(d):=2(2d+1)/(2d+3)
.

Case 1:
Suppose
1\leq p\leq p_{*}(d)
.

Then
2(2d+1)/(2d-1)\leq p^{\prime}\leq\infty
. In this case, using (
B.11
), we have

(4.10)

\varrho(p^{\prime})=2d\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\right)-1=d\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\right)-1.

The sum (
4.8
) becomes

(4.11)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+d)^{m(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{r^{\prime}})+1}}

which converges by (
4.9
).

Case 2:
Suppose
p_{*}(d)\leq p\leq 2
. Then
2<p^{\prime}\leq 2(2d+1)/(2d-1)
.

In this case, again using (
B.11
), we have

(4.12)

\varrho(p^{\prime})=\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}\right).

The sum (
4.8
) becomes

(4.13)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+d)^{m(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{r^{\prime}})+(d-\frac{1}{2})(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})}}.

If
m=1
, and
p<2
, then
m(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{r^{\prime}})=1
and
(d-\frac{1}{2})(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})>0
.
If
m>1
then
m(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{r^{\prime}})>1
by (
4.9
). Therefore, the series converges as long as
(m,p)\neq(1,2)
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

It is interesting to observe that this argument excludes the endpoint estimate
p=2
only when
m=1
. Notice that in the Heisenberg case, for which
m=1
, in
[
3
]
the endpoint estimate
p=2
has been proved for radial data by an ad hoc argument (inspired from
[
16
]
).

\displaystyle|E_{\alpha\alpha}^{\lambda}(x,z)|=|\langle\pi_{\lambda}(x,z)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle|\leq 1

so

\displaystyle|e_{k}^{\lambda}(x,z)|\leq\sum_{|\alpha|=k}1=\frac{(k+d-1)!}{k!(d-1)!}.

Therefore

\displaystyle|\kappa_{\mu}(x,z)|\leq\frac{\textrm{vol}(S)\mu^{d+m-1}}{(2\pi)^{d+m}(d-1)!}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{(k+d-1)!}{k!(2k+d)^{d+m}}<\infty,

whence the series in the definition
\kappa_{\mu}
converges uniformly to a bounded continuous function on
G
.
∎

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
5.1
.

8.2.
Extended Space

Let
\mathbb{G}={\mathbb{R}}\times G
be the extended space and
\widehat{\mathbb{G}}=\mathbb{R}\times(\mathfrak{v}^{*}\setminus\{0\})
.
Here
\mathcal{L}_{2}
denotes the space of Hilbert-Smith operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with the norm

\|A\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}:=\left(\tr\big[\sqrt{A^{*}A}\big]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Definition 8.6
.

Let
L^{2}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})=L^{2}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}},\mathcal{L}_{2})
be the space of all measurable families of operators
\Theta(\mu,\lambda)\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
for almost all
(\mu,\lambda)\in\widehat{\mathbb{G}}
such that

(8.7)

\|\Theta\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})}^{2}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}\|\Theta(\mu,\lambda)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}^{2}d\sigma_{\widehat{G}}(\lambda)d\mu<\infty.

Notice that
L^{2}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})
is a Hilbert space with the obvious inner product.

Then the Plancherel theorem takes the form

(8.8)

\langle\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}(f),\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}(g)\rangle_{L^{2}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})}=\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{G})}.

Remark 8.7
.

As stated previously, we may think of any element
\Theta\in\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})
as a family of operators
(\mu,\lambda)\mapsto\Theta(\mu,\lambda)
parameterized by
(\mu,\lambda)\in\widehat{{\mathbb{R}}}\times(\mathfrak{v}^{*}\setminus\{0\})
or as a scalar valued function
\Theta(\mu,\lambda,\alpha,\beta)=\langle\Theta(\mu,\lambda)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\beta}^{\lambda}\rangle
defined on
\widehat{{\mathbb{R}}}\times(\mathfrak{v}^{*}\setminus\{0\})\times{\mathbb{N}}^{2d}
. Then
L^{2}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})
is isomorphic to the Hilbert space of functions
(\mu,\lambda,\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\Theta(\mu,\lambda,\alpha,\beta)
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}|\Theta(\mu,\lambda,\alpha,\beta)|^{2}d\sigma_{\widehat{G}}(\lambda)d\mu<\infty.

8.3.
Polar coordinates

Recall from Section
3
that we defined the Heisenberg fan by

\displaystyle\Sigma:=\{(\mu,\lambda)\in\widehat{{\mathbb{R}}}\times(\mathfrak{v}^{*}\setminus\{0\})\mid\mu=|\lambda|(2k+d),\ \text{for some }k\in{\mathbb{N}}\},

Recall that we can decompose

\Sigma=\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\Sigma_{k},\qquad\Sigma_{k}:=\{(\mu,\lambda)\in\widehat{{\mathbb{R}}}\times(\mathfrak{v}^{*}\setminus\{0\})\mid\mu=|\lambda|(2k+d)\},

and defining

(8.9)

\displaystyle\langle d\Sigma_{k},\Theta\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+m}}\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}\Theta\left(|\lambda|(2k+d),\lambda,\alpha,\alpha\right)|\lambda|^{d}d\lambda.

(8.10)

\displaystyle\langle d\Sigma,\Theta\rangle:=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}\Theta\left(|\lambda|(2|\alpha|+d),\lambda,\alpha,\alpha\right)|\lambda|^{d}d\lambda

Writing (

gives

where

is the unit sphere.
The rescaling
\rho\mapsto\mu=\rho/(2k+d)
yields

\displaystyle\langle d\Sigma_{k},\Theta\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+m}}

which in particular gives

\displaystyle\langle d\Sigma,\Theta\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+m}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}}\frac{1}{(2k+d)^{d+m}}\int_{S}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Theta\left(\mu,\frac{\mu\omega}{2k+d},\alpha,\alpha\right)\mu^{d+m-1}d\mu\,d\sigma_{S}(\omega).

.

Later, for technical considerations, it will be convenient to multiply the measure

with a cutoff function
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},[0,1])
as follows

8.4.
More on the Fourier side

Recall that
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}=\mathcal{P}_{\mu}^{-\Delta}
is the spectral projector for
-\Delta
. It is a standard fact in the Fourier theory of groups (see
[
11
]
) that, for
f\in\mathcal{S}(G)
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mathcal{P}_{\mu}f)(\lambda)=\mathcal{F}_{G}(f)(\lambda)\mathcal{P}^{H(\lambda)}_{\mu}

where
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}^{H(\lambda)}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to\Pi(\mu,\lambda)
is the spectral projector onto the
\mu
-eigenspace of the operator
H(\lambda)
. Note that when
\mu=|\lambda|(2k+d)
for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}^{H(\lambda)}=P_{k}(\lambda)
, and when
\mu\neq|\lambda|(2k+d)
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the projecton
\mathcal{P}_{\mu}^{H(\lambda)}
is zero. It turns out that

d\Sigma(\mu,\lambda)=\mathcal{P}_{\mu}^{H(\lambda)}d\sigma

where
d\sigma
is the measure on
\Sigma
.

For any bounded operator
A\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, let

|A|:=\sqrt{A^{*}A}.

Define
L^{2}(d\Sigma)
to be the set of all measurable families of operators
\Theta(\mu,\lambda):\Pi(\mu,\lambda)\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for
(\mu,\lambda)\in\Sigma\subset\widehat{\mathbb{G}}
such that

(8.11)

\|\Theta\|_{L^{2}(d\Sigma)}^{2}:=\int_{\Sigma}\tr\big[|\Theta(\mu,\lambda)|^{2}d\Sigma(\mu,\lambda)\big]<\infty.

Remark 8.9
.

We may think of an element
\Theta\in L^{2}(d\Sigma)
as a scalar valued function
(\mu,\lambda,\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\Theta(\mu,\lambda,\alpha,\beta)
on the subset
\Sigma\times{\mathbb{N}}^{2d}
with
\mu=|\lambda|(2|\alpha|+d)
. Then
L^{2}(d\Sigma)
is isomorphic to the Hilbert space of all such functions such that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}|\Theta(|\lambda|(2|\alpha|+d),\lambda,\alpha,\beta)|^{2}d\sigma_{\widehat{G}}(\lambda)<\infty.

Lemma 8.10
.

Let
{\rm pr}_{2}:\widehat{\mathbb{G}}\to\widehat{G}
given by
(\mu,\lambda)\mapsto\lambda
and let
\theta:\widehat{G}\to\mathcal{L}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
. If
\Theta:\Sigma\subset\widehat{\mathbb{G}}\to\mathcal{L}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
is defined by
\Theta=\theta\circ{\rm pr}_{2}|_{\Sigma}
, for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then

(8.12)

\|\Theta\|_{L^{2}(d\Sigma)}=\|\theta\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{G})}.

Proof of Lemma
8.10
.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\tr\big[|\Theta(\mu,\lambda)|^{2}d\Sigma(\mu,\lambda)\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\tr\big[|\Theta(\mu,\lambda)|^{2}d\Sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\tr\big[|\Theta(\mu,\lambda)|^{2}P_{k}(\lambda)\big]d\sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}\tr\big[|\Theta(|\lambda|(2k+d),\lambda)|^{2}P_{k}(\lambda))|\big]d\sigma_{\widehat{G}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}\tr\big[|\theta(\lambda)|^{2}P_{k}(\lambda))\big]d\sigma_{\widehat{G}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}\tr\big[|\theta(\lambda)|^{2}\big]d\sigma_{\widehat{G}}(\lambda)\qed

8.5.
Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}
on tempered distributions

Let
\mathcal{F}^{t}_{\mathbb{G}}:\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})\to\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})
be the transpose of the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}
with respect to the bilinear pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{G})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{G})\to{\mathbb{C}}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})\times\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})\to{\mathbb{C}}
. That is, for all
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{G})
and
\Theta\in\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}),

(8.13)

\langle f,\mathcal{F}^{t}_{\mathbb{G}}\Theta\rangle:=\langle\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f,\Theta\rangle.

The group Fourier transform is defined on tempered distributions as follows:

(8.14)

\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}:\begin{cases}\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{G})\longrightarrow\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})\\
u\longmapsto\left(\Theta\mapsto\langle u,\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{t}\Theta\rangle\right).\end{cases}

Lemma 8.11
.

For any
\Theta\in S(\mathbb{G})
, the inverse Fourier transform of
\Theta
is related to the transpose via

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{t}\Theta=\iota^{*}\circ\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\mathbb{G}}\Theta

where
\iota:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{G}
is the involution

\iota(t,x,z)=(-t,-x,-z)
.

Considering the cutoff measure
d\Sigma_{\psi}
as in Remark
8.8
, we write in the sense of tempered distributions

(8.15)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{-1}(d\Sigma_{\psi}),\qquad\text{or}\qquad\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}(\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}})=d\Sigma_{\psi}.

The presence of the cutoff is used to guarantee the boundedness of the convolution kernel.

Proposition 8.12
.

The tempered distribution
\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}
is in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{G})\cap C(\mathbb{G})
and is given by

(8.16)

\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}(t,x,z)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+m}}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{1}{(2k+d)^{d+m}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{S}e^{i\mu t}e_{k}^{\mu\omega/(2k+d)}(x,z)\psi(\mu)\mu^{d+m-1}d\sigma_{S}(\omega)d\mu

where
e_{k}^{\lambda}:=\sum_{|\alpha|=k}E_{\alpha\alpha}^{\lambda}
and
d\sigma_{S}
is the intrinsic surface measure on the unit sphere
S\subset\mathfrak{v}^{*}
.

Proof.

By Definition (
8.14
) and Lemma
8.11
, the tempered distribution
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}(\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}})
acts on a test functions
\Theta=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f\in\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})
via

(8.17)

\langle\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}(\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}),\Theta\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})\times\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})}:=\langle\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}},\iota^{*}f\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{G})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{G})}.

By definition of
d\Sigma
given above

\displaystyle\langle d\Sigma_{\psi},\Theta\rangle_{\mathcal{M}^{+}(\Sigma)\times\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+m}}\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}}\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}\Theta\left(|\lambda|(2|\alpha|+d),\lambda,\alpha,\alpha\right)\psi(|\lambda|(2|\alpha|+d))|\lambda|^{d}d\lambda

If we take
\Theta=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f
, for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{G})
, then

\displaystyle\Theta\left(\mu,\lambda,\alpha,\alpha\right)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-i\mu t}\langle E_{\alpha\alpha}^{\lambda},f(t,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)\times\mathcal{S}(G)}dt.

Writing

e_{k}^{\lambda}:=\sum_{|\alpha|=k}E_{\alpha\alpha}^{\lambda}
,
one obtains

\displaystyle\langle\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}},\iota^{*}f

\displaystyle\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{G})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{G})}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+m}}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}e^{-i|\lambda|(2k+d)t}\langle e_{k}^{\lambda/(2k+d)},\iota^{*}f(t,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)\times\mathcal{S}(G)}\psi(|\lambda|(2|\alpha|+d))|\lambda|^{d}d\lambda

Thus

(8.18)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}(t,x,z)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d+m}}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\int_{\mathfrak{v}^{*}}e^{i|\lambda|(2k+d)t}e_{k}^{\lambda}(x,z)\psi(|\lambda|(2k+d))|\lambda|^{d}d\lambda

Taking polar coordinates
\lambda=\rho\omega
with
\rho\in(0,\infty)
and
\omega\in S\subset\mathfrak{v}^{*}
the unit sphere with measure
d\sigma_{S}
, followed by the substitution
\mu=\rho(2k+d)
gives the expression for
\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}
.

To prove boundedness of
\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}
, note that by the Cauchy Schwartz Inequality,

\displaystyle|E_{\alpha\alpha}^{\lambda}(x,z)|=|\langle\pi_{\lambda}(x,z)\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda},\Phi_{\alpha}^{\lambda}\rangle|\leq 1

so

\displaystyle|e_{k}^{\lambda}(x,z)|\leq\sum_{|\alpha|=k}1=\frac{(k+d-1)!}{k!(d-1)!}.

Therefore

\displaystyle|\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}(t,x,z)|\leq\frac{\textrm{vol}(S)\|(\cdot)^{d+m-1}\psi\|_{L^{1}(0,\infty)}}{(2\pi)^{d+m}(d-1)!}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\frac{(k+d-1)!}{k!(2k+d)^{d+m}}<\infty,

whence
\kappa_{\Sigma_{\psi}}
is a bounded continuous function on
\mathbb{G}
.
∎

8.7.
Restriction and extension operators

For
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{G})
, the restriction operator introduced in Section
5
can be written as follows

\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}f:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f|_{\Sigma}.

The meaning of this expression is the restriction of the map
(\mu,\lambda)\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f(\mu,\lambda)
to
(\mu,\lambda)\in\Sigma\subset\widehat{\mathbb{G}}
followed by restriction of the operator
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f(\mu,\lambda):L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
to the subspace
\Pi(\mu,\lambda)\subset L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

More explicitly, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}f(\mu,\lambda):=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f(\mu,\lambda)\mathcal{P}_{\mu}^{H(\lambda)},\quad(\mu,\lambda)\in\Sigma.

Remark 8.13
.

The restriction operator
\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}f
corresponds to restriction of the scalar-valued Fourier transform
(\mu,\lambda,\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f(\mu,\lambda,\alpha,\beta)
to the set

\{(\mu,\lambda,\alpha,\beta)\in\widehat{{\mathbb{R}}}\times(\mathfrak{v}^{*}\setminus\{0\})\times{\mathbb{N}}^{2d}:\quad\mu=|\lambda|(2|\alpha|+d)\}.

Notation.
In all this section we implicitly assume to work with the cutoffed measure
d\Sigma_{\psi}
but for simplicity we remove
\psi
from the notation and we simply write
d\Sigma
for the measure given in Remark
8.8
and by
\kappa_{\Sigma}
the cutoffed convolution kernel as in Proposition
8.12
.

Let us also introduce the
k
-th restriction operator defined analogously for
\Sigma_{k}
:

\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}f:=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f|_{\Sigma_{k}}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}f(\mu,\lambda)=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f(\mu,\lambda)\circ P_{k}(\lambda),\quad(\mu,\lambda)\in\Sigma_{k},

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}f=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}f.

For
\Theta\in L^{2}(d\Sigma)
define the Fourier extension operator by

(8.19)

\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}\Theta(t,x,z):=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{-1}(\Theta d\Sigma).

Similarly, define the
k
-th Fourier extension operator by

(8.20)

\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma_{k}}\Theta(t,x,z):=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{-1}(\Theta d\Sigma_{k}).

One computes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{-1}(\Theta d\Sigma_{k})(x,z):=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\psi(\mu)e^{i\mu t}\tr\big[\pi_{\lambda}(x,z)^{*}\Theta(\mu,\lambda)d\Sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\psi(\mu)e^{i\mu t}\tr\big[\pi_{\lambda}(x,z)^{*}\Theta(\mu,\lambda)P_{k}(\lambda)\big]d\sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}\Theta=\sum_{k\in N}\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma_{k}}\Theta.

Lemma 8.14
.

For all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma_{k}}=\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}^{*}
with respect to
L^{2}(d\Sigma_{\psi})
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{G})
. Therefore
\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}^{*}
with respect to
L^{2}(d\Sigma)
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{G})
.

Proof.

For
\Theta\in L^{2}(d\Sigma)
and
f\in\mathcal{S}(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})

\displaystyle\langle\Theta,\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}f\rangle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}\tr\big[\Theta(\mu,\lambda)^{*}\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}(f)(\mu,\lambda)d\Sigma(\mu,\lambda)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\int_{\Sigma_{l}}\tr\big[\Theta(\mu,\lambda)^{*}\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}(f)(\mu,\lambda)P_{l}(\lambda)\big]\psi(\mu)d\sigma_{l}(\mu,\lambda).

Then observe that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}(f)(\mu,\lambda)P_{l}(\lambda)=\delta_{kl}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f(\mu,\lambda)P_{k}(\lambda).

Continuing,

\displaystyle\langle\Theta,\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}f\rangle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\tr\big[\Theta(\mu,\lambda)^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f(\mu,\lambda)P_{k}(\lambda)\big]\psi(\mu)d\sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{G}}e^{i\mu t}\tr\big[\Theta(\mu,\lambda)^{*}\pi_{\lambda}(x,z)^{*}P_{k}(\lambda)\big]f(x,z)\,dtdxdz\,\psi(\mu)d\sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{G}}\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\overline{e^{-i\mu t}\tr\big[\pi_{\lambda}(x,z)^{*}\Theta(\mu,\lambda)P_{k}(\lambda)\big]}\,\psi(\mu)d\sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)f(t,x,z)\,dtdxdz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{G}}\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\overline{e^{-i\mu t}\tr\big[\pi_{\lambda}(x,z)^{*}\Theta(\mu,\lambda)P_{k}(\lambda)\big]}\,\psi(\mu)d\sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)f(t,x,z)\,dtdxdz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{G}}\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\Sigma_{k}}\Theta(\mu,\lambda)}f(t,x,z)\,dtdxdz.\qed

Proposition 8.15
.

For
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{G})
,

(8.21)

\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}^{*}\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}(f)=f*_{\mathbb{G}}\kappa_{\Sigma}

where
\iota:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{G}
is group inversion, and
\kappa_{\Sigma}
the convolution kernel defined in Proposition
8.12
.

Proof.

Fix
k,l\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}^{*}\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{l}}f(t,x,z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\psi(\mu)e^{i\mu t}\tr\big[\pi_{\lambda}(x,z)^{*}\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{l}}(\mu,\lambda)d\Sigma_{l}(\mu,\lambda)\big]

\displaystyle=\delta_{kl}\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\psi(\mu)e^{i\mu t}\tr\big[\pi_{\lambda}(x,z)^{*}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f(\mu,\lambda)d\Sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)\big]

since
P_{l}(\lambda)d\Sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)=\delta_{kl}d\Sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)
.
By Fubini’s Theorem and absolute integrability of the integrand, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{k}}^{*}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma_{l}}f(t,x,z)=

\displaystyle=\delta_{kl}\int_{G}f(t^{\prime},x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\int_{\Sigma_{k}}\psi(\mu)e^{i\mu(t-t^{\prime})}\tr\big[\pi_{\lambda}((x,z)^{*}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}))d\Sigma_{k}(\mu,\lambda)\big]dt^{\prime}dx^{\prime}dz^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\delta_{kl}\int_{G}f(t^{\prime},x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{-1}(\psi d\Sigma_{k})((-t^{\prime},-x^{\prime},-z^{\prime})(t,x,z))dt^{\prime}dx^{\prime}dz^{\prime}

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}^{*}\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}f

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f*_{\mathbb{G}}\{\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}^{-1}(\psi d\Sigma_{k})\}=f*_{\mathbb{G}}\kappa_{\Sigma}.\qed

8.8.
Wave Equation

In the case of the wave equation, we consider the sets

(8.22)

\Sigma^{\pm}=\{(\mu,\lambda)\in\widehat{\mathbb{G}}:|\mu|^{2}=|\lambda|(2k+d),\,\pm\mu>0\,\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}}\}

With respect to the surfaces
\Sigma^{\pm}
(or for the compact subset
\supp\psi\cap\Sigma^{\pm}
) the map
\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma^{\pm}}f=\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f|_{\Sigma^{\pm}}
is defined by the restriction of the map
(\mu,\lambda)\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f(\mu,\lambda)
to
(\mu,\lambda)\in\Sigma^{\pm}
followed by the restriction of the operator
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbb{G}}f(\mu,\lambda)\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
to the finite dimensional eigenspace
\Pi(|\mu|^{2},\lambda)=\ker(\mu^{2}I-H(\lambda))\subset L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.
Each component
\Sigma_{k}^{\pm}\subset\Sigma^{\pm}
is equipped with the scalar measure
d\sigma_{k}^{\pm}={\rm pr}|_{\Sigma_{k}^{\pm}}^{*}d\sigma_{\widehat{G}}
on
\Sigma_{k}^{\pm}
. As in the Schrödinger case, we define

Given

Appendix D
The inhomogeneous case

The argument is standard. For completeness we report it here.
Denoting by
(\mathcal{U}(t))_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
the solution operator of the Schrödinger equation, namely
\mathcal{U}(t)u_{0}
is the solution with
f=0
at time
t
associated with the data
u_{0}
, then similarly to the Euclidean case
(\mathcal{U}(t))_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
is a one-parameter group of unitary operators on
L^{2}
. Moreover, the solution to the inhomogeneous equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}i\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=f\\
u_{|t=0}=0\,,\end{array}\right.

writes

(D.1)

u(t,x,z)=-i\int^{t}_{0}\mathcal{U}(t-t^{\prime})f(t^{\prime},x,z)dt^{\prime},

Let us check that it satisfies, for all admissible pairs
(p,q)

(D.2)

\|u\|_{L^{r}_{s}L_{t}^{q}L^{p}_{Y}}\leq C\|f\|_{L_{t}^{1}H^{\sigma}}

with
\sigma=\frac{Q}{2}-\frac{2}{q}-\frac{2d}{p}

Let us first assume that, for all
t
, the source term
f(t,\cdot)
is frequency localized in in the unit ball
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
in the sense of Definition
A.2
, and recall that according to the results, if
g
is frequency localized in a unit ball, then for all
2\leq p\leq q\leq\infty

(D.3)

\|\,\mathcal{U}(\cdot)g\|_{L^{r}_{\mathfrak{v}}L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{2}}\,.

Taking advantage of (
D.1
), we have
for all
z
,

\|u(t,\cdot,z)\|_{L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\mathcal{U}(t)\mathcal{U}(-t^{\prime})f(t^{\prime},\cdot,z)\|_{L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}}dt^{\prime}.

Therefore, still for all
z
,

\|u(\cdot,\cdot,z)\|_{L_{t}^{q}L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\mathcal{U}(\cdot)\mathcal{U}(-t^{\prime})f(t^{\prime},\cdot,z)\|_{L_{t}^{q}L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}}dt^{\prime}.

Invoking (
D.3
), we deduce that

\|u\|_{L^{r}_{\mathfrak{v}}L^{q}_{t}L^{p}_{\mathfrak{h}}}\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|f(t^{\prime},\cdot)\|_{{L^{2}}}dt^{\prime}.

t
