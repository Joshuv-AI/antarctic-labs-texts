An endoplasmic reticulum-enriched nanogel couples ferroptotic tumor damage with macrophage reprogramming for triple-negative breast cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Heying Chen, Yilu Ni, Jiaruo Tang, Yongyuan Ma, Jie Zhang, Jiayu Zhang, Hongyan Sun, Jun Chen, Zhenglin Yang, Dongqing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is characterized by severely immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which leads to tumor ferroptosis resistance and dominant protumor M2 macrophages, restraining innate-to-adaptive antitumor immune cascade. Herein, an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-enriched pH/redox-sensitive SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX nanogel was constructed to realize dual ER-targeted manipulation on TNBC cells and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) to elicit an ER-centered innate-to-adaptive immune amplification axis. In TNBC cells, nanogel-induced ER stress inhibits the GSH-GPX4 axis and accelerates lipid peroxidation, triggering ER-originated ferroptosis and immunogenic cell death (ICD) to release antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) for immune priming. In macrophages, nanogel activates ER-dependent STING/NF-κB pathways without ferroptosis, facilitating M2-to-M1 polarization and inflammatory TME remodeling. The dual ER-initiated pathways synergistically facilitate dendritic cell (DC) maturation, enhance intratumoral CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration and build long-term systemic immune memory. In 4T1 TNBC models, the nanogel efficiently inhibits primary tumor growth, postoperative recurrence, distant rechallenge and lung metastasis with favorable biosafety. This work validates ER as a core regulatory hub linking tumor ferroptosis and macrophage reprogramming, providing an organelle-targeted strategy for durable TNBC immunotherapy.Keywords: ER-Enriched nanogel, Ferroptosis, Macrophage reprogramming, STING/NF-κB signaling, Triple-negative breast cancer

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), defined by the lack of hormone receptors and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) amplification, remains a major clinical challenge [[1], [2], [3]]. Beyond its limited treatment options, TNBC is also suffered by a high propensity for early metastasis and postoperative recurrence, compromising therapeutic outcomes [4,5]. Compounding the treatment difficulty is TNBC's inherently immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) characterized by tolerogenic antigen-presenting cells (APCs), pro-tumor M2 phenotype tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), restricted dendritic cell (DC) maturation and antigen cross-presentation, and poor cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) infiltration [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. This cold-tumor feature further exacerbates TNBC's insensitivity and resistance to conventional therapies such as chemotherapy, creating a perpetuating cycle of treatment failure [10,11]. To break this bottleneck, an innovative strategy is urgently needed that not only eliminates TNBC cells efficiently but also synergistically activates both innate and adaptive immunity, driving robust CTL activation and intratumoral infiltration.

Ferroptosis, an emerging form of regulated cell death, has emerged as a promising avenue for cancer therapy [12,13]. Distinct from traditional apoptosis and necrosis, it regulates cell death through iron-related lipid peroxidation (LPO) cascades [14,15]. Ferroptotic tumor cells release immunogenic cell death (ICD) signals, creating conditions for antigen cross-presentation and immune activation [16,17]. However, TNBC cells have evolved several mechanisms to resist ferroptosis. On the one hand, they sense the hypoxic microenvironment to upregulate hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), thereby downregulating cytoplasmic levels of unsaturated lipids, the key substrate for ferroptosis [18,19]. Simultaneously, they use the glutathione-glutathione peroxidase 4 (GSH-GPX4) axis to scavenge lipid peroxides, further reinforcing ferroptosis resistance [20].

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER), a central organelle for protein folding, redox homeostasis, and lipid metabolism, has emerged as a pivotal subcellular target to overcome this resistance and amplify ferroptosis-mediated ICD [14,[21], [22], [23]]. ER-targeted drug delivery disrupts lipid homeostasis, accelerates the production and diffusion of LPO, and thereby exacerbates ER stress [[24], [25], [26]]. Concurrently, ER disruption directly downregulates GPX4 expression, rendering TNBC cells more susceptible to ferroptosis [27,28]. In a reciprocal loop, ER-targeted ferroptosis further damages ER membranes, promoting the release and translocation of calreticulin (CRT) to the cell surface [29,30]. This “eat-me” signal is efficiently recognized by dendritic cells (DCs), triggering their maturation, which is a critical step in activating adaptive immunity [23,31]. Beyond tumor cells, the ER also serves as an important signaling hub in immune cells, where stress-associated pathways can shape innate immune activation. As an ER-resident adaptor, STING is closely linked to ER homeostasis and trafficking, and emerging evidence suggests that ER-associated perturbation in macrophages can potentiate STING-centered signaling and downstream inflammatory reprogramming [32,33], thereby providing an opportunity to connect organelle-directed tumor damage with macrophage-mediated remodeling of the immunosuppressive TME [34].

Importantly, tumor ferroptosis and macrophage immune activation represent interconnected processes within the tumor microenvironment rather than independent therapeutic events [35]. Ferroptosis-associated ICD in tumor cells promotes the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and tumor-derived immunogenic signals, facilitating immune recognition and activation [9]. Meanwhile, macrophage activation through innate immune pathways, particularly STING-associated inflammatory signaling, promotes M1-like polarization and enhances antitumor immune responses [36,37]. Therefore, simultaneously inducing ferroptotic tumor damage and macrophage immune reprogramming may establish a coordinated tumor–immune regulatory axis to overcome immunosuppressive TMEs [38].

Given the central role of ER in both ferroptotic regulation and innate immune signaling, ER-associated intervention provides a potential strategy to bridge tumor cell destruction and immune remodeling [39,40]. In tumor cells, ER-associated perturbation can amplify ferroptosis-mediated ICD through lipid metabolic disruption and oxidative stress amplification [9,41]. In macrophages, modulation of ER-associated signaling may facilitate STING activation and inflammatory phenotype switching [42]. Thus, spatially coordinated regulation of ER-associated ferroptosis in tumor cells and immune activation in macrophages may provide a synergistic approach for cancer immunotherapy.

Despite substantial advances in nanomedicine-based cancer therapy, current nanoplatforms remain largely focused on improving drug stability, tumor accumulation, and controlled release, whereas precise regulation of intracellular trafficking and organelle-associated signaling remains challenging [43]. Moreover, existing co-delivery systems for chemotherapeutic agents and immune modulators primarily achieve simultaneous cargo delivery but lack coordinated control over intracellular distribution and downstream biological responses. Therefore, the development of multifunctional nanoplatforms capable of integrating intracellular spatial regulation, tumor cell death induction, and immune modulation represents an important direction for next-generation cancer therapy.

Herein, we engineered SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX, a redox- and pH-responsive immunoregulatory nanogel integrating superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONS), CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG ODN), and doxorubicin (DOX), to address key barriers to effective TNBC immunotherapy (Scheme 1A). Assisted by magnetic targeting and the enhanced permeability and retention effect, the nanogel preferentially accumulates in tumors and undergoes intracellular disassembly under acidic and reductive conditions, thereby enabling DOX release and SPION degradation. This process couples chemotherapy with iron-dependent oxidative injury, while the released Fe2+ and Fe3+ disrupts redox homeostasis and induces potent ferroptosis in tumor cells. Notably, the nanogel exhibits selective ER engagement, which amplifies lipid peroxidation, membrane damage, and ER-associated ferroptotic stress, thereby enhancing ICD-associated immunostimulatory signaling in tumor cells (Scheme 1B). In parallel, the nanogel preferentially engages the ER in macrophages and induces ER stress, which is accompanied by enhanced STING and NF-κB signaling and drives macrophage reprogramming toward a proinflammatory antitumor phenotype. CpG ODN and SPIONS further reinforce inflammatory remodeling within the TME (Scheme 1C). Through the coordinated induction of ER-directed ferroptotic tumor damage and macrophage-centered innate immune activation, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX promotes T-cell activation and CTL infiltration, thereby eliciting a self-amplifying antitumor immune response against TNBC growth, metastasis, and recurrence (Scheme 1D).

Results

Synthesis and characterizations of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX nanogel

In this study, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX nanoplatform was constructed to efficiently co-deliver multiple therapeutic agents to tumors, inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells while regulating the TME. The preparation and characterization processes were delineated briefly as follows. First, ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONS) were synthesized via pyrolysis, following a previously reported protocol. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that SPIONS exhibited a regular spherical morphology with excellent size uniformity, an average diameter of approximately 8 nm (Fig. S1). A thin oleic acid shell was observed coating on their surfaces. To endow SPIONS with multifunctional capabilities, their surfaces were subjected to hydrophilic ligand exchange and aldehyde derivatization, facilitating the construction of composite nanocarriers. The shell layer of the carrier was composed of a polyethylene glycol-poly(N-isopropylacrylamide)-polyamidoamine (PEG-PNIPAM-polyamidoamine) copolymer. Subsequently, SPIONS were covalently cross-linked with this copolymer via imine bonds to form the SPIONS@P nanocarrier. Further encapsulating CpG oligonucleotides and doxorubicin (DOX) yielded the nanogel, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX.

Nuclear magnetic resonance hydrogen spectroscopy (1H NMR) confirmed the successful synthesis of the cationic polymer r-HBAPP via the Michael addition reaction (Fig. S2). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) analysis was used to monitor PNIPAM and SPIONS@P preparation process. In the FT-IR spectrum of PNIPAM (Fig. 1A), characteristic peaks of its constituent monomers were clearly identified. For instance, peaks at ∼1730 cm−1 (attributed to the C=O stretching vibration of ester bonds) and 1100 cm−1 (assigned to C-O-C stretching vibration) were originated from PEG acrylate. The peak at ∼1650 cm−1, corresponding to the C=O stretching vibration of amide groups, confirmed the presence of NIPAM, APMAH, and r-HBPAA. Notably, the disappearance of the C=C double bond peaks (typically between 1600 and 1640 cm−1) in each monomer spectrum demonstrated that free-radical polymerization had proceeded successfully. For the FT-IR spectrum of SPIONS@P, in addition to the characteristic peaks of the PNIPAM copolymer, a distinct peak emerged ∼575 cm−1, which was attributed to the Fe-O stretching vibration of SPIONS. Concurrently, a series of peaks in the 600-800 cm−1 range were observed, corresponding to the out-of-plane bending vibrations of C-H bonds in the organic backbone of the PNIPAM-based polymer. These findings collectively indicated that the PNIPAM polymer and SPIONS had been successfully assembled into composite nanoparticles. Zeta potential measurements (Fig. 1B) further confirmed the surface functionalization and cross-linking effects of SPIONS, where the surface charge of the particles exhibited corresponding changes with polymer modification, indirectly verifying the successful construction of the carrier. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) showed that the average particle size of SPIONS@P was approximately 200 nm (Fig. 1C), while that of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX increased to approximately 260 nm after drug loading, confirming the successful encapsulation of therapeutic agents into the carrier.

The stability of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 10% (v/v) serum was evaluated via DLS, and results showed no significant change in hydrodynamic diameter over 2 days (Fig. 1D), confirming its favorable biological stability, which meets the requirements for in vivo delivery. A vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) was used to characterize the magnetic properties of SPIONS and SPIONS@P (Fig. 1E). SPIONS showed a saturation magnetization (Ms) of approximately 57.0 emu/g, with a low coercivity (Hc) of 10.67 Oe and a low remanent magnetization (Mr) of 1.40 emu/g. After polymer coating, SPIONS@P retained a high Ms of approximately 52.0 emu/g, while Hc and Mr remained low at 9.25 Oe and 1.06 emu/g, respectively. The slight decrease in Ms was attributed to the introduction of the nonmagnetic polymer shell. The very low coercivity, remanence, and Mr/Ms ratios indicated negligible magnetic hysteresis and confirmed that SPIONS@P retained the superparamagnetic behavior of the SPION core. The retained high Ms supports the magnetic responsiveness of SPIONS@P, whereas the negligible Hc and Mr suggest minimal residual magnetization after removal of the external magnetic field, supporting its suitability for magnetically guided tumor delivery. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 1F) demonstrated that the surface functionalization of SPIONS and subsequent cross-linking assembly with the shell polymer did not alter their internal crystalline structure, ensuring the stability of magnetic properties and iron ion release capability.

Drug loading efficiency assays showed that SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX achieved the loading efficiency of 70% for CpG ODNs and 50% for DOX-meeting the dosage requirements for synergistic co-delivery of multiple drugs. As shown in Fig. 1G, TEM and DLS results indicated a significant reduction in the particle size of the nanogel under pH 6.5 conditions (simulating the acidic TME), confirming its pH responsiveness and ability to trigger drug release at tumor sites. In vitro DOX release experiments further explored the stimuli-responsive release behavior of the nanogel. Initially, DOX release under pH 7.4 and 6.5 conditions were investigated, with results showing that DOX release was significantly accelerated under pH 6.5 conditions (Fig. 1H). Subsequently, DOX release was evaluated at varying dithiothreitol (DTT) concentrations (DTT, a commonly used chemical reducing agent for evaluating reduction-sensitive release behavior). With increasing DTT concentration, the nanomaterial released greater amount of DOX (Fig. 1I), confirming the responsiveness of the nanogel to reductive stimuli. To further assess redox-responsive release under a more physiologically relevant condition, glutathione (GSH, 10 mM) was additionally used to mimic the highly reductive intracellular environment of tumor cells. As shown in Fig. S3, DOX release was also markedly promoted under acidic and GSH-containing conditions. These results, together with the DTT-triggered release data, further demonstrate that SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX exhibits pH/redox-responsive release behavior under tumor-relevant acidic and reductive microenvironments.

ER-enriched nanogels induce GPX4-suppressed ferroptotic ICD in tumor cells

Ferroptosis is characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and redox imbalance. [44,45]. Given the central role of the ER in lipid metabolism, oxidative protein folding, calcium storage, and membrane homeostasis, ER perturbation can sensitize cells to ferroptosis, while ferroptotic lipid peroxidation can further aggravate ER dysfunction (Fig. 2A) [14,15].

To determine whether SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX could initiate ER-associated ferroptosis, its subcellular distribution was first examined in 4T1 cells. Organelle colocalization imaging showed preferential ER accumulation of the nanogel, with a Pearson's correlation coefficient close to 0.9 and higher than that observed for lysosomes or mitochondria (Fig. 2B). Time-dependent imaging further showed that ER colocalization peaked at 12 h and decreased at 24 h, likely reflecting intracellular nanogel disassembly and drug release (Fig. 2C). Free DOX predominantly accumulated in the nucleus, whereas nanogel-delivered DOX exhibited preferential ER-associated localization (Fig. S4), suggesting nanogel-mediated intracellular trafficking. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated enhanced intracellular DOX delivery compared with free DOX in 4T1 cells (Fig. S5), supporting the advantages of the nanogel-mediated delivery strategy.

Consistently, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX regulated ER stress-related proteins, including GRP78, CHOP, HMOX1, and ERO1A, indicating ER stress activation following ER-enriched nanogel accumulation (Fig. 2D). We next examined the GSH-GPX4 antioxidant axis, a key defense system against ferroptosis. GPX4 expression decreased with increasing nanogel concentration, as shown by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 2E). Immunoblotting further confirmed GPX4 downregulation after SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX treatment, comparable to the classical ferroptosis inducer RSL3, whereas Ferrostatin-1 partially reversed this effect (Fig. 2F) [46,47].

GPX4 suppression was also observed in both 4T1 and HepG2 cellsand was further confirmed in concentration- and time-dependent analyses (Fig. S6–S9). Consistently, GPX4 enzymatic activity decreased with increasing nanogel concentration and prolonged incubation time (Fig. S10–S11). In parallel, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX reduced the GSH/GSSG ratio over time, indicating GSH depletion and impaired antioxidant capacity (Fig. 2G). These results demonstrate that the nanogel disrupts the GPX4-dependent lipid peroxide detoxification system.

The functional consequence of this redox disruption was evaluated by cell viability assays. SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX inhibited 4T1 cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 2H), with similar dose-dependent cytotoxicity observed in 4T1 and HepG2 cells (Fig. S12). Its cytotoxic effect was comparable to that of RSL3 under the tested conditions (Fig. S13), and broad antiproliferative activity was observed across multiple cancer cell lines, including MCF-7, 4T1, and HeLa cells (Fig. S14–S15). Compared with free drugs and single-component nanogels, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX exhibited the strongest antitumor efficacy, indicating that the enhanced therapeutic effect resulted from the integrated nanogel design rather than the additive effects of individual components. Importantly, Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) and GSH supplementation both rescued nanogel-induced cytotoxicity, confirming that lipid peroxidation and GSH depletion are functionally involved in tumor cell death (Fig. 2I and J).

Lipid peroxidation was directly assessed using C11-BODIPY581/591 staining. SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX markedly increased intracellular lipid peroxidation to a level comparable to RSL3, whereas Ferrostatin-1 reduced this accumulation (Fig. 2K). Additional C11-BODIPY581/591 analysis further supported lipid peroxide generation (Fig. S16). DCFH-DA staining also showed increased intracellular ROS after nanogel treatment, indicating enhanced oxidative stress during ferroptotic injury (Fig. S17). Together with GPX4 downregulation, GPX4 activity inhibition, and GSH/GSSG depletion, these findings establish that SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX induces ferroptosis through suppression of the GSH-GPX4 axis and enhancement of lipid peroxidation/ROS accumulation. To further distinguish ferroptosis from apoptosis, we performed additional rescue experiments using the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK. Compared with Fer-1 treatment, Z-VAD-FMK showed a relatively limited protective effect against nanogel-induced cytotoxicity (Fig. S18), indicating that apoptosis may contribute to, but is not the predominant mechanism underlying, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX-mediated tumor cell death.

Finally, we examined whether nanogel-induced ferroptotic damage could generate immunostimulatory signals [14]. Immunofluorescence analysis showed increased HMGB1 release and calreticulin exposure after SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX treatment (Fig. 2L and M), indicating the induction of ICD-associated immune activation cues. Collectively, these results demonstrate that ER-enriched SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX activates ER stress, suppresses the GSH-GPX4 antioxidant pathway, promotes lipid peroxidation, and triggers ferroptotic ICD in tumor cells.

Organelle remodeling provides subcellular evidence for nanogel-induced ferroptotic injury in tumor cells

Given that SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX induced ER stress, disrupted the GSH-GPX4 antioxidant axis, and promoted lipid peroxidation in tumor cells, we next examined whether these molecular events were accompanied by ferroptosis-associated organelle remodeling. Since ferroptosis is closely linked to oxidative membrane damage, HIS-SIM super-resolution imaging was used to visualize ER, mitochondrial, and lysosomal morphology after nanogel treatment.

Representative images showed concentration-dependent organelle alterations in cells treated with 150 μg/mL and 300 μg/mL SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX compared with untreated controls (Fig. 3A–C). Quantitative analysis revealed pronounced mitochondrial remodeling, including reduced mitochondrial size and perimeter, together with increased solidity, indicating mitochondrial shrinkage and condensation (Fig. 3D–F). These changes are consistent with ferroptosis-associated mitochondrial injury and support the lipid peroxidation-mediated damage demonstrated in Fig. 2.

ER morphology was also markedly affected. The average ER branch length decreased after nanogel treatment, indicating simplification of the ER network (Fig. 3G). This observation is consistent with the ER-enriched nanogel accumulation and ER stress activation shown in Fig. 2, further supporting the involvement of ER-associated ferroptotic injury. In contrast, lysosomes exhibited enlargement, as reflected by increased lysosomal size and perimeter after nanogel treatment (Fig. 3H and I). This lysosomal expansion may reflect increased degradative demand in response to oxidatively damaged membranes and organelles during ferroptosis progression.

Collectively, HIS-SIM imaging revealed a coordinated organelle remodeling pattern characterized by mitochondrial shrinkage, ER network simplification, and lysosomal enlargement. Together with the biochemical evidence in Fig. 2, these subcellular changes provide morphological support for SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX-induced ferroptotic injury driven by oxidative membrane damage and ER-associated stress.

ER-enriched nanogel activates STING/NF-κB signaling for macrophage reprogramming

The ER-associated ferroptotic injury and organelle remodeling observed in tumor cells established SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX as an effective inducer of organelle-directed tumor damage. Given the central role of macrophages in the immunosuppressive TNBC microenvironment, we next examined whether this ER-engaged nanogel could reprogram macrophages toward a proinflammatory antitumor phenotype. Unlike tumor cells, macrophage modulation was proposed to rely mainly on innate immune signaling and phenotypic remodeling rather than ferroptotic cytotoxicity.

Confocal imaging showed that SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX progressively accumulated in the ER of RAW264.7-derived macrophages from 3 h to 24 h, as evidenced by increasing overlap with ER-Tracker staining (Fig. 4A). Quantitative colocalization analysis further confirmed time-dependent ER enrichment, with a high Pearson's correlation coefficient at 24 h (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX showed negligible cytotoxicity toward both M1 and M2 macrophages (Fig. 4C), and GPX4 expression remained largely unchanged after treatment (Fig. S19). Subsequently, C11-BODIPY581/591 staining results demonstrated that the nanogel did not increase intracellular lipid peroxidation levels in RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. S20). These results indicate that macrophage remodeling is not driven by canonical ferroptotic injury, but by an immunoregulatory response associated with ER engagement.

Based on these findings, we proposed that SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX activates ER-associated STING/NF-κB signaling to drive macrophage reprogramming (Fig. 4D). Immunoblotting analysis confirmed increased phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 in M0, M1, and M2 macrophages after nanogel treatment (Fig. 4E), demonstrating activation of the STING-TBK1-IRF3 innate immune axis across different macrophage states.

We next examined whether STING activation was accompanied by inflammatory polarization. In RAW264.7-derived macrophages, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX upregulated iNOS and enhanced NF-κB signaling (Fig. 4F). Similar results were observed in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), where nanogel treatment increased iNOS while decreasing the M2-associated markers Arg1 and CD206, together with NF-κB activation (Fig. 4G). These results indicate that SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX promotes macrophage repolarization from an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype toward an inflammatory M1-like state. Flow cytometry further confirmed increased CD86 expression in M0 and M2 macrophages and BMDMs after nanogel treatment (Fig. 4H–I, S21). Among the tested formulations, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX induced the strongest CD86 upregulation, supporting the advantage of integrating SPIONs, CpG ODN, and DOX within one nanogel platform (Fig. S22).

To further elucidate the role of STING signaling in nanogel-mediated macrophage activation, RAW264.7 macrophages were treated with the STING inhibitor H-151. Inhibition of STING markedly attenuated nanogel-induced phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3 (Fig. S23A–C), accompanied by reduced NF-κB activation and decreased expression of M1-associated markers (Fig. S23D–F). These results indicate that STING signaling is an important mediator of nanogel-induced macrophage polarization. Furthermore, blockade of NF-κB signaling using TPCA-1 significantly impaired nanogel-induced M1 macrophage reprogramming. Specifically, TPCA-1 treatment suppressed NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, decreased iNOS expression, and increased Arg1 expression (Fig. S24), demonstrating that NF-κB activation is required for effective macrophage phenotype modulation. Interestingly, TPCA-1 treatment also resulted in a moderate reduction in STING phosphorylation, suggesting potential reciprocal regulation between NF-κB and STING signaling during nanogel-induced macrophage activation. Collectively, these pharmacological inhibition studies demonstrate that the STING/TBK1/IRF3 and NF-κB signaling pathways are functionally involved in SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX nanogel-mediated M1 macrophage reprogramming.

Functionally, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX markedly enhanced NO and TNF-α secretion in M2 macrophages (Fig. 4J and K). Intracellular O2 probe staining further suggested increased oxygen levels and altered redox metabolism after treatment (Fig. S25), which may contribute to inflammatory remodeling. Finally, macrophages pretreated with SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX significantly inhibited 4T1 cell proliferation in a transwell coculture system (Fig. 4L), confirming that nanogel-induced macrophage reprogramming translated into enhanced tumor suppression.

Collectively, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX engages the ER in macrophages without inducing obvious ferroptotic cytotoxicity, activates STING-TBK1-IRF3 and NF-κB signaling, promotes M2-to-M1-like repolarization, enhances inflammatory mediator production, and strengthens macrophage-mediated tumor inhibition. These results establish SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX as a dual-mode platform that couples ER-associated ferroptotic tumor damage with macrophage-centered innate immune reprogramming.

Magnetically guided SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX suppresses primary tumor growth and metastasis in vivo

The in vivo therapeutic efficacy of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX was evaluated in a subcutaneous 4T1 tumor model. When tumors reached approximately 80 mm3, mice were intravenously treated with different formulations every two days for four doses. For nanogel-treated groups, magnetic guidance was applied to the tumor region to enhance local accumulation (Fig. 5A). The tumor-targeting ability of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX was first assessed using Cy5-labeled formulations. In vivo fluorescence imaging showed that the tumor signals peaked at 12 h after injection and was markedly enhanced under magnetic guidance (Fig. 5B). Ex vivo imaging further confirmed preferential tumor accumulation with limited distribution in major organs, supporting the delivery advantage of the magnetically guided nanogel platform.

SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX produced the most potent inhibition of primary tumor growth among all treatment groups (Fig. 5C-D). Free CpG ODNs or free DOX showed only limited therapeutic effects, whereas SPIONS@P-CpG and SPIONS@P-DOX achieved moderate tumor suppression, indicating that nanogel-mediated delivery improved both immunomodulatory and chemotherapeutic efficacy. Consistently, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX resulted in the lowest tumor weight after treatment (Fig. 5E and Fig. S26). No obvious body weight loss was observed in any group, suggesting favorable systemic tolerance (Fig. S27).

Lung metastasis represents the most prevalent metastatic way in TNBC [48]. The antimetastatic effect was further evaluated by H&E staining of lung tissues. SPIONS@P-CpG, SPIONS@P-DOX, and especially SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX markedly reduced lung metastatic lesions compared with control treatments (Fig. 5F). TUNEL staining showed increased tumor cell death after SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX treatment (Fig. 5G). Meanwhile, GPX4 immunofluorescence revealed reduced GPX4 expression in tumor tissues, consistent with ferroptosis induction in vivo (Fig. 5H and Fig. S28). Consistently, transmission electron microscopy further confirmed that tumor cells treated with SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX exhibited typical ferroptotic morphology, including mitochondrial cristae loss and increased matrix condensation (Fig. 5I).

Together, these results demonstrate that magnetic guidance enhances tumor accumulation of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX nanogel, enabling efficient suppression of primary tumor growth and lung metastasis. The in vivo therapeutic efficacy is associated with the integrated effects of tumor-targeted delivery, chemotherapy, ferroptosis induction, and immunomodulation.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals coupled ferroptosis induction and immune activation

To comprehensively investigate the regulatory effects of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX treatment on the immune components of TME, 4T1 tumor tissues were harvested at 14 d post-administration of the nanogel formulations for transcriptomic profiling. Subsequent bioinformatic analysis focused on genes annotated to immune-related processes, with the aim of delineating the global immune landscape of TME following treatment. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering patterns across experimental groups, indicating significant transcriptomic differences induced by the nanogel-treatment (Fig. S29). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that genes upregulated after SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX treatment were mainly associated with immune responses (Fig. 6A). Relative to the untreated control group, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX treatment further resulted in increased expression of genes involved in three key biological pathways: ferroptosis, immune responses, and apoptotic signaling cascades. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis of upregulated genes in the SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX group identified significant enrichment in the pathway critical to immune activation, including Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway, and T cell receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 6B).

Notably, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX group exhibited ER localization and ER stress, confirming its targeted regulatory effect on the ER (Fig. 6A,C-D). The expression of key genes indicates enhanced T cell activation (CD4, PTPRC), M1 macrophage polarization and NF-κB signaling pathway activation (TNFα, IL-1β), accompanied by a significant downregulation of immunosuppression-related genes (TGFβ-1, IL-10) and strengthened cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions. These findings collectively suggest an increase in anti-tumor immune infiltration post-treatment and the induction of a robust immune response in vivo. The elevated IL-7 levels observed in the treatment group may be interferon-induced and involved in immune system responses. Significant alterations in key ferroptosis-related genes (PTGS2, SLC7A11, GPX4) indicate marked ferroptosis induction within the tumor tissue (Fig. 6E and F). Protein-protein interaction network analysis further revealed the expression patterns of critical genes associated with ferroptosis (Fig. S30). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further corroborated these observations (Fig. 6G–J). Meanwhile, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was employed to characterize the ferroptosis and immune cell infiltration landscape of the tumor microenvironment. The results demonstrated that the experimental group achieved higher enrichment scores for ferroptosis as well as multiple antitumor immune cell populations (Fig. S31). In addition, correlation analysis of ferroptosis signature scores and activated macrophage infiltration scores uncovered a positive correlation (Fig. S32). The normalized enrichment scores of functional gene sets from all samples further supported this finding (Fig. S33). Subsequently, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed to validate the expression of ferroptosis- and immune-related genes (Fig. S34). The results demonstrated that the nanogel significantly downregulated ferroptosis-suppressive genes, including Gpx4 and Slc7a11, while upregulating the ferroptosis-associated gene Ptgs2 in tumor tissues. Moreover, immune infiltration-associated genes (Cd4 and Ptprc) and M1 macrophage activation-related genes (Tnf-α and Il-1β) were increased, accompanied by decreased expression of immunosuppressive genes (Tgf-β1 and Il-10). These findings confirm that the nanogel induces ferroptotic responses in tumors and promotes antitumor immune activation, further supporting the transcriptomic analysis.

Taken together, these transcriptomic data suggested that SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX nanogel modulated the TME by activating immune responses, potentially through the regulation of macrophage and T cell function, and inducing tumor cell ferroptosis, implying its multifunctional roles in antitumor therapy. These findings were consistent with observations from our prior in vitro cellular experiments, which demonstrated the nanogel's capacity to induce ferroptosis in 4T1 cells. Notably, the therapeutic efficacy of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX was hypothesized to stem from its stable targeting of the endoplasmic reticulum, which drove both ferroptosis and immunogenic cell death (ICD), thereby activating robust antitumor immune responses.

In vivo antitumor immune regulatory mechanisms

The transcriptomic enrichment of immune-response, NF-κB, and T cell receptor signaling pathways prompted us to further validate immune remodeling at the cellular level. Flow cytometry was performed to characterize macrophage polarization, dendritic cell maturation, and T cell activation in tumors, draining lymph nodes, and spleens.

Tumor-infiltrating macrophages were first analyzed to determine whether the macrophage reprogramming observed in vitro occurred in vivo. SPIONS@P-CpG and SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX increased CD86+ M1-like macrophages and decreased CD206+ M2-like macrophages in tumor tissues, leading to a sustained elevation of the M1/M2 ratio over time (Fig. 7A and Fig. S35-S36). These results demonstrate effective remodeling of TAMs from an immunosuppressive phenotype toward a proinflammatory antitumor state. We next examined dendritic cell maturation in draining lymph nodes, a key step linking ferroptotic/ICD-associated tumor damage to adaptive immune activation. SPIONS@P-CpG and SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX significantly increased the proportion of mature DCs, defined as CD11c+MHC II+CD86+ cells (Fig. 7B and Fig. S37), indicating enhanced antigen-presenting activation.

T cell responses were then evaluated in systemic and lymphoid immune compartments. At day 14, SPIONS@P-CpG and SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX predominantly increased the proportion of CD3+ T cells in the spleen and draining lymph nodes, whereas the relative proportions of helper T cells (CD3+CD4+) and cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+) showed only modest changes (Fig. S38–S39). By day 21, more pronounced increases in helper T cells (CD3+CD4+) and cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+) were observed following CpG-containing treatments (Fig. 7C–G, 7J, Fig. S41-42), indicating a time-dependent evolution from an early increase in the overall T-cell population toward subsequent remodeling of helper and cytotoxic T-cell subsets.

Finally, intratumoral T cell responses were evaluated. At day 14, CpG-containing treatments predominantly increased the proportion of tumor-infiltrating CD3+ T cells, whereas the relative proportions of T helper (CD3+CD4+) and cytotoxic T cells (CD3+CD8+) within the CD3+ population showed only modest changes (Fig. S40). By day 21, SPIONS@P-CpG and SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX significantly increased the proportions of both T helper and cytotoxic T cells within the intratumoral CD3+ population (Fig. 7H–I, 7K and Fig. S41-S42), supporting a progressive enhancement of intratumoral T cell responses.

Collectively, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX drives a coordinated antitumor immune cascade involving TAM repolarization, DC maturation, T cell activation, and intratumoral effector T cell infiltration. These results demonstrate that the nanogel converts the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment into an inflamed, T cell-responsive state.

SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX prevents postoperative recurrence and distant metastasis through systemic immune memory

Durable control of postoperative recurrence and distant metastasis remains a major challenge in TNBC therapy, particularly because residual tumor cells can rapidly regrow and disseminate after local treatment [49,50]. To evaluate whether SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX nanogel could establish long-term antitumor protection beyond primary tumor suppression, a postoperative recurrence model was constructed by surgically removing the majority of primary 4T1 tumors before treatment (Fig. 8A). Compared with control treatments, SPIONS@P-CpG and SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX almost completely prevented local tumor recurrence (Fig. 8B and C), with 100% survival through 60 days (Fig. S43). These results indicate that the nanogel-based immunotherapy effectively controlled residual tumor outgrowth after surgery.

To further determine whether this protection extended beyond the primary tumor site, a contralateral 4T1 rechallenge model was established. SPIONS@P-CpG and SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX strongly suppressed distant tumor growth after rechallenge, suggesting the generation of systemic antitumor immunity (Fig. 8D and E). Consistently, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX significantly increased splenic CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62L− effector memory T cells (Fig. 8F and G). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated a significant increase in splenic CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62L+ central memory T (Tcm) cells following nanogel treatment (Fig. S44). The concurrent expansion of Tcm and CD44+CD62L− effector memory T (Tem) subsets suggest that the nanogel induces sustained systemic antitumor immunity. Enhanced CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration was also observed in distant tumors, accompanied by increased TNF-α production (Fig. 8H and I). These findings demonstrate that SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX establishes functional immune memory capable of mediating systemic tumor surveillance.

Metastasis of TNBC is a primary driver of disease-related mortality [51,52]. The ability of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX to suppress hematogenous metastasis was further evaluated in a 4T1-Luc lung metastasis model. Bioluminescence imaging showed progressive lung tumor growth in control groups, whereas lung tumor signals were nearly undetectable in the SPIONS@P-CpG and SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX groups throughout the observation period (Fig. 8J). H&E staining confirmed a marked reduction of metastatic lung nodules after nanogel treatment (Fig. 8K). Importantly, H&E staining of major organs showed no obvious treatment-induced pathological injury, supporting the biosafety of these formulations (Fig. S45). Naturally, to further validate biosafety, we examined serum biochemical markers in mice. The results indicate that the nanogel has nearly no adverse impacts on the heart, liver and kidney, and triggers substantially weaker changes in these markers relative to free DOX (Fig. S46). This highlights the in vivo stability of the nanogel as a targeted delivery platform.

Collectively, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX provides durable antitumor protection by preventing postoperative recurrence, suppressing distant tumor rechallenge, increasing effector memory T cells, enhancing T cell infiltration into distant tumors, and inhibiting lung metastasis. These results highlight the novelty of the platform: ER-associated ferroptotic tumor damage and macrophage-centered immune reprogramming are translated into systemic T cell-mediated immune memory, enabling long-term control of both residual and disseminated TNBC.

Discussion and conclusion

This study presents an ER-enriched nanogel strategy that couples ferroptotic tumor damage with macrophage-centered immune reprogramming for TNBC immunotherapy. Unlike conventional chemoimmunotherapeutic nanoplatforms that mainly rely on tumor accumulation and drug release, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX uses ER engagement as a mechanistic entry point to connect organelle stress, regulated tumor cell death, and antitumor immune activation. Rather than serving as a simple cargo delivery system, this nanogel introduces intracellular spatial regulation as an additional design dimension, enabling coordinated control of tumor cell fate and immune responses. This design directly addresses key barriers in TNBC, including ferroptosis resistance, low tumor immunogenicity, M2-like macrophage dominance, and insufficient cytotoxic T cell infiltration. It should be noted that the ER enrichment described here represents preferential ER-associated accumulation mediated by the physicochemical properties of the nanogel, rather than receptor-specific ER targeting. The amphiphilic membrane-interactive cross-linker is proposed to contribute to this behavior by facilitating interactions with intracellular membrane systems. Thus, the uniqueness of this platform does not rely on a classical ligand-mediated organelle targeting strategy, but on the ability of the integrated nanogel architecture to regulate intracellular trafficking and organelle-associated biological functions.

The ER-enriched distribution of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX amplified ferroptotic vulnerability in tumor cells. ER engagement induced ER stress, disrupted the GSH-GPX4 antioxidant axis, and promoted lipid peroxidation/ROS accumulation. These molecular events were accompanied by ferroptosis-associated organelle remodeling, including mitochondrial shrinkage, ER network simplification, and lysosomal enlargement. Importantly, ferroptotic injury also generated ICD-associated signals, including calreticulin exposure and HMGB1 release. Therefore, ER-associated ferroptosis functions not only as a tumor-killing mechanism but also as an immunogenic conversion process that transforms tumor damage into signals capable of initiating antitumor immunity.

A distinct mechanism was observed in macrophages. Rather than inducing ferroptotic cytotoxicity, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX engaged the ER and activated the STING-TBK1-IRF3/NF-κB signaling cascade, leading to M2-to-M1-like repolarization and increased inflammatory mediator production. This distinction is mechanistically important: tumor cells were eliminated through ferroptotic damage, whereas macrophages were reprogrammed through innate immune signaling. CpG ODN further strengthened immunostimulation, while SPION-derived iron contributed to oxidative stress and ferroptosis induction, allowing the nanogel to integrate chemotherapy, ferroptosis, and immune remodeling within one platform. This differential regulation of tumor cells and immune cells represents a key advantage of the nanogel design, in which tumor cells undergo selective ferroptotic elimination while macrophages are functionally redirected toward sustained antitumor immunity.

The in vivo results further demonstrated that this dual-mode mechanism translated into coordinated antitumor immunity. SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX enhanced tumor accumulation under magnetic guidance, suppressed primary tumor growth, reduced metastasis, and reshaped the tumor immune microenvironment. Transcriptomic and flow cytometric analyses showed activation of ferroptosis- and immune-related pathways, TAM repolarization, DC maturation, T cell activation, and intratumoral effector T cell infiltration. These immune changes further supported durable protection against postoperative recurrence, distant tumor rechallenge, and lung metastasis, accompanied by increased effector memory T cells and favorable biosafety.

Overall, this work identifies ER-enriched ferroptosis as a mechanistically actionable strategy for converting organelle-directed tumor stress into systemic antitumor immunity. The conceptual advance of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX lies in shifting nanomedicine design from conventional drug delivery toward organelle-regulating therapy, where intracellular spatial control is exploited to synchronize tumor destruction and immune remodeling. More broadly, this study provides a rational framework for designing organelle-targeted nanomedicines that overcome immunosuppressive barriers in aggressive and metastatic tumors.

In summary, we developed an ER-enriched multifunctional nanogel, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX, for TNBC chemoimmunotherapy. The nanogel accumulated in tumors under magnetic guidance and engaged the ER to induce ER stress, GSH-GPX4 disruption, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptotic ICD in tumor cells. In macrophages, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX activated the STING-TBK1-IRF3/NF-κB axis without causing obvious ferroptotic cytotoxicity, thereby driving inflammatory repolarization of the tumor microenvironment. This dual-mode mechanism promoted DC maturation, T cell activation, cytotoxic T cell infiltration, and systemic immune memory, resulting in effective suppression of primary tumor growth, postoperative recurrence, distant tumor rechallenges, and lung metastasis. These findings establish ER-enriched ferroptosis as a promising therapeutic entry point and provide an organelle-directed nanomedicine strategy for durable TNBC immunotherapy. Beyond TNBC therapy, this work highlights the broader potential of engineering nanomaterials with programmable intracellular trafficking properties to regulate organelle-associated biological processes. Future development of organelle-interactive nanoplatforms with tunable localization behaviors may provide new opportunities for precisely manipulating cell fate and immune responses in diverse cancer types and immune-related diseases.

Materials and methods

Cells culture

4T1 cells (murine breast cancer cells, American Type Culture Collection, ATCC), were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium containing 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin solution. MCF-7 cells, HepG2 cells and RAW264.7 macrophages (ATCC) were cultured in DMEM (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin solution. All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Animal use and care

BALB/c female mice (6-8 weeks, 16-20 g) were obtained from Beijing Vitalstar Biotechnology Co.,Ltd. The animals were maintained in accordance with the guidelines of Laboratory Animal Care, and the research protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Sichuan Academy of Medical Sciences & Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital (SYXK(川) 2023-0277).

Cytotoxicity in vitro

To explore the cytotoxicity of nanogel, a CCK8 assay was used to analyze the cytotoxicity of the nanogel. 4T1 cells, RAW264.7 cells, MCF-7 cells, HepG2 cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 6000 cells. After culturing for 24 h, the cells were incubated with different nanoparticles at various concentrations. After in the dark for 24 h, washed each well with PBS for 3 times. CCK8 (10%) solution was added to each well and incubated for about 2 h, and measured by multifunctional microplate reader at 450 nm. The relative cell viability was calculated as the following equation: Cell Viability (%) = (ODsamples-ODblank)/(ODcontrol-ODblank) × 100%. For the ability of nanogel to reverse the polarity of Macrophages, the RAW264.7 macrophages were induced with the 200 ng/mL of LPS and 10 ng/mL of IFN-γ for 24 h to obtain M1 macrophages, and 20 ng/mL of IL-4 for 24 h to obtain M2 macrophages. RAW264.7 cells (types M0, M1, or M2) were seeded at a density of 104 in 6-well plate, and SPIONS@P-CpG nanoparticles were added in medium and co-cultured for 6 h. Then the cells were collected and added to the upper 8-sized transwell chamber and coincubation with 4T1 cells in lower chamber. After 24 h, the lower cells were collected for the analysis of cytotoxicity.

Nanogel-induced ferroptosis HIS-SIM super-resolution imaging

HeLa cells were cultured in 8-well glass-bottom dishes and treated with varying concentrations of nanogel (0, 150, and 300 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, live-cell fluorescent staining was performed using ER-Tracker Green (1 μM), MitoTracker Deep Red (50 nM), and LysoTracker Red DND-99 (50 nM) for 30 min to label the ER, mitochondria, and lysosomes, respectively. Super-resolution imaging was then conducted using a commercial HIS-SIM system (Super Century Co.) equipped with a 100× oil immersion objective to capture nanoscale morphological changes in these organelles. The acquired super-resolution images were processed and analyzed with ImageJ software, where parameters including mitochondrial size, perimeter, solidity, ER average branch length, as well as lysosomal size and perimeter were quantified to statistically evaluate the concentration-dependent organelle damage during nanogel-induced ferroptosis.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), NO measurements

RAW264.7 cells were seeded on 6 well plates at a density of 100000 cells. 24 h later, different nanoparticles were added in medium and cultured for 6 h and 24 h in the dark. Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit and NO Assay Kit were employed the production of H2O2 and NO.

The generation of O2in vitro

The probe of [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 was used to detect the generation of O2, the fluorescence can be strongly weakened by O2 at 514 nm. RAW264.7 cells were transferred on 20 mm confocal cell dishes or 6 well plates at a density of 100000 cells. RAW264.7 cells were incubated with [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 for 6 h at a concentration of 5 μM and washed with PBS for three times. Different groups of nanoparticles were added in the culture medium and coincubation for 24 h, respectively. The cells were washed with PBS and imaged with the laser scanning confocal fluorescence microscope at the excitation of 514 nm.

TNF-α analysis

RAW264.7 cells were seeded on 6 well plates at a density of 100000 cells for 24 h, different nanoparticles were added in the culture medium, respectively. After culturing for 24 h in the dark, TNF-α ELISA Kits were employed the production of TNF-α.

Preparation of BMDMs and BMDCs

The female BALB/c mice were sacrificed, and collected the femurs and tibiae, and removed the muscle attachments. The intact bones were sterilized in PBS containing 10% penicillin-streptomycin for 10 min and washed three times. Both bone joint heads were cut off, and flushed the bone cavities with DMEM medium. After the cells were collected and centrifugated at 400 g for 5 min, the red blood cells (RBCs) were processed with lysis buffer. Then the cells were collected and cultured in DMEM medium containing 20% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were obtained with the DMEM medium containing M-CSF (20 ng/mL) for 1 week. For M1 macrophages (from BMDMs), the medium was replaced with the medium containing M-CSF (20 ng/mL), LPS (20 ng/mL) and IFN-γ (10 ng/mL) incubated for 1 weeks. For M2 macrophages (from BMDMs), the medium was replaced with the medium containing M-CSF (20 ng/mL) and IL-4 (20 ng/mL) incubated for 1 weeks. The adherent cells were cultured in the conditioned DMEM medium (containing 20% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 20 ng/mL of GM-CSF, and 20 ng/mL of IL-4) for 1 week to obtain the bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs).

Analysis of macrophage polarization in vitro

For the repolarization of macrophages, M2 macrophages, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages (from RAW264.7 cells) were seeded in 6-well plate at a density of 1 × 105, different groups of nanoparticles (300 μg/mL) were added in medium and co-cultured for 24 h. Then the cells were collected and stained with anti-CD86-PE for 30 min at 4 °C, and subjected to flow cytometry analysis. Moreover, 1 × 105 M0, M1 or M2 (from BMDMs) were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Then the medium was replaced with complete medium mixed with different groups of nanoparticles (300 μg/mL) and incubated for another 24 h, the cells were stained with anti-CD86-PE and anti-CD206-Alexa 647 for 30 min at 4 °C and analyzed with flow cytometry. In addition, the expression of iNOS, CD206 and Arg1 were measured using Western Blot. To evaluate the changes in signaling pathways in facilitating macrophage repolarization, the total protein in different groups were extracted, and the expression of p65, p-p65, cGAS, STING, p-STING, TBK1, p-TBK1, IRF3, p-IRF3, IκBα, and p-IκBα was measured. To investigate the regulatory mechanism of nanogel on macrophage polarization, inhibitors targeting STING pathways (H151) and NF-κB pathways (TPCA-1) were utilized. Specifically, 1 × 105 M0, M1 or M2 (from RAW264.7) were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Then the medium was replaced with complete medium containing 300 μg/mL nanogel with/without H151 (2 μM) or TPCA-1 (1 μM) and incubated for another 24 h. Then, the total protein was extracted, and Western blot was used to measure the expression of markers.

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to evaluate ferroptosis-associated ICD markers, including CRT exposure and HMGB1 release. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were acquired using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

In vivo tumor targeting

Female BALB/c mice at 6-8 weeks were used to construct tumor model. The 4T1 cells (1 × 106) were dispersed in 60 μL of PBS and subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse. When the tumors reached a volume of ∼80 mm3, the mice (n = 3) were divided into 3 groups, PBS, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX and SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX plus magnet. The groups of SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX and SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX plus magnet were intravenously injected the nanoparticles solution with/without the magnets on tumor sites for 2 h. After 24 h, the animal imaging system (the Lumina XR) was used to analyze the nanoparticles distribution. Additionally, the tumor tissues and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) of the mice were collected and analyzed by the animal imaging system.

In vivo antitumor treatment

Female BALB/c mice at 6-8 weeks were injected subcutaneously with 1 × 106 4T1 cells per mouse on the back of the right flank. When the initial tumor sizes were 80 mm3 (on average), the 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were randomized into 7 groups (n = 8), PBS, SPIONS@P plus magnet, Free DOX, CpG ODNs, SPIONS@P-CpG plus magnet, SPIONS@P-DOX plus magnet, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX plus magnet, respectively. Mice were intravenously administered at a SPIONS@P dose of 5.0 mg kg−1, a CpG ODNs dose of 0.1 mg kg−1 and a DOX dose of 2 mg kg−1 every other day for a total of 4 injections. Magnets were placed above the tumor tissues for 2 h post injection. The tumor volumes and body weights were recorded every two days during 21 days since the first injection. The tumor volume was calculated as follows: volume (mm3) = length × width2/2. The relative body weights were corresponding changes compared with that at the first day. On 14th and 21st day, three mice from each group were sacrificed, and tumor tissues, spleens and lymph nodes were collected for flow cytometry analysis as well as TEM detection, immunohistology (Ki67) and immunofluorescence (GPX4, TUNEL) staining. Simultaneously, the main organs and tumor tissues of mice were harvested for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. Furthermore, total RNA was extracted from tumor tissues in group PBS and SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX plus magnet for transcriptome sequencing. qPCR was subsequently performed to verify the expression changes of relevant genes. The qPCR primer sequences are shown in Table S1.

Inhibition of tumor recurrence and distant effects

Female BALB/c mice at 6-8 weeks were injected subcutaneously with 1 × 106 4T1 cells and randomly divided into 7 groups and intravenously administered different treatments for 4 times. After 21 days treatments, ∼90% of the primary tumors were excised and recorded as the tumor recurrence model, and injected subcutaneously with 5 × 105 4T1 cells on the back of another flank as the distant tumor model. The tumor volumes also were recorded every two days over 21 days. The tumor tissues, spleens and lymph nodes were collected for flow cytometry analysis.

Flow cytometry assay of cells in vivo

To investigate the immunomodulation of the nanoplatform, the removed tumor tissues, spleens and lymph nodes were collected and digested to prepare single-cell suspensions for the flow cytometry. Briefly, the suspensions of tumor tissues, spleens and lymph nodes were stained with anti-CD3e-FITC, anti-CD8a-PE, and anti-CD4-APC antibodies for the T cell activation in different treatments and time points. The cells of tumor tissues were labeled with anti-CD45-APC-Cy7, anti-CD11b-FITC, anti-F4/80-BV421™, anti-CD86-PE, and anti-CD206-Alexa 647 antibodies to analyze the repolarization of M2 macrophages to M1 macrophages. The suspensions of lymph nodes were also stained with anti-CD11c-Violet 421™, anti-MHC I-APC, anti-MHC II-FITC, and anti-CD86-PE to determine the DC maturation and antigen presentation. For memory T cell assay, the suspensions of spleens were stained with anti-CD3e-FITC, anti-CD8a-PE, anti-CD62L-PerCP-Cy5.5, and anti-CD44-BV421™ antibodies to evaluate the memory T (TEM) cells using a flow cytometry assay.

Inhibition of tumor metastasis

Female BALB/c mice (6-8 weeks) were intravenously injected with 5 × 105 4T1-Luc cells per mouse. 2 days later, the mice were analyzed by the animal imaging system (the Lumina XR) to evaluate the successful establishment of the tumor model. The were randomized into 7 groups (n = 6), PBS, SPIONS@P plus magnet, Free DOX, CpG ODNs, SPIONS@P-CpG plus magnet, SPIONS@P-DOX plus magnet, SPIONS@P-CpG-DOX plus magnet, respectively. Mice were intravenously administered with different treatments every other day for a total of 4 injections. The mice were determined by the animal imaging system every 6 days. Simultaneously, the lung tissues of mice were harvested for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining.

Safety evaluation

To evaluate the safety of different nano-formulations in vivo, body weight changes were recorded. The hearts, livers, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were stained with H&E. For the detection of serum biochemical indicators, mice were injected with PBS, free DOX, and nanogel respectively. Seven days of post-injection, serum samples were harvested and analyzed using an automatic animal biochemical analyzer.

Statistics

All data were expressed as the mean value ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was determined using unpaired Student's t-test (two-tailed) with *p < 0.05; *p < 0.05; ***p < 0.01. All data are represented as means ± SDs. Statistical analyses were performed using Student's t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). “#” represents the intragroup comparison with “Ctr,” and “*” indicates the intergroup comparison. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the data were marked with (*) or (#) for P < 0.05, (**) or (##) for P < 0.01, and (***) or (###) for P < 0.001.
