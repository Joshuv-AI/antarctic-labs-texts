Prevention and management of pulmonary vascular complications in anatomic lung resections: a narrative review
Authors: Ryan Cassidy, William Robinson, Grant Dong, Lana Schumacher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivePulmonary artery (PA) injury during anatomic lung resection remains a rare but potentially catastrophic complication. With the increasing adoption of robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS), the absence of tactile feedback and the growing use of neoadjuvant therapies introduce unique challenges. This review aims to summarize the epidemiology, risk factors, prevention strategies, and intraoperative management of vascular injuries in the era of minimally invasive thoracic surgery.MethodsA narrative review of the literature was performed, with inclusion of English-language studies between 2005 and 2026 focusing on studies evaluating intraoperative vascular complications during video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and RATS lung resections. Emphasis was placed on reported incidence, risk factors, perioperative outcomes, and technical strategies for prevention and management. Exclusion criteria included preprints and studies without mention of vascular injury or anatomic lung resection.Key Content and FindingsThe incidence of PA injury during minimally invasive lung resection remains low, with reported rates ranging from 1.5–2.9% for VATS and 0.5–2.6% for RATS. Identified risk factors include upper lobe resections, left-sided tumors, bulky or calcified lymphadenopathy, prior thoracic surgery, pleural adhesions, advanced tumor stage, squamous histology, and use of neoadjuvant therapy. Preventative strategies include detailed preoperative planning with three-dimensional computed tomography and careful dissection informed by bronchovascular anatomy. Intraoperative management is multifactorial and relies on adherence to the “four P’s”: poise, pressure, preparedness, and proximal control. While many injuries can be managed minimally invasively, prompt conversion to thoracotomy remains critical when hemostasis cannot be achieved safely.ConclusionsPA injuries during lung resection require a high level of surgical awareness, preparation, and technical proficiency. As the use of RATS and induction therapies continues to expand, structured strategies for prevention and management are essential to maintain patient safety and optimize outcomes.Keywords: Thoracic surgery, robotic surgery, narrative review

Introduction

Lung cancer is the number one cause of cancer mortality globally (1). Though advances in medical therapies for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) have been made over the last two decades, surgery remains the preferred modality to treat early-stage NSCLC. Historically, the surgical standard for the management of NSCLC has evolved. Robotic-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (RATS) has become increasingly utilized relative to video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) in performing both lobar and sub-lobar lung cancer resections (2). Recent studies have shown non-inferiority between anatomic segmentectomy and lobectomy in terms of overall survival in early-stage NSCLC (3). Perioperative and neoadjuvant strategies such as immunotherapy and chemotherapy are now increasingly used in those with locoregionally advanced NSCLC (4).

Avoiding vascular injuries during lung resection is a tenet of safe thoracic surgery. With the increasing use of robotic platforms come several advantages. These include three-dimensional (3D) visualization, 10× magnification, higher resolution optics, and wristed instruments. However, RATS lacks the tactile feedback seen in VATS and open surgical techniques. Though with recent advances in the robotic platform (such as the Davinci 5) there has been an improvement to this end. One preclinical study examining force-feedback technology on the updated Davinci 5 platform found a decrease of up to 55% in the maximal force exerted when using high sensitivity force-feedback. This may help surgeons avoid force-related injuries on the robotic platform (5). With the increasing amount of lung resections taking place in a post-induction setting, the advantages of minimally invasive approaches must contend with these obscured operative fields as to avoid injuring vascular structures while maintaining oncologic adequacy.

Minimally invasive techniques are not immune from operative complications. Pulmonary artery (PA) injuries remain amongst the most feared intraoperative complications in any form of thoracic surgery. Preventing and managing PA injuries requires experience, multifaceted preparation by the surgical team, and a coordinated team-oriented approach in the face of such disastrous situations. This is especially important with minimally invasive techniques as prompt control of bleeding may be more difficult and require emergent conversion to open thoracotomy.

We performed a comprehensive review of the literature on the management of vascular complications during anatomic lung resection, with an emphasis on PA injury and in the context of minimally invasive thoracic surgery. This narrative review is meant to illustrate the current strategies to prevent and manage vascular injuries in lung resection cases. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1244/rc).

Methods

A narrative review of the literature was conducted to evaluate the incidence, risk factors, prevention strategies, and intraoperative management of PA injuries during lung resection. A comprehensive search of the PubMed/MEDLINE database was performed using the following prompt: (PA injury OR Pulmonary Artery Injury) AND (Segmentectomy OR Wedge Resection OR Lung Resection OR Pneumonectomy OR VATS OR RATS).

Studies were selected based on relevance to intraoperative vascular injury in the setting of thoracic surgery, with particular emphasis on minimally invasive approaches, including VATS and RATS. Additional articles were identified through review of references from selected studies. Both retrospective and prospective studies, as well as relevant review articles, were included.

Articles were evaluated for reported incidence of PA injury, associated risk factors, perioperative outcomes, and described techniques for prevention and intraoperative management. Each study was screened through its abstract, results, and conclusion to assess relevancy, and only those specific to our topic were included. Epidemiology of PA injuries and risk factors

Both VATS and RATS are associated with low rates of conversion to thoracotomy for intraoperative complications (6). A recent study of the Society for Thoracic Surgeons (STS) General Thoracic Surgery Database found a significant difference in conversion rates between VATS and RATS (11% vs. 6%, P<0.001) (7). Studies examining outcomes between VATS and RATS lobectomies have generated mixed results but seem to suggest benefits in length of stay (LOS), blood loss, and lymphadenectomy (8). The rate of PA injury during minimally invasive lung resections is low. There are no studies that demonstrate a difference between VATS and RATS with respect to PA injuries, with rates reported between 1.5–2.9% and 0.5–2.6% for VATS and RATS respectively (6,9).

The PORTaL study, which aimed to investigate survival differences between RATS, VATS and open lobectomy, found a difference in conversion rate between VATS and RATS lobectomy with a 10% and 4% conversion rate respectively in their sample. This study excluded patients who received neoadjuvant treatment, however. In a second study, using the same data set, the patients who received neoadjuvant treatments were included to examine risk factors and outcomes related to conversion in minimally invasive lobectomy. This again demonstrated a significantly different conversion rate between VATS and RATS (12.9% vs. 3.6%, P<0.001). Clinical stage was an independent predictor of conversion as was prior cardiac surgery, neoadjuvant therapy, and tumor size. The conversion rate for RATS cases decreased over time during their study; this may reflect advantages gained by surgeon experience. Anatomical reasons were the most common reason for conversion in this study. Conversion for vascular reasons (such as PA injury) occurred more frequently in the RATS group. However, the authors do note that most of these conversions occurred after adequate hemostasis had been achieved and without hemodynamic instability (10,11).

PA injuries, even if dealt with promptly, can severely alter patient outcomes after lung cancer operations. Though data specific to PA injuries is somewhat limited, one study examining intraoperative blood loss in thoracic surgery cases demonstrated a longer LOS and higher complication rate in patients who had higher than usual intraoperative bleeding. It is important to note that 72% of patients classified into this group were identified as having the PA as the source of their intraoperative bleeding (12).

There are several factors associated with increased rates of intraoperative PA injuries. Anatomically speaking, studies have shown a stronger association with conversion in cases involving left sided lesions and lesions of the upper lobes (6,7). Several factors contribute to these findings. First, there is a propensity for NSCLC to occur in the upper lobes, increasing the volume of upper lobectomies relative to lower lobectomies (9). From a surgical perspective, the large right upper lobe truncus branch and highly variable left upper lobe arterial anatomy may make injury more likely (6). In a study by Chen et al., oncologic factors such as T stage and squamous cell histology have been shown to be independent risk factors for intraoperative bleeding (12). This study also found that a history of thoracic surgery and presence of pleural adhesions were both associated with increased risk of significant intraoperative bleeding. Another study found that bulky and/or calcified lymphadenopathy was a major contributor to PA injuries. This same study also identified neoadjuvant therapy as a potential risk factor for vascular injury (13).

Preventing vascular injuries

With increasing frequency of sublobar resections being performed, knowledge and understanding of the pulmonary bronchovascular anatomy is of the utmost importance in performing safe lung resections. The use of three-dimensional computed tomography (3D CT) has afforded a better understanding of spatial lung anatomy for surgeons preparing for anatomic resections. 3D CT has been shown to allow excellent preoperative identification of surgically relevant PA branches (14). This facilitates safe dissection of bronchovascular structures and affords surgeons more confidence in their understanding of the anatomy. To this end, some studies have shown improved perioperative outcomes in cases where 3D CT was available, these include decreased intraoperative blood loss (15).

With increasing numbers of post-induction lung resections taking place, there may be patients deemed a high risk for intraoperative vascular injuries. Liang and colleagues reported increased VATS case complexity after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy including greater tissue edema, increased vascular fragility, tighter visceral pleura, and challenging lymph node dissection (16). In the NEOSTAR trial, a subjective scale of surgical complexity found that 40% of operations after neoadjuvant immunotherapy were considered more difficult than similar resection without previous treatment. While 71% of studies in this cohort were initiated as open thoracotomies, this increased subjective difficulty likely persists regardless of approach. Both studies, however, did not find objective increases in post-operative complications (17).

In some cases, especially in patients with pulmonary hypertension, proximal tumors and bulky hilar adenopathy involving the PA, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) can be a useful adjunct. There is very little available data on the use of CPB in complex lung resection (18,19). However, studies have demonstrated that the use of CPB in lung resections for NSCLC can be safe (20,21). We have reported the use of peripheral CPB to facilitate safe, robotic resection of a large left upper lobe squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) in a patient with pulmonary hypertension and a history of Mantle field radiation (22). As demonstrated in Figure 2, this allowed adequate decompression of an engorged left main PA and facilitated complete vascular control of the left lung during dissection.

Ultimately, experience of the operating surgeon is another factor to consider when examining the incidence, and management, of PA injuries. Decaluwe et al., in their study examining intraoperative complications during lung resections as they relate to surgeon experience, found a significant correlation between conversion to thoracotomy for non-oncologic reasons (either technical issues or intraoperative complications) and surgeon experience (23). Interestingly, this study did not find a statistically significant relationship between surgeon experience and vascular injuries. Conversely, in a study examining the incidence and management of intraoperative catastrophes during robotic lung resections, Cao et al. found an association between surgeon experience and rate of catastrophic events. It is important to note, that injury to the PA or pulmonary vein was the most common event measured during this study and encompassed 80% of all included events (13). Tchouta et al. demonstrate better LOS and mortality outcomes in patients undergoing robotic lobectomy high volume centers (24).

While both VATS and RATS are commonly used, each offers advantages and disadvantages in the context of intraoperative vascular injury. RATS offers superior stereoscopic visualization as well as better angles to access and repair injuries due to increased articulation of instruments. These benefits may help explain the lower conversion rates seen in RATS compared to VATS after PA injury.

Although rates of conversion have been shown to be lower in RATS compared to VATS, emergency conversion due to vascular injury is more likely. Thus, there are concerns of potential delays in conversion as the surgeon is away from the surgical field. Servais et al. found that while RATS required greater volume intraoperative transfusion during conversion, there was no difference in perioperative mortality or major complications (7).

Management of pulmonary vascular injuries

When encountered with a vascular injury or bleeding from the PA during a lung resection, especially on minimally invasive platforms, it is of the utmost importance for the surgeon to remain calm and remember foundational techniques for achieving vascular control. The PA is somewhat unique in that, when injured, there can be high volume rapid blood loss (6). As is the case across almost all specialties, the reaction of the surgeon will dictate the demeanor of the entire surgical team in the face of major intraoperative bleeding. Cerfolio et al. advocate for “the four P’s” to approach bleeding from the PA which are poise, pressure, preparedness, and proximal control (25). Temporally speaking, the first of the “P’s” is preparedness. As a surgeon, identifying potentially difficult cases is paramount. At our institution, we have a process for complex case booking. This includes preparing an open vascular tray in the operating room (OR), having our extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)/CPB team on standby, and having blood in the room which is not our practice for routine cases. Further, it is critically important that each member of the operative team be well practiced in their response to intraoperative disasters. Education and simulation are at the core of having a prepared operative team, and multiple studies have shown higher team performance after simulation-based training has been implemented. Logistically, the technical aspect of safely converting to an open thoracotomy in the face of major bleeding is not a straightforward endeavor. Watkins et al. have published their stepwise approach to prompt control of bleeding on the robotic platform and simultaneous preparation for conversion if needed (26). Their technique involves a limited, anterior thoracotomy by the bedside assistant as an access site for a sponge stick. Once this incision is made, pressure can be transferred to the bedside assistant while the robot is undocked. When preparing to convert, it is helpful to insert a video assisted camera through one of the anterior 8 mm robotic port sites to better visualize the injury and ensure adequate pressure is held during a controlled, muscle sparing thoracotomy (26). Once pressure is transferred to the bedside assistant and visualization achieved, the surgeon can scrub in as the robot no longer has control of the bleeding. At this point, the thoracotomy can be extended, and the robot can be safely backed away. This approach was developed through an iterative process simulated over years and underscores the importance of preparedness when a PA injury occurs that cannot be repaired minimally invasively. Ultimately, the decision to convert is individualized and relies on the stability of the patient, experience of the OR team, and the comfort level of the surgeon when it comes to dealing with bleeding on the robotic platform.

Studies by Bludevich et al. have also emphasized the importance of team-based simulation to optimize team performance in an emergency (27,28). One study examined team performance before and after simulated scenarios of intraoperative bleeding in robotic thoracic cases. In this study, the number of critical misses was improved (2.55 to 0.25 per scenario) after high fidelity simulations were employed (27). In a previous study, this group used high fidelity simulation of an intraoperative PA injury over six total operative teams to analyze performance. In total, 28 latent safety threats (LSTs) were identified with nine LSTs being repeated throughout simulations (28). To this end, a Delphi consensus study including 21 expert thoracic surgeons found that emergency undocking training and simulation were almost unanimously recommended in managing situations requiring emergency conversion to an open thoracotomy (29).

Poise is the critical first step to consider when encountered with bleeding from the PA. As already mentioned, this projects a sense of calmness to the OR staff and anesthesia team that will allow optimal team performance. It is worth mentioning here, especially considering the previously mentioned advantages of RATS, that there are inherent challenges posed by this platform. With the surgeon located away from the patient at the robotic console, there is potential for error especially if these scenarios have not been simulated or encountered by the team in the past. This owes primarily to potential communication issues with the bedside assistant, adequate bedside assistant response, and the added logistics of undocking the robot in a controlled fashion (26). Simultaneous with showing poise is the application of direct pressure over the area of bleeding. It is important to note that the PA is a low-pressure system, and gentle pressure will usually suffice to achieve hemostasis. Holding excessive pressure may only serve to propagate the vascular injury. Based on available literature and the preference of the senior author, direct pressure using a rolled sponge is most optimal to control PA bleeding in this instance (6,13,25). The lung parenchyma itself can be used to apply pressure. However, there is no guarantee that the lung has been dissected enough prior to encountering major bleeding (25). Topical hemostatic agents have been described as an adjunct to direct pressure, or as a temporizing measure while preparing to convert to a thoracotomy (6).

When a PA injury occurs, even when the first three P’s are adhered to, bleeding may not stop with direct pressure and may require repair. This necessitates the fourth P, proximal control. Depending on the location of the injury this may be achieved with a vascular stapler, especially if the injury is distal from the main PA and involves only a branch. However, it is often necessary to gain more proximal control of the PA. This can be done intrapericardially or extrapericardially using a doubly wrapped vessel loop or an atraumatic vascular clamp (Figure 4). Preferably, gaining proximal control with a robotic bulldog clamp prior to starting a difficult dissection can help avoid undue blood loss if an injury does occur. It is often not necessary to get distal venous control in these cases (6). With proximal control achieved the injury should be able to be safely exposed to start a repair. This is most often done with a nonabsorbable, monofilament suture. It is important to note that this is all feasible to complete on the robotic platform but conversion to open thoracotomy should not be delayed if hemostasis is not achieved on a minimally invasive platform in a timely manner (13,25). As outlined by Cerfolio et al., conversion to a thoracotomy, especially while bleeding is controlled with manual pressure, is a multifactorial decision. Factors to consider include location and extent of injury, surgeon comfort level on the robotic platform, level of difficulty of the remaining portion of the case, and the hemodynamics of the patient (25).

Conclusions

PA injury during minimally invasive lung resection, while uncommon, remains one of the most feared intraoperative complications in thoracic surgery. The continued expansion of robotic-assisted approaches and the increasing use of neoadjuvant therapies introduce additional technical complexity and may heighten the risk of vascular injury. A thorough understanding of pulmonary vascular anatomy, meticulous preoperative planning—including the use of three-dimensional imaging—and careful intraoperative techniques are essential to minimizing this risk. When encountered with a difficult hilum, such is the case with some re-operative cases or those with central tumors, it is important to achieve central vascular control before proceeding with dissection.

When PA injury does occur, successful management depends on maintaining composure and adhering to fundamental principles of vascular control. The “four P’s”—poise, pressure, preparedness, and proximal control—provide a practical and effective framework for managing these high-stakes situations. Although many injuries can be addressed using minimally invasive techniques, surgeons must maintain a low threshold for conversion to thoracotomy to ensure timely hemorrhage control and patient safety.

Ultimately, prevention and management of PA injuries require not only technical proficiency but also coordinated team dynamics and institutional preparedness. As minimally invasive thoracic surgery continues to evolve, ongoing emphasis on training, simulation, and standardized approaches to intraoperative crises will be critical to optimizing outcomes in this challenging clinical scenario.
