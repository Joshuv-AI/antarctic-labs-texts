Embedding Perturbation may Better Reflect the Uncertainty in LLM Reasoning
Qihao Wen, Jiahao Wang, Yang Nan, Pengfei He, Ravi Tandon, Han Xu

Abstract
Large language Models (LLMs) have achieved significant breakthroughs across diverse domains; however, they can still produce unreliable or misleading outputs. For responsible LLM application, Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) techniques are used to estimate a model’s uncertainty about its outputs, indicating the likelihood that those outputs may be problematic. For LLM reasoning tasks, it is essential to estimate the uncertainty not only for the final answer, but also for the intermediate steps of the reasoning, as this can enable more fine-grained and targeted interventions. In this study, we explore what UQ metrics better reflect the LLM’s “intermediate uncertainty” during reasoning. Our study reveals that an LLMs’ incorrect reasoning steps tend to contain tokens which are highly sensitive to the perturbations on the preceding token embeddings. In this way, incorrect (uncertain) intermediate steps can be readily identified using this sensitivity score as guidance in practice. In our experiments, we show such perturbation-based metric achieves stronger uncertainty quantification performance compared with baseline methods such as token (generation) probability and token entropy. Besides, different from approaches that rely on multiple sampling, the perturbation-based metrics offer better simplicity and efficiency.

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable performance across a wide range of applications, including natural language understanding
(
2
;
4
)
, reasoning
(
29
;
28
)
, and decision-making
(
3
)
. For the responsible deployment of LLMs, accurate uncertainty quantifying (UQ) metrics
(
14
;
12
;
24
)
are crucial, as they enable users to assess whether a model’s prediction can be trusted: low uncertainty indicates a confident response, whereas high uncertainty suggests that the output requires further scrutiny or should be rejected. For LLM reasoning tasks, it is important to measure not only the uncertainty of the final outcome, but also the uncertainty in each intermediate reasoning step. Such information can provide deep insights into when and how uncertainty arises during reasoning and further enhance fine-grained and targeted interventions, such as the reflection-based
(
33
)
or self-correction mechanisms
(
15
)
in reasoning specific models. In our paper, we refer this form of uncertainty as “intermediate uncertainty” in LLM reasoning.

To measure this intermediate uncertainty, existing UQ methods for LLMs are inadequate, as they are often highly complex or struggle to capture the long-term context dependency for auditing reasoning errors. For example, multiple sampling-based approaches
(
28
;
14
;
34
;
11
)
are commonly used to assess the consistency across mutiple generated responses by checking whether they produce the same final answer.
However, intermediate uncertainty estimation may require such comparison for each individual reasoning step, leading to
a large number of sampling trials and additional auxiliary LLM judgments
(
14
)
.
Besides, alternative methods
(
24
;
16
)
focus on the internal statistics of LLMs, such as token probabilities, entropy, and their variants
(
7
)
. These approaches typically assume that tokens with lower generation probabilities indicate higher model uncertainty. However, they can be strongly influenced by word frequency in natural language
(
7
)
, which limits their ability to reliably reflect the correctness of the underlying reasoning process.
Moreover, recent studies
(
17
)
demonstrate that such methods also have limited capacity to capture long-range contextual dependencies, making them less effective at detecting problematic steps that arise from incorrect deductions based on earlier context.

In this work, we develop new metrics for intermediate uncertainty quantification that better capture such contextual dependencies. Specifically, we investigate the internal dynamics of LLMs during the reasoning process, with a particular focus on measuring the sensitivity of each token to small perturbations in its preceding context. Given a reasoning problem, we first obtain the complete response generated by the LLM. For each token, we then introduce a minor perturbation to the embeddings of its preceding tokens to examine whether the generation probability of the target token changes significantly. As illustrated in Figure
1
, tokens that exhibit a higher sensitivity to such perturbations (e.g., the token “4”) may indicate a substantial hesitation on the estimated probability for that token, therefore implying potential errors. In other words, this metric measures how strongly a token is supported by its preceding reasoning context, rather than how frequent it is in natural language. Notably, since LLMs operate in an auto-regressive manner, the generation of each token depends solely on its preceding tokens and is not affected by subsequent tokens. Thus, this sensitivity can be efficiently measured by perturbing embeddings of all tokens in the entire response.

We conduct the main experiments on two representative reasoning tasks, for pure logic reasoning
(
25
)
, and math reasoning
(
5
;
9
)
. We validate the high efficacy of the proposed metrics for intermediate uncertainty, by showing they are good at flagging the steps where the LLM start to make mistakes. Compared to multiple sampling strategies
(
28
;
14
)
, the perturbation-based metrics demonstrate much better simplify and efficiency. We also point out potential limitations of the proposed metrics: (1) They may not exhibit strong UQ performance to detect LLM’s factual hallucination. This is because such errors often originate from the model’s internal knowledge rather than from contextual reasoning. Therefore, perturbations on preceding tokens may not effectively capture uncertainty related to factual correctness. (2) These metrics will have reduced effectiveness in tasks or models that exhibit high variability in word choice or reasoning pathway selection. For example, if an LLM can adopt multiple alternative but still correct pathways to solve a task, perturbation-based metrics may reflect uncertainty in pathway selection, rather than solely the uncertainty in correctness of the answer.

2
Related Works

2.1
Uncertainty Quantification of General ML

Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) has been extensively studied in general machine learning (ML), particularly in supervised learning settings such as classification and regression
(
13
;
22
)
. Existing literature typically distinguishes between two major types of uncertainty
(
13
)
: aleatoric uncertainty, which arises from inherent noise or ambiguity in the data, and epistemic uncertainty, which stems from limitations in the model itself due to insufficient knowledge. In this paper, we focus on uncertainty arising during the reasoning process of LLMs, which can be categorized as epistemic uncertainty. In practice, we often observe that LLMs exhibit internal failures, such as misusing previous conclusions, performing incorrect calculations, or making flawed logical deductions. These errors suggest that the observed uncertainty is primarily induced by the model’s reasoning process itself, rather than by ambiguity in the input data or task formulation.

2.2
Uncertainty Quantification for LLM Reasoning

LLM reasoning tasks pose additional challenges for uncertainty estimation.
For UQ under reasoning tasks, a major line of work assesses reasoning reliability through multiple sampling and aggregating them into a single uncertainty signal
(
28
;
31
;
30
;
1
)
. The core intuition is that the disagreement across multiple sampled answers can indicate the inconsistent reasoning paths, which implies greater uncertainty and a higher likelihood of reasoning failure. However, these works only focus on the uncertainties of the entire reasoning process, lacking a fine-grained analysis of intermediate steps. Besides, there are some other UQ methods targeting on fine-grained and process-aware reliability signals beyond a single confidence score on the final answer, which are more related to our focus
(
24
;
16
;
7
;
34
)
. Some of them
(
24
;
16
)
leverage internal statistics of LLMs such as token probabilities and entropy as the uncertainty signals. Some works
(
7
;
34
)
focus on factual hallucination detection, depending on cross-sample entailment consistency and local alternative-token semantics, which also leads to high inference costs and typically coarse-grained uncertainty estimates. However, recent work such as
(
17
)
finds that these methods are insufficient to capture long-term context, making them difficult to be applied for capturing procedural reasoning errors, such as incorrect intermediate inferences.

3
Perturbation-Based UQ Metrics

In this section, we formally discuss the proposed strategy for measuring intermediate uncertainty, which is to estimate:
how an LLM is uncertain about its generation throughout its reasoning process
. We first present the necessary definitions. Then, we introduce basic scores such as token probability and entropy, and discuss their insufficiency for achieving this purpose. Finally, we present the details of the proposed perturbation-based UQ metrics.

3.1
Preliminary and Definitions

We consider a given LLM and query it to perform a reasoning task. Given the input query
\mathbf{q}=(x_{1},...,x_{m})
, e.g., a math reasoning question with
m
tokens, the LLM generates a response with
n
tokens and we denote it as
\mathbf{r}=(x_{m+1},x_{m+2},...x_{m+n})
. For some of the metrics that are introduced later in this section, we will extract the embeddings of all tokens (including both input tokens and output tokens). It will result in
(m+n)
embedding vectors defined as
\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{h}_{1},...,\mathbf{h}_{m+n})
, where each
\mathbf{h}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
d
is the dimension of token embedding space. Based on these notations, we give the following definition of LLM’s calculation on the (generation) probability of each next token:

Definition 3.1

(
Token (Generation) Probability
)
.

Given the input tokens and existing generated tokens
(x_{1},...,x_{t-1})
and
t>m
, an autoregressive LLM calculates the probability of each potential next token
v
in vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
as:

\Pr(v\mid x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t-1})\text{\penalty\ \penalty\ for\penalty\ \penalty\ }v\in\mathcal{V}

For the actually sampled token
x_{t}
in the generated response, we denote the token probability of
x_{t}
as:

\mathrm{P}(x_{t})=\Pr(x_{t}\mid x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t-1})

(1)

In practice, it is also frequently formed in Negative Log Likelihood (NLL) as:
\mathrm{NLL}(x_{t})=-\log\mathrm{P}(x_{t})
. Besides, another related notion is token entropy. Instead of measuring the sampling probability, it measures the dispersion of the next-token’s probability distribution over the vocabulary.

Definition 3.2

(
Token Entropy
)
.

The entropy of the distribution when generating token
x_{t}\penalty\ (t>m)
is defined as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{Ent}(x_{t})=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\Big(&\Pr(v\mid x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t-1})\cdot\\
\log&\Pr(v\mid x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t-1})\Big)\end{split}

(2)

Intuitively, token probability indicates how certain the LLM model is about generating the token
x_{t}
, while the entropy indicates the possibility of choosing other tokens different from
x_{t}
. Thus, these metrics might possibly reflect the model’s confidence on the correctness of the generated token
x_{t}
during the reasoning process for estimating “intermediate uncertainty”. For example, one can identify the problematic or uncertain steps by inspecting the tokens with a low probability or high entropy. Notably, previous studies
(
19
)
also show these scores can provide non-trivial efficacy in mistake forecasting for some tasks such as doing selection or phrase-level question answering.

However, for reasoning tasks with open-form generation, these metrics can become less effective. This is because the reasoning process can involve words or n-gram phrases inherently having low probabilities in natural language.
For example, they can be transition words to start a new sentence. As illustrated in an example in Figure
1
, the starting word “we” is gven a lower probability (a higher NLL) than all other tokens. Similarly, we also frequently find the sign “$” to start a math equation receives lower probability. Yet, this does not suggest a potential error in reasoning. Moreover, these metrics like token probability and entropy may struggle to capture long-term dependencies between a given token and its preceding context
(
17
)
, making them insufficient for identifying potential errors that originate from incorrect deductions based on earlier contexts.

3.2
Perturbation-Based Token-level UQ Metrics

To alleviate the aforementioned challenge and better capture the reasoning uncertainty, we propose to examine each generated token’s stability under small perturbations on the embeddings of its preceding tokens. The intuition is that the tokens whose generation is weakly grounded in the preceding context are more likely to be disrupted by such perturbations. Moreover, for the tokens with inherent lower language frequency, their probability may remain low without a significant variance under perturbation. In the following, we introduce how to conduct these perturbations and how the resulting uncertainty is quantified.

Random Perturbation.
We consider applying random Gaussian noise
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=(\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{2},...,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{m+n})
, which are
(m+n)
i.i.d. small noises added onto all token embeddings, including the input query
\mathrm{q}
and the response
\mathrm{r}
. We can define the perturbed embeddings for each token as

\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i}=\mathbf{h}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m+n
.
Notably, since LLMs are autoregressive, the output probability for each generated token
x_{t}
only depends on its preceding tokens from
x_{1}
to
x_{t-1}
.
In this way, for each generated token
x_{t}
, we can define the new token probability after embedding perturbation as:

\tilde{\mathrm{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}(x_{t})=\Pr\!\left(x_{t}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{t-1}\right).

(3)

To measure the sensitivity of
x_{t}
to such random perturbation, we calculate the variance of its token probability under such perturbations by making samplings on
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
:

\mathrm{Pert.}(x_{t})=\mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\!\left[\tilde{\mathrm{P}}_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\!\left(x_{t}\right)\right].

(4)

Adversarial Perturbation.

Instead of making sampling and conducting LLM inference for multiple times, we also consider strategies based on adversarial perturbation
(
8
)
. In detail, we slightly modify the embeddings
\mathbf{H}
along the direction that decreases the total log-probability of the generated response, which can be achieved by subtracting the gradient:

\hat{\mathbf{H}}=\mathbf{H}-\alpha\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{H}}\Big(\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+n}\log\Pr(x_{i}\mid\mathbf{H})\Big),

(5)

or achieved by subtracting the signed gradient:

\hat{\mathbf{H}}=\mathbf{H}-\alpha\cdot\text{sgn}\nabla_{\mathbf{H}}\Big(\sum_{i=m+1}^{m+n}\log\Pr(x_{i}\mid\mathbf{H})\Big),

(6)

where
\alpha
is a small constant. Then, we aim to find the generated tokens that are tremendously disrupted by such a perturbation, via examining the metric:

\begin{split}\mathrm{Pert.}(x_{t})&=\log\Pr\left(x_{t}\mid\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\right)\\
&\quad-\log\Pr\left(x_{t}\mid\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{t-1}\right)\end{split}

(7)

which is to find the tokens having a significantly decreased probability after perturbation. In our paper, we name the perturbation without the sign operator as
l_{2}
-adversarial perturbation, and the one with the sign operator as
l_{\infty}
-adversarial perturbation, following the conventions in adversarial learning
(
8
;
18
)
.

4
Experiment

In this section, we conduct experiments to evaluate whether the proposed UQ metrics can effectively identify intermediate uncertainty in LLM reasoning. Specifically, we examine whether these metrics provide informative signals for locating erroneous reasoning steps, under pure logical reasoning (Section
4.1
) and mathematical reasoning (Section
4.2
), and then we provide additional analysis.

4.1
Find Uncertain Steps in Pure-logic Reasoning

Setups.

We focus on pure logical reasoning tasks from the BIG-Bench dataset
(
25
)
, including Logical Deduction (ranking objects by relative order), Tracking Shuffled Objects (tracking objects through permutations), and Web of Lies (identifying the liar from mixed truthful and false statements). We consider three representative open-source LLMs including Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
(
6
)
, Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct
(
32
)
and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3
(
10
)
. For the generality and simplicity of our evaluation, we fix the system prompt as “You are a helpful assistant.” Since it is a reasoning task, we set a low sampling temperature of all LLMs to be
T=0.2
. In Appendix
B.1
, we consider another temperature
T=0.5
, and we draw consistent conclusions.

In our evaluation, we compare the token-level UQ metrics based on internal signals, including the Neg Log-Likelihood (NLL), token entropy (Eq.
2
), token sensitivity to random perturbation (Eq.
4
) and token sensitivity to adversarial perturbation (Eq.
7
). For all these metrics, larger values indicate higher uncertainty. Besides, we also consider adapting multiple-sampling strategy
(
28
)
for this objective and we extend the details to Section
4.4
. For perturbation-based metrics, we globally set the default number of noise sampling and in Random Perturbation to be
r=20
, with standard deviation
\sigma=0.001
. For Adversarial Perturbation, we set the constant
\alpha=0.0001
. Ablation studies on these hyperparameters is provided in Section
5.2
.

Evaluation Pipeline.
To test the utility of these UQ metrics for intermediate uncertainty, we ask the question:
Can one identify the uncertain (problematic) intermediate steps following the guidance of these metrics?
To answer this question, we generate the complete model responses and concentrate our analysis to those with incorrect final answers.
For each incorrect response, we identify its earliest reasoning step that an error occurs, which is the most likely point where model uncertainty emerges
1
1

1

There could be multiple wrong steps in one response. However, later wrong steps can be confidently generated based on previous wrong steps, so we focus on the investigating the uncertainty when generating the first wrong step. To find the “first wrong step”, we query a much more advanced GPT-5.2 model
(
23
)
to read through the generated answers (more details are in Appendix
A
)
. Then, we assess whether tokens with high UQ scores overlap with this first wrong step.
Formally, we define the first wrong step
\mathbf{r}_{w}\subseteq\mathbf{r}
as a subsequence of the whole response
\mathbf{r}
. Given a token-level UQ metric
\mathrm{Q}(\cdot)
, we use
\mathrm{Q}^{(k)}
to denote the
k
-th largest value among all generated tokens. This UQ method is considered to successfully detect the first wrong step
if
at least one token from
\mathbf{r}_{w}
appears among the top-
k
tokens ranked by UQ score, i.e.,

\exists\,x_{t}\in\mathbf{r}_{w}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathrm{Q}(x_{t})\geq\mathrm{Q}^{(k)},

(8)

suggesting the metric can distinguish the wrong step from the whole text. Thus, we report this successful detection rate over multiple incorrect responses as the evaluation metric.

Observations.

Table
1
reports the successful detection rate for each UQ metric by collecting 50 wrong answers in each BIG-Bench subset, and we consider different
k=3,5
and
k=1\%
of sequence length. From the results, we can see the perturbation-based methods have a clear and consistent advantage over the NLL and Entropy scores, especially in Llama and Mistral. Comparing the three perturbation based methods,
l_{\infty}
-Adv. Pert. demonstrates better stability and stronger performance than the other two
. Besides, we find
l_{2}
-Adv. Pert. has a relatively lower efficacy in Mistral, as this model has a much larger gradient than other LLMs (but we set
\alpha=0.0001
for all LLMs). For Qwen model, we find the improvement is relatively small. We will provide more insights to the mechanisms of these metrics through the “case studies” in the subsequent part, which may help explain why the advantage of perturbation-based metrics in Qwen is less significant.

In Table
1
, we observe even for the best-performing metric, there are a substantial number of failure cases across each subset. Through manual inspection, we conjecture that these failures may stem from three main factors: (1) the LLM can remain highly confident even when making errors; (2) the identification of the earliest error step by GPT-5.2 is not perfectly accurate; and (3) there exist multiple, or even many, uncertainty locations within a response. Although the first wrong step often exhibits higher scores than its neighboring tokens, it does not consistently rank among the highest-scoring positions.

Case Studies.
In Figure
2
, we provide two representative examples in Llama and Qwen. As shown Case 1 (Llama), the model wrongly recalls a previous conclusion, and claims “Vernell is telling the truth”. The perturbation-based metrics can precisely locate to the token “telling” where the model begins to deviate. For the NLL metric, it flags several other tokens such as “lying” and the transition word “Since” in addition to the token “telling”, which makes the wrong reasoning step not outstanding within the overall output. In Case 2 (Qwen), the model incorrectly infers “second newest is tractor” from prior fact “the tractor is the second oldest.” Although perturbation-based metrics assign a higher score to the first incorrect token “Tr” than its neighbors, other tokens like “overall” and “what” have even higher scores. This may suggest Qwen inherently has higher variability in word choice or reasoning pathway selection. For example, following the content “let’s summarize” (in middle of Case 2), there can be multiple plausible continuations different from the actually generated token “what”, and the token “what” itself is not an reasoning error. The UQ metrics cannot distinguish reasoning uncertainty from such variability, resulting a decreased detection performance.

4.4
How is the Efficiency?

In this part, we show perturbation-based metrics indeed have a high efficiency. For example, in Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, with a single NVIDIA GH200 GPU, the average execution time for the Rand. Pert., which has 20 trials of noise sampling and re-inference is approximately 0.85 second for one case, while faster metrics such as

l_{\infty}
-Adv. Pert. and
l_{\infty}
-Adv. Pert. execute approximately 0.04 second per case, demonstrating their high efficiency.

We also compare these metrics with Multiple Sampling strategy
(
28
)
, by adapting it for sentence-level error detection. Given a question, we first conduct the LLM inference for 20 times. Then, for a randomly chosen answer, for each sentence in its reasoning chain, we check whether it shares the semantic similarity to some parts of another generated response, and we check the proportion of the samples which has this similarity to the target sentence (by querying this LLM itself). We define the sentence with least coincidence to other responses as the “most uncertainty” sentence in the response, and we check whether it overlaps with the first wrong sentence (judged by GPT-5.2).
In Figure
4
, we report this overlapping rate as well as the running time of this strategy. For the perturbation-based UQ metrics which are token-wise, we can also calculate the average value inner each sentence and then obtain this overlapping rate. From the result (5 math datasets under Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), we find they achieve approximately similar performance for detecting erroneous steps, but the multiple sampling-based strategy requires much longer computational time.

5.1
Hallucination Detection

We investigate the FactScore benchmark
(
20
)
to evaluate the effectiveness of UQ metrics in identifying factual errors (hallucinations), when LLMs are prompted to compose celebrity biographies. In the setting, each LLM response is decomposed into individual factual statements. Then, we compute the average UQ value across in each statement, which serves as an indicator of the model’s uncertainty regarding that statement. Table
4
reports the AUROC and Average Precision (AP) for all UQ metrics considered in our study. The results show that perturbation-based metrics fail to achieve competitive performance on this task. One possible explanation is that factual errors often exhibit weaker dependencies between generated tokens and their prior context compared to reasoning-intensive tasks. As a result, perturbation-based metrics, which rely heavily on such contextual dependencies, are less effective. This limitation may further extend to tasks that jointly involve intensive reasoning and factual knowledge, where different sources of uncertainty may coexist.

In our main experiments in Section

\sigma

The intensity value
\alpha
in Adv. Pert. to be 0.0001. Intuitively,
\sigma
and
\alpha
are chosen to guarantee the perturbation on the original embedding vectors are not significant. In this subsection, we conduct further ablation study to change these hyperparameters and observe the metric performance. The result is shown in Figure

0.0001\leq\sigma\leq 0.01

\sigma

with a value of
0.0001
serves as a suitable hyperparameter, yielding the best performance for the
l_{\infty}

l_{2}

, where we find the gradients occasionally exhibit large magnitudes, which may adversely affect the efficacy of the
l_{2}
-Adv. Pert. metric.

6
Conclusion and Limitation

In this paper, we propose perturbation-based token-level uncertainty quantification methods that deliver better efficacy to detect the erroneous steps in LLM reasoning. These methods also simultaneously enjoy high efficiency.
Although outperforming existing approaches on token-level UQ estimation, such strategies still fail to perfectly identify all potential failure cases, such as the occurrence of LLM hallucinations. Moreover, they may not reliably disentangle uncertainty about the “correctness of each reasoning step” from uncertainty arising from the “selection among multiple plausible reasoning pathways”. Stronger uncertainty signals therefore remain to be explored and leveraged to enable more reliable backtracking and localization of the tokens that trigger reasoning failures.

Appendix A
Prompts

We list all the prompts adopted by this work here, as shown in Figure
7
. These prompts are used for Correctness Check, Incorrect Intermediate Step Analysis, Incorrect Intermediate Step Attribution, Reasoning Error Classification and Multi-Sampling Reasoning Step Consistency.

Correctness Check
is applied to identify incorrect answers during collection of reasoning error cases in Section
4.1
and Section
4.2
.

Incorrect Intermediate Step Analysis
is applied as the first part of identifying the earliest reasoning error. It analyzes the answer step by step before identifies a critical error, serving as an intermediate thinking to better locate the error. There are two versions of prompts, adjusted for optimizing different types of tasks, where
Incorrect Intermediate Step Analysis (Pure-logical Task)
is applied to logical reasoning tasks in Section
4.1
and
Incorrect Intermediate Step Analysis (Mathematical Tasks)
is applied to math reasoning tasks in Section
4.2
.

Incorrect Intermediate Step Attribution
is the second part of earliest reasoning error identification, that extracts the final decision referring to the question, output answer and the previous analysis. This prompt is applied in Section
4.1
and Section
4.2
.

Reasoning Error Classification
is an automation that classify reasoning error for analysis in Section
4.2
.

Multi-Sampling Reasoning Step Consistency
is applied in Section
4.4
for the multiple-sampling based UQ method to record intermediate consistency. It help judges whether a reasoning step in the target output exist in one of the sampled reasoning.
