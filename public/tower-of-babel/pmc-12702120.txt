Hyaluronic acid-based microgels loaded with fluticasone furoate for bleomycin-induced acute lung injury treatment
Authors: Bo Liu, Michael Lush, Mollie R Nielsen, Brady J Sillman, Mena Asha Krishnan, Benson J Edagwa, Kristina Bailey, Bin Duan, Han-Jun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute lung injury (ALI) is a serious clinical condition characterized by intense inflammation and impaired pulmonary function. Excessive inflammatory response and reactive oxygen species (ROS) can cause pulmonary edema and subsequently acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). In this study, we developed fluticasone furoate (FF)-loaded hyaluronic acid (HA)-amino phenylboronic acid (PBA)-epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) based microgels (HPE@FF) as a novel therapeutic platform for ALI treatment. HPE@FF microgels were prepared by a facile one-pot method, integrating biocompatibility, intrinsic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and sustained FF delivery. The submicron-size microgels (0.1–1.6 μm) with 93 % encapsulation efficiency effectively reduced intracellular ROS in human lung fibroblast and mouse RAW264.7 cells in vitro. Moreover, HPE@Res significantly downregulated pro-inflammatory gene expressions (Tnf, Il6, and Tgfb) and cytokine secretions (TNF-α and IL-6) in vitro. In a rat model, intratracheal administration of HPE@FF provided prolonged pulmonary retention for up to 28 days, markedly alleviated pulmonary inflammation, improved histological outcomes, and restored lung function and arterial blood gas levels. Collectively, this study presents an innovative microgel-based drug delivery system that advances the design of steroid formulations and offers promising clinical potential for ALI and related respiratory diseases.Keywords: Microgel, Antioxidant, Anti-inflammatory, Acute lung injury, Pulmonary functions

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) is a severe pulmonary inflammatory condition caused by various factors such as bacterial or viral pneumonia, sepsis, and trauma, with a mortality rate of approximately 30–40 % [1,2]. Generally, the acute inflammatory response resulting from an imbalance between pro- and anti-inflammatory signals plays a central role in the pathogenesis of ALI, leading to ventilation-perfusion mismatch, alveolar tissue collapse, and reduced lung compliance [3]. Excessive inflammatory response and impaired self-repair can cause pulmonary edema and, subsequently, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), which is characterized by hypoxemia, and alveolar fluid accumulation [4]. The current standard of clinical care for ALI/ARDS includes mechanical ventilation with lung protective strategies, coupled with judicious fluid management, nutritional support, and targeted treatment of any known underlying cause of injury or disease [5,6]. So far, there are no clinically approved drugs that can effectively reduce mortality of patients with ALI/ARDS [7]. Therefore, there is a critical unmet need for the development of more effective treatment strategies to reverse the conditions or slow the progression in the severe and lethal lung injury syndromes.

Although the lung is an obvious target for site-specific delivery of therapeutics for respiratory airway diseases, novel drug delivery strategies are required to overcome key physiologic barriers and achieve efficient delivery [[8], [9], [10]]. Biomaterial-based systems with the ability of programmed drug delivery offer promising strategies for the treatment of ALI, including nanoparticles, hydrogels, and nano- and microgels [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. In particular, nano- and microgels are three-dimensional, chemically and/or physically cross-linked polymeric networks that form particles ranging from 10 nm to 100 μm in diameter and possess high water content, biocompatibility, and desirable mechanical properties [15,16]. These properties offer great potential for the utilization of polymer-based nano- and microgels in drug delivery, such as a tunable size from nanometers to micrometers and an internal network for the incorporation of biomolecules [15,17]. The matrix of nano- and microgels can be made from natural or synthetic polymers or their combination including hyaluronic acid (HA), chitosan, dextrin, poly(L-glutamic acid), etc. [13] For example, Yan et al. reported a zinc ion coordinated carboxymethyl chitosan-HA microgel with the diameter around 4 μm for pulmonary drug delivery, in which the drug had a retention time of up to 36 h in the lung [18]. Joscelyn et al. reported an enzyme-responsive nanoparticle-inside-microgel pulmonary drug delivery system with the mean size ranged from 4 to 8 μm, which could reach the deep lung, transport across mucosal surfaces and protease-triggered release of payload in diseased lung microenvironments [19]. Chen et al. developed micro-robotic swarms consisting of magnetic microgels to achieve intrabronchial targeted delivery, demonstrating in vivo delivery into the deep branches of the bronchial trees along with sustained drug release [20]. Therefore, the ideal microscale carriers should be designed to incorporate these properties: 1) avoid mucociliary clearance and achieve deep lung delivery with suitable size; 2) avoid rapid alveolar macrophage clearance as the particles might be removed by the alveolar macrophage within 30 min after pulmonary administration [21]; 3) sustained drug release [10].

Herein, we developed a novel multifunctional microgel as drug delivery system for the treatment of ALI, along with antioxidative, anti-inflammatory properties. HA is a major component of the extracellular matrix in vertebrates, and has been widely used for biomedical applications, such as drug delivery, and tissue engineering [22]. Native high molecular weight HA is nonimmunogenic, biocompatible, biodegradable, and inherently noninflammatory in nature, making it a promising candidate material for drug delivery system. [23]. Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), isolated from green tea, has intrinsic properties such as antioxidant, anti-inflammation, and radical scavenger effects, which is promising to regulate inflammatory and oxidative stress in ALI [24]. Fluticasone furoate (FF) is a synthetic corticosteroid used to treat a variety of inflammatory conditions and is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as an over-the-counter medication for hay fever and upper respiratory allergy symptoms [25]. In the present work, we constructed FF-loaded HA-based microgels (HPE@FF) using a one-pot approach. We then analyzed their physiochemical properties and examined their biocompatibility, as well as antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, in human lung fibroblast (HLF) and mouse-derived RAW264.7 cells in vitro. Finally, we evaluated the efficacy of HPE@FF microgels for lung function repair in a rat bleomycin-induced ALI model in vivo, confirming the microgel as a promising pulmonary drug delivery system.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of HA-PBA-EGCG loading FF (HPE@FF) microgels

The HPE@FF microgel was prepared via an in situ conjugation reaction among HA, 3-aminophenylboronic acid (PBA), and EGCG, as described in our previous reports [26,27]. Briefly, HA (40 mg; 600–1200 kDa, Bloomage Biotech) was dissolved in 15 mL of milliQ water overnight at 4 °C. The pH of this solution was subsequently adjusted to 6.5 by adding MES buffer (1 M, pH 5.5; Fisher Chemical). Then, EGCG (7 mg; AstaTech) and 3-aminophenylboronic acid (PBA; 20 mg; AstaTech) were dissolved in 1 mL DMSO. FF drug (8 mg; Sigma-Aldrich) was directly added into EGCG/PBA mixture solution, which was then dropped into the HA aqueous solution and left at room temperature (RT) for 20 min. Next, 4-(4,6-Dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazin-2-yl)-4-methylmorpholinium chloride (DMTMM; 58 mg; TCI Chemical) were added dropwise, followed by a 3-day reaction at RT. The final mixtures were dialyzed (molecular weight cutoff of 3.5 kDa, MWCO, Spectrum) at 4 °C for 3 days, freeze-dried, and prepared for use. As a negative control, HPE microgels were prepared using a similar procedure. For the preparation of fluorescein-labeled HPE microgels, cyanine 7.5 (Cy7.5)-labeled HA was first synthesized via an amination reaction between fluorescein cyanine 7.5 amine (Lumiprobe, 0.1 % w/w relative to HA) and HA. Subsequently, the Cy7.5-labeled HPE microgels were prepared following the previously described procedure [26,27].

Characterization of HPE and HPE@FF microgels

The morphology of the microparticles was visually characterized by a scanning electron microscope (SEM, FEI Quanta 200). The polydispersity index (PDI) of the particles was calculated according to the formula [28]. PDI = the square of the standard deviation divided by the mean particle diameter. The size of the microgel particles was calculated by ImageJ software.

Ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) analysis

The drug content and encapsulation efficiency of HPE@FF microgels were determined using UPLC coupled with a Photodiode Array (PDA) detector. The microgels (1 mg/mL) were incubated with 1 mL DPBS at 37 °C for various time points (0, 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 day). At each time point, the supernatant DPBS solution (1 mL) was collected after centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. After, an equal amount of fresh DPBS was added to resuspend the microgel sample. The collected supernatant samples were analyzed using a Waters Acquity H-class Plus UPLC-PDA and Empower 3 software (Version 7.50) to determine the concentration of the drug that has been released. Separation was performed using a Phenomenex Kinetex 5 μm C18 100 Å (150 × 4.6 mm) column at 38 °C and isocratic mobile phase of 35 %–0.01 % (v/v) acetic acid in HPLC-grade Water and 65 % HPLC-grade Acetonitrile at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Drug was detected at 240 nm with a retention time of 3.23 min. Drug content was determined relative to peak areas of drug standards (0.05–200 μg/mL). Each sample was tested in triplicate. Cell culture

Primary human lung fibroblasts (HLF) were isolated from deidentified normal lungs obtained from the Nebraska Organ Retrieval System in accordance with the guidelines of the University of Nebraska Institutional Review Board [29]. The HLF were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) containing 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1 % (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (P/S, Gibco). All the experiments were performed using cells passage 6 or lower. RAW264.7 cells (ATCC) were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) medium supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % Pen-Strep. All the cells were cultured at 37 °C incubator with 5 % CO2.

In vitro cytocompatibility of HPE and HPE@FF microgels

The cytotoxicity of HPE and HPE@FF microgels on HLFs and RAW264.7 cells were evaluated by MTT assays (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide, Sigma). In this study, we used microgel extract media to evaluate their effects on cells in vitro experiments, following a previously reported protocol with slightly modification [30]. Briefly, HPE and HPE@FF microgels were first resuspended in the culture medium at various concentrations (0.5, 1, 2 mg/mL) and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The extract media were collected after the HPE or HPE@FF microgel mixture were centrifugated at the speed of 4500 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The HLFs were seed into 48-well plate (1 × 104 per well) and cultured in the DMEM/F12 media. The RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 48-well (1 × 104 per well) and cultured in RPMI 1640 media. After incubation overnight at 37 °C, the culture media were removed, and the cells were cultured in extract media for further 24 h. Then, the cells were washed with PBS after the extract media were discarded. About 450 μL of culture medium containing 50 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 4 h. Subsequently, the medium was discarded, and 500 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added and shaken at 100 rpm for 30 min to dissolve the blue crystals in each well of the 48-well plate. Finally, the solution was transferred from the 48-well plate into a 96-well plate at 50 μL per well and the OD value was measured at 570 nm (OD570) using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1 model). The untreated cells incubated with the normal culture media served as a control.

In vitro hemolysis assay

The hemolytic study was carried out on HPE and HPE@FF microgels according to a modified protocol [26]. Human red blood cells (RBCs) were provided by the Elutriation Core Facility at the University of Nebraska Medical Center (UNMC). After being washed 3 times, RBCs were resuspended in PBS containing HPE or HPE@FF microgels (2 mg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. Then, the RBC suspension was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min. The RBC suspensions in the microcentrifuge tubes were photographed, and the OD values at 541 nm were measured. The RBC sample in only PBS was used as the negative control group, and a 1 % Triton X-100 (Sigma) aqueous solution was used as the positive control group. The hemolysis ratio was calculated according to the following equation: Hemolysis (%) = (OD – OD negative)/(OD positive -OD negative) × 100.

In vitro measurement of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS)

In this study, intracellular ROS levels were measured using the fluorescent probe, 2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA, EMD Millipore), which is hydrolyzed to H2DCF and subsequently oxidized to the fluorescent product DCF after crossing the plasma membrane. The microgel extract medium was pre-prepared after microgels were co-incubated in the cell culture medium overnight at 37 °C. PBS was included as the negative control to represent the baseline condition without oxidative stress, while H2O2 treatment served as the positive control to induce ROS generation. The H2O2 + HPE extract medium and H2O2 + HPE@FF extract medium groups were then compared with both PBS (baseline) and H2O2 (injury model) to evaluate the protective and therapeutic effects of the microgels under oxidative stress. HLFs and RAW264.7 cells (1 × 104/well in 48-well plates) were cultured in cell culture medium at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, the cells were loaded with culture medium containing H2DCFDA (10 μM) and further incubated for another 45 min in the dark at 37 °C. After being washed with DPBS buffer three times, the cells were pretreated with collected microgel extract medium containing H2O2 (1 mM) for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed with DPBS buffer three times, and the intracellular ROS concentration was measured by detecting the DCF fluorescence intensity with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 525 nm through a microplate reader. The cells (1 × 105/well in 24-well plates) were also imaged using a CLSM to visualize the DCF fluorescence after being treated using the same procedure.

In vitro inflammatory response of RAW264.7 to HPE and HPE@FF microgels

Briefly, 2 × 105 RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and then cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) containing 10 % FBS, 1 % P/S. After overnight incubation, the cell culture medium was replaced with extract media (HPE or HPE@FF microgels) and then cultured for an additional overnight period. RAW264.7 cells were then stimulated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, PeproTech) in the presence of microgel extract media. After overnight incubation, the culture supernatants were collected to evaluate the cytokine levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) by using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (R&D Systems) according to manufacturer's instructions. The cells were also collected to analyze gene expression by qPCR.

qPCR analysis

The total RNA from mouse specimens was analyzed by qPCR. Briefly, the total RNA was isolated from cells or freshly collected tissues using QIA-shredder and RNeasy mini-kits (QIACEN). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized by following the protocol of an iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories). For the quantitative analysis of the total RNA expression, qPCR amplification was performed in a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific) with the use of SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). The cDNA samples were analyzed for genes of interest and for the housekeeping gene GAPDH. The target genes included Tnf, Il6, and Tgfb, as listed in Table S1. The relative transcription level of each target gene was calculated using the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method.

Bleomycin-induced acute lung injury

Experiments were performed on adult, male, 250–300 g Sprague-Dawley rats purchased from the Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA, United States) following the previous reported method [31]. Briefly, Rats were randomly divided into 5 groups: sham rats (1 group) and bleomycin-exposed rats (four groups). In four groups, intratracheal bleomycin (2.5 mg/kg, ∼150 μL) was instilled intratracheally to the lungs under 3 % isoflurane anesthesia to induce acute lung injury. Sham control rats underwent intratracheal instillation of saline. The ALI rats were followed by administration of PBS (injury only control, n = 6), HPE (500 μg, 150 μL, n = 4), FF (65 μg, 150 μL, n = 4), FF-loaded HPE (500 μg, 150 μL, n = 6). The FF dose was adopted at approximately 260 μg/kg, based on a previously reported regimen with slight modifications [32,33]. All groups were randomly selected for evaluation of lung functions at day 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 post-treatment.

IVIS imaging

The retention time of intratracheally injected HPE microgels in the lung was evaluated using IVIS (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer), which detected fluorescence from Cy7.5 labeled HPE microgels. Briefly, Cy7.5-labeled HPE microgels (150 μL, 20 mg/mL) were resuspended in DI water and then intratracheally injected into lung following ALI model procedure. After 1 day and 28 days, the rats were sacrificed in a CO2 chamber and immediately dissected to remove the lung, heart, liver, spleen, and kidney. The organs were quickly washed with PBS buffer to remove excess blood from the necropsy process. The extracted organs were placed on a black, non-emitting background for fluorescence analysis. The excitation filter was set at 644 nm, and the emission filter was set at 665 nm.

Respiratory function analysis

The rats were measured using whole-body plethysmography (WBP) where the following respiratory parameters were recorded prior to bleomycin exposure (week 0, 1, 2, 3, 4): respiratory rate (RR), tidal volume (TV), minute volume (MV), inspiratory time (Ti), expiratory time (Te), relaxation Time (Tr), expiratory flow at 50 % TV (EF50), peak inspiratory flow (PIF), peak expiratory flow (PEF), relative time to peak expiratory flow (Rpef), enhanced pause (Penh). A single dose of intratracheal bleomycin was instilled and subsequent WBP recordings were performed weekly within 4 weeks. After chamber acclimation, baseline recordings were obtained before bleomycin exposure. Animals were weighed and placed in the Rat WBP standard chamber for a 20-min acclimation period, followed by a 1-h recording. Data was recorded using DSI FinePointe WBP software (Data Sciences International, St. Paul, MN, USA).

Arterial blood gas analysis

The artery on the ventral aspect of the rat tail was used for collection of small amounts of blood (∼0,1 mL) for analyzing arterial blood gas on 4 days post treatment. The animal was restrained with a commercial restrainer so that its tail was accessible. The tail was prepared aseptically by alternating alcohol prep pads and iodine prep pads three times and the artery was then punctured using a 24 G needle. A small volume of blood (∼0.1 mL) was gently aspirated into the syringe for blood gas analysis (iSTAT, Abbott, Chicago, United States). After sample collection, the needle was removed, and a gauze swab was pressed firmly on the puncture site to stop bleeding. Blood gases information: pO2 (partial pressure of oxygen), pCO2 (partial pressure of carbon dioxide), and sO2 (oxygen saturation).

Histological analysis

Fresh lung tissues were harvested and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for about 24 h and then embedded in paraffin and sectionalized. Each section was 5 μm thick and was stained with hematoxylin eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome following the standard protocol.

Statistic analysis

All quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Statistical analysis was performed using an unpaired Student's t-test for comparisons between two groups, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Tukey's multiple comparison test. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. A p-value <0.05 was considered significant for all statistical analyses.

Results

Preparation and characterization of HPE@FF microgels

The HA/PBA/EGCG@FF microgels were fabricated via a one-step polyphenolic condensation reaction [26]. As shown in Fig. 1A, we first dissolved the PBA, EGCG and FF in DMSO with the molar ratio of PBA: EGCG = 8:1. Then the FF-loaded HPE microgels were obtained by the copolymerization of HA and the PBA-EGCG mixture in a 1:1 M ratio (PBA: COOH group in HA) in the present of a coupling agent DMTMM at ambient environment. As shown in Fig. 1B, the freeze-dried microgel samples could be resuspended in water to form an emulsion (inserted photo). Fig. 1C shows that HPE@FF exhibited a spherical morphology, with a range of particle size from 100 nm to 1.6 μm (mean particle size 500 ± 22 nm), as calculated using ImageJ based on the SEM images. Furthermore, the particle size of HPE@FF (1.435 ± 0.006 μm) in aqueous solution was larger than that in the dry state (Fig. S1). This difference may be attributed to the hydration of microgels, which arises from their swelling behavior [34].

Preparation and characterization of HPE@FF microgels. (A) Schematic illustration for preparing HPE@FF using a one-pot conjugation reaction; (B) The size distribution of HPE@FF microgels; (C) The SEM image showing spheric-like morphology of HPE@FF microgels; (D) The accumulative FF drug release profile within 4 weeks (n = 4).

In our previous research, the percentages of EGCG and PBA in the HPE microgels were 26.9 % and 34.5 %, respectively [26]. Based on these values, we prepared HPE@FF with an FF drug LE of 14 % (w/w), as calculated. HPLC analysis showed that the final LE of FF in HPE@FF microgels was approximately 13 % (w/w), with an EE of FF drug around 93 % (w/w). Furthermore, the release of FF from the HPE@FF microgels were calculated based on the standard curve (Fig. S2). As shown in Fig. 1D, HPE@FF exhibited slow and sustained release behaviors over 28 days. The release profile of FF showed about 13 % burst releases and 29 % cumulative drug release of FF over this period. These results demonstrated that the HPE@FF microgels, with high EE of FF, can be fabricated using a facile approach, offering great potential for localized therapeutic delivery in ALI.

Cytocompatibility of HPE@FF microgels in vitro

We then examined the cytocompatibility of HPE@FF microgels in vitro using MTT assays on HLFs and RAW264.7 cells. For this purpose, the extract supernatant of HPE and HPE@FF microgels, obtained incubation in culture medium for 24h at 37 °C, was used to treat the cells. As shown in Fig. 2A, the extract supernatant from each microgel medium had similar minimal effects on the viability of HLFs compared to PBS control group. Although the viability of HLFs treated with HPE@FF microgels was slightly lower than that with HPE microgels, the difference was not statistically significant. For RAW264.7 cells, the extract supernatant from HPE and HPE@FF microgels showed no effect on cell viability (Fig. 2B). Consistent with MTT assay results, the Live/Dead staining of L929 fibroblasts also showed high cell viability and good cytocompatibility for both HPE and HPE@FF microgels (Fig. S3). Furthermore, we labeled HPE with the Cy5.5 dye and treated HLFs and Raw264.7 cells with Cy5.5-labeled HPE microgels. After overnight co-culture, the fluorescence signals were detected around HLFs and RAW264.7 cells without affecting cell growth (Fig. S4), indicating that the HPE microgels can be internalized while maintaining cytocompatibility in vitro.

For the hemolysis test, the HPE and HPE@FF microgels were directly incubated with human RBCs in a 1x PBS buffer at 37 °C. As shown in Fig. 2C, the hemolysis rates for both HPE and HPE@FF groups were below 1 %, indicating negligible hemolytic activity towards human RBCs for all tested samples. Fig. 2D also showed that the supernatants of the HPE and HPE@FF groups were colorless, like the PBS negative control and distinct from the reddish color of the lysis buffer positive control, which resulted from RBC hemolysis.

Antioxidative and anti-inflammatory properties of HPE@FF microgels in vitro

Due to the free phenol groups and the dynamic nature of boronic esters in the HPE microgels, HPE and HPE@FF microgels exhibit promising antioxidative properties [[35], [36], [37]]. The in vitro antioxidative efficiency of these microgels was evaluated using an ROS scavenging test. HLFs and RAW264.7 cells were treated with microgel extract media containing H2O2, which can stimulate excessive intracellular ROS production. Meanwhile, nonfluorescent H2DCFDA, a cell permeable probe dye, was used to detect the intracellular ROS level, as DCF is highly fluorescent in the presence of ROS. As shown in Fig. 3A and C, green fluorescence signals were barely detected from the HPE and HPE@FF microgel treated groups which, similar to the blank PBS group, indicating that both HPE and HPE@FF microgel extract media did not induce excessive ROS in HLFs and RAW264.7 cells compared to the positive H2O2 group. Quantitative analysis of the DCF fluorescence signal intensity further demonstrated that HPE and HPE@FF microgels could significantly reduce intracellular ROS in both cell types (Fig. 3B–D). Consistently, the 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free radical scavenging assay showed that HPE@FF effectively scavenged DPPH radicals (Fig. S5) and that the antioxidative effect was successfully maintained after the FF was incorporated within the composite HPE microgels.

(A) The alleviation of oxidative stress in HLF cells (A) and RAW264.7 cells (C) was monitored via ROS probe (H2DCFDA) staining after treatments with PBS, H2O2, H2O2 +HPE; H2O2+HPE@FF; The oxidized H2DCFA (DCF) fluorescence signal intensity in HLF cells (B) and RAW264.7 cells (D) was quantified in a 96-well microplate reader (Ex = 488 nm, Em = 535 nm) (n = 5). ns: not significant, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

To evaluate the anti-inflammatory properties of HPE@FF microgels in vitro, RAW264.7 cells, a mouse macrophage cell line, were used to assess their inflammatory response to LPS stimulation in the presence or absence of microgels (HPE and HPE@FF). LPS, an endotoxin extracted from the cell wall of Gram-negative bacteria, directly activates macrophages and accelerates their inflammatory response. As shown in Fig. 4A, RAW264.7 cells were incubated in HPE or HPE@FF microgel extract media overnight and subsequently treated with microgel extract media containing LPS for another 6 h. After treatment, the culture media were collected for evaluation of cytokine secretion, and the cells were used to examine the inflammation-related gene expression. We quantitatively analyzed the expression of several pro-inflammatory genes, including Tnf, Il6, and Tgfb by RT-PCR (primers shown in Table S1). As shown in Fig. 4B–D, the relative expression of these genes was significantly upregulated in RAW264.7 cells after LPS activation and significantly downregulated in the groups treated with microgel extract media. We further measured the secretion of inflammatory cytokines by RAW264.7 cells, as shown in Fig. 4E and F. As expected, the secretion levels of TNFα, and IL-6 were significantly increased after LPS stimulation. Treatment with HPE or HPE@FF extract media significantly decreased the secretions of these two pro-inflammatory cytokines. Interestingly, the HPE@FF group showed slightly higher expression of the three genes and increased secretion of the two cytokines compared to the HPE group. This modest upregulation may be attributed to potential side effects of FF, a corticosteroid known to have complex immunomodulatory properties [38,39]. However, the differences between the HPE and HPE@FF groups were not statistically significant. Taken together, these results demonstrated that both HPE and HPE@FF microgels exert anti-inflammatory effects by downregulating pro-inflammatory gene expressions and cytokine secretions.

The retention time of HPE microgels in vivo

We first determined the in vivo retention time of HPE microgels. HPE was labeled with Cy7.5 dye and then intratracheally administrated into lung (Fig. 5A). As shown in Fig. 5B, fluorescent signals from Cy7.5-labeled HPE microgels were detected in lung tissue one day after administration, with negligible fluorescence observed in other major organs (heart, liver, spleen, and kidney). On day 28, fluorescence in the lung tissue remained detectable, although the signal intensity had decreased compared to day 1. These results demonstrated that HPE microgels were retained in the lung tissue for up to four weeks, with minimal accumulation in other major organs throughout the 28-day period.

Efficacy of HPE@FF microgels on lung functions in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic effect of HPE@FF, the ALI model was induced by the intratracheal injection of bleomycin (Fig. 5A). The sham rat received an injection of equivalent volume of saline. ALI mice were treated with HPE, FF, and HPE@FF to assess the in vivo therapeutic efficacy. Firstly, we assessed the parameters of wet lung weights and wet lung/body weight ratios to further quantify the degree of pulmonary injury (Fig. S6). As shown in Fig. S6, both lung wet weight and lung-to-body weight ratio were significantly increased at day 7 following bleomycin treatment in the Saline, HPE, FF, and HPE@FF groups, compared with Sham group. However, these parameters were markedly reduced in the HPE@FF group relative to the Saline group, indicating that HPE@FF effectively alleviated lung edema. Furthermore, histological analysis of lung tissues was conducted to evaluate the in vivo therapeutic effect of HPE@FF microgels for ALI rats. After 28 days of injection, the rats were sacrificed, and the lungs were harvested for the histological assessment of lung injury. Tissue sections were stained with H&E and Masson's trichrome. As shown in Fig. 5C, compared to the sham group, bleomycin injection led to the remarkable lung injury in the ALI group (Saline), as indicated by extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells, thickened bronchial wall, and damaged alveoli structure. Collagen depositions were also markedly increased in ALI group. Notably, ALI rat model treated with FF and HPE@FF showed a significant reduction in degeneration, hemorrhage, edema, necrosis and congestion in the alveolar septum, along with a clear decrease of inflammatory cells in lung tissues. ALI rats treated with HPE also showed alleviated pathologic changes, albeit to a lesser degree. In addition, we performed immunofluorescence staining for CD68 (a macrophage marker) and iNOS (an inflammatory marker) to further elucidate the macrophage-mediated inflammatory response (Fig. S7). As expected, lungs from the Sham group contained few CD68 positive cells, whereas the Saline, HPE, FF, and HPE@FF groups exhibited markedly increased macrophage infiltration, with the most pronounced accumulation observed in the Bleomycin and HPE groups at day 28. Importantly, macrophage numbers were reduced in the treatment of groups, particularly in the HPE@FF group, which showed an attenuation of macrophage infiltration compared with the Bleomycin group at day 28. Furthermore, elevated iNOS expression was observed in the Saline group, indicating an intensified inflammatory state. In contrast, the HPE@FF-treated lungs exhibited a lower iNOS expression, suggesting effective suppression of inflammation. Collectively, these results suggested that significant remission of acute lung inflammation after treatment with FF or HPE@FF.

To further evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of HPE@FF microgel, we carried out respiratory function analysis in ALI rats (Fig. 6). The pulmonary functions were longitudinally evaluated with WBP within four weeks post-bleomycin in rats. EF50 represents the flow rate at which 50 % of the tidal volume of an individual breath has been expelled, measured in milliliters per second, and serves as an indicator of mid-expiratory flow dynamics. PIFb (Peak inspiratory flow during breathing) represents the patient's ability to generate inspiratory force, while PEFb (peak expiratory flow during breathing) is primarily indicative of large airway patency and overall expiratory muscle strength. Collectively, these three parameters (EF50, PIFb, and PEFb) are valuable for evaluating both expiratory and inspiratory airflow function. As shown in Fig. 6A–C, EF50, PIFb, and PEFb levels increased in all ALI model groups (ALI control, HPE, FF, HPE@FF) during the first week post-bleomycin administration compared to baseline (W0), indicating compensatory changes in respiratory mechanics. Notably, rats treated with HPE@FF showed significantly lower mean values for these parameters at week one relative to the other groups, suggesting a reduction in excessive respiratory effort. From week two to four, EF50, PIFb, and PEFb values in the FF- and HPE@FF-treated groups gradually returned to baseline levels (W0), whereas these parameters remained more variable in the ALI control and HPE-treated groups. Especially, The EF50 parameter in HPE@FF group showed significantly lower than that of in Injury group within 4 weeks (Fig. 6A). These trends indicated that HPE@FF treatment effectively mitigated airway obstruction and promoted stabilization of airflow dynamics within four weeks.

Lung function analysis based on respiratory parameters: (A) EF50; (B) PIFb; (C) PEFb, (D) RR, (E) Ti, and (F) Te. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

In addition, we evaluated respiratory rate (RR), inspiratory time (Ti), and expiratory time (Te) to assess breathing efficiency. As illustrated in Fig. 6D, RR was markedly elevated in all groups at week one post-bleomycin, indicating reduced respiratory efficiency due to breathing distress. Among all groups, the HPE@FF-treated groups demonstrated significantly lower RR values at week one, indicating improved respiratory efficiency. Notably, rats treated with HPE@FF showed significantly lower mean values for RR parameters at week 1, 2, 3 and 4 relatives to the Injury group. Ti and Te parameters provide insights into the temporal structure of the respiratory cycle. As shown in Fig. 6E and F, both Ti and Te were reduced from week one through week four post-bleomycin, compared to baseline levels (W0). The HPE@FF-treated groups exhibited lower mean Ti values, but higher Te values compared to the other groups in week 1 and week 2, resulting in a Ti < Te pattern. In addition, the parameters of Penh, Rpef, TV, and MV were also evaluated in these group as shown in Fig. S8. Compared with the ALI group, the HPE@FF group showed a trend improvement in Penh, Rpef, TV, and MV, although these changes did not reach statistical significance except for Rpef (W4) and MV (W1 and W4). Collectively, this breathing profile indicates a more efficient and balanced respiratory pattern, reflecting improved lung compliance and reduced expiratory resistance after HPE@FF treatment.

To assess the degree of lung tissue hypoxia post-bleomycin treatment, arterial blood gas analysis was evaluated in rats from four groups on day 3(Fig. 7). As shown in Fig. 7A and B, compared to FF-treated rats, HPE@FF-treated rats exhibited significantly increased blood pO2 (higher than 60 mmHg) and sO2 7 days post-bleomycin, indicating that HPE@FF significantly attenuated hypoxemia during early stage of acute lung injury period. In addition, the level of pCO2 in injury and FF groups were significantly higher than that of in Sham group, while there was no significant difference between Sham group and HPE@FF group. HPE@FF-treated rats also exhibited significantly lower pCO2 on day 7 compared to the Injury and FF groups (Fig. 7C). Meanwhile, the levels of TCO2, HCO3, and BEecf in HPE@FF-treated rats were significantly lower than that in FF-treated rats even though they were higher than that of Sham group without significant difference (Fig. 7D–F). The average pH value and Lac value in HPE@FF-treated rats were slightly higher than that in FF-treated rats (Fig. S9A and B). However, no statistical difference for the parameters of the pH value and HCO3 level exhibited in these two groups. Collectively, these results indicated that the HPE@FF could ameliorate the blood acidosis and maintain arterial blood gas level close to the normal level (pO2>80 mmHg) in ALI rats.

Discussion

ALI/ARDS is a common clinical syndrome of acute respiratory failure with substantial morbidity and mortality, defined by the acute onset of noncardiogenic pulmonary edema [1,40]. Because of their obvious anti-inflammatory role, glucocorticoids have been used to treat ALI/ARDS for decades, especially during the Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic [3,41,42]. In the current study, we developed a multifunctional HPE microgel as an FF corticosteroid drug delivery system and characterized the efficacy of HPE@FF for bleomycin-induced ALI therapy.

Multifunctional HPE microgels were facilely prepared by one-pot conjugation reaction of HA, EGCG and PBA under ambient conditions. This microgel-based drug delivery system offers several advantages. First, both EGCG and PBA provide multifunctionality, including antioxidative and anti-inflammatory activities [26,27,35]. Second, the catechol groups in EGCG enable the formation of reversible boronic ester bonds with PBA, serving as dynamic cross-linkers to promote effective and sustained drug release [24]. Third, HPE microgel particles with submicron size (0.1–1.6 μm) and spherical morphology are well-suited for deep lung deposition, enabling efficient pulmonary drug delivery [43]. Local drug administration offers several key benefits over systemic delivery, such as minimizing undesirable systemic effects, reducing drug dosage due to higher local bioavailability, and improving drug stability and efficiency owing to lower nuclease activity in lung [[44], [45], [46]]. Furthermore, intratracheal injection directly delivers drugs to the lung, preventing drug loss caused by the retention of nasal drops or inhalants in the nasal cavity or oral cavity [43]. Therefore, this HPE@FF microgel system, incorporating sustained FF release, offers new therapeutic potential for ALI treatment.

Owing to the hydrophobic property of FF, a high EE of 93 % was achieved, with the FE of 13 %. The release profile exhibits an initial burst release within 24 h, providing a rapid therapeutic effect during the early stage of ALI characterized by cytokine storm. Moreover, the sustained release of FF for 28 days supports long-term therapeutic efficacy in vivo. Together, these findings suggest that HPE microgels are well-suited for deep lung deposition, effective pulmonary drug delivery, and prolonged drug release in an ALI model.

In vitro cellular assays demonstrated that our HPE microgels were cytocompatible and hemocompatible without impairing HLFs and RAW264.7 cell viability and proliferation, and without causing any hemolysis when directly contacting blood (Fig. 2). In this study, human RBCs were intentionally used for the hemolysis assay because they are more clinically relevant and widely accepted for evaluating blood biosafety in vitro. This approach allows for a more direct prediction of potential clinical translation, even though the in vivo animal model was rat. During acute inflammation in ALI lung, activated macrophages and neutrophils release numerous cytotoxic substances, including ROS, various proinflammatory cytokines, which can induce oxidative stress, trigger inflammatory cytokine storm, and cause lung tissue damage [47]. EGCG, the major catechin in green tea, has strong antioxidant activity due to its two tri-phenolic groups, which enable efficient scavenging of ROS and free radicals [48]. These properties contribute to its therapeutic potential in various diseases such as cancer, diabetes, and stroke, and contribute to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. [49]. Consistent with these reported effects, our in vitro results demonstrate that HPE@FF microgel extract media effectively scavenged ROS generated in HLFs and RAW264.7 cells following H2O2 stimulation. Additionally, HPE@FF extract medium reduced the TNF-α and IL-6 secretion and downregulated proinflammatory gene expressions (Tnf, Il6, and Tgfb) in RAW264.7 cells. Previous studies have also shown that FF exhibited the highest cellular accumulation and slowest efflux rate among clinically used inhaled corticosteroids, resulting in greater tissue retention and prolonged anti-inflammatory activity [38]. Therefore, EGCG-based HPE@FF microgel system has great potential in ROS-scavenging and provides a promising strategy to effectively downregulate inflammatory-related cytokine secretion and gene expression for efficient ALI therapy.

Subsequently, we employed a bleomycin-induced ALI model in rats to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of HPE@FF. To assess in vivo retention and biocompatibility, Cy7.5-labeled HPE microgels were first intratracheally administered into the lung. Fluorescence imaging showed that the HPE microgels remained at lung site for at least 28 days (Fig. 5B). The long-term retention of submicron-sized HPE microgels makes them suitable for local effective pulmonary drug delivery through lung deposition [43]. As aforementioned, drugs administered by intratracheal injection are rapidly absorbed into lung, which not only shortens the time between administration and the onset of action, but also reduces the risk of drug inactivation (e.g., liver first-pass metabolism by intravenously administration) [43]. In current study, we intratracheally administrated HPE@FF into lung of bleomycin-induced ALI rat and further assessed the inflammation status at the histological level (Fig. 5C). H&E staining images showed that HPE@FF was highly effective in treating lung injury. Rats treated with HPE@FF showed significantly alleviated pathological changes after 7 days of the intervention. In addition, both lung wet weight and lung-to-body weight ratio were markedly reduced in the HPE@FF group compared with the Saline group at day 7, indicating effective mitigation of lung edema (Fig. S6). IF staining results further demonstrated that HPE@FF treatment attenuated macrophage infiltration and reduced iNOS expression in the lung at day 28 compared with the Bleomycin group, with iNOS expression appearing lower and more organized (Fig. S7). The observed attenuation of lung injury following HPE@FF treatment might be attributed to the synergistic anti-inflammatory effects of FF and HPE [38,42].

Pulmonary function tests are widely used in respiratory medicine to objectively assess lung function in clinical settings [50,51]. These tests are essential for evaluating patients with respiratory diseases for monitoring the efficacy of therapeutic interventions to guide further treatment [52]. In current study, we conduct two categories of pulmonary function tests to evaluate the efficacy of HPE@FF in ALI, including respiratory parameters, and arterial blood gas parameters. In the pulmonary respiratory tests, EF50, PIFb, PEFb, RR, Ti, and Te are key parameters used to describe breathing patterns. The parameters, EF50, PIFb, and PEF, could reflect inspiratory and expiratory flow, and provide information regarding the large and small airways. The data from the current study showed that severe inspiratory and expiratory flow occurred at week 1 post bleomycin compared to groups at baseline week 0 (Fig. 6A–C). This could be related to increased elastic recoil and altered breathing mechanics in the injured lung, leading to faster mid-expiratory flows despite impaired gas exchange [53]. At 2–4 weeks post-bleomycin, these three parameters deceased as post-recovery from ALI, which is consistent with previous report [54]. Interestingly, the FF-loaded microgel could downregulated many respiratory parameters at week 1 and week 2. HPE@FF treatment may have reduced lung stiffness and restored more normal compliance as indicated in our histological results (Fig. 5C), thereby lowering inspiratory and expiratory driving pressures (Fig. 6A–C), indicating that HPE@FF microgels attenuate lung injury syndromes and enhance the pulmonary recovery. In parallel, the HPE@FF group showed a trend improvement in Penh, Rpef, TV, and MV compared with the ALI group (Fig. S8), although these changes did not reach statistical significance except for Rpef (W4) and MV (W1 and W4). The directional changes are consistent with a potential protective effect of the treatment on airway resistance and ventilatory efficiency. Although there was no significant difference for Penh, TV, Rpef parameters among the rats with or without treatment, the airway mechanics and ventilatory function were modulated for these parameters in HPE@FF group. The lack of significant differences may be attributed to variability among animals and the limited sample size, which reduced statistical power to detect moderate effects. Taken together, these respiratory parameters results suggest that the HPE@FF may confer beneficial effects on lung function recovery.

It has been reported that poor gas exchange caused by disruption of the normal alveolar-capillary endothelial barrier in ALI leads to systemic hypoxemia [55]. Arterial blood gas is a blood measurement test and a useful diagnostic tool that assists with treatment and therefore improves clinical outcomes [55,56]. In order to assess the respiratory gas exchange function 7 days post lung injury, we investigated the role of arterial blood gas parameters in rats post-treated with bleomycin and microgel with without FF system, including pO2, sO2, pCO2, TCO2, HCO3, BEecf, pH, and Lac. Our blood gas data indicated that pO2, sO2, pCO2, TCO2, HCO3 and BEecf 7 days post intratracheal administration of bleomycin was quite severe, which is consistent with our previous reports that lung hypoxia was formed [31]. The major findings in present study are that the HPE@FF administration, rather than FF alone, was able to reverse the pronounced hypoxemia caused by bleomycin-induced lung injury. The partial pressure and saturation of oxygen in HPE@FF group were markedly restored, which is most likely attributable to the significant attenuation of lung edema, thereby enhancing gas exchange efficiency.

More importantly, lung function assessments provided clinically relevant insights beyond histological or molecular outcomes. HPE@FF treatment effectively alleviated airway obstruction and stabilized airflow dynamics within four weeks (Fig. 6). In addition, HPE@FF improved systemic conditions by mitigating blood acidosis and maintaining arterial blood gas levels closer to normal compared with FF treatment alone (Fig. 7). Taken together, these results suggest that HPE@FF may offer systemic and functional benefits beyond those achieved with FF alone.

Our HPE@FF system provides a biocompatible delivery platform with lung-targeting and sustained release properties, thus addressing key pharmacological limitations of free FF. For example, systemic corticosteroids are known to increase the risk of side effects, such as oral candidiasis and dysphonia, diabetes-related outcomes and bone fractures [39]. Although promising, our current study has several limitations. First, we did not evaluate whether and how the FF-loaded HPE microgels regulate the inflammation and resident macrophages and neutrophils in the lung, including the ROS-scavenging and anti-inflammatory effects of HPE@FF in ALI rats. Our current study provides preliminary evidence demonstrating the feasibility of HPE microgel as a therapeutic payload vehicle, their biocompatibility, and their ability to promote lung function recovery. Future studies should focus more on the detailed biological mechanisms of FF-loaded HPE microgels to better understand the interaction with immune cells, lung repair, and respiratory functions under pathological conditions. Second, future work is needed to optimize the delivery platform. Detailed quantitative analysis of series grafting and loading ratio will help optimize the formulation. The dose and timing of HPE@FF administration should be optimized to maximize the therapeutic advantage of HPE@FF. Third, systematic in vivo biosafety evaluations of HPE@FF microgels, including long-term toxicity, immune responses, and biodistribution, are required, as these assessments are essential prior to clinical translation.

Conclusions

In summary, we successfully developed and tested a versatile FF-loaded HPE microgel system with anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory properties for the intratracheal pulmonary delivery of FF to the inflamed lung in the bleomycin-induced ALI rat model. In vitro, HPE@FF was biocompatibility with HLFs and RAW264.7 cells, effectively scavenged ROS, and downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. Following intratracheal injection, HPE@FF exhibited retention time of at least 28 days in the lung tissue. HPE@FF inhibited excessive pro-inflammatory immune cells accumulation in the injured lung and prevented collagen deposition in the injured lung, thereby promoting the respiratory function recovery and protecting arterial blood gas level in ALI rats. Collectively, this biocompatible delivery strategy enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of FF against ALI.
