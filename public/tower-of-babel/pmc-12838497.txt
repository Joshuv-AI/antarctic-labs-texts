Determinants of childhood anaemia in Nigeria: a public health perspective using quantile regression analysis
Authors: Talani Mhelembe, Shaun Ramroop, Faustin Habyarimana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Nigeria, anaemia is still a major public health issue for children under five, and its high prevalence is caused by a variety of risk factors. Conventional regression techniques frequently ignore the ways in which these risk factors affect various population segments. The distributional effects of environmental and sociodemographic factors on childhood haemoglobin levels are investigated in this study using quantile regression.MethodData from the 2021 Nigeria Integrated Malaria Survey (NIMS) and the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) household dataset were combined to produce a pooled sample. Using SPSS, a quantile regression analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between haemoglobin levels in children aged 6–59 months and a number of risk factors, such as altitude, child age, region, housing materials, household wealth index, mosquito net usage, sex, recent fever, and malaria status. Quantile regression has an advantage over binary logistic regression in that it makes it possible to ascertain how the independent factors affect the full distribution of the dependent variable.ResultsThe results of the investigation showed that risk factors had different effects on haemoglobin levels depending on the distribution. Children with substandard housing materials, those from the poorest households, and those from certain locations (north-east, north-west, and south-east) had considerably lower haemoglobin levels, especially at the lower quantiles. Higher haemoglobin levels were linked to protective factors such as growing older, being a woman, sleeping beneath mosquito nets, not having had a recent illness, and having a negative malaria status; these effects were frequently more noticeable at quantiles. These results demonstrate how the most vulnerable children bear a disproportionate amount of the anaemia burden.ConclusionQuantile regression provided a nuanced understanding of the determinants of childhood anaemia, revealing that socio-economic disadvantage, regional disparities, and malaria infection exert the greatest impact among children with the lowest haemoglobin levels. Universal and targeted interventions addressing poverty, housing quality, malaria prevention, and equitable access to health resources are essential to reduce the burden of anaemia and promote health equity.RecommendationsInterventions for the most vulnerable children should be given top priority by policymakers and medical professionals, especially for those living in high-burden areas and the quintiles with the lowest incomes. Improving socioeconomic conditions, expanding access to high-quality healthcare and nutrition, and improving malaria control measures (such universal mosquito net coverage) are all important strategies. It is advised that more studies employ quantile regression to track the reduction of childhood anaemia and inform focused public health initiatives.

Introduction

Anaemia is considered a most life-threatening public health concern [6, 9, 19, 24], especially in developing countries, where children are more vulnerable [6, 29]. In Nigeria and around the world, the incidence of anaemia in children, especially those under five, remains a serious problem [24, 29]. It is a severe burden on underprivileged children with long-term health and socio-economic consequences [24]. Anaemia can be defined in many ways [5, 9, 14, 21]. This current study defines anaemia as the low blood haemoglobin concentration [5, 29]. Childhood haemoglobin (Hb) concentration level is commonly classified based on the cut-off point [9] as follows \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Hb<7.0g/dL$$\end{document} as severe anemia, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10.0g/dL\le Hb\le 10.9g/dL$$\end{document} as mild, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$7.0g/dL\le Hb\le 9.9g/dL$$\end{document} as moderate anemia, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Hb\ge 11.0g/dL$$\end{document} as non-anemic [9, 29].

According to several research in the literature, raising the mother's household income and educational attainment will lower her likelihood of developing anaemia [6, 8, 9, 13, 17]. The likelihood of childhood anaemia is also increased by the child's age, family size, and exposure to malaria, according to additional studies [5, 12]. A study by Ogunsakin et al. [24] suggested that the policymakers in Nigeria should apply a pragmatic approach to help address the menace of childhood anaemia [24]. According to the Nigeria Indicator Malaria Survey report [23], the percentage of low haemoglobin levels among children aged between 6–59 months old decreased from 13% in 2010 to 8% in 2021 [23]. This suggests that research like the current study is necessary to uncover some of the risk factors connected to anaemia in order to help policymakers and government health organisations further build measures to reduce the prevalence of anaemia in Nigerian children.

Several studies in literature used the binary as a response variable where a child is either anaemic or not anaemic [5, 6, 14], to name a few. All these studies considered the mean average of the dependent variable or Gaussian distribution [9]. However, the upper and lower quantiles of the outcome variables may depend on the covariates differently from the centre [9]. The inability to analyse the quantiles of interest is a drawback of the aforementioned models. The goal of the current study is to examine the influence of predictor variables on the various quantiles of interest in childhood haemoglobin levels using quantile regression, which offers flexibility.

This study's primary goal is to use quantile regression models to identify the risk factors linked to the prevalence of childhood anaemia. At the same time, multiple imputations using chained equations (MICE) are used to address missing observations.

Methods and materials

Data source

Study design

This population-based cross-sectional study used secondary data from demographic health surveys (DHS).

Study population

The Nigeria Malaria Indicator Survey (NMIS) data from 2021 were used in this investigation. The National Malaria Elimination Programme (NMEP) of the Federal Ministry of Health (FMoH), in partnership with the National Population Commission (NPC) and the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS), conducted the 2021 Nigeria Malaria Indicator Survey (NMIS), which is used in this study [23]. For the 2021 NMIS, a two-phase sampling approach was used. A probability proportional to the enumeration area (EA) size was used to choose 568 EAs in the first stage. The number of households that call the EA home determines its size. To make the sample representative of each state, it was carefully chosen. As a consequence, there were 568 clusters nationwide, 195 in cities and 373 in rural areas [23].

The 2021 NMIS employed three questionnaires: the Biomarker Questionnaire, the Woman's Questionnaire, and the Household Questionnaire [23]. The questionnaires were modified to represent the population and health issues pertinent to Nigeria, based on the model questionnaires developed by the DHS Program. Following their completion in English, the surveys were translated into Hausa, Yoruba, and Igbo [23]. The National Health Research Ethics Committee of Nigeria (NHREC) and the ICF Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved the survey protocol [23].

The data from the DHS was from the 2021 NIMS, and the children sample was selected from the household recode [23]. The total population of children was 9648,according to the NIMS report, the selected children for anaemia were between 6–59 months old [23]. A thorough case study was carried out, and some predictors had missing observations. After that, quantile regression was used to model the data, and imputation was performed to address the missing observations. The imputation techniques that can be used with missing data are described in the section below.

However, this study compared the complete case and imputed data based on statistics of the standard error resulting from the model. Multiple imputation via chained equations

The assumption was that the data was missing at random (MAR) [20]. Unbiased results with accurate estimates for the standard errors (SE) are obtained, when multiple imputation is applied to a dataset, that is MAR,[25]. In this study, multiple imputation by chained equations was used. Chained equations are also known as fully conditional specification (FCS) [10], and it is the algorithm adopted by the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) Enterprise. This approach is more flexible to imputation since it is designed to handle different types of variables (binary, continuous, categorical, and ordinal) and does not assume the multivariate normality of the data [10, 18]. Assuming the multivariate normality can often lead to truncation and inflated standard errors or wrongful inference with the loss of information [10]. As applied in SAS, imputation by FCS is also an iterative process that starts by imputing every missing value with random draws from the distribution of the non-missing values [10]. SAS Enterprise Guide version 8.3 was used to perform the imputation process.

Variables

Dependent variable

The outcome variable of interest is the haemoglobin concentration in blood measured in grams per decilitre (g/dL). The NIMS 2021 provided adjusted and unadjusted childhood haemoglobin concentration levels to altitude; this study selected the adjusted haemoglobin.

Independent variables

The independent variables used in this study were also used in various studies modelling childhood anaemia [6, 9, 14, 24, 26] among others. This serves as the theoretical foundation for the current investigation. Demographic, geographic, and socioeconomic factors were used to categorise these independent variables: the results of the malaria rapid diagnostic test (RDT), the mother's educational attainment, the household's electricity status, the wealth index, the number of children who slept under a mosquito net the night before the survey, the knowledge of malaria prevention methods, the child's sex, the status of child fever in the two weeks prior to the survey, the altitude level, the child's age in months.

Statistical analysis

Box-plot

Based on a study by Hossain and Majumder [11], a boxplot is very important if the analysis is required to deal with a large dataset. The study by Talukder et al. [28] explored the boxplot for the response variable used in the study. The boxplot uses a five-number summary to give information about the dataset being used. The boxplot summarizes the data measured on an interval scale that can be used to identify if the distribution is skewed and if there are outliers in the data being used [28]. The outliers can be visualized by the graphical plot that is produced from the analysis. This study used the boxplot to detect normality from the dataset. Based on the findings of the boxplot, quantile regression was used to analyse the association between the independent and dependent variables. The analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 24.

The results from the analysis represented in Table 4 show that the baseline values for the haemoglobin level at the different quantiles consistently increases from the lower quantile (q = 0.25) to the upper quantile (q = 0.95) when all the other covariates are kept at a value of zero. Discussion

The advantage of quantile regression over logistic regression (binary) is that it allows for the independent variables' impact on the dependent variable's complete distribution to be determined [9]. Furthermore, outliers and extreme data are usually less influential in quantile regression due to the inherent robustness of the model [9, 28]. The prevalence of anaemia in children under 5 years in Nigeria influenced the study that was carried out to determine some of the risk factors that might influence the high prevalence of anaemia in children. The DHS household and NIMS 2021 were merged to create a pooled sample. The results were obtained from SAS and SPSS software. A quantile regression model was performed in SPSS to determine the risk factors associated with anaemia levels in children between 6 and 59 months old. The results from the model showed that altitude level, age of the child in months, region, primary wall material, main roof material, main wall material, household wealth index, number of children who slept under a mosquito net the night before the survey, sex of the child, whether the child had fever or not in the past two weeks before the survey, and child’s malaria status.

In general, this study's findings are consistent with those of comparable research. According to the study, children from households with different altitudes have a positive (marginal) steadily increasing haemoglobin levels, suggesting that they are less likely to suffer from anaemia. This suggests that altitude levels have a minimal influence in childhood haemoglobin levels. It was observed that as the child's age increases, the child's haemoglobin level also increases; this implies that as children grow, the risk of experiencing anaemia also decreases. The finding is similar to that in other studies [6, 14]. According to the current study's findings, children from the north-eastern, north-western, and south-eastern regions of Nigeria have lower haemoglobin levels than those from the south-western region. This suggests that the geography also affects haemoglobin levels. This further suggests that anaemia is more common in children from north-eastern, north-west, and south-east Nigeria. The region was significant in the current study, showing a consistent result similar to another study conducted by Lemoine and Tounian [19].

The haemoglobin levels of children from families with primary floor material that is natural or other have lower haemoglobin levels at the highest (95th), and lower (25th) percentile, respectively, as compared to children from a household where the primary floor material is rudimentary. The haemoglobin levels of children from families with primary wall material that is finished have higher haemoglobin levels at the highest percentile as compared to children from a household where the primary wall material is rudimentary. Children from households where the principal roof material is composed of natural or finished materials, respectively, have higher levels of haemoglobin than children from homes where the predominant roof material is rudimentary, putting them at lower risk of having anaemia.

The results further show that children from the poorest households show a decreasing haemoglobin level at the lowest percentiles, compared to those from the richest households. This implies that children from the poorest households are most likely to have anaemia than those from the richest households. According to the study's findings, male children are more likely than female children to suffer from childhood anaemia. This result is consistent with other research findings in the literature [21, 24]. On the contrary, studies by Habyarimana et al. [9] and Khulu & Ramroop [14] found that anaemia was higher among female children than males. The results show that fever negatively impacts haemoglobin levels,therefore, a child with a fever is more likely to have anaemia. This is consistent with results found in other studies [5, 9]. In contrast, the studies by Gaston et al. [5] and Habyarimana et al. [9] compared the children with fever to those without fever. The positive outcome of fever negatively impacts the haemoglobin level. It could be influenced because fever is mainly associated with other morbidity, such as malaria, cough, and diarrhoea, which also affect childhood anaemia [9].

The results show that a mosquito net positively impacts haemoglobin levels; therefore, a child from a household where all the children slept under a mosquito net the night before the survey is less likely to have anaemia than a child from a family where only some children slept under a mosquito net. The result is consistent with what Gaston et al. [6] found. This might be influenced by mosquito nets being used to prevent mosquito bites, which leads to malaria. Malaria is also one of the comorbidities of anaemia [9]. The results of the study further imply that households that are favourable in terms of socio-economic can easily have proper facilities and can afford a comfortable lifestyle. The result is similar to other studies [6, 9, 24].

The study's findings further showed a significant effect of the household wealth index on childhood haemoglobin levels. Children born to low-income families have been found to have an increased risk of childhood anaemia compared to children born in wealthy families. This might be influenced by the fact that wealthy families can afford to buy nutritious foods, which may help to prevent anaemia [7–9, 21, 22]. Lastly, the results showed that the results from malaria RDT significantly affect childhood haemoglobin levels. A child who tested negative for malaria had a reduced chance of being anaemic. A study that was focused on malaria risk factors showed that an anaemic child had a high chance of being diagnosed with malaria compared to those who were not anaemic [4].

Study Limitations

The current study used cross-sectional data, and one of the disadvantages of this type of dataset is the inability to determine causality. Therefore, a longitudinal study is suggested to address this problem.

Recommendations

The study suggests improving household facilities such as toilets and building materials. Furthermore, they suggest the prevention of fever in children under 5 years, eradicating illiteracy within the mothers/caregivers, and poverty among the different households. The above can be achieved through encouragement of caregivers to attend mandatory training at clinics or nearby schools, where the different factors can be discussed and how to better prevent anaemia. Media announcements, podcasts, and flyers can also be the best way to get the message across within the high-risk areas. Conclusion

The current study’s main aim was to identify the risk factors that influence anaemia in children from Nigeria who are between 0 and 59 months old. Quantile regression was used to determine the different impact on different percentiles of haemoglobin levels i.e. 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th percentiles. The findings of the study suggest that the following factors influence the haemoglobin levels in children, which then leads to anaemia: altitude level, age of the child in months, region, primary wall material, main roof material, main wall material, household wealth index, number of children who slept under a mosquito net the night before the survey, sex of the child, whether the child had fever or not in the past two weeks before the survey, and child’s malaria status.

This study demonstrates the value of quantile regression in uncovering the nuanced and heterogeneous effects of socio-demographic and environmental factors on childhood haemoglobin levels in Nigeria. Unlike traditional binary logistic regression, quantile regression enabled the identification of risk factors whose impact varies across the entire distribution of haemoglobin, providing a more comprehensive understanding of childhood anaemia.

Key findings indicate that children from the poorest households, those living in regions such as the north-east, north-west, and south-east, and those with suboptimal housing materials are disproportionately affected at the lower quantiles of haemoglobin distribution, highlighting their heightened vulnerability to anaemia. Conversely, protective factors such as increasing age, female gender, absence of recent fever, sleeping under mosquito nets, and negative malaria status are associated with higher haemoglobin levels, with their effects often more pronounced at specific quantiles.

Clinically, these results underscore the importance of targeted interventions. Efforts to reduce childhood anaemia should prioritize the most disadvantaged groups, particularly those at the lowest haemoglobin quantiles by addressing poverty, improving housing conditions, enhancing malaria prevention, and ensuring equitable access to health-promoting resources. The quantile-specific approach also suggests that universal interventions, such as promoting mosquito net use and malaria testing, can yield substantial benefits across the population, but may be especially impactful for those at greatest risk.

Overall, this analysis provides actionable insights for policymakers and health practitioners, advocating for both targeted and population-wide strategies to effectively reduce the burden of childhood anaemia and promote health equity in Nigeria.
