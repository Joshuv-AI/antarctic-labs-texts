Memory effects in a dynamical decoupling process by parity kicks
S. C. Hou, X. Y. Zhang, Si-wen Li, X. X. Yi

Abstract
We establish a simple quantitative relationship between the environmental memory effects and the characteristics in a dynamical decoupling process. In contrast to previous works, our measures of non-Markovianity are tailored and extended to evaluate the strength of memory effects in dynamical decoupling. We find that if each kick commutes with the dynamical map of the uncontrolled system, then the change of the final dynamical map or the final state brought by the control (called the “effect of control”) is upper (lower) bounded by the summation (difference) of the strengths of memory effects with and without control. Our finding is illustrated with a dissipative quantum Rabi model by numerical simulations where one or many cycles of parity kicks are implemented on the qubit. Besides, the results show that under certain conditions, the effect of control or the increase of performance by the control may be simply proportional to the strength of memory effects with or without control.

Intuitively, the dynamical decoupling of a near-Markovian quantum process leads to a low efficiency and very weak

performance brought by the control and proportional to the strength of memory effects in the controlled or
uncontrolled process.

Memory effects with specific time instants

Intuitively, there exists some special time instants in a dynamical decoupling process
that are closely related to the environmental memory effects.
For example, in one cycle of parity kicks described by Eq. (
7
), the information about
the system’s history in
[0,\tau]
[stored in
\rho_{SE}(\tau)
] is exploited to time-reverse the
dynamics after the first kick and ultimately eliminate the influence of environment at
2\tau
(after the second kick).
This motivates us to focus on the memory effects associated with specific time instants (e.g.,
t_{1}=\tau
and
t_{2}=2\tau
in a
parity kick cycle), rather than maximize the memory effects over all the possible

t_{1}
and
t_{2}
in a time interval like
[0,\infty]
or
[0,t_{max}]
. Such a treatment is
expected to help us establish simple and quantitative connections between the dynamical decoupling
characteristics and strength of memory effects. Moreover, the computational effort would be significantly
reduced since the maximization over the time instants are not required.

According to Eq. (
24
), one can quantify the strength of memory effects (in terms of dynamical maps)
associated with two specific time instants
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
(let
t_{0}
be fixed) simply by

\displaystyle N_{M}^{t_{1:2}}=D[\rho_{T(t_{2},t_{0})},\rho_{T(t_{2},t_{1})T(t_{1},t_{0})}],

(26)

regardless of the system initial state. Similarly, the strength of memory effects
with specific
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
conditioned on the initial state
\rho_{S}(t_{0})
is
given by

\displaystyle N_{M}^{t_{1:2}}[\rho_{S}(t_{0})]=D[\rho_{S}(t_{2}),\rho^{\prime}_{S}(t_{2})].

(27)

Obviously, the above two measures are special cases of Eq. (
24
) and (
25
)
with the same physical interpretations and satisfy
0\leqslant N_{M}^{t_{1:2}}\leqslant N_{M}\leqslant 1
and

0\leqslant N_{M}^{t_{1:2}}[\rho_{S}(t_{0})]\leqslant N_{M}[\rho_{S}(t_{0})]\leqslant 1
. Any nonzero

N_{M}^{t_{1:2}}
or
N_{M}^{t_{1:2}}[\rho_{S}(t_{0})]
is a sufficient condition for non-Markovianity.
The strengths of memory effects defined by Eq. (
26
) and (
27
) can
be straightforwardly applied to Eq. (
7
) by letting
t_{1}=\tau
(after the first kick)
and
t_{2}=2\tau
(after the second kick) to evaluate to what degree the system’s final dynamical map

T(t_{2},t_{0})
[or the final state
\rho_{S}(t_{2})
] depends on its history before the first kick.

To evaluate the strength of memory effects in a dynamical decoupling process with more than 2 kicks, e.g.,
more than one cycle of Eq. (
7
), we extend Eq. (
26
) and (
27
)
to their multi-time forms. Notice that the divisibility condition Eq. (
19
) for a quantum Markov process
can be extended to

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\Lambda_{M}(t_{n},t_{0})=\Lambda_{M}(t_{n},t_{n\!-\!1})\cdots\Lambda_{M}(t_{2},t_{1})\Lambda_{M}(t_{1},t_{0})

(28)

for
t_{0}\leqslant t_{1}\cdots\leqslant t_{n}
. Following similar procedures discussed above, we arrive at
the non-Markovian multi-time inequality in terms of the dynamical map
T(t_{b},t_{a})
:

\displaystyle T(t_{n},t_{0})\neq T(t_{n},t_{n-1})\cdots T(t_{2},t_{1})T(t_{1},t_{0}).\quad

(29)

The multi-time inequality can also be interpreted as memory effects from the perspective of
the environment as follows. Let the left-hand and the right-hand sides of Eq. (
29
)
act on a system initial state
\rho_{S}(t_{0})
so that
\rho_{S}(t_{n})=T(t_{n},t_{0})\rho_{S}(t_{0})
and

\rho^{\prime}_{S}(t_{2})=T(t_{n},t_{n-1})T(t_{n-1},t_{n-2})\cdots T(t_{1},t_{0})\rho_{S}(t_{1})
. The system-environment evolutions
described by the latter equation can be understood as that by the former equation subjecting to
n-1

initializations of the environment state at
t_{1,2,\cdots,n-1}
. The initialization at
t_{j}

erases the system’s history in the time interval
[t_{j-1},t_{j}]
as done in Eq. (
23
).
Therefore,
\rho_{S}(t_{n})\neq\rho^{\prime}_{S}(t_{n})
signifies that the environment (as well as the
system-environment correlations) remembers the system’s histories
which in turn influences the future system at
t_{n}
. Then, the strength of memory effects with

n
specific time instants
t_{1,2,\cdots,n}
(
t_{0}
is fixed) is given by

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!N_{M}^{t_{1:n}}=D[\rho_{T(\!t_{n},t_{0}\!)},\rho_{T(t_{n},t_{n\!-\!1})\cdots T(t_{2},t_{1})T(t_{1},t_{0})}].

(30)

Similarly, the strength of memory effects with specific
t_{1,2,\cdots,n}

conditioned on a particular system initial state
\rho_{S}(t_{0})
is given by

\displaystyle N_{M}^{t_{1:n}}[\rho_{S}(t_{0})]=D[\rho_{S}(t_{2}),\rho^{\prime}_{S}(t_{2})]

(31)

where
\rho_{S}(t_{2})
and
\rho^{\prime}_{S}(t_{2})
are obtained from Eq. (
29
) as mentioned before.
Then the strengths of memory effects with many specific time instants can be applied to a
general dynamical decoupling processes described by one or more cycles of Eq. (
9
)
by letting
t_{0}=0
, and
t_{j}=j\tau
(the time instant after each kick). Within the definitions
Eq. (
30
) and (
31
), it is easy to see that
0\leqslant N_{M}^{t_{1:n}}\leqslant 1
and

0\leqslant N_{M}^{t_{1:n}}[\rho_{S}(t_{0})]\leqslant 1
are satisfied. However, the values of
N_{M}^{t_{1:2}}
and

N_{M}^{t_{1:n}}
may not be compared directly since the number of environment initializations
are different in the two measures, so do
N_{M}^{t_{1:2}}[\rho_{S}(t_{0})]
and
N_{M}^{t_{1:n}}[\rho_{S}(t_{0})]
.

IV
Memory effects and dynamical decoupling

It is known that a non-Markovian environment is necessary for the success of a dynamical decoupling
control of an open quantum system. Therefore, a system with weak memory effects may lead to failure of
dynamical decoupling. On the other hand, in an ideal dynamical decoupling process where
infinitely frequent (
t_{c}\rightarrow 0
) instantaneous kicks act on the system, the system may be perfectly
decoupled from the environment so that the controlled dynamics becomes effectively unitary and thus Markovian.
These seemingly contradictive results motivate us to consider the memory effects in both uncontrolled and
controlled processes to better understand their roles in a dynamical decoupling process.

We assume a separable system-environment initial state of the form
Eq. (
16
) with a fixed environment initial state and
an arbitrary system initial state. Then any uncontrolled system evolutions
starting from
t_{a}
can be expressed in terms of Eq. (
18
) as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\bar{\rho}_{S}(t_{b})

\displaystyle\!=\!

\displaystyle\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})\rho_{S}(t_{a})

(32)

\displaystyle\!=\!

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{E}[e^{-iH_{0}(t_{b}-t_{a})}\rho_{S}(t_{a})\otimes\rho_{E}(t_{a})e^{iH_{0}(t_{b}-t_{a})}].

where
H_{0}
and
\rho_{E}(t_{a})
are given by Eq. (
1
) and (
17
).
Similarly, the system evolution starting from
t_{a}
under the controlled total Hamiltonian Eq. (
3
) is given by

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{b})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(t_{b},t_{a})\rho_{S}(t_{a})

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{E}[U(t_{b},t_{a})\rho_{S}(t_{a})\otimes\rho_{E}(t_{a})U(t_{b},t_{a})^{{\dagger}}]

where
U(t_{b},t_{a})=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{t_{a}}^{t_{b}}H_{c}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}
. For example, for a cycle of ideal parity kicks
starting at
t_{a}=0
, there is
U(\tau,0)=U(2\tau,\tau)=K_{P}e^{-iH_{0}\tau}
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{j}\rho=K_{j}\rho K_{j}^{{\dagger}}
where
\mathcal{K}_{j}
represents the dynamical map describing
the action of an ideal kick
K_{j}
on a density matrix
\rho
. For example, for parity kicks,
there is
\mathcal{K}_{j}\rho=\mathcal{K}_{P}\rho=K_{P}\rho K_{P}^{{\dagger}}
. It is seen that

\displaystyle\tilde{T}[j\tau,(j-1)\tau]=\mathcal{K}_{j}\bar{T}[j\tau,(j-1)\tau]

(34)

for a dynamical decoupling process stating at
t=0
where
j=1,2,\cdots,n
.

Furthermore, we refer to

\displaystyle E=D[\rho_{\tilde{T}(t_{f},0)},\rho_{\bar{T}(t_{f},0)}]

(35)

as the effect of a dynamical decoupling control (from
0
to
t_{f}=kt_{c}
)
on the dynamical map
T(t_{f},0)
. It represents the difference between
the controlled (practically attainable) dynamical map
\tilde{T}(t_{f},0)
and the
uncontrolled one
\bar{T}(t_{f},0)
. Correspondingly, the effect of a dynamical decoupling control
on the final state is given by

\displaystyle E[\rho_{S}(0)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D[\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{f}),\bar{\rho}_{S}(t_{f})]

(36)

where
\rho_{S}(0)
is the initial state,
\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{f})=\tilde{T}(t_{f},0)\rho_{S}(0)
is
the controlled final state and
\bar{\rho}_{S}(t_{f})=\bar{T}(t_{f},0)\rho_{S}(0)
is uncontrolled one.
Within the notations introduced above, we will explore the quantitative connections between the
characteristics of a dynamical decoupling task and the strengths of memory effects (in both
controlled and uncontrolled evolutions).

IV.1
One cycle of parity kicks

Let
\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}
denote the strength of memory effects for one cycle of
parity kicks where
t_{1}=\tau
and
t_{2}=t_{c}=2\tau
(after each kick) and
t_{0}=0
.
Using the condition Eq. (
34
),
\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D[\rho_{\tilde{T}(2\tau,0)},\rho_{\tilde{T}(2\tau,\tau)\tilde{T}(\tau,0)}]

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D[\rho_{\tilde{T}(2\tau,0)},\rho_{\mathcal{K}_{P}\bar{T}(2\tau,\tau)\mathcal{K}_{P}\bar{T}(\tau,0)}].

In contrast, for the same process but without control, the strength of memory effects
with the same time instants, denoted by
\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}
, is given by

\displaystyle\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D[\rho_{\bar{T}(2\tau,0)},\rho_{\bar{T}(2\tau,\tau)\bar{T}(\tau,0)}].

(38)

It is observed that if
\mathcal{K}_{P}
commutes with
\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})
,
i.e.,
\mathcal{K}_{P}\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})=\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})\mathcal{K}_{P}
,
then the effect of two parity kicks
E=D[\rho_{\tilde{T}(2\tau,0)},\rho_{\bar{T}(2\tau,0)}]

is bounded by
\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}
and
\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}
as

\displaystyle|\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}-\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}|\leqslant E\leqslant\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}+\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}},

(39)

which is a main finding of our work. The relation can be straightforwardly proved by virtue
of the condition
\mathcal{K}_{P}T(2\tau,\tau)\mathcal{K}_{P}T(\tau,0)=\mathcal{K}_{P}\mathcal{K}_{P}\bar{T}(2\tau,\tau)\bar{T}(\tau,0)=\bar{T}(2\tau,\tau)\bar{T}(\tau,0)
and
the triangle inequality

\displaystyle D(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\leqslant D(\rho_{1},\rho_{3})+D(\rho_{2},\rho_{3})

(40)

that the trace distance obeys. The above result shows that under certain conditions,
the upper and lower bounds of the control effect (on the final dynamical map) is
determined by the strength of memory effects in both the controlled and uncontrolled
process. Meanwhile, the difference between the controlled and uncontrolled dynamical
map is a manifestation of memory effects since
E=0
if

\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}=\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}=0
.

If we are interested in the quantum states rather than the dynamical maps in a cycle of
parity kicks, the above results can be formulated following similar steps.
As discussed above, the strength of memory effects in one cycle of parity kicks
with the initial state
\rho_{S}(0)
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}[\rho_{S}(0)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D[\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{c}),\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}_{S}(t_{c})]

(41)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{c})=\tilde{T}(2\tau,0)\rho_{S}(0)
and
\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}_{S}(t_{c})=\tilde{T}(2\tau,\tau)\tilde{T}(\tau,0)\rho_{S}(0)=\mathcal{K}_{P}\bar{T}(2\tau,\tau)\mathcal{K}_{P}\bar{T}(\tau,0)\rho_{S}(0)
.
In the absence of control, the strength of memory effects with the same time instants is

\displaystyle\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}[\rho_{S}(0)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D[\bar{\rho}_{S}(t_{c}),\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{S}(t_{c})]

(42)

where
\bar{\rho}_{S}(t_{c})=\bar{T}(2\tau,0)\rho_{S}(0)
and
\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{S}(t_{c})=\bar{T}(2\tau,\tau)\bar{T}(\tau,0)\rho_{S}(0)
.
Then the effect of control on the final states
E[\rho_{S}(0)]=D[\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{c}),\bar{\rho}_{S}(t_{c})]
satisfies

\displaystyle|\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}[\rho_{S}(0)]-\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}[\rho_{S}(0)]|\leqslant E[\rho_{S}(0)]

\displaystyle\leqslant\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}[\rho_{S}(0)]+\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}[\rho_{S}(0)]

(43)

if
\mathcal{K}_{P}
commutes with
\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})
. Similarly, the change of the final states
(brought by control) is upper and lower bounded by the memory effects in the
controlled and uncontrolled process. Meanwhile,
E[\rho_{S}(0)]>0

is a manifestation of memory effects.

IV.2
General case

Our theory can be generated to a general dynamical decoupling process with
n
kicks (
n\geqslant 2
),
specifically, one or more cycles of Eq. (
9
) where the kicks are designed by
Eq. (
II.2
). Consider a dynamical decoupling process (from 0 to
t_{f}
) with
k

cycles while each cycle contains
n_{g}
kicks. When
k>1
, the kicks satisfies
K_{j+n_{g}}=K_{j}
.
The process can be described by
\tilde{T}(n\tau,0)
where
n=kn_{g}
. Let
t_{0}=0
and
t_{j}=j\tau

(after each kick), then the strength of memory effects in the dynamical decoupling process satisfies

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!D\{\rho_{\tilde{T}(n\tau,0)},\rho_{\tilde{T}[n\tau,(n\!-\!1)\tau]\cdots\tilde{T}(2\tau,\tau)\tilde{T}(\tau,0)}\}

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!D\{\!\rho_{\tilde{T}(n\tau,0)},\rho_{\mathcal{K}_{n}\!\bar{T}[n\tau,(\!n\!-\!1\!)\tau]\cdots\mathcal{K}_{2}\!\bar{T}(2\tau,\tau)\mathcal{K}_{1}\!\bar{T}(\tau,0)}\!\}

following the definition Eq. (
30
) and the condition Eq. (
34
).
Using the same time instants
t_{1,2,\cdots,n}
, the strength of memory effects without
control is given by

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!D\{\rho_{\bar{T}(n\tau,0)},\rho_{\bar{T}[n\tau,(n\!-\!1)\tau]\cdots\bar{T}(2\tau,\tau)\bar{T}(\tau,0)}\}.

(45)

With the condition Eq. (
15
) and the triangle inequality of trace distance,
it is straightforward to verify that if each
\mathcal{K}_{j}
commutes with
\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})
, then
the effect of control on the dynamical map satisfies

\displaystyle|\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}-\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}|\leqslant E\leqslant\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}+\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}.

(46)

The result reduces to Eq.(
39
) when
n=2
(
k=1
and
n_{g}=2
) and can be applied
to more than one cycle of parity kicks (
k>1
and
n_{g}=2
). If
n>2
, the interpretation
of
\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}
(
\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}
) as memory effects is slightly different with
that of
\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}
(
\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}
) as discussed in Sec.
III
B.

Similarly, the strength of memory effects for a general dynamical decoupling process
with the initial state
\rho_{S}(0)
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}[\rho_{S}(0)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D[\tilde{\rho}_{S}(kt_{c}),\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}_{S}(kt_{c})]

(47)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{S}(kt_{c})=\tilde{T}(n\tau,0)\rho_{S}(0)
and
\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}_{S}(kt_{c})=\tilde{T}[n\tau,(n-1)\tau]\cdots\tilde{T}(2\tau,\tau)\tilde{T}(\tau,0)\rho_{S}(0)=\mathcal{K}_{n}\tilde{T}[n\tau,(n-1)\tau]\cdots\mathcal{K}_{2}T(2\tau,\tau)\mathcal{K}_{1}T(\tau,0)\rho_{S}(0)
. In the uncontrolled process, the strength of memory effects is

\displaystyle\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}[\rho_{S}(0)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D[\bar{\rho}_{S}(kt_{c}),\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{S}(kt_{c})]

(48)

where
\bar{\rho}_{S}(kt_{c})=\bar{T}(n\tau,0)\rho_{S}(0)
and

\bar{\rho}^{\prime}_{S}(t_{c})=\bar{T}[n\tau,(n-1)\tau]\cdots\bar{T}(2\tau,\tau)\bar{T}(\tau,0)\rho_{S}(0)
.
At last, the effect of a general dynamical decoupling control on the final state, i.e.,

E[\rho_{S}(0)]=D[\tilde{\rho}_{S}(kt_{c}),\bar{\rho}_{S}(kt_{c})]
, satisfies

\displaystyle|\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}[\rho_{S}(0)]-\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}[\rho_{S}(0)]|\leqslant E[\rho_{S}(0)]

\displaystyle\leqslant\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}[\rho_{S}(0)]+\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}[\rho_{S}(0)]

(49)

if each
\mathcal{K}_{j}
commutes with
\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})
.

IV.3
Effect and performance of control

Let us further discuss the meaning of
E
and
E[\rho_{S}(0)]
mentioned above. Consider that the target of a
dynamical decoupling control is to realize a particular type of dynamical maps or quantum states at
t_{f}=kt_{c}
.
In this work, our target dynamical map
T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)
and target state
T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)

at
t_{f}
are defined by

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)\rho_{S}(0)

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-iH_{S}t_{f}}\rho_{S}(0)e^{iH_{S}t_{f}}

which are the unitary operation governed by
H_{S}
and the corresponding final state conditioned on the
initial state
\rho_{S}(0)
. In ideal cases, e.g., continuous ideal kicks, or a proper total Hamiltonian

H_{0}
with ideal kicks that leads to perfect decoupling for any
t_{c}
, there are
\tilde{T}(t_{f},0)=T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)
and

\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{f})=\rho_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f})
. Then the effect of control can be interpreted as
the decrease of the distance between
T(t_{f},0)
and
T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)
or that between

\rho_{S}(t_{f})
and
\rho_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f})
brought by the control. In other words, the effect of
control represents the increase of the performance defined by

\displaystyle P=1-D[\rho_{T(t_{f},0)},\rho_{T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)}]

(51)

\displaystyle\mathrm{or}\ \

\displaystyle P[\rho_{S}(0)]=1-D[\rho_{S}(t_{f}),\rho_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f})]

(52)

brought by the control where
\rho_{T(t_{f},0)}
[
\rho_{S}(t_{f})
] represents the final dynamical
map (final state) without or with control. For example, for perfect decoupling,
\Delta P=\tilde{P}-\bar{P}=E

where
\tilde{P}=1-D[\rho_{\tilde{T}(t_{f},0)},\rho_{T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)}]=1
is the performance
with control, and
\bar{P}=1-D[\rho_{\bar{T}(t_{f},0)},\rho_{T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)}]
is that
without control. The interpretation approximately holds
if
\tilde{T}(t_{f},0)\approx T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)
and

D[\rho_{\tilde{T}(t_{f},0)},\rho_{\bar{T}(t_{f},0)}]\gg D[\rho_{\tilde{T}(t_{f},0)},\rho_{T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)}]
.
In terms of quantum states, these conditions are
\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{f})\approx\rho_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f})
and

D[\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{f}),\bar{\rho}_{S}(t_{f})]\gg D[\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{f}),\rho_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f})]
.
In the case of ineffective dynamical decoupling, i.e.,
\tilde{T}(t_{f},0)\neq T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)

or
\tilde{\rho}_{S}(t_{f})\neq\rho_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f})
the above interpretation may still
hold if the control makes the uncontrolled final dynamical map (state) move toward the target one.

Besides its relationship with the control performance,
any nonzero
E
or
E[\rho_{S}(0)]
is a manifestation of non-Markovianity (under certain conditions).
If Eq. (
46
) or (
49
) can be reduced to some simpler forms,
the effect
E
or
E[\rho_{S}(0)]
may directly represent the strength of
memory effects in the controlled or uncontrolled dynamics.

V
Example

V.1
Model and control

In this section, we illustrate our theory with a quantum Rabi model where
a qubit interacts with a dissipative harmonic oscillator in a Markovian reservoir
at temperature
T
. The parity kicks are implemented on the qubit, i.e., the system (denoted by
S
),
to protect it from the influence of the environment (the dissipative oscillator, denoted by
E
).
The Hamiltonian of the qubit and oscillator is given by

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{S}+H_{E}+H_{S\!E}

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\omega_{S}\sigma_{z}+\omega_{E}a^{{\dagger}}a+g(\sigma^{+}+\sigma^{-})(a^{{\dagger}}+a).

where
\omega_{S}
(
\omega_{E}
) is the transition frequency of the qubit (oscillator),
\sigma_{z}
is
the Pauli
z
matrix,
g
is the coupling strength,
\sigma-
(
\sigma^{+}
) is the lowering (raising) operator
of the qubit and
a
(
a^{{\dagger}}
) is the annihilation (creation) operator of the oscillator.
The dynamics of the
\rho_{SE}
without control is described by the standard Lindblad master equation

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{S\!E}=\mathcal{L}(\rho_{S\!E})=-i[H_{0},\rho_{S\!E}]+\mathcal{D}(\rho_{S\!E})

(54)

where
\mathcal{D}(\rho_{S\!E})=\gamma(\bar{n}+1)(a\rho_{S\!E}a^{{\dagger}}-\frac{1}{2}a^{{\dagger}}a\rho_{S\!E}-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{S\!E}a^{{\dagger}}a)+\gamma\bar{n}(a^{{\dagger}}\rho_{S\!E}a-\frac{1}{2}aa^{{\dagger}}\rho_{S\!E}-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{S\!E}aa^{{\dagger}})
.
Here
\gamma
is the dissipation rate of the oscillator and
\bar{n}=1/(e^{\beta\omega_{E}}-1)
is the
mean excitation number of the oscillator at temperature
T
where
\beta=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}
.
As discussed before, we consider evolutions starting from the condition
\rho_{SE}(t_{I})=\rho_{S}(t_{I})\otimes\rho_{E}(t_{I})

where
\rho_{S}(t_{I})
and
t_{I}
are arbitrary. Besides, we assume that
\rho_{E}(t_{I})
is governed by

\rho_{E}(t_{I})=e^{\mathcal{L}_{E}t}\rho_{E}(0)
before an evolution where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{E}(\rho_{E})=-i[H_{E},\rho_{E}]+\mathcal{D}(\rho_{E}).

(55)

In this work, we assume that
\rho_{E}(0)
is a thermal state at temperature
T
, then

\rho_{E}(t_{I})
is stationary with respect to Eq.(
55
) and is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{E}(t_{I})=\rho_{E}^{\mathrm{th}}=\sum_{n_{0}=0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{n}}{(1+\bar{n})^{n_{0}+1}}|n_{0}\rangle\langle n_{0}|.

(56)

The parity kicks are chosen as
K_{P}=\sigma_{z}
, which is assumed to be ideal. It leads to an
instantaneous phase change in the qubit’s quantum state. Staring from
t=0
, the controlled dynamics can
be described by

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{S\!E}=\mathcal{L}_{t}(\rho_{S\!E})=-i[H_{0}+H_{1}(t),\rho_{S\!E}]+\mathcal{D}(\rho_{S\!E})

(57)

where
H_{1}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta(t-t_{j})H_{P}
that realizes
K_{P}=\sigma_{z}
at
t_{j}=j\tau
. It is easy
to seen that conditions Eq. (
5
) and (
6
)
are satisfied for our scheme. Therefore, the qubit can be dynamically decoupled from its environment after one
or more cycles of parity kicks if
t_{c}
is short enough. Staring from the condition

\rho_{SE}(0)=\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho_{E}^{\mathrm{th}}
with arbitrary
\rho_{S}(0)
, the Choi-Jamiółkowski matrix of
the target final dynamical map
T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)
is given by

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rho_{T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)}\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\![\mathbb{I}\!\otimes\!T_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f},0)](|\psi_{AS}\rangle\langle\psi_{AS}|)

(58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!(\mathbb{I}\!\otimes\!e^{-iH_{S}t_{f}})(|\psi_{AS}\rangle\langle\psi_{AS}|)(\mathbb{I}\otimes e^{iH_{S}t_{f}})

where
t_{f}=kt_{c}
and
|\psi_{AS}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|g\rangle_{A}|g\rangle_{S}+|e\rangle_{A}|e\rangle_{S})

is the maximally entangled state of the ancillary system and our system. If we are interested in the
control of quantum states, the target final state is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{tar}}(t_{f})=e^{-\frac{i}{2}\omega_{S}\sigma_{z}t_{f}}\rho_{S}(0)e^{\frac{i}{2}\omega_{S}\sigma_{z}t_{f}}.

(59)

Similar control schemes have been theoretically and experimentally investigated
with a damping JC model to study the coherence (decoherence) of the oscillator through the behaviors
of the qubit
Morigi
;
Meunier
. These schemes amount to using one cycle of parity kicks while
omitting the second kick since it does not change the interested observable.

V.2
Commutation relation between
\mathcal{K}_{P}
and
\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})

To investigate the role of memory effects with our theory, the condition

\mathcal{K}_{P}\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})=\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})\mathcal{K}_{P}
is required. In general, the dynamical map

\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})
depends on the master equation (
54
) and the environment initial state

\rho_{E}(t_{I})
. With a thermal environment initial state Eq. (
56
) and
K_{P}=\sigma_{z}
,
we find that
\mathcal{K}_{P}
indeed commutes with
\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})
. This condition can be straightforwardly
verified by numerically simulating Eq. (
54
) before and after the kick (with different qubit initial states),
or directly calculating and comparing the Choi-Jamiółkowski matrices
\rho_{\mathcal{K}_{P}\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})}
and

\rho_{\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})\mathcal{K}_{P}}
by simulations. Besides numerical evidences, we provide
an analytical explanation based on the forms of Eq. (
54
) and Eq. (
56
) and
the properties Eq. (
5
) and (
6
).
In our work, the evolution of the qubit in the absence of control could be understood as

\rho_{S}(t_{b})=\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})\rho_{S}(t_{a})=\text{Tr}_{E,R}[e^{-iH_{S\!E\!R}(t_{b}-t_{a})}\rho_{S}(t_{a})\otimes\rho_{E}^{\mathrm{th}}\otimes\rho_{R}^{\mathrm{th}}e^{iH_{S\!E\!R}(t_{b}-t_{a})}]
where

\displaystyle H_{S\!E\!R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{0}+H_{R}+H_{E\!R}

(60)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{0}+\sum_{j}\omega_{j}b_{j}^{{\dagger}}b_{j}+\sum_{j}(g_{j}ab_{j}^{{\dagger}}+g_{j}^{*}a^{{\dagger}}b_{j})

represents the Hamiltonian of the qubit, the oscillator, a Markovian reservoir (denoted by R)
and their interactions. The reservoir is in a thermal state at temperature
T
, which is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{R}^{\mathrm{th}}=\prod_{j}\sum_{n_{j}=0}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{n}(\omega_{j})}{[1+\bar{n}(\omega_{j})]^{n_{j}+1}}|n_{j}\rangle\langle n_{j}|.

(61)

Then the
E\!-\!R
initial state can be concisely expressed as

\displaystyle\rho_{E}^{\mathrm{th}}\otimes\rho_{R}^{\mathrm{th}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum p_{\mathbf{n}}|\mathbf{n}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n}|

(62)

where
|\mathbf{n}\rangle=|n_{0},n_{1},n_{2}\cdots\rangle
,
p_{\mathbf{n}}=p_{n_{0},n_{1},n_{2}\cdots}
is the diagonal element
in the basis
|\mathbf{n}\rangle
, and all the non-diagonal elements are zero. Now we divide the
S\!-\!E\!-\!R

Hamiltonian into
H_{+}=H_{S}+H_{E}+H_{R}+H_{E\!R}
and
H_{-}=H_{S\!E}
such that
\sigma_{z}H_{+}\sigma_{z}=H_{+}
and

\sigma_{z}H_{-}\sigma_{z}=-H_{-}
. Moreover, it is seen that the action
H_{+}|\tilde{n}\rangle
(
\langle\tilde{n}|H_{+}
)
turns
|\mathbf{n}\rangle
(
\langle\mathbf{n}|
)
into bases with the same total excitation number (or annihilate it), e.g.,
the term
g_{1}ab_{1}^{{\dagger}}
in
H_{+}
turns
|n,n_{1},n_{2}\cdots\rangle
into
|n_{0}-1,n_{1}+1,n_{2}\cdots\rangle
(up to a coefficient)
if
n_{0}\geqslant 1
. In contrast,
H_{-}|\mathbf{n}\rangle
(
\langle\mathbf{n}|H_{-}
) turns
|\mathbf{n}\rangle
(
\langle\mathbf{n}|
)
into bases with one more or one less excitations (or annihilate it). Now the evolution of the qubit in the absence of
control can be described by

\displaystyle\rho_{S}(t_{b})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})\rho_{S}(t_{a})

(63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{n}^{\prime}}\langle\tilde{n}^{\prime}|U_{s}\rho_{S}(t_{a})\otimes(\sum_{\bar{n}}p_{\tilde{n}}|\mathbf{n}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n}|)U_{s}^{{\dagger}}|\tilde{n}^{\prime}\rangle

where
U_{s}=I-i(H_{+}+H_{-})(t_{b}-t_{a})-(H_{+}+H_{-})^{2}(t_{b}-t_{a})^{2}/2+\cdots
is the Taylor series of
e^{-iH_{S\!E\!R}(t_{b}-t_{a})}
.
Note that Eq. (
63
) can be expressed as a summation of infinite terms, i.e.,
\rho_{S}(t_{b})=\sum_{l}\rho_{S}^{(l)}
.
Any term
\rho_{S}^{(l)}
satisfies

\displaystyle\rho_{S}^{(l)}\propto\langle\mathbf{n}^{\prime}|H_{\alpha}\!\cdots\!H_{\alpha}\rho_{S}(t_{a})\otimes|\mathbf{n}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n}|H_{\alpha}\!\cdots\!H_{\alpha}|\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\rangle

(64)

where
\alpha
represents
+
or
-
and the number of
H_{\alpha}
in Eq. (
64
) ranges from 0 to
\infty
.
The coefficient in Eq. (
64
) is ignored for simplicity now and later.

It can be verified that if the number of
H_{-}
in Eq. (
64
) is odd, then
\rho_{S}^{(l)}=0
.
For example, there are three
H_{-}
in the term
H_{-}H_{+}\rho_{S}(t_{a})\otimes|\mathbf{n}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n}|H_{-}H_{-}H_{+}
.
See that
H_{-}H_{+}
turns
|\mathbf{n}\rangle
into bases with 1 more or 1 less excitations
(denoted by
|\mathbf{n},\pm 1\rangle
, or annihilate it), whereas
H_{-}H_{-}H_{+}
turns

\langle\mathbf{n}|
into bases with 2 more, 2 less or invariant excitations (denoted by
\langle\mathbf{n},+0,\pm 2|
,
or annihilated it). Then
\langle\mathbf{n}^{\prime}|\hat{\rho}_{S}\otimes|\mathbf{n},\pm 1\rangle\langle\mathbf{n},+0,\pm 2|\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\rangle=0

where
\hat{\rho}_{S}
is transformed from
\rho_{S}(t_{a})
by the Hamiltonians on the left-hand side of
\rho_{S}(t_{a})
.
Similarly, any term with an odd number of
H_{-}
turns
|\mathbf{n}\rangle
and
\langle\mathbf{n}|
into bases with
different excitation numbers or annihilate one (or two) of them. Then, any term obtained by expanding Eq. (
64
),
if not annihilated, satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{n}^{\prime}|\bar{\rho}_{S}(t_{a})\otimes|\mathbf{n},+n_{\mathrm{odd}}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n},+n_{\mathrm{even}}|\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(65)

\displaystyle\mathrm{or}\ \langle\mathbf{n}^{\prime}|\bar{\rho}_{S}(t_{a})\otimes|\mathbf{n},+n_{\mathrm{even}}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n},+n_{\mathrm{odd}}|\mathbf{n}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(66)

where
n_{\mathrm{odd}}=\pm 1,\pm 3,\pm 5\cdots
and
n_{\mathrm{even}}=0,\pm 2,\pm 4\cdots
. So
\rho_{S}^{(l)}=0
is
guaranteed. On the other hand, if the number of
H_{-}
is even in Eq. (
64
), there is

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}H_{\alpha}\!\cdots\!H_{\alpha}\rho_{S}(t_{a})\otimes|\mathbf{n}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n}|H_{\alpha}\!\cdots\!H_{\alpha}\sigma_{z}

(67)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n_{\mathrm{even}}}H_{\alpha}\!\cdots\!H_{\alpha}\sigma_{z}\rho_{S}(t_{a})\otimes|\mathbf{n}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n}|\sigma_{z}H_{\alpha}\!\cdots\!H_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}\!\cdots\!H_{\alpha}\sigma_{z}\rho_{S}(t_{a})\sigma_{z}\otimes|\mathbf{n}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n}|H_{\alpha}\!\cdots\!H_{\alpha}

since
\sigma_{z}H_{-}=-H_{-}\sigma_{z}
and
\sigma_{z}H_{+}=H_{+}\sigma_{z}
. Now we know that every term in Eq. (
63
) with an even
number of
H_{-}
satisfies Eq. (
67
) for any system initial state. By inserting Eq. (
67
) back into
Eq. (
63
),
\mathcal{K}_{P}\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})=\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})\mathcal{K}_{P}
is obtained.

Following similar analysis or through numerical simulations, it is able to verify that

\mathcal{K}_{P}\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})=\bar{T}(t_{b},t_{a})\mathcal{K}_{P}
might also holds for more related models. For example,
a dissipative (or undamped) JC model, our model where the oscillator’s initial state satisfies

\rho_{E}(t_{I})=\sum_{n_{0}=0}^{\infty}p_{n_{0}}|n_{0}\rangle\langle n_{0}|
(not limited to thermal states),
a qubit coupled to multiple dissipative (or undamped) oscillators,
a qubit coupled to a set of interacting oscillators, etc. Moreover, as known from the pseudomode
theory
Garraway1997A
;
Garraway1997B
and its recent progresses
Trivedi
;
Pleasance
;
Tamascelli
,
the reduced dynamics of an open quantum system with a complex environment (infinite modes) is equivalent
to that with a simpler environment (finite modes each subject to a Lindblad evolution) under certain conditions.
Therefore, it is possible to apply our theory to more complex environments
Albarelli
to study
the role of memory effects in a parity kick process.

\omega_{E}

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we investigate quantitative connections between the environmental memory effects and
the characteristics of a dynamical decoupling process, especially, that controlled by parity kicks.
Considering that there exists two special time instants (corresponding to two kicks) in one cycle of parity
kicks which are linked to the history-past dependence of the system, we tailor our measurements of memory
effects to evaluate the strengths of memory effects associated with two specific time instants. For a
decoupling process with more than 2 specific time instants (e.g., many cycles of parity kicks), we
extend our measurements of memory effects to their multi-time forms. Our measures of memory effects in
terms of dynamical maps and quantum states can be interpreted as the history-past dependence of the system
dynamics and is easy to calculate. Our main finding is that if the dynamical map of each kick commutes
with the system’s dynamical map without control, then the effect of dynamical decoupling
(
E
or
E[\rho_{S}(0)]
) is upper (lower) bounded by the summation (difference) of the strengths
of memory effects with and without control. That means (under certain conditions),
the environmental memory effects with and without control together determine to what degree the system can
be changed, and potentially, the performance (without control) can be increased by the control.
On the other hand, the effect of the control or the increase of the performances brought by
the control is a manifestation of non-Markovianity.

We apply our theory to a dissipative quantum Rabi model where the qubit is controlled by parity kicks and
the oscillator is in a Markovian reservoir at finite temperature. The effects of control, their bounds and the
performances with and without control are numerically simulated and compared in different regimes, which verifies our finding.
Besides, the relationship among these quantities may take simple forms in particular regimes. For example, in
one cycle of parity kicks, there is
\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}\approx\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:2}}\approx 2E\approx 2\Delta P

if the cycle time is short enough. In the case of many cycles, if the frequency of kicks is high enough, there are

\bar{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}\approx E\approx\Delta P
and
\tilde{N}_{M}^{t_{1:n}}\approx 0
. Besides, it is found
that the control might be inefficient with particular kick frequencies. Our work provides new insights into
the role of memory effects in dynamical decoupling and methods to measure the strength of memory effects by
parity kicks.
