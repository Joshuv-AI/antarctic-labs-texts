Nominal Equational Narrowing: Rewriting for Unification in Languages with Binders
Maribel Fernández, Daniele Nantes-Sobrinho, Daniella Santaguida

Abstract
Narrowing extends term rewriting with the ability to search for solutions to equational problems. While first-order rewriting and narrowing are well studied, significant challenges arise in the presence of binders, freshness conditions and equational axioms such as commutativity. This is problematic for applications in programming languages and theorem proving, where reasoning modulo renaming of bound variables, structural congruence, and freshness conditions is needed. To address these issues, we present a framework for nominal rewriting and narrowing modulo equational theories that intrinsically incorporates renaming and freshness conditions. We define and prove a key property called nominal ℰ \mathcal{E} -coherence under freshness conditions, which characterises normal forms of nominal terms modulo renaming and equational axioms. Building on this, we establish the nominal ℰ \mathcal{E} -lifting theorem, linking rewriting and narrowing sequences in the nominal setting. This foundational result enables the development of a nominal unification procedure based on equational narrowing, for which we provide a correctness proof. We illustrate the effectiveness of our approach with examples including symbolic differentiation and simplification of first-order formulas.

1.1
Nominal Equational Reasoning At a Glance

This section highlights the distinctive features of nominal rewriting and equational reasoning in the presence of binding, contrasting them with their first-order counterparts through illustrative conceptual examples. These examples demonstrate behaviours unique to nominal languages—phenomena that cannot be directly replicated in first-order frameworks and which have shaped the design of our approach. While the material here is not essential for understanding the technical content of the paper, it serves to build intuition and motivation for the constructions developed later. Readers
less familiar with nominal techniques may prefer to return to this section as needed to clarify concepts introduced in later sections.

Nominal Equational Reasoning

There is a wealth of techniques to reason modulo first-order equational theories, that is, theories defined by a set
\mathcal{E}
of first-order axioms — identities of the form
l\approx r
, where
l
and
r
are first-order terms (terms without abstractions or atoms). Examples of first-order theories include associativity (
\mathcal{A}
), commutativity (
\mathcal{C}
), and idempotency (
\mathcal{I}
) of operators. For instance, the theory of commutativity for conjunction can be expressed as
\mathcal{C}=\{X\wedge Y\approx Y\wedge X\}
.

However, even when
\mathcal{E}=\emptyset
, the nominal structure yields a richer and more expressive behaviour compared to standard first-order equality:

Example 1
.

Consider a signature
\Sigma=\{\forall,R\}
, where
\forall
is unary and
R
is a binary symbol. First, observe that the
\alpha
-equivalence class of the ground term
\forall[a]R(a,b)
consists of all terms obtained by renaming the bound atom:

[\forall[a]R(a,b)]_{\alpha}=\{\forall[a]R(a,b),\forall[c]R(c,b),\forall[d]R(d,b),\ldots\}.

Now, consider a term with a variable, say
\forall[a]R(a,X)
. The
\alpha
-equivalence class of this term is not simply obtained by renaming
a
to any atom: such renamings are valid only if the new atom is fresh for
X
. In particular,
\forall[a]R(a,X)\approx_{\alpha}\forall[c]R(c,X)
only if
a,c\#X
. Consequently, the larger the freshness context we assume, the larger the
\alpha
-equivalence class might become. For instance, under the context
a,b,c\#X
, we have:

\forall[a]R(a,X)\approx_{\alpha}\forall[b]R(b,X)\approx_{\alpha}\forall[c]R(c,X).

The nominal framework allows us to express richer theories that involve binding and freshness. For example, the theory
\mathcal{E_{\forall_{C}}}=\{\emptyset\vdash\forall[a]\forall[b]X\approx\forall[b]\forall[a]X\}
expresses that the
\forall
-quantifier is commutative, while the theory
\mathcal{E_{\forall_{\text{e}}}}=\{a\#X\vdash\forall[a]X\approx X\}
captures the elimination of vacuous quantification, relying on explicit freshness conditions.

When
\mathcal{E}\neq\emptyset
, nominal algebra equality modulo
\mathcal{E}
defines what we call an (
\alpha\cup\mathcal{E}
)-equivalence class of terms. The following example illustrates this point using the equational theory
\mathcal{E}_{\forall_{\mathcal{C}}}
, which states that the
\forall
-quantifier is commutative - a property that cannot be formally captured in standard first-order term languages.

Example 2
.

Consider the signature
\Sigma=\{\forall,f,>\}
, where
f
and
>
are binary symbols. Consider
\mathcal{E}_{\forall_{\mathcal{C}}}=\{\emptyset\vdash\forall[a]\forall[b]X\approx\forall[b]\forall[a]X\}
.
The
\alpha,\mathcal{E}_{\forall_{\mathcal{C}}}
-equivalence class of the term
s=\forall[a]\forall[b](f(a,b)>0)
includes

[s]_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{\forall_{\mathcal{C}}}}}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\forall[a]\forall[b](f(a,b)>0),\forall[b]\forall[a](f(a,b)>0),\\
&\forall[c]\forall[b](f(c,b)>0),\forall[b]\forall[c](f(c,b)>0),\\
&\forall[c]\forall[d](f(c,d)>0),\forall[d]\forall[c](f(d,c)>0),\ldots\end{aligned}\right\}.

Observe that both
\alpha
-renaming of bound variables and the commutativity of quantifiers (via
\mathcal{E}_{\forall_{\mathcal{C}}}
) contribute to generating new representatives of the same equivalence class.

Nominal Rewriting

In general, computation is driven by rewriting. When a set of identities

\mathcal{E}
guides computational reasoning, the first step is often to attempt to orient these identities into rewrite rules. Given a nominal identity, say
\nabla\vdash l\approx r
, a nominal rewrite rule
[
25
]
is obtained by orienting the identity either from left to right,
\nabla\vdash l\to r
or from right to left,
\nabla\vdash r\to l
. An expected condition for well-formed nominal rewrite rule
\nabla\vdash l\to r
is that
V(r,\nabla)\subseteq V(l)
and that
l
is not a variable. Here,
V(-)
denotes the set of (meta-)variables of
(-)
.

For example, the identity (
2
) could be oriented in the following nominal rewrite rule:
R=a\#X\vdash\forall[a](X\wedge Y)\to X\wedge\forall[a]Y
. Note that the name
a
is representing an object-level variable, therefore, we could have written this rule as

R^{(a\ b)}=b\#X\vdash\forall[b](X\wedge Y)\to X\wedge\forall[b]Y
. We write
R^{\pi}
to denote the rewrite rule obtained from
R
by applying the renamings defined by the permutation
\pi
. We call a set of rewrite rules
equivariant
when it is closed under the action of permutations
(-)^{(a\ b)}
for all atoms
a
and
b
. When we write our nominal rewrite rules, we do not give all the possible variants, but we work implicitly with the
equivariant closure

\mathcal{R}
of the rewrite rules.

To apply a rewrite step in a term
\forall[c](R(a,Z)\wedge\neg(R(a,c)))
we need to match it with the lhs of some possibly renamed version of the rule
R
. If
c\#Z
then we have a step
\forall[c](R(a,Z)\wedge\neg(R(a,c)))\to R(a,Z)\wedge\forall[c](\neg(R(a,c)))
.
Checking that a nominal rule
R
matches a term
s
is a polynomial problem
[
12
]
, but checking whether
s
matches
R^{\pi}
for some
\pi
is an NP-complete problem in general
[
14
]
. For efficiency we are interested in conditions to make this problem polynomial,
for this reason, we will work with
closed
systems, which intuitively corresponds to working with rules without free atoms.
We come back to this in §
5
.

Closed systems have many applications. All systems that stem from functional programming, including the axiomatisation of the
\lambda
-calculus, are closed. Additionally, all systems that can be specified within a standard higher-order rewriting formalism are also closed
[
26
]
.

Nominal Rewriting Modulo a Theory

Some theories, like commutativity, may not be oriented without losing important properties such as termination. To overcome this situation, we may
decompose our arbitrary theory into two different sets: one ordered (the usual set of rules), and another unordered (the identities). For instance, consider the theory
\mathcal{T}
defined by the equivariant closure of the identities:

\left\{\begin{aligned} a\#X&\vdash\forall[a]X\approx X,\\
\emptyset&\vdash\forall[a]\forall[b]Y\approx\forall[b]\forall[a]Y\end{aligned}\right\}

These identities come from a well-known fragment of first-order logic and describe standard properties of quantifiers. For simplicity, we do not consider the commutativity of

\wedge
or
\vee
here, though it could be included as additional identities if needed.

The first identity is best oriented from left-to-right to avoid generating infinite equivalence classes due to expansion. Note that the equivalence class of a term such as
\forall[b]b
would be infinite, including, for example, terms like
\forall[c](\forall[b]b),\penalty\ \forall[a](\forall[c](\forall[b]b))
and so on—not merely due to

\alpha
-equivalence, but also due to repeated, semantically vacuous quantification. Left-to-right orientation prevents this unbounded expansion.

In contrast, the second identity naturally lends itself to reasoning within an equivalence class that is already infinite due to

\alpha
-renaming. However, the application of the identity itself (i.e., commuting
\forall
-abstraction) does not introduce (additional) structural infinite variability beyond that already implied by

\alpha
-equivalence. As such, it can be safely treated as part of the unordered equational component.

Following this reasoning, we decompose
\mathcal{T}
as
\mathcal{R{\cup}E}
where
\mathcal{R}
is obtained by the equivariant closure of the rule

R=\{a\#X\vdash\forall[a]X\to X\}
and
\mathcal{E}
is the equivariant closure of the schema

\{\emptyset\vdash\forall[a]\forall[b]Y\approx\forall[b]\forall[a]Y\}
. We can verify that
\mathcal{R}
is terminating and confluent modulo
\mathcal{E}
. We will come back to this in §
3
, see Example
7
.

Nominal rewriting modulo an equational theory is inherently more involved than its first-order counterpart. However, it remains significantly more tractable than analogous mechanisms in full higher-order rewriting frameworks, striking a balance between expressiveness and manageability.

Unification Modulo a Theory via Closed Narrowing

As expected, nominal
\mathcal{R,E}
-narrowing is a generalisation of nominal
\mathcal{R,E}
-rewriting which uses nominal
\mathcal{E}
-unification instead of nominal
\mathcal{E}
-matching, i.e., both the left-hand side of the rule and the term to be rewritten can be instantiated. Nominal
\mathcal{R,E}
-narrowing will be used as a strategy for solving nominal unification problems modulo a theory
\mathcal{T}
. The idea is to decompose

\mathcal{T}
as
\mathcal{R{\cup}E}

where

\mathcal{E}
is a set of non-oriented identities for which a unification algorithm exists and
\mathcal{R}
is a set of rewriting rules which is
\mathcal{E}
-convergent (see Definition
2
).

For example, consider the set
\mathcal{T}
of identities:

\{c\#P\vdash P\land\forall[c]Q\approx\forall[c](P\land Q),\penalty\ \emptyset\vdash\forall[a]\forall[b]Y\approx\forall[b]\forall[a]Y\}

and the decomposition into rules

\mathcal{R}=\{c\#P\vdash P\land\forall[c]Q\to\forall[c](P\land Q)\}
and identities
\mathcal{E}=\{\emptyset\vdash\forall[a]\forall[b]Y\approx\forall[b]\forall[a]Y\}.

In order to solve the
\mathcal{T}
-unification problem between
s_{0}=\forall[c](P_{1}\land\forall[c^{\prime}]Q_{1})
and
t_{0}=\forall[c^{\prime}](P_{2}\land\forall[d]Q_{2})
, each with their own freshness constraints:

(c^{\prime}\#P_{1}\vdash s_{0})\;_{?}{\overset{\mathcal{T}}{\approx}}_{?}\;(c\#P_{2}\vdash t_{0}),

we apply sequences of
\mathcal{R,E}
-narrowing narrowing steps (denoted
\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{R,E}}
) on the two terms in parallel, until we obtain two new terms and that are
\mathcal{E}
-equivalent under a possibly new freshness context
\Delta_{n}
. That is,

\{c^{\prime}\#P^{\prime},c\#P^{\prime}\}\vdash(s_{0},t_{0})\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{R,E}}\ldots\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{R,E}}\Delta_{n}\vdash(s_{n},t_{n})

where
\Delta_{n}\vdash s_{n}\approx_{\mathcal{E}}t_{n}
. This way, we can guarantee that the initial terms are
\mathcal{T=R{\cup}E}
-equivalent. We will come back to this procedure with a more interesting example in §
5.2
.

To establish the correctness of our nominal unification procedure via narrowing, we first prove a key result that connects

\mathcal{R,E}
-rewriting sequences to
\mathcal{R,E}
-narrowing sequences. Specifically, we show how one can
lift
a derivation from rewriting to narrowing, and vice versa (Theorem
2
). To prove the completeness of our unification procedure modulo

\mathcal{T}
, we show that this correspondence also holds in the closed setting (Theorem
4
).

Refinements and Challenges

As in the
first-order approach, nominal narrowing may be non-terminating, meaning that this (
\mathcal{R\cup E\cup\alpha}
)-unification procedure might not terminate, even when only a finite number of unifiers exist (see Example
13
).
When
\mathcal{E=\emptyset}
, refinements were proposed in first-order approaches for the restricted case when
\mathcal{R\cup\emptyset}
-unification is finitary and the narrowing relation induced by
\mathcal{R}
is terminating
[
31
,
34
,
21
,
42
,
43
]
. However, in the nominal framework, even when
\mathcal{E}=\emptyset
, we are always working modulo the
\alpha
-equivalence theory. Therefore, the usual first-order approach does not directly translate to our framework.
In §
6
we prove that
closed
basic narrowing is terminating and a complete unification algorithm exists (Theorem
7
).

In exploring alternative refinements, we focused on the behaviour of nominal narrowing with respect to a non-empty equational theory. This presents several challenges. First, we are dealing with infinite equivalence classes. Second, it is well known that first-order narrowing modulo

\mathcal{E}
can generate infinite derivation sequences—particularly in the presence of associativity and commutativity axioms—as illustrated in
[
18
,
51
]
. Such behaviour compromises both termination and, in some cases, the completeness of

\mathcal{E}
-unification via narrowing (see Example
14
).

Most of the alternative approaches to first-order unification modulo
\mathcal{T}
using
\mathcal{R,E}
-narrowing techniques rely on restricting the equational theory
\mathcal{E}
to satisfy some requirements including the existence of
\mathcal{E}
-unification algorithms that produce a finite, minimal, and complete set of unifiers. Unfortunately, in the nominal setting, unification algorithms are currently only available for very limited theories—most notably, commutativity. As a result, further refinements cannot be pursued without first advancing the state of the art in nominal unification techniques.

This gap highlights a significant opportunity for future research and motivates our decision to defer the development of such refinements to future work (see §
6.2
).

3
Nominal Rewriting Modulo Theories

An
equational nominal rewrite system
(ENRS) is a set of nominal identities
\mathcal{T}
that can be partitioned into two components: a set
\mathcal{R}
of oriented nominal rewrite rules–possibly featuring freshness constraints such as
\nabla\vdash l\to r
–and a set
\mathcal{E}
of unordered (equational) identities. We denote this decomposition as
\mathcal{R}\cup{\alpha}\cup\mathcal{E}
, or more concisely,
\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
.

In the nominal framework, we work modulo
\alpha
-equivalence, meaning all terms are considered up to
\alpha
-renaming. With an ENRS, we generalise this by reasoning modulo combined
\alpha,\mathcal{T}
-equivalence classes, where part of
\mathcal{T}
may be oriented into a set of nominal rewrite rules (
\mathcal{R}
), while the rest (
\mathcal{E}
) remains as equational axioms. This combination gives rise to the composite rewriting relation:
\approx_{\mathcal{E}}\circ\to_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\approx_{\mathcal{E}}
, whose step-reduction is defined below.
As said before, we work implicitly with the
equivariant closure
of rewrite rules
\mathcal{R}
.

When
\mathcal{E}=\emptyset
, this reduces to plain nominal rewriting
\approx_{\alpha}\circ\to_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\approx_{\alpha}
, as introduced in
[
25
]
:

\frac{s\equiv\mathbb{C}[s^{\prime}]\hskip 9.24994pt\Delta\vdash\nabla\theta,\hskip 9.24994pts^{\prime}\approx_{\alpha}l\theta,\hskip 9.24994ptt\approx_{\alpha}\mathbb{C}[r\theta]}{\Delta\vdash s\to_{\mathcal{R}}t}

for a substitution
\theta
,
subterm
s^{\prime}
of
s
, position
\mathbb{C}
and rule
\nabla\vdash l\to r\in\mathcal{R}
.
As expected, to find the substitution
above to build a rewriting step, we need to solve a (nominal) matching problem.
As usual, we will write
\to^{+}
to denote at least one-step reduction, and
\to^{*}
to denote a finite (possibly zero) number of reductions.

In this paper, we are interested in the case
\mathcal{E}\neq\emptyset
, and the rewrite relation is defined in
(\alpha,\mathcal{E})
-equivalence classes:

Definition 2

(Nominal
\mathcal{R/E}
-rewriting)
.

Let

\mathcal{R}{\cup}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}

be an ENRS. The relation
\to_{\mathcal{R/E}}
is induced by the composition
\approx_{\mathcal{E}}\circ\to_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\approx_{\mathcal{E}}
. A nominal term-in-context
\Delta\vdash s
reduces with
\to_{\mathcal{R/E}}

\mathcal{E}
-equivalence class reduces via
\to_{\mathcal{R}}
as below:

\Delta\vdash(s\to_{\mathcal{R/E}}t)
iff there exist
s^{\prime},t^{\prime}
such that
\Delta\vdash(s\approx_{\mathcal{E}}s^{\prime}\to_{\mathcal{R}}t^{\prime}\approx_{\mathcal{E}}t)
.

We say that
\mathcal{R}
is
\mathcal{E}
-confluent
if whenever
\Delta\vdash s\to_{\mathcal{R/E}}^{*}t
and
\Delta\vdash s\to_{\mathcal{R/E}}^{*}u
, there exist terms

such that
\Delta\vdash t\to_{\mathcal{R/E}}^{*}t^{\prime}
,
\Delta\vdash u\to_{\mathcal{R/E}}^{*}u^{\prime}
and
\Delta\vdash t^{\prime}\approx_{\mathcal{E}}u^{\prime}
.
Also,
\mathcal{R}
is said to be
\mathcal{E}

6
Refinement: Nominal Basic
Closed
Narrowing

In the previous section, we saw that the closed narrowing relation
\rightsquigarrow_{\mathcal{R,E}}^{c}
provides a sound and complete
(\mathcal{R\cup\alpha\cup E})
-unification procedure. As in the first-order approach, narrowing may be non-terminating, meaning that this nominal
(\mathcal{R\cup\alpha\cup E})
-unification procedure might not terminate (see Example
13

]

In this section, we introduce a refinement of nominal closed narrowing - called

\mathcal{E}=\emptyset

In a basic derivation, narrowing is never applied to a subterm introduced by a previous narrowing substitution
[
5
,
1
,
41
]

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{R}\cup\alpha
, that can be oriented into a convergent NRS
\mathcal{R}
with embedded
\alpha
-equivalence.

We conclude the section with a discussion of the narrowing technique for the case
\mathcal{E}\neq\emptyset
, we highlight the limitations of the current approach and outline directions for future work.

\mathbb{C}\equiv[c][\_]
of the representative
[c]((a+b)+(a+b))
, there are no (strictly) internal redexes. The application of rule
X+X+Y\to Y
introduces the redex
[c](b+b)
. However, if we take another representative of
s_{0}\rho_{0}
, say
[c](a+(a+(b+b)))
, now the derivation starting at the
position
\mathbb{C}
, applying the same rule, is not innermost anymore.

Breaking the Hullot’s Property affects the completeness of the algorithm for
\mathcal{AC}
-unification procedure using
\mathcal{AC}
-narrowing, as the original proof of completeness - in the first-order approach - relies on the property. The existence of a finite minimal complete set of
\mathcal{AC}
-unifiers for unification problems modulo
\mathcal{AC}
is well-known
[
49
,
8
]
.

Basic closed narrowing works modulo
\alpha
-equivalence classes because the elements in the class are not structurally affected by renaming, there are no structural changes in the terms and the subterms at each position except for the renamed atoms - and this does not affect the proof of Lemma
6
. However, dealing with theories including
\mathcal{AC}\cup\alpha
will be as problematic in the nominal framework as it is in the first-order approaches.

Alternative Methods and Current Limitations

There are methods addressing both the non-termination of basic narrowing and incompleteness issues of
\mathcal{E}
-unification using narrowing in the first-order approaches
[
51
,
18
,
23
]
. But these are not easily transferable to the nominal framework due to some limitations of the existing developments. Most of the alternative approaches to first-order unification using narrowing techniques rely on restricting the equational theory
\mathcal{E}
to satisfy (some of the) following requirements:

1.

\mathcal{E}
is regular, that is, for each
s\approx t\in\mathcal{E}
, we have
V(s)=V(t)
;

2.

An
\mathcal{E}
-unification algorithm exists;

3.

\mathcal{E}
is finitary;

4.

\mathcal{R}
is
\mathcal{E}
-confluent and
\mathcal{E}
-terminating;

5.

\to_{\mathcal{R,E}}
is
\mathcal{E}
-coherent.

The current limitation for the nominal framework lies in requirements 2 and 3: the equational theories for which nominal unification has been developed are limited to
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{AC}
. Besides, the current developments identified that these equational theories interact with freshness and
\alpha
-renaming in a way that disrupts the finitary property of nominal
\mathcal{C/AC}
-unification (see Example
8
).
Alternative approaches that yield finitary theories (using, e.g., fixed-point equations to express solutions) are under development.

7
Related Work

Narrowing has traditionally been used to solve (first-order) equations in initial and free algebras modulo a set of equations. It is also used to integrate functional and logic programming
[
20
,
37
]
. Narrowing was originally introduced for theorem proving
[
31
]
, but nowadays it is used in type inference
[
47
]
and verification of cryptographic protocols
[
40
]
, amongst other areas. Narrowing gives rise to a complete
\mathcal{E}
-unification procedure if
\mathcal{E}
is defined by a convergent rewrite system, but it is generally inefficient. Several strategies have been designed to make narrowing-based
\mathcal{E}
-unification procedures more efficient by reducing the search space (e.g., basic narrowing
[
31
]
and variant narrowing
[
23
]
, the latter inspired by the notion of
\mathcal{E}
-variant
[
18
]
) and sufficient conditions for termination have been obtained
[
31
,
23
,
1
]
.

Nominal unification is closely related to higher-order pattern unification
[
36
]
and and there has been prior work on equational extensions in this setting. Prehofer
[
44
]
introduced higher-order narrowing along with variants–such as lazy narrowing, conditional narrowing, pattern narrowing–and explored their use as inference mechanisms in functional-logic programming (see also
[
30
]
). Nominal extensions of logic and functional programming languages are already available (see, e.g.,
[
48
,
15
]
), and nominal narrowing could play a similar role in these languages.

Initial efforts to address equational nominal unification included integrating the theories of
\mathcal{A}
ssociativity (
\approx_{\alpha,\mathcal{A}}
),
\mathcal{C}
ommutativity (
\approx_{\alpha,\mathcal{C}}
) and
\mathcal{A}
ssociativity-
\mathcal{C}
ommutativity (
\approx_{\alpha,\mathcal{AC}}
) with
\alpha
-equality
[
3
]
. Various algorithms for nominal unification modulo commutativity (
\mathcal{C}
-unification) and formalisations of their correctness in proof assistants have been developed
[
6
,
4
]
, as well as algorithms for nominal
\mathcal{C}
-matching
[
4
]
and nominal
\mathcal{AC}
-matching
[
7
]
.
Further investigations into nominal unification include exploring a
letrec
construct and atom variables
[
46
]
.
These developments reveal significant differences between first-order and nominal languages, highlighting the challenges of extending equational unification algorithms to languages with binders. For example, the theory of
\mathcal{C}
-unification has nullary type if
\alpha
-equivalence is considered
[
4
]
contrasting with the finitary type of first-order
\mathcal{C}
-unification
[
10
]
, and a direct extension of the Stickel-Fages first-order
\mathcal{AC}
-unification algorithm was shown to introduce cyclicity in solutions
[
7
]
,
differing from the original first-order approach.

The techniques presented here for the definition of nominal narrowing modulo
\alpha
and
\mathcal{E}
could serve as a basis to extend Prehofer’s results to deal with higher-order pattern-unification modulo equational theories
\mathcal{T}
that cannot be presented by convergent rewrite rules, but could be represented by a convergent
\mathcal{R}
modulo
\mathcal{E}
.
Moreover, nominal
\mathcal{R,E}
-narrowing could serve as a basis for extensions of first-order rewriting-based languages, such as Maude. The use of narrowing modulo equational axioms makes Maude an excellent platform to develop applications such as security protocol analysis, and incorporating
\alpha
will facilitate applications that require manipulating syntax with binding.

8
Conclusion and Future work

In this work, we present an approach to solving equational nominal unification problems via narrowing extended with axioms. We proposed definitions for nominal
\mathcal{E}
-rewriting and
\mathcal{E}
-narrowing and proved some properties relating them, obtaining the
\mathcal{E}
-Lifting Theorem, when
\mathcal{R}
is an
\mathcal{E}
-convergent NRS,
\to_{\mathcal{R,E}}
is
\mathcal{E}
-coherent and a complete algorithm for nominal
\mathcal{E}
-unification exists.
We illustrated the technique with an example of symbolic differentiation.
In future work we will explore applications in rewriting-based programming languages and theorem provers, as well as applications in the symbolic analysis of security protocols. Several tools, such as Maude
[
38
]
, Deepsec
[
16
]
, Tamarin
[
11
]
, ProVerif
[
17
]
, etc, use equational reasoning.

The current working hypothesis to constrain approaches to theorem proving and logic programming is that it is not necessary to compute finite complete sets of unifiers: it is sufficient to decide the solvability of the
\mathcal{E}
-unification problems. Viola
[
51
]
proposed an alternative narrowing strategy by introducing rule extensions.
Other alternative methods, which we aim to work on in the future, are to use Variant Narrowing
[
23
,
24
]
or Needed Narrowing
[
2
]
.
Variant Narrowing works
with rules
\mathcal{R}
modulo axioms
\mathcal{E}
and is complete for any
\mathcal{R{\cup}E}
satisfying some key conditions, and avoids many unnecessary narrowing sequences that would result from complete narrowing. Also, if
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the finite variant property modulo
\mathcal{E}

[
18
]
, then it can be refined into a terminating and complete narrowing algorithm.

The finite variant property enables the reduction of problems modulo an equational theory
\mathcal{E}
to problems modulo a subtheory
\mathcal{E^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{E}

[
18
]
.
It can be essential for
constraint-solving problems, such as intruder derivability constraints
[
19
]
or disunification problems, which explains our desire to study and work with these approaches in the future.
