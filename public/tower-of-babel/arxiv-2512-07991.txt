Coordinate-free utility theory
Safal Raman Aryal

Abstract
. Standard decision theory seeks conditions under which a preference relation can be compressed into a single real-valued function. However, when preferences are incomplete or intransitive, a single function fails to capture the agent’s evaluative structure. Recent literature on multi-utility representations suggests that such preferences are better represented by families of functions. This paper provides a canonical and intrinsic geometric characterization of this family. We construct the ledger group U ⁡ ( P ) U(P) , a partially ordered group that faithfully encodes the native structure of the agent’s preferences in terms of trade-offs. We show that the set of all admissible utility functions is precisely the dual cone U ∗ U^{*} of this structure. This perspective shifts the focus of utility theory from the existence of a specific map to the geometry of the measurement space itself. We demonstrate the power of this framework by explicitly reconstructing the standard multi-attribute utility representation as the intersection of the abstract dual cone with a subspace of continuous functionals, and showing the impossibility of this for a set of lexicographic preferences.



1.
Introduction

The central problem of mathematical decision theory is the representation problem: given a
set of alternatives
P
and a preference relation
\succeq
, under what conditions can one
assign each alternative a real number in such a way that higher numbers represent preferred
outcomes? In the classical paradigm, conditions such as completeness, transitivity, and
continuity (in particular, an Archimedean axiom) guarantee that
\succeq
can be
represented by a single real-valued utility function. Debreu’s theorem formalized this
principle: if every pair of alternatives is comparable and the preference order is
continuous, then there exists a
u
such that
x\succeq y
if and only
if
u(x)\geq u(y)

[
1
]
.

However, many economically significant preferences violate completeness or exhibit
multi-dimensional structure. Examples include partial orders in social choice,
vector-valued outcomes in finance, or lexicographic comparisons in multi-criteria decisions.
In such settings, compressing preferences into a single real-valued function either
discards meaningful information or forces arbitrary aggregation. The
multi-utility

framework generalizes representation by allowing a family
\mathcal{U}
of real-valued
functions, such that
x\succeq y
if and only if
u(x)\geq u(y)
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
. This view is well established. Szpilrajn’s extension theorem guarantees
that any partial order can be embedded into a total order, implying that any incomplete
preference can be represented by at least one utility function
[
6
]
.
Aumann’s result extends this to stochastic settings, showing that incomplete
preferences over lotteries can be represented by sets of expected utility functions

[
2
]
.

Subsequent work has refined this framework. Ok showed that any reflexive, transitive binary
relation admits a vector-valued utility representation under weak continuity conditions

[
3
]
; Dubra, Maccheroni, and Ok extended this to preferences over lotteries

[
4
]
; Evren and Ok provided topological characterizations for continuous
multi-utility representations
[
5
]
. Further refinements have emerged from
the order-theoretic side: every quasiorder is the intersection of linear extensions,
and the minimum number required corresponds to the poset-theoretic dimension.
Candeal recently characterized exactly when a finite poset admits a multi-utility
representation in terms of this dimension
[
7
]
.

While existence results abound, the family
\mathcal{U}
is typically constructed
abstractly or treated as a black box. Even where convex cones are employed explicitly – such
as in the mixture-space framework of Dubra, Maccheroni, and Ok – the linear structure is
assumed exogenously via the Independence Axiom on lotteries. This paper proposes a
coordinate-free alternative that geometrizes the problem without requiring the agent to
evaluate probability distributions. We construct an algebraic object – the
ledger
group

U(P)
– which encodes all evaluative increments permitted by the agent’s
preferences. Concretely, we generate a free abelian group on
P
, quotient out indifference
and cycles, and order it using the preference relation. The resulting torsion-free ordered
abelian group
U(P)
is canonical: any utility assignment into an ordered group factors
uniquely through it. This structure generalizes earlier constructions in the literature,
such as the difference preorders studied by Pivato
[
8
]
.

To describe all admissible utility functions, we tensor
U(P)
with
\mathbb{R}
to
obtain a real vector space
V=U(P)\otimes\mathbb{R}
and define a convex cone

C\subset V
of permissible trade-offs. The dual cone
U^{*}=\{\varphi\in V^{*}\mid\varphi(C)\geq 0\}
then characterizes
all
real-valued utilities
compatible with the original preferences. Each such linear functional corresponds
to a utility map via
u=\varphi\circ\eta
, and the set
U^{*}
serves as the complete
space of admissible representations.
This geometric perspective reframes classical questions. Instead of asking whether a
utility function exists, we ask about the shape of
U^{*}
. When does it contain a unique
ray? When is it polyhedral? What happens under topological constraints? Our
framework makes these questions tractable. The bipolar theorem ensures that the
closure of the preference cone corresponds to unanimous agreement among all functionals
in
U^{*}
. For finite
P
, the structure becomes combinatorial:
U(P)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}
,
and
C
becomes a polyhedral cone, making
U^{*}
a polyhedron defined by finitely many
inequalities – a structure accessible via linear programming.

This approach clarifies classical phenomena. Pareto preferences on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
yield a
dual cone of additive linear forms with nonnegative coefficients. Lexicographic
preferences correspond to degenerate cones, with continuity constraints collapsing
U^{*}

to a single ray. The framework generalizes both. By intersecting
U^{*}
with appropriate
subspaces – for instance, the space of continuous functionals with respect to the Euclidean
topology – we recover known representation theorems and identify where and why
discontinuities arise.

Our formulation resonates with deep structural themes in economics. It parallels the
classical activity analysis of Koopmans, which modeled production possibilities as
coordinate-free convex cones
[
20
]
, and aligns with the fundamental theorem
of asset pricing
[
9
]
. In finance, the absence of arbitrage
guarantees the existence of a separating pricing functional (analyzed as a risk-neutral measure).
Analogously, we show that the absence of algebraic cycles (rationality) guarantees the
existence of a utility functional. The dual cone
U^{*}
acts as the “support” of beliefs
consistent with an agent’s ordering, much as the set of martingale measures represents
ambiguity in incomplete markets
[
10
]
.

In summary, we invert the traditional perspective taken in decision theory. Rather than
asking whether preferences admit a numerical utility, we first construct the canonical
structure
U(P)
they implicitly define, and then characterize the geometry of its dual
to talk about all preference relations and utility functions at once. Utility theory
becomes a study of cones and linear functionals. In Section 2 we construct
U(P)
and
prove its universal property. Section 3 develops the dual cone
U^{*}
and shows
that in finite cases, the study of it reduces to polyhedral geometry and integer programming.
Section 4 illustrates the framework by deriving classical results about both multi-attribute and lexicographic
representations as special cases, and Section 5 concludes the investigation with a bird’s eye
view of the study of utility theory. Throughout, we rely only on standard abstract algebra and
convex analysis and introduce all required tools as needed.

2.
The algebra of evaluative increments

We begin by formalizing the notion of an algebra of trade-offs based on the agent’s preferences.
In standard utility theory, the difference between the utility of two goods,
u(b)-u(a)
,
is treated as a real number. In our coordinate-free approach, we treat the transition
from one alternative to another as a formal sum in a free structure, postponing the
assignment of numerical values until the algebraic structure is established.

Let
P
be a set of alternatives. We assume the agent possesses a preference relation
represented by a reflexive directed graph
R\subseteq P\times P
, where
(a,b)\in R

(denoted
a\succeq b
) implies
a
is at least as good as
b
. We denote the
reflexive-transitive closure of
R
by
\leq
.

To capture the additive structure of “improvement”, we utilize the concept of a free
abelian group. Economists can think of this object as a space of “inventories”. An
element in this space is simply a list of counts of various alternatives, such as
“two of alternative
a
, minus three of alternative
b
”.

Definition 1
.

The
free abelian group generated by
P
, denoted
F(P)
, is defined by:

(1)

Elements:
Formal finite sums
\sum_{p\in P}n_{p}[p]
, where
n_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(2)

Operation:
Component-wise addition of integer coefficients.

(3)

Basis:
The set
\{[p]\mid p\in P\}
forms a basis, meaning every element has a unique representation as a sum.

In our context, we interpret the formal difference
[b]-[a]
as the directed trade
moving from
a
to
b
. Just as a vector
v=b-a
in physics represents a displacement,

[b]-[a]
represents an evaluative displacement. The preference relation
R
identifies
which of these displacements the agent considers favorable. We collect these into a
monoid called the positive cone.

Definition 2
.

The
positive cone

C_{R}\subseteq F(P)
is the submonoid generated by the elementary favorable differences. Formally,

C_{R}=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}([a_{k}]-[b_{k}])\;\bigg|\;m\in\mathbb{N},\,a_{k}\succeq b_{k}\text{ in }R\right\}.

It is essential to verify that this algebraic construction does not lose information about
the original preferences. We show that the cone faithfully mirrors the order structure.

Proposition 3
.

The vector difference
[y]-[x]
lies in the positive cone
C_{R}
if and only if
y\geq x

(where
\geq
denotes the reflexive-transitive closure of
R
).

Proof.

For the first direction, if
y\geq x
, there exists a path
x=z_{0},z_{1},\dots,z_{k}=y
such that

z_{i+1}\succeq z_{i}
in
R
. Then
[y]-[x]=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}([z_{i+1}]-[z_{i}])
.
Since each term is a generator of
C_{R}
and
C_{R}
is closed under addition, the sum lies
in
C_{R}
.

For the converse direction, suppose
[y]-[x]\in C_{R}
. Then

[y]-[x]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}([a_{j}]-[b_{j}])
for some pairs with
a_{j}\succeq b_{j}
. In the
free group
F(P)
, this equality implies that the net flow into any node other than
x

and
y
is zero, the flow out of
x
is 1, and the flow into
y
is 1. Interpreting the
pairs
(b_{j},a_{j})
as directed edges in a multigraph, this formal sum constitutes a flow
from
x
to
y
. By the Flow Decomposition Theorem from graph theory, this flow decomposes into a simple
path from
x
to
y
plus a set of cycles. Since a path exists from
x
to
y
via edges
in
R
, we have
y\geq x
.
∎

This result confirms that the algebraic object
C_{R}
contains the complete informational
content of the preferences. It captures the
ordinal
content via cone membership
(
x\preceq y\iff[y]-[x]\in C_{R}
) and, by lifting to the group structure, introduces a

cardinal
content. The group operation allows for the addition and subtraction of
utility increments, enabling the abstract quantitative comparison of trade-off intensities
(e.g.,
([b]-[a])+([d]-[c])
) before any numerical values are assigned.

The pair
(F(P),C_{R})
thus constitutes a preordered group, where the group operation and
preorder respect each other. However, this structure is too
large: it treats every alternative as algebraically distinct, even if the agent is
indifferent between them. To obtain the canonical utility structure, we must perform a
reduction. This reduction conceptually happens in two steps: first we identify equivalent
bundles, and second we remove algebraic inconsistencies known as
torsion
.

Definition 4
.

Let
\sim_{R}
be the equivalence relation on
F(P)
defined by
x\sim_{R}y
if and only
if
y-x\in C_{R}
and
x-y\in C_{R}
. This identifies bundles that are mutually reachable
via favorable trades. The
raw ledger group
is the quotient
U(R):=F(P)/\sim_{R}
.

If the agent’s preferences contain strict cycles (e.g.,
a\succ b\succ c\succ a
),
the raw ledger
U(R)
will contain
torsion elements
. In group theory, a torsion
element is a non-zero element
t
such that
nt=0
for some integer
n
. Economically,
this represents a sequence of strict improvements that “loops back” to the start –
a phenomenon often called a “money pump”. A consistent utility theory requires a
torsion-free structure, as no real number can sum to itself to equal zero unless it is zero.
Thus, we must perform a second quotient to enforce rationality.

Definition 5
.

Let
\operatorname{Tor}(U(R))
be the torsion subgroup of the raw ledger (the set of all elements with
finite order). The
ledger group

U(P)
is defined as the torsion-free quotient:

U(P):=U(R)/\operatorname{Tor}(U(R)).

We denote the canonical map
\eta:P\to U(P)
given by the composition of the two
quotient maps.

The ledger group
U(P)
is the fundamental object of our theory. By construction, it is a
torsion-free abelian group. If
P
is finite,
U(P)
is isomorphic to an integer lattice

\mathbb{Z}^{k}
.

The significance of
U(P)
lies in its universality. It is the minimal algebraic
structure required to encode the consistent core of the preferences. Any attempt to
assign numerical utilities to
P
(for example, in
\mathbb{R}
) must respect the structure of
U(P)
. This is
formalized by the following factorization theorem, which establishes
U(P)
as the
“universal domain” for utility.

Theorem 6
.

Let
G
be any ordered abelian group (e.g.,
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbb{Z}
). There is a canonical
bijection between the set of order-preserving maps
u:P\to G
and the set of positive
group homomorphisms
\psi:U(P)\to G
. Specifically, for every representation
u
,
there exists a unique homomorphism
\psi
such that the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces P}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U(P)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces u}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}

Proof.

We first establish existence. Since
F(P)
is free, the map
u:P\to G
extends
uniquely to a homomorphism
\hat{\psi}:F(P)\to G
defined by linearity. For any
generator of the preference cone
d=[a]-[b]
where
a\succeq b
, order-preservation
implies
u(a)\geq_{G}u(b)
, so
\hat{\psi}(d)\geq_{G}0
. Since
G
’s positive cone is
closed under addition,
\hat{\psi}
maps the entire cone
C_{R}
into the non-negative
elements of
G
.

Next, we handle the quotients. If
x\sim_{R}y
, then
\hat{\psi}(x)=\hat{\psi}(y)
,
so the map descends to the raw ledger
U(R)
. Furthermore, since
G
is an ordered
abelian group, it is necessarily torsion-free (an element
g>0
implies
ng>0

for all
n
, so
ng
cannot be 0). Therefore, any homomorphism from
U(R)
to
G

must map all torsion elements to zero. Thus,
\hat{\psi}
descends uniquely to the
torsion-free quotient
U(P)
. Uniqueness follows from the fact that
P
generates the group.
∎

In practice, we often entangle our study of the agent’s intrinsic preferences with
the specific measuring stick used to describe them (usually the real line
\mathbb{R}
). This
theorem proves that these are distinct:
U(P)
captures the pure, coordinate-free
algebraic and order-theoretic structure of the preferences, while any specific utility
function
u:P\to\mathbb{R}
is merely a linear projection (or “shadow”) of this structure
onto a numerical scale. By proving that
every
possible consistent utility
representation factors uniquely through
U(P)
, the theorem lets us isolate the algebraic
and order-theoretic aspects of studying preferences from the geometric and topological ones.
For a reference on the algebraic facts used in this section, see
[
11
]
.

3.
The geometry of the dual cone

While
U(P)
captures the logic of preference perfectly, it is a discrete structure.
Economic analysis typically requires real-valued representations to support calculus,
convexity, and optimization. We bridge this gap by extending the discrete group

U(P)
into a real vector space structure over
\mathbb{R}
.

To do this, we use the tensor product. Conceptually, tensoring with
\mathbb{R}
allows us to
multiply our “inventory lists” not just by integers, but by real numbers
(e.g., “2.5 units of improvement”). This transforms our lattice into a vector space
V
.

Definition 7
.

The tensor product
U(P)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
is the unique real vector space
V
equipped
with a bilinear map
\phi:U(P)\times\mathbb{R}\to V
satisfying the universal property
that any bilinear map from
U(P)\times\mathbb{R}
to a vector space factors through
V
.
It is worth noting that the tensor product naturally annihilates torsion:
if
t
is a torsion element such that
nt=0
, then in the vector space,

t\otimes 1=\frac{1}{n}(nt\otimes 1)=0
. Thus,
V
automatically
captures only the consistent, linearizable core of the preferences.

This construction provides the “utility space” where convex analysis can take place.

Definition 8
.

Let
V=U(P)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
. The
real preference cone

C_{\mathbb{R}}
is the convex
conical hull of the image of the positive cone:

C_{\mathbb{R}}=\operatorname{cone}(\eta(C_{R}))=\left\{\sum\alpha_{i}(v_{i})\mid\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},v_{i}\in\eta(C_{R})\right\}\subseteq V.

We now define the space of all possible real-valued utility functions. In linear
algebra and functional analysis, the space of linear measurements on a vector
space is known as its
dual space
. Economists can think of this as the space of
“shadow prices”. If
V
is a space of commodity bundles, the dual space

V^{*}
is the space of price vectors that assign values to those bundles.
In our context, a “price vector” is simply a utility function: it assigns a real
number to every trade, representing the value of that trade. This space is also analogous
to the measurement space dual to the state space in physics.

Definition 9
.

The
dual cone of utility representations
, denoted
U^{*}
, is the set of all linear
functionals on
V
that assign non-negative values to every favorable trade.

U^{*}:=(C_{\mathbb{R}})^{\circ}=\left\{\varphi\in V^{*}\mid\varphi(v)\geq 0\text{ for all }v\in C_{\mathbb{R}}\right\}.

Each element
\varphi\in U^{*}
corresponds to a specific utility function

u=\varphi\circ\eta
. The set
U^{*}
is not merely a collection of functions;
it is a rigid geometric object (a convex cone) in the dual space
V^{*}
. This cone
completely characterizes the agent’s preferences.

To prove this, we require a topological setting that allows us to apply duality
theorems from analysis without imposing extraneous constraints like standard metric continuity. Standard utility
theory typically assumes the topology of Euclidean space. However,
V
is an abstract
vector space constructed from preferences, not a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To ensure that
our algebraic dual space coincides with the topological dual space, we equip
V
with
the
finest locally convex topology
.

Definition 10
.

The finest locally convex topology on a vector space
V
is defined by the collection
of all seminorms on
V
. Its most important property for our purposes is that

every
linear
functional
\varphi:V\to\mathbb{R}
is topologically continuous with respect to this topology.

By choosing this topology, we ensure that we do not accidentally rule out “strange”
utility functions (like lexicographic ones) that might be discontinuous in a
standard metric, and thus keep our dual cone faithful to the preferences. If the economist
requires a stronger notion of regularity (such as Euclidean continuity), the geometry we will put on the dual cone will
make it easy to do so, as we will see in the next section.

We now invoke the Bipolar Theorem, a fundamental result in functional analysis.

Theorem 11
.

Let
V
be a locally convex topological vector space. Let
K\subseteq V
be a convex
cone containing the origin. The bipolar of the cone, denoted
(K^{\circ})^{\circ}
,
is the set of all vectors
v\in V
such that every non-negative functional on
K
is
also non-negative on
v
. The theorem states that:

(K^{\circ})^{\circ}=\overline{K}

where
\overline{K}
is the topological closure of
K
.

This theorem connects the algebraic world of measurements (unanimity among functions)
with the geometric world of sets (closure of the cone). Applying it to our setting
yields the fundamental duality result of our framework.

Theorem 12
.

Equip
V
with the finest locally convex topology. For any trade
x\in V
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

Unanimity: Every admissible utility function endorses the trade
x
. That is,
\varphi(x)\geq 0
for all
\varphi\in U^{*}
.

(2)

Primal Validity: The trade
x
lies in the topological closure of the preference cone
C_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

Condition (1) states that
x
lies in the polar of the dual cone,
(U^{*})^{\circ}
. By our
definition, the dual cone is the polar of the primal cone:
U^{*}=(C_{\mathbb{R}})^{\circ}
. Thus,
we are investigating the set
((C_{\mathbb{R}})^{\circ})^{\circ}
. Since
C_{\mathbb{R}}
is a convex cone
containing the origin, the Bipolar Theorem asserts that
((C_{\mathbb{R}})^{\circ})^{\circ}
is
exactly the closure of
C_{\mathbb{R}}
in the finest locally convex topology.
∎

This theorem is the geometric justification for multi-utility theory. It asserts that we
do not need to arbitrarily construct a family of functions to represent incomplete
preferences; that family exists canonically as the dual cone
U^{*}
. The “utility”
of the agent is the cone itself.

A significant advantage of this algebraic approach is its computational implications
for finite domains. In many applications – such as combinatorial auctions or social
choice – the set of alternatives
P
is finite. In this setting, the abstract machinery
collapses into well-known tools from integer programming and polyhedral geometry.

When
P
is finite,
U(P)
is a finitely generated free abelian group. This implies it is
isomorphic to an integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
, where
k\leq|P|
. The primal cone
C_{\mathbb{R}}

becomes a
rational polyhedral cone
, generated by a finite set of vectors. This
leads to a stronger duality result. To prove it, we rely on the Minkowski-Weyl theorem.

Theorem 13
.

A subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a finitely generated convex cone (the convex hull of a finite set of
rays) if and only if it is the intersection of a finite number of linear half-spaces.
Such cones are called polyhedral cones and are always topologically closed.

Using this theorem, we can show that in the finite case, the topological closure required
by the Bipolar Theorem is redundant.

Proposition 14
.

If
P
is finite, then the cone
C_{\mathbb{R}}
is closed. Consequently,

x\preceq_{R}y\iff\forall\varphi\in U^{*},\varphi([x])\leq\varphi([y]).

Proof.

Since
P
is finite,
C_{\mathbb{R}}
is generated by a finite set of difference vectors. By the
Minkowski-Weyl theorem,
C_{\mathbb{R}}
is a polyhedral cone and is therefore closed in the standard
topology (which coincides with the finest locally convex topology for finite dimensions).
Thus
\operatorname{cl}(C_{\mathbb{R}})=C_{\mathbb{R}}
. The result follows immediately from the Geometric Duality Theorem.
∎

This result bridges the gap between abstract utility theory and algorithmic decision making.
It implies three computational facts.
First, it is very natural to construe queries on preferences in terms of feasibility testing.
Checking if
x\preceq y
is equivalent to checking if the vector
[y]-[x]
lies in
the cone
C_{\mathbb{R}}
. This is an integer linear programming feasibility problem. Secondly, it ties abstract
questions about potentially exotic preferences to concrete questions on optimization:
maximizing utility over a finite set corresponds to solving a linear program over the
polyhedron
C_{\mathbb{R}}
. Finally, this approach also makes it easier to construct utilities from a given
finite set of preferences: a specific utility function
\varphi\in U^{*}
is equivalent to
finding a feasible solution to a system of linear inequalities.
Thus, for finite choice sets, the “multi-utility” representation is a computationally
computable object defined by a finite set of linear constraints (albeit possibly
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to deal
with in some cases, though good approximations exist).

4.
Multi-attribute and lexicographic preferences

The framework developed above deals with the space of
all
possible utility
functions, including those that might be highly irregular or discontinuous with respect to topologies
economic theorists are used to dealing with. However, in applied
economics, we are often interested only in functions that satisfy specific regularity
properties, such as continuity with respect to the topology of a commodity space. We now
illustrate the power of this framework by explicitly analyzing the representability of
two types of preferences: “nice” multi-attribute preferences, and “scary” lexicographic
preferences, and showing what the geometric formalism gives us.

Before presenting our specific examples, we outline the general strategy. In each case,
we first identify the dual cone
U^{*}
generated by the raw logic of the
preferences. This cone contains
all
linear representations, regardless of their
regularity. Separately, we define a subspace
\mathcal{M}
of functionals that satisfy the
modeler’s external constraints (such as continuity with respect to the commodity space
topology). The set of admissible “standard” utility functions is then the intersection

U^{*}\cap\mathcal{M}
. This geometric separation allows us to pinpoint exactly whether a
failure of representation arises from the preference logic itself or from the imposition of
the topological constraints.

We begin with the multi-attribute example. Consider a set of alternatives
P=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, interpreted as bundles of two goods. We assume
the agent strictly prefers more of each good to less, but possesses an incomplete
preference relation: they cannot necessarily compare bundles with conflicting quantities.
The preference relation is the standard Pareto partial order:
x\succeq y
if and only if

x_{1}\geq y_{1}
and
x_{2}\geq y_{2}
. Standard theory tells us this agent is represented by the
family of weighted sums
u(x)=\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\alpha_{2}x_{2}
with weights
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
.
We will derive this result geometrically.

We begin with the algebraic structure.
The free group
F(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is of uncountable rank, generated by every point in the plane. The
ledger group
U(P)
is the quotient of this free group by the relations implied by the
Pareto order. The positive cone
C_{R}
is generated by all formal differences
[x]-[y]

where the vector difference
x-y
lies in the non-negative orthant
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}
. In
the utility space
V=U(P)\otimes\mathbb{R}
, the preference cone
C_{\mathbb{R}}
is the convex hull of
these vector differences.

We now construct the abstract dual cone.
The dual cone
U^{*}
consists of
all
linear functionals
\varphi:V\to\mathbb{R}
that are
non-negative on Pareto improvements. Note that
V
is an abstract vector space of formal
sums, distinct from the commodity space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. A generic element
\varphi\in U^{*}
might
be highly pathological; for instance, it could be a discontinuous Hamel basis functional
that respects the order but maps bounded sets to infinity. These functions exist
mathematically but are useless for economics.

We can now impose our topological constraints.
We are interested only in utility functions that respect the standard Euclidean topology of
the commodity space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R})
be the space of continuous
linear maps from the commodity space to the reals. By the universal factorization theorem,
any such map
f
lifts to a functional
\tilde{f}
on
V
defined by
\tilde{f}([x])=f(x)
.
This defines a subspace of “regular” measurements
\mathcal{M}\subset V^{*}
.

We now take our intersection.
The set of admissible continuous utility functions is given by

\mathcal{U}_{rep}=U^{*}\cap\mathcal{M}
. An element
f\in\mathcal{M}
is of the
form
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\alpha_{2}x_{2}
. For this functional to lie in
U^{*}
,
it must map every positive increment in the primal cone to a non-negative number. The
increments generating
C_{\mathbb{R}}
are the vectors in the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}
.
Thus, we require the condition that
\alpha_{1}v_{1}+\alpha_{2}v_{2}\geq 0
for all vectors

v_{1},v_{2}\geq 0
. Testing the basis vectors
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
, we immediately recover
the condition
\alpha_{1}\geq 0
and
\alpha_{2}\geq 0
.

This derivation reveals the underlying logic of representation theorems in our framework.
The primal cone
C_{\mathbb{R}}
encodes the logic of the preferences (Pareto dominance).
The abstract dual cone
U^{*}
encodes all logically possible representations, including
discontinuous ones. The constraint space
\mathcal{M}
encodes the modeler’s choice of
regularity. The final representation is the intersection
U^{*}\cap\mathcal{M}
.
When preferences are incomplete, as in this example, the intersection is a non-trivial
cone (the first quadrant of the dual space), confirming that a family of functions is
required. If the preferences were complete, the primal cone would widen to a half-space,
and the intersection would collapse to a single ray, recovering the standard unique
utility function.

We now illustrate the framework in a setting where the admissible utility
representations are
necessarily discontinuous
with respect to the Euclidean topology. Consider a set of alternatives

P=\mathbb{R}^{2}
interpreted again as bundles of two goods. The agent evaluates
bundles lexicographically:
(x_{1},x_{2})\succcurlyeq_{\mathrm{lex}}(y_{1},y_{2})
if and only if
either
x_{1}>y_{1}
, or
x_{1}=y_{1}
and
x_{2}\geq y_{2}
. It is well known that such
preferences admit no Euclidean-continuous real-valued representation on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; every
representing function must exhibit a jump discontinuity along vertical lines. We now
derive this fact from the geometry of the ledger group and its dual cone.

The algebraic construction proceeds exactly as before. The free group
F(\mathbb{R}^{2})

has one generator
[x]
for each point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and the preference cone

C_{\mathrm{lex}}
is generated by all differences
[x]-[y]
with
x\succcurlyeq_{\mathrm{lex}}y
.
In the utility space
V=U(P)\otimes\mathbb{R}
, the primal cone
C_{\mathrm{lex}}
is the convex
hull of these generators. Unlike the Pareto case, this cone is
non-polyhedral
and
contains the entire half-space of “first-coordinate improvements”:

(x_{1},x_{2})-(y_{1},y_{2})\in C_{\mathrm{lex}}\quad\text{whenever }x_{1}>y_{1},

together with the ray generated by
(0,1)
for bundles with equal first coordinate.

We now characterize the abstract dual cone. A linear functional
\phi\in V^{*}
lies in

U^{*}_{\mathrm{lex}}
if and only if it assigns a non-negative value to every generator of the primal
cone. Consider any such
\phi
. Positivity on all vectors with
x_{1}>y_{1}
forces

\phi(1,0)>0,

since otherwise
\phi
would assign non-positive value to arbitrarily large improvements in
the first coordinate. Likewise, positivity on the ray generated by
(0,1)
(the second-coordinate
comparison along ties) implies

\phi(0,1)\geq 0.

Thus every element of the abstract dual cone is of the form

\phi(x_{1},x_{2})=\alpha x_{1}+\beta x_{2}

with
\alpha>0
and
\beta\geq 0
. Note that
every
such pair
(\alpha,\beta)
is
admissible in the abstract dual space
V^{*}
; the apparent uniqueness of the lexicographic
utility arises only after we impose regularity.

To recover the classical lexicographic functional, we now impose the modeler’s continuity
requirements. Let
M
denote the space of linear functionals on
P=\mathbb{R}^{2}
that
are continuous with respect to the standard Euclidean topology. For a functional

f\in M
, continuity requires that it be bounded on bounded sets. However, any functional

f(x)=\alpha x_{1}+\beta x_{2}
with
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
assigns strictly positive
value to every direction in the first quadrant, and thus cannot reproduce the abrupt
discontinuities of the lexicographic relation. To represent lexicographic preferences,

f
must satisfy the condition that second-coordinate improvements are given infinitely
small weight relative to first-coordinate improvements. Formally, the only Euclidean-continuous
representatives of lexicographic order in the abstract dual space are those for which

f(x_{1},x_{2})=\alpha x_{1}\quad\text{with }\alpha>0,

together with a limiting device that assigns value to
(0,1)
only on the “tie” hyperplane.

Intersecting the abstract dual cone
U^{*}_{\mathrm{lex}}
with the continuous subspace
M

therefore collapses the family of admissible functionals to the
lexicographic utility ray
:

U_{\mathrm{rep}}=U^{*}_{\mathrm{lex}}\cap M=\{\,f(x_{1},x_{2})=\alpha x_{1}\mid\alpha>0\,\}.

This computation makes transparent why lexicographic preferences cannot be represented
by a single continuous function of both coordinates: the primal cone
C_{\mathrm{lex}}

contains directions that force any representing functional to assign strictly positive value
to
(1,0)
, but only non-negative and
infinitesimally small
value to
(0,1)
. The
abstract dual cone
U^{*}_{\mathrm{lex}}
contains many discontinuous functionals with these
properties, but the continuity constraint cuts the cone down to a single ray. As in the
multi-attribute example, the form of the representation is obtained as an intersection

U_{\mathrm{rep}}=U^{*}_{\mathrm{lex}}\cap M,

clarifying the geometric origin of lexicographic utility and the inevitability of its
discontinuity in the standard topology on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

5.
Conclusion

This paper has argued that the question “does a utility function exist?” is an
incomplete framing for decision theory. For any preference relation satisfying
minimal consistency (transitivity), the utility object
U(P)
exists as a well-defined
algebraic structure. Real-valued utility functions are not fundamental entities; they are
elements of the dual space
U^{*}
. This geometric perspective clarifies that
“pathologies” such as incompleteness or non-representability can be naturally analyzed
as geometric features of the dual cone, rather than defects of the agent. By working
directly with the ledger group and its dual, we obtain a coordinate-free theory of utility
that is robust, universal, and provides a rigorous language for analyzing the
dimensionality of human evaluation.

A central reason the ledger group
U(P)
deserves to be called the
universal
utility
domain is the factorization property established in Theorem 5. Any order-preserving
utility representation
u:P\to G
into an ordered abelian group
G
extends uniquely
to a positive group homomorphism via freeness, descends to the raw ledger
U(R)
by
compatibility with the preference cone, and eliminates torsion automatically because
G

is torsion-free. Consequently, every such representation factors uniquely through
U(P)

via a linear map. This universal property characterizes
U(P)
up to unique isomorphism:
it is the initial object among torsion-free ordered groups receiving an order-preserving
map from
P
. In this sense,
U(P)
contains exactly the evaluative content forced by
the preferences and nothing extraneous. The full space of admissible real-valued utilities
is precisely the dual cone
U^{*}
of linear measurements on this canonical core.

This framework yields a fundamentally different workflow for utility theory. One begins
with nothing more than a directed preference relation (equivalent modulo torsion to a
preorder) – imposing no continuity axioms, completeness, or exogenous mixture structure –
and uses it to construct the canonical group
U(P)
and its real vector space

V=U(P)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
. From that point on, the preorder itself is no
longer manipulated: its entire informational content has been compressed into a single
algebraic object. All substantive analysis is carried out on
V
or on its dual cone

U^{*}\subseteq V^{*}
. The workflow becomes linear and geometric: the dimensionality
of evaluative freedom is
\dim V
; the full set of admissible utilities is the cone

U^{*}
; extremal utilities correspond to extreme rays; and regularity constraints
(continuity, monotonicity) amount to intersecting
U^{*}
with specified subspaces
of
V^{*}
. Crucially, testing whether
x\succeq y
becomes a feasibility check –
is
[y]-[x]\in C_{R}
? – which for finite sets reduces to integer linear programming.
This isolates the discrete and not naturally additive nature of preferences in a single
construction, allowing the rest of decision theory to proceed using the well-understood
machinery of convex analysis.

The geometric duality established here also offers a rigorous foundation for
statiatical approaches to estimating utility. Standard approaches to utility estimation
often require imposing functional forms that may violate the intrinsic structure of the
agent’s preferences. In contrast, the dual cone
U^{*}
defines the canonical support for
any probability measure over the space of utility functions. From this perspective,
learning becomes a process of geometric refinement: each observed preference

x\succeq y
imposes a linear constraint corresponding to a half-space, and updating
beliefs reduces to intersecting the prior cone
U^{*}
with this half-space.
This transformation allows us to quantify
identifiability
geometrically;
the solid angle of
U^{*}
serves as a precise measure of the remaining ambiguity
regarding the agent’s values.

At a deeper level, this approach succeeds because it inverts the traditional methodology.
Rather than asking whether preferences admit a numerical representation into a
pre-specified target space, we first extract the intrinsic algebraic structure
determined by the preferences, then characterize the space of representations as
measurements of this structure. By generating the vector space endogenously via the
tensor product rather than assuming a mixture space, we extend the logic of the
fundamental theorem of asset pricing and activity analysis to the domain of individual
choice. The dual cone
U^{*}
exists for every preference relation, providing a
canonical multi-utility representation that degenerates to a single function precisely
when preferences are sufficiently regular to admit compression into the real line.
