Prompt‑Guided Relational Reasoning for Social Behavior Understanding with Vision Foundation Models
Thinesh Thiyakesan Ponbagavathi, Chengzheng Yang, Alina Roitberg

Abstract
Group Activity Detection (GAD) involves recognizing social groups and their collective behaviors in videos. Vision Foundation Models (VFMs), like DinoV2, offer excellent features, but are pretrained primarily on object-centric data and remain underexplored for modeling group dynamics. While they are a promising alternative to highly task-specific GAD architectures that require full fine-tuning, our initial investigation reveals that simply swapping CNN backbones used in these methods with VFMs brings little gain, underscoring the need for structured, group-aware reasoning on top. We introduce Prompt-driven Group Activity Detection (ProGraD) – a method that bridges this gap through 1) learnable group prompts to guide the VFM attention toward social configurations, and 2) a lightweight two-layer GroupContext Transformer that infers actor-group associations and collective behavior. We evaluate our approach on two recent GAD benchmarks: Cafè, which features multiple concurrent social groups, and Social-CAD, which focuses on single-group interactions. While we surpass state-of-the-art in both settings, our method is especially effective in complex multi-group scenarios, where we yield a gain of 6.5 % 6.5\% (Group mAP @ @ 1.0) and 8.2 % 8.2\% (Group mAP @ @ 0.5) using only ∼ \sim 10M trainable parameters. Furthermore, our experiments reveal that ProGraD produces interpretable attention maps, offering insights into actor‑group reasoning. Code and models will be released.

Kim et al. 2024

;
Ehsanpour et al. 2020
;
Ehsanpour et al. 2022
)

This involves predicting who is acting, with whom, and where these groups are spatially located. This is challenging, as the model must infer group membership on the fly, while also reasoning about both individual and group-level behaviors – all in messy, real-world environments.
Most existing methods
(
Kim et al. 2024
;
Tamura, Vishwakarma, and Vennelakanti 2022
)
rely on fully fine-tuned CNN backbones and complex spatiotemporal group reasoning modules, making them costly to train and reliant on heuristic group formation strategies.

This aligns with prior analyses indicating that VFMs, primarily trained on object-centric data, struggle to capture fine-grained multi-object relations and social interactions critical for GAD
(
Wagner and Harmeling 2025
)
.
Likewise, existing probing methods
(

;
Lee et al. 2019
;
Ponbagavathi and Roitberg 2025
)
are primarily designed for classification or retrieval and lack the structured reasoning required for actor-group associations and collective behavior modelling.

(2) We design a minimal two-layer GroupContext Transformer that enables efficient, interpretable reasoning over social formations, supporting group localization, membership inference, and joint activity recognition. Related Work

Group Activity Detection

Group Activity Detection (GAD) extends traditional group activity recognition
(
Ehsanpour et al. 2022
;
Tamura, Vishwakarma, and Vennelakanti 2022
;
Zhou et al. 2022
)
by requiring models to localize multiple groups within a scene and classify each group’s activity. This involves both group membership prediction and group-level activity classification. Early approaches
(
Ehsanpour et al. 2022
;
Han et al. 2022b
;
Li et al. 2022
)
employed graph neural networks (GNNs) with spectral clustering to model actor relationships.While effective in structured environments, these methods relied on non-task-specific clustering algorithms, leading to slow inference and limited adaptability in dynamic real-world scenes.
Recent transformer-based methods address these shortcomings by unifying localization and reasoning. HGC
(
Tamura, Vishwakarma, and Vennelakanti 2022
)
performs group localization in the 2D coordinate space by applying Deformable DETR
(
Carion et al. 2020
)
to match actors to predicted group centers using spatial cues. In contrast, Practical GAD introduces learnable group tokens that enable matching in the embedding space, allowing the model to reason over semantic similarity rather than relying solely on spatial proximity.
Other transformer-based group activity recognition (GAR) approaches
(
Gavrilyuk et al. 2020
;
Han et al. 2022a
;
Li et al. 2021
)
also rely on CNN backbones to extract frame-level features, which are then processed through stacked spatial-temporal transformer blocks to model inter-actor relations and group-level dynamics. However, the limited semantic capacity of CNN-based features often necessitates deep transformer decoders to compensate, increasing decoder complexity and training time. In contrast, our method, ProGraD, leverages a pretrained transformer-based vision foundation model as the backbone, introduces learnable group prompts to extract group-specific semantics from the backbone, and replaces complex deformable transformers with a lightweight two-layer decoder, resulting in a more efficient and semantically informed approach to GAD.

Figure 2:
Overview of ProGraD
. Our framework consists of three key components: (1) a frozen feature extractor enhanced with learnable group prompts that encode spatial and group reasoning across frames; (2) a GroupContext Transformer (GCT) comprising a Grouping Attention Layer for group formation and a Contextual Attention Layer for actor-group interaction reasoning; and (3) lightweight prediction heads that estimate group activity, actor activity, and embedding projections. This modular design enables efficient and interpretable Group Activity Detection.

Visual Prompt Tuning in Vision Transformers

Visual Prompt Tuning
(
Jia and Others 2022
)
has emerged as a parameter-efficient strategy for adapting large pretrained vision transformers to downstream tasks by injecting a small set of learnable soft prompts into the input sequence. It has demonstrated success in various tasks like classification
(
Jia and Others 2022
;
Zeng et al. 2024
)
, segmentation
(
Liu et al. 2024
;
Ren et al. 2025
)
, detection
(
Feng et al. 2022
;
Medeiros et al. 2025
)
, and image-to-video transfer
(
Wasim et al. 2023
;
Liu, Li, and Yu 2024
)
by guiding the backbone to extract task-relevant features. In group activity recognition, PromptGAR
(
Jin et al. 2025
)
relies on hard prompts (bounding boxes, keypoints, area definitions) and uses a complex two-way transformer decoder with cross-modal interaction and relative-instance attention. Importantly, it addresses the simpler task of Group Activity Recognition (GAR) by classifying the activity of a manually selected group, and does not perform group localization or handle multiple concurrent groups—key requirements in Group Activity Detection (GAD). Therefore, we do not include it in our comparisons.
Additionally, unlike prior works that treat the number of prompts as a tunable hyperparameter, our method selects the number of group prompts based on the maximum number of annotated groups in the dataset. This principled design avoids heuristic tuning and ensures better alignment with real-world group configurations.
In contrast, our method, ProGraD, introduces a novel use of prompting by injecting learnable group prompts into the DINOv2 backbone and employing a robust decoder for actor-group reasoning and group activity prediction, offering a more efficient and generalizable framework for GAD.

ProGraD: Prompt Driven Group Activity Detection

We propose
ProGraD
-
Pro
mpt Driven
Gr
oup
A
ctivity
D
etection - a lightweight yet effective architecture for group activity detection that adapts pretrained Vision Foundation models (VFMs) with minimal trainable parameters, following the parameter-efficient fine-tuning
(
Jia and Others 2022
;
Zeng et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Pan et al. 2022
)
(PEFT) paradigm. The core idea is to introduce
learnable group prompts
that guide the model in capturing group-specific dynamics, alongside a two-layer
GroupContext Transformer
that enables interaction among actors, scene features, and group context.
ProGraD jointly predicts group membership, group activity, and individual actions, while handling unknown and varying group counts. Figure
2
provides an overview of the architecture, which consists of the following key components: Feature extractor, GroupContext Transformer, prediction heads, and training objective.

Problem definition and notation

Given a video
X=\{X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{T}\}
, our goal is to detect social groups and classify their collective activities. Each frame
X_{t}
is processed independently through a VFM, such as DinoV2
(
Oquab et al. 2024
)
, yielding patch-level tokens
I_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}
, where
N
is the number of patch tokens and
D
is the feature dimension. These frame features are stacked to obtain Image features
I=\{I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{T}\}
. We additionally extract actor tokens
A=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{M}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times D}
using ROI Align guided by actor bounding box annotations, where
M
denotes the total number of actor instances across frames.
To enable structured group-level reasoning, we introduce a set of learnable group prompts
G_{prompt}=\{g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{K}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}
, which act as task-specific queries that attend to relevant actor and scene regions.
The overall task is to jointly predict: (1) individual action labels for all detected actors, (2) group membership assignments of actors, and (3) group activity labels for the identified social groups.

Feature Extractor

We extract both global scene features and localized actor representations from each frame using a frozen VFM. To adapt the model for group activity understanding without full fine-tuning, we introduce prompt-based conditioning and a lightweight RoI-based actor token extraction strategy.

Prompt-driven Feature Extraction.
Naively swapping CNN backbones with VFMs yields little gain for GAD (Table
2
). We hypothesize this is due to object‑centric pretraining that overlooks social configurations. To address this, we introduce learnable group prompts, injected into the VFM’s input sequence, which steer attention toward socially meaningful regions such as clusters of interacting actors or shared objects (e.g., tables). Unlike prior prompt‑tuning methods that treat prompt count as a hyperparameter, we fix
K
to the maximum number of annotated groups per dataset. This one‑prompt‑per‑group design enforces disentangled representations and improves group coverage, as validated in Table
6
. The output is a set of prompt‑conditioned scene tokens that carry group‑aware semantics, forming a richer foundation for downstream reasoning.

RoI-based Actor Token Generation.
We extract actor‑specific tokens using 1
\times
1 RoI pooling from the VFM’s frozen feature maps, producing a single embedding per actor. This compact representation preserves spatial precision and integrates seamlessly with transformer‑based reasoning. Compared to prior methods that use flattened 5
\times
5 RoI grids, our design reduces dimensionality without losing essential actor context, enabling a leaner reasoning module.

Figure 3:
RoI-based actor token extraction pipeline. Given actor bounding boxes and feature maps from a frozen vision backbone, 1×1 RoI pooling is used to extract fixed-size actor tokens. These tokens serve as spatially grounded inputs for downstream reasoning in the ProGraD architecture.

GroupContext Transformer

The GroupContext Transformer (GCT) is a lightweight two‑layer decoder designed to reason jointly over actors, group tokens, and global scene context. Let
M
,
K
,
T
, and
D
denote the number of actors, groups, frames, and feature dimension, respectively. The decoder refines actor tokens and learnable group tokens in two stages: first, by inferring actor–group associations, and second, by enriching these tokens with global scene context. The outputs are enriched actor
\mathbf{A}_{ctx}
and group tokens
\mathbf{G}_{ctx}
used for predicting individual actions, group activities, and group membership scores.

Grouping Attention Layer.
The first layer initializes a set of learnable group tokens
\mathbf{G}_{init}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times T\times D}
, which serve as queries attending to the actor tokens
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times T\times D}
. This cross‑attention aggregates information from all detected actors into the group tokens:

\mathbf{G}_{grp}=\text{LN}\left(\mathbf{G}_{init}+\text{FFN}\left(\text{Attn}(\mathbf{G}_{init},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A})\right)\right)

(1)

Through this attention mechanism (Equation
1
), group tokens aggregate information from potentially distant or temporally separated actors, forming a coherent representation of group composition. The subsequent residual connection, feed‑forward network (FFN), and layer normalization (LN) yield the updated group tokens
\mathbf{G}_{grp}
that reflect actor-group interactions.

Contextual Attention Layer.

After learning group compositions, we enrich both actor and group representations by incorporating global scene context. We first concatenate the updated group tokens
\mathbf{G}_{grp}
with the actor tokens
\mathbf{A}
and define this joint set as
Z=[\mathbf{A},|,\mathbf{G}_{grp}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(M+K)\times T\times D}
. These queries attend to the image features
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{f}W_{f}\times T\times D}
, which are scene-level patch tokens.

\mathbf{[\mathbf{A}_{ctx}\,\|\,\mathbf{G}_{\text{ctx}}]}=\text{LN}\left(Z+\text{FFN}\left(\text{Attn}(Z,\mathbf{I},\mathbf{I})\right)\right)

(2)

This step injects contextual cues—such as spatial arrangement, background objects, and regions likely to support social interaction—into the group and actor embeddings, enabling more accurate group activity detection. These enriched actor
\mathbf{A}_{ctx}
and group
\mathbf{G}_{ctx}
embeddings, which are used in the prediction heads for action recognition, group activity classification, and group membership inference.

Prediction heads

We use lightweight feed‑forward networks for the final predictions, as used by prior work
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
. Specifically, the group activity head classifies the activity of each predicted group, the actor activity head predicts the individual action of each actor, and the membership head projects actor and group tokens into a shared embedding space. Actor–group affinities are computed via dot products, assigning each actor to the group with the highest score.

Training Objective

We adopt the multi-task loss from Practical GAD
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
, combining: (i) individual action classification (
\mathcal{L}{\mathrm{ind}}
), (ii) group activity classification (
\mathcal{L}{\mathrm{group}}
), (iii) actor–group membership prediction (
\mathcal{L}{\mathrm{mem}}
) via Hungarian matching
(
Kuhn 1955
)
, and (iv) a contrastive group consistency loss (
\mathcal{L}{\mathrm{con}}
) inspired by InfoNCE
(
Oord, Li, and Vinyals 2018
)
.
The total loss is:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{ind}}+\sum_{i}\mathcal{L}_{\text{group}}+\lambda_{m}\sum_{i}\mathcal{L}_{\text{mem}}+\lambda_{c}\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}

(3)

Here,
\lambda_{m}
and
\lambda_{c}
are hyperparameters that control the relative importance of the membership prediction loss and the contrastive consistency loss, respectively.

Experiments

Dataset and Implementation Details.
We evaluate ProGraD on Café
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
and Social-CAD
(
Ehsanpour et al. 2020
)
. Café is a multi-camera dataset with 6-second clips from six café locations, featuring 3–14 actors per clip, annotated with 3.5M bounding boxes, group memberships, and six group activities :
queuing
,
ordering
,
selfie
,
eating
,
studying
, and
fighting
, plus
singleton
labels for ungrouped individuals. Social-CAD contains 44 videos with bounding boxes, individual actions (
walking
,
talking
,
queueing
,
waiting
,
crossing
), and group labels derived from the majority of individual actions. Since Social-CAD primarily includes singleton or loosely connected actors, it serves as a benchmark for sparse interactions. We use DinoV2‑Base
(
Oquab et al. 2024
)
(ViT‑B/14) as a frozen backbone, training randomly initialized group prompts with the two‑layer GroupContext Transformer and prediction heads. We sample
T=5
frames for Café and
T=1
for Social‑CAD using segment‑based sampling
(
Lin, Gan, and Han 2019
)
, following prior work. Models are trained for 30 epochs using AdamW with a mini‑batch of 32. Loss weights are set to
\lambda_{m}=5.0
,
\lambda_{c}=2.0
, and temperature
\tau=0.2
. More implementation details are provided in the supp. material.

Evaluation Metrics.
We follow the official protocols of Café and Social‑CAD. On Café, we report Group mAP and Outlier mIoU introduced and used by the authors of
(
Kim et al. 2024
)
. Group mAP jointly assesses group activity classification and spatial localization using Group IoU at multiple thresholds (e.g., 0.5 or 1.0), while Outlier mIoU evaluates the accurate identification of singleton actors. On Social-CAD
(
Ehsanpour et al. 2020
)
dataset, where group instances are predominantly singletons, we report Social Accuracy (percentage of correctly predicted social group assignments) and Membership Accuracy (proportion of actors correctly assigned to their social group, including singletons). Together, these metrics comprehensively capture classification, localization, and outlier detection performance.

We compare ProGraD against prior state-of-the-art methods including

)

(

Café dataset.
ProGraD achieves state-of-the-art results on the Café with a Group mAP
@
1.0 of 17.03, Group mAP
@
0.5 of 39.11, and an Outlier mIoU of 67.89—marking the highest reported performance across all metrics. Notably, ProGraD outperforms the strongest baseline, Practical GAD, by 6.2 points on Group mAP
@

@

We attribute these gains to ProGraD’s prompt-based adaptation for effectively leveraging frozen VFM features, coupled with a lightweight GCT transformer for disentangling contextual and group-level reasoning. ProGraD also improves individual action recognition (74.04% vs. 69.02%), showing that better group reasoning benefits actor-level predictions. Unlike prior work that heuristically tunes group counts, ProGraD fixes the number of group tokens to the dataset’s maximum groups, simplifying training and improving stability. When fully fine‑tuned, ProGraD further improves across all metrics, demonstrating scalability from an efficient frozen backbone setup to a high‑capacity setting and indicating that enhancing PEFT for complex multi‑group reasoning remains a promising direction.

Ablation Studies

Impact of GroupContext Transformer Components.

To understand the contribution of each component in our GroupContext Transformer, we ablate the two key attention layers: the Grouping Attention Layer, which is responsible for modeling group membership, and the Contextual Attention Layer, which encodes interactions across actor and group tokens in the global context. As shown in Table
4
removing either component leads to a notable drop in performance. Without the Grouping Attention Layer, Group mAP
@
1.0 drops from 17.03 to 14.08, and Outlier mIoU to 64.56. Removing the Contextual Attention Layer causes a larger decline (10.97 mAP
@
1.0, 61.83 mIoU), underscoring their complementary roles—Grouping Attention helps in group assignment for the actors, whereas contextual attention captures broader relationships - both intra and inter-group relationships, providing critical context for accurate activity recognition.

presents a comparison of attention maps between SOTA Practical GAD

Practical GAD produces diffuse and overlapping patterns that offer limited insights into group member associations, leading to lower interpretability and reduced performance in complex scenes. In contrast, ProGraD generates sharper and more structured attention maps that separate social groups and isolate outliers, resulting in more coherent group reasoning. This targeted attention not only enhances interpretability but also contributes to stronger recognition performance in multi-group scenarios. Failure cases, marked in red, highlight challenges such as occlusions and mirrors. In occluded scenes, obstructed spatial cues lead ProGraD to include outliers in a nearby group incorrectly. In environments with mirrors, the model mistakenly attends to reflections, disrupting group assignment. These examples underscore the remaining limitations of attention-based reasoning under visual ambiguity and motivate future extensions for spatial disambiguation and context-aware filtering.

Conclusion and Limitations

We present ProGraD, a simple and efficient framework that adapts frozen Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) for structured group activity detection with a lightweight GroupContext Transformer and a soft prompt-based design for modeling actor-group interactions. It achieves state-of-the-art performance on the Café dataset and Social-CAD, while using only
\sim
10M trainable parameters. It performs well across both dense and sparse group scenarios, providing high interpretability via structured, group-specific attention patterns. Importantly, these gains are not simply due to using a large backbone: while existing GAD decoders fail to fully exploit DINOv2, ProGraD succeeds in both frozen and fine‑tuned settings, underscoring the adaptability of our design.

While ProGraD provides a lightweight and effective way to equip vision foundation models with reasoning over multi-party social behavior, it has limitations. By building on frozen, pre-trained VFMs, the method benefits from broad generalization and requires only training a small number of parameters.
However, the backbone itself, e.g., DINO v2 is still a large transformer and inference still requires a full forward pass through the large VFM, keeping the overall model size substantial.
Additionally, our method assumes access to ground‑truth actor detections, and—like prior methods—its robustness under noisy or automated detections remains unexplored.
Finally, performance may degrade in highly occluded or visually ambiguous scenes, indicating the need for future work on robust attention mechanisms, integrated detection and spatiotemporal priors for improved group reasoning.
