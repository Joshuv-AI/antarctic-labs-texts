Effectiveness of low-fidelity versus high-fidelity interprofessional simulation on the acquisition of theoretical and practical knowledge of arterial blood gas testing: a parallel randomized controlled trial (ELVHIS-ABG-RCT) protocol
Authors: Alessio Conti, Fabio Bidoggia, Federico Abate Daga, Alice Ferraris, Roberto Penso, Chiara Piovan, Laura Simionato, David Lembo, Savino Sciascia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEducation in medical and nursing curricula aims to build a strong theoretical foundation and practical skills, essential for addressing the complex challenges of healthcare delivery. Interprofessional learning fosters teamwork and improves patient care by enhancing collaboration across disciplines. Simulation-based education provides a safe environment for critical thinking and interprofessional collaboration, particularly in procedures like arterial blood gas (ABG) testing. Despite its potential, research comparing low- and high-fidelity simulation in interprofessional contexts remains limited, presenting an opportunity to explore how different modalities impact knowledge and skills in healthcare education.MethodsA single center, superiority, parallel, randomized controlled trial will compare the effectiveness of high-fidelity versus low-fidelity interprofessional simulation in improving knowledge and skills related to the ABG test among medical and nursing students. Participants will be assigned to either the intervention or control group. All participants will watch a 12-minute educational video on ABG, then be divided into small groups of six participants each, consisting of three medical and three nursing students. Students in the intervention group will participate in a high-fidelity simulation activity consisting of three clinical case scenarios conducted in an immersive medical training room with an actor simulating the patient and an ABG arm simulator for simulating the procedure. The control group will perform the same clinical case scenarios in a low-fidelity simulation room, using non-interactive paper-based documentation. The primary outcome will be the acquisition of theoretical knowledge and practical skills, assessed by independent observers using a six-item checklist during the simulation activities. Secondary outcomes will include self-efficacy, self-confidence in learning, interprofessional education, and satisfaction with the simulation experience, evaluated through validated questionnaires administered both before and after the intervention. The target sample size is 36 checklist observations.DiscussionThis study will contribute to interprofessional simulation-based practices by evaluating the effectiveness of high-fidelity versus low-fidelity interprofessional simulation, even for restricted clinical content. This may pave the way for a more structured and consistent implementation of high-fidelity interprofessional simulation, promoting their standardization and integration from the earliest stages of undergraduate curricula. Introduction

Education during medical and nursing curricula is targeted at developing a progressive knowledge base and the practical skills required for competent healthcare delivery. Students in these fields undergo rigorous training that emphasizes theoretical understanding and skill development in clinical scenarios [1, 2]. This approach is designed to prepare them for the multifaceted challenges of healthcare, where rapid decision-making and practical competence are essential [3, 4]. However, achieving the required proficiency often requires more than traditional classroom teaching; it necessitates innovative educational strategies that integrate knowledge acquisition with real-world clinical application [3, 5, 6].

Interprofessional learning (IPL) plays a critical role in the education of healthcare professionals (HCPs) by fostering collaboration and communication across disciplines. Effective interprofessional teamwork is increasingly recognized as a cornerstone of high-quality healthcare [7]. By engaging students from diverse professional backgrounds, IPL aims to break down silos, promote mutual respect, and improve patient outcomes. It provides a structured opportunity for students to understand the roles, responsibilities, and perspectives of their peers in other disciplines, enabling more cohesive and effective healthcare delivery [8]. Importantly, IPL also aligns with the growing emphasis on patient-centered care, which requires coordinated efforts from multidisciplinary teams [8, 9].

While IPL often occurs naturally during clinical practice, undergraduate education offers a unique window to introduce and reinforce these principles in a structured and systematic manner [9]. Clinical settings provide rich, real-world opportunities for interprofessional interaction, but the high-stakes nature of these environments can limit the ability of students to experiment, fail, and learn. Consequently, there is a need to enhance IPL during undergraduate education through innovative and controlled educational interventions [10]. Early exposure to interprofessional collaboration ensures that students are better prepared to navigate the complexities of healthcare delivery when they transition to professional practice [11].

Simulation-based education is a viable solution to address these needs, offering a controlled, safe, and replicable environment for learning [12]. This approach allows students to engage in realistic scenarios without the risk of harm to patients, showing to enhance knowledge acquisition, attitudes toward teamwork, and practical skills [12, 13]. By mimicking real-world scenarios, simulation fosters critical thinking, decision-making, and problem-solving in a way that traditional didactic methods cannot. Importantly, simulation has been shown to be particularly effective in promoting IPL [14]. High-fidelity simulations, which employ advanced technology to create immersive, lifelike experiences, and low-fidelity simulations, which focus on simplified and cost-effective learning environments, both have distinct advantages [15, 16]. However, the comparative effectiveness of these modalities, particularly in interprofessional education, remains a topic of ongoing investigation [17, 18].

Arterial blood gas (ABG) testing is one of the most critical diagnostic tools in clinical practice, used to evaluate a patient’s oxygenation, ventilation, and acid-base status [19]. Despite its importance, ABG testing can be technically challenging, potentially painful for patients, and prone to misinterpretation [20]. Accurate performance and interpretation of ABG tests are crucial for timely and appropriate clinical decision-making. Errors in these processes can lead to suboptimal patient care, underscoring the need for comprehensive training. Both medical and nursing professionals must acquire the theoretical knowledge and practical skills necessary to perform and interpret ABG tests [21]. Furthermore, because ABG testing often involves interprofessional collaboration, where doctors and nurses discuss findings to arrive at clinical decisions, it is critical to foster these collaborative skills early in their training [21, 22].

Simulation-based interprofessional training provides an opportunity to address these challenges. By recreating realistic scenarios involving ABG testing, simulation allows students to practice both the technical aspects of the procedure, and the collaborative processes required for effective interpretation and application of results [12]. Early exposure to such interprofessional scenarios helps students develop a deeper understanding of their own roles and those of their peers, fostering a more cohesive approach to patient care [13, 23].

Previous studies on interprofessional simulation have focused on a wide range of clinical skills, including cardiopulmonary resuscitation [24], surgical teamwork [25], and emergency response scenarios [26]. These studies have consistently demonstrated the benefits of simulation in improving technical skills, fostering better communication, and enhancing overall teamwork. However, research on the application of simulation to specific diagnostic procedures, such as ABG testing, is limited. While studies have highlighted the effectiveness of high-fidelity simulation in certain settings, evidence comparing the outcomes of low-fidelity versus high-fidelity simulations, particularly in the context of interprofessional education, remains sparse [16]. Moreover, existing studies often focus on single-discipline simulations, neglecting the collaborative aspects of clinical practice that are critical for patient safety and effective care.

To the authors’ knowledge, no studies have previously examined the comparative effectiveness of low-fidelity versus high-fidelity interprofessional simulation on the acquisition of both theoretical knowledge and practical skills related to ABG testing. This gap in the literature highlights an important opportunity to explore how different simulation modalities impact learning outcomes in an interprofessional context. Such research is essential to inform best practices in healthcare education, ensuring that future professionals are equipped with the skills and knowledge needed to provide high-quality care. Addressing this gap will also provide valuable insights into the cost-effectiveness and practicality of implementing different types of simulation-based training in medical and nursing curricula.

This study, therefore, aims to evaluate the relative effectiveness of low-fidelity and high-fidelity interprofessional simulations in enhancing theoretical and practical knowledge of ABG testing. By addressing this gap, the study seeks to contribute to the growing body of evidence on the role of simulation in interprofessional education and inform the development of more effective and accessible training programs for HCPs.

Aims and hypotheses

Using an experimental methodology, this study will evaluate the effectiveness of an interprofessional simulation intervention for medical and nursing students through three distinct objectives and hypotheses.

The primary aim is to compare the effectiveness of high-fidelity versus low-fidelity interprofessional simulation in improving knowledge and skills related to the ABG test. The hypothesis is that participants in the high-fidelity interprofessional simulation group will develop better knowledge and skills for ABG testing compared to those in the low-fidelity group.

The secondary aims are to evaluate the effectiveness of high-fidelity interprofessional simulation compared to low-fidelity interprofessional simulation in (i) enhancing self-efficacy and self-confidence in learning, and (ii) improving interprofessional education and satisfaction with the simulation experience. It is hypothesized that participants in the high-fidelity interprofessional simulation group will report higher levels of self-efficacy, self-confidence, interprofessional education, and satisfaction with the learning experience post-intervention compared to those in the low-fidelity interprofessional simulation group.

Methods

This study protocol adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines.

Study design

The study will be a single center, superiority, parallel, randomized controlled trial. Outcome measures will be administered pre-intervention (before the interprofessional simulation) and post-intervention (after the interprofessional simulation). An open-label design will be applied, as it would be unfeasible to deliver both the intervention (high-fidelity simulation) and control treatment (low-fidelity simulation) to participants in blind.

Setting

The University of Torino was founded in 1404 and is among the oldest universities in Italy. It is now one of the largest in the Italian territory, open to international research and education, and hosting about 80,000 students across all disciplines, except for Engineering and Architecture. Among the seven departments comprising the School of Medicine at the University of Torino, the Department of Clinical and Biological Sciences delivers the program in Medicine and Surgery as well as the bachelor’s degree in nursing.

The program in Medicine and Surgery lasts six years, is conducted at the teaching facilities of San Luigi Gonzaga University Hospital, and is entirely taught in English. The first-year cohort consists of 100 medical students, with a total of over 600 students attending throughout the six years. Clinical rotations for medical students begin at the end of the third year of the program.

The bachelor’s degree in nursing is led at a separate campus situated closely to the San Luigi Gonzaga University Hospital, where nursing students complete most of their clinical placements starting from the end of the first year of the three-year program. The courses are taught in Italian, with 150 students enrolled in each year, for a total of approximately 450 students currently attending the program.

The theoretical and practical principles of ABG test are taught as part of the Cardiovascular and Respiratory Systems course in the third year of the medical program, and as part of the Medical Nursing course in the second year of the bachelor’s degree in nursing. Classes incorporate clinical case discussions, and both programs include simulated hands-on activities. The ABG technique and its interpretation are further refined during clinical rotations and placements.

Since 2018, the Department of Clinical and Biological Sciences has been conducting an interprofessional learning activity in which third-year nursing students mentor medical students prior to the start of their clinical rotations in hospital wards and at the simulation center. This interprofessional activity involves a series of technical skill-building sessions, both simulated and in vivo, focused on vital signs collection, venous sampling, and cannulation.

The ELVHIS-ABG-RCT will be conducted at the Center for Advanced Medical Simulation, a 240-square-meter facility located on the campus of the San Luigi Gonzaga University Hospital. The simulation center comprises eight rooms, three of which are smaller and specifically designed for training. These smaller rooms are equipped with virtual simulators to practice minimally invasive surgical techniques (e.g., arthroscopy, laparoscopy) and to perform invasive procedures on training models (e.g., ABG sampling, bladder catheterization, chest drainage). They also support instruction in clinical examination techniques and semeiotics (e.g., chest, abdominal, and cardiovascular assessments). A larger room is designed to replicate a real hospital environment and can be configured as either an acute care ward or an intensive care unit. It is equipped with two high-fidelity mannequin simulators. The centerpiece of the simulation center is the immersive medical training room, a 20-square-meter space designed to fully immerse students in a 360° clinical scenario (Immersive and Interactive Space by Gener8™, Barnsley - UK). The room features wall-mounted projectors displaying real-life images taken from the wards where students conduct their clinical rotations and placements. It is also equipped with an audio system that simulates authentic hospital sounds and a vibrating floor to replicate outdoor scenarios, such as ambulance or helicopter transports. Both the larger room and the immersive room are monitored and controlled by a technician from a dedicated control room, which is equipped with one-way glasses, environmental cameras, and an audio system for seamless monitoring and control. The simulation center is further enhanced by a room featuring an advanced touch-screen anatomical table for virtual dissection of the human body, as well as a plenary room designed for planning and reviewing activities conducted during simulations.

Recruitment

Students who meet the eligibility criteria will be invited to participate in the study through an email sent by the medical program and bachelor’s degree in nursing secretariats. The inviting email will provide information about the purpose of the study, eligibility criteria, and basic logistical information (e.g., location, duration). Additionally, a detailed information form will be attached to the email, providing participants with more comprehensive details to help them evaluate their potential involvement, including that the activity is formative, focused on ABG, and that they can independently acquire knowledge of the procedure. Interested students will be asked to reply to the email to confirm their enrollment in the study. Recruitment will continue until the required sample size is reached, with an upper cap of ten groups (60 participants), depending on student availability and simulation center resources.

Randomization

After agreeing to participate in the study, medical and nursing students will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the high-fidelity or low-fidelity interprofessional simulation group, using SPSS V.29.0.2 software (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Randomization will be conducted separately for medical and nursing students to ensure equal representation of participants in both groups and to balance their presence, thereby allowing for an IPL experience. To maintain independence and objectivity, an independent member of the research team, who will remain blinded to the participants’ group assignments, will conduct the randomization process [27].

Procedure

After randomization, participants will be assigned sequentially into up to ten groups to form up to five high-fidelity interprofessional simulation groups and up to five low-fidelity interprofessional simulation groups (Fig. 1). Each group will consist of six students, including three medical students and three nursing students. Groups will be invited to participate in two at the same time (one high-fidelity group and one low-fidelity group) via email, specifying the time and location for their session at the simulation center. The invitation email will not specify in which of the two groups they are allocated.Fig. 1Consolidated Standards of reporting trials flow diagram

Participants will be received at the simulation center and taken on a brief visit to its rooms. The etiquette to be maintained during simulation activities will also be explained. At the end of the visit, they will be taken to a plenary room outside the simulation center. The members of the research team will conduct a brief, structured pre-briefing covering learning objectives, expectations, ground rules, procedures, and opportunities for clarification before beginning the activity. This information will also be provided in a written information sheet given to each participant. Students will then be asked to review and sign the informed consent form to confirm their participation in the study.

Participants will be asked to complete the baseline assessment by completing a questionnaire set. A 12-minute educational video will be shown, summarizing the principles, technique, and interpretation of ABG testing. The video will cover the definition and purpose of the ABG test, the diagnostic and clinical rationale for conducting it, and the technique, including patient assessment, required equipment, factors to consider before performing the procedure, questions to ask the patient, identification of the puncture site, sampling steps, and proper disposal of materials. Additionally, a brief interpretation of ABG test results will be provided, focusing on pH values, carbon dioxide levels, and bicarbonate levels to identify the four main diagnoses: respiratory acidosis, metabolic acidosis, respiratory alkalosis, and metabolic alkalosis. The symptoms and potential triggers of each condition will also be presented.

At the end of the video presentation, participants will be informed about which group they have been assigned to. They will then be guided by the research team to the designated rooms, where they will learn whether they will participate in the high-fidelity or low-fidelity interprofessional simulation. The two groups will only meet at the conclusion of all simulation activities. Participants will be asked to maintain confidentiality and refrain from discussing the methods and content of the simulation activities with colleagues who have not yet participated in the study.

Interventions

Participants in both groups will be presented with three clinical cases of increasing difficulty. Each case is based on real clinical scenarios that have been used over the years to train students and will be adapted for the purpose of this study. Each case and evaluation tool will be tested and reviewed by third-year medical and nursing students to check for clarity, sequencing, and feasibility across both fidelity conditions.

The groups, consisting of six participants (both in high-fidelity interprofessional simulation and low-fidelity interprofessional simulation), will be divided into three pairs for each clinical case. Each pair will include one medical student and one nursing student, requiring them to work as a team. This approach will create three different pairings across the clinical case scenarios, allowing participants to experience IPL with various partners.

The first case will take place in a medical ward and will involve an elderly man with a suspected pneumothorax leading to respiratory acidosis. In the second case, participants will have to identify metabolic acidosis in a diabetic woman in a general surgery ward. The third case will present metabolic alkalosis in a marathon runner who arrives at the emergency department.

Clinical records, including admission letters, examination reports, electrocardiogram traces, X-ray images, and laboratory test results, will be prepared for each clinical case on hospital letterhead or sourced from hospital archives. These records will be adapted as needed to provide participants with a realistic and authentic experience.

Each clinical case will last 10 minutes and require participants to assess the patient, perform the necessary actions to get the arterial blood sample, and correctly interpret the ABG test. In the first case, participants will identify and prepare all the materials needed to perform the ABG test. In the second case, the medical student will perform the sampling, while in the third case, the nursing student will carry out the sampling. Each pair of students will conclude the clinical case scenario by providing a diagnosis.

Thus, while the simulation activities will have the same content and procedure within the clinical cases, they will differ in the level of fidelity with which they will be conducted.

High-fidelity interprofessional simulation

The interprofessional simulation activity will be conducted in the immersive medical training room, with the walls and sounds adapted to reflect the settings of the three clinical case scenarios (medical ward, surgical ward, and emergency department). The room will be equipped with a bed, a multi-parameter monitor connected to the control room, which participants can use to measure vital signs (pulse, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, temperature, and respiratory rate), and a glucometer. An oxygen flowmeter with nasal cannulas and a mask will also be mounted on a wall. A cart will be stocked with materials for performing ABG, along with additional items to challenge the students. Additionally, a small table will feature an ABG arm simulator (by Kyoto Kagaku, Ltd) for simulating the procedure.

A researcher will introduce the clinical case by reading a brief description to the two participants working together. Each pair will receive a clinical record at the end of the introduction and will be given a maximum of 10 minutes to address each case.

The patient will be personified by an actor with a medical background who will follow a structured script written according to the basic objectives of the learning activity, as presented in the educational video. These objectives include assessing the patient, identifying the need for ABG, and considering any relevant factors before proceeding with the procedure.

The simulation laboratory technician will adjust the patient’s vital signs or provide objective examination findings when participants use the monitor or request additional information. In the first clinical case, when participants determine that an ABG is necessary, the technician will instruct them to collaboratively prepare the materials required for the procedure. In the subsequent two cases, the technician will specify which team member should perform the procedure on the simulator. Afterward, participants will receive the ABG test results on a printout from a point-of-care analyzer and will be asked to communicate the diagnosis.

Low-fidelity interprofessional simulation

This interprofessional simulation activity will be conducted in the large room that will be equipped with three tables and six chairs. A researcher will provide instructions to the participants and inform them when they need to switch partners, which will occur every 10 minutes.

Each table will receive a folder containing the same materials for each of the three clinical cases. The first page will include a printed description of the clinical case, followed by a question requiring participants to arrange in the correct order the actions they need to perform, such as assessing the patient, taking vital signs, and examining the clinical documentation. The next section will provide the same clinical information given to the high-fidelity group, followed by a brief description of the objective clinical examination and vital signs. A subsequent page will ask participants to answer a question about the interventions or exams they would perform, such as administering oxygen or other therapies depending on the case, requesting additional X-rays or neurological tests, or performing an ABG. For the first case, participants will complete a checklist of all the equipment needed to perform an ABG. For the other two cases, they will be asked to place the phases of the ABG procedure in the correct order. The last page of the folder will include the ABG test results on a printout from a point-of-care analyzer, followed by a four-item multiple-choice question requiring participants to identify the correct diagnosis.

Debriefing

At the end of the simulation activities, all students from both groups will be taken separately to designated plenary rooms, where a member of the research team will conduct a debriefing session. The debriefing will use the plus/delta method, during which participants will be asked a series of questions to identify the strengths and weaknesses they experienced during the simulation activity [28]. These strengths and weaknesses will be recorded on a flip chart to facilitate discussion and encourage reflection among participants, focusing on both clinical and relational aspects that emerged during the simulation activities. At the conclusion of the debriefing session, each participant will be asked to complete a set of questionnaires.

Outcome measures

The trial outcomes will be assessed through a combination of structured observations during interprofessional simulation activities and validated questionnaires completed by participants (Table 1). The validated questionnaires will be administered at two time points: baseline (T0) and the end of the intervention (T2), while structured observations will take place during the interprofessional simulation activities (T1). In both the high-fidelity and low-fidelity simulation groups, two independent observers will be assigned from the research team, remaining the same throughout the study to ensure consistency in evaluations. The observers will assess the performance of each couple of participants within a single clinical case. All three scenarios will be evaluated. Primary outcomes

Theoretical knowledge and practical skills will be assessed using a six-item checklist specifically developed for this study (provided as Supplementary Material). This tool will be used to evaluate each clinical case completed by pairs of participants in both high-fidelity and low-fidelity interprofessional simulations. Three of the six items will assess theoretical knowledge, focusing on patient assessment, the correct sequence of steps to be followed (including performing an ABG), and the identification of the diagnosis at the conclusion of the clinical case. Practical skills will be evaluated through three items, which include patient identification and consent acquisition, performance of the technique (including the Allen’s test), and sample preparation to be sent to the point-of-care analyzer. Observers will rate the checklist at the team level, based on completion of prespecified actions by the dyad as a whole; the score is not intended to reflect individual competence.

Each item will be rated by observers using a three-point Likert scale (0 = absent, 1 = partially performed, 2 = performed correctly). In the high-fidelity interprofessional simulation group, observers will complete the checklist in real-time as the clinical case progresses. In the low-fidelity interprofessional simulation group, the checklists will be completed at the end of each case based on the participants’ responses. Scoring in the low-fidelity group reflects the accurate description and sequencing of essential steps, as derived from the standardized paper-based materials, rather than from their physical execution. The checklist will be applied in each of the three scenarios, yielding a total score (0–12) per dyad (nursing student and medical student) and per scenario. The unit of analysis for the primary outcome is the dyad-case performance.

The checklist was developed by the research team, who identified content domains directly aligned with those presented in the educational video and consistent with the competencies expected to be acquired by first-year nursing and second-year medical students. The draft instrument underwent content validation by six senior simulation educators, following established procedures for expert judgement [29]. To further ensure clarity, sequencing, and feasibility, the checklist was pilot-tested with a group of third-year students, allowing us to confirm its applicability and usability in both high- and low-fidelity environments.

To standardize scoring across scenarios and study arms, a brief manual with item-specific anchors, specifying the minimum observable/verbalized criteria required for scoring (0, 1, and 2), will be developed. This will reduce subjectivity and minimize rater drift over time. All observers will undergo standardized training prior to study start, including review of the scoring manual and calibration scoring on practice cases. Inter-rater reliability will be assessed prior to data collection using item-level weighted kappa coefficients and an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for the total score. If agreement is suboptimal, additional training and recalibration will be conducted before formal scoring begins.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes will explore the broader educational impact of the intervention by assessing changes in self-efficacy, self-confidence in learning, perceptions of interprofessional collaboration, readiness for interprofessional practice, and satisfaction with the simulation experience. Validated questionnaires will be administered at baseline (T0) and after the intervention (T2) to evaluate these domains (Table 1).

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE) will be used to measure participants’ belief in their ability to perform effectively in general situations, which are transferable to the educational context [30]. The GSE consists of 10 statements, with respondents indicating their level of agreement on a four-point Likert scale (from 0 = not all true to 4 = exactly true). Scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores reflecting stronger generalized self-efficacy beliefs. The scale has demonstrated good construct, convergent, and criterion validity, with high Cronbach’s alpha values (0.79–0.91) [31, 32].

The Student Satisfaction and Self-Confidence in Learning (SCLS) scale is a 13-item tool generally used in educational settings involving simulations [33]. The SCLS includes statements about students’ attitudes toward the instructions received during simulation, focusing on their satisfaction with the learning experience and their self-confidence in acquiring the necessary information. For each statement, respondents complete a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The SCLS supports a two-factor structure, representing the dimensions of satisfaction and confidence in learning, and has demonstrated satisfactory reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha values for the overall scale and subscales typically ranging from 0.77 to 0.97 [34, 35].

The Interdisciplinary Education Perception Scale (IEPS) is a widely used 18-item tool designed to measure perceptions of interprofessional collaboration and education among healthcare students and professionals [36]. The IEPS includes 18 statements, with respondents indicating their level of agreement on a six-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 6 = strongly agree). Originally proposed as a four-factor model, the IEPS is now considered a two-factor instrument, focusing on cooperation and teamwork and positivity, with good reliability values [37, 38].

The Readiness for Interprofessional Learning Scale (RIPLS) consists of 19 items divided in four subscales: teamwork and collaboration, negative professional identity, positive professional identity, and roles and responsibilities [39]. Respondents indicate their level of agreement on a five-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The RIPLS has been widely used to assess readiness for collaborative practice in healthcare settings, showing acceptable degrees of reliability and validity [40, 41].

The Satisfaction with Simulation Experience (SSE) scale will be used to assess participants’ satisfaction levels during the interprofessional simulation activities [42]. This 18-item instrument has been widely used in simulation-based learning experiences involving healthcare students and professionals, demonstrating reliability and validity (Cronbach’s alpha values above 0.85) in assessing educational outcomes across various settings [43, 44]. The SSE scale measures respondents’ agreement on a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) and evaluates satisfaction across three main components: debriefing and reflection, clinical reasoning, and clinical learning.

The following sociodemographic data will be collected for each participant: age, gender, university courses taken or degrees obtained, and type of high school attended (scientific/technological, classical/literary, or other).

Sample size

To detect a statistically significant difference in the primary outcome (the observation refers to a checklist score), defined as the mean difference in the 6-item checklist applied by observers for the assessment of participants’ knowledge and skills in the high-fidelity interprofessional simulation group compared with those in the low-fidelity interprofessional simulation group, we hypothesized a between-group mean difference (Δ) of 3 points on the total checklist score (0–12), with a standard deviation (SD) of 2 points. The researchers considered the chosen mean difference (Δ) an educationally meaningful change of 25% in the single scenario, rather than an item-level effect.

where d represents the effect size, calculated as the mean difference divided by the standard deviation. Using these parameters, the required sample size is 16 observation per group, resulting in a total of 32 observations for the study. Considering a 10% dropout rate, a target sample size of 36 observations has been set. The calculation was performed using Python, which provides precise computation of z-scores and sample size based on the specified parameters.

Given the group structure, each session session yields 9 checklist observations. Therefore, achieving 36 checklist observations requires a minimum of four scheduled groups in total (two per arm), corresponding to 24 participants. For pedagogical and logistical reasons, we plan to schedule up to ten groups, which would generate up to 90 checklist observations.

Statistical analysis

Data process and analysis will be conducted using SPSS V.29.0.2 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Continuous variables will be presented as mean ± SD and compared using t-tests for independent groups. Categorical variables will be presented as percentages and compared between groups using χ2 tests.

The Shapiro-Wilik test will be conducted to assess the normality of the primary and secondary outcome data, obtaining means, SDs, medians, minimum and maximum values, interquartile ranges (IQRs), and frequencies.

If the repeated measures data meet the normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions, a series of repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) will be performed. In the case of a non-normal data distribution, the Mann Whitney U test will be used for between-group comparisons, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test will be applied to evaluate pre- and post- changes within each group. Analyses will account for the clustered/repeated nature of checklist observations within groups/dyads, as prespecified. Because the checklist is a newly developed tool, standardized effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals will be reported to support the interpretation of results. This will support interpretation in the event that the observed between-group difference is smaller than the effect assumed in the sample size calculation.

A statistical significance level will be set at a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05.

Data management and monitoring

To protect their privacy, each participant will be assigned a numeric code. Data will be double-entered by two research assistants and stored electronically to minimize errors, with printed documents also retained. Both paper and electronic data will be securely stored, with regular backups on a protected server owned by the University of Torino. Upon study completion, data will be retained for at least five years to ensure regulatory compliance. After this period, data will be destroyed in accordance with privacy regulations, ensuring complete physical and digital deletion.

Ethics approval and dissemination

The Ethics Committee of the University of Torino approved the study protocol (Resolution No. 0361521/2024). All eligible participants will be informed about the study aims and intervention details before signing the consent form. The trial will strictly follow version 1.0 of the protocol. Any modifications to the protocol will be formally submitted to the Ethics Committee of the University of Torino for review. The protocol will adhere to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Any deviations from the protocol will be documented and reported.

Research findings will be shared through presentations at professional scientific meetings and publications in peer-reviewed journals.

Discussion

Simulation-based education in healthcare is considered a promising method for enhancing knowledge, skills, and confidence [12, 13]. Additionally, simulation has proven effective in reducing errors and improving teamwork by providing a safe environment for applying theoretical knowledge, practicing clinical skills, and mastering communication [14]. These aspects are crucial for ensuring patient safety and delivering high-quality healthcare [12]. While substantial evidence supports the integration of simulation into healthcare curricula, standardized practices and improved incorporation into healthcare systems are still needed to fully realize its potential in enhancing educational outcomes and preparing students for real-world clinical challenges [12, 45]. Similarly, although IPL is recognized as a valuable approach to fostering teamwork and clarifying the roles of different healthcare professionals [23, 46], its implementation from the early stages of undergraduate education remains limited despite its potential to facilitate role transitions and improve job satisfaction [14, 47].

The results of this study will contribute to interprofessional simulation-based practices by evaluating the effectiveness of high-fidelity versus low-fidelity interprofessional simulation, even for restricted clinical content, such as ABG performance and interpretation. This protocol intentionally restricts eligibility to students who have not yet received formal instruction on ABG in their curriculum, as the study aims to evaluate an introductory, standardized early learning experience on ABG delivered through interprofessional simulation, rather than advanced mastery. The learning objectives are deliberately limited to the fundamental tasks and reasoning processes explicitly addressed in the learning materials and scenarios. Accordingly, the intervention is designed to complement, not replace, the more comprehensive ABG education students will receive in the later years of their training. Determining whether an ABG is indicated, prioritizing it among competing clinical needs, and interpreting its results in relation to a dynamically evolving patient condition typically requires coordinated discussion and critical appraisal between nurses and physicians. Simulation provides an optimal environment to recreate these non-linear, team-based decision processes, which cannot be adequately assessed through isolated knowledge or practice testing. By addressing an area that remains underexplored, this study may support a more structured and consistent implementation of high-fidelity interprofessional simulation, promoting their standardization and integration from the earliest stages of undergraduate curricula. Considering the higher costs of high-fidelity simulation in terms of human resources and activity organization [12, 13], our findings may also help institutions assess the potential benefits of this approach, not only in terms of immediate learning but, more importantly, in terms of satisfaction and long-term retention of HCPs, who are increasingly needed amid the ongoing workforce shortage. To this end, future work should quantify costs and resource requirements across fidelity levels to better inform decisions on scalability.

Potential limitations of the study include the sample size, which could be considered small. Nevertheless, it is consistent with sample sizes commonly reported in experimental simulation research, particularly in studies involving procedural competencies and interprofessional designs, where smaller, well-controlled cohorts are both pedagogically and methodologically warranted. Furthermore, the sample size was determined a priori through a rigorous power calculation using a conservative significance level and an adequate desired power. For these reasons, despite the limited sample, this study was designed and powered as a hypothesis-testing trial rather than a pilot or feasibility study. [48]. Although the minimum number of scheduled groups needed to achieve the targeted number of checklist observations is limited, we plan to run additional groups up to the prespecified cap to maximize student access to interprofessional simulation practice within available resources. This also increases the precision of effect estimates and supports more robust subgroup and sensitivity analyses, while maintaining the same standardized intervention delivery. Another limitation may concern the duration of the intervention. Yet, the three structured ten-minute scenarios are consistent with International Nursing Association for Clinical Simulation and Learning Standards recommending brief, focused learning activities [49]. This format is particularly suitable for procedural skills such as ABG sampling and interpretation, where short, high-density cycles of assessment and action promote the development of procedural fluency and early automatism. A further limitation is that the primary outcome checklist is newly developed for this study; although it underwent expert content validation and feasibility testing in a cohort of students from different years than those of the participants who will be enrolled, its broader psychometric properties will require further evaluation in future work. The performance checklist includes a limited number of items, intentionally aligned with the brief, introductory nature of the scenarios. While this improves feasibility and reduces rater burden, it may concentrate measurement error and limit the precision and robustness of the total score. We will therefore report inter-rater reliability estimates and confidence intervals to support interpretation. A fourth potential limitation is the single-center design. Nevertheless, this approach was deliberately selected to ensure a fully standardized learning environment, reducing variability in simulation facilities, faculty delivery, and fidelity resources. Such standardization enhances internal validity and supports more reliable inter-group comparisons. From an internal perspective, the interprofessional simulation activity may be influenced by the evaluation by external observers and the use of an actor in the high-fidelity group. Moreover, by design, the low-fidelity condition elicits procedural workflow through paper-based sequencing rather than hands-on psychomotor execution; therefore, the quality of physical technique cannot be directly assessed in the low-fidelity arm. These limitations will be mitigated through structured observer training, consistent assignment of observers to either the high-fidelity or low-fidelity groups, and preliminary assessment their inter-rater agreement. Additionally, the actor will follow a structured script, and the restricted duration of the clinical scenarios will minimize deviations. Finally, the assessment is planned at a single time point, interpreted as dyad-level procedural competence, with no follow-up retention assessment, as all students will watch to the same educational video before the interventions, but they will soon begin internship activities, which could influence their knowledge and skills.
