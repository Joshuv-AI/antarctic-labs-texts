Time-to-maximum Analysis Using Rapid Processing of Perfusion and Diffusion Software for Assessment of the Risk of Delayed Cerebral Ischemia after Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage
Authors: Shimpei TSUBOKI, Tatemi TODAKA, Yukihiro IMAOKA, Satoshi NAMITOME, Seigo SHINDO, Yasuyuki KAKU, Akitake MUKASA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perfusion computed tomography predicts and diagnoses delayed cerebral ischemia after subarachnoid hemorrhage, but challenges in precision and interpretation limit its adoption. This study investigated whether tissue time-to-maximum analysis during the peak term of cerebral vasospasm, processed via Rapid Processing of Perfusion and Diffusion, can stratify delayed cerebral ischemia risk in patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage. In this retrospective cohort study, patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage treated surgically or endovascularly from March 2021 to March 2025 at the Japanese Red Cross Kumamoto Hospital underwent perfusion computed tomography approximately 10 days after the onset. Time-to-maximum parameters were computed via Rapid Processing of Perfusion and Diffusion and analyzed using logistic regression to assess their association with subsequent delayed cerebral ischemia. The primary endpoint was the development of delayed cerebral ischemia. Of the 181 patients initially screened, 101 were included in the study. The cohort included 81 women, with a mean age of 65 years. Among the 101 patients, 22 developed delayed cerebral ischemia. Compared with those without delayed cerebral ischemia, the delayed cerebral ischemia group had higher preoperative World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grades and a higher frequency of significant cerebral vasospasm. The volume of time-to-maximum > 4 sec showed the strongest independent association with delayed cerebral ischemia. Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified a threshold of 31 mL for time-to-maximum > 4 sec, yielding a sensitivity of 0.91 and a specificity of 0.96. In multivariable logistic regression adjusting for World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade and cerebral vasospasm, time-to-maximum > 4 sec was associated with delayed cerebral ischemia development. The incorporation of time-to-maximum analysis via Rapid Processing of Perfusion and Diffusion may standardize perfusion computed tomography interpretation and enable delayed cerebral ischemia risk assessment.

Introduction

Cerebral vasospasm (CVS) and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) are the predominant prognostic determinants in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). Although several prophylactic measures have been devised to reduce the risk of CVS, it remains a challenging aspect in the SAH management.1-4) While efficacious endovascular interventions are available alongside conventional medical therapies for CVS, the temporal window from the onset of vasospasm to the onset of irreversible cerebral ischemia is narrow. Hence, there is an urgent need to develop potent diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for identifying patients at risk. Recent studies have suggested that perfusion computed tomography (PCT) may be useful for predicting or diagnosing DCI after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage.5-11) Although PCT performed in the acute or early vasospasm phase has been reported to predict subsequent DCI, its prognostic accuracy in the early phase is limited. In contrast, PCT performed during the peak phase of vasospasm or at the time of neurological symptom onset may better reflect clinically relevant perfusion impairment.10,11) However, variability in evaluation methods and the complexity of quantitative analysis have limited the widespread clinical adoption of PCT.5,7,9-11) Recently, the time-to-maximum of a deconvolved tissue residue function (Tmax) has gained recognition as a superior predictor of hypoperfused tissue volume in acute ischemic stroke.12-17) Measurement of Tmax using Rapid Processing of Perfusion and Diffusion (RAPID) software allows for automated quantification, reducing variation among facilities and users. A previous report substantiated the utility of Tmax in the identification of CVS.18) However, there is a paucity of reports regarding DCI prognosis. This study explored the relationship between PCT Tmax and DCI incidence using the RAPID software, aiming to 1) utilize PCT Tmax for the assessment of the risk of DCI, and 2) establish criteria for its detection.

Materials and Methods

Study design and patient population

This study is a retrospective cohort study and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Japanese Red Cross Kumamoto Hospital (approval number: 605). The manuscript is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational cohort studies. Informed consent was not required because of the observational nature of the retrospective analysis of anonymized data acquired at our institution. This retrospective cohort study included patients with aSAH who underwent surgical or endovascular treatment at the Japanese Red Cross Kumamoto Hospital between May 2021 and May 2025. Clinical, imaging, and demographic data were obtained from the electronic medical records. Individuals with a World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) grade of 5 were excluded because patients with this grade do not undergo acute-phase surgical or endovascular treatment at our institution. Patients who had undergone parent artery occlusion or trapping, or had a history of cervical or intracranial arterial occlusion, were also excluded, as their inclusion might compromise the accuracy of cerebral perfusion assessment. With respect to the management of cerebral vasospasm, the institutional standard treatment consists of general supportive care and pharmacological treatment with fasudil hydrochloride, cilostazol, and ozagrel sodium. From 2024 onward, clazosentan sodium was administered in selected cases at the discretion of the attending physician, who also decided the endovascular treatment for patients who developed delayed cerebral ischemia. At our institution, all patients with aSAH underwent PCT imaging between days 8 and 12 after the onset of SAH. The following day, 14 post-SAH, either computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed. Patients who, for any reason, did not undergo PCT 8-12 days after the onset or who did not undergo evaluation for DCI were excluded. Furthermore, as an exploratory study, the relationship between the presence of CVS and PCT parameters in association with the development of DCI was examined. The primary endpoint was the development of DCI, and the diagnostic ability of PCT Tmax values obtained on days 8-12 after SAH onset for predicting DCI development was examined. The following data were collected from the electronic medical records and statistically analyzed: age, sex, preoperative WFNS grade, anatomic location of the aneurysm, type of surgery, presence of significant CVS, date of PCT, radiographic data (including PCT), date of DCI diagnosis, and the modified Rankin scale (mRS) score at discharge.

Perfusion imaging data

All imaging studies were performed according to a standardized institutional protocol using a 160-mm, 320-row multidetector CT scanner (Aquilion ONE Vision; Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Tochigi, Japan). Perfusion CT (PCT) was acquired with an axial field of view of 240-270 mm, adjusted by the attending radiologic technologist to ensure complete coverage from the foramen magnum to the vertex. Images were reconstructed with a slice thickness of 1 mm. PCT was obtained using cine mode acquisition with a gantry rotation time of 1 sec (tube voltage, 80 kV). Tube current was set at 350 mA for the initial acquisition and then reduced to 150 mA for subsequent scans. Iodinated contrast material was administered intravenously at a dose of 50 mL via an 18-gauge or larger antecubital vein using a power injector at a rate of 5 mL/sec, followed by a 50-mL saline flush at the same rate. The initial scan was initiated 6 sec after the start of contrast injection. Subsequently, images were acquired every 2 sec for 16 consecutive acquisitions beginning at 11 sec after contrast injection, followed by 6 additional acquisitions at 5-sec intervals. This acquisition protocol allowed high temporal-resolution assessment of contrast bolus passage through the cerebral circulation. Single- and multiphase CT angiography images were reconstructed at the CT acquisition workstation using Ziostation2 (Ziosoft Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Time-density curves for arterial input and venous output functions were obtained from the contralateral internal carotid artery and the superior sagittal sinus, respectively. Perfusion maps and quantitative perfusion parameters were generated using RAPID software (iSchemaView, Menlo Park, CA, USA).

DCI assessment

The definition of DCI was based on previous literature concerning DCI and PCT,5,7,19) and symptomatic CVS was included as part of the criteria. DCI was defined as the presence of at least 1 of the following findings:

1. The emergence of focal neurological deficits―such as motor limb weakness, aphasia, hemineglect, and/or hemianopia―lasting or fluctuating for at least 1 hour.

2. The development of a global neurological deficit, defined as a decrease of ≥2 points on the Glasgow Coma Scale, attributable to ongoing cerebral ischemia and persisting or fluctuating for a minimum duration of one hour.

3. The appearance of new cerebral infarctions on radiographical imaging that cannot be accounted for by other clinical, radiological, or laboratory abnormalities, and that do not occur immediately following aneurysm occlusion procedures, as assessed using MRI or non-contrast CT performed during the clinical course. PCT images were not used for the assessment of DCI.

DCI was independently evaluated by 2 neuro-interventionalists (S.T. and T.T.; both have board certification from the Japan Neurosurgical Society and the Japanese Society for Neuroendovascular Therapy, each with >10 years of clinical experience) based on the patient's radiological data. Any disparities in assessments were resolved by consensus.

Significant CVS assessment

The primary criterion widely employed in the diagnosis of severe vasospasm entails a reduction in vessel caliber exceeding 50%, with a classification of moderate when within the range of 25-50% and mild if falling <25%, affecting various intracranial vessels.20,21) CVS was defined as a reduction in arterial diameter of ≥50% compared to that observed on the initial CT angiography. Arterial diameters were measured manually using 1-mm-slice-thick 2-dimensional images obtained from PCT or Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), with the criterion met in at least 1 vessel. The evaluation included vessels located in the internal carotid artery; M1 and M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery; A1 and A2 segments of the anterior cerebral artery; and P1 and P2 segments of the posterior cerebral artery, the vertebral artery, and the basilar artery. The CVS determination was independently conducted by 2 neuro-interventionalists (S.T. and T.T.; both have board certification from the Japan Neurosurgical Society and the Japanese Society for Neuroendovascular Therapy), relying solely on the patient's imaging dataset. Any divergent opinions were harmoniously reconciled through consensus.

Statistical analyses

As this study included all available data from the initiation of the imaging examination protocol to the most recent period, no formal statistical sample size calculation was performed. Patients were divided into 2 groups according to clinical outcomes. Variables with normal distributions, verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test, were expressed as means and standard deviations and compared using the t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. Univariate logistic regression was performed to calculate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted for continuous variables, and areas under the curves (AUCs) were compared using DeLong's test. Multivariate logistic regression was performed using the variable with the largest AUC, adjusted for WFNS grade, which is strongly associated with DCI.22) As an exploratory analysis, this study assessed differences in PCT parameters between the non-DCI and DCI groups in cases with significant CVS, focusing on the parameter identified as most useful for diagnosing DCI based on logistic regression analysis. In addition, among the included patients, the predictive ability of Tmax analysis for the development of DCI was also evaluated in those who underwent PCT during the early vasospasm phase (days 4-7). Interobserver agreement for the presence or absence of DCI and significant CVS was evaluated using the κ statistic. Additionally, missing outcome data (DCI) were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, with 20 imputations (m = 20) being performed in R (version 4.5.2) using the mice package. Imputation models included baseline clinical variables and imaging parameters used in the primary analyses, assuming missing at random. All statistical analyses, except for multiple imputation, were performed using JMP Pro 17.1.0, with statistical significance set at a threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

In total, 181 patients with aSAH underwent surgical or endovascular intervention at Japanese Red Cross Kumamoto Hospital. Two patients were excluded because of variations in operative procedures. One patient was excluded based on a history of carotid artery occlusion. Twenty-six patients were excluded based on their classification of WFNS grade 5. Furthermore, 43 patients were excluded because they had not undergone PCT, and an additional 8 were excluded due to a lack of imaging evaluation 14 days after onset, which precluded DCI evaluation. Ultimately, 101 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure S1).

Of the 101 patients, 20 (20%) were male, and the mean age was 65.5 years. The median preoperative WFNS grade was 2. Notably, endovascular interventions were administered in 41 cases (41%). Twenty-two patients (22%) developed DCI, and 47 patients (47%) had significant CVS. The eligible patient cohort was dichotomized into 2 groups based on the presence or absence of DCI. This categorization yielded significant differences in preoperative WFNS grade (median [IQR], 2.5 [1.75-4] vs 1 [1-4], p = 0.03), significant CVS incidence (95% vs. 33%, p < 0.001), and mRS score at discharge (median [IQR], 4 [2-5] vs 2 [1-3], p = 0.02) (Table 1). There were no significant differences between the 2 groups with respect to the pharmacological agents used for the management of cerebral vasospasm. The median day of DCI diagnosis was 14 days after onset. In 20 of the 22 cases, DCI was diagnosed on or after the day immediately following PCT, whereas in the remaining 2 cases, PCT was performed at the time of symptom onset. No data were missing for any of the analyzed variables.

ROC were generated for the volumes of Tmax > 4, 6, and 8 sec, and the AUCs were compared using a DeLong's test. The volume of Tmax > 4 sec showed the largest AUC (Tmax > 4 sec vs Tmax > 6 sec: 95% CI 0.055-0.29; p < 0.001, Tmax 4 sec vs Tmax > 8 sec: 95% CI 0.21-0.43; p < 0.001, Tmax > 6 sec vs Tmax > 8 sec: 95% CI 0.045-0.23; p = 0.004), with a threshold of 31 mL yielding a sensitivity of 0.91 and specificity of 0.94 (Figure 1). A multivariate logistic regression analysis assessing the incidence of DCI in conjunction with the variables of Tmax > 4 sec and preoperative WFNS grade, which showed notable discrepancies between the 2 cohorts, revealed that only the volume of Tmax > 4 sec exhibited an independent and meaningful correlation with DCI onset (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03-1.08, p < 0.001) (Table 3). To account for missing outcome data, multiple imputation was performed for 5 cases that were excluded from the primary analysis due to difficulty in assessing the occurrence of DCI. Multiple imputation yielded stable pooled estimates. In the pooled logistic regression analysis, Tmax > 4 sec remained significantly associated with the development of DCI (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03-1.08, p < 0.001). Based on this association, an ROC curve was generated using the imputed dataset to evaluate the diagnostic performance of Tmax > 4 sec for DCI, yielding a mean AUC of 0.95. Furthermore, in multivariable logistic regression analysis including Tmax > 4 sec and WFNS grade, Tmax > 4 sec remained independently associated with the development of DCI (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03-1.07, p < 0.001). Overall, the results remained unchanged after accounting for missing outcome data using multiple imputation. Among patients with significant CVS, the volume of Tmax > 4 sec was significantly greater in the DCI group compared with the non-DCI group (median [IQR]: 109 mL [72-208] vs 0 mL [0-11.25], p < 0.001) (Figure 2).

Representative case

A 60-year-old woman with a ruptured anterior communicating artery aneurysm underwent coil embolization. On the day after surgery, fasudil hydrochloride and ozagrel sodium were administered intravenously, and oral cilostazol was initiated. Although her postoperative course was uneventful, perfusion CT performed 8 days after onset demonstrated a conspicuous perfusion delay in the right cerebral hemisphere, with a volume of Tmax > 4 seconds of 311 mL (Figure 3a). The patient remained asymptomatic and was therefore managed with augmented fluid therapy. Subsequently, on the ninth day after onset, she complained of dysarthria, and an MRI was performed. MRA revealed severe CVS in the bilateral anterior cerebral, right middle cerebral, and internal carotid arteries (Figure 3b). No evidence of early ischemic changes was detected on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) using MRI (Figure 3c), prompting ongoing patient surveillance. However, over time, the patient developed left-sided hemiplegia and cognitive impairment. DWI by MRI on day 11 revealed extensive early ischemic changes within the right cerebral hemisphere (Figure 3d). At this point, a DCI diagnosis was made. As the ischemic lesion was already extensive, endovascular treatment was not performed; however, medical treatment was strengthened. Finally, the left hemiplegia persisted, and the patient was transferred for rehabilitation with an mRS score of 4.

Discussion

This retrospective study examined the association between PCT analysis during the peak term of CVS, particularly Tmax volume derived from RAPID software, and the occurrence of DCI after aSAH. The results demonstrated that the volume of Tmax > 4 sec was strongly associated with subsequent DCI, with high diagnostic accuracy at a threshold of 31 mL. These findings suggest that standardized and automated Tmax analysis may complement conventional morphological assessment of vasospasm and provide clinically relevant risk stratification. Additionally, in the exploratory analysis, among patients who developed significant CVS, we found that the volume of Tmax >4 s was greater in the DCI group than that in the non-DCI group, which tends to indicate that among patients with radiologically evident CVS, those who developed DCI also experienced impaired perfusion. In contrast, patients with CVS who showed no evidence of perfusion deficits did not develop DCI. These findings thus indicate that evaluating only morphological vasospasm would be insufficient for an accurate assessment of DCI risk and that perfusion-based evaluations can provide complementary and clinically relevant information.

Recent studies have suggested that PCT may be useful for predicting or diagnosing DCI after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage.23-26) Several investigations have reported that PCT performed in the acute phase or early vasospasm period can predict subsequent DCI development.5-9) However, the optimal timing of PCT acquisition and its clinical utility remain controversial. Although early-phase PCT may detect subtle perfusion abnormalities, its predictive accuracy is limited, and its contribution to clinical decision-making―particularly regarding the initiation of invasive treatments such as endovascular intervention―appears modest.5,6,8) In line with our findings, early-phase PCT in the present study demonstrated limited predictive performance for subsequent DCI development. In contrast, PCT performed during the peak phase of vasospasm or at the time of neurological symptom onset has been reported to more accurately reflect clinically relevant perfusion impairment and potentially reversible ischemic changes, thereby enabling better identification of patients at high risk for DCI.10,11) Consistent with these reports, the present study demonstrated that PCT performed during the vasospasm peak enabled early and highly accurate diagnosis of DCI.

In this study, the timing of PCT acquisition was set between days 8 and 12 post-onset, representing a relatively broad window. This approach was adopted in the context of a retrospective design to ensure that imaging overlapped with a period generally considered to correspond to the peak phase of cerebral vasospasm.22) In practice, approximately 80% of patients underwent PCT between days 8 and 10 after onset, which is consistent with the previously reported temporal profile of vasospasm. Among patients who developed DCI, PCT was performed at a median of day 9. Collectively, these findings provide evidence that a timing of approximately 9 days post-onset, corresponding to the peak period of vasospasm, may represent a clinically meaningful time window for PCT acquisition, during which an impairment of cerebral perfusion can be assessed prior to the occurrence of irreversible ischemic injury, and when supplementary therapeutic interventions may still be considered. However, given that we did not undertake a direct comparison of the diagnostic performance of PCT at different acquisition time points, we were unable to definitively determine the optimal timing of PCT. Further prospective studies with pre-defined imaging schedules will accordingly be required to establish the most appropriate time window for a PCT-based risk assessment of DCI.

In addition, quantitative evaluation of PCT parameters varies widely across studies, and the analysis methods are often complex and time-consuming. These limitations have hindered the widespread adoption of PCT in routine clinical practice. To address these challenges, the present study focused on the standardization and simplicity of perfusion assessment by employing RAPID software, which facilitates rapid and reproducible quantitative analysis. RAPID is an infarct volume measurement software that can be used to analyze the cerebral blood flow, cerebral blood volume, mean transit time, and Tmax perfusion delay areas based on PCT or MRI in approximately 6 minutes.27) Because the analysis is centralized, there is little variation among facilities. This test is widely used clinically in the field of acute cerebral infarction and is highly reliable. As such, it is considered a more accessible and acceptable assessment method for many clinicians. This study's results have the potential to change the method of CVS and DCI diagnosis from the traditional phase of assessing CVS based on vessel morphology to evaluating DCI risk by assessing cerebral perfusion using PCT.

The mechanism that contributes the most to DCI is CVS progression, resulting in decreased cerebral perfusion, which leads to cerebral ischemia as perfusion loss exceeds the reserve capacity. In the perfusion area where CVS occurs, cerebral perfusion pressure decreases, and Tmax, time to peak, and mean transit time are prolonged. Initially, blood flow is maintained by collateral blood flow and the cerebral vascular reserve; however, as vasospasm progresses, it becomes difficult to maintain cerebral blood flow, leading to cerebral infarction.28) Furthermore, patients with vasospasm often present with impaired consciousness due to factors such as nonconvulsive status epilepticus, narcotic analgesic use, or electrolyte abnormalities, making ischemic symptoms difficult to distinguish.29,30) Before cerebral infarction occurs, early screening of patients at high risk for DCI will allow for the selection of patients who require additional therapeutic intervention to prevent DCI and may lead to an improved prognosis. Therefore, regular or early PCT assessment may help identify such cases promptly and enable timely therapeutic intervention. In this context, the appropriate Tmax threshold warrants careful consideration. In acute ischemic stroke, a Tmax > 5.5 sec has been reported to have the highest sensitivity and specificity for the penumbral region,31) and a Tmax > 6 sec in the RAPID analysis has been used as an indicator of the penumbral region in several recent randomized trials.32-34) In contrast, in this study, Tmax > 4 sec provided the best diagnostic performance for identifying DCI after SAH. This discrepancy may also be attributable to differences in imaging timings assessed in the present study and those adopted in previous acute ischemic stroke studies. In acute ischemic stroke, perfusion imaging is typically performed at a time at which ischemia has become established due to abrupt arterial occlusion. In contrast, in the present study, PCT was primarily performed prior to the onset of overt DCI development, during the progressive phase of vasospasm. Consequently, the observed Tmax >4 s threshold may reflect earlier or milder perfusion impairment preceding irreversible infarction rather than fully developed ischemia. However, given that perfusion imaging at the time of confirmed infarction was limited in this cohort, the precise mechanisms underlying the strong association between Tmax >4 s and DCI remain speculative. Further studies incorporating additional perfusion parameters and larger datasets are accordingly warranted to clarify the pathophysiological basis of the perfusion changes associated with DCI.

In this study, the management of cerebral vasospasm was performed in accordance with our institutional standard treatment strategy, which consists of general supportive care and pharmacological therapy based on fasudil hydrochloride, cilostazol, and ozagrel sodium. In addition, after 2024, clazosentan sodium was administered in selected cases at the discretion of the attending physician. Although we detected no clear association between the management strategies used for cerebral vasospasm and the occurrence of DCI, given that treatment selection was non-randomized and the number of patients was limited, this study was not specifically designed to evaluate the efficacy of specific therapeutic interventions. As such, the present findings should be interpreted primarily in the context of perfusion-based risk assessment rather than treatment efficacy. Accordingly, Tmax analysis could make PCT a more integral tool in future clinical management and diagnostics.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the number of patients with DCI was small (n = 22). These findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Validation in larger, multicenter cohorts will be necessary. Second, in this study, a substantial number of considered cases were initially excluded, which necessarily reduced the size of the analyzable cohort. The main reasons for exclusion were an absence of PCT and a WFNS grade of 5. The primary rationale for not performing perfusion CT was contraindications to contrast media, including allergic reactions and impaired renal function. The exclusion of these cases may thus have resulted in a study population biased toward specific patient sub-groups, potentially introducing selection bias relating to disease severity. Consequently, the findings of this study will be applicable mainly to patients with mild to moderate subarachnoid hemorrhage and should not be directly extrapolated to patients with WFNS grade 5 or those unsuitable for perfusion CT. Third, the definition of DCI used in this study incorporated symptomatic vasospasm, which differs from the widely accepted definition proposed by Vergouwen et al.35) This broader definition was adopted to evaluate Tmax analysis as an early screening tool, though it may limit comparability with other studies. Fourth, perfusion CT was performed between days 8 and 12 after onset according to institutional protocol. Therefore, early DCI events may have been missed. Serial imaging at earlier and multiple time points may provide a more complete assessment.

In addition, the timing of PCT acquisition presents a limitation. PCT was generally performed during the peak phase of cerebral vasospasm. Although no patients showed established ischemic lesions on the same-day MRI or non-contrast CT at the time of PCT, PCT was performed at the time of symptom onset in 2 cases, providing imaging data concurrent with DCI diagnosis. In both cases, early ischemic changes on MRI were absent or minimal at the time of PCT, with subsequent infarct progression. These findings suggest that potentially reversible ischemic regions were present at the time of PCT acquisition and do not contradict the utility of PCT for early DCI risk assessment. Finally, this was a single-center study conducted with RAPID software. Although RAPID is widely available and routinely used in ischemic stroke, validation across different centers and imaging platforms will be required to establish external generalizability. Given that in the evaluation of CVS, arterial diameter measurements were not automated, the potential influence of observer variability cannot be completely excluded.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides preliminary evidence that Tmax analysis using RAPID software may enhance risk assessment of DCI after SAH. Standardized perfusion evaluation may allow improved identification of high-risk patients beyond morphological assessment of vasospasm and contribute to more informed clinical decision-making. In conclusion, the presence of a Tmax > 4 sec in PCT holds the potential to facilitate early assessment of DCI risk and the occurrence of DCI after SAH.
