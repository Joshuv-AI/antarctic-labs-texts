GDF15 nanotherapy ameliorates NLRP3-associated redox imbalance and cardiac injury in sepsis
Authors: Yue Guo, Qinxue Wang, Xinyi Chang, Yuhan Zhao, Sicong Wang, Sijia Zhang, Qiulun Lu, Yi Han, Yong Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM) is a life-threatening complication of sepsis, characterized by acute cardiac dysfunction and high mortality. Despite its prevalence and severity, SICM lacks effective targeted therapies. Therefore, we developed macrophage membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles (MGP) for the targeted delivery of recombinant human GDF15 (rhGDF15), aiming to enhance its therapeutic efficacy. In this study, MGP was designed to encapsulate rhGDF15. MGP displayed favorable stability and biocompatibility in vitro and significantly improved left ventricular function and contractility in a lipopolysaccharide (LPS)–induced murine SICM model. Mechanistically, GDF15 binds to MYPT1, inhibiting AKT-mediated phosphorylation of YBX-1 at serine 102 and preventing its nuclear translocation. Cytosolic retention of YBX-1 suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β release, which are critical drivers of inflammation and oxidative stress in SICM. This redox-inflammatory link was supported by DHE staining, which demonstrated that MGP treatment attenuated LPS-induced superoxide production. Furthermore, dual-luciferase reporter assay and Ybx-1 knockout experiments confirmed that YBX-1 regulates Nlrp3 expression. Collectively, the study has established a biomimetic nanocarrier that enhances rhGDF15 delivery and delineated a novel GDF15-MYPT1-YBX-1 axis that regulates NLRP3-driven inflammation and oxidative stress in SICM, providing a mechanistic basis for therapeutic intervention in septic cardiac injury.

Introduction

Sepsis is defined as a systemic inflammatory response syndrome triggered by infection, which is characterized by immune dysfunction and multi-organ failure, representing a major threat to global patient mortality [1]. Among the most severe complications of sepsis, sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM) is characterized by impaired cardiac contraction, diastolic dysfunction, and myocardial cell injury, coupled with a lack of effective, specific therapeutic interventions [2,3]. The pathogenesis of SICM is complex, encompassing multiple mechanisms such as inflammatory mediator release, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis [4]. The rising incidence of sepsis has heightened the urgency for a deeper understanding of its molecular mechanisms and the development of precise therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), an important immunomodulatory molecule, has attracted widespread attention due to its roles in inflammation regulation, metabolic homeostasis, and tissue repair [5,6]. Elevated expression of GDF15 has been reported in various acute and chronic inflammatory diseases, suggesting its potential to modulate immune inflammation and reduce tissue damage [7,8]. Particularly in cardiovascular disorders, GDF15 exerts anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective effects through multiple signaling pathways, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target for SICM [9,10]. However, the specific application of GDF15 in sepsis and its associated cardiac injury, along with the limited understanding of its in vivo delivery mechanisms, necessitates further exploration of efficient drug delivery systems to enhance its therapeutic efficacy.

The advancement of nanotechnology offers novel therapeutic strategies for treating sepsis and inflammatory disorders. Nanocarriers can be designed with high biocompatibility and robust drug-protective capabilities, which improve the pharmacokinetic properties of therapeutics, enhance targeted delivery efficiency, and thereby significantly augment treatment efficacy [11,12]. In particular, cell membrane-coated nanoparticles confer superior biomimetic properties to the delivery system [13], reducing immune recognition and clearance, extending circulation time [14], and facilitating active targeting of pathological lesions [15]. Macrophage-derived membranes, due to their intrinsic inflammation-homing capabilities and immunoregulatory functions, have become a focal point in the development of nanotherapeutic approaches for inflammatory diseases [16,17]. Currently, an increasing number of nanocarriers have been permitted by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on the market, such as poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), polylactic acid (PLA), and polycaprolactone (PCL) [18,19]. Macrophage membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles are not only designed for controlled drug release but also preferentially accumulate at inflammation sites through membrane surface receptor-mediated targeting, effectively mitigating the pathological microenvironment in SICM [20].

Based on these advantages, macrophage membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles encapsulating rhGDF15, referred to as MGP, were designed, fabricated, and systematically evaluated for their biodistribution, targeting capability, and therapeutic efficacy in a murine model of SICM. MGP was demonstrated to exhibit excellent biocompatibility, prolonged circulation time, effective targeting of inflamed myocardial tissue, significant improvement of cardiac function in SICM mice, and attenuation of myocardial inflammation and macrophage infiltration. Furthermore, a series of in vitro and in vivo studies elucidated the protective role of GDF15 delivered by MGP, which attenuated lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammatory responses, mitigated the concomitant oxidative burden, and preserved myocardial structural integrity.

This work not only highlights the therapeutic significance of GDF15 in septic myocardial injury but also integrates nanotechnology with a biomimetic membrane-coating strategy, establishing a novel nanoplatform for precision intervention in SICM. These findings are expected to advance the development of targeted therapies for inflammatory cardiovascular diseases and demonstrate substantial scientific and translational potential.

Materials and methods

Serum sample collection from patients

Serum samples were obtained from patients at the time of admission to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Inclusion criteria included a diagnosis of sepsis based on Sepsis-3 criteria, age ≥18 years, and provision of written informed consent from the patient or a legally authorized representative. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy or lactation, pre-existing chronic heart failure (NYHA class II–IV), or concurrent acute or subacute myocardial infarction. We recorded baseline characteristics, diagnostic information, clinical parameters including the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, laboratory results, and prognosis data. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. 2020-SR-055).

Animal experiments

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC-2205045) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Nanjing Medical University, and Gdf15−/− mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). To minimize experimental variability and focus on cellular mechanisms in sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction, only male mice were used, thereby avoiding potential confounders associated with the estrous cycle in females [21]. The sepsis model was induced by intraperitoneal injection of LPS (Sigma, USA) at a dose of 12 mg/kg. MGP nanoparticles were administered intravenously (i.v.) via the tail vein at a dose of 0.1 mg/kg/day [22], once daily for three consecutive days, with the last dose given 30 min prior to LPS injection. Control mice received an equal volume of normal saline. Following a one-week acclimatization period, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups based on a computer-generated randomization sequence to ensure unbiased allocation. A sample size of n = 6 per group was determined based on power analysis conducted using preliminary data and established effect sizes from prior published studies in similar models of SICM [23]. All subsequent procedures, including drug administration, echocardiographic assessment, and data analysis, were performed by investigators blinded to group assignments. Mice were euthanized 24 h after LPS administration for serum and heart tissue collection. Each mouse was considered an independent experimental unit.

Cell culture and treatment

Rat cardiomyocyte H9c2 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) were isolated from the femurs and tibias of 4–5-week-old C57BL/6J mice and cultured in DMEM containing 10 % FBS, 1 % penicillin-streptomycin, and 50 ng/mL GM-CSF (Novoprotein, China) for 7 days to allow for differentiation. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2. MGP was administered at a concentration of 10 ng/mL simultaneously with LPS (100 ng/mL) stimulation. To assess the indirect effects on cardiomyocytes, BMDM were treated with LPS for 24 h, and the supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting conditioned media was mixed 1:1 with fresh media and co-cultivated with H9c2 cells.

Echocardiography

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and subjected to transthoracic echocardiography using the Vevo®3100 high-resolution imaging system (Fujifilm Visual Sonics, Canada). All imaging was conducted by a trained technician blinded to group allocation. Left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) were recorded from M-mode images [43].

Histology and Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Heart tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemical analysis. IHC was performed using primary antibodies against Ly6G (1:500, Servicebio, Cat# GB11229) and CD68 (1:200, Servicebio, Cat# GB113109) [44]. Images were acquired under a light microscope (BX63, Olympus, Japan).

Immunofluorescence staining

For tissue immunofluorescence, paraffin-embedded cardiac sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, autofluorescence quenching, membrane permeabilization, and blocking, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C [45]. For frozen sections, after warming, permeabilization, and blocking, samples were also incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. For cellular immunofluorescence, cells were cultured on slides, fixed with paraformaldehyde, permeabilized, blocked, and incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. The list of antibodies is in Supplementary Table S1. Slides were sealed with anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI (SouthernBiotech, Cat# 0100-20) and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (BX63, Olympus, Japan).

Serum measurement of GDF15 and IL-6

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were conducted using commercial kits to quantify serum levels of human GDF15 (Proteintech, Cat# KE00108), mouse GDF15 (Proteintech, Cat# KE10082), and mouse IL-6 (MULTI SCIENCES, Cat# EK206).

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from heart tissue and cultured cells using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Japan) per the manufacturer's protocol. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, China). qPCR was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, China) on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2∧-ΔΔCt method [46]. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Protein extraction and Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with PMSF (Beyotime, China) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated on 10 % SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto 0.2 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature [47]. Signals were detected using the Tanon 5200 Multi system (Tanon, Shanghai, China), and band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

siRNA transfection

Human Ybx-1 siRNA and a negative control siRNA were synthesized by GenePharma (Suzhou, China). Transfection was performed in BMDM using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Transfection efficiency was assessed 48 h post-transfection using qPCR and Western blotting.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Potential binding sites between YBX-1 and the Nlrp3 promoter were predicted using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.genereg.net/). The wild-type (WT) and mutant Nlrp3 promoter regions were cloned into the pmirGLO plasmid (GENEWIZ, China). These constructs, along with pcDNA3.1-YBX-1 or pcDNA3.1-NC, were co-transfected into 293T cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 24 h, Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (MeilunBio, Cat# MA0518-1).

Data and statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. All bioinformatic analyses were performed with R software environment (version 4.4.2). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)], categorical variables as n (%). Comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical data were compared using Pearson's chi-square test. Correlations between continuous variables (e.g., GDF15 and EF or SOFA score) were assessed using Spearman correlation coefficient. Predictive performance of GDF15 and SOFA for SICM was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC) comparisons. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were used to identify independent risk factors for SICM; variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate models to adjust for confounders. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Results showed that serum GDF15 levels were significantly higher in the SICM group compared with controls (6,268 ng/mL vs. 3,299 ng/mL; Fig. 1D). GDF15 expression is upregulated in a murine model of SICM

To investigate the role of GDF15 in SICM, we established a mouse model by intraperitoneally injecting C57BL/6J mice with LPS (12 mg/kg), followed by sampling 24 h later (Fig. 2A). Given that septic cardiomyopathy is characterized by impaired contractility, we evaluated cardiac function using echocardiography 24 h post-LPS injection. The results showed that EF and FS were significantly reduced in SICM mice (Fig. 2B). Consistent with clinical findings, GDF15 and IL-6 levels were significantly elevated in the serum of SICM mice (Fig. 2C). H&E staining and immunohistochemical staining revealed marked inflammatory cell infiltration in the cardiac tissue of SICM mice. (Fig. 2D). Western blot and qPCR analysis indicated that GDF15 expression was markedly upregulated in the cardiac tissue of SICM mice (Fig. 2E) rather than in other organs (Supplementary Fig. S1A), along with increased expression of Bnp, inflammatory cytokines Il-1β and Il-6, and endothelial adhesion molecules Icam-1 and Vcam-1 (Fig. 2F). To determine the primary cellular source of GDF15 in the heart, we analyzed publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data from the GEO dataset. The analysis showed that GDF15 was predominantly enriched in cardiac macrophages (Supplementary Fig. S1B), with significantly higher expression in the SICM group compared with controls (Fig. 2G). To further validate these findings, we isolated BMDM from WT mice. The results demonstrated that LPS stimulation induced upregulation of Gdf15 mRNA expression. In macrophages, LPS stimulation significantly increased expression of Il-6 and Il-1β, as well as M1 polarization marker Nos2 and pro-oxidative enzyme Ptgs2 (Fig. 2H). Taken together, these results indicate that GDF15 expression is markedly upregulated in the hearts of SICM mice and that macrophages are a key cellular source.

Genetic deletion of GDF15 exacerbates cardiac dysfunction and inflammation in LPS-induced SICM

To investigate the role of GDF15 in the development of LPS-induced SICM, we utilized Gdf15−/− mice. WT and Gdf15−/− mice were randomly divided into four groups: WT + Saline, WT + LPS, Gdf15−/− + Saline, and Gdf15−/− + LPS (Fig. 3A). Cardiac function was evaluated using echocardiography 24 h after LPS administration. Compared with WT mice, Gdf15−/− mice exhibited significantly worsened cardiac dysfunction, as indicated by further reductions in EF and FS (Fig. 3B). Histological analysis by H&E staining revealed marked inflammatory cell infiltration in the WT + LPS group, which was further exacerbated in the Gdf15−/− + LPS group (Fig. 3C). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated significantly increased infiltration of CD68+ macrophages in the myocardial tissue of Gdf15−/− mice following LPS challenge. Notably, neither H&E nor immunofluorescence staining revealed significant differences in CD68+ macrophage infiltration between WT and Gdf15−/− mice under saline-treated conditions, suggesting that GDF15 deficiency does not alter baseline myocardial histology (Fig. 3D). TUNEL staining also demonstrated an increase in myocardial apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. S2). qPCR analysis showed that the mRNA levels of Bnp, Il-1β, Il-6, and Mcp-1 were significantly upregulated in the WT + LPS group compared to the WT + Saline group, and were further elevated in the Gdf15−/− + LPS group (Fig. 3E). Characterization and inflammatory targeting of macrophage membrane-coated rhGDF15 nanoparticles

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of rhGDF15 (half-life: 2–3 h) [24] in alleviating SICM and to exploit the superior biocompatibility and disease-targeting capability of nanocarriers in inflammatory conditions [25], we employed macrophage membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles as a comprehensive delivery strategy.

First, rhGDF15-loaded PLGA nanoparticles (GP) were successfully fabricated using a water-in-oil emulsion method [26]. Subsequently, macrophage membranes were extracted and processed by ultrasonication, then fused with GP to obtain MGP (Fig. 4A). The drug loading content (DLC) and drug loading efficiency (DLE) of GP were approximately 84.21 % and 0.724 %, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that GP exhibited a monodisperse spherical morphology with an average diameter of ∼200 nm, whereas MGP showed a larger hydrodynamic size, confirming successful macrophage membrane coating while retaining uniform sphericity. Both GP and MGP displayed negative zeta potentials, suggesting favorable colloidal stability (Fig. 4B). Western blot analysis confirmed the presence and enrichment of key membrane proteins derived from macrophages on the nanoparticle surface [27](Fig. 4C). Given that these nanoparticles are intended for intravenous administration, hemocompatibility is a critical safety criterion for clinical translation. We evaluated the hemolytic potential of MGP in vitro using a direct contact method. At a concentration of 1 mg/mL, no visible hemolysis was observed. Optical density (OD) measurements showed no significant difference between MGP and the negative control, indicating that MGP is non-hemolytic (Fig. 4E). We further assessed the in vitro cytocompatibility of MGP with RAW264.7 macrophages, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), and human aortic smooth muscle cells (HASMCs). After 24h incubation with MGP at concentrations of 10–100 ng/mL, no significant change in cell viability was detected, demonstrating excellent biocompatibility (Fig. 4F).

Subsequently, we assessed MGP internalization by macrophages using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Enhanced cellular uptake was observed under LPS-stimulated inflammatory conditions compared to unstimulated controls, demonstrating that inflammation promotes macrophage engagement with MGP (Fig. 4G). Moreover, time-course analysis showed a progressive increase in MGP uptake by macrophages, confirming a time-dependent internalization pattern (Fig. 4H). Taken together, these comprehensive characterizations validate the successful development of MGP as a stable and efficient rhGDF15 delivery system.

MGP ameliorates LPS-induced SICM in mice by improving cardiac dysfunction

To investigate the protective mechanism of GDF15 on cardiac function, we administered MGP (0.1 mg/kg/day) via the tail vein for 3 days prior to the establishment of the SICM model, followed by intraperitoneal injection of LPS (Fig. 5A). Echocardiography revealed that MGP had no significant effect on the cardiac function of saline-treated mice, but significantly improved LPS-induced cardiac dysfunction, with marked improvements in EF and FS (Fig. 5B). qPCR analysis of the mRNA expression of Bnp, Il-1β, Il-6 and Mcp-1 in the heart tissue of the mice showed that the expression of these genes was significantly downregulated in the LPS + MGP group compared to the LPS group (Fig. 5C). To evaluate the cardiac targeting efficiency of MGP, we compared the biodistribution of PLGA, free Cy5 dye, Cy5-labeled PLGA nanoparticles (Cy5-PLGA), and macrophage membrane-coated Cy5-PLGA (M/Cy5-PLGA) in LPS-challenged mice. Ex vivo imaging of hearts harvested 24 h post-injection revealed more intense fluorescent signals in the M/Cy5-PLGA group than in all other groups. Quantitative analysis confirmed that the cardiac fluorescence intensity of M/Cy5-PLGA was significantly higher than that of both Cy5-PLGA and free Cy5 (Fig. 5D), providing unequivocal evidence that the macrophage membrane coating confers active targeting to the inflamed heart. Biodistribution profiles in other major organs are shown in Supplementary Fig. S4A, highlighting the cardiac accumulation.

Immunofluorescence staining of CD68 and H&E staining in the heart tissue showed that MGP significantly reduced the infiltration of CD68+ macrophages in the myocardium of SICM mice (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. S4B). Together, our results show that the macrophage membrane coating not only promotes prolonged circulation but also confers heart-specific targeting. This leads to an increased local drug concentration, which potentiates the therapeutic effects of rhGDF15, alleviates inflammatory responses, and improves cardiac dysfunction in mice with SICM. In summary, our findings demonstrate that MGP significantly inhibits inflammatory responses and reduces macrophage infiltration in the myocardium, thereby effectively ameliorating cardiac dysfunction in SICM mice.

MGP suppresses LPS-induced macrophage secretion of inflammatory factors and reduces myocardial cell damage via the NLRP3 pathway

As previously described, our results demonstrate that MGP exerts protective effects in SICM-induced cardiac dysfunction. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed a significant association between sepsis and the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome (Fig. 6A). To further validate the regulatory role of GDF15 on inflammation, we treated BMDM with LPS (100 ng/mL) and co-treated them with MGP (10 ng/mL) for 24 h. qPCR analysis revealed that LPS significantly promoted the expression of Il-1β, Il-6, Mcp-1 and Ptgs2. In contrast to the LPS group, the LPS + MGP group exhibited significant downregulation of these specific inflammatory factors (Fig. 6B). Dihydroethidium (DHE) staining, specifically detecting superoxide anion, revealed that LPS treatment significantly elevated intracellular oxidative burden in macrophages. The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome has been widely reported in the pathogenesis of SICM [[28], [48]]. To explore the effect of GDF15 on this pathway, we used Western blot to detect the expression of NLRP3 and its adaptor ASC, as well as downstream inflammatory factors IL-1β and its precursor pro-IL-1β. The results showed that LPS treatment significantly upregulated the expression of NLRP3 in macrophages, with no significant change in the expression of ASC, and significantly increased the expression of IL-1β and pro-IL-1β (Fig. 6D). qPCR experiments confirmed the same effect (Fig. 6E). To further investigate the contribution of the delivery system to the therapeutic efficacy, we treated LPS-stimulated macrophages with free rhGDF15, empty PLGA nanoparticles, GP, or MGP. Western blot analysis revealed that free rhGDF15 induced only a modest reduction in NLRP3 and IL-1β expression, whereas MGP treatment produced the strongest inhibition of inflammasome activation, significantly outperforming both free rhGDF15 and GP (Supplementary Fig. S5C). These data indicate that the enhanced bioactivity is directly linked to the macrophage-mimetic delivery platform, which facilitates cellular uptake and enables targeted delivery.

To clarify the mechanism by which LPS-induced macrophage activation contributes to SICM, we added the supernatant from LPS-treated macrophages to the culture medium of cardiomyocytes (Fig. 6F). Our results showed that direct LPS treatment of cardiomyocytes did not significantly affect the mRNA expression of Il-1β and Il-6, and Western blot analysis also failed to detect significant changes in the expression of NLRP3 protein in cardiomyocytes (Supplementary Fig. S6). However, the supernatant from LPS-treated macrophages significantly promoted the expression of NLRP3 protein in cardiomyocytes, and the addition of MGP significantly inhibited this increase in NLRP3 expression (Fig. 6G). In summary, these results suggest that GDF15 suppresses the activation of the NLRP3 pathway, thereby inhibiting the secretion of inflammatory factors by LPS-activated macrophages and reducing myocardial cell damage.

MGP exerts anti-inflammatory effects via the MYPT1/AKT/YBX-1 signaling pathway

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which GDF15 suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation and inflammatory responses, we performed immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry to identify interactions in macrophages. We found that MYPT1 (Myosin Phosphatase Target Subunit 1) interacts with GDF15. MYPT1 is a critical regulatory subunit of myosin phosphatase, and its core function is to regulate the dephosphorylation of myosin light chain, thereby modulating contractility and cytoskeletal dynamics in non-muscle and smooth muscle cells [29]. Using immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analysis, we identified a protein interaction between GDF15 and MYPT1 (Fig. 7A). Based on Z-DOCK prediction, a potential binding site between GDF15 and MYPT1 was identified, with pink regions representing GDF15 and green regions representing MYPT1, and the binding domain highlighted in a box (Fig. 7B). Co-immunoprecipitation assay demonstrated the interaction of GDF15 with MYPT1 in BMDM and LPS stimulation enhanced their association (Fig. 7C). To explore the downstream regulatory effects of GDF15 and MYPT1, we found that MYPT1 may exert its anti-inflammatory function by dephosphorylating YBX-1. YBX-1 is a multifunctional RNA/DNA-binding protein containing an evolutionarily conserved cold shock domain, which enables it to preferentially bind to DNA/RNA sequences and act as a transcriptional repressor or activator [30]. Previous studies have shown that YBX-1 is upregulated in inflammatory diseases, but its specific role in SICM remains unclear [31]. Based on this, we hypothesized that GDF15 exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through binding to MYPT1 and promoting dephosphorylation of YBX-1, thereby preventing its nuclear translocation and downstream signaling. Western blot analysis revealed that under LPS stimulation, the phosphorylation level of YBX-1 (p-YBX-1) was significantly increased. Additionally, it has been shown that the phosphorylation of YBX-1 at serine 102 is mediated by the AKT kinase [32]. We detected the phosphorylation status of AKT and found that LPS significantly activated AKT, while MGP treatment significantly inhibited this activation (Fig. 7E). These findings suggest that GDF15 may suppress AKT phosphorylation, thereby reducing YBX-1 phosphorylation. Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated that the nuclear content of YBX-1 in LPS-treated macrophages was significantly increased, and this nuclear translocation was significantly reduced in the MGP-treated group (Fig. 7F). In summary, the experimental data indicate that GDF15 exerts anti-inflammatory effects by binding to MYPT1, dephosphorylating YBX-1, and blocking its nuclear translocation, thereby regulating downstream inflammatory signaling pathways.

Notably, in Ybx-1 knockdown cells, LPS-induced Nlrp3 and Il-1β levels were significantly lower than in control cells, with no significant changes observed in Asc expression (Fig. 8A and B). To further explore the relationship between GDF-15, YBX-1, and NLRP3, qPCR revealed that MGP treatment significantly suppressed LPS-induced Nlrp3 expression. Moreover, the effects of MGP treatment were consistent with those of Si-Ybx-1. (Fig. 8C). These results indicate that MGP-mediated suppression of NLRP3 activation in LPS-stimulated macrophages depends on YBX-1 expression.

Given that YBX-1 functions as a transcription factor, we further explored its molecular mechanism in regulating Nlrp3 transcription. We hypothesized that YBX-1 directly interacts with the Nlrp3 promoter to modulate its transcriptional activity. To test this, we co-transfected YBX-1 overexpression plasmids with a luciferase reporter construct containing the Nlrp3 promoter in HEK293T cells (Fig. 8D). The results showed that YBX-1 overexpression significantly enhanced Nlrp3 promoter activity compared to the control group (Fig. 8E). LPS treatment further activated the Nlrp3 promoter, while MGP treatment significantly inhibited this luciferase activity (Fig. 8F). Our functional genetic and reporter assay, supported by prior evidence of YBX-1 binding to the Nlrp3 promoter [33], strongly suggests that YBX-1 transcriptionally regulates Nlrp3 expression. Taken together, these findings indicate that YBX-1 regulates Nlrp3 transcriptional activity, thereby influencing inflammatory cytokine release. This regulatory mechanism significantly impacts the release of inflammatory cytokines in response to LPS challenge.

Discussion

Sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy, one of the most severe complications of sepsis, has remained a major challenge in clinical and basic research owing to its complex pathophysiology and the absence of effective treatments [[34], [49], [50]]. This study systematically explored the role of GDF15 in SICM and its targeted delivery via nanotechnology, integrating insights from inflammatory regulation, molecular signaling, and nanocarrier theory. The findings not only deepen our understanding of the scientific implications but also highlight the clinical potential of GDF15 in SICM.

Inflammation represents a central pathological process in sepsis and associated cardiac injury, characterized by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, immune cell activation, and complex interactions within inflammatory signaling pathways [35]. GDF15, a stress-induced cytokine, has been shown to significantly increase in various inflammatory and stress conditions, exhibiting dual functions in modulating immune homeostasis and reducing inflammatory damage [36]. Our study, through multi-layered analysis of clinical samples and public databases, validated the significant upregulation of serum GDF15 in sepsis and SICM patients, with levels closely correlated to cardiac function markers and organ failure scores. These findings underscore the potential of GDF15 as a biomarker, aligning with the current immunomodulatory theory—where the immune system must rapidly activate to combat infection while simultaneously being appropriately suppressed to prevent excessive damage. GDF15 occupies a pivotal position in this balance, reflecting the dynamic adjustment of the inflammatory microenvironment [37].

Concerning the protective mechanisms of GDF15, a novel signaling pathway was identified wherein GDF15 binding to MYPT1 regulates YBX-1 phosphorylation, thereby suppressing its nuclear translocation and NLRP3 inflammasome activation. In parallel, DHE analysis confirmed attenuated superoxide production, indicating mitigation of oxidative burden. These independent but synergistic effects advance sepsis pathogenesis understanding. The NLRP3 inflammasome serves as a central hub for inflammation, while oxidative stress constitutes a distinct pathological axis in SICM [38]. YBX-1, a transcriptional regulator, was found to modulate Nlrp3 gene expression, with its nuclear translocation controlled by the phosphorylation of AKT [39,40]. This mechanism suggests that GDF15, via MYPT1, inhibits AKT activation, reduces YBX-1 nuclear translocation, thereby blocking the inflammatory cascade associated with the NLRP3 inflammasome and reducing oxidative damage, as validated by superoxide quantification. These findings expand the functional role of GDF15 into two discrete domains and establish YBX-1 as a modular regulator, enriching the dual-pathology framework for SICM.

In the field of nanocarrier-based delivery, macrophage membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles were utilized to achieve efficient targeted delivery of rhGDF15. The design of the carrier leveraged the “self-recognition” properties of cell membranes, effectively mimicking the chemotactic behavior of natural immune cells and overcoming the limitations of traditional protein-based drug delivery, such as rapid clearance and non-specific distribution [25]. The application of nanotechnology aligns with the principles of pharmacokinetics and drug delivery, optimizing drug spatial distribution and targeting, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing potential side effects [41]. This represents a fusion of precision medicine and biomaterials, demonstrating the technical feasibility of the MGP platform in supporting the functional activity of therapeutic proteins in complex inflammatory microenvironments.

Notably, the integration of animal models and clinical data in this study strengthened the credibility and translational potential of the findings. The identification of GDF15 as a clinical biomarker not only provides an auxiliary tool for early detection and prognosis in sepsis patients but also offers a solid basis for the development of GDF15-based therapeutic strategies. Meanwhile, the in vivo treatment experiments in animal models validated the efficacy of the nanocarrier-delivered GDF15, bridging the gap between molecular mechanisms and clinical applications and promoting new therapeutic strategies of SICM.

This study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size and single-source clinical data necessitate further validation through multi-center, large-scale, prospective studies to confirm the broad applicability of GDF15 as a diagnostic and therapeutic biomarker. While the animal model used is representative, interspecies physiological differences may affect long-term safety and immune responses, requiring systematic evaluation. Our data support a model in which GDF15 binding to MYPT1 suppresses AKT-mediated YBX-1 phosphorylation. However, direct validation through MYPT1 knockdown or phosphatase inhibition assays is required to definitively establish its essential role in this pathway [42], a key aim of our ongoing work. Similarly, while our genetic and reporter assay support the notion that YBX-1 transcriptionally regulates Nlrp3 expression, definitive evidence of promoter binding via chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays in our model would strengthen this conclusion. Future studies employing ChIP-qPCR or ChIP-seq will be essential to fully elucidate this regulatory mechanism. Additionally, large-scale production and stability of the nanocarrier, as well as its pharmacokinetics and toxicology, remain critical challenges for clinical translation. Furthermore, the dual nature of GDF15—acting both as a passive reflection of inflammation and as an active therapeutic agent—suggests a time- and space-dependent mechanism of action, which requires further precise characterization in dynamic pathological settings.

Future research should be prioritized along several key avenues. Comprehensive single-cell omics data should be utilized to refine the role of the GDF15 signaling axis within distinct immune cell subpopulations and to elucidate its associated intercellular communication networks. Furthermore, genetic and pharmacological tools should be employed to precisely define the causal role of MYPT1 in mediating the observed cardioprotective effects. ChIP assays are also needed to directly demonstrate YBX-1 occupancy at the Nlrp3 promoter. Concurrently, nanocarrier design should be optimized for multifunctional combination therapies. The application of GDF15-targeted therapy should be expanded to other sepsis-related organ injuries and inflammatory cardiovascular diseases to assess its broader therapeutic potential. Finally, clinical trial design must be advanced to facilitate the efficient translation of these foundational findings into clinical practice.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that MGP is capable of effectively attenuating SICM through the targeted delivery of rhGDF15. Mechanistically, the binding of GDF15 to MYPT1 inhibits AKT-mediated YBX-1 phosphorylation at Ser102, blocking its nuclear translocation and subsequent transcriptional activation of the Nlrp3 promoter, thereby suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome-driven IL-1β release and associated oxidative stress. MGP treatment improved cardiac function in vivo by interrupting this inflammatory-oxidative cascade (Fig. 9).
