Abstract
. We prove that the group SAut k ​ ( 𝔸 2 ) \mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2}) is simple as an algebraic group of infinite dimension, over any infinite field k \mathrm{k} , by proving that any closed normal subgroup is either trivial or the whole group. In higher dimension, we show that closed normal subgroups contain all tame automorphisms. The case of finite fields, very different, is also discussed.

14R10,14R20

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathrm{k}
be a field and
n\geq 1
be an integer. The group
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
of polynomial automorphisms of
\mathbb{A}^{n}
(see Section
2
for precise definitions and reminders) is, despite much work, highly not understood for
n\geq 3
. For
n=3
and
\mathrm{char}(\mathrm{k})=0
, the group is known to be not generated by affine and triangular automorphisms: there are
non-tame automorphisms

[
SU04
]
, like for instance the famous Nagata automorphism of
[
Nag72
, Section 2.1]
(see Example
3.8
). We however do not have a set of explicit generators for any
n\geq 3
and any field
\mathrm{k}
. The group
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
is much better understood, for any field
\mathrm{k}
. It has a well-known structure of an amalgamated product, which is very useful for a lot of questions on the group: one knows its finite subgroups, algebraic subgroups, the possible dynamical degrees, …

The group
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is contained in
\mathrm{End}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\simeq\mathrm{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{n}
, on which one can put two natural topologies (see
[
Sta12
]
), one being weaker than the other one. The group
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
then has the structure of an
infinite dimensional algebraic group
, or
ind-group
, as explained in
[
Sha81
]
(see also
[
Kam96
,
Kum02
,
FK18
]
). One can then ask for the structure of the group, and in particular ask for possible closed normal subgroups. Note that this only makes sense when
\mathrm{k}
is infinite, as otherwise
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is a discrete groupe. The Jacobian gives a natural surjective group homomorphism

\mathrm{Jac}\colon\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{G}_{M}(\mathrm{k})\simeq\mathrm{k}^{*}

whose kernel is the closed (in any of the two topologies) normal subgroup
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
. One can then show that any non-trivial closed normal subgroups of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
contains all tame automorphisms
[
FK18
, Proposition 15.11.5]
(see Proposition
E
below for a generalisation).

The main question in this context, still open in general, is whether
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is simple as an ind-group. In
[
Sha81
, Theorem 5]
, it is claimed that
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is simple as an ind-group, but the proof uses the fact that the associated Lie algebra is simple, and thus also uses the Lie correspondence established in
[
Sha81
, Theorem 1]
, which is know to be false (see
[
FK18
]
). The arguments used in
[
FK18
, Proposition 15.11.5]
dot not work here, as the conjugation by an homothety, not contained in
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
, is needed. The simplicity of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
was then open until now, for each
n\geq 2
. We prove it for
n=2
and give a first step for
n\geq 3
:

Theorem A
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be an infinite field and let
n\geq 1
be an integer. If
N\subseteq\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is a closed normal subgroup, then either
N=\{\mathrm{id}\}
or
N
contains any tame automorphism of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
. In particular, if
n=2
, then
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
is simple as an ind-group.

In Theorem
A
, the closedness is asked in any of the two topologies defined before. If
\mathrm{k}
is a local field, one can also put a topology on
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
using the topology of the field, and the result still will work. The construction actually proves that the results hold for any topology such that for each
f\in\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}[t]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
and each closed subgroup
N\subseteq\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
, if replacing
t
with any element of
\mathrm{k}^{*}
gives an element of
N
, then replacing by
t
by zero also gives an element of
N
.

Without the topology, the group
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
is known to be not simple since a long time (this is the main result of
[
Dan74
]
, see also
[
FL10
]
for a modern and more detailed treatment). The (non-)simplicity of the group
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
as an abstract group is however open for each
n\geq 3
and each infinite field
\mathrm{k}
(for finite fields, it suffices to look at the actions on the set of
\mathrm{k}
-rational points and the kernel will give normal subgroups).

In
[
Bla16
]
, it was actually proven that the conjugacy class of a general element of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
was closed, contrary to the case of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
: for most elements
f\in\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
, the set
\{afa^{-1}\mid a\in\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})\}
is closed. This was considered as a first step towards a proof of the non-simplicity of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
as an algebraic group. The next step actually does not work. Writing

\mathrm{Tra}_{n}(\mathrm{k})=\{(x_{1}+v_{1},\ldots,x_{n}+v_{n})\mid(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in\mathrm{k}^{n}\}

the group of translations, we prove that for each non-trivial
f\in\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
, there is a non-trivial translation in the closure of

\{\rho(\tau^{-1}f^{-1}\tau f)\rho^{-1}\mid\rho,\tau\in\mathrm{Tra}_{n}(\mathrm{k})\}.

This implies that a non-trivial closed normal subgroup of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
contains all translations:

Proposition B
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be an infinite field, let
n\geq 2
be an integer. Each closed normal subgroup
N\subseteq\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
with
N\not=\{\mathrm{id}\}
contains all translations.

It then remains to see that any normal subgroup of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
containing all translations contains all tame automorphisms of Jacobian
1
. This is true for each infinite field and was proven by Drew Lewis in
[
Lew20
, Theorems 6 and 9]
; we include the proof in Section
3.3
, as it is short and nice, for self-containedness. Theorem
A
follows from Proposition
B
and Proposition
C
below.

Proposition C
.

[
Lew20
, Theorems 6 and 9]

Let
\mathrm{k}
be an infinite field and let
n\geq 2
be an integer.

(1)

Any normal subgroup
N\subseteq\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
containing all translations contains also
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
.

(2)

Any normal subgroup
N\subseteq\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
containing
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
contains all tame automorphisms of Jacobian
1
.

Note that if
\mathrm{k}
is finite, then most of the results become false. As said before, we cannot really work with the topology here, so for finite fields
\mathrm{k}
we consider the group as a discrete one, and obtain the following results in dimension
2
, that is opposite to Proposition
C
:

Proposition D
.

For each finite field
\mathrm{k}
, there is a surjective group homomorphism

\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})\twoheadrightarrow(\mathrm{k}[x],+)

whose kernel contains all translations and all elements of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathrm{k})
.

In particular,
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
is not a perfect group, is not finitely generated, and the normal subgroup generated by
\mathrm{SAff}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
is a strict subgroup of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
.

There exists moreover a a surjective group homomorphism

\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})\twoheadrightarrow(\mathbb{F}_{2}[x],+)

whose kernel contains all translations but does not contain
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
.

We finish this introduction by giving a result on
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
.

Proposition E
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be an infinite field, let
n\geq 2
. If
N\subseteq\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is a normal subgroup, with
\mathrm{Jac}(N)=\mathrm{k}^{*}
, then
N=\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
.

2.
Notation and reminders

If
R
is an integral domain and
n\geq 1
is an integer, we denote by
\mathrm{End}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
the set of
R
-endomorphisms
of the affine space
\mathbb{A}^{n}
. Each element
f\in\mathrm{End}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
can be written as
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
, where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
are polynomials in
n
variables, and corresponds to the endomorphism

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(f_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\ldots,f_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))

of
\mathbb{A}^{n}
. It also corresponds to an element
f^{*}\in\mathrm{End}_{R}(R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])
that sends
P
onto
f\circ P=P(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
.

The
derivative
of
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
is the matrix

D(f)=Df=(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}})_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathrm{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]).

and its determinant is the
Jacobian
of
f
, that is

\mathrm{Jac}(f)=\det(Df)\in\mathrm{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}].

We can compose endomorphisms; the group of invertible elements is denoted by
\mathrm{Aut}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
and consists of
R
-automorphisms
of the affine space
\mathbb{A}^{n}
.
As
D(f\circ g)=g^{*}(Df)\cdot Dg
for all
f,g\in\mathrm{End}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
, one finds that
\mathrm{Jac}(f)\in(R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])^{*}=R^{*}
for each
f\in\mathrm{Aut}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
(the converse implication is the classical Jacobian conjecture, in the case where
R
is a field of characteristic
0
). We obtain a natural group homomorphism

\mathrm{Jac}\colon\mathrm{Aut}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\to R^{*}

whose kernel is the group
\mathrm{SAut}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
of
automorphisms of Jacobian
1
. Apart from this subgroup, the group
\mathrm{Aut}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
contains some classical subgroups, of respectively linear automorphisms, translations, affine automorphisms and triangular automorphisms:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathrm{GL}_{n}(R)&=&\{(a_{11}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{1n}x_{n},\ldots,a_{n1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{nn}x_{n})\mid\det((a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{n})\in R^{*}\}\\
\mathrm{Tra}_{n}(R)&=&\{(x_{1}+v_{1},\ldots,x_{n}+v_{n})\mid(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in\mathrm{k}^{n}\}\\
\mathrm{Aff}_{n}(R)&=&\{\tau\circ\alpha\mid\alpha\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(R),\tau\in\mathrm{Tra}_{n}(R)\}=\mathrm{Tra}_{n}(R)\rtimes\mathrm{GL}_{n}(R)\\
\mathrm{Tri}_{n}(R)&=&\{(a_{1}x_{1}+b_{1},\ldots,a_{n}x_{n}+b_{n})\mid a_{i}\in R^{*},b_{i}\in R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1}],1\leq i\leq n\}\end{array}

The group generated by affine and triangular automorphisms is the tame subgroup:

\mathrm{Tame}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})=\langle\mathrm{Aff}_{n}(R),\mathrm{Tri}_{n}(R)\rangle=\langle\mathrm{GL}_{n}(R),\mathrm{Tri}_{n}(R)\rangle.

The equality
\mathrm{Tame}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})=\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
is known to be true for any field
\mathrm{k}
(Jung-Van der Kulk’s theorem,
[
Jun42
]
). However,
\mathrm{Tame}_{\mathrm{k}[x]}(\mathbb{A}^{2})\subsetneq\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[x]}(\mathbb{A}^{2})

[
Nag72
, Theorem 1.4]
and
\mathrm{Tame}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{3})\subsetneq\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{3})
if
\mathrm{char}(\mathrm{k})=0

[
SU04
]
.

If
B
is an integral domain and
A
is a subring of
B
, we have an inclustion
\mathrm{Aut}_{A}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\subseteq\mathrm{Aut}_{B}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
for each
n\geq 1
. We will often study this for the rings
\mathrm{k}\subseteq\mathrm{k}[t]\subseteq\mathrm{k}[t,\frac{1}{t}]
, where
\mathrm{k}
is a field. If
g\in\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[t]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
, one can replace
t
with any element of
\mathrm{k}
and obtain an element of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
. Hence,
g
corresponds to a map

\mathbb{A}^{1}(\mathrm{k})\to\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n}),

that corresponds actually a morphism, when
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is endowed with the structure of an
ind
-group (see
[
FK18
]
). For this, we fix a degree
d
, define

\mathrm{End}_{\leq d}(\mathbb{A}^{n})=\{f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\in\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\mid\deg(f_{i})\leq d\forall i\},

that has the structure of an affine space defined over any field and observe that we have closed embeddings

\mathrm{End}_{\leq 1}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{End}_{\leq 2}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{End}_{\leq 3}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\hookrightarrow\cdots

We may then put a topology on
\mathrm{End}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
, for each infinite field
\mathrm{k}
, such that a subset is closed if its intersection with any
\mathrm{End}_{\leq d}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is closed. This makes
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
locally closed in
\mathrm{End}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
and
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
to be closed. Moreover, the topology is also the strongest one that makes all morphisms
A\to\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
induced by elements of
\mathrm{Aut}_{A}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
continuous (see
[
Bla16
]
). As explained before, there is a weaker topology one can also put on
\mathrm{End}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
(see
[
Sta12
]
). Here,
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is still closed in both
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
and
\mathrm{End}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
.

3.
Translations - Centralisers and normal subgroups

As explained in the introduction, we say that an element of
\mathrm{Aut}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
(where
n\geq 1
is an integer and
R
is an integral domain) is a
translation
if it is of the form

(x_{1}+c_{1},x_{2}+c_{2},\ldots,x_{n}+c_{n})

where
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\in R
.

3.1.
Centralisers of translations

A simple calculation (Lemma
3.2
below) shows that the group of translations is its own centraliser in
\mathrm{End}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
when
R
is an infinite integral domain. For this, we first need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
R
be an infinite integral domain, let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
f\in R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial in
n
variables such that

f(x_{1}+c,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

for each
c\in R
. Then
f\in R[x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

Proof.

We add one variable and consider the polynomial

g=f(x_{1}+x_{n+1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})-f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}].

Replacing
x_{n+1}
with any element
c\in R
, the polynomial
g
becomes zero. We now prove that this implies that
g
is the zero polynomial. For this, we embedd
R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
into
K[x_{n+1}]
, where
K
is the field of fraction of the integral domain
R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. After doing this,
g
becomes a polynomial in one variable over an integral domain, which has infinitely many roots and is thus the zero polynomial.

We have then proven the following equality in
R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1}]
:

f(x_{1}+x_{n+1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}).

Replacing
x_{1}
with
0
and
x_{n+1}
with
x_{1}
, we obtain

f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=f(0,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in R[x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}].\qed

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
R
be an infinite integral domain, let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
f\in\mathrm{End}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
be an element that commutes with all translations. Then,
f
is itself a translation.

In other words, the group of translations is its own centraliser in
\mathrm{End}_{R}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
.

Proof.

We write
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
, with
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

For each
c\in R
, the endomorphism
f
commutes with
\tau=(x_{1}+c,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
. The
n
coordinates of
f\circ\tau=\tau\circ f
give the following
n
equations:

\displaystyle f_{1}(x_{1}+c,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})+c

\displaystyle f_{i}(x_{1}+c,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=f_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\text{ for each }i\in\{2,\ldots,n\}.

This implies that
f_{1}-x_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}
all belong to the set

\Delta=\{q\in R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]\mid q(x_{1}+c,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\text{ for each }c\in R\}.

Lemma
3.1
implies that
\Delta=R[x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
. So we have found that

f_{1}-x_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}\in R[x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}].

In other words, these polynomials do not depend on
x_{1}
.

For each
i\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
, we use the fact that
f
commutes with

\tau=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i}+c,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})

for each
c\in R
and obtain similarly that

f_{1},\ldots,f_{i-1},f_{i}-x_{i},f_{i+1},\ldots,f_{n}\in R[x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}],

i.e. these polynomials do not depend on
x_{i}
.

Hence, for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we have proven that
f_{j}
does not depend on the other variables, so
f_{j}\in R[x_{j}]
, and moreover that
f_{j}-x_{j}
does not depend on
x_{j}
, so
f_{j}-x_{j}\in R
. This implies that
f
is a translation.
∎

Example 3.3
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be a finite field with
q
elements. The automorphism

f=(x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{2}^{q},x_{2})\in\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})

commutes with all translations. The hypothesis that
R
is infinite is then necessary in Lemma
3.2
. Note that
f
acts trivially on the
q^{2}
elements of the set
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathrm{k})
of
\mathrm{k}
-rational points of
\mathbb{A}^{2}
.

3.2.
Bijections on finite sets of points

In
[
Mau01
]
, the action of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
on the set
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathrm{k})
has been studied by Stefan Maubach, for any finite field
\mathrm{k}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q
elements. The main result consists of showing that all permutations are obtained if
q=2
or
q
is odd, and that if
q\geq 4
is even, the image of the group of tame automorphisms
\mathrm{Tame}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
in the group of permutations of
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathrm{k})
is the alternating group.

The result of Maubach gives an interesting way to search for non-tame automorphisms over finite fields. The existence of an element of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
acting by an odd permutation on
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathrm{k})
, where
k=\mathbb{F}_{2^{r}}
with
r\geq 2
would yield such an element.

We now consider the action of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
and in particular of the translations.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathrm{k}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field with
q
elements, let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let us consider the action of the group

\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\cap\mathrm{Tame}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})

of tame automorphisms of Jacobian
1
on the set
\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathrm{k})\simeq\mathrm{k}^{n}
. Then, the following hold:

(1)

The action of every translation is even, i.e. contained in the alternating group
\mathrm{Alt}(\mathrm{k}^{n})
, if and only
(q,n)\not=(2,1)
.

(2)

The action of every element of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\cap\mathrm{Tame}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is even if and only if
q>2
.

Proof.

We write
q=p^{r}
where
p=\mathrm{char}(\mathrm{k})
is a prime number and
r\geq 1
.

(1)
: A non-trivial translation is equal to
(x_{1}+v_{1},\ldots,x_{n}+v_{n})
for some
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in\mathrm{k}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. The order of such an element is then equal to
p
. If
p
is odd, the induced permutation is then even. We may thus assume that
p=2
. In that case, the orbits have all size
2
and the number of orbits is then
\frac{\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert^{n}}{2}=\frac{(2^{r})^{n}}{2}=2^{rn-1}
. We then obtain an even permutation, except when
rn=1
. This latter case corresponds to
(q,n)=(2,1)
and to the translation
(x_{1}+1)
of
\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}
.

(2)
: If
q=2
, we consider
(x_{1}+x_{2}x_{3}\cdots x_{n},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
(which corresponds to
(x_{1}+1)
if
n=1
), that is a tame automorphism of Jacobian
1
. It is of order
2
and permutes exactly two points, namely
(1,1,\ldots,1)
with
(0,1,\ldots,1)
(this corresponds to
(0)
and
(1)
if
n=1
). It is thus an odd permutation.

We now assume that
q>2
and prove that the action of every element of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\cap\mathrm{Tame}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is always even. This group is generated by
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
and by elements of the form

e_{s}=(x_{1}+s(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),

where
s\in\mathrm{k}[x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
. Moreover,
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
is generated by elementary matrices conjugate to
e_{s}
with
s
linear. It then suffices to show that the action of every
e_{s}
is even. As before, we may assume that
p=2
, as the order of
e_{s}
is
p
. Every orbit has then order
2
and it remains to see that the number of orbits is even, or equivalently that the number of points moved is divisible by
4
. As
4
divides
q
, it divides
\lvert\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathrm{k})\rvert
. We then only need to see that the number of fixed points is divisible by
4
. The set of fixed points being
\mathbb{A}^{1}\times X
where
X
is the zero locus of

, this is again given by the fact that
4
divides

.

Normal subgroups containing affine automorphisms

In this section, we prove that the normal subgroups generated by all translations or by linear elements contains all tame automorphisms. The result has already be proven if

and

The following proposition corresponds to

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be a field and let
n\geq 1
be an integer.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

There exists a normal subgroup
N\subseteq\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})

that contains all translations but does not contain
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
.

(2)

\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert=2
and
n=2
.

Proof.

\ref{TranslationsSLn2}\Rightarrow\ref{TranslationsSLn1}
: Suppose that
n=2
and
\mathrm{k}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
. The group
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
then acts on the four points of
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathrm{k})
, and the action of every translation is even (lemma
3.4
(1)
). However, the element
(x_{1}+x_{2},x_{2})
permutes the two points
(0,1)
and
(1,1)
and fixes the points
(0,0)
and
(1,0)
. It is then an odd permutation. The normal subgroup
N\subseteq\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
consisting of automorphisms inducing an even permutation on the four points then contains all translations but does not contain
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
.

\ref{TranslationsSLn1}\Rightarrow\ref{TranslationsSLn2}
: By contraposition, we assume that
n\not=2
or that
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert\not=2
and prove that every normal subgroup
N\subseteq\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
that contains all translations also contains
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
. If
n=1
, then
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
only consists of translations, so the result holds. We may then assume
n\geq 2
.

For each
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{2},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})\in\mathrm{k}^{n-1}
, the translation

\tau_{\epsilon}=(x_{1},x_{2}+\epsilon_{2},\ldots,x_{n}+\epsilon_{n})

belongs to
N
. Moreover, for each polynomial
q\in\mathrm{k}[x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
, the element

e_{q}=(x_{1}+q(x_{2},\ldots,q_{n}),x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

belongs to
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
. Hence, the commutator
h_{q,\epsilon}=(e_{q}^{-1}\circ\tau_{\epsilon}^{-1}\circ e_{q})\circ\tau_{\epsilon}
belongs to
N
. We write explicitely
h_{q,\epsilon}
as

h_{q,\epsilon}=(x_{1}+q(x_{2}+\epsilon_{2},\ldots,x_{n}+\epsilon_{n})-q(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}).

To prove that
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
is contained in
N
, we only need to show that
e_{\lambda x_{2}}=(x_{1}+\lambda x_{2},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
belongs to
N
for each
\lambda\in\mathrm{k}
. Indeed, every elementary matrix of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
with diagonal entries equal to
1
and only one other non-zero entry is conjugated to some
e_{\lambda x_{2}}
by permutations, and all of them generate
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
.

If
\mathrm{char}(\mathrm{k})\not=2
, we choose
q=\frac{\lambda}{2}x_{2}^{2}
,
\epsilon_{2}=1
and obtain
h_{q,\epsilon}=(x_{1}+\lambda x_{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in N
. Composing with the translation
(x_{1}-\frac{\lambda}{2},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
, we obtain
e_{\lambda x_{2}}\in N
as desired.

If
n\geq 3
, we choose
q=x_{2}x_{3}
and
(\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3})=(0,\lambda)
and obtain
e_{\lambda x_{2}}=h_{q,\epsilon}\in N
as desired.

The remaining case is when
n=2
and
\mathrm{char}(\mathrm{k})=2
. By assumption, we have
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert\geq 3
. We choose
\theta,\mu,\nu\in\mathrm{k}
and choose two possibilities for
(q,\epsilon_{2})
, namely
(\theta\nu x_{2}^{3},\mu)
and
(\mu\nu x_{2}^{3},\theta)
. This gives two elements for
h_{q,\epsilon}\in N
, namely

\displaystyle(x_{1}+\theta\mu\nu x_{2}^{2}+\theta\mu^{2}\nu x_{2}+\theta\mu^{3}\nu,x_{2})\in N,

\displaystyle(x_{1}+\theta\mu\nu x_{2}^{2}+\theta^{2}\mu\nu x_{2}+\theta^{3}\mu\nu,x_{2})\in N.

The composition of these two elements with the translation
(x_{1}+\theta\mu\nu(\theta^{2}+\mu^{2}),x_{2},)
gives

(x_{1}+\theta\mu\nu(\theta+\mu)x_{2},x_{2})\in N.

As
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert\geq 3
, we may choose
\theta,\mu\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
, both different and then
\nu=\frac{\lambda}{\theta\mu(\theta+\mu)}
to obtain
e_{\lambda x_{2}}\in N
as desired.
∎

The following result is due to Drew Lewis, we recall the proof, as it is short, for self-containedess.

Proposition 3.7
.

[
Lew20
, Theorem 9]

Let
\mathrm{k}
be a infinite field and let
n\geq 2
be an integer. Every normal subgroup

N\subseteq\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})

that contains
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
also contains all tame automorphisms of Jacobian
1
.

Proof.

As the tame group is generated by
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
and elementary automorphisms, it suffices to show that

e_{s}=(x_{1}+s(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in N

for each
s\in\mathrm{k}[x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
. As
e_{s}\circ e_{s^{\prime}}=e_{s+s^{\prime}}
, we only need to obtain each monomial. As in the proof of Proposition
3.6
, we use some commutators.

For each
\alpha=(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in(\mathrm{k}^{*})^{n-1}
, the diagonal matrix

\delta=({(\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{n})^{-1}}x_{1},\alpha_{2}x_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}x_{n})\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})

belongs to
N
. Moreover, for each polynomial
q\in\mathrm{k}[x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
, the element

e_{q}=(x_{1}+q(x_{2},\ldots,q_{n}),x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

belongs to
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
. Hence, the commutator
u_{q,\alpha}=(e_{q}^{-1}\circ\delta^{-1}\circ e_{q})\circ\delta
belongs to
N
. We write explicitely
u_{q,\alpha}
as

u_{q,\alpha}=(x_{1}+\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{n}q(\alpha_{2}x_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}x_{n})-q(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}).

For all integers
i_{2},\ldots,i_{n}\geq 0
we may choose
q(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=\xi x_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}
with
\xi\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
and obtain

u_{q,\alpha}=(x_{1}+(\alpha_{2}^{i_{2}+1}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{i_{n}+1}-1)\xi x_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in N.

As
\mathrm{k}
is infinite, we may choose
\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
such that
\alpha_{2}^{i_{2}+1}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{i_{n}+1}\not=1

and choose
\xi
to obtain

(x_{1}+\lambda x_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in N

for each
\lambda\in\mathrm{k}
. This happens for each monomial, so we obtain all tame automorphisms.
∎

Example 3.8
.

All calculations given below are inspired from
[
MP09
, Theorem 3.3]
, but one does not need to read the above article to follow the calculations.
Let
\mathrm{k}
be a field and let us write
\Delta=x_{1}x_{3}+x_{2}^{2}
. For each
\alpha\in\mathrm{k}
, we define

N_{\alpha}=(x_{1}-2\alpha x_{2}\Delta-\alpha^{2}x_{3}\Delta^{2},x_{2}+x_{3}\alpha\Delta,x_{3})\in\mathrm{End}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{3})

One then calculate
(N_{\alpha})^{*}(\Delta)=\Delta
and then
N_{\alpha}\circ N_{\beta}=N_{\alpha+\beta}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathrm{k}
, which implies that
N_{\alpha}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{A}^{3})
for each
\alpha\in\mathrm{k}
, with
(N_{\alpha})^{-1}=N_{-\alpha}
. Moreover,
\mathrm{Jac}(N_{\alpha})=1
for each
\alpha\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
. The element
N_{1}\in\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{3})
is the classical
Nagata automorphism
of
[
Nag72
, Section 2.1]
.

For each
u\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
, we write
L_{u}=(ux_{1},x_{2},u^{-1}x_{3})\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathrm{k})
We again have
(L_{u})^{*}(\Delta)=\Delta
and then obtain that

(L_{u})^{-1}\circ N_{\alpha}\circ L_{u}=N_{\frac{\alpha}{u}}

for each
\alpha\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
. This implies that
N_{\alpha}\circ L_{u}\circ N_{-\frac{\alpha}{u}}=L_{u}
, and thus

N_{\alpha}\circ(L_{u}\circ N_{\frac{\alpha(u-1)}{u}})\circ N_{\alpha}^{-1}=L_{u},

which implies that
L_{u}\circ N_{\frac{\alpha(u-1)}{u}}
is conjugate to
L_{u}
in
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{3})
, so
N_{\frac{\alpha(u-1)}{u}}
is in the normal subgroup generated by
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathrm{k})
. As soon as
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert>2
, we may choose
u\in\mathrm{k}\setminus\{0,1\}
and choose
\alpha\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
so that
\frac{\alpha(u-1)}{u}
is any possible value in
\mathrm{k}^{*}
. Hence
N_{\beta}
is in the normal subgroup generated by
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
for each
\beta\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
. In particular, the Nagata automorphism is contained in any normal subgroup of

that contains
\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathrm{k})
.

3.4.
Quotients of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
when
\mathrm{k}
is finite

The proof of Proposition
3.7
(second part of Proposition
C
) really needs that the field is infinite. In this section, we prove that the results are actually false over any finite field. Indeed, we provide a surjective group homomorphism
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})\to\mathrm{k}[x]
whose kernel contains all translations, and also all linear maps if
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert>2
, for each finite field
\mathrm{k}
. This will give the proof of Proposition
D
and show a very different behaviour from the case of infinite fields, where
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
is a perfect group, and where the normal subgroup generated by linear maps or translations is everything (Proposition
C
). The construction uses the following natural vector space
V
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be a field and let
V\subseteq\mathrm{k}[x]
be the
\mathrm{k}
-linear subspace generated by

\{s-as(ax+b)\mid s\in\mathrm{k}[x],a\in\mathrm{k}^{*},b\in\mathrm{k}\}.

Then, the following hold:

(1)

\mathrm{k}\subseteq V
;

(2)

If
\mathrm{k}
is infinite, then
V=\mathrm{k}[x]
.

(3)

If
\mathrm{k}
is finite with
q
elements, the classes of
(x^{q-1}(x^{q}-x)^{m(q-1)-1})_{m\geq 1}
in
\mathrm{k}[x]/V
are linearly independent. In particular,
\mathrm{k}[x]/V
has infinite dimension.

(4)

If
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert>2
, then
cx+d\in V
for all
c,d\in\mathrm{k}
.

(5)

If
\mathrm{k}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
, then
V=\mathrm{k}[x(x+1)]
, so
x\not\in V
.

Proof.

(1)
-
(2)
-
(4)
: For each
i\geq 0
, if
a\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
is such that
a^{i+1}\not=0
, we may choose
s=x^{i}
,
b=0
and obtain

s-as(ax+b)=(1-a^{i+1})x^{i}\in V.

Choosing
i=0
, we obtain
1\in V
, which proves
(1)
. If
\mathrm{k}
is infinite,
V
contains all monomials and thus
V=\mathrm{k}[x]
, whence
(2)
. If
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert\not=2
, with
i=1
we obtain
x\in V
, which proves
(4)
.

(3)
:
Suppose now that
\mathrm{k}
is a finite field with
q
elements. We consider the polynomial

R=x^{q}-x=\prod_{\nu\in\mathrm{k}}(x-\nu).

For all
a\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
and
b\in\mathrm{k}
, the map
x\mapsto ax+b
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}^{1}_{\mathrm{k}}
, so
R
and
R(ax+b)
have the same roots. This gives
R(ax+b)=a^{q}R=aR
.

The elements
m_{i,j}=x^{i}R^{j}
, with
0\leq i\leq q-1
and
j\geq 0
form a basis of
\mathrm{k}[x]
as they have all possible degrees. Any element of
V
is then a linear combination of elements of the form

m_{i,j}-am_{i,j}(ax+b)=(x^{i}-a^{j+1}(ax+b)^{i})R^{j}.

Such a polynomial is a linear combination of elements of the form
m_{u,j}
with
0\leq u\leq i
, and the coefficient of
m_{i,j}
is
1-a^{i+j+1}
. Hence, the coefficient of any polynomial
m_{q-1,j}
of any element of
V
is zero as soon as
a^{q+j}=1
for each element
a\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
, i.e. when
q+j
is a multiple of
q-1=\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert
. This corresponds to ask that
j=m(q-1)-1
for some
m\geq 1
, and thus proves
(3)
.

(5)
: If
\mathrm{k}=\mathbb{F}_{2}
, then
V
is the linear subspace generated by
s+s(x+1)
with
s\in\mathrm{k}[x]
. The polynomial
R
is now
R=x^{2}+x=x(x+1)
. As the polynomials
x^{i}R^{j}
with
i\in\{0,1\}
and
j\geq 0
form a basis of
\mathrm{k}[x]
, we obtain

\mathrm{k}[x]=\mathrm{k}[R]\oplus x\mathrm{k}[R].

Hence, every
s\in\mathrm{k}[x]
can be written as
s=c+dx
with
c,d\in\mathrm{k}[R]
. This gives
s(x)+s(x+1)=d
and thus proves that
V=\mathrm{k}[R]
.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be a finite field and let
V\subseteq\mathrm{k}[x]
be the
\mathrm{k}
-linear subspace generated by

\{s-as(ax+b)\mid s\in\mathrm{k}[x],a\in\mathrm{k}^{*},b\in\mathrm{k}\}.

Then, we have a unique surjective group homomorphism

\rho\colon\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{k}[x]/V

that sends
(x_{1},x_{2}+s(x_{1}))
onto the class of
s
for each
s\in\mathrm{k}[x]
.

Proof.

We first prove that if
\rho
exists, it is unique and surjective. For each
s\in\mathrm{k}[x]
, the elements

e_{s}=(x_{1},x_{2}+s(x_{1}))\text{ and }\quad e_{s}^{\prime}=(x_{1}-s(x_{2}),x_{2})

are conjugated by
(-x_{2},x_{1})\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathrm{k})
. So
\rho(e_{s})=\rho(e_{s}^{\prime})
, is the class of
s
in
\mathrm{k}[x]/V
. As
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
is generated by elements of the form
e_{s}
and
e_{s}^{\prime}
, this shows that
\rho
is unique. It also shows that
\rho
is surjective, as we may choose any
s\in\mathrm{k}[x]
.

To show that the definition on all
e_{s}
extends to a well-defined group homomorphism
\rho
, we define two group homomorphisms on
T
and
A
, the triangular and affine groups:

\rho_{T}\colon T\to\mathrm{k}[x]/V,\quad\rho_{A}\colon A\to\mathrm{k}[x]/V

and check that
\rho_{T}|_{A\cap T}=\rho_{A}|_{A\cap T}
. As
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
is the amalgamated product of
T
and
A
over their intersection, this will give the result.

We first consider the triangular group
T=\mathrm{Tri}_{2}(\mathrm{k})\cap\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
:

T=\{(ax_{1}+b,a^{-1}x_{2}+s(x_{1}))\mid a\in\mathrm{k}^{*},b\in\mathrm{k},s\in\mathrm{k}[x]\}\simeq\mathrm{k}[x]\rtimes\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{A}^{1}).

The action of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{A}^{1})
on
\mathrm{k}[x]
is the following one:
ax_{1}+b\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{A}^{1})
acts on
s\in\mathrm{k}[x]
as
s\mapsto as(ax+b)
. We define
\rho_{T}((ax_{1}+b,a^{-1}x_{2}+s(x_{1})))
to be the class of
s
. To check that this is a well-defined group homomorphism, we just need to check that
s
and
s(ax+b)
have the same class, which is exactly given by the definition of
V
. Moreover,
\rho_{T}(e_{s})
is indeed the class of
s
, for each
s\in\mathrm{k}[x]
, as desired.

Now we go to the affine group
A=\mathrm{Aff}_{2}(\mathrm{k})\cap\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
:

A=\{(ax_{1}+bx_{2}+\tau,cx_{2}+dx_{1}+\epsilon)\mid\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathrm{k}),(\tau,\epsilon)\in\mathrm{k}^{2}\}

We then have
A=\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathrm{k})\rtimes(\mathrm{k}^{2},+)
.
If
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert>2
, we define
\rho_{A}(A)=\{0\}
. If
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert=2
, then
A
acts on the four points of
\mathbb{A}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
. We send an element of
A
onto
x
or
0
, depending if the permutation is odd or even. This gives a group homomorphism
\rho_{A}\colon A\to\mathrm{k}[x]/V
.

We have now defined
\rho_{T}
and
\rho_{A}
and prove that
\rho_{T}|_{A\cap T}=\rho_{A}|_{A\cap T}
. The group
A\cap T
is equal to

A\cap T=\{(ax_{1}+b,a^{-1}x_{2}+s(x_{1}))\mid a\in\mathrm{k}^{*},b\in\mathrm{k},s\in\mathrm{k}[x]\deg(s)\leq 1\}.

The image
\rho_{T}(f)
of
f=(ax_{1}+b,a^{-1}x_{2}+s(x_{1}))
is the class of
s
. If
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert>2
, the class of
s
is trivial since
\deg(s)\leq 1
(Lemma
3.9
(4)
), so we obtain
\rho_{T}(f)=\rho_{A}(f)=0
.
If
\lvert\mathrm{k}\rvert=2
, then
f=(x_{1}+b,x_{2}+s(x_{1}))
and the translations being even (Lemma
3.4
(1)
), the element
\rho_{A}(f)
is equal to
\rho_{A}((x_{1},x_{2}+x_{1}))
if
\deg(f)=1
and to
0
if
\deg(f)\leq 0
. This implies that
\rho_{A}(f)=\rho_{T}(f)
as
\mathrm{k}\subseteq V
(Lemma
3.9
(1)
).
∎

We can now give the proof of Proposition
D
:

Proof of Proposition
D
.

As
\mathrm{k}
is finite, Proposition
3.10
gives a unique surjective group homomorphism
\rho\colon\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{2})\twoheadrightarrow\mathrm{k}[x]/V
that sends

onto the class of
s
for each
s\in\mathrm{k}[x]
.

By Lemma
3.9

, every translation is in the kernel. Moreover,
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathrm{k})

-
(5)
).

4.
Families of automorphisms

Before working with elements of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[t,\frac{1}{t}]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
and
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[t]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
, where
\mathrm{k}
is a field, we recall the following lemma, probably known to all specialists. We give back a proof for a lack of reference. We will apply the next lemma to the case where
A=\mathrm{k}[t]
and
B=\mathrm{k}[t,\frac{1}{t}]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
B
be an integral domain and let
A\subseteq B
be a subring. For each integer
n\geq 1
, we have

\mathrm{Aut}_{A}(\mathbb{A}^{n})=\{f\in\mathrm{End}_{A}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\cap\mathrm{Aut}_{B}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\mid\mathrm{Jac}(f)\in A^{*}\}.

Proof.

As
\mathrm{Aut}_{A}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\subseteq\mathrm{End}_{A}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\cap\mathrm{Aut}_{B}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
and as the Jacobian of an element of
\mathrm{Aut}_{A}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
belongs to
A^{*}
, we have the inclusion

\mathrm{Aut}_{A}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\subseteq\{f\in\mathrm{End}_{A}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\cap\mathrm{Aut}_{B}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\mid\mathrm{Jac}(f)\in A^{*}\}.

Conversely, let us take an element

with

is a translation, we may assume that
f
fixes the origin
(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{A}^{n}
. We then write

with

and may write

Replacing

with
0

\det(M)=\mathrm{Jac}(f)\in A^{*}
, so

with
(f_{1,1},\ldots,f_{n,1})^{-1}\circ f

\mathrm{Jac}(f)=1

As

, that we write similarly as
f
as
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
, where
g_{i}=g_{i,1}+g_{i,2}+\cdots+g_{i,D}
and
g_{i,j}\in B[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is homogeneous of degree

, and

is the maximal degree of all polynomials

. We now prove, by induction on

for each

. For this, we use that

, which means that

for each

. This gives

(
\spadesuit
)

x_{i}=\sum_{m=1}^{D}g_{i,m}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})=\sum_{m=1}^{D}g_{i,m}(x_{1}+f_{1,2}+\cdots+f_{1,d},\ldots,x_{n}+f_{n,2}+\cdots+f_{n,d}).

The homogeneous part of degree
1
of (

♠

‣
4

, which implies that

for each

. We then assume that

and prove that

for each

and each

of (

‣

4.1.
Closed normal subgroups of
\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be a field, let
n\geq 1
be an integer and let
g\in\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[t]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\setminus\{\mathrm{id}\}
be such that replacing
t
with zero one obtains
g(0)=\mathrm{id}\in\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
.

There exist coprime integers
a,b\geq 1
such that the following hold:

(1)

For each extension
\mathrm{k}\subseteq L
and each
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})\in L^{n}
, writing

\rho_{\epsilon}=(x_{1}+t^{-b}\epsilon_{1},\ldots,x_{n}+t^{-b}\epsilon_{n})\in\mathrm{Aut}_{L[t,\frac{1}{t}]}(\mathbb{A}^{n}),

the element

h_{\epsilon}=(\rho_{\epsilon})^{-1}\circ g(t^{a})\circ(\rho_{\epsilon})

belongs to
\mathrm{Aut}_{L[t]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
and replacing
t
with zero in
h_{\epsilon}
gives a translation

h_{\epsilon}(0)\in\mathrm{Aut}_{L}(\mathbb{A}^{n}).

Here,
g(t^{a})\in\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[t^{a}]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is the element obtained by replacing
t
with
t^{a}
in
g
.

(2)

The set
\{\epsilon\in\mathbb{A}^{n}\mid h_{\epsilon}(0)\not=\mathrm{id}\}
is a dense open subset of
\mathbb{A}^{n}
. In particular, for each infinite extension
\mathrm{k}\subseteq L
, there exists
(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})\in L^{n}
such that
h_{\epsilon}(0)\in\mathrm{Aut}_{L}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
is a non-trivial translation.

Proof.

We write
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the element
g_{i}\in\mathrm{k}[t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is such that
g_{i}(0)=x_{i}
, where we replace here
t
with
0
. We can then uniquely write
g_{i}
as

g_{i}=x_{i}+\sum_{j\geq 1,m\geq 0}q_{i,j,m}t^{j}

where each
q_{i,j,m}\in\mathrm{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is homogeneous of degree
m
.

We now choose the two coprime integers
a,b\geq 1
such that

\frac{a}{b}=\max\left.\left\{\frac{m}{j}\right|q_{i,j,m}\not=0\right\}.

This is possible since
g
is not the identity, so at least one
q_{i,j,m}
is not zero.

For each field extension
\mathrm{k}\subseteq L
and each
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})\in L^{n}
, we write

\rho_{\epsilon}=(x_{1}+t^{-b}\epsilon_{1},\ldots,x_{n}+t^{-b}\epsilon_{n})\in\mathrm{Aut}_{L[t,\frac{1}{t}]}(\mathbb{A}^{n}),

as in the statement of the proposition. Replacing
t
with
t^{a}
in
g
, we obtain
g(t^{a})\in\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[t^{a}]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})\subseteq\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[t]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
. Conjugating this element by
\rho_{\epsilon}
, we obtain

h_{\epsilon}=(\rho_{\epsilon})^{-1}\circ g(t^{a})\circ(\rho_{\epsilon})\in\mathrm{Aut}_{L[t,\frac{1}{t}]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})

We now prove that
h_{\epsilon}
is actually an element of
\mathrm{Aut}_{L[t]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
and that replacing
t
with
0
, we obtain a translation
h_{\epsilon}(0)\in\mathrm{Aut}_{L}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
.

For
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the
i
-th coefficient of
h
is

h_{i}=x_{i}+\sum_{j\geq 1,m\geq 0}q_{i,j,m}(x_{1}+t^{-b}\epsilon_{1},\ldots,x_{n}+t^{-b}\epsilon_{n})\cdot t^{aj}

As
aj-bm\geq 0
if
q_{i,j,m}\not=0
(this inequality is equivalent to
\frac{m}{j}\leq\frac{a}{b}
), we find that
h_{i}\in L[t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, i.e. there is no denominator in
t
in
h_{i}
. Moreover, replacing
t
with
0
in
h_{i}
gives

h_{i}(0)=x_{i}+\sum_{\frac{m}{j}=\frac{a}{b}}q_{i,j,m}(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})=x_{i}+c_{i},

where
c_{i}\in L
.

The Jacobian of
g
being an invertible element of
\mathrm{k}[t]
, it belongs to
\mathrm{k}^{*}
. Hence, the Jacobian of
h_{\epsilon}
is the same element of
\mathrm{k}^{*}
, which implies that

(Lemma
4.1
applied to the integral domains

).

Replacing
t
with
0
in

give a translation

, as
h_{i}(0)=x_{i}+c_{i}
for each

. This proves then
(1)
.

To prove
(2)
, we observe that the translation
h_{\epsilon}(0)
is the identity if and only if

for each

. This corresponds to ask that

\sum_{\frac{m}{j}=\frac{a}{b}}q_{i,j,m}(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})=0

for each

and thus gives an open subset of

. It remains to see that this open subset is not empty, which corresponds to show that some

exists such that the polynomial

is not the zero polynomial. By definition of

and

, there exists

such that
q_{i,j,m}\not=0
and such that
\frac{m}{j}=\frac{a}{b}
. We fix such an

and then observe that all polynomials

with
\frac{m}{j}=\frac{a}{b}
have distinct degrees, as

and
\frac{a}{b}
determine

. Hence all non-zero polynomials appearing in the sum defining

are linearly independent over

and thus

is not the zero polynomial. This gives the fact that open subset of
(2)
is not empty, and hence contains points over each infinite extension of
\mathrm{k}
, which proves
(2)
.

.

Proof of Proposition
B
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be an infinite field, let
n\geq 2
be an integer and let

be a closed normal subgroup with

.

We first prove that

contains a non-trivial translation.
We choose an element

. If

and obtain

Lemma 4.4
.

[
MP09
, Lemma 4.2]

\mathrm{k}
be a field with

and let

be an integer. Any normal subgroup

containing

Proof.

It suffices to show that show that

e_{s}=(x_{1}+s(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}),x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in N

for each

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be a field, let
n\geq 1
and let

(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{A}^{n}

\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[t]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})

t
with
0
in
g
gives

We write

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mathrm{k}
be an infinite field, let
n\geq 2
and let
N
be a closed normal subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
. If
N\not=\{\mathrm{id}\}
, then
N
contains
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
.

Proof.

We take
h\in N\setminus\{\mathrm{id}\}
and choose two different points
p,q\in\mathbb{A}^{n}(\mathrm{k})
such that
h(p)=q
(always possible as
\mathrm{k}
is infinite). Replacing
h
with a conjugate by a translation, we may assume that
p=(0,\ldots,0)
. Replacing with a conjugate by an element of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
, we may assume that
q=(1,0,\ldots,0)
.

We choose
\beta=(x_{1},x_{2}+x_{1}(x_{1}-1)^{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathrm{SAut}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
that fixes both
p
and
q
, and consider

f=h^{-1}\circ\beta^{-1}\circ h\circ\beta\in N.

As
\beta
fixes
p
and
q
and
h
sends
p
onto
q
, we find that
f(p)=p
, i.e.
f
fixes the origin. Moreover, the linear part of
f
is the Jacobian matrix
Df=(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}})_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathrm{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])
of
f
evaluated at the origin
p
, that is equal to

(Df)_{p}=(Dh^{-1})_{q}\cdot(D\beta^{-1})_{q}\cdot(Dh)_{p}\cdot(D\beta)_{p}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathrm{k}).

As
h
sends
p
onto
q
and
h^{-1}\circ h=\mathrm{id}
, we find that
(Dh^{-1})_{q}=((Dh)_{p})^{-1}
. Moreover,
(D\beta^{-1})_{q}=((D\beta)_{q})^{-1}
is the identity and
(D\beta)_{p}
is a non-trivial elementary matrix, since
\frac{\partial x_{1}(x_{1}-1)^{2}}{\partial x_{1}}
vanishes at
q
but does not vanish at
p
. Hence,
(Df)_{p}=(D\beta)_{p}
is a non-trivial elementary matrix in
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})
.

Writing
\alpha=(tx_{1},\ldots,tx_{n})\in\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[t,\frac{1}{t}]}
, the element

g=\alpha^{-1}\circ f\circ\alpha

is an element of
\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{k}[t]}(\mathbb{A}^{n})
that is such that replacing
t
with
0
in
g
gives

(Lemma
4.6
).

Replacing
t
with
t_{0}\in\mathrm{k}^{*}
in
g
, we obtain a conjugate of
f
, that belongs then to
N
. As
N
is closed, this implies that
g(0)=(Df)_{p}\in N
. As this latter is non-trivial elementary matrix, all such matrices are contained in
N
and then
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathrm{k})\subseteq N.
