Electroporation-mediated site-specific knock-in of a human coding sequence into the bovine ALB locus in embryos
Authors: Kyeong-Hyeon Eom, Soo-Young Yum, Gyeong-Min Gim, Woo Jae Son, Young-Chai Kim, Jae-Yoon Choi, Dong-Hyeok Kwon, Goo Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEfficient delivery of genome-editing tools into embryos is essential for the production of gene-edited cattle. Somatic cell nuclear transfer and microinjection are commonly used techniques, but electroporation has recently gained attention owing to its technical simplicity, high-throughput capacity, and suitability for large-scale applications. However, its application has been largely restricted to gene knockout owing to the presence of the zona pellucida and size limitations of deliverable materials. To address these challenges, in this study, we optimized electroporation-mediated knock-in (KI) strategies in bovine embryos using single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotides (ssODNs) and adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors as donors.ResultsInitial experiments using ssODN donors with asymmetric homology arms (10 bp left and 25 bp right) resulted in a KI blastocyst (BL) frequency of 13.9 ± 14.1%, although outcomes varied widely across replicates. By contrast, AAV donors carrying 61 bp cargo flanked by 500 bp homology arms enabled consistent KI events, after optimizing the concentration of AAV and length of homology arm. Using the optimized system, a 2.2 kb human albumin coding sequence was successfully inserted into the bovine albumin locus, yielding KI BL frequency of 55.6 ± 41.6%.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings demonstrate that electroporation combined with AAV-mediated donor delivery represents an efficient strategy for generating transgenic bovine embryos, which is a crucial first step toward producing transgenic cattle as bioreactors for high-value protein production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-026-01180-8.

Background

Genetic engineering broadly encompasses two primary objectives: (1) deletion of target genes to enable functional characterization and (2) the precision insertion of exogenous genes at defined genomic loci to evaluate their biological effects. Recent advances in gene-editing technologies, particularly the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-associated protein 9 (CRISPR–Cas9) system, have revolutionized the efficiency with which these objectives can be achieved. Although these methodologies have been extensively applied in rodent models, their adoption in livestock species—particularly cattle—has gained significant traction in recent years because of their economic and biological importance. Indeed, in cattle, several studies have demonstrated the generation of gene-edited animals with traits such as a hornless phenotype, muscle hypertrophy, heat tolerance, reduced allergenicity, and enhanced disease resistance [1–7]. While modifying endogenous genes is highly promising, generating transgenic cattle through insertion of exogenous genes is also critically important, especially for the development of bovine bioreactors for biomedical material production. Although Escherichia coli and other bacteria have traditionally been used for recombinant protein production, their lack of post-translational modifications and the limited productivity of mammalian cell lines such as Chinese hamster ovary cells underscore the advantages of cattle as living bioreactors [8–12]. As demonstrated in multiple studies, endogenous gene deletion has been relatively efficient, whereas the integration of exogenous genes into the bovine genome remains technically challenging.

Two main approaches have been used for inserting exogenous genes. The first is random integration, in which a promoter–protein coding sequence (CDS)–polyadenylation (pA) cassette is packaged into vectors such as plasmid DNA, retroviruses, lentiviruses, or transposons and integrated at random sites into the genome (albeit with certain positional preferences). The second is site-specific knock-in (KI), in which a sequence of interest is inserted at a specific genomic locus. Although random integration has produced live transgenic cattle as first reported by Garrels et al. [13], it carries important limitations, including potential disruption of critical endogenous genes during the integration process and transcriptional “silencing” owing to the use of exogenous promoters [14–19]. By contrast, although KI in bovine embryos has been considered inefficient, several studies have demonstrated successful targeted mutations and even insertion of large sequences (~5.1 kb) by co-injection of Cas9 protein–single-guide RNA (sgRNA) ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes with donor templates, resulting in the generation of live animals [4, 20, 21]. These studies validated the feasibility of direct KI in bovine zygotes. However, microinjection requires embryo-by-embryo manipulation, highly skilled personnel, expensive equipment, and intensive labor and time. To overcome these limitations, recent efforts have explored electroporation as an alternative delivery method for bovine embryos [22–24]. One limitation of electroporation compared with microinjection is that only relatively small molecules, such as RNPs or small DNA fragments, are efficiently introduced, whereas larger molecules are not. To overcome this, studies have started using adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors, initially demonstrated in mouse embryos. Unlike other viral vectors, AAV can penetrate the zona pellucida and diffuse into zygotes, making it a promising vehicle for delivering KI donor sequences [25–29].

In the present study, we attempted electroporation-mediated, zygote-directed KI in bovine embryos. First, we optimized electroporation conditions to maximize the efficiency of double-strand break (DSB) induction. Using these conditions, we conducted two sets of KI experiments. In the first, we combined RNPs with single-stranded oligodeoxynucleotide (ssODN) donors to test short, asymmetric homology arms. In the second, we combined RNPs with AAV vectors as donor delivery vehicles. For the AAV-mediated approach, we first determined the optimal viral concentration using a short KI sequence and then compared KI efficiencies across donors with different lengths of the homology arms. Finally, using optimized conditions, we attempted to insert a 2.2 kb human albumin CDS into the endogenous bovine ALB locus and successfully confirmed KI events at the embryo stage.

Methods

In vitro production of bovine embryos

Immature oocytes were aspirated from 4 to 8 mm follicles on the ovaries from slaughterhouses and matured in tissue culture-based medium for 22–24 h, as previously reported [30]. For in vitro fertilization (IVF), motile spermatozoa were selected using a Percoll gradient, as previously described [30]. Briefly, frozen-thawed semen was centrifuged on a discontinuous Percoll gradient (45–90%) at 366 × g for 15 min. To prepare the 45% Percoll solution, 1 mL of capacitation-Tyrode’s albumin lactate pyruvate (TALP) medium was mixed with 1 mL of 90% Percoll. The sperm pellet was washed twice with 3 mL of capacitation-TALP medium and centrifuged at 366 × g for 5 min. Washed motile spermatozoa (1–2 × 106 sperm/mL) were co-incubated with mature oocytes for 18–20 h in 50 μL microdrops of IVF-TALP medium, covered with mineral oil (Nidacon, Cat. No. NO-100), in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 38.5 °C. After co-incubation, cumulus cells were removed from the presumptive zygotes. Zygotes were cultured in a two-step, chemically defined culture medium under mineral oil in an atmosphere of 5% O2 and 5% CO2 at 38.5 °C [18, 30]. The cleavage rate refers to the proportion of embryos that reached the 8–16 cell stage, which corresponds to the period of major embryonic genome activation (EGA) in bovine embryos [31]. Cleavage rate was assessed 3 days after fertilization. For clarification, the blastocyst (BL) rate was subsequently evaluated 7 days after fertilization. The information on the number of embryos used in each experiments is summarized in Supplementary table S1.

The voltages and polarity of poring pulses were tested in the experiments shown in Fig. 1, although the results were from a single replicate of the experiment. AAV preparation and transduction

Single-stranded AAV vectors (serotypes DJ, DJ/8, and 6) carrying a GFP expression cassette under the CMV promoter were purchased from GeneCopoeia (Rockville, MD, USA). Single-stranded AAV-6 vectors carrying the knock-in donor templates (human ALB coding sequences with poly A signal flanked by homology arms) were purchased from VectorBuilder (Chicago, IL, USA). The AAV vectors were diluted to the desired titers using embryo growth media. The AAV vectors were diluted to the desired dosages using embryo growth media. To deliver the genome-editing components sequentially, pools of 30 to 60 electroporated zygotes or untreated embryos for GFP experiments in Figure 4, were immediately transferred into a 100 μL drop of in vitro culture medium. The calculated amount of AAV was supplemented into the drop based on the titer of the original vials. The AAV amounts reported in this study represent the total number of genome copies (GC) added per 100 μL culture drop. After 72 h of co-incubation, the embryos were washed and moved to fresh culture medium for further development. For GFP-AAV transduction, GFP-positive embryos were counted only when they reached the late morula or blastocyst stage without morphological signs of toxicity or arrest. These assessments were performed by skilled embryologists to ensure consistent classification of early-stage cavity formation.

Guide RNA design

sgRNAs targeting green fluorescent protein (GFP) and bovine PRNP sequences described in previous studies were used [23, 32]. For the bovine BLG and ALB loci, where site-specific KI was intended, sgRNAs were designed using the Cas-Designer software (http://www.rgenome.net/cas-designer/). Candidate sgRNAs were identified on the ARS-UCD 1.2 bovine reference genome, targeting sites as close as possible to the endogenous translation start site to facilitate precise KI and translation of exogenous CDSs. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, sgRNAs were synthesized using the Precision gRNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A29377). Residual DNA were removed using DNase I. Purified sgRNA was eluted in nuclease-free water and quantified the concentration using a microvolume spectroscopy (Implen, Munich, Germany). The sgRNA sequences were as follows: sgGFP, 5′-CGTCGCCGTCCAGCTCGACC-3′; sgPRNP, 5′-AAAAACCAACATGAAGCATG-3′; sgBLG, 5′-GTCACGCTTCTGAGCTCTGC-3′; and sgALB, 5′-CTATCAACCCCACAAAACTT-3′.

Mutation detection in embryos

For embryos, single blastocysts were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and transferred to 10 μL of direct PCR buffer (Viagen, Cedar Park, TX, USA) containing 1 μL proteinase K (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Samples were incubated at 50 °C for 30 min, followed by 85 °C for 3 h. PCR amplification of the target sequence was performed under the following conditions: 94 °C for 5 min; 35–45 cycles of 94 °C for 20s, 58 °C for 30s, 72 °C for 40s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were analyzed for mutations using T7 endonuclease I (T7E1, NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) or Sanger sequencing using PCR primers. For the T7E1 assay, PCR products were denatured and re-annealed by heating followed by slow cooling. If a non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) event had occurred, heteroduplexes formed between mutant and wild-type amplicons, resulting in DNA distortions that were recognized and cleaved by T7E1, producing smaller bands detectable by agarose gel electrophoresis [33]. For junction-PCR in KI experiments, the PCR products were cloned into plasmid using a TA-cloning kit, before analysis (Biofact, Daejeon, South Korea). M13 reverse primer was used for sequencing of the TA-cloned PCR products. Sequencing results were analyzed using Synthego ICE software (https://ice.editco.bio/#/). Sequences of primers and KI cargo used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Representative Sanger sequencing results for KI detection are shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

Targeted deep sequencing

PCR amplicons were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the deep sequencing platform. Target sites were first amplified to ~1,000 bp from extracted genomic DNA using Maxime™ polymerase chain reaction (PCR) PreMix i-StarTaq (Intron Biotechnology, Cat. No. 25,167). These first-round PCR products were then amplified to ~ 220 bp using second-round primers containing custom index sequences. A third PCR was subsequently performed to add adapter sequences required for next-generation sequencing on the MiniSeq platform (Illumina). Third-round PCR products were pooled and purified using a PCR purification kit (MN, Gel and PCR Cleanup Kit, Cat. No. 740,609). The purified library was sequenced according to the Illumina protocol. MiniSeq sequencing results were saved in FASTQ format and analyzed using Cas-Analyzer (http://www.rgenome.net).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs). For comparisons involving proportional data (e.g., cleavage and blastocyst rates), differences between two groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test, while differences among multiple groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test, following arcsine square-root transformation to ensure normality of the distribution. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism v.8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Results were derived from combined data from three replicates.

Results

Optimization of electroporation-mediated DSB induction in bovine embryos

Before attempting KI experiments, electroporation conditions were optimized to improve the efficiency of DSB induction. To optimize the electrical parameters, we tested electroporation using a validated sgRNA targeting the PRNP gene, which had previously been used to generate knockout cattle [23]. Although embryo knockout and full-term animal production were previously achieved using a 15 V monodirectional pulse, our optimization tests revealed that blastocyst formation rates and embryo morphology remained comparable up to 20 V (Fig. 1A, B). Mutated BL frequency assessed by the PCR band shift and T7E1 assay continued to increase up to 20 V (Fig. 1C). Because blastocyst formation rates decreased sharply at 25 V, 20 V monodirectional pulses were selected for all subsequent experiments.

After establishing optimal electroporation conditions, we evaluated the concentration of gene-editing components using BLG as the target. We compared the original composition used in our previous study (Cas9 at 200 ng/μL + sgRNA at 100 ng/μL; 1×) with a formulation containing triple the concentration (Cas9 at 600 ng/μL + sgRNA at 300 ng/μL; 3×) [23]. Embryo development tended to be slightly lower in the 3× group than in the control and 1× groups, but the differences were not significant (Fig. 2A, B). Similarly, the proportion of embryos with detectable mutations did not differ significantly among groups. However, within individual embryos, the overall editing efficiency was significantly higher in the 3× group (Fig. 2C, D). Based on these results, the 3× reagent composition was selected for subsequent KI experiments.

Electroporation-mediated KI using ssOdns as donors

Among the various donor formats for KI, we first selected ssODNs, considering the limited pore size of the zona pellucida of bovine zygote and the size constraints associated with electroporation-mediated delivery. Our goal was to KI the CDS of human epidermal growth factor (hEGF; 165 bp), one of the smallest recombinant protein sequences reported [34]. Considering the potential future use of hEGF-expressing cattle as bioreactors, BLG, which encodes a major bovine milk protein and known milk allergen, was selected as the target site for KI. Owing to the ~200 bp size limitation of commercially synthesized ssODNs, asymmetric homology arms were designed to maximize KI efficiency, with a 10 bp left homology arm and 25 bp right homology arm (Fig. 3A) [35–37]. Compared with the control group, no significant differences were observed in embryo development (Fig. 3B, C). However, PCR assays for KI detection produced only very weak bands (Fig. 3D). All PCR products were extracted and subjected to Sanger sequencing; several samples yielded expected KI sequences whereas others showed random chromatograms, possibly owing to low KI efficiency. Across four independent experiments, the total KI BL frequency based on the presence of PCR-detected bands was 26.6 ± 16.9%. When considering only embryos in which KI was successfully confirmed by sequencing, the KI BL frequency was 13.9 ± 14.1%. A GFP expression cassette was packaged into AAV vectors of three different serotypes (DJ, DJ/8, 6), and embryo infection efficiency was assessed at the 8–16 cells stage. GFP-positive embryos were observed more frequently with the AAV6 serotype (11/16) than with the DJ (2/24) or DJ/8 (0/8) serotypes. Discussions

Although various genome-engineering tools beyond the CRISPR-Cas9 system have recently been developed, the production of transgenic cattle ultimately requires efficient delivery of these components into embryos to generate transgenic embryos first. Among the major methods for creating genetically modified embryos, somatic cell nuclear transfer and microinjection allow the direct physical introduction of genetic material into embryos, offering the advantage of being relatively unrestricted by donor size. However, these methods require micromanipulation using an embryo manipulator, expensive equipment, highly skilled technicians, and intense labor because each embryo must be injected individually [15, 18, 20, 24, 30, 39–41]. As an alternative, electroporation has gained increasing attention for embryo editing. Although electroporation is widely used for introducing genetic material into cultured cells, the presence of the zona pellucida in embryos limits the size of materials that can be introduced [27, 42]. Nevertheless, RNP complexes are sufficiently small to diffuse through electroporation-generated pores, enabling effective genome editing in mouse, rat, and later extending to bovine embryos with the successful production of live calves [23, 25, 43]. Because RNPs can be efficiently introduced via electroporation, this approach is particularly advantageous for endogenous gene knockout. However, for KI applications that require delivery of exogenous sequences, the size limitations of donor templates remain a major bottleneck [24].

Site-specific KI of exogenous sequences generally requires induction of DSBs at the target site and provision of a donor template containing left and right homology arms during DNA repair [44, 45]. Depending on the DNA repair mechanism engaged, the composition and size of the donor template can vary. Given the limited size of constructs that can be introduced into embryos by electroporation, ssODNs used for single-strand annealing (SSA) repair represent one of the few available options [44, 45]. Thus, our initial KI attempts employed ssODNs. However, in the present study, KI detection using ssODNs was inconsistent and inefficient, preventing us from concluding that efficient KI had been achieved. Several factors may underlie this inefficiency, including target-specific cleavage efficiency, repair bias, the high cost and size limitation of ssODN synthesis, limited flexibility in donor design optimizations, and the long genomic distance from the endogenous pA sequence, which complicates stable protein expression [45]. Therefore, ssODNs were not further optimized for achieving the large-fragment KI required for bioreactor-type transgenic cattle. Nevertheless, previous studies suggest that long ssODNs may be effectively utilized for bovine embryo KI in the future [46].

Beyond SSA, homologous recombination (HR) remains the most representative DNA repair pathway used for KI. However, HR efficiency in bovine embryos is generally considered to be extremely low [20, 41]. More recently, alternative repair pathways, including NHEJ, microhomology-mediated end joining, and homology-mediated end joining (HMEJ), have been explored to enhance KI efficiency [45]. Among these strategies, HMEJ has been shown to be particularly promising. In this approach, double-stranded DNA donors (e.g., plasmid constructs) containing homology arms are either digested externally (targeted in vitro linearized DNA; TILD) or cleaved intracellularly by incorporating sgRNA target sites at both ends, thereby promoting highly efficient KI compared with traditional HR [44, 47]. In bovine embryos, HMEJ-based KI has been reported to improve overall KI rates and increase the proportion of non-mosaic KI events [41]. Using this strategy, a ~ 3.1 kb sequence was successfully integrated into the bovine genome, resulting in the production of live calves [20]. Although AAV is a single-stranded DNA virus, it converts to a double-stranded form following infection, enabling HR-like repair mechanisms to operate [48]. Therefore, combining AAV-mediated donor delivery with HMEJ-based strategies may further enhance KI efficiency in future applications. Alternatively, it is also highly plausible that the single-stranded viral genome directly participates in the recombination process via other pathways, such as SSA. Whether the precise recombination event is mediated by the single-stranded or the double-stranded complementary version remains to be elucidated in this work.

RNP electroporation combined with AAV donor delivery in bovine embryos has been reported previously [26]. However, a direct comparison of KI efficiencies requires careful consideration of profound methodological differences, particularly regarding treatment timing and donor design. For example, some studies apply AAV during fertilization, whereas others introduce it after zygote formation, and the length of exposure differs widely. In addition, the use of self-complementary AAV versus single-stranded AAV introduces further variation [25, 26, 28, 38]. Therefore, AAV serotype selection, treatment timing, and exposure conditions will need further optimization in future studies to further improve KI outcomes in bovine embryos. Previous study by Lin et al. exposed bovine oocytes to AAV during fertilization [26]. While this early intervention maximizes the exposure window before the first cleavage, the required premature denuding of cumulus-oocyte complexes severely reduces blastocyst development rates. In contrast, our approach introduces AAV after zygote formation. Although this slightly delays the onset of transduction, it preserves the crucial cumulus cells during initial fertilization, thereby maintaining high developmental competence. Furthermore, an essential distinction lies in the integration mechanism. Lin et al. designed their donor vector with target gRNA sequences flanking the homology arms, employing HMEJ strategy. Although HMEJ can facilitate high integration frequencies, the linearized donor fragments inherently carry a substantially higher risk of unintended random integration. Conversely, our study strictly utilized HR mechanism without donor cleavage to minimize random integration events. Additionally, by utilizing NGS, our study provides a deeper quantitative assessment of not only general editing rates but also precise KI mosaicism within individual embryos. Therefore, while both studies demonstrate the feasibility of large-fragment KI, our optimized strategy offers a distinct balance of high developmental viability, high-fidelity HDR, and comprehensive mosaicism evaluation. Future studies focusing on AAV serotype selection and exposure conditions will further improve these KI outcomes in bovine embryos. The observed variability in embryo development and KI efficiency likely reflects the inherent biological heterogeneity of abattoir-derived bovine oocytes. In further study, increasing the number of embryos per condition could further stabilize these values.

In this study, homology arm-targeted deep sequencing was performed after AAV-mediated donor delivery, allowing assessment of mosaicism rates at the single-embryo level. This provides predictive information regarding expected mosaicism in calves derived from embryo transfer. Despite differences in donor strategies, KI fundamentally relies on DSB induction by CRISPR-Cas9, which carries inherent risks of off-target effects. To mitigate such risks, several approaches are under investigation, including the use of Cas9 nickase or prime editors for more precise sequence insertion prior to donor exposure [49, 50]. Although the sgRNAs selected in this study showed no predicted off-target sites with up to two mismatches in the bovine genome, potential off-target sites containing three mismatches were identified. Accordingly, off-target effects should be carefully assessed in future studies to ensure the genetic safety and stability of resulting animals.

Additionally, mosaicism may arise during KI events, and direct assessment of mosaicism for large donor sequences remains technically challenging. In this study, while the individual embryo KI rate was determined using a 61 bp test cargo (Fig. 6D), the limited read length of the short-read sequencing platform precluded comprehensive analysis of the full length of the 2.2 kb human ALB donor. Consequently, although our findings provide indirect evidence of expected mosaicism rates, future studies incorporating long-read sequencing technologies will be essential to fully characterize integration patterns and genomic stability of large donors in bovine embryos.

Additionally, some limitations on the analyses in this study still exist. A fundamental limitation of direct PCR analysis of blastocysts is the potential presence of residual cumulus cells attached to the zona pellucida, which may influence mosaicism rates in both mutation induction and KI analyses, necessitating more reliable methodologies to obtain definitive results. Also, although the generation of small indels and large deletions are controlled in a relative, correlated manner [51], it is important to note that the mutation efficiencies calculated using ICE analysis in this study primarily reflect the frequency of small insertions and deletions. Because ICE analysis relies on short-read Sanger sequencing amplicons, embryos carrying large-sized deletions may be unanalyzable or underrepresented. Therefore, the actual mutation rates, inclusive of large deletions, might be higher than those reported by ICE analysis alone, which remains a technical limitation of the current evaluation method. Furthermore, our study lacks an AAV-only control group (without Cas9 RNP), which is ideally required to assess the background persistence of unintegrated AAV episomes and to strictly rule out potential confounding effects during PCR screening. Although we mitigated this risk by verifying the precise integration junctions of all positive amplicons via Sanger and deep sequencing, the absence of this specific control group remains a methodological limitation. Additionally, for the single-embryo PCR analysis, we did not routinely perform multiplex PCR with a housekeeping gene. Therefore, the possibility of false-negative results arising from DNA extraction failure in some embryos cannot be entirely ruled out, although such events would likely be distributed randomly across experimental groups and not alter the relative comparisons. Finally, the current study did not assess whether the confirmed knock-in events in the blastocysts were mono-allelic or bi-allelic. Ideally, a long-range PCR spanning both homology arms would resolve this. Furthermore, without the detection of full-length sequences, there is still the possibility of partial knock-in events, although positive results from junction PCRs on both arms make full-length KI events highly probable. However, amplifying the entire modified locus from the crude and minute DNA of a single embryo proved technically challenging. This is likely due to the significant size discrepancy between the WT and KI alleles, resulting in amplification bias toward the shorter WT allele, compounded by the relatively low proportion of KI alleles within mosaic embryos, as observed in our deep sequencing data (Fig. 6D). Given the long generation intervals of cattle, establishing the precise zygosity of transgenic embryos is critical for future breeding strategies. Therefore, future studies employing advanced quantitative assays or long-read sequencing, as well as embryo transfer to verify human ALB protein expression in the serum of live-born calves, will be necessary to accurately determine the allelic status and confirm the full-length KI events.

Conclusion

We assessed the efficiency and practicality of electroporation-based strategies for delivering donor DNA to achieve KI in bovine embryos. Among the tested methods, AAV-mediated donor delivery demonstrated more consistent KI efficiency than ssODNs. We further optimized this approach by demonstrating that 500 bp homology arms tended to yield a higher Knock-in BL frequency compared with 200 bp homology arms, although the difference was not statistically significant. Using these optimized conditions, we successfully performed site-specific insertion of an approximately 2.2 kb sequence containing the human albumin CDS into the endogenous bovine ALB locus in in vitro-produced embryos. These results establish a foundational platform for generating transgenic embryos, advancing the efforts toward producing cattle capable of expressing human albumin in place of bovine albumin in their blood. Future research will focus on embryo transfer and the production of live calves carrying this site-specific, large-fragment KI.
