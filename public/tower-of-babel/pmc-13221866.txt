Mycotic Pseudoaneurysm of Saphenous Vein Graft
Authors: Adnan Halboni, Asem Ayyad, Asrar Abdou, Fares Sukhon, Pedro Villablanca, James Lee, Henry Kim, Khaldoon Alaswad, Dimitrios Apostolou, Mohammad Al-Qarqaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMycotic aortocoronary saphenous vein graft (SVG) aneurysms and pseudoaneurysms are rare, with variable clinical presentations.Case SummaryA 73-year-old man with a history of coronary artery bypass surgery 23 years prior presented with lateral ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and bacteremia. Echocardiography, invasive coronary angiography, and computed tomography angiography identified a large mycotic pseudoaneurysm arising from the vein graft distal anastomosis site to the obtuse marginal artery. Ensuing hemodynamic compromise resulted from mixed septic and cardiogenic shock, requiring mechanical circulatory support followed by surgical ligation and excision of the pseudoaneurysm.DiscussionMycotic aneurysms and pseudoaneurysms of the SVG to coronary arteries should be considered in patients with prior coronary bypass surgery presenting with cardiac symptoms and signs of active infection.Take-Home MessagesMycotic aneurysms and pseudoaneurysms of the SVG are a rare entity and may occur from extracardiac infectious sources. Multimodality imaging and multidisciplinary team discussion is essential for diagnosis and management.

History of Presentation

The patient was a 73-year-old man who had previously undergone coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) with left internal mammary artery (LIMA) to the left anterior descending artery, sequential saphenous vein graft (SVG) to the ramus and obtuse marginal (OM) arteries, and SVG to a right coronary artery that was known to be occluded, as well as a recent lower extremity endovascular intervention. He presented to an outside hospital emergency department with reports of chest pain and left leg pain for 2 days. On initial physical examination, the patient demonstrated erythematous, tender skin changes in the medial left thigh region consistent with cellulitis. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated leukocytosis and initial high-sensitivity troponin level of 30 ng/L (reference: <19 ng/L), which subsequently increased to >9,000 ng/L. Standard medical treatment of acute coronary syndrome was initiated, and emergent coronary angiography was pursued. The left main and right coronary arteries were found to be chronically occluded, the LIMA to left anterior descending artery was patent, the SVG to the right coronary artery was chronically occluded, and the sequential SVG to the ramus and OM was not adequately opacified and was presumed to be occluded distally beyond the ramus anastomosis site. Given progressive hemodynamic compromise, a percutaneous axial mechanical circulatory support (MCS) device was placed, no coronary intervention was performed, and the patient was transferred to our tertiary care center for further management. The patient was intubated before transfer owing to respiratory failure.

Past Medical History

Our patient had undergone CABG 23 years prior and had preserved left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction at 55%. He had extensive peripheral artery disease with numerous prior interventions, the most recent at around 2 weeks before presentation which included balloon angioplasty and atherectomy of the left superficial femoral artery. The patient also had a history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and active tobacco use.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included acute myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock, mechanical complications related to myocardial infarction, and septic shock given cellulitis and leukocytosis.

Investigations

Upon arrival at our hospital, repeat coronary angiography was performed given inadequate visualization of the sequential SVG to the ramus and OM arteries. Selective SVG angiography showed a patent graft only to the ramus artery, however the distal part of the sequential graft to the OM branch drained into a large aneurysmal space on the lateral border of the heart (Figures 2A and 2B, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3). Echocardiography demonstrated mildly reduced LV ejection fraction (40%), mitral posterior leaflet tethering with severe mitral regurgitation, and a large fluid collection compressing the left atrium and basal LV anterolateral wall at the atrioventricular groove (Figure 3, Video 4, Video 5, Video 6). Management

The patient was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit and was started on broad-spectrum antibiotics, still requiring hemodynamic support with vasopressors and MCS. Subsequent blood cultures grew methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) bacteria. The cardiac surgery team evaluated the patient, and he was taken for urgent surgical repair. The patient was found to have a large ruptured mycotic pseudoaneurysm of the SVG to the OM artery. Ligation and excision of the mycotic pseudoaneurysm with repair with bovine pericardium was performed via left thoracotomy (Figures 5A and 5B, Videos 8 and 9). Outcome and Follow-Up

Postoperatively, the patient continued to require MCS. He demonstrated intact neurological function and remained responsive when sedation was weaned. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed severe primary ischemic mitral regurgitation due to posterior leaflet tethering (Video 10). In the setting of persistent hemodynamic instability, pulmonary edema, and a complex, high-risk route to revascularization, a multidisciplinary team discussion was commenced to pursue a mitral valve transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (MV-TEER) given favorable approach and anatomy and reassured by multiple negative blood cultures. After MV-TEER, MCS was weaned and removed successfully. Ejection fraction improved to 54%, with moderate residual mitral regurgitation. Unfortunately, the patient's course was later complicated by recurrent septic shock secondary to intra-abdominal infection seen on computed tomography. He was progressively too unstable to undergo any surgical intervention and died almost 4 weeks after initial presentation.

Discussion

Aortocoronary SVG aneurysms are a rare complication after CABG and can present early—within a year of surgery—or after more than a decade. Mycotic aneurysm and pseudoaneurysm subsets are considerably rarer, with only a few cases reported in the literature. In a 2021 state-of-the-art review by Vinciguerra et al,1 only 4 of the 368 SVG aneurysms found in the literature from 1974 to 2019 were of mycotic etiology (3 true aneurysms and 1 pseudoaneurysm). We identified 3 additional reports of mycotic aneurysms in the literature, attesting to the rarity of this condition.2, 3, 4 Despite an incomplete understanding of the etiology of aneurysmal dilatation of SVG, several mechanisms have been proposed; some of them contribute to the early formation of aneurysms, such as surgical technique and mechanical tension forces, while others may contribute to their late formation, such as atherosclerotic changes and mechanical weakness of the vessel wall, possibly secondary to disruption of the vasa vasorum.1,5 Clinically, SVG aneurysms span a wide spectrum of presentations ranging from an incidental finding on imaging, angina, myocardial infarction, or, more dramatically, shock or rupture and tamponade.1,5 Patients with mycotic aneurysm may also present with signs of infection or septic shock. A multimodality diagnostic and imaging approach using echocardiography, computed tomography angiography, and invasive angiography is essential to establish the diagnosis, evaluate for potential complications, provide crucial anatomic information including relation to neighboring structures, and guide management.

In our case, the patient presented acutely with lateral ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) that progressed to shock and multiorgan failure. The initial etiology of the shock was likely the STEMI, as evident by an elevated LV end-diastolic pressure of 22 mm Hg on the initial left heart catheterization. Despite MCS, the patient remained in shock, and a right heart catheterization performed at the time of the repeat angiography demonstrated a right atrial pressure of 13 mm Hg, a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 12 mm Hg, a cardiac output of 4.4 L/min, and a cardiac index of 2.34 L/min/m2, and in the setting of severe leukocytosis of 23.5 K/μL (3.8-10.6 K/μL), this supported an etiology of mixed cardiogenic and septic shock. The involvement of the OM artery and posterior mitral leaflet tethering evident on transesophageal echocardiography resulted in primary ischemic severe mitral regurgitation, which further worsened the hemodynamic compromise. Tissue culture from the pseudoaneurysm demonstrating MSSA, which is a common skin pathogen, identified the most likely source to be cellulitis and bacteremia complicating a recent endovascular lower extremity intervention. Although the source in our case is unique, and to our knowledge has not been described before, infection sources in mycotic SVG aneurysms are varied; surgical site infections are commonly described, and other sources of bacteremia (such as gastrointestinal or catheter-related infections) have been reported.2, 3, 4,6, 7, 8, 9 These reports reinforce the diversity of this pathology and the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion even in a nontypical presentation.

One novelty in our approach to this case was the use of MCS to optimize hemodynamics until definitive therapies were delivered. This advantage may not have been widely available in prior descriptions of SVG aneurysm management, especially in the setting of hemodynamic compromise, and must certainly be considered in similar clinical settings as a temporary bridge to more definitive interventions.

Management of SVG aneurysms and pseudoaneurysms largely depends on clinical presentation, acuity, and patient characteristics. However, in the case of mycotic pseudoaneurysm or hemodynamic compromise, surgery remains the mainstay of treatment.

Conclusions

Aortocoronary SVG mycotic aneurysms and pseudoaneurysms are very rare, with variable clinical presentation and duration from index surgery. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients with prior bypass grafting who present with acute coronary syndrome, hemodynamic instability, and signs of infection or shock. A multimodality imaging approach is critical for accurate diagnosis and anatomical characterization; multidisciplinary heart team collaboration is crucial in managing this condition and its complications, such as heart failure and valvular disease.
