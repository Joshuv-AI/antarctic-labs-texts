Single-molecule electrical characterization of photoinduced aggregation evolution
Authors: Rongqin Zhu, Xiaoyan Xu, Chenzi Li, Xuanying Chen, Ihor Sahalianov, Glib V Baryshnikov, Rui Wang, Hao Sun, Hans Ågren, Qi Zou, Liangliang Zhu, He Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElucidating the structural evolution of molecules during photoinduced aggregation remains a central challenge in molecular science, particularly in solution where aggregation processes occur dynamically. Here, we present an electrical strategy to characterize the evolution of photoinduced aggregation at the single-molecule level. Using persulfurated arenes as a model system, photoexcitation enhances intermolecular interactions that drive molecular assembly and induce conformational stabilization within aggregated species once the irradiation is halted. These stabilized conformations generate distinct ratiometric conductance signatures that can be reliably detected using scanning tunneling microscope-break junction measurements. Time-dependent irradiation experiments reveal the progressive evolution of aggregation, enabling electrical monitoring of structural changes during the assembly process. Complementary theoretical analyses further reveal the correlation between molecular geometry and conductance response. This work establishes a direct link between photoinduced aggregation evolution and single-molecule conductance, providing an electrical perspective for investigating structural dynamics in photoresponsive molecular assemblies.Subject terms: Excited states, Photochemistry, Molecular electronics

Introduction

Light can induce structural reorganization within numerous π-conjugated molecules and further modulate intermolecular interactions, often leading to photoinduced aggregation and related excited-state phenomena such as aggregation-induced emission1–4. Such processes critically influence the photophysical behavior of functional organic materials and have been widely exploited in the design of optoelectronic devices5–7 and photoresponsive materials8–10. In particular, photoexcitation-induced aggregation (PEIA) provides a versatile strategy for dynamically controlling molecular conformation and self-assembly using light11,12. To date, photo-induced aggregation has been primarily investigated using ensemble spectroscopic techniques, including ultraviolet–visible absorption, photoluminescence, and transient spectroscopy. While these methods provide valuable information on excited-state populations and aggregate formation, they cannot directly resolve the evolving structural features or charge-transport behavior of individual molecules during the dynamic aggregation. Meanwhile, structural characterization approaches such as electron microscopy or Raman spectroscopy are generally restricted to solid-state or crystalline samples, making them difficult to apply to dynamically evolving aggregates in solution. As a result, resolving photoinduced aggregation at the single-molecule level and elucidating its influence on molecular charge transport remain central yet unresolved challenges.

Acquiring single-molecule electrical signals that faithfully reflect molecular geometries is crucial for elucidating aggregation dynamics. The scanning tunneling microscope break junction (STM-BJ) technique, which enables repeated formation of single-molecule junctions and real-time conductance measurements in solution, offers a sensitive way to correlate molecular structure with electrical and related charge transport properties13–21. Previous studies have successfully applied the STM-BJ technique to probe photoresponsive molecules22,23, such as azobenzene and diarylethylenes (Supplementary Table 1)24,25, where conductance variations originate from distinct structural differences between individual photoisomers. More recently, the technique has been extended to study supramolecular systems26,27. However, its application to photoexcitation-induced aggregation remains largely unexplored. To breakthrough this limitation, we hypothesize that hexa(thioaryl)benzenes represent a promising molecular scaffold due to their unique rotatable C-S-C bonds, which ideally lead to pronounced conformational differences between monomers and various aggregated species (Fig. 1a)11,12,28–34. Photoexcitation induces backbone twisting and strengthens intermolecular interactions, facilitating molecular motion, collision, and aggregation35–39. Upon de-excitation, the aggregated state can spontaneously dissociate in solution, but can be delayed by creating metastable states through multiple non-covalent interactions38,40. These characteristics make hexa(thioaryl)benzenes an ideal model for probing the structural evolution during PEIA at the single-molecule level using electrical measurements.

To make use of this opportunity, we designed a dipyridinyl-hexa(thioaryl)benzene derivative (HTB-2Py) as a representative model, the end pyridyl groups of which can help bind gold electrodes in STM-BJ measurements. Meanwhile, the compound exerts PEIA with the ability of conformational locking of photo-regulated geometries due to enhanced multiple non-covalent interactions28–30. Upon this design, the photoexcitation-induced aggregates formed under varying irradiation durations exhibit robust molecular conformational locking, which can persist for over 24 h once the irradiation is halted, and is sufficient in time scale for single-molecule conductance signal acquisition and reading (normally 30–40 min). Consequently, the dynamic single-molecule conductance can be interpreted as electrical readouts that correlate with molecular conformational differences specific to each distinct aggregation state (Fig. 1b). Using the STM-BJ technique, we observed a ratiometric dual conductance characteristic, namely, a high-conductance (HC) signal and a low-conductance (LC) one in the dark. Upon continuous 365 nm light irradiation, the HC signal remained stable, while the conductance of the LC state shifted from ~10−4.02 G0 to ~10−4.69 G0. Time-dependent measurements revealed a progressive aggregation process, and concentration-dependent studies further elucidated the kinetics of conformational change. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further indicate that the dihedral torsion change upon photoexcitation leads to the localization of molecular frontier orbitals and the enhancement of electron scattering, thereby suppressing charge transport efficiency through junctions. This work establishes a direct correlation between photoinduced aggregation behavior and single-molecule conductance responses, using dihedral-angle torsion as a key structural descriptor, thereby providing a new electrical approach for investigating photoresponsive behavior at the molecular level.

Results and discussion

Molecular design

To elucidate the structural evolution during PEIA at the single-molecule level, we designed HTB-2Py, incorporating two para-pyridyl groups as terminal anchoring moieties (Figs. 1a and 2b). For comparison, 4-(phenylthio)pyridine (PTP), tetrafluorodipyridyl-di(thioaryl)benzene (4F2Py), hexa(thioaryl)benzene (HTB), and 1-(amino)hexa(thioaryl)benzene (HTB-NH2) were also synthesized and employed as control compounds to fabricate single-molecule junctions (Fig. 2). Detailed synthetic procedures and chemical structural characterizations are also provided in the Supplementary Figs. 1–5. The design here fully considers the PEIA ability, the conformational locking brought by the introduction of end groups, and the variant dual-conductance characteristic caused by multiple anchoring sites, which responds to molecular conformational change. HTB-2Py is eventually found as an optimized molecule for its efficient PEIA followed by molecular conformational locking ability, which can be monitored by the change in single-molecule dual conductance ratio (vide infra), and the dynamic evolution can exhibit a temporal depth. Hirshfeld surface analysis of the crystal packing of HTB-2Py highlights the rationality of the existing multiple intermolecular interactions, including C–H···π, C–H···N, and C–H···S hydrogen bonds (Fig. 2b), and detailed crystallographic data are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns of HTB-2Py samples prepared from the corresponding solution before and after 365 nm light irradiation display distinct differences, indicating a crystal phase change (Supplementary Fig. 6), which further proves the possibility of molecular conformational alteration. The photo-regulated conformations are expected to be retained in the aggregated state via a kinetically trapping effect and can be captured using the STM-BJ technique (Fig. 1b).

Photoexcitation-induced aggregation characterization

To monitor the possible photoinduced aggregation process, we performed dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) experiments. DLS data confirmed the size evolution of HTB-2Py in 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene (TCB) upon UV irradiation and demonstrated the reversibility of the aggregation process upon long-term dark storage (~24 h, Fig. 3a). Such a long self-recovery process indicates a molecular conformational locking effect, which is sufficient in time scale for the following single-molecule conductance signal acquisition and reading (normally 30–40 min). Signals below 1 nm are within the lower detection limit of the DLS instrument and may originate from noise or background interference, which were not considered in further analysis. TEM images further revealed that well-dispersed molecules without discernible morphology (Fig. 3b) aggregated into clusters exceeding 100 nm in morphological size following 365 nm irradiation (Fig. 3c).

The UV–vis absorption spectra of HTB-2Py in TCB exhibits prominent band at approximately 304 nm (Fig. 3d). Irradiation at 365 nm leads to the emergence of new band between 375 nm and 475 nm, whose intensity gradually increase and reach a photostationary state within ~30 s. Concurrently, a pronounced enhancement in emission spanning 420–550 nm is observed (Fig. 3e), accompanied by cyan-blue luminescence. These findings are consistent with previously reported photoresponsive behaviors of hexa(thioaryl)benzene derivatives, namely, PEIA modulates the excited-state relaxation pathway28–30. Combining the change in the aggregation behavior mentioned above, these results confirm that HTB-2Py exhibits a typical photoexcitation-controlled aggregation-induced emission (AIE) behavior (PEIA-AIE)28,29,41,42.

Notably, no emission change was observed upon UV irradiation at 77 K (Supplementary Fig. 7), as cryogenic temperatures suppress molecular motion and thereby hinder the aggregation process and associated dynamic photophysical behavior29,30. Moreover, higher concentrations facilitated stronger intermolecular interactions and collisions, promoting both aggregation and emission enhancement under photoexcitation (monitored by greater emission enhancement, Supplementary Fig. 8). It is worth noting that TCB is a relatively optimized solvent for PEIA from the perspective of rapid emission enhancement (Supplementary Fig. 9). Not all solvent environments are suitable for PEIA of HTB-2Py, as an ideal solvent environment should be conducive to the differential solubility of initial state and twisted state12,29. For comparison, HTB-NH2 shows no obvious change in the UV–vis absorption spectra under identical irradiation conditions in TCB (Supplementary Fig. 10), indicating less PEIA efficiency under these conditions.

To elucidate the origin of the cyan-blue luminescence of HTB-2Py, photoluminescence (PL) lifetime measurements were conducted. The PL lifetime at 460 nm in TCB was determined to be 0.576 ns (Fig. 3f), confirming a fluorescence signal (rather than phosphorescence) as the primary emission mechanism. Femtosecond transient absorption (TA) spectra revealed that the singlet excited state (S1) was continually enhanced by prolonging the photoirradiation period, while the triplet excited state (T1) traded off (Supplementary Fig. 11), suggesting that S1 conformational change effectively hinders the intersystem crossing. Furthermore, the 1H NMR spectra recorded before and after 365 nm light irradiation showed no discernible chemical shift changes (Supplementary Fig. 12), supporting that PEIA of HTB-2Py is a purely conformation-based physical process without covalent bond transformation.

To gain mechanistic insights into the structural basis of PEIA, we analyzed the optimized geometries of HTB-2Py at different electronic states using density functional theory. The dominant conformational factor variation is the characteristic dihedral torsion C1-C2-S-C3 that changes from 116.5° in the equilibrium geometry of initial state to 87.3° of twisted state (Fig. 1a). In addition, six representative hydrogen atoms (H1–H6) were selected to construct geometric reference points (Fig. 3g). In the twisted state, the connection of H1–H3 and H4–H6 forms two nearly parallel triangles relative to initial state (Fig. 3h), indicating enhanced conformational regularity upon photoexcitation. This geometric feature likely facilitates intermolecular packing in the twisted state, thus promoting photoinduced aggregation and contributing to the observed AIE effect. It is worth noting that this conformational analysis is purely theoretical, which highlights the need for experimental analysis at the single-molecule level (see below).

Single-molecule conductance investigations

To investigate the single-molecule charge transport properties of HTB-2Py, we performed single-molecule conductance measurements using the STM-BJ technique43,44. In a typical measurement, the STM tip is repeatedly driven in and out of contact with a gold substrate to form and break Au–molecule–Au junctions. Over 2000 individual conductance traces were collected (typically within 30–40 min) and compiled into two-dimensional (2D) conductance–displacement histograms (Fig. 4a–d) and one-dimensional (1D) conductance histograms (Fig. 4e). The conductance peak observed at G0 corresponds to a direct Au–Au point contact, where G0 = 2e2/h ≈ 77.5 μS45.

Single-molecule conductance characterization of HTB-2Py at 300 mV bias in 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene (0.1 mM in TCB).

a 2D conductance–displacement histograms in the dark. b 2D conductance–displacement histograms after 365 nm light irradiation for 10 s (indicative of low-level conformational distortion). c 2D conductance–displacement histograms after 365 nm light irradiation for 30 s (indicative of high-level conformational distortion). d 2D conductance–displacement histograms after 24 h recovery. e 1D conductance histograms of HTB-2Py recorded after varying durations of 365 nm light irradiation (0 s, 10 s, and 30 s), and following recovery in the dark (24 hours).

We first measured the single-molecule conductance of HTB-2Py at a concentration of 0.1 mM in the dark. As shown in Fig. 4a, two distinct conductance plateaus were observed, corresponding to an HC state at ~10−2.44 G0 and an LC state at ~10−4.02 G0. After accounting for a snap-back distance of ~0.5 nm46,47, the junction lengths agree well with the ground-state DFT-optimized structures of HTB-2Py (Supplementary Fig. 13). To determine the origin of the HC state, control experiments were performed using structurally related compounds PTP and HTB under identical conditions. Both control molecules exhibited conductance values centered around 10−2.44 G0 (Supplementary Figs. 14, 15), consistent with the HC state of HTB-2Py. The plateau lengths for the HC state and the two control molecules were all ~0.10 nm (Supplementary Figs. 14c, 15c). These results indicate that the HC state likely arises from two junction configurations (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 13): (i) binding of the gold electrodes to the nitrogen atom of the pyridine ring and an adjacent sulfur atom (Type 1), or (ii) simultaneous binding to the para-substituted sulfur atoms of the central benzene ring (Type 2)48. To elucidate the origin of the LC state, additional control experiments were conducted using 4F2Py and HTB-NH2, which also displayed two conductance plateaus (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17). These correspond to the similar HC and LC states observed in HTB-2Py. After considering the snap-back distance of 0.5 nm, the corresponding plateau lengths (Supplementary Figs. 16c, 17c) align with the DFT-optimized junction lengths (Supplementary Figs. 16g–i, 17g–i). This suggests that the LC state of HTB-2Py originates from a junction analogous to that of HTB-NH2, where one electrode binds to the nitrogen atom and the other to a para-substituted sulfur atom (Type 3).

To investigate the conductance of HTB-2Py after photoirradiation, we employed ex situ 365 nm light irradiation to trigger the PEIA effect, followed by single-molecule conductance measurements under ambient conditions, where the twisted-state conformations are effectively locked during the measurement timescale. With increasing irradiation time, the LC state gradually undergoes a significant conductance shift from ~10−4.02 G0 to ~10−4.69 G0, while the HC state remains nearly unchanged (Fig. 4). Notably, the 2D conductance–displacement histograms for the LC state exhibited similarly inclined conductance plateaus, and the corresponding full width at half maximum (FWHM) observed in the 1D conductance histograms remains nearly constant, indicating that the junction formation and elongation processes do not significantly alter the intramolecularly twisted geometries in aggregations49,50. The junction lengths after UV irradiation are consistent with those measured in the dark and with the DFT-optimized twisted-state geometries of HTB-2Py (Supplementary Fig. 18), indicating that the anchoring sites remain unchanged after UV exposure. The decrease in LC values highlights a gradual evolution of molecular conformations upon the aggregation degree during the PEIA process (see also the dynamic conductance data with more irradiation time intervals in Supplementary Fig. 19). Notably, the original LC state at ~10–4.02 G0 is restored in the dark for 24 h (Fig. 4d), suggesting reversibility (i.e., dissociation) of the aggregation process. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the conductance modulation arises from reversible, aggregation-induced conformational twisting.

To further validate the proposed junction configurations, we conducted flicker noise analysis using power spectral density (PSD) measurements51. Flicker noise is sensitive to electrode-molecule-electrode interactions, and it is analyzed by normalizing the noise power to Gn. Through-bond coupled junctions have a characteristic n value around 1, while through-space coupled junctions have an n value close to 252,53. The scaling exponents for the HC and LC states in the dark were determined to be 1.10 and 1.22, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 20a, b), indicating that all junctions with different anchoring types are dominated by through-bond transport. After 30 s of UV irradiation, the LC state exhibited a slightly increased scaling exponent of 1.27 (Supplementary Fig. 20c), which shows consistent charge transport behavior with the dark-state measurements, indicating that the charge transport remains through-bond dominated upon photoexcitation.

To explore the kinetics of photoinduced aggregation evolution from a single-molecule perspective, we carried out conductance measurements of HTB-2Py at concentrations ranging from 0.01 mM to 1 mM (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 21, 22). Since the HC state is unaffected by photoexcitation, the conductance of the HC state can serve as an internal reference to enable a ratiometric (dual-parameter) analysis to improve the accuracy of the measurements under different concentration conditions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 23, under dark conditions, the HC/LC ratios remain nearly constant across all concentrations, underscoring the intrinsic stability of the dual-conductance signals. In contrast, upon 365 nm light irradiation, the HC/LC ratio increases significantly with both irradiation time and molecular concentration−rising from 33 to 72 at 0.01 mM, and from 34 to 380 at 1 mM as the exposure duration extends from 0 to 30 s. This temporal evolution further highlights that molecular aggregation and conformational locking are both light- and concentration-dependent, with faster kinetics at higher concentrations.

Moreover, bias-dependent measurements confirm that both the HC and LC states exhibit relatively stable conductance values over a range of applied biases (Supplementary Fig. 24), further supporting that the observed conductance decrease is primarily governed by photoinduced conformational locking upon aggregation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the dual-conductance signatures obtained from STM-BJ measurements enable effective identification of conformationally locked molecular structures formed during photoinduced aggregation, providing an electrical readout of structural variations associated with different aggregation species.

Theoretical calculations

To gain deeper insight into the charge transport mechanisms of HTB-2Py in single-molecule junctions, we performed DFT calculations combined with nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) formalism using the QuantumATK package54,55. As presented in Supplementary Fig. 25, the calculated transmission coefficients for the proposed junction configurations (Type 1–Type 3) exhibit good agreement with the experimentally observed conductance values. Although the experimentally dominant LC state was assigned to Type 3 configurations, we considered an alternative scenario (Type 4) in which both pyridine moieties serve as anchoring sites to the gold electrodes. To evaluate this possibility, we modeled a molecular junction with Au–N coordination bonds formed at both pyridine termini. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 25b, the resulting transmission of Type 4 displays an extremely low conductance (~10−5.8 G0), that is below the measurable detection threshold. The low conductance of Type 4 is more reasonably attributed to its extended transport pathway. In addition, considering that DFT-based transport calculations often overestimate conductance relative to experimental measurements56, this configuration would not contribute appreciably to the measurable conductance signals and is therefore not expected to be experimentally observable. The transmission curve of twisted state for the Type 3 configuration shows a remarkable decrease near the Fermi level compared to the initial state (Fig. 5a), indicating suppressed charge transport in the twisted state. This reduction originates from significant conformational distortions, including a decrease in key dihedral angles: C1-C2-S-C3 decreases from 116.5° in the initial state to 87.3° in the twisted state (see Fig. 1a), disrupting π-conjugation and thereby reducing charge delocalization.

To test the generality of this conformational locking mechanism, we extended the theoretical studies to several relevant systems with photoexcitation-induced conformational variability (Fig. 5b, c). For all these systems, twisted-state geometry optimizations revealed pronounced torsional distortions relative to the initial structures (Supplementary Fig. 26). Transmission spectra further showed that decreasing torsional angles lead to a decrease in conductance for the corresponding twisted conformations (Fig. 5c). Once such twisted-state geometries are conformationally locked in a certain solvent environment, STM-BJ measurements can thus resolve the corresponding dihedral angles. These results demonstrate that it is possible to generalize this approach and highlight its potential for analyzing conformational evolution within aggregations at the single-molecule level.

To further elucidate the influence of conformational twisting on charge transport, we calculated the molecular projected self-consistent Hamiltonian (MPSH) orbitals, as presented in Fig. 5d. In the initial state, the highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) are delocalized across the entire π-conjugated skeleton, including contributions from the p-orbitals of the sulfur atoms and the π-orbitals of the benzene rings, indicating efficient orbital overlap and charge transport. In contrast, the twisted state shows localization of both the HOMO and LUMO on the central benzene core, reflecting a breakdown to some extent of the extended conjugation network and diminished charge transport capability. Additionally, we analyzed the transmission pathways of HTB-2Py in both initial and twisted states (Fig. 5e). In the initial state, electron transport proceeds smoothly along the π-framework with minimal backscattering, as indicated by the predominance of forward-directed (highlighted in blue) transmission channels. Conversely, in the twisted state, the transmission pathway is disrupted by enhanced scattering and reflection (highlighted in red), correlating with the observed conductance suppression. These results collectively support the experimentally observed conductance decrease, confirming that conformational locking stabilizes a twisted molecular geometry that reduces planarity, restricts π-conjugation, thereby impairs charge transport through the molecular junction.

In conclusion, we have developed a single-molecule strategy to resolve photoinduced aggregation evolution through the combination of conformational stabilization with STM-BJ technique. Upon photoexcitation-induced aggregation, the model hexa(thioaryl)benzene molecule bearing tailored anchoring groups forms gradually twisted geometries to enable electrical readout of a structurally locked conformation via single-molecule conductance. Accordingly, we observed a distinct ratiometric dual-conductance behavior along with a photo-induced conformational dynamics, characterized by a HC and a LC state in the dark. Upon continuous 365 nm irradiation, the HC state remained stable, whereas the LC state shifted from ~10⁻4.02 G0 to ~10⁻4.69 G0. The resulting conductance signatures faithfully preserve structural characteristics associated with the photoinduced aggregation evolution, enabling electrical readout of molecular conformations. This work establishes a mechanistic foundation for analyzing dynamic structural evolution in photoresponsive assemblies and demonstrates the broader potential of single-molecule conductance measurements to probe complex molecular transformations.

Methods

Synthesis and characterization

Unless otherwise stated, all the chemicals were obtained from a commercial supplier and used without further purification. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analyses were performed on silica-gel plates, and the flash chromatography was carried out using silica-gel columns. 1H and 13C NMR spectra were obtained using a Bruker AM-400 MHz and an Ascend-600 MHz instrument, using tetramethylsilane as an internal standard. Chemical shifts and coupling constants (J) were given in ppm and Hz, respectively. High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) spectra were carried out on a Waters Xevo G2-XS TOF mass spectrometer. The X-ray diffraction intensity data were collected at 170 K on a Rigaku RAXIS RAPID IP imaging plate system with MoKα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å). The crystal was grown by slowly diffusing n-heptane into the solution of HTB-2Py in ethyl acetate. The CIF files for crystallographic date have been uploaded in Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre, and the CCDC numbers are listed in Supplementary Table 2 (See http://www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk.). Powder X-ray diffraction data were obtained using a Rotating Anode X-ray Powder diffractometer (18KW/D/max2550VB/PC) at room temperature.

Photophysical characterizations

A TL-3201LED fluorescence microscope (equipped with a 20MP USB3.0 digital camera) was used for the fluorescence microscopy measurements. An artificial manufactured microscope thermal plate with the temperature console WT-4000H (Wei-Tu Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) was used to heat samples and sustain ambient temperature during the characterization.

Single-molecule conductance measurements

Single-molecule conductance measurements were carried out using the X-tech STM-BJ instrument, and the data were analyzed using XMe open-source code, available at https://github.com/Pilab-XMU/XMe_DataAnalysis. The gold tips were fabricated using the gold wires (99.999%, 0.25 mm diameter), which were melted into beads and subsequently mounted onto the piezo. The gold was evaporated on a silicon wafer to fabricate the substrate, which was placed below the tips. In the STM-BJ measurement, the gold tip is firstly controlled by a stepper motor to get to the approximate position to contact the substrate. The bias was kept at a fixed value and the real-time conductance was recorded using an I-V converter with a sampling rate of 20 kHz.

Computational methodology

All electronic structures in this work were computed using the Gaussian 16 suite of the program. The initial-state structures were optimized at the DFT level in a gas phase using the B3LYP functional with the basis set of 6–31 + G(d,p)57,58. The twisted-state structures were obtained via TD-DFT using a tight convergence criterion. To calculate the transmission of these molecules, the optimized molecular structures were placed between the two gold electrodes to construct the single-molecule devices, in which the surface of the electrode was cut into a pyramid structure and the anchor group was tied to the gold atom at the top of the pyramid. In the initial configuration of the device, the N–Au distance was controlled at about 2.25 Å, and the S–Au distance was controlled at about 2.5 Å. To accelerate the calculations, the left gold electrodes were fixed, the optimized molecular structures and the right gold electrodes were kept rigid, and device optimization focused on the adsorption geometry between the electrodes and the anchoring groups. The optimization and transmission functions of the single-molecule device were calculated using GGA and PBE functional with the NEGF approach in Atomistix Tool Kit (ATK) package54,55,59,60. The geometries of single-molecule devices were optimized in a gas atmosphere with k-points sampling (2 × 2 × 132) with a force threshold of 0.05 eV/Å. The single-ζ polarized (SZP) basis set was used for the Au atoms, and the double-ζ polarized (DZP) basis set was used for other atoms, with a real-space grid defined with an equivalent energy cut-off of 75 Hartree.
