Secondary Pulmonary Alveolar Proteinosis Associated With Ruxolitinib After Bone‐Marrow Transplantation: A Case With Transient Improvement Following Drug Discontinuation
Authors: Taiki Yanagi, Maki Asami‐Noyama, Kaoru Yamamoto, Yoshinobu Hoshii, Yasushi Ichiyama, Masatoshi Okimura, Tetsuya Fujii, Junki Suizu, Misa Harada, Keita Murakawa, Kazuki Hamada, Toshiaki Utsunomiya, Yoriyuki Murata, Keiji Oishi, Nobutaka Edakuni, Tomoyuki Kakugawa, Tsunahiko Hirano, Kazuto Matsunaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRuxolitinib is a Janus kinase (JAK) 1/2 inhibitor used to treat steroid‐refractory graft‐versus‐host disease, wherein pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) is a rare complication. We report the case of a 47‐year‐old woman developing respiratory failure 8 months after commencing ruxolitinib treatment. Chest computed tomography revealed bilateral ground‐glass opacities. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid was milky and periodic acid–Schiff–positive, and Mycobacterium intracellulare was detected. Anti‐granulocyte–macrophage colony‐stimulating factor antibodies were negative, and the patient was diagnosed with secondary PAP. Ruxolitinib withdrawal led to initial radiological and clinical improvement, prior to initiating antimycobacterial therapy. To date, only three cases of ruxolitinib‐associated secondary PAP have been reported, all complicated by nontuberculous mycobacterial infection. This case represents a unique clinical course of ruxolitinib‐associated secondary PAP, wherein initial radiological and clinical improvements occurred following drug withdrawal, prior to antimycobacterial therapy. This case highlights the need to consider PAP as a rare complication in patients receiving ruxolitinib.Keywords: graft‐versus‐host disease, Janus kinase inhibitor, pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, ruxolitinib, secondary pulmonary alveolar proteinosis

Introduction

Ruxolitinib is a Janus kinase (JAK) 1/2 inhibitor approved for the treatment of myelofibrosis, polycythemia vera and steroid‐refractory graft‐versus‐host disease (GVHD). Although haematological toxicities and opportunistic infections associated with ruxolitinib treatment have been well‐documented, reports of pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) are exceedingly rare. Herein, we present a case of secondary PAP (SPAP) associated with ruxolitinib, in which radiological and clinical improvements were observed following drug discontinuation.

Case Report

A 47‐year‐old woman with a 7‐year history of peripheral T‐cell lymphoma presented with respiratory failure. Three years previously, she had undergone an unrelated‐donor bone‐marrow transplant for a relapse, and her lymphoma remained in remission. However, she developed persistent gastrointestinal GVHD, which was managed with prednisolone (6 mg daily) and tacrolimus. Ruxolitinib was initiated 8 months before admission for GVHD. Three months before admission, she developed exertional dyspnea, which gradually worsened, and 3 days before hospitalization, she experienced increased fatigue and was brought to the emergency department.

On arrival, her oxygen saturation was 92% at 5 L/min via a mask. A pre‐symptom onset chest computed tomography (CT) detected no remarkable ground‐glass opacities (Figure 1A). On arrival, chest CT revealed bilateral geographic ground‐glass opacities with a partial crazy‐paving pattern (Figure 1B). These opacities were devoid of nodular or centrilobular components and showed a bilateral distribution with predominance in the subpleural regions, compatible with PAP. Laboratory tests revealed elevated KL‐6 (4277 U/mL), C‐reactive protein (6.39 mg/dL) and lactate dehydrogenase (387 U/L) levels, with a normal white blood cell count (7050/μL) and β‐D‐glucan levels. Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) yielded milky fluid (Figure 2A). Cytology revealed foamy macrophages against a background of periodic acid–Schiff (PAS)‐positive staining (Figure 2B), and Papanicolaou staining revealed a pale finely granular material with light‐green affinity (Figure 2C). The degree of foamy change was moderate, and no significant nuclear atypia of alveolar macrophages was observed. Although quantitative BAL fluid (BALF) analyses, including differential cell counts, Giemsa or Diff‐Quick staining and histopathological confirmation, were not available, the macroscopic appearance and cytological findings, including PAS‐positive and Papanicolaou‐stained amorphous material, were considered to be compatible with PAP.

Polymerase chain reaction analyses were negative for Pneumocystis jirovecii but positive for cytomegalovirus (CMV). However, blood CMV antigenemia was negative, and no cytopathological cells indicative of CMV infection were identified in BALF cytology, indicating the low likelihood of CMV pneumonia. Bacterial cultures of the BALF were also negative. Serum anti‐GM‐CSF antibody levels were below the detection limit (< 0.3 U/mL; reference range < 1.7 U/mL).

SPAP was suspected, and ruxolitinib was accordingly discontinued. Initial differential diagnoses included CMV pneumonia, Pneumocystis pneumonia and organizing pneumonia; consequently, ganciclovir, therapeutic‐dose trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole and prednisolone 1 mg/kg were started. After PAP was confirmed, ganciclovir was discontinued, prednisolone was tapered to 10 mg, and trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole was adjusted for prophylactic dosing.

On Day 16, follow‐up CT revealed improvement in the ground‐glass opacities, although new consolidation with contractile changes had appeared in the right lower lobe (Figure 1C). Transbronchial lung biopsy on Day 18 revealed Masson bodies without organisms, consistent with organizing pneumonia.

The patient's oxygenation improved, and she was weaned off oxygen on Day 22. Although Mycobacterium intracellulare had been detected in BALF samples from the initial and second bronchoscopies, treatment was deferred in the absence of corresponding CT findings. Follow‐up CT on Day 29 demonstrated persistent ground‐glass opacities with a reduction in consolidation. No nodular or centrilobular components were observed.

During hospitalization, gastrointestinal GVHD worsened following the cessation of ruxolitinib but improved in response to an increase in the prednisolone dose to 15 mg daily. The patient was discharged on Day 36. The clinical course during hospitalization is summarized in Figure 3. However, although she remained stable for several months, approximately 6 months after the initial hospitalization, she was re‐hospitalized for worsening respiratory failure. Chest CT revealed progression of the bilateral alveolar opacities, although owing to a high risk of GVHD flare, the prednisolone dose could only be tapered from 15 to 12 mg daily.

Segmental lung lavage was performed twice via bronchoscopy. However, although the initial lavage resulted in mild radiological improvement, her respiratory status deteriorated after the second procedure and no further lavage was performed.

Given the continued isolation of Mycobacterium intracellulare , antimycobacterial therapy with clarithromycin and ethambutol was initiated, and the patient was discharged on long‐term oxygen therapy.

Discussion

PAP can be classified as autoimmune (APAP), SPAP or congenital. Most cases are APAP, characterized by anti‐GM‐CSF autoantibodies, whereas these antibodies are lacking in SPAP, for which the underlying conditions include haematological malignancies, infections or drug exposure [1]. In the present case, typical imaging and BALF findings, together with negative anti‐GM‐CSF antibodies and chronic GVHD, tended to indicate SPAP. However, the diagnosis of SPAP in this case is based on clinical and cytological findings and lacks quantitative BALF data, Giemsa or Diff‐Quick staining and histopathological confirmation.

To date, only three cases of ruxolitinib‐associated SPAP have been reported: one of a patient treated for GVHD following allogeneic stem cell transplantation [2] and two of patients with primary myelofibrosis [3, 4]. All three cases were complicated by nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infection and managed with a combination of ruxolitinib discontinuation and antimycobacterial therapy. However, although the patient with GVHD survived, both patients with myelofibrosis died.

For the patient reported here, who also had post‐transplant GVHD, there were initial radiological and clinical improvements following the withdrawal of ruxolitinib before antimycobacterial therapy initiation, a feature that has not previously been described. Although Mycobacterium intracellulare was repeatedly isolated from BALF, there were no confirmatory radiological findings typical of NTM pulmonary disease, such as nodular or branching opacities, at the initial or relapse presentation. The early improvement before commencing antimycobacterial therapy indicated that NTM infection was unlikely to have been the primary factor contributing to PAP. However, the possibility that NTM infection contributed to PAP development or exacerbation cannot be excluded.

The co‐occurrence of SPAP and NTM infections may reflect the ruxolitinib‐associated impairment of alveolar macrophage function. Granulocyte–macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF) signalling via JAK2 is essential for the terminal differentiation and surfactant‐processing capacity of alveolar macrophages; in this regard, the inhibition of JAK2 by ruxolitinib can impair GM‐CSF‐mediated macrophage maturation, leading to a reduction in the catabolism of surfactant lipids and proteins, and subsequent intra‐alveolar accumulation of proteinaceous material characteristic of PAP. This mechanism would provide a biologically plausible explanation for ruxolitinib‐associated SPAP, and the associated immunosuppressive effect may predispose patients to opportunistic infections such as NTM. However, a causal relationship between ruxolitinib and SPAP cannot be definitively established, as multiple factors may have contributed, including post‐transplant immune dysregulation, possible changes in alveolar macrophage populations, corticosteroid exposure, tacrolimus administration and ruxolitinib treatment.

In contrast to previous observations, the patient described herein showed initial radiological and clinical improvements following ruxolitinib withdrawal, in the absence of any supplementary antimycobacterial therapy or whole‐lung lavage at resolution. Although the dosage of prednisolone was increased to manage gastrointestinal GVHD, corticosteroids have been established to have an adverse effect on PAP by suppressing alveolar macrophage function [5]. In this case, corticosteroids may have had dual effects, potentially improving organizing pneumonia while adversely affecting PAP through the suppression of alveolar macrophage function.

This case represents a unique clinical course of ruxolitinib‐associated SPAP, which was characterized by initial improvement following drug discontinuation before antimycobacterial therapy. However, despite continued ruxolitinib withdrawal, the patient later experienced a recurrence of PAP. By suppressing alveolar macrophage function and impairing surfactant catabolism, corticosteroids have been established to exacerbate PAP [5], and we accordingly assume that these mechanisms were relevant in the present case. As tapering of prednisolone was difficult owing to gastrointestinal GVHD, persistent corticosteroid exposure may have contributed to a relapse via impaired macrophage‐mediated surfactant metabolism.

This case serves to emphasize the therapeutic dilemma and complexity of managing SPAP in patients with haematological disorders, for whom treatment decisions are often based on competing risks and limited options. When ruxolitinib‐treated patients present with dyspnea, a broad range of differential diagnoses, including opportunistic infections and PAP, should be considered, with bronchoscopy serving as a useful diagnostic tool.
