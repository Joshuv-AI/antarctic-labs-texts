Seasonal and Long-Term Underground Hydrogen Storage in Tight Reservoir: Hysteresis Effects and Scaling Assessment for Energy Transition at the Mae Moh Coal-Fired Power Plant
Authors: Nattirut Yingsirisit, Romal Ramadhan, Seyyed A. Hosseini, Honggeun Jo, Hung Vo Thanh, Nakorn Tippayawong, Suparit Tangparitkul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thailand’s largest coal-fired power plant at Mae Moh is transitioning toward renewable energy, yet no scalable storage solution is currently available. The current study assesses underground hydrogen storage (UHS) in the basin’s tight sandstone formation, emphasizing the critical but often overlooked role of hysteresis in governing multiphase flow. A representative geological model was developed and tested through 50-year reservoir-scale simulations to evaluate hydrogen plume migration, reservoir pressure evolution, and recovery under seasonal storage cycles. Results show that hysteresis effects in tight sandstone substantially reduce recovery, limiting the base case to 37% by the final cycle with significant pressure build-up. An improved dual-production well design enhanced pressure management and raised final-cycle recovery to 65%, which had not yet fully plateaued at the end of the 50-year horizon. Scaling analyses, however, indicate that the basin’s limited areal extent (<150 km2) is smaller than the ∼242 km2 required to meet regional demand. These findings indicate both the technical feasibility and spatial limitations of UHS in tight formations, offering a first-order basis for site selection and storage design.

Introduction

The global energy transition requires reducing fossil fuel reliance and associated carbon dioxide emissions. − Hydrogen is a promising energy carrier for this shift due to its zero-carbon combustion, high gravimetric energy density (∼120–142 kJ/g), and potential as a long-duration storage medium. However, its extremely low volumetric density (0.0082 kg/m3 at 25 °C) presents a major storage challenge compared to methane (0.657 kg/m3), , requiring roughly 2.5–3 times more volume to deliver equivalent energy. While existing technologies, e.g., high-pressure tanks, cryogenic storage, solid adsorption, and chemical carriers, are viable for small-scale use, − they fall short for grid-scale or seasonal storage. To enable large-scale hydrogen deployment, a more scalable and secure solution is needed. Underground Hydrogen Storage (UHS) has emerged as a leading candidate to meet this demand. ,

UHS involves the injection of hydrogen into subsurface formations, utilizing available pore space as a high-capacity storage medium. This makes UHS a promising solution for large-scale energy storage, particularly for accommodating seasonal mismatches between energy supply and demand. UHS operations typically comprise three stages: hydrogen injection during energy surplus periods, withdrawal during demand peaks, and an intermediate shut-in or ‘idle’ phase to monitor subsurface conditions. − In practice, surplus electricityoften generated from intermittent renewable sourcesis converted into hydrogen via electrolysis and stored underground until needed.

Geological formations suitable for UHS include salt caverns, depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs, and deep saline aquifers. ,, Salt caverns offer excellent sealing capacity and minimal hydrogen loss due to their impermeable structure; however, they are geographically limited and not widely available. Depleted hydrocarbon fields benefit from existing geological data and infrastructure, but typically contain residual hydrocarbons that may react with injected hydrogen, leading to potential losses and contamination. ,, In contrast, saline aquifers are broadly distributed and offer large pore volume, making them attractive candidates for large-scale, intermediate-term hydrogen storage. Nevertheless, key challenges persist, including subsurface geological heterogeneity, hydrogen losses from microbial consumption and capillary trapping during multiphase flow, alterations in porosity and permeability driven by geochemical reactions, and potential leakage associated with compromised caprock integrity. −

UHS operations in saline formations primarily involve complex multiphase fluid displacement processes. During injection, hydrogen displaces formation water through drainage process, whereas during withdrawal, hydrogen is produced from a water-saturated formation through imbibition process. , These alternating displacement mechanisms pose significant challenges due to the contrasting capillary and wetting behaviors involved, − and are central to the focus of the current study.

The Mae Moh Power Plant, Thailand’s largest coal-fired facility, has long supplied electricity to the northern region using lignite mined from the adjacent Mae Moh Basin. As Thailand advances its national energy transition goals, there is increasing pressure to phase down coal and replace it with renewable sources such as solar and biomass. However, the intermittent and seasonal nature of these sources raises concerns about their reliability for continuous power supply. This transition presents an opportunity to repurpose the Mae Moh Basin for UHS. With its subsurface formations, the basin could enable large-scale, seasonal energy storageallowing surplus renewable electricity to be converted into hydrogen and stored underground for later use. UHS at Mae Moh could thus help stabilize the grid while supporting regional decarbonization.

Subsurface assessments conducted in the context of carbon capture and storage have identified that the Mae Moh Basin is predominantly underlain by tight sandstone formations. These tight formations exhibit very low porosity and permeability, which significantly limit fluid mobility and pose operational challenges for subsurface energy storage applications. Despite these limitations, such formations may offer favorable containment due to their mechanical integrity and overlying seals. However, their capacity to support efficient hydrogen injection and withdrawalcritical for UHSremains uncertain. This raises an important technical challenge: can tight formations like those in the Mae Moh Basin be feasibly adapted for seasonal hydrogen storage?

Wang et al. simulated UHS in a tight reservoir (<1 mD) and found that high injection rates caused rapid pressure build-up, increased fracturing risks, and reduced storage efficiency. Plume migration in such systems was weakly influenced by gravity, as buoyancy effects were suppressed by small pore-throat sizes and poor vertical connectivity. In contrast, Ershadnia et al. investigated a high-porosity (∼20%) and high-permeability (∼100 mD) reservoir and observed strong buoyancy-driven plume rise toward the reservoir top. This contrast demonstrates the fundamental differences in storage behavior between tight and more permeable formations. Moreover, reservoir tightness amplifies hysteresis in fluid displacement. Kumbhat and Ebigbo showed that hysteresis lowers hydrogen mobility, disperses the plume, and increases brine production during early cycles, while Lysyy et al. reported that neglecting hysteresis can overestimate working capacity and recovery by 34% and 85%, respectively. When included, the recovery factor declined from 68 to 37%. Collectively, these studies highlight hysteresis as a critical control on UHS performance in tight formations, underscoring the need to explicitly account for it in simulation and design.

Despite rapid progress in UHS research, the coupled influence of reservoir tightness and flow hysteresis on long-term storage performance remains insufficiently characterized. Existing studies have predominantly focused on high-permeability systems, where buoyancy governs hydrogen migration, leaving uncertainties in pressure evolution, recovery behavior, and operational feasibility in tight formations. Understanding these interactions is essential to accurately evaluate UHS potential in low-permeability reservoirs and to guide the design of effective storage strategies.

The current study has two main objectives: (i) to assess the capacity and long-term performance of UHS in the Mae Moh tight sandstone formation using reservoir-scale simulations, and (ii) to design an annual operational plan for seasonal hydrogen storage as a prospective renewable replacement for the Mae Moh coal-fired power plant. Regional renewable energy resources were evaluated and converted into hydrogen-equivalent values to establish a representative seasonal storage profile. A numerical reservoir model was developed to examine hydrogen plume dynamics, reservoir pressure responses, and overall storage performance. To improve recovery, an alternative configuration incorporating a second production well was also investigated. Finally, the simulation results were scaled up to evaluate the basin’s potential to support seasonal UHS at regional demand levels.

Reservoir Simulation and Seasonal Storage Design

Geological Background of the Mae Moh Basin

The Mae Moh Basin, located in Lampang Province of northern Thailand, covers an area of ∼ 150 km2 and is underlain by Triassic basement rocks. Surface geological mapping and stratigraphic data indicate that the basin comprises four main sedimentary formations: (i) a coarse-to-fine-grained red siliciclastic sandstone, (ii) a thick, massive limestone, (iii) fine-grained turbidites interbedded with gray mudstone, and (iv) a reddish-gray argillaceous limestone.

In the current study, the focus is placed on the deepest formationthe red siliciclastic sandstone (hereafter referred to as “sandstone”)as it experiences the greatest overburden stress, making it suitable for high-pressure environments required for compressed hydrogen storage. This sandstone unit begins at a depth of approximately 1,020 m and is overlain by a thick sequence of massive limestone, which serves as an effective seal formation. The presence of this overlying limestone provides a structural seal, forming a favorable geological trap for underground gas storage. ,

Reservoir Grid and Simulation

A representative reservoir grid of the sandstone formation was constructed for numerical simulation in the current study. The reservoir was modeled as an anticline-shaped structure with a dip angle of 10°, discretized into a 3D grid comprising 50 × 50 × 30 cells. This configuration represents a modeled area of 1 km2 with a reservoir thickness of 370 m. Petrophysical properties were assigned based on measured data, with porosity and permeability ranging from 1.6% to 2.3%, and from 0.27 to 8 mD, respectively. A sequential Gaussian simulation approach was applied to distribute these properties spatially, as detailed in Section S1 of the Supporting Information. A vertical-to-horizontal permeability ratio of 0.1 was assumed.

CMG-GEM (Computer Modelling Group Ltd., Canada) was employed to simulate fluid phase behavior, pressure–volume–temperature (PVT) relationships, and compositional changes within the reservoir. The simulator uses the Peng–Robinson equation of state and applies finite-difference methods to solve material and energy balance equations. Hydrogen injection and production were both modeled using a single vertical well, perforated at a depth of 1,316 m. The reservoir was assumed to be fully water-saturated (initial water saturation = 1.0) and closed to external flow boundaries. Hydrogen dissolution and microbial reactions were not considered in the current study, as both processes remain negligible under the reservoir conditions of the Mae Moh Basin (∼60 °C, 10 MPa): the low hydrogen solubility limits dissolution to below 0.015 wt %, while microbial consumption is bounded by the finite availability of electron acceptors (principally sulfate) rather than by storage duration, remaining below 2 wt % even over the multiyear horizon considered. , Rock–fluid interfacial properties were also excluded, consistent with the macroscopic-scale focus of the reservoir model. To specifically evaluate the impact of relative permeabilities (k r ) hysteresis on fluid flow behavior in tight formations, the k r functions for gas and water phases were defined and are further detailed in the following subsection. To isolate the effect of hysteresis, the simulation excluded the use of cushion gas as typically practicing in some works.

In addition to modeling fluid flow behavior, geomechanical constraints were incorporated to ensure the safety and structural integrity of the UHS system. Reservoir pressure during simulation was maintained well below the estimated fracture resistance, based on geomechanical and pressure properties reported by Ramadhan et al. and Chaodumrong and Burrett, with supplementary estimates adapted from Zhang, Maneeintr et al. and Hubbert and Willis. The initial reservoir pressure was assumed to correspond to the hydrostatic pressure at the top of the reservoir, calculated to be 9.71 MPa. A geothermal gradient of 30 °C/km was adopted, based on regional estimates by Sridej and Wannakomol. Relative Permeabilities and the Hysteresis

The k rg and k rw used in the current study were adopted from Liu et al., who reported experimental measurements on tight sandstone samples with a porosity of 2.11% and a permeability of 2.1 nD. While the porosity of these samples is comparable to that of the target Mae Moh sandstone (1.6–2.3%), their absolute permeability differs by several orders of magnitude from the target range of 0.27–8 mD. Both rocks, however, fall within the tight-formation class, in which relative permeability is governed primarily by pore-throat structure and capillary-dominated displacement rather than by the absolute permeability magnitude. The adopted k r curves are therefore considered representative of the multiphase flow behavior in the target reservoir, as shown in Figure a.

Simulation Scenarios and Seasonal Storage Design

To investigate the impact of reservoir tightness on fluid displacement hysteresis, two simulation scenarios were considered: one that incorporates hysteresis effects and one that does not. These are hereafter referred to as (i) the hysteresis case and (ii) the nonhysteresis case, respectively, with the hysteresis case designated as the base case throughout the study.

Each reservoir simulation was designed to model an annual UHS operation composed of three sequential stages: (i) a continuous hydrogen injection period, (ii) a one-month shut-in or “idle” phase to monitor reservoir pressure and hydrogen plume behavior, and (iii) a production phase. During the injection period, reservoir pressure was constrained to remain below 75% of the fracture pressure threshold (i.e., 13.1 MPa). In the production period, reservoir pressure was kept below the initial reservoir pressure of 9.71 MPa. These reservoir pressure constraints dictate the hydrogen injection and production rates in each operational cycle. The injection rate was dynamically adjusted in response to the development of maximum bottom-hole pressure accordingly.

The simulation was conducted over a 50-year period to observe the long-term behavior of the hydrogen plume within the reservoir. Operational performance was assessed throughout the simulation, including the development of reservoir pressure, cumulative hydrogen injection and production, and the hydrogen recovery factor.

In the current study, the current energy demand was assumed to be replaced by prospective renewable energy sources. The demand estimate was based on the three-year average electricity production of the Mae Moh coal-fired power plant, which currently supplies power to northern Thailand. Renewable energy inputs were assumed to be sourced from biomass and solar power, both of which are subject to seasonal availability constraints. ,

The renewable energy sources were converted into their hydrogen-equivalent values (in kt of hydrogen) and scaled appropriately to match the defined energy demand, as detailed in Section S2 of the Supporting Information. This allowed monthly energy excess and shortage to be quantified throughout the year. Based on the resulting energy profiles, the hydrogen injection and withdrawal (i.e., production) periods of UHS operations were scheduled to align with periods of energy excess and shortage, respectively.

Monthly energy excess and shortage were quantified by subtracting the renewable energy supply from the corresponding energy demand, as detailed in Section S2 and illustrated in Figure . The consecutive months with cumulative energy shortage were designated as the withdrawal period, while those with cumulative energy excess were assigned as the injection period. Reservoir Pressure Build-up, Hydrogen Plume Behavior, and Operation Performance

The average reservoir pressure varied in response to the UHS operational stages: hydrogen injection led to pressure build-up, while hydrogen production caused pressure drawdown. These pressure fluctuations evolved over the 50-year simulation period, as shown in Figure a. During the initial eight cycles, the pressure differences between the end of injection and the end of production were more pronounced compared to later cycles. For example, in the base case, the reservoir pressure increased to 10.7 MPa at the end of the first injection stage, then declined to 9.8 MPa following the production. In contrast, by the final cycle, both injection and production stages ended with similar pressures in the range of 10.8–10.9 MPa. This progressive narrowing of pressure fluctuation was attributed to the increasing accumulation of ‘unproduced’ hydrogen within the reservoir (to be discussed below). Over time, the unproduced or residual hydrogen caused elevated initial reservoir pressures at the start of each new cycle, gradually increasing the average reservoir pressure throughout the simulation period.

Development of average reservoir pressure over 50 annual UHS operation cycles. The base case with hysteresis is shown in black solid line, and is compared with the nonhysteresis case (a: blue doubled line) and the improved case (b: pink doubled line).

Throughout the 50-year operation, reservoir pressure was carefully managed to remain below the maximum allowable pressure of 13.1 MPa, corresponding to 75% of the formation’s fracture pressure. The bottom-hole pressure, representing the maximum fluid pressure within the reservoir, was continuously monitored to ensure compliance with this limit (Figure S5 in Section S3). This constraint directly influenced the allowable hydrogen injection rate in each cycle, see Figure S7.

When compared to the base case that includes hysteresis effects (black solid line in Figure a), the nonhysteresis case (blue doubled line in Figure a) exhibited lower average reservoir pressures throughout the simulation. This difference is attributed to capillary-trapped hydrogen in the base case, caused by the behavior of k rg during the imbibition (i.e., production) stage, which does not reach zero gas saturation (S g ≠ 0; see Figure b). As a result, a portion of hydrogen remains trapped within the reservoir and contributes to pressure build-up over successive cycles.

Hydrogen plume development over the 50-year simulation is illustrated in Figure . Initially, the plume expanded both laterally and vertically; however, starting from the eighth cycle, vertical migration became more pronounced due to buoyancy-driven segregation (i.e., gravity segregation). Lateral spreading intensified between the 13th and 20th cycles as more hydrogen accumulated in the reservoir. By the final cycle (50th), both vertical and lateral segregation were clearly evident, with the highest hydrogen saturation concentrated near the top of the reservoir structure.

Cross-sectional profiles of hydrogen plume behavior, represented by gas saturation (S g ) at the 3rd, 8th, 13th, 20th, and 50th cycles. Results are shown for the base case (a–e), the nonhysteresis case (f–j), and the improved case (k–o). Saturation values are indicated by the color scale.

In the base case, hydrogen plume development showed less vertical and lateral expansion compared to the nonhysteresis case, except during the early cycles (Figure a,b compared with Figure f,g). For example, in later cycles, hydrogen saturation remained concentrated near the perforation zone in the base case (Figure d,e), whereas it was more dispersed in the nonhysteresis case (Figure i,j). This difference is attributed to the stronger capillary effect under hysteretic conditions, which resisted upward hydrogen migration driven by buoyancy.

The hydrogen plume accumulation observed in later cycles (e.g., the 50th cycle) is consistent with the increasing average reservoir pressure shown in Figure a. Greater accumulation in the base case (e.g., Figure e) contributed to higher reservoir pressures, which in turn constrained the allowable initial bottom-hole pressure at the start of subsequent injection periods (Figure S5).

It is important to note that the unproduced hydrogen can be categorized into two components: (i) hydrogen remaining due to plume segregation and lateral expansion, and (ii) capillary-trapped hydrogen which occurs exclusively when hysteresis is considered.

Hydrogen injection and production performance are shown in Figure a, with the corresponding hydrogen recovery factor presented in Figure c. Overall, both cumulative injection and production increased with cycle number. This trend reflects the reservoir’s progressively increased capacity to store hydrogen, as well as the gradual production of hydrogen that had remained unproduced in earlier cycles. However, cumulative hydrogen production remained significantly lower than injection due to losses associated with gravity segregation and the subsequent lateral spreading of hydrogen outside the effective production zone.

Cumulative hydrogen injection and production over 50 annual UHS operation cycles, shown in terms of hydrogen mass (kt), comparing the base case with hysteresis (black lines) to the nonhysteresis case (a: blue lines) and the improved case (b: pink lines). Hydrogen recovery factors over the same period compare the base case with hysteresis (black lines) to the nonhysteresis case (c: blue line) and the improved case (d: pink line).

The hydrogen recovery factor increased significantly over the first 13 cycles, after which the rate of improvement declined (Figure c). This trend aligns with the evolving plume dynamics discussed earlier, where lateral spreading and capillary trapping limited further recovery gains. Overall, the performance of the base case was slightly lower than that of the nonhysteresis case, primarily due to hydrogen retention from capillary trapping. By the final cycle, the recovery factor in the base case was approximately 4% lower than in the nonhysteresis case. Because the base and nonhysteresis cases differ only in the presence of imbibition hysteresis, this ∼4% gap isolates the contribution of capillary trapping to the recovery loss: whereas gravity segregation and lateral spreading limit recovery in both cases, the hysteresis-specific fractionhydrogen fixed at the residual gas saturation of ∼0.175 (Section )is trapped irreversibly and accumulates as unproduced hydrogen over successive cycles. Notably, the recovery factor had not fully plateaued by the end of the 50-year simulationmost evidently in the improved case, which approached ∼65% while still rising (Figure d)so additional recovery could be attained over a longer horizon.

In the current study, the hysteresis effect is particularly pronounced in tight reservoirs, as evidenced by the observed 4% reduction in hydrogen recovery (Figure c). This result is consistent with the findings of Jadhawar and Saeed, who reported a smaller recovery difference of 2.8% in conventional reservoir (nontight, with 19% porosity and 500 mD permeability). These findings highlight the critical importance of incorporating hysteretic effects when evaluating hydrogen storage in tight formations.

Performance Improvement by Double Production Wells

As demonstrated in the previous section, capillary hysteresis significantly affects UHS performance in tight reservoirs. To mitigate this effect and enhance operational efficiency, a dual-production well strategy was implementedhereafter referred to as the “improved case”. This section evaluates the performance of this well configuration under the same reservoir conditions, accounting for the presence of hysteresis.

In the improved case, a second production well was introduced within the same production zone, positioned 150 m laterally from the original well and 62 m shallower. While the first well remained perforated at 1316 m, the second well employed a dynamic perforation strategy to enhance hydrogen recovery. Initially, it was perforated at 1254 m for the first to 13th cycle, and then repositioned to a shallower depth of 1057 m from 14th cycle onward. This perforation relocation was designed based on the hydrogen plume distribution observed in the base case (Figure a–e), with the objective of targeting “unproduced” hydrogen trapped by gravity segregation and capillary effects.

The reservoir pressure build-up in the improved case was noticeably lower than in the base case, with a reduction of approximately 0.5 MPa observed by the final cycle (Figure b). A similar trend was observed in bottom-hole pressures, which remained lower throughout the simulation, thereby accommodating higher volumes of hydrogen injection (Figure S5 and S7).

Despite the influence of gravity segregation and lateral plume expansion, the amount of unproduced hydrogen in the improved case was notably lower than in the base case, owing to enhanced production capability enabled by the second well and its adaptive perforation strategy. The hydrogen plume developed accordingly, as shown in Figure k–o. For example, in the final cycle, residual hydrogen was visibly reduced in the improved case (Figure o) compared to the base case (Figure e).

The improved case yielded a substantial increase in cumulative hydrogen injection and production (Figure b), primarily due to the reduction in bottom-hole pressures enabled by enhanced reservoir pressure management. This pressure reduction was facilitated by the decreased volume of unproduced hydrogen retained within the reservoir. As a result, the hydrogen recovery factor improved significantlyfrom 37% in the base case to 65% in the improved case by the final cycle.

Seasonal and Long-Term Performance of Underground Hydrogen Storage

Based on the injection and withdrawal periods defined in Section , the annual hydrogen injection and withdrawal volumes over the 50-year simulation period are illustrated in Figure . These values are derived from the cumulative injection and production results presented in the previous sections.

Comparison of simulated seasonal and long-term UHS between the base case (a) and the improved case (b). In each panel, hydrogen injection is shown in green and hydrogen withdrawal in brown. Dashed lines represent the 1st operational cycle, while solid lines represent the 50th cycle. The inset in (a) presents a magnified view of production data in the base case.

In the base case (Figure a), hydrogen injection was significantly greater than withdrawal during the first cycle but gradually approached a more balanced profile in subsequent cycles. While hydrogen withdrawal remained relatively constant across the 50-year period (ranging from 9.5 to 10.8 t/cycle), the injection capacity declined over time due to progressive increases in reservoir pressure, as discussed previously (Figure ). As withdrawals consistently lagged behind injections, the overall operation became constrained by the withdrawal capacity. This highlights the detrimental impact of gravity segregation and capillary trapping, both of which reduce hydrogen mobility and recoverability in the reservoir. This withdrawal limitation also partly reflects the absence of cushion gas, which could provide pressure support to improve early cycle withdrawal.

In the improved case (Figure b), hydrogen withdrawals increased substantially as a result of the dual-production well configurationrising from 11.8 t in the first cycle to 34.6 t in the final cycle. Overall, the withdrawal volumes were significantly higher than those of the base case. This enhanced withdrawal capacity enabled more pore space to become available, thereby facilitating continued hydrogen injection over timefrom 76.6 t in the first cycle to 35.8 t in the final cycle. Nevertheless, similar to the base case, the withdrawal volume remained the limiting factor in UHS operation, ultimately governing the system’s overall performance.

Given the estimated energy shortage of 8.39 kt per year (Figure ), the hydrogen storage capability of a 1 km2 area of the studied reservoir is limited to ∼9.5 t for the base case (Figure a) and up to ∼34.6 t for the improved case (Figure b). These results imply that an area extent of roughly 883 km2 would be required in the base case, and about 242 km2 in the improved case, to meet the regional hydrogen demand. However, according to Tulapan et al., the total area of the Mae Moh Basin is reported to be less than 150 km2, indicating that the reservoir is insufficient to support seasonal UHS operations at the required scaleeven under the improved configuration. These required-area estimates should be regarded as approximate: they assume uniform reservoir properties, whereas the basin is heterogeneous, and they derive from a finite 50-year horizon over which recovery had not yet plateaued. A longer simulation would likely raise the recoverable volume per unit area, so the present figures are best interpreted as an upper bound rather than a definitive requirement. Notably, the improved configuration entails the cost of an additional well, and a dedicated economic assessment would be needed to establish whether its efficiency gain is economically feasible.

Conclusions

The UHS operation has been examined as a renewable energy facility to replace the fossil-based energy in northern Thailand. Tight reservoir characteristics of available georesources were investigated for their capability to store and produce hydrogen using reservoir simulation, with emphasis on the hysteresis k r effect on operational performance. The seasonal and long-term UHS operations were also designed to align with local renewable energy availability. The main conclusions can be drawn, as follows:1.When considering the hysteretic k r effect, reservoir pressure response was significantly influenced due to capillary-trapped hydrogen, which became immobile during the production phase. This resulted in hydrogen accumulation and pressure build-up over time, particularly in later cycles. Hydrogen plume dynamics consequently evolved with gravity segregation and lateral spreading, reducing production performance. The hydrogen recovery factor in the base case was less than 40%, and approximately 4% lower than the nonhysteresis case. These effects are clearly pronounced in tight reservoirs compared to conventional systems and must be considered in storage design.2.When a second production well was added with perforation relocation at a shallower depth, operational performance improved substantially. The dual-well configuration enabled withdrawal from zones affected by gravitational segregation and hysteresis-induced trapping. This reduced reservoir pressure, enhanced injectivity, and allowed greater hydrogen injection. The hydrogen recovery factor improved from 37% in the base case to 65% in the improved case, confirming that well configuration and placement are critical for enhancing recovery in tight UHS formations.3.When applying the simulation results to seasonal and long-term UHS operations, the study revealed significant scaling limitations. In the base case, annual hydrogen injection declined from 76.6 to 15.8 t, with production dropping to 9.5 t. In the improved case, annual production increased to 34.6 t by the final cycle. However, based on a baseline extrapolation that assumes uniform reservoir properties, the reservoir area required to meet the regional hydrogen demand of 8.39 kt/year was estimated at ∼883 km2 (the base case) and ∼242 km2 (the improved case), both of which exceed the reported area of the Mae Moh Basin (<150 km2). Therefore, while technically feasible at a localized scale, the tight sandstone reservoir is too small for standalone seasonal storage, though these estimates carry substantial uncertainty given the basin’s heterogeneity.

In the base case, annual hydrogen injection declined from 76.6 to 15.8 t, with production dropping to 9.5 t. In the improved case, annual production increased to 34.6 t by the final cycle. However, based on a baseline extrapolation that assumes uniform reservoir properties, the reservoir area required to meet the regional hydrogen demand of 8.39 kt/year was estimated at ∼883 km2 (the base case) and ∼242 km2 (the improved case), both of which exceed the reported area of the Mae Moh Basin (<150 km2). Therefore, while technically feasible at a localized scale, the tight sandstone reservoir is too small for standalone seasonal storage, though these estimates carry substantial uncertainty given the basin’s heterogeneity.

In addition, the current findings should be interpreted within the scope of the model’s assumptions. The simulations resolve multiphase flow and hysteresis at the reservoir scale but exclude, or hold constant, several processes that may influence long-term containment and recovery: hydrogen dissolution and microbial reactions, mineral geochemical reactions, wettability alteration, molecular diffusion, and the detailed capillary-sealing efficiency of the caprock. Incorporating these coupled effects, together with basin-scale heterogeneity and a longer operational horizon, represents an important direction for future work.
