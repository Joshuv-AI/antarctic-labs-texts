Long-read structural variant discovery and targeted short read genotyping enables population scale characterization of structural variation in rhesus macaques
Authors: Karina Ray, Christina Mulch, Samuel M. Peterson, Sebastian Benjamin, Nathan Gullicksrud, Adam J. Ericsen, Eric J. Vallender, Betsy M. Ferguson, Jeffrey D. Wall, Benjamin N. Bimber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDue to their close evolutionary relationship with humans, rhesus macaques are an important pre-clinical model. While genetic diversity driven by short nucleotide variation has long been studied in rhesus macaques, there is comparatively little known about structural variation, with most published studies focused on cross-species comparative analyses. Understanding the degree and implications of intraspecies structural variation is essential to all biomedical research using rhesus macaques as a model.ResultsHere we present long-read sequencing of 59 rhesus macaques, identifying a catalog of 339,334 structural variants (SVs), which we subsequently genotype in a cohort of 2,645 individuals with short read whole genome sequencing data to create the largest public dataset of rhesus macaque SVs. These data reveal population structure within rhesus macaque SVs based on both geographic ancestry and to a lesser degree, breeding center. While there is evidence of strong purifying selection against SVs within exons, 0.7% of SVs overlap exons, with an average of 16.9 rare SVs per subject predicted to have a high impact on protein coding sequences. Notably, rhesus macaque SVs are dominated by Alu retrotransposition events, which comprise 55.7% of SVs and suggest significantly different modes of SV formation relative to humans and great apes.ConclusionsThis dataset represents the largest study of structural variation in rhesus macaques to date and demonstrates use of both long and short-read datasets to generate SV genotype data. These data enable the consideration of structural variation impact in rhesus macaque-based research and will also aid the development of primate pangenomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03873-3.

Background

Structural variants (SVs) are large genomic alterations, often defined as variants with length greater than 50 base pairs [1]. Due to the larger variant size, structural variation is responsible for a greater share of heritable variation, at least in terms of base pairs, than single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) [2, 3]. Structural variation has been implicated in many human diseases, including cancer, rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 and type 2 diabetes, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and autism [3–5]. Types of SVs can include deletions, insertions, inversions, translocations and copy number variation. In contrast to SNVs, structural variation is much more complicated to detect using short read sequence data, and the algorithms used to call SVs from short read data have variable accuracy and often produce inconsistent results [6]. The expansion and decreased cost of long read sequencing technologies has enabled the accurate identification of SVs, and consequently our understanding of structural variation in diverse populations [7, 8].

Patterns of structural variation between species can yield information about evolutionary history and speciation and can inform the interpretation of human variation [3, 7]. Like SNVs, SVs have population structure [9], and there is also an important need to understand population-level structural variation within a species. Among non-human primates, macaques (including rhesus and cynomolgus macaques) are the dominant model organisms for biomedical research, including significant research involving infectious disease, neurobiology, reproductive studies, and more [10–22]. The earliest studies of macaque structural variation used hybridization arrays to identify copy number variation [23, 24]. These were followed by studies of macaque copy number using read depth patterns in short-read data [25, 26]. While these studies provide foundational information about patterns of structural variation, they each examined a small number of individuals, ranging from 1 to 20. Further, they were based on older, less complete genome assembly builds, which makes these data difficult to directly compare with current generation data. More recent studies have used long read sequencing to identify macaque structural variation, although cohort sizes remain small, and these studies focused primarily on cross-species analyses [27, 28]. A publication that generated a telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assembly for cynomolgus macaques also included long-read sequencing and 20 haplotype-resolved assemblies from 10 rhesus macaques [29]. The largest rhesus macaque SV dataset published to date involves SVs called from short-read whole genome data of 562 rhesus macaques, which identified 75 human-specific inversions [30]. While these studies generate an important foundation, rhesus macaques are genetically diverse, with a 2.5-fold higher overall nucleotide diversity than humans, necessitating both SV discovery and genotyping in substantially larger cohorts [31].

Here we present a multi-phased approach to identify and genotype macaque structural variation at population scale. Using long-read sequencing from 59 rhesus macaques, we identified 339,334 unique SVs. We then performed targeted genotyping in a cohort of 2,645 rhesus macaques with Illumina short-read data, which we demonstrate can produce highly accurate genotype information. These data represent the largest SV dataset generated from any non-human primate species to date. We demonstrate significant differences in the patterns of structural variation within rhesus macaques relative to apes, including an SV landscape dominated by Alu retrotransposition. We further show that patterns of structural variation vary by geographic origin, recapitulating patterns observed in SNVs. We detect an average of 79,737 SVs per macaque, with an average of 16.9 rare SVs per individual predicted to have a high impact on protein coding sequences. Collectively, these data provide an important resource to advance understanding of the role of structural variation in macaque-based biomedical research and evolutionary studies.

Results

Cohort design and long-read structural variant detection

To provide a survey of structural variation within captive rhesus macaque populations, we selected 59 Indian-origin macaques housed at three different breeding colonies. Demographic information is provided in Table S1. These subjects are comprised of clinically healthy animals, with two family trios included for use in quality control analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted, followed by sequencing on either the PacBio CLR or CCS platforms using one SMRT cell per animal (see methods). The primary difference between the CLR and CCS approaches is that CLR selects longer fragment sizes and each fragment is sequenced with fewer passes. In contrast, CCS selects slightly smaller fragments, but each fragment is sequenced multiple times to produce a consensus sequence. Therefore, the effective read length is shorter for CCS, but sequencing accuracy is higher. Our data matched these expectations, with the CCS data producing an average of 2,646,176 reads/animal with a median read length of 12,222 bp, and the CLR data producing an average of 6,682,217 reads/animal with a median read length of 22,654.5 bp (Fig. 1A-B).Fig. 1Dataset quality control and comparison of SV callers. For sequence alignment and SV calling, we compared multiple analysis pipelines. Data were aligned to the latest public reference genome (MMul_10) using pbmm2, followed by SV calling using either pbsv, Sniffles2, or cuteSV [6, 32]. For Sniffles2, we processed each sample individually, followed by joint genotyping/force-calling on the entire set, avoiding the need to merge genotypes. For pbsv and cuteSV, which support separate CLR and CCS modes, we processed the samples for each technology separately and then merged the resulting datasets using bcftools. For consistency across callers, we consider only variants with length of 50 bp or greater. In addition to the long-read SV data, we generated Illumina short-read whole genome sequence data on 40 of the rhesus macaques, which was processed using a macaque-adapted GATK-based pipeline to call SNVs [20, 33, 34]. The accuracy and consistency of each caller was evaluated using multiple methods. Each caller produced similar numbers of SVs, and similar composition of SV types (Fig. 1C). The largest callset was generated by pbsv (347,703), with 306,189 SVs detected by Sniffles2 and 313,138 SVs detected by cuteSV. In all cases, insertions were the most common SV type (Fig. 1C). Interestingly, even though Sniffles2 predicted fewer unique SVs, it called a higher number of SVs/sample than both pbsv and cuteSV, which were similar (Fig. 1D). Of note, both pbsv and Sniffles called different rates of SV/sample for the CCS and CLR data, although pbsv called fewer SVs/sample for CLR data while Sniffles2 generated considerably more SVs/sample for CLR than CCS samples (Fig. 1D, triangles). Sniffles2 also had the higher number of singleton SVs (Fig. 1E). Relative to SNV data, all SV datasets had a much higher rate of Mendelian violations, with relatively minor differences between callers (Fig. 1F). Finally, we used inferred kinship coefficients to evaluate genotype accuracy. First, we contrasted kinship coefficients based on pedigree data against kinship coefficients inferred from the SV data (calculated using the KING algorithm, implemented in plink2) [35, 36]. Negative values for inferred kinships are expected from KING when individuals are from highly dissimilar populations [35]. The SV genotypes produced by pbsv show strong correlation with expectations from the pedigree (Fig. 1G), while Sniffles2 data consistently predict a much higher degree of kinship (Fig. 1H), and cuteSV consistently predicts a slightly lower kinship than expected from pedigree data (Fig. 1I). Because it is possible for individuals to have more distant or cryptic familial relationships than reflected in available pedigree data, we also compared the kinship coefficients calculated using Illumina-based SNV genotypes (generated on the same samples) relative to PacBio SV data. Once again, we detected strong concordance between pbsv and the reference SNV-based coefficients, while sniffles2 genotypes predicted higher kinship coefficients than expected, and cuteSV under-estimated kinship (Fig. 1J-L). There are several possible explanations for this, including the fact that Sniffles2 is not optimized for CLR data. In contrast, pbsv has separate profiles, optimized for each data type. Based on these results, we selected the pbsv callset for subsequent analyses.

Finally, although pbsv predicted 8,369 translocations and break-ends, manual inspection of the raw read data revealed high rates of miscalls and likely artifacts. One common category among these predicted translocations was the joining of an unplaced contig to a primary chromosome, with all putative translocations joining the same contig/chromosome pair (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). It is likely that many of these reflect errors in the underlying assembly, as opposed to true variation. We also noted that a substantial number of predicted break-ends overlapped with a separate deletion SV, making the break-end call redundant. Due to the high artifact rate, predicted translocations have been removed from subsequent analyses.

Characteristics of rhesus macaque structural variation

After filtration, we identified 339,334 unique SVs, with insertions being the most common category (61.1%), followed by deletions (31.3%) (Fig. 2A). Duplications and inversions were also detected, although at much lower levels. We obtained an average of 71,450.7 SVs per sample. Our data highlight several significant differences between the landscape of structural variation in rhesus macaques relative to great apes and humans. The SVs detected in this cohort are predominantly < 350 bp in length (83.1% of the total) (Fig. 2B). Longer SVs were less common; however, 1,232 SVs greater than 10 kb in length were detected. Mobile elements are a major source of structural variation across all primates [24, 27]. This was also true in our dataset, with Alu elements, which have a length of ~ 330 bp, comprising 66.0% of all SVs (Fig. 2B). LINE elements, with a size of ~ 6 kb, were a significant source of SVs (10.8%) but far less abundant than Alu elements. The dramatic expansion of Alu transposition in rhesus macaques is noteworthy, given that this is a significant difference from humans and great apes, where mobile element insertions comprise ~ 15% of SVs[38]. This implies that Alu retrotransposition is not only a major mechanism in speciation but remains a major factor in intraspecies diversity [39].

SVs are not distributed evenly across the genome. While most autosomes had a similar overall SV density, with approximately 100 SVs per MB, indicating correlation between chromosome length and number of SVs, chromosome 19 was an outlier (Fig. 2C). It had a nearly threefold higher density of SVs, a difference driven largely by an increase in Alu elements, and consistent with prior reports (Fig. 2C) [26]. SVs are also not evenly distributed within chromosomes (Fig. 2D). As expected, there is a much higher density of SVs at the ends of rhesus macaque chromosomes, consistent with published data[40]. We also identified several localized intrachromosomal regions with especially high densities of SVs. The highest of these corresponds to the MHC region (chromosome 4). The macaque MHC region has a high degree of gene copy number variation between subjects, and therefore this high density of SVs is expected [28, 41–44]. The localized hot spots on chromosomes 11 and 18 are each close to the centromeres.

Targeted SV genotyping using short read data enables population-scale analysis

Long read data provides more accurate detection of SVs; however, these technologies are still considerably more expensive than short read sequencing. Further, there is already a large body of existing public short read sequencing data for macaques [20]. While de novo SV detection using short read data is problematic, genotyping for known SVs can be more consistent [6, 45–47]. To examine this possibility, we used the tool Paragraph to perform targeted genotyping for SVs using Illumina whole genome sequencing datasets from 2,645 rhesus macaques. Because Paragraph does not support genotyping for inversions, the dataset was limited to 317,954 SVs. Most SVs were called in > 75% of samples, with insertions and deletions being called at higher rates than duplications (Fig. 3A). To evaluate accuracy, we subset the 40 rhesus macaques with both PacBio and Illumina data and compared the SV genotypes obtained from each. The number of SVs called per sample was extremely similar between the two, with an average of 79,737.1 SVs per macaque identified using the Illumina data (Fig. 3B). The pbsv pipeline, which has CLR-specific settings, predicted fewer SVs/animal in the CLR data; however, the Illumina/paragraph-based SV calling predicted similar rates between CCS and CLR, suggesting that pbsv may be undercounting the true SVs present in these datasets. Genotype accuracy was evaluated by treating the PacBio genotypes as ground truth and categorizing each Illumina genotype as either: 1) concordant, 2) false positive, meaning the short-read data was called as a variant and the reference PacBio was not, 3) false negative, meaning the data short-read was scored as wild-type and the reference PacBio identified a variant, and 4) incorrect genotype, meaning that an SV was detected in the short-read data, but the genotype differed from that observed in the PacBio data (e.g., a homozygous site called a heterozygote) (Fig. 3C). The latter category was separated because a common usage of SV data is to identify subjects encoding a given SV, followed by targeted genotyping. Thus, identifying the presence of the SV, even with an incorrect genotype, has value. Overall, accuracy varied based on SV length and genotype (Fig. 3C and Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Homozygous reference sites were genotyped at high accuracy, with extremely low false-positive rates for SVs above 200 bp in length. For SVs between 200 and 10,000 bp in length (which comprise 70.7% of total SVs), the false negative rate was ~ 12% for heterozygous sites. For homozygous variant sites, the incorrect genotype was called ~ 10% of the time; however, ~ 8% of the time the SV was detected (Fig. 3C, labeled “Detected SV/Incorrect Genotype”). The false negative rate was considerably higher for SVs > 10,000 bp in length, which represents 810 total SVs, and suggests there are limits to large SV detection using short-read data.

To further evaluate accuracy, we contrasted the pairwise kinship coefficients inferred using either SNV data or SV data (Fig. 3D). While the vast majority of rhesus macaques in this cohort are unrelated (indicated by the negative coefficients[35]), there is strong concordance between the kinship estimates generated from each dataset. The kinship coefficients predicted by SV data tend to be slightly lower than the SNV-based estimates. Given the initial SV callset was generated from only 59 rhesus macaques, it likely does not include many rare SVs or SVs specific to alternate populations. While it is possible that our callset lacks some population-specific SVs, the average number of SVs called per subject was relatively consistent between breeding centers. In contrast, technical factors such as read depth did have an impact on the number of SVs detected (Fig. 3E and Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Collectively, these data demonstrate that targeted SV genotyping using short-read data can produce highly accurate SV genotypes, although accuracy will vary based on characteristics of each site.

Population structure of macaque SVs

By leveraging existing short read data, we dramatically expanded the size of our cohort, generating genome-wide SV data from 2,645 rhesus macaques, from 12 distinct colonies. These data provide much more accurate measurements of allele frequencies and the opportunity to examine population structure within rhesus macaque SVs. Within rhesus macaques, there is a well-established population structure based on geographic origin, which separates Indian-origin from Chinese-origin rhesus macaques [48]. This structure was originally reported using sparse sequencing of 1,476 SNVs; however, it has been affirmed using short-read whole genome sequencing SNV-based methods [31, 48].

While the existence of rhesus macaque population structure is well understood and has been regularly published in PCA analyses, to our knowledge, no publications have reported comprehensive panels of highly discriminatory SNVs that distinguish Indian- and Chinese-origin rhesus macaques. Using our Illumina SNV data, we identified a subset of samples to use as references for Chinese-origin (n = 140), and Indian-origin (n = 1681) macaques. We then calculated population-specific allele frequencies at each SNV (n = 53.6 million) and identified 59,878 SNVs with extremely high allele frequency in one population (> 0.8) and low frequency in the other (< 0.3). We further generated a standalone tool to score ancestry based on this reference panel, producing a score scaled between 0–1 that represents concordance between the sample’s genotypes and each reference SNV panel. Our approach is tolerant to missing data, and therefore amenable to variable types of genomic data, including whole genome, whole exome, and reduced representation data such as genotype-by-sequencing or RadSeq. We used this approach to score all subjects for geographic origin, which was subsequently used to interpret the population structure identified by SV data. Because this pipeline is of general use to all macaque-based genomic research, the SNVs and scoring tool have been made available (Additional file 1: Fig. S4 and Table S2). These analyses are described in greater detail in the Supplemental Text.

Next, we examined the population structure predicted by the SV data. After pruning related individuals from our dataset (using plink2/KING cutoff of 0.05), we performed PCA on the remaining 1,521 individuals (Fig. 4A). PC1 explained 45.51% of variance and identified two distinct populations. The SNV-based ancestry score confirms that these correspond to Indian- and Chinese-origin rhesus macaques, confirming that SVs have similar population structure in rhesus macaques as SNVs. The larger dataset also provides more representative allele frequency data than the 59 rhesus macaques with long-read data. As expected, most SVs were rare (33.2% had AF < 0.05), and minor allele frequencies (MAFs) were similar between deletions (median MAF 0.083), insertions (median MAF 0.077), duplications (median MAF 0.019, and inversions (median MAF 0.00) (Fig. 4C). Of note, 2.5% of SVs had allele frequencies > 0.98, suggesting these represent either genome assembly errors or sites of rare SVs present in the reference subject that was sequenced to generate the genome assembly. These were primarily deletions (n = 6,512), although 1,356 fixed insertions were also identified. Of these, 46% were called in fewer than half of samples. We also identified 14,708 SVs, primarily deletions, nearly all of which had allele frequency close to 0.5, with excess heterozygosity < 0.005. These sites also likely represent artifacts in the genome assembly and have been removed from these analyses (listed in Table S3). The identification of such sites is aided by use of large cohorts, and this information can inform the interpretation of smaller datasets.

As noted previously, Alu retrotransposition events represent ~ 55% of SVs in macaques. The median MAF of Alu deletions in the expanded data set was 0.212, as compared to 0.048 for non-Alu deletions, suggesting that these events are less deleterious. The allele frequencies for insertion, duplication, and inversion Alu events were comparable to non-Alu SVs.

Impact of macaque SVs on protein coding sequences

Next, we examined the functional impact of SVs. First, we categorized the SVs based on their location relative to genes and exons (Fig. 5A). As a comparison, we contrasted the SV data against the Macaque Genotype and Phenotype (mGAP) dataset, which is a catalog of 53 million short nucleotide variants (SNVs) identified in a cohort of 3,562 rhesus macaques[20]. First, we examined the location of SNVs and SVs relative to genes. Overall, the distribution of SNVs and SV was remarkably similar, although SVs within exons are less common than SNVs, comprising only 0.3% of total SVs, in contrast to 2.7% of SNVs. This is expected as almost all SVs in exons are likely to be deleterious, whereas a significant proportion of SNVs in exons are selectively neutral. To more formally examine the distribution of SVs relative to functional elements we performed odds ratio testing for each category of SVs (Fig. 5B). Negative values indicate that SVs overlap the indicated functional element at lower rates than predicted by random chance, while positive values indicate that SVs detected in that category of functional elements are enriched. The negative values for exon-spanning SVs are consistent with a deleterious effect and purifying selection. Interestingly, duplications were not depleted from within exons, a finding that is consistent with prior publications; however, it should be noted that exon-spanning duplications are rare events (n = 759, or 3% of duplications) [25]. The enrichment of SVs in SINE/LINE elements (indicated by positive values), is expected since many SVs are SINE/LINE elements (Fig. 5B). We identified many instances of direct Alu insertions into coding exons, including SVs within DENND2C, TALDO1, LCA5L, CDH16, FCGR3 and RIDA. In addition to direct disruption of an exon, Alu elements can modify gene expression through more cryptic mechanisms, such as intronic insertions that alter splicing [49]. Comparison with human SV datasets

Next, we compared macaque SVs relative to human datasets. We used liftOver tools to map SVs from MMul_10 to the human genome (GRCh38). Even with new liftOver tools adapted for SVs, only about 50% of deletions, duplications, and inversions successfully lifted (Fig. 5C) [50]. Liftover was slightly better for insertions, which is expected since their reference length is just one base-pair, with a success rate of ~ 75%. Extremely similar liftOver rates were seen for both GRCh38 and the older GRCh37 build. We identified just 718 SVs that overlapped the human gnomAD SV dataset, when requiring a minimum of 75% reciprocal overlap (Fig. 5D) [51, 52]. We identified 3 SVs that were nearly identical to human ClinVar variants (defined as 90% reciprocal overlap), including a 154,235 bp duplication in complement factor H (CFH; ClinVar ID nssv18792867), a gene associated with macular degeneration. There were an additional 116 rare SVs predicted to have high impact on protein coding that overlapped the same gene as a ClinVar variant, including a 2,282 bp deletion in interleukin 17 receptor C (IL17RC). Mutations in IL17RC have been associated with impaired response to fungal infection [53].

Common coding SVs impacting repetitive motifs

While most protein coding SVs are deleterious, genes with repetitive motif structures can be more tolerant to structural variation that alters the copy number of the repeated motifs. The S100 fused-type protein family is one example, found within the epidermal differentiation complex on chromosome 1. This region includes the CRNN, RPTN, TCHH, FLG and FLG2 genes, and each gene contains a conserved region rich in repeat motifs [54]. We identified many common SVs (defined as AF > 0.05) within these exons that alter the number of repeat motifs. As in humans, the rhesus macaque CRNN gene contains two threonine-rich 60 amino acid repeats, but rhesus macaques are polymorphic for an insertional SV that adds an additional motif copy. Similarly, the sequenced cohort included four different haplotypes of the RPTN gene which differed predominantly by the number of 12-mer repeats present in exon 3. Relative to the reference gene, SVs represented either a loss of two of these repeats or a gain of 8 or 9. Common coding SVs were also observed in the mucin gene family. Members of this family encode either secretory or transmembrane proteins containing redundant polypeptide sequences composed of tandemly repeated gene-specific amino acid motifs that vary in size from 8 to 169 amino acids in humans [55]. We identified substantial variation in all mucin family genes, which coincided with alterations specific to the size of the tandem repeats (although the length of tandem repeats in some rhesus orthologs differed slightly than those of humans). Additionally, the perilipin gene family, which is responsible for the stabilization of lipid droplets through lipase access restriction, has a conserved domain consisting of heavily glycosylated 33-amino acid repeats, each composed of three imperfect 11-mer tandem repeats [56]. The number of repeats observed varies substantially between genes and species. Our data suggest that intraspecies variation exists in rhesus macaques for PLIN4 and PLIN5 which each have an SV that results in a loss of 3 or gain of 2 repeats, respectively.

Large SVs that impact protein coding

A total of 158 SVs were identified that spanned an entire gene, including a 65,256 bp inversion spanning CD1C, a glycoprotein structurally related to MHC Class I, primarily known for presentation of lipid and glycolipid antigens to T cells [57]. Additionally, tandem duplication events were identified which included the copy of whole genes including GJB6 and MNP2. While structural variants that include the deletion of the GJB6 gene have been linked to non-syndromic hearing impairment [58, 59], the consequences of gene duplications have not been studied.

We also identified several large deletions that spanned genes. One example is a 70,798 bp deletion that removes six coding exons of the CNTN6 gene. While the reading frame of the transcripts stays in-frame, the SV removes three of the six N-terminal Ig domains critical to protein architecture [60]. Loss-of-function of CNTN6 has been shown to cause neurological deficits in mice [61] and rare copy number variants of the gene in humans have been associated with risk of Tourette Syndrome, abnormal sensory perception and autism spectrum disorder [62, 63]. Short-read genotyping was able to successfully identify a monkey homozygous for this deletion, suggesting that the SV is not lethal; however, the identified monkey is deceased and was not able to be closely observed for phenotypic analysis. Another deletion of note spans 5,452 bp of the MIA3 gene and removes eight exons, disrupting the reading frame and causing early termination. MIA3 has two major isoforms that code for TANGO1S and TANGO1L, proteins with roles trafficking secretory cargo from the endoplasmic reticulum [64]. Combinatorial loss of function of both isoforms has been shown to cause severe procollagen secretion deficits [65], has been associated with non-viability in mice due to skeletal formation deficits [66], and has been linked to a novel syndrome in humans characterized by skeletal abnormalities, dentinogenesis imperfecta and hearing loss [67]. As this deletion is expected to delete exons shared between the two major isoforms of the gene it represents a potential model for this rare human disease. While expanded genotyping of individuals with short-read coverage identified more carriers of the SV, no homozygous individuals have been identified to date.

Discussion

Structural variation is an important yet incompletely understood category of genetic variation, with the potential to exert strong functional consequences. While several studies have previously examined structural variation in macaques, these generally analyze a handful of individuals and focus on cross-species comparative genomics, rather than intraspecies diversity. Understanding the latter is essential for the interpretation of research using macaques as a model organism. Here we present the results of a two-fold strategy to characterize rhesus macaque intraspecies structural variation. First, we performed SV-discovery using long-read data from 59 rhesus macaques, identifying 339,334 unique SVs. To enable population-level analyses, we performed targeted genotyping of these SVs from 2,645 short-read datasets, creating the largest published macaque SV dataset to date. These data demonstrate that SVs recapitulate established population structure and reveal an SV landscape dominated by Alu retrotransposition. The stark difference between structural variation in humans and great apes, which is dominated nonallelic homologous recombination, and the Alu-dominated landscape in macaques is consistent with a prior comparative genomics study using data from five macaques [39]. Our data expand upon this by demonstrating that differential patterns of Alu retrotransposition drive within-species structural variation, with Alu insertion or deletion events comprising 55.7% of rhesus macaque SVs. We identified an average of 71,450.7 SVs per macaque, and an average of 16.9 rare SVs per individual predicted to have high impact on protein coding. Collectively, these data provide an important resource to integrate structural variation into the interpretation of genomic data from macaque-based studies.

The strategy presented here can serve as a blueprint for the study of SVs in for other model organisms. While long-read data is superior at resolving SVs, the cost per sample remains several times higher than short-read data and many species have a pre-existing body of short read datasets. Further, high-depth short read data is generally superior at resolving SNVs, limiting the general use of long-read sequencing. The approach demonstrated here: SV-discovery using a moderately sized dataset of long-read data, followed by targeted genotyping of these SVs in a much larger cohort of short-read datasets, could rapidly and cost-effectively advance knowledge of SVs in many model organisms.

Long-read sequencing has enabled a rapid growth in the quality and number of genomic assemblies available for non-human primate species [28–30]. Improvements to a genome assembly typically involve the filling of gaps and placement of previously unplaced contigs. Because a structural variant is defined as a change relative to a reference genome, the accuracy and completeness of that assembly has a significant impact on structural variant calls. Thus, a site that manifested as structural variation relative to an earlier genomic build may represent a flaw in the original assembly, and the same input data aligned to a newer assembly can yield a different result. We identified many examples of this in our data, including 10,021 likely assembly artifacts that manifest as SVs with allele frequencies of ~ 0.5 or ~ 1.0. Identifying such sites using large datasets can aid the interpretation of structural variation data generated from smaller cohorts. More accurate and complete genomic assemblies will improve SV calling.

While the proliferation of new genome builds, and even personalized assemblies, is a tremendous opportunity, it also creates challenges. For decades, genome assemblies have tended to evolve slowly and deliberately. Most genomic data is represented and analyzed relative to a genome build, and while it is possible to compare data generated across different genomic assemblies, this introduces obstacles and technical artifacts. Long-reads reduce the effort required to generate the genomic scaffolds; however, the annotation of that assembly, including fundamental information like a gene model, is not always accurate. Newer representations of genomic diversity, such as the pangenome graphs that are being developed will be essential; however, significant adaptation of software will be needed to effectively work within this new paradigm.

There are several limitations to this study. While our cohort of 59 long-read datasets is among the largest published to date for macaques, these SVs do not represent the entirety of structural variation in rhesus macaques, and our targeted genotyping is limited to this set of SVs. This approach will likely underrepresent deleterious SVs, since these sites are expected to be under purifying selection and rare. Additional long-read sequencing and SV discovery is necessary to expand the catalog of known SVs in the species.

Conclusions

Collectively, the data presented here significantly enhance our understanding of structural variation within rhesus macaques, the major non-human primate species used in biomedical research. While there is a clear advantage in the use of long-reads for SV discovery, we demonstrate that existing short read data can generate highly accurate SV genotypes for known SVs, raising the potential for incorporation of SV information into more studies. Our data also highlight unique features of rhesus macaque SV formation and identify functionally similar variants that suggest common pathogenic effects in both rhesus macaques and humans.

Methods

Genomic DNA extraction and PacBio sequencing

High molecular weight (HMW) genomic DNA was extracted from blood or tissue samples using the Gentra Puregene kit (Qiagen), with slight modifications to avoid shearing through sample pipetting or exposure to elevated temperatures. Extractions were performed at ONPRC Primate Genetics Core, ENPRC Genomics Core and TNPRC Genetics and Genome Banking Core (RRID:SCR_024615). Extracted genomic DNA samples were sent to the University of Oregon Genomics & Cell Characterization Core Facility, which performed standard quality control procedures to verify samples met DNA length requirements prior to library preparation. Libraries were sequenced on a PacBio Sequel II system, using one SMRT cell 8 M per library. Samples were sequenced using either CLR or CCS chemistry, as indicated in Table S1. Raw data have been deposited under BioProject PRJNA382404, and SRA accession numbers are listed in Table S1.

Long-read sequence pre-processing, alignment, and variant calling

Long-read data was aligned to the MMul_10 reference genome (GCA_003339765.3) using pbmm2 (version 1.17.0). CLR datasets were aligned with the options: –preset = SUBREAD and –median-filter. CCS data were aligned with the option –preset = CCS. To call variants with pbsv, the command ‘pbsv discover’ was run per sample with default parameters. Next, ‘pbsv call’ was run on the CLR samples and CCS samples independently to create separate VCFs (the argument –ccs was supplied when calling CCS samples), which were then merged using bcftools. The resulting VCF was further processed using GATK 4.6.1.0 to calculate and annotate the VCF with chromosome counts and allele frequencies. Sniffles2 (version 2.5) was run in an analogous manner, with each BAM being processed first using the “sniffles –snf” command (with default options), followed by join calling (using the option –combine-pctseq 0). The resulting VCF was processed using GATK, identical to the pbsv VCF. CuteSV (version 2.1.1) genotyping was performed individually on aligned BAMs, using the defaults for CLR samples: –genotype, –max_cluster_bias_INS 100, –diff_ratio_merging_INS 0.3, –max_cluster_bias_DEL 200, –diff_ratio_merging_DEL 0.5, or the default for CCS samples: –genotype, –max_cluster_bias_INS 1000, –diff_ratio_merging_INS 0.9, –max_cluster_bias_DEL 1000, –diff_ratio_merging_DEL 0.5 [32]. Individual genotypes were combined with SURVIVOR (version 1.0.7) merge, using max distance between breakpoints = 1000 bp, and minimum size of SVs = 50 bp. The merged VCF was then used as the -Ivcf input to force-call each aligned BAM with cuteFC (version 1.0.0), using the previously described defaults for CCS or CLR inputs. The force-called genotypes were merged with SURVIVOR, and the resulting VCF was processed in the same manner as the pbsv and sniffles2 VCF. The variant data have been submitted to the European Variation Archive under project PRJEB102389 [68, 69].

Short read sequencing and data processing

Illumina short read data were obtained from the Macaque Genotype and Phenotype Resource (mGAP), version 2.5, using a previously published NHP-adapted short variant calling and annotation pipeline [19, 20]. This pipeline is based on the GATK best-practice pipeline and utilizes Trimmomatic [70], bwa-mem [71, 72], and GATK [33, 34]. Additionally, the SVs were called from all whole genome datasets using Paragraph (version 2.4a), run with default settings [45]. The per-sample outputs from paragraph were merged into a single VCF using bcftools, followed by identical processing with GATK as described for the PacBio VCFs.

Mobile element annotation

Mobile element annotations were processed separately for insertions and deletions/inversions. Deletions and inversions were annotated by intersecting these VCF entries with Mmul10 RepeatMasker bedfile using bedtools intersect, requiring a minimum of 25% overlap. Entries with the greatest percent overlap were selected when multiple mobile elements intersected with the same SV. For each insertion, the consensus sequence ± 25 bp from the VCF was obtained with “bcftools consensus -H LA” and saved in FASTA format. The sequence was blasted against the rhesus macaque database rmblast-2.14.0 with RepeatMasker (version 4.1.5), with the following options: RepeatMasker -u -a -noisy -species 'macaca mulatta' -rmblast_dir rmblast-2.14.0 -engine RMBLAST. Outputs were parsed with parseRM_merge_interrupted, (version 1.0) to correct coordinates based on the nesting blocks of interrupted repeats. Results were filtered if the repeatmasker match was < 25% overlap or < 25% substitutions in matching region compared to the consensus. If more than one match per insertion was found, the result with the highest % overlap and lowest % substitution rate was chosen.

Kinship and population structure analysis

Inferred kinship coefficients were calculated using the KING algorithm, implemented in Plink2, excluding chromosomes X, Y, MT, and unplaced contigs, limiting to bi-allelic sites [35, 36]. Population structure and PCA were calculated using plink2, with the following options: –king-cutoff 0.05, –maf 0.01, and excluding chromosomes X, Y, MT, and unplaced contigs [36]. Kinship coefficients were calculated using pedigree data using the R package kinship2 [73].

Measurement of concordance between samples and a reference population

To measure concordance between individual samples and a reference population, we developed VariantConcordanceScore, implemented as part of DISCVRseq [74]. This tool accepts a reference VCF with sites and allele frequencies for a set of discriminatory alleles for a given population, along with a VCF containing samples. The tool iterates each reference site and maintains a cumulative sum of allele frequencies, per sample, for each matching site/allele. It also calculates the min/max possible score each sample could obtain based on the set of overlapping sites. The latter is designed to accommodate potentially sparse data, such as whole exome sequencing or reduced representation data. The final concordance score is calculated as: (CumulativeAF-MinPossibleAF)/(MaxPossibleAF-MinPossibleAF), which produces a score bounded by 0–1.

Functional annotation of SVs

SnpEff was used to calculate the predicted impact of each SV, using the NCBI gene build (version 103) [75]. Liftover between MMul_10 and both GRCh37 and GRCh38 was performed using the enhanced bcftools/liftover plugin [50]. To identify overlaps between rhesus macaque and human SVs, bedtools intersect [76] was used. ClinVar SVs were obtained in VCF format from dbVar (nstd102). SVs from gnomAD were obtained from dbVar study nstd166. Repetitive region annotations (used for SINE and LINE elements) were obtained from NCBI for the MMul_10 genome (GCA_003339765.3). Gene upstream regions were defined as 5000 bp upstream of the transcription start site defined in the GTF file (strand-dependent). Excess heterozygosity was calculated using bcftools [76].
