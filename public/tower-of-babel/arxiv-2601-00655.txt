Interpretability-Guided Bi-objective Optimization: Aligning Accuracy and Explainability
Kasra Fouladi, Hamta Rahmani

Abstract
This paper introduces Interpretability-Guided Bi-objective Optimization (IGBO), a framework that trains interpretable models by incorporating structured domain knowledge via a bi-objective formulation. IGBO encodes feature importance hierarchies as a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) via Central Limit Theorem-based construction and uses Temporal Integrated Gradients (TIG) to measure feature importance. To address the Out-of-Distribution (OOD) problem in TIG computation, we propose an Optimal Path Oracle that learns data-manifold-aware integration paths. Theoretical analysis establishes convergence properties via a geometric projection mapping 𝒫 \mathcal{P} and proves robustness to mini-batch noise. Central Limit Theorem-based construction of the interpretability DAG ensures statistical validity of edge orientation decisions. Empirical results on time-series data demonstrate IGBO’s effectiveness in enforcing DAG constraints with minimal accuracy loss, outperforming standard regularization baselines. Keywords: Interpretable Machine Learning, Bi-objective Optimization, Explainable AI (XAI), Temporal Integrated Gradients, Optimal Path Oracle, Structured Regularization, Central Limit Theorem

I
Introduction

Deep learning models have achieved remarkable success in sequential prediction tasks across domains such as healthcare and finance. However, their inherent complexity often renders them opaque “black boxes,” limiting trust and deployment in safety-critical scenarios where understanding the model’s reasoning is as important as its accuracy. Post-hoc explanation methods, including Integrated Gradients
[
24
]
and LIME
[
17
]
, attempt to illuminate this reasoning by analyzing trained models. A fundamental limitation persists: these methods are applied
after
training, providing no guarantee that the model’s internal decision-making aligns with established domain knowledge or desired behavioral constraints during the learning process itself
[
15
]
. Consequently, a model may achieve high accuracy by exploiting spurious, non-causal, or ethically problematic correlations in the data—a risk unacceptable in high-stakes applications.

To build more trustworthy systems, interpretability must be integrated directly into the training objective. A common approach adds a regularization term penalizing undesirable behavior. However, such methods often treat interpretability as a monolithic, soft constraint, offering practitioners limited control over the precise trade-off between accuracy and interpretability. More critically, they lack a formal mechanism to enforce
structured
,
relational
constraints that reflect complex domain expertise, such as hierarchies of feature importance. Additionally, gradient-based attribution methods like Integrated Gradients suffer from the OOD problem when computing gradients along straight-line paths between inputs and baselines.

This work proposes a principled framework to address these gaps:
Interpretability-Guided Bi-objective Optimization (IGBO)
. IGBO formalizes interpretable model training as two interconnected bi-objective optimization problems: (1) training the main model
F_{\theta}
to simultaneously minimize a primary task loss
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
and an interpretability loss
\mathcal{H}(\theta)
via a geometric projection mapping
\mathcal{P}
(Algorithm
1
), and (2) training an Optimal Path Oracle
G
to generate data-manifold-aware integration paths for robust gradient computation. The interpretability loss is derived from two key components: (1) a
Temporal Integrated Gradients (TIG)
metric that provides a differentiable measure of feature importance over time, and (2) a
Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)
defined via Central Limit Theorem-based construction (Section
III-D
) that encodes permissible relative importance relationships between features (e.g., feature
A
should be more important than feature
B
).

Optimal Path Oracle:
To address the OOD problem in TIG computation, we introduce a learnable oracle that generates sequences of intermediate points forming the shortest possible path within the data distribution. This oracle is trained with dual objectives: minimizing path length and ensuring generated points are in-distribution. During integration, we employ a flexible strategy using
K
oracle-generated anchor points and
M-K
interpolated points, creating a tunable computational trade-off between path fidelity and efficiency.

Gradient-Based Optimization:
Both bi-objective problems require navigating trade-offs between competing gradients. We employ a geometric gradient combination rule based on the cosine similarity between gradient vectors. When gradients are aligned, we use a convex combination; when they conflict, we combine their orthogonal components. This approach provides descent guarantees for both objectives while avoiding the need for secondary optimization subroutines.

Application:
The IGBO framework not only provides immediate interpretability guarantees but also opens avenues for human-in-the-loop model alignment and task-specific model specialization, which we explore in later sections (Section
III
,
Discussion
).

•

II
Related Work

Our work synthesizes concepts from interpretable AI, gradient-based multi-objective optimization, robust attribution methods, and the geometry of loss landscapes. We position IGBO within these interconnected research streams.

Integrating Interpretability into Training:
Moving beyond post-hoc explanation methods like LIME
[
17
]
and SHAP
[
13
]
, recent approaches integrate interpretability directly into the training loop. Methods like Right for the Right Reasons
[
18
]
regularize input gradients to align with human annotations, focusing on local, per-instance explanations. Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs)
[
10
]
enforce interpretability by routing predictions through human-specified concepts, requiring concept-level labels. Other works use symbolic constraints or logic rules to guide learning
[
14
]
. In contrast, IGBO enforces
global, relational constraints
on aggregate feature importance via a domain-expert DAG, without requiring per-instance annotations or explicit concept labels. This provides a structured prior over model behavior, complementing approaches that integrate domain knowledge to ensure explanation fidelity
[
15
,
19
]
. Our work also relates to methods imposing sparsity or simplicity constraints but differs in its focus on explicit, relational importance hierarchies.

Multi-Objective Gradient-Based Optimization:
IGBO relies on gradient-based techniques for multi-objective optimization. The Multi-Gradient Descent Algorithm (MGDA)
[
2
,
4
]
provides a general framework for finding descent directions common to multiple objectives, often requiring solving a quadratic program. For the prevalent two-objective case, IGBO’s geometric update rule provides a closed-form solution equivalent to MGDA, eliminating the optimization subroutine. This connects IGBO to gradient-based multi-task learning
[
21
]
, where conflicting gradients are a central challenge, and to Pareto-frontier tracing methods
[
11
]
. Recent work on multi-objective optimization includes adaptive weighting schemes
[
16
]
and gradient manipulation techniques
[
12
]
, but these typically do not consider interpretability-specific constraints as structured objectives.

Gradient-based Attribution and Robust Path Methods:
Our TIG builds directly upon Integrated Gradients (IG)
[
24
]
, inheriting its axiomatic properties. Recent work has identified limitations in IG, particularly its sensitivity to the choice of baseline
[
23
]
and the fact that straight-line integration paths often traverse OOD regions, leading to unreliable attributions
[
7
]
. Several approaches attempt to address these issues: Expected Gradients
[
3
]
averages over multiple baselines; Blurring Integrated Gradients
[
25
]
applies smoothing; and adversarial training methods aim to produce more robust attributions
[
6
]
. Our Optimal Path Oracle introduces a novel direction by
learning
a data-manifold-aware integration path, fundamentally changing how the attribution integral is computed. This approach is conceptually related to works exploring shortest paths on manifolds for explanations
[
5
]
but differs in its fully differentiable, learned formulation and integration into a bi-objective training loop.

Geometry of Loss Landscapes and Gradient Alignment:
Understanding and leveraging the geometry of high-dimensional loss landscapes is crucial for optimization
[
9
]
. The geometric update rule in IGBO explicitly operates within the low-dimensional subspace spanned by two gradients. This connects to research on gradient conflict analysis in multi-task learning
[
26
]
, where gradient directions are manipulated to improve convergence. The use of orthogonal gradient components when objectives conflict relates to techniques for escaping saddle points
[
1
]
and methods that project gradients to find mutually beneficial directions
[
20
]
. IGBO’s approach is tailored specifically for navigating the accuracy-interpretability trade-off with clear geometric intuition, providing a practical instance of these geometric principles.

Probabilistic Graph Construction:
Our method for constructing the interpretability DAG via Central Limit Theorem approximation (Section
III-D
) relates to statistical methods for causal discovery
[
22
]
and confidence-based edge orientation
[
8
]
. However, unlike causal discovery methods that infer structure from data, our approach uses statistical confidence to operationalize expert knowledge about feature importance hierarchies, with theoretical guarantees on acyclicity (Corollary
V-A6
) and transitivity (Theorem
V-A5
).

III
Methodology

The IGBO framework consists of two interconnected bi-objective optimization processes, both of which rely on gradient-based techniques for balancing competing objectives. These processes are:

1.

Training the main sequential model
F_{\theta}
to balance
predictive accuracy
and
interpretability constraints
;

2.

Training an optimal path generator
G
to produce data-manifold-aware integration paths for robust gradient computation in TIG.

This section formalizes the interpretability prior, introduces the TIG-based importance measure, and details the proposed Routing Oracle for reliable path integration.

III-A

Interpretability Prior Definition

Sequential Model Definition

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
denote the input space of multivariate time series of length
T
and feature dimension
d
. A sequential model processes an input
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{T}]\in\mathcal{X}
through a parameterized transition function
g_{\theta}
and an output function
f_{\theta}
. The hidden state
\mathbf{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
at time
t
evolves as:

\mathbf{h}_{t}=g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1}),\quad t=1,\dots,T,

(1)

with
\mathbf{h}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
. The output at time
t
is given by
y_{t}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})\in\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, the full sequence-to-sequence model
F_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{T}
maps an input sequence to an output sequence, where its
t
-th component is
[F_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})]_{t}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{h}_{t-1})
.

Temporal Integrated Gradients.

To obtain a differentiable measure of feature importance over time, we extend Integrated Gradients
[
24
]
to sequential data. For a given input
\mathbf{X}
and a baseline
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
, the importance of feature
k
at time
t
along a path
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{A}
connecting
\gamma(0)=\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
to
\gamma(1)=\mathbf{X}
is defined as:

\text{TIG}_{t,k}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{X},\theta)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(\gamma(\alpha))}{\partial\mathbf{X}_{t,k}}\cdot\frac{d\gamma_{t,k}(\alpha)}{d\alpha}\,d\alpha.

(2)

For the special case of a straight-line path
\gamma_{\text{lin}}(\alpha)=\alpha\mathbf{X}+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
, this simplifies to the standard IG formulation:

\text{TIG}_{t,k}^{\gamma_{\text{lin}}}(\mathbf{X},\theta)=(\mathbf{X}_{t,k}-\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{t,k})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(\gamma_{\text{lin}}(\alpha))}{\partial\mathbf{X}_{t,k}}\,d\alpha.

(3)

This integral aggregates the sensitivity of the model’s output to feature
(t,k)
along the entire path
\gamma
. The standard implementation uses the straight-line path
\gamma_{\text{lin}}(\alpha)=\alpha\mathbf{X}+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
. However, a major drawback of this linear interpolation is that intermediate points
\gamma_{\text{lin}}(\alpha)
often lie outside the training data distribution (OOD). Since
F_{\theta}
is not trained to behave consistently in OOD regions, the resulting gradients—and hence the attributions—can be unreliable and noisy.

III-A
1
Routing Oracle: Learning Data-Manifold-Aware Integration Paths

Motivation and Design Philosophy

To circumvent the OOD problem inherent in straight-line integration paths, we propose a
Routing Oracle
(also referred to as a
Pathfinder
) that learns to generate piecewise-linear paths adhering to the data manifold while minimizing total path length. By ensuring all intermediate integration points reside within regions where the model has been properly trained, the oracle yields stable and reliable gradient computations for TIG.

Flexible Implementation Options

The oracle is designed with practical deployment in mind, offering several configuration choices:

•

Baseline selection:
The baseline
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
can be chosen per sample (e.g., using dataset statistics) or set globally (e.g., a zero sequence). For maximum simplicity,
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
can be fixed as a hyperparameter.

•

Path complexity control:
The number of anchor points
K
can be tuned according to the available computational budget. Larger
K
allows the path to better follow the data manifold but increases inference cost. For deterministic high-throughput deployment,
K
can also be fixed as a hyperparameter.

This flexibility lets practitioners balance attribution quality against computational cost for their specific application.

Oracle Architecture and Recurrence Structure

The routing oracle is implemented as a recurrent neural network
G:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{X}^{*}
that, given a baseline
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
, a target input
\mathbf{X}
, and a desired number of steps
K
, generates a sequence of
K
anchor points:

\displaystyle[\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{K}]=G(\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{X},K;\theta_{G}).

The architecture employs a recurrence that explicitly accounts for the remaining path length. Let
\mathbf{h}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
be a hidden state. Starting with
\mathbf{h}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{p}_{0}=\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
, the
i
-th point is produced as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{i}=r^{\text{output}}_{\theta_{G}}\bigl(K-i+1,\;\mathbf{X},\;\mathbf{p}_{i-1},\;\mathbf{h}_{i-1}\bigr),

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{i}=r^{\text{hidden}}_{\theta_{G}}\bigl(K-i+1,\;\mathbf{X},\;\mathbf{p}_{i-1},\;\mathbf{h}_{i-1}\bigr),

where
r
is a learned function. The explicit inclusion of the remaining-step count
(K-i+1)
allows the network to plan the path efficiently and adapt its behavior as the endpoint approaches.

Two-Phase Training Procedure

Training is performed in two distinct phases to ensure stability and reuse existing knowledge of the data distribution:

1.

Discriminator pre-training:
We employ the discriminator
D(\cdot;\theta_{D})
from a pre-trained Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) as a frozen
validity assessor
. This discriminator, already trained to distinguish real data from synthetic samples, provides a reliable estimate of
\Pr(\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{A})
without requiring additional training.

2.

Oracle training:
With
D
fixed, we train
G
to minimize a composite loss that combines path shortness and point validity:

\mathcal{L}_{G}(\theta_{G})=\mathbb{E}_{(K,\mathbf{X}^{\prime},\mathbf{X})}\bigl[\mathcal{L}_{\text{path}},\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{valid}}\bigr],

(4)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{\text{path}}=\\
&\frac{\|\mathbf{X}^{\prime}-\mathbf{p}_{1}\|^{2}+\|\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{p}_{K}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}\|\mathbf{p}_{i+1}-\mathbf{p}_{i}\|^{2}}{K+1},\end{split}

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{valid}}=\frac{-1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\log D(\mathbf{p}_{i};\theta_{D}).

(6)

Training the oracle is a bi-objective optimization problem.

Integration Strategy

For the actual TIG computation with oracle-generated paths, we approximate the integral in Eq. (
2
) using numerical integration. Given
M
integration points
\mathbf{q}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{q}_{M}
along the path (including oracle anchors and interpolated points), we compute:

\text{TIG}_{t,k}^{\text{Oracle}}(\mathbf{X},\theta)\approx\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\frac{\partial F_{\theta}(\mathbf{q}_{j})}{\partial\mathbf{q}_{j,t,k}}\cdot(\mathbf{q}_{j+1,t,k}-\mathbf{q}_{j,t,k}),

(7)

which corresponds to a Riemann sum approximation of the line integral along the piecewise-linear path connecting the oracle’s anchor points.

Transferability and Reusability

A significant practical advantage of the Routing Oracle is its transferability across models. Once trained on a representative dataset
\mathcal{D}
, the same oracle can be applied to compute TIG for
any
model
F_{\theta}
trained on
\mathcal{D}
, without retraining. This enables:

•

Consistent evaluation:
All models are assessed using identical integration paths, allowing fair comparison of their feature-importance patterns.

•

Computational economy:
The costly training phase is performed only once per data domain.

•

Streamlined deployment:
New models can be interpreted immediately using a pre-existing, validated oracle, which is especially valuable in regulated domains (e.g., healthcare or finance) where explanation consistency is critical.

III-B

Argument Satisfaction Function
H_{k}(\mathbf{X},\theta)

We aggregate temporal attributions to obtain a single scalar score for each feature
k
over the entire input sequence. Rather than representing raw feature importance,
H_{k}(\mathbf{X},\theta)
is interpreted as an
Argument Satisfaction
score: it quantifies how well the cumulative Temporal Integrated Gradients of feature
k
explain the model’s deviation from a chosen baseline across time.

At each time step, we measure the residual between the observed output change and the portion attributed to feature
k
, and convert this residual into a bounded agreement score via a sigmoid mapping:

\begin{split}h_{t,k}(\mathbf{X},\theta)&=\left|F_{\theta}(\mathbf{X})[t]-F_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})[t]-\sum_{i=1}^{t}\text{TIG}_{i,k}[t]\right|,\\
H_{k}(\mathbf{X},\theta)&=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{1+\beta\cdot h_{t,k}}\in[0,1],\;\beta>0.\end{split}

(8)

Higher values of
H_{k}
indicate stronger satisfaction of the explanatory argument that feature
k
contributes meaningfully to the model’s prediction. The normalization ensures comparability across features and yields a smooth, differentiable quantity suitable for optimization.

Interpretability Graph DAG as a Structured Prior:
Domain expertise is encoded as a Directed Acyclic Graph
G=(V,E)
. Each node
v\in V
corresponds to an input feature. A directed edge
v\rightarrow u
implies the semantic prior that, on average over the data distribution
\mathcal{D}
, feature
u
should exhibit a higher
Argument Satisfaction
score than feature
v
. To allow for controlled flexibility and avoid overly rigid constraints, we formalize this as the requirement that the
expected
difference in importance scores lies within a specified interval:

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[H_{v}(\mathbf{X},\theta)-H_{u}(\mathbf{X},\theta)\right]\in[\epsilon_{u,v},\delta_{u,v}],

(9)

where
0<\epsilon_{u,v}\leq\delta_{u,v}
are user-defined hyperparameters. The lower bound
\epsilon_{u,v}
enforces a minimum meaningful margin of importance, ensuring
u
is demonstrably more important than
v
. The upper bound
\delta_{u,v}
prevents the optimization from pushing the difference to unrealistic extremes, which could destabilize training or force the model into pathological parameter regions. This interval-based formulation offers more nuanced control than a simple inequality constraint.

III-C

Interpretability Loss

To integrate the DAG constraint from Eq. (
9
) into the training objective, we construct a differentiable interpretability loss function
\mathcal{H}:\Theta\to[0,1]
. Since the expectation in Eq. (
9
) is over the data distribution, we work with its Monte Carlo estimate computed on a mini-batch.

For a batch of data
B=\{\mathbf{X}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{X}^{(|B|)}\}
sampled i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
, we first compute the empirical mean importance difference for each edge
(u,v)\in E
:

d_{u,v}(\theta;B)=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{\mathbf{X}\in B}\left[H_{v}(\mathbf{X},\theta)-H_{u}(\mathbf{X},\theta)\right].

(10)

The batch interpretability loss is then defined as the sum of hinge-like penalties that activate when
d_{u,v}
falls outside the desired interval
[\epsilon_{u,v},\delta_{u,v}]
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}(\theta;B)=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\Big[&\max(\epsilon_{u,v}-d_{u,v}(\theta;B),\;0)\\
+&\max(d_{u,v}(\theta;B)-\delta_{u,v},\;0)\Big].\end{split}

(11)

The first term penalizes differences that are too small (below
\epsilon_{u,v}
), and the second term penalizes differences that are too large (exceeding
\delta_{u,v}
). If
d_{u,v}(\theta;B)\in[\epsilon_{u,v},\delta_{u,v}]
, both terms evaluate to zero for that edge, incurring no penalty. The full loss
\mathcal{H}(\theta)
is defined as the expectation of
\mathcal{H}(\theta;B)
over batches
B\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}
. Crucially, because
H_{k}(\mathbf{X},\theta)
is differentiable with respect to
\theta
(flowing through
F_{\theta}
, the path integral, and the aggregation), the gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{H}(\theta;B)
can be computed efficiently via standard backpropagation through the entire computation graph.

III-D

Graph Construction via Central Limit Approximation

This subsection gives a concise, implementation-oriented description of the DAG construction procedure and points to the formal probabilistic statements and proofs collected in Section
V-A
.

Overview

We build a directed edge
u\to v
when the batch-mean importance score of feature
u
is sufficiently larger than that of
v
with high confidence. All probabilistic claims below are justified by the CLT-based lemmas and theorems in Section
V-A
.

Per-batch statistics

For a batch
B
of size
|B|
define the batch mean

\displaystyle s_{k}(B)=\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{\mathbf{X}\in B}H_{k}(\mathbf{X},\theta).

Under the assumptions of Lemma
V-A2
(i.i.d. sampling and finite second moments) each
s_{k}(B)
is approximately normal with mean
\mu_{k}
and variance
\mathrm{Var}(H_{k})/|B|
. When multiple independent batches
B^{(1)},\dots,B^{(N)}
are available, the per-batch means
s_{k}^{(i)}
are i.i.d.; see Lemma
V-A2
and the discussion that follows it.

Edge orientation rule (practical form)

For a candidate ordered pair
(u,v)
let
d_{u,v}^{(i)}=s_{u}^{(i)}-s_{v}^{(i)}
. Using the CLT and the variance expression of Lemma
V-A3
we obtain the normal approximation

\displaystyle d_{u,v}^{(i)}\approx\mathcal{N}\!\Big(\mu_{u}-\mu_{v},\;\sigma_{uv}^{2}\Big),

\displaystyle\sigma_{uv}^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(H_{u})+\mathrm{Var}(H_{v})-2\mathrm{Cov}(H_{u},H_{v})}{|B|}.

Hence the plug-in estimator for the edge probability is

\displaystyle\widehat{P}(s_{u}>s_{v})=\Phi\!\Big(\frac{\bar{s}_{u}-\bar{s}_{v}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(s_{u}-s_{v})}}\Big),

where
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(s_{u}-s_{v})
is obtained either by within-batch variance divided by
|B|
(Lemma
V-A3
) or by the empirical between-batch variance of
\{s_{u}^{(i)}-s_{v}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. The numerical decision is

\displaystyle u\to v\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\widehat{P}(s_{u}>s_{v})>\alpha,

for a chosen
\alpha>0.5
. The equivalence between the probabilistic rule and the mean-ordering (for
\alpha=0.5
) is formalized in Theorem
V-A4
.

Acyclicity and transitivity

- If
\alpha=0.5
, Theorem
V-A4
shows the rule reduces to ordering by population means
\mu_{k}
; orienting edges by this total order yields a DAG (Corollary
V-A6
).
- For
\alpha>0.5
, transitivity (and hence acyclicity) follows under the margin/variance conditions made explicit in Theorem
V-A5
; the required triangle‑type bound on standard deviations is a direct consequence of the
L^{2}
triangle inequality (see the proof of Theorem
V-A5
).

Practical recommendations (summary)

Refer to Section
V-A
for formal statements and proofs. In practice:

•

Prefer the between-batch empirical variance of
s_{k}
when
N
is moderate to large (robust uncertainty quantification).

•

If only a single batch is available, estimate
\mathrm{Var}(H_{k})
with the unbiased sample variance (divide by
|B|-1
) and obtain
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(s_{k})=\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(H_{k})/|B|
.

•

Use bootstrap or permutation tests and/or truncated‑normal corrections when
|B|
is small or
\mu_{k}
is near the boundaries
[0,1]
(see Remark
V-A7
).

•

To ensure transitivity in practice, check the margin/variance conditions of Theorem
V-A5
or increase
|B|
/
\alpha
as needed.

III-E

Gradient-Based Optimization via Projection Mapping

The IGBO framework gives rise to two distinct bi-objective optimization problems: (1) training the main model
F_{\theta}
by minimizing
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
and
\mathcal{H}(\theta)
simultaneously, and (2) training the path generator
G(\theta_{G})
by minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{path}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{valid}}
. Both scenarios require combining gradients from competing objectives to find a parameter update direction that respects both.

Let
\mathbf{g}_{1}=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}_{2}=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{2}
denote the gradients of two objectives
\mathcal{J}_{1}
and
\mathcal{J}_{2}
with respect to the parameters of interest (either
\theta
or
\theta_{G}
).

Convergence Conditions:

Before proceeding to the projection method, we first identify three conditions under which simultaneous descent is either impossible or unnecessary. If any of these conditions holds, the algorithm terminates early without proceeding to the projection step:

1.

Maximally Conflicting Gradients
:
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}=-\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|
(gradients are exactly opposite)

2.

Negligible First Gradient
:
\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|<\epsilon
(first objective is near optimum)

3.

Negligible Second Gradient
:
\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|<\epsilon
(second objective is near optimum)

In these cases, specialized handling is required rather than the projection method described below.

Fundamental Limitation of Convex Combinations:

A naive approach uses a strict convex combination (in order to avoid checking many other cases
\lambda=0,1
is ignored):

\mathbf{v}_{\text{naive}}(\lambda)=\lambda\mathbf{g}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{g}_{2},\quad\lambda\in(0,1).

(12)

However, this fails when gradients conflict: there exists no
\lambda\in(0,1)
that guarantees
\mathbf{v}_{\text{naive}}(\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}>0
and
\mathbf{v}_{\text{naive}}(\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}>0
simultaneously. In fact:

•

For
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}\geq 0
: for every
\lambda\in(0,1)
works

•

For
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}<0
:
just some of

\lambda\in(0,1)
guarantee both

Complete Solution via Projection Mapping:

Computational Complexity Analysis

Training with IGBO introduces additional computational overhead compared to standard training, primarily due to the computation of TIG and the interpretability loss. We analyze the time and memory complexity for both standard training and IGBO.

Standard Training:

For a sequential model processing input of length
T
, the per-iteration complexity is dominated by forward and backward propagation:

•

Forward Pass:

O(T\cdot F)
, where
F
is the cost per time step.

•

Backward Pass (Primary Loss):

O(T\cdot F)
.

•

Total Time:

O(T\cdot F)
per sample.

•

Memory:

O(T\cdot h)
which
h
is consumed additional memory per step.

IGBO Training:

The additional computations in IGBO include:

1.

Oracle Query:
finding
K
points that satisfy the mentioned conditions
O(K\cdot G)
.

2.

TIG Computation:
Requires computing gradients of output w.r.t input at
M
integration points. For one sample:
O(M\cdot T\cdot F)
.

3.

Feature Importance
H_{k}
:
Aggregation over time:
O(T^{2}\cdot d)
, where
d
is feature dimension.

4.

Interpretability Loss:
Edge-wise comparisons:
O(|E|)
.

5.

Gradient of
\mathcal{H}
:
Backpropagation through TIG computation:
O(M\cdot T\cdot F)
.

Parallelization Effects:

•

Without GPU (Sequential):

\displaystyle\textbf{Time}=O(\text{MAX}\{M\cdot T\cdot F,K\cdot G,T^{2}\cdot d,d^{2}\}),

\displaystyle\text{ since in simple DAGs }|E|\in O(d^{2})

(which is usually equivalent to
O(M\cdot T\cdot F+K\cdot G)
)

•

With GPU (Parallel):

–

The Oracle finding each point that satisfies the mentioned conditions:
G\rightarrow O(\mathcal{G})
.

–

TIG computations for different
\alpha
values parallelizable:
M\rightarrow O(\log M)

–

Feature Importance
H_{k}
computations parallelizable:
T^{2}\cdot d\rightarrow O(\log T)

–

Edge computations parallelizable:
|E|\rightarrow O(\log d)

–

O(\log d)\subset O(F)
because in every forward step model cannot compute the scalar output in a time complexity better than
O(\log d)
.

–

Time
:
O(\log M\cdot T\cdot F+K\cdot\mathcal{G})

Memory Overhead:

IGBO requires storing intermediate gradients for TIG computation and using the oracle:

•

Standard:

O(T\cdot h)

•

IGBO (Sequential TIG computation + Oracle query):

O(T\cdot h+Q)

•

IGBO (Parallel TIG computation + Oracle query):

(for computing all
M
points simultaneously) Additionally, with time complexity trade-off we have:

\textbf{Memory}=O(M^{a}\cdot T\cdot h+\mathcal{Q})

–

\textbf{Time}=O(M^{1-a}\cdot T\cdot F+K\cdot\mathcal{G})

where

.

The geometric update function (Eqs. (
13
)) adds only

operations in sequential processing and

V-A

Graph Construction via CLT

V-A
1
Preliminaries and notation

Let
H_{k}:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
be measurable (and differentiable in
\theta
where needed). For a single batch
B=\{\mathbf{X}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
sampled i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
define

\displaystyle\mu_{k}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{D}}[H_{k}(\mathbf{X},\theta)],\qquad s_{k}(B):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{k}(\mathbf{X}^{(i)},\theta).

For a pair
(u,v)
set

\displaystyle D^{(i)}:=H_{u}(\mathbf{X}^{(i)},\theta)-H_{v}(\mathbf{X}^{(i)},\theta),

\displaystyle d_{u,v}(B):=s_{u}-s_{v}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{(i)}.

V-A
2
Lemma 2 (CLT for bounded importance scores)

Claim.
If
\{\mathbf{X}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. from
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathrm{Var}(H_{k}(\mathbf{X},\theta))<\infty
, then

\displaystyle s_{k}\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_{k},\;\frac{\mathrm{Var}(H_{k})}{n}\right)\quad(n\to\infty).

Proof.

Apply the classical CLT to the i.i.d. bounded variables
H_{k}(\mathbf{X}^{(i)},\theta)
. Since
\mathbb{E}[H_{k}]=\mu_{k}
and
\mathrm{Var}(H_{k})<\infty
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,(s_{k}-\mu_{k})\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Var}(H_{k})).

Rescaling yields the stated asymptotic distribution for
s_{k}
. Boundedness ensures finite moments and that any Gaussian mass outside
[0,1]
vanishes as
n\to\infty
.
∎

V-A
3
Lemma 3 (Mean and variance of shared-batch differences)

Claim.
With
D^{(i)}
as above,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{u,v}(B)]=\mu_{u}-\mu_{v},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\big(d_{u,v}(B)\big)=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\mathrm{Var}(H_{u})+\mathrm{Var}(H_{v})-2\mathrm{Cov}(H_{u},H_{v})\Big).

Proof.

Linearity of expectation gives
\mathbb{E}[d_{u,v}]=\mathbb{E}[H_{u}]-\mathbb{E}[H_{v}]=\mu_{u}-\mu_{v}
. Independence across samples implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\!\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{(i)}\Big)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Var}(D^{(i)})=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}(D^{(1)}).

Expanding
\mathrm{Var}(H_{u}-H_{v})=\mathrm{Var}(H_{u})+\mathrm{Var}(H_{v})-2\mathrm{Cov}(H_{u},H_{v})
yields the result.
∎

V-A
4
Theorem 4 (Probability threshold equivalence)

Claim.
Suppose
d_{u,v}(B)
is approximated by

\displaystyle d_{u,v}(B)\approx\mathcal{N}\!\big(\mu_{uv},\sigma_{uv}^{2}\big),\quad\mu_{uv}:=\mu_{u}-\mu_{v},

\displaystyle\sigma_{uv}^{2}:=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(H_{u})+\mathrm{Var}(H_{v})-2\mathrm{Cov}(H_{u},H_{v})}{n}.

Then for any
\alpha>0.5
,

\displaystyle P(s_{u}>s_{v})>\alpha\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mu_{uv}>z_{\alpha}\,\sigma_{uv},

and in particular

\displaystyle P(s_{u}>s_{v})>0.5\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mu_{u}>\mu_{v}.

Proof.

Under the normal approximation,

\displaystyle P(s_{u}>s_{v})=P(d_{u,v}>0)=1-\Phi\!\Big(\frac{0-\mu_{uv}}{\sigma_{uv}}\Big)=\Phi\!\Big(\frac{\mu_{uv}}{\sigma_{uv}}\Big).

Thus
P(s_{u}>s_{v})>\alpha
iff
\Phi(\mu_{uv}/\sigma_{uv})>\alpha
, i.e.
\mu_{uv}/\sigma_{uv}>z_{\alpha}
. Setting
\alpha=0.5
gives
z_{0.5}=0
and the stated equivalence.
∎

V-A
5
Theorem 5 (Transitivity under margin/variance conditions)

Claim.
Let
\alpha>0.5
. If

\displaystyle P(s_{u}>s_{v})>\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad P(s_{v}>s_{w})>\alpha,

and the corresponding margin inequalities hold

\displaystyle\mu_{u}-\mu_{v}\geq z_{\alpha}\,\sigma_{uv},\qquad\mu_{v}-\mu_{w}\geq z_{\alpha}\,\sigma_{vw},

then, using the triangle inequality for
L^{2}
norms,

\displaystyle\sigma_{uw}\leq\sigma_{uv}+\sigma_{vw},

and consequently
P(s_{u}>s_{w})>\alpha
.

Proof.

From the margin inequalities,

\displaystyle\mu_{u}-\mu_{w}=(\mu_{u}-\mu_{v})+(\mu_{v}-\mu_{w})\geq z_{\alpha}(\sigma_{uv}+\sigma_{vw}).

Define
X:=H_{u}-H_{v}
and
Y:=H_{v}-H_{w}
; then
H_{u}-H_{w}=X+Y
. Interpreting standard deviations as
L^{2}
norms,
\sigma_{uv}=\|X\|_{2}
,
\sigma_{vw}=\|Y\|_{2}
, and
\sigma_{uw}=\|X+Y\|_{2}
. By Minkowski (triangle inequality in
L^{2}
),

\displaystyle\sigma_{uw}=\|X+Y\|_{2}\leq\|X\|_{2}+\|Y\|_{2}=\sigma_{uv}+\sigma_{vw}.

Combining the two displayed inequalities yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{u}-\mu_{w}}{\sigma_{uw}}\geq\frac{z_{\alpha}(\sigma_{uv}+\sigma_{vw})}{\sigma_{uw}}\geq z_{\alpha},

hence
P(s_{u}>s_{w})=\Phi\big((\mu_{u}-\mu_{w})/\sigma_{uw}\big)\geq\Phi(z_{\alpha})=\alpha
.
∎

V-A
6
Corollary 3 (Acyclicity of the induced graph)

Claim.
The orientation rule
u\to v
iff
P(s_{u}>s_{v})>0.5
induces a strict total order consistent with
\{\mu_{k}\}
and therefore a DAG. For
\alpha>0.5
, if all observed pairwise decisions satisfy the margin/variance conditions of Theorem
V-A5
, the induced graph is acyclic.

Proof.

For
\alpha=0.5
, Theorem
V-A4
implies
u\to v
iff
\mu_{u}>\mu_{v}
. Ordering nodes by decreasing
\mu_{k}
yields a strict total order; orienting edges according to this order cannot produce directed cycles.

For
\alpha>0.5
, assume every observed directed edge satisfies the margin condition
\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}\geq z_{\alpha}\sigma_{ij}
. By repeated application of Theorem
V-A5
along any directed path, the composed margin accumulates while the composed standard deviation is bounded by the sum of pairwise standard deviations; thus every directed path implies the corresponding direct pairwise inequality and cannot close into a directed cycle without violating the margin conditions. Hence the graph is acyclic.
∎

V-A

Remarks on finite-sample corrections and estimation

When
n
is moderate and some
\mu_{k}
lie near
0
or
1
, the truncated-normal nature of
s_{k}
can bias the Gaussian plug-in probability. Practical remedies include computing
P(s_{u}>s_{v})
under a bivariate truncated-normal model, using bootstrap/permutation tests for
s_{u}-s_{v}
, or conservatively inflating
\sigma_{uv}
. These corrections vanish asymptotically.

V-A
8
Estimator guidance

When multiple independent batches
B^{(1)},\dots,B^{(N)}
are available, prefer the between-batch empirical variance of the batch means

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(s_{k})=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(s_{k}^{(i)}-\bar{s}_{k}\big)^{2}

for uncertainty quantification. If only a single batch is available, estimate
\mathrm{Var}(H_{k})
with the unbiased sample variance (divide by
n-1
) and obtain
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(s_{k})=\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(H_{k})/n
. Use bootstrap or permutation methods when boundary effects or small
n
make the CLT approximation questionable.

These lemmas, theorems, and remarks provide the formal backbone for the construction described in Section

; refer to them when invoking probabilistic claims or when justifying acyclicity and transitivity properties of the induced interpretability DAG.

V-B
1
Preliminary Definitions

Let
\mathbf{g}_{1}=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}_{2}=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{2}
be two non-zero gradient vectors. Define the set of
simultaneous descent directions
as:

C_{\text{SD}}=\{\mathbf{v}=\alpha\mathbf{g}_{1}+\beta\mathbf{g}_{2}:\alpha,\beta>0,\,\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}>0\text{ and }\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}>0\}.

(14)

V-B
2
Lemma 1 ”Limitation of Naive Convex Combination”

Claim:
When
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}<0
, there exists no
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that the naive convex combination
\mathbf{v}_{\text{naive}}(\lambda)=\lambda\mathbf{g}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{g}_{2}
guarantees
\mathbf{v}_{\text{naive}}(\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}>0
and
\mathbf{v}_{\text{naive}}(\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}>0
simultaneously for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Only some values of
\lambda
satisfy both conditions.

Proof.

Compute the dot products:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\text{naive}}(\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}+(1-\lambda)(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\text{naive}}(\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})+(1-\lambda)\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}.

For
\mathbf{v}_{\text{naive}}(\lambda)
to be a simultaneous descent direction, we need both expressions positive. This gives two inequalities:

1.
\lambda\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}+(1-\lambda)(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})>0

2.
\lambda(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})+(1-\lambda)\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}>0

Since
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}<0
, the first inequality implies:

\displaystyle\lambda>\frac{-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}{\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}.

The second inequality implies:

\displaystyle\lambda<\frac{\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}.

Thus,
\lambda
must satisfy:

\displaystyle\frac{-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}{\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}<\lambda<\frac{\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}.

This interval is non-empty only if:

\displaystyle\frac{-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}{\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}<\frac{\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}.

Cross-multiplying (note denominators are positive since
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}<0
) yields:

\displaystyle-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})(\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}))<\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}(\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})).

Simplifying:

\displaystyle-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}+(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})^{2}<\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}-\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}).

Cancelling
-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}
on both sides gives:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})^{2}<\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2},

which always holds by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality when
\mathbf{g}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}_{2}
are not collinear. However, when
\mathbf{g}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}_{2}
are exactly opposite (
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}=-\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|
), the interval collapses. For general conflicting gradients (
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}<0
), the interval exists but does not cover all
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Thus, not every
\lambda\in(0,1)
yields a simultaneous descent direction.
∎

3
Theorem 1 ”Aligned Gradients Case”

Claim:
If
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}\geq 0
, then for any
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the vector
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)=\lambda\mathbf{g}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{g}_{2}
belongs to
C_{\text{SD}}
. Moreover, every vector in
C_{\text{SD}}
can be expressed as
\eta\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\eta>0
.

Proof.

First, verify the descent property. For
\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{g}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{g}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}+(1-\lambda)(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})\geq\lambda\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}>0,

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})+(1-\lambda)\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}\geq(1-\lambda)\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}>0,

since
\lambda\in(0,1)
,
\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|,\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|>0
, and
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}\geq 0
. Hence
\mathbf{v}\in C_{\text{SD}}
.

For the converse, let
\mathbf{w}=\alpha\mathbf{g}_{1}+\beta\mathbf{g}_{2}\in C_{\text{SD}}
with
\alpha,\beta>0
. Choose
\lambda=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\in(0,1)
and
\eta=\alpha+\beta>0
. Then:

\displaystyle\eta\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)=(\alpha+\beta)\left(\frac{\alpha\mathbf{g}_{1}+\beta\mathbf{g}_{2}}{\alpha+\beta}\right)=\alpha\mathbf{g}_{1}+\beta\mathbf{g}_{2}=\mathbf{w}.

Thus
\mathbf{w}
has the desired form.
∎

4
Theorem 2 ”Conflicting Gradients Case”

Claim:
If
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}<0
, then for any
\lambda\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)=\lambda\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2},

where
\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}=\mathbf{g}_{2}-\frac{\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}}\mathbf{g}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}=\mathbf{g}_{1}-\frac{\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}}\mathbf{g}_{2}

C_{\text{SD}}
. Moreover, every vector in
C_{\text{SD}}
can be expressed as
\eta\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\eta>0
.

Proof.

First, note that
\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{g}_{1}
, and
\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{g}_{2}
. Compute:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}

\displaystyle=0.

Now let
\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda(\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1})+(1-\lambda)(\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1})=(1-\lambda)\|\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}\|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda(\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})+(1-\lambda)(\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})=\lambda\|\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}\|^{2}.

Since
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}<0

and

\mathbf{g}_{2}

\|\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}\|^{2}>0
and
\|\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}\|^{2}>0

\lambda\in(0,1)

\mathbf{v}\in C_{\text{SD}}
.

For the converse, let
\mathbf{w}=\alpha\mathbf{g}_{1}+\beta\mathbf{g}_{2}\in C_{\text{SD}}
with
\alpha,\beta>0
. We seek
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\eta>0
such that:

\displaystyle\eta\left(\lambda\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}\right)=\alpha\mathbf{g}_{1}+\beta\mathbf{g}_{2}.

Express
\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}
and
\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}
in terms of
\mathbf{g}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{1}^{\perp 2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{1}-\frac{\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}}\mathbf{g}_{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{2}^{\perp 1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{g}_{2}-\frac{\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}}\mathbf{g}_{1}.

Substitute into the equation and equate coefficients of
\mathbf{g}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\eta\left(\lambda\left(-\frac{\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}}\right)+(1-\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha,

\displaystyle\eta\left(\lambda+(1-\lambda)\left(-\frac{\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\beta.

This is a linear system in
\eta
and
\lambda
. Solving yields:

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{\alpha\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}-\alpha\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}{(\alpha+\beta)\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}-(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})(\alpha\|\mathbf{g}_{2}\|^{2}+\beta\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2})},

\displaystyle\eta=\frac{\alpha\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}-\beta(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}{\|\mathbf{g}_{1}\|^{2}-\lambda(\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2})}.

Since
\alpha,\beta>0
and
\mathbf{g}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}<0
, the denominator for
\lambda
is positive, and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Also
\eta>0
. Thus every
\mathbf{w}\in C_{\text{SD}}
can be represented as
\eta\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)
.
∎

5
Corollary 1 ”Descent Property”

Claim:
For any valid
\lambda\in(0,1)
and step size
\eta>0
sufficiently small, the update
\theta^{\prime}=\theta-\eta\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)
decreases both objectives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}(\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{1}(\theta)-\eta C_{1}(\lambda)+O(\eta^{2}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{2}(\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{2}(\theta)-\eta C_{2}(\lambda)+O(\eta^{2}),

where
C_{1}(\lambda),C_{2}(\lambda)>0
.

Proof.

By Taylor expansion:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1}(\theta^{\prime})=\mathcal{J}_{1}(\theta)-\eta\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}+O(\eta^{2}).

From Theorems
V-B3
and
V-B4
, we have
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}>0
. Set
C_{1}(\lambda)=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{1}>0
. Similarly for
\mathcal{J}_{2}
with
C_{2}(\lambda)=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)\cdot\mathbf{g}_{2}>0
.
∎

Noise Robustness Properties

V-C
1
Theorem 3 ”Robustness to Gradient Noise”

Claim:
Let
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1}=\mathbf{g}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2}=\mathbf{g}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{2}
be noisy gradient estimates with
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i})=\sigma_{i}^{2}I
. Then the expected update direction
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1},\tilde{\mathbf{g}_{2}},\lambda)]
is
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)+O(\sigma^{2})
, and the variance is
O(\sigma^{2})
.

Proof.

The projection function
\mathcal{P}
is smooth (piecewise linear) in its arguments. By Taylor expansion around
(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{1},\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{2},\lambda)=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2},\lambda)+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial\mathbf{g}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}+O(\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^{2}).

Taking expectation, the linear terms vanish since
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}]=0
, leaving bias of order
\sigma^{2}
. The variance comes from the linear terms:
\mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial\mathbf{g}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}\right)=O(\sigma_{i}^{2})
.
∎

2
Corollary 2 ”Effect of Batch Size”

Claim:
If gradients are estimated using mini-batches of size
B
, then
\sigma^{2}\propto 1/B
. Thus both bias and variance of the update direction scale as
O(1/B)
.

Proof.

For i.i.d. samples, the variance of the gradient estimate scales inversely with batch size:
\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{0}^{2}/B
. Substituting into Theorem
V-C1
gives the result.
∎

Discussion

IGBO’s framework for encoding domain knowledge via DAG constraints opens several interesting research directions. First, the DAG construction methodology naturally accommodates human expert input when gradient information is unavailable. In deployment scenarios where models are treated as black-box computational functions, expert-provided feature importance hierarchies can directly instantiate the DAG, making IGBO applicable even when model internals are inaccessible. Second, the interpretability DAG extracted from a well-performing model provides a blueprint for task-specific model compression. By identifying which feature relationships are essential for a particular task, IGBO can guide the design of smaller, specialized models that retain performance while eliminating unnecessary complexity. This represents a shift from post-hoc explanation toward proactive model design informed by interpretability constraints.

VI
Conclusion and Future Work

We have introduced IGBO, a novel framework for training machine learning models to adhere to domain-specific interpretability constraints. By formalizing these constraints via a DAG acting on a differentiable feature importance function
H_{k}(\mathbf{X},\theta)
, and by deriving a geometrically motivated update rule that dynamically decomposes task and interpretability gradients, IGBO provides a principled method to navigate the accuracy-interpretability trade-off. Theoretical analysis proves convergence to Pareto-stationary points via projection mapping
\mathcal{P}
(Theorems
V-B3
,
V-B4
) and establishes robustness to gradient noise (Theorem
V-C1
). Our CLT-based graph construction provides statistical guarantees on DAG acyclicity and transitivity.

The empirical evaluation demonstrated IGBO’s ability to enforce complex DAG constraints with less than 5% relative accuracy degradation compared to standard training, while achieving over 80% DAG satisfaction rates. The Optimal Path Oracle successfully generated in-distribution integration paths, improving attribution consistency by over 20% compared to linear paths.

Limitations and Future Work:

•

Computational Overhead:
The computation of
H_{k}(\mathbf{X},\theta)
, involving multiple gradient evaluations for the path integral, adds overhead per training step. While parallelizable, future work should investigate more efficient, approximate attribution methods that retain differentiability but are cheaper to compute.

•

DAG Specification and Human Expertise:
The current framework requires the DAG and intervals
[\epsilon,\delta]
to be specified by an expert. In scenarios where gradient access is limited or models are treated as black-box computational functions, human expert annotations can directly provide feature importance rankings. Future work could explore using pairwise comparison data from domain experts to construct the DAG, bypassing gradient computation entirely. This human-in-the-loop approach aligns IGBO with real-world deployment where interpretability constraints often originate from expert knowledge rather than model internals.

•

Task-Specialized Model Compression:
Beyond interpretability itself, IGBO enables a pathway toward task-specific model optimization. Given a large model that performs well on a particular task, the structured interpretability DAG extracted under IGBO captures task-relevant feature dependencies. This structure can guide the training of a smaller, task-specialized model that preserves performance while discarding unnecessary capacity. Unlike parameter-efficient fine-tuning approaches that adapt large models in-place, this perspective emphasizes constructing compact models optimized explicitly for a single task, using interpretability as a mechanism for structured knowledge transfer.

•

Extension to More Objectives:
The geometric update rule is formulated for two objectives. Extending this principle to handle three or more concurrent objectives (e.g., accuracy, interpretability, fairness, robustness) is a challenging but promising direction for building models that satisfy multiple desiderata simultaneously.

•

Theoretical Refinements:
Further analysis could establish non-asymptotic convergence rates or study the geometry of the Pareto frontier discovered by IGBO.

In conclusion, IGBO offers a rigorous and practical pathway to move beyond post-hoc explanations, enabling the creation of models whose internal decision-making processes are inherently guided by, and thus more aligned with, human-understandable semantic structures.
