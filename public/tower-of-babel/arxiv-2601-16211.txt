Why Can’t I Open My Drawer? Mitigating Object-Driven Shortcuts in Zero-Shot Compositional Action Recognition
Geo Ahn, Inwoong Lee, Taeoh Kim, Minho Shim, Dongyoon Wee, Jinwoo Choi

Abstract
We study Compositional Video Understanding (CVU), where models must recognize verbs and objects and compose them to generalize to unseen combinations. We find that existing Zero-Shot Compositional Action Recognition (ZS-CAR) models fail primarily due to an overlooked failure mode: object-driven verb shortcuts . Through systematic analysis, we show that this behavior arises from two intertwined factors—severe sparsity and skewness of compositional supervision, and the asymmetric learning difficulty between verbs and objects. As training progresses, the existing ZS-CAR model increasingly ignores visual evidence and overfits to co-occurrence statistics. Consequently, the existing model does not gain the benefit of compositional recognition in unseen verb-object compositions. To address this, we propose RCORE , a simple and effective framework that enforces temporally grounded verb learning. RCORE introduces (i) a composition-aware augmentation that diversifies verb–object combinations without corrupting motion cues, and (ii) a temporal order regularization loss that penalizes shortcut behaviors by explicitly modeling temporal structure. Across two benchmarks—Sth-com and our newly constructed EK100-com— RCORE significantly improves unseen composition accuracy, reduces reliance on co-occurrence bias, and achieves consistently positive compositional gaps. Our findings reveal object-driven shortcuts as a critical limiting factor in ZS-CAR and demonstrate that addressing them is essential for robust compositional video understanding. The code is available at https://github.com/KHU-VLL/RCORE .

Introduction

Many human actions can be decomposed into two semantic primitives—verbs and objects—and robust video understanding requires recognizing each component and reasoning over their interaction as a composition
goyal2017something
;
mat2020sthelse
;
damen2022ek100
;
diba2020hvu
;
bae2024devias
.
Zero-Shot Compositional Action Recognition (ZS-CAR) formalizes this goal: a model must correctly identify unseen verb–object combinations despite only being exposed to a sparse subset of compositions during training
li2024c2c
.

However, existing ZS-CAR models exhibit a characteristic and persistent failure: they mispredict verbs in unseen compositions by relying on
object-driven shortcuts
.
In other words, rather than learning verb semantics from temporal evidence, models exploit objects as the dominant cue whenever such shortcuts correlate with training labels.
To the best of our knowledge, this failure mode has not been explicitly identified or systematically addressed in prior ZS-CAR approaches
li2024c2c
;
jung2025crr
.
We show that this shortcut failure mode arises from two intertwined issues of the ZS-CAR setting:

(i) Sparsity and skewness of compositional supervision.

The combinatorial nature of verb–object pairs yields extreme under-coverage of the compositional space,
e.g
.
only
\sim 14.0\%
label coverage ratio in the Something-Else
mat2020sthelse
and EPIC-KITCHENS-100
damen2022ek100
datasets.
This induces strong co-occurrence statistics to the deep learning model as depicted in Figure
1
(a).

(ii) Asymmetric learning difficulty between verbs and objects.

Objects are recognizable from single frames, while verbs require multi-frame temporal reasoning, as shown in Figure
1
(b).
This imbalance naturally encourages models to treat objects as
easy
predictive shortcuts
nam2020lff
;
li2018resound
;
singh2020dont
;
geirhos2018cuecon
;
scimeca2022shortcut
, especially under sparse data regimes.

Our analysis reveals that the existing ZS-CAR model amplifies these issues during training.
They increasingly bias predictions toward frequently co-occurring compositions, exhibit growing false-seen and false-co-occurrence prediction ratios, and ultimately yield
negative compositional gaps
—indicating that they fail to extract any benefit from compositional reasoning beyond independent verb and object recognition. (See Section
3
for details.)
These observations call into question the robustness of current ZS-CAR approaches and motivate the need for techniques that explicitly target shortcut mitigation.

We propose
RCORE
(Robust COmpositional REpresentations), a simple and effective framework designed to mitigate object-driven shortcut and promote temporally grounded verb representations.

RCORE
consists of two components:
(i) a composition-aware augmentation that synthesizes plausible unseen verb–object combinations while preserving the temporal structure of the original video; and
(ii) a temporal order regularization objective that penalizes reliance on static cues by contrasting original and perturbed temporal representations.
Together, these components address the two root causes of failure in ZS-CAR: compositional sparsity and asymmetric learning difficulty.

Across both datasets,
RCORE
significantly strengthens verb recognition, reduces co-occurrence bias, produces positive compositional gaps, and improves unseen composition accuracy over strong conditional baselines.
Our findings indicate that the primary obstacle in compositional video understanding is not model capacity but model behavior: ZS-CAR models fail because they resort to object-driven shortcuts.
By directly tackling this failure mode,
RCORE
provides a simple path toward more reliable and generalizable ZS-CAR.

In this work, we make the following major contributions:

•

Identifying the core failure mode of ZS-CAR.

We show that existing models rely on
object-driven verb shortcuts
, caused by the sparsity of compositional supervision and the asymmetric learning difficulty between verbs and objects—an issue not explicitly recognized in prior work.

•

Diagnosing limitations of the ZS-CAR evaluation protocol.

We reveal that common practices, such as test-set bias calibration, obscure true compositional generalization.

shows consistently favorable performance improvements across diverse evaluation scenarios, metrics, and datasets.

Related Work

Zero-shot compositional action recognition.

Zero-Shot Compositional Action Recognition (ZS-CAR)
li2024c2c
aims to generalize to novel verb–object combinations not seen during training.
Unlike zero-shot (or open-set) action recognition
chatterjee2023oap
;
bao2021dear
;
zhai2023soar
, ZS-CAR retains the same verb and object classes across train and test splits and evaluates a model’s ability to compose known primitives.
It addresses the performance degradation issue on novel unseen combinations of known concepts, which arises from the model’s lack of compositional generalization ability.
Conditional learning
li2024c2c
;
jiang2025dhd
has been effective for estimating joint verb–object probabilities, while other work
jung2025crr
pursues improved feature disentanglement.
However, as our analysis reveals in Section
3
and Section
5.2
, these approaches remain susceptible to
object-driven shortcut behavior
and progressively overfit co-occurrence statistics.

Compositional zero-shot Learning in image understanding.

Compositional Zero-Shot Learning (CZSL) has been widely studied in the image domain
misra2017red
;
naeem2021czsl
;
mancini2021compcos
;
wang2023cond
;
wu2025cond
;
nayak2023csp
.
CZSL enables models to recognize novel combinations of previously learned primitive concepts, such as objects and their states.
Prior works have approached this problem through feature disentanglement
saini2022disen
;
zhang2024disen
, modular architectures
purushwalkam2019taskdriven
, and CLIP-based prompt learning
nayak2023csp
;
li2024context
.
Unlike image-based CZSL, ZS-CAR requires modeling temporal dynamics for verbs, making the problem fundamentally different.
Our work focuses specifically on the video domain and highlights a failure mode—object-driven verb shortcuts—that does not arise in image-only CZSL.

Shortcut learning.

Shortcut learning is a pervasive issue in visual recognition.
Several studies show that models tend to rely on
easy-example
biases
wang2017fast
;
kumar2017hide
;
scimeca2022shortcut
or
easier-to-learn
cues—such as color or texture—when these cues correlate with labels
nam2020lff
;
geirhos2018cuecon
.
A line of work attributes this behavior to co-occurrence statistics in the training data
agrawal2018dont
;
singh2020dont
.
In video understanding, similar shortcut behaviors occur when action models rely on backgrounds rather than motion
li2018resound
;
whycantchoi
;
wang2021be
;
ding2022fame
;
bae2024devias
.
Building on these observations, we argue that ZS-CAR introduces an additional structural imbalance: verbs require temporal reasoning, whereas objects can be recognized from a single frame.
This learning asymmetry amplifies shortcut tendencies and leads models to predict verbs primarily from object cues—a failure mode we diagnose and directly address in this work.

3
Diagnosis: Why ZS-CAR Models Fail?

3.1
Zero-Shot Compositional Action Recognition

We study Zero-Shot Compositional Action Recognition (ZS-CAR)
li2024c2c
, where the goal is to recognize verb–object compositions that might never appear in the training set.
Let
\mathbb{Y}^{V}
and
\mathbb{Y}^{O}
denote the sets of verb and object labels, and let each action label be a pair
\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{y}^{V},\mathbf{y}^{O})\in\mathbb{Y}^{V}\times\mathbb{Y}^{O}
.
The training set is

\mathbb{D}_{\text{train}}=\{(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
and its set of observed (seen) compositions is

\mathbb{Y}_{\text{seen}}=\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})\in\mathbb{D}_{\text{train}}\}
.
The space of unseen compositions is

\mathbb{Y}_{\text{unseen}}=(\mathbb{Y}^{V}\times\mathbb{Y}^{O})\setminus\mathbb{Y}_{\text{seen}}
.
We define the validation and test sets analogously.
The validation and test sets contain both seen and unseen compositions, enabling evaluation of compositional generalization.
ZS-CAR therefore measures whether a model can correctly compose familiar verbs and objects into novel pairs at test time.

3.2
Diagnostic Metrics

For better understanding the underlying behavior of ZS-CAR models, we present two diagnostic metrics: (1) training bias metrics to measure a model’s overfitting and (2) Compositional Gap to assess the benefits of compositional modeling.

Training bias metrics

To measure how the model overfits to the training set statistics, we introduce two types of misclassification ratios:
False Seen Prediction
and
False Co-occurrence Prediction
ratios.
False Seen Prediction (FSP) ratio denotes the ratio of unseen composition samples incorrectly predicted to seen composition labels.
Out of these mispredicted samples, False Co-occurrence Prediction (FCP) ratio is likewise defined as the proportion of those assigned to frequently co-occurring labels.
Here, a composition label is considered ‘frequently co-occurring’ if its occurrence is higher than one standard deviation above the mean frequency in the training data.

Compositional Gap

We introduce
Compositional Gap
to examine whether a model genuinely benefits from understanding videos in a compositional manner.
Compositional Gap (
\Delta_{\text{CG}}
) is defined as

\Delta_{\text{CG}}=\mathrm{Acc}^{C}-(\mathrm{Acc}^{V}\times\mathrm{Acc}^{O}),

(1)

where
\text{Acc}^{C}
is the composition accuracy (%) computed from the logits over the composition space, whereas
\text{Acc}^{V}
and
\text{Acc}^{O}
are the verb and object accuracies (%) derived from the logits over their individual component spaces.
A positive
\Delta_{\text{CG}}
indicates that the model truly gains an advantage from its compositional architecture.

3.3
Empirical Diagnosis of Object-driven Shortcuts in ZS-CAR

In this section, we systematically diagnose the key failure mode in ZS-CAR—object-driven shortcuts.
We demonstrate that these shortcuts prevent ZS-CAR models from learning robust verb representations, consequently leading to poor generalization to unseen compositions.
We present additional evidence of object-driven shortcut learning in ZS-CAR models in Section
5
and the supplementary material.

Objects are easier to learn than verbs.

To investigate the learning difficulty of verbs and objects, we train a randomly initialized model on a subset of Sth-com
li2024c2c
.
Based on the observation that the easier-to-learn cue is captured earlier and exhibits higher accuracy
nam2020lff
, we can analyze the learning difficulty of each component.
In Figure
2
(a), the results show a clear trend: the model learns objects faster and consistently achieves higher accuracy for objects than for verbs.
While intuitively expected, these results empirically verify that
objects are inherently easier to learn than verbs
in compositional action recognition.

Object-driven shortcuts in ZS-CAR

Under strong co-occurrence priors, models
choose
shortcuts
emerged from easier-to-learn cues, rather than learn robust representations
nam2020lff
;
scimeca2022shortcut
;
geirhos2018cuecon
;
bahng2019rebias
;
li2018resound
;
whycantchoi
.
We argue this shortcut learning behavior accelerates in ZS-CAR due to the asymmetric learning difficulty.
To verify this, inspired by prior work
scimeca2022shortcut
, we train models on a perfectly biased training set and test on two distinct splits—bias-aligned seen compositions and bias-conflict unseen compositions— utilizing Sth-com
li2024c2c
.
As shown in Figure
2
(b), in the both cases of the randomly initialized ViT
dosovitskiy202vit
and CLIP
radford2021clip

Paradoxically, CLIP
li2024c2c
, well-known for its superior representational power, exhibits the highest object accuracy (94.0%) and the lowest verb accuracy (1.9%) concurrently on unseen compositions.
These results demonstrate models tend to fall into object-driven shortcuts to exploit co-occurrence priors in the training data, resulting in poor verb representations.

Object-driven shortcuts directly harm unseen generalization.

Next, we investigate whether the state-of-the-art (SOTA) model also fails to generalize to unseen compositions as a consequence of these object-driven shortcuts.

RCORE

We introduce
RCORE
, a learning framework that mitigates
object-driven shortcut learning
in zero-shot compositional action recognition.

RCORE
improves compositional generalization by strengthening verb–object representations under sparse supervision through two complementary components:
(i) VOCAMix, a composition-aware augmentation that expands the variety of verb–object combinations, and
(ii) TORC, a temporal order regularization loss that promotes temporally grounded verb learning over static object cues.
Following prior work
li2024c2c
, we use AIM
yang2023aim
, a CLIP-based
radford2021clip
parameter-efficient spatio-temporal encoder, as our backbone.
Figure
4
(a) gives an overview of
RCORE
.

Feature extraction.

Given an augmented video
\hat{\mathbf{X}}
with
T
frames, the backbone encoder outputs frame-level features
\mathbf{f}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
We then transform them into verb features
\mathbf{F}^{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D}
and an object features
\mathbf{f}^{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
using dedicated verb and object encoders.
We obtain text embeddings for verbs and objects,
\mathbf{e}^{V}
and
\mathbf{e}^{O}
, by feeding class-specific prompts (e.g.,
“a photo of
{
verb/object
}
”
) into the text encoder.

4.1
Composition-Aware Augmentation

To alleviate the sparsity of compositional training data, we propose Verb–Object Composition-Aware Augmentation (VOCAMix), which synthesizes plausible but unseen combinations while preserving the temporal structure of the primary video.

As shown in Figure
4
(b), VOCAMix replaces the motion region of a video with a static object region from another sample in the mini-batch.
Given a primary video
\mathbf{X}_{i}
with label
(\mathbf{y}_{i}^{V},\mathbf{y}_{i}^{O})
and a randomly selected video
\mathbf{X}_{j}
with object label
\mathbf{y}_{j}^{O}
(
i\neq j
), we construct an augmented video
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
and a soft object label:

\displaystyle h(\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{i}(k,:))

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\,h(\mathbf{X}_{i}(k,:))+\lambda\,h(\mathbf{X}_{j}(\lfloor T/2\rfloor,:)),

(2)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{O}_{i}

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)\,\mathbf{y}_{i}^{O}+\lambda\,\mathbf{y}_{j}^{O},

(3)

where
h(\cdot)
is a high motion-region estimation function,
k\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
is the frame index, and
\lambda\in[0,1]
controls the strength of object injection.
We adopt a simple, learning-free estimator
ding2022fame
for
h(\cdot)
.
We keep the verb label
\mathbf{y}_{i}^{V}
unchanged to preserve temporal semantics.
By injecting a consistent static object across the temporal axis,
VOCAMix preventing disruption of temporal cues

while expanding compositional diversity. while synthesizing novel compositions.

4.2
Temporal Order Regularization Loss

To counter object-driven shortcuts in verb learning, we introduce the Temporal Order Regularization for Composition (TORC) loss.
TORC loss forces the model to rely on temporal structure: it penalizes alignment with temporally incorrect feature sequences and suppresses confident verb predictions when temporal ordering is corrupted.
This regularization steers the model away from static object cues and strengthens temporally grounded verb representations.

Temporal perturbation.

Given frame-level features
\mathbf{F}=(\mathbf{f}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{f}_{T})
, we construct two perturbed variants:
(i) a reversed sequence
\mathbf{F}_{\text{rev}}=(\mathbf{f}_{T},\dots,\mathbf{f}_{1})
, and
(ii) a temporally shuffled sequence
\mathbf{F}_{\text{shuffled}}=\pi(\mathbf{F})
, where
\pi
is a random permutation sampled from
\mathcal{P}
.
We then feed
\mathbf{F}_{\text{rev}}
and
\mathbf{F}_{\text{shuffled}}
into the verb encoder to obtain perturbed verb features
\mathbf{f}^{V}_{\text{rev}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\mathbf{f}^{V}_{\text{shuffled}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

TORC loss.

We first push the model to distinguish forward and reversed temporal semantics.
We minimize the cosine similarity between the original verb feature
\mathbf{f}^{V}
and the reversed feature
\mathbf{f}^{V}_{\text{rev}}
:

\displaystyle L_{\cos}=\cos(\mathbf{f}^{V},\mathbf{f}^{V}_{\text{rev}}),\vskip-5.0pt

(4)

where
\cos(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|}
.
Temporal reversal often flips action meaning (e.g., opening vs. closing), yet the state-of-the-art model
li2024c2c
yields a high cosine similarity (
0.91
; see Figure
5
), indicating weak temporal discrimination.

Next, we regularize the model to avoid confident verb predictions when temporal structure is disrupted.
Given the set of verb text embeddings
\mathbb{E}^{V}=\{\mathbf{e}^{V}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathbb{Y}^{V}|}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathbb{Y}^{V}|\times D}
,
we define the negative entropy loss
L_{\text{ent}}
as:

\displaystyle L_{\text{ent}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathbb{Y}^{V}|}p_{i}\log p_{i},\vskip-5.0pt

(5)

where
p_{i}
denotes the predicted probability of the
i
-th verb class based on the cosine similarity between visual and textual features, and
\tau
is a temperature parameter.
Maximizing entropy prevents the model from inferring verbs from static spatial cues alone, enforcing reliance on temporal evidence.
We define the TORC loss as:
L_{\text{TORC}}=L_{\cos}+L_{\text{ent}}
.

Component loss.

We compute verb/object logits using cosine similarity between visual features and their corresponding text features.
We apply standard cross-entropy losses for verb and object prediction:
L_{\text{com}}=L_{V}+L_{O}
.

Composition loss.

Following prior work
li2024c2c
, we combine marginal scores
(\mathbf{p}_{V},\mathbf{p}_{O})
with conditional logits
(\mathbf{p}_{V|O},\mathbf{p}_{O|V})
to obtain the composition prediction
\hat{\mathbf{y}}
as
\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\texttt{Softmax}(\mathbf{p}_{O}\mathbf{p}_{V|O}+\mathbf{p}_{V}\mathbf{p}_{O|V})
.
We employ the cross-entropy loss:
L_{\text{comp}}=L_{\text{CE}}(\hat{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y})
.

Margin loss: mitigating co-occurrence bias.

Highly frequent verb–object pairs often dominate predictions, even for unseen compositions.
To counter this, we introduce a margin loss that penalizes frequent-but-incorrect compositions whose logits approach the ground-truth logit:

\displaystyle L_{M}=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{margin}}}\max\left(0,\log\hat{\mathbf{y}}(c)-\log\hat{\mathbf{y}}(g)+m\right),

(6)

where
g\in\{1,\dots,|\mathbb{Y}^{V}|\times|\mathbb{Y}^{O}|\}
denotes the ground-truth index, and
\mathcal{C}_{\text{margin}}
is the intersection of top-
K
incorrect predictions and the set of frequently co-occurring compositions.
This term explicitly discourages reliance on dataset co-occurrence priors.

Total loss.

We train
RCORE
with the combined objective
L_{\text{total}}
defined as

L_{\text{total}}=\alpha L_{\text{com}}+\beta L_{\text{comp}}+\gamma L_{\text{TORC}}+\delta L_{M}
,
where
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
, and
\delta
control the contribution of each term.

5.1
Experimental setup

Limitations of conventional ZS-CAR evaluation.

Existing ZS-CAR works
li2024c2c
;
jiang2025dhd
;
jung2025crr
rely on the
closed-world
protocol, where inference is restricted to a small set of compositions that appear in the validation or test split.
This protocol hides a model’s incorrect tendency to over-predict seen compositions and obscures co-occurrence-driven behaviors.
A more serious methodological flaw is the use of
test-set–tuned
bias calibration.
Selecting the bias term with access to test labels systematically inflates unseen accuracy and prevents meaningful comparison across methods.

Our evaluation setup.

To expose genuine generalization, we adopt an
open-world, unbiased
protocol by default:
(i) inference spans the full space
\mathbb{Y}^{V}\times\mathbb{Y}^{O}
, and
(ii) no test labels are used to tune biases.
We also report:
(a)
open-world biased
results using a realistic
validation set
-tuned bias, and
(b) closed-world H.M. and AUC for comparison with existing methods.
All results in Section
3
also follow the default open-world, unbiased protocol.

Datasets.

We evaluate on two ZS-CAR benchmarks: Sth-com
li2024c2c
and our curated EK100-com.
Sth-com is derived from Something-Something V2
goyal2017something
and Something-Else
mat2020sthelse
,
containing 79K videos with 161 verbs and 248 objects.
Following prior work
yun2022time
;
laura2021temporal
, we also evaluate on the
Temporal
subset consisting of verbs requiring temporal reasoning.
We additionally introduce the EPIC-KITCHENS-100-composition (EK100-com) benchmark, constructed by repurposing EPIC-KITCHENS-100
damen2022ek100
following the Sth-com protocol.
EK100-com includes 71K egocentric videos, 82 verbs, and 228 objects.
Full construction details are provided in the Section
A
.

Evaluation metrics.

We report top-1 composition, verb, and object accuracy on seen and unseen compositions, along with their harmonic mean (H.M.).
We also measure the compositional gap
\Delta_{\mathrm{CG}}
(
1
) which indicates whether a model benefits from compositional reasoning beyond independent verb/object predictions.
(see Section
3
)
A higher
\Delta_{\mathrm{CG}}
reflects stronger compositional synergy.

Baselines.

We primarily compare
RCORE
against C2C
li2024c2c
, the current SOTA ZS-CAR method.
To isolate the effect of conditional learning, we also evaluate two independent baselines.
AIM
yang2023aim
is the CLIP
radford2021clip
-based spatio-temporal adapter model used as our backbone.
C2C w/o conditioning augments AIM with all architectural components of C2C except the conditional modeling itself.
We additionally include closed-world results of a recent ZS-CAR approach
jung2025crr
.

Effects of
RCORE
components.

In Table
4

RCORE
framework.
When used individually, TORC loss is the most effective by providing explicit temporal modeling objectives.
Notably, it significantly improves verb recognition performances across both seen and unseen compositions (63.6% vs. 65.7% on seen compositions and 54.4% vs. 56.8% on unseen compositions).
Importantly, we can achieve the highest ZS-CAR performance by employing all three components together during training.
This synergistic improvement is achieved by effectively tackling dataset sparsity with VOCAMix, reinforcing temporal order modeling with TORC, and alleviating co-occurrence bias with Margin loss.

Effects of TORC components.

Table
4
(b) evaluates the roles of
L_{\cos}

L_{\text{ent}}

We show the each component of TORC can encourage performance improvement.
Each term individually improves performance, while using both together yields the largest gains in unseen verb accuracy.

Effects of VOCAMix.

In Table
4
(c), we validate the effectiveness of our proposed composition-aware VOCAMix by comparing it with widely adopted mixing augmentations.
We argue that conventional image-based augmentations are insufficient for composition verb-object learning.
Specifically, CutMix
yun2019cutmix
allows for mixing backgrounds with corresponding labels and Mixup
zhang2018mixup
blends verbs and objects concurrently, failing to preserve the temporal difference information that is essential for verb recognition.
As a result, these methods yield a lower H.M. score than the baseline.
In contrast, our VOCAMix, aiming to generate realistic unseen composition samples, shows modest performance gains over the baseline.
Furthermore, we observe that mixing a static frame into the estimated foreground region shows additional performance boost (+0.2 points in the H.M.) over applying it to the entire background (denoted as ‘VOCAMix w/o F.E.’).

Effects of Margin loss.

In Table
4
(d), we validate the effectiveness of our proposed Margin loss.
To mitigate the model’s reliance on dataset co-occurrence priors while maintaining baseline performance, we employ our method and validate its effectiveness by comparing it with the widely-used large-margin softmax loss.
The large-margin softmax (L-softmax)
liu2016lsoftmax
loss is implemented by multiplying the angle
\theta
between the input feature vector and the target classifier by a margin
m
(
e.g
.
,
\cos(m\theta)
), thereby forcing the model to learn stricter decision boundaries with narrower angular margins.
We apply this L-softmax loss into composition classification loss,
consistent with the implementation schedule of our Margin loss.
Compared to the L-softmax
liu2016lsoftmax
approach, our proposed Margin loss successfully reduces FCP ratio while preserving performance.
This demonstrates the effectiveness of our strategy, which selectively targets frequent-but-incorrect logits not the entire logits.

6
Conclusions

We revisit zero-shot compositional action recognition and show that existing methods fail mainly due to object-driven shortcuts, stemming from compositional sparsity and the asymmetric difficulty of learning verbs versus objects.
We also find that the widely used closed-world, test-tuned evaluation protocol conceals this failure, overstating model generalization.
We introduce
RCORE
, a simple framework that targets these issues directly:
VOCAMix enlarges the effective compositional support, and TORC loss enforces temporal order sensitivity to reduce reliance on static object cues.
Across two benchmarks and under a realistic open-world, unbiased evaluation setting,
RCORE
consistently improves unseen composition recognition performances, indicating genuine compositional reasoning.
Our results highlight that both proper evaluation and shortcut-aware training are essential for progress in compositional video understanding.

In this section, we provide details about our curated ZS-CAR benchmark, EPIC-KITCHENS-100-composition (EK100-com).
We construct EK100-com by repurposing EPIC-KITCHENS-100 (EK100)
damen2022ek100
following the same protocol of constructing Sth-com
li2024c2c
.
In particular, we use the original training (67217 samples) and validation (9668 samples) split of EK100
damen2022ek100
and split them as follows:
(1) we filter the data to include only compositions with more than five samples each.
(2) we ensure that all compositions present in the validation set also exist in the training set.
After initial filtering, the training set comprises 62,790 samples with 1,331 unique compositions and the validation set contains 8,657 samples, which consist of 833 seen compositions and 3 unseen compositions.
(3) we randomly select half of the compositions from both the training and validation sets and swap these subsets.
(4) Finally, we split the validation and test set with a ratio of
3:4
.
We provide the statistics of EK100-com in Table
5
and Figure
7
, and some seen/unseen composition examples of EK100-com in Figure
8
.

Beyond expanding the pool of publicly available ZS-CAR benchmarks, our repurposed EK100-com exhibits two key properties that make it a compelling ZS-CAR benchmark.
First, unlike Something-Something V2
goyal2017something
, where videos may involve multiple objects associated with a verb, videos in EK100
damen2022ek100
depict actions consisting of a single verb and a single object.
Therefore, the training data of EK100-com provides explicit object supervision for the models.
Second, since EK100
damen2022ek100
has long-tailed distribution, the label coverage ratio of EK100-com is 7.5%, which is much lower than that of Sth-com
li2024c2c

In this section, we provide comprehensive details of experimental setup and implementation details of our diagnosis for Zero-Shot Compositional Action Recognition (ZS-CAR) in Section 3 in the main paper.
All notations used in this supplementary document are consistent with those defined in the main paper.
As mentioned in the main paper, we adopt the state-of-the-arts model, C2C
li2024c2c

Details about our diagnosis in Figure

In Figure

We train a randomly initialized ViT
dosovitskiy202vit
with joint spatio-temporal attention on our selected subset of the Sth-com
li2024c2c
dataset.
We aim to select a subset that maximizes the number of samples while maintaining a sufficient number of distinct labels.
As a result, the selected subset is composed of 10 verb classes: ‘Moving sth and sth away from each other’, ‘Moving sth and sth closer to each other’, ‘Moving sth away from sth’, ‘Moving sth closer to sth’, ‘Moving sth down’, ‘Moving sth up’, ‘Pushing sth from left to right’, ‘Pushing sth from right to left’, ‘Pushing sth so that it falls off the table’, and ‘Pushing sth so that it slightly moves’; and 10 object classes: ‘Ball’, ‘Battery’, ‘Book’, ‘Bottle’, ‘Coin’, ‘Glass’, ‘Knife’, ‘Paper’, ‘Remote’, and ‘Spoon’.
We construct the training set consisting of 2,555 samples from the original Sth-Com
li2024c2c
training and validation samples, while the validation set comprises 260 samples from the original Sth-Com
li2024c2c
test set.
For training, we employ a multi-task learning approach using separate linear classifiers for verbs and objects, taking the ViT’s CLS token as input.
In the main paper, we show that objects are easier to learn than verbs by monitoring the learning curves of a randomly initialized ViT.
Furthermore, we substantiate in Section
C
demonstrating that this asymmetric learning difficulty leads the model to exploit object-driven shortcuts in compositional video understanding.

Details about our diagnosis in Figure

4\times 4

li2024c2c

This set contains four verb labels (‘Moving sth down’, ‘Moving sth up’, ‘Pushing sth from left to right’, and ‘Pushing sth from right to left’) and four object labels (Book, Remote, Glass, Bottle).
The training set consists of the four diagonal pairs (refer to Figure
2

The test set comprises two distinct splits: seen compositions (25 samples per pair) and unseen compositions (30 samples per pair).
We use 8 frames for input videos.

We define two diagnostic metrics, False Seen Prediction (FSP) ratio and False Co-occurrence Prediction (FCP) ratio.
FSP measures the proportion of unseen validation samples incorrectly classified as seen compositions:

\displaystyle\textit{False Seen Prediction}=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{D}_{\text{val}}^{\text{unseen}}|}{\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathbb{D}_{\text{val}}^{\text{unseen}}|}\mathbb{I}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\in\mathbb{Y}_{\text{seen}})},

(7)

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function.
FCP further quantifies the fraction of these false-seen predictions that correspond to frequently co-occurring verb–object pairs in the training data:

\displaystyle\textit{False Co-oc. Prediction}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathbb{D}_{\text{val}}^{\text{unseen}}|}\mathbb{I}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\in\mathbb{Y}_{\text{freq}})}{\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathbb{D}_{\text{val}}^{\text{unseen}}|}\mathbb{I}(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\in\mathbb{Y}_{\text{seen}})},

(8)

where
\mathbb{Y}_{\text{freq}}
denotes the set of frequently observed verb–object pairs in the training set.

\mathbb{Y}_{\text{freq}}
is defined with the frequency distribution of the training set
P_{\mathbb{D}_{train}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}_{\text{freq}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{Y}_{\text{freq}}^{O\mid V}\cap\mathbb{Y}_{\text{freq}}^{V\mid O},

(9)

where
\mathbb{Y}_{\text{freq}}^{O\mid V}
is defined as the set of pairs where the conditional probability of an object given a verb in the training data is greater than
\mu+\sigma
.
Here,
\mu
and
\sigma
represent the mean and standard deviation of the conditional probabilities calculated on the training set.
The
\mathbb{Y}_{\text{freq}}^{V\mid O}
is defined similarly for the conditional probability of a verb given an object.

(\mu_{O|V}+\sigma_{O|V},\mu_{V|O}+\sigma_{V|O})
are calculated as
(0.13,0.19)
for the Sth-com
li2024c2c
dataset and
(0.23,0.40)
for EK100-com.

B.2
Implementation details on
RCORE

Experimental Setup.

We implement our method using PyTorch and conduct all experiments on 8 NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs.
For video preprocessing, we uniformly sample frames from each video to generate clips of size
3\times 16\times 224\times 224
(channels
\times
frames
\times
height
\times
width).

Model Architecture.

We utilize CLIP-B/16
radford2021clip
as our visual/text backbone.
Following prior work
li2024c2c
, we adopt AIM
yang2023aim
, a parameter-efficient spatio-temporal adapter for CLIP
radford2021clip
, as the video encoder.
We also construct the verb encoder with two temporal convolution layers with ReLU activations, while the object encoder employs temporal average pooling followed by a two-layer MLP with ReLU activations.
We use CoOp-style
zhou2022coop
learnable text prompts for all models.

Training Strategy.

We train all models, including baselines and
RCORE
, for 30 epochs with a total batch size of 128.
The base learning rate is set to
5\times 10^{-4}
for Sth-com
li2024c2c
and
1\times 10^{-4}
for EK100-com, initialized with a 3-epoch warmup.
Following prior work
li2024c2c
, we adopt closed-world training setting.
We empirically find that setting the softmax temperature to 0.07, same as the implementation of CLIP
radford2021clip
, results in better ZS-CAR performance than the value of 0.01, used in prior work
li2024c2c
.
Therefore, we adopt this temperature value for both C2C
li2024c2c
and
RCORE

yang2023aim
with independent modeling.
For VOCAMix, we employ the unsupervised foreground estimation algorithm FAME
ding2022fame
to identify high-motion regions.
FAME
ding2022fame
isolates the moving foreground from background regions based on frame differences and color statistics.
To generate the mixed samples, we first sample the mixing coefficient from a
\text{Beta}(2.0,2.0)

yun2019cutmix
;
zhang2018mixup
.
We then scale it by 0.2 for Sth-com
li2024c2c

\lambda

We summarize the hyperparameters used in this work in Table

.

Loss configuration.

For the total loss, we set the weights for the verb and object components to
\alpha=0.2
and for the composition loss to
\beta=1.0

li2024c2c
.
As demonstrated in Table

\gamma

\delta

Inference.

During inference, we adopt a single-view, single-crop protocol.

In this section, we provide further evidence demonstrating that existing ZS-CAR learning approaches are susceptible to object-driven shortcut learning.

Failure case analysis of the existing ZS-CAR model.

In Table
7
and Table
8

li2024c2c

D.1
Comprehensive ablation results

In Table
9
, we provide the comprehensive ablation study results to examine each design choice of
RCORE
, using the Sth-com
li2024c2c
dataset.

Effects of TORC loss with different temporal aggregation methods.

In Table
9
(d), we demonstrate that the weak temporal order modeling caused by object-driven shortcut learning cannot be resolved merely by employing more sophisticated temporal aggregation methods.
We compare the baseline’s temporal average pooling with attention pooling (Attn. pool). In attention pooling, a linear layer computes frame-wise attention weights from the input verb feature sequences, aggregating them via a weighted sum to obtain the final verb feature for classification.
While attention pooling yields a slight improvement in verb accuracy, it fails to reduce the compositional gap (
\Delta_{CG}
) on unseen compositions.
In contrast, our TORC loss achieves significant improvements in H.M. with both aggregation methods.
Notably, the combination of temporal average pooling and TORC loss enables the model to overcome the negative compositional gap.

Effects of top-
K
selection for Margin loss.

In Table
9
(e), we investigate the impact of the hyperparameter
K
, which determines the number of top-ranked logits to which the Margin loss is applied.
We find that applying the margin selectively to the frequent-but-incorrect logits within the top-10 (
K=10
) reports the highest H.M., outperforming the approach of applying it to all seen composition logits (
K=3451
) (36.4% vs. 35.5%).
This suggests that penalizing only the most confusing candidates—specifically those that frequently co-occur with the input components—effectively mitigates co-occurrence bias without compromising the model’s overall performance.
For instance, given an input like ‘(Pretending to tear, Paper)’, our intention is to penalize plausible but incorrect distractors such as ‘(Tearing, Paper)’ or ‘(Folding, Paper)’, while avoiding penalties on irrelevant classes like ‘(Throwing, Paper)’ that are already ranked low.

We present extended results corresponding to the experiment shown in Figure

(c) of the main paper, including all versions of the Temporal/Static split.
Inspired by prior work
laura2021temporal
;
yun2022time
, we rigorously evaluate the temporal modeling capabilities of both the baseline (C2C
li2024c2c
) and our
RCORE
by partitioning the Sth-com
li2024c2c
dataset into Temporal and Static splits.
Sevilla et al.
laura2021temporal
originally defined these splits based on cognitive science experiments on Something-Something V2
goyal2017something
verb classes: a Temporal split (18 verbs) where temporal information matters, and a Static split (18 verbs) where such information is redundant.
