Associations among subthreshold depression, cognitive bias, and mentalization in university students
Authors: Yi Zeng, Beiyu Liu, Haomiao Yu, Yingnan Jia, Hao Chen, Pinpin Zheng, Haihong Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this study is to investigate the epidemiological status of subthreshold depression among undergraduate students in Shanghai and to explore the correlations among subthreshold depression, cognitive bias, and mentalization.MethodsThe present study employed a stratified random sampling approach, segmented by academic year, with investigators entering classes to conduct online questionnaire surveys. A total of 3,330 participants were included in the final analysis from October to December 2023.ResultsThe average score for subthreshold depression among the 3,330 undergraduates assessed was 81.52, with 41.5% meeting the criteria for subthreshold depression. Regression analysis suggested that hypermentalizing was negatively associated with subthreshold depression (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.84–0.88, P < 0.001), while hypomentalizing was positively associated with subthreshold depression (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04–1.11, P < 0.001). Higher level of cognitive bias was independently associated with higher odds (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.05, P < 0.001). Associations were broadly consistent across subgroups. The inverse association of hypermentalizing with subthreshold depression varied by self-assessment of health status after BH–FDR correction.ConclusionThere are significant correlations among subthreshold depression, cognitive bias, and mentalization in university students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25930-z.Keywords: Subthreshold depression, Cognitive bias, Mentalization, Reflective function

Introduction

According to the World Mental Health Report published by the World Health Organization in 2022, nearly one billion people worldwide suffer from mental disorders, and every 40 s, someone dies by suicide globally; thus, the significance of mental health in modern society is increasingly prominent [1]. Depression is a mood disorder characterized by a significant and persistent depressed mood caused by various factors. Its core symptoms include depressed mood, loss of interest, and lack of energy [2]. It is characterized by a high global prevalence rate of approximately 3.8%[3], a recurrence rate affecting nearly two-thirds of patients over time [4], and a significant suicide rate, with studies indicating that 15% of individuals diagnosed with depression ultimately die by suicide [5]. Depression has become a significant public health issue that harms human physical and mental health, with approximately 350 million people suffering worldwide. The World Health Organization [6] estimates that depression ranks third in the global disease burden and is projected to rise to the first position by 2030. An epidemiological survey in China showed that the lifetime prevalence of depression in the population is 3.9%, with 1.7% in men and 2.5% in women [7]. Social isolation and the pain of losing loved ones due to the COVID-19 pandemic have significantly increased the risk of depression. According to existing studies, the COVID-19 pandemic led to 53.2 million (95% UI 44.8–62.9 million) additional cases of major depressive disorder globally in 2020, representing a 27.6% (25.1–30.3%) increase in prevalence compared to pre-pandemic levels [8].

Subthreshold depression is a subclinical state characterized by depressive symptoms that do not meet the full diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder as defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5)[9]. It is often considered a transitional phase to depression [10], and it increases the likelihood of future depressive episodes, with the risk of developing major depression being more than five times greater than that in the general healthy population [5]. In recent years, the incidence of subthreshold depression has rapidly increased, particularly among adolescents and individuals who are in high-stress occupations. Epidemiological studies have shown that the prevalence of subthreshold depression among adolescents can reach as high as 16.5%[11]. Although subthreshold depression does not meet the diagnostic threshold for depression, it can still have serious adverse effects on an individual’s subjective well-being and psychosocial functioning [12]. Subthreshold depression can occur at various life stages, with adolescence being a high-incidence period. Numerous studies have found that individuals with subthreshold depression during adolescence are more likely to develop depression in adulthood. Shankman et al.[13] conducted a 15-year follow-up study and found that, at baseline levels of 14–18 years old, 35.5% of individuals with subthreshold depression experienced depressive episodes between 1 year later and 30 years of age. Rohde et al..‘s[14] longitudinal study also found that 11.7% of adolescents with subthreshold depression developed depression in the following year, and another 30.6% experienced it within the subsequent six years. A meta-analysis found that individuals with subthreshold depression are more likely to develop depression than those without subthreshold depression [15]. Cuijpers et al..[16] discovered that among the population aged 18, 64.8% of those with subthreshold depression developed depression within two years, with a significant difference in incidence rates compared to individuals without subthreshold depression, yielding a risk ratio (RR) of 4.47. In summary, the incidence of subthreshold depression affects the prevalence of affective disorders, particularly depression. Early intervention in individuals with subthreshold depression can effectively alleviate depressive symptoms and reduce the likelihood of subsequent depression, with both psychological and pharmacological interventions playing a significant role [17].

There are several well-accepted theoretical explanations for the development of subthreshold depression that focus on psychology, sociology, and genetics domains; among these theories, cognitive theory is particularly influential [18]. The cognitive models of depression, developed by Beck in the 1960s [19], posit that dysfunctional attitudes and cognitive biases play a significant role in the onset of depression, contributing to cognitive susceptibility to the condition. Cognitive biases lead individuals to interpret information in a wholly negative or pessimistic manner, including distortions such as arbitrary inference, exaggeration or minimization, and selective negative focus. Dysfunctional attitudes and cognitive bias represent relatively independent psychological states with a certain level of stability and can be considered trait-like symptoms of depressive disorders [20]. Extensive evidence demonstrates that depression is characterized by cognitive biases across multiple stages of information processing, including attention [21], interpretation [22], memory processes [23], and cognitive control deficits [24]. Longitudinal studies further indicate that these biases prospectively predict the onset and maintenance of depressive symptoms. For instance, attentional biases toward negative information [25], negative processing biases [26], and impaired cognitive control [27] have been linked to increased vulnerability to depression. Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy (CBT), developed based on cognitive theory, is a structured, short-term and effective psychotherapeutic approach developed by A.T. Beck in the 1960s and has become one of the most widely used methods for depression intervention [28].

The practice of utilizing cognitive bias correction to improve depression primarily focuses on changing cognitive content, that is, encouraging patients to understand the same problem from different perspectives, which can provide quick and effective positive feedback but does not fundamentally change patients’ cognitive patterns or functions. Severe psychological disorders are always associated with defects in cognitive functioning, not only in the distortion of thoughts but also in the processing patterns that produce them. Mentalization is the ability to understand one’s own and others’ mental states, an ability to reflect on oneself from the outside and others from the inside [29], which allows for the regulation of emotions and behaviours through reflective engagement with the emotions, beliefs, intentions, and motivations of oneself and others [30]. Mentalization-based treatment (MBT) originated in the 1990 s when the British psychologist Peter Fonagy proposed and validated the concept [31] and intervention techniques [32] of mentalization, gradually developing the theory of mentalization, which elaborated its significance and functions and led the empirical research on mentalization in the field of psychotherapy. Mentalization theory posits that mentalization is the primary process in the origin, development and recovery of various psychological dysfunctions, with psychological disorders commonly viewed as arising from a lack of mentalization skills, leading to the psychological misinterpretation of one’s own and others’ experiences [30]. Mentalization interventions were initially designed to treat borderline personality disorder but have since evolved to treat other types of psychological disorders, including depression. Reflective function is the core capability of mentalization, which involves the rational sensing and reflection of oneself and the understanding of others’ thoughts and feelings and is one of the main measurable indicators of mentalization [33]. Studies have shown that reflective function is related to the core clinical characteristics of emotional and behavioural dysregulation in individuals with mental disorders [34] such as borderline personality disorder [35], eating disorders [36], schizophrenia [33], and depression [37]. Reflective function provides a means for the assessment and operationalization of mentalization and is increasingly applied in empirical psychological research.

While CBT demonstrates moderate short-term efficacy for subthreshold depression, its long-term benefits wane, with 33.4% of patients experiencing recurrence after CBT [38]. MBT, though effective in clinical depression [39], has received limited attention in subthreshold depression populations. Current interventions predominantly target isolated mechanisms—either cognitive distortions or mentalization deficits—with minimal exploration of their synergistic potential. Given these limitations, this study adopts an exploratory approach to investigate the relationship between mentalizing capacity and cognitive biases in subthreshold depression, drawing on Beck’s cognitive theory and Fonagy’s mentalization framework as theoretical guidance.

This cross-sectional study examined associations among mentalization, cognitive bias, and subthreshold depression in undergraduates. We fitted 3 logistic regression models to estimate the associations of hypermentalizing, hypomentalizing, and cognitive bias with the odds of subthreshold depression, and evaluated effect modification by demographic characteristics via interaction terms and subgroup analyses. These findings may inform future research on the synergistic potential of integrating cognitive and mentalization-based interventions.

Methods

Participants and sampling methods

The participants of this study were undergraduate students enrolled in universities in Shanghai. From October to December 2023, a total of 8 undergraduate institutions were selected from the universities in Shanghai, with 2–3 institutions chosen from each type—comprehensive, technical, and normal—to ensure disciplinary representation, while considering accessibility and willingness to participate. The study employed a stratified random sampling approach. Within each selected university, participants were stratified by academic year, and one class per grade was randomly selected using a random number generator. Investigators entered the selected classes to administer online questionnaire surveys. This survey was ethically reviewed by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health, Fudan University (approval number: IRB#2023-04−1037), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Questionnaire

A cross-sectional survey was conducted utilizing the Self-Subthreshold Depression Scale to assess subthreshold depression, the Dysfunctional Attitude Scale to evaluate cognitive bias, and the short form of the Chinese version of the Reflective Functioning Questionnaire to measure mentalization capabilities.

Developed in Mainland China by Junhui Gong, Junpeng Zhang, and colleagues at Beijing University of Chinese Medicine using Chinese college samples, the Self-Subthreshold Depression Scale (STDS) has demonstrated good reliability and validity, with its factor structure, internal consistency, and test-retest reliability established in those populations [40]. The scale consists of 30 items across four dimensions, namely, social dysfunction (10 items), low self-concept (8 items), depressive mood (6 items), and physiological dysfunction (6 items), which basically cover the behavioural, cognitive, emotional, and physiological manifestations of subthreshold depression. The assessment period is the most recent month. Scores are assigned on a scale ranging from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (5 points), yielding a cumulative score ranging from 30 to 150. Higher scores signify increased severity of subthreshold depression, with a threshold value of 90 indicating the onset of subthreshold depression [41].

The Dysfunctional Attitude Scale (DAS) measures dysfunctional attitude, which is characterized by irrational, distorted cognitions about oneself, the world around, and the future and is a common tool used for assessing cognitive bias. The DAS was created by Weissman and Beck (1978) [42] and consists of 40 items to measure deep-seated, concealed dysfunctional cognitive distortions; it has good reliability and validity [43]. Higher scores reflect heightened severity of cognitive distortions, with the normative average score for the general population being less than 130. Dysfunctional attitudes were measured with the Chinese version of the the 40-item Form A of the DAS. In Chinese youth, including secondary school and university students aged 15 to 23, the scale shows good internal consistency and four-week test-retest reliability. It also demonstrates good structural validity, and positive correlations with depressive symptoms support construct validity [44].

The Reflective Functioning Questionnaire-8 (RFQ-8) is a concise scale with good reliability and validity that was developed by Fonagy. It contains 8 items with options ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” measuring two distinct dimensions: hypermentalizing (RFQ-C) and hypomentalizing (RFQ-U). Hypermentalizing reflects excessive or rigid attributions of mental states (e.g., overinterpreting others’ intentions without evidence), while hypomentalizing indicates reduced awareness of emotions or motivations (e.g., difficulty understanding one’s own actions). Responses are rated on a 7-point Likert scale, with higher scores on either subscale signifying greater impairments in reflective functioning [45]. The Chinese version of the RFQ-8 was introduced and translated by Chinese scholars and has been shown to have good reliability and validity in both clinical and nonclinical samples in China [46]. It is a reliable and effective tool for evaluating the level of mentalization and can be used for assessing adult reflective functioning.

Statistical analysis

Two prespecified respondent-level attention checks were embedded to assess within-construct consistency, each comprising a content-matched item pair rated on a 5-point Likert scale: a global rating of sleep quality coupled with an oppositely worded sleep-symptom item, and a behavioral-frequency item indexing active health-information seeking coupled with a reverse-worded item indexing low engagement. Verbatim item wording for both attention-check pairs are provided in Appendix 1. The negatively worded item in each pair was reverse-scored to align directions (x∗=6 − x). For each pair we computed the within-respondent absolute difference |Δ| = |x1 − x2∗|, which ranges from 0 to 4. Responses were flagged and excluded if |Δ| ≥ 3 for either pair, or if both pairs had |Δ|=2.These prespecified thresholds were chosen to capture directionally opposing or systematically discordant selections while tolerating minor within-person variability [47]. Prior studies support this pairwise reverse-coded consistency approach for identifying inattentive or content-nonresponsive responding [48, 49].

After applying these attention checks and excluding flagged respondents, we performed descriptive analyses of sociodemographic characteristics, subthreshold depression status, and mentalization levels. Only the STDS has a Chinese-validated screening cut-off (scores ≥ 90 indicate subthreshold depression) [41]. Because no universally accepted Chinese-validated cut-offs exist for the DAS or RFQ-8, we treated these measures as continuous variables to avoid arbitrary dichotomisation and loss of information. Continuous variables are described by means and standard deviations (SDs), whereas categorical variables are described by numbers and frequencies. Construct validity was assessed using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), with Bartlett’s test of sphericity and the Kaiser‒Meyer‒Olkin (KMO) measure (values greater than 0.7 are generally considered suitable for factor analysis) [50]. It was conducted using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with promax rotation. The number of factors was fixed to 4, as per the original scale’s validated structure [40]. Factor loadings and the explained variance for each dimension of the scales were described. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha after EFA refinement. For the two nonnormally distributed variables, RFQ-C and RFQ-U, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was used if the demographic characteristic variables were dichotomous, and the Kruskal‒Wallis test was used if the demographic characteristic variables were unordered and multicategorical. The level of significance was set at α = 0.05. Spearman’s bivariate correlation analysis was used to preliminarily explore the relationships between mentalization and cognitive bias and between mentalization and subthreshold depression.

Logistic regression models were constructed with high and low levels of subthreshold depression as outcome variables, mentalization and cognitive bias as independent variables, and demographic characteristics as control variables. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated to quantify the strength and precision of associations with subthreshold depression. The model fit of the regression analysis was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) to compare the goodness of fit of the models. Model 1 included sex, grade, academic discipline, ethnicity, only-child status, family location, father’s and mother’s education, parental marital status, monthly per-capita family income, grade ranking, and self-assessed health. Model 2 additionally included hypermentalizing and hypomentalizing. Model 3 further included cognitive bias. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to prespecified stratification variables drawn from the covariates in Model 3. Within each stratum, Model 3 was refit, retaining only covariates that still exhibited within-stratum variability; the stratifying variable for that analysis was not included as a covariate in the within-stratum model. Between-subgroup heterogeneity was assessed in the full sample by adding the relevant interaction terms to Model 3 and conducting joint Wald tests; interaction P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH–FDR) procedure and reported as q values. q < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were performed utilizing SPSS 22.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and R software (version 4.3.1).

Reliability and validity analysis of the STDS and the RFQ-8

The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for each dimension of the STDS ranged from 0.87 to 0.93, indicating good internal consistency of the scale. The Pearson correlation coefficients between each item and the total score ranged from 0.28 to 0.83, p < 0.001, all above 0.2, suggesting that each item was significantly correlated with the overall questionnaire. Bartlett’s test of sphericity suggested a KMO value of 0.97, p < 0.001, indicating that the scale was suitable for factor analysis. The factor loadings for each item ranged from 0.35 to 0.91, with a total explained variance of 64.7%.

For the RFQ-8, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the RFQ-C and RFQ-U subscales were 0.89 and 0.76, respectively, with Pearson correlation coefficients between each item and the total score ranging from 0.71 to 0.85, p < 0.001. Bartlett’s test of sphericity indicated KMO values of 0.87 and 0.82 for the RFQ_C and RFQ_U subscales, respectively, p < 0.001, suggesting that both were suitable for factor analysis. The factor loadings for each item ranged from 0.69 to 0.85, with a total explained variance of 65.2% and 49.3%, respectively. In conclusion, the RFQ-8 has good reliability and validity (see Appendix Table S2).

Outcomes

Prevalence of subthreshold depression among survey respondents

We conducted onsite, group-administered data collection and obtained 3,500 returns (100%). Of these, 3,366 were complete (96.17%) and 134 were incomplete (3.83%) and therefore excluded from analysis. Applying the prespecified attention checks, an additional 36 cases were excluded (1.07% of complete questionnaires), yielding an analytic sample of 3,330 respondents (98.93% of complete questionnaires; 95.14% of all returns). For categorical reporting, precedence was assigned to the sleep-quality paired item over the health-information paired item when both were triggered; this hierarchy affected category labels only and did not alter inclusion decisions. Under this convention, exclusions comprised 20 cases attributed to the sleep-quality pair (0.59% of complete returns) and 16 to the health-information pair (0.48% of complete returns).

In the analytic sample (n = 3,330), 1,460 were female (43.8%), and 1,870 were male (56.2%); 1,231 were first-year undergraduates (37%), 1,037 were second-year students (31.1%), 627 were third-year students (18.8%), and 435 were fourth/fifth-year students (13.1%). There were 857 humanities and social sciences students (25.7%) and 2,473 science, engineering, medical, and agricultural students (72.3%); 3,002 were of Han nationality (90.2%), and 328 were of minority nationality (9.8%). The survey also collected data on only-child status, family location, parental education, parental marital status, monthly per capita family income, grade ranking, and self-assessment of health status.

The average score for subthreshold depression among the 3,330 undergraduates assessed was 81.52, which is less than the established threshold of 90 points for subthreshold depression. Of the participants, 1,382 (41.5%) scored at a high level, and 1,948 (58.5%) at a low level. The analysis of demographic factors related to subthreshold depression levels revealed significant associations with multiple variables. Sex, academic discipline, only-child status, family location, parental education, parents’ marital status, family income, academic performance within the university, and self-assessment of health status all showed significant associations with subthreshold depression scores of the participants (see Table 1). For the different dimensions of the STDS, the mean scores for items related to social dysfunction, low self-concept, depressive mood and physiological dysfunction were 2.63, 2.72, 2.78 and 2.79, respectively.

The correlation between subthreshold depression and mentalization

The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to assess the distributional disparities in RFQ-C and RFQ-U scores across the high and low subthreshold depression groups. The analysis revealed a marked distinction between the groups: the high-level subthreshold depression group had a mean RFQ-C score of 2.64, a value significantly divergent from the 7.60 average score characterizing the low subthreshold depression group. Similarly, for RFQ-U, the mean score of the high-level group was 4.22, which significantly differed from the 2.08 mean score of the low-level group (see Appendix Table S3).

Associations of hypermentalizing, hypomentalizing and cognitive bias with subthreshold depression

With subthreshold depression as the dependent variable (low level as the reference); and hypermentalizing, hypomentalizing, and cognitive bias as the independent variables; and demographic characteristics as covariates, 3 logistic regression models were constructed. The results are shown in Table 2. Model 1, which included all demographic variables, had an AIC of 4005.315 and an R2 of 0.131. Among the demographic factors, mother’s education, grade ranking, and self-assessment of health status showed significant associations with subthreshold depression. Model 2, which incorporated mentalization levels into Model 1, had an AIC of 3315.761 and an R2 of 0.284, suggesting that hypermentalizing was negatively associated with subthreshold depression (OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.79–0.82, P < 0.001), while hypomentalizing was positively associated with subthreshold depression (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.07–1.13, P < 0.001). Model 3, which added the level of cognitive bias to Model 2, had an AIC of 2983.951 and an R2 of 0.358. Compared to Model 2, Model 3 had a smaller AIC value, indicating a better fit; the OR for hypermentalizing increased to 0.86 (95% CI: 0.84–0.88, P < 0.001), while the OR for hypomentalizing decreased to 1.08 (95% CI: 1.04–1.11, P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses based on Model 3 were performed to assess the consistency of associations across population strata. Across the 36 joint interaction tests (three exposures and twelve stratification variables), the associations were broadly similar across subgroups. After BH–FDR control, the only interaction that remained statistically significant was between hypermentalizing and self-assessment of health status (interaction P < 0.001; q < 0.001; Fig. 1). Within self-assessment of health status strata, ORs were: excellent 0.78 (95% CI: 0.73–0.83; n = 770), relatively good 0.84 (95% CI: 0.80–0.87; n = 1207), average 0.92 (95% CI: 0.89–0.95; n = 1171), and poor 0.81 (95% CI: 0.65–0.99; n = 182); given the small size of the “poor” stratum, that estimate may be less stable. By contrast, the interaction of hypermentalizing with grade ranking showed only nominal significance (interaction P = 0.041; q = 0.372), with within-stratum ORs ranging from 0.84 to 0.92 across ranking levels; the interaction of hypomentalizing with academic discipline was likewise nominal (interaction P = 0.036; q = 0.372), with ORs of 1.15 in humanities/social sciences and 1.07 in science/engineering/medical/other. Forest plots for hypomentalizing (Fig. 2) and for cognitive bias (Fig. 3) illustrate the broadly similar patterns across subgroups. No evidence of effect modification was found by sex, academic grade, ethnicity, only-child status, family location, father’s and mother’s education, parental marital status, or monthly per-capita family income (all q > 0.05).

Discussion

Nearly half of the participants Met the detection criteria for subthreshold depression

The average level of subthreshold depression among undergraduates in Shanghai (81.52 points) is close to the critical value of 90 points, with 41.5% meeting the criteria for subthreshold depression, which is higher than the rates found in previous research among Chilean adolescents [11] and among the general Korean population [51] but consistent with research among Chinese university students [52] and among European college students [53]. Compared with other studies, the prevalence of subthreshold depression among university students is relatively higher [11, 51–53], which may be associated with the academic and employment pressures faced by university students, as well as the high expectations for their own development.

Upon comparing the mean scores across 4 dimensions of the STDS, the dimensions concerning depression, mood and physiological dysfunction yielded higher scores. This indicates that university students with subthreshold depression may be more prone to experiencing disturbances in these dimensions. An analysis of the demographic distribution characteristics of subthreshold depression showed that among the surveyed undergraduates from universities in Shanghai, the detection rate of subthreshold depression was higher for females, students from humanities and social sciences, nononly children, rural students, students whose parents had a low educational level, unmarried parents, families with low per capita monthly income, students with lower grades, and students with poor self-assessed health status. This requires heightened attention. The results of regression analysis suggest that university students have higher odds of experiencing subthreshold depression if their mothers possess lower educational levels and if the students themselves fall within the bottom quartile of their university grade rankings. This association is consistent with the tendency of more educated parents to dedicate greater quantities of quality time to their children [54]. Furthermore, within the majority of Chinese households, mothers typically assume a more substantial role in the nurturing and caregiving of young children. Compared with fathers, mothers often exert a more profound impact on the initial stages of their children’s personality development and socialization processes [55]. Students with lower academic performance struggle to achieve a sense of accomplishment and recognition, leading to greater psychological stress [56]. In turn, negative emotions can further disrupt their regular study lives, thereby engendering a pernicious cycle [57].

The mentalization of participants was highly correlated with the level of subthreshold depression

The overall average score for hypermentalizing among Shanghai’s undergraduates was 5.54, with 37.5% scoring above 7; for hypomentalizing, the overall average score was 2.97, with 13.1% scoring above 7. Among them, males had higher levels of hypermentalizing, while females displayed greater degrees of hypomentalizing. Demographic distribution characteristics suggest that factors such as being an only child, family location, parental education, family income, grade, and self-assessment of health status were significantly correlated with hypermentalizing. Similarly, these factors, along with academic discipline, were significantly related to hypomentalizing. The aforementioned findings suggest that family is associated with influencing the mentalization abilities of university students, which is consistent with the conclusions of existing research [58, 59].

The analysis of the relationship between subthreshold depression and mentalization revealed that RFQ-U scores (hypomentalizing level) were significantly positively correlated with subthreshold depression, consistent with Fonagy and Luyten’s theoretical framework, which posits that impaired mentalization disrupts emotion regulation by limiting reflective processing of affective states [60]. The association between hypomentalizing and subthreshold depression can be explained through impaired emotion regulation and maladaptive coping strategies. Specifically, individuals with low mentalization capacity struggle to comprehend their own and others’ psychological states such as emotions, beliefs, and intentions [61], leading to maladaptive coping strategies such as avoidance or impulsive behaviours when faced with emotional distress, increasing vulnerability to depression [62].

Conversely, individuals with high mentalization capacity demonstrate greater emotional regulation skills, enabling them to interpret and respond to emotional cues in a flexible and adaptive manner [45]. For instance, higher mentalization scores are associated with secure attachment styles, which buffer against emotional dysregulation by fostering reflective functioning [60]. Previous research has also shown that higher levels of mentalization and mentalization styles that are closer to the abundant type, which is characterized by highly accurate mentalization with minimal non-reflective or externally/internally focused patterns, indicate higher levels of psychological health [63]. However, contradictory evidence highlights that empathy, a construct correlated with mentalization—may paradoxically elevate depressive risk in adolescents [64]. This discrepancy arises from empathy’s dual mechanisms: cognitive empathy (understanding others’ mental states) and affective empathy (sharing others’ emotions), which exert divergent effects [64, 65]. Cognitive empathy, often linked to mentalization, may serve as a protective factor by facilitating social cue interpretation and emotion regulation through reflective processing [66]. In contrast, affective empathy, particularly empathic stress, may heighten depressive vulnerability by amplifying negative affect without adequate regulation [65]. Future studies should disentangle these mechanisms to clarify the nuanced interplay between mentalization and depression.

Subthreshold depression was significantly correlated with cognitive bias and mentalization

In the logistic regression, sequentially adding mentalization indices (RFQ-C, RFQ-U; Model 2) and then cognitive bias (Model 3) improved model fit (AIC 4005.315 to 3315.761 to 2983.951; R² 0.131 to 0.284 to 0.358), supporting incremental validity for mentalization and additional explanatory value of cognitive bias. Considering the improvement in model fit from Model 1 to Model 3 and the stability of the coefficients, mentalization indices (lower RFQ-C, higher RFQ-U) and higher cognitive bias scores may be correlates for risk stratification; causal inference awaits longitudinal validation. Aligning with Fonagy’s mentalization theory, the development of mentalization capacity is shaped by multidimensional factors, including attachment security, emotion regulation efficacy, and cognitive integration [45]. Specifically, cognitive biases may constrain the integration of internal and external information, while emotion regulation deficits hinder flexible interpretation of mental states [67]. This theoretical framework suggests that subthreshold depression may stem from a dynamic imbalance between mentalization capacity and cognitive bias. This framework is consistent with our finding that the mentalization indices (low RFQ-C, high RFQ-U) and stronger cognitive bias are each associated with higher odds of subthreshold depression and consistent with their incremental validity. The mentalization indices index representational and integrative capacity, whereas cognitive bias reflects systematic evaluative distortions; these are related yet not identical processes, and therefore complementary.

In subgroup analyses using the most adjusted model, the pattern of associations was broadly stable across all strata, supporting the generalizability of the main findings. After BH–FDR control, only the hypermentalizing and self-rated health interaction remained significant (P_interaction < 0.001; q < 0.001): ORs were 0.78 (excellent), 0.84 (relatively good), 0.92 (average), and 0.81 (poor). These findings suggest that self-rated health may modify the strength of the association between hypermentalizing and subthreshold depression. Poor self-rated health likely indexes heightened negative affect and somatic preoccupation, which can amplify the tendency of hypermentalizing, thereby strengthening its link to subthreshold depressive symptoms [68].

Currently, interventions for subthreshold depression include CBT [69], mindfulness-based interventions [70], and group-based behavioural activation with mindfulness [71]. The study’s results indicate that mentalization training and cognitive bias modification (e.g., CBT; behavioural activation with mindfulness) may provide complementary avenues for improvement in subthreshold depression. If university students’ mentalization capacities can be effectively enhanced, enabling them to correctly comprehend their own and others’ psychological states, thereby diminishing the misinterpretation of information, it is possible to decrease the level of subthreshold depression. Furthermore, by effectively improving cognitive biases, it may be feasible to buffer the adverse impact of hypomentalizing on subthreshold depression to some extent.

Although this study revealed associations between mentalization and subthreshold depression, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Future longitudinal studies are needed to validate temporal sequences.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precluded causal inferences and the determination of temporal relationships between mentalization and subthreshold depression. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal pathways. Second, the sample predominantly comprised college students in Shanghai, China, limiting generalizability to older adults, clinical populations, or culturally diverse groups. Third, while standardized scales were used to ensure measurement validity, reliance on self-reported data for mentalization and subthreshold depression may introduce common method bias. Future research should adopt multimodal approaches to enhance objectivity. Additionally, sample distribution imbalances across demographic categories and the limited selection of variables in multivariable analyses may constrain the comprehensiveness of findings. Future studies should prioritize diverse sampling strategies and broader variable inclusion to strengthen ecological validity and theoretical robustness.

In conclusion, there were significant correlations among subthreshold depression, cognitive bias, and mentalization in university students. Consequently, the development of an intervention model for subthreshold depression that focuses on the modulation of mentalization and the amelioration of cognitive biases through cognitive function has both theoretical significance and practical utility.
