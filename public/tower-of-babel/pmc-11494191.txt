Reaching machine learning leverage to advance performance of electrocatalytic CO2 conversion in non-aqueous deep eutectic electrolytes
Authors: Ahmed Halilu, Mohamed Kamel Hadj-Kali, Hanee Farzana Hizaddin, Mohd Ali Hashim, Emad M. Ali, Suresh Bhargava
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Deep eutectic electrolytes (DEEs) show promise for future electrochemical systems due to their adjustable buffer capacities. This study utilizes machine learning algorithms to analyse the carbon dioxide reduction reaction (CO2RR) in DEEs with a buffer capacity of approximately 10.21 mol/pH. The objective is to minimize undesired hydrogen evolution reactions (HER) and render CO2RR dominant in a membrane cell. The CO2RR process was found to be non-adiabatic, as the time of nuclear motion for CO32− in K2CO3 product, through CO2●− trapping, is 0.368 femtoseconds shorter than the 1.856 × 10−3s charge transfer relaxation time. Microkinetic analysis reveals that the rate of CO2RR to CO2●− is 2.14 × 103 mol/cm2/s2 with a rate constant of 2.1 × 1010 cm/s. Our findings demonstrate that ensemble and k-Nearest Neighbours algorithms learn the CO2RR dataset, achieving a prediction accuracy of over 99%. The models were verified visually and quantitatively by overlaying predicted and experimental dataset. Diagnostic and SHAP analyses highlighted the gradient boost ensemble algorithm, predicting asymptotic current densities of -4.114 mA/cm2 or -13.340 mA/cm2, with high turnover frequencies (TOF) of 3.79 × 1010 h-1 or 12.30 × 1010 h-1 for CO2●− or K2CO3 generation on silver electrodes, respectively. These results consider both accuracy and robustness against overfitting, providing an opportunity to optimize future non-aqueous electrolytes for convenient TOF measurements at industrially relevant current densities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74893-5.

Introduction

The electrochemical reduction of CO2 (CO2RR) offers a promising avenue for sustainable carbon capture and utilization. This technology can address greenhouse gas emissions and advance production of specialty chemicals and alternative fuel that play important role in modern chemical industry1. Towards scalable proliferation of CO2RR, the current densities often remain relatively low (≤ 20 mA/cm2) due to electrolytes stability issues and interference by hydrogen evolution reaction (HER)2.

Currently, CO2RR has achieved current responses exceeding 200 mA/cm2 at reduced overpotential on stable electrodes3. However, the use of aqueous electrolytes in these efforts presents a challenge of salt precipitation, which worsens the impact of HER and disrupts the desired current response for CO2RR3. Additionally, the low solubility of CO2 in aqueous electrolytes and the formation of passivation scale on electrocatalysts lead to variations in local pH, which is another common issue4,5. It is well known that when the scale accumulation exceeds the threshold of CO2 solubility in aqueous electrolytes, it causes flooding of the electrocatalyst, hindering the flow and diffusion of CO2 at the interface and reducing the interfacial CO2 concentration6. Given these challenges associated with aqueous electrolytes, it is necessary to explore non-aqueous alternatives to promote sustainable CO2RR.

The DEEs are advantageous as non-aqueous electrolytes due to their low proton donating capacity. They also demonstrate enhanced solubility of CO2, which contributes to improved performance in the CO2RRprocess7. Specific DEEs, such as ChCl-EG (1:2) and ChCl-Urea, have shown the capability to convert CO2 to CO by utilizing their wide stability windows and high CO2 solubility. This has resulted in Faradaic efficiencies (FE) of 78% and 96%, respectively8,9. These FE values surpass those observed in the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), which typically remain below 10% and 20%, respectively. The low HER observed in DEEs is generally attributed to the utilization of hydrogen functionality, facilitated by hydrogen bonding noncovalent interactions during their formation10,11. By leveraging hydrogen functionality, the stability of DEEs is enhanced, making them suitable as a medium for various electrochemical reactions. This stability is achieved by maintaining lower proton concentrations, which helps to mitigate the undesired generation of hydrogen as a by-product during CO2RR. Certain DEEs, such as reline, ethaline, 1,2-propeline, and 1,3-propeline, demonstrate reduced stability with a cathodic limit of -1.5 V7. Their tunability makes them advantageous for application in CO2RR. This sets them apart from traditional non-aqueous electrolytes like acetonitrile, which is susceptible to attack by hydride, resulting in decomposition into a 3-aminocrotonitrile anion during CO2RR, especially at -2.4 V applied potential12.

TheCO2RR to produce CO has been widely studied using Silver (Ag) electrodes in various electrolytes. The Faradaic efficiencies of these experiments range from 15–96%.13 However, the mechanistic understanding of the CO2RR on Ag surfaces is still incomplete. Additionally, the adsorption of DEEsonto Gold (Au) and Glassy Carbon (GC) electrocatalyst surfaces has been observed, but its impact on catalytic activity is not well-established10,11. Some evidence suggests that reaction intermediates may bind to the electrocatalyst surface14,15. These challenges have a significant impact on the mechanistic steps of the reduction reaction process, specifically on the formation of CO2●− and CO, which are crucial intermediates for producing Cn+ products in DEEs which usually demonstrate considerably higher polarization resistance compared to traditional electrolytes. Additionally, they possess moderate buffering capacities, which are beneficial for maintaining pH stability while permitting necessary fluctuations, thus allowing for efficient electrochemical reactions. Despite the promising potential of DEEs as suitable electrolytes, detail experimental confirmation is still required.

The aim of this study is to develop a precise and simple framework for evaluating the performance of electrochemical reactions. This was achieved by utilizing turnover frequency (TOF) as a descriptor, with the aid of machine learning (ML) techniques deployed on CO2RR in non-aqueous DEEs. Several machine learning models were compared within a regressor framework to train a current density profile obtained from a long-term CO2RR. These ML models analyse the dataset of current density, identify the stabilization point, and calculate the average value as the asymptote. The capacity of the ML model predictions was then validated against the measured current density to visually represent the CO2RR. Unlike previous reports that only focus on analyzing mass transport, separation, and electrode screening for CO2RR, our report is different and advance determination of asymptotic current density (I∞) in long-term CO2RR16–19. The detected I∞ is useful in quantifying the maximum turnover frequency (TOF) achievable for CO2RR, even in the presence of mass transfer limitations. However, this is not the case when using kinetic current density, which is typically done at low overpotential, close to the equilibrium potential, and not restricted by mass transport processes20. In furtherance, different compositions of DEEs derived from tetrabutylammonium chloride and diethanolamine (TBACl-DEA) were synthesized and characterized for the CO2RR. Microelectrokinetic analysis of CO2RR, which includes Tafel analysis, was conducted due to the involvement of various potential reaction pathways and HER. A comprehensive investigation was necessary to gain a thorough understanding of the local microenvironment and the influence it has on the process. This understanding is essential in determining the distribution of reactive and non-reactive species on the Helmholtz plane, as well as the interactions between these species. We have identified the reaction regime and determined the rate-limiting step that controls the CO2RR in TBACl-DEA. Additionally, we have analyzed the location of the reaction on the Helmholtz plane to provide justification for the measured TOF values.

Methodology

Materials

The ultra-pure CO2 gas was obtained from Gas Link SDN (Malaysia). The precursors required for synthesizing DEEs, including diethanolamine (DEA; >98% purity) and tetrabutylammonium chloride (TBACl; >98% purity), were acquired from Sigma Aldrich (Darmstadt, Germany). Potassium hydroxide pellets were also obtained from Sigma Aldrich (Darmstadt, Germany). The electrocatalyst used in this study consisted of a Pt wire/5.7 cm counter electrode from BASi Inc., as well as commercially available Ag, Au, and GC with an outer diameter (OD) of 6 mm and inner diameter (ID) of 3 mm. An Ag/AgCl (6 mm) reference electrode was used. Additional materials included a Nafion-117 membrane and a fabricated CO2 electrolyzer.

Synthesis of DEEs

The preparation followed a procedure similar to one that has been previously reported21. In this process, hydrogen bond acceptor (TBACl) and hydrogen donor (DEA) molecules are combined in a known ratio and subjected to a temperature of 70 °C for 8 h, resulting in the formation of a homogeneous solution21,22. Among the various mixtures prepared, those that exhibited stability as liquids at room temperature (1:3, 1:4, 1:5, and 1:6 compositions of TBACl: DEA) were chosen for the CO2 electroreduction experimentation. All the TBACl-DEA mixtures contain less than 1% moisture content according to the Karl Fischer titration.

The thermal behavior of the DEE samples operating under the heat flux option was assessed using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC-8000, Perkin Elmer, U.S.A.). The DSC measurements were conducted in an inert N2 environment with a flow rate of 20 mL/min. Each sample, consisting of 5 mg of DEEs, was placed within aluminium pans, and multiple scans were performed using cooling and heating rates of 10 °C/min, within the temperature range of 70 °C to 200 °C. An isothermal step at 200 °C for 30 min was carried out to eliminate excess water. Furthermore, a 10-minute holding period at 70 °C was introduced to enhance the signal quality for any potential glass transition temperature and the melting point at the eutectic point (Figure S1; Supplementary Information S.1).

Additionally, the viscosity of the DEEs was determined using a DV-II + Pro Extra Brookfield viscometer at a temperature of 25 °C, as previously reported23, and the results are presented in Table S1 (Supplementary Information). The density of the DEEs was determined using a DM40 Mettler TOLEDO density meter, covering a temperature range of 25 °C to 90 °C in increments of 10 °C, and the results are shown in Table S2 (Supplementary Information).

Electrochemical analysis

All electrochemical experiments were conducted using a Bio-logic potentiostat (Sp-300, ± 10 V) in a two-chamber cell configuration, with a Nafion 117 membrane separating the chambers. Platinum served as the counter electrode, while Ag/AgCl was used as the reference electrode. When necessary, acetonitrile (ACN) was used as the supporting electrolyte. To determine the electrochemical stability window of the catholyte (TBACl-DEA), cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis was performed at the open circuit potential. CV scans were carried out over the voltage range of − 3 V to 3 V vs. Ag/AgCl using the Bio-logic potentiostat (Sp-300). Prior to each CV and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) experiment, CO2 was purged through the electrolyte for 10 min at a flow rate of 0.127 standard cubic feet per hour (scfh). The experiments were conducted over the voltage range of − 3 V to 0 V vs. Ag/AgCl. The pH of the CO2-saturated electrolyte was determined to be 8.34. All potentials were referenced against the Ag/AgCl electrode. To prepare the Ag, Au, and GC working electrodes, a conditioning process was conducted. This involved running 5 cycles of cyclic voltammetry (CV) scans at a scan rate of 100 mV/s. The applied potential range for these cycles was from − 0.5 to − 2.0 V (vs. Ag/AgCl). Electrolysis experiments were carried out using chronoamperometry, with the applied potential for CO2 reduction reactions (CO2RR) maintained for a duration of 3 h. Continuous sparging of CO2 was employed throughout the electrolysis to induce turbulence, effectively simulating stirring within the system. During the experimental procedure, the generation of CO was expected. However, to prevent its accumulation, a constant flare was employed, while the detection of CO2●− was facilitated using aqueous KOH as a trapping agent. Attenuated total reflection infrared spectroscopy (ATR-IR) was used for the detection process. Furthermore, density functional theory infrared (DFT-IR) calculations were performed on the sampled catholyte post-reaction to provide additional insights. Tafel plot data was obtained via potentiostatic sweeps, where steady-state currents were measured at various applied potentials ranging from − 1.0 to − 1.4 V vs. Ag/AgCl, with a step size of 0.05 V. Steady-state conditions were considered achieved for each potential when the change in current fell below 5.0%. Impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis was carried out under an applied potential for CO2 polarization, using an amplitude of 10 mV and covering a frequency range of 100 kHz to 50 mHz.

DRT calculations

The impedance spectra recorded were processed using Tikhonov regularization and cross-validation technique to create a distribution of relaxation time (DRT) profile24. Only data points within the frequency range of 100 kHz to 50 mHz were used for this transformation. In addition, Kramers-Krönig (KK) integrity checks were performed on the EIS data following standard procedures, which involved calculating relative deviations25,26. Interpreting impedance spectra can be difficult due to overlapping effects and non-ideal processes. The DRT approach helps evaluate EIS by fitting impedance data with an infinite series of RC-elements connected in series with an Ohmic resistance. However, due to the ion-blocking nature of the GC, Au, and Ag electrodes used in this study, capacitive behavior was observed at low frequencies (where ω\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\to\:$$\end{document}0). To ensure accurate DRT results, capacitive reactance was subtracted from the complex impedance in the low-frequency region. Each peak in the DRT function corresponds to a physicochemical process, with the area under each peak representing its resistance. Calculating the DRT function G(τ) presents an ill-posed mathematical problem, which requires the selection of a regularization parameter λ27. Careful selection of λ is crucial to obtain meaningful results, as an improperly chosen parameter may lead to oscillations, false peaks, or the merging of multiple processes into a single peak. Various criteria for selecting the regularization parameter λ have been proposed, including singular value decomposition (SVD) of matrices and L-curve heuristics28–30. However, cross-validation provides a direct measure of how well the regularization performs in terms of predictive accuracy, which is often the main objective in practical applications, especially in data-driven modeling.

Results and discussion

Non-covalent interaction of CO2 with TBACl-DEA

In retrospect, it is known that DEE have more than 5 times capacity to solubilize CO2. This can implicate both outer and inner sphere reaction towards CO2RR. The sigma profile (Fig. 2a) and sigma potential (Fig. 2b) analysis of screening charge density provides reasonable indication of the existence of non-covalent interaction between CO2 and TBACl-DEA for possible outer-sphere reaction where the electrolyte inclusiveness, rather than solely the electrode, governs the catalytic CO2RR. In Fig. 2a, the peaks located at σ values below − 0.83 and above 0.83 imply the tendency to serve as hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, respectively37. Conversely, in Fig. 2b, the negative values at σ < −0.83 and positive values at σ > 0.83 denote an inclination toward hydrogen bond donation and acceptance, respectively. The more negative σ-potential value of chloride moiety in TBACl-DEA emphasis the role of the anion in influencing CO2 solubility. The COSMO-surface of CO2 interaction site with each TBACl-DEA site is visualized in Fig. 2c.

CO2 electroreduction in TBACl-DEA electrolyte

By examining the buffer capacities of DEEs and using ATR-IR/DFT-IR analysis to identify transient species, this study provides a thorough understanding of the characteristics of CO2RR, specifically on electrodes such as Ag, Au, and GC, which are known for their blocking properties. The TBACl-DEA remains stable until − 2.4 V (Figure S2), and fortunately, the CO2 electroreduction occurs at applied potentials lower than − 2.4 V. In Fig. 3a, the cathodic cyclic voltammograms (CV) of Ag, Au, and GC in CO2-saturated TBACl-DEA (1:3) recorded an earlier increase in negative current density compared to the respective scan in N2-purged TBACl-DEA (1:3; blank) electrolyte. This suggests that the increasing current is caused by the formation of a product from CO2RR. To determine the nature of these products, CO2 electrolysis at -1.5 V using an Ag electrode was performed for 3 h. Similar to electrolysis of CO2 without a trace amount of proton source, the presence of CO2●− was confirmed by using KOH as a trapping agent to produce K2CO3 (Fig. 3b and c). The general reaction for in-situ K2CO3 production is CO2 + 2KOH → K2CO3 + H2O. The CO2●− trapping process was performed during continuous CO2RR into the TBACl-DEA over a 3-hour duration at − 1.5 V, while maintaining a temperature of 100 °C (Fig. 3b). We found that the addition of 0.1 M KOH in the CO2RR stream increases the current response from − 6.06 mA/cm2 for CO2●− generation to − 12.19 mA/cm2 for CO2●− trapping and subsequent K2CO3 production (Fig. 3b and c). The KOH acts as the active center for interaction with the reactant molecule. Upon ionization of KOH, the K+ acts as an oxidant that can dimerize with CO2●−, facilitating a reaction to form K2CO338.

The identification of K2CO3 was subsequently confirmed through ATR-IR measurements. According to ATR-IR, K2CO3 exhibits a symmetrical stretching mode of ν1 in the range of 1430–1500 cm. Analyzing the ATR-IR spectrum of K2CO3 in this frequency range can provide valuable information about the composition and molecular structure of the compound. It confirms the presence of the carbonate ion and its vibrational characteristics. After confirming the ATR-IR using DRT-IR, slight variations are observed. The DFT-IR is then performed theoretically, as described in supplementary information S.3.1. Therefore, the peaks at 1440–1416 cm-11 for ATR-IR or DFT-IR, respectively, correspond to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{v}_{1}$$\end{document} symmetrical stretching mode of the majority of the CO32− ions in K2CO3. The position of this peak falls within the typical range for this vibrational mode, indicating the presence of carbonate ions. Moreover, the presence of a peak at 1592–1665 cm-1 suggests the existence of a bicarbonate (HC\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\text{O}}_{3}}^{-}$$\end{document}) species coordinated with potassium cations. This could be attributed to the detection of a small amount of moisture in TBACl-DEA, which was measured using Karl Fischer titration to be around 50 ppm. This level does not affect the generation of CO2●−, which is short-lived and detectable, as previously reported, using the tip generation/substrate collection (TG/SC) mode of scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM)39. The assignment of CO2RR to CO2●− is debatable, as the electrolyte may decompose, particularly when operating above the cathodic limit. In our case, CO2RR to CO2●− occurs below the cathodic limit of TBACl-DEA (− 2.4 V). The TBACl-DEA did not decompose and maintained stable pH. The pH of the CO2/TBACl-DEA non-aqueous electrolyte showed a slight decrease from 8.34 to 8.29, 8.12, and 8.11 for the Ag, Au, and GC electrodes, respectively. This suggests that the CO2/TBACl-DEA system maintains an alkaline environment on a bulk scale. Additionally, the buffer capacities of TBACl-DEA using Ag, Au, and GC electrodes were determined to be 9.626, 2.187, and 2.092 mol/pH, respectively (Fig. 3d). These values indicate the ability of TBACl-DEA to resist pH changes within a CO2 environment.

The CV depicted in Fig. 3a affirm the stability of the TBACl-DEA electrolyte, evident from the nearly zero Faradaic current. The onset potentials for CO2RR differed among the Au, Ag, and GC electrodes, appearing at − 1.280 V, − 1.229 V, and − 1.148 V, respectively, at a scan rate of 100 mV/s. Correspondingly, the peak potentials (Ep) for CO2RR were noted at − 1.575 V, − 1.866 V, and − 1.919 V vs. Ag/AgCl, utilizing Au, Ag, and GC electrodes, respectively. Reaction regime of CO2RR in TBACl-DEA

CO2 reduction reaction is indeed a complex process with multiple reaction pathways and often accompanied by side reactions such as the HER or decomposition of the electrolyte. While Tafel slope analysis has been a common method to understand electrokinetics, it is not sufficient to fully describe the intricacies of CO2RR reaction regime. The non-covalent interaction of CO2 with TBACl-DEA from COSMO-RS analysis already indicate possible outer sphere reaction by virtue of association. The determining factor for CO2RR dominance, particularly in non-aqueous TBACl-DEA electrolytes, is the gradient of CO2 concentration at the electrode interface. The results presented in Table 2 show that CO2RR dominates over HER or OER in TBACl-DEA, even though OER occurs in the anode chamber, we did not take account of it. Despite the TBACl-DEA containing less than 1% moisture, CO2RR is dominant due to enhanced mass transport of CO2 brought about by interaction with TBACl-DEA which reduces the interfacial gradients of CO2 concentration. This is evident by increase in the viscosities of TBACl-DEA when saturated with CO2 (Table S1 and Table S3). Additionally, the Tafel slope values exceed 120 mV/decade across different compositions of TBACl-DEA for the Ag, Au, and GC-based surface electrode material investigated. This pattern is consistent with previous observations of CO2RR to CO2●−, where the Tafel slopes appear within the Tafel region at a low overpotential, indicating the rate-limiting step of the reaction44.

At < 1% moisture content in TBACl-DEA electrolyte, CO2RR dominate but to understand this observation, an intentional increase in the moisture content to 5% in the absence of CO2 saturation results in Tafel slope values of HER falling below those observed for CO2RR (Table 2). The 5% H2O can promote both the HER and the OER simultaneously. Particularly, the Tafel slopes for the HER vary depending on the composition of TBACl-DEA and the electrode used. In preference, HER is moderate in systems such as Ag/TBACl-DEA (1:4), Ag/TBACl-DEA (1:5), Ag/TBACl-DEA (1:4), GC/TBACl-DEA (1:3), GC/TBACl-DEA (1:4), and Au/TBACl-DEA (1:6). However, HER is faster in systems like GC/TBACl-DEA (1:5), GC/TBACl-DEA (1:6), Ag/TBACl-DEA (1:3), Ag/TBACl-DEA (1:6), Au/TBACl-DEA (1:3), Au/TBACl-DEA (1:5), and Au/TBACl-DEA (1:6). The presence of H2O enables a proton-coupled electron transfer as the initial step, followed by a proton transfer as the subsequent step, resulting in the observed Tafel slope for HER as documented in Table 245. In the anode chamber, the Tafel slopes for OER, according to Eq. 11 and data in Table 2, are less than 60 mV/decade. This suggests a lack of significant electrocatalyst involvement in the OER process46.

When saturating the 5% H2O-TBACl-DEA with CO2, the Tafel slope exceeded 60 mV but remained below 170 mV/decade (Table S8; Supplementary Information). This change in the Tafel slope for CO2-saturated 5% H2O-TBACl-DEA indicates a decrease in the interfacial concentration gradient of CO2, favouring the prevalence of CO2RR over the HER. These observations may be due to variations in site blocking caused by the accumulation of cations within the outer Helmholtz plane (OHP) when using GC, Ag, or Au electrodes. The site-blocking phenomenon can occur because the chemically inert reduction of CO2 requires substantial energy beyond its energy gap of 13.7 eV. This requirement mainly involves nucleophilic attacks at the carbon, facilitating the dissociation of the C = O bond with an energy requirement of -750 kJ to form CO2●−.45 Under the electroreduction conditions, non-isolated TBA+ cations accumulation near the electrocatalyst surface is evident from a steep slope of impedance-frequency relationship (Fig. 4b, Figure S4), repelling H2O, reduce flooding, and promoting CO2RR to CO2●−. Rate limiting step

In general, the investigation of the rate-limiting step for CO2RR in DEE can be conducted by comparing the heterogeneous electron transport rate constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{et}$$\end{document}; Eqs. 13),48 with the reverse reaction rate constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{r}$$\end{document}), which was previously discussed in Sect. 3.3. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{r}$$\end{document}>\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{et}$$\end{document}, the electron transfer will be the rate-limiting and association step will be rate-liming when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{r}$$\end{document}<\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{et}$$\end{document}. To determine \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{et}$$\end{document} non-adiabatic criterion must be fulfilled including considering the reorganization energy for the CO2RR process in TBACl-DEA. Non-adiabatic CO2RR occurs when the time for nuclear motion of the product formed is lower than that of electron transfer.13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${k}_{et}=\left(\frac{2\pi\:}{{\hslash}}\right)*{\left|V\right|}^{2}*\lambda\:\text{exp}(\frac{{-\varDelta\:G}^{0}}{k\_BT})$$\end{document}

The vibrational frequency (1440 cm-1) of the CO₃²⁻ moiety in K₂CO₃ corresponds to a nuclear motion timescale of 0.368 femtoseconds. The relaxation time for electron transfer was determined by deconvoluting the individual CO2RR process using DRT analysis of EIS data, generated under the same conditions as those used for producing K₂CO₃. Figure 4a shows a comparative EIS data for CO2/TBACl-DEA and TBACl-DEA. The bulk resistance is higher in CO2/TBACl-DEA and lower in TBACl-DEA. In the lower frequency region, a small semicircle appears for TBACl-DEA, indicating the presence of Warburg impedance which is not prevalent for CO2/TBACl-DEA. In Figure S4a and S4b, absence of Warburg impedance for CO2/TBACl-DEA is more obvious considering the slope of the impedance-frequency dependence which becomes almost zero (Figure S4a) unlike that for ordinary TBACl-DEA, showing observable steep slope around the low frequency region (Figure S4b). This characteristic makes CO2/TBACl-DEA suitable for promoting an outer sphere reaction. On the other hand, the slope of the impedance-frequency dependence is steeper and not zero, indicating a slight interaction between TBACl-DEA and the electrode surface. Therefore, it can be inferred that TBACl-DEA has the ability to switch from exhibiting Warburg impedance, which implies diffusion limitation, to the absence of Warburg impedance simply by saturating it with CO2.

To ensure the accuracy of DRT peak deconvolution, further validation of the EIS data was performed using relative deviation according to Kramers-Krönig (KK) analysis, with the results presented in Table S9. A strong indication that CO2●− or K₂CO₃ species originate from the stepwise CO2RR process within the TBACl-DEA electrolyte is the distinct separation of unique relaxation times (τ) and the corresponding polarization resistance (Rpol), as demonstrated by the DRT analysis results in Fig. 4. In the case of Pure TBACl-DEA, four DRT peaks were found with higher polarization resistance compared to CO2/TBACl-DEA which show only three DRT peaks. The low polarization resistance in CO2/TBACl-DEA supports the fact that CO2 solvation by TBACl-DEA creates ions capable of lowering the energy requirements for electroreduction. In other words, the TBACl-DEA offer a catalytic effect. Since we are interested in measuring the time for charge transfer the DRT peak P1 in the CO2/TBACl-DEA is the focal point. DRT peak P2 corresponds to CO2 diffusion, and P3 corresponds to ion diffusion. Hence, the DRT peak P1, which appeared at frequency of 85.76 Hz with a polarization resistance of 1129.71Ω is equivalent to a relaxation time of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{1.856\times\:10}^{-3}$$\end{document}s. In comparison with the time for nuclear motion observed earlier (0.368 femtoseconds), it is higher confirming that the CO2RR process in TBACl-DEA electrolyte is non-adiabatic. This result justifies the use of Eq. 13 to estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{et}\:$$\end{document}for CO2RR in the different TBACl-DEA compositions irrespective of GC, Ag, and Au electrodes (Table 3 and Table S7). These results, particularly in DEE, represent a significant advancement compared to earlier reports on CO2RR kinetics in aqueous electrolytes which are usually assessed through Tafel analysis alone and using Ag electrode45,48. In our study, not only Ag electrode was used, but GC and Au electrodes were included as well, and Tafel analysis was performed with detail electrokinetic analysis. Ultimately, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{et}$$\end{document} was computed for CO2RR in different compositions of the DEE systems. It is evident that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{k}_{et}\gg\:{k}_{r}\:\:$$\end{document}and regardless of the electrode used, suggesting that the association step is the rate-limiting step for CO2RR in TBACl-DEA. Additionally, the reaction predominantly occurs in the outer sphere, as demonstrated by the near-zero impedance-frequency slope for CO2-saturated TBACl-DEA (Figure S4a and S4b). This suggests that the inner sphere reaction is likely insignificant compared to the outer sphere reaction.

TOF of CO2 Electroreduction in TBACl-DEA

The TOF of CO2RR was computed in this study using Eq. 1, which is a function of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\text{I}}_{{\infty\:}}$$\end{document}. Using different ML models, we found that it can be effectively predicted from typical experimental data, particularly for long-term electrolysis at even industrially relevant current densities. This reinforces the relevance of our investigation in optimizing electrochemical systems. Meanwhile, a detailed electrokinetic analysis confirmed that the CO2●− radical from CO2RR is the primary electron acceptor during the initial stages of the reaction, while TBACl-DEA remains stable. This stability arises from the difference in electrophilicity between CO2●− (3.794 eV) and the TBACl-DEA electrolyte (1.559 eV), as shown in the frontier molecular orbitals (Figure S.3 and Supplementary Information S.3). Also, TBACl-DEA’s stability can arise from its high LUMO orbital energy (0.197 eV or 1.23 × 10¹² V), where the iso-surface centers on the TBACl moiety. The high energy required to distribute the iso-surface on the TBACl can likely be the reason why TBACl-DEA has an appreciable buffer capacity, where the pH does not significantly change even after 3-hour CO2 electrolysis. Consequently, the current density portrayed in Fig. 3a and b can only stem from CO2 electrolysis leading to the formation of CO2●−, which ultimately stabilizes within the electrolyte medium. Moreover, this assertion holds under the premise that the Tafel slope of CO2RR significantly surpasses that of HER. Stabilization of CO2●− may also occur if the moisture content within the electrolyte is minimized to less than 5%, thus limiting the product selectivity to other hydrocarbons or HER. While the current density originates from CO2 electrolysis within TBACl-DEA, some levels of noise cannot be directly employed for turnover frequency (TOF) analysis. So, we demonstrate how \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{I}}_{{\infty\:}}$$\end{document} can be reliably determined using ML models trained on the CO2 electrolysis data at different durations (0.5 and 3 h) in TBACl-DEA (1:3), offering predictive power and optimization opportunities for electrochemical processes.

Model optimization for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{I}}_{{\infty\:}}$$\end{document} prediction

The hyperparameters of the Bagging, RF, GB, and KNN ML models were refined, and the optimized values are presented in Table S10 of the supplementary information. These refined parameters were then utilized to train the machine learning models using a dataset obtained from experimental CO2 electrolysis conducted in the TBACl-DEA (1:3) solvent system. Subsequently, the machine learning models were employed to predict the current density pattern. The performance of the machine learning models was evaluated based on the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) and R² values, as shown in Fig. 5. The performance of the models is visually represented through parity plots, which illustrate the distribution of the training and test datasets around the ideal diagonal line. Figure 5a-d present a comparative analysis of the Bagging, RF, GB, and KNN ML models in predicting the current density for CO2RR using a Ag electrode. Each model was trained on experimental data and subsequently tested, with both training and testing performances displayed in the parity plots31,32. Across all models, the predictions closely align with the experimental data, as evidenced by high R² values approaching or exceeding 0.999 and minimal MAPE. This indicates that each model effectively captures the relationship between experimental and predicted current densities. Such alignment suggests that the machine learning models are performing well, accurately predicting the experimental CO2RR current density. Overall, the ML models demonstrate high efficacy in learning and predicting current response patterns of CO2RR, as evidenced by their residual error density distribution centered around zero (Figure S5).

In the Bagging model (Fig. 5a), the R² values for both training and test data reach 0.9999 and 0.9998, respectively, while the MAPEs for training and test are low at 5.836 × 10-5 and 1.765 × 10-4. This demonstrates the Bagging model’s exceptional performance in fitting the data and predicting current density for CO2RR. Similarly, the RF model (Fig. 5b) yields comparable results, with R² values of 0.999 for both training and testing datasets, and MAPE values of 6.174 × 10-5, reflecting high predictive accuracy. Both ensemble methods, Bagging and RF, show robust model performance with minimal deviation in their predictions, suggesting their initial reliability in electrochemical system modeling. The GB model (Fig. 5c) and KNN model (Fig. 5d) also perform well, with R² values of 0.999 for both training and test datasets. However, the MAPE values differ slightly, with the GB model showing higher errors for training (9.224 × 10-5) and test (2.710 × 10-4) data compared to the KNN model (MAPE: 1.542 × 10-4 for training, 2.444 × 10-4 for test). While the GB and KNN models perform slightly less accurately than the Bagging and RF models, their R² values remain extremely high, indicating that they are still suitable for modeling current density predictions in CO2RR process. These parity plots (Fig. 5a-d) are commonly used to visualize the performance of ML models by comparing predicted values with actual experimental values, but it is not sufficient on its own to fully confirm the performance of an ML model due to limited insight into errors distribution. Therefore, diagnostic analysis helps identify these regions and understand why the model might be performing in certain cases.

The diagnostic analysis performed, monitoring MAPE error with iteration counts offer deeper insight into the performance of the various ML models when predicting the current density in CO2RR. The results are presented in Figure S6a-d, and by analyzing these curves, we can assess the stability, consistency, and generalizability of each model, which are essential for optimizing the predictive accuracy of electrochemical systems. In the Bagging model (Figure S6a), the MAPE values for both training and test data fluctuate considerably over 25 iterations. Training errors stabilize around 0.75%, while test errors range from 0.7 to 0.9%, indicating a degree of instability in the Bagging model’s predictions. This variation may suggest that although Bagging reduces variance by aggregating multiple base models, its ability to generalize to new data is not always consistent for CO2RR in deep eutectic electrolytes. The variance in test MAPE might reflect overfitting in certain iterations, where the model is overly tuned to the training data. While the Bagging model demonstrates strong predictive power, the diagnostic curve implies that its reliability across different datasets may require further tuning or regularization to achieve stable performance. For the RF model (Figure S6b), the diagnostic curve also exhibits some fluctuations in MAPE but with lower volatility than Bagging. The training MAPE hovers around 0.75%, while the test MAPE shows variability ranging from 0.7 to 0.875%. Similar to Bagging, the RF model demonstrates occasional spikes in test error, suggesting potential overfitting during some iterations. However, the RF model appears more stable overall, with less drastic swings in MAPE values. RF’s iterative behavior reflects its reliance on decision tree ensembles, where the model may struggle to maintain consistency across all iterations when predicting current density in electrochemical systems. Nevertheless, the RF model still performs reasonably well in minimizing error over multiple iterations, even if occasional performance drops are observed. In contrast, the GB model (Figure S6c) presents a patently different diagnostic curve. The MAPE steadily decreases over 25 boosting iterations, with training and test errors converging towards approximately 0.5%. This suggests that the GB model is highly effective at reducing bias through its boosting technique, which focuses on correcting errors made by prior iterations. The steady improvement in both training and test errors indicates that GB is more capable of achieving consistent and generalizable predictions for CO2RR in deep eutectic electrolytes. The convergence of errors between training and testing implies that the GB model effectively balances both bias and variance, offering strong predictive performance without significant risk of overfitting. Lastly, the KNN model (Figure S6d, Table S10) shows a nearly flat diagnostic curve, with minimal variation in MAPE across iterations. Both training and test errors remain below 0.015%, indicating that the KNN model achieves highly accurate predictions with little discrepancy between training and test data. The low error values and stability of the KNN model suggest that it is well-suited for modeling current density in CO2RR under the presented conditions. However, the extremely low MAPE values also raise questions about the potential limitations of KNN, such as its dependency on the proximity of data points. In some cases, KNN models may struggle to generalize well if the test data diverges significantly from the training data distribution, though this is not observed in our analysis.

Experimental validation for CO2RR current density asymptote

In addition to using indirect metrics to indicate the viability of the ML models, with emphasis on the GM model outperforming KNN, RF, and Bagging based on the diagnostic analysis shown in Figure S6, we further conducted a model interpretability analysis using SHAP to gain deeper insights into the importance of features. This understanding is critical for ensuring that the model aligns with the physical and chemical principles of typical CO2RR experiments. CO2RR can be conducted for various durations to achieve the desired current densities relevant for industrial applications2. However, accurately measuring the asymptotic current density of CO2RR for a specific selective product is often challenging13,44.

Consequently, the CO2RR data presented in Fig. 3b were used as the primary dataset to validate the predictive capacity of the ML models in estimating the asymptotic current density since the CO2RR spanned 3 h. We also included the CO2RR data for 0.5 h. Therefore, the four ML models; Bagging, RF, GB, and KNN were used, even though the GB model is preferable based on the diagnostic curve (Figure S6c). Figures S7a-d show the visualization of the current density profile by overlaying the ML model predictions on the true experimental values for both CO2RR and CO2RR/KOH processes. These models effectively captured the current profile patterns in the CO2RR experimental data and provided precise predictions with minimal error, as indicated by the MSE < < 1. The ML models demonstrated excellent performance in predicting the asymptotic current density for both CO2RR (0.5 h and 3 h) and CO2RR/KOH (3 h), with the results presented in Table 4.

At this stage, since the GB model’s diagnostic curve (Figure S6c) shows a decreasing pattern of MAPE with increasing iterations, we recommend that, in addition to the parity plot, diagnostic and SHAP analyses provide valid judgments regarding suitable results for machine learning modeling of the electrochemical reaction dataset. The credibility and interpretability of the ML model’s predictions related to current densities in CO2RR were confirmed through a SHAP analysis. The results of the SHAP analysis for the 3-hour CO2RR system are shown in Fig. 6a, while Fig. 6b presents the analysis for the CO2RR system with KOH trapping (CO2RR/KOH). Both analyses use current density as the dependent variable, with Gibbs free energy of activation (ΔG), transient turnover frequency (TOF), diffusion coefficient (D), and reaction time (t) as independent variables, which are determined using standard methods.

In the SHAP summary plots for the CO2RR system in Fig. 6a, ΔG and TOF stand out as the most influential features affecting current density. Specifically, ΔG, representing the thermodynamic favourability of the reaction, shows a negative dependence on current density. Lower values of ΔG contribute to higher current densities, as highlighted in Fig. 6a(ii), emphasizing the importance of overcoming the energy barrier in the CO2RR process. TOF, on the other hand, exhibits a strong negative correlation with SHAP values in Fig. 6a(i), suggesting that faster turnover rates reduce current density. While the diffusion coefficient (D) has a limited effect at lower values, its impact increases significantly at higher values, as demonstrated by the trend in Fig. 6a(iii). This indicates that mass transport limitations become more prominent as diffusion increases. In contrast, reaction time (t) appears to have a relatively minor influence on SHAP values, suggesting that it plays a lesser role compared to ΔG and TOF in determining current density. Interestingly, Fig. 6a(iv) examines the combined effects of ΔG and time (t) on the model output. ΔG continues to exhibit a negative correlation with current density, while the influence of time (t) is relatively minimal overall. However, the interaction between these two variables suggests that as ΔG decreases, the significance of time becomes somewhat more relevant, though still limited compared to the other variables.

In the CO2RR/KOH system (Fig. 6b), where KOH trapping is applied, the analysis reveals a shift in the importance of the variables. TOF becomes the dominant factor influencing current density, as indicated by the larger SHAP values in Fig. 6b(i). This suggests that KOH enhances the reaction kinetics that promote the formation of K2CO3, leading to faster turnover rates. Although ΔG still plays an important role in determining current density, its influence is somewhat reduced compared to the CO2RR system without KOH, as seen in Fig. 6b(ii). This reduction in ΔG’s significance may indicate that KOH stabilizes intermediate species and alters the reaction pathway, making the system less dependent on thermodynamic favourability. Moreover, the influence of diffusion (D) is even smaller in the CO2RR/KOH system, as illustrated in Fig. 6b(iii), indicating that KOH trapping reduces the importance of mass transport limitations. In Fig. 6b(iv) for the CO2RR/KOH system, the interaction between ΔG and time (t) exhibits a distinct pattern compared to the CO2RR system without KOH. While ΔG remains negatively correlated with current density, the role of time is further diminished, suggesting that in the KOH-enhanced system, reaction time becomes even less significant. This indicates that the overall reaction is more kinetically driven, likely due to the stabilization of intermediates by KOH, which reduces the reliance on longer reaction times to achieve optimal current densities. In Figure S8a-d, feature t exhibits a distribution of SHAP values that spans both sides of zero, indicating that it has both positive and negative impacts on the ML model output for the 0.5-hour CO2RR.

This SHAP analysis highlights the differences between the CO2RR and CO2RR/KOH systems. In Figure S9, a smaller confidence interval (CI) indicates greater stability in feature importance estimates, enhancing model interpretability and reliability based on SHAP values. The RF model shows moderate CI ranges for the CO2RR/KOH training set but low CI ranges for the CO2RR training and test sets, indicating stability. Bagging performs similarly to RF, while KNN shows slightly higher CI ranges for the CO2RR/KOH training set but maintains low ranges for the CO2RR dataset. GB stands out by maintaining lower CI ranges for both CO2RR training and test sets, suggesting strong stability and reliability. Although GB shows moderate CI ranges for CO2RR/KOH, its consistent performance for the CO2RR dataset makes it the optimal model when prioritizing stability and reliable feature importance, especially for CO2RR predictions.

Conclusions

This study investigated machine learning models that predict the asymptotic current density in electrochemical reactions, thereby aiding in determining the optimal turnover frequency of the process. A comprehensive dataset containing at least 10,000 current density profiles of CO2 electroreduction in deep eutectic electrolytes was used to train four different ML models: Bagging, Gradient Boost, Random Forest, and K-nearest neighbour. Hyperparameter tuning, optimization, validation, and interpretability strategies were conducted to evaluate the model performance comprehensively. The computational performance of the ML models was compared by analyzing their efficiency and effectiveness throughout the training and testing processes. All models demonstrated remarkable accuracy in predicting the asymptotic current response. The K-nearest neighbour, Bagging, Random Forest, and Gradient Boost algorithms achieved MAPE errors of 1.542\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}, 6.174\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:{10}^{-5}$$\end{document}, 6.174\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:{10}^{-5}$$\end{document} and 9.224\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:{10}^{-5}$$\end{document}, respectively, when training on the CO2 electroreduction dataset. In the testing phase of the same dataset, these algorithms recorded MAPE errors of 2.444\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}, 1.765\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}, 6.174\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:{10}^{-5}\:$$\end{document}and 2.71\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}, respectively. These MAPE errors indicate an ultra-high accuracy of approximately 99.9999% for all ML models in predicting the asymptotic current density for CO2 electroreduction in deep eutectic electrolytes. Upon further diagnostic analysis, the Gradient Boost model was found to be more stable, as the MAPE error continued to decrease with increasing boosting iterations. This is acceptable since, in the experimental validation, the predicted current density perfectly overlapped with the experimental CO2 electroreduction values. Our findings indicate that the use of any deep eutectic electrolytes with a cathodic limit of -2.4 V can enhance CO2 electroreduction at a rate of 7.21×107 s-1, even with a moisture content of up to 5%. Under 1% moisture composition, we obtained current density data for a minimum of 3 h of reaction, involving CO2 saturation of the deep eutectic electrolytes at a rate of 0.127 scfh, while minimizing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and promoting outer sphere reactions. The results demonstrate the feasibility of using machine learning models to measure the asymptotic current density of long-term electrochemical reactions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on the measurement of asymptotic current density using machine learning modeling in relation to electrochemical reactions.. Through in-depth SHAP analysis for the CO2RR system, skewed SHAP values of ΔG, TOF, D, and t toward positive indicate that the process is heavily influenced by thermodynamics driven by Gibbs free energy, kinetics (TOF), and mass transfer (D, t). This contrasts with the CO2R/KOH system, where the SHAP values for TOF are evenly distributed, suggesting a more balanced or neutral kinetic influence. Meanwhile, the features ΔG, D, and t are skewed negatively, indicating that increases in these parameters may negatively impact the electrochemical reaction system’s performance, possibly due to different process constraints. Overall, studying the turnover frequency (TOF) measurement of typical electrochemical reactions using the asymptotic current density can enhance our understanding of how to scale up the process in an industrial setting, rationally design electrocatalysts, and optimize non-aqueous electrolyte systems.
