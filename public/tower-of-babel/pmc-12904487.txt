Rosacea overlapping the malar rash: A diagnostic challenge in early systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Antônio Roberto Garcia Júnior, Paulo Sergio Pina, Marília Trierveiler, Camilla Vieira Esteves
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease with heterogeneous manifestations, often overlapping with other conditions and complicating diagnosis. Oral lesions, though less common than cutaneous involvement, may offer important diagnostic clues. We report a 51-year-old female with rosacea, who developed persistent oral ulcers initially linked to a dental prosthesis. Lesions worsened despite adjustments and corticosteroid therapy, and the first biopsy was inconclusive. A second biopsy showed features of lupus erythematosus, including basal layer degeneration, subepithelial lymphocytic infiltrate, and PAS-positive basement membrane thickening. Serology revealed low-titter ANA (1:160) without additional autoantibodies. The coexistence of rosacea and lupus-like lesions delayed diagnosis, highlighting the need for clinicopathological correlation. Over 2 years of multidisciplinary follow-up, the patient achieved remission of oral and cutaneous lesions despite persistently low ANA titters. This case underscores the diagnostic complexity of overlapping dermatologic conditions and reinforces the importance of interdisciplinary management and long-term follow-up in suspected SLE.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, inflammatory autoimmune disease that predominantly affects women. It remains a complex and poorly understood condition, with heterogeneous clinical manifestations that often make establishing a definitive diagnosis particularly challenging.[12]

Cutaneous lesions are frequently the first clinical signs of SLE, with the malar (butterfly) rash being the most characteristic an erythematous, flat or slightly raised rash distributed over the cheeks and nasal bridge, typically exacerbated by sun exposure.

Oral manifestations, although less frequent, are clinically significant. These lesions most commonly affect the buccal mucosa, hard palate, and lower lip, and may present as ulcers, erythematous patches, or keratotic plaques. Their prevalence varies across populations, affecting approximately 30% of patients worldwide, depending on diagnostic criteria, ethnicity, and study design. In the Brazilian population, reported prevalence ranges from 1.9% to 6.9%.[3456]

Finally, this report presents a rare case of SLE with two overlapping mucocutaneous conditions, highlighting the diagnostic complexity and reinforcing the importance of early recognition for prompt and effective treatment.

CASE REPORT

A 51-year-old white female presented with oral ulcers, initially attributed to an ill-fitting fixed dental prosthesis. The patient reported that her dentist attempted to adjust the prosthesis and prescribed a topical corticosteroid; however, the ulcers persisted, causing localized pain, difficulty with oral hygiene, and an inability to eat certain foods, such as citrus fruits. Her medical history included arterial hypertension, managed with Micardis® HCT (Telmisartan 40 mg/Hydrochlorothiazide 12.5 mg), and ongoing dermatological treatment for rosacea.

An extraoral examination revealed an erythematous rash across the nose, cheeks, and forehead [Figure 1a]. Intraoral examination showed bilateral erosions and ulcerations on the oral mucosa, surrounded by white striae, as well as atrophic and hyperkeratotic areas [Figures 1b and c]. The provisional differential diagnoses included erosive lichen planus, lichenoid reaction, and discoid lupus erythematosus.

(a) Extraoral examination showing an erythematous rash with a butterfly wing distribution pattern. (b and c) Intraoral erosions and ulcerations surrounded by white striae, with areas of atrophy and hyperkeratinisation on the oral mucosa

Before a biopsy was performed, the patient underwent treatment with nystatin and had her prosthesis adjusted. Histopathological analysis from the biopsy revealed acute inflammatory infiltrate, but the findings were inconclusive. After the biopsy, the erythematous rash [Figure 2a] and oral pain worsened, raising a clinical suspicion of SLE [Figures 2b and c].

(a) Exacerbation of erythematous facial rash, initially associated with rosacea. (b and c) Oral mucosa after one week of treatment with clobetasol propionate 0.1%

Given the persistence of symptoms and the inconclusive result of the initial biopsy, a second tissue sample was deemed necessary to refine the diagnosis. In view of the exacerbated oral pain and intense mucosal inflammation, topical clobetasol propionate 0.1% was prescribed for seven days to reduce symptoms and allow a more representative biopsy.

It is important to note that topical corticosteroid delivery in the oral cavity is considerably limited by salivary dilution, mucosal turnover, and mechanical disruption, which significantly reduce drug contact time and bioavailability. In this context, despite clobetasol’s well-documented potential to alter epithelial architecture, the short duration of use, combined with the inherent limitations of mucosal drug absorption, minimized the likelihood of histological masking. Moreover, its use in the management of chronic vesiculobullous oral diseases is well supported in the literature, with favourable outcomes and minimal adverse effects.

Microscopic examination revealed a hyperkeratinized and atrophic epithelium with loss or shortening of the rete ridges, basal layer degeneration, and leukocyte exocytosis. Lymphocytic infiltration was noted within the lamina propria, predominantly subepithelial, and surrounding blood vessels. A discrete subepithelial zone free of inflammatory infiltrate was observed, and PAS staining demonstrated thickening of the basement membrane. These histopathological features supported the diagnosis of lupus erythematosus [Figures 3a and b].

(a) Photomicrograph of oral mucosa showing hyperkeratinised, atrophic stratified squamous epithelium with basal cell degeneration, leukocyte exocytosis, and subepithelial lymphocytic/perivascular infiltrate H&E (haematoxylin and eosin stain, ×100). (b) Periodic acid Schiff (PAS) staining demonstrating thickening of the basement membrane (×400)

At the time of the initial evaluation, the combination of a malar rash and histopathological features consistent with lupus raised a clinical suspicion of early SLE. Serological testing revealed a positive ANA at a titter of 1:160, a low-titter result that may be observed in autoimmune rheumatic diseases but is not, in isolation, diagnostic. Other markers, including anti-Ro, anti-La, haematological, and renal parameters, were within normal limits. Given this scenario, the patient was placed under continuous dermatological surveillance.

Over a 2-year follow-up period, serial laboratory tests confirmed persistently low ANA titters, with no serological conversion or emergence of additional autoantibodies. Interestingly, the patient has also developed clinical features consistent with fibromyalgia, including diffuse musculoskeletal pain and fatigue, and is under multidisciplinary management. This longitudinal observation highlights the importance of clinicopathological correlation and sustained follow-up in suspected lupus cases, particularly when early serological findings raise but do not confirm the possibility of systemic disease.

DISCUSSION

This case represents a rare scenario of true disease coexistence, in which the patient had an established diagnosis of rosacea and later developed oral and facial manifestations consistent with SLE. It highlights the diagnostic complexity involved in identifying SLE when a chronic dermatologic condition with overlapping features is already present. The persistent facial erythema, initially attributed solely to rosacea, contributed to a delayed recognition of the concurrent autoimmune disorder.

Several studies have reported the clinical similarity between rosacea and SLE; however, the true coexistence of both conditions in the same patient is rarely documented. In one study four patients with rosacea-like facial erythema and papules were ultimately diagnosed with SLE after histopathological and immunological evaluations, notably with positive ANA results.[7] These cases reflect the diagnostic overlap between SLE and rosacea, particularly in patients with isolated facial involvement, but did not confirm the simultaneous presence of both diseases.

Oral lesions in SLE, although less frequent than cutaneous involvement, are clinically important and may precede or coincide with systemic signs.[3456] Their presentation, often as ulcers, keratotic plaques, or erythematous areas, shares features with other immune-mediated mucosal disorders, including erosive lichen planus and lichenoid reactions, making differential diagnosis essential.[8]

In this case, histopathological confirmation was critical. The decision to perform a second biopsy following symptom progression and clinical re-evaluation underscores the importance of clinicopathological correlation and maintaining a high index of suspicion in cases with overlapping features.

The bilateral distribution, keratotic striae, and histopathological findings (including basal cell degeneration, PAS-positive basement membrane, and subepithelial lymphocytic infiltrate) associated with the positive ANA result supported the autoimmune etiology and guided appropriate management.[5]

In cases of mucocutaneous involvement, establishing a differential diagnosis becomes particularly critical, especially when clinical features overlap with other immune-mediated vesiculobullous and lichenoid disorders such as Lichen Planus (LP), Pemphigus Vulgaris (PV), and Mucous Membrane Pemphigoid (MMP). These conditions often present with similar oral manifestations, making accurate distinction essential for proper diagnosis and effective clinical management.

While direct immunofluorescence (DIF) remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis, it is not always accessible in all clinical settings. In such situations, careful assessment of the clinical presentation in combination with routine histopathological examination using haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining becomes indispensable.

A comparative analysis of the key clinical and histological features of Lupus Erythematosus (LE), LP, PV, and MMP can serve as a valuable diagnostic tool in the absence of DIF. This comparison is based on key characteristics observable without direct immunofluorescence, emphasizing clinical presentation and haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) findings. In the present case, despite histopathological findings strongly suggestive of lupus, laboratory tests were inconclusive, with low ANA titters, normal hemogram, and normal renal function markers. Therefore, the final diagnosis was made by the dermatologist, underscoring the importance of collaboration among health professionals. After two years of follow-up, the patient remains under multidisciplinary care, with recurrent oral and facial lesions, especially during periods of emotional stress. CONCLUSION

This case report reinforces the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in establishing complex diagnoses, particularly when laboratory findings are inconclusive. It also highlights the relevance of long-term follow-up, as the patient continued to present with oral and facial lesions, especially during periods of emotional stress.
