Strengthening student mental health support systems in India: reflections on the Supreme Court's landmark guidelines
Authors: Seema Mehrotra, Lakshmi Vijayakumar
Published: 2025

On July 25, 2025, the Hon'ble Supreme Court of India issued fifteen binding guidelines to prevent student suicides, in a landmark judgment delivered by Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta in Sukdeb Saha v. State of Andhra Pradesh & Ors.1 These guidelines mark a significant step toward strengthening mental health support systems across educational institutions and coaching centres. These are broad in scope and encompass elements such as accessible mental health policies, counselling services, training of teaching and non-teaching staff, establishment of referral protocols, redressal pathways, and other protective measures. The judgment also requires the Union of India to file a compliance affidavit detailing implementation measures and monitoring mechanisms across states and union territories. We share our perspectives here as mental health professionals engaged in public health initiatives.

While these binding guidelines align with existing frameworks such as the National Suicide Prevention Strategy,2 UMMEED,3 and UGC4 guidelines on student wellbeing; several aspects of the judgment warrant particular attention. These include: (a) the requirement for trained professionals, mandating appointment of counsellors with appropriate academic qualifications and supervised training; (b) mandatory staff sensitization-teaching and non-teaching personnel to be trained by mental health professionals in early identification, emotional support, and referral; (c) systematic documentation-institutions expected to regularly record uptake of services, referrals, and trainings; (d) minimum staffing norms, stipulating that institutions with more than 100 students must have at least one counsellor, with efforts to maintain optimal student–counsellor ratio.

These guidelines have potentially far-reaching impact, but meaningful implementation of any such guidelines demands more than tokenistic compliance. Their effective implementation requires sincere deliberation, sustained systemic commitment, and collective willingness among stakeholders to confront complex challenges. We enumerate some of these challenges pertaining to counselling services and training a) Lack of granular, nuanced data: for instance, the All-India Survey on Higher Education reports the percentage of institutions with counsellors as part of infrastructure listings.5 Details regarding the qualifications of counsellors in schools and colleges, roles performed, service models, functional status, or service uptake remain inaccessible. Availability of robust national-level data to understand the ground realities would be a crucial first step towards planning implementation. b) Definitional inconsistencies: There is wide variability in how the term “counsellor” is defined across educational campuses in the context of supporting student mental health and wellbeing, encompassing designated but untrained faculty from various disciplines, to lay counsellors, and special educators, among others. This further complicates efforts to develop a clear understanding of service provision capacity within educational institutions. Beyond data gaps, additional barriers include budgetary constraints,6 a shortage of trained professionals,7,8 variability in the quality of supervised training of service providers, ambiguities on their roles and responsibilities in campuses, and low uptake of available mental health services, often driven by limited awareness, stigma, and lack of trust in confidentiality.8,9

To begin addressing these systemic gaps, we propose a few potential measures, not definitive or comprehensive, but intended as starting points for further reflection and collaborative action with special reference to provision of mental health services and training of teaching and non-teaching staff:I.Dealing with data gaps and definitional inconsistencies1.Stakeholder consultations to build consensus on standardizing nomenclature, minimum qualifications, and training requirements for in-campus mental health service providers, in a manner that balances the need for acceptable service quality with the existing scarcity of trained professionals2.Establish structured reporting mechanisms at district and state levels to monitor access and uptake of counselling services in educational institutions, aligned with Supreme Court directives on district-level monitoring committees.II.Addressing scarcity of trained human resources in campuses1.Introduce dedicated budgetary provisions for counselling services in educational settings, using central–state cost-sharing through new schemes or by expanding existing health and wellness programs.2.Assess feasibility of deploying a shared pool of counsellors across government institutions, and mandate minimum standards for mental health access as a criterion for accreditation of private institutions.3.Develop SOPs for ethical hybrid service delivery, including online and telephonic counselling, to improve access and address geographic and linguistic barriers.4.Leverage public–private partnerships to develop student-friendly, affordable delivery models across districts.III.Provisioning training and capacity building for service providers and staff1.Implement standardized competency-building programs for mental health service providers in educational institutions to ensure consistent quality and delivery of a range of promotive, preventive and counselling services2.Deploy scalable training programs for faculty and staff in empathetic communication, early risk identification, and timely support, with sensitivity to diverse student wellbeing needs, including those from disadvantaged backgrounds.IV.Building responsive and evolving mental health support systems in campuses1.Establish national mechanisms for periodic student feedback to improve service responsiveness.2.Allocate targeted funding for implementation research to enable evidence-informed, acceptable and scalable student mental health solutions, co-developed in partnerships with all stakeholders

These reflections are offered in the spirit of strengthening collective efforts toward building inclusive and accessible mental health support systems for students across India. These reflections are intentionally narrow in scope, focused only on the guidelines that pertain to counselling and training. The judgment lays a vital foundation, but its real impact will depend on the sincerity and consistency with which these guidelines are implemented and monitored on the ground to evolve robust systems.
