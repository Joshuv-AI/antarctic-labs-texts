Constructing Magic Squares: An Integer Constraint Satisfaction Problem and a Fast Approach
João Vitor Pamplona, Maria Eduarda Pinheiro, Luiz-Rafael Santos

Abstract
Magic squares are a fascinating mathematical challenge that has intrigued mathematicians for centuries. Given a positive (and possibly large) integer n n , one of the main challenges that still remains is to find, within a reasonable computational time, a magic square of order n n , that is, a square matrix of order n n with unique integers from a min a_{\min} to a max a_{\max} , such that the sum of each row, column, and diagonal equals a constant 𝒞 ⁡ ( A ) \mathcal{C}(A) . In this work, we first present an integer constraint satisfaction problem for constructing a magic square of order n n . Nonetheless, the solution time of this problem grows exponentially as the order increases. To overcome this limitation, we also propose a fast approach that constructs magic squares depending on whether n n is odd, singly even, or doubly even . Moreover, we provide a proof of the correctness of this novel approach. Our numerical results show that our method can construct magic squares of order up to 70 000 70\,000 in less than 140 140 seconds, demonstrating its efficiency and scalability.



1
Introduction

Magic Squares are numerical puzzles that have captivated mathematicians and enthusiasts for centuries. While
their
exact origins remain a mystery, a Chinese myth speaks of a turtle rising from the depths of the Lo River during a deluge. Adorning its shell was a peculiar design: a three-by-three grid bearing unique numbers, each row, column, and diagonal summing to fifteen. Mathematically, it can be expressed as the matrix:

\begin{bmatrix}4&9&2\\
3&5&7\\
8&1&6\end{bmatrix}.

There
is other evidence
of Magic Squares not only in China but India as well, dating back to before our era. For instance, a magic square of order
3
appears in an 8th-century Arabic manuscript linked to Apollonius of Tyana
[
3
]
.

Around 1530,
von Nettesheim [22]
wrote
De Occulta Philosophia
, in which he discussed Magic Squares of order 3 to 9. Those squares were associated with astrological planets: Saturn (associated with order 3), Jupiter (order 4), Mars (order 5),
the Sun
(order 6), Venus (order 7), Mercury (order 8), and
the Moon
(order 9).

Formally, given an integer
n\geq 3
, and a positive integer
a_{\min},
one can define a
magic square of order
n
as a square matrix
A\coloneqq[a_{ij}]\in\mathds{N}^{n\times n}
with
magic constant

\mathcal{C}(A)\coloneqq\dfrac{n(n^{2}+1)}{2}+n(a_{\min}-1),

that satisfies the following properties:

1.

Each component in
A
is unique.

2.

The minimum element in
A
is
a_{\min}
and the maximum is
a_{\max}\coloneqq a_{\min}+n^{2}-1
,

3.

For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}=\mathcal{C}(A)
,
i.e.
, the sum of the elements of each column is
\mathcal{C}(A)
.

4.

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}=\mathcal{C}(A)
. Thus, the sum of the elements of each row is equal to
\mathcal{C}(A)
.

5.

The trace of
A
is equal to
\mathcal{C}(A)
, that is,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}=\mathcal{C}(A)
.

6.

The sum of the secondary diagonal is equal to
\mathcal{C}(A)
,
i.e.
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n-i+1}=\mathcal{C}(A).

More than being a mathematical puzzle, Magic Squares have been used in applications such as cryptography, computer science, and even in the design of games. For instance,
Fahimi [12]

shows that Magic Squares create a very tiny part of square matrices
whose
mass center and their geometrical center coincide.
Fahimi and Javadi [11]
presents some of the most important unsolved problems and some physical applications of Magic Squares. Some Linear Algebra
properties
of Magic Squares are also presented in
Lee et al. [16]
,
Essen [10]
,
Loly et al. [18]
. Besides these properties, Magic Squares are also studied even in pure Algebra, as can be seen in
Westbury [23]
,
Landsberg and Manivel [15]
.

In this short note, we present two novel approaches for constructing Magic Squares of arbitrary order. Our first approach frames the problem as a Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP). This strategy of reformulating mathematical puzzles as CSPs is well-established in optimization literature. Prior work includes CSP models for edge-matching puzzles
[
1
]
, the classic Sudoku puzzle
[
6
]
, and even a variation known as the Jidoku puzzle
[
19
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, the only prior work that formulates the construction of Magic Squares as a CSP model is that of
Crawford et al. [7]
. However,
their approach only considers
a_{min}=1
. Moreover, their computational experiments are limited to Magic Squares of order
7
only, taking 487 seconds to solve. In contrast, the CSP model presented in this work is applicable to Magic Squares of any order. Although this model always yields a valid Magic Square when a solution is found, it becomes computationally expensive for large values of
n
.

To overcome this limitation, we introduce a second approach that leverages the divisibility properties of
n
to efficiently generate Magic Squares. To put our
method
in perspective with existing
approaches
, the work by
Cruz and Grandchamp [8]
proposed a two-phase heuristic for solving magic squares. However, the authors report that the method proposed by them requires over two hours to construct a magic square of order 500, whereas our
method
completes the task in under
0.5
milliseconds; see our numerical experiments below. Moreover,
Liu et al. [17]
focuses on an algorithm to construct all magic squares of order
4
.
Recent works by
Takemi et al. [21]
,
Takemi et al. [20]
introduced multistage evolutionary algorithms for magic square construction. Their experimental results show a solution time of
6
minutes for a magic square order 90; meanwhile, our
algorithm
achieves this in under
{10}^{-4}
seconds.

Most of the approaches mentioned above do not offer a guarantee of correctness. In contrast, we establish the validity of our fast approach and ensure it consistently produces a magic square. Furthermore, we provide extensive numerical results showing reliability across a wide range of cases, for magic squares of orders as large as
n=$70\,000$
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the CSP model for constructing Magic Squares. In Section
3
, we introduce our
fast
approach, which is divided into three cases based on the order of the Magic Square.
Section
4
is devoted to proving the correctness of this novel approach
.
In Section
5
, we present numerical results comparing the performance of
both approaches (the CSP formulation and the fast approach).
Finally, in Section
6
, we offer concluding remarks and discuss potential future research directions.

2
Integer Constraint Satisfaction Problem

In this section
,
we show that a magic square of order
n
with magic constant
\mathcal{C}(A)
can be found by solving a constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) where the variables are binary. More precisely, given
i,j\in[1,n]:=\{1,\cdots,n\}
and a number
k\in[a_{\min},a_{\max}],
the variable
x_{ij}^{k}

takes the following values

x_{ij}^{k}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if number $k$ is in $\{i,j\}$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence, our goal is to find
x\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n\times n^{2}}
that satisfies the following
properties

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{ij}^{k}

\displaystyle=1,\quad k\in[a_{\min},a_{\max}],

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=a_{\min}}^{a_{\max}}x_{ij}^{k}

\displaystyle=1,\quad i,j\in[1,n],

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=a_{\min}}^{a_{\max}}kx_{ij}^{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}(A),\quad i\in[1,n],

(2.1c)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=a_{\min}}^{a_{\max}}kx_{ij}^{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}(A),\quad j\in[1,n],

(2.1d)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=a_{\min}}^{a_{\max}}kx_{ii}^{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}(A),

(2.1e)

\displaystyle\sum_{i+j=n+1}\sum_{k=a_{\min}}^{a_{\max}}kx_{ij}^{k}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}(A),

(2.1f)

\displaystyle x_{ij}^{k}\in\{0,1\},\quad

\displaystyle i,j\in[1,n],\quad k\in[a_{\min},a_{\max}].

(2.1g)

The rationale of the model is that, for instance, Constraints (
2.1a
) and (
2.1b
) ensure, respectively, that each number can be
assigned to
only one cell and each cell must contain a number
k\in[a_{\min},a_{\max}]
. The guarantee that the sum of each row and column of
A
is equal to
\mathcal{C}(A)
is given by Constraints (
2.1c
) and (
2.1d
). Constraints (
2.1e
) and (
2.1f
) enforce that the trace and the sum of the secondary diagonal of
A

are

\mathcal{C}(A)
. Finally, Constraint (
2.1g
)
leads
to

x_{ij}^{k}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if number $k$ is in $\{i,j\}$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

for all
i,j\in[1,n]
, and
k\in[a_{\min},a_{\max}].

Based on that, each
entry

a_{ij}
of the magic square
A
is given by

a_{ij}=k\in[a_{\min},a_{\max}]:x_{i,j}^{k}=1,\quad i,j\in[1,n].

We conjecture that the problem is NP-hard, though a formal proof is beyond the scope of this work
. Our focus here is on solution techniques, not a formal complexity analysis
.

As mentioned earlier, an integer constraint satisfaction problem can be computationally challenging,
a fact that is corroborated
by the numerical tests presented later in this paper.

To mitigate this computational burden, in the next section we propose a new algorithm that can efficiently construct Magic Squares of any size. This
algorithm
offers a substantial performance improvement compared to the traditional CSP solver, or other known methods, especially for larger orders.

3
A Fast Approach

In this section, we present algorithms for constructing Magic Squares of arbitrary order
n
. Without loss of generality, we consider
a_{\min}\coloneqq 1
. If
a_{\min}\neq 1,
after solving the problem with
a_{\min}=1
and getting
a_{ij}
, we can redefine
a_{ij}
to be
a_{ij}+a_{\min}-1
.

We categorize our approach into three distinct cases, each requiring a specific algorithmic solution:

1.

Odd Order
: When
n
is odd, we employ the well-established Siamese Method, a technique attributed to
De la Loubère [9]
;

2.

Doubly Even Order
: For
n
divisible by
4
, we introduce a novel algorithm that leverages a specific pattern of number placement within the square;

3.

Singly Even Order
: When
n
is even but not divisible by
4
, we propose a novel
approach
that systematically fills the square, ensuring the magic square sum property.

3.1
Odd Magic Squares

The Siamese method, also known as the De La Loubere method,
attributed to
De la Loubère [9]
, is a well-known algorithm for constructing odd-order magic squares. It is based on the principle of placing numbers in a specific pattern to ensure that the sum of each row, column, and diagonal is equal. The algorithm works as follows:
the numbers are distributed in order
, from
1
to
n^{2}
, in the positions of the matrix
A\coloneqq[a_{ij}]\in\mathds{N}^{n\times n}
, with
n
odd. Recall that
a_{\min}=1
and
a_{\max}=n^{2}
. The steps to achieve the desired magic square are
summarized
next:

1.

The integer
1
is placed in the central position of the first
row
; that is,
set

a_{1,(n+1)/2}=1
.

2.

For any
k=1,\ldots,n^{2}-1
already allocated,
k+1
will be allocated in the position that remains:

(a)

on the upper right diagonal, if
it exists
;

(b)

in the last row of the column to
the
right, if there is no upper row;

(c)

in the first column of the top row, if there is no column on the right;

(d)

in the same column but in the
row below
if the top right diagonal position is already occupied or if
k
is allocated in
a_{1,n}
.

The correctness of the Siamese method is demonstrated in
Ball and Coxeter [2]
. Specifically, for any odd
n
, the matrix
A
generated by the Siamese method is a magic square with the magic constant
\mathcal{C}(A).

Doubly Even Magic Squares

We now delineate our

is doubly even, meaning that
n
is divisible by
4

detailed in

4
Correctness of our novel approaches

In this section, we establish the correctness of Algorithms
1
and
2
, which are designed to construct magic squares of doubly even and singly even orders, respectively.

4.1
Algorithm
1

We begin by proving the correctness of Algorithm
1
, that is, that our method yields a valid magic square for all doubly even values of
n
. We first establish a lemma concerning the symmetry of the summations.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n
be a doubly even integer, and define the outer and inner index sets as
\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{out}}}:=\{1,\dots,\frac{n}{4}\}\cup\{\frac{3n}{4}+1,\dots,n\}
and
\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{in}}}:=\{\frac{n}{4}+1,\dots,\frac{3n}{4}\}
. The sum of the integers in the middle two quarters is equal to the sum of the integers in the first and last quarters. That is:

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{out}}}}j=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{in}}}}j.

(4.1)

Proof.

Consider
n=4k
for some
k\in\mathds{N}
. Using the arithmetic series formula
\sum_{j=a}^{b}j=\frac{(b-a+1)(a+b)}{2},
we evaluate both sides of (
4.1
). The left-hand side, consisting of the first and last quarters of the range, yields:

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{out}}}}j=\sum_{j=1}^{k}j+\sum_{j=3k+1}^{4k}j=\frac{k(k+1)}{2}+\frac{k(7k+1)}{2}=4k^{2}+k.

Similarly, the right-hand side comprises the middle two quarters:

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{in}}}}j=\sum_{j=k+1}^{3k}j=\frac{2k(4k+1)}{2}=4k^{2}+k.

That is, the identity holds for any doubly even
n
.

∎

We now show that the sum of each row of
A
produced by Algorithm
1
is equal to the magic constant
\mathcal{C}(A).

Lemma 4.2
.

Assuming
n
is doubly even, Algorithm
1
produces a matrix
A
where each row sum is equal to the magic constant
\mathcal{C}(A)=\dfrac{n(n^{2}+1)}{2}
.

Proof.

As a consequence of the block decomposition in Step
2
and the definition of the elements
a_{ij}
in Step
3
, for the rows in the outer index set,
i\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{out}}}
, we fix
i
and apply Lemma
4.1
to obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{out}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(}(i-1)n+j{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}+\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{in}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(}n^{2}+1-(n(i-1)+j){\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\dfrac{n}{2}((i-1)n)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{n}{2}}(n^{2}+1)-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{n}{2}}(n(i-1))+\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{out}}}}j-\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{in}}}}j}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\dfrac{n(n^{2}+1)}{2}=\mathcal{C}(A)}.\end{array}

The case for
i\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{in}}}
is strictly analogous to the one above. Therefore, for every row
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the sum of the elements is
\mathcal{C}(A)
.

∎

We now prove that the sum of the elements in each column of the matrix generated by Algorithm
1
is also
\dfrac{n(n^{2}+1)}{2}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Given that
n
is doubly even, the matrix
A
generated by Algorithm
1
satisfies the column sum property, where each sum equals the magic constant:

\mathcal{C}(A)=\frac{n(n^{2}+1)}{2}.

Proof.

In view of the block decomposition in Step
2
and the definition of the entries
a_{ij}
in Step
3
, for any column index
j\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{out}}}
, we fix
j
and using Lemma
4.1
obtain:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{in}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(}(i-1)n+j{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}+\displaystyle\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{out}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(}n^{2}+1-(n(i-1)+j){\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\dfrac{n}{2}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-n}+j+n^{2}+1+n-j)+n\left(\displaystyle\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{out}}}}i-\displaystyle\sum_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}i}\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{in}}}}i\right)}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\dfrac{n(n^{2}+1)}{2}=\mathcal{C}(A)}.\end{array}

As with the row cases in Lemma
4.2
, the proof for column indices
j\in\mathcal{B}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{in}}}
is analogous to the one above. Hence, for each column of
A
the sum of the elements is
\mathcal{C}(A).

∎

Using the Lemmas above, the following theorem establishes the correctness of Algorithm
1
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Assuming
n
is doubly even, Algorithm
1
produces a magic square matrix with magic constant
\mathcal{C}(A)=\dfrac{n(n^{2}+1)}{2}
.

Proof.

In view of Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
, it remains only to show that the sums of the entries along both the main and secondary diagonals are equal to
\mathcal{C}(A){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}.}

Note that the main and secondary diagonals only have elements in the
X
blocks. Hence,

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(}(i-1)n+i{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-n^{2}+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(n+1)i}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-n^{2}+(n+1)\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}(n+1)}{2}}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{n}{2}(-2n+(n+1)^{2})}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{n}{2}(-2n+n^{2}+2n+1)}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{n}{2}(n^{2}+1)}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}(A),}&\\
\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n-i+1}}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(}(i-1)n+n-i+1{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n(-n+n+1)+\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(n-1)i}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n+(n-1)\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}(n+1)}{2}}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{n}{2}(2+(n+1)(n-1){\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{n}{2}(2+n^{2}-1)}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{n}{2}(n^{2}+1)}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{C}(A).}&\\
\end{array}

That is, both diagonals sum to
\mathcal{C}(A)
, proving that for a doubly even
n
, Algorithm
1
generates a magic square.

∎

4.2
Algorithm
2

The design of Algorithm
2
is grounded in the principle of
offset compensation
across quadrants. Therefore, to establish its correctness, the following lemmas are essential.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
n
be a singly even integer. The matrix
A
constructed up to Step
2
of Algorithm
2
satisfies the following properties:

(P1)

Every column sum is equal to the magic constant
\mathcal{C}(A)
.

(P2)

Each of the first
n/2
rows has a sum of
\mathcal{C}(A)-\frac{n^{3}}{8}
.

(P3)

Each of the last
n/2
rows has a sum of
\mathcal{C}(A)+\frac{n^{3}}{8}
.

(P4)

The main diagonal sum is
\mathcal{C}(A)-\frac{n^{3}}{4}
.

(P5)

The secondary diagonal sum is
\mathcal{C}(A)+\frac{n^{3}}{4}
.

Proof.

In Step
2
, by constructing each sub-matrix
A^{\ell}
as an odd-order magic square via the Siamese method, we ensure that each quadrant possesses an internal magic constant
\mathcal{C}(A^{\ell})
, given by:

\mathcal{C}(A^{\ell})=\frac{p(p^{2}+1)}{2}+p\cdot(a^{\ell}_{\min}-1)

where
p=n/2
and

a_{\min}^{1}=1,\quad a_{\min}^{2}=\frac{n^{2}}{4}+1,\quad a_{\min}^{3}=\frac{2n^{2}}{4}+1,\quad a_{\min}^{4}=3\frac{n^{2}}{4}+1.

Using these facts, we can now prove the properties above:

(P1)

For the columns in the left half, the sum is given by the sum of the magic constants of the vertical quadrants,
\mathcal{C}(A^{1})+\mathcal{C}(A^{4})
. Substituting the values for
a^{1}_{\min}
and
a^{4}_{\min}
and rearranging the terms, we obtain:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{C}(A^{1})+\mathcal{C}(A^{4})&=&\dfrac{p(p^{2}+1)}{2}+p(a^{1}_{\min}-1)+\dfrac{p(p^{2}+1)}{2}+p(a^{4}_{\min}-1)\\
&=&p(p^{2}+1)+p\left(2+3\dfrac{n^{2}}{4}-2\right)\\
&=&\dfrac{n}{2}\left(\dfrac{n^{2}}{4}+1\right)+\dfrac{3n^{3}}{8}=\dfrac{n^{3}}{8}+\dfrac{n}{2}+\dfrac{3n^{3}}{8}\\
&=&\dfrac{n(n^{2}+1)}{2}=\mathcal{C}(A).\end{array}

An analogous derivation applies to the right half of the columns, where
\mathcal{C}(A^{3})+\mathcal{C}(A^{2})=\mathcal{C}(A)
. Thus, by construction, every column of the composite matrix
A
satisfies the magic constant property.

(P2)

A row in the upper half sums to
\mathcal{C}(A^{1})+\mathcal{C}(A^{3})
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{C}(A^{1})+\mathcal{C}(A^{3})&=&\dfrac{p(p^{2}+1)}{2}+p(a^{1}_{\min}-1)+\dfrac{p(p^{2}+1)}{2}+p(a^{3}_{\min}-1)\\
&=&p(p^{2}+1)+p\left(\dfrac{n^{2}}{2}\right)=\dfrac{n}{2}\left(\dfrac{n^{2}}{4}+1+\dfrac{n^{2}}{2}\right)\\
&=&\dfrac{n\left(\dfrac{3n^{2}}{4}+1\right)}{2}=\dfrac{n(n^{2}+1)}{2}-\dfrac{n}{2}\dfrac{n^{2}}{4}\\
&=&\mathcal{C}(A)-\dfrac{n^{3}}{8}.\end{array}

(P3)

Given a row in the lower half, the sum of all elements in this row satisfies
\mathcal{C}(A^{4})+\mathcal{C}(A^{2})
, and using the second row of the expression above, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{C}(A^{2})+\mathcal{C}(A^{4})&=&p(p^{2}+1)+p\left(n^{2}\right)=\mathcal{C}(A^{1})+\mathcal{C}(A^{3})+p\left(\dfrac{n^{2}}{2}\right)\\
&=&\mathcal{C}(A)-\dfrac{n^{3}}{8}+\dfrac{n^{3}}{4}=\mathcal{C}(A)+\dfrac{n^{3}}{8}.\end{array}

(P4)

Observe that the main diagonal sum is
\mathcal{C}(A^{1})+\mathcal{C}(A^{2})
, and, again using the second row of expression in (P2), we obtain:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{C}(A^{1})+\mathcal{C}(A^{2})&=&p(p^{2}+1)+p\left(\dfrac{n^{2}}{4}\right)=\mathcal{C}(A^{1})+\mathcal{C}(A^{3})-p\left(\dfrac{n^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&=&\mathcal{C}(A)-\dfrac{n^{3}}{8}-\dfrac{n^{3}}{8}=\mathcal{C}(A)-\dfrac{n^{3}}{4}.\end{array}

(P5)

The sum of the secondary diagonal is
\mathcal{C}(A^{3})+\mathcal{C}(A^{4})
. And, using the first equality of expression in (P4), we obtain:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{C}(A^{4})+\mathcal{C}(A^{3})&=&p(p^{2}+1)+p\left(\dfrac{5n^{2}}{4}\right)\\
&=&\mathcal{C}(A^{1})+\mathcal{C}(A^{2})+p\left(n^{2}\right)\\
&=&\mathcal{C}(A)-\dfrac{n^{3}}{4}+\dfrac{n^{3}}{2}=\mathcal{C}(A)+\dfrac{n^{3}}{4}.\end{array}

∎

Given Lemma
4.5
, it remains to be shown that the permutations executed in Steps
2
–
2
satisfy three critical conditions: first, that the sum of the columns of
A
is maintained; second, that the exchange process effectively redistributes the numerical mass by adding
n^{3}/8
to the rows of the first half and subtracting it from the second half; and finally, that the diagonals are corrected by adding
n^{3}/4
to the main diagonal while simultaneously removing the same amount from the anti-diagonal. This redistribution ensures that all row and diagonal sums equal
\mathcal{C}(A)
.

Note that the exchanges in Step
2
are strictly vertical. Consequently, the column membership of each element remains unchanged, ensuring that the column sums established in Lemma
4.5
are invariant under these operations. To facilitate the verification of the remaining conditions regarding the row and diagonal sums, we present the following lemmas.

Lemma 4.6
.

Assuming
n
is a singly even natural number, the exchanges
A^{1}_{1}\leftrightarrow A^{4}_{1}
,
A^{1}_{3}~\leftrightarrow~A^{4}_{3}
, and
w_{1}\leftrightarrow w_{4}
in Step
2
of Algorithm
2
add a total value of
c\coloneqq\frac{3n^{3}-6n^{2}}{16}
to each row in the upper half of the matrix, while simultaneously removing the same value
c
from each row in the lower half.

Proof.

By Step
2
, each quadrant
A^{\ell}
is a magic square with a minimum element
a^{\ell}_{\min}=\frac{(\ell-1)n^{2}}{4}+1
. Consequently, the elements of
A^{\ell}
satisfy the following offset relationship:

a_{ij}^{\ell}=a_{ij}^{1}+\dfrac{(\ell-1)n^{2}}{4},\quad\forall\ell\in[1,4].

(4.2)

Specifically, the relationship between the values in
A^{4}
and
A^{1}
is given by
a^{4}_{ij}=a^{1}_{ij}+\frac{3n^{2}}{4}
. Thus, when the exchanges 1, 2, and 3 in Step
2
occur, each element from
A^{4}
moved to the upper half adds the offset value of
\frac{3n^{2}}{4}
to its respective row. Conversely, each element from
A^{1}
moved to the lower half results in the removal of
\frac{3n^{2}}{4}
from those row sums.

Because the blocks
A^{\ell}_{r}\in\mathds{N}^{\frac{n-2}{4}\times\frac{n-2}{4}}
and
w^{\ell}\in\mathds{N}^{\frac{n-2}{4}}
for
\ell\in[2,3]
and
r\in[1,4]
, we permute exactly
\frac{n-2}{4}
elements in each row. That is, the total numerical mass added to each row in the upper half is
\frac{3n^{2}}{4}\times\frac{n-2}{4}=c
, with the same amount
c
being removed from each row in the lower half.

∎

It remains to determine the impact of the fourth exchange in Step
2
on the row sums.

Lemma 4.7
.

Consider
n
to be a singly even natural number. The exchange
B^{3}_{2}\leftrightarrow B^{2}_{2}
presented in Step
2
of Algorithm
2
removes a total value of
d\coloneqq\frac{n^{3}-6n^{2}}{16}
from each row in the upper half of the matrix, while simultaneously adding the same value
d
to each row in the lower half.

Proof.

By Expression (
4.2
), the relationship between the values in
A^{3}
and
A^{2}
is given by
a^{3}_{ij}=a^{1}_{ij}+\frac{2n^{2}}{4}
and
a^{2}_{ij}=a^{1}_{ij}+\frac{n^{2}}{4}
. Thus,
a^{3}_{ij}=a^{2}_{ij}+\frac{n^{2}}{4}
. This means that when the exchange
B^{3}_{2}\leftrightarrow B^{2}_{2}
is performed, for each exchanged element in the rows of the upper half, we remove the offset value of
\frac{n^{2}}{4}
. Analogously, for each exchanged element in the rows of the lower half, we add
\frac{n^{2}}{4}
.

Since
B^{\ell}_{2}\in\mathds{N}^{\frac{n}{2}\times\frac{(n-6)}{4}}
, for
\ell\in[2,3],
we permute exactly
\frac{n-6}{4}
elements per row; thus, the total numerical mass removed from each row in the upper half is
\frac{n^{2}}{4}\times\frac{n-6}{4}=d
, with the same amount
d
being added to each row in the lower half.

∎

After the analysis of the rows, it remains to investigate how these exchanges redistribute the numerical mass along the diagonals.

Lemma 4.8
.

The matrix
A
generated by Algorithm
2
satisfies the property that both the main and secondary diagonal sums are equal to the magic constant
\mathcal{C}(A)
.

Proof.

Following the logic established in the previous lemmas, we track the displacement of numerical mass along the diagonals. For the main diagonal, the exchanges
A^{1}_{1}\leftrightarrow A^{4}_{1}
and
w_{1}\leftrightarrow w_{4}
affect exactly
k=\frac{n-2}{4}+1=\frac{n+2}{4}
elements. Each of these elements
a_{ii}
is replaced by an element with an offset larger by
\frac{3n^{2}}{4}
. Consequently, this portion of the main diagonal sum increases by:

\left(\frac{n+2}{4}\right)\times\frac{3n^{2}}{4}=\frac{3n^{3}+6n^{2}}{16}.

Additionally, the exchange
B^{3}_{2}\leftrightarrow B^{2}_{2}
involves
\frac{n-6}{4}
elements on the main diagonal, which are increased by
\frac{n^{2}}{4}
. Referring back to Property (P4) of Lemma
4.5
, the final sum is:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ii}&=&\mathcal{C}(A)-\dfrac{n^{3}}{4}+\dfrac{3n^{3}+6n^{2}}{16}+\dfrac{n-6}{4}\times\dfrac{n^{2}}{4}\\
&=&\mathcal{C}(A)-\dfrac{n^{3}}{4}+\dfrac{3n^{3}+6n^{2}}{16}+\dfrac{n^{3}-6n^{2}}{16}\\
&=&\mathcal{C}(A)+\dfrac{-4n^{3}+4n^{3}}{16}=\mathcal{C}(A).\end{array}

Regarding the secondary diagonal, the first
\frac{n+2}{4}
elements
a_{i,n-i+1}
are affected by the exchanges
A^{1}_{1}\leftrightarrow A^{4}_{1}
and
w_{1}\leftrightarrow w_{4}
, which decrease the sum of these entries by
\frac{3n^{2}}{4}
per element. Furthermore, the exchange
B^{3}_{2}\leftrightarrow B^{2}_{2}
involves
\frac{n-6}{4}
elements on the secondary diagonal that are decreased by
\frac{n^{2}}{4}
.

Analogous to the main diagonal, we obtain:

\displaystyle sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i,n-i+1}=\mathcal{C}(A)+\dfrac{n^{3}}{4}-\dfrac{3n^{3}+6n^{2}}{16}-\dfrac{n-6}{4}\times\dfrac{n^{2}}{4}=\mathcal{C}(A).

Thus, both diagonal sums converge to the magic constant.

∎

With all these lemmas at hand, we can prove the main theorem.

Theorem 4.9
.

When
n
is singly even, Algorithm
2
produces a magic square matrix with magic constant
\mathcal{C}(A)=\dfrac{n(n^{2}+1)}{2}
.

Proof.

By Property (P1) of Lemma
4.5
and the fact that the exchanges in Step
2
are strictly vertical, the sum of each column in matrix
A
, generated by Algorithm
2
, remains
\mathcal{C}(A)
. Regarding the diagonals, Lemma
4.8
establishes that both diagonal sums equal
\mathcal{C}(A)
. We shall now address the row sums.

The result of Lemma
4.6
combined with properties (P2) and (P3) of Lemma
4.5
, demonstrate that after performing the first three exchanges in Step
2
of Algorithm
2
, the row sums are significantly adjusted. Specifically, each of the first
n/2
rows now possesses a sum of:

\mathcal{C}(A)-\frac{n^{3}}{8}+\frac{3n^{3}-6n^{2}}{16}=\mathcal{C}(A)+\frac{n^{3}-6n^{2}}{16},

while the rows in the lower half (the last
n/2
rows) conversely result in a sum of
\mathcal{C}(A)-\frac{n^{3}-6n^{2}}{16}
.

Lemma
4.7
shows that the fourth exchange in Step
2
corrects this remaining imbalance. Hence, the sum of each row is also
\mathcal{C}(A)
and the matrix
A
is a magic square.

∎

6
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we proposed two approaches to construct a magic square of order
n
. The first approach is a Constraint Satisfaction Problem model, which guarantees a valid magic square. The second algorithm, called the
Fast Approach
, utilizes the classical Siamese method when the order
n
is odd. For even orders, we incorporate two novel techniques, applied depending on whether the order is doubly even or singly even. These techniques rely on simple operations, such as element swaps.
We also provide a proof of correctness for the proposed approach.
Numerical results indicate that the computational time required for the CSP formulation increases rapidly as the order grows, as expected. In contrast
,

our fast approach
constructed magic squares for all
n
between
3
and
5000
in under
0.3
seconds, and for
n
around
70\,000
in less than
140
seconds. Future research could explore
the potential extension of the proposed approach
to related combinatorial structures,
such as Sudoku or the
n
-Queens problem.
