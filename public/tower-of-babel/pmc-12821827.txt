Noisy galvanic vestibular stimulation and static balance in parkinson’s disease: a multimodal resting‑state fMRI feasibility study
Authors: Yun Su Hwang, Jihwan Min, Yongseon Yoo, Jin-Ju Kang, Marianne Dieterich, Seung-Bae Hwang, Jong-Min Lee, Sun-Young Oh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostural instability in Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a major contributor to falls and functional decline, yet remains poorly responsive to dopaminergic therapy. Noisy galvanic vestibular stimulation (nGVS), a non-invasive neuromodulation technique, has shown potential to enhance postural control by augmenting multisensory integration. However, the neural mechanisms and temporal dynamics underlying its effects remain incompletely understood.ObjectiveTo evaluate the behavioral and neurophysiological effects of a single session of nGVS in patients with mild-to-moderate PD using standardized clinical measures and resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI).MethodsForty-one idiopathic PD patients underwent clinical assessments and rs-fMRI at three time points: pre-nGVS, immediately post-stimulation, and one week later. Postural function was quantified using the Berg Balance Test (BBT), Functional Reach Test (FRT), Timed Up and Go (TUG), and Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) for discomfort. Imaging analyses included seed-based connectivity, independent component analysis (ICA), and amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF/fALFF).ResultsnGVS elicited significant improvements in static and anticipatory balance, with BBT gains persisting after one week. These behavioral effects were accompanied by transient increases in visual and sensorimotor network activity, short-lived ALFF/fALFF enhancement in occipital and parietal regions, and sustained connectivity between the pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN) and superior frontal gyrus. Gait-related outcomes showed more modest and delayed changes.ConclusionnGVS selectively engages intrinsic networks supporting static postural control, producing partially sustained behavioral benefits and circuit-specific neuroplasticity. These findings support its potential as a non-invasive intervention targeting axial symptoms in PD and suggest imaging-based biomarkers for future stratified therapeutic application.Trial registrationThis study was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS), Republic of Korea, under the identifier KCT0007058, registered on Mar 04, 2022.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder marked by dopaminergic neuronal loss and α-synuclein aggregation [1–4]. While tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia often improve with dopaminergic therapy, axial symptoms—impaired stance and gait—frequently persist despite medication and even deep brain stimulation (DBS), contributing to falls, hospitalization, loss of independence, and reduced quality of life [5, 6, 7, 8], This therapeutic gap highlights the need for safe, mechanism‑guided interventions that target the circuits underlying postural control.

Noisy galvanic vestibular stimulation (nGVS) delivers subthreshold stochastic noise to vestibular afferents, leveraging stochastic resonance to enhance signal detection [39, 41, 42]. Through stochastic resonance, subthreshold vestibular noise can improve detectability of weak afferent signals in nonlinear neural systems, thereby facilitating multisensory integration across vestibulo‑thalamo‑cortical and brainstem–cortical loops [39, 41, 42]. The vestibular system connects with widespread cortical and subcortical regions involved in postural control, including the cerebellum, thalamus, insula, pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN), and frontal cortices [8–14]. Anatomically, vestibular signals are distributed across a widespread cortical–subcortical network encompassing the cerebellum, thalamus, insula, pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN), and frontoparietal cortices—regions central to postural regulation and multisensory integration [15–17]. Early reports suggest that nGVS can reduce sway and improve balance in PD; however, most studies were limited by small samples, heterogeneous protocols and outcomes, and a predominant focus on behavioral endpoints with little insight into neural mechanisms or durability beyond the stimulation window [12, 18]. Addressing these gaps requires integrating standardized clinical measures with imaging biomarkers that capture circuit‑level effects over time [12–22].

Here, we evaluated the impact of a single, MR‑compatible, subthreshold nGVS session on postural function and intrinsic brain network activity in patients with mild‑to‑moderate PD using standardized clinical assessments and resting‑state fMRI at baseline, immediately post‑stimulation, and one week later. We hypothesized that nGVS would preferentially modulate circuits supporting static postural control and multisensory integration—manifest as transient engagement of visual and sensorimotor networks—and produce sustained strengthening of brainstem–frontal connectivity, particularly between the PPN and superior frontal gyrus (SFG), rather than robust changes in networks governing dynamic gait. This combined behavioral–neuroimaging approach is intended to inform the development of nGVS as a circuit‑targeted neuromodulatory strategy for axial dysfunction in PD.

Methods

Participants

Patients with idiopathic Parkinson’s disease (PD) were consecutively recruited at Jeonbuk National University Hospital between November 2022 and August 2024. Diagnoses were confirmed by a movement disorder specialist using the UK Brain Bank Criteria, and all patients showed reduced dopamine transporter uptake on ¹⁸F-FP-CIT PET scans [23, 24].

Eligible participants were ≥ 50 years old, Hoehn and Yahr stage ≤ 3 in the “on” state, and on stable medication for ≥ 4 weeks. To avoid vestibular confounds, individuals with unilateral or bilateral vestibulopathy were excluded based on bedside and video head impulse tests. Other exclusions included MMSE < 24, atypical parkinsonism, high fall risk, major comorbidities affecting mobility, psychiatric illness, or MRI contraindications.

Study design

This repeated-measures within-subject study evaluated the immediate and short-term effects of individually calibrated subthreshold nGVS on stance, gait, balance, and brain activity in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD). Two visits were conducted one week apart (Fig. 1). Assessments were performed at three time points: T0 (baseline), T1 (immediately after a single 6-min nGVS), and T2 (one-week follow-up). At Visit 1, participants first completed baseline clinical tests and a baseline rs-fMRI (no stimulation). Immediately thereafter, while still supine in the scanner bore with the scanner idle, they received 6 min of nGVS. The immediate post-nGVS rs-fMRI was acquired after current offset, followed by a secondary clinical assessment battery conducted outside the scanner. No fMRI volumes were acquired during stimulation. Electrodes were placed before the baseline rs‑fMRI (before enter the MR bore) and removed after the T1 rs‑fMRI (after the “immediate post-GVS scan”). No electrodes were present during the T2 rs‑fMRI at one week. The T1 rs‑fMRI immediately followed the 6‑min nGVS with the scanner restarted after current offset. Post‑scan clinical tests were performed in the same visit immediately after exiting the scanner.At Visit 1, baseline clinical characteristics were included disease duration, UPDRS Part III, Hoehn and Yahr stage, and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD). Four standardized assessments—the Berg Balance Test (BBT), Functional Reach Test (FRT), Timed Up and Go (TUG), and Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) for discomfort. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) was performed at each time point to capture stimulation-induced changes in intrinsic connectivity.

nGVS was applied during the baseline fMRI session to enable concurrent assessment of neural modulation. Clinical testing was repeated immediately afterward and at the one-week follow-up to evaluate persistence of effects. The structured protocol comprised: (1) Baseline assessment, (2) nGVS intervention, (3) Immediate post-stimulation assessment, and (4) One-week follow-up.

Galvanic vestibular stimulation application

Noisy GVS (nGVS) was administered between the baseline and the immediate post-nGVS rs-fMRI scans; no fMRI volumes were acquired during stimulation. A battery-powered MR-compatible constant-current stimulator (neuroConn DC-Stimulator Plus, Germany) delivered band-limited Gaussian noise (0–100 Hz) at 0.8 mA for 6 min through 5 × 7 cm mastoid electrodes. Noise bandwidth was selected a priori from prior stochastic-resonance work to cover low‑frequency vestibulospinal bands while minimizing cutaneous sensations; band‑limiting is implemented by the device’s embedded digital filters. No fMRI volumes were acquired during stimulation. Conductive rubber electrodes (5 × 7 cm) were affixed over the mastoids using MR‑safe conductive gel and tape. Prior to stimulation, the device’s impedance check confirmed skin–electrode impedance below the manufacturer’s threshold for safe current delivery; numerical values were not recorded. All participants received this single, fixed subthreshold amplitude, and none perceived vestibular or cutaneous sensations. Each participant’s vestibular perceptual threshold was determined through a standardized incremental protocol, and stimulation intensity was set below threshold (≤ 0.8 mA) [25, 26].

Imaging data acquisition and preprocessing

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) was conducted on a Philips Achieva 3 T scanner (Eindhoven, Netherlands) at Jeonbuk National University Hospital to assess neural connectivity changes following nGVS. Participants were instructed to remain awake with eyes open, fixating on a crosshair to reduce motion artifacts. Functional images were acquired using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, FOV = 220 mm², voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3 mm³, matrix = 64 × 64).

Preprocessing of fMRI data was conducted using DPARSF (Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI) [27] and Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12) software implemented in MATLAB. The first five volumes were discarded for signal stabilization. Standard preprocessing steps included slice timing correction, realignment (with exclusion criteria of >2 mm or >2° movement), spatial normalization to MNI space, resampling to 3 mm³ voxels, and smoothing with a 5 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Linear detrending and band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) were applied to reduce noise and retain physiologically relevant fluctuations, ensuring data consistency across time points for functional connectivity analysis.

Clinical assessment

Clinical assessments were conducted at three time points: baseline (pre-GVS), immediately after nGVS stimulation, and one-week post-stimulation. The evaluation focused on both subjective and objective measures of stance, gait and balance function to determine the effects of nGVS. The primary clinical outcomes included:

Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) to assess perceived discomfort during movement, ranging from 0 (no discomfort) to 10 (severe discomfort).Berg Balance Test (BBT) to evaluate postural stability and fall risk, with a higher score indicating better balance [28]. Functional Reach Test (FRT) to measure dynamic balance by assessing the maximum forward reach distance [29]. Timed Up and Go Test (TUG) to assess functional mobility by measuring the time required to rise from a seated position, walk 3 m, turn around, and return to the chair [30].

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jeonbuk National University Hospital (Approval No. 2020-05-001). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Statistical analyses

Clinical data analysis

Clinical data were analyzed using SPSS v25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Repeated-measures ANOVA assessed changes in gait and balance across three time points (pre-, post-nGVS, and one-week follow-up). Post-hoc comparisons used Bonferroni correction, with significance set at p < 0.05. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise noted.

Resting-state fMRI analysis

To investigate nGVS-induced neural effects, resting-state fMRI data were analyzed using three complementary approaches: Independent Component Analysis (ICA), Amplitude of Low-Frequency Fluctuation (ALFF/fALFF), and Seed-Based Functional Connectivity.

Independent Component Analysis (ICA) Group ICA was conducted using the GIFT toolbox to identify intrinsic connectivity networks (ICNs), including the Default Mode (DMN), Sensory Motor (SMN), Cognitive Control (CCN), Visual (VIS), and Dorsal Attention Networks (DAN) (Fig. 2A). The infomax algorithm and Icasso validation ensured reliable component extraction [31]. Network-level differences across time points were assessed using multivariate ANCOVA (MANCOVAN) [32, 33].

Amplitude of Low-Frequency Fluctuation (ALFF) and Fractional ALFF (fALFF) ALFF and fALFF were calculated to quantify spontaneous low-frequency oscillations (0.01–0.08 Hz). ALFF values were normalized to the global mean; fALFF was computed as the ratio of low-frequency to total power. Statistical comparisons across time points were performed using ANCOVA with FWE correction (p < 0.05), followed by paired t-tests.

Seed-Based Functional Connectivity Analysis Seed-to-voxel correlations were computed using regions of interest (ROIs) relevant to posture and motor control: the parietal operculum, pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN), and caudate. ANOVA assessed time-dependent changes in connectivity, with FDR correction applied. Paired comparisons identified specific temporal shifts in connectivity.

This multimodal analysis framework enabled a comprehensive assessment of localized and network-level functional plasticity following nGVS, highlighting potential neural mechanisms underlying postural improvements in PD.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Forty-one patients with idiopathic PD were enrolled (27 males, 14 females; mean age 68.3 ± 9.4 years). The cohort exhibited mild-to-moderate motor symptoms (mean UPDRS Part III: 17.8 ± 10.3; Hoehn and Yahr stage: 1.72 ± 0.62) with a mean disease duration of 2.9 ± 3.7 years. Symptom lateralization was balanced (46.3% right-dominant; 53.7% left-dominant). Levodopa equivalent daily dose averaged 357.4 ± 284.9 mg. Detailed subdomain scores included bradykinesia (8.1 ± 5.3), tremor (2.4 ± 2.1), rigidity (3.7 ± 3.0), and gait/balance (1.4 ± 1.4), representing a clinically suitable population for evaluating postural interventions (Table 1).

Effects of noisy GVS on gait and balance function

The effects of nGVS on gait and balance were assessed using four validated clinical measures: NRS for perceived discomfort, BBT for postural stability, FRT for dynamic balance, and the TUG test for functional mobility. Assessments were conducted at three time points: Phase 1 (baseline, pre-stimulation), Phase 2 (immediately post-stimulation), and Phase 3 (one-week follow-up). Detailed results are presented in Table 2.

Perceived Discomfort (NRS) nGVS significantly reduced subjective discomfort associated with movement. Mean NRS scores decreased from 4.23 ± 2.00 at baseline to 3.89 ± 2.03 immediately after stimulation (p = 0.001). Although a slight rebound was noted at the one-week follow-up (4.12 ± 1.93), the overall reduction across time points remained statistically significant (p = 0.002).

Postural Balance (BBT and FRT) Scores on the BBT improved significantly following stimulation, increasing from 47.22 ± 7.52 to 49.05 ± 6.39 (p = 0.002). Although a slight decline was observed after one week (48.90 ± 5.87), performance remained significantly above baseline (p = 0.010), with a robust overall effect of time (p = 0.001). Dynamic balance, as measured by FRT, also improved post-stimulation (25.84 ± 6.05 to 27.20 ± 6.17, p = 0.019). This effect partially diminished at the follow-up (26.60 ± 6.27), but the global effect across all time points remained significant (p = 0.025).

Functional Mobility (TUG) No significant immediate effect of nGVS was observed on TUG performance (15.44 ± 5.21 vs. 15.15 ± 5.81, p = 0.869). However, TUG scores improved slightly at the one-week follow-up (14.35 ± 4.05), and the overall time effect reached statistical significance (p = 0.029).

Multiplicity‑aware interpretation (post hoc). Because four clinical outcomes were analyzed (NRS, BBT, FRT, TUG), we examined overall time effects with both familywise (Bonferroni) and false‑discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg, q = 0.05) adjustments across outcomes. After Bonferroni correction, BBT (p_adj = 0.004) and NRS (p_adj = 0.008) remained significant, whereas FRT (p_adj = 0.100) and TUG (p_adj = 0.116) did not. Under FDR control, all four overall time effects met the significance threshold; however, the TUG effect was small and evident only at one week. Pairwise post‑hoc p‑values in Table 2 remain Bonferroni‑adjusted within outcome and are unchanged.

GVS-induced changes in brain activity and functional connectivity

Network-level modulations: group ICA and functional network connectivity

Resting-state fMRI revealed modulation of intrinsic brain networks following nGVS. Group ICA identified significant changes in network activity across the three time points: baseline (Phase 0), immediately after stimulation (Phase 1), and one week later (Phase 2). Figure 2A displays the group-level independent components, while Fig. 2B presents voxel-wise statistical activation maps comparing intrinsic network activity at baseline (Phase 0) and immediately following stimulation (Phase 1). Multivariate comparisons were performed using MANCOVA. Notably, regional activity within the visual network increased after stimulation (Phase 1 vs. Phase 0), particularly in occipital and associative visual areas, as shown by red-highlighted clusters in Fig. 2B. These findings suggest transient enhancement of visuospatial processing and multisensory interaction and integration following vestibular input.

In parallel, functional network connectivity (FNC) analysis identified dynamic changes in inter-network coupling between immediately after stimulation (Phase 1) and one week later (Phase 2), visualized in Fig. 3. One-week after stimulation, reduced connectivity was observed (indicated in warm colors) between several large-scale networks, including: DMN–SMN, DMN–CCN, SMN–VIS, SMN–DAN, CCN–VIS, and VIS–DAN. Additionally, the enhanced connectivity was observed (indicated in cool colors) in the following network pairs at one week follow-up: DMN–VIS, DMN–DAN, SMN–CCN, and CCN–DAN. These findings collectively indicate that nGVS transiently augments integration across motor, attentional, and visuospatial systems, which may underlie the behavioral improvements in balance and postural control observed immediately after stimulation.

Regional oscillatory activity: ALFF and fALFF analyses

To quantify changes in spontaneous low-frequency neural oscillations, voxel-wise analyses of ALFF and fALFF were performed. Figure 4; Table 3 summarize the results of ANCOVA (p < 0.05, FWE-corrected), identifying five significant clusters with differential patterns across the three time points: baseline (Phase 0), immediately after nGVS (Phase 1), and one week post-stimulation (Phase 2). ALFF analysis revealed four clusters with temporal changes. Two clusters, located in the middle temporal gyrus and olfactory cortex, showed no significant change immediately after stimulation; however, ALFF in the middle temporal gyrus decreased significantly at one week (Phase 2 vs. Phase 0), indicating delayed deactivation in associative temporal regions. The remaining two clusters—within the superior frontal gyrus and calcarine cortex—exhibited elevated ALFF immediately after stimulation, which returned to baseline at follow-up, suggesting transient enhancement of local oscillatory activity in sensorimotor and visual areas.

fALFF analysis identified one cluster in the cuneus with a transient increase immediately after stimulation, followed by a return to baseline at one week.

These findings indicate that nGVS modulates spontaneous neural activity in a region- and time-dependent manner, with short-lived increases in visual and sensorimotor regions and delayed suppression in associative temporal areas.

To characterize region-specific changes in functional connectivity following nGVS, seed-based analyses were performed using the priori regions of interest (ROIs): the parietal operculum (C1), pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN; C2), and caudate (C3). Discussion

In a within‑subject, three‑time‑point study (n = 41), a single session of subthreshold, MR‑compatible nGVS was associated with immediate gains in static and anticipatory balance (BBT, FRT), partial one‑week retention for BBT, and modest/delayed change in TUG. Resting‑state fMRI showed short‑lived increases in visual/sensorimotor activity and a sustained rise in pedunculopontine nucleus–superior frontal gyrus (PPN–SFG) connectivity. These observations are consistent with engagement of multisensory circuits relevant to stance; however, in the absence of a sham‑controlled comparison, they should be viewed as preliminary associations rather than definitive evidence of neuromodulation.

Across the four clinical endpoints (NRS, BBT, FRT, TUG), overall time effects were observed (Table 2). When accounting for multiple primary outcomes, inferences differ by correction method: using a conservative Bonferroni correction across the four outcomes, BBT and NRS remain significant while FRT and TUG do not; under Benjamini–Hochberg FDR (q = 0.05), all four meet the threshold, though the effect magnitude for TUG is small and delayed. Accordingly, we emphasize BBT as the most robust signal, treat FRT/TUG as exploratory, and highlight that subjective discomfort (NRS) is particularly vulnerable to expectancy bias. These considerations, together with the potential for practice effects on repeated motor testing, reinforce the need for sham‑controlled blinding and pre‑specified multiplicity control in subsequent trials.

Notably, a recent study by Peto et al. challenged the generalizability of nGVS by reporting no significant improvements in gait performance (including stride variability and gait velocity) in PD patients with mild-to-moderate impairment [34]. While some participants exhibited static balance improvement, the absence of dynamic gait benefits suggests that nGVS may preferentially influence circuits involved in postural stabilization rather than continuous locomotor output [35]. This selective and partially sustained improvement in BBT, compared to the more transient FRT and NRS changes, further suggests that nGVS primarily modulates vestibulo-thalamo-cortical and PPN-related circuits [8, 12, 14, 36–39], which contribute to upright stance and multisensory integration and require vestibular graviceptive (otolith) input, rather than networks mediating dynamic gait execution [40–45].

Vestibular–cortical modulation and neurophysiological correlates

Resting-state analyses revealed short-lived increases in intrinsic network activity immediately after nGVS within the visual network (VIS) and sensorimotor network (SMN), particularly in occipital, associative visual, and pre/postcentral regions (Fig. 2B), accompanied by one-week–delayed, transient reconfiguration of inter-network coupling on FNC analysis, with reduced DMN–SMN/CCN and SMN–VIS/DAN connectivity and increased DMN–VIS/DAN and SMN–CCN coupling (Fig. 3). This pattern is compatible with a brief facilitation of visuovestibular integration that could assist stance control [16, 46, 47]. However, because there was no sham‑controlled blinding, we cannot exclude non‑specific contributors such as arousal, attentional engagement, or scanner acclimatization; thus these network‑level effects should be interpreted as associative signals rather than proof of neuromodulation. To address analytic multiplicity, the ICA/MANCOVAN, ALFF/fALFF, and seed‑based pipelines should be considered separate analytic families; reported clusters are family‑wise corrected within each family, and cross‑family inferences remain exploratory. Durable network reconfiguration may require repeated or task‑coupled stimulation, a question best tested prospectively under sham control.

Complementing the network findings, ALFF/fALFF revealed transient increases in low‑frequency oscillatory power within visual/sensorimotor cortices immediately post‑stimulation and a delayed decrease in middle temporal gyrus at one week (Table 3; Fig. 4) [48–50]. This dissociation—early enhancement followed by later down‑regulation—suggests a temporal gradient of adaptation, but again remains vulnerable to time‑dependent variability in the absence of a control arm.

Circuit‑specific adaptation and the pedunculopontine nucleus

Seed‑based analyses identified a sustained increase in PPN–SFG connectivity that was present immediately and at one week, a transient rise in parietal operculum–precentral coupling, and a progressive decline in caudate–frontal coupling (Fig. 5). The PPN–SFG finding is anatomically plausible for stance regulation and aligns with the one‑week retention of BBT improvement (Table 2), but without sham control we avoid causal claims and frame PPN–SFG coupling as a candidate mechanistic endpoint for preregistration in future trials.

The pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN) is a key integrative hub for sensory, motor, and arousal-related inputs and plays a central role in postural tone and gait initiation [50–52]. It receives excitatory input from the motor cortex and inhibitory projections from basal ganglia output structures, with efferents projecting to the thalamus, reticular formation, and spinal cord [47, 51]. PPN dysfunction contributes to axial symptoms in PD that are often resistant to dopaminergic treatment [53, 54]. Although direct structural effects of GVS on PPN circuits remain unconfirmed, prior studies suggest functional modulation is possible [43, 47]. In our study, seed-based functional connectivity analysis revealed a sustained increase in connectivity between the PPN and the SFG, persisting one week after stimulation. This enduring enhancement contrasts with the transient parietal operculum–precentral gyrus connectivity and suggests that brainstem–frontal pathways may be more amenable to short-term plastic reorganization via nGVS. Given the role of the SFG in anticipatory postural adjustments and executive control of balance, this pattern may reflect early-stage remodeling of locomotor circuits and may underlie the observed retention of postural improvements (BBT) at follow-up (Table 2).

Conversely, connectivity between the right caudate and SFG progressively declined across the three time points. This may reflect a compensatory shift from striatal–prefrontal loops, which govern higher-order motor planning (disease related), toward more automatic, brainstem- and cerebellar-mediated postural strategies (normal behaviour). Such reorganization has been observed in response to both pharmacological and neuromodulatory interventions in PD [37, 45]. Given the caudate’s role in error monitoring and cognitive motor control, the observed decoupling may indicate either reduced cognitive load following vestibular recalibration or adaptive disengagement from dysfunctional fronto-striatal networks.

These findings underscore the temporally distinct and circuit-specific effects of nGVS. While opercular–motor connectivity appears short-lived, enduring PPN–SFG coupling may serve as a biomarker of lasting adaptation. Future studies should explore whether repeated stimulation enhances this plasticity and whether baseline connectivity predicts treatment response.

Mechanisms underlying selective persistence of effects

A key observation in this study is the differential time course of nGVS-induced clinical effects—specifically, the sustained improvement in static balance (BBT) versus the transient gains in discomfort (NRS) and dynamic mobility (FRT, TUG). This dissociation supports the idea that nGVS preferentially engages circuits involved in multisensory integration and postural maintenance, rather than those governing dynamic motor execution; yet practice effects and outcome‑specific test–retest properties could also contribute, reinforcing the need for sham‑controlled replication. Static balance relies on the continuous integration of visual, vestibular (especially graviceptive otolith), and proprioceptive inputs, processed through interconnected subcortical–cortical loops involving the cerebellum, PPN, thalamus, and SMA [55, 56]. Subthreshold vestibular noise may transiently enhance signal integration across these hubs, aiding in verticality perception and postural alignment. In contrast, dynamic tasks like FRT and TUG rely on more complex systems—cortico-striatal-thalamic loops, cerebellar locomotor areas, and brainstem pattern generators—and may require more intensive or repeated stimulation to show durable changes [46, 55, 57, 58].

Imaging results mirror this profile (transient VIS/SMN changes; sustained PPN–frontal coupling), suggesting that if nGVS‑related plasticity exists, it may preferentially stabilize brainstem–frontal pathways that support static balance [8, 48, 59]. A multi‑session, task‑coupled protocol—pre‑registering BBT as the single primary endpoint and PPN–SFG connectivity as the primary imaging endpoint with familywise control across modalities—will be essential to test durability and extend any benefits to dynamic mobility.

Limitations and design considerations for the next powered, sham‑controlled trial

A sham condition was not included in this feasibility study, as the primary objective was to assess the tolerability, safety, and detectability of behavioral and neural effects of subthreshold nGVS within a single MRI session. Inclusion of a sham arm would have extended scan duration and participant fatigue. Future randomized, sham-controlled studies will be necessary to confirm these preliminary findings.

Beyond the absence of sham‑controlled blinding, this feasibility study is limited by its single‑session exposure (precluding dose–response and durability inferences), single‑center recruitment, lack of prospective power justification, potential heterogeneity in medication state and testing time‑of‑day, and analytic multiplicity across behavioral and imaging domains. rs-fMRI provides high spatial but limited temporal resolution, which restricts sensitivity to fast vestibular dynamics compared with EEG-based approaches. Moreover, concurrent electrophysiological or physiological recordings were not obtained, which could have provided complementary temporal information. These issues constrain causal attribution and generalizability and should be corrected in the subsequent phase. Because stimulation and scanning were not performed concurrently, this study characterizes post-stimulation neural effects rather than real-time brain responses to nGVS. Additionally, the persistence of BBT improvement and PPN–frontal coupling at one week could reflect short‑term after‑effects of sensory recalibration; definitive causal attribution will require multi‑session, sham‑controlled protocols.

To address these limitations, the next study will: (i) preregister one primary clinical endpoint—Berg Balance Test (BBT)—at a pre‑specified timepoint, with a hierarchically tested key secondary (e.g., gait speed or TUG) and familywise error control via a gatekeeping hierarchy (Hochberg within families, as appropriate); (ii) prospectively power a randomized, double‑blind, sham‑controlled trial using variance estimates from the present dataset and an a priori minimal clinically important difference for BBT; and (iii) prespecify a single primary imaging endpoint (e.g., PPN–SFG connectivity), controlling familywise error across networks/seeds and analytical families (ICA, ALFF/fALFF, seed‑based) using nonparametric permutation with threshold‑free cluster enhancement (TFCE). We will also fix or stratify medication state and time‑of‑day, standardize assessor procedures, incorporate familiarization runs to mitigate practice effects, include blinding/expectancy checks, and predefine a mixed‑effects model (baseline‑adjusted) as the primary analysis set. Parallel‑group allocation will be preferred to avoid carryover; if a crossover design is considered, it will include an empirically justified washout and formal tests for period and carryover effects.
