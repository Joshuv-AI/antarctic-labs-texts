Physiological and Cardiac Effects of Weight Loss in Adolescents With Atypical Anorexia Nervosa Versus Those Undergoing Bariatric Surgery: A Retrospective Comparative Study
Authors: Tomer Mevorach, Amit Goldstein, Orly Lavan, Shelly Shteinling, Adi Sela, Silvana Fennig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAdolescents with atypical anorexia nervosa (AAN) experience cardiac complications at rates comparable to typical anorexia nervosa, despite remaining within or above a normal weight range. The physiological mechanisms underlying this paradox remain poorly understood. This study is the first to compare cardiac outcomes between adolescents with AAN and those who underwent bariatric surgery, a population experiencing similarly rapid and substantial weight loss within a medically supervised context.MethodsA retrospective study compared adolescents with AAN (n = 102, 82% female, mean age 15.0 ± 1.6 years) and adolescents who underwent bariatric surgery (n = 90, 67% female, mean age 16.4 ± 1.1 years) on weight loss magnitude and pace, minimum heart rate, thyroid function, physical activity and laboratory indices.ResultsDespite losing more weight at a faster rate, the bariatric surgery group had significantly higher minimum heart rates (83.6 vs. 62.2 bpm, p < 0.001) and no cases of clinically significant bradycardia, compared with 22.5% in the AAN group. Rate of weight loss did not correlate with minimum heart rate in either group. Exploratory analyses suggested an association between T3 levels and minimum heart rate, warranting further investigation.ConclusionWeight loss magnitude and rate alone do not determine cardiac risk in AAN. The nutritional context and physiological adaptations specific to eating disorders appear to have a role in the cardiac outcome. Adolescents with AAN require proactive cardiac monitoring regardless of current weight or pace of weight loss.

Introduction

Atypical anorexia nervosa (AAN), classified in DSM‐5 under Other Specified Feeding and Eating Disorders, describes individuals who meet all criteria for anorexia nervosa (AN) except that, despite significant weight loss, their weight remains within or above the normal range [1]. The paradox at the heart of AAN is that cardiac complications, including bradycardia, hypotension and orthostatic instability, occur at rates comparable to those seen in typical AN, despite the absence of underweight status [2, 3, 4]. Bradycardia, defined as a resting heart rate below 50 bpm, is amongst the most common and clinically significant complications, often requiring hospitalisation [5, 6, 7, 8]. Existing evidence suggests that the magnitude and rate of weight loss, rather than absolute weight, are key drivers of physiological risk in AAN, with important implications for diagnosis and clinical monitoring [9, 10]. Despite this recognition, the physiological mechanisms underlying cardiac instability in AAN remain poorly understood [11, 12], partly due to the lack of an appropriate comparison group. Adolescents who undergo bariatric surgery represent a clinically relevant and underused control population: like those with AAN, they experience rapid, substantial weight loss from a higher baseline weight, but within a medically supervised framework with nutritional preparation, professional guidance and metabolic monitoring [13]. Importantly, post‐bariatric adolescents do not typically develop the same degree of bradycardia or hemodynamic instability, suggesting that weight loss magnitude and pace alone do not fully explain cardiac complications in eating disorders. This contrast offers a unique opportunity to disentangle the physiological effects of weight loss itself from those attributable to disorder‐specific factors.

This study is the first to directly compare physiological outcomes between adolescents with AAN and adolescents who underwent bariatric surgery. We examined differences in the magnitude and rate of weight loss, cardiac outcomes and potential contributing factors. By leveraging bariatric surgery as a naturalistic model of medically supervised rapid weight loss, this design offers novel mechanistic insight into why AAN carries cardiac risk that cannot be explained by weight loss metrics alone.

Methods

Participants and Data Collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records. Adolescents who underwent bariatric surgery between 2010 and 2021 were identified from the surgical registry, whereas adolescents with AAN were identified from paediatric inpatient ward records. All participants were aged 10–19 years. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Schneider Children Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB) and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Rabin Medical Center IRB (approval number: RMC0290‐22). Given the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was waived. The AAN group included adolescents hospitalised due to medical instability, complete food refusal or inability to be managed in an outpatient setting. Diagnoses were confirmed by paediatricians and psychiatrists according to DSM‐5 criteria [1]. The bariatric surgery group included adolescents who underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy or Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass. In accordance with standard clinical practice in Israel, all bariatric candidates completed a preoperative lifestyle modification programme [14]. Physiological data for this group were collected 6–12 months postoperatively during routine ambulatory follow‐up. Extracted variables included heart rate, blood pressure, laboratory measures (electrolytes, thyroid function), anthropometric data, cardiac history, purging behaviours (self‐reported vomiting episodes) and physical activity. For the AAN group, cardiovascular and laboratory parameters were extracted from inpatient records during the acute hospitalisation. For the bariatric surgery group, these parameters, including thyroid function tests, were extracted from routine ambulatory follow‐up visits conducted 6–12 months postoperatively. Weight loss was calculated as the difference between maximum weight (self‐reported in AAN and objectively measured in the bariatric group) and minimum weight (measured at admission in both groups) and was standardised as percentage of body weight lost over time.

Clinically significant bradycardia was defined as a minimum heart rate below 50 bpm. In the AAN group, 23 participants (22.5%) met this threshold; no cases were identified in the bariatric surgery group. Physical activity was self‐reported, and excessive exercise was defined as structured physical activity exceeding 1 h/day, consistent with established clinical criteria for compulsive exercise in eating disorders [15].

Study Design

This retrospective study compared physiological responses to significant weight loss across two clinically distinct adolescent populations: one with pathological weight loss (AAN) and one with medically induced weight loss (bariatric surgery). The groups were not matched by premorbid weight or hospitalisation context; rather, this naturalistic comparison was intentional and designed to isolate physiological mechanisms of weight loss from those attributable to the disorder‐specific context of AAN. This design enhances ecological validity and provides a translational framework for understanding cardiac risk in AAN. To confirm the novelty of this comparison, a literature search was conducted prior to analysis; no previous study was identified that directly compared physiological outcomes in adolescents with AAN and adolescents who underwent bariatric surgery.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.0). Group differences in weight loss magnitude, pace and minimum heart rate were assessed using independent‐samples t‐tests for normally distributed variables and Mann–Whitney U tests otherwise. Pearson correlations examined associations between physiological variables, with p‐values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. An ANCOVA examined whether thyroid function (T3), percent weight loss and duration of weight loss accounted for group differences in minimum heart rate. Missing data were handled by listwise deletion. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's d where applicable. A significance threshold of α = 0.05 was applied throughout.

Duration of weight loss did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.279).

Weight Loss

Adolescents who underwent bariatric surgery lost significantly more weight in absolute terms (mean = 28.0 kg, SD = 11.6) compared with those with AAN (mean = 13.7 kg, SD = 11.6; t(178.3) = −7.86, p < 0.001), with a large effect size. The bariatric surgery group also lost weight at a significantly faster rate (mean = 0.226 kg/day, SD = 0.383) than the AAN group (mean = 0.125 kg/day, SD = 0.141; t(111.7) = −2.32, p = 0.022). Minimum Heart Rate and Bradycardia

Despite greater and faster weight loss, the bariatric surgery group had a significantly higher minimum heart rate (mean = 83.6 bpm, SD = 13.1) compared with the AAN group (mean = 62.2 bpm, SD = 15.7; t(172.78) = −9.25, p < 0.001; 95% CI [−26.03, −16.88]). Clinically significant bradycardia (minimum heart rate < 50 bpm) was present in 23 AAN participants (22.5%) and in none of the bariatric surgery participants. Notably, no significant correlation was found between the pace of weight loss and minimum heart rate within either group (bariatric surgery: r = −0.05, p = 0.633; AAN: r = −0.09, p = 0.409), indicating that faster weight loss did not predict lower heart rate in either population. Thyroid Function and Heart Rate

A significant positive correlation was found between T3 levels and minimum heart rate across the full sample (r = 0.531, p < 0.001), indicating that lower T3 was associated with lower heart rates. No significant correlation was found between TSH and minimum heart rate (r = −0.136, p = 0.358). The T3–heart rate relationship is illustrated in Figure 3.

Pearson's correlation with trend line of the association between T3 values and the minimal heart rate in the whole sample including both groups.

To examine whether group differences in minimum heart rate were accounted for by thyroid function, an ANCOVA was conducted with group as the independent variable, minimum heart rate as the dependent variable, T3, percent weight loss and duration of weight loss as covariates. Both group membership (F(1, 47) = 8.3, p = 0.006) and T3 (F(1, 47) = 23.0, p < 0.001) were significant independent predictors of minimum heart rate. The group‐by‐T3 interaction was not significant (F(1, 46) = 2.5, p = 0.12), suggesting that the relationship between T3 and heart rate did not differ by group. Importantly, significant group differences in minimum heart rate p|ersisted after controlling for T3, percent weight loss and duration of weight loss (F(1, 42) = 11.5, p = 0.002), indicating that T3 only partially accounts for the cardiac differences between groups. These analyses should be interpreted with caution, as complete thyroid data were available for only a subset of participants (n = 52), limiting statistical power and the generalisability of this specific finding.

Physical Activity and Purging

More adolescents with AAN reported engaging in excessive physical activity compared with those in the bariatric surgery group. However, within each group, no significant association was found between excessive exercise and minimum heart rate (AAN: W = 328, p = 0.061; bariatric surgery: W = 628.5, p = 0.603). Similarly, the AAN group reported more purging episodes than the bariatric surgery group; electrolyte values did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that purging‐related dehydration alone does not account for cardiac differences.

Discussion

This study is the first to directly compare physiological responses to significant weight loss in adolescents with AAN and those who underwent bariatric surgery. The central and clinically important finding is a clear paradox: despite losing more weight at a faster rate, adolescents who underwent bariatric surgery had markedly higher minimum heart rates and no cases of clinically significant bradycardia, compared with 22.5% in the AAN group. This dissociation between the magnitude of weight loss and cardiac outcome is the study's primary contribution, and it directly challenges the assumption that weight loss rate is the dominant driver of cardiac risk in AAN.

Weight Loss Does Not Explain Cardiac Risk

Prior work has identified rapid weight loss as a key predictor of bradycardia and medical instability in adolescents with AN and AAN [9, 10, 13]. Our findings do not contradict this within eating disorder populations, but show that weight loss magnitude and pace alone are insufficient explanations when comparing etiologically distinct groups. In neither group did rate of weight loss correlate significantly with minimum heart rate, suggesting that between‐group cardiac differences are driven by other factors not directly related to weight loss. A likely contributor to this divergence is premorbid weight. The bariatric surgery group began with significantly higher baseline BMIs, and prior research shows that premorbid BMI strongly predicts both weight loss magnitude and downstream medical outcomes [16, 17]. Individuals with higher premorbid weight may require greater absolute weight loss before reaching physiological thresholds associated with hemodynamic instability. This suggests a protective effect of higher baseline weight, which may partly explain why bariatric surgery patients, despite greater weight loss, remained relatively cardioprotected compared with their AAN counterparts. Notably, the bariatric group also underwent structured preoperative nutritional preparation, unlike the unguided, often extreme caloric restriction characteristic of AAN. This qualitative difference in the nutritional context of weight loss may also be important [18].

Thyroid Function as a Partial Mediator

T3 levels were positively correlated with minimum heart rate across the full sample, and both T3 and group membership remained significant independent predictors of heart rate after adjustment. This finding is consistent with the known role of thyroid hormones in regulating cardiac chronotropy and overall metabolic rate [19], and with evidence that T3 decreases as a function of weight loss and caloric restriction independent of TSH [20]. However, given the limitations of a single retrospective T3 measurement without fT4 or rT3, and the possibility that malnutrition independently drives both lower T3 and bradycardia, this finding should be interpreted with caution. T3 is best understood here as one plausible pathway amongst several, rather than a definitive mediating mechanism.

Physical Activity and Purging

More adolescents with AAN reported excessive physical activity than those in the bariatric surgery group, consistent with the known high prevalence of compulsive exercise in this population [21]. However, no significant association was found between exercise level and minimum heart rate within either group, suggesting that compulsive exercise does not directly explain the cardiac differences observed. Similarly, although the AAN group reported more purging episodes, the absence of significant electrolyte differences between groups argues against dehydration or purging‐related metabolic disturbance as the primary explanation for lower heart rates in AAN. The non‐significant association between excessive exercise and minimum heart rate should be interpreted cautiously given the sample size and reliance on self‐report. It is plausible that chronic compulsive exercise contributes to vagally mediated bradycardia in AAN, a mechanism well established in athletic populations [22], but this study may have been underpowered to detect it. The absence of significant electrolyte disturbances despite greater purging frequency in the AAN group suggests that the bradycardia observed is unlikely to reflect metabolic or dehydration‐related mechanisms, and instead points towards a neurally or hormonally mediated aetiology.

Clinical Implications

These findings have direct implications for clinical monitoring and risk stratification. They suggest that cardiac risk in AAN cannot be reliably inferred from weight loss metrics alone, and that clinicians should maintain a low threshold for cardiac assessment even in adolescents who are not severely underweight. The absence of bradycardia in the bariatric surgery group despite greater weight loss underscores the importance of the nutritional and metabolic context of weight loss. Future prospective research should examine hormonal mediators, including leptin, ghrelin and longitudinal thyroid function, as well as nutritional composition in shaping cardiac adaptation trajectories across these populations.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. The retrospective design precludes causal inference, and the groups were not matched on premorbid BMI, hospitalisation context or sex distribution, reflecting their naturalistic clinical composition. An important limitation is the difference in clinical context between the two groups. Data for the AAN group were collected during acute inpatient hospitalisation, typically in the setting of medical instability or failure of outpatient management, whereas data for the bariatric surgery group were collected during routine ambulatory follow‐up 6–12 months after surgery. Therefore, the observed differences in minimum heart rate may partly reflect the acute illness state and hospitalisation context of the AAN group rather than weight loss physiology alone. It is possible that heart rates in adolescents with AAN would differ if assessed several weeks after medical stabilisation in an outpatient setting. In contrast, the medical supervision, structured nutritional intervention, and routine monitoring provided before and after bariatric surgery may have been protective in the bariatric group.

Reliable height measurements at both time points were unavailable, preventing calculation of weight loss as a standardised %mBMI change. Baseline weights in the AAN group were self‐reported, introducing potential recall bias. Additional limitations include reliance on self‐reported data for purging frequency and physical activity. Although the overall sample size was relatively large for this comparison, the study was not adequately powered to determine the independent effects of severe caloric restriction, excessive exercise or purging on cardiac function. Thyroid function was measured at a single time point, and the limited sample with complete T3 data constrains the ANCOVA analyses. Finally, as an exploratory study with multiple comparisons, results should be interpreted with appropriate caution pending replication in larger, prospective cohorts.

Conclusion

This study is the first to directly compare physiological responses to marked weight loss in adolescents with AAN and adolescents undergoing bariatric surgery, using a novel and clinically relevant control group. Greater and more rapid weight loss in the bariatric group was not associated with greater cardiac complications, suggesting that weight loss magnitude and rate alone do not explain cardiac risk in AAN. Rather, nutritional context, metabolic milieu and eating disorder‐specific physiological adaptations may play a central role. Clinically, these findings support proactive cardiac monitoring in adolescents with AAN regardless of current weight or apparent rate of weight loss. Thyroid function may be relevant, but should not yet inform routine risk stratification until replicated in longitudinal studies with more complete data. Prospective hypothesis‐driven studies are needed to clarify hormonal, nutritional and autonomic mechanisms and to inform evidence‐based guidelines for this underrecognized population.
