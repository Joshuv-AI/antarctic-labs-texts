Prevalence and Associated Factors of Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease Across Obesity Metabolic Phenotypes in Chronic Schizophrenia
Authors: Yu Ma, Xinyue Chen, Huachen Wang, Ying Wang, Qing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic schizophrenia patients often present with exacerbated clinical symptoms and higher rates of fatty liver disease. This study is designed to compare the occurrence rates of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and associated factors among hospitalized chronic schizophrenia patients with different obesity metabolic phenotypes.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we retrospectively analyzed a cohort of 302 chronic schizophrenia patients aged 18–70 years. Patients were classified according to their obesity status (defined as body mass index ≥28 kg/m2) and metabolic health profile, resulting in four distinct obesity metabolic phenotypes: metabolically healthy non-obese (MHNO), metabolically unhealthy non-obese (MUNO), metabolically healthy obese (MHO), and metabolically unhealthy obese (MUO). Their sociodemographic information, biochemical and lipid parameters were retrospectively collected.ResultsIn this study, we found that the occurrence rate of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) among chronic schizophrenia patients with prolonged hospitalization was 54.6%. Within the MHNO grouping, MASLD-positive and MASLD-negative subgroups showed significant differences in key metabolic indicators, diabetes status, antipsychotic polypharmacy, and triglyceride-glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI). Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that Antipsychotic polypharmacy (OR=2.656, P=0.007, 95% CI=1.299–5.429) and TyG-BMI (OR = 1.043, P <0.001, 95% CI = 1.029–1.057) were independently associated with MASLD in the MHNO group.ConclusionMetabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease is prevalent among chronic schizophrenia patients, with a notable prevalence gradient across obesity metabolic phenotypes. For metabolically healthy non-obese chronic schizophrenia patients, the importance of regular metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease screening cannot be overlook ed.Keywords: schizophrenia, MASLD, obesity metabolic phenotype, prevalence, associated factors, inflammation

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe chronic mental disorder, with patients often exhibiting positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and impaired cognitive function. It is typically onset in late adolescence or early adulthood, with a lifetime prevalence of approximately 0.4%, making it one of the most burdensome psychiatric conditions globally. Schizophrenia severely impairs patients’ social and occupational functioning, imposing a heavy economic burden on families and society.1,2 Due to the combined effects of negative symptoms, long-term use of antipsychotic medications, sedentary behavior, and poor dietary habits, individuals with schizophrenia face increased risks of cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction, and suicide.3 However, in clinical practice, psychiatrists often focus excessively on patients’ mental symptoms, frequently overlooking weight and metabolic health issues in individuals with schizophrenia.3

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a clinical condition defined by a cluster of interrelated metabolic disorders, encompassing the following components: abdominal obesity, hypertension, lipid metabolism abnormalities, and hyperglycemia.4 Compared to the general population, individuals with schizophrenia exhibit a significantly higher incidence of metabolic syndrome. In China, a study involving 632 chronic schizophrenia patients revealed a prevalence rate of 27.5% for metabolic syndrome among this population. This elevated prevalence likely results from multiple contributing factors, such as antipsychotic drug-related metabolic side effects and overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms between schizophrenia and metabolic disorders.5 Research by Lang et al also demonstrates that the occurrence rate of metabolic syndrome is higher among first-episode, untreated schizophrenia patients, with BMI, smoking, and insulin resistance being associated with metabolic syndrome.6 Metabolic associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) is commonly recognized as linked to metabolic abnormalities. Given the prevalent metabolic issues among schizophrenia patients, it is necessary to further investigate the clinical characteristics of coexisting MAFLD in chronically hospitalized patients with schizophrenia.7

MAFLD, the updated terminology proposed by an international expert panel in 2020, supersedes the former designation of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).8 In 2023, a Delphi consensus endorsed by multiple international liver societies further refined the nomenclature to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which has since become the recommended terminology.9 MASLD is widely conceptualized as the hepatic counterpart of metabolic syndrome. This disease affects nearly 25% of adults globally, making it the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide. As a primary cause of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, MASLD imposes a significant disease burden and mortality rate, resulting in substantial health and economic burdens for all societies.10,11 MASLD can lead to various metabolic disorders and raise the risk of cardiovascular diseases, such as insulin resistance, hypertension, overweight or metabolic dysfunction, and elevated blood lipids.12 According to the French NASH-CO study, a large population-based survey, the estimated prevalence of MASLD is around 18.2% among the public.13 However, schizophrenia has also been found to coexist with metabolic conditions due to prolonged sedentary behavior, poor dietary habits, and the use of atypical antipsychotic medications. Consequently, schizophrenia is associated with an elevated comorbidity risk for MASLD.14

MASLD is fundamentally driven by chronic low-grade inflammation, characterized by immune cell infiltration, pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and dysregulation of the gut–liver axis.15 Similarly, systemic inflammation is a well-recognized feature of schizophrenia, supported by evidence from postmortem studies and peripheral biomarker analyses.16 This shared inflammatory basis offers a mechanistic rationale for exploring MASLD in individuals with schizophrenia and implies that chronic inflammation may serve as a biological bridge connecting schizophrenia, antipsychotic-induced metabolic abnormalities, and the development of MASLD.

Obesity and metabolic disorders are closely associated with MASLD, sharing many common pathophysiological characteristics.17 However, the two do not necessarily coexist. With deepening understanding of obesity heterogeneity, the academic community has introduced the concept of “obesity metabolic phenotypes”, categorizing individuals based on obesity status and metabolic health into four types: metabolically healthy non-obese (MHNO), metabolically unhealthy non-obese (MUNO), metabolically healthy obese (MHO), and metabolically unhealthy obese (MUO).18 Studies indicate that patients with chronic schizophrenia are more likely to be obese relative to the general population. Risk factors for obesity include elevated triglyceride and LDL levels, and BMI is positively correlated with the severity of positive symptoms in these patients.19,20 A study involving hospitalized non-obese schizophrenia patients in China indicates that the prevalence of MASLD among non-obese schizophrenia patients is approximately 25.0%.21 A French cross-sectional study revealed a 36.6% prevalence of MASLD among chronic schizophrenia patients.22 However, despite numerous studies reporting the occurrence rate and associated factors of obesity or MASLD in schizophrenia, limited understanding remains regarding the association between schizophrenia and comorbid MASLD across different obesity-metabolic phenotypes. Current research lacks investigation into the occurrence rates and associated factors of MASLD in chronic schizophrenia patients stratified by distinct obesity metabolic phenotypes. Therefore, our study aims to investigate the occurrence of fatty liver disease among distinct obesity metabolic categories in schizophrenia, particularly within the MHNO subgroup, along with its associated factors. We hypothesize that the MASLD prevalence is underestimated in the MHNO subgroup, and that certain clinical or metabolic profiles may influence the risk of fatty liver disease.

Materials and Methods

Participants

We retrospectively reviewed the records of patients with schizophrenia who were continuously hospitalized for at least 2 years at Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital between August 2022 and October 2022. Anonymous data regarding general demographic characteristics and test outcomes were gathered. All schizophrenia diagnoses met ICD-10 criteria and were independently verified by two senior psychiatrists. The following inclusion criteria were applied: 1) age 18–70 years; 2) currently receiving antipsychotic medication, with no current or past diagnosis of hepatitis or other known conditions that can induce fatty liver disease; 3) no history of using hepatoprotective agents; 4) absence of chronic alcohol consumption. Key exclusion criteria comprised: 1) history of substance abuse or dependence; 2) pregnant women; 3) neurodegenerative diseases; 4) concurrent major organic brain disease or other severe systemic illnesses; 5) conditions like malnutrition or Wilson’s disease that could independently cause hepatic steatosis. For patients with missing key laboratories or those with incomplete imaging data, no data imputation was performed. All eligible inpatients who met the inclusion criteria during the study period were included in the analysis; no convenience sampling was applied. After applying these criteria, 302 participants were included in the final analysis. A schematic overview of the participant selection logic is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Sociodemographic Data

Sociodemographic and relevant clinical data were collated by trained research assistants from the electronically archived medical records at Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital, including age, gender, height, weight, education level, and duration of illness. Data on hypertension, diabetes history, antipsychotic medication use, and Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) scores were also collected. Trained nurses measured blood pressure, height, and weight. We computed the body mass index (BMI) according to the standard equation: weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). We defined antipsychotic polypharmacy (APP) was characterized by the concurrent administration of two or more antipsychotic drugs.

Clinical Assessment

The BPRS was administered by two attending psychiatrists who had received standardized training in BPRS administration prior to the study. The BPRS total score, which reflects global symptom severity, is calculated based on the following five domains: positive symptoms (eg, hallucinations, grandiosity, disorganized thoughts), negative symptoms (eg, emotional withdrawal, blunted affect), emotional distress (eg, anxiety, depression), resistance (eg, hostility, suspiciousness), and arousal (e``g, agitation, tension).23

Blood Biochemical Collection

This study comprehensively assessed a panel of serum biomarkers pertinent to metabolic and hepatic health. The evaluated indicators encompassed parameters of glucose and lipid metabolism (fasting blood glucose [FBG], triglycerides [TG], total cholesterol [TC]), renal function (uric acid [UA], creatinine), detailed lipid profiling (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], apolipoprotein A1 [ApoA1], apolipoprotein B [ApoB]), and liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], alkaline phosphatase [ALP], and γ-glutamyl transferase [GGT]). All measurements were based on data collected during routine clinical care while hospitalized. Following an overnight fast of at least 12 hours, venous blood samples were obtained from each participant. Healthcare professionals drew blood from the antecubital vein using EDTA vacuum tubes. The samples were then promptly delivered to a laboratory for subsequent analysis using automated biochemical analyzers (Beckman Coulter AU5800, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). We used the TyG index as a measure of insulin resistance, calculated via the following formula: TyG index = ln (fasting triglycerides [mg/dL] × fasting glucose [mg/dL])/2.24 Additionally, the FIB-4 index was calculated according to previous reporting methods.25 The triglyceride-glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI) was calculated as TyG × BMI, providing an integrated measure of insulin resistance and adiposity in a single variable.26

Definition of MASLD

As the primary diagnostic modality for fatty liver disease, ultrasound imaging was performed as part of routine admission assessments shortly after blood collection to align with metabolic evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography was performed by two experienced sonographers using a Philips EPIQ 7 PLUS ultrasound system (Philips Healthcare, Bothell, WA, USA). As these examinations were part of routine inpatient admission assessments, the sonographers were unaware of the study objectives at the time of imaging. Hepatic steatosis was diagnosed based on standard ultrasonographic criteria. The detection of hepatic steatosis by ultrasound, after excluding secondary causes (such as significant alcohol intake, malnutrition, Wilson’s disease), led to a diagnosis of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) in accordance with current international diagnostic criteria.9,27 To confirm MASLD, hepatic steatosis detected via ultrasound must co-occur with at least one criterion from among the following: being overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 24 kg/m2), the existence of at least one core metabolic dysregulation in non-obese individuals or having type 2 diabetes. For this study, metabolic dysregulation was operationally defined as meeting any of the following parameters: systolic blood pressure ≥130 mmHg or diastolic ≥85 mmHg; high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) below 1.0 mmol/L (men) or 1.3 mmol/L (women); triglycerides at or above 1.70 mmol/L; or fasting plasma glucose ≥6.1 mmol/L.

Definition of Phenotype

This study classified patients into four obesity-metabolic phenotypes based on China’s adult obesity criteria (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2) and the international definition of metabolic syndrome: Metabolically Healthy Non-Obesity (MHNO): BMI < 28 kg/m2 with ≤ 1 metabolic dysregulation; Metabolically Unhealthy Non-Obesity (MUNO): BMI < 28 kg/m2 with ≥2 metabolic dysregulations; Metabolically Healthy Obese (MHO): BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2 with ≤1 metabolic dysregulation; Metabolically Unhealthy Obese (MUO): BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2 with ≥2 metabolic dysregulations. Metabolic dysregulations were defined using the four criteria from the MASLD diagnostic standards.8,28

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analyses were carried out with SPSS software (version 26.0). Descriptive analysis was first conducted for general demographic data and test results. Continuous data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on this assessment, variables were summarized accordingly: those following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared between two groups with independent samples t-tests or among multiple groups with one-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. Non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median (interquartile range) and were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test (for two-group comparisons) or the Kruskal–Wallis H-test (for multi-group comparisons). Categorical variables are reported as frequency (percentage), with between-group differences assessed by chi-square tests. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

To identify potential associated factors for MASLD among schizophrenia patients in the MHNO cohort, we initially performed univariate comparisons between the MASLD and non-MASLD subgroups. Prior to regression modeling, multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), and any variable with a VIF value greater than 5 was removed to ensure model stability. Logistic regression analysis (forward: conditional) was subsequently performed on variables showing significant differences. The overall model significance was assessed using the Omnibus test, and model goodness-of-fit was evaluated with the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, where a non-significant result (P > 0.05) indicated adequate fit. The Nagelkerke R2 was reported to quantify the proportion of variance explained by the model. A sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate whether the forward stepwise variable selection strategy may have excluded clinically relevant covariates. Sex, age, and hypertension status were simultaneously entered into a multivariable logistic regression model (enter method) alongside variables identified by the stepwise procedure, regardless of their univariate significance. We constructed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves using GraphPad Prism 9.5 to assess the predictive performance of the identified associated factors. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated, and predictive accuracy was considered acceptable for AUC values within the 0.7–0.8 range.

Results

Comparison of Baseline Characteristics in Schizophrenia Patients with or Without MASLD

This study included 302 long-term hospitalized patients with chronic schizophrenia (175 males, 58.0%; 127 females, 42.0%), among whom the prevalence of MASLD was 54.6% (165/302). Compared with the non-MASLD group, patients in the MASLD group exhibited significantly higher levels of triglycerides (TG), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), uric acid (UA), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and TyG-BMI (P<0.001). Additionally, they demonstrated a higher occurrence rate of diabetes (20.0% vs 5.8%) and hypertension was more prevalent (23.6% vs 11.7%). Furthermore, the two groups differed significantly in their distribution of obesity metabolic phenotypes (P<0.001). Liver enzyme levels (ALT, AST, GGT) were markedly higher in the MASLD group relative to the non-MASLD group (P<0.01). However, the groups did not differ markedly in terms of age, gender, disease duration, education level, or psychiatric symptom severity (as measured by the BPRS total score) (See Table 1 for details).

Comparative Analysis of Clinical Characteristics and MASLD Prevalence Differences Among Different Obesity Metabolic Phenotypes

Patients were categorized into four groups based on obesity and metabolic health status: Metabolically Healthy Non-Obese (MHNO, n=199), Metabolically Unhealthy Non-Obese (MUNO, n=39), Metabolically Healthy Obese (MHO, n=38), and Metabolically Unhealthy Obese (MUO, n=26). The prevalence of MASLD showed a significant gradient across groups (P<0.001): 38.7% (77/199) in MHNO, 74.4% (29/39) in MUNO, 89.5% (34/38) in MHO, and 96.2% (25/26) in MUO (Table 2). Except for gender distribution, which exhibited significant variation across the four groups (P=0.045), no statistically significant intergroup variations were observed in age, disease duration, and BPRS scores. Regarding metabolic characteristics, the MUO group exhibited the most pronounced metabolic abnormalities: marked elevation in total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), TyG-BMI, apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and uric acid (UA) contrasted with the other three groups (P<0.001). The MUO group also had the highest liver enzyme levels, with a median ALT of 26.5 U/L, markedly higher than the MHNO group (12 U/L) (P<0.001).

Associated Factors for MASLD in the MHNO Group

In the MHNO subgroup, the prevalence of MASLD was 38.7%. To further investigate associated factors for MASLD in this population, patients were categorized into MASLD-positive (n=77) and MASLD-negative (n=122) groups for univariate analysis. Results showed that the MASLD-positive group had markedly higher prevalence of diabetes, antipsychotic polypharmacy (APP), triglycerides (TG), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), uric acid (UA), fasting blood glucose (FBG), ALT, and TyG-BMI contrasted with the negative group (all P < 0.05) (Table 3). Prior to regression modeling, multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), and any variable with a VIF value greater than 5 was removed to ensure model stability (Supplementary Table S1). The logistic regression model was statistically significant (Omnibus χ2 = 62.446, df = 2, P < 0.001) and demonstrated adequate goodness-of-fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test: χ2 = 5.328, df = 8, P = 0.722; Nagelkerke R2 = 0.366). The event-per-variable ratio was 38.5 (77 events/2 predictors). Results indicated that antipsychotic polypharmacy (OR=2.656, P=0.007, 95% CI=1.299–5.429) and TyG-BMI (OR = 1.043, P <0.001, 95% CI = 1.029–1.057) constituted independent associated factors for MASLD in the MHNO group (Table 4). A sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate whether the forward stepwise selection strategy may have excluded clinically relevant covariates. Age, sex, and hypertension status were forced into the multivariable model (enter method) alongside antipsychotic polypharmacy and TyG-BMI. The full model remained statistically significant (Omnibus χ2 = 70.457, df =5, P < 0.001), with adequate goodness-of-fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test: χ2 = 5.499, df = 8, P = 0.703; Nagelkerke R2 = 0.405). Antipsychotic polypharmacy (OR=3.478, P=0.002, 95% CI=1.597–7.574) and TyG-BMI (OR = 1.046, P <0.001, 95% CI = 1.031–1.060) remained robustly associated with MASLD. Age, sex, and hypertension status were not independently associated with MASLD in this analysis (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

Based on the available literature, this is among the first observational study conducted in a large public medical institution in China to investigate the occurrence rate and associated factors of MASLD among long-term hospitalized chronic schizophrenia patients with different obesity-metabolic phenotypes. Current literature lacks comprehensive studies in the field. Our findings offer incremental insights into the link between schizophrenia and MASLD, potentially informing clinical management strategies. The study results indicate a high prevalence of MASLD (54.6%) among long-term hospitalized chronic schizophrenia patients, with a significant prevalence gradient ranging from 38.7% in the MHNO group to 96.2% in the MUO group. Compared to those without MASLD, schizophrenia patients with MASLD showed significant elevations in related lipid indicators and key liver enzymes, along with reductions in high-density lipoprotein and the FIB-4 index. Finally, further subgroup analysis indicated that antipsychotic polypharmacy and TyG-BMI composite index were factors independently associated with MASLD in the MHNO group.

Our study found that patients with MASLD exhibit significant metabolic dysregulation compared to those without MASLD, consistent with the pathophysiology of this disease. They are commonly present with higher BMI, hypertension, diabetes, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia. Furthermore, elevated liver enzyme levels (such as ALT, AST, GGT, etc.) correlate with fatty liver detected by abdominal ultrasound, demonstrating the direct impact of metabolic dysfunction on the liver. However, we note that the FIB-4 index in the MASLD group was significantly lower than in the non-MASLD group, seemingly contradicting the prevailing notion that “MASLD drives liver fibrosis”. The Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index is a non-invasive scoring system that incorporates age, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and platelet count. Compared with other noninvasive clinical scores, it demonstrates superior diagnostic accuracy in assessing advanced fibrosis.29 A cohort study by Kamada et al found that when FIB-4 scores were below 1.25, the incidence of future liver-related events was extremely low. However, the study also indicated that in patients with early-stage MASLD, those with FIB-4 scores in the upper normal range (≥1.25), particularly those ≥2.5, experienced a sharp increase in the risk of future intrahepatic and extrahepatic events.30 In the study cohort, the median FIB-4 scores for MASLD and non-MASLD patients were 1.00 and 1.13, respectively, both below 1.25, indicating neither group had progressed to significant fibrosis. However, marked elevations in liver enzyme levels and the TyG index were demonstrated among patients with MASLD, suggesting a normal FIB-4 score cannot rule out early steatosis and metabolic stress. Despite liver biopsy being the diagnostic gold standard for fatty liver disease, abdominal ultrasound remains the preferred screening tool in clinical settings due to its non-invasiveness, simplicity, and wide availability. In this study, abdominal ultrasound identified hepatic steatosis in all MASLD cases, whereas FIB-4 scores remained in the non-fibrotic range (<1.25) for both groups. While this observation does not constitute a formal diagnostic comparison, it suggests that a normal FIB-4 score alone may be insufficient to rule out early-stage hepatic steatosis.

This study found that the overall prevalence of MASLD among long-term hospitalized chronic schizophrenia patients was 54.6%. This prevalence is markedly higher than rates reported in community-based cohorts. For instance, a wide-scale survey conducted in France found an MASLD prevalence of only 18.2%.13 This highlights the elevated metabolic risk faced by individuals with schizophrenia. Previous studies have also investigated the occurrence rate of MASLD among individuals with schizophrenia. Reporting from Li et al indicated a 54.8% prevalence in their cross-sectional cohort of 310 schizophrenia patients receiving prolonged inpatient care.31 A French cross-sectional study revealed a 36.6% prevalence of MASLD among chronic schizophrenia patients,22 significantly higher than the rate reported for hospitalized individuals with broader psychiatric disorders (17.6%).32 This divergence may stem from inherent differences in the characteristics of the study populations: the latter sample included individuals with various psychiatric disorders, whereas the present study focused on chronic schizophrenia patients who exhibited more severe symptoms, longer hospitalization durations, and generally received long-term antipsychotic medication. Notably, even in relatively younger, predominantly male schizophrenia cohorts (such as Yan et al’s study), the prevalence of MASLD remained high (49.5%).33 These findings collectively suggest that chronicity of illness, prolonged hospitalization, and sustained use of antipsychotic medications among individuals with schizophrenia may be closely associated with the high prevalence of MASLD. Longitudinal studies confirm an increasing trend in metabolic abnormalities following initiation of antipsychotic treatment.34 These findings suggest that future research should prioritize comprehensive cross-regional characterization and prospective follow-up. This approach is essential for elucidating the roles of biological, lifestyle, and therapeutic factors in the diagnosis and management of MASLD.

We found that the combined use of antipsychotic drugs is associated with MASLD in chronic schizophrenia patients who are metabolically healthy non-obese (MHNO). Antipsychotic medications form the cornerstone of treatment for schizophrenia and other psychiatric disorders. However, the therapeutic reliance on second-generation antipsychotics in recent years has triggered significant metabolic alterations in patients, including increased appetite, weight gain, insulin resistance and dyslipidemia. These changes further elevate the risk of metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease.35,36 Although the guidelines do not recommend the combined use of medications, in clinical practice, patients with schizophrenia often receive two or more antipsychotic drugs simultaneously. Reports indicate that the prevalence of combined antipsychotic therapy increases with factors such as higher hospitalization rates and greater disease severity. Additionally, research by Centorrino et al found that schizophrenia patients receiving combination drug therapy had a higher risk of adverse reactions compared to those receiving monotherapy, even when total antipsychotic drug doses were similar.37 Jeon et al propose that the mechanism of antipsychotic polypharmacy involves heightened receptor occupancy (dopamine, histamine H1, adrenergic α1/α2, muscarinic M3). Although beneficial for some symptoms, this pharmacological profile elevates hyperprolactinemia risk, a key contributor to substantial weight gain.38 These findings underscore the critical need for clinicians to select appropriate monotherapy antipsychotics in chronic schizophrenia patients with comorbid MASLD.

Our study also indicates that among patients in the MHNO group, chronic schizophrenia patients with MASLD exhibit higher TyG-BMI indices compared to those without MASLD. Obesity and dysregulated cytokine levels are prevalent in chronic schizophrenia patients receiving antipsychotic medication, with elevated BMI in these patients associated with reduced IL-2 levels and an unfavorable lipid profile.39 Emerging evidence suggests that schizophrenia patients have distinct, BMI-stratified metabolic profiles that serve as differential predictors of cognitive and clinical symptom trajectories. Obese patients demonstrate higher waist-to-hip ratios, blood glucose, and lipid levels, and exhibit more pronounced positive symptoms.40 Even within the non-obese range, a higher BMI may reflect increased visceral obesity, where the accumulation of visceral fat represents a core pathophysiological driver of MASLD. The impact of BMI on MASLD is not determined by a single pathway but results from multifaceted interactions with various biochemical processes. For instance, BMI can elevate triglyceride levels, thereby promoting hepatic lipid accumulation; reduce HDL-C levels, diminishing the liver’s protective function; and increase certain branched-chain amino acid levels, which further influence MASLD development by promoting insulin resistance and hepatic lipogenesis.41,42 Kaya et al noted in their research report that higher BMI and diabetic status are key determinants of the development of marked fibrosis and steatohepatitis-associated sclerosing cholangitis in MASLD patients.43 As obesity progresses, individuals eventually develop insulin resistance, which is closely associated with MASLD. This resistance interacts with factors such as lipid metabolism disorders, collectively promoting the onset and progression of MASLD.44 Under conditions of insulin resistance, an individual’s adipocytes undergo excessive lipolysis, releasing large amounts of free fatty acids into the bloodstream. The hepatic influx of excess free fatty acids through the portal vein exceeds the functional limits of the hepatocellular metabolic machinery, leading to intracellular accumulation. Consequently, they are forced to be converted into triglycerides for storage, leading to hepatic steatosis. Furthermore, as the disease progresses, mitochondrial dysfunction occurs, leading to incomplete fatty acid oxidation and the production of substantial quantities of reactive oxygen species. This further exacerbates oxidative stress and accelerates the progression of fatty liver disease.45 The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a well-established and reliable surrogate marker for insulin resistance. It is calculated based on fasting plasma glucose and triglyceride levels. Numerous studies demonstrate that the TyG index and its related parameters (eg, TyG-WC, TyG-BMI) hold significant predictive value for cardiovascular disease risk and mortality. They serve as effective surrogate predictors of cardiovascular events and mortality in MASLD patients.46,47 Notably, the TyG-BMI composite index—which integrates insulin resistance (TyG) and adiposity (BMI) into a single metric—allowed us to circumvent the collinearity that would have confounded separate modeling of these two variables. As a composite measure, TyG-BMI captures the dual contributions of metabolic dysregulation and excess adiposity to hepatic steatosis, thereby offering a more integrated risk assessment for MASLD than either component alone. Previous evidence has demonstrated that TyG-BMI outperforms the TyG index alone in evaluating insulin resistance and is significantly associated with metabolic dysfunction-related outcomes across diverse populations.26 Collectively, these findings support TyG-BMI as a practical and cost-effective screening tool for identifying MASLD risk in non-obese schizophrenia patients, particularly in clinical settings where comprehensive metabolic evaluations are not readily accessible.

This study has several limitations. First, the diagnosis of hepatic steatosis relied on abdominal ultrasonography rather than liver biopsy or magnetic resonance imaging-based proton density fat fraction, which may have resulted in misclassification of mild steatosis. Furthermore, formal assessment of inter-rater reliability between the two sonographers was not performed, as the examinations were conducted as part of the routine clinical workflow rather than a prespecified research protocol. Second, as our sample only included chronic schizophrenia patients undergoing long-term hospitalization, the findings cannot be generalized to outpatients or other patient populations. Third, due to limited sample size, only approximately 30 patients were available for each of the remaining three obesity-metabolic subtypes, preventing further investigation into their respective risks for MASLD. This necessitates cautious interpretation of our findings. Fourth, potential confounders including dietary habits, physical activity, sleep disorders, and smoking status were not fully accounted for, which may have influenced the observed association between metabolic abnormalities and schizophrenia. Fifth, this study only examined the combined use of antipsychotic medications without classifying specific antipsychotic drugs, which could compromise the validity of the findings. The findings cannot be generalized to the impact of different antipsychotic drugs on MASLD. These limitations will be addressed in future studies. Finally, although waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, and waist-to-height ratio have been correlated with MASLD, the retrospective nature of this study restricted our ability to incorporate these parameters. This study should be considered preliminary, and our findings require cautious interpretation. Future multicenter, large-scale prospective studies are warranted to further supplement and confirm these results.

Conclusion

Our research indicates that chronic schizophrenia patients with prolonged hospitalization exhibit a higher prevalence of MASLD, approximately 54.6%, with a striking prevalence gradient across obesity-metabolic phenotypes. Within the MHNO group, considered “low risk”, higher TyG-BMI and combined antipsychotic medication use were independently associated with MASLD. TyG-BMI model showed moderate discriminatory performance for identifying MASLD in this subgroup. These results highlight the necessity of introducing timely detection and preventive measures in at-risk populations to reduce metabolic complications. To this end, future studies should employ longitudinal designs to unravel how genetic predispositions, lifestyle choices, and treatment regimens interactively shape metabolic outcomes in schizophrenia.
