PD-L1+ exosomes as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in multiple myeloma: implications for CAR-T efficacy and combination immunotherapy
Authors: Peng Xu, Wen-Jie Yu, Si-Han Wu, Yang Liu, Can Huang, Yue-Xuan Rao, Yan-Hua Yue, Zhi-Ling Yan, Wei Sang, Zhen-Yu Li, Tao You, Yan Lin, Jiang Cao, Qing-Yun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has reshaped the treatment landscape for relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM), durable remission remains challenging. T-cell dysfunction and exhaustion are key obstacles to long-term efficacy. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-positive exosomes may contribute to tumor-associated immunosuppression, but their clinical and functional relevance in multiple myeloma (MM) remains unclear.MethodsA total of 89 patients with MM were enrolled, including 34 patients with RRMM treated with CAR-T therapy. PD-L1+ exosome levels in peripheral blood (PB) and bone marrow (BM) were quantified by flow cytometry using exosome-coupled beads, and soluble PD-L1 (sPD-L1) was measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Associations with clinicopathological features, treatment response, and progression-free survival (PFS) were analyzed. Effects of RRMM patient sample-derived PD-L1+ exosomes on CD8+ T-cell activation, cytokine production, and exhaustion phenotypes were assessed using in vitro experiments. The therapeutic potential of GW4869 combined with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibody to enhance CAR-T efficacy was further evaluated using in vitro and in vivo models.ResultsPD-L1+ exosome levels in PB and BM were significantly elevated in newly diagnosed MM and RRMM compared with healthy controls, and were closely associated with adverse prognostic features, including high tumor burden, extramedullary lesions, and high-risk cytogenetic features, whereas plasma sPD-L1 showed no significant clinical correlations. In RRMM patients receiving CAR-T therapy, pretreatment BM and PB PD-L1+ exosome levels discriminated best response (complete response versus less than complete response), with BM showing better predictive performance (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, 0.846; optimal cutoff, 65.0%). High pretreatment PD‑L1+ exosome levels were associated with low peak levels of CAR transgene, interferon-γ, and tumor necrosis factor-α, as well as shorter PFS. In addition, elevated BM PD‑L1+ exosome level remained an independent adverse factor for PFS in multivariable analysis. In vitro, RRMM patient sample-derived PD-L1+ exosomes inhibited CD8+ T cell activation and effector cytokine production, and promoted terminal exhaustion, which was not fully reversed by anti-PD-1 antibody alone. In preclinical models, GW4869 plus anti-PD-1 antibody enhanced CAR-T-mediated antitumor activity in both cell-based assays and the MM xenograft model.ConclusionPD‑L1+ exosomes may serve as biomarkers of disease aggressiveness and CAR‑T therapy outcomes in MM. By impairing CD8+ T cell function and promoting terminal exhaustion, they may contribute to immune resistance. Combined exosome-release pathway and PD‑1 blockade warrants further investigation as a strategy to enhance CAR‑T efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40364-026-00980-6.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM), the second most common hematologic malignancy, is a clonal plasma cell disorder characterized by the presence of monoclonal protein in serum or urine and evidence of end-organ damage, including hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency, anemia, and bone lesions [1–4]. Although novel therapies including immunomodulatory agents, proteasome inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation have significantly improved patient outcomes, relapse and refractoriness remain major clinical challenges [2, 5]. In recent years, chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has shown remarkable efficacy in relapsed/refractory MM (RRMM), substantially reshaping the therapeutic landscape [6–8]. However, durable remission is not achieved in all patients, and a considerable proportion eventually experience relapse or disease progression after CAR-T therapy [9, 10]. Emerging evidence suggests that this limited durability may be partly attributable to progressive CAR-T-cell dysfunction and exhaustion within the tumor microenvironment [11, 12]. Therefore, understanding the immune-suppressive bone marrow microenvironment (BMM) that drives CAR-T exhaustion is essential to developing effective combination therapies.

The development and progression of MM are highly dependent on complex interactions between malignant plasma cells and the BMM [1, 13]. The BMM is characterized by an immune-suppressive milieu comprising regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and multiple inhibitory cytokines, all of which can impair the function of infiltrating effector T cells [14, 15]. Among the pathways involved in tumor immune escape, the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis is particularly important. Binding of PD-L1 expressed on tumor cells to PD-1 on T cells promotes T‑cell exhaustion and suppresses anti‑tumor immunity [16]. However, previous clinical trials of immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in MM have yielded limited benefits, suggesting that additional immune-suppressive mechanisms or alternative modes of PD-L1 delivery may contribute to immune evasion in this disease [17–20].

In recent years, extracellular vesicles (EVs), particularly exosomes, have emerged as key mediators of intercellular communication in tumor immune regulation. Exosomes carry proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids from their parent cells, enabling the distant delivery of biological information and remodeling of recipient cell functions [21, 22]. Recent studies implied that tumor-derived exosomes expressing high levels of PD-L1 could directly bind to PD-1 on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, exerting immune-suppressive effects similar to those of membrane-bound PD-L1 on tumor cells and thereby mediating immune evasion. Furthermore, exosomal PD-L1 may act as an endocrine molecule, transferring to cells with low or no PD-L1 expression and promoting tumor progression [20, 23, 24]. In our previous study on diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, high pretreatment plasma levels of PD-L1+ exosomes were significantly associated with multiple adverse prognostic indicators, and dynamic changes in these levels during treatment effectively monitored therapeutic response [25]. Previous studies also indicated that exosomes derived from MM cells promoted MM progression and induced drug resistance, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression [19, 26, 27]. However, research on the expression levels and immunosuppressive functions of PD-L1+ exosomes in MM patients remains limited.

Thus, in this study the levels of PD‑L1+ exosomes in peripheral blood (PB) and bone marrow (BM) samples from MM patients, its correlation with clinicopathological features and response to CAR‑T therapy, and its effects on CD8+ T cell function and exhaustion phenotype was analyzed. In addition, the in vitro and in vivo models were also used to evaluate the combination of the sphingomyelinase inhibitor GW4869 and an anti‑PD‑1 antibody in the context of CAR‑T therapy. This study might provide clues in improving the understanding of exosome‑mediated immunosuppression in the MM micro-environment and preliminary experimental evidence for optimizing CAR‑T‑based therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study enrolled a total of 89 patients with MM treated at the Department of Hematology, Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, and the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (The First People’s Hospital of Changzhou) from March 2020 to October 2025. Regarding disease status, 28 patients had newly diagnosed MM (NDMM), 19 were in complete remission (CRMM), and 42 had RRMM. For each patient, PB and BM samples were obtained at the same disease-status assessment time point. Table 1 summarizes the clinicopathological characteristics of the enrolled patients. All patients met the diagnostic criteria established by the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG), and treatment response was assessed according to IMWG efficacy guidelines [28]. Among the 42 patients with RRMM, 34 received autologous CAR‑T cell therapy, including either single‑target B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) CAR‑T cells or BCMA/CD19 dual‑target CAR‑T cells. The CAR‑T cell manufacturing procedures and patient eligibility criteria for RRMM were consistent with previously published protocols [29, 30]. All CAR‑T‑treated patients received lymphodepleting conditioning with fludarabine (30 mg/m2/day, days −5 to −3) and cyclophosphamide (750 mg/m2/day, day −5) prior to CAR-T infusion. This study was approved by the hospital ethics committees (approval numbers: KL003-01, KL062-01, CL014-01) and followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

All PB and BM specimens were pre-processed within 2 hours of collection. If BM samples were excessively viscous, an equal volume of sterile phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS) was added for 1:1 dilution to reduce viscosity. Subsequently, all samples were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C to remove cellular components, and the supernatant was transferred to new tubes and centrifuged again at 3,000 × g for 15 minutes to further remove residual cells and debris. The resulting supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until unified testing for PD‑L1+ exosomes and soluble PD‑L1.

Clinical data collection, variable definitions, and follow-up

Baseline clinical characteristics collected in this study included age, sex, M protein subtype, disease status, revised international staging system (R-ISS) stage, tumor burden, high‑risk cytogenetic abnormalities, extramedullary lesions, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and β2‑microglobulin (β2‑MG). High tumor burden was defined as clonal plasma cells or bone marrow plasma cells accounting for ≥50% of nucleated cells in bone marrow assessment. Extramedullary disease (EMD) was defined separately as the presence of soft-tissue plasmacytoma or extramedullary lesions confirmed by imaging and/or pathological evaluation. EMD was not included in the definition of high tumor burden. Patients with EMD but bone marrow plasma cell involvement < 50% were classified as EMD-positive but not high tumor burden. For the subgroup of RRMM patients receiving CAR‑T cell therapy, in addition to the above baseline characteristics (assessed using the most recent test results within 15 days before lymphodepleting conditioning), the following clinical data were also collected: prior treatment history, type of CAR‑T product infused, cytokine levels (IFN‑γ, TNF‑α, IL‑2), and CAR transgene levels (recorded within one month after CAR‑T infusion).

Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were retrospectively assessed and graded according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) consensus criteria [31]. Progression‑free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from CAR‑T infusion to disease progression or death from any cause. All patients were followed up until December 2025, with follow‑up data obtained from inpatient/outpatient medical records and telephone interviews. The median follow‑up time for the entire cohort was 26.2 months (interquartile range: 16.5–38.4).

Exosome Isolation and identification

Exosomes were isolated and purified by ultracentrifugation following previously established standard protocols [32], which had been validated in our previous studies [25]. In brief, thawed plasma or bone marrow supernatant was initially centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 40 minutes at 4 °C to remove larger particulate debris and subsequently passed through a 0.22 μm filter. The resulting filtrate was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 120,000 × g for 60 minutes at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and washed by an additional round of ultracentrifugation under identical conditions (120,000 × g, 60 minutes, 4 °C). Finally, the purified exosome pellet was resuspended in 400 μL of PBS and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

The morphology of purified exosomes was observed using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi H-7650, Japan) at 80 kV (Supplementary Figure S1A). Briefly, a drop of the purified exosome suspension was placed on a copper grid, allowed to adsorb for 1 minute, and excess liquid was removed with filter paper. The grid was then stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 30 seconds, the staining solution was removed with filter paper, and the grid was dried for 1 minute before observation and image capture. Meanwhile, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight NS300, Malvern Panalytical, UK) was used to determine the particle size distribution of exosomes (Supplementary Figure S1B).

Flow cytometry of Exosome‑bound beads

Exosomes were coupled to aldehyde/sulfate latex beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. A37304) and analyzed by flow cytometry. Briefly, 100 μL of exosome suspension was mixed with 1 mL of PBS, and then 5 μL of latex beads was added, followed by incubation with rotation for 30 minutes at room temperature. Thereafter, PBS containing 100 mM glycine and 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was added to terminate the reaction, and incubation with rotation continued for another 30 minutes at room temperature. The mixture was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 5 minutes, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed three times with PBS. After adding 5 μL of Fc receptor blocker (BD Pharmingen; Cat. No. 564,220) and incubating for 10 minutes, CD63 antibody (BD Pharmingen; Cat. No. 561,924), CD274 antibody (BD Pharmingen; Cat. No. 557,924), and IgG1κ isotype control antibody were added, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. The CD63 antibody was used as an exosome marker to gate on beads bound to CD63‑positive exosomes. Results showed high CD63 expression in extracted plasma exosomes, with positive rates ranging from 95.72% to 98.65% (Supplementary Figure S1C). Using the IgG1κ isotype control antibody as the gating control, the proportion of PD‑L1+ exosomes in PB and BM specimens was determined based on the fluorescence threshold set by the isotype control (Supplementary Figure S1D). Accordingly, results are reported as the percentage of PD-L1-bearing exosome-bound beads rather than as cell-of-origin-defined myeloma-derived exosomes.

Before routinely applying this method to monitor PD‑L1+ exosome levels from PB and BM, its reproducibility was validated. Exosomes were extracted from plasma of 2 MM patients; each exosome sample was divided into three portions, and each portion was tested in three replicate wells for PD‑L1 staining (total of 3 × 9 test wells). Flow cytometry results, expressed as the percentage of PD‑L1+ exosomes, showed good reproducibility with an experimental error of only 5% (Supplementary Figure S1E).

Soluble PD-L1 Detection

Plasma soluble PD‑L1 (sPD‑L1) levels were measured using a commercial enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Abcam; Cat. No. ab214565). All samples and standards were tested in duplicate according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Isolation of CD8+ T cells and function assays

Peripheral blood from healthy donors was collected, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using Ficoll‑Paque™ PLUS density gradient media (Cytiva; Cat. No. 17‑1440‑02). CD8+ T cells were then purified by negative selection using the CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec; Cat. No. 130‑096‑495) combined with the autoMACS® Pro Separator (Miltenyi Biotec; Cat. No. 130‑092‑545). A total of 2 × 105 T cells were seeded in U‑bottom 96‑well plates and stimulated with CD3/CD28 T Cell Activator (STEMCELL Technologies; Cat. No. 10,971) at 25 μL/mL, together with IL‑2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. GMP200‑02‑50UG) at a final concentration of 100 IU/mL and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 5–6 hours for activation. After activation, pre‑quantified low PD‑L1+ exosomes (below the upper quartile) or high PD‑L1+ exosomes (above the upper quartile) were added to the culture and co‑incubated for an additional 24 hours; the control group received an equal volume of PBS. PD-L1+ exosomes used in this assay were isolated from RRMM patient samples and stratified into low and high groups according to predefined quartile-based criteria. Healthy donor-derived CD8+ T cells were used to assess the effects of RRMM-derived PD-L1+ exosomes under controlled in vitro conditions. In the anti‑PD‑1 antibody intervention experiment, following CD8+ T cell activation with IL‑2/CD3/CD28 for 5–6 hours, PD‑1 antibody (R&D Systems; Cat. No. AF1086) was added to a final concentration of 30 μg/mL, or an IgG control was added as an isotype control, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour. Then, high PD‑L1 exosomes were added and co‑incubated with CD8+ T cells for an additional 24 hours.

After co‑culture, cells were collected and analyzed by flow cytometry. Antibodies used for surface staining included: anti‑CD8 antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 344,713), anti‑CD69 antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 310,903), anti‑CD279 (PD‑1) antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 329,913), and anti‑CD366 (Tim‑3) antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 345,013). For intracellular cytokine detection, cells were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Sigma‑Aldrich; Cat. No. P1585) at 50 ng/mL, ionomycin calcium salt (Sigma‑Aldrich; Cat. No. I3909) at 1 μg/mL, and brefeldin A (Sigma‑Aldrich; Cat. No. B7651) at 10 μg/mL for 4 hours. Subsequently, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained for nuclear/intracellular markers using the eBioscience™ Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. 00‑5523‑00). Intracellular staining antibodies included: anti‑IFN‑γ antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 502,511), anti‑TNF‑α antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 502,908), anti‑IL‑2 antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 500,325); the nuclear transcription factor TCF‑1 was stained with anti‑TCF1 (TCF7) antibody (BioLegend; Cat. No. 655,203). Results were expressed as percentages of CD8+ CD69+ T cells, CD8+ IFN‑γ+ T cells, CD8+ TNF‑α+ T cells, and CD8+ IL‑2+ T cells. Progenitor exhausted T cells (Texprog) were defined as PD-1+ TCF-1+ TIM-3− CD8+ T cells, whereas terminally exhausted T cells (Texterm) were defined as PD-1+ TCF-1− TIM-3+ CD8+ T cells, which were established phenotypic frameworks of exhausted CD8+ T-cell differentiation [33–35].

Cell culture and in vitro CAR-T combination intervention

MM cell lines (RPMI-8226 and OPM-2) were purchased from Cobioer Biotechnology Co., Ltd. RPMI-8226 cells were cultured in iscove’s modified dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) and OPM-2 cells in RPMI-1640 medium, each supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, at 37 °C in 5% CO₂.

BCMA CAR‑T cells used for in vitro experiments were prepared using a construct consistent with our center’s previous studies. Briefly, after PBMC collection by apheresis, CD3+ T cells were isolated using anti‑CD3 immunomagnetic beads and transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding the BCMA chimeric antigen receptor. The CAR construct contained an anti‑BCMA single‑chain variable fragment (scFv), a 4‑1BB costimulatory domain, and a CD3ζ signaling domain. Transduced T cells were expanded in vitro for 10–14 days before use in subsequent co‑culture experiments.

To evaluate the feasibility of inhibiting the exosome release pathway, RPMI‑8226 and OPM‑2 cells were seeded in 12‑well plates. When cells reached the logarithmic growth phase, they were treated with GW4869 (Selleck; Cat. No. S7609) at concentrations of 0, 1, 3, 5, or 10 μmol/L for 12, 24, or 48 hours. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK‑8 assay (Dojindo Molecular Technologies; Cat. No. CK04), and culture supernatants were simultaneously collected for exosome release analysis. Based on previous studies using GW4869 in MM and bone marrow microenvironment models [36, 37], together with our pilot dose-time optimization experiments, 5 μmol/L for 24–48 h was selected as the working pretreatment condition, as this regimen showed minimal effects on MM cell viability while providing stable inhibition of exosome secretion. To explore whether the enhanced CAR-T-mediated cytotoxicity observed with GW4869 was associated with inhibition of exosome release, an exogenous exosome rescue experiment was further established based on the BCMA CAR‑T + GW4869 group. Specifically, exosomes derived from RPMI‑8226 or OPM‑2 cells were quantified and added back to the BCMA CAR‑T + GW4869 system at a total protein concentration of 100 μg/mL, followed by co‑culture for an additional 24 hours. After co-culture, tumor cell viability was assessed by flow cytometry using Calcein-AM staining (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat. No. C3099). Briefly, cells from each co-culture group were collected, washed with PBS, and incubated with Calcein-AM working solution for 30 minutes at 37 °C. After washing and resuspension in PBS, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry. Cell debris and obviously dead cells were first excluded based on FSC/SSC characteristics. T cells were then excluded by CD3 gating, and the remaining CD3-negative population was further gated according to the scatter characteristics of RPMI-8226 or OPM-2 cells. Tumor cell viability was calculated as the proportion of Calcein-AM-positive cells within the gated tumor cell population. To assess the direct effect of GW4869 on CAR-T-cell viability, CAR-T cells were treated with vehicle control or 5 μM GW4869 for 24 hours. Cell viability were evaluated using a Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) (BioLegend; Cat. No. 640,922) followed by flow-cytometric analysis.

To evaluate effects of combining GW4869 with anti‑PD‑1 antibody on the anti‑tumor activity of BCMA CAR‑T cells, RPMI‑8226 and OPM‑2 cells were seeded in 12‑well plates. The following treatment groups were established: control, BCMA CAR‑T, BCMA CAR‑T + anti‑PD‑1, BCMA CAR‑T + GW4869, and BCMA CAR‑T + GW4869 + anti‑PD‑1. GW4869 was used to pretreat tumor cells at the determined working concentration (5 μmol/L) for 24 hours. Anti‑PD‑1 antibody (R&D Systems; Cat. No. AF1086) was added at the start of co‑culture at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL and preincubated for 1 hour before adding effector cells. The 1 hour anti-PD-1 preincubation was used to allow pathway blockade before CAR-T/MM cell co-culture. BCMA CAR‑T cells were then added at an effector‑to‑target ratio (E: T) of 1:1 and co‑cultured for 24 hours. For drug interaction analysis, the tumor cell plus CAR-T group was defined as the reference condition and normalized to zero effect for zero-interaction potency (ZIP)-based synergy modeling. Drug interaction between GW4869 and anti-PD-1 was assessed using the ZIP model under the fixed CAR-T co-culture condition. A ZIP score > 10 was considered indicative of a positive interaction between GW4869 and anti-PD-1, rather than formal synergy with CAR-T cells.

MM xenograft mouse model and in vivo CAR-T combination intervention

Six‑ to eight‑week‑old female NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice were housed under specific pathogen‑free conditions. RPMI‑8226 cells were washed with PBS, resuspended in 100 μL of PBS at a concentration of 5 × 106 cells, and injected subcutaneously into the right axilla to establish a xenograft model. When tumors reached approximately 50 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to five groups for tumor growth analysis (n = 5 per group) [1]: control group (PBS) [2]; CAR‑T monotherapy group (4 × 106 CAR-T cells via tail vein injection) [3]; CAR‑T + anti‑PD‑1 group (anti-PD-1 antibody administered intraperitoneally at 100 μg per dose three times per week, starting on day 5 after CAR-T infusion) [4]; CAR‑T + GW4869 group (GW4869 at 2.5 mg/kg in PBS containing 5% DMSO injected intraperitoneally three times per week, starting on day 3 before CAR‑T injection) [5]; CAR‑T + GW4869 + anti‑PD‑1 group (combination of the above regimens). GW4869 was initiated before CAR-T infusion to reduce tumor-derived exosome release before effector-cell administration, whereas anti-PD-1 was started after CAR-T infusion to target the early post-infusion phase of CAR-T activation and expansion in vivo. Tumor volumes were measured every 3 days and calculated as (length × width2)/2. Body weight and general condition were monitored throughout the experiment. In addition, an independent survival cohort with the same group allocation and treatment schedule was established (n = 10 per group). Mice in the survival cohort were monitored until tumor volume reached the predefined endpoint of 1500 mm3, and survival was recorded accordingly. All animal procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (Approval number: 2,022,081,105) and were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0, GraphPad Prism 9.0, and R 4.4.3 software were used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as median (range) or mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann‑Whitney U test, independent samples t‑test, one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or Kruskal‑Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Correlation analysis was performed using Spearman’s rank correlation. ROC curves were used to evaluate the performance of PD‑L1+ exosome levels in predicting the best response to CAR‑T therapy, and the optimal cut‑off value was determined by the Youden index. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan‑Meier method and the log‑rank test. Univariate Cox regression was used to screen variables associated with PFS (with p < 0.05 for inclusion), and multivariate Cox regression was used to identify independent risk factors. Significance levels are indicated as follows: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant (p > 0.05).

Results

Clinical characteristics of MM patients

This study included 89 patients with MM, and their clinicopathological characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 56 years (range, 35–77 years). The cohort showed a male predominance (n = 48, 53.9%). The most common M protein subtype was IgG (n = 54, 60.7%), followed by IgA (n = 13, 14.6%). Regarding disease status, 28 patients (31.5%) had NDMM, 19 (21.3%) had CRMM, and 42 (47.2%) had RRMM. According to the R‑ISS, 52 patients (58.4%) were classified as stage III. High tumor burden was observed in 29 patients (32.6%), high‑risk cytogenetic abnormalities in 22 (24.7%), and extramedullary lesions in 35 (39.3%). Elevated LDH levels were found in 59 patients (66.3%), and serum β2‑MG levels ≥ 5.5 mg/L in 62 patients (69.7%). Correlation with disease status

Stratified analysis by disease status indicated that PD‑L1+ exosome levels in both PB and BM were significantly higher in patients with NDMM and RRMM than those in the healthy controls (all p < 0.01). No significant differences were observed between the RRMM and NDMM groups (all p > 0.05). In contrast, PD‑L1+ exosome levels in patients with CRMM were markedly reduced and did not differ significantly from those in healthy controls (all p > 0.05) (Figs. 1A, 1B). As for the plasma sPD‑L1 levels, no significant differences were observed among healthy controls, NDMM, CRMM, and RRMM groups (all p > 0.05; Supplementary Figure S2G). Correlation analysis was further performed according to disease status using paired PB and BM samples. In RRMM patients, BM PD-L1+ exosome levels were significantly positively correlated with matched PB PD-L1+ exosome levels (p < 0.001; Fig. 1C). Similar positive correlations were also observed in NDMM patients (p = 0.0035; Fig. 1F) and CRMM patients (p = 0.0439; Fig. 1I). In contrast, neither BM nor PB PD-L1+ exosome levels were significantly correlated with plasma sPD-L1 levels in RRMM, NDMM, or CRMM patients (all p > 0.05; Figs. 1D, 1E, 1G, 1H, 1J, 1K). Correlation with clinicopathological Features

PD-L1+ exosome levels were further analyzed according to key clinicopathological features, with subgroup sizes explicitly stated as follows: R-ISS stage I/II versus III, 37 versus 52; high tumor burden, yes versus no, 29 versus 60; extramedullary lesions, yes versus no, 35 versus 54; high-risk cytogenetic features, yes versus no, 22 versus 67; serum LDH, normal versus elevated, 30 versus 59; and serum β2-MG, <5.5 versus ≥5.5 mg/L, 27 versus 62. In BM samples (Figs. 2A–2F), PD‑L1+ exosome levels were significantly higher in patients with R‑ISS stage III, high tumor burden, extramedullary lesions, high‑risk cytogenetic abnormalities, and elevated β2‑MG than in that of their respective control groups (all p < 0.05). In PB samples (Figs. 2G–2L), PD‑L1+ exosome levels were also significantly elevated in patients with high tumor burden, extramedullary lesions, high‑risk cytogenetic abnormalities, and elevated β2‑MG (all p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed between patients with R‑ISS stage I/II and stage III (p > 0.05; Fig. 2G). In both sample types, no significant differences were found between patients with elevated versus normal LDH levels (all p > 0.05; Figs. 2E, 2K). In contrast, plasma sPD‑L1 levels showed no significant differences across stratifications by R‑ISS stage, tumor burden, high‑risk cytogenetics, extramedullary lesions, LDH, or β2‑MG (all p > 0.05; Supplementary Figure S2A–F). These results suggest that PD‑L1+ exosome levels are closely associated with adverse clinicopathological features in MM patients and may serve as a potential marker of disease aggressiveness. Efficacy and prognosis prediction in CAR‑T‑treated patients

Among the 34 RRMM patients who received CAR‑T therapy, ROC curve analysis revealed that pretreatment BM PD‑L1+ exosome levels predicted the best response (CR vs. Non‑CR) with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.846 (95% CI: 0.68–0.92), an optimal cutoff value of 65.0%, a sensitivity of 80.0%, and a specificity of 94.7%. For PB PD‑L1+ exosome levels, the AUC was 0.735 (95% CI: 0.61–0.87), with an optimal cutoff of 41.5%, a sensitivity of 73.3%, and a specificity of 78.9%. Correlation with CAR transgene expansion, cytokine release, and CRS, and ICANS

Further analysis of the relationship between PD‑L1+ exosome levels and CAR transgene and cytokine release showed that, regardless of sample type (BM or PB), patients in the high PD‑L1+ exosome level group had significantly lower peak CAR transgene levels than those in the low level group (BM: p < 0.01; PB: p < 0.05; Figs. 2A, 2B). Regarding cytokine levels, patients in the BM high PD‑L1+ exosome level group exhibited significantly lower peak IFN‑γ and TNF‑α levels than those in the low-level group (IFN‑γ: p < 0.01; TNF‑α: p < 0.001; Figs. 2C, 2E). In PB, the high PD‑L1+ exosome level group also had significantly lower peak TNF‑α levels (p < 0.05; Fig. 4F), whereas the difference in peak IFN‑γ levels was not statistically significant (p > 0.05; Fig. 2D). Analysis of CRS severity showed no statistically significant association between PD-L1+ exosome levels and CRS grade in this cohort (grade 1–2 vs. ≥3; PB: p = 0.613; BM: p > 0.999; Supplementary Table S1). However, because only four patients developed grade ≥ 3 CRS, this negative finding should be interpreted cautiously. All 34 CAR-T-treated patients were classified as ICANS grade 0; therefore, no further correlation analysis between PD-L1+ exosome levels and ICANS severity was performed.

To further assess the potential impact of CAR-T product heterogeneity, we performed an exploratory subgroup analysis comparing patients treated with BCMA CAR-T therapy and those treated with BCMA/CD19 CAR-T therapy. As summarized in Supplementary Table S2, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in CR/non-CR response, PFS, peak CAR transgene levels, peak IFN-γ, TNF-α, or IL-2 levels. Pretreatment BM and PB PD-L1+ exosome levels were also not significantly different between the two groups.

These results suggest that high PD‑L1+ exosome levels may impair anti‑tumor immune responses by inhibiting CAR‑T cell expansion and effector cytokine release, whereas the relationship between PD-L1+ exosome levels and severe CRS remains inconclusive because of the limited number of grade ≥ 3 CRS events.

In vitro functional assays demonstrated that co‑culturing RRMM patient sample-derived PD‑L1+ exosomes with activated healthy donor-derived CD8+ T cells significantly reduced the proportion of CD8+ CD69+ T cells compared with the no‑exosome control (p < 0.0001), with a stronger inhibitory effect observed in the high PD‑L1+ exosome group than that in the low PD‑L1+ exosome group (p < 0.01). Adding anti‑PD‑1 antibody to the high PD‑L1+ exosome condition did not significantly reverse the decrease in CD8+ CD69+ T cells levels (p > 0.05; Fig. 3A). Further assessment of effector cytokine production revealed that both low and high PD‑L1+ exosome groups reduced the proportions of IFN‑γ+, TNF‑α+, and IL‑2+ CD8+ T cells, with the high PD‑L1+ exosome group exhibiting the greatest inhibition (all p < 0.05; Fig. 3B). GW4869 and Anti‑PD‑1 Antibody Enhanced CAR‑T Anti‑Tumor Effect In Vitro

To evaluate the feasibility of targeting the exosome release pathway, effects of GW4869 on MM cell viability and exosome release were analyzed. Treatment with 1–10 μM GW4869 had no significant effect on cell viability (all p > 0.05; Supplementary Figure S3A). As the concentration of GW4869 increased, the proportion of CD63‑positive exosomes gradually decreased (p < 0.001; Supplementary Figure S3A). Because CD63 is a pan-exosomal marker, this finding indicates reduced total exosome secretion rather than selective inhibition of PD-L1+ exosomes. Time‑course experiments at 5 μM showed that the inhibitory effect on exosome release increased with treatment duration, peaking at approximately 24 hours (p < 0.01; Supplementary Figure S3B). In the co‑culture system of CAR‑T and MM cells (Supplementary Figure S3C), CAR‑T treatment significantly reduced tumor cell viability, and adding GW4869 further decreased viability (p < 0.01). Supplementation with exogenous exosomes partially rescued tumor cell viability (p < 0.05), supporting the possible involvement of reduced exosome release in the GW4869-associated enhancement of CAR-T-mediated cytotoxicity. Direct exposure to 5 μM GW4869 for 24 hours did not significantly affect CAR-T-cell viability compared with control (p > 0.05; Supplementary Figure S3D), suggesting that the enhanced CAR-T-mediated cytotoxicity observed under the tested in vitro conditions was unlikely to be primarily attributable to direct GW4869 cytotoxicity. However, other direct effects on CAR-T-cell function and in vivo exposure cannot be excluded and require further pharmacokinetic and in vivo validation.

In the tumor microenvironment co‑culture model (Fig. 4A), CAR‑T treatment alone significantly reduced the viability of RPMI‑8226 and OPM‑2 cells (p < 0.001). The addition of anti‑PD‑1 antibody to CAR‑T therapy resulted in only a slight decrease in cell viability (p > 0.05), indicating the limited benefit of PD‑1 blockade. Treatment with CAR‑T + GW4869 further reduced tumor cell viability (p < 0.01); notably, the triple combination of CAR‑T, GW4869, and anti‑PD‑1 antibody reduced tumor cell viability to the lowest level (p < 0.001). Because the co-culture assay was performed at a single E:T ratio of 1:1 for 24 hours, these findings should be interpreted as enhanced CAR-T-mediated antimyeloma activity under the tested fixed-dose condition. Drug interaction analysis showed a positive interaction between GW4869 and anti-PD-1 antibody under the tested conditions, with ZIP scores of 13.159 and 14.933 in RPMI-8226 and OPM-2 cells, respectively (Fig. 4B). However, because a complete dose-response matrix incorporating variable CAR-T E:T ratios, GW4869 concentrations, and anti-PD-1 concentrations was not performed, these data do not establish formal synergy between CAR-T cells and the GW4869/anti-PD-1 combination. Thus, the enhanced antimyeloma activity observed with the triple combination should be interpreted as enhanced or potentially additive activity under fixed-dose conditions. In vivo evaluation of GW4869 combined with anti-PD-1 antibody in enhancing CAR-T therapy

Based on these in vitro findings, we further evaluated the in vivo anti‑tumor efficacy of the combination in a MM xenograft mouse model (Fig. 5A). NSG mice were subcutaneously inoculated with RPMI‑8226 cells, and after tumor establishment, they were randomly divided into five groups: control, CAR‑T monotherapy, CAR‑T + anti‑PD‑1, CAR‑T + GW4869, and CAR‑T + GW4869 + anti‑PD‑1. Our results (Fig. 5B–C) showed that compared with the control group, tumor growth was significantly suppressed in the CAR‑T monotherapy group (p < 0.05). The CAR‑T + anti‑PD‑1 group exhibited a slight but not statistically significant reduction in tumor volume relative to the CAR‑T monotherapy group (p > 0.05). The CAR‑T + GW4869 group displayed markedly enhanced tumor growth inhibition (p < 0.05). Discussion

Although CAR‑T therapy has shown remarkable efficacy in RRMM, the occurrence of non‑response or early progression in a subset of patients remains a major clinical challenge in its application. In this study, our results indicated that PD‑L1+ exosome levels in the PB and BM of MM patients were significantly elevated and were closely associated with multiple adverse prognostic indicators and poor response to CAR‑T therapy. In vitro experiments revealed that PD‑L1+ exosomes suppressed CD8+ T cell activation and drove their differentiation toward a terminally exhausted phenotype, and this effect was not fully reversed by anti‑PD‑1 antibody. In both in vitro and in vivo settings, the combination of GW4869 and anti‑PD‑1 antibody enhanced CAR-T-mediated anti-tumor activity. Together, these findings provide new insights into immune evasion in MM and suggest that targeting the exosome release pathway may represent a potential strategy to enhance CAR-T therapeutic efficacy and improve response durability.

Our study first showed that PD‑L1+ exosome levels in PB and BM of patients with NDMM and RRMM were significantly elevated and were closely associated with disease activity and multiple adverse prognostic indicators. This result is consistent with previous reports in gastric cancer, pancreatic cancer, and non‑small cell lung cancer, supporting that circulating PD‑L1+ exosomes may serve as the potential biomarker for assessing disease progression and poor prognosis in various malignancies [38–41]. The positive correlation between PD‑L1+ exosome levels in BM and PB suggests that PB levels may serve as a non‑invasive “liquid biopsy” marker of the in situ immunosuppressive status of the BM, offering a more convenient alternative to tissue biopsy. In contrast, in this study, plasma sPD‑L1 levels showed no significant correlation with clinicopathological parameters or disease activity in MM patients, nor was there a significant association between sPD‑L1 and PD‑L1+ exosome levels. This is not entirely unexpected, as previous studies on the clinical relevance of sPD-L1 in MM and other malignancies have yielded inconsistent results [42–44]. Mechanistically, sPD-L1 is thought to arise mainly through proteolytic shedding or alternative splicing [45], whereas exosomes are generated through the endosomal pathway and may more faithfully recapitulate the molecular features of their parent tumor cells [46]. This difference may partly explain why PD-L1+ exosomes, compared with sPD-L1, could better capture the immunosuppressive status of the tumor microenvironment and serve as a more informative liquid-biopsy biomarker. This interpretation is consistent with observations in head and neck cancer, gastric cancer, and melanoma, in which PD‑L1+ exosomes displayed closer associations with disease activity or clinical outcome than that of soluble or total circulating PD-L1 [47–50]. Although the reduced levels of PD-L1+ exosomes in CRMM patients suggest a potential association with active myeloma disease status, the cellular origin of these exosomes could not be definitively determined in this study. Future studies using fresh samples, tumor-derived exosome immunocapture, MM-specific exosome markers, or single-vesicle analysis are needed to better define the cellular source of PD-L1+ exosomes in MM.

More importantly, in 34 patients with RRMM receiving CAR‑T therapy, we found that pretreatment PD‑L1+ exosome levels in both PB and BM could discriminate the best response (CR vs. Non‑CR) to CAR‑T therapy, with optimal cut‑off values determined by ROC curves. Patients with low pretreatment PD‑L1+ exosome levels exhibited higher peak CAR transgene levels, higher peak levels of effector cytokines (IFN‑γ and TNF‑α), and longer PFS, with no significant difference in CRS incidence. Multivariate analysis further confirmed that BM PD‑L1+ exosome level was an independent prognostic factor for PFS. Notably, in the subgroup receiving PD‑1 inhibitor maintenance therapy, the difference in PFS was more pronounced, suggesting that PD‑L1+ exosomes could predict CAR‑T efficacy and are associated with poorer PFS under PD‑1 inhibitor maintenance. Our results also revealed that the higher PD‑L1+ exosome levels were associated with greater inhibition of CD8+ effector T cell activation and a higher proportion of CD8+ T cells differentiating into Text e r m cells, and that this effect was not fully reversed by anti‑PD‑1 antibody. In this functional assay, RRMM patient-derived PD-L1+ exosomes were co-cultured with healthy donor-derived CD8+ T cells, allowing assessment under controlled in vitro conditions but not fully recapitulating the matched autologous immune context of MM patients. In addition, activated T-cell proliferation, IL-6 production, and CD4+ T-cell responses were not evaluated; therefore, cytokine production and exhaustion phenotypes should be interpreted as partial readouts of CD8+ T-cell function. Future studies using matched patient-derived co-culture systems and more comprehensive immune microenvironment models are needed to further define the effects of PD-L1+ exosomes on T-cell proliferation, CD4+ T-cell immunity, and inflammatory cytokine regulation. This finding echoes studies in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, where tumor-derived PD-L1+ EVs promoted CD8+ T-cell terminal exhaustion and anti-PD-1 resistance, with BATF implicated in this process [35]. Consistently, Chen et al. reported that PD‑L1+ tumor‑derived exosomes suppress antitumor immunity by inducing tumor‑specific CD8+ T‑cell exhaustion during metastasis [51]. Likewise, Cox et al. demonstrated that PD‑L1+ EVs induce CAR‑T cell exhaustion in chronic lymphocytic leukemia, an effect not fully reversed by PD‑L1 blocking antibodies [52]. Together, these findings suggest that PD-L1+ exosomes in MM may contribute to reduced responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade by promoting terminal T-cell exhaustion. However, membrane PD-1 expression intensity was not specifically quantified before and after PD-L1+ exosome exposure, and anti-PD-L1 blockade against PD-L1+ exosomes was not performed. Thus, the incomplete restoration by anti-PD-1 does not exclude the involvement of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. In addition, PD-L1 may not be the only functional mediator of exosome-induced T-cell dysfunction, as other exosomal cargos, including immunosuppressive proteins, enzymes, lipids, or regulatory miRNAs, may also contribute to impaired T-cell activation and exhaustion. Further studies using PD-L1-specific blocking strategies, comprehensive cargo profiling, and pathway-specific validation are warranted.

Previous studies demonstrated that inhibiting exosome secretion using the sphingomyelinase inhibitor GW4869 increased cortical bone volume and enhanced bortezomib sensitivity in MM models [36, 53]. Similarly, Zhong et al. reported that EVs carry PD‑L1, thereby impairing CAR‑T cell proliferation, migration, and function, whereas blocking EVs secretion restores CAR‑T cell infiltration and antitumor activity [54, 55]. In agreement with these observations, our results also showed that combining GW4869 with an anti‑PD‑1 antibody potentiated the antitumor activity of CAR‑T cells more effectively than that either treatment alone. However, the dosing schedules used in this study were designed for mechanistic proof-of-concept rather than direct clinical translation, and the anti-PD-1 antibody used here was a research-grade polyclonal antibody rather than an approved therapeutic monoclonal antibody. In addition, although GW4869 reduced CD63-positive vesicle release in vitro and exogenous exosome supplementation partially rescued tumor-cell viability, genetic validation of exosome inhibition and direct measurement of vesicle suppression in vivo were not performed; therefore, potential off-target effects of GW4869 cannot be excluded. Furthermore, CAR-T-cell immunophenotypes after GW4869 treatment were not systematically characterized, and the subcutaneous xenograft model did not fully recapitulate bone marrow- or blood-disseminated MM. Future studies using clinically relevant PD-1 blockade, optimized dosing schedules, genetic validation of exosome inhibition, and orthotopic or disseminated MM models are needed to better define the translational potential of this strategy. Collectively, these results suggest that GW4869 combined with anti-PD-1 may represent a potential strategy to enhance CAR-T efficacy in MM, although further validation is required.

This study has several limitations. First, the limited sample size, particularly in the CAR-T-treated subgroup, is a key limitation of this study and may have reduced the statistical power of subgroup analyses, multivariable modeling, and CRS-related analyses. In addition, the BM and PB PD-L1+ exosome cut-off values have not yet been validated in larger independent cohorts; therefore, these thresholds should be considered exploratory and may be subject to overfitting. Larger independent prospective cohorts are needed to validate the predictive value of PD-L1+ exosomes. Second, the molecular mechanisms through which PD‑L1+ exosomes suppress T cells have only been preliminarily explored here. Given the heterogeneity of exosomes, PD-L1+ exosomes may also reflect broader immunosuppressive exosomal cargo or overall tumor-derived exosome burden rather than a purely PD-L1-specific effect. Therefore, the specific contribution of exosomal PD-L1 requires further validation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that PD‑L1+ exosome levels in PB and BM of MM patients are significantly elevated and closely correlate with disease activity, poor prognosis, and poor response to CAR‑T therapy. PD‑L1+ exosomes inhibit CD8+ T cell activation and induce terminal exhaustion, and this effect cannot be fully reversed by anti‑PD‑1 antibody. The combination of GW4869 with anti‑PD‑1 antibody enhanced CAR-T-mediated antitumor activity in preclinical models. These findings suggest that PD‑L1+ exosomes may serve dual roles as both a prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target, and this combination warrants further investigation as a strategy to improve the depth and durability of CAR-T responses.
