Engaging youth in biodiversity education through visual narrative
Authors: Kyra Ricci, Kathleen Lu, Grascen Shidemantle, Jessica Hua
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEngaging youth in early and sustained conservation education has important implications for promoting positive attitudes and behaviors in those who will become the future of conservation and management. Toward this goal, visual narratives (comic books, graphic novels) are an increasingly popular method used by conservation scientists to educate young people due to their approachable use of art and narrative storytelling. However, no studies have directly assessed how visual narratives compare with more traditional forms of conservation education for youth. We asked, how does education about biodiversity through visual narrative affect student perceptions and knowledge of science content relative to a traditional resource, and is there a novelty effect when using visual narrative versus traditional resources? To assess our questions, we utilized a semistructured approach to develop a biodiversity education program. Specifically, we developed an original graphic novel (visual narrative treatment) and a slideshow presentation (traditional treatment) with the same content to educate children about wetland biodiversity. We recruited, trained, and randomized 26 third‐grade teachers to deliver either the visual narrative or traditional resource in their classrooms. Students completed pretest, posttest, and follow‐up surveys assessing their perceptions of science and knowledge of the lesson content. Students in the visual narrative treatment held more positive perceptions of science (by 3.79%, p = 0.001), whereas students in the traditional treatment performed better on content quizzes (by 7.97%, p = 0.002). We found evidence for a novelty bias when using the visual narrative but not the traditional resource. These findings point to the importance of understanding the target audience and clearly defining educational goals. Overall, our results contribute to broader understanding of the relative benefits and limitations of conservation education through nontraditional means and of practices for successfully delivering effective, accessible, and rewarding conservation education to educators and youth.

INTRODUCTION

Although most conservation and management decisions are made by adults, children become stakeholders, landowners, and conservation professionals and thus represent the future of conservation (Špur et al., 2020). One area that plays a critical role in child development of positive conservation attitudes and awareness is early education in environmental science and stewardship. For example, environmental education before the age of 11 can create a sustained sense of connectedness to nature (Liefländer et al., 2013), and children become less likely to become exploitative toward the environment when they engage in early and sustained education about nature (Liefländer & Bogner, 2018). Increasing access to educational opportunities for youth from backgrounds traditionally underrepresented in conservation has been identified as a key action to diversifying the field (Smith et al., 2017). Despite the clear need for promoting conservation education for youth from all backgrounds, opportunities for accessible, early, and sustained education in topics related to conservation biology for children and their educators remain limited.

A key barrier to covering conservation biology topics in elementary education is limited resources (e.g., time, support, expertise) available to educators to develop and implement curricula on current science topics (Chen & Xiao, 2021; Kaptan & Timurlenk, 2012). One opportunity to alleviate this barrier is for scientists to use their content expertise to aid in developing resources and training for teachers (Drayton & Falk, 2006; Kim & Fortner, 2007). Although scientists may be experts in content, they reciprocally do not have the educators’ expertise or training in how to best teach children. Thus, there is an opportunity for scientists and educators to combine their expertise to create scientifically accurate, age‐appropriate, and accessible resources that can be understood by children and—importantly—can be deployed by educators. In the citizen science literature, a field where community members and career scientists collaborate in the cocreation or cocollection of scientific data and knowledge, semistructured approaches have had success (Kelling et al., 2019; Paradinas et al., 2021). In citizen science contexts, a semistructured design is characterized by clear‐cut parameters set by researchers while allowing for flexibility for the community volunteer. For example, although researchers may establish the methodology of sampling used, instead of controlling for a specific time and location for observation (structured design), a semistructured design would allow for community collaborators to participate at a time and location that is accessible for them. This added variation is then collected as data and integrated into the analysis, balancing increased accessibility with rigorous study design (Kelling et al., 2019). In the context of conservation biology education, a semistructured approach that leverages the expertise of both scientists (i.e., scientific content) and educators (i.e., education best practices for dissemination timing and approach) may be especially effective in improving the accessibility of conservation topics to both teachers and their students. For example, using this approach, research team members can clearly define the parameters of the factual information (i.e., developing educational resources, training teachers in content) while allowing for flexibility in teaching style and implementation timing in an effort to increase accessibility and leverage the valuable knowledge of teachers.

One educational resource that is gaining increased attention, especially for youth education, is visual narrative (Johanes, 2018; Mota et al., 2021; Tatalovic, 2009). Visual narratives (e.g., graphic novels, comic books) are hypothesized to contribute to student learning, especially of abstract concepts such as those in science, technology, engineering, and math fields, primarily due to their unique combination of engaging visual art and narrative storytelling (Adnan et al., 2019; Johanes, 2018). Visual arts, especially those representing topics related to environmental and ecological sciences, are increasingly understood to promote emotional engagement for broad audiences (Lesen et al., 2016), highlighting the potential for visual arts to promote deep affective engagement in an educational setting. Furthermore, when used to support text, the processing of visual artistic information can effectively promote a reading experience that highlights the most important information to facilitate deeper understanding for students (Mayer & Gallini, 1990). Frameworks of narrative processing and reading comprehension suggest that narrative structures can help young readers construct mental representations of text to help infer meaning (Kraal et al., 2018; van den Broek, 1994). For example, previous work has shown that narrative texts promote more accurate inferences than expository texts for both high‐ and low‐comprehending readers of ages 7–8 years (Kraal et al., 2018). Thus, combining the powerful effects of compelling visual arts and narrative storytelling can promote an experience that combines the best aspects of both by promoting affective engagement, clarifying abstract concepts, highlighting important information, and aiding in the construction of conceptual representations of a text's meaning.

Students find visual narratives engaging and enjoyable as an educational resource (Adnan et al., 2019; Aleixo & Norris, 2010; Farinella, 2018). Additionally, visual narratives are viewed as accessible supporting resources for students who require additional literacy assistance (Crawford, 2004; Frey & Fisher, 2008; Schwarz, 2006), which can help to broaden the number and type of students with varied learning styles who are able to engage with the content. However, the increased engagement and enjoyment of visual narratives do not necessarily translate to improved knowledge gains or achievement of learning outcomes and are believed to be biased by a novelty effect, whereby the novelty of the resource alone (i.e., novelty bias) is the source of increased enjoyment (Caldwell, 2012; Farinella, 2018; Hosler & Boomer, 2011; Tatalovic, 2009). Although patterns are beginning to emerge from these previous investigations, our understanding of the relative benefits and limitations of the use of visual narrative in childhood science education is limited by the lack of controlled, empirical studies (Farinella, 2018) (but see Hosler & Boomer [2011] and Spiegel et al. [2013] for controlled studies with college and teenage students, respectively). Thus, to clearly understand the effects of visual narrative on student perceptions of science, knowledge of content, and the potential of a novelty bias, there remains a need for randomized controlled studies assessing the relative benefits and limitations of visual narratives in youth conservation education when directly compared with more traditional methods of teaching about the same topics.

We addressed these gaps by utilizing a semistructured approach and asking the following questions in the context of elementary biodiversity education. First, does conservation education through visual narrative improve student perceptions of science more than education through traditional, nonvisual narrative means (Q1)? Second, does conservation education through visual narrative improve student knowledge of lesson content more than education through traditional means (Q2)? Finally, is the use of visual narrative in conservation education biased by a novelty effect (Q3)? Consistent with the current understanding of the use of comic books (a type of visual narrative similar to graphic novel) in science education as reviewed by Farinella (2018), we hypothesized that student perceptions of science are affected by treatment and time, such that students who receive a visual narrative lesson improve more in their positive perceptions than students who receive a traditional lesson (H1). We also hypothesized that student knowledge of lesson content is affected by time only, such that all students improve in their knowledge of content equally regardless of lesson type (H2). Finally, due to the novel visual and storytelling elements of the visual narrative resource, we hypothesized that the visual narrative treatment but not the traditional treatment is biased by a novelty effect, such that as students experience less novelty, overall outcomes for students (perceptions and knowledge) are also less positive (H3) (Hosler & Boomer, 2011; Tatalovic, 2009).

METHODS

Resource development

To develop our conservation biology educational resources, we focused on the topic of amphibian biodiversity. We chose amphibian biodiversity for 3 reasons. First, biodiversity and biodiversity loss are key topics in conservation and sustainability (Rockström et al., 2009; United Nations, 2015; White et al., 2023), and it has been specifically suggested that focusing on a small number of species (e.g., amphibians) can positively affect biodiversity learning in primary schools (Randler et al., 2005). Second, children's books about animal biodiversity have been found to be biased toward mammals and away from native species, leading to a call for broadening the portrayal of native, nonmammal species in order to help children broaden their understanding of biodiversity and the animal kingdom (Hooykaas et al., 2022). Finally, the topic aligned with our lab's expertise in amphibian disease ecology and ecotoxicology. Thus, we focused our resource development on educating third‐grade students (ages 8–9) about biodiversity in the wetlands of Broome County, New York (USA), with a specific focus on amphibian biodiversity.

To develop a visual narrative resource, we recruited a team of 5 undergraduate students to write and illustrate an original, educational graphic novel (Amphibian Adventurers: Beyond the Vernal Pool) (Appendix S1). For a comparative traditional resource, we developed an educational slideshow presentation containing the same factual information in the first 3 chapters of the graphic novel (e.g., names, photos, and habitats of local species and their adaptations). Although the traditional resource did contain some visuals (i.e., photographs), they were not artistic representations and did not support a narrative (Appendix S2).

Next, we developed 2 versions of a lesson plan to educate students about biodiversity and disease ecology: one version with the first 3 chapters of the visual narrative as a supporting resource and the other with the traditional slideshow as a supporting resource. The purpose of the lesson plan was to provide clear guidelines for teachers in how to implement the lessons in the classroom alongside the standardized resources (i.e., either the visual narrative or provided slideshow). The information and content covered in both lesson plans were the same, with minor variations in presentation style to account for the variation in the resources (Table 1; full lesson plans in Appendix S3). This lesson plan was developed in collaboration with education professionals at the Board of Cooperative Educational Services of New York (BOCES) to provide content that met New York State Science Learning Standards for third grade and to be presented in a format that was familiar and accessible to teachers.

Teacher recruitment and training

Third‐grade classrooms were recruited via advertisements to local teachers through the Roberson Museum and Science Center Educational Advisory Board, an announcement at the monthly meeting of local superintendents, and a professional development workshop opportunity posted on the BOCES website. Following teacher registration, a recruitment letter was sent home with each student in a participating classroom accompanied by parent informed consent and child assent forms. This protocol was approved by the Binghamton University (protocol 00003316) and University of Wisconsin–Madison (protocol 2022‐1400) Institutional Review Boards.

Twenty‐six classrooms of third‐grade students (ages 8–9 years) were recruited from 10 schools across 7 local districts from Broome County, New York, and Susquehanna County, Pennsylvania (USA). Due to logistical constraints of sample data being collected directly from minors, individual child demographic data were not collected. However, aggregated data on school‐wide statistics from the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES, 2023) indicated that schools in our study represented a variety of socioeconomic and geographic classifications. Schools in our study were classified as rural (n = 5), suburban (n = 4), and city (n = 1), and the percentage of students who qualified for free or reduced lunch across schools ranged from 26% to 80% in the 2021–2022 school year (NCES, 2023).

On 23 and 24 February 2022, recruited teachers attended one of 2 educational workshops where the research team explained the purpose and process of the research, provided instructional materials, and gave a teaching demonstration of both versions of the lesson plan. After the demonstrations, teachers were randomized to teach either the visual narrative (12 teachers) or traditional PowerPoint (14 teachers) lesson with one of the 2 standardized resources developed by the research team. Teacher randomization was uneven between the 2 treatments to account for uneven class sizes between each teacher. Thus, we prioritized even student distribution between the 2 treatments as opposed to even teacher distribution. During the workshops, teachers were split into groups based on their assigned treatment and given time to discuss questions and comments about teaching the lesson among each other and with the research team. Specifically, teachers were instructed to discuss their plans for integrating the lesson into their classrooms by blending the researcher teaching demonstration with their own teaching style and available classroom resources. For example, some teachers discussed plans to teach the lesson utilizing an additional technology, a collaborative virtual whiteboard (Google Jamboard), that was equipped in their classroom and typically integrated into their lessons. Additionally, teachers discussed a few suggestions for improving the resources. For example, several teachers suggested enlarging accompanying student resources (e.g., an animal information sheet for students to write in during the lesson [Appendix S4]) to accommodate the large space practically needed for students to write at this age. Teacher suggestions from the workshop were integrated into the final versions, and the lesson plans and associated resources were provided to all teachers via email the following week.

Teacher implementation

Between February 2022 and May 2022, teachers were instructed to teach their assigned lesson to their students. As part of the semistructured approach, a specific date was not required in order to accommodate the varied educational curricula of the different classrooms. The lesson was designed to be 40 min in duration. As such, some teachers split the lesson into multiple sessions (e.g., two 20‐min sessions or four 10‐min sessions), whereas others completed the entire lesson in one sitting based on the individual needs of their classrooms and the time available to them for science instruction. Thus, although all students in the study received the same lesson, some classrooms completed their lesson in a single day, whereas others completed the lesson across several days (Table 2).

Assessment

To assess perceptions of science, students completed a survey composed of items adapted from previously validated assessments to examine a constellation of factors comprising the construct perceptions of science, including attitudes, engagement, and trust (Nadelson et al., 2014; Pell & Jarvis, 2001; Silver & Rushton, 2008; Wang et al., 2016). Items were combined to develop a new, single scale. This single scale was designed to be interpreted as a whole to better understand student holistic perceptions of science as opposed to examining each individual construct (e.g., attitudes toward science) separately. Items included statements such as “I would like to be a scientist,” and responses were recorded on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from “strongly agree or like it a lot” to “strongly disagree or dislike,” accompanied by a smiley face that reflected the level of the scale (Table 3). The overall reliability of this 16‐item perceptions scale was α = 0.728 (α > 0.7 considered acceptable [Tavakol & Dennick, 2011]). To assess student knowledge, we developed a brief, age‐appropriate quiz based on the content of the educational resources and the lessons (Table 3).

Students were assessed immediately before their teacher began the lesson (pretest), immediately after their teacher completed the lesson (posttest), and again at a third point after the conclusion of the lesson at the discretion of the teacher to prioritize flexibility for the differing needs of each class (follow‐up). Both the perception survey and content quiz were delivered digitally via Qualtrics. To track students over time while accounting for the variation in lesson length between the classrooms, surveys at each point were anonymously connected to each student with a unique classroom and student identification number.

Statistical analyses

Overall, 480 third‐grade students were included in our educational program. Students who completed surveys at times and dates that were inconsistent with the rest of their class (n = 4), those who had high missingness across all 3 surveys (pretest, posttest, follow‐up) (>90%, n = 5), and one small class that had overall inconsistent dates of survey completion (n = 6; traditional treatment classroom 25) were excluded from analysis.

In our population of children, which likely included individuals that responded inattentively or insincerely, we expected to encounter multivariate outliers in this data set. Thus, it was important to identify and remove multivariate outliers prior to analysis; however, identification of multivariate outliers via Malanahobis distance requires complete cases with no missing data. Our data set included missing data, primarily attributed to students missing one or more waves of data collection (e.g., a student was absent from class during an assessment day). This missingness pattern of intermittent dropout or completely random dropout is considered a special case of missingness in which, although there is a pattern of missingness and the mechanism of missingness is known, the data can be treated as missing completely at random (MCAR; Diggle & Kenward, 1994; Hedeker & Gibbons, 1997). Thus, to identify multivariate outliers, missing outcome values (perceptions of science and knowledge of lesson content) were derived using mean imputation for each treatment group. Overall patterns of responding on both the perception survey and knowledge quiz were then tested to determine whether they were statistically aberrant compared with others using Malanahobis distance, and these multivariate outliers were removed from subsequent analysis (n = 118). Although we acknowledge that mean imputation is considered inferior to other more rigorous methods, such as multiple imputation (MI) and full information maximum likelihood (FIML), we tested all models on both the imputed and nonimputed data sets to ensure that mean imputation did not bias our models. All main effects and interactions were the same for both the raw and imputed data sets, and we took this to indicate that mean imputation did not bias the effects in our models. We thus present results for only the imputed data set to improve clarity in the manuscript.

Of the 25 classrooms that completed both pretest and posttest assessments, nine classrooms did not complete the follow‐up assessment. To present a more conservative model, only students in classrooms that completed all 3 surveys were included in the final analyses (n = 177; 95 traditional, 82 visual narrative).

To understand how perceptions of science and knowledge of lesson content differed among treatment groups at each assessment point, we used multilevel mixed regression models. Multilevel models allow for examination of multilevel data or nested data structures (e.g., students within teachers). Within longitudinal studies, a repeated measurement is also viewed as a multilevel data structure (e.g., multiple observations within one student) (Peugh, 2010). In this case, the multilevel structure of our data was observations at each time point (level 1) within each individual student (level 2) within each teacher's classroom (level 3).

Mixed‐effect models also allow for both fixed effects (i.e., parameters that are directly manipulated or repeatable levels of factors) and random effects (i.e., parameters that vary randomly but affect individual variance). In this case, we directly manipulated treatment (visual narrative versus traditional [fixed effect]) and had a repeated factor of time because we assessed each student over time at 3 points (repeated fixed effect). Students were nested within teachers, a factor that was intentionally not manipulated by the study design but had the potential to influence individual student variance (random effect). Specifically, the number of days between each measurement point (pretest, posttest, and follow‐up) varied according to each individual teacher and classroom needs. Thus, the number of days between pretest and posttest measurements and the number of days between posttest and follow‐up measurements were used as a proxy for teacher variation. These 2 duration variables were correlated (r = 0.256, p < 0.001) and reduced to a single proxy variable representing teacher‐level variance.

We ran separate multilevel mixed regression models for each outcome variable (i.e., perceptions and knowledge). We compared each outcome by treatment group (fixed effect) and time (level 1 repeated fixed effect) when accounting teacher variation via proxy factor (level 3 random effect). We used restricted maximum likelihood (REML) in all models (see Appendix S5). For all significant main effects, we conducted Bonferroni‐corrected pairwise comparisons.

Because we allowed teachers to decide how to deliver the lesson based on their classroom needs, the number of days between pretest and posttest ranged from zero (same day) to 15 days depending on the classroom (Table 1). Thus, the number of days between pretest and posttest reflected the number of days between students first encountering the lesson and completing the lesson, allowing us to test for an effect of novelty between the 2 treatments. For example, students taking the posttest after experiencing a single 40‐min session experienced higher novelty of the material compared with students taking the posttest after experiencing four 10‐min sessions across several weeks.

To test for an effect of novelty on student perceptions of science and knowledge of content, we conducted bivariate correlations between our outcome variables (perceptions and knowledge at posttest and follow‐up) and the number of days between pretest and posttest. We conducted separate analyses at each time point (i.e., posttest and follow‐up separately) and found the same statistically significant trend for each time point. Thus, we present the combined data for both time points together to improve clarity when interpreting outcomes overall. All analyses were conducted in SPSS 28 (IBM).

RESULTS

Perceptions of science

We found a significant main effect of treatment (F 200.64 = 11.24, p = 0.001) but no main effects of time (F 344.60 = 1.39, p = 0.251) or treatment × time (F 344.60 = 0.26, p = 0.768) on student perceptions of science (Figure 1). We did not detect a significant effect of variance associated with teacher (Wald's z = 1.541, p = 0.123).

Student (n = 177) perceptions of science scores calculated as the sum of 16 Likert scale items on a 5‐point scale (including metrics on attitudes, engagement, and trust) before treatment, after treatment, and at follow‐up (80, most positive score; 16, least positive score; error bars, standard errors; traditional treatment, lesson with PowerPoint slideshow; visual narrative, lesson with graphic novel).

Pairwise comparisons indicated that students in the visual narrative treatment group held more positive perceptions of science than students in the traditional group overall, differing by 3.79% (p = 0.001). The treatment groups did not significantly differ in their perceptions of science at pretest measurements (p = 0.091), indicating that the overall differences between the treatment groups were not driven by baseline differences.

Knowledge of lesson content

We found significant main effects of time (F 348.94 = 102.73, p < 0.001) and treatment (F 211.60 = 9.51, p = 0.002) but no significant main effect of treatment × time (F 348.94 = 1.58, p = 0.208) on student knowledge of lesson content (Figure 2). We did not detect a significant effect of variance associated with teacher (Wald's z = 1.512, p = 0.128).

Student (n = 177) quiz scores calculated as percent correct for a 5‐item quiz regarding lesson content before treatment, after treatment, and at follow‐up (error bars, standard errors; traditional treatment, lesson with PowerPoint slideshow; visual narrative, lesson with graphic novel).

Pairwise comparisons for knowledge over time indicated that quiz scores overall significantly increased by 42.8% between pretest and posttest (p < 0.001), indicating that the lessons improved student knowledge of the lesson content overall. There was no change in quiz scores between posttest and follow‐up measurements (p = 1.00), indicating students overall retained the knowledge gained in the lesson when assessed at follow‐up.

Students in the traditional treatment group performed better on the knowledge assessment than students in the visual narrative group overall, differing by 7.97% (p = 0.002). Pairwise comparisons again indicated that the treatment groups did not significantly differ at pretest measurements (p = 0.336), indicating that the overall differences between the treatment groups were not driven by baseline differences.

Novelty effect

Bivariate correlations indicated that the greater the number of days between pretest and posttest measurements, the less positive the student perception of science scores overall at posttest and follow‐up (r = −0.165, p < 0.001). Specifically, this effect was present for students in the visual narrative group (r = −0.326, p < 0.001) but not for students in the traditional group (r = 0.013, p = 0.812) (Figure 3). Similarly, we found that the greater the number of days between pretest and posttest measurements, the lower the quiz scores assessing knowledge of lesson content overall (r = −0.093, p = 0.015). Once again, this effect was present for students in the visual narrative group (r = −0.193, p < 0.001) but not for students in the traditional group (r = 0.004, p = 0.936) (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

Factors that influence perceptions of science

When examining student perceptions of science in visual narrative versus traditional treatment groups, we found that students in the visual narrative treatment held more positive perceptions of science overall than students in the traditional treatment. This finding is consistent with previous work that shows visual narratives can improve student appreciation for topics covered in an educational lesson more than their peers who receive no treatment, indicating that visual narratives do have the potential to improve student perceptions of science topics (Richter et al., 2015; Topkaya, 2016). Additionally, science visual narratives engage children in the age range of 7–10 years (Weitkamp & Burnet, 2007). One strength of our study relative to previous work is the controlled design. This direct comparison strengthens the evidence from previous work that there is a significant benefit to using visual narrative over traditional methods in terms of developing positive perceptions of science in children of ages 8–9. Although there was a significant positive effect in the visual narrative treatment, the effect size was relatively small. This may be due to the fact that the students in our study held relatively positive starting perceptions of science at pretest (average pretest perception score = 62.68; range of measure = 16–80). Indeed, the detected differences between the treatment groups may be considered especially interesting given the relatively small window of opportunity for change. Future studies should consider the implications of this small yet significant effect of perceptions on related outcomes (e.g., future interest in science or science careers). Taken together, these findings strengthen the case for using visual narrative in early conservation education to reinforce positive perceptions of science for youth.

Although we found an overall difference in perceptions of science between the 2 treatment groups, we did not see a significant change in perceptions over time in either treatment group. One explanation for this finding could be the specific metrics of perceptions assessed by our instrument. We aimed to capture more trait‐level (i.e., associated with personality and more stable over time) perceptions as opposed to state‐level (i.e., associated with current feeling and behavior) perceptions with our instrument. Specifically, the items from our instrument were sourced from questionnaires intended to measure more general levels of attitudes, engagement, and trust in science as opposed to present emotional and behavioral states (Nadelson et al., 2014; Pell & Jarvis, 2001; Silver & Rushton, 2008; Wang et al., 2016). We aimed to measure these trait‐level perceptions because we were primarily interested in the potential long‐term implications of the lessons on stable traits. However, other conservation education interventions with children show that situational perceptions and emotions have been correlated with outcome metrics such as environmentally friendly behavior, knowledge, and inclusion of nature in self (Fröhlich et al., 2013), indicating that these state‐level perceptions may play an important role. Thus, future iterations of similar conservation interventions should assess both trait‐level perceptions of science as well as direct, transient perceptions (e.g., direct behavioral assessment during the lesson, levels of current enjoyment and emotion).

The short‐term nature of this intervention (i.e., a 40‐min lesson) may not have had enough of an impact to instill trait‐level change in students in either treatment group. Future programs may consider examining long‐term visual narrative intervention (e.g., integration into multiple lessons throughout the school year) to assess whether longer programs can affect trait‐level perception change. However, long‐term interventions may pose significant feasibility challenges due to factors such as lack of science visual narrative resources and associated educational materials for the variety of topics that teachers need to cover (Schwarz, 2006) and the significant time commitment required for new, larger scale programs by already overworked elementary teachers and administrators (Alson, 2019). Thus, although an interesting future target for understanding how and if student trait‐level perceptions change over time, the relative inaccessibility of longer term interventions to educators may also potentially be counterproductive to important goals of broadening access and reach.

An additional feature of our survey instrument was that it primarily measured perceptions of science generally as opposed to perceptions of conservation biology specifically (though 2 of our 16 items did specifically address environmental science). The general population tends to hold relatively positive perceptions of science (National Science Foundation, 2022) and, indeed, the students in our study also held relatively positive perceptions of science at pretest. The finding that they did not become more positive after the lesson is in line with other studies where students have high pretest scores (Leeming et al., 1993). Additionally, attitudes toward science can differ significantly between domains of inquiry in adults (Allum et al., 2008); thus, it is possible that student perceptions of conservation science specifically differed from their assessed perceptions of science generally. However, one study of conservation education found that children also hold relatively positive attitudes toward conservation (specifically, sea turtle conservation) at pretest, and again, student attitudes do not shift after educational intervention (Dimopoulos et al., 2008). Practitioners in this area of conservation education should thus carefully consider the goals of educational interventions and target key populations accordingly because individuals with relatively positive pretest perceptions of science and conservation topics may have reached a ceiling of how positive their perceptions can be. Future efforts to improve perceptions of science and access to science topics may be better suited for populations who do not initially hold these positive perceptions. For example, elementary school students tend to have higher interest in science than vocational high and junior high school students (Hong & Lin, 2011); thus, outcomes in these populations may have more opportunity for change.

Factors that influence knowledge acquisition

When examining knowledge of lesson content, students in the traditional treatment performed better than students in the visual narrative treatment on quizzes overall, and these differences were not due to baseline differences between the groups. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have examined nontraditional resources when communicating about conservation biology topics in other age groups (Ricci et al., 2023). Although we initially hypothesized that content acquisition would be the same between the treatment groups, one potential explanation for our finding that students in the traditional treatment performed better on the knowledge assessment could be that the traditional lesson plan minimized distractions from the core educational topics, thus leading to more clarity in which topics and knowledge students should extract and retain for assessment. In contrast, the graphic novel contained not only the core facts intended to be learned, but also visual and narrative (e.g., story, characters, dialogue) elements. Although these elements may have been understood by the students, the scientific concepts contained within them may not have provided sufficient clarity or focus (Jee & Anggoro, 2012), and the visuals may have been overly distracting (Khoii & Forouzesh, 2010). Thus, our findings suggest that knowledge acquisition goals were more effectively met using a traditional resource over a visual narrative resource, highlighting that there may be a trade‐off between achieving goals of increased engagement and goals of increased content understanding when utilizing visual narrative as an educational resource. However, students in the visual narrative treatment also gained and retained relevant content knowledge from the lesson. Overall, these findings support the idea that scientists and science educators should clearly define their desired student outcomes before designing educational programs in order to develop resources that maximize effectiveness to meet their specific goals.

Novelty effect of visual narratives

The number of days between pretest (before lesson) and posttest (after lesson) measurements affected students in the visual narrative treatment but not in the traditional treatment. Specifically, when the duration of time between measurements was shorter, students in the visual narrative treatment held more positive perceptions of science than their peers in the traditional treatment, and perceptions declined for the visual narrative students as the number of days between measurements increased. A similar trend was found for student quiz scores, although students in the visual narrative group scored consistently lower than their peers in the traditional group even when experiencing shorter time between measurements. These findings indicate a potential effect of a novelty bias in the visual narrative treatment but not the traditional treatment, a concern that has been raised but not directly assessed for the use of visual narrative in education (Farinella, 2018; Hosler & Boomer, 2011). Indeed, with the lack of long‐term studies assessing the potential influence of novelty, some have raised the concern that the beneficial results seen from the use of visual narrative may have nothing to do with the medium at all (Tatalovic, 2009). Although our findings support a novelty bias, it is not necessarily detrimental to the case for visual narratives in education. Indeed, moderately novel stimuli have been shown to facilitate learning and curiosity (Boeve‐de Pauw et al., 2019), indicating that novelty can promote positive learning and affective experiences. Additionally, although we interpret our finding as evidence for a novelty bias, we did not directly assess teachers’ or students’ previous experience with visual narratives either for personal use or in the classroom. Future work in this area should consider directly assessing the level of novelty that students and their teachers experience when using visual narratives as an educational resource in the classroom (e.g., by assessing their background exposure to the medium) to better understand the influence of the novelty effect on student perceptions and knowledge.

Overall, we found that youth biodiversity education through visual narrative led to more positive perceptions of science than education through a traditional PowerPoint, whereas the traditional lesson was more effective for student acquisition of content knowledge. We also found evidence for a novelty bias in the use of visual narratives for youth education. Together, these findings point to the importance of understanding the target audience and clearly identifying educational objectives (e.g., to engage, to inform, or some combination of both). In the case of visual narrative in youth conservation education, there does not appear to be one approach that is best suited for all goals. Additionally, despite the differences in teaching style that arose due to the semistructured design, we saw clear differences within the treatment groups in both perceptions of science and knowledge of content, suggesting that these outcomes are robust to variation in teaching style. This has implications for accessibility and implementation because teachers can maintain their personal teaching style, whereas students can achieve similar positive outcomes while utilizing the same resources. It is also interesting to note that by making resources and training more accessible to teachers (as opposed to researchers teaching the students directly), it is likely that not only students in our study but also future students of these teachers will benefit from the collaboration. Future work should consider the potential interplay among teacher, treatment, and timing to better understand whether and how additional latent factors (e.g., importance of science instruction to the teacher or their administration) may be influencing teacher choice in lesson timing or teacher enthusiasm.

In conclusion, we assessed the relative benefits and limitations of conservation education through nontraditional means while leveraging the expertise of both researchers and educators. We demonstrated that by doing so, one can further understand when and how to use these resources effectively toward the goal of implementing accessible and rewarding conservation education for youth and their educators.
