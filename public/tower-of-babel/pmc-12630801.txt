BIGH3 is a mediator of TGFβ-Induced collagen formation in fibrosis and pancreatic cancer and a potential therapeutic target
Authors: Nicholas Willumsen, Kim Henriksen, Cecilie Bager, Anette T. Hansen Selnø, Khaled E. Mohamed, Diana J. Leeming, Mark Alexander Skarsfeldt, Neel I. Nissen, Rasmus S. Pedersen, Jeppe Thorlacius-Ussing, Morten H. Dziegiel, Man-Hung Eric Tang, Lars N. Jørgensen, Hadi M. H. Diab, Inna M. Chen, Julia S. Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fibroblast-driven deposition of extracellular matrix, particularly type III collagen, is linked to poor outcomes in patients with liver fibrosis, fibrotic solid tumors such as hepatocellular carcinoma and pancreatic cancer as well as in patients with other fibrotic diseases. The pro-peptide of type III collagen (PRO-C3) serves as a serum biomarker of active collagen formation. PRO-C3 is elevated and prognostic in patients with liver fibrosis and pancreatic cancer, suggesting that targeting mechanisms behind PRO-C3 elevation could benefit these individuals. In this study, we sought to identify genetic variants associated with circulating PRO-C3 levels as potential therapeutic targets for fibrotic diseases and cancer. A genome-wide association study (GWAS) of the PERF cohort (n = 4968) identified variants in the gene TGFBI (BIGH3/βigH3), a known risk factor for tumor fibrosis, as associated with elevated PRO-C3. Further analyses of serum samples from patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and liver fibrosis confirmed significant correlations between PRO-C3 and BIGH3 levels. Functional studies in a fibroblast model demonstrated that BIGH3 induces PRO-C3 in a dose-dependent manner, which was blocked by an anti-BIGH3 antibody, indicating a causal role. BIGH3 expression was observed in macrophages and fibroblasts, especially under TGFβ stimulation, positioning BIGH3 as a downstream mediator of TGFβ-induced fibrosis. Finally, blocking BIGH3 in a TGFβ-induced fibrosis model led to reduced PRO-C3 levels, demonstrating that BIGH3 is local mediator of pathological TGFβ signaling. This highlights that BIGH3 may be implicated in TGFβ-induced fibrosis and underscore the potential for treatment of pathological fibrotic processes by inhibiting BIGH3 in patients with elevated PRO-C3 levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24463-0.

Introduction

Fibroblast activities are central to fibrosis (desmoplasia) and are a significant complication of many types of cancer1,2. This desmoplastic reaction appears to be an exacerbation of a tissue injury that contributes to the formation of a ‘fibrous cap’ surrounding and intertwining the tumor and contributing to an overall worsening of the disease. Fibroblast activities are also central to fibrosis of the liver, lung, kidney, gut and heart, and have been shown to be prognostic for liver related outcomes as well as stage and outcomes of hepatocellular carcinoma3–5.

At the molecular level, the desmoplastic reaction is characterized by deposition of a large amount of extracellular matrix (ECM) that forms a dense, collagen-rich fibrotic stroma. It is thought to be caused by an activation and differentiation of fibroblasts into cancer associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which then leads to accelerated collagen and ECM deposition6,7. Transforming growth factor receptor beta (TGFβ) is considered the master regulator of fibrogenesis, but other cytokines can impact the desmoplastic reaction as well8,9.

The generation of a fibrotic tumor microenvironment is also linked to resistance to many types of therapies including anti-cancer immunotherapy10–12. Hence there is a medical need to search for ways to identify cancer patients with a fibrotic phenotype and find novel therapeutic opportunities for this specific type of tumor. Quantifying the synthesis of type III collagen through measurements of the N-terminal pro-peptide (PRO-C3) has been shown to reflect tissue fibrosis across several fibrotic diseases13. PRO-C3 has been shown to be highly expressed in vitro upon stimulation of quiescent fibroblast with TGFβ and intrinsically by CAFs14. Thus, PRO-C3 is a measure of fibrotic activity. PRO-C3 has also been extensively studied in patients with solid tumors with significant associations to poor prognosis across indications and modalities 4,15–26. Additionally PRO-C3 levels are prognostic for progression in other fibrotic diseases, such as hepatic fibrosis26,27, lung fibrosis28, and kidney fibrosis29, and as such there is substantial interest in determining pathways leading to increased circulating PRO-C3 as they have therapeutic value.

It is well-known that TGFβ induces a pro-fibrotic response, however targeting of TGFβ in cancer and fibrosis is complex and goes beyond just an anti-fibrotic effect, as TGFβ is a highly pleiotrotic molecule30. Thus, while many novel therapies are emerging in oncology to target the TGFβ pathway31, the search for genotypes and molecular endotypes of cancer patients with active tumor fibrosis may be beneficial and guide alternative approaches to treat these cancer patients. Interestingly, Transforming Growth Factor-Beta-Induced protein (TGFBI, also known as BIGH3) is a well-known TGFβ-responsive gene initially identified in a human lung adenocarcinoma cell line A54932, and multiple in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that BIGH3 is a protein involved in pathological conditions associated with fibrosis, desmoplasia, and tumorigenesis in pancreatic cancer33–40.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) are an effective method to search for genotypes and that are exhibited in various diseases through detection of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of multiple individual genes based on linkage disequilibrium. GWAS in cancers are numerous41–44. To date, GWAS of most common cancer types have been performed and many SNPs have been identified that are associated with increased risks of cancer42. In many, if not all, cases the GWAS are linked to a clinical parameter and presence of disease, hereby providing important information on how to identify certain (sub)types of cancer and revealing novel pathways (and hence potential new drug targets) associated with the presence of cancer. However, what these studies do not investigate is the disease activity of a given process in relation to underlying progression of disease. There is a medical need to identify the processes associated with disease activity, fibrosis activity. Linkage studies connecting individual SNPs to disease activity measures, such as fibrotic activity (PRO-C3), may be one approach to identify causal mechanisms and thereby provide insight into novel treatment regimens for cancer patients and other patients with strong fibrotic processes.

In the present study, we explored the association between SNPs and PRO-C3 using the Prospective Epidemiological Risk Factor (PERF) study to delineate a potential new target for the treatment of tumor fibrosis. Following the genetic linkage, we used a combination of patients with advanced pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and liver fibrosis as well as in vitro cell models to determine to what extent our identified target had a causal effect on PRO-C3 (fibrosis). This approach could provide a biomarker-driven novel drug target to treat patients with pancreatic cancer and active fibrotic disease, identified by high serological levels of PRO-C3 and consequent a worse outcome.

Materials and methods

The PERF cohort

The Prospective Epidemiological Risk Factor study (PERF) study was a follow-up study of Danish post-menopausal women (n = 4968) aiming at identifying risk factors associated with age-related diseases44. The study participant inclusion process was performed in the same ways as described by Tang M-HE et al.,45. Baseline characteristics of subjects included in the PERF study are shown in Table S1. The study was carried out in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization – Guideline for Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) and the study protocol was approved by the local ethics committees. Serum and DNA samples were collected from subjects who gave written consent for this specific analysis.

The BIOPAC cohort and healthy controls

Serum samples from patients with advanced PDAC (n = 171) were from the Danish “Biomarkers in patients with pancreatic cancer (BIOPAC) – can they provide new information of the disease and improve diagnosis and prognosis of the patients” study (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT03311776). The study has been described elsewhere46,47. The BIOPAC protocol was approved by the Danish Regional Committee on Health Research Ethics (VEK ref. KA-20060113; and the retrospective protocol VEK H-17039022) and the Data Protection Agency (j.nr. 2006-41-6848, 2012-58-0004, HGH-2015-027; I-Suite j. nr. 03960; and PACTIUS P-2020-834). The study was carried out in accordance with the Danish Regional Committee on Health Research Ethics. All subjects gave written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, version 8. Serum samples were collected before operation or before starting palliative chemotherapy. Clinical data were collected prospectively. The patients were followed until January 2023 or death, whichever came first. Baseline characteristics of the patients included in the BIOPAC study are shown in Table S2. Healthy controls (n = 20) for comparing biomarker levels to BIOPAC were obtained from BioIVT (Westbury, NY, USA). According to the vendor, sample collection was approved by an Institutional Review Board or Independent Ethical Committee and patients gave their informed consent (WIRB® Protocol #20161665). This cohort included 10 female and 10 men with a median age of 58 years of age (range 45–72 years of age).

Subjects with liver disease

100 serum samples from age and gender matched subjects were purchased from Discovery Life Sciences (Huntsville, Ala, USA): healthy controls (n = 25), patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD, n = 25), patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH, n = 25) and patients with liver cirrhosis (n = 25).

Genotyping (PERF)

Genotyping was performed using a custom-made Illumina Global Screening Array (693143 probes) in collaboration with deCODE Genetics, Iceland. SNP imputation was carried out using the Michigan Imputation Server48, using a HRC r1.1.2016, EUR reference panel. Phasing was performed with ShapeIt2 and the imputation with Minimac3. Positions are reported as in the GRCh37 reference Standard probe-level filtering has been performed using a minimum probe call rate of 97%, a minor allele frequency greater or equal to 1% and a Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium p-value cut-off greater or equal to 1e-6. No filtering on multi-allelic SNPs has been performed. To address possible cryptic relatedness between subjects, we calculated an identity-by-descent (IBD) coefficient using Plink genome function49. Inbreeding coefficient (IBC) was calculated using the plink-ibc function. We removed subjects, on a one side of a pair basis, using a minimum PI_HAT cut-off value of 0.1875 and a cut-off of less than − 0.1 or greater than 0.1 was applied to the Fhat2 coefficient.

Assessment of soluble biomarkers

BIGH3 (Abcam, cat# ab220651) and PRO-C3 (Nordic Bioscience, cat# 1700AF06) were measured blinded in serum by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Similarly, BIGH3 was measured in cell culture supernatants in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (R&D Systems, Catalog #: DY2935).

Single-cell RNA-seq data

The single-cell RNA-Seq data that can be found in the Genome Sequence Archive (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/) under the accession CRA001160. As outlined previously, samples were collected from 24 patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) prior to treatment at the time of surgery and from 11 non-PDAC patients, 3 of which were patients with bile duct or duodenal tumors and 8 of which were patients with benign pancreatic tumors e.g. pancreatic cysts and which included 41,986 cells from PDAC and 15,544 cells from non-PDAC samples50. We only included cells annotated with a cell type and excluded cells with under 200 genes per cell, genes with under 3 cells per gene and cells with over 10% mitochondrial genes. We used the approach previously delineated by Thorlacius-Ussing et al., for the further analysis to characterize cell types expression either or both of COL3A1 and BIGH351.

Fibroblasts cultures (scar in a jar)

The Scar in a Jar model has been extensively described elsewhere14,52,53. The experiments shown are representative of three individual replicates, each conducted with n = 4–8 per condition.

Expansion culture

Human Pancreatic fibroblasts (PFs) or Human Lung fibroblasts were cultured in VitroPlus III, low serum (Neuromics cat# PC00B1, Edina, MN, USA) in rat tail type I collagen (cat# P8188, Innoprot, Derio, Bizkaia, Spain) coated culture flask (5 µg/cm2) at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

BIGH3 induction and inhibition experiments

High binding 96-well plates (cat#3361, Corning, New York, USA) were coated with different concentrations of full-length BIGH3 (produced by Bristol Myers Squibb (BMS)) for 1 h at 37 °C, after any remaining binding sites were blocked with 2% BSA in PBS for 20 min at 37 °C. The fibroblasts were lifted and seeded in the pre-coated 96-well plates at a cell density of 15,000 cells per well and allowed to attach. After 24 h (day 0), the media was changed to Ficoll media (DMEM with 0.4% FBS, 1% P/S, 56.25 mg/mL 70 kDa Ficoll (cat# 17-0310-50 GE healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA), 37.5 mg/mL 400 kDa Ficoll (17-0300-50, GE healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA), and 0.05 mg/mL L-ascorbic acid (cat# A9256, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). In the function blocking experiments, anti-BIGH3 (18B3) or control IgG were added to the culture medium every third day, and the supernatants were removed and frozen for biomarker analyses.

TGFβ induction and inhibition experiments

The Scar-in-a-jar using TGFβ1 was conducted as described above, with the exception that 0.3ng/mL of TGFβ1 (cat#7754-BH R&D systems, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) was added as a soluble stimulator and added upon every medium change, and the function blocking was also conducted as described above. The supernatants were collected and frozen for analysis of biomarkers.

Macrophage cultures

The CD14 + isolation was performed as previously described54. Briefly, human monocytes were isolated from peripheral blood by centrifugation on a Ficoll-Paque gradient (Amersham Pharmacia) and magnetically sorted using a CD14 + magnetic bead isolation kit (Dynabeads M-450, Invitrogen, Europe), resulting in a more than 95% pure population of CD14 + cells. CD14 + monocytes were then seeded at a density of 50,000/well in 96-well plates and cultured in αMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 25 ng/ml of macrophage-colony stimulating factor (M-CSF) (R&D Systems) for 3 days. From day 3 and onwards the cells were cultured in medium containing 25 ng/mL M-CSF or in the presence or absence of either 1 ng/mL of TGFβ1 (cat#7754-BH R&D systems, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) or Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (cat#tlrl-peklps, InvivoGen, France), and the supernatants were collected for assessment of BIGH3. TNF-α (Cat#DY210, R&D Systems) was measured in cells treated with M-CSF in comparison with cells treated with M-CSF + TGFβ1.

Statistics

Population-based genetic variation in the PERF dataset was captured using EIGENSTRAT Smartpca 7.2.0 to perform an iterative principal component analysis (PCA) of the study population with available genotypes on the non-imputed filtered variants using the default parameters55,56. A robust and optimized variable selection procedure was used to include the variables in the genome-wide association study (GWAS). A 50-times randomized, 5-fold cross-validated elastic net was performed to select variables associated with log2 levels of PRO-C3. Visualization of the distribution of coefficient estimates was done using standard geom_boxplots. Linear additive regression was performed on the GWAS study population (n = 4968) to identify genetic associations with log2-transformed serum PRO-C3 levels using plink v1.90p adjusted for age and the three leading principal components. Conservative significance thresholds based on the number of screened variants were defined as equal to 6.5e-9 i.e. 0.05/N, and 1.3e-7, i.e. 1/N, N = 7,672,338, for the genome-wide and suggestive values, respectively. The Manhattan-plot visualizations were made using the R package qqman57. In the BIOPAC cohort, PRO-C3 and BIGH3 levels were correlated by use of the Spearman correlation coefficient. Mann-Whitney was used to compare biomarker levels between healthy controls and patients with PDAC. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to assess the difference in overall survival (OS) between patients with high (upper tertile, T3), medium (middle tertile, T2) and low (lower tertile, T1) levels of PRO-C3 and BIGH3, respectively. In addition, a univariate Cox proportional-hazard regression model was used to calculate the hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence interval (Cl) for OS. Evaluation of differential gene expression analysis between PDAC and non-PDAC was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For each cell type, scaled gene expression values (Z-scores) were compared between PDAC and non-PDAC conditions using the wilcox.test () function in R. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Genes with FDR-adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Data are presented as dot plots where the size of each dot represents the percentage of cells where the gene was detected in the selected population of cells and the strength of the color of the dot represents the average expression (Z-scores, average expression scaled from − 0.5 to 2.0). For cell culture experiments, statistical analysis was conducted using the Brown-Forsythe and Welch one-way ANOVA test (assuming normal distribution, but non-equal standard deviations, SDs).

Results

SNPs in the BIGH3 gene are associated with serum PRO-C3 levels

To explore the association between genetic variants and the fibrosis activity biomarker PRO-C3 we performed a GWAS analysis on log2-transformed PRO-C3 levels at baseline using an optimized variable selection procedure consisting of a cross-validated elastic net using samples from the PERF study. The results summarized as a Manhattan plot (Fig. 1) showed that Log2(PRO-C3) levels were associated with BIGH3/TGFBI/VTRNA2-1 locus on Chromosome 5 (effect size 0.070, 95%CI [0.050–0.090] log2 ng/mL, p-value = 8.42e-12). Moreover, significant variants in the locus were across the BIGH3 gene and in strong linkage with lead SNP rs2073511 (Fig. S1). These data indicate that SNPs in the BIGH3 gene are associated with PRO-C3 levels.

Serum PRO-C3 levels are correlated to serum BIGH3 levels in pancreatic cancer and liver fibrosis, supporting a common link

PRO-C3 has previously been shown to be highly elevated prognostic for poor OS in PDAC14. To further characterize the relation between BIGH3 and PRO-C3, samples from 178 patients with PDAC and 20 healthy controls were analyzed for both biomarkers. As shown in Fig. 2A and B, serum BIGH3 (p = 0.0002) and serum PRO-C3 (p < 0.0001) were significantly elevated in patients with PDAC compared to healthy controls. Moreover, the two biomarkers showed strong positive correlation (Spearman r = 0.665, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2C), supporting a functional relation between these proteins. To investigate if the link between PRO-C3 and BIGH3 was unique to PDAC or indicative of a common fibrotic link, we correlated circulating levels of these two analytes in a cohort containing healthy, MAFLD, MASH and cirrhotic patients, and as seen in Fig. 2D, there is a correlation of similar magnitude to the one observed in PDAC. This trend clearly indicates a common link between fibrosis, BIGH3 and PRO-C3.

Serum PRO-C3 levels are associated with poor OS in pancreatic cancer

To confirm that PRO-C3 was associated with OS, and explore if BIGH3 was associated with OS, in pancreatic cancer, we first used Kaplan-Meier plots to assess the potential difference in overall survival (OS) time between patients divided into tertiles (T1: low, T2: middle, T3: high biomarker levels) for PRO-C3 and BIGH3, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3, stratifying patients based on PRO-C3 (log-rank p = 0.0074), but not BIGH3 (log-rank p = 0.43), resulted in significantly different OS curves according to each biomarker strata. In support, univariate Cox proportional-hazard regression modelling showed that high PRO-C3 (T3) was predictive of poor OS (HR: 1.85, 95%CI: 1.27–2.71) whereas BIGH3 was not (HR: 1.27, 95%CI: 0.87–1.86). Together these findings indicate that PRO-C3 is a superior prognostic biomarker than BIGH3 when measured in serum of patients with advanced PDAC and fit well with data indicated that the biologically active BIGH3 variant is present locally in the matrix of the lesions.

BIGH3 is an inducer of PRO-C3 in fibroblasts and can be blocked by anti-BIGH3 antibody treatment

After establishing a correlation between BIGH3 and PRO-C3, we next sought to determine whether BIGH3 was also an inducer of PRO-C3 in pancreatic fibroblasts cultured in vitro in the scar-in-a-jar model. As shown in Fig. 4A, incubating pancreatic fibroblasts in the presence of increasing doses (25 nM to 200 nM) of BIGH3 resulted in dose-dependent increases in PRO-C3. Moreover, a similar phenomenon was seen for lung fibroblast (Fig. 4B). Taken together this indicates that BIGH3 is an inducer of PRO-C3 and other collagens in pancreatic fibroblasts, suggesting that BIGH3 is a driver of fibroblast activation leading to more collagen and thereby fibrosis. Importantly, the induction of collagen type III synthesis was measured using PRO-C3- and thereby links directly to the initial finding of a relationship between the BIGH3 SNPs and PRO-C3 levels in circulation.

To further investigate the causal nature of the BIGH3 induction, we used the antibody 18B3 previously shown to block BIGH358, in the scar-in-a-jar model system with pancreatic fibroblast and evaluated the expression of PRO-C3. As shown in Fig. 4C, incubation with the anti-BIGH3 antibody virtually eliminated the induction of PRO-C3 elicited by 50nM of BIGH3, while no inhibition was seen with the control IgG antibody. This suggests that anti-BIGH3 antibody blocks the fibrotic processes indicated by the synthesis of type III collagen (PRO-C3).

Macrophages and fibroblasts are the primary source of BIGH3

We evaluated COL3A1 and BIGH3 gene expressions in a single-cell RNA-seq dataset of 24 PDAC samples and 11 non-PDAC samples50,51. We used the cell types defined in the original dataset including macrophages, stellate cells, fibroblasts, ductal cells, endocrine cells, endothelial cells, acinar cells, T-cells, and B-cells. In all cases, the fold-change relative to the average expression in all other cell types of COL3A1 and BIGH3 was significantly different when comparing non-PDAC to PDAC samples (Fig. S2). As shown in Fig. 5, macrophages from PDAC and non-PDAC, and fibroblasts and stellate cells from PDAC were the primary source of BIGH3: 52% of macrophages from non-PDAC and71% of macrophages from PDAC samples were expressing BIGH3; and 52% of fibroblasts and 51% of stellate cells from PDAC were expressing BIGH3 compared to 16% and 15%, respectively, in non-PDAC (and with much lower average expression compared to PDAC). In comparison, expression of COL3A1 was restricted to the fibroblasts in PDAC (98%) and non-PDAC (77%) and stellate cells in PDAC (87% versus 22% in non-PDAC) with a markedly stronger expression signal in PDAC. For macrophages, 2% and 33% of cells express COL3A1 in non-PDAC and PDAC, respectively, and at very low levels compared to the average expression.

BIGH3 is synthesized by macrophages and fibroblasts treated with TGFβ

Based on the scRNAseq data and as TGFβ is known to promote both the differentiation of non-activated macrophages into a tumor associated macrophage (M2) polarization and activate cancer associated fibroblasts59,60, we characterized to what extent TGFβ induces BIGH3 expression in human CD14 + monocyte-derived macrophages and fibroblasts. When comparing macrophages kept only in the presence of M-CSF to macrophages exposed to TGFβ or LPS, we observed a potent induction of BIGH3 release by TGFβ treatment and inhibition of BIGH3 by LPS treatment (Fig. 6A). Measuring TNF-α in cells treated with M-CSF in comparison with cells treated with M-CSF + TGFβ showed significant reductions in TNF-α when cells were treated with TGF-β (Fig. S3), indicating that TGF-β drives macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory, M2-like, phenotype. In addition, we showed that pancreatic fibroblasts exposed to TGFβ also produced BIGH3 (Fig. 6B). These findings suggest that TGFβ-induced tumor fibrosis is associated with both immunosuppressive macrophages and activated fibroblasts producing BIGH3.

Anti-BIGH3 antibody treatment blocks synthesis of type III collagen (PRO-C3) induced by TGFβ in fibroblasts

To further investigate the direct relationship between TGFβ, BIGH3 and PRO-C3, we used the anti-BIGH3 antibody with pancreatic fibroblast treated with TGFβ and evaluated the expression of PRO-C3. As shown in Fig. 7, incubation with the anti-BigH3 antibody significantly reduced the TGFβ induced PRO-C3 levels, while no reduction of PRO-C3 was seen with the isotype control IgG antibody. Interestingly, the anti-BIGH3 antibody reduced the signal below that of the control, which could indicate involvement of BIGH3 in autoactivation of fibrosis, a finding which is also supported by the presence of BIGH3 in solution even in the absence of TGFβ (Fig. 6). These data support that BIGH3 is a downstream mediator of pathological TGFβ induced fibrosis and highlight the relevance of targeting BIGH3 in patients with pancreatic cancer with elevated PRO-C3 levels.

Discussion

There is a medical need to identify safe and efficacious treatments blocking fibrotic processes for solid tumors and many fibrotic indications. Here we described a novel approach, as we start with circulating levels of the known fibroblast activity marker PRO-C3 and search for SNPs related to disease activity rather than diagnostic status. Through this process we associate fibroblast activity, disease activity, to a pathway driving fibroblast activation and thereby identify a relevant target for lowering fibrosis activity. In this study we performed a GWAS using a cohort of Danish post-menopausal women (PERF) to identify SNPs associated to circulating levels of PRO-C3. The GWAS analysis showed that PRO-C3 levels were associated with variants located across the BIGH3 gene locus. Importantly, a series of other studies have identified SNPs in the BIGH3 region as protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL)61–68. As PRO-C3 has recently garnered significant attention for its potential as a biomarker of tumor fibrosis4,15–26 , we next used a combination of a PDAC clinical study and in vitro cell models to determine to what extent BIGH3 had a relation to tumor fibrosis (PRO-C3). PDAC was chosen as the ‘proof-of-principle’ tumor indication, as it is a very stromal and collagen rich tumor type with ongoing desmoplasia (tumor fibrosis)69,70. In the clinical study of PDAC, we found a strong correlation between BIGH3 and PRO-C3 in serum and when culturing pancreatic fibroblasts, a dose-dependent induction of PRO-C3 was observed which furthermore could be blocked by adding an anti-BIGH3 antibody (18B3, previously shown to block BIGH358, suggesting causality. Lastly, we showed that BIGH3 was expressed by TGFβ treated macrophages and fibroblasts, and that blocking BIGH3 in a TGFβ-induced fibrosis experiment resulted in attenuated PRO-C3 production, indicative of an anti-fibrotic effect. Interestingly, macrophages are the precursors of bone resorbing osteoclasts, and the bone field has intensely searched for a coupling signal from osteoclasts to osteoblasts (fibroblast-like cells)71,72, and here we describe a macrophage-derived coupling signal to fibroblasts.

The finding of elevated levels of BIGH3 and PRO-C3 in PDAC, and the strong correlation between the two, supports the pathological relevance of BIGH3 in this fibrotic tumor type and fits well with other studies showing an elevation of BIGH3 in both tumor tissue and circulation73,74. Interestingly, a recent pan-cancer analysis evaluated the association between BIGH3 and prognosis and immune characteristics75. In this analysis, BIGH3 expression was upregulated in most tumors, and in many cases, including PDAC, it was correlated to prognosis, consistent with the trend in our data. Although the prognostic value of PRO-C3 was superior to BIGH3 in the present PDAC study, other studies have shown that elevation of BIGH3 in circulation is associated with poor OS in PDAC73,76–78. One reason for this discrepancy is that PRO-C3 is a small peptide readily secreted from tissues as part of collagen fiber formation, whereas BIGH3 is a local mediator that more readily resides in the tissue, and a much larger molecule.

Further supporting that BIGH3 is a pro-fibrotic molecule are the data showing that the function blocking 18B3 antibody reduced the levels of PRO-C3 produced by the activated fibroblast. These findings are in alignment with studies showing that 18B3 in pre-clinical mouse models of pancreas cancer reduce tumor growth and impact the tumor microenvironment40,58. In fact, when combining these findings, it is apparent that BIGH3 exerts a multitude of pathological responses in the pancreatic cancer microenvironment, and as such is a highly interesting target for treatment of tumor fibrosis. In this context, although whether the central part of these effects also included a pro-fibrotic drive remained to be studied, it is interesting to note that previous studies have indicated BIGH3 as a versatile molecule which has multiple properties such as induction of adhesion, migration, tumorigenesis, angiogenesis, and inflammation35.

Finally, we used a combination of scRNAseq and cell cultures to understand the cellular sources of BIGH3. The scRNAseq showed high expression in cancer associated fibroblasts and cancer associated stellate cells as well as in macrophages from both PDAC and non-PDAC tissues. In the cell cultures, we found that both pancreatic fibroblasts and macrophages exposed to TGFβ expressed high levels of BIGH3. These data confirm previous studies of the source of BIGH3 showing that fibroblasts and macrophages exposed to TGFβ produce significant levels of BIGH358,79–81 and again place the interaction between immunosuppressive macrophages and stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment high of the list of therapeutic targets for resolution of fibrosis, and thereby an increased probability for success when combining with classical anti-tumor treatments, which fail due to fibrosis in the tumor microenvironment.

While targeting fibrosis in cancers, such as PDAC, is extremely important, it is of interest that PRO-C3 levels are indicative of progression in other fibrotic diseases, and as such PRO-C3 is an important indicator of diseases turning fibrotic. Therefore, finding targets such as BIGH3 that allow removing the fibrotic driver across these diseases in patients with active fibrosis as identified by elevated PRO-C3 is of great therapeutic interest across fibrotic conditions.

There are limitations to this study. We focused primarily on pancreas cancer although with supportive findings from liver fibrosis and lung fibroblasts, suggesting a common fibrotic factor. Additional cancer indications and fibrotic diseases should be investigated. Furthermore, the scRNA-seq datasets are limited in size. The causality between BIGH3 and PRO-C3 is based on in vitro data and future ex vivo or in vivo studies are warranted to investigate causality in a more complex setting, and for ultimately moving the findings forward towards clinical applicability.

In conclusion, we present a new route for drug discovery, namely identifying SNPs related to a disease activity marker reflecting fibrosis, namely PRO-C3 (the N-terminal pro-peptide of type III collagen). We found that serum PRO-C3 levels were associated with SNPs in the BIGH3 gene and that serum PRO-C3 levels were associated with poor OS in pancreatic cancer and correlated to serum BIGH3 levels. Moreover, we found that BIGH3 induced PRO-C3 in fibroblasts and this could be prevented by adding an anti-BIGH3 antibody. Finally, we confirmed that both fibroblasts and macrophages produced BIGH3 when exposed to TGFβ. Taken together, these findings indicate that BIGH3 is a driver of tumor/organ fibrosis and that BIGH3 could be a target to treat in patients with PDAC and organ fibrosis that present with high PRO-C3 levels.
