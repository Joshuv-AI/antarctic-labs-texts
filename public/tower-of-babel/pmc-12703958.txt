Parental policy-related worry and low-income families' well-being in the context of U.S. executive orders: A cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Jiying Ling, Lorraine B Robbins, Kenneth Resnicow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective This study examined the relationship between parental worry about current U.S. executive orders, particularly regarding access to federal assistance programs, and family well-being. Methods Cross-sectional data collected in April–May 2025 from 140 low-income parents in the Midwestern U.S. were analyzed. Parents and preschoolers were non-randomly recruited from Head Start organizations. Parents completed an online survey assessing their policy-related worry, stress, anxiety, depression, mindfulness, parent-child relationship, food insecurity, and their preschoolers' social, emotional, and behavioral health. Results Among parents, 24.7 % reported no worry, 11.0 % a little, 20.1 % some, 19.5 % a lot, and 20.8 % extreme worry; 3.9 % were unsure. Parental policy-related worry was dichotomized into low and high. Compared to low-worry parents, high-worry parents reported greater anxiety (p = .01), higher household food insecurity (p < .01), lower mindfulness (p < .01), and more parent child conflict (p = .03). Regarding preschoolers' developmental functioning, preschoolers of low-worry parents showed greater social skills (p = .01), fewer problem behaviors (p < .01), and lower levels of sadness (p < .01), fear (p = .03), and anger (p < .01) than those of high-worry parents. Conclusions Even when not explicitly targeting families, executive orders may contribute to poor health outcomes in low-income households. Public health efforts should recognize policy-related stressors and promote trauma informed, resilience-building interventions to support family well-being and child development.Keywords: Policy, Health, Family, Child, Low-income

Introduction

In the United States (U.S.), new administrations usually rely on executive orders in the first few months to set priorities (Graber, 2021). Across 10 recent presidencies, total executive orders issued ranged from 162 to 381 within four or eight years, averaging about 51 per year (Peters and Woolley, 2025). President Donald Trump, who was inaugurated in January 2025, signed 143 executive orders in his first 100 days in office, the highest in U.S. history (Wendling and Siu, 2025). Executive orders are written directives from the President to executive agencies. They carry legal effects when grounded in constitutional or statutory authority and are subject to judicial review. Congress can limit or supersede their effects through legislation or appropriations (Graber, 2021). Unlike statutes, executive orders can be amended or revoked by the current or a subsequent President.

Several federal assistance programs provide essential resources to help low-income families in the U.S. meet basic household and health needs and support early childhood development (Marson et al., 2023). From January to May 2025, several U.S. executive orders were introduced that might impact this population, including Making America Healthy Again (creates a commission to review existing federal programs, such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistant Program, Medicaid [health insurance], and Head Start [child development program], all of which are currently beneficial to low-income families), Ending Taxpayer Subsidization of Open Borders (instructs agencies to tighten eligibility verification so federal benefits are provided only to eligible persons under existing law), and Expanding Educational Freedom and Opportunity for Families (directs agencies to support school-choice initiatives through guidance and existing funding; The White House, 2025). Individuals usually interpret policies differently based on shared information, personal attitude, and prior experiences (Thorson, 2024). Therefore, some low-income families may worry that these executive orders could plausibly affect their eligibility or access to vital federal assistance programs even before any changes are actually made.

Prior literature has demonstrated the associations among policy-related stress, worry, and poorer mental health (Held et al., 2022; Vîslă et al., 2022; Weitzman et al., 2011). For example, a menta-analysis of 120 correlational studies concluded that general worry was strongly related to poorer mental health (Vîslă et al., 2022). In a sample of 378 mothers of toddlers, parental worry was associated with children's social-emotional problems (Weitzman et al., 2011). Additionally, among 264 Mexican and Central American adults, policy-related stress was linked to higher anxiety and depressive symptoms (Held et al., 2022). However, it is unknown whether parental worry about changes in federal assistance programs due to recent executive orders relates to their own mental health or their preschoolers' socioemotional outcomes. To address this gap, this study investigated the associations between parental policy-related worry and family health outcomes, including parental stress, anxiety, depression, parent-child relationship, mindfulness, and household food insecurity, as well as their preschoolers' social skills, problem behaviors, and emotional well-being (e.g., sadness, anger, fear, and positive affect). Our objective was not to evaluate whether specific U.S. executive orders are beneficial or harmful. Instead, we focused on examining whether parents' perceived policy-related worry was associated with their families' well-being. In this study, worry was defined as a cognitive preoccupation with potential threats to oneself or close others (Boehnke et al., 1998). Compared to anxiety, which is a sustained and generalized affective state that usually interferes with daily functioning; worry is a more temporary, moderate in intensity, and specific to a particular event or situation.

Grounded in the Conservation of Resources Theory (Hobfoll, 2011), which posits that perceived or actual loss of critical resources triggers psychological distress, we hypothesized that increased parental policy-related worry about losing federal assistance programs would be associated with elevated stress, anxiety, and depression among low-income parents. These mental health challenges, in turn, may be linked to more conflict in the parent-child relationship, reduced mindfulness, and greater household food insecurity, potentially compromising preschoolers' social, emotional, and behavioral functioning.

Methods

Study design and population

The study involved analysis of cross-sectional data from a cluster randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the effects of a mindfulness-based lifestyle intervention on promoting whole child health. The study was approved by a local university institutional review board and parental consent was obtained before data collection. Low-income preschoolers and their parents were nonrandomly recruited from Head Start centers in Michigan (Midwestern U.S.).

Measures

Between April and May 2025, parents completed an online survey via Qualtrics measuring perceived stress, anxiety, depression, parent-child relationship, mindfulness, and household food insecurity, as well as their preschoolers' social skills, problem behaviors, and emotional well-being. The measures used and their reliability and validity are detailed in the Supplementary Materials. Parents also answered one multiple-choice question to indicate their level of policy-related worry: How much do you worry that the recent U.S. presidential executive orders may negatively affect your eligibility for federal assistance programs such as Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, Medicaid, or housing aid? The response choices included not at all, a little, somewhat, a lot, extremely, and unsure.

Statistical analysis

Using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 30, chi-square tests or one-way ANOVAs were used to examine the correlations between worry level (a categorical variable) and family sociodemographic characteristics. One-way ANOVAs were conducted to examine the associations between parental policy-related worry level and family health outcomes. To improve interpretation, we dichotomized responses into ‘low worry’ (not at all, a little) and ‘high worry’ (somewhat to extremely), while excluding the ‘unsure’ group. Multivariate general linear models were then performed to further examine the correlations between policy-related worry (low vs. high) and health outcomes.

Results

Among the 140 participating parents, 24.7 % reported no worry, 11.0 % a little, 20.1 % some, 19.5 % a lot, and 20.8 % extreme worry; 3.9 % were unsure. As shown in Table 1, preschoolers' mean age was 46.9 months, with 49.4 % identified as male, 11.7 % as Hispanic, 8.4 % as Black, and 17.5 % as multiracial. Parents' mean age was 31.8 years, 6.4 % were male, 42.1 % identified as single, 41.4 % were unemployed, and 39.3 % reported an annual family income below $20,000. Notably, none of the family demographic characteristics were significantly associated with parental policy worry (Supplementary Materials).

Table 2 demonstrates the family well-being outcomes by parental policy-related worry level. According to the results from one-way ANOVAs, parents' worry level was significantly associated with their anxiety (p = .04), conflict in the parent-child relationship (p < .01), mindfulness (p < .01), food insecurity (p < .01), and preschoolers' social skills (p < .01), problem behaviors (p < .01), sadness (p = .04), and anger (p < .01). Notably, parents who selected ‘unsure’ reported the lowest anxiety, the second-highest mindfulness and preschoolers' social skills, and the second-lowest levels of preschoolers' sadness (after the ‘not at all’ group). Similarly, results from multivariate general linear models showed that parents with high worry exhibited significantly higher levels of anxiety (M = 2.2 vs. 1.4, p = .01) and lower levels of mindfulness (4.1 vs. 4.7, p < .01) compared to those with low worry. Likewise, high-worry parents experienced significantly greater household food insecurity than their low-worry counterparts (3.1 vs. 1.4, p < .01). Conflict in the parent-child relationship was also higher among high-worry parents than those reporting low worry (16.7 vs. 14.3, p = .03).

Regarding preschoolers' developmental functioning, preschoolers of low-worry parents demonstrated significantly greater social skills than those whose parents had high worry (58.7 vs. 47.0, p = .01). These preschoolers also exhibited significantly fewer problem behaviors than their counterparts (42.6 vs. 60.9, p < .01). In addition, preschoolers whose parents reported low worry showed significantly lower levels of sadness (46.0 vs. 50.0, p < .01), fear (44.6 vs. 48.6, p = .03), and anger (46.9 vs. 52.2, p < .01) than those of parents reporting high worry.

Discussion

This study found significant associations between a higher level of parents' policy-related worry about changes in their eligibility for federal assistance programs and poorer family well-being outcomes, including compromised social, emotional, and behavioral functioning among preschoolers. Elevated policy-related worry was also linked to increased conflict in the parent-child relationship and greater food insecurity, suggesting a cascading impact of policy-related uncertainty on overall family well-being (Antonakakis and Gupta, 2017). Consistent with literature linking parental anxiety to child developmental outcomes through a possibly unstable and insecure family environment (Wang et al., 2024), the study's results support this pathway by demonstrating significant correlations of parental policy-related worry with food insecurity, parent-child conflict, and preschoolers' decreased developmental functioning. These results underscore the critical role of a nurturing and supportive family environment in mitigating the negative effects of parental psychological distress on family well-being. Promoting a warm and responsive home environment may help buffer children from the detrimental developmental consequences associated with parental worry, as a healthy family environment fosters more engaged and emotionally attuned parenting behaviors, which are essential for promoting optimal emotional, behavioral, and social development in early childhood (Newland, 2015). Future research should confirm this pathway through a large, longitudinal study.

An unexpected finding was that parents selecting ‘unsure’ reported the lowest anxiety; and after the ‘not at all’ group, their preschoolers had the second-lowest sadness level and were second-highest in social skills. Two plausible explanations may account for this occurrence. First, ‘unsure’ may indicate low perceived threat or low involvement with the policy issue. When parents appraise little personal relevance, affective arousal can be minimal. Second, these parents may cope by not engaging with appraised distressing or uncertain information, which may have yielded lower anxiety in the short term. Importantly, ‘unsure’ is not equivalent to ‘no worry,’ as prior research found that ‘unsure’ represented a distinct attitude rather than agreement or disagreement (Champ et al., 2005). Additionally, the ‘unsure’ group also reported higher mindfulness, aligning with evidence suggesting that mindfulness may buffer worry and support family well-being under uncertainty (Bögels and Emerson, 2019).

Given the cross-sectional design, it is certainly plausible that parents with poorer mental health may be more sensitive to policy changes and thus report greater worry. This possible occurrence, coupled with this study's findings, suggests a potential bidirectional relationship between policy-related worry and mental health. Another consideration is that some outcomes may operate as mediators linking parental policy-related worry to family well-being, as one prior study of 509 adults found that anxiety mediated the effects from worry to depression (Gana et al., 2002). Mindfulness may even serve as a moderator that buffers the links between policy-related worry and family well-being. Longitudinal designs are warranted to test directionality and mechanisms in future studies. Additionally, our sample included families enrolled in U.S. federal Head Start programs, but their participation in other federal assistance programs was unknown. Future studies should collect these data and examine how enrollment in various federal assistance programs influences families' worry levels and health outcomes.

The elevated level of the observed parental worry about federal assistance programs underscores the importance of clear and trustworthy communication about executive orders and highlights the need for accessible and plain-language explanations to help families understand public policy-making procedures and potential policy impacts (Peter et al., 2024). Without such efforts, misperceptions fueled by limited health literacy or misinformation may further destabilize the family environment and hinder optimal child development, particular in low-income families (Budak et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2009). These findings emphasize the deep interconnectedness of structural policy, parental mental health, and child well-being. Public health and social service systems should consider not only the direct effects of executive orders, but also the unintended psychological consequences of policy-related fear and confusion. Investing in family-centered, resilience-building supports, such as mindfulness programs, mental health services, and clear policy guidance, may be crucial for promoting the health and stability of vulnerable families during times of political uncertainty.
