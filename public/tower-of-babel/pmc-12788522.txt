Incidence and Risk Factors for Surgical Site Infections in Pediatric Abdominal Surgery: A Multicenter Cohort Study
Authors: Ankita, Nishant G. Xess, Kavita Tirkey, Shyam Sundar Sahu, Abhishek Ranjan, Abhishek Kumar Singh, Priya Shalini Lakra, Hirendra Birua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Surgical site infections (SSIs) are among the most common postoperative complications in pediatric abdominal surgeries. Understanding the incidence and risk factors is crucial for effective prevention and improved outcomes.Materials and Methods:A prospective multicenter cohort study was conducted over 18 months involving 600 pediatric patients (aged between one month and 14 years) undergoing abdominal surgeries. Data on patient demographics, surgical characteristics, and postoperative outcomes were collected and analyzed to identify SSI risk factors.Results:The overall incidence of SSIs was 11.5%. Significant risk factors included contaminated surgical wounds (P < 0.001), operative duration > 2 h (P = 0.002), emergency surgery (P = 0.007), and presence of comorbidities such as malnutrition or immunosuppression (P < 0.05).Conclusion:SSIs remain a significant burden in pediatric abdominal surgeries. Timely identification of modifiable risk factors can help reduce the incidence and improve patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Surgical site infections (SSIs) are a frequent cause of postoperative morbidity, prolonged hospital stay, and increased healthcare costs, particularly in the pediatric population. Children, due to their unique anatomical, physiological, and immunological characteristics, exhibit a variable risk profile for SSIs when compared to adults.[12] Among pediatric surgical procedures, abdominal surgeries are particularly prone to SSIs due to exposure to gastrointestinal flora and the complexity of interventions.[3]

The global incidence of SSIs in pediatric surgery ranges from 2% to 20%, depending on surgical type, perioperative care standards, and institutional protocols. Despite advances in surgical techniques and infection control, SSIs remain a persistent challenge, especially in low- and middle-income countries. In India, limited multicenter data are available that systematically evaluate SSI incidence and risk factors in the pediatric surgical population, particularly for abdominal surgeries, which involve higher bacterial load and complexity.[45]

Pediatric abdominal surgeries vary widely—from elective hernia repairs to emergency laparotomies for peritonitis or intestinal obstruction. Factors, such as nutritional status, age, preexisting comorbidities, hygiene practices, and postoperative care, significantly influence outcomes in children. Moreover, aspects, such as wound classification, surgical duration, type of anesthesia, and intraoperative contamination, are well-established contributors to SSIs in adults, yet need specific validation in pediatric contexts.[678]

A comprehensive evaluation of the incidence and risk factors of SSIs in pediatric abdominal surgeries can guide better preoperative assessment, risk stratification, and targeted preventive strategies such as timely antibiotic prophylaxis and stringent surgical protocols. Identifying modifiable risk factors is crucial for enhancing patient outcomes, optimizing hospital resource use, and reducing antibiotic resistance through rational antibiotic use.[4]

The current study aims to bridge this knowledge gap by evaluating the incidence and risk factors of SSIs in pediatric abdominal surgeries through a multicenter cohort study design. This study spans across three tertiary care centers in eastern India and includes a representative sample of both elective and emergency surgeries, encompassing a diverse pediatric population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective, multicenter cohort study was conducted at three tertiary care hospitals from January 2023 to June 2024. A total of 600 pediatric patients aged between one month and 14 years who underwent abdominal surgeries (elective and emergency) were enrolled. Patients were followed postoperatively for 30 days for signs of SSI as per CDC criteria. Exclusion criteria included patients with preexisting infections or incomplete records. Data on demographics, wound classification, operative time, comorbidities, emergency status, preoperative hospital stay, and antibiotic use were collected using a standardized case record form. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA), employing Chi-square tests for categorical variables and logistic regression to identify independent risk factors (P < 0.05 considered significant).

Out of the 600 enrolled patients, 69 developed SSIs, leading to an overall incidence of 11.5%.

DISCUSSION

This multicenter study provides a comprehensive overview of the burden and predictors of SSIs in pediatric abdominal surgery. The incidence rate of 11.5% aligns with findings from similar studies in low-resource settings, emphasizing the continued relevance of SSIs in pediatric surgical care. This rate is higher than in some developed nations, likely due to disparities in infection control measures, infrastructure, and perioperative practices.

Emergency surgery was identified as a significant risk factor for SSIs (16.7%, P = 0.007), possibly due to suboptimal preoperative preparation and higher levels of contamination. This aligns with prior studies by Aiken et al. and Al-Harbi et al., who observed a twofold increase in SSIs following emergency procedures. Elective surgeries offer better opportunity for optimization of patient condition, sterile preparation, and timely antibiotic administration.[9]

The study also found that longer operative time (>2 h) and contaminated or dirty wound classification significantly increased the odds of SSIs. Extended operative durations may contribute to tissue trauma, increased exposure to environmental pathogens, and breaks in sterile technique. Wound contamination is an expected and consistent predictor, particularly in gastrointestinal perforation or necrotizing enterocolitis cases.[10]

Malnutrition and immunosuppressive conditions, like leukemia, were independently associated with higher SSI rates. These findings are consistent with observations by Murni et al., who reported that immunocompromised status and poor nutrition compromise host defenses, leading to delayed wound healing and infection. Pediatric patients, especially in low- and middle-income countries, often present with underlying nutritional deficiencies that remain unaddressed before surgery.[11]

A key modifiable factor identified in our study was the timely administration of antibiotic prophylaxis. A significant increase in SSI was noted among those who either did not receive prophylaxis or received it late. This reinforces the importance of adhering to surgical antibiotic prophylaxis guidelines, as highlighted by the WHO and CDC.

Although younger age (<1 year) and male sex were more prevalent among SSI cases, these did not reach statistical significance. However, the trend suggests that neonates and infants, due to their immature immunity and skin barrier, could be more vulnerable, warranting further focused research.[12]

Our study’s strengths include its multicenter design, adequate sample size, and prospective data collection. However, limitations exist. Being conducted in tertiary centers, results may not be generalizable to primary or rural health settings. Microbiological data on causative organisms and resistance patterns were not included in this analysis, which could have enriched the understanding of pathogen-specific risks and treatment efficacy.

CONCLUSION

This multicenter cohort study highlights an 11.5% incidence of SSIs in pediatric abdominal surgeries. Emergency procedures, contaminated wounds, prolonged operative time, malnutrition, immunosuppression, and delayed or absent prophylactic antibiotics are significant risk factors. By addressing these modifiable elements, surgical teams can reduce postoperative infections and improve recovery outcomes in pediatric patients. Further studies incorporating microbiological surveillance and interventional strategies are recommended.
