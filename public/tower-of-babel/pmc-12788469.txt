A Comparative Evaluation of Dimensional Stability of Implant Impressions with Delayed vs Immediate Pouring Using Polyether and Polyvinyl Siloxane: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Kishan Choithani, Akhilesh Patoliya, Chirag R. Vaniya, Raj Shukla, Hiral Karia, Dhara Bajania, Milap Karia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Dimensional stability of impression materials is crucial in implant prosthodontics. Delay in cast pouring can introduce errors that affect prosthesis fit.Aim:To evaluate and compare the dimensional stability of casts obtained from polyether and polyvinyl siloxane impression materials when poured immediately, after 24 hours, and after 48 hours.Materials and Methods:Forty-eight impressions were made on a reference mandibular edentulous model with four implants using the direct pick-up technique. Impressions were divided into two groups (polyether, polyvinyl siloxane) and three subgroups each (immediate pour, 24 hours, 48 hours). Casts were measured for linear and angular distortions using a coordinate measuring machine.Results:Mean inter-implant distances and angular deviations were statistically analyzed using ANOVA. Polyether showed superior dimensional stability at all time points compared to polyvinyl siloxane. Delayed pouring introduced significant distortion in both materials.Conclusion:Immediate pouring is recommended for optimal accuracy. Polyether demonstrated better stability than polyvinyl siloxane with delayed pouring.

INTRODUCTION

In the evolving field of implant prosthodontics, accuracy in transferring the intraoral implant position to the working cast is critical to ensure passive fit of the final prosthesis and long-term osseointegration success. Even minimal inaccuracies can result in misfits that generate unwanted stresses, which may cause mechanical complications like screw loosening or even implant failure.

Over the years, various factors have been studied to optimize the precision of implant impressions. These include impression techniques (direct vs indirect), tray types, splinting methods, impression copings, splinting materials, and impression materials themselves. Polyether and polyvinyl siloxane (PVS) elastomeric impression materials are widely used for their excellent detail reproduction and elastic recovery.[123]

While numerous studies have explored splinting versus non-splinting, tray designs, and different impression materials for multiunit implant frameworks, another equally important clinical variable is the time interval between impression making and cast pouring. In a busy clinical practice, immediate pouring is not always feasible. Practical delays of several hours to days can occur due to scheduling, transportation, or laboratory workload.[4]

Elastomeric impression materials are designed to exhibit high dimensional stability, yet studies have demonstrated that storage time can still affect their accuracy due to continued polymerization, stress relaxation, or loss of volatile components. For implant prosthodontics, where precision is paramount, understanding the effect of delayed pouring is essential.

Polyether impression material is hydrophilic, with excellent wetting and flow properties, but it is dimensionally more rigid. It is claimed to have superior dimensional stability over time compared to PVS. Polyvinyl siloxane, in contrast, is known for its high elastic recovery but is hydrophobic and can be affected by syneresis or imbibition if not handled properly.[5]

Many manufacturers recommend immediate or same-day pouring. However, in routine practice, clinicians may need to store impressions overnight or longer. Previous research has mostly focused on single-unit crowns and bridges, while fewer studies have explored how delayed pouring affects dimensional accuracy in multiple-implant scenarios.[678]

Hence, this study aims to assess the effect of delayed pouring times (0, 24, and 48 hours) on the dimensional accuracy of implant impressions made using polyether and polyvinyl siloxane materials. The study uses the direct pick-up impression technique, which is widely accepted for its superior accuracy compared to the indirect method.

The findings from this study are expected to guide clinicians and dental laboratories on appropriate timelines for pouring implant impressions to avoid dimensional distortions that can compromise the fit of implant frameworks.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design: In vitro comparative study.

Reference Model: A mandibular edentulous acrylic model with four parallel implants (Uniti Equinox, D-3.7 mm, L-13 mm) placed in the symphyseal region following standard implant placement guidelines.

Impression Materials:

Group A: Polyether (Impregum, 3M ESPE)

Group A: Polyether (Impregum, 3M ESPE)

Group B: Polyvinyl siloxane (Aquasil, Dentsply)

Group B: Polyvinyl siloxane (Aquasil, Dentsply)

Sample Size: 48 impressions (24 per material, 8 per pouring time).

Subgroups:

Subgroup 1: Immediate pour (within 30 min)

Subgroup 1: Immediate pour (within 30 min)

Subgroup 2: 24-hour storage

Subgroup 2: 24-hour storage

Subgroup 3: 48-hour storage

Subgroup 3: 48-hour storage

Impression Technique: Direct open-tray pick-up technique. Standard custom trays were fabricated with window access for impression posts.

Procedure: Impression copings were attached to the implants and tightened to the recommended torque. Tray adhesive was applied. Impressions were made using the respective material and allowed to set per manufacturer’s instructions. For delayed pouring, impressions were stored in sealed containers at 23 ± 2°C and 50% relative humidity. Type IV dental stone was used to pour casts.

Measurement: Inter-implant distances in X, Y, Z axes and inter-implant angulations were measured using a coordinate measuring machine (CMM, Mitutoyo) with 2 mm probe. Reference points were gingival formers placed on implant analogs.

Statistical Analysis: Mean values were compared using ANOVA and post hoc Tukey tests (SPSS v26). Significance was set at P < 0.05.

DISCUSSION

The current study evaluated the impact of pouring delay on the dimensional stability of impressions for multiple dental implants. The results highlight a clinically significant trend: delaying cast pouring introduces distortions, with polyether demonstrating superior dimensional stability compared to polyvinyl siloxane.

The findings align with previous literature indicating polyether’s inherent stability due to its low shrinkage and minimal stress relaxation over time. In contrast, PVS materials are more prone to volatile loss and stress relief, resulting in increased dimensional changes if not poured promptly.[9]

In this study, casts poured immediately showed the least deviation for both materials. Notably, a 24-hour delay began to reveal measurable distortion, and by 48 hours, discrepancies reached levels that could compromise passive fit, particularly for PVS.

These results are relevant because passive fit is crucial for the biomechanical success of implant-supported prostheses. Distortions may lead to misfits that generate unintended stress on implants and surrounding bone, potentially causing complications like screw loosening or implant failure.[10]

The methodology controlled key variables by standardizing the impression technique, model, and measurement method. Using the CMM ensured precise detection of minute deviations, strengthening the validity of the findings.[1112]

A limitation of this study is its in vitro design, which does not fully replicate intraoral factors like saliva, blood, and soft tissue displacement. Additionally, the controlled storage conditions may not reflect all real-world variations, such as temperature and humidity fluctuations in a dental office.

Future research could expand on this by exploring longer storage times, the impact of disinfectant immersion, or varying storage temperatures. Clinical studies could further validate these results under real patient conditions.

From a clinical perspective, this study reinforces the manufacturer’s recommendations that elastomeric impressions, especially for multiple implants, should ideally be poured immediately or within a few hours. If delays are unavoidable, polyether may be preferable due to its better stability over time.

Ultimately, clinicians should coordinate with laboratories to minimize delays and handle impressions properly during storage to safeguard dimensional accuracy. Simple measures like sealing impressions in airtight containers at a stable room temperature can help maintain integrity for a limited period.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, it can be concluded that immediate pouring of impressions is preferable to ensure dimensional accuracy for multiple implant prostheses. Polyether impression material demonstrated superior dimensional stability compared to polyvinyl siloxane at 24- and 48-hour intervals. Clinicians should strive to pour implant impressions as soon as possible to avoid compromising prosthesis fit.
