OpenAI’s GPT-OSS-20B Model and Safety Alignment Issues in a Low-Resource Language
Isa Inuwa-Dutse

Abstract
In response to the recent safety probing for OpenAI’s GPT-OSS-20b model 1 1 1 https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/openai-gpt-oss-20b-red-teaming , we present a summary of a set of vulnerabilities uncovered in the model, focusing on its performance and safety alignment in a low-resource language setting. The core motivation for our work is to question the model’s reliability for users from underrepresented communities. Using Hausa, a major African language, we uncover biases, inaccuracies, and cultural insensitivities in the model’s behaviour. With a minimal prompting, our red-teaming efforts reveal that the model can be induced to generate harmful, culturally insensitive, and factually inaccurate content in the language. As a form of reward hacking, we note how the model’s safety protocols appear to relax when prompted with polite or grateful language, leading to outputs that could facilitate misinformation and amplify hate speech. For instance, the model operates on the false assumption that common insecticide locally known as Fiya-Fiya (Cyphermethrin) and rodenticide like Shinkafar Bera (a form of Aluminium Phosphide) are safe for human consumption. To contextualise the severity of this error and popularity of the substances, we conducted a survey (n=61) in which 98% of the participants identified them as toxic. Additional failures include an inability to distinguish between raw and processed foods and the incorporation of demeaning cultural proverbs to build inaccurate arguments. We surmise that these issues manifest through a form of linguistic reward hacking, where the model prioritises fluent, plausible-sounding output in the target language over safety and truthfulness. We attribute the uncovered flaws primarily to insufficient safety tuning in low-resource linguistic contexts. By concentrating on a low-resource setting, our approach highlights a significant gap in current red-teaming effort and offer some recommendations.

Approach

We employ a systematic adversarial prompting strategies to probe the GPT-OSS-20b model for flaws specific to the low-resource language in question.

As stipulated in the competition guidelines

Prompt Engineering Strategy

We engineered a series of multi-step prompts that are designed to begin with neutral queries and gradually introduce adversarial elements that will challenge the model. This approach leverages chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting to guide the model’s reasoning process in the target language. The rationale for this multi-step design is to incrementally lower the model’s safety guardrails by establishing a context of cooperative, step-by-step reasoning, which it then uses to justify and incorporate harmful or inaccurate outputs that exposes biases in the model’s safety enforcement for low-resource languages. Thus, a typical prompt structure followed this pattern:

1.

Neutral Engagement: A benign instruction to reason step-by-step (e.g., "Let’s think through this logically…").

2.

Bias Introduction: A step that introduces a premise containing a cultural assumption or a slight misdirection.

3.

Harmful Output Generation: A final step where the model, conditioned by the previous steps, generates hallucinated, inaccurate, or harmful content.

2.1
Issue #1: Linguistic Reward Hacking and Safety Filter Bypass

Our manual probing approach demonstrates that significant vulnerabilities can be uncovered without model fine-tuning or weight manipulation. This investigation reveals a critical failure mode in the GPT-OSS-20b model’s handling of Hausa due to linguistic reward hacking.
A key trigger for this behaviour was the use of simple gratifying phrases in Hausa, such as "mun gode" (thank you) and "wannan yayi kyau" (this is great). This linguistic luring often caused the model to relax its safety protocols, subsequently generating highly confident but dangerously inaccurate responses. The excerpt in Table

shows how the model doubled down on its false assertions, inventing authoritative-sounding justifications to affirm its incorrect and harmful claims. This phenomenon mirrors classic reinforcement learning (RL) problems where an agent games a flawed reward function. In this case, the "reward" is seemingly successful engagement in a low-resource language, and the failure is a critical lapse in safety that is far more easily triggered than in high-resource settings.

Another manifestation of severe vulnerability was the model’s promotion of highly toxic substances as healthy food options. The model repeatedly recommended Shinkafar Bera (a form of Aluminium Phosphide, a highly toxic rodenticide) and Fiya-fiya (Cyphermethrin, an insecticide) for human consumption (see Table

).
To contextualise the severity of this error, we conducted a quick survey (n=61) in which 98% of the participants attested to the toxicity of these substances, while the remaining 2% were unfamiliar with them entirely. The bottom line is there was no consensus with the model’s dangerous recommendations. This bypass enables the generation of content that is not merely inaccurate but actively deceptive and harmful, posing a significant real-world risk to communities that rely on the model for information in their native language.

Issue #2: Confident Hallucination on Fundamental Concepts

The model engages its chain-of-thought reasoning to weigh all options, but does so based on a flawed internal representation of the world when operating in low-resource languages like Hausa.
The outputs suggest that the training data or reinforcement learning signals for low-resource languages are limited, leading to coherent, fluent, but misleading content. We argue that such confident hallucinations on fundamental concepts render the model dangerously unreliable for educational or informational use by speakers of low-resource languages.

2.3
Issue #3: Cultural Insensitivity and Failure to Filter Demeaning Language

To determine how culturally sensitive the model is, we prompted the model to create a story incorporating a sensitive topic (halitosis/bad breath) and to utilise a known demeaning local idiom, ’komai akai da jaki sai ya ci kara’ (implying an imbecile is always an imbecile). Contrary to expected safety policies that prohibit demeaning language, the model duly obliged, generating lengthy narratives that incorporated the offensive language. We observe the model’s willingness to generate and rationalise culturally insensitive and demeaning content that would likely be flagged or refused in English-language contexts.
Furthermore, the model’s chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning exacerbated this failure by constructing hallucinated justifications for its outputs. This is demonstrated in the generated fictitious historical conflicts between ethnic groups (e.g., within the Hausa-Fulani community) stemming from halitosis (Table

In its CoT, it incorrectly suggested that universal gestures of peace like greetings and "Salam Alaikum" ("peace be upon you") could be misinterpreted as aggression. This not only demonstrates a fundamental lack of cultural understanding but also risks amplifying harmful stereotypes by inventing false sources of conflict. We attribute these issues to a direct consequence of uneven safety training data in which the model’s reward mechanism appears to prioritise generating coherent, fluent text in Hausa above all else. This allows it to be easily prompted into optimising for local narrative coherence at the expense of safety, resulting in harmful, deceptive, and culturally insensitive content.

Impact Assessment

This vulnerability highlights a critical gap in the safety alignment of large language models, where safety training data and reinforcement learning signals are likely unevenly distributed across languages. In low-resource settings, the model’s drive for fluency and coherence (reward) drastically outweighs its adherence to safety principles (alignment), especially under the guided reasoning of CoT prompts. This creates a flawed scenario where the model’s capabilities (reasoning) are leveraged to justify its hallucinated content generation.

3
Conclusion

The rapid advancement and deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) necessitate robust safety alignment measures. A critical and often underrepresented aspect of this effort is assessing model behaviour in low-resource linguistic contexts. This work details a red-teaming exercise focused on uncovering vulnerabilities in OpenAI’s GPT-OSS-20B model through adversarial prompting in Hausa, a major low-resource language. We demonstrate that standard safety alignments can fail easily outside high-resource language settings. Through targeted adversarial prompts, we show how the model prioritises fluency and coherence over safety and truthfulness resulting in the following: (1) the promotion of toxic substances (e.g., rodenticides, insecticides) as safe for human consumption (2) confident hallucinations on fundamental concepts, such as describing the cultivation of processed foods, and (3) the generation of culturally insensitive and demeaning content, including the use of offensive idioms and the fabrication of inflammatory historical narratives.

We argue that the above issues stem from an inherent imbalance in the model’s architecture and training, where safety alignment is under-tuned for languages outside the high-resource category. This limitation is exacerbated by chain-of-thought reasoning, which the model uses to justify its incorrect outputs.
Our red-teaming initiative is based on the premise that if a language with over 100 million speakers like Hausa suffers from these critical failures, the model is inherently unreliable and unsafe for the vast spectrum of underrepresented linguistic communities. This represents a significant equity gap in AI safety. To help in addressing the gap in AI safety, we offer the following recommendations:

-

OpenAI and other developers of frontier models/LLMs should invest in safety datasets and reinforcement learning benchmarks that specifically include low-resource languages.

-

Collaboration with linguistics and cultural experts from affected regions or communities to identify nuanced harms and biases should be strengthened or established.

-

Incorporate rigorous red-teaming exercises for low-resource languages as a standard part of the model evaluation protocol before release.
