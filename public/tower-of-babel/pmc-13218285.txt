Association of impaired ammonia excretion with survival in dogs with stable chronic kidney disease
Authors: Autumn N Harris, Alexis Cooper, Rebeca A Castro, Andrew J Specht, Shelly L Vaden, Kirsten L Cooke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn people with chronic kidney disease (CKD), inadequate renal ammonia excretion contributes to metabolic acidosis and is associated with worse outcomes. However, the prognostic relevance of ammonia excretion in dogs with CKD remains unclear.Hypothesis/ObjectivesA reduced urine ammonia-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) would be associated with shorter survival and faster disease progression in dogs with stable CKD.AnimalsFifty client-owned dogs with International Renal Interest Society (IRIS) stages II-IV CKD receiving a therapeutic renal diet.MethodsProspective, observational longitudinal study. Dogs were followed for up to 12 months or until death. Progressive CKD was defined as a > 25% increase in serum creatinine from baseline. Dogs were categorized based on UACR < 2.0 or ≥ 2.0. Associations with survival and CKD progression were assessed using Cox proportional hazards and Kaplan–Meier analyses.ResultsMost dogs were IRIS stage II (41/50, 82%). Dogs with UACR < 2.0 at enrollment had a significantly greater risk of death (HR 3.045; 95% CI, 1.372-7.102) and shorter median survival (189 vs 445 days, P = .008). Dogs with UACR < 2.0 also had a significantly shorter time to CKD progression (median 132 vs 445 days; P = .0002). In addition, UPC ratio < 1.0 was associated with a lower risk of death (HR 0.351; 95% CI, 0.142-0.879).Conclusions and clinical importanceLower UACR (<2.0) was associated with higher case fatality and faster disease progression in dogs with azotemic CKD. Urine ammonia-to-creatinine ratio might be a useful biomarker for identifying dogs which could benefit from alkali therapy.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common condition in dogs and presents a clinical challenge due to its progressive nature and the difficulty of early detection. Identifying reliable prognostic markers and potential therapeutic targets in the early stages of CKD remains a critical need in veterinary medicine. One well-recognized complication of CKD is metabolic acidosis, reported in up to 71% of affected dogs and approximately 40% of humans.1,2 Metabolic acidosis contributes to a range of adverse clinical outcomes, including protein-energy wasting, skeletal muscle catabolism, mineral and bone disorders, impaired cardiac function, and disruptions in glucose metabolism, all of which can reduce the quality of life and survival.

A key contributor to the development of metabolic acidosis is renal ammonia excretion.3–5 In people, reduced 24-h urinary ammonia excretion is associated with accelerated CKD progression and increased case fatality rate.4 Experimental studies in dogs show that the canine kidney produces ammonia from glutamine under basal conditions, with excretion increasing in response to acid loading.6 While reference intervals for the urinary ammonia-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) have been established in healthy dogs, limited data are available regarding its prognostic importance in CKD in dogs.7 Recent evidence suggests that UACR declines with worsening renal function in dogs, but the mechanisms governing ammonia excretion and acid–base compensation in naturally occurring CKD remain poorly understood.8

To date, no prospective studies have evaluated the association between urinary ammonia excretion and long-term clinical outcomes in dogs with CKD. The objective of this study was to determine whether urinary ammonia excretion, measured as UACR, is associated with CKD progression and survival time in dogs with CKD from a veterinary teaching hospital. We hypothesized that lower UACR would be associated with more rapid disease progression and reduced survival.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a prospective, longitudinal cohort study conducted at the University of Florida (UF) Small Animal Hospital and the North Carolina State University (NCSU) Veterinary Hospital. Both institutions’ Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (UF IACUC #202111549; NCSU IACUC #24-308) approved the study protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from all dog owners before enrollment.

Study cohort

Client-owned dogs aged 1 year or older were eligible for inclusion if they presented to either hospital for management or monitoring of International Renal Interest Society (IRIS) stages II-IV CKD between June 2022 and August 2024. Dogs were eligible if they were diagnosed with stable CKD, defined by IRIS staging criteria (serum creatinine > 1.4 mg/dL or symmetric dimethylarginine > 18 μg/dL) and lack of appropriate urine concentration (urine specific gravity [USG] < 1.035) on at least 2 occasions at least 2 weeks apart, with stable azotemia (<25% change in creatinine between measurements). All enrolled dogs had to be eating a therapeutic renal diet for at least 28 days and were well-hydrated as determined by physical examination and serial body weight measurements. Exclusion criteria included evidence of active urine sediment (>5 white blood cells per high-power field [HPF], > 5 red blood cells per HPF, or bacteriuria), or receipt of alkali therapy, potassium-sparing diuretics, or the need for hospitalization for CKD management, or a documented history of congestive heart failure. Dogs were not excluded on the basis of systemic hypertension, use of antihypertensive medications (including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin-receptor blockers, or amlodipine), or the presence of a heart murmur alone.

Staging criteria

Dogs were staged according to the IRIS guidelines, using serum creatinine concentration: stage II was defined as 1.4-2.0 mg/dL, stage III as 2.1-5.0 mg/dL, and stage IV as greater than 5.0 mg/dL. Substaging was based on urine protein-to-creatinine (UPC) ratio and systolic blood pressure (SBP). Dogs were considered nonproteinuric with a UPC ratio < 0.2, borderline proteinuric with a UPC ratio between 0.2 and 0.5, and proteinuric with a UPC ratio > 0.5. Blood pressure substaging was as follows: normotensive if SBP < 140 mmHg, prehypertensive if between 140 and 159 mmHg, hypertensive if between 160 and 179 mmHg, and severely hypertensive if ≥ 180 mmHg. Dogs were considered to have metabolic acidosis based on IRIS treatment recommendations if their serum bicarbonate concentration was ≤ 18 mEq/L.

Clinical evaluation and laboratory testing

At enrollment, all dogs underwent a complete physical examination, including body weight and body condition scoring. Systolic blood pressure was measured using Doppler. Blood samples were collected via venipuncture, and urine samples were obtained via free catch or cystocentesis. Blood samples were used to perform a PCV, total protein, and serum biochemistry panel, including glucose, albumin, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, sodium (Na+), potassium, Cl−, bicarbonate (HCO3−), calcium, phosphate, and calculated anion gap. During the enrollment visit, at least 5 mL of urine was obtained by free catch or cystocentesis. Urinalysis included measurement of USG using a refractometer, semiquantitative dipstick testing by visual inspection, and microscopic evaluation of the sediment for red and white blood cells, as well as bacteriuria. The UPC ratio was measured from urine samples. All analyses were conducted at the Clinical Pathology Laboratories of the respective institutions on the same day as sample collection.

Dogs were re-evaluated with a serum biochemistry panel every 3 months for a total of 4 visits over a 12-month study period or until the endpoint of death. Treatments, including the decision to euthanize, were not standardized and were determined at the discretion of the attending clinician and the dog owner, based on the dog’s clinical status and laboratory findings.

Urinary ammonia sample collection and analysis

After collection, 0.5-1.0 mL of urine was placed into a sterile conical tube with 1 mL of mineral oil to prevent evaporation within 5 min. The samples were processed by placing urine into cryotubes within 4 h of initial collection and stored at −80°C for no longer than 6 months. Samples were then batched for analysis.9 Frozen urine samples were thawed and then centrifuged at 2100 g for 2 min (Thermo Scientific Sorvall Legend micro21R) with 400 μL of supernatant removed to measure ammonia and creatinine. A commercially available enzymatic assay (Ammonia Reagent Assay; Pointe Scientific, Canton, MI) was used to measure urine ammonia concentrations. Urine creatinine concentrations were determined using the modified Jaffe method with a commercially available assay (Creatinine Assay Kit (ab204537); Abcam, Cambridge, MA). The CV and total observed error (TEobs) for both assays have been previously reported.10 A SpectraMax ABS plus microplate reader (Molecular Devices) was used for ammonia (mmol/L) and creatinine (mg/dL) measurements. To keep units consistent for ammonia and creatinine, measures for the latter were converted from mg/dL to mmol/L by multiplying by 0.055. Data collection and outcome definitions

Demographic data, including age, breed, sex, and weight, were recorded. The serum chemistry panel results were recorded. Progression of CKD in each dog was defined as an increase in serum creatinine concentration of > 25% from baseline at enrollment during the 12-month study period, consistent with previously published veterinary criteria. To reduce misclassification due to biological variability, the increase was required to be persistent on subsequent follow-up evaluations. Study outcomes recorded included death during the study period, survival to study end, loss to follow-up, progression or stability of CKD, cause of death (renal vs nonrenal), and survival in days.

Statistical analysis

All data were recorded in a Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Office, 2019) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Dogs were categorized based on UACR (< 2.0 vs ≥ 2.0) and disease trajectory (progressive vs nonprogressive CKD). Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean (± SD) and as the median (range) if non-normally distributed. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize all variables including demographic, biochemical, and outcome data.

Comparison between groups was made using the Mann–Whitney U test or Fisher’s exact test for continuous or categorical data, respectively. To evaluate the discriminatory ability of UACR for predicting CKD mortality, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. The optimal UACR cut-off value was determined using the Youden index from the ROC analysis and subsequently used to stratify dogs (UACR < 2.0 vs ≥ 2.0) for survival and progression analyses. The ROC-derived UACR cut-off was generated within this cohort and should be considered exploratory; performance estimates (eg, sensitivity/specificity) may be optimistic due to derivation and evaluation in the same dataset. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to evaluate time to death and time to CKD progression, with differences assessed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. In addition to all-cause mortality, a cause-specific survival analysis was performed for deaths due to renal disease. For this analysis, renal death was treated as the event of interest, and dogs that died from nonrenal causes were censored at the time of death. We acknowledge that this cause-specific approach assumes that the risk of the event of interest is independent of the competing risk of nonrenal death. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify predictors of death. Urine ammonia-to-creatinine ratio was specified a priori as the primary predictor of interest. To reduce overfitting relative to the number of observed events, a parsimonious multivariable model was constructed including serum phosphorus and UPC ratio, given their established prognostic importance in canine CKD, and serum bicarbonate based on its mechanistic relationship to renal ammoniagenesis and acid–base homeostasis. Variables were modeled as binary predictors using established clinical reference intervals or clinically relevant thresholds. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed graphically using log–log survival plots. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. A Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple testing for all analyses.

Results

Study cohort

Fifty client-owned dogs with stable IRIS stages II-IV CKD were enrolled between June 2022 and August 2024. The median age was 12 years (range: 1-16.5 years), and the median body weight was 15.1 kg (range: 1.8-67.0 kg). The study population included 19 spayed females, 3 intact females, 27 neutered males, and 1 intact male. Dogs were distributed across IRIS stages as follows: 41 (82%) in stage II, 7 (14%) in stage III, and 2 (4%) in stage IV (Table 1). At enrollment, all dogs had been eating a therapeutic renal diet for at least 28 days. Based on systolic blood pressure and UPC ratios, 18 (36%) were classified as nonproteinuric, 10 (20%) as borderline proteinuric, and 22 (44%) as proteinuric. Eleven (22%) dogs were normotensive, 17 (34%) were prehypertensive, 6 (12%) were hypertensive, and 16 (32%) were severely hypertensive. At baseline, 14 dogs (28%) met the criteria for metabolic acidosis (HCO3− ≤ 18 mEq/L).

Dogs with a UACR < 2.0 had a significantly shorter renal-specific MST of 270 days compared to 445 days for dogs with UACR ≥ 2.0 (P = .02).

Discussion

A major finding of this prospective study is that lower UACRs were strongly associated with shorter survival and accelerated disease progression. Dogs with a UACR < 2.0 at enrollment had more than a 3-fold greater risk of death and experienced substantially shorter survival times compared with dogs with higher UACR values. Similarly, dogs with a UACR < 2.0 progressed more rapidly, with the median time to progression markedly shorter than in dogs with UACR ≥ 2.0. These findings suggest that UACR might serve as a clinically meaningful prognostic biomarker in naturally occurring CKD in dogs and provide new insights into the role of renal ammoniagenesis in its pathogenesis and progression.

The potential prognostic power of UACR is rooted in renal physiology. The primary mechanism by which the kidney generates new bicarbonate to maintain acid–base homeostasis is through the metabolism of glutamine to ammonia.1,11,12 The net acid–base benefit of this process depends on the fate of the ammonia generated; for a net bicarbonate gain to occur, the ammonia must be secreted and excreted in the urine as ammonium.13 If the ammonia is instead returned to the systemic circulation via the renal vein, it is metabolized by the liver in a process that consumes the equivalent amount of bicarbonate generated by the kidney, resulting in no net acid excretion.13,14 Reductions in ammonia excretion with progressive kidney dysfunction, which occur in dogs, cats, and humans, are likely multifactorial and include a reduced number of functioning nephrons, impaired uptake of glutamine by proximal tubular cells, and a diminished NH₃ concentration gradient within the renal interstitium, which limits effective ammonia secretion into the tubular lumen.3,4,15,16 Impairment in ammoniagenesis likely contributes to the development of metabolic acidosis, a common complication of CKD in both humans and dogs and cats.2,17,18 A low UACR might therefore signify a failure of net acid excretion, leading to acid retention, which contributes to skeletal muscle catabolism and acceleration of CKD progression, as shown in people.3,4,19

Only 28% of the dogs met the criteria for overt metabolic acidosis (HCO3− < 18 mEq/L) at baseline. Despite this, UACR strongly predicted outcome, suggesting that impairment in ammonia excretion precedes measurable declines in serum bicarbonate and might identify a state of subclinical acid retention or impending metabolic acidosis.19 Therefore, UACR might represent an earlier, more sensitive indicator of impaired acid–base balance. The strong performance of UACR in ROC analysis further emphasizes its potential utility as a screening tool for risk stratification in clinical practice.

The multivariable Cox model was constructed to evaluate the independent prognostic value of UACR while maintaining model parsimony relative to the number of observed events. Urine ammonia-to-creatinine ratio was specified a priori as the primary predictor of interest. Serum phosphorus and UPC ratio were included based on their well-established prognostic importance in CKD, and serum bicarbonate was incorporated given its mechanistic relationship to renal ammoniagenesis and acid–base homeostasis.19,20 Within this framework, UACR < 2.0 remained independently associated with greater risk of death in the multivariable model, whereas conventional markers such as serum phosphorus and bicarbonate did not retain independent significance. Urine protein-to-creatinine < 1.0 emerged as a protective factor. This pattern suggests that impaired ammonia excretion reflects a distinct pathophysiologic domain centered on tubular ammoniagenesis and net acid excretion that is not fully captured by traditional markers of mineral metabolism or glomerular barrier integrity. Importantly, UACR also remained significantly associated with renal-specific mortality in cause-specific analyses, supporting its biological relevance to progressive renal dysfunction rather than serving merely as a nonspecific marker of systemic illness. Collectively, these findings suggest that UACR might represent a clinically meaningful indicator of tubular functional reserve and compensatory capacity, providing complementary prognostic insight beyond existing biomarkers in canine CKD.

Our findings concerning the UPC ratio reinforce the well-established role of proteinuria as a critical, independent prognostic indicator for survival and progression in dogs with CKD. Proteinuria, particularly of glomerular origin, reflects abnormal leakage across the glomerular filtration barrier and is mechanistically linked to accelerated CKD progression through the resultant tubulointerstitial inflammation and fibrosis.21 The current IRIS guidelines classify CKD into substages based on UPC ratio severity, with a threshold of UPC ratio ≥ 0.5 defining the proteinuric substage and suggesting the initiation of anti-proteinuric therapy. In our study, the comparison using this standard IRIS cut-off UPC ratio ≥ 0.5) did not yield a statistically significant difference in median survival time (MST = 227 days vs 445 days; P = .122). However, when a higher threshold was applied, a UPC ratio ≥ 1.0 was significantly associated with a markedly shorter median survival time (MST = 161 days; P = .025) compared to dogs with a UPC ratio < 1.0 (MST = 355 days). This finding is consistent with other veterinary studies, which have similarly reported a higher threshold, specifically UPC ratio values ≥ 1.0 as a more robust, independent cut-off for identifying the subgroup at a significantly greater risk of uremic crisis and death.20 The prognostic separation observed at the UPC ratio ≥ 1.0 threshold in our early-stage cohort underscores a continuum of risk that might accelerate disproportionately as protein loss intensifies, suggesting that the most severe prognostic risk is not captured until this higher level of protein loss is reached.

From a clinical perspective, these findings raise the possibility that UACR could be used alongside UPC ratio and IRIS staging to guide therapeutic decision-making. In human CKD, alkali supplementation reduced the rate of kidney function decline and improved survival, and there is growing interest in similar strategies in veterinary medicine.22 Dogs with low UACR (<2.0) may represent a subset in whom the buffering capacity of renal ammoniagenesis is already exhausted or impaired, and might particularly benefit from early alkali therapy, dietary modification to reduce net acid load, or more intensive monitoring. At the same time, our results reinforce the central importance of controlling proteinuria, as maintaining a UPC ratio < 1.0 appears protective in this cohort, consistent with prior evidence.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, UACR was measured at a single baseline time point, preventing assessment of intra-individual variability or longitudinal trends in ammonia excretion. Second, the UACR cut-off was derived using ROC analysis within this cohort. As this represents the first available canine dataset evaluating UACR as a prognostic biomarker in this manner, no pre-specified or externally validated threshold exists. Consequently, performance estimates and risk stratification based on this ROC-derived cut-off may be optimistic due to derivation and evaluation in the same dataset. The identified threshold should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Prospective studies in independent cohorts are required to validate and calibrate a clinically actionable UACR cut-off. Third, although all dogs were maintained on a therapeutic renal diet, other aspects of case management, such as antihypertensive and antiproteinuric therapy, were clinician-dependent and may have influenced outcomes. Fourth, our survival analysis for renal-specific case fatality utilized a cause-specific approach where nonrenal deaths were treated as censored data. This method might overestimate the cumulative incidence of renal death as it does not account for competing risks in the same manner as a subdistribution hazards model (eg, Fine–Gray analysis). Consequently, the reported renal-specific survival estimates should be interpreted with caution, as dogs that died of nonrenal causes were removed from the risk set rather than being maintained as a competing event. Fifth, we did not systematically record body condition score or muscle condition score, which are recognized prognostic factors in dogs with CKD and might affect creatinine concentration and nitrogen balance. Finally, acid–base status was assessed solely by serum bicarbonate, and venous blood gas analysis and urine pH was unavailable, limiting detailed characterization of metabolic acidosis. Fifth, our inclusion criteria required inadequate urine concentrating ability for CKD diagnosis, which may have excluded dogs with primarily glomerular disease that still retained urine concentrating capacity despite having clinically relevant stages II-III CKD. In addition, the study cohort included relatively few IRIS stage IV dogs, limiting the extrapolation of these findings to dogs with end-stage renal disease. However, this might also represent a strength when evaluating predictors of progression, as identifying prognostic markers earlier in the course of disease is arguably more clinically useful. Finally, it is possible that some dogs with actual CKD progression were not detected using the diagnostic criteria applied in this study. Progression was defined by changes in serum creatinine concentration, a noninvasive marker of renal function widely adopted in veterinary nephrology and used as a progression endpoint in prior studies, but this approach may lack sensitivity to subtle or early changes in glomerular filtration rate.23

In conclusion, lower UACR values (<2.0) are independently associated with reduced survival and CKD progression in dogs. We propose that UACR might serve as a clinically useful and noninvasive biomarker to identify dogs with impaired net acid excretion which might benefit from early, targeted alkaline therapeutic intervention.
