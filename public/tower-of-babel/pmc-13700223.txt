Electrically assisted bicycles in adults: a scoping review of health outcomes and relevance to rehabilitation
Authors: João Pedro de Santana Silva, Adriele de Morais Nunes, Ricardo Rodrigues da Silva, Renato Max Faria, Saint-Clair Gomes Bernardes Neto, Íllia Nadinne Dantas Florentino Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectrically assisted bicycles (e-bikes) have gained attention as a low-barrier strategy for promoting physical activity among individuals with different levels of exercise tolerance. Although most research has been conducted in community and active-transport settings, some outcomes may be relevant to rehabilitation. This scoping review mapped e-bikes among adults and synthesized health outcomes with potential relevance to rehabilitation.MethodsFollowing JBI methodology and PRISMA-ScR reporting guidance, we searched major health databases and supplementary sources from inception to 25 September 2025. We included experimental and observational studies involving adults aged ≥ 18 years who used pedal-assist e-bikes. No restriction was imposed on intervention or exposure duration. Two reviewers independently screened the records, extracted the data, and assessed methodological quality using the Effective Public Health Practice Project tool.ResultsOf 8,242 records identified, 14 studies were included. Most involved healthy adults and were conducted in Europe, with few studies including clinical populations. E-bike protocols varied widely, but several elicited moderate-intensity physiological responses and were associated with greater cycling participation and lower perceived exertion. Evidence on metabolic, cognitive, and psychological outcomes was limited. All included studies received an overall weak methodological quality rating. Study duration ranged from single-session assessments to nine-month interventions, while one community program operated for approximately 21 months.ConclusionsE-bikes may offer a feasible, low-barrier means of promoting physical activity and cycling participation while reducing perceived exertion. However, the evidence remains exploratory because most studies involved small samples and healthy participants, and few were conducted in clinical rehabilitation settings. Further controlled trials are needed before e-bikes can be routinely recommended for rehabilitation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01904-5.Keywords: Electrically assisted bicycles, Rehabilitation, Chronic diseases

Introduction

Rehabilitation programs are effective non-pharmacological interventions that complement the management of chronic diseases. In conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure, stroke, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, arthritis, chronic pain syndromes, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and cancer, exercise training should be individualized, and professional supervision may be beneficial.

Aerobic exercise is a core component of rehabilitation and is used to reduce disease-related symptoms and improve physical functioning [1]. Common modalities include walking, running, cycling, and swimming. Cycling may improve aerobic capacity, muscle strength, and overall physical well-being [2]. The World Health Organization recommends at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity or 75 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic physical activity per week, or an equivalent combination of both, to promote health and prevent chronic diseases [3].

Electrically assisted bicycles (e-bikes) have emerged as an alternative for individuals with limited exercise tolerance. Electrical assistance may enable users to cycle for longer periods and cover greater distances, thereby supporting continued participation in physical activity during and after rehabilitation [4, 5]. E-bike use has also been associated with greater enjoyment than conventional cycling while still eliciting meaningful energy expenditure and power output [6].

A systematic review by Bourne et al [7]. found moderate evidence that e-bike use may improve cardiorespiratory fitness in physically inactive individuals. The reported physiological responses were consistent with moderate-intensity exercise, ranging from 67.1% to 79.1% of maximal heart rate and from 51% to 75% of peak or maximal oxygen uptake. However, the use of e-bikes in clinical and rehabilitation contexts has not been comprehensively mapped.

Therefore, this scoping review aimed to map e-bike use among adults, describe the reported protocols, and synthesize health outcomes with potential relevance to rehabilitation. It also aimed to identify gaps in the evidence and determine the extent to which current findings may inform future clinical and rehabilitation research.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews and reported according to the PRISMA-ScR guidance [8]. A protocol document was made publicly available through an Open Science Framework project on 22 November 2025, after study screening, data extraction, and data analysis had been completed (https://osf.io/pknm4). Therefore, the document should be considered retrospectively posted rather than prospectively registered. Because it was made available after completion of the review procedures, it did not function as a prespecified protocol against which deviations could be formally assessed.

The methods reported in the present manuscript reflect the procedures actually performed during the review.

Search strategy

The following databases were searched: MEDLINE via PubMed, Embase, SciELO, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Web of Science. Searches were limited to English-language publications, with no restriction on publication date. The initial search was conducted on July 1, 2025, and the final update was performed on September 25, 2025.

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms and was adapted to the syntax and indexing characteristics of each database. The complete search strategy used for MEDLINE via PubMed is presented in Table 1. To identify additional potentially eligible records not retrieved through the bibliographic database searches, OpenGrey and Google Scholar were searched using the term “electrically assisted bicycle.” These sources were used as supplementary search tools; however, grey literature documents were not eligible for inclusion. Only studies published as peer-reviewed journal articles that met all other eligibility criteria were included in the review.

Detailed search strategies for all databases, database coverage periods, and dates of the final searches are provided in Supplementary Table S1. All databases were searched from inception to September 25, 2025. For Google Scholar, results were sorted by relevance, and the first 20 pages were screened.

Studies were eligible if they: (1) included adults aged ≥ 18 years; (2) evaluated pedal-operated e-bikes that required active cycling and provided additional assistance through an electric motor; (3) used an experimental or observational design; (4) reported at least one health-, exercise-, or participation-related outcome potentially relevant to rehabilitation, such as physical activity, exercise intensity, oxygen uptake, heart rate, perceived exertion, metabolic responses, muscular or biomechanical demands, functional capacity, feasibility, adherence, or psychological outcomes; and (5) were published in a peer-reviewed journal. OpenGrey and Google Scholar were searched only as supplementary sources to identify additional peer-reviewed journal articles. No minimum or maximum intervention, exposure, or follow-up duration was required for inclusion.

Studies were not required to have been conducted within a formal rehabilitation program. Non-clinical and community-based studies were eligible when they provided information relevant to exercise prescription, physiological demand, functional participation, or the feasibility of future rehabilitation applications.

Studies using exclusively qualitative designs were excluded. Mixed-methods studies were eligible only when they included a clearly identifiable quantitative component that met all other eligibility criteria. For these studies, only quantitative data were extracted and included in the synthesis. Qualitative findings were not used to support the health-outcome synthesis.

Study screening and selection

A standardized data-charting spreadsheet was developed by the review team based on the review questions and eligibility criteria. The searches yielded 8,242 records. After 69 duplicates were removed, 8,173 records remained for title and abstract screening.

Two reviewers (JPSS and ANM) independently screened titles and abstracts against the predefined eligibility criteria. At this stage, 8,100 records were excluded.

The remaining 73 full-text articles were independently assessed by the same reviewers.

Following full-text assessment, 59 articles were excluded because of an ineligible population, intervention, study design, or absence of relevant outcomes.

Fourteen studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the review.

Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. When consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (INDFL) adjudicated. The study selection process is presented in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

Data extraction and quality assessment

An adapted version of the Cochrane Data Extraction Form was used. The extracted information included bibliographic details, study aims, design, year and country of publication, participant characteristics, health conditions, e-bike intervention or exposure characteristics, outcomes, and main findings. For mixed-methods studies, data extraction was restricted to the quantitative component relevant to the review questions.

Two reviewers (JP and AN) independently extracted the data using the predefined form. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. No contact with the authors of the studies included was required to obtain missing or additional information.

The extracted data were synthesized narratively according to study characteristics, participant characteristics, e-bike protocols, and reported health outcomes.

Methodological quality assessment

Although methodological quality assessment is not mandatory in scoping reviews, it was conducted to contextualize the robustness of the available evidence and identify recurrent methodological limitations in this emerging field. The assessment was not used as an eligibility criterion, and all studies meeting the inclusion criteria were retained regardless of their methodological rating.

Methodological quality was assessed using the Effective Public Health Practice Project Quality Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies (EPHPP) [9]. The six domains contributing to the global rating were selection bias, study design, confounders, blinding, data collection methods, and withdrawals and dropouts. Intervention integrity and the appropriateness of the statistical analyses were also evaluated qualitatively but did not contribute to the global rating.

Two reviewers (JP and AN) independently assessed methodological quality. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. Each study received an overall rating of strong, moderate, or weak according to the EPHPP criteria. The assessment results were synthesized narratively and presented in Table 3.

Results

Study selection

The searches identified 8,242 records. After 69 duplicates were removed, 8,173 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 8,100 were excluded, and 73 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility.

Following full-text assessment, 59 articles were excluded because of an ineligible population, intervention, study design, or absence of relevant outcomes. Fourteen studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the review. The study selection process is presented in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

Of the 14 included studies, 12 (85.7%) were published within the previous 10 years. Ten studies (71.4%) were conducted in Europe, two in the United States, one in Australia, and one in Japan.

Eight studies used randomized experimental designs, including parallel-group, crossover, and repeated-measures approaches. Four used non-randomized intervention, pre–post, pilot, or feasibility designs. One was a prospective observational cohort study, and one was a descriptive evaluation of a community-based pilot program. Study characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Methodological quality of the included studies

The methodological quality assessment is presented in Table 3. All studies received an overall weak rating because each had at least two domains rated as weak.

Selection bias was rated as weak in 12 studies (85.7%) and moderate in two (14.3%). Study design was rated as strong in nine studies (64.3%), moderate in two (14.3%), and weak in three (21.4%). Confounder control was rated as strong in nine studies (64.3%) and weak in five (35.7%). Blinding was rated as weak in all studies.

Data collection methods were rated as strong in eight studies (57.1%), moderate in five (35.7%), and weak in one (7.1%). The withdrawals and dropouts domain received the most consistently favorable ratings, with 12 studies (85.7%) rated as strong, one (7.1%) as moderate, and one (7.1%) as weak.

Overall, the most frequent methodological limitations involved selection bias and blinding, whereas study design, confounder control, data collection methods, and reporting of withdrawals and dropouts generally received more favorable ratings.

Participant characteristics

Across the 14 studies, 2,317 participants were enrolled or included at baseline. When reported, mean age ranged from 22.8 to 64.0 years. Most studies included both men and women, although some samples were restricted to one sex.

Seven studies included generally healthy adults or community participants without a specific clinical diagnosis. Four focused on clinical populations: two included individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus, one included individuals with coronary artery disease, and one included women following breast cancer treatment. Other populations included income-eligible community residents, parents of young children, postal workers, recreational cyclists, sedentary women, and habitual bicycle users.

Most studies were conducted in community-based, outdoor, occupational, or free-living settings, whereas fewer recruited participants from clinical or rehabilitation-related contexts.

Intervention characteristics

E-bike protocols and exposure conditions varied substantially. Five studies used single-session or short-term experimental assessments involving predefined routes or cycling conditions. The remaining studies evaluated free-living use, community programs, e-bike loans, or structured interventions lasting from two weeks to nine months. The community pilot program described by Goins et al. was conducted from August 2022 to May 2024.

Protocol dose was defined according to cycling distance, session duration, weekly frequency, or total exposure period. Predetermined routes ranged from approximately 46 m in a simulated postal-delivery circuit to 10 km in outdoor cycling trials. Structured interventions included prescriptions such as at least 30 min of cycling on three days per week, two supervised sessions per week, or unrestricted use during an e-bike loan period. Several studies did not prescribe a minimum frequency, duration, or distance.

Most protocols were conducted in outdoor, community-based, occupational, or free-living settings. A smaller number used controlled laboratory, simulated occupational, or athletics-track conditions. Electrical assistance ranged from standardized low, medium, or high support levels to participant-selected assistance. In several studies, the assistance setting was neither standardized nor reported. Protocol and exposure characteristics are presented in Table 4.

Health outcomes

The included studies assessed physical activity, cardiorespiratory and perceptual responses, metabolic and vascular outcomes, muscular and biomechanical responses. Quantitative indicators of feasibility, including retention, adherence, and e-bike use, were generally favorable in the included clinical studies.

Several studies reported greater cycling participation, duration, distance, or moderate-to-vigorous physical activity following access to e-bikes. However, Haufe et al. found lower cycling-related moderate-to-vigorous physical activity among e-bike users than among conventional cyclists. In Goins et al., the absence of baseline data and a comparator group precluded conclusions regarding changes in physical activity.

Experimental studies generally reported physiologically meaningful responses, often within the moderate-intensity range. Higher levels of electrical assistance generally reduced oxygen uptake, heart rate, power output, muscle activation, blood lactate, and perceived exertion.

Evidence on metabolic outcomes was limited. Alessio et al. reported increased time with glucose between 70 and 120 mg/dL and reduced carotid–femoral pulse wave velocity, but no significant changes in fasting glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR, HbA1c, blood lipids, blood pressure, or anthropometric measures. Bourne et al. reported exploratory findings favoring the intervention, although the study was not powered to determine efficacy.

Feasibility and acceptability were generally favorable in clinical populations. One controlled study involving older adults also reported improvements in selected cognitive and mental health outcomes. The outcomes and main findings are synthesized in Table 5.

Discussion

This scoping review mapped the use of electrically assisted bicycles across populations and settings relevant to rehabilitation. Most studies were conducted in high-income countries, particularly in Europe, where cycling infrastructure, active mobility policies, and access to e-bikes may have facilitated both implementation and research. This geographic concentration limits the transferability of the findings to low- and middle-income settings, where infrastructure, cost, climate, traffic safety, and patterns of bicycle use may differ substantially [10, 15, 16].

Most studies involved healthy or community-based participants, whereas few included clinical populations, such as individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus or coronary artery disease and women following breast cancer treatment [12, 14, 20]. E-bikes were therefore investigated mainly as tools for active mobility and physical activity promotion rather than as structured therapeutic interventions. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as evidence potentially relevant to rehabilitation rather than as direct evidence of effectiveness within formal rehabilitation programs.

The protocols varied considerably in duration, frequency, cycling distance, setting, and level of electrical assistance [13, 21]. Some studies used fixed routes or acute experimental conditions, whereas others evaluated free-living use, e-bike loans, or longer community-based programs. Assistance settings, exercise intensity, progression criteria, and physiological monitoring were also inconsistently reported. This heterogeneity limits direct comparisons and prevents the definition of standardized dose–response parameters for rehabilitation.

Most protocols were conducted outdoors. Findings from the included studies indicate that outdoor e-bike use may support cycling participation and engagement. In addition, contextual evidence outside the present review has suggested that outdoor e-bike use may provide enjoyment, autonomy, environmental stimulation, and opportunities for social participation [24]. However, these factors were rarely measured directly in the included studies, and their influence on adherence and long-term engagement remains unclear.

Several protocols elicited physiological responses consistent with moderate-intensity physical activity. However, this does not indicate that e-bike interventions are standardized or clinically validated. Future studies should report frequency, duration, intensity, route characteristics, level of electrical assistance, progression criteria, and monitoring procedures in sufficient detail to support replication and clinical translation [25].

The most consistent findings were related to cycling participation, duration, and distance traveled. Electrical assistance may reduce some of the physical demands of conventional cycling, enabling users to cycle for longer periods or cover greater distances with lower perceived exertion [10, 11, 18]. These characteristics may be particularly relevant for individuals with reduced exercise tolerance. Nevertheless, the small number of studies involving clinical populations prevents firm conclusions regarding therapeutic effectiveness [14, 20].

Cardiorespiratory responses were generally compatible with moderate-intensity aerobic exercise. Studies assessing oxygen uptake, heart rate, cycling time, and perceived exertion indicated that e-bike use can provide a physiologically meaningful stimulus while reducing cardiorespiratory and muscular demands compared with conventional cycling. These responses varied according to electrical assistance, route characteristics, participant fitness, and protocol design and should therefore be considered when translating e-bike use into rehabilitation practice [21, 22].

Evidence regarding metabolic outcomes was limited. Alessio et al. reported an increase in the percentage of time with glucose between 70 and 120 mg/dL after one week of e-bike use, whereas fasting glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR, HbA1c, blood lipids, blood pressure, and anthropometric measures remained unchanged [11]. A reduction in carotid–femoral pulse wave velocity was also observed, indicating a favorable vascular response. Bourne et al. reported exploratory findings favoring the intervention for some glycemic outcomes, but the pilot trial was not powered to establish efficacy [12]. The current evidence is therefore insufficient to support conclusions regarding sustained improvements in glycemic control or insulin sensitivity.

Cognitive and psychosocial outcomes were evaluated less frequently. One controlled study involving older adults reported improvements in selected measures of executive function, processing speed, and mental health [17]. These effects may have resulted from electrical assistance, increased physical activity, outdoor exposure, or a combination of these factors. Additional controlled studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms involved.

The methodological quality assessment was important for interpreting the findings. All included studies received an overall weak rating, primarily because of recurrent limitations in selection bias and blinding. Ratings were more favorable for study design, confounder control, data collection methods, and reporting of withdrawals and dropouts. Accordingly, the findings were interpreted cautiously and were not considered sufficient to establish causal relationships or definitive clinical recommendations. Greater emphasis was placed on outcomes that were consistent across studies and supported by stronger individual methodological domains.

The evidence was also limited by small samples, acute or short-term protocols, uncontrolled designs, and inconsistent reporting of intervention characteristics. Although several studies used randomized crossover or repeated-measures designs, few were adequately powered to evaluate clinical effectiveness in rehabilitation populations. These limitations restrict causal interpretation, comparison across studies, and the development of evidence-based exercise prescriptions.

Clinical representation remains limited. Evidence is scarce for populations commonly referred to rehabilitation, including individuals with chronic respiratory or cardiovascular disease, neurological impairments, frailty, severe obesity, and postoperative functional limitations. These groups may have reduced exercise tolerance, balance impairments, or specific safety requirements. The feasibility and effectiveness of e-bike use in such populations cannot be inferred from findings obtained primarily in healthy adults.

The concentration of studies in high-income countries further limits generalizability. The feasibility of e-bike interventions may be influenced by cycling infrastructure, access and affordability, climate, traffic conditions, and healthcare organization. Context-specific research is therefore required before e-bikes can be broadly incorporated into rehabilitation services.

Research gaps and directions for future studies

Future research should prioritize adequately powered controlled trials comparing electrically assisted cycling with conventional cycling, stationary cycle ergometry, usual rehabilitation care, or other forms of aerobic exercise. Studies should include clearly defined clinical populations, standardized eligibility criteria, appropriate comparators, and follow-up periods sufficient to assess short- and long-term outcomes.

Protocols should report cycling frequency, duration, distance, intensity, electrical assistance, route characteristics, supervision, progression criteria, and adherence. Objective monitoring of heart rate, oxygen uptake, power output, blood pressure, and perceived exertion should be incorporated whenever feasible. Adverse events, falls, traffic-related incidents, mechanical problems, and reasons for withdrawal should also be reported systematically.

Further studies are required in populations with cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological, metabolic, and oncological conditions [11, 14]. Research in low- and middle-income countries is also needed to evaluate feasibility under different socioeconomic, infrastructural, climatic, and healthcare conditions.

In addition to physiological and functional outcomes, future studies should assess adherence, quality of life, psychological well-being, participation in daily activities, maintenance of physical activity after rehabilitation, accessibility, user preferences, and cost-effectiveness [21, 22]. These outcomes are necessary to determine whether e-bikes can be sustainably integrated into outpatient, community-based, and home-based rehabilitation programs.

Clinical implications and relevance for rehabilitation

E-bikes may provide an additional option for promoting aerobic activity among individuals who have difficulty tolerating conventional cycling. Electrical assistance can reduce relative muscular and cardiorespiratory demands while allowing longer exercise durations or greater travel distances. This may be useful when fatigue, symptoms, or low exercise tolerance limit participation [17, 20].

For physiotherapists and multidisciplinary rehabilitation teams, the level of electrical assistance could potentially be adjusted according to functional capacity, symptoms, route characteristics, and therapeutic goals. However, current studies do not provide standardized criteria for assistance levels, exercise progression, intensity prescription, or clinical eligibility. E-bike use should therefore be preceded by individualized assessment and accompanied by appropriate monitoring [11, 21].

E-bike interventions may also support the transition from supervised rehabilitation to community-based physical activity by integrating exercise into transportation and daily routines. Environmental and safety factors must nevertheless be considered, including cycling skills, balance, traffic exposure, route accessibility, weather conditions, infrastructure, and the individual’s ability to operate the bicycle safely.

At present, e-bikes should be considered a complementary option rather than a replacement for established rehabilitation modalities. Their routine clinical implementation requires stronger evidence regarding effectiveness, safety, adherence, accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and long-term maintenance of physical activity.

Conclusions

Electrically assisted bicycles may provide a feasible, low-barrier means of promoting physical activity, cycling participation, and longer travel distances with lower perceived exertion than conventional cycling. Several studies also reported cardiorespiratory responses consistent with moderate-intensity exercise.

However, the evidence remains limited by methodological heterogeneity, inconsistent reporting of intervention dose and electrical assistance, small samples, and the limited inclusion of clinical populations. Evidence regarding metabolic outcomes, safety, and long-term therapeutic effects is still insufficient.

Adequately powered controlled trials with standardized protocols, objective physiological monitoring, systematic safety assessment, and longer follow-up are required before e-bikes can be routinely integrated into evidence-based rehabilitation.
