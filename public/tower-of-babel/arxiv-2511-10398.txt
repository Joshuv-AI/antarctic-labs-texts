Spectral rigidity of Liouville tori
Joscha Henheik, Vadim Kaloshin, Yunzhe Li, Amir Vig

Abstract
. We show that Laplace isospectral deformations within a conformal class of generic Liouville metrics on the two-dimensional torus that are linear in the deformation parameter are necessarily trivial. Two of the main ingredients in our proof are a noncancellation result for the wave trace and an analysis of the second order variational formula for the energy functional associated to closed geodesics. Noncancellation allows us to detect parts of the length spectrum from the Laplace spectrum and conclude rational integrability for the deformed geodesic flow (Liouville metrics are folklorically conjectured to be the only Riemannian metrics with integrable geodesic flow on the torus). We then use the second variational formula to show how the preservation of a single rational torus is sufficient to conclude triviality of the deformation, assuming linearity. We also present some evidence that our hypothesis of linearity may indeed be necessary.



1.
Introduction and main result

Let
(X,g)
be a compact Riemannian manifold and
\Delta_{g}
its associated Laplace-Beltrami operator. We denote by
\text{Spec}(-\Delta_{g})
the set of eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{g}
, i.e.

\text{Spec}(-\Delta_{g})=\{\lambda^{2}\geq 0:\exists u:X\to\mathbb{R}\text{ such that }-\Delta_{g}u=\lambda^{2}u\}

Suppose
(g_{\varepsilon})=(g_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})}
is a one-parameter family of Riemannian metrics on
X
.
We say that the family
(g_{\varepsilon})
is
isospectral
if
\text{Spec}(-\Delta_{g_{\varepsilon}})=\text{Spec}(-\Delta_{g_{0}})
for all
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
.

In this paper, we study spectral rigidity of
Liouville metrics
on the two-dimensional torus. That is, we consider the case
X=\mathbb{T}^{2}
, and take
g_{0}
to be a Riemannian metric whose line element is of the form

(1)

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\big(1+f_{1}(x_{1})+f_{2}(x_{2})\big)(\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2}).

It is a well-known folklore conjecture that Liouville metrics are the only integrable metrics on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, which can be thought of as a closed manifold analogue of the famous Birkhoff conjecture (see Section
3
for the definition of integrability and Section
2
for a comparison with billiards).
Our main result, formulated below, roughly states the following:

Every isospectral deformation within a conformal class which is linear in the deformation parameter
\varepsilon
is necessarily trivial.

Theorem 1.1

(Main result)
.

Let
(g_{\varepsilon})
be a family of Riemannian metrics on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
of the form

(2)

\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{\varepsilon}=\big(1+f_{1}(x_{1})+f_{2}(x_{2})+\varepsilon U(x_{1},x_{2})\big)(\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2})\,,

where
f_{1},f_{2}
belong to a generic set of smooth functions and
U\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
is arbitrary. If the family
(g_{\varepsilon})
is isospectral for small
\varepsilon
, then
U\equiv 0
.

In the remainder of this introduction, we discuss our main result (Section
1.1
) and describe the main steps and ideas of its proof (Section
1.2
).

1.1.
Discussion of our main result

1.1.1.
Future directions

Recently, much of the literature on inverse spectral problems has focused on chaotic systems with or without boundary as well as integrable billiard dynamics in the plane. In future work, we hope to extend our rigidity result above to the following nondeformational setting:

Let

1.1.2.
On the linearity assumption in Theorem
1.1

While the linearity assumption in Theorem
1.1
may appear to be an artificial limitation, it might in fact be necessary, as the following example indicates.

Example 1.4

(Two rivers)
.

Consider the Euclidean metric
g_{0}=\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and consider a conformal perturbation of the form
g_{1}=(1+f(x_{2}))(\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2})
, where
f
is a smooth nonnegative and compactly supported function, depending only on the second coordinate. If (for simplicity)
f
is even and sufficiently small, then geodesics on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
which pass through the support of
f
must enter and exit at the same angle relative to the
x_{1}
axis. Now consider the metric (of revolution)
g_{2,c}=\big(1+f(x_{2}-c)+f(x_{2})\big)\big(\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2}\big)
, where
c
is a parameter which translates one copy of
f
so that
g_{0}
is perturbed along two, disjoint horizontal strips, which we call rivers (see Figure
1
). If
f
is sufficiently small, there are two types of closed geodesics on
(\mathbb{T}^{2},g_{2,c})
. There are orbits which pass through both rivers at a nonzero angle, swim across in a symmetric but curved fashion, and then exit back into the flat region, where they continue in a straight line. Then there are the orbits which swim downstream along the supports of
f(x_{2})
or
f(x_{2}-c)
. If one changes the constant
c
, it is clear that neither type of orbit changes its length, but for different
c\mod 1
corresponding to disjoint rivers, the metrics
g_{2,c}
are not isometric!

Figure 1
.
Two rivers (Example
1.4
): Perturbing the flat metric along horizontal blue strips (the
rivers
), we can then translate one of these rivers while preserving the length spectrum of the metric. More precisely, by varying the distance between the two blue regions, we produce nonisometric perturbations which are nonetheless length isospectral.

Since the Laplace spectrum is typically accessed via the length spectrum (see also Section
2
), this example suggests that there may exist length – but not Laplace – isospectral metrics in the family
(g_{2,c+\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
, which is clearly deformed nonlinearly in
\varepsilon
. However, this example might be ruled out as something ‘nongeneric’.

The construction of two rivers clearly generalizes to any finite number of (narrow) rivers. We also believe that there are more general examples which can be obtained by rearrangements. We plan to investigate these in future work. The idea to construct length isospectral metrics via rearrangements is inspired by unpublished work of Abbondandolo and Mazzucchelli. However, we should note that length isospectrality does not necessarily imply Laplace isospectrality, even under generic assumptions. We believe that the metrics in Example
1.4
are not Laplace isospectral.

In the Laplace spectral literature, constructions like that in Example
1.4
are not new. For example, Zelditch presents an example in
[
88
]
: Consider the round metric on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and add two disjoint bumps. Translating these bumps around
\mathbb{S}^{2}
clearly results in nonisometric metrics. The Laplace spectrum may very well change, but the
heat invariants
do not. The heat invariants are coefficients in the expansion

(3)

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\,e^{t\Delta}=\sum_{j}e^{-t\lambda_{j}^{2}}\sim t^{-d/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}t^{k},\quad\text{as}\,\,t\to 0.

It is known that these heat invariants have the form

(4)

\displaystyle a_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Theta_{k}[g]\,\mathrm{d}{\operatorname{Vol}}_{g},

for some universal polynomials in the curvature of the metric
g
, together with finitely many (depending on
k
) of its covariant derivatives. It might seem strange that identical heat invariants do not necessarily yield identical Laplace spectra
\lambda_{j}^{2}
, but the key point is that the right hand side of (
3
) is not in general a convergent sum, even after renormalizing by a power of
t
. Equality of
\text{Tr}\,e^{t\Delta}
for all
t
would indeed imply isospectrality, but its Taylor coefficients at
t=0
do not.

Theorem 1.6

(Rigidity by preservation of one rational invariant torus)
.

Let
(g_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})}
be a conformal family of Riemannian metrics on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
of the form

(5)

g_{\varepsilon}=(V(x_{1},x_{2})+\varepsilon U(x_{1},x_{2}))(\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2})\,,\qquad U,V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

\varepsilon

U\equiv 0
.

The basic idea underlying the proof of Theorem
1.6
is to consider a family of conformal metrics
g_{\varepsilon}=V_{\varepsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}x^{2}
with a corresponding family
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
of
g_{\varepsilon}
-geodesic segments having fixed end points, and analyze the

\varepsilon\mapsto E_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})=\int_{0}^{1}V_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t))\;|\dot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
\gamma_{\varepsilon}

If the

Formally expanding in powers of
\varepsilon

(6)

d^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(1)},\gamma^{(1)})+E^{(2)}(\gamma^{(0)})=0,

where we have used the notation

\gamma_{\varepsilon}=\gamma^{(0)}+\varepsilon\gamma^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3}),\qquad E_{\varepsilon}=E^{(0)}+\varepsilon E^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}E^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3}).

If
V_{\varepsilon}(x)=V_{0}(x)+\varepsilon U(x)
, i.e., a deformation which is linear in
\varepsilon

d^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(1)},\gamma^{(1)})=0

We remark that this is the

On the other hand, by minimality of
\gamma^{(0)}

Combining this with the series expansion of

\gamma^{(0)}
as its critical point.
Finally, by comparing the associated Euler-Lagrange equations for

Finally, we use the assumed existence of a rational torus to find, for each

Background and related results

Inverse spectral problems arise in many contexts. Two of the most well studied are:

(1)

Can you hear the shape of a drum?
This question, posed by Mark Kac in 1966
[
54

, asks whether or not one can recover the underlying geometry of a Riemannian manifold (drum)
(X,g)

2

2

Here,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta_{g}u=\lambda^{2}u,\\
Bu|_{\partial X}=0,\end{cases}

(2)

Can you recover the geometry of a domain (i.e. a manifold with or without boundary) from knowledge of the lengths of closed geodesics (which we denote by
\mathcal{L}(X,g)

Each of these questions has a number of close relatives. For example, recovering a potential energy function from knowledge of its associated Schrödinger spectrum is formally analogous to (
1
). As another example, accompanying the length spectrum in (
2
) is the so called
marked

In physics, when one solves the wave or Schrödinger equations to obtain the wave functions for a quantum particle, separation of variables leads one to study eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the Laplacian. For this reason, the class of inverse problems which resemble (
1
) are said to be “quantum” inverse problems. The famous Bohr correspondence principle asserts that in the high-energy regime (large eigenvalue or small Planck’s constant), the behavior of quantum wave functions should resemble that of the underlying classical dynamics, i.e., solutions to Hamilton’s equations for the relevant classical Hamiltonian. Inverse problems pertaining to the length spectrum, as in (
2

In the remainder of this section, we discuss the necessary background in addition to several related results on quantum and classical inverse spectral problems. For further details and additional references, we refer the reader to the surveys

2.1.
From quantum to classical inverse problems: the Poisson relation

There is a close mathematical relation between (
1
) and (
2
) of semiclassical nature, which, to some extent, formalizes the Bohr correspondence principle. This is most evident through the
Poisson relation
, which dictates that

(7)

\displaystyle\text{SingSupp}\,\,\text{Tr}\,\left(\cos t\sqrt{-\Delta}\right)\subset\pm{\mathcal{L}(X,g)}.

The left hand side of (
7
) is the distributional trace of the wave group

\displaystyle w(t):=\sum\cos t\lambda_{j}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}),

where
\lambda_{j}
are eigenvalues of the Laplacian. This is an entirely spectral quantity, while the length spectrum on the right hand side of (
7
) is purely geometric.

To tackle the quantum (Laplace spectral) inverse problem, one often uses the Poisson relation (
7
) together with its more refined counterpart, the Poisson summation formula. To describe the Poisson summation formula, let
L\in\mathcal{L}(X,g)
be isolated and assume that all periodic geodesics
\gamma
of length
L
are nondegenerate in the sense that
\det(\text{Id}-P_{\gamma})\neq 0
, where
P_{\gamma}
is the linearized Poincaré map associated to
\gamma
. Denote by
\widehat{\rho}(t)
a compactly supported test function which is
1
in a neighborhood of
L
and contains no other lengths of closed geodesics in its support. The Poisson summation formula then reads

(8)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{it\lambda}\widehat{\rho}(t)w(t)\mathrm{d}t\sim\sum_{\gamma:\text{length}(\gamma)=L}\frac{L_{\gamma}^{\#}i^{\sigma_{\gamma}}}{|\det(I-P_{\gamma})|^{1/2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}B_{\gamma,j}\lambda^{-j}\quad\text{as}\,\,\lambda\to\infty,

where
\sigma_{\gamma}
is the Morse index of
\gamma
and
L_{\gamma}^{\#}
is its primitive period. The coefficients
B_{\gamma,j}
contain geometric information about the underlying manifold in the form of integrals of polynomials in the metric and its derivatives over each closed geodesic of length
L
. The Poisson summation formula (
8
) is a generalization of the Selberg trace formula, which was developed for hyperbolic surfaces in 1956 (
[
77
]
). It was followed by the Gutzwiller trace formula in the physics literature, which relates the eigenvalues of a Schrödinger operator to the underlying classical Hamiltonian dynamics (
[
43
]
). In the mathematical literature, the trace formula was first proved for Riemannian manifolds by Colin de Verdiere (
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
) and Duistermaat-Guillemin (
[

]

[

and Guillemin-Melrose (

) to the case of manifolds with boundary.

The invariants
B_{\gamma,j}
in (
8

, which are the coefficients of a direct expansion of

, as opposed to its Fourier transform. If all geodesics are isolated and nondegenerate, the wave trace is asymptotic to

\displaystyle w(t)\sim\begin{cases}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Re}\left(a_{0,k-d}(t+i0)^{k-d}\right)&\text{at}\,\,t=0,\\
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Re}\left(a_{L,k-1}(t-L+i0)^{k-1}\log(t-L+i0)\right)&\text{at}\,\,t=L\in\mathcal{L}(X,g)\backslash\{0\},\end{cases}

modulo

\displaystyle a_{L,j}=\sum_{\text{length}(\gamma)=L}a_{\gamma,j}

accounting for the contributions of different geodesics when the length spectrum is multiple. We note that there are more general formulas when periodic orbits are degenerate but belong to a clean submanifold of fixed points for the geodesic flow.

2.2.
Applying the Poisson relation: Obstacles and related results

Generically, the wave invariants

are nonzero and one can read off the length spectrum from the Laplace spectrum. If the length spectrum is multiple, different periodic orbits of the same length could have identical wave invariants except for Maslov indices which differ by
2\mod 4

[

,
[

One important case in which there is no risk of cancellation is in the setting of negative curvature. Since there are no conjugate points, all Morse indices are trivial and Laplace isospectral domains must also be length isospectral. Guillemin and Kazhdan exploited this relation to show deformational spectral rigidity of negatively curved surfaces (
[
38
]
,
[
39
]
). If the length spectrum is constant along a deformation
(X,g_{\varepsilon})
, then

(10)

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\dot{g}\,\mathrm{d}s=0

for each closed geodesic
\gamma
, where the first variation
\dot{g}=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\big|_{\varepsilon=0}g_{\varepsilon}
of the metric is a symmetric
2
-tensor. Rigidity then follows from injectivity of the so called
X-ray transform
(
10

[
29
]
with the X-ray transform replaced by the linearized isospectral operator. In general, existence of conjugate points complicates the analysis of both the wave trace and the X-ray transform, often jeopardizing its injectivity. For example, on the sphere, any odd function is in the kernel of the X-ray transform. For integrable systems, there are almost always conjugate points.

In the papers
[
55
]
and
[
60
]
, it was shown that the length spectrum can indeed be obscured by the wave trace if distinct orbits ”conspire” against one another to cause cancellations in the sum (
9

[

They do however have the same length spectrum, not accounting for multiplicity. She also produces a continuous family of isospectral but nonisometric two step nilmanifolds in
[

Theorem 4.11

Let

be a closed interval in the length spectrum of

Proof.

Remark 4.12

This is a geodesic flow analogue of the results in

and

A Marvizi-Melrose type formula for the wave trace follows immediately from Theorem

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
I
be a closed interval in the length spectrum which does not contain the length of any periodic orbit having a pair of conjugate points. Then, the wave trace of
(\mathbb{T}^{2},g_{\varepsilon})
has an oscillatory integral representation near
I
of the form

\displaystyle w(t)=\operatorname{Re}\left\{e^{i\pi\frac{1-d}{4}}\sum_{\gamma\in C(I)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}e^{i\vartheta(\psi_{\gamma}^{2}(x)-t^{2})}b_{\gamma}(x,\vartheta)dxd\vartheta\right\},

where
b_{\gamma}
is an elliptic Kohn-Nirenberg symbol of order
1/2
with positive principal part
b_{\gamma,0}>0
and
\psi_{\gamma}(x)
is the “
\gamma
-loop function,” which returns the length of the unique geodesic loop based at
x
in the homology class
[\gamma]
. The index set
C(I)
is the collection of all homology classes which have periodic orbits of length belonging to the interval
I
.

A similar microlocal trace formula for isolated but potentially degenerate periodic orbits appears in
[
73
]
, where the integral over the base manifold
X=\mathbb{T}^{2}
is replaced by one over a Poincaré section with a corresponding symplectic density. In that case, the generating function for the first return map may not coincide with the loop function – which does not necessarily exist – and may depend on additional fiber variables, precisely because of the possibility of conjugate points. In the absence of conjugate points, we found it easier to follow the standard WKB approach so that we could be precise about the nature of Morse indices arising in the trace formula.

4.5.
Noncancellation

We now move on to show noncancellation in the wave trace. It is actually more convenient to consider decay (or lack thereof) of the resolvent trace, whose absence is equivalent to the existence of singularities in the wave trace. Define

\displaystyle I(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{it\lambda}\widehat{\rho}(t)w(t)dt.

This coincides with the trace of the
smoothed resolvent

(36)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{it\lambda}\widehat{\rho}(t)E(t,x,y)dt=\rho(\mu)\ast\mu R(\mu,x,y)\big|_{\mu=\lambda}\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)_{\lambda}\times X\times X),

with
R(\mu,x,y)
being the Schwartz kernel of
(-\Delta-\mu^{2})^{-1}
; equivalently,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{it\lambda}\widehat{\rho}(t)\cos t\sqrt{-\Delta}dt=2\pi\left(\rho(\lambda+\sqrt{-\Delta})+\rho(\lambda-\sqrt{-\Delta})\right)

is a smoothing operator when
\rho\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
. Note that
w(t)
is
C^{\infty}
on
\text{supp}\widehat{\rho}
if and only if
I(\lambda)=O(\lambda^{-\infty})
. In order to show that
w(t)
is indeed singular (i.e. that there are no cancellations), we will show that
I(\lambda)\neq O(\lambda^{-\infty})
. For the remainder of this section, we will let
(X,g)=(\mathbb{T}^{2},g_{\varepsilon})
for notational simplicity.

Lemma 4.14
.

For
I
as in Theorem
4.13
and
\widehat{\rho}
supported near
I
, equal to one there and containing no other lengths of closed geodesics in its support, we have

(37)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{it\lambda}\widehat{\rho}(t)w(t)dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X}\widehat{\rho}(t)e^{it\lambda}E_{N}(t,x,x)dxdt+O(\lambda^{\frac{3d}{2}-N}),\quad N\geq\frac{3d}{2}+1.

Proof.

If
\widetilde{X}
were compact, the lemma would follow immediately from Proposition
4.4
. Instead, we use the cutoffs in Lemma
4.8
. We have

\displaystyle\int_{X}E(t,x,x)dx=

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma}\int_{\widetilde{X}}\chi_{\text{CdV}}(\widetilde{x})E(t,\widetilde{x},\gamma\widetilde{x})d\widetilde{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma}\bigg(\int_{\widetilde{X}}\chi_{\text{CdV}}(\widetilde{x})(E(t,\widetilde{x},\gamma\widetilde{x})-E_{N}(t,\widetilde{x},\gamma\widetilde{x}))

\displaystyle+\chi_{\text{CdV}}(\widetilde{x})E_{N}(t,\widetilde{x},\gamma\widetilde{x})\bigg)d\widetilde{x}.

For bounded
t
, there are only finitely many terms in the sum over
\Gamma
, so the first term

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma}\int_{\widetilde{X}}\chi_{\text{CdV}}(\widetilde{x})(E(t,\widetilde{x},\gamma\widetilde{x})-E_{N}(t,\widetilde{x},\gamma\widetilde{x}))d\widetilde{x}

is locally
C^{N-\frac{3d}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
and its Fourier transform decays as stated in the Lemma. The latter term is exactly
\int_{X}E_{N}(t,x,x)dx
, interpreted in the sense of distributions.
∎

Remark 4.15
.

As before, restricting
E(t,x,y)
to the diagonal implicitly makes the assumption that its wavefront set does not meet the conormal bundle of the diagonal. While this is indeed true for
t\neq 0
, it is more convenient to interpret the above formula by taking the trace
after
evaluating the
t\to\lambda
Fourier transform, in which case the regularized resolvent trace is always well defined.

Proof of Theorem

We are now ready to use the parametrix construction above to prove length isospectrality in Theorem

to

Lemma 4.16
.

The regularized resolvent trace has the form

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{it\lambda}w(t)dt=\left(\sum_{\gamma\in C(\ell_{0})}\int_{\widetilde{X}}e^{i\lambda\psi_{\gamma}(x)}A_{\gamma}(x)dx\right)\lambda^{\frac{d-1}{2}}+O(\lambda^{-\frac{d-3}{2}}).

where
A_{\gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\widetilde{X})
has compact support.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\pm}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{X}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{it\lambda\pm i\vartheta(\widetilde{\psi}^{2}(x,\gamma x)-t^{2})}|t|\widehat{\rho}(t)W_{k,\pm}(x,\gamma x)\vartheta_{+}^{\frac{d-1}{2}-k}d\vartheta dxdt.

We may insert cutoff
\chi\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)_{\vartheta})
which vanishes for
\vartheta<1
and is identically equal to one for
\vartheta>2
at the expense of modifying
E(t,x,y)
by a smooth kernel which contributes a rapidly decaying contribution to the regularized resolvent. Changing variables
\vartheta\mapsto\eta=\vartheta/\lambda
, we get an oscillatory integral of the form

\displaystyle\lambda\sum_{\pm}\sum_{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X}e^{i\lambda\Phi_{\pm}(t,x,\eta)}|t|\widehat{\rho}(t)B_{\gamma,\pm}(\lambda\eta,t,x)dxd\eta dt,

where
B_{\gamma,\pm}
is defined by Borel summing the amplitudes
\chi(\eta/\lambda)W_{k,\pm}
over
k
and

\displaystyle\Phi_{\pm}(t,x,\eta)=t\pm\eta(\widetilde{\psi}^{2}(x,\gamma x)-t^{2}).

Note that the sum over
\gamma\in\Gamma
is locally finite on
\text{supp}\widehat{\rho}
, so there are no issues with convergence. We replace
B_{\gamma,\pm}
with its truncated and regularized expansion

(38)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}W_{k,\pm}(x,\gamma x)\chi(\eta)(\lambda\eta_{+})^{\frac{d-1}{2}-k}+O(\chi(\eta/\lambda)(\lambda\eta)^{\frac{d-3}{2}-N}),

so that the trace then becomes

\displaystyle\lambda^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\sum_{\pm}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\sum_{k=0}^{N}e^{\pm i\pi\mu}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X}e^{i\lambda\Phi_{\pm}(t,x,\eta)}|t|\widehat{\rho}(t)W_{k}(x,\gamma x)\eta_{+}^{\frac{d-1}{2}}dxd\eta dt+O(\lambda^{\frac{d-1}{2}-N}),

where the remainder estimate follows from (
38
) and integration by parts. The four stationary points of the phases
\Phi_{\pm}
with respect to
(t,\widetilde{\vartheta})
are

\displaystyle d_{t,\eta}\Phi_{+}=0\iff\begin{cases}t=\pm\widetilde{\psi}(\widetilde{x},\gamma\widetilde{x})\\
\eta=\pm\frac{1}{2\widetilde{\psi}(\widetilde{x},\gamma\widetilde{x})},\end{cases}

\displaystyle d_{t,\eta}\Phi_{-}=0\iff\begin{cases}t=\pm\widetilde{\psi}(\widetilde{x},\gamma\widetilde{x})\\
\eta=\mp\frac{1}{2\widetilde{\psi}(\widetilde{x},\gamma\widetilde{x})}.\end{cases}

The point
\eta=-\frac{1}{2\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x)}
doesn’t belong to the domain of the
\eta
-integral and similarly, since
\widehat{\rho}
localizes in
t
, we can also ignore the stationary point where
t=-\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x)
. We therefore consider only the stationary points
t,\eta>0
of
\Phi_{+}
, at which the Hessian is given by

(39)

\displaystyle D^{2}\Phi_{+}\left(\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x),\frac{1}{2\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x)}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x)\\
-2\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x)&-\frac{2}{\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x)}\end{pmatrix}.

As we work away from
t=0
,
\widetilde{\psi}
is nonvanishing and we can replace the integrand by its stationary phase approximation:

\displaystyle\pi\sum_{k=0}^{N}\lambda^{\frac{d-1}{2}-k}e^{i\pi\mu}\int_{X}e^{i\lambda\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x)}\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x)\widehat{\rho}(\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x))\frac{W_{k}(x,\gamma x)}{(2\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x))^{\frac{d-1}{2}-k}}dx+O(\lambda^{\frac{d-1}{2}-N}).

Since we have not applied the stationary phase approximation to the remainder, we take
N\geq 1
, group together the terms with
1\leq k\leq N
, which together contribute an error of order
O(\lambda^{\frac{d-3}{2}})
, and then retain only the contribution from the main term with

. From (
39

\mu=-2
and we pick up an extra copy of

4.8
to obtain

\displaystyle A_{\gamma}=-i\pi\chi_{\text{CdV}}(x)\pi_{\widetilde{X}\to X}^{*}\left(\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x)\widehat{\rho}(\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x))\frac{W_{0}(x,\gamma x)}{(2\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x))^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\right)\in C^{\infty}(\widetilde{X}),

which completes the proof.
∎

and a subsequence

that converges to
\widetilde{x}_{0}
entirely within

, then there exists

in the interior of
\text{supp}\beta_{i_{0}}^{*}\chi_{\text{CdV}}
; otherwise

, which cannot happen (cf. Remark

is

would also be a degenerate critical point at which

is positive. This covers the case where the critical value of

lies in the interior of
\text{supp}\widehat{\rho}
.

If instead, the critical value were an accumulation point at the boundary of
\text{supp}\widehat{\rho}
, then it would imply that this boundary point belongs to the length spectrum, which cannot happen in view of our construction of
\widehat{\rho}
. Hence, we may consider the case of only finitely many nondegenerate critical points, with critical values in the interior of
\text{supp}\widehat{\rho}
. Since the oscillatory integral is two dimensional, a stationary phase expansion at each critical point produces the leading order terms

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\frac{e^{i\lambda\ell_{j}}e^{\frac{i\pi\mathrm{sgn}D^{2}\psi_{\gamma}}{4}}}{\sqrt{\det D^{2}\psi_{\gamma}}},

where
\psi_{\gamma}(x)=\widetilde{\psi}(x,\gamma x)
is the
\gamma
-loop function. The signatures are congruent to the rank and must be either
+2,0
or
-2
. Cancellation could only occur if one critical point had
\text{sgn}D^{2}\psi=-2
and another with
\text{sgn}D^{2}\psi=+2
. However, this would correspond to a nondegenerate local

, and we know that
t_{\varepsilon}
is minimal amongst orbits within the each of the homology classes
\gamma\in C(\ell_{0})
. There could only be cancellation if

would be equal to a common constant for each

,

, contradicting our assumption that there were no degenerate critical points of the phase. We conclude that there can be no cancellation, so

and
t_{\varepsilon}=\ell_{0}
. The same argument applies with
t_{\varepsilon}
replaced by

.

In case
(2)
, collapse of the
\gamma
-length spectrum implies the existence of a rational torus, all of whose orbits have length

. In that case, the phase function is degenerate and Soga’s Theorem again implies that

is not rapidly decreasing.
∎

Note that in the proof above, we used that the lengths (assumed to be rotational) of periodic orbits at which the wave trace is singular are isolated. In general this may not hold, even in the completely integrable setting, for oscillatory lengths (cf. Definition
3.1
). Consider the following example, suggested to us by J. de Simoi:

Example 4.18
.

Take a smooth function
f
on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
which has infinitely many critical values accumulating at some finite number
L
. Revolving its graph around the circle, we obtain a metric of revolution on the torus, corresponding to a completely integrable Hamiltonian system. The periodic orbits for which the argument of
f
is a critical point have lengths which accumulate at
L
, despite the discrete indexing of orbits by homology class. In this case, either (1) there are infinitely many rational tori whose orbits belong to the homology class
(1,0)
with lengths approaching
L
or (2) these orbits may in fact belong to the separatrix, a measure zero region in
T^{*}\mathbb{T}^{2}
where the foliation by invariant tori is not smooth. In either case, these orbits contribute only to the oscillatory length spectrum
\mathcal{L}^{\osc}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)
in (
12
) and if the foliation is smooth, then these tori project vertically onto
\mathbb{T}^{2}
at some point and are excluded by our noncoincidence assumption. By contrast, Theorem
3.7
guarantees that for graph-rational tori, there is exactly one torus and all of its foliating orbits are minimal.

The necessity of two dimensions is also evident in the proof above. It was used (1) to apply Soga’s Theorem
4.17
and (2) to argue that the index of the Hessian can be only
0
or
\pm 2
, which prevents cancellation from nondegenerate critical points of the
\gamma
-loop functions.

5.
A second variation approach: Proof of Theorem
1.6

In this section, we present the proof of Theorem
1.6
. The basic idea is to compute the second variation of the
energy
functional (instead of the customary first variation of the length functional) and show that, thanks to the dependence on
\varepsilon
being only linear, the unperturbed geodesic
\gamma^{(0)}
is a critical point of both the unperturbed energy functional
E^{(0)}
and the first order expansion
E^{(1)}
; see (
50
) below. Combining the two associated Euler-Lagrange equations then allows us to conclude that
U\equiv 0
.

To start the proof, suppose a deformation
(g_{\varepsilon})
of the form (
5
) preserves a rational torus of homology class
(m_{1},m_{2})
and the length of its closed geodesics.
Fix
a,b
in the universal cover
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
b=a+(m_{1},m_{2})
.
The
energy

E_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)
of a curve
\gamma(t)=(\gamma_{1}(t),\gamma_{2}(t))
joining
a
and
b
with respect to the metric
g_{\varepsilon}
is defined as

(45)

E_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}[V(\gamma(t))+\varepsilon U(\gamma(t))]|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

The study of geodesics as critical points of the energy functional is classical. We refer to
[
66
]
for more details.

By Corollary
3.8
, for each
\varepsilon
, there exists in the preserved rational torus a geodesic joining
a
and
b
which depends smoothly on
\varepsilon
.
Let us denote such a unit-speed geodesic segment by
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}:[0,L]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
L
is the geodesic distance between
a
and
b
, and define another geodesic segment
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
by reparametrizing
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
with constant speed so that
\gamma_{\varepsilon}(0)=a
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}(1)=b
.
Explicitly, we have
\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)=\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(Lt)
.
Note that
L
is independent of
\varepsilon
by assumption.
By (
45

\gamma_{\varepsilon}
can be computed as

(46)

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\big(V(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t))+\varepsilon U(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t))\big)L^{2}|\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\varepsilon}(Lt)|^{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L\int_{0}^{L}\big(V(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t))+\varepsilon U(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t))\big)|\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{\varepsilon}(t)|^{2}\mathrm{d}t=L^{2}.

To obtain the last equality, we used the fact that
\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}
has unit-speed parametrization with regard to

. Hence, we deduce that the energy
E_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})=L^{2}
is constant in
\varepsilon

Before proceeding, we recall some elementary facts on the calculus of variations of the energy functional. Let
\omega:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a
C^{1}
curve with fixed endpoints

\omega(0)=a,\qquad\omega(1)=b.

A
variation vector field along
\omega
with fixed endpoints
is a
C^{1}
map

\delta:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}\qquad\text{such that}\quad\delta(0)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\delta(1)=0,

and we regard
\delta(t)
as a vector based at
\omega(t)
.
Given a variation vector field
\delta
, we define the
first variation
of the energy functional
E
at
\omega
in the direction
\delta
by

(47)

\mathrm{d}_{\omega}E(\delta):=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}E\bigl(\alpha(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)\bigr)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0},

where
\alpha:[0,1]\times(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is any
C^{1}
map such that

\alpha(t,0)=\omega(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial\varepsilon}(t,0)=\delta(t)\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,1].

(One checks that the value of (
47
) does not depend on the choice of
\alpha
.)
Similarly, given two variation vector fields
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
along
\omega
with fixed endpoints, we define the corresponding
second variation
of
E
by

(48)

\mathrm{d}_{\omega}^{2}E(\delta_{1},\delta_{2}):=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\varepsilon_{1}\,\partial\varepsilon_{2}}E\bigl(\alpha(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})\bigr)\Big|_{\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=0},

where now
\alpha:[0,1]\times(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is any
C^{1}
map satisfying

\alpha(t,0,0)=\omega(t),\qquad\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial\varepsilon_{1}}(t,0,0)=\delta_{1}(t),\qquad\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial\varepsilon_{2}}(t,0,0)=\delta_{2}(t).

Again, the value of (
48
) is independent of the particular choice of
\alpha
.
These notions give the usual characterization of geodesics; see, for example,
[
66
, §§12–13]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

The path
\omega
is a geodesic if and only if
\mathrm{d}_{\omega}E(\delta)=0
for every variational vector field
\delta
along
\omega
with fixed end points.
In addition, if
\omega
is a minimal geodesic, then the bilinear pairing

(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\mapsto\mathrm{d}_{\omega}^{2}E(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})

is positive semi-definite.

We now come back to the proof of Theorem
1.6
.
Let us write the
\varepsilon
-expansion of

and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
as follows

(49)

\gamma_{\varepsilon}=\gamma^{(0)}+\varepsilon\gamma^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\gamma^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3}),\qquad E_{\varepsilon}=E^{(0)}+\varepsilon E^{(1)}.

Explicitly, we have

(50)

E^{(0)}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}V(\gamma(t))|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{and}\quad E^{(1)}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}U(\gamma(t))|\dot{\gamma}(t)|^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

Using definitions (
47
) and (
48
) with
\alpha(t,\varepsilon)=\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)
and
\alpha(t,\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})=\gamma_{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}(t)
respectively, we compute the
\varepsilon
-expansion of

:

(51)

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})=

\displaystyle E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(0)})+\varepsilon\Big(E^{(1)}(\gamma^{(0)})+\mathrm{d}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(1)})\Big)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}\Big(\mathrm{d}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(1)}(\gamma^{(1)})+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(1)},\gamma^{(1)})+\mathrm{d}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(2)})\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3}).

On the other hand, since
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is by construction a geodesic with respect to

, Theorem
5.1
implies that
d_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}E_{\varepsilon}(\delta)=0
for any variation
\delta:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
with fixed end-point. Expanding in
\varepsilon
yields

(52)

0=d_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}E_{\varepsilon}(\delta)=\mathrm{d}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\delta)+\varepsilon\Big(\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\delta,\gamma^{(1)})+\mathrm{d}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(1)}(\delta)\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})

Using (
52
) with
\delta=\gamma^{(2)}
and
\delta=\gamma^{(1)}
, we can simplify (

) into

(53)

E_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})=E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(0)})+\varepsilon E^{(1)}(\gamma^{(0)})-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(1)},\gamma^{(1)})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3}).

We arrive at the second order condition

(54)

\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(1)},\gamma^{(1)})=0.

By Proposition
3.7
part (1), the geodesic
\tilde{\gamma}^{(0)}
of the rational torus is a minimizer of the
g_{0}
-Riemannian distance between its end-points.
Therefore, the reparametrized curve
\gamma^{(0)}
minimizes the energy functional
E^{(0)}
.
It follows that the Hessian
\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}
is positive semi-definite.

We remark that the proof of Proposition
3.7
(3) actually shows that any geodesic lying in a rational torus has no conjugate points over a period. This is consistent with the Morse index theorem, which states that the dimension of the maximal subspace on which

is negative definite coincides with the number of conjugate points along
\gamma
, counted with multiplicity.
In particular, for any variation
\delta
fixing endpoints and any

, we have

0\leq d^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(1)}+c\delta,\gamma^{(1)}+c\delta)=\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(1)},\gamma^{(1)})+2c\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(1)},\delta)+c^{2}\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\delta,\delta).

Combining with (
54
), the above inequality for arbitrary

forces
\mathrm{d}^{2}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(0)}(\gamma^{(1)},\delta)=0
for arbitrary variation
\delta
fixing endpoints.
Substituting into (
52
), we deduce that
\mathrm{d}_{\gamma^{(0)}}E^{(1)}=0
, i.e., the undeformed geodesic
\gamma^{(0)}
is also a critical point of the perturbing term
E^{(1)}
, in addition to being a critical point of the original energy functional
E^{(0)}
.
In these two cases, the Euler-Lagrange equations satisfied by
\gamma^{(0)}
write respectively

\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)|^{2}\nabla_{\gamma^{(0)}(t)}U=\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)\nabla_{\gamma^{(0)}(t)}U\cdot\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)+U(\gamma^{(0)}(t))\ddot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)

and

\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)|^{2}\nabla_{\gamma^{(0)}(t)}V=\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)\nabla_{\gamma^{(0)}(t)}V\cdot\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)+V(\gamma^{(0)}(t))\ddot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t).

Taking the Euclidean inner product with
\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)
and rearranging, we obtain from the first equation

0=\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)|^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}U(\gamma^{(0)}(t))+U(\gamma^{(0)}(t))\ddot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)\cdot\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t).

For

such that

, the above equation gives

-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log|U(\gamma^{(0)}(t))|=\frac{\ddot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)\cdot\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)}{|\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)|^{2}}.

Since the same equation with
U
replaced by
V
also holds, we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log U(\gamma^{(0)}(t))=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log V(\gamma^{(0)}(t))
.
It follows that
U(\gamma^{(0)}(t))
is a constant multiple of
V(\gamma^{(0)}(t))
over each connected component of
\{t\mid U(\gamma^{(0)}(t))\neq 0\}
.
Actually, since
V>0
, by continuity of
V
and
U
there exists a constant
c
such that
U(\gamma^{(0)}(t))=cV(\gamma^{(0)}(t))
for all
t
.
Now, using the first order condition of (

E^{(1)}(\gamma^{(0)})=0
.
Using the explicit formula (
50
) and the relation (

(55)

0=E^{(1)}(\gamma^{(0)})=\int_{0}^{1}U(\gamma^{(0)}(t))|\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)|^{2}\mathrm{d}t=c\int_{0}^{1}V(\gamma^{(0)}(t))|\dot{\gamma}^{(0)}(t)|^{2}\mathrm{d}t=cE^{(0)}(\gamma^{(0)})=cL^{2}.

Since
L>0
, the above equation forces

and therefore

vanishes on the image of
\gamma^{(0)}
.
Finally, since the family of closed geodesics
\gamma^{(0)}
of a rational torus foliates
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, we can apply the same argument to every such geodesics
\gamma^{(0)}
to deduce that

everywhere.
The proof of Theorem
1.6
is finished.

Remark 5.2
.

The preceding argument requires the deformation to preserve not only a rational torus itself, but also the
period
of the rational torus (guaranteed by the isospectral assumption).
One can of course deform the metric nontrivially within the class of Liouville metrics, however at the cost of nontrivially changing the spectrum.

Appendix A
On the Noncoincidence Condition (NCC)

In this appendix, we prove the genericity of NCC within the class of Liouville metrics.
Therefore, the conditions on
f_{1},f_{2}
for Theorem
1.1
to hold are generically satisfied.
We emphasize that a property holds
generically
if it holds on a residual set, i.e., a countable intersection of open dense sets.

Proposition A.1
.

The noncoincidence condition (NCC) up to
T=\infty
(resp.
T<\infty
) holds for a generic set (resp. an open dense set) of Liouville metrics on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
More precisely, for pairs of functions
(f_{1},f_{2})
that are generic (resp. belong to an open dense set) with respect to the analytic,
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
, or
\mathcal{C}^{r}
(
r\geq 2
) function-space topology and satisfy
f_{1}(x_{1})+f_{2}(x_{2})>-1
, the corresponding Liouville metric

g=\bigl(1+f_{1}(x_{1})+f_{2}(x_{2})\bigr)\,(\mathrm{d}x_{1}^{2}+\mathrm{d}x_{2}^{2})

satisfies NCC up to
T=\infty
(resp.,
T<\infty
)

Before presenting the proof of Proposition
A.1
, we recall the
Maupertuis principle
, a useful tool to describe geodesics of Liouville metrics. Take a compact Riemannian manifold,
(M,g)
, and let

(56)

\widetilde{H}(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\widetilde{g}^{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}-V(x)

be a natural mechanical Hamiltonian function on
T^{*}M
, where
V\in C^{2}(M)
denotes some potential function. Moreover, let
T_{h}=\{\widetilde{H}(x,\xi)=h\}
be an isoenergy submanifold for some
h>-\min_{x}V(x)
. We then note that
T_{h}
is an isoenergy submanifold for another system with Hamiltonian function

(57)

{H}(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}\frac{\widetilde{g}^{ij}(x)}{h+V(x)}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\,,

as well. That is, we have
T_{h}=\{{H}(x,\xi)=1\}
. Now, the

states that the integral curves for the Hamiltonian vector fields generated by
{\widetilde{H}}
and
H

T_{h}
coincide.
Finally, note that the vector field generated by
{H}
gives rise to the geodesic flow of the Riemannian metric
g

g_{ij}(x)=(h+V(x))\widetilde{g}_{ij}(x)\,.

We will use this for

(58)

In this case, the orbits are determined up to time reparametrization by the mechanical Hamiltonian

(59)

\tilde{H}(x,\xi)=\frac{|\xi|^{2}}{2}-f_{1}(x_{1})-f_{2}(x_{2})

The projections of orbits onto

\begin{cases}x_{1}^{\prime}(t)=\pm\sqrt{2(e+f_{1}(x_{1}(t)))}\\

From the definition, the Riemannian length of such orbits over an arbitrary time interval

(61)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{(1+f_{1}(x_{1}(t))+f_{2}(x_{2}(t)))(x_{1}^{\prime}(t)^{2}+x_{2}^{\prime}(t)^{2})}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{2}(1+f_{1}(x_{1}(t))+f_{2}(x_{2}(t)))\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{2}(e+f_{1}(x_{1}(t)))\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{2}(1-e+f_{2}(x_{2}(t)))\mathrm{d}t.

Suppose

.
Then the changes of variables

\int_{0}^{T}\!\sqrt{2}\,\bigl(e+f_{1}(x_{1}(t))\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t=\sqrt{2}\int_{x_{1}(0)}^{x_{1}(T)}\frac{e+f_{1}(x_{1})}{\sqrt{2\bigl(e+f_{1}(x_{1})\bigr)}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}=\int_{x_{1}(0)}^{x_{1}(T)}\!\sqrt{e+f_{1}(x_{1})}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}.

-f_{1}
.
In this case, the closed geodesic is contained in
[x_{1}^{\min},x_{1}^{\max}]\times\mathbb{T}
and its length is given by

(69)

2n\int_{x_{1}^{\min}}^{x_{1}^{\max}}\sqrt{e+f_{1}(x_{1})}dx_{1}+|m|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sqrt{1-e+f_{2}(x_{2})}dx_{2}.

•

The symmetric statement holds for closed geodesics in the homology class
(m,0)
.

We now present the proof of Proposition
A.1
.

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

Recall that
\mathcal{L}^{m,n}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)
is defined as the set of lengths of closed geodesics of
(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)
with homology class
(m,n)
.
For notational convenience, we will denote
\ell^{m,n}_{g}=\min\mathcal{L}^{m,n}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)
for
mn\neq 0
.
Recall also that by definition, we have
\mathcal{L}^{\osc}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)=\bigcup_{k>0}\mathcal{L}^{k,0}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)\cup\mathcal{L}^{0,k}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)
.
By countability and symmetry, to show the claimed result, it suffices to prove that for any fixed pair of nonzero integers
(m,n)
, the set of Liouville metrics
g
satisfying

(70)

\ell^{m,n}_{g}\notin\bigcup_{k>0}\mathcal{L}^{0,k}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)

is open and dense with respect to the considered functional topology.

Step 1: an
a priori
bound on homology classes.

We first show that we can restrict to a finite number of homology classes
(0,k)
in (
70
). More precisely, we claim that for fixed metric
g
and
(m,n)
there exists
K>0
for any
k>K
, a closed geodesic with homology class
(0,k)
has length strictly larger than
\ell^{m,n}_{g}
.
To see this, let
0<d:=\min\mathcal{L}^{0,1}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)
.
Then by a standard argument of the Aubry-Mather theory on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
(see for example
[
7
, Ch. 3]
), every minimal closed geodesic with homology class
(0,k)
is given by
k
-times iteration of a minimal closed geodesic with homology class
(0,1)
.
Hence, we have
\min\mathcal{L}^{0,k}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)\geq kd\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
and there exists
K
sufficiently large satisfying the stated property.
As a consequence, to prove (
70
), it suffices to restrict to finitely many homology classes
(0,k)
and show that

(71)

\ell^{m,n}_{g}\notin\bigcup_{1\leq k\leq K}\mathcal{L}^{0,k}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)

holds for an open dense set of
g
.

Step 2: openness.

Our next goal is to show the openness of condition (
71
).
We begin by reformulating (
71
) as follows.
Lift the system to universal cover and consider the equivalent setting of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
periodic metrics
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and still denote by
\Phi_{g}^{t}
the associated geodesic flow on the sphere bundle
S\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Write

(72)

\displaystyle A_{g}(t):=

\displaystyle\{(\xi,\Phi^{t}_{g}(\xi))\ :\ \xi\in S\mathbb{R}^{2},\ \pi(\xi)\in[0,1]^{2}\},

\displaystyle B_{k,j}:=

\displaystyle\{(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\ :\ \xi\in S\mathbb{R}^{2},\ \pi(\xi)\in[0,1]^{2},\ \xi^{\prime}=\tau_{(k,j)}(\xi)\}

where
\pi:S\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the canonical projection and where
\tau_{(k,j)}
is the deck translation on
S\mathbb{R}^{2}
associated with
(k,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, i.e., it shifts the basepoint
\pi(\xi)
by
(k,j)
leaves the direction unchanged.
Note that
[0,1]^{2}
is a fundamental domain for
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
Now, observe that (
71
) is equivalent to

(73)

A_{g}(\ell_{g}^{m,n})\cap\Big(\bigcup_{1\leq k\leq K}B_{0,k}\Big)=\varnothing.

Note that
A_{g}(\ell_{g}^{m,n})
and
B_{0,k}
,
k=1,\ldots,K
are compact.
Since
\ell_{g}^{m,n}
depends continuously on
g
, by continuity of the geodesic flow the set
A_{g}(\ell_{g}^{m,n})
depends continuously on
g
.
It follows that, if (
73
) holds for some
g
then it holds for all
g^{\prime}
sufficiently close to
g
.
Equivalently, the condition (
71
) is open, as desired.

Step 3: density.

The key idea is to choose an interval
I
such that the annulus
I\times\mathbb{T}\subset\mathbb{T}^{2}
does not intersect orbits in the homology classes
(0,k)
,
|k|\leq K
, and then perturb
f_{1}
over
I
to change
\ell^{m,n}_{g}
without affecting
\bigcup_{1\leq k\leq K}\mathcal{L}^{0,k}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)
.
By Lemma
A.2
, there are two types of closed geodesics contributing to
\mathcal{L}^{0,k}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)
: those contained in a circle
\{x_{1}\}\times\mathbb{T}
for some critical point
x_{1}
of
f_{1}
, and those not contained in such circles. In the first case, the geodesics are
k
-fold iterates of a
(0,1)
closed geodesic lying on a critical torus.
13
13

13

If
x_{1}
is a local minimum of
f_{1}
, then this geodesic is hyperbolic and corresponds to the separatrix in the phase diagram.

We now focus on the second type.
Note that by a small perturbation, we can always assume
f_{1}
is nonconstant, so the set of critical points of
f_{1}
is not the entire
\mathbb{T}
.
Recall that by Lemma
A.2
, such geodesics are associated with
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and satisfy

(74)

2n\int_{x_{1}^{\min}}^{x_{1}^{\max}}\frac{dx_{1}}{\sqrt{e+f_{1}(x_{1})}}=|k|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dx_{2}}{\sqrt{1-e+f_{2}(x_{2})}}\quad\text{for some }e\in(-\max f_{1},-\min f_{1})

with
(x_{1}^{\min},x_{1}^{\max})
being a connected component of
\{x_{1}\in\mathbb{T}\ :\ e+f_{1}(x_{1})>0\}
such that the annulus
[x_{1}^{\min},x_{1}^{\max}]\times\mathbb{T}
contains the closed geodesic.
Then, for bounded
|k|
, the above equation implies that the interval
(x_{1}^{\min},x_{1}^{\max})
stays bounded away from every local minimum of
f_{1}
, for, if not, the integral on the left hand side diverges, contradicting the boundedness of
|k|
.
Therefore, we can pick a nonempty open interval
I\subset\mathbb{T}
such that 1) the interval
I
does not contain critical points of
f_{1}
and 2) the interval
I
does not intersect the region
[x_{1}^{\min},x_{1}^{\max}]
corresponding to any orbit of the second type in the homology class
(0,k)
,
|k|\leq K
.
By construction,
I\times\mathbb{T}
does not intersect
any
closed geodesics with homology class
(0,k)
,
|k|\leq K
.

Next, choose a perturbation
f_{1}\mapsto f_{1}+\varepsilon\varphi
(with
f_{2}
left unperturbed) such that
\varphi
is a smooth nonzero and nonnegative function supported in
I
and denote by
g_{\varepsilon}
the corresponding perturbed Liouville metric.
Since
\varphi
vanishes on critical points of
f_{1}
by the choice of
I
, the lengths of closed geodesics of the first type remain unchanged.
For small
\varepsilon
, we can assume the perturbed function
f_{1}+\varepsilon\varphi
creates no new critical points on
I
and hence this perturbation does not create additional geodesics of the first type.
On the other hand, since closed geodesics of the second type do not intersect the perturbed region
I\times\mathbb{T}
, their lengths also remain unchanged throughout the perturbation.
Hence, we deduce that
\bigcup_{1\leq k\leq K}\mathcal{L}^{0,k}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g_{\varepsilon})=\bigcup_{1\leq k\leq K}\mathcal{L}^{0,k}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)
for all
\varepsilon
sufficiently small.
Taking the derivative of the length functional, we find that the first-order variation of
\ell^{m,n}_{g_{\varepsilon}}
in
\varepsilon
is given by the integral

(75)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\ell^{m,n}_{g_{\varepsilon}}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\varphi(\gamma_{1})}{\sqrt{1+f_{1}(\gamma_{1})+f_{2}(\gamma_{2})}}|\dot{\gamma}|

over one period, where
\gamma
is any closed geodesic in the
(m,n)
-rational torus.
Since
\gamma
intersects
I\times\mathbb{T}
and
\varphi\not\equiv 0
, the above integral is strictly positive.
By
C^{1}
-continuity and strict monotonicity, the quantity
\ell^{m,n}_{g_{\varepsilon}}
covers an open neighborhood of
\ell^{m,n}_{g}
as
\varepsilon
varies in a small interval around
0
.

Finally, by the standard argument using Sard’s Lemma (see for example
[
50
]
), the spectrum
\mathcal{L}^{\osc}(\mathbb{T}^{2},g)
has zero Lebesgue measure and hence does not contain an interval. It follows that there exists arbitrarily small
\varepsilon\neq 0
such that (
71
) holds for
g_{\varepsilon}
.
This proves the density in the
C^{r}
category for
r\in[2,\infty]
.

Lastly, approximate
\varphi
sufficiently well by analytic functions, and apply openness of (
71
) (Step 2) to conclude density in the analytic category as well.
∎

Remark A.3
.

In the preceding proof, only Step 3 requires the metric to be Liouville. Steps 1–2 hold for the general class of Riemannian metrics on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.
