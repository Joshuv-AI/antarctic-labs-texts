Effects of plyometric training combined with tDCS on lower-limb muscle activation and cardiopulmonary function in male amateur soccer players: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Jeong-Il Kang, Dae-Joong Yang, Hyeong-Seok Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose] This study aimed to investigate the effects of combining transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) with plyometric training on lower-limb muscle activation and cardiorespiratory function in male amateur soccer players.[Methods] Thirty male amateur soccer players were randomly assigned to an experimental group (n = 15) or a control group (n = 15). The experimental group performed plyometric training combined with transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), whereas the control group performed plyometric training with sham stimulation. The intervention was conducted three times per week for four weeks. Lower-limb muscle activation (RF, VL, VM, and GCM) and cardiorespiratory function (MIP, MIFR, VO2max, Wmax, and MEC) were assessed before and after the intervention. Data were analyzed using a two-way mixed-design ANOVA.[Results] Both groups showed significant within-group improvements in muscle activation and cardiorespiratory variables (p < 0.05). However, the experimental group demonstrated significantly greater enhancements in RF, VL, VM, and GCM activation compared with the control group (p < 0.05). Between-group comparisons also revealed superior improvements in MIFR and Wmax in the experimental group (p < 0.05), while VO2max increased significantly over time in both groups without between-group differences. A two-way mixed-design ANOVA indicated significant group × time interactions for muscle activation and selected cardiorespiratory indices (p < 0.05), supporting enhanced neuromuscular and physiological adaptations in the experimental group.[Conclusion] Combining tDCS with plyometric training enhanced lower-limb muscle activation and cardiorespiratory function more effectively than plyometric training alone in amateur soccer players.

INTRODUCTION

Athletic performance in soccer is highly dependent on lower extremity muscle strength and explosive power, both of which play a pivotal role in high-intensity, repetitive movements such as jumping, sprinting, and change-of-direction tasks [1]. Neuromuscular efficiency of the lower limbs is a critical determinant not only of sport-specific performance but also of injury risk reduction, as appropriately timed muscle activation and coordinated muscle contractions contribute to enhanced sport-specific performance capacity [2]. Compared to professional athletes, amateur soccer players exhibit considerable variability in physical fitness levels and training intensity, resulting in disparities in lower extremity neuromuscular function and athletic performance disparities that may lead to diminished competitive performance and an elevated risk of injury [3]. Furthermore, sustaining high-intensity exercise requires not only peripheral muscular adaptation but also well-regulated cardiorespiratory endurance and the capacity of the central nervous system to modulate fatigue and perceived effort [4]. Given that soccer demands repeated sprinting and sustained physical activity throughout a match, there is a growing need for training strategies capable of simultaneously improving aerobic capacity as indexed by maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) cardiorespiratory endurance, and neuromuscular function [4].

Among the available training modalities, plyometric training has been widely adopted as a representative method for enhancing lower extremity muscle function. This form of training promotes neuromuscular and musculoskeletal adaptations in healthy adults by improving the efficiency of the stretch-shortening cycle, increasing motor unit recruitment rates, enhancing the elastic properties of the muscle-tendon unit, and optimizing motor unit activation patterns [5]. Prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses examining combined upper and lower extremity plyometric training have reported significant improvements across multiple performance-related variables, including muscle strength, power, sprint speed, agility, and flexibility [6]. Plyometric training has also been shown to contribute to improvements in lower extremity muscle activation and reductions in injury risk in specific athletic populations [7]. Moreover, plyometric training has been reported to enhance not only anaerobic power but also aerobic capacity, establishing it as an effective training modality capable of simultaneously improving cardiorespiratory endurance and neuromuscular function [8]. Studies combining electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) with plyometric training have demonstrated greater improvements in jump performance, sprint capacity, and reactive-elastic power compared to plyometric training alone, suggesting that the integration of additional neurostimulatory input with plyometric training may yield augmented training adaptations [9,10].

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) has recently emerged as a promising non-invasive brain stimulation technique for enhancing athletic performance [11]. tDCS is known to modulate the excitability of the primary motor cortex (M1), thereby improving motor unit recruitment, muscle activation patterns, and the management of central fatigue during exercise [11]. A recent meta-analysis reported that combining tDCS with exercise training produced significant improvements in maximal muscle strength and lower extremity explosive power, with particularly pronounced effects observed in tasks involving large muscle groups or in individuals with a substantial training background [12]. A separate systematic review found that anodal tDCS applied over the primary motor cortex can improve specific exercise performance indices most notably endurance-related measures such as cycling time to exhaustion although the consistency of findings across studies remains limited due to considerable variability in experimental protocols [11].

In studies conducted with elite athletes, repeated tDCS application was found to optimize physiological and psychological states, improve heart rate and autonomic nervous system responses, and facilitate recovery following high-intensity exercise, suggesting that tDCS may exert beneficial effects on cardiovascular regulation in addition to its influence on peripheral muscle function [13]. Furthermore, combining tDCS with jump training has been shown to enhance jump performance [14], and evidence of improved feedforward activation of lower extremity muscles following tDCS application has also been reported [15], corroborating the potential role of tDCS in augmenting neuromuscular control and muscle activation.

Despite accumulating evidence that plyometric training and tDCS can each independently improve neuromuscular function and athletic performance, studies examining their combined application remain scarce. In particular, research investigating the synergistic effects of tDCS on high-intensity training outcomes in amateur soccer players is highly limited, and randomized controlled trials assessing the effects of tDCS-augmented plyometric training on lower extremity muscle activation and cardiorespiratory endurance are lacking. Furthermore, studies simultaneously evaluating electromyography (EMG)-based muscle activation and cardiorespiratory function indices such as VO2max following this combined intervention strategy are insufficient.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the effects of plyometric training combined with tDCS on lower extremity muscle activation and cardiorespiratory function in male amateur soccer players. It was hypothesized that the combined intervention would result in greater improvements in lower extremity muscle activation and cardiorespiratory function compared with plyometric training alone, and that the tDCS-combined group would demonstrate larger effect sizes than the control group.

METHODS

Participants

This study was conducted over a four-week period with amateur soccer players residing in M city, Republic of Korea. All participants were provided with a thorough explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential risks associated with participation. Only individuals who provided written informed consent including acknowledgment of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence were enrolled.

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 software (Franz Faul, University of Kiel) based on a repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA; within-between interaction), with the following parameters: effect size (f) = 0.30, significance level (α) = 0.05, statistical power (1− β) = 0.80, correlation among repeated measures = 0.5, and sphericity correction (ε) = 1 [16]. The analysis indicated that a minimum of 24 participants was required; accounting for potential attrition, a total of 30 participants were recruited.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: participants aged 19-35 years with at least one year of competitive soccer experience, ability to complete the study intervention and maximal exercise tolerance testing, no history of injury or surgery affecting the lower back or lower extremities that had restricted physical activity within the preceding three months, and no known musculoskeletal or cardiovascular disease. Exclusion criteria included. a history of or current seizures or convulsions, or any contraindication to tDCS such as epilepsy, brain tumor, or cerebrovascular disease, current use of implanted electronic medical devices, serious skin conditions, wounds, or infections at electrode placement sites including the scalp or lower extremities, current use of psychotropic medication for conditions such as depression, and a history of dyspnea or syncope during high-intensity exercise.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Sehan University (IRB approval number SH-IRB 2026-016) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Study Design

This study employed a randomized controlled trial (RCT) design in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines (Figure 1). A total of 30 participants were randomly allocated in equal numbers (n = 15 per group) to either the experimental group (tDCS combined with plyometric training) or the control group (sham tDCS combined with plyometric training) using a randomization system (version 1.0, Mahmood Saghaei, Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Iran).

To minimize selection bias during group allocation, a research assistant not directly involved in the study prepared sequentially numbered sealed opaque envelopes. Following completion of baseline assessments, participants opened the envelopes in sequential order to maintain allocation concealment. Although complete blinding of the treating therapist was not feasible given the nature of the intervention, participant blinding was achieved through a sham protocol in which control group participants wore an identical headset to that used in the experimental group. In the sham condition, current was delivered for an initial 30-second rampup phase and subsequently discontinued, a method that is widely recognized as a standardized approach in tDCS research. Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation throughout the study. It is acknowledged as a methodological limitation, however, that participants were not asked to guess their group assignment following the intervention, precluding formal verification of blinding success.

The primary outcome was lower-extremity muscle activity, assessed using surface electromyography of the rectus femoris (RF), vastus lateralis (VL), vastus medialis (VM), and gastrocnemius (GCM). The secondary outcomes were inspiratory function, assessed using maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and maximal inspiratory flow rate (MIFR), and cardiorespiratory function, assessed using maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max), peak power output (Wmax), and maximal energy consumption (MEC).

All participants underwent identical assessments before and after the intervention in the same measurement room. The assessments included age, body height, body weight, dominant foot, muscle activity, inspiratory function, and maximal exercise tolerance testing to evaluate physical characteristics and pulmonary function. To minimize potential confounding variables during exercise testing, all participants were instructed in accordance with the exercise testing guidelines of the Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology (CSEP) to abstain from caffeine consumption and smoking for at least two hours prior to testing, and from alcohol consumption for at least six hours prior to testing. Participants were also advised to refrain from moderate-(3-6 metabolic equivalents METs) to high-intensity ( > 6 METs) physical activity for 24 hours preceding each test session. On the morning of each test day, participants were instructed to consume their usual breakfast; however, additional dietary control beyond this was not implemented.

Intervention

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS)

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a non-invasive brain stimulation technique that modulates neural excitability in targeted cortical regions through the transcranial application of low-intensity electrical current. In the present study, stimulation was delivered at an intensity of approximately 2 mA over the primary motor cortex using the Halo Sport headset (Halo Sport Headset, Halo Neuroscience, USA), a commercially available consumer-grade tDCS system, with the electrodes positioned over the C3, Cz, and C4 sites. The Halo Sport device was selected because its wearable and self-contained configuration allowed tDCS to be administered concurrently with dynamic plyometric exercise without substantially restricting whole-body movement. This consumer-grade device allows the user to configure stimulation intensity and mode (selected as ‘Leg, Core & Arms’ in the present study) via a dedicated application, after which the current is automatically regulated by the device’s internal algorithm. The stimulation intensity was selected with reference to the optimal range of 1.6-2.0 mA identified for enhancing exercise performance in the meta-analysis by Jiang et al. [17].

Electrode pads were thoroughly moistened with normal saline to ensure adequate contact with the scalp. To minimize skin discomfort and ensure safe current delivery, stimulation was initiated with a 30-second ramp-up phase during which current intensity was gradually increased to the target level, and terminated with a 30-second ramp-down phase during which current was gradually decreased [18]. In the sham condition, participants wore an identical headset; however, current delivery was discontinued after the initial 30-second period, ensuring that participants remained unaware of whether active stimulation was being administered. As the device is designed to automatically interrupt current delivery when electrode contact is unstable, the researcher continuously monitored electrode contact quality and device function throughout all sessions [18].

However, because Halo Sport is a consumer-grade system, investigator control over the electrode montage and current-delivery parameters is more limited than that available with conventional research-grade tDCS devices, and the resulting intracranial current distribution cannot be independently verified. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within the specific Halo Sport configuration and stimulation protocol used in the present study.

Plyometric training

Training sessions were scheduled in accordance with each participant’s individual availability and were conducted three times per week for four weeks, with each session lasting 50 minutes. Each session consisted of a warm-up (5 minutes), main exercise (40 minutes), and cool-down (5 minutes), with stretching performed during both the warmup and cool-down phases. The main exercise component comprised a circuit-based plyometric program consisting of eight exercises, each performed for 20 repetitions across three sets, with a three-minute inter-set rest period. All training sessions were performed while wearing the tDCS headset; the experimental group performed plyometric training with active tDCS, whereas the control group performed the identical plyometric protocol with sham tDCS [19]. Exercise intensity was monitored using the Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale developed by Borg [20], with a target intensity of RPE 15-17 established for the main exercise phase. Progressive overload was applied to the jump training component, with moderate-intensity jumps prescribed during weeks 1-2 and high-intensity jumps introduced during weeks 3-4 (Table 2).

Measurement Tools and Procedures

Measurement of lower extremity muscle activity

Lower extremity muscle activity was measured using a wireless surface electromyography (sEMG) system (BTS FreeEMG 1000, BTS Bioengineering, Italy). Muscle activity was recorded from the rectus femoris (RF), vastus lateralis (VL), vastus medialis (VM), and gastrocnemius (GCM). Before electrode placement, the skin was cleaned with alcohol to reduce skin impedance, and electrode placement was performed in accordance with the Surface Electromyography for the Non-Invasive Assessment of Muscles (SENIAM) guidelines to optimize signal quality. The sEMG signals were sampled at 1,000 Hz. A band-pass filter of 20-450 Hz and a 60-Hz notch filter were applied [21]. The recorded signals were converted to root mean square (RMS) values for analysis. To normalize the electromyographic data, the percentage of reference voluntary contraction (%RVC) method was used, in which muscle contraction during a specific movement was used as the reference voluntary contraction (RVC).

The RVC task was performed in an approximately 90° knee-flexion position, corresponding to a low-squat posture. This posture was considered appropriate for functionally activating the target muscles simultaneously because it resembles the lower-limb flexion pattern observed during the eccentric loading phase of a plyometric vertical jump [22]. The %RVC method was selected to assess changes in normalized EMG amplitude relative to a standardized, task-specific low-squat posture. This approach allowed all four target muscles to be evaluated under the same functional condition, whereas MVIC normalization would have required additional muscle-specific maximal testing procedures, including separate testing conditions for the knee extensors and gastrocnemius. Therefore, the normalized values should be interpreted as EMG amplitude relative to the selected reference task rather than as a percentage of maximal muscle activation. Participants were instructed to maintain this posture for 5 seconds. For RVC calculation, the middle 3 seconds of the recorded signal were used after excluding the first and last 1 second of the measurement period [22]. Measurements were performed on the participant’s dominant lower limb. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the mean value was used for data analysis. Disposable EKG electrodes (2225H, 3M) were used for signal acquisition, and the inter-electrode distance was maintained at 2 cm.

Measurement of inspiratory function

Inspiratory function was measured using an electronic inspiratory resistance device (POWERbreathe K5, POWERbreathe ® International Ltd., UK). Maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) and maximal inspiratory flow rate (MIFR) were assessed. Participants were instructed to maintain an upright posture with the neck and trunk properly aligned and to seal their lips firmly around the mouthpiece to prevent air leakage. After exhaling to residual volume (RV), they were instructed to perform a maximal, forceful, and rapid inspiration for approximately 1-2 seconds.

To ensure accurate measurements, the examiner provided standardized verbal cues, such as “exhale,” “inhale,” and “good.” Measurement data were recorded and stored at a sampling frequency of 500 Hz using Breathe Link live feedback software. The device has demonstrated high reliability, with intra-rater reliability ranging from 0.96 to 0.99 and inter-rater reliability ranging from 0.93 to 0.98 [23,24]. Measurements were performed three times, and the highest value was used for data analysis. A 3-minute rest period was provided between trials to minimize inspiratory muscle fatigue.

Maximal exercise stress test

The maximal exercise stress test was performed using a cycle ergometer (Ergoline ergoselect 100K, Ergoline GmbH, Lindenstrasse, Bitz, Germany). Maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max), peak power output (Wmax), and maximal energy consumption (MEC) were measured. Heart rate was continuously monitored using a Polar H1 wireless heart rate sensor (Kempele, Finland), which was attached over the xiphoid process of the sternum.

All participants were instructed to wear comfortable clothing. They were asked to refrain from strenuous physical activity and alcohol consumption for 24 hours before the test and to restrict caffeine intake for at least 12 hours before the test and intervention sessions. In addition, participants were instructed to avoid food, fluid intake, and smoking for 3 hours before the test [25].

Before the test, the saddle height was adjusted for each participant. Participants then performed a 3-minute warmup at a resistance of 75 W while maintaining a pedaling cadence of 55-60 rpm. Thereafter, the workload was progressively increased by 25 W every minute, and the test was continued until the participant could no longer maintain pedaling [26].

The test was terminated immediately when any of the following criteria were met: (1) the participant failed to maintain a cadence of 55-60 rpm for more than 5 seconds; (2) the rating of perceived exertion reached ≥ 17 or the participant voluntarily requested to stop; (3) the participant reached the age-predicted maximal heart rate based on the Gellish equation; or (4) symptoms requiring test termination occurred according to the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM, 2014) exercise testing termination criteria [27].

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of the data and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. A two-way mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA), with group as the between-subjects factor and time as the within-subjects factor, was conducted to examine the main effects of group and time, as well as the group × time interaction effect, for changes before and after the intervention. When a significant main effect of time or group × time interaction was observed, simple effects analyses were additionally performed to identify within-group and between-group differences across time points. With-in-group comparisons were performed using paired t-tests, and between-group comparisons were performed using independent t-tests. Bonferroni correction was applied only to the p-values obtained from these follow-up simple-effects comparisons, including the within-group pre-post comparisons and between group comparisons, to control the family-wise Type I error rate. The correction was not applied to the omnibus ANOVA tests for the main effects of group and time or the group × time interaction. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

RESULTS

Comparison of Muscle Activity Results

The results of lower-extremity muscle activity are presented in Table 3. The analysis of the main effect of time showed significant differences in all variables, including RF, VL, VM, and GCM (p < 0.001), indicating that muscle activity increased in both groups after the intervention. The main effect of group was significant for VM and GCM, whereas no significant group effect was observed for RF and VL. A significant group × time interaction effect was observed for all variables (p < 0.001), confirming that the changes in muscle activity differed between groups according to the intervention. In the simple effects analysis, both the experimental and control groups showed significant improvements after the intervention. In particular, the experimental group demonstrated significantly greater changes than the control group in all variables.

Comparison of Cardiopulmonary Function Results

The results of cardiopulmonary function are presented in Table 4. The analysis showed a significant main effect of time for all variables, including MIP, MIFR, VO2max, Wmax, and MEC (p < 0.001), indicating that both groups demonstrated improvements after the intervention. No significant main effect of group was observed for any variable. However, a significant group × time interaction effect was observed for all variables (p < 0.001), confirming that the changes differed between groups according to the intervention. In the simple effects analysis, both the experimental and control groups showed significant improvements after the intervention. In particular, the experimental group demonstrated significantly greater changes in MIFR and Wmax than the control group.

DISCUSSION

This randomized controlled trial investigated the effects of combining transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) with plyometric training on lower-extremity muscle activity and cardiopulmonary function in male recreational soccer players. The main findings showed that the experimental group demonstrated greater overall improvements than the control group. In particular, significant group × time interaction effects were observed for all measured muscles, including the rectus femoris (RF), vastus lateralis (VL), vastus medialis (VM), and gastrocnemius (GCM). Among the cardiopulmonary variables, the experimental group showed greater changes in maximal inspiratory flow rate (MIFR) and peak power output (Wmax). These findings suggest that combining tDCS with high-intensity lower-extremity training may enhance both neuromuscular activation and selected physiological adaptations.

tDCS has been reported to increase the excitability of the primary motor cortex and to improve motor unit recruitment and muscle activation patterns [28]. This provides a plausible physiological mechanism for the increased muscle activity observed in the present study. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies. Moshashaei et al. [15] reported that combining neuromuscular training with anodal tDCS in taekwondo athletes with dynamic knee valgus produced greater improvements in feedforward activity of the lower-extremity muscles than sham tDCS, suggesting that tDCS may enhance muscle pre-activation during functional performance. Cates et al. [14] also reported that repeated tDCS combined with vertical jump training improved vertical jump performance in elite athletes, which was interpreted as the result of optimized neural factors involved in force production and intermuscular coordination. Together, these findings suggest that tDCS may have provided an additive neuromodulatory effect when combined with plyometric training.

The effectiveness of plyometric training itself for improving lower-extremity muscle function has also been well documented. Marković and Mikulic [5] suggested that plyometric training induces neural and musculoskeletal adaptations and improves strength, power, stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) function, jumping, sprinting, agility, and endurance performance, while also serving as a safe and effective training method that may reduce the risk of lower-extremity injury, particularly in female athletes. Deng et al. [6], in a systematic review and meta-analysis, reported that upper- and lower-extremity plyometric training produced meaningful improvements ranging from small to large effects across multiple fitness components, including upper- and lower-extremity muscle power, sprint speed, strength, agility, and flexibility. Wilkerson et al. [7] reported that 6 weeks of plyometric jump training significantly increased peak hamstring torque in female collegiate basketball players, indicating improvements in neuromuscular characteristics that may contribute to knee joint stability and reduce the risk of anterior cruciate ligament injury. In the present study, the significant main effect of time observed in all muscles in both the experimental and control groups is consistent with these previous findings and suggests that plyometric training alone can sufficiently improve lower-extremity muscle activity. However, the greater magnitude of change in the experimental group suggests that tDCS may have accelerated or amplified the adaptive effects of plyometric training.

Evidence from studies combining neuromuscular stimulation with plyometric training also indirectly supports the present findings. Herrero et al. [10] and Martínez-López et al. [9] reported greater improvements in jump and sprint related performance when electromyostimulation was combined with plyometric training than when plyometric training was performed alone. Although electromyostimulation and tDCS differ in their stimulation targets and physiological mechanisms, these findings support the broader concept that adding neuromodulatory or neuromuscular stimulation to plyometric training may enhance neuromuscular adaptations.

A comparable pattern was also observed for cardiopulmonary function. In the present study, significant main effects of time and group × time interaction effects were observed for VO₂max, maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), MIFR, Wmax, and maximal energy consumption (MEC). In particular, the simple effects analysis showed significant between-group differences in MIFR and Wmax. Koryagina et al. [13] reported that seven sessions of tDCS in triathletes improved the economy of cardiac function, mobilization reserves, and recovery processes, and suggested that tDCS may contribute to recovery facilitation and performance optimization in elite athletes across various sports. Machado et al. [11], in a systematic review and meta-analysis, reported that anodal tDCS applied over the primary motor cortex before exercise significantly increased cycling time to exhaustion, although this finding was largely influenced by a limited number of studies and no consistent effect was observed for isometric strength. Nevertheless, previous evidence suggests that tDCS may influence fatigue perception and exercise performance by modulating primary motor cortex excitability during high-intensity exercise. In this context, the greater improvements in MIFR and Wmax in the experimental group may reflect the contribution of central neuromodulation to exercise performance.

However, despite these positive interpretations, conflicting findings have also been reported in studies using Halo Sport. Garner et al. [29] and Auten et al. [30] reported that the Halo Sport condition did not produce significant improvements in high-intensity cycling performance compared with the sham condition. Judge [31] also found that this device did not induce significant changes in corticospinal excitability either at rest or during exercise. In contrast, Wang et al. [32] reported improvements in muscular endurance during an isometric fatigue task, and Huang et al. [33] reported improvements in repeated sprint ability. These findings suggest that the effects of Halo Sport may differ depending on the characteristics of the task, outcome measures, and stimulation protocol. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted with caution, and the observed improvements should not be attributed solely to the direct neurophysiological effects of tDCS.

Meanwhile, VO₂max significantly improved in both groups, but no significant between-group difference was observed. This may be because the primary effect of tDCS is mainly associated with central neural mechanisms, such as increased descending corticospinal drive and reduced central fatigue, rather than direct peripheral aerobic adaptations that determine VO2max, including increased cardiac output, capillary density, and oxidative enzyme activity within skeletal muscle [34]. Previous studies have consistently reported that M1 tDCS can significantly prolong exercise performance time without inducing substantial changes in cardiopulmonary variables such as oxygen uptake, minute ventilation, and heart rate [35]. In addition, considering that significant structural adaptations related to peripheral aerobic capacity generally require more than 5 weeks of intervention [36], the 4-week intervention period in the present study may not have been sufficient to elicit differential improvements in VO2max between groups.

Another important strength of the present study is that it simultaneously assessed sEMG-based lower-extremity muscle activity, inspiratory function, and cardiopulmonary function using a maximal exercise stress test. Previous studies have generally focused on a single outcome domain, such as muscle activity, exercise performance, or cardiopulmonary function, when examining tDCS combined with exercise or plyometric training alone. However, few studies have examined both neuromuscular and cardiopulmonary responses simultaneously to verify the systemic and multidimensional adaptations induced by combined training. In this regard, the present study has academic significance because it comprehensively analyzed the effects of tDCS combined with plyometric training at the levels of neuromuscular activation, cardiopulmonary function, and voluntary motor control.

Despite these strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the sample size was limited to 30 participants, and all participants were male recreational soccer players; therefore, caution is required when generalizing the findings to other athletic populations or the general population. Second, because the intervention period was limited to 4 weeks, long-term adaptations and retention effects could not be determined. Third, a single fixed tDCS stimulation protocol was used; therefore, the optimal stimulation intensity, site, and timing could not be explored. In addition, treatment fidelity, including plyometric training intensity and the accuracy of jump performance, could not be fully controlled, which may have contributed to variability in individual responses. Fourth, Halo Sport, which was used in the present study, is a consumer-grade tDCS headset and may differ from research-grade tDCS devices in terms of consistency of current delivery and electrode contact. As discussed above, because some previous studies have questioned the neurophysiological efficacy of this device, future studies should compare consumer-grade and research-grade tDCS devices and include direct measurements of corticospinal excitability using transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS). Fifth, the sEMG signals were normalized using %RVC rather than MVIC. Although this approach provided a standardized, task-specific reference related to the eccentric loading phase of plyometric exercise and enabled the target muscles to be evaluated under a common functional posture, %RVC does not represent muscle activation relative to maximal voluntary capacity. Therefore, the normalized values should be interpreted as activation relative to the selected low-squat reference task and should not be directly compared with values obtained in studies using MVIC-based normalization.

Taken together, the findings of the present study suggest that tDCS combined with plyometric training may be more effective than plyometric training alone for improving lower-extremity muscle activity and selected cardiopulmonary function variables in male recreational soccer players. These results support the possibility that tDCS may augment the neuromuscular and selected physiological adaptations induced by plyometric training. Therefore, tDCS-assisted plyometric training may be considered a practical adjunctive strategy for improving neuromuscular function and physiological capacities related to exercise performance in sports settings. However, given the limited sample size, short intervention period, use of a single stimulation protocol, and participant characteristics restricted to male recreational soccer players, further studies are needed to include diverse populations, long-term follow-up, optimization of stimulation parameters, and sport-specific performance outcomes such as sprinting, agility, and jump performance.
