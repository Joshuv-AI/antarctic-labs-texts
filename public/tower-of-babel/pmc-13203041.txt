Could AAV-vectored delivery of broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) reduce virologic failure in children diagnosed with perinatal HIV?
Authors: Kaiya L Pringle, Mauricio A Martins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntiretroviral therapy (ART) has transformed HIV infection into a manageable chronic condition, but its success depends on lifelong adherence, which can be challenging for children living with HIV (CLWH) and their caregivers, particularly in resource-limited settings. Passive immunization with HIV-1 broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) can maintain virologic suppression in CLWH after ART withdrawal and offers a promising complementary strategy. However, current bNAb delivery relies on repeated administrations of clinical-grade protein biologics, an approach that would be difficult to scale in regions with high rates of vertical HIV-1 transmission. Here, we argue that adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector-mediated gene transfer could overcome this limitation by enabling long-term expression of HIV-1 bNAbs in skeletal muscle. We discuss recent preclinical studies showing that a single neonatal administration of AAV vectors to rhesus macaques was safe and resulted in multi-year bNAb expression. Levels were sufficient to block simian-HIV (SHIV) infection in mucosal challenge models of HIV-1 transmission via breastfeeding and sexual intercourse. Ongoing studies are evaluating the potential of this strategy to durably suppress viremia in SHIV-infected rhesus macaques undergoing ART discontinuation. We believe clinical studies are warranted to evaluate the ability of AAV-expressed HIV-1 bNAbs to achieve durable ART-free virologic control in CLWH.KEYWORDS: AAV-vectored delivery, HIV-1 broadly neutralizing antibodies, virologic failure, children living with HIV, long-acting therapeutics

More than four decades after the discovery of HIV, global efforts to control the epidemic through prevention, diagnosis, and treatment have achieved major public health successes. Despite these advances, HIV remains a major global health burden. In 2024, an estimated 40.8 million people were living with HIV, and 1.3 million new infections occurred worldwide.1,2 The introduction of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has fundamentally improved the clinical management of HIV by enabling effective suppression of viremia and long-term preservation of immune function through combination drug regimens. Despite these advances, ART is not curative, and its success depends on uninterrupted treatment and ongoing clinical follow-up. For ART to achieve the best results, individuals must be diagnosed early, rapidly linked to care, and maintain consistent engagement in treatment, within a behavioral and structural continuum that remains fragile even after decades of programmatic investment.3 Additionally, in the past year, reductions in U.S.-supported international HIV funding have been associated with measurable declines in service. Between February and May 2025, 15,000 fewer individuals initiated ART (a 14% reduction), viral load testing declined by up to 44% in children, and treatment interruptions increased by nearly 40%. In Mozambique alone, modeling studies project 83,000 additional infections and 14,000 excess HIV-related deaths by 2030.4 These disruptions risk reversing decades of progress in global HIV control, exacerbating the persistent barriers faced by vulnerable populations and leaving those who already struggle with obtaining HIV care at risk. Funding instability that jeopardizes routine care and reliable access to antiretrovirals highlights a critical need for interventions that can maintain virologic suppression with reduced dependence on frequent clinic visits, complex supply chains, and continuous funding streams.

Many people, particularly in low- and middle-income areas, initiate ART only after substantial disease progression because of late diagnoses and/or persistent barriers to access.5 Others who begin treatment struggle to remain engaged because of stigma, socioeconomic hardship, mental health difficulties, or the logistical burden of lifelong clinic visits and daily medication use.6 The consequences of interrupted care are substantial. The loss of viral suppression leads to immunologic decline, virologic rebound, drug resistance, and increased risk of transmission.

This burden is particularly pronounced in pediatric populations. In 2023, approximately 1.4 million children under 15 years of age were living with HIV globally, with 87% residing in sub-Saharan Africa, where mother-to-child transmission is the major source of pediatric infections.7 Early initiation of ART in infants improves survival, promotes immune recovery, and in some cases limits the size of the viral reservoir.8 However, children with perinatally acquired HIV face unique biological and social vulnerabilities, including immature immune systems, reliance on caregivers for medication adherence, and transitions across multiple care environments.9 As a result, virologic failure, defined as the inability to achieve or sustain viral suppression during ART, disproportionately affects children, contributing to drug resistance and disease progression in pediatric populations. Across global settings, substantial proportions of children disengage from HIV care within the first year of ART, with higher rates of attrition and loss to follow-up observed compared with adults.10 This effect has driven continued interest in therapeutic approaches capable of providing durable virologic control with reduced dependence on daily lifelong adherence. In the pediatric HIV setting, two related but distinct goals guide antibody-based interventions: prophylactic administration around the time of exposure to prevent infection, and therapeutic use in infants and children with established HIV to achieve durable virologic suppression. Prophylactic neonatal gene therapy studies in nonhuman primates primarily model passive immunization strategies designed to prevent transmission during the perinatal period, thereby informing development of interventions to block early infection events. In parallel, therapeutic studies in perinatally infected children focus on sustaining long-term viral control and minimizing virologic rebound despite ongoing immune and viral maturation. Integrating insights from both prevention and treatment paradigms is essential to advancing strategies that reduce virologic failure and improve long-term outcomes for children living with or at risk of HIV infection.

One promising strategy to address the high rates of virologic failure in children living with HIV (CLWH) is passive immunization with HIV-1 broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs).11 Rather than administering these molecules as recombinant proteins, we and others are pursuing gene transfer-based approaches, such as adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated delivery, to enable sustained in vivo bNAb production.12 In this strategy, long-lived host cells, such as skeletal myocytes, are transduced to continuously secrete IgG therapeutics, allowing a single intramuscular (IM) administration to confer durable passive immunity against HIV-1 without the need for repeated dosing. Despite this potential, achieving consistent therapeutic expression of HIV-1 bNAbs following AAV-mediated gene transfer has proven challenging, in part because these antibodies frequently elicit host anti-drug antibody (ADA) responses that curb transgene expression.12 HIV-1 bNAbs typically contain high levels of somatic mutations acquired through prolonged germinal center maturation.13 While this high mutational burden underlies their virus neutralization breadth and potency, it also increases their immunogenicity when expressed by AAV-transduced host cells. Consistent with this notion, the degree of sequence divergence between HIV-1 bNAbs and their inferred germline precursors has been shown to correlate inversely with ADA reactivity in AAV/bNAb-treated rhesus macaques.14 Together, these observations indicate that intrinsic properties of HIV-1 bNAbs, including the extent of somatic hypermutation, play a central role in determining the durability of AAV-mediated expression and should, therefore, be carefully considered when selecting candidate IgG molecules for gene-based delivery.

HIV-1 sequence diversity and rapid viral escape continue to pose concerns for antibody-based interventions, particularly in CLWH with established, heterogeneous reservoirs. Multispecific IgG therapeutics, including bi- and tri-specific antibodies capable of simultaneously targeting multiple conserved Env epitopes, have demonstrated enhanced promising activity in preclinical models and early clinical studies, suggesting a promising strategy to limit viral escape compared with single bNAbs or narrowly targeted combinations.15,17,18 At the same time, experimental studies of vectored immunotherapy demonstrate that HIV-1 can evolve resistance mutations under antibody pressure, particularly when antibody levels are insufficient to fully suppress replication. This highlights the importance of maintaining adequate expression levels and neutralization breadth in gene-based delivery strategies.16,17

Importantly, AAV/bNAb treatment success is strongly affected by the developmental state of the host immune system at the time of vector dosing. During the neonatal period, the immune system is biased toward tolerance rather than immunity to foreign antigens,19 with direct implications for the outcome of gene therapy with AAV/bNAb vectors. Work from our group has provided preclinical evidence supporting this approach. In neonatal rhesus macaques, an AAV-8 vector expressing a rhesus (rh)-IgG1 version of the HIV-1-specific bNAb 3BNC117 was administered 24–48 hours after birth.20 Nearly 90% of monkeys treated with this AAV-8/rh-3BNC117-IgG1 vector developed persistent bNAb expression with little or no ADAs. Notably, transgene expression lasted for years without redosing, with AAV-8/rh-3BNC117-IgG1-treated macaques still expressing the bNAb at three years of follow-up as they approached adolescence. In simian-HIV challenge models designed to mimic HIV-1 transmission through breastfeeding and sexual intercourse, neonatal gene therapy with the AAV-8/rh-3BNC117-IgG1 vector afforded significant protection against infection compared to age-matched macaques challenged in parallel.20 These findings show that neonatal IM gene therapy with AAV/bNAb vectors can result in durable HIV-1 protection in primates.

A second feature of early life that favors AAV-vectored delivery of IgG therapeutics relates to pre-existing immunity to AAV vectors. In adults, neutralizing antibodies against one or more AAV serotypes are highly prevalent and can substantially impair vector transduction,21 representing a major barrier to the efficacy and scalability of AAV-based gene therapies. In contrast, although newborns can exhibit high AAV seroprevalence at birth due to passively acquired maternal IgG,22 these antibodies decline naturally over the months following birth. Longitudinal serological studies have shown that most infants become effectively AAV-naïve between approximately 6 and 12 months of age, creating a predictable developmental window during which vector uptake is unlikely to be limited by pre-existing humoral immunity.23 This window may be particularly advantageous for AAV/bNAb-based strategies administered intramuscularly, a route that appears less susceptible to pre-existing anti-AAV immunity than systemic administration.24 In addition, infants are more likely than adults to be diagnosed with HIV shortly after transmission because of routine postnatal health evaluations that are followed in most countries. Together, these considerations highlight the unique advantages of deploying IM AAV/bNAb therapies during early life.

In parallel, clinical studies in children offer complementary evidence on the feasibility, effectiveness, and acceptability of antibody-based interventions in closely monitored pediatric settings. The Tatelo study, conducted in Botswana among very early treated, virally suppressed CLWH, evaluated monthly infusions of two bNAbs (VRC01LS and 10-1074) as an alternative to daily ART.25 In this cohort, 44% of participants maintained viral suppression for 24 weeks post ART withdrawal while receiving bNAbs alone, exceeding the prespecified threshold for success. Small reservoirs size and susceptibility of archived proviruses to neutralization by 10-1074 were identified as key correlates of virologic success. Additionally, caregiver preference data from Botswana indicated that monthly intravenous bNAb infusions were favored over daily ART, underscoring the acceptability of long-acting bNAb-based regimens for virologic control.26 At the same time, the partial efficacy observed in Tatelo provides a useful framework for planning future bNAb-based interventions in CLWH. These results underscore the need for broader and more potent combinations of IgG therapeutics to contend with the diversity of HIV-1 isolates in children.

Despite encouraging efficacy and acceptability data, important uncertainties persist regarding the long-term immunologic effects of sustained bNAb exposure. Broadly neutralizing antibodies have demonstrated favorable safety profiles in clinical trials to date, and there is no evidence that sustained exposure to high levels of antiviral antibodies is detrimental to the host. Translational and implementation challenges are central to determining real-world impact, but emerging gene-based antibody delivery platforms offer clear paths to addressing many of these limitations. Preexisting immunity to AAV serotypes, constraints on vector manufacturing capacity, and the inability to readily redose with the same capsid currently limit the scalability and flexibility of AAV-based approaches. Recent clinical data using non-viral genetic delivery strategies, such as electroporation-delivered DNA-encoded monoclonal antibodies (DMAbs), highlight complementary solutions. In a phase 1 study in healthy adults, IM electroporation of plasmid DNA encoding SARS-CoV-2-neutralizing antibodies resulted in durable in vivo antibody expression for more than 72 weeks. Notably, despite the perceived immunostimulatory effects of IM electroporation, none of the 39 evaluable participants developed detectable ADA responses.27 This platform enables rapid antibody redesign, scalable manufacturing, and repeat dosing highlighting the potential of non-viral gene delivery strategies for durable and adaptable antibody expression.

While IM electroporation may not be the most appropriate delivery platform for pediatric populations, this study demonstrates that nonviral genetic approaches can sustain protective antibody levels in vivo, offering a foundation for future pediatric applications. Alongside its potential, the translation of such interventions into neonates will require careful consideration of ethical, regulatory, and long-term safety challenges, particularly in resource-limited settings, where robust pharmacovigilance systems, extended follow-up frameworks, and community-engaged consent processes will be essential. Ultimately, these approaches point toward a future in which in vivo delivery of pathogen-specific monoclonal antibodies may offer a flexible, durable, and globally scalable alternative for the prevention and treatment of HIV and other infectious diseases. Rather than representing a singular solution, AAV-mediated delivery should be viewed as one component of a broader and evolving landscape of genetic antibody technologies.

In summary, preclinical and clinical studies highlight the complementary potential of antibody-based interventions to prevent pediatric HIV transmission and maintain durable viral suppression among CLWH, offering a path toward interventions that reduce reliance on lifelong ART. Neonatal AAV-mediated delivery of bNAbs represents a particularly promising approach, capable of sustaining high antibody levels from a single administration during a window of immune tolerance. At the same time, insights emerging from preclinical gene transfer studies and pediatric bNAb trials help define the biological, immunologic, and practical boundaries within which these strategies are most likely to succeed. These include the impact of antibody immunogenicity on durable expression, the need for sufficient breadth to limit viral escape, and the importance of scalable deployment strategies in resource-limited settings. Although challenges related to manufacturing, re-dosing, and large-scale deployment remain, continued advances in vector engineering and delivery technologies position AAV-mediated antibody expression as a promising foundation for durable pediatric HIV interventions with reduced reliance on continuous ART.28 Moving forward, continued investment in addressing these hurdles, will be essential to advancing proof-of-concept successes to develop safe, durable, and widely implementable interventions that meaningfully reduce the burden of pediatric HIV.

Biographies

Kaiya L. Pringle is a research associate in the Department of Immunology and Microbiology at The Herbert Wertheim UF Scripps Institute (Jupiter, Florida), working in the Martins Lab. Their research focuses on viral vector–based gene delivery and antibody therapeutics, including AAV-mediated delivery of anti-Env broadly neutralizing antibodies for sustained viral suppression in nonhuman primate models. Kaiya is preparing for doctoral training in immunology and virology.

Dr. Mauricio A. Martins is an associate professor at the University of Florida. His lab is developing practical and scalable interventions to prevent and functionally cure HIV infection. His research focuses on genetic delivery of HIV-1 IgG therapeutics to long-lived cells (like muscle cells) to achieve multi-year immunity against HIV after a single administration.
