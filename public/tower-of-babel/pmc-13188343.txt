The serial multiple mediating effects of experiential avoidance and safety behaviors on the relationship between fear of negative evaluation on social anxiety in early adults
Authors: Yujie Du, Sung-Man Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the serial multiple mediating role of experiential avoidance and safety behaviors in the relationship between fear of negative evaluation and social anxiety among early adults. It also analyzes structural connections between core variables. This research aims to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the fear of negative evaluation that induces social anxiety and its development and persistence.MethodsThis study employed an online survey methodology. A total of 426 individuals participated, of whom 313 were included in the final analyses. Structural equation modeling was used to examine the structural relationships among the variables.ResultsThe findings indicated separate partial mediation effects through experiential avoidance and safety behaviors in linking fear of negative evaluation with social anxiety. A sequential mediation pathway emerged in which fear of negative evaluation initially heightened experiential avoidance, and subsequently intensified anxiety symptoms via increased safety behaviors. This pattern suggests a cyclical reinforcement mechanism in cognitive avoidance strategies that promote maladaptive behavioral responses.ConclusionsBy clarifying the dual mediating roles of experiential avoidance and safety behaviors, this study enhances the comprehension of social anxiety etiology. The theoretical implications suggest that therapeutic approaches that address both cognitive avoidance patterns and behavioral safety strategies may significantly alleviate social anxiety symptoms. Subsequent investigations should implement longitudinal designs and controlled experimental paradigms to establish temporal precedence and causal interactions among study variables.

Introduction

Social anxiety (SA) is a common psychological experience characterized by strong fear in social situations where one may be evaluated by others [1]. Conceptualized along a continuum, SA ranges from mild and subclinical manifestations to more severe expressions [2]. When this anxiety is excessive relative to typical social demands (e.g., everyday interactions at school or in occupational settings) and leads to marked functional impairment, it is clinically conceptualized as social anxiety disorder (SAD), historically termed social phobia [1, 2]. Data shows that young adults experience SAD at rates ranging from 0.2% to 12% worldwide, with numbers demonstrating an upward trend [2]. The transition period of early adulthood involves major psychosocial developments and is accompanied by increased psychological pressure related to decision-making regarding education, career development, and romantic relationships. According to the 2021 Korean National Mental Health Survey, the 12-month prevalence of anxiety disorders was highest among individuals aged 18–29 years (4.0%), followed by those aged 30–39 years (3.6%), ranking third among all age groups [3].

Cognitive and cognitive-behavioral models of SAD conceptualize concerns about evaluation, particularly fear of negative evaluation (FNE), as an important factor in the development and maintenance of the disorder [4–6]. Although Clark and Wells’ cognitive model [4] does not explicitly use the term FNE, it remains closely related to concerns about being negatively evaluated by others. The model proposes that when individuals perceive a risk of being negatively evaluated, social situations are appraised as threatening, even in the absence of objective danger. This appraisal process triggers heightened self-focused attention, whereby individuals rely on internal sensations, thoughts, and impressions as indicators of how they are being judged by others. In response to these perceived threats, individuals engage in safety behaviors (SB) aimed at preventing anticipated negative outcomes, which ultimately contribute to the maintenance of social anxiety over time. Building on this perspective, Rapee and Heimberg [5] proposed a cognitive-behavioral model of social phobia in which SA arises from fears of being negatively evaluated and is activated and maintained when individuals compare their mental representation of the self with perceived audience standards.

According to Johnson et al. [6], the experience of FNE is a direct factor that leads to the development of SAD. Individuals with FNE tend to worry intensely about how others perceive them and are afraid of negative evaluations from others. They believe that negative judgments are likely to occur frequently, overestimate others’ negative evaluations, and vigorously engage in various avoidance behaviors as protective measures [4, 5].

Avoidance behavior is a maintenance factor for SAD [7]. It can be subdivided into trait-based experiential avoidance (EA) [8] and background-based SB [9, 10]. EA is a core construct of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and refers to an avoidance tendency, characterized by rigidity and inflexibility, in which individuals are unwilling to engage with unpleasant internal experiences (e.g., thoughts, feelings, and emotions), and attempt to control or avoid such experiences [11]. In contrast, SB represents the core issue of Clark and Wells’ cognitive model of SAD [4], and refers to the mental processes and behavioral strategies employed to avert or mitigate perceived catastrophic outcomes in social contexts [12]. SB is distinct from adaptive coping and constitutes a dysfunctional emotional regulation strategy. When fear is based on genuine threats, the response is adaptive; however, in the absence of actual danger, the response is classified as SB and considered maladaptive [13].

Although avoidance strategies may temporarily alleviate acute anxiety, prolonged reliance on these strategies tends to exacerbate anxiety symptoms over time, ultimately increasing dependence on SB [4, 14]. Moreover, the greater the level of effort exerted to suppress internal experiences, the stronger the rebound effect, often resulting in adverse outcomes [15, 16]. In individuals with anxiety disorders, the inability to regulate distressing internal experiences induces emotional distress and behavioral restrictions, thereby aggravating anxiety symptoms and reinforcing its cyclical relationship with EA [14].

Individuals who perceive SA as inherently detrimental tend to suppress internal experiences associated with it; however, such suppression is often counterproductive. Those with high levels of EA tend to rely on dysfunctional emotion regulation strategies, applying specific coping strategies rigidly across situations without considering whether these strategies are actually effective in a given context. Consequently, the coping strategies they adopt are often maladaptive [17], including SB aimed at managing distress and avoiding anxiety-provoking situations. EA inherently contributes to emotional distress and maladaptive behavioral patterns; individuals with excessive EA are more likely to avoid high-risk situations that could trigger these negative consequences [14].

For instance, in evaluative situations such as meetings, individuals with SAD often experience excessive concern about the possibility of being negatively evaluated. According to the cognitive model of SAD by Clark and Wells [4], anticipation of negative evaluation leads individuals to appraise social situations as threatening, triggers anxiety symptoms, and shifts attention toward the self, away from external social cues. They may closely monitor their anxiety symptoms and worry that others will perceive them as anxious and consequently evaluate them negatively. As a result, they may attempt to control or suppress these anxiety symptoms during social interactions [4]. Such a tendency to control internal experiences is what we refer to as EA [11]. Such a tendency may lead individuals to engage in SB to avoid experiencing SA symptoms [9], such as making excuses to evade meetings or social gatherings. However, this can lead to adverse consequences, such as reduced wages or strained interpersonal relationships, which may further worsen their SA symptoms.

Multiple studies have shown that patients with SA display SB at higher rates than the general population [18, 19]. The excessive use of SB often creates more anxiety that impairs their social abilities [4, 5]. When no threat materializes during the use of SB, individuals tend to attribute the absence of negative outcomes to their reliance on these behaviors [20]. Moreover, when they encounter anxiety-provoking situations, SB tends to distract them, diverting their focus to perform the SB rather than reappraising the situation or perceived threat, thereby further intensifying the symptoms of SA [4, 18, 21].

Existing research has independently established correlations among FNE, EA, SB, and SAD [6, 9, 18, 22, 23]. Studies have confirmed that EA partially mediates the impact of FNE on SAD [24]. Additionally, a pathway analysis investigating the influence of FNE on interpersonal problems revealed that individuals with higher FNE tended to employ more SB [25]. However, relatively few studies have investigated EA and SB together, and none have comprehensively integrated FNE, EA, SB, and SAD to explore the relationships and mechanisms among these variables. Several studies have examined the relationship between EA and SB [9, 10].

Illustratively, Bae and Won’s [9] study investigating multidimensional EA and SB impacts on SA revealed strong positive associations between these constructs, with mediation analyses indicating SB’s partial intermediary role in the EA-SA relationship. However, their study did not employ all the subfactors of the Subtle Avoidance Frequency Examination (SAFE), a widely used instrument for assessing SB. Instead, only the first subfactor, “subtle safety behaviors,” was utilized. The researchers classified SB into problematic preventive and non-problematic restorative categories, hypothesizing that the subtle safety behaviors subfactor conceptually corresponds to preventive SB and is therefore suitable for measurement. Nevertheless, the subfactor classification defined in SAFE differs fundamentally from the preventive–restorative categorization. Consequently, relying solely on the subtle safety behaviors subfactor may not comprehensively capture the multifaceted nature of SB.

In contrast, Kirk et al. [10] applied path analytical methods to examine individuals with SA and panic. Their results revealed a significant association between EA and preventive SB, but demonstrated EA’s non-significant predictive capacity regarding these behavioral patterns. This discrepancy might stem from a methodological focus on the temporal dimensions of preventive SB, instead of the implementation of behavioral typology classification.

Therefore, the present study aims to overcome limitations of previous research by employing the SAFE for SB assessment and examining the sequential mediating roles of EA and SB in the relationship between FNE and SA (Fig. 1).

Methods

Participants

Following the ethical clearance (approval no. 2024-03-005) from Dankook University’s Institutional Review Board, a web-based, self-administered survey (Google Forms platform) was distributed to early adults residing in Seoul, Gyeonggi-do, Chungcheongdo, and adjacent areas between May 11 and May 18, 2024. Participants were recruited through online community-based channels, including social networking platforms (e.g., KakaoTalk group chats and WeChat groups) and public online forums (e.g., Naver Café), where the survey link was openly shared.

Participants were eligible if they were aged between 19 and 35 years (inclusive) and provided informed consent. The initial recruitment yielded 426 consenting participants, who received prepaid mobile gift cards as compensation. After applying the exclusion criteria (2 duplicate entries, 2 age-ineligible cases, 62 straight-line responses), outliers were screened across all continuous study variables. Following a robust statistical approach, values exceeding the range of the median ± 2.5 times the median absolute deviation for each variable were classified as outliers. Based on this criterion, 47 cases were identified and removed. The final sample consisted of 313 valid datasets that were included in the analyses [26]. The cohort comprised 185 female (59.1%) and 128 male (40.9%) participants.

Age stratification revealed that 98 (31.3%) participants were aged 19–25, 142 (45.4%) were 26–30, and 73 (23.3%) were 31–35 (M = 27.21, SD = 4.181). Educational attainment distribution included: ≤high school diploma (n = 22, 7.0%), vocational college graduate (n = 45, 14.4%), current university enrollment (n = 70, 22.4%), bachelor’s degree completion (n = 142, 45.4%), and graduate level or higher (n = 34, 10.9%). In terms of employment status, 203 participants (64.9%) were employed, 16 (5.1%) were unemployed or job-seeking, 89 (28.4%) were students, and 5 (1.6%) reported other statuses. Measures

Social anxiety

This investigation employed two psychometric instruments, the Social Phobia Scale (SPS) and Social Interaction Anxiety Scale (SIAS), initially created by Mattick and Clarke [27], with subsequent cultural adaptation and psychometric validation by Kim [28]. These complementary measures operate as an integrated assessment system where composite scores are derived from cumulative item responses across both tools. The SPS comprises 20 evaluative statements, whereas the SIAS comprises 19 diagnostic items. A five-point Likert-type scale, with response anchors ranging from “not at all (0)” to “extremely (4),” was used; elevated composite scores reflected heightened SA severity. Internal consistency analyses demonstrated excellent reliability coefficients; α = 0.955 for SPS and α = 0.936 for SIAS.

Fear of negative evaluation

This investigation employed the Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale (BFNE), initially pioneered by Watson and Friend [29], streamlined by Leary [30], and later culturally adapted and psychometrically validated by Lee and Choi [31] to assess individual differences in apprehension toward negative social appraisal. The instrument comprises 12 diagnostic statements evaluated through a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from “not at all (1)” to “very much (5)”; elevated composite scores reflect intensified fear responses. Psychometric evaluation revealed exceptional internal consistency (α = 0.916) for BFNE in the current sample.

Experiential avoidance

The 24-item brief form of the Korean version of the Multidimensional Experiential Avoidance Questionnaire (K-MEAQ-24), based on the original questionnaire by Gámez et al. [32], culturally adapted and psychometrically refined by Lee and Yoo [33], was used in this study. The instrument encompasses 24 evaluative statements measured through a six-point Likert-type scale, ranging from “strongly disagree (1)” to “strongly agree (6)”. This construct includes behavioral avoidance, distress aversion, procrastination, distraction/suppression, repression/denial, and distress endurance. Psychometric analysis determined robust internal consistency for the current dataset (α = 0.832).

Safety behaviors

This study employed the SAFE, initially pioneered by Cuming et al. [34], with subsequent cultural adaptation and psychometric validation conducted by Koo et al. [35], to quantify protective behavioral patterns. This instrument encompasses 32 evaluative statements using a five-point Likert-type scale (1=“never” to 5=“always”). The construct delineates three behavioral dimensions: subtle SB, behavior to hide physical symptoms, and proactive SB. Elevated composite scores reflect increased engagement in SB. Psychometric evaluation demonstrated excellent internal consistency for the measure (α = 0.954).

Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v26) and AMOS (v23) [36, 37]. The initial analytical procedure conducted descriptive statistics to characterize the participants’ demographics. Subsequent reliability assessments of the measurement instruments were performed through computation of Cronbach’s α coefficients. Bivariate correlation coefficients were computed to investigate the intervariable relationships among FNE, EA, SB, and SAD. It was preceded by extreme value analysis to verify multivariate normality assumptions.

Following Anderson and Gerbing’s [38] sequential analytical framework, a confirmatory factor analysis preceded the structural equation modeling evaluations. The final analytical stage incorporated demographic covariates (gender and age) while implementing nonparametric resampling procedures (5,000 iterations) with user-defined estimates to assess both individual and sequential mediation pathways.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics for SA indicated that the mean sum score of the SPS was 34.67 (SD = 19.23). Based on ROC-derived cut-off values proposed in Korean clinical validation studies (SPS > 28) [39], 180 participants (57.5%) exceeded the threshold for clinically relevant SA symptoms. Similarly, the mean sum score of the SIAS was 38.29 (SD = 16.58). Using the Korean ROC-based cut-off value (SIAS > 40), 143 participants (45.7%) scored above the threshold. Notably, 128 participants (40.9%) exceeded both cut-off thresholds.

Correlation analysis

The initial analytical procedures produced descriptive analyses and Pearson correlations for all variables included in the study. The correlation results are presented in Table 2. Normality assumptions were confirmed as absolute skewness values (≤ 0.07) and kurtosis measures (≤ 0.13) remained within the established thresholds [40]. Bivariate analysis demonstrated that FNE exhibited robust positive associations with EA (r = 0.454, p < 0.01), SB (r = 0.597, p < 0.01), and SA (r = 0.619, p < 0.01). Furthermore, EA was significantly correlated to SB (r = 0.633, p < 0.01) and SA (r = 0.672, p < 0.01). Notably, the strongest inter-variable relationship emerged between SB and SA (r = 0.836, p < 0.01).

Preliminary Diagnostic Tests

Before testing for mediating effects, Harman’s single-factor test was performed to assess common method bias (CMB), as all variables were collected using self-report questionnaires at the same time points. The results showed that the first factor accounted for 33.097% of the total variance, which is below the threshold of 40%, indicating that CMB was not a serious concern in this study [41].

Given that Harman’s single-factor test has been criticized for its limited sensitivity in detecting common method variance, we further conducted a CFA-based test by specifying a single-factor measurement model in which all observed indicators loaded onto one latent factor. Model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). The single-factor model demonstrated poor fit, with a chi-square value of 16913.887 (df = 5564), CFI = 0.529, TLI = 0.520, and RMSEA = 0.081 (90% CI [0.079,0.082]), indicating that common method bias is unlikely to account for the observed relationships, consistent with established methodological recommendations [42].

Additionally, to assess multicollinearity, we computed the variance inflation factor (VIF) for the main variables (FNE total, EA total, and SB total). All VIF values ranged from 1.577 to 2.090, well below the cutoff value of 10, indicating the absence of problematic covariance among the variables [43].

Examination of the measurement model

Following Anderson and Gerbing’s two-step approach [38], we first evaluated the measurement model prior to testing the structural model. All study variables were modeled as latent variables and were defined by multiple observed indicators derived from their respective scales, with item parcels constructed to enhance model parsimony.

Specifically, for FNE, which demonstrated a unidimensional factor structure, an item-to-construct balance approach was used to create three parcels. Items were first ranked in descending order based on their factor loadings. They were then assigned sequentially to parcels from the first to the last parcel (Parcel 1 to Parcel 3), after which the assignment direction was reversed (Parcel 3 to Parcel 1). This forward–reverse allocation cycle was repeated until all items were distributed, thereby ensuring a balanced distribution of high- and low-loading items across parcels [44]. For EA and SB, a domain-representative approach was adopted, whereby items reflecting different conceptual domains were evenly distributed and aggregated to form parcels [45]. EA was represented by four parcels and SB by three parcels. SA was modeled using two indicators corresponding to the SPS and SIAS, consistent with the original measurement structure of social anxiety. This parceling strategy was used to preserve content validity while reducing model complexity.

Measurement model evaluations revealed excellent fit indices (χ²=137.571, df = 48, p < 0.001), with CFI = 0.976 and TLI = 0.968. The RMSEA was 0.077 (90% CI [0.062,0.093]), slightly exceeding the threshold of 0.05, but remaining within the acceptable parameters (0.08), indicating an adequate model fit. These outcomes collectively validated the psychometric adequacy of the measurement framework. All standardized factor loading coefficients between the observed variables and their corresponding latent constructs were statistically significant (p < 0.001). The detailed loading ranges were as follows: FNE (0.89–0.92), EA (0.83–0.87), SB (0.92–0.96), and SA (0.89–0.95). These psychometric properties provide strong evidence for the construct validity of all the latent variables.

Examination of the structural model

Model evaluations demonstrated CFI and TLI values exceeding 0.95, along with RMSEA estimates below 0.08, suggesting an acceptable model fit (Table 3). Path analysis indicated that FNE had significant positive effects on EA (β = 0.506, p < 0.001), SB (β = 0.408, p < 0.001), and SA (β = 0.227, p < 0.001). Furthermore, EA was found to significantly influence both SB (β = 0.469, p < 0.001) and SA (β = 0.252, p < 0.001). Finally, SB had a substantial positive impact on SA (β = 0.561, p = 0.001), as illustrated in Fig. 2. The final structural model explained 86.1% of the variance in social anxiety (R²=0.861). Age and gender were included as covariates in the structural model. Gender showed a significant direct association with social anxiety (p < 0.001), whereas age did not show a significant effect (p > 0.05). Importantly, controlling for these demographic variables did not alter the significance or direction of the hypothesized structural paths.

Testing for mediating effect verification

A nonparametric bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 resamples was conducted, using user-defined estimators to evaluate individual and sequential mediating effects of EA and SB, with 95% confidence intervals calculated for each indirect effect (Table 4). The indirect effect of FNE on SA through EA was statistically significant (β = 0.128, p < 0.001; 95% CI [0.078,0.188]). A significant indirect effect also emerged through SB (β = 0.229, p < 0.001; 95% CI [0.156,0.310]). Crucially, the indirect effect of FNE on SA, through the sequential mediation of EA and SB, was also statistically significant (β = 0.133, p < 0.001; 95% CI [0.090,0.188]). The exclusion of zero within all confidence intervals substantiates the statistical significance of these mediation pathways.

Discussion

This study examined the mediating roles of EA and SB; the structural relationships among key variables; and the association between FNE and SA among early adults.

First, the initial hypothesis was supported, indicating that EA partially mediated the relationship between FNE and SA. Specifically, FNE exerts direct and indirect effects (through EA) on SA. This is consistent with the findings of Seong and Kim [24], who observed that individuals who are highly sensitive to negative social evaluations are more likely to engage in EA, thereby increasing their vulnerability to SA.

Second, SB were found to partially mediate the relationship between FNE and SA. This finding suggests that individuals who are more fearful of being negatively evaluated tend to engage in SB as a coping strategy in social situations. Although these behaviors were initially intended to reduce perceived social threats, they may paradoxically and unintentionally maintain or even exacerbate symptoms of SA over time. This finding aligns with the cognitive-behavioral models of SA, which propose that SB prevent individuals from refuting their negative beliefs, thereby contributing to the persistence of anxiety [46].

Third, the individual and sequential mediating roles of EA and SB in the relationship between FNE and SA were found to be statistically significant. Notably, the sequential pathway, from FNE to EA, then to SB, and ultimately to SA, demonstrated a stable and robust effect size (β = 0.133, p < 0.001). This suggests that SA is more likely to be maintained through the interplay of multiple psychological mechanisms rather than a single factor. Individuals with elevated FNE are more inclined to habitually avoid aversive internal experiences. In anxiety-provoking social contexts, they tend to rely on SB to mitigate perceived threats. This cascade of avoidance processes may lead to the onset or exacerbation of SA. These findings are consistent with those of previous research, emphasizing the critical roles of EA and SB in the development and maintenance of SA [9].

In addition, among the three mediation pathways examined, the indirect effect through SB was numerically the largest (β = 0.229, p < 0.001), indicating that SB showed the strongest indirect association linking FNE and SA within the present model. Individuals with higher levels of FNE may be more inclined to engage in SB when anticipating potential social threat, which may provide short-term relief from anxiety. However, such behaviors can restrict opportunities for corrective learning and reinforce maladaptive interpretations, such as attributing the absence of negative outcomes to the use of SB rather than the actual safety of the situation. In East Asian cultural contexts, sensitivity to social evaluation tends to be elevated, reflecting strong cultural emphases on social harmony, modesty, and behavioral restraint. Prior cross-cultural research indicates that socially reticent behaviors, including emotional suppression, avoidance of assertive self-expression, and heightened self-monitoring to prevent social disruption, are more culturally consistent and less socially penalized in East Asian societies than in Western contexts [47]. Thus, certain forms of SB may be interpreted as culturally normative politeness, such as limiting eye contact, reducing verbal participation, and carefully regulating one’s speech and behavior, thereby reinforcing their use and potentially impeding long-term therapeutic progress.

Furthermore, the robustness of the sequential indirect model was reinforced by controlling for key demographic covariates, specifically gender and age. The indirect association from FNE to SA, via EA and SB, remained statistically significant after adjusting for these variables. This indicates that the indirect pathway was robust beyond the inclusion of demographic factors.

The findings of this study have important theoretical and practical implications. In terms of theory, it expands the current literature on SA by identifying the mediating roles of EA and SB in its development. Interventions that target EA and modify SB seem effective in reducing SA. For instance, approaches such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) or ACT facilitate the adaptive regulation of FNE while diminishing EA and SB.

Nevertheless, there are several limitations to this study. First, although the structural model explained a substantial proportion of variance in SA (R² = 0.861), the strong association observed between SB and SA (r = 0.836) suggests a considerable degree of conceptual proximity between these constructs in the present data. Although statistical diagnostics did not indicate multicollinearity concerns, SB may, to some extent, reflect behavioral expressions closely intertwined with the experience of SA itself. Therefore, the magnitude of effects associated with SB and the high explained variance should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Future research may benefit from incorporating behavioral assessments, observer-rated evaluations, or experimental designs, rather than relying solely on self-report questionnaires, to further disentangle the distinct and shared components of these constructs. Second, employing a cross-sectional design prevented the establishment of causal relationships among the variables. Accordingly, although the proposed mediation model is theoretically grounded, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting patterns of association rather than evidence of temporal ordering or developmental processes among the variables. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs to rigorously examine the temporal relationships among the variables. Third, the reliance on self-report questionnaires may introduce a social desirability bias into the responses. In addition, although the sample included individuals with diverse educational and employment backgrounds, participants were recruited through online community-based channels, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Moreover, given that the functional interpretation of SB may be shaped by cultural norms, the present findings should be generalized to Western cultural contexts with caution. To mitigate these limitations, subsequent studies should incorporate experimental designs, employ diverse sampling methods, and consider cross-cultural comparisons to enhance the generalizability of the findings across different cultural contexts. Furthermore, building upon these results, researchers could develop and evaluate intervention strategies for SA, thereby facilitating their application in real-world counseling and therapeutic settings.
