Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists as adjunctive therapy in refractory hypertriglyceridemia following recurrent pancreatitis
Authors: Daisuke Miura, Stephen Nicholls, Anju E Joham, Michael Braude, Chau Thien Tay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 39-year-old Chinese man was first evaluated at a multidisciplinary metabolic dysfunction–associated liver disease clinic following 6 episodes of hypertriglyceridemia-induced pancreatitis over 9 years despite maximal therapy with statins, fibrates, and omega-3 fatty acids. Peak triglycerides reached 2329 mg/dL (Système International [SI]: 26.3 mmol/L; reference range, <177 mg/dL [SI: <2 mmol/L]). Comorbidities included poorly controlled diabetes, recurrent hypoglycemia, and steatotic liver disease with moderate fibrosis. Despite historical safety concerns regarding glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in prior pancreatitis, semaglutide was cautiously initiated. This achieved sustained triglyceride normalization to 213 mg/dL (SI: 2.4 mmol/L), weight loss, glycemic improvement, hypoglycemia resolution, improved liver elastography parameters, and insulin cessation without pancreatitis recurrence. This case demonstrates that glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists warrant consideration in treatment-resistant hypertriglyceridemia with prior pancreatitis when conventional therapy fails, challenging traditional contraindications in carefully selected patients under multidisciplinary care.Keywords: hypertriglyceridemia, pancreatitis, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, incretin-based combination therapies

Introduction

Hypertriglyceridemia represents the third leading cause of acute pancreatitis after gallstones and alcohol [1], accounting for ∼20% of the cases [2]. Systematic review evidence demonstrates that hypertriglyceridemia-induced acute pancreatitis (HTGP) is associated with worse pancreatitis severity, higher mortality rates (20% vs 15%), and recurrence rates (up to 65% vs 23%) compared with other etiologies [2].

Acute treatment comprises plasmapheresis and insulin infusion, with a transition to long-term lipid-lowering therapies [3]. Chronic prevention relies predominantly on lifestyle management (including dietary fat restriction, alcohol cessation, and regular exercise) and fibrate therapy (fenofibrate and gemfibrozil) as first-line pharmacology treatment, with adjunctive therapies including statins, omega-3 fatty acids, and niacin providing additional lipid-lowering support [4]. However, a substantial subset exhibits inadequate reduction despite maximized combination therapy, leading to recurrent pancreatitis and a critical management gap.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) are incretin-based therapies originally developed for type 2 diabetes management. Beyond their glycemic effects, these agents have favorable lipid profile effects with large-scale cardiovascular outcome trials confirming substantial triglyceride reductions alongside established cardiovascular mortality benefits [5]. However, early postmarketing surveillance and observational studies raised safety concerns regarding potential pancreatitis adverse events, creating a clinical paradox that has historically precluded their therapeutic consideration in patients with pancreatitis [6].

Recent mechanistic studies and updated safety analyses suggested concerns of GLP-1RA-induced pancreatitis may be overstated, warranting reconsideration of their therapeutic potential in those with refractory hypertriglyceridemia [7, 8]. Herein, we present a case of recurrent HTGP with triglyceride levels refractory to statin–fenofibrate combination, where subsequent GLP-1RA introduction achieved sustained triglyceride normalization, illustrating an innovative approach for treatment-resistant hypertriglyceridemia management.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old Chinese man with complex metabolic syndrome was first evaluated in the Monash Health multidisciplinary metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) clinic by a hepatologist, endocrinologist, and dietitian in October 2022. His medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) diagnosed at age 29, premature ischemic heart disease with 2 coronary stents, dyslipidemia with severe hypertriglyceridemia and recurrent HTGP, all originally diagnosed in China. Of 6 lifetime episodes of HTGP, 2 occurred in China and 4 following his arrival in Australia since 2016. MASLD was diagnosed in 2017 by ultrasound. His medication regimen at the time of the October 2022 MASLD clinic review comprised ezetimibe 10 mg daily, simvastatin 80 mg daily, fenofibrate 145 mg daily, fish oil 9 g daily, metoprolol 23.75 mg daily, perindopril 4 mg daily, metformin 1000 mg twice daily, empagliflozin 10 mg daily, biphasic isophane insulin twice daily (20 units in the morning, 40 units in the evening), and aspirin 100 mg daily. There was a strong family history of metabolic disease, with his mother developing ischemic heart disease in her 60s in the setting of dyslipidemia, and his father having T2DM. He worked as plasterer and was an ex-smoker.

Diagnostic assessment

At the October 2022 index clinic review, examination revealed a body mass index (BMI) of 27.4 kg/m2 (Asian-specific threshold for obesity: ≥25.0 kg/m2) with no features of lipodystrophy. Total cholesterol was 209 mg/dL (Système International [SI]: 5.4 mmol/L; reference range, <213 mg/dL [SI: <5.5 mmol/L]); triglyceride was 399 mg/dL (SI: 4.5 mmol/L; reference range, <177 mg/dL [SI: <2.0 mmol/L]), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was 8.3% (SI: 67 mmol/mol; target <7% [SI: <53 mmol/mol]), and urine albumin:creatinine ratio was 215 mg/g (SI: 24.3 mg/mmol; reference range, <30 mg/g [SI: <2.5 mg/mmol]). Transient elastography showed a liver stiffness of 9.1 kPa (reference range, <5.0 kPa), suggestive of moderate fibrosis. The controlled attenuation parameter was 329 dB/m (reference range, <236 dB/m), indicating severe steatosis. Alanine transaminase was elevated at 86 U/L (reference range, 5-40 U/L), gamma-glutamyl transferase 54 U/L (reference range, 5-50 U/L), with normal alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin.

Treatment

Dietary counseling was provided by the treating dietitian, emphasizing alcohol abstinence, a Mediterranean diet with restriction of saturated fat and simple carbohydrates, alongside coffee consumption which has emerging evidence for hepatoprotective benefit in MASLD. Formal physical activity was deferred given his occupational physical demands. Insulin was progressively titrated by the treating endocrinologists, with escalation of biphasic isophane insulin to a maximum total daily dose of 66 units and empagliflozin to 25 mg daily. Despite these interventions, weight remained stable throughout follow-up and glycemic control remained suboptimal, with HbA1c reaching 8.2% (SI: 66 mmol/mol) in 2023 (Table 1), complicated by recurrent hypoglycemic episodes that compromised both occupational function and quality of life.

The patient was concurrently referred to the Victorian Heart Institute lipid clinic, where hypertriglyceridemia remained uncontrolled despite maximal lipid-lowering therapy. Lipid-lowering therapy was rationalized with transition from simvastatin 80 mg to atorvastatin 80 mg for optimized low-density lipoprotein (LDL) reduction. Follow-up coronary angiography demonstrated significant left anterior descending artery disease and a chronically occluded right coronary artery, with the patient listed for coronary artery bypass grafting. At that review in June 2024, triglycerides were 823 mg/dL (SI: 9.3 mmol/L) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol 27 mg/dL (SI: 0.7 mmol/L; reference range, >39 mg/dL [SI: >1.0 mmol/L]). Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol could neither be calculated in the setting of severe hypertriglyceridemia nor be measured directly.

Nearly 2 years on from the index clinic review, in August 2024, the MASLD clinic's endocrinologist initiated semaglutide and titrated to the maximum dose of 1 mg weekly over a course of 3 months.

Outcome and follow-up

With the initiation of semaglutide, body weight decreased from a baseline of 84.1 kg (BMI 27.3 kg/m2) to 75.5 kg (BMI 24.5 kg/m2) over 10 months (−8.6 kg; −10.2%). Glycemic control improved markedly, with HbA1c declining to 6.7% (SI: 50 mmol/mol) 4 months after starting semaglutide. This allowed for complete cessation of insulin while continuing metformin 2 g daily, empagliflozin 25 mg daily, and semaglutide 1 mg weekly. Hypoglycemic episodes resolved completely.

By the subsequent review, a year later in June 2025, HbA1c had risen to 7.6% (SI: 60 mmol/mol) in the context of dietary relapse; however, this remained improved relative to presemaglutide values, and insulin was not restarted given sustained weight loss and reinforcement of dietary measures. His lipid profile demonstrated substantial improvement, with triglycerides reduced to a historical nadir of 213 mg/dL (SI: 2.4 mmol/L) alongside total cholesterol of 124 mg/dL (SI: 3.2 mmol/L; Fig. 1, Table 1). Notably, he experienced no recurrence of pancreatitis during follow-up.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the therapeutic potential of GLP-1RAs in managing treatment-resistant hypertriglyceridemia. Hypertriglyceridemia affects 15% to 20% of adults globally with severe elevations (>1000 mg/dL [SI: >11.4 mmol/L]) conferring substantial pancreatitis risk [9]. Triglyceride hydrolysis by pancreatic lipase generates excessive free fatty acids, causing direct acinar lipotoxicity, acinar necrosis, and microcirculatory disruption via trypsinogen activation [1]. Genetic susceptibility, particularly “cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator” mutations (26.1% vs 1.3% in controls), may further predispose to HTGP [10].

Acute HTGP management centers on rapidly lowering triglycerides. Plasmapheresis removes triglyceride-rich lipoproteins directly from circulation and can lower levels by 50% to 80% in a single session [11], but availability and cost limit access. Intravenous insulin activates lipoprotein lipase and suppresses hepatic very-LDL (VLDL) production [12]; it is inexpensive and widely available but requires close glucose monitoring. Neither addresses the underlying lipoprotein metabolic dysfunction, leaving patients vulnerable to recurrence.

Chronic management combines lifestyle modifications with pharmacotherapy targeting distinct metabolic pathways. Weight reduction, dietary fat restriction, and alcohol cessation are foundational but often insufficient. Fibrates activate peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor-α, upregulating lipoprotein lipase [13]; statins inhibit 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase, secondarily decreasing VLDL production [14]; niacin inhibits hepatic diacylglycerol acyltransferase-2, suppressing VLDL synthesis [15]; and omega-3 fatty acids downregulate hepatic VLDL–triglyceride synthesis and enhance β-oxidation [16]. Substantial patient subsets nonetheless exhibit treatment resistance, necessitating approaches targeting alternative pathways.

Emerging agents, such as angiopoietin-like protein 3 inhibitors or antisense oligonucleotides targeting apolipoprotein C-III, show promising efficacy in clinical trials but remain investigational and not yet widely available [17]. In contrast, GLP-1RAs are an established class with widespread availability and extensive safety data from diabetes management. Large-scale cardiovascular outcome trials have consistently demonstrated cardioprotective effects, and a recent meta-analysis of 32 884 participants reported significant triglyceride reductions of 6.78% alongside other cardiovascular benefits [18].

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists reduce triglycerides through several pathways. Slowing of gastric emptying and intestinal lipid absorption leads to reduced postprandial chylomicron excursions. Glucagon-like peptide-1 signaling directly inhibits intestinal chylomicron secretion from enterocytes, further dampening triglyceride peaks. The weight loss induced by GLP-1RAs can also improve hepatic insulin sensitivity, suppress VLDL production, and reduce hepatic steatosis [19]. These effects converge to lower both fasting and postprandial triglycerides, which are key drivers of pancreatitis risk.

Despite these mechanistic benefits, use of GLP-1RAs in patients with a history of pancreatitis has historically been approached with caution. Early postmarketing pharmacovigilance reports raised concern for an association between incretin-based therapies and pancreatitis, with proposed mechanisms involving pancreatic ductal cell proliferation, increased enzyme secretion, and altered gallbladder motility [20], resulting in mandatory Food and Drug Administration safety labeling updates in 2009. However, subsequent large observational studies and real-world data have consistently failed to confirm a causal link, challenging these safety signals [6]. In fact, recent analyses suggest a lower incidence of pancreatitis in GLP-1RA users compared with other glucose-lowering therapies, possibly reflecting their beneficial metabolic effects [7]. The Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency have since noted that assertions of causal associations are inconsistent with current evidence, supporting reconsideration of contraindications in appropriately selected patients when other therapeutic options are limited.

This case demonstrates triglyceride normalization following initiation of semaglutide after failure of intensive conventional therapy, including fibrates, high-dose statins, and omega-3 supplementation, with no further episodes of pancreatitis observed during the period of GLP-1RA therapy to date. Notably, a recent case report by Guralwar et al [21] similarly demonstrated successful use of incretin-based therapy in HTGP using liraglutide, followed by tirzepatide. Both cases support incretin-based agonists as adjuncts to conventional lipid-lowering regimens, acting through triglyceride metabolism, weight regulation, and glycemic control.

This report has several limitations, including relatively short follow-up, which limits assessment of long-term durability of triglyceride reduction and prevention of recurrent pancreatitis. Additionally, formal genetic evaluation of hypertriglyceridemia and lipoprotein phenotyping were not performed. However, a monogenic etiology was considered unlikely given the absence of lipodystrophic features and onset in the context of poorly controlled T2DM. The coexistence of premature ischemic heart disease and severe hypertriglyceridemia raises the possibility of an underlying polygenic or combined lipid disorder, such as familial combined hyperlipidemia [22]. Lipoprotein electrophoresis and apolipoprotein profiling would have enabled more precise phenotyping and risk stratification and are recommended in similar cases.

While the evidence remains preliminary, the convergence of mechanistic plausibility, contemporary safety data, and clinical experience makes a compelling case for prospective evaluation of GLP-1RAs and emerging incretin-based combination therapies in refractory hypertriglyceridemia. In the interim, these agents warrant consideration as adjunctive therapy to prevent recurrent pancreatitis in carefully selected patients under close expert supervision.
