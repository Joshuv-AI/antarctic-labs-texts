A Note on Hyperbolic Relaxation of the Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard system for incompressible two-phase flow
Jens Keim, Hasel-Cicek Konan, Christian Rohde

Abstract
. We consider the two-phase dynamics of two incompressible and immiscible fluids. As a mathematical model we rely on the Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard system that belongs to the class of diffuse-interface models. Solutions of the Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard system exhibit strong non-local effects due to the velocity divergence constraint and the fourth-order Cahn-Hilliard operator. We suggest a new first-order approximative system for the inviscid sub-system. It relies on the artificial-compressibility ansatz for the Navier-Stokes equations, a friction-type approximation for the Cahn-Hilliard equation and a relaxation of a third-order capillarity term. We show under reasonable assumptions that the first-order operator within the approximative system is hyperbolic; precisely we prove for the spatially one-dimensional case that it is equipped with an entropy-entropy flux pair with convex (mathematical) entropy. For specific states we present a numerical characteristic analysis. Thanks to the hyperbolicity of the system, we can employ all standard numerical methods from the field of hyperbolic conservation laws. We conclude the paper with preliminary numerical results in one spatial dimension. Keywords: two-phase flow, diffuse-interface modelling, Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard system, first-order hyperbolic relaxation



1.
Introduction

The incompressible motion of a viscous one-phase fluid is governed by the Navier–Stokes system. In absence of external forces and setting the density of the fluid to be
1
it is given in
d\in\{2,3\}
space dimensions
by the equations

(1.1)

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{rcccl}&&\divergence{\bf{u}}&=&0,\\[6.45831pt]
{\bf{u}}_{t}&+&({\bf{u}}\cdot\gradient){\bf{u}}+\gradient p&=&\nu\Delta{\bf{u}}\end{array}\quad\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T).\end{array}

Here
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is an open set and
T>0
some end time. By
\nu>0
we denote the viscosity parameter.
The unknowns are the velocity
{\bf{u}}={\bf{u}}({\bf{x}},t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the pressure (perturbation)
p=p({\bf{x}},t)\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Classical solutions of (
1.1
) dissipate the kinetic energy.

The divergence constraint
\eqref{NS}_{1}
leads to a strong non-locality for solutions of the Navier–Stokes system such that the pressure is governed by an elliptic differential equation.
A classical approach to avoid this non-locality is the artificial-compressibility approximation, see
[
5
,
4
,
6
]
and
[
27
,
28
]
. In this case,
the Navier–Stokes system is approximated by the set of evolution equations

(1.2)

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\alpha p_{t}^{\alpha}&+&\displaystyle\divergence{\bf{u}}^{\alpha}&=&0,\\[8.61108pt]
{\bf{u}}_{t}^{\alpha}&+&\displaystyle({\bf{u}}^{\alpha}\cdot\gradient){\bf{u}}^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}(\gradient\cdot{\bf{u}}^{\alpha}){\bf{u}}^{\alpha}+\gradient p^{\alpha}&=&\nu\Delta{\bf{u}}^{\alpha}\end{array}\quad\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,T).\end{array}

Adding a time derivative for the pressure and scaling it with a small
parameter
\alpha>0
, the pressure is governed now by an evolution equation. For
\alpha\to 0
solutions
{\bf{u}}^{\alpha}({\bf{x}},t)
and
p^{\alpha}=p^{\alpha}({\bf{x}},t)
of the system
(
1.2
) recover the solutions
{\bf{u}}
and
p
of the original Navier–Stokes system (
1.1
). The additional correction
term
\frac{1}{2}(\gradient\cdot{\bf{u}}^{\alpha}){\bf{u}}^{\alpha}
in (
1.2
)
2
ensures that classical solutions dissipate the sum of kinetic energy and the potential contribution
\alpha(p^{\alpha})^{2}/2
. Most important, the structure of the artificial compressibility approximation allows to use methods from the field of hyperbolic evolution equations to understand the first-order sub-system (obtained by setting
\nu
to be zero) in (
1.2
). This has in particular consequences for the numerical simulation. One can employ numerical methods for systems of hyperbolic evolution laws to discretize this sub-system of (
1.2
). In such a way also convection-dominated flow regimes can be handled on the numerical level, see e.g.
[
15
,
23
,
22
]
.

In this note, we focus on the dynamics of incompressible two-phase flows and aim at developing
a framework that transfers the artificial-compressibility approach into this realm. We consider as a model problem a simplified version of the Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard (NSCH) equations as originally proposed in
[
17
]
. The NSCH model couples the Navier–Stokes equations with the fourth-order convective Cahn–Hilliard equation
for the phase dynamics. Thus, it belongs to the class of diffuse-interface
(or phase-field) approaches, see
[
2
]
.
The major challenge to approximate the NSCH system using a first-order system is therefore the treatment of the coupled convective Cahn–Hilliard
equation. We refer to
[
8
,
11
]
for approximation techniques for the pure Cahn–Hilliard evolution.
In this work, we rely on a friction-type approximation that mimics the structure of Euler-Korteweg systems which have been considered as approximations of the
Cahn–Hilliard equations in, e.g.,
[
21
,
25
]
. Roughly speaking, the fourth-order operator in the Cahn–Hilliard equation is substituted by a third-order Korteweg term. The last step to obtain a
first-order evolution system (always ignoring the viscous part of the stress tensor which is not touched) is the use of the relaxation technique from

[
24
]
. We present our new approximative system with a first-order sub-system in (
4.1
).

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
, we give a short outline of the NSCH system and its thermodynamical structure.
In Section
3
, we propose the friction-type approximation of the NSCH system and we show that it is a thermodynamically consistent system of evolution equations.
In Section
3.1
, we introduce our new approximative system and show that it obeys a natural energy dissipation rate (see Theorem
4.2
). Moreover, we show under fair conditions in one spatial dimension that
it involves a hyperbolic first-order sub-system similar
to the one in the artifical-compressibility approximation (
1.2
). For the one-dimensional sub-system,
we present a numerical characteristic analysis
to understand the qualitative behaviour of the eigenvalues of the flux Jacobian.
In Section
5
, we present extensive numerical results for the one-dimensional version, i.e., the system (
4.7
) below.

2.
The Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard system

In this section, we review a simplified version of the Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard (NSCH) system as originally introduced in
[
17
]
. In view of the approximations which we will introduce in the sequel, we recall the energy dissipation equation for solutions of the initial boundary problem for the NSCH system. Finally, we mention a result on the existence of strong solutions in two space dimensions.

The domain of the flow is the bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
,
with boundary
\partial\Omega
. The outer normal of
\partial\Omega
will be denoted by

{\bf{n}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{d-1}
. For some end time
T>0
, we
define the space-time domain

\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T)
.

2.1.
Formulation of the NSCH system

Let us consider the dynamics of two incompressible and immiscible fluids at constant temperature.
There are two approaches to model such two-phase flows: the sharp-interface approach and the diffuse-interface approach. In the sharp-interface ansatz, one obtains a free-boundary value problem
which separates the two bulk domains by a well-defined phase boundary as codimension-
1
manifold.
We focus here on the diffuse-interface (or phase-field) approach, where the phase boundary is smeared out with an interfacial width
\gamma>0
, see Figure
2.1
.

Figure 2.1
.
Sketch of a diffuse-interface configuration with phase boundary of width
\gamma
.

If the parameter
\gamma
tends to zero, one expects that an appropriate sharp-interface model is attained in the limit. Here, we are not interested in the sharp-interface limit and therefore
fix the parameter
\gamma
throughout the entire note.

As a meanwhile well-known diffuse-interface approach, we start from the classical Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard (NSCH) system, which can be traced back to
[
17
]
.
It is a combination of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations for the hydromechanical dynamics and the Cahn–Hilliard equation for the two-phase dynamics. The governing partial differential equations are given by

(2.1)

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{rcccl}&&\divergence{\bf{u}}&=&0,\\[6.45831pt]
{\bf{u}}_{t}&+&({\bf{u}}\cdot\gradient){\bf{u}}+\gradient p&=&-c\gradient\mu(c)+\nu\Delta{\bf{u}},\\[6.45831pt]
c_{t}&+&\divergence(c\vecu)-\divergence(\grad\mu(c))&=&0,\\[6.45831pt]
&&\mu(c)&=&W^{\prime}(c)-\gamma\Delta c\end{array}\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{T}.\end{array}

The hydromechanical unknowns in (
2.1
) are the pressure (perturbation)
p=p(\bm{x},t)\in{\mathbb{R}}
and the velocity field
\bm{u}=\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Furthermore, we search for the phase-field variable

c=c(\bm{x},t)\in[-1,1]
which acts as phase indicator (see Figure
2.2
). For later use, we define the state vector
{\bm{U}}=(p,{\bf{u}}^{T},c)^{T}\in\mathcal{U}
with the
state space
\mathcal{U}
given by

{\mathcal{U}}=\left\{(p,{\bf{u}}^{T},c)^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\,\left.\right|\,c\in[-1,1]\right\}.

We denote the given fixed viscosity parameter by
\nu>0
, implicitly assuming that the viscosity is independent of the phase-field variable
c
. Moreover, we assumed that the densities of the two fluids are the same and equal to
1
.
The quantity
\mu(c)
in (
2.1
)
represents the chemical potential which involves the free energy function
W:[-1,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Note that we consider here a special version of the
Cahn–Hilliard equation where the mobility is supposed to be constant (equal to
1
).

Concerning the free energy function
W
, we choose in this paper the quartic polynomial

(2.2)

{W}(c)=\dfrac{1}{4}(c^{2}-1)^{2}\quad\forall\;c\in[-1,1],

which has a double-well structure with two minima in the pure phase states, see Figure
2.2
. A
physically more grounded alternative is the Flory-Huggins logarithmic potential

(2.3)

{\mathcal{W}}(c)=\frac{\theta}{2}\left[(1+c)\,\ln(1+c)+(1-c)\ln(1-c)\right]-\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}c^{2}\quad\quad(0<\theta<\theta_{0}).

For the connection of the quartic potential in (
2.2
) to the Flory-Huggins logarithmic potential, we refer the reader to
[
13
]
and the references therein.
The fourth-order polynomial
W
and the logarithmic form
\mathcal{W}
are depicted in Fig.
2.2
. Here, the red curve corresponds to the polynomial potential in (
2.2
) and the blue to the logarithmic potential in (
2.3
) for
\theta=0.4
and
\theta_{0}=1.0
.

Figure 2.2
.
Graphs of the logarithmic free energy
\mathcal{W}
(red) and the quartic free energy
W
(blue).

To close the NSCH system (
2.1
), we assume
homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition for
\bm{u}
and homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions for
c
and
\mu(c)
, which are

(2.4)

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\bm{0},\;\gradient c\cdot\bm{n}=0,\;\gradient\mu(c)\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\partial\Omega\times(0,T).

Appropriate initial conditions for
{\bf{u}}
and
c
have to be added.

2.2.
Energy dissipation for the NSCH system (
2.1
)

A major step towards well-posedness of a system of evolution equations in continuum mechanics
is the verification of an energy dissipation estimate. This can also be understood as
thermodynamical consistency, i.e., a statement on the validity of the second law of thermodynamics.

For the NSCH system, we define the energy expression

E[{\bf{u}}^{T},c,{\bf{d}}^{T}]=\frac{1}{2}{|{\bf{u}}|^{2}}+W(c)+\frac{\gamma}{2}{|{\bf{d}}|^{2}}\quad\left({\bf{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,c\in[-1,1],\,{\bf{d}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right),

which is just the classical van der Waals energy if the velocity vanishes and
{\bf{d}}
is identified with the gradient of the phase field variable.
Then
we have the following statement on thermodynamical consistency.

Theorem 2.1

(Energy dissipation for (
2.1
))
.

Let
\nu,\gamma>0
and let
{\bm{U}}=(p,{\bf{u}}^{T},c)^{T}:\bar{\Omega}_{T}\to\mathcal{U}
be a classical solution of the initial boundary value problem for (
2.1
) with the boundary conditions from (
2.4
).

Then, we have for all
t\in(0,T)
the energy dissipation rate

(2.5)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}E[{\bf{u}}^{T},c,(\gradient c)^{T}](\bm{x},t)\;d\bm{x}=-\int_{\Omega}\left(|\gradient\mu(c(\bm{x},t))|^{2}+\nu|\gradient\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)|^{2}\right)d\bm{x}\leq 0,

The energy statement (
2.5
) is well-known. We recall here its proof to enable
a comparison with the proofs of the Theorems
3.2
,
4.2
below that contain corresponding statements for approximative models.

Proof
(of Theorem
2.1
).
We derive the energy inequality (
2.5
) for the NSCH system by multiplying (
2.1
)
2
with
\bm{u}
. Integration over
\Omega
leads to

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}{|\bm{u}|^{2}}\;d\bm{x}+\int_{\Omega}((\bm{u}\cdot\gradient)\bm{u})\cdot\bm{u}\;d\bm{x}+\int_{\Omega}\gradient p\cdot\bm{u}\;d\bm{x}=-\int_{\Omega}c\gradient\mu(c)\cdot\bm{u}\;d\bm{x}+\nu\int_{\Omega}\Delta\bm{u}\cdot\bm{u}\;d\bm{x}.

The use of the theorem of Gauß, the divergence constraint
\divergence{\bf{u}}=0
and the boundary condition
on
{\bf{u}}
from (
2.4
) imply

(2.6)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}{|\bm{u}|^{2}}\;d\bm{x}&=&\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}c\gradient\mu(c)\cdot\bm{u}\;d\bm{x}-\int_{\Omega}\nu|\gradient\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)|^{2}d\bm{x}.\end{array}

Now, we multiply (
2.1
)
3
with
\mu(c)
and get after integration
by the definition of
\mu
the equation

(2.7)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}W(c)+\frac{\gamma}{2}|\gradient c|^{2}\;d\bm{x}+\int_{\Omega}\divergence{(c \bm{u})}\mu(c)\;d\bm{x}=-\int_{\Omega}|\gradient\mu(c(\bm{x},t))|^{2}d\bm{x}.

For the last line, we used the Neumann boundary conditions in (
2.4
).
The result (
2.5
) follows now from
adding up (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) using once again the boundary conditions (
2.4
).
∎

We see that the energy
E
decreases in time along solutions of the NSCH system. The dissipation rate is driven by the gradient in the chemical potential and the gradient in the velocity times the viscosity parameter. We consider equation (
2.5
) as an a-priori estimate on solutions of the NSCH system which is the ground for any well-posedness result. For the sake of completeness, we note a recent result on the existence of strong global-in-time solutions
proven in
[
13
]
. By a strong solution we mean a triple
(p,{\bf{u}}^{T},c)^{T}:\bar{\Omega}_{T}\to\mathcal{U}
such that all equations in (
2.1
) hold almost everywhere in
\Omega_{T}
.
To formulate the result, we introduce the Hilbert spaces
\bm{V}_{\theta}
and
\bm{H}_{\theta}
,
which are
the closures of
C^{\infty}_{0,\theta}(\Omega)
with respect to the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-norm
and with respect to the
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
-norm, respectively.
Thereby,
C^{\infty}_{0,\theta}(\Omega)
is the space of divergence-free vector fields in
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
.
The space
C([0,T];(W^{2,q}(\Omega))_{w})

consists of all functions
f\in L^{\infty}([0,T];W^{2,q}(\Omega))
such that

t\mapsto\langle\varphi,f\rangle
is continuous for all
\phi
in the dual space of
W^{2,q}(\Omega)
.

We adopt the following theorem from
[
13
]
for our initial boundary value problem.

Theorem 2.2

(The existence of strong solutions of the NSCH system)
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain with
C^{3}
-boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Assume that initial data
\bm{u}_{0}\in\bm{V}_{\theta}(\Omega)
and
c_{0}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
are given such that
\gradient c_{0}\cdot\bm{n}=0
holds on
\partial\Omega
and such that
\mu_{0}=W^{\prime}(c_{0})-\gamma\Delta c_{0}
is in
H^{1}(\Omega)
with
W
from (
2.2
).

Then, there exists a strong solution
(p,\bm{u}^{T},c)
of the initial boundary value problem for (
2.1
), (
2.4
) satisfying

\displaystyle p\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\bm{u}\in C([0,T];\bm{V}_{\theta})\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;\bm{H}_{\theta}),

\displaystyle c\in C([0,T];(W^{2,q}(\Omega))_{w})\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

for any
q\in[2,\infty)
.

The boundary conditions (
2.4
) on the phase-field variable hold almost everywhere in
\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
.

We conjecture that analogous well-posedness results can be derived for the approximative systems which we will introduce in the next two sections.

3.
A friction-type approximation of the Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard system

In this section, we introduce an approximative system for the NSCH system (
2.1
).
We localize the Navier–Stokes part of (
2.1
) by employing the artificial compressibility
approximation in the sense of
[
4
]
. The coupled fourth-order Cahn–Hilliard equation in (
2.1
) is substituted by a friction-type system. This approach exploits the variational structure of Euler–Korteweg systems which has been established in, e.g.,
[
21
]
or
in homogenization approaches for two-phase flows, see e.g.
[
25
]
.
We present the complete description of the friction-type approximation in Section

3.1
, and we provide an associated energy-dissipation result in Theorem
2

of Section
3.1
.

3.1.
Formulation of the friction-type approximation

For the approximate system, we introduce two small parameters, namely the artificial-viscosity parameter
\alpha>0
and the friction parameter
\delta>0
. We collect both in the parameter vector
\epsilon=(\alpha,\delta)^{T}
.
Let the state space be given by

\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=\left\{(p,{\bf{u}}^{T},c,{\bf{v}}^{T})^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,|\,c\in[-1,1]\right\}.

We search for the pressure
p^{\epsilon}=p^{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)
, the velocity

\bm{u}^{\epsilon}=\bm{u}^{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)
, the phase field variable

c^{\epsilon}=c^{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)
and the flux variable
\bm{v}^{\epsilon}=\bm{v}^{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)
such that
{\bm{U}}^{\epsilon}=(p^{\epsilon},({\bf{u}}^{\epsilon})^{T},c^{\epsilon},({\bf{v}}^{\epsilon})^{T})^{T}:\Omega_{T}\to\tilde{\mathcal{U}}

satisfies the evolution equations

(3.1)

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{rcccl}p_{t}^{\epsilon}&+&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\divergence{\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}&=&0,\\[8.61108pt]
{\bf{u}}_{t}^{\epsilon}&+&\displaystyle({\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}\cdot\gradient){\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2}(\gradient\cdot{\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}){\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}+\gradient p^{\epsilon}&=&-c^{\epsilon}(\gradient( W'(c^{\Upsilon}) - \gamma\Delta c^{\Upsilon} ))+\nu\Delta{\bf{u}}^{\epsilon},\\[8.61108pt]
c_{t}^{\epsilon}&+&\divergence(c^{\Upsilon} \vecu^{\Upsilon})+\divergence{\bf{v}}^{\epsilon}&=&0,\\[8.61108pt]
\bm{v}^{\epsilon}_{t}&+&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\gradient(W'(c^{\Upsilon}) - \gamma\Delta c^{\Upsilon})&=&\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\delta}{\bf{v}}^{\epsilon}\end{array}\qquad\text{in}\;\Omega_{T}.\end{array}

The free energy
W
and the parameters
\nu,\gamma
are chosen as in Section
2
.

Appropriate initial data have to be prescribed. We impose the boundary conditions

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}=\bm{0},\;\gradient c^{\epsilon}\cdot\bm{n}=0,\,{\bf{v}}^{\epsilon}\cdot{\bf{n}}=\bm{0}\text{ on }\;\partial\Omega\times(0,T).

In comparison with the NSCH system (
2.1
), the friction-type approximation (
3.1
) contains
only differential operators up to order three. However, the state space
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
is now (
2d+2
)-dimensional.

Remark 3.1
.

We expect that solutions
\bm{U}^{\epsilon}
of the initial boundary value problem for
(
3.1
) converge to a solution
\bm{U}
of the initial boundary value problem for
(
2.1
) as
|\epsilon|
tends to 0.

Let us first consider the passage
\alpha\to 0
. If we ignore the term
-c^{\epsilon}(\gradient( W'(c^{\Upsilon}) - \gamma\Delta c^{\Upsilon} ))
from the right hand side of
\eqref{NSEKapproximation}_{2}
, we get the classical
artificial-compressibility approximation of the incompressible Navier–Stokes system.
If we then resolve (
3.1
)
4
for
{\bf{v}}^{\epsilon}
and plug the result in
(
3.1
)
3
we obtain

c_{t}^{\epsilon}+\divergence(c^{\Upsilon} \bm{u}^{\Upsilon})-\divergence\left(\gradient( W'(c^{\Upsilon}) - \gamma\Delta c^{\Upsilon} )\right)=\delta{\bf{v}}^{\epsilon}_{t}.

If the friction parameter
\delta
tends to zero we (formally) attain in the limit the
convective Cahn–Hilliard equation as in (
2.1
)
3
.

3.2.
Energy dissipation for the friction-type system (
3.1
)

The friction-type system (
3.1
) obeys a natural
energy dissipation law. To formulate it we introduce the
energy term

E^{\epsilon}[p,{\bf{u}}^{T},c,{\bf{v}}^{T},{\bf{d}}^{T}]=\frac{\alpha}{2}{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}{|{\bf{u}}|^{2}}+\dfrac{\delta}{2}|{\bf{v}}|^{2}+W(c)+\frac{\gamma}{2}{|{\bf{d}}|^{2}}\quad\left((p,{\bf{u}},c,{\bf{v}},{\bf{d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[-1,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\right).

Note that all terms in the energy
E^{\epsilon}
are quadratic expresssions except the free energy function
W
.

Theorem 3.2

(Energy dissipation for (
3.1
))
.

Let
\nu,\gamma>0
. For
\epsilon\in(0,\infty)^{2}
let
\bm{U}^{\epsilon}:\bar{\Omega}_{T}\to\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
be a classical solution of the initial boundary value problem for (
3.1
) with the boundary conditions from (
3.2
).

Then we have for all
t\in(0,T)
the energy dissipation rate

(3.3)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}E^{\epsilon}\left[(\bm{U}^{\epsilon})^{T},(\gradient c^{\epsilon})^{T}\right]({\bf{x}},t)\;d\bm{x}=-\int_{\Omega}\left(|{\bf{v}}^{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)|^{2}+\nu|\gradient{\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)|^{2}\right)d\bm{x}\leq 0.

The estimate (
3.3
) renders the friction-type approximation to be thermodynamically consistent. Note that the energy dissipation rate coincides in the limit
|\epsilon|\to 0
with the rate
for the NSCH model in (
2.5
).

Proof
(of Theorem
3.2
). Define
\mu^{\epsilon}=W^{\prime}(c^{\epsilon})-\gamma\Delta c^{\epsilon}
. We multiply the equations in (
3.1
) with
the components of the (variational) derivative of the energy
E^{\epsilon}
, that are
\alpha p^{\epsilon}
,
{\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}
,
\mu^{\epsilon}
,
\delta{\bf{v}}^{\epsilon}
, respectively, and obtain after summing up in a straightforward way

{\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\left[(\bm{U}^{\epsilon})^{T},(\gradient c^{\epsilon})^{T}\right]+\divergence{ \left(p^{\Upsilon} \vecu^{\Upsilon} \right) }+\frac{1}{2}\divergence{ \left( \vecu^{\Upsilon} {|\vecu^{\Upsilon} |}^2\right) }\displaystyle{}+\divergence{\left( c^\Upsilon\mu^\Upsilon\vecu^\Upsilon\right)}+\divergence{\left(\mu^\Upsilon\vecv^\Upsilon\right) }}=-{\bf{v}}^{\epsilon}\cdot{\bf{v}}^{\epsilon}+\nu\Delta{\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}\cdot{\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}.

From this equation we get the energy dissipation rate (
3.3
)
by integration over
\Omega
and integration by parts exploiting the boundary conditions
(
3.2
).
∎

As noted above the system (
3.1
) is still of third order and we aim –neglecting the viscosity operator–
for a final first-order approximation
of the NSCH system (
2.1
). Moreover, in view of the non-monotone
shape of
W^{\prime}
, it is not likely that the first-order operator in
the friction-type approximation results in a hyperbolic system.
Therefore, we we come up with yet another approximation in the next section.

4.
The relaxed friction-type approximation of the Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard system

We proceed with the presentation of the final approximative system for the NSCH system
(
2.1
). We employ a relaxation technique from
[
24
]
(see e.g.

[
7
,
20

for more recent work in this direction) to obtain a

4.1.
Formulation of the relaxed friction-type approximation

For the relaxed friction-type system we need next to the artificial-viscosity parameter
\alpha>0
and the friction parameter
\delta>0
a relaxation parameter
\beta>0
. All approximation
parameters are summarized in the vector
\varepsilon=(\alpha,\delta,\beta)^{T}
. As before we introduce a
state space

\bar{\mathcal{U}}=\left\{(p,{\bf{u}}^{T},c,{\bf{v}}^{T},\omega)^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\,|\,c\in[-1,1]\right\}.

We search for the pressure
p^{\varepsilon}=p^{\varepsilon}(\bm{x},t)
, the velocity

{\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}={\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}(\bm{x},t)
, the phase-field variable

c^{\varepsilon}=c^{\varepsilon}(\bm{x},t)
, the flux variable
{\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon}={\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon}(\bm{x},t)
and
the relaxation variable
\omega^{\varepsilon}=\omega^{\varepsilon}(\bm{x},t)
such that
{\bm{U}}^{\varepsilon}=(p^{\varepsilon},({\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon})^{T},c^{\varepsilon},({\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon})^{T},\omega^{\varepsilon})^{T}:\Omega_{T}\to\bar{\mathcal{U}}
solves

(4.1)

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{rcccl}p_{t}^{\varepsilon}&+&\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\divergence{\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}&=&0,\\[8.61108pt]
{\bf{u}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}&+&\displaystyle({\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\gradient){\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}(\gradient\cdot{\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}){\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}+\gradient p^{\varepsilon}+c^{\varepsilon}\gradient\left(W^{\prime}\bigl(c^{\varepsilon}\bigr)+\frac{1}{\beta}c^{\varepsilon}\right)&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}c^{\varepsilon}\gradient\omega^{\varepsilon}+\nu\Delta{\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon},\\[12.91663pt]
c_{t}^{\varepsilon}&+&\divergence(c^{\varepsilon}\vecu^{\varepsilon})+\divergence{\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon}&=&0,\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle{\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon}_{t}&+&\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\delta}\gradient\left(W^{\prime}(c^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\beta}c^{\varepsilon}\right)&=&\displaystyle-\frac{{\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon}}{\delta}+\frac{1}{\delta\beta}\gradient\omega^{\varepsilon},\\[8.61108pt]
&&\displaystyle-\gamma\Delta\omega^{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\beta}\omega^{\varepsilon}&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}c^{\varepsilon}\end{array}\text{ in }\Omega_{T}.\end{array}

The free energy
W
and the parameters
\nu,\gamma
are chosen as before. We complete the initial
boundary value problem for
the system (
4.1
) by appropriate initial data and the boundary conditions

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}=\bm{0},\;{\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\cdot{\bf{n}}=0,\gradient\omega^{\varepsilon}\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T).

Note that the Neumann conditions for the phase field variable in (
3.2
) are transferred to the relaxation variable
\omega^{\varepsilon}
.

Remark 4.1
.

We expect that solutions
\bm{U}^{\varepsilon}
of the initial boundary value problem for
(
4.1
) converge to a solution
\bm{U}
of the initial boundary value problem for
(

) as

tends to 0. In Remark

we discussed already the partial limits
\alpha,\delta\to 0
.
Let us therefore fix
\alpha,\delta
and consider the relaxation limit
\beta\to 0
. We set

{\bf{u}}\equiv 0
and obtain –ignoring the index
\epsilon
and setting
\delta=1
– from the decoupled equations (
3.1
)
2
and (
3.1
)
3

the one-dimensional model problem

(4.3)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}c_{t}+v_{x}&=&0,\\[8.61108pt]
v_{t}+{(W^{\prime}(c))}_{x}&=&\displaystyle-v+{\gamma}c_{xxx}.\end{array}

The corresponding relaxed approximation writes as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}c^{\beta}_{t}+v^{\beta}_{x}&=&0,\\[8.61108pt]

4.2.
Energy dissipation for the relaxed friction-type system (
4.1
)

As for the models discussed in Sections
2
,
3
, we can prove an energy-dissipation rate for the relaxed friction-type system (
4.1
) that ensures its thermodynamical consistency. We start from the
energy term

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle E^{\epsilon}[p,{\bf{u}}^{T},c,{\bf{v}}^{T},\omega,{\bf{e}}^{T}]=\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}{|{\bf{u}}|^{2}}+\dfrac{\delta}{2}|{\bf{v}}|^{2}+W(c)+\frac{1}{2\beta}(c-\omega)^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}{|{\bf{e}}|^{2}}\\[12.91663pt]
\hskip 170.71652pt\left((p,{\bf{u}},c,{\bf{v}},\omega,{\bf{e}})\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[-1,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\right)\end{array}

and provide

Theorem 4.2

(Energy dissipation for (
4.1
))
.

Let
\nu,\gamma>0
. For
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)^{3}
let
\bm{U}^{\varepsilon}:\bar{\Omega}_{T}\to\bar{\mathcal{U}}
be a classical solution of the initial boundary value problem for (
4.1
) with the boundary conditions from (
4.2
).

Then we have for all
t\in(0,T)
the energy dissipation rate

(4.5)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}E^{\varepsilon}\left[(\bm{U}^{\varepsilon})^{T},(\gradient\omega^{\varepsilon})^{T}\right](\bm{x},t)\;d\bm{x}=-\int_{\Omega}\left(|{\bf{v}}^{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)|^{2}+\nu|\gradient{\bf{u}}^{\epsilon}(\bm{x},t)|^{2}\right)d\bm{x}\leq 0.

Proof.

As in the proofs of Theorems
2.1
,
3.2
, we multiply the
evolution equations in (
4.1
) by the following components of the variational
derivative of
E^{\varepsilon}
:

\alpha p^{\varepsilon},({\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon})^{T},W^{\prime}(c^{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\beta}(c^{\varepsilon}-\omega^{\varepsilon}),\delta({\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon})^{T}.

After summing up these results and using the product rule we get

(4.6)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}&&\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}{(p^{\varepsilon})^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}{|{\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}}+W(c^{\varepsilon})+\dfrac{\delta}{2}|{\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{\beta}c^{\varepsilon}_{t}(c^{\varepsilon}-\omega^{\varepsilon})\\[12.91663pt]
&+&{}\displaystyle\divergence{ \left(p^{\eps} \vecu^{\eps} \right) }+\frac{1}{2}\divergence{ \left( {|\vecu^{\eps} |}^2 \vecu^{\eps} \right) }\\[8.61108pt]
&+&\displaystyle\divergence{ \left( c^{\varepsilon} \left( W'(c^{\varepsilon}) + \frac 1{\beta} (c^{\varepsilon}
- \omega^\eps)\right)\vecu^\eps\right)}\\[12.91663pt]
&+&\displaystyle\divergence\left(\left(W^{\prime}\bigl(c^{\varepsilon}\bigr)+\frac{1}{\beta}(c^{\varepsilon}-\omega^{\varepsilon})\right){\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\right)\\[12.91663pt]
&&={}-{\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon}\cdot{\bf{v}}^{\varepsilon}+\nu\Delta{\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}\cdot{\bf{u}}^{\varepsilon}.\end{array}

In turn we multiply the elliptic constraint
\eqref{firstorderNSEK}_{5}
by
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{t}
to deduce

\frac{\gamma}{2}\frac{d}{dt}|\gradient\omega^{\varepsilon}|^{2}-\divergence{( \omega^\eps_t \grad\omega^\eps)}+\frac{1}{\beta}\omega^{\varepsilon}_{t}\left(\omega^{\varepsilon}-c^{\varepsilon}\right)=0.

The addition of the last line and (
4.6
) directly provides after integration and using the boundary conditions (
4.2
) the result (
4.5
).

∎

4.3.
A hyperbolic sub-system of the relaxed friction-type approximation (
4.1
)

In the preceding Section
4.2
, we proved that the relaxed friction-type system (
4.1
) is thermodynamically consistent.
In view of its numerical discretization another property is even more important. Let us restrict ourselves to the case
d=1
and introduce the reduced state space

{\mathcal{Q}}=\left\{(p,u,c,v)^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}\,|\,c\in[-1,1]\right\}.

Skipping the index vector
\varepsilon
and using

the equations
\eqref{firstorderNSEK}_{1}-\eqref{firstorderNSEK}_{4}
can be rewritten for

{\bm{Q}}:\Omega_{T}\to\mathcal{Q}
in the form

(4.7)

{\bm{Q}}_{t}+{{\bf{f}}({\bm{Q}})}_{x}={\bm{S}}({\bm{Q}},\omega),

with

{\bf{f}}({\bm{Q}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\dfrac{1}{\alpha}u\\[6.45831pt]
\displaystyle\frac{3}{4}u^{2}+p+G(c)\\[6.45831pt]
cu+v\\[6.45831pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\left(W^{\prime}(c)+\dfrac{1}{\beta}c\right)\\
\end{array}\right),\quad{\bm{S}}({\bm{Q}},\omega)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\[6.45831pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}c\omega_{x}+\nu u_{xx}\\[6.45831pt]
0\\[6.45831pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}v+\frac{1}{\delta\beta}\omega_{x}\end{array}\right).

The function

is supposed to satisfy

We will prove that the closed homogeneous system of conservation laws given by

. That means that the flux Jacobian

,
which is given by

\end{pmatrix},

satisfies

\lambda_{1}({\bm{Q}}),\ldots,\lambda_{4}({\bm{Q}})\in{\mathbb{R}},\qquad{\rm{span}}\{{{\bf{r}}}_{1}({\bm{Q}}),\ldots,{{\bf{r}}}_{4}({\bm{Q}})\}={\mathbb{R}}^{4}.

in the right-hand-side term

in (

(\eta,q):{\mathcal{Q}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

)

is convex and the compatibility relation

(4.10)

(\gradient\eta({\bm{Q}}))^{T}D{\bf{f}}({\bm{Q}})=(\gradient q({\bm{Q}}))^{T}

holds for all
{\bm{Q}}\in\mathcal{Q}
.

Theorem 4.3

(Hyperbolicity of (
4.8
))
.

Let
\alpha,\delta>0
and let
\beta
satisfy

(4.11)

\beta<-\left(\min_{c\in[-1,1]}\left\{\phantom{a^{2}_{1}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\min\{W^{\prime\prime}(c),0\}\right\}\right)^{-1},

Then, the pair
(\eta,q):{\mathcal{Q}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

with

(4.12)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\eta({\bm{Q}})&=&\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}{u}^{2}+W(c)+\frac{1}{2\beta}c^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}{v}^{2},\\[8.61108pt]

is an entropy/entropy-flux pair for (
4.8
).

The system of first-order conservation laws (
4.8
) is hyperbolic in
\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

The Hessian matrix
\bm{H}\eta
of

from (

Thus, the condition (

The flux Jacobian

has been given in (

).
We obtain

\begin{array}[]{c}(\gradient\eta({\bm{Q}}))^{T}D{\bf{f}}({\bm{Q}})=(\alpha p,u,W^{\prime}(c)+\beta^{-1}c,\delta v)D{\bf{f}}({\bm{Q}})=\begin{pmatrix}u\\[6.45831pt]
\displaystyle p+\frac{3}{2}u^{2}+c(W^{\prime}(c)+\beta^{-1}c)\\[6.45831pt]
uG^{\prime}(c)+(W^{\prime}(c)+\beta^{-1}c)u+v(W^{\prime\prime}(c)+\beta^{-1})\\[6.45831pt]

With the definition of

5.
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present numerical experiments for the first-order relaxed friction-type approximation (
4.1
) of the NSCH equations in one spatial dimension
(see (
4.7
)).
These experiments are on the one hand designed to prove numerically that the presented first-order relaxed friction-type model is able to accurately approximate the solution of original Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system in the limit of
|\varepsilon|\rightarrow 0
and on the other hand to illustrate the wave structure of its hyperbolic sub-system.
To this end, besides the full set of equations of the first-order friction-type model in (
4.7
), we additionally investigate two reduced versions of it.
For the first sub-system, we only consider the hyperbolic part of (
4.7
).
Hence, we omit any contributions due to the source terms or the elliptic constraint for
\omega^{\varepsilon}
.
The second system is also based on the hyperbolic sub-system however with the additional contribution of the friction-type source term
-\frac{v}{\delta}
taken into account.
In the following, these three systems are denoted as the
relaxed friction-type model
, the
hyperbolic sub-system without sources
and the
hyperbolic sub-system with friction
, respectively.

The considered numerical experiments are organized as follows, first, we discuss results for the
hyperbolic sub-system with and without sources
with Riemann initial data and different choices of the relaxation parameters
\varepsilon
in Section
5.1
and
5.2
.
This is followed by different numerical results obtained with the
relaxed friction-type model
for a stationary droplet, a spinodal decomposition and a setup which features the phenomena of Ostwald ripening in Section
5.3
.
Finally, to illustrate the robustness of the
relaxed friction-type model
, we revisit the setup with the Riemann initial data and perform simulations with the full system.

5.1.
The relaxed friction-type model without source terms

The present section is devoted to the numerical investigation of the hyperbolic sub-system of (
4.7
).
For this, we considered two different types of Riemann initial data, first, a
compression-like
initial condition, given by

(5.1)

(p^{\varepsilon},u^{\varepsilon},c^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})(x,0)=\begin{cases}\vskip 7.11317pt(0,+1,1,0):\penalty\ x\leq 0,\\
(0,-1,1,0):\penalty\ x>0,\end{cases}

and second, a
expansion-like
initialization, defined as

(5.2)

(p^{\varepsilon},u^{\varepsilon},c^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})(x,0)=\begin{cases}\vskip 7.11317pt(0,-1,2,0),\;x\leq 0,\\

5.3.
The relaxed friction-type approximation of the NSCH system

Finally, we consider numerical experiments with the full
relaxed friction-type model
for the NSCH equations.
The goal of these test cases is on the one hand to illustrate numerically the convergence of the approximate model towards the one-dimensional NSCH equations and on the other hand to discuss the sensitivity of the results on the choice of the approximation parameters
\varepsilon
.
For this, three different types of one-dimensional test cases are considered, namely the relaxation of a droplet to equilibrium, a spinodal decomposition and a one-dimensional Ostwald ripening.
Finally, to highlight the robustness of the
relaxed friction-type model
, we revisit the previously used Riemann data and show results obtained with the approximate formulation of the NSCH equations.

If not stated otherwise, in the following, the
relaxed friction-type model
is discretized with a conservative second-order finite-differences scheme.
The time stepping is fully implicit by utilizing an implicit Runge-Kutta method of the Radau IIA family of fifth-order.
The domain is discretized with
N=1000
points and periodic boundary conditions are employed if not stated otherwise.
In addition to the presented friction-type approximation of the NSCH equations, also the original NSCH equations are considered as a reference.
However, since only one-dimensional setups with a uniform velocity field are investigated, the original NSCH equations reduce to the Cahn-Hilliard (CH) equations under these circumstances.
Hence, instead of the NSCH equations, the CH equation are solved as a reference.
For this, we utilized a conservative fourth-order finite-differences discretization as proposed in
[
8
]
.
The same time stepping strategy as for the friction-type approximation model is utilized.
Concerning the physical parameters, the double-well potential in (
2.2
)
is considered and the capillary parameter is fixed to
\gamma=0.001
over all simulations.

5.3.1.
Stationary Droplet

The first test case which we consider is a liquid droplet in its vapor which relaxes to its equilibrium state.
This test case is chosen to investigate the influence of the relaxation parameter
\beta
on the obtained results.
For this,
\beta
is varied in between
\beta\in\{0.1,0.01,0.001\}
while the remaining relaxation parameters,
\alpha=10^{-4}
and
\delta=10^{-8}
, remained fixed.
The simulations are performed on the space-time interval
\Omega_{T}=(-1,1)\times(0,1)
.
The initialization of the droplet in terms of the phase-field variable is given by

(5.3)

\displaystyle c(x,0)=c^{\varepsilon}(x,0)=-\tanh{\left( 10 (|x|-0.5) \right)}.

This corresponds to a droplet with radius
r=0.5
whose center is initially located at
x_{ini}=0

The pre-factor of
10
in the
\tanh
-function is used to diffuse the phase interface of the droplet in comparison to its equilibrium profile for the given free energy potential, in (
2.2
), and the chosen capillary parameter
\gamma

Moreover, for the the
relaxed friction-type model
, the artificial pressure and the velocity are both initialized as
p^{\varepsilon}(x,0)=u^{\varepsilon}(x,0)=0

Finally, the flux variable at
t=0
is defined as
v^{\varepsilon}(x,0)=\left(W^{\prime}(c)+\frac{1}{\beta}\left(c^{\varepsilon}-\omega^{\varepsilon}\right)\right)_{x}
, where for the sake of clarity, the dependence of
c^{\varepsilon}
and
\omega^{\varepsilon}
on space and time is omitted.
Since for the evaluation of
v(x,0)
the additional order parameter
\omega^{\varepsilon}
is required,
the elliptic constraint in (
4.7

\omega^{\varepsilon}
in advance.
For this, a second-order central finite-differences discretization is utilized.
Due to the linear nature of the elliptic constraint, the corresponding coefficient matrix can be inverted once at the beginning of the simulation and reused afterwards during each time step.

6.
Conclusions and Outlook

In this paper we investigated the diffuse-interface approach for incompressible two-phase flows based on the widely used NSCH system
(
2.1
). We provided a novel approximative system that basically
consists of a first-order sub-system, see (
4.1
). As our main results, we
proved that the entire system is thermodynamically consistent and that the first-order sub-system is –at least in one space dimension– hyperbolic in the
relevant state space.
For exemplary cases we could showcase a numerical
characteristic analysis.
Finally, we presented numerical results for the spatially one-dimensional version (
4.7
).

This note contains partial results of a forthcoming paper which addresses more general NSCH models and the numerical solution of the relaxed friction-type system (
4.1
) in multiple space dimensions
[
19
]
. The major challenge in this regard is the development of an asymptotic-preserving scheme that employs an implicit-explicit time discretization to overcome the inherent stiffness of the three-parameter problem (
4.1
). The question of the convergence of solutions
\bm{U}^{\varepsilon}
of the initial boundary value problem for (
4.1
) to solutions
\bm{U}
of the corresponding solution of the initial boundary value problem for the limiting NSCH model (
2.1
) remains open. Possibly it can be done using the ideas for a two-parameter problem as in
[
18
]
. We only considered a simplified version of the Navier-Stokes-Cahn-Hilliard class.
It would be interesting to explore whether
c
-dependent mobilities can be introduced, whether
fluids with different densities and viscosities could be handled
[
1
]
, or even systems involving more than two phases
[
3
,
26
]
.
