Inferior ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction Presenting as Isolated Elbow Pain: A Case Report
Authors: Luca Galvagno, Carmelo A Di Franco, Giulia M Evola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute coronary syndromes typically present with chest pain; however, atypical presentations without chest discomfort are not uncommon and may pose significant diagnostic challenges. We report the case of a 60-year-old man who presented to the emergency department with isolated left elbow pain, without chest pain or other typical symptoms. Initial evaluation revealed normal vital signs, and the clinical presentation was suggestive of a musculoskeletal condition. Due to persistence of symptoms, further evaluation was performed approximately three hours after presentation to the emergency department. An electrocardiogram was obtained at that time. Electrocardiography demonstrated ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads (II, III, and aVF), consistent with acute myocardial infarction. Laboratory testing subsequently revealed elevated high-sensitivity troponin I (244 ng/L; reference range 0-34.2 ng/L). A diagnosis of inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction was established. The patient was admitted to the coronary care unit for definitive management. Further details regarding coronary angiography and revascularization were not available. Outcome data following hospitalization were not available. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges associated with atypical presentations of myocardial infarction and underscores the importance of maintaining a low threshold for electrocardiographic evaluation, even in the absence of chest pain.

Introduction

Although chest discomfort remains the predominant symptom of acute coronary syndromes (ACS), up to one-third of patients with myocardial infarction may present without chest pain. Atypical presentations include symptoms such as isolated limb pain, epigastric discomfort, dyspnea, or other non-chest-pain manifestations. These presentations may delay electrocardiographic (ECG) evaluation and increase the risk of missed or delayed diagnosis, particularly in the emergency department setting [1].

In these cases, symptoms localized outside the thoracic region may lead to diagnostic uncertainty and delayed recognition. Upper limb discomfort, particularly involving the left arm, is a well-described manifestation of myocardial ischemia, although it is usually associated with concurrent chest symptoms [2].

Isolated non-thoracic presentations, including cases presenting with jaw pain, shoulder pain, or epigastric discomfort in the absence of chest pain, have been previously reported [3]. We report a case of inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) initially presenting as isolated, non-traumatic elbow pain and discuss how persistence of unexplained upper limb pain in a patient with cardiovascular risk factors should prompt early ECG evaluation [4].

Case presentation

A 60-year-old man presented to the emergency department with isolated left elbow pain of recent onset, lasting for several hours prior to presentation. There was no history of trauma. The pain was localized to the posterior aspect of the left elbow, poorly defined, and described as a deep, non-sharp discomfort. It was persistent, not clearly related to movement or palpation, and showed no clear relieving or exacerbating factors. No radiation was reported.

He denied chest pain, dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, vomiting, syncope, palpitations, epigastric discomfort, back pain, neck or jaw pain, neurologic symptoms, and any recent exertional symptoms. His past medical history was unremarkable. He had no known cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, or smoking, and no prior history of coronary artery disease. He was not taking any regular medications and had no known drug allergies.

On initial evaluation, vital signs were within normal limits, including a blood pressure of 120/70 mmHg, heart rate of 68 bpm, and oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. The patient was alert and hemodynamically stable. Physical examination revealed localized pain in the left elbow without swelling, erythema, or deformity. There was no tenderness to palpation, and the range of motion was preserved. Distal pulses were palpable and symmetric, with normal capillary refill. Sensory and motor function of the upper limb were intact. Cardiovascular examination revealed normal heart sounds without murmurs, and lung auscultation was unremarkable. No peripheral edema was noted.

At triage, the patient was assigned a low-priority category due to the absence of chest pain or other typical features of ACS. The initial working diagnosis was a musculoskeletal condition affecting the left upper limb.

Given the isolated peripheral symptomatology, normal vital signs, and absence of concerning associated features, an ECG was not performed during the initial evaluation. Differential diagnoses considered included musculoskeletal pain and localized soft tissue pathology.

The persistence of symptoms without a clear musculoskeletal explanation prompted re-evaluation and further diagnostic workup. At re-evaluation, approximately three hours after initial presentation, a 12-lead ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm at approximately 75 bpm, with a normal PR interval (126 ms), QRS duration (98 ms), and QTc (437 ms). ST-segment elevation was observed in the inferior leads (II, III, and aVF), consistent with an acute inferior STEMI. Reciprocal ST-segment depression in the lateral leads was not clearly appreciable.

However, transfer to a cardiology unit for definitive management was arranged immediately after diagnosis.

Discussion

ACS classically presents with chest pain; however, atypical manifestations are not uncommon and may occur in a substantial proportion of patients, sometimes in the absence of chest discomfort [5]. Isolated peripheral pain as the sole presenting feature of STEMI is rare. Unusual presentations, including limb pain or other non-thoracic symptoms, have been described in the literature and may contribute to delayed diagnosis [6,7].

Isolated upper limb pain has been described as a potential atypical manifestation of myocardial ischemia, most commonly involving the left arm and typically occurring in association with chest discomfort. However, presentation as truly isolated, non-traumatic elbow pain without any accompanying typical or systemic symptoms appears to be uncommon in the literature.

This case adds to existing reports by highlighting an instance of inferior STEMI presenting exclusively as elbow pain, leading to an initial misattribution to a musculoskeletal cause and delayed ECG evaluation. It underscores the need for a low threshold for ECG acquisition in patients with unexplained upper limb pain, even in the absence of chest symptoms.

The perception of upper limb pain in myocardial ischemia is generally explained by the convergence of cardiac visceral afferent fibers with somatic afferents at the level of overlapping spinal cord segments. Cardiac afferents, typically entering the spinal cord at upper thoracic levels (approximately T1-T5), may converge with somatic afferents supplying the upper limb, leading to referred pain in the arm or adjacent regions [8]. This mechanism may lead to myocardial ischemia being perceived as isolated upper limb pain, even in the absence of thoracic symptoms.

Atypical presentations may contribute to diagnostic uncertainty and delayed recognition of ACS. This case illustrates how an apparently benign, non-traumatic musculoskeletal presentation can increase the risk of premature diagnostic closure in the emergency department, potentially leading to delayed ECG evaluation and diagnosis.

Patients presenting with atypical or non-thoracic symptoms may still have ACS when the clinical context is compatible. In the emergency department, evaluation should include early ECG alongside cardiac troponin testing, with results interpreted in conjunction with symptoms, vital signs, physical examination, and cardiovascular risk profile [9,10].

ECG remains the first-line diagnostic tool for identifying STEMI, and diagnosis should not be delayed while awaiting troponin results. Troponin testing plays a complementary role, particularly in the evaluation of non-ST-segment elevation presentations. Recent real-world evidence from a high-volume Turkish emergency department showed that the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) 0/1-hour high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) algorithm achieved 100% sensitivity and 100% negative predictive value for ruling out non-STEMI (NSTEMI), supporting the diagnostic value of systematic ECG and troponin-based assessment in suspected ACS [11].

In patients presenting with atypical or non-thoracic symptoms, ACS should remain a diagnostic consideration when the clinical context is compatible. In particular, older patients or those with cardiovascular risk factors presenting with persistent, unexplained, non-traumatic upper limb pain should prompt a lower threshold for obtaining a 12-lead ECG. Early recognition of atypical presentations is essential to reduce diagnostic delay and improve clinical outcomes [12].

Conclusions

Acute myocardial infarction may occur in the absence of chest pain and, in rare cases, may present with isolated peripheral symptoms such as elbow pain, potentially leading to delayed recognition and treatment. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for ACS in patients presenting with atypical or non-thoracic symptoms. Early ECG evaluation should be considered when non-traumatic upper limb pain is unexplained, persistent, or occurs in patients with cardiovascular risk factors, even in the absence of chest pain. Such an approach may help reduce diagnostic delay and improve clinical outcomes.
