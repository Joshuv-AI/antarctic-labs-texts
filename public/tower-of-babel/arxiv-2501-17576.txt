Model-checking real-time systems: revisiting the alternating automaton route
Patricia Bouyer, B Srivathsan, Vaishnavi Vishwanath

Abstract
. Alternating timed automata (ATA) are an extension of timed automata where the effect of each transition is described by a positive boolean combination of (state-reset) pairs. Unlike classical timed automata, ATA are closed under complementation and are therefore suitable for logic-to-automata translations. Several timed logics, including Metric Temporal Logic (MTL), can be converted to equivalent 1-clock ATAs (1-ATAs). Satisfiability of an MTL formula reduces to checking emptiness of a 1-ATA. A straightforward modification of the 1-ATA emptiness algorithm can be applied for model-checking timed automata models against 1-ATA specifications. Existing emptiness algorithms for 1-ATA proceed by a region construction, and are not satisfactory for implementations. On the other hand, emptiness for classical timed automata proceeds by zone-based methods that have been extensively studied with well developed implementations. Therefore, logic-to-automata tools typically perform a sophisticated conversion of the formula into a network of timed automata, sometimes, via a 1-ATA as an intermediate step. Our goal in this work is to initiate the study of zone-based methods directly for 1-ATAs. The challenge here, as opposed to timed automata, is the fact that the zone graph may generate an unbounded number of variables. We first introduce a deactivation operation on the 1-ATA syntax to allow an explicit deactivation of the clock in transitions. Using the deactivation operation, we improve the existing MTL-to-1-ATA conversion and present a fragment of MTL for which the equivalent 1-ATA generate a bounded number of variables. Secondly, we develop the idea of zones for 1-ATA and present an emptiness algorithm which explores a corresponding zone graph. For termination, a special entailment check between zones is necessary. Our main technical contributions are: (1) an algorithm for the entailment check using simple zone operations and (2) an 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hardness for the entailment check in the general case. Finally, we adapt our methods to the problem of model-checking timed automata models against 1-ATA specifications. We observe that when the timed automaton is strongly non-Zeno or when the 1-ATA generates a bounded number of variables, a modified entailment check with quadratic complexity can be applied.

1.
Introduction

Model-checking real-time systems.

The task of verifying if a model
A
(typically a network of automata) satisfies a specification
\varphi
(a formula in a logic) can be cast as the emptiness of an automaton
A\times B_{\neg\varphi}
, where
B_{\neg\varphi}
is an automaton recognizing the set of behaviours that violate the specification – in other words, an automaton corresponding to
\neg\varphi
. This is the well-known model-checking paradigm. In the context of real-time systems,
timed automata

[
5
]
are the de-facto choice for the automaton model. For the logic part, metric temporal logic (MTL) is a natural extension of the widely used linear temporal logic (LTL), incorporating timing constraints in the modalities. However, timed logics cannot be easily converted to timed automata, since they are not closed under complementation.

A popular choice for logic-to-automata translations for timed logics, is the model of
1-clock Alternating Timed Automata (1-ATAs)

[
38
,
35
]
. 1-ATAs are closed under complementation, and are incomparable in expressive power to (multi-clock) timed automata. The power of alternation enables resetting fresh copies of the single clock at different positions along the word. Therefore, while reading a timed word, a 1-ATA can keep track of the time elapsed from multiple positions in the word. This feature is quite convenient to handle timed modalities like
\operatorname{U}_{I}
, which is an Until operator with an interval constraint
I
. Therefore, the model-checking problem reduces to checking the emptiness of
A\times B_{\neg\varphi}
where
A
is a timed automaton and
B_{\neg\varphi}
is a
1-ATA. This is a rather natural formulation of the model-checking problem in the real-time setting. Surprisingly, there is no tool that implements an algorithm to this problem, to the best of our knowledge. Decidability of this question follows from
[
36
]
and
[
35
]
. However it is based on extended regions and not suitable for practical implementations.

On the other hand, emptiness for classical timed automata has been extensively studied over the last three decades, and there are mature and well developed tools like UPPAAL
[
33
]
, PAT
[
40
]
, LTSMin
[
30
]
, TChecker
[
27
]
, Theta
[
41
]
. Algorithms for classical timed automata are based on
zones
. These are convenient data structures that symbolically capture reachable configurations of a timed automaton. Our goal is to eventually adapt the best known methods from timed automata literature to solve the emptiness of
A\times B_{\neg\varphi}
. The crucial missing piece in this picture is a zone-based algorithm to handle 1-ATAs, like
B_{\neg\varphi}
. This is the subject of this paper.

The challenge.

Undoubtedly, the biggest challenge in developing a zone-based procedure for 1-ATA is the unbounded size of the configurations that it generates (illustrated in Fig.
4
). As said earlier, a 1-ATA maintains several copies of its clock, each of them storing the time since a specific position. This not only adds technical difficulty in describing zone graphs, but it also makes some of the operations used in the computation algorithmically hard. In this work, we pinpoint the challenges and provide some efficient algorithmic solutions for some restricted cases of 1-ATA which arise from fragments of MTL.

Contributions.

As a first contribution, we introduce a new
deactivation
operation for the clock, on transitions. When a clock is deactivated, the branch of the 1-ATA that passes through the transition maintains only the control state, and not the clock value (until the clock gets reactivated again through a reset). This explicit deactivation helps reducing the number of active clock copies maintained by the 1-ATA. To substantiate this idea, we improve the MTL-to-1-ATA construction of
[
38
]
using the deactivation operator and identify a fragment that induces 1-ATAs with
bounded width
– these are automata which contain a bounded number of active clock copies in any reachable configuration.

Next, we present a definition of a zone graph for the enhanced 1-ATAs with the deactivation operation, and provide an algorithm to compute the zone successors. For termination of the zone graph computation, we adapt the entailment relation between zones, studied in
[
1
]
, to our setting. Our main technical contribution is a close study of this entailment check.

•

We provide an algorithm for the entailment check using standard zone operations known from the timed automata literature.

•

We prove that deciding non-entailment is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard, by reducing the monotone 3-SAT problem to the non-entailment check.

•

Then, we present a modified entailment check, with quadratic complexity, for 1-ATAs with bounded width.

A shorter version of this work, reporting on all the contributions mentioned above, appeared in
[
15
]
. In this extended version, we firstly include all the missing proofs and present a new elaborate section on extending the zone graph algorithm to the product
A\times B_{\neg\varphi}
in order to solve the model-checking problem. As a new contribution, we focus on the case when
A
is
strongly non-Zeno
, that is, there is a number
k
such that in every
k
actions of
A
, at least
1
time unit elapses. We prove that when
A
is strongly non-Zeno, the number of “useful configurations” generated by the product
A\times B_{\neg\varphi}
is bounded. Hence, the modified entailment check with quadratic complexity can be applied to the zone graph of
A\times B_{\neg\varphi}
. This concept of strong non-Zenoness appears frequently in timed automata literature, for instance
[
42
,
8
,
9
]
. Models that are not strongly non-Zeno can have
timelocks
– an infinite number of events happen in a finite time interval. Such models are not realistic and there is a body of work on detecting timelock freedom in timed automata models
[
42
,
17
]
.

Our new observation indicates that for model-checking the practically useful class of strongly non-Zeno automata against specifications given by 1-ATAs, our approach is promising, since a bounded number of variables are generated during the computation, and furthermore all zone operations have the same efficient complexity as in the classical timed automata setting. We believe this is encouraging, since it paves the way for model-checking the full Metric Temporal Logic over realistic timed automata models, as long as we consider finite words. We remark that in the untimed setting, Linear Temporal Logic over finite words (LTLf) has recently gained substantial attention due to its practical applicability (see
[
10
]
for an exposition).

Related work.

Recall the casting of the model-checking question as an emptiness of
A\times B_{\neg\varphi}
where
B_{\neg\varphi}
is a 1-ATA. Since timed automata tools are mature, some of the existing techniques convert
B_{\neg\varphi}
into a timed automaton. MightyL
[
19
]
is a tool that converts a fragment of MTL called Metric Interval Temporal Logic (MITL) into a network of timed automata, by observing special properties of the 1-ATA obtained from MITL
[
18
]
. A conversion of MITL into a network of generalized timed automata
[
3
]
has been developed
[
4
]
, and a tool incorporating MITL model-checking is available
[
2
]
. Both these works deal with the MITL fragment. To the best of our knowledge, the only available logic-to-automaton procedure for the full MTL fragment is the MTL-to-1-ATA conversion of
[
38
]
. Hence, our work would be the first attempt at an implementable algorithm for the full MTL model-checking (over finite timed words).

The core zone based algorithm for timed automata reachability was proposed in
[
20
]
. Termination of the zone enumeration has been an intricate topic since then
[
16
,
11
,
28
,
26
]
. We refer the reader to the surveys
[
13
,
39
]
for a more detailed exposition on this topic. An important ingredient in the zone enumeration is a simulation operation between zones (similar in spirit to the entailment check as said above). For extended models of timed automata – like timed automata with diagonal constraints
[
16
]
and event-clock automata
[
7
]
, there is a translation to classical timed automata. Therefore emptiness for these extended automata can be reduced to emptiness of a bigger timed automaton. However, previous works have observed that zone-based algorithms which work directly on the extended automata perform better than the zone-based algorithms that run on equivalent bigger timed automata:
[
26
]
shows it for timed automata with diagonal constraints;
[
3
]
introduces
generalized timed automata
which can model event-clock automata and provides a comparison of the direct method versus the conversion to timed automata. The main reason is that the conversion to timed automata adds many control states which cannot be handled well by the current zone-based algorithms. Algorithms which work directly on the extended automata encode the extra information using zones and manage the explosion using zone simulation techniques. For timed automata with diagonal constraints, the simulation check is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. Nevertheless an algorithm using basic zone operations has been proposed
[
26
]
and has been shown to work well on examples. This history of successful zone-based methods on extensions of timed automata encourages us to look for direct zone based methods for 1-ATAs as well.

Alternating Timed Automata appeared in
[
38
,
35
]
. With two clocks, emptiness is undecidable, simply because universality for 2-clock timed automata is undecidable
[
5
]
. Emptiness for 1-ATA is known to be decidable with a non-primitive recursive complexity – one potential reason why 1-ATAs have been ignored for direct manipulations. However, several timed logics have been compiled into 1-ATA
[
18
,
31
]
and multiple restrictions of 1-ATAs with better complexity, like very weak 1-ATA
[
19
]
, 1-ATA with reset-free loops
[
31
]
have been independently considered in the literature.

In the untimed setting, emptiness of Alternating Finite Automata (AFA) can be performed by a
de-alternation
that computes an equivalent NFA on-the-fly. To deal with large state-spaces, techniques based on antichains have been extensively explored in the literature
[
44
,
22
,
23
]
. Essentially, the algorithms maintain an antichain of the state space, induced by various simulation preorders. Several heuristics based on preprocessing of the automaton
[
43
]
and making use of SAT solvers
[
29
]
have been investigated. A comparative study of various AFA emptiness algorithms can be found in
[
24
]
.

In the timed setting, the closest to our work is
[
1
]
, which presents a zone-based approach for answering universality of 1-clock timed automata. Our zone construction for 1-ATA builds on this approach. The entailment check between zones that we use has been proposed in
[
1
]
. However, there is no algorithm given for the entailment check. An approximation of the check is encoded as an SMT formula. In this work, we make a comprehensive study of this entailment check in terms of algorithms and complexity.

Organization.

in the transition system above. This problem has been proven decidable with non-primitive recursive complexity in

35
]
. The decidability result relies on the construction of extended regions, on which a well-quasi order can be defined. In this paper, following standard techniques for timed automata
[
20
,
13
]
, we design a symbolic zone-based approach to decide the emptiness problem for 1-ATA. Before we delve into it, we consider a special restriction of 1-ATAs.

1-ATA with bounded width

We define the

of a configuration

\gamma
. For instance,
\{(q_{0},1.2),(q_{1},0),(q_{1},\bot)\}
has width

. From Example

, we notice that the run can be extended to generate configurations of larger and larger width. Each transition out of

generates two more states, and the existing states with

remain.

{defi}

A 1-ATA
\mathcal{A}
has width
k
if every
reachable
configuration starting from the initial configuration of
\mathcal{A}
has width
\leq k
. A 1-ATA has

if there exists a
k
such that the 1-ATA has width
k
.

It can be checked that

of Fig.

have unbounded width.
We will see that this is in fact a useful subclass of 1-ATA, because this leads to a global bound on the number of different active clock values that need to be tracked during the emptiness check.

3.
Improving the MTL-to-1-ATA construction

In this section, we revisit the MTL-to-1-ATA construction of
[

,
38
]
and propose a modified construction incorporating the deactivation operation. Subsequently, we identify a fragment of MTL which yields bounded width 1-ATAs with the proposed modification. The syntax of MTL is described using the grammar:

\displaystyle\varphi:=a\mid\lnot a\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\lor\varphi\mid\operatorname{X}_{I}\varphi\mid\widetilde{\operatorname{X}}_{I}\varphi\mid\varphi\operatorname{U}_{I}\varphi\mid\varphi\widetilde{\operatorname{U}}_{1}\varphi

where

and

and
\operatorname{U}_{I}
as we do, all formulas can be converted into negation normal form

38
]

Given a timed word
w=(d_{1},a_{1}),\dots,(d_{n},a_{n})
, a position

and an MTL formula
\varphi
, we define

as follows:

•

(w,i)\models a
if

, and
(w,i)\models\lnot a
if

•

if
(w,i)\models\varphi_{1}
and
(w,i)\models\varphi_{2}

•

(w,i)\models\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}
if
(w,i)\models\varphi_{1}
or
(w,i)\models\varphi_{2}

•

(w,i)\models\operatorname{X}_{I}\varphi
if

and

•

if
i=n
, or

, or

•

if there is some

such that

,

and for all

•

and

, or there is some

We say a timed word

We define

{defi}

[One-sided MTL]
The syntax of one-sided MTL is defined as:

\varphi:=a\mid\lnot a\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\lor\varphi\mid\operatorname{X}_{I}\varphi\mid\widetilde{\operatorname{X}}_{I}\varphi\mid\psi\operatorname{U}_{I}\varphi\mid\varphi\widetilde{\operatorname{U}}_{1}\psi

The key idea is that in the
\operatorname{U}_{I}

\widetilde{\operatorname{U}}_{I}
modality the right branch is an LTL formula. For example, the formula
(\mathbf{F}a)\operatorname{U}_{[1,2]}c
, is a one-sided MTL formula. Notice that in the Flat-MTL fragment
[
14
]
, the Until formulas either have bounded intervals, or have pure LTL formulas on the left branch. Here, we only allow the latter kind of formulas. Also, this fragment is different from MITL
[
6
]
as we do not forbid singular intervals.
Here is the main result of this section:

Lemma 2
.

For every one-sided MTL formula
\varphi
, there is a bound
k_{\varphi}
such that the 1-ATA
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
has width
k_{\varphi}
.

Proof 3.2
.

We first define
k_{\varphi}
inductively:

–

if
\varphi
is a pure LTL formula, then
k_{\varphi}=1
,

–

if
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}
, then
k_{\varphi}=\max(k_{\varphi_{1}},k_{\varphi_{2}})
,

–

if
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}
, then
k_{\varphi}=k_{\varphi_{1}}+k_{\varphi_{2}}
,

–

if
\varphi=\operatorname{X}_{I}\varphi_{1}
or
\widetilde{\operatorname{X}}_{I}\varphi_{1}
, then
k_{\varphi}=k_{\varphi_{1}}
,

–

if
\varphi=\psi\operatorname{U}_{I}\varphi_{2}
with
\psi
an LTL formula, then
k_{\varphi}=k_{\varphi_{2}}
,

–

if
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\widetilde{\operatorname{U}}_{I}\psi
with
\psi
an LTL formula, then
k_{\varphi}
= 1 +
k_{\varphi_{1}}
.

We will prove that the number of active states present in every reachable configuration of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
, starting from the initial configuration
\{(\varphi_{init},0)\}
is at most
k_{\varphi}
.

We first observe that because of the way we deactivate clocks in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
for every transition
\delta^{\prime}(\psi,a)
on an LTL subformula
\psi
, once some transition deactivates the clock
x
in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
, the automaton never reactivates
x
. This means if
\gamma
is a minimal model of some transition
\overline{x}.\delta^{\prime}(\psi,a)
on a valuation
v
, any configuration reachable from
\gamma
will have no active states.

We now look at the case when
\varphi
is an LTL formula. Initially, we have the configuration
\{(\varphi_{init},0)\}
containing one active state. By definition,
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{init},a)=\bar{x}.\delta^{\prime}(\varphi,a)
. As observed above, this means except for the initial configuration which has a single active state, every reachable configuration has no active states.

Next, when
\varphi
is not a pure LTL formula, we will prove the required statement by proving that for any subformula
\varphi^{\prime}
of
\varphi
, if a configuration
\gamma
is a minimal model of
\delta^{\prime}(\psi,a)
for some
a
on some valuation
v
, then any configuration reachable from
\gamma
in
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
has at most
k_{\psi}
active states. We can see that because
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{init},a)=x.\delta^{\prime}(\varphi,a)
, any reachable configuration from
\{(\varphi_{init},0)\}
will be a configuration reachable from some minimal model of
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi,a)
on
0
, and so proving this claim will give us the required proof. We now prove our claim by structural induction on
\varphi^{\prime}
:

Case
\varphi^{\prime}=a
or
\lnot a
.

The only transitions from
\delta^{\prime}(a,a)
or
\delta^{\prime}(\lnot a,a)
are
\operatorname{\textbf{true}}
or
\operatorname{\textbf{false}}
, meaning if there exists a minimal model of
\delta^{\prime}(a,a)
or
\delta^{\prime}(\lnot a,a)
, it is the empty configuration, hence the claim holds here.

Case
\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}
or
\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}
.

Looking at the case where
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are both not pure LTL formulas, the transition for disjunction is
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2},a)=\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{1},a)\lor\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{2},a)
. Meaning any configuration that is a minimal model of
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi^{\prime},a)
on
v
is either a minimal model of
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{1},a)
on
v
, or of
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{2},a)
on
v
. Similarly, for the case of conjunction,
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2},a)=\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{1},a)\land\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{2},a)
, so any minimal model of
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi^{\prime},a)
on
v
will be a minimal model of both
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{1},a)
and
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{2},a)
on
v
. Using induction hypothesis on
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
, we can prove the claim for this case. The cases where only one of
\varphi_{1}
or
\varphi_{2}
is purely LTL can be proved similarly. When both
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are pure LTL formulas, we notice that the transitions are now
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi^{\prime},a)=\overline{x}.\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{1},a)\lor\overline{x}.\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{2},a)
and
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi,a)=\overline{x}.\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{1},a)\land\overline{x}.\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{2},a)
respectively. Similar to earlier observation, we see that any configuration that is a minimal model of
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi^{\prime},a)
will have no active variables, and because the clock will not get activated in any configuration reachable from a minimal model of
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{1},a)
or
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi_{2},a)
, the claim will hold.

Case
\varphi^{\prime}=\operatorname{X}_{I}\varphi_{1}
.

We have
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi^{\prime},a)
to be either
\overline{x}.(\operatorname{X}_{I}\varphi_{1})^{r}
or

. Meaning any minimal model of
\delta^{\prime}(\varphi^{\prime},a)
on some
v
will be of the form
\{((\operatorname{X}_{I}\varphi_{1})^{r},0)\}
or
\{((\operatorname{X}_{I}\varphi_{1})^{r},\bot)\}
. From there, as
\delta^{\prime}((\operatorname{X}_{I}\varphi_{1})^{r},a)
is either

or

, the next configuration will be a minimal model of
\delta(\varphi_{1},a)
for either
0
or
\bot
, or will be
\emptyset
. By induction hypothesis, any configuration reachable from it will have atmost
k_{\varphi_{1}}
active states. The proof will follow similarly for

.

Case

. This means in general, any configuration reachable from a minimal model of

active states. When

Case

on

\delta^{\prime}(\varphi^{\prime},a)
on some

will have at most

We now move on to a formal definition of the zone graph and the procedure to compute successors. Fix a 1-ATA
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,q_{0},F,\delta)

\mathcal{A}

\operatorname{loc}(x_{q,i})=q
,
\operatorname{loc}(x^{\prime}_{q,i})=q
. For a set of variables

4.1.
Zones and nodes.

A
zone
is a conjunction of constraints of the form
(y\sim k)
or
(y-x\sim k)
, where
{\sim}\in\{<,\leq,>,\geq\}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x,y
are variables. We write
\textit{Var}(Z)
for the set of variables used in
Z
. We define
\operatorname{loc-sign}(Z):=\operatorname{loc-sign}(\textit{Var}(Z))
. To deal with the inactive valuations, we define the nodes of a zone graph to be of the form
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
where
Z
is a zone and
\operatorname{IA}\subseteq\{x_{q,0}\mid q\text{ is a location of }\mathcal{A}\}
is a set of variables that are currently inactive. Notice that we always use variables with index
0
in
\operatorname{IA}
, and hence there are finitely many choices for the
\operatorname{IA}
component of nodes. We say
\textit{Var}(Z,\operatorname{IA})=\textit{Var}(Z)\cup\operatorname{IA}
and
\operatorname{loc-sign}(Z,\operatorname{IA})=\operatorname{loc-sign}(Z)\cup\operatorname{loc-sign}(\operatorname{IA})
. We will call the pair
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
a
node
.

We say that a configuration
\gamma
satisfies a node
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
, denoted as
\gamma\models(Z,\operatorname{IA})
, if there is a surjection
h:\textit{Var}(Z,\operatorname{IA})\mapsto\gamma
such that (i)
\operatorname{loc}(y)=\operatorname{loc}(h(y))
for every
y\in\textit{Var}(Z,\operatorname{IA})
, (ii) for every
x_{q,0}\in\operatorname{IA}
,
val(h(x_{q,0}))=\bot
and (iii) replacing every variable
y\in\textit{Var}(Z)
with
val(h(y))
satisfies all the zone constraints. We can now define
\llbracket(Z,\operatorname{IA})\rrbracket=\{\gamma\mid\gamma\models(Z,\operatorname{IA})\}
.
This means we can look at zones as a representation of a set of configurations. For convenience, we will simply use
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
to refer to both the zone and the set
\llbracket(Z,\operatorname{IA})\rrbracket
of configurations. For instance, we will use
\gamma\in(Z,\operatorname{IA})
to mean
\gamma\in\llbracket(Z,\operatorname{IA})\rrbracket
. We illustrate these notions on an example.

Computing successors.

Given a node

and a letter

. On

and
(q_{0}\land x.q_{1},[1,1]\land q_{2})
. In Fig.
4
we depict the only successor on

Assume
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
is a non-empty node with
\textit{Var}(Z)=\{x_{q_{1},i_{1}},x_{q_{2},i_{2}},\dots,x_{q_{k},i_{k}}\}
(indices
1
to
k
) and

(indices
k+1
to
m
). For an

, we define:

\operatorname{target}((Z,\operatorname{IA}),a)=\{(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})\mid C_{j}\text{ is a disjunct in }\delta(q_{j},a)\text{ for }1\leq j\leq m\}

Pick
(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})
. Our goal is to compute the successor node
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\xrightarrow{a,(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})}(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
. If some

then according to our definition, there is no model for it, w.r.t. to any valuation. We discard such targets. Let us assume none of the
C_{j}
is
\operatorname{\textbf{false}}
. We do the following sequence of operations:

Time elapse.

Compute node
(Z_{1},\operatorname{IA}_{1})
where
\llbracket(Z_{1},\operatorname{IA}_{1})\rrbracket=\{\gamma+\delta\mid\delta\geq 0\}
and
\operatorname{IA}_{1}=\operatorname{IA}
. The zone
Z_{1}
represents the closure of
Z
w.r.t. time successors. It can be computed using a standard DBM technique as in
[
12
]
.

Guard intersection.

For every active variable
j
ranging from
1
to
k
, and for every interval
I_{j}\in C_{j}
, add the constraints corresponding to
x_{q_{j},i_{j}}\in I_{j}
. After adding all the constraints, we tighten the constraints using an operation known as

in the timed automata literature
[
12
]
. For instance if
x^{\prime}=x,y^{\prime}=y
and

, we derive the constraint
x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}
. Let the resulting node be
(Z_{2},\operatorname{IA}_{2})
with
\operatorname{IA}_{2}=\operatorname{IA}_{1}
. We have
\llbracket(Z_{2},\operatorname{IA}_{2})\rrbracket:=\{\gamma\in(Z_{1},\operatorname{IA}_{1})\mid\gamma\text{ satisfies all clock constraints in the transition}\}
.

Reset and move to new variables.

Since we have dealt with intervals already, we can now assume that each
C_{j}
is a conjunction of atoms of the form
q
,
x.q
and
\bar{x}.q
, or
C_{j}=\operatorname{\textbf{true}}
. If
C_{j}=\operatorname{\textbf{true}}
for all
1\leq j\leq m
, the successor of
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
is a special empty node
(Z_{\emptyset},\{\})
where
Z_{\emptyset}
denotes a zone with
\llbracket Z_{\emptyset}\rrbracket=\emptyset
(see Fig.
5
, the successor of
(\langle x_{\varphi,1}\geq 0\rangle,\{\})
for instance). Otherwise, we will iteratively compute a new set of constraints
\Phi
, and a new set of variables
\operatorname{IA}_{3}
. Initially,
\Phi:=\operatorname{\textbf{true}}
,
\operatorname{IA}_{3}=\emptyset
. Pick each variable
x_{q_{j},i_{j}}
, with
j
ranging from
1
to
m
in some order, and consider all the atoms in the conjunction
C_{j}
:

(1)

if
q
is an atom of
C_{j}
: when
1\leq j\leq k
(active variable), add
x^{\prime}_{q,\ell}=x_{q_{j},i_{j}}
to
\Phi
, where

is the smallest index (greater than
2
) such that
x^{\prime}_{q,\ell}
is not used in
\Phi
; when
k+1\leq j\leq m
(inactive variable), add
x^{\prime}_{q,0}
to
\operatorname{IA}_{3}
,

(2)

if
x.q
is an atom of
C_{j}
, add
x^{\prime}_{q,1}=0
to
\Phi
,

(3)

if
\bar{x}.q
is an atom of
C_{j}
, add
x^{\prime}_{q,0}
to
\operatorname{IA}_{3}
.

Define
Z_{3}=Z_{2}\land\Phi
.

Remove old variables.

Tighten all the constraints of
Z_{3}
by the canonicalization procedure. After canonicalization, remove all the old unprimed variables, and remove the primes from the newly introduced variables, i.e.,
x^{\prime}_{q,1}
becomes
x_{q,1}
and so on. This new node is the required

.

4.3.
Zone graph of a 1-ATA

The initial node is
(Z_{0},\operatorname{IA}_{0})
where
Z_{0}:=(x_{q_{0},1}=0)
(with
q_{0}
being the initial state) and
\operatorname{IA}_{0}=\emptyset
. There is a special node
(Z_{\emptyset},\{\})
denoting the empty configuration, with
\llbracket Z_{\emptyset}\rrbracket=\emptyset
. Successors are systematically computed by enumerating over all the outgoing targets, and performing the successor computation as explained earlier: we add an edge
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\xrightarrow{(a,C_{1},\dots,C_{m})}(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
when
(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
is the result of applying
(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})
on
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
, as above. The resulting graph that is computed is called the zone graph of the 1-ATA. A node
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
is said to be accepting if for every
x\in\textit{Var}(Z)\cup\operatorname{IA}
, we have
\operatorname{loc}(x)
to be accepting. In particular, the special node
(Z_{\emptyset},\{\})
is accepting. We now proves that the zone graph is sound and complete using the following lemma which shows that the successor computation as described above is correct.

Here are some remarks about the statement of the lemma. For each transition
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\xrightarrow{(a,C_{1},\dots,C_{m})}(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
, we wish to say two things: each configuration in the target node
(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
is the result of picking some configuration in the source node, performing a delay and then applying the update; and conversely, every successor of a configuration in the source
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
via a delay and a transition given by
(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})
appears in the target. However, we need to care for an extra detail: recall that a configuration
\gamma
belongs to
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
if there exists a surjection
h_{\gamma}
that maps variables in
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
to states of
\gamma
. In particular, the number of states
\ell
in
\gamma
could be smaller than the number of variables
m
in
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
since
h_{\gamma}
might map multiple variables to the same state of
\gamma
. Hence we cannot simply write
(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})
as the outgoing action from
\gamma
and instead we need to find the action corresponding to each state of
\gamma
via the mapping
h_{\gamma}
. This is exactly what the statement of the next lemma makes precise. We assume that
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
is a node with active variables
\{x_{q_{1},i_{1}},x_{q_{2},i_{2}},\dots,x_{q_{k},i_{k}}\}
and inactive variables
\{x_{q_{k+1},i_{k+1}},\dots,x_{q_{m},i_{m}}\}
where
i_{k+1}=\cdots=i_{m}=0
.

Lemma 3
.

For the successor computation
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\xrightarrow{a,(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})}(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
, we have
(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
to be the set of all
\gamma^{\prime}
s.t. there exists: (1) a
\gamma\in(Z,\operatorname{IA})
with
\gamma=\{(q_{1},v_{1}),\dots,(q_{\ell},v_{\ell})\}
, (2) a mapping
h_{\gamma}:\textit{Var}(Z,\operatorname{IA})\to\gamma
, and (3) a delay
d\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, satisfying the following:

\gamma\xrightarrow{~d,a,(C^{\prime}_{1},C^{\prime}_{2},\dots,C^{\prime}_{\ell})~}\gamma^{\prime}

where for all
1\leq p\leq\ell
, we have
C^{\prime}_{p}=C_{j}
when
(q_{p},v_{p})=h_{\gamma}(x_{q_{j},i_{j}})
.

Proof 4.1
.

Let
S
be the set of all
\gamma^{\prime}
as defined above. We will show that

. The successor computation proceeds in the following steps:

\displaystyle(Z,\operatorname{IA})\xrightarrow{\text{ time elapse }}(Z_{1},\operatorname{IA}_{1})\xrightarrow{\text{ guard }}(Z_{2},\operatorname{IA}_{2})\xrightarrow{\text{ update }}(Z_{3},\operatorname{IA}_{3})\xrightarrow{\text{remove, rename}}(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})

To prove

Pick
\gamma^{\prime}\in S
. We will prove that
\gamma^{\prime}\in(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
. Since

, we know that there exists some

,

, and

that satisfy the criteria stated in the lemma.
Let

\gamma\xrightarrow{~d~}\gamma_{1}\xrightarrow{a,(C^{\prime}_{1},\dots,C^{\prime}_{\ell})}\gamma^{\prime}

Firstly, we deduce that

, due to the correctness of the time successor computation in classical zones

Theorem 4
.

The zone graph is sound and complete.

Proof 4.2
.

We need to prove that for every path
(Z_{0},\operatorname{IA}_{0})\xrightarrow{a_{1},C^{1}}(Z_{1},\operatorname{IA}_{1})\dots(Z_{n-1},\operatorname{IA}_{n-1})\xrightarrow{a_{n},C^{n}}(Z_{n},\operatorname{IA}_{n})
in the zone graph, there exists a path in the configuration graph of the form

such that

\gamma_{0}\in(Z_{0},\operatorname{IA}_{0})
by definition. Also, by Lemma

\gamma\in(Z,\operatorname{IA})
and
\gamma\xrightarrow{d,a,C}\gamma^{\prime}
for some

,
\gamma^{\prime}\in(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
. This means that for some path
\gamma_{0}\xrightarrow{t_{1},a_{1},C^{{}^{\prime}1}}\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{n-1}\xrightarrow{t_{n},a_{n},C^{{}^{\prime}n}}\gamma_{n}
of the configuration graph, we can always get a path
(Z_{0},\operatorname{IA}_{0})\xrightarrow{a_{1},C^{1}}(Z_{1},\operatorname{IA}_{1})\dots(Z_{n-1},\operatorname{IA}_{n-1})\xrightarrow{a_{n},C^{n}}(Z_{n},\operatorname{IA}_{n})
in the zone graph with
\gamma_{i}\in(Z_{i},\operatorname{IA}_{i})
for each
0\leq i\leq n
. Now to prove the soundness direction, looking at a path
(Z_{0},\operatorname{IA}_{0})\xrightarrow{a_{1},C^{1}}(Z_{1},\operatorname{IA}_{1})\dots(Z_{n-1},\operatorname{IA}_{n-1})\xrightarrow{a_{n},C^{n}}(Z_{n},\operatorname{IA}_{n})
of the zone graph, because each of these zones are non-empty and using Lemma

, we can see that a path
\gamma_{0}\xrightarrow{t_{1},a_{1},C^{{}^{\prime}1}}\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{n-1}\xrightarrow{t_{n},a_{n},C^{{}^{\prime}n}}\gamma_{n}
exists in the configuration graph.

5.
The entailment relation

Zone enumeration in 1-ATAs suffers from two sources of infinity: (1) the width of the zone (number of active states) can increase in an unbounded manner, and (2) the constants appearing in the zone constraints can be unbounded too. For timed automata, the number of clocks (and hence the width of the zones) is fixed. However, the second challenge does manifest and there is a long line of work coming up with better termination mechanisms that tackle the unbounded growth of constants. The termination mechanism is essentially a subsumption relation between zones, which allows to prune the search.

For zone-based universality in 1-clock timed automata, an
entailment
relation between zones was used as a termination mechanism
[
1
]
. We provide an algorithm for this check that makes use of zone operations from the timed automata literature. We then prove that the entailment is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. As the entailment check is an important operation in the zone graph, done each time a new zone is added, the
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness illuminates the difficulty caused due to point (1) above. Finally, for 1-ATA with bounded width, where (1) is not an issue any more, we provide a slight modification to the test, which makes it polynomial-time checkable, and yet ensures termination.

The entailment check is based on an equivalence between configurations
\gamma\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}\gamma^{\prime}
, first proposed in
[
36
]
. The equivalence can be adapted to our setting by considering the
\bot
states by adding that there is a one-to-one correspondence between inactive states in
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
. Fixing a 1-ATA
\mathcal{A}
:

{defi}
[Region Equivalence]
Let
\mathsf{M}\in\mathbb{N}
be the largest constant appearing in
\mathcal{A}
. For two configurations
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
, we say that
\gamma
is region equivalent to
\gamma^{\prime}
, or that
\gamma\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}\gamma^{\prime}
, if we can define a bijection
\pi:\gamma\to\gamma^{\prime}
such that for every
(q,v),(q_{1},v_{1}),(q_{2},v_{2})\in\gamma
:

•

\operatorname{loc}(\pi(q,v))=q
,

•

\textit{val}(\pi(q,v))=\bot
iff
v=\bot
,  and
0\leq\textit{val}(\pi(q,v))\leq\mathsf{M}
iff
0\leq v\leq\mathsf{M}
,

•

if
0\leq v\leq\mathsf{M}
, then
\lfloor v\rfloor=\lfloor\textit{val}(\pi(q,v))\rfloor
, and
\operatorname{fract}(v)=0
iff
\operatorname{fract}(\textit{val}(\pi(q,v)))=0
,

•

if
0\leq v_{1},v_{2}\leq\mathsf{M}
, then
\operatorname{fract}(v_{1})\leq\operatorname{fract}(v_{2})
iff
\operatorname{fract}(\textit{val}(\pi(q_{1},v_{1})))\leq\operatorname{fract}(\textit{val}(\pi(q_{2},v_{2})))
.

By definition, the empty configuration can only be equivalent to itself.
We now observe that
\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}
is a time-abstract bisimulation on the configurations.

Lemma 5
.

For configurations
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
,
\gamma_{3}
such that
\gamma_{1}\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}\gamma_{3}
and
\gamma_{1}\xrightarrow{d,a,C}\gamma_{2}

and configuration
\gamma_{4}
such that
\gamma_{3}\xrightarrow{d^{\prime},a,C}\gamma_{4}
and
\gamma_{2}\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}\gamma_{4}
.

The proof for this is similar to the standard one for region equivalence in timed automata and hence is omitted. The equivalence
\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}
is defined on configurations with the same size. However, as noticed in Fig.
4
, the size of the configurations could be unbounded, and we need a way to relate configurations of different sizes.

{defi}

[Entailment relation]
For configurations
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
, we say that
\gamma
is entailed by
\gamma^{\prime}
, or
\gamma\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}\gamma^{\prime}
, if there exists a subset
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\gamma^{\prime}
such that
\gamma\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}\gamma^{\prime\prime}
.

For nodes
(Z,\operatorname{IA}),(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
, we say
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
, or
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
is entailed by
(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
, if for all
\gamma^{\prime}\in(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
, there exists
\gamma\in(Z,\operatorname{IA})
s.t.
\gamma\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}\gamma^{\prime}
.

When
\mathsf{M}
is clear from the context, we simply write
\sqsubseteq
instead of
\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}
.

For readers familiar with subsumption relations from timed automata literature, the entailment may seem confusing: in timed automata literature, we say
Z\sqsubseteq Z^{\prime}
if for all valuations of
Z
(zone on the left), there exists an equivalent valuation in
Z^{\prime}
(zone on the right). In the entailment relation for 1-ATA zones as in Definition
5
, it is the opposite. Notice that when
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
the node
(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
on the right has more number of variables than
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
the zone on the left. So it is a “bigger” zone in terms of the number of variables. Here is the intuition about how we make use of the entailment relation for 1-ATA zone graphs.

The idea is that when
\gamma\sqsubseteq\gamma^{\prime}
, if
\gamma^{\prime}
reaches an accepting configuration, then so does
\gamma
. In other words, if
\gamma^{\prime}
(the bigger configuration) is able to discharge all its obligations, the small configuration
\gamma
(with fewer obligations) is also able to discharge its own obligations. The same idea holds in the case of nodes. If
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\sqsubseteq(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
, then if
(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
reaches an accepting node, then so does
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
. Therefore, if we reach
(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
during the zone enumeration, while
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
has already been visited, we can stop exploring
(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
. We prove this formally in Lemma
7
. Before that, we provide an example to illustrate the definition of the entailment relation.

5.1.
Algorithm for the entailment check

To get to an algorithm to check entailment on two zones, we look at the cases when the entailment will not hold, i.e. the conditions when
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\not\sqsubseteq(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
. We observe that
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\not\sqsubseteq(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
if either
\operatorname{IA}\not\subseteq\operatorname{IA}^{\prime}
, or

there is some
\gamma^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
s.t. for every
\gamma\in Z
and every
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\gamma^{\prime}
,
\gamma\not\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}\gamma^{\prime\prime}

(1)

As the first case is easy to check, we assume
\operatorname{IA}\subseteq\operatorname{IA}^{\prime}
and focus on the second case, which means that for this particular
\gamma^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
, for each of its subsets
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
, there is no configuration that is region equivalent to it in
Z
. We see that for
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\gamma^{\prime}
such that
\operatorname{loc-sign}(\gamma^{\prime\prime})\neq\operatorname{loc-sign}(Z)
, there will trivially be no region equivalent configuration for
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
in
Z
. Thus we look at the subsets
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
such that
\operatorname{loc-sign}(\gamma^{\prime\prime})=\operatorname{loc-sign}(Z)
and investigate the entailment check (
1
).

Consider a one-to-one mapping
r:\textit{Var}(Z)\to\textit{Var}(Z^{\prime})
that preserves locations: that is, for every

, we have
\operatorname{loc}(x)=\operatorname{loc}(r(x))
. Notice that

is an injection (one-to-one) but not necessarily a surjection (onto), since

be the set of variables of
\textit{Var}(Z^{\prime})
that are in the range of

. Rename variables

to

, making

to

and rename

associates a real to each variable

and
Z^{\prime}_{r}
. Then, we translate

.

For two zones

.

5.2.
Complexity

We first analyze the complexity of the algorithm outlined above to check non-entailment for two zones. It will take time
\mathcal{O}(|\textit{Var}(Z)|^{2})
to compute each
N^{\prime}_{r}
(detailed in Appendix
A
). Also, the number of mappings
r
could be exponential in
|\textit{Var}(Z)|
as it is bounded by the number of subsets of size
|\textit{Var}(Z)|
in
\textit{Var}(Z^{\prime})
, which is given by
{|\textit{Var}(Z^{\prime})|}\choose{|\textit{Var}(Z)|}
. The overall complexity is
\mathcal{O}({{|\textit{Var}(Z^{\prime})|}\choose{|\textit{Var}(Z)|}}\cdot|\textit{Var}(Z)|^{2})
. In practice, one can envisage an algorithm that enumerates each location preserving
r
, and keeps the intersection of
N^{\prime}_{r}
among all the
r
enumerated so far. This intersection will be a union of zones. If at some point, the intersection becomes empty, the algorithm can stop. Otherwise, the algorithm continues until all location-preserving maps are considered.

We now focus on getting a lower bound for the non-entailment check. Let
V=\{p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n}\}
be a set of propositional variables, taking values either
\top
(true) or
\bot
(false). An assignment fixes a value to each of the variables. A positive literal is an element of
L_{+}=V
and a negative literal is an element of
L_{-}=\{\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2},\dots,\bar{p}_{n}\}
. When
p_{i}=\top
, the literal
\bar{p}_{i}=\bot
and vice-versa. We denote by
L:=L_{+}\cup L_{-}
, the set of all literals. A
3
-clause (hereafter called simply a clause)
C
is a disjunction of
three
literals. The clause is said to be monotone if either all its literals are positive, or all its literals are negative:
C\subseteq L_{+}
or
C\subseteq L_{-}
. A boolean formula in
3
-CNF form is a conjunction of clauses:
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{i=m}C_{i}
. A
3
-CNF formula is monotone if each of its clauses is monotone. The
MONOTONE-3-SAT
problem: given a monotone
3
-CNF formula, is it satisfiable? This problem is known to be
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
[
25
]
.

Let
1-ATA-Zone-Non-Entailment
be the decision problem which takes as input two nodes
(Z,\operatorname{IA})
,
(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
, and a constant
\mathsf{M}
, and checks if
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\not\sqsubseteq(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
, where
\sqsubseteq
makes use of the region equivalence w.r.t
\mathsf{M}
.

Theorem 9
.

1-ATA-Zone-Non-Entailment
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

To prove the theorem, we present a reduction from

5.3.
Entailment for bounded width 1-ATAs

Suppose we start with a 1-ATA
\mathcal{A}
with width
k
. Then in the zone graph computation as explained in Section
4
, every variable name
x_{q,i}
will have index
i\leq k
. For each state
q
, there are at most
k
variables in the zone (along with potentially an
x_{q,0}
in the inactive part). In such a scenario, we can use a modified entailment check that only compares zones with the same set of variable names and thereby avoids the costly identification of subsets.

Let
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
be two zones such that
\textit{Var}(Z)=\textit{Var}(Z^{\prime})
. Let
\iota:\textit{Var}(Z)\to\textit{Var}(Z^{\prime})
be the identity mapping. Let
\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}^{\iota}
be the equivalence of Definition
5
with
h
being the identity function
\iota
. So,
\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}^{\iota}
is simply the classical region equivalence of
[
5
]
. This can be naturally lifted to zones:
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\sqsubseteq^{b}_{\mathsf{M}}(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
if
\operatorname{IA}\subseteq\operatorname{IA}^{\prime}
and for every
\gamma^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
there is some
\gamma\in Z
such that
\gamma\simeq^{\iota}_{\mathsf{M}}\gamma^{\prime}
. The check
\sqsubseteq^{b}
is identical to a simulation test
\preccurlyeq_{\mathsf{M}}
used in the timed automata literature
[
28
]
, but with the direction reversed:
(Z,\operatorname{IA})\sqsubseteq^{b}_{\mathsf{M}}(Z^{\prime},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
iff
\operatorname{IA}\subseteq\operatorname{IA}^{\prime}
and
Z^{\prime}\preccurlyeq_{\mathsf{M}}Z
. This test can be done with quadratic complexity and is known to be a well-quasi-order on zones with a fixed number of variables.

Notice that
\sqsubseteq^{b}
implies
\sqsubseteq
, but not the other way around. Therefore, the zone graph obtained by
\sqsubseteq^{b}
is correct. Notice that using
\sqsubseteq
may lead to more pruning, in principle. However, the entailment check
\sqsubseteq^{b}
is efficient. Moreover, as there are at most
|Q|\times k
number of variables generated, and the relation
\sqsubseteq^{b}
induces a well-quasi order for each
j\leq|Q|\times k
, the overall graph will terminate. This is because if not, there is an infinite path in the zone graph with an infinite subsequence of zones over the same set of variables, which is non-increasing w.r.t
\sqsubseteq^{b}_{\mathsf{M}}
, which is a contradiction.

6.
Extending the zone graph for the model-checking problem

We come back to the model-checking problem: given a timed automaton model
\mathcal{A}
and a specification given as a
1
-ATA
\mathcal{B}
, check whether
L(\mathcal{A})\subseteq L(\mathcal{B})
. This problem is known to be decidable for finite words (
[
38
]
), and can be reduced to checking emptiness of
L(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}^{c})
, where
\mathcal{B}^{c}
is the complement of
\mathcal{B}
, also a 1-ATA. In this section, we develop a zone graph computation for the product
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a timed automaton and
\mathcal{B}
is a 1-ATA. Observe that this subsumes model-checking MTL specifications against timed automata models, since MTL specifications can be converted to 1-ATAs (Section
3
). The plan of this section is as follows.

(1)

We formally define the semantics of a product
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
where
\mathcal{A}
is a timed automaton and
\mathcal{B}
is a
1
-ATA.

(2)

We define a zone graph for
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
and extend the entailment
\sqsubseteq
to this
compound zone graph
.

(3)

When
\mathcal{B}
has a bounded width, the number of variables generated in the zone graph of
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
is bounded and the modified entailment
\sqsubseteq_{b}
(with quadratic complexity) can be adapted for this compound zone graph.

(4)

Finally, we consider the case when
\mathcal{A}
is strongly non-Zeno, that is, there is a number
k
such that every
k
consecutive actions of
\mathcal{A}
incurs a duration of at least
1
time unit. When
\mathcal{A}
is strongly non-Zeno, we observe that the number of variables within the maximum constant
\mathsf{M_{A}}
is bounded. Using this observation, we propose a new entailment relation for the compound zone graph of
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
that has a quadratic complexity – essentially, the entailment uses
\sqsubseteq_{b}
for the variables within
\mathsf{M_{A}}
and does a simple constant time check for the variables above
\mathsf{M_{A}}
.

We recall the definition and semantics of timed automata
[
5
]
that we will use in this section:

{defi}
[Timed Automata]
A timed automaton (TA) is given by a tuple
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,Y,q_{0},F,\delta)
, where
Q
is a finite set of locations,
q_{0}\in Q
and
F\subseteq Q
are the initial and final locations respectively,
Y
is a finite set of clocks, and the transition relation
\delta\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times\Phi(Y)\times\mathcal{P}(Y)\times Q
, with
\Phi(Y)
being the set of constraints generated by
\varphi:=y\sim c\mid\varphi\land\varphi
for
\sim\mathord{\in}\{<,\leq,>,\geq\}
,
c\in\mathbb{N}
, and
y\in Y
, and
\mathcal{P}(Y)
is a set of clocks. A transition (which we sometimes call an
edge
) of
\mathcal{A}
is of the form
e=(q,a,g,R,q^{\prime})
where
g\in\Phi(Y)
is called a guard and
R\subseteq Y
is the set of clocks that are reset.

Given a TA
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,Y,q_{0},F,\delta)
with
Y=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\}
, a clock valuation on
\mathcal{A}
is a vector
\bar{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\dots,\nu_{m})
where each
\nu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Given
d\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
R\subseteq Y
,
\bar{\nu}+d=(\nu_{1}+d,\dots,\nu_{m}+d)
and
[R]\bar{\nu}=\bar{\nu}^{\prime}
such that
\nu^{\prime}_{i}=0
if
y_{i}\in R
,
\nu^{\prime}_{i}=\nu_{i}
otherwise. Given a clock valuation
\bar{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{m})
and guard
g
,
\bar{\nu}\models g
if replacing
y_{i}
with
\nu_{i}
satisfies the constraints of
g
.

We fix a TA
\mathcal{A}=(Q_{A},\Sigma,Y,q_{A},F_{A},\delta_{A})
with
m
clocks and maximum constant
\mathsf{M_{A}}
, and a 1-ATA
\mathcal{B}=(Q_{B},\Sigma,q_{B},F_{B},\delta_{B})
with maximum constant
\mathsf{M_{B}}
for the rest of this section. We say the maximum constant of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
combined is
\mathsf{M}=\max(\mathsf{M_{A}},\mathsf{M_{B}})
.

{defi}

[Compound Configurations]
A compound configuration is of the form
\Gamma=(p,\bar{\nu},\gamma)
, where
p\in Q_{A}
is a location of
\mathcal{A}
,
\bar{\nu}
is a clock valuation of
\mathcal{A}
, and
\gamma=\{(q_{1},v_{1}),\dots,(q_{n},v_{n})\}
is a configuration of
\mathcal{B}
. A compound configuration is accepting if
p\in F_{A}
and
\gamma
is an accepting configuration of
\mathcal{B}
.

We will define two kinds of transitions for
\Gamma
:

(1)

Timed Transitions: for
d\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we define
\Gamma+d
as
(p,\bar{\nu}+d,\gamma+d)
, and we add edge
\Gamma\xrightarrow{d}\Gamma+d
for all
d\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

(2)

Discrete Transitions: for
a\in\Sigma
, an edge
e=(p,a,g,R,p^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{A}
, and a combination
C=(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})
where each
C_{i}
is a disjunct of
\delta_{B}(q_{i},a)
, we add an edge
\Gamma\xrightarrow{a,e,C}\Gamma^{\prime}
, where
\Gamma^{\prime}=\{p^{\prime},\bar{\nu}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\}
, if
\gamma\xrightarrow{a,C}\gamma^{\prime}
,
\bar{\nu}\models g
, and
\bar{\nu}^{\prime}=[R]\bar{y}
.

The semantics is given by a
compound configuration graph
where the initial node is
\Gamma_{0}=(q_{A},\bar{\nu_{0}},\gamma_{0})
where
\bar{\nu_{0}}=(0,\dots,0)
and
\gamma_{0}=\{(q_{B},0)\}
and the successor computation is as defined above. A run on this graph for a timed word
w=(d_{1},a_{1})\dots(d_{n},a_{n})
is a sequence of transitions of the form
\Gamma_{0}\xrightarrow{d_{1}}\Gamma^{\prime}_{0}\xrightarrow{a_{1},e_{1},C^{1}}\dots\xrightarrow{a_{n},e_{n},C^{n}}\Gamma_{n}
, where it is an accepting run if
\Gamma_{n}
is an accepting compound configuration. By our transitions, it is easy to see that a word will have an accepting run on both TA
\mathcal{A}
and 1-ATA
\mathcal{B}
if and only if the word has an accepting run in the compound configuration graph. Checking emptiness of
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
reduces to checking whether the compound configuration graph as an accepting run.

6.1.
Compound zone graph

We can now define the extended zone graph. We again assume that we have an infinite supply
Var
of variable names
x_{q,1},\dots,x_{q,i}
for each
q\in Q_{B}
, and for a variable
x_{q,i}
, define
\operatorname{loc}(x_{q,i})=q
. In the rest of the section, we will use both
Var
(the set of variables) and
Y
(the set of clocks of
\mathcal{A}
) in the definitions. Let us remark that we will refer to the elements of
Var
as variables and the elements of
Y
as clocks.

{defi}

A compound zone
Z_{AB}
is a conjunction of constraints of the form
(x\sim k)
or
(x-z\sim k)
where
\sim\mathord{\in}\{<,\leq,\geq,>\}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
x,z\in\textit{Var}~\cup~Y
. We will define
\textit{Var}(Z_{AB})\subseteq\textit{Var}
as the set of variables appearing in
Z_{AB}
and
\operatorname{loc-sign}(Z_{AB})=\operatorname{loc-sign}(\textit{Var}(Z_{AB}))
.

A node in the zone graph of
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
is of the form
N=(p,Z_{AB},\operatorname{IA})
where
p\in Q_{A}
is a location of
\mathcal{A}
,
Z_{AB}
is a compound zone, and
\operatorname{IA}\subseteq\{x_{q,0}\mid q\text{ is a location of }\mathcal{B}\}
is the set of inactive variables. For a node
N=(p,Z_{AB},\operatorname{IA})
, we say
\textit{Var}(N)=\textit{Var}(Z_{AB})\cup\operatorname{IA}
and
\operatorname{loc-sign}(N)=\operatorname{loc-sign}(Z_{AB})\cup\operatorname{loc-sign}(\operatorname{IA})
.
A node
N=(p,Z_{AB},\operatorname{IA})
is accepting if
p\in F_{A}
and for every
x\in\textit{Var}(N)
,
\operatorname{loc}(x)\in F_{B}
.

We can now naturally extend the definition of a compound configuration satisfying a node. Given a node
N=(p,Z_{AB},\operatorname{IA})
, we say that a compound configuration
\Gamma=(p^{\prime},\bar{\nu},\gamma)
satisfies
N
, written as
\Gamma\models N
, if
p=p^{\prime}
and there exists a surjection
h:\textit{Var}(N)\mapsto\gamma
such that (i) for every
x\in\textit{Var}(N)
,
\operatorname{loc}(x)=\operatorname{loc}(h(x))
, (ii) for every
x_{q,0}\in\operatorname{IA}
,
\textit{val}(h(x_{q,0}))=\bot
, and (iii) replacing every clock in
Y
with the respective clock value in
\bar{\nu}
and every variable
x\in\textit{Var}(Z_{AB})
with
\textit{val}(h(x))
satisfies all the constraints of
Z_{AB}
. We will define
\llbracket N\rrbracket
as the set of compound configurations
\{\Gamma\mid\Gamma\models N\}
.

Computing Successors.

To compute successors of such compound nodes, we build on the successor computation of Section
4
. We need to now include a target outgoing edge for the TA
\mathcal{A}
along with the set of disjuncts we select as target for
\mathcal{B}
. Assuming
N=(p,Z_{AB},\operatorname{IA})
is a non-empty node with
\textit{Var}(Z_{AB})=\{x_{q_{1},i_{1}},\dots,x_{q_{k},i_{k}}\}
(indices 1 to
k
) and
\operatorname{IA}=\{x_{q_{k+1},0},\dots,x_{q_{n},0}\}
(indices
k+1
to
n
), we extend the definition of the target set for an action
a\in\Sigma
as follows, where
e
is an edge of
\mathcal{A}
of the form
(p,a,g,R,p^{\prime})
:

target(N,a)=\{(e,(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})\mid C_{j}\text{ is a disjunct in }\delta_{B}(q_{j},a)\text{ for }1\leq j\leq n)\}

Picking one
(e,(C_{1},\dots,C_{n}))
from this set where no
C_{j}
contains
\operatorname{\textbf{false}}
, the time elapse computation remains the same as before. For the guard intersection, we first compute
Z_{AB}\cap g
, and on the resulting node, we perform the guard intersection step in the successor computation of Section
4
using the target
(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})
, to get a node
(p,Z^{2}_{AB},\operatorname{IA}_{2})
.
The computation to reset and move to new variables will remain unchanged on the
\mathcal{B}
variables, and additional constraints
\bigwedge_{y^{\prime}\in R}(y^{\prime}=0)
will be added to the computed zone. After the canonicalization and removing old variables to compute
Z^{\prime}_{AB}
and
\operatorname{IA}^{\prime}
, the successor node will finally be
(p^{\prime},Z^{\prime}_{AB},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
.

We can now define the compound zone graph. The initial node will now be
N_{0}=(q_{A},Z^{0}_{AB},\emptyset)
where
Z^{0}_{AB}
is a zone with constraints
\bigwedge_{y\in Y}(y=0)\land(x_{q_{B},1}=0)
, and the successor nodes are computed as defined above. We can see the following:

Theorem 10
.

The compound zone graph is sound and complete.

Proof 6.1
.

The proof for this will be very similar to the 1-ATA zone graph proofs for Lemma
3
and Theorem
4
, and therefore the full proof is omitted here. The key difference is that for this case, the zone graph computation also has to incorporate the transition edges taken by the TA
\mathcal{A}
, i.e. that the guard constraints and resets are incorporated in the successor node. Since we ensure the guard constraints are satisfied by all variables and clocks in a successor node, the successor node will contain exactly those compound configurations that can, after some time delay, satisfy the guard of an edge along with the intervals involving the transitions of
\mathcal{B}
.

Extending the entailment relation

Similar to the 1-ATA zone graph, this zone graph can also be potentially infinite, so we still require a way to prune the zone graph. We will do so by extending the entailment relation for this case.

{defi}

[Flattening compound configurations]
Let
\Gamma=(p,\bar{\nu},\gamma)
be a compound configuration with
\bar{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{m})
. We define
\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}
to be a configuration
\gamma\cup\{(p^{i},\nu_{i})\mid 1\leq i\leq m\}
where
p^{i}
is a fictitious location, created as a copy of location
p
for clock
i
.

{defi}

For compound configurations
\Gamma=(p,\bar{\nu},\gamma)
and
\Gamma^{\prime}=(p^{\prime},\bar{\nu}^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})
, we say
\Gamma\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}\Gamma^{\prime}
if their flattened configurations are equivalent, that is
\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}\simeq_{\mathsf{M}}\Gamma^{\prime}_{\operatorname{flat}}
according to Definition
5
. Secondly, we say
\Gamma\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}\Gamma^{\prime}
if
\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}\Gamma^{\prime}_{\operatorname{flat}}
.

This definition can be extended to compound nodes: we say that node
(p,Z_{AB},\operatorname{IA})\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}(p^{\prime},Z^{\prime}_{AB},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
if for every
\Gamma^{\prime}\in(p,Z^{\prime}_{AB},\operatorname{IA}^{\prime})
, there is some
\Gamma\in(p,Z_{AB},\operatorname{IA})
such that
\Gamma\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}\Gamma^{\prime}
. The algorithm from Section
5
can be directly adapted to compute the current entailment for the compound zone graph.

6.2.
Bounded cases for model checking

We now look at restrictions on
\mathcal{A}
and

that will give us a compound zone graph with a global bound on the variables (and clocks) used in every reachable node. We can notice straightaway that because there is a constant number of clocks

to be a 1-ATA with width

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a timed automaton with

in the compound zone graph of

is a zone over atmost

We can use the same reasoning as in Section

to argue that the more efficient entailment check

is sufficient here too. We now look at a restriction of
\mathcal{A}
that will lead to a similar situation. We recall the definition of strongly non-zeno timed automata below.

{defi}

A timed automaton

is strongly non-zeno if there exists a constant

such that for any run of
\mathcal{A}
with length
\geq k

Restricting the TA

to be strongly non-zeno does not directly ensure that the zone graph for
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}
has a bounded number of variables, but we can notice the following.

Consider a node
N=(p,Z_{AB},\operatorname{IA})
and a variable

is
irrelevant
in

if in
every

is strictly bigger than

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a TA which is strongly non-Zeno with a constant

. Let

be an arbitrary 1-ATA. Then, in any reachable node

in the compound zone graph of

where

is the set of states of
\mathcal{B}

Proof 6.2
.

Let
Y
be the set of clocks of
\mathcal{A}
.
We first remark that every reachable zone

is
totally ordered

, the constraints of
Z_{AB}
implies

or
z\leq x
. The ordering reflects the order of resets along the path leading to

atom in

We will show that for any reachable node

of the zone graph, there are at most

relevant variables in

. For convenience, we will assume that the indices of variables of

are ordered according to the ordering of their values:
x_{q,i}\leq x_{q,j}

Now, given a

of the form

for

with

along the path from the initial node

to the current node

>\mathsf{M}

Using the above lemma, we see that in the zone graph, however the number of variables might grow in the graph, there are only a bounded number of them relevant to us with respect to the entailment relation. Given a compound zone

Lemma 13
.

Given two nodes

and

\textit{bd}(N)\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}\textit{bd}(N^{\prime})

\textit{ubd}(N)\subseteq\textit{ubd}(N^{\prime})

Proof 6.3
.

Let
\Gamma\in N
and
\Gamma^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}
. We inherit the definition of

and

at the configuration level in a natural way. We will now show that:

\displaystyle\Gamma\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}\Gamma^{\prime}\text{ iff }\textit{bd}(\Gamma)\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}\textit{bd}(\Gamma^{\prime})\text{ and }\textit{ubd}(\Gamma)\subseteq\textit{ubd}(\Gamma^{\prime})

(2)

Notice that proving the above statement completes the proof of the lemma.

Let us now prove (
2

From Definition

\displaystyle\Gamma\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}\Gamma^{\prime}\text{ iff }\Gamma_{\operatorname{flat}}\sqsubseteq_{\mathsf{M}}\Gamma^{\prime}_{\operatorname{flat}}

As the final observation, as the number of relevant variables in every reachable node is bounded (Lemma

is strongly non-Zeno.

7.
Conclusion

In timed automata literature, 1-ATAs have played the role of an excellent technical device for logic-to-automata translations. Perhaps, owing to the high general complexity, there have been no attempts at aligning the analysis of 1-ATAs to the well developed timed automata algorithms. Typically, 1-ATAs are converted to equivalent network of timed automata, with a blowup in the control states. Our aim in this paper is to pass on the message that these conversions of 1-ATAs to TAs may not really be needed and instead one could embed the analysis of 1-ATAs into the current timed automata algorithms. To substantiate this claim, we have given a zone-based emptiness algorithm for 1-ATAs. We have also shown how we can lift it to a model-checking algorithm, by providing a zone graph for the product of a timed automaton
\mathcal{A}
with a 1-ATA
\mathcal{B}
. When
\mathcal{A}
is strongly non-Zeno or when
\mathcal{B}
has a bounded-width, the zone graph computation uses efficient operations, with the same complexity as in timed automata. We have also demonstrated a logical fragment of MTL which induces bounded width 1-ATAs. One important idea that we wish to highlight is the addition of an explicit deactivation operation for 1-ATAs. This is a simple trick that can have a substantial impact on the analysis.

We believe that the theoretical foundations we lay here pave the way for studying further optimizations, implementing the ideas and understanding the practical impact. As part of future work, it would be interesting to study whether the best known simulation techniques for timed automata
[
13
]
can be incorporated into the 1-ATA zone graphs. What about ATAs with multiple clocks? They are undecidable, of course. But, are there good restrictions that make the modeling more succinct and still enable a zone-based analysis? It would be interesting to extend the notion of boundedness on multi-clock ATAs and find out if there are fragments of MTL (MITL for instance) that are expressible by it. This study also motivates the question of understanding zone-based liveness algorithms for ATAs, in particular, bounded-width ATAs.
Another direction is to explore the extensive literature based on antichain algorithms studied for alternating finite automata emptiness, in the real-time setting. Here is another natural problem that we leave open: given a 1-ATA
\mathcal{A}
and a constant
k
, decide whether
\mathcal{A}
has width
k
?

,

Lemma 18
.

\varphi
is satisfiable iff

Z_{\varphi}\not\sqsubseteq Z^{\prime}_{\varphi}
.

Proof A.5
.

To prove one direction, we assume that
\varphi
is satisfiable, and
\alpha
is a satisfying assignment of the variables of
\varphi
. From Lemma
17
, we know that there is some
\gamma^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}_{\varphi}
such that
\alpha_{\gamma^{\prime}}=\alpha
. Now, if there was some
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\gamma^{\prime}
such that
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\in Z_{\varphi}
, then from Lemma
16
, we get that
\alpha_{\gamma^{\prime}}
falsifies at least one clause in
\varphi
, which is a contradiction to the fact that
\alpha
was a satisfying assignment. Thus no such
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
exists, and for every
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\gamma^{\prime}
,
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\not\in Z_{\varphi}
, meaning
Z_{\varphi}\not\sqsubseteq Z^{\prime}_{\varphi}
. To prove the other direction, we assume that
Z_{\varphi}\not\sqsubseteq Z^{\prime}_{\varphi}
. This means there is some
\gamma^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}_{\varphi}
such that for every
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\gamma^{\prime}
,
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\not\in Z_{\varphi}
. Now, we claim that the corresponding
\alpha_{\gamma^{\prime}}
is in fact a satisfying assignment of
\varphi
. To prove this, we first see that by Lemma
14
,
\alpha_{\gamma^{\prime}}
is a valid assignment. To prove it is a satisfying assignment, we assume on the contrary that
\alpha_{\gamma^{\prime}}
is not a satisfying assignment, meaning it falsifies at least one clause of
\varphi
. This means by Lemma
16
, there is some
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\gamma^{\prime}
such that
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\in Z_{\varphi}
, which is a contradiction. Hence
\alpha_{\gamma^{\prime}}
is a satisfying assignment, and so
\varphi
is satisfiable.
