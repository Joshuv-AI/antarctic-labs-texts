Towards an ethos of donor-funding responsive to the needs of the SRHR movement
Authors: TK Sundari Ravindran
Published: 2024

This special issue of SRHM on “Donor funding for SRHR advocacy and movement-building” is a part of the platform for knowledge exchange and thinking space initiated by SRHM in collaboration with AmplifyChange.* The platform was motivated by the complex and changing funding landscape for SRHR advocacy and movement-building and aimed to initiate a dialogue between diverse stakeholders on how SRHR funding can better support advocacy efforts and movement-building across the world. The special issue comes at a time when sexual and reproductive rights are under threat from restrictive laws and policies, and the ultraconservative mobilisation against “gender ideology”.

The context – the need for a resilient SRHR movement

The term “advocacy” is broadly understood as “calling for change and guiding decision makers towards solutions”, and involves “building political, financial or public support to bring about action for change”.1 Advocacy and movement-building have been central to putting sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR) on the global agenda. It was through the sustained collective advocacy at various levels, from the grassroots to multilateral forums, led by feminist and women's rights organisations, that support for SRHR was won at the International Conference for Population and Development (ICPD) in Cairo in 1994. Since 1994, issue-specific advocacy on abortion, family planning and violence against women has gained momentum. Several waves of advocacy by movements and organisations have put on the agenda the rights, including sexual and reproductive rights, of persons living with HIV and AIDS, LGBTQI persons, sex workers and persons living with a disability, among others. Advocates for the wide range of SRHR issues come from different sectors. Lawyers, policymakers, managers, service providers, judiciary – have all been involved in making changes in SRHR happen. SRHR advocacy efforts have been directed at multiple levels: global, regional, national, and local, with or without interconnections. For example, at the global level, there have been advocacy attempts to influence international policies via multilateral agencies and forums such as UN Treaty Monitoring Bodies, resulting in strong global human rights standards. Financial commitments to advance gender equality have been made by initiatives such as the Generation Equality Campaign,2 while the Sustainable Development Goals have centre-staged gender equality and sexual and reproductive rights.3 At the national level, advocacy efforts have used strategic litigation at courts, lobbying at parliaments, and multi-level movement building and coalition work, to mention only a few. In many countries, these efforts have shifted population control-based policies to rights-based family planning agendas, and restrictive abortion laws have been liberalised. Advances have been made in some countries in recognising the rights of sex workers and trans persons and in decriminalising same-sex sexual conduct. Grassroots efforts have empowered women and girls and marginalised populations to know and claim their human rights, including sexual and reproductive rights.

At the same time, sexual and reproductive rights continue to be threatened. In many countries, legislative, constitutional, and judicial processes are being used to restrict SRHR, including access to safe abortion and recognition of LGBTQI equality and rights. Anti-gender politics has become the hallmark of right-wing populism, which is rising in many parts of the world. In addition, anti-abortion and anti-LGBTQI rights policies and movements have gained opportunities to grow and thrive globally and locally. The space for civil society mobilisation and advocacy is shrinking in many settings, with varied manifestations, such as legally prohibiting the reception of foreign funding in India and adopting laws that restrict the freedom of association and the freedom of speech in Hungary and Russia. Many UN forums are witnessing conservative opposition to gender equality and SRHR, compromising the adoption of comprehensive SRHR agendas. The recent reversal of Roe vs. Wade by the US Supreme Court in 2022 represents another major setback to SRHR.

The need of the hour is for a resilient SRHR movement that fights back to regain lost ground. Adequate funding is a prerequisite for this to be possible. However, we know relatively little about the sources, extent, and nature of funding for SRHR advocacy and movement-building and the challenges therein. The articles in this special issue represent a first attempt at presenting the perspectives of advocates and donors on the challenges involved in mobilising funding for SRHR movement-building and advocacy. They also provide suggestions for what needs to happen to alter this situation.

We identified potential authors to write papers for this issue through a consultative process involving interviews with civil society organisations and donor representatives. Recognising the limited academic research on this subject, this special issue goes beyond the regular range of journal article types and includes an interview and a roundtable article.

Two virtual roundtable dialogues, organised by SRHM with AmplifyChange, were held in September 2022. These were designed to create a space for diverse stakeholders to listen to and better understand each other's perspectives. These perspectives were then documented in a roundtable article.

The papers in this issue: exploring the complexities of donor-funding from movement and donor perspectives

Six of the seven articles in this issue cover two broad areas. The first is about the SRHR advocacy funding landscape. These articles lay out the challenges in mobilising donor resources, and the responses to these challenges on the part of donors and women's rights organisations. The second set of articles explores new methodological developments in the assessment of advocacy efforts. The roundtable article covers multiple issues related to donor funding for SRHR advocacy from the perspectives of donors and SRHR advocates and activists.

The issue opens with an article by Alex le May and colleagues from AmplifyChange, a non-profit organisation committed to supporting SRHR advocacy and activist groups in the global South. This article is among the first to provide concrete evidence beyond anecdotes on the struggle for sustained and flexible SRHR advocacy funding in lower- and middle-income countries (LMICs). His paper reports on an Amplify Change study exploring the funding sources for SRHR advocacy that their LMIC partners rely on and their experiences with alternatives to grant funding from high-income country donors.

With demand far outstripping supply, the funding landscape leaves smaller organisations led by structurally excluded groups vulnerable to donor pressures. Lisa Lamont Adams's article centres on the perspectives of activists with disabilities from the global South on donor funding for their intersectional activism. Highlighting the lack of prioritisation given to disability by SRHR donors and the SRHR movement, the article puts forth constructive suggestions to both constituencies for a more equitable allocation of resources for SRHR advocacy to address the needs of structurally excluded groups.

This issue also features two articles on advocacy and action for funding women's rights organisations engaged in local activism and movement building. The first is by colleagues from Women's Fund Asia, and the second is based on an interview with the feminist, movement-support organisation Association for Women's Rights in Development (AWID). We hope these articles will inspire work to promote resource justice for SRHR advocacy and movement-building.

Women's Funds constitute a unique subgroup of donors whose origins lay in the pressing need to create a focused pool of funds for supporting women's rights organisations, many of whose work includes SRHR advocacy. Starting with Mama Cash (1983) and the Global Fund for Women (1987), international and regional Women's Funds now number close to two hundred. Women's Funds are often both fundraisers and donors committed to supporting and strengthening local women's rights groups and movements by providing them with financial and other resources. Many have expanded their mandate to support organisations representing sexual and gender minorities, sex workers and persons with disabilities.4 In their article for this special issue, Chugh and Gaind of Women's Fund Asia provide an overview of Women's Fund Asia's philosophy and strategies for feminist funding and resource justice advocacy and their efforts to provide sustained and flexible financing to support spontaneous activism and long-term movement-building.

AWID is well known for its pioneering and evidence-based advocacy for better resourcing women’s rights. AWID’s Resourcing Feminist Movements (RFM) Initiative has contributed significantly to evidence-building on the donor funding ecosystem for gender equality. The Initiative has highlighted the problems and outlined solutions for how donors can better serve women's rights movements. In an interview with SRHM, Cindy Clark (former co-executive director, AWID), Kasia Staszewska (Resourcing Feminist Movements Manager, AWID), and Tenzin Dolker (Resourcing Feminists Movements Lead, AWID) talk about the achievements of the RFM initiative as well as the challenges it has encountered. They also reflect on the potential lessons the SRHR movement can draw from AWID's work and build on it.

Advocacy and movement-building are inherently dynamic, uncertain, and long-term processes that depend on political and social movements and global and local political, economic, and developmental factors. These characteristics of advocacy and movement-building work could deter funding by many donor agencies who tend to adopt short funding cycles and short-term result-oriented evaluation frameworks to assess progress and successes. The complexities of assessing progress in advocacy and movement-building have fuelled innovative work on a range of possible approaches and techniques for advocacy monitoring and evaluation.5–7 However, very few of these have an SRHR focus. Barbara Klugman's 2011 article in Reproductive Health Matters (RHM) is an exception.8 Drawing on lessons learned from her involvement in advocacy for reproductive rights during the transition from apartheid to democracy in South Africa, which achieved policy wins and suffered implementation failures, Klugman proposed broad outcome categories against which to assess progress in advocacy efforts (or lack of), in an ongoing manner. SRHM is happy to include in this issue Klugman's reflections on contemporary advances in advocacy evaluation applicable to SRHR and, more broadly, social justice advocacy initiatives. Another article by Alex le May highlights the value of prospective evaluation methodology to monitor SRHR advocacy, with illustrative examples from two African countries.

Many issues and voices remain unrepresented in this special issue. The roundtable article seeks to remedy these lacunae to some extent. The article brings together the voices of current and past donors and advocates from SRHR and related movements across generations from around the globe. It focuses on the three main themes of the dialogue:

The dynamics of funding for SRHR advocacy and movement building;

The dynamics of funding for SRHR advocacy and movement building;

Assessing/measuring progress and success in advocacy and movement-building: discussing experiences of donors and SRHR organisations; and

Assessing/measuring progress and success in advocacy and movement-building: discussing experiences of donors and SRHR organisations; and

What needs to happen to ensure better donor support for SRHR advocacy and movement-building: perspectives from the SRHR movement and the donor community.

What needs to happen to ensure better donor support for SRHR advocacy and movement-building: perspectives from the SRHR movement and the donor community.

Besides providing valuable insights from experienced donors, activists/advocates, and those who have been on both sides of the table, the article lays out concrete and useful suggestions to promote donor support for SRHR advocacy and movement-building, including monitoring and evaluation and more effective mechanisms for mutual accountability.

Future directions for research and action

We are at a crucial historical juncture wherein a strong SRHR movement is needed to regain lost ground and take firm steps forward. It is time for focused attention on research and action that would enable us to do so.

Our search for articles on this issue made us realise how little has been researched and written on donor funding for SRHR advocacy and movement-building. Organisations such as AWID have undertaken pioneering research in mapping the extent, sources, and recipients of funding for women's rights, and more broadly the funding ecosystem. There is an urgent need for similar research on funding for sexual and reproductive health and rights. Another research priority is on documenting the many advocacy strategies used by SRHR activists at different levels, and the mechanisms and processes used for assessing progress in advocacy. More detailed accounts of the kind of prospective research undertaken by AmplifyChange grantees would prove valuable to SRHR advocates everywhere. It would be especially important to hear the voices of those advocating for the rights of marginalised communities about these issues.

SRHR advocates and activists have specific “asks” from donors. Donors who support SRHR should demonstrate the political will to make substantial, long-term investments towards SRHR advocacy and movement-building. Donors should also explore ways of overcoming bureaucratic hurdles, reforming ways of doing business and rethinking strategies that can lead to a more equitable, harmonised, and strategic distribution of funding.

There is need for change also within the SRHR movement. The movement is divided across issues and constituencies, which has weakened its impact. There is an urgent need for leadership within the movement that is inclusive and brings together groups in a spirit of cooperation rather than competition. Working together, the movement will have the ability to influence donor agendas, hold them accountable, and mobilise resources that benefit the movement as a whole.
