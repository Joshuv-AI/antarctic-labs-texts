A hierarchical splitting approach for N-split differential equations Thanks: Submitted to the editors August 10, 2026.
Kevin Schäfers, Michael Günther

Abstract
We propose a hierarchical splitting approach to differential equations that provides a design principle for constructing splitting methods for N N -split systems by iteratively applying splitting methods for two-split systems. We analyze the convergence order, derive explicit formulas for the leading-order error terms, and investigate self-adjointness. Moreover, we discuss compositions of hierarchical splitting methods in detail. We further augment the hierarchical splitting approach with multiple time-stepping techniques, turning the class into a promising framework at the intersection of geometric numerical integration and multirate integration. In this context, we characterize the computational order of a multirate integrator and establish conditions on the multirate factors that guarantee an increased convergence rate in practical computations up to a certain step size. Finally, we design several hierarchical splitting methods and perform numerical simulations for rigid body equations and a separable Hamiltonian system with multirate potential, confirming the theoretical findings and showcasing the computational efficiency of hierarchical splitting methods.

Splitting methods, Geometric numerical integration, Multirate integration.

Introduction

In many technical applications, such as in multi-physics problems, initial value problems (IVPs) of
N
-split
ordinary differential equations (ODEs) of the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\mathbf{f}^{\{m\}}(\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{0},

(1)

arise.
In (
1
), the right-hand side is split additively into
N\geq 2
partitions, based on characteristics such as stiffness, nonlinearity, dimension, dynamical behavior, and computational cost.
In such cases, it is advantageous to employ splitting approaches to numerically integrate the individual partitions by utilizing dedicated methods.
For instance, a splitting into stiff and non-stiff parts enables the numerical treatment with implicit-explicit (IMEX) methods
[
2
]
.

Several techniques already exist that allow the simultaneous use of dedicated numerical integration schemes for (
1
).
For instance, in the Runge–Kutta (RK) framework, there are partitioned Runge-Kutta (PRK) methods
[
18
]
, additive RK (ARK) methods
[
12
,
21
]
, and generalized ARK (GARK) methods
[
28
]
.
Another promising approach constitutes the class of splitting methods
[
23
,
9
]
.
In contrast to RK-based methods, splitting methods are not limited to RK methods for numerical integration of the subsystems.
On the other hand, the RK-based frameworks also allow for coupling subsystems for a sub-step which may result in superior stability properties of the overall numerical scheme, as it is done for example in the compound step approach
[
14
]
in multirate time integration.
If the flows of all subsystems are approximated via RK methods, a splitting method can be formulated as a GARK method.
This subclass of GARK methods is also known as fractional-step RK (FSRK) methods
[
35
]
.

In addition to numerous works on splitting methods for two-split systems, there are also some works that deal with splitting methods for
N>2
partitions, see e.g.
[
34
,
36
]
.
Given a step size
h>0
, the Lie–Trotter splitting
[
39
]

\Phi_{h}=\varphi_{h}^{\{2\}}\circ\varphi_{h}^{\{1\}},

(2)

and the Strang splitting
[
37
]

\Phi_{h}=\varphi_{h/2}^{\{1\}}\circ\varphi_{h}^{\{2\}}\circ\varphi_{h/2}^{\{1\}},

(3)

can be generalized to
N\geq 2
partitions, resulting in the methods

\Phi_{h}=\varphi_{h}^{\{N\}}\circ\ldots\circ\varphi_{h}^{\{2\}}\circ\varphi_{h}^{\{1\}},

(4)

and

\Phi_{h}=\varphi_{h/2}^{\{1\}}\circ\varphi_{h/2}^{\{2\}}\circ\ldots\circ\varphi_{h/2}^{\{N-1\}}\circ\varphi_{h}^{\{N\}}\circ\varphi_{h/2}^{\{N-1\}}\circ\ldots\circ\varphi_{h/2}^{\{2\}}\circ\varphi_{h/2}^{\{1\}}.

(5)

These methods became natural choices for
N
-split systems.
Based on the methods (
4
) and (
5
), we can derive splitting methods for
N
-split systems of arbitrarily high convergence order using composition techniques
[
43
,
38
,
27
]
.
Unfortunately, the number of applications of the underlying base scheme increases exponentially with the desired convergence order. Furthermore, composition schemes often have large truncation errors
[
26
]
.
Therefore, we should always strive to derive splitting methods by directly solving the order conditions, which, as per Gröbner’s Lemma
[
19
, III Lemma 5.1]
, can be derived from the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula for
N=2
partitions or via its recent generalization to
N\geq 2
partitions
[
36
]
.
For the GARK framework, a comprehensive order theory derived from NB-series
[
1
]
is also available
[
28
]
.
Both approaches share a common limitation: for
N
-split systems, the number of order conditions increases significantly with
N
, turning the construction of higher-order methods into a computationally challenging task.
Given a number of partitions
N
, we can solve the nonlinear system with the order conditions up to a certain order
p
, resulting in a set of integrator coefficients for that particular
N
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, there is currently no established design principle to generalize a given method for
N
-split systems to a method for
(N+1)
-split systems.

This paper presents a novel class of splitting methods for
N
-split systems referred to as
hierarchical splitting methods
.
These methods apply splitting methods for two-split systems in a hierarchical manner.
For these methods, the order theory from splitting methods for two-split systems naturally extends.
Given a hierarchical splitting method for
N
-split systems, it is straightforward to extend it to a method for
(N+1)
-split systems.
Furthermore, we will include multiple time stepping techniques
[
40
,
32
,
41
]
, thereby transforming it into a valuable framework at the intersection of geometric numerical integration and multirate integration
[
31
,
25
]
.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows:
in
Section

2
, we formally define hierarchical splitting methods. We discuss their convergence order and provide explicit formulas for the principal error terms.
Subsequently, in
Section

3
, we extend the hierarchical splitting approach by incorporating multiple time stepping techniques. We further characterize the

of a multirate integrator and specify the step size restrictions that must be met to attain a higher computational order.
In
Section

4
, we conduct numerical simulations for a free rigid body and a modified version of the Fermi–Pasta–Ulam problem, demonstrating the efficiency of the proposed methods.
The paper concludes with a summary and outlook.

2.1
Iterative splitting of the vector field

Let
\mathbf{f}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{m\in\boldsymbol{v}}\mathbf{f}^{\{m\}}(\mathbf{x})
for any subset
\boldsymbol{v}\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}

The iterative process starts by splitting the vector field
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}
into two partitions

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{f}^{\{\boldsymbol{j}\}}(\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}

\displaystyle\subset\{1,\ldots,N\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{i}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\boldsymbol{j}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{r}.

We compute a numerical approximation to the
t

\varphi_{t}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}:=\varphi_{t}
of (
1
) with a splitting method for two-split systems,

\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}=\varphi_{b_{s^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{j}\}}\circ\varphi_{a_{s^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}\circ\ldots\circ\varphi_{b_{1}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{j}\}}\circ\varphi_{a_{1}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}.

For an iterative splitting, we need to further partition
\mathbf{f}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}
and
\mathbf{f}^{\{\boldsymbol{j}\}}
. Let us consider the set
\boldsymbol{i}
. We distinguish between two cases.
If
\lvert\boldsymbol{i}\rvert=1
, we do not want to further split the vector field
\mathbf{f}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}
.
For this

If
\lvert\boldsymbol{i}\rvert>1

\mathbf{f}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}

This is accomplished by splitting the set into
\boldsymbol{i}=\boldsymbol{v}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\boldsymbol{w}
and applying another splitting method for two-split systems which replaces

by

\Phi_{a_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}=\varphi_{a_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}b_{s^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\circ\varphi_{a_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}a_{s^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\circ\ldots\circ\varphi_{a_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}b_{1}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\circ\varphi_{a_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}a_{1}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}},\quad j=1,\ldots,s^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}.

This process is iteratively repeated until a partition in singletons is achieved, and suitable numerical integration schemes are selected to compute the flows of the elementary subsystems.

2.2
Convergence order and accuracy

The order conditions of splitting methods for
N
-split systems can be derived from the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula, or strictly speaking via its generalization to
N\geq 2
partitions
[
36
]
.
For investigating the convergence order and the leading order error terms of hierarchical splitting methods, we will make use of the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Consider (non-commuting) matrices
\boldsymbol{A}^{\{m\}},\boldsymbol{B}^{\{m\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and arbitrary scalar parameters
\alpha_{i}^{\{m\}},\beta_{i}^{\{m\}}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,\ldots,s
with
s\in\mathbb{N}
, and
m=1,\ldots,N
with
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Additionally, let
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, as
h\to 0
, it holds

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{s}\prod_{m=1}^{N}\exp\big(\alpha_{i}^{\{m\}}h\boldsymbol{A}^{\{m\}}+\beta_{i}^{\{m\}}h^{p+1}\boldsymbol{B}^{\{m\}}+\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\boldsymbol{C}(h)+h^{p+1}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}^{\{m\}}\boldsymbol{B}^{\{m\}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2})\right),

where
\exp(\boldsymbol{C}(h))=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{s}\prod_{m=1}^{N}\exp\big(\alpha_{i}^{\{m\}}h\boldsymbol{A}^{\{m\}}\big)\right)
.

Proof.

A first-order Taylor expansion of the individual factors yields

\displaystyle\exp\Big(\alpha_{i}^{\{m\}}h\boldsymbol{A}^{\{m\}}+\beta_{i}^{\{m\}}h^{p+1}\boldsymbol{B}^{\{m\}}+\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2})\Big)

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\big(\alpha_{i}^{\{m\}}h\boldsymbol{A}^{\{m\}}\big)+\beta_{i}^{\{m\}}h^{p+1}\boldsymbol{B}^{\{m\}}+\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2}).

We can substitute (
8
) into the product over
i
and
m
and expand. Since every perturbation
\beta_{i}^{\{m\}}h^{p+1}\boldsymbol{B}^{\{m\}}+\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2})
is of order
h^{p+1}
, any term involving a product of perturbations contributes to the remainder term
\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2})
.
Therefore, only the terms containing exactly one perturbation enter the leading order error term.
Consequently, collecting all terms and inserting
\exp(\boldsymbol{C}(h))
yields

\exp(\boldsymbol{C}(h))+h^{p+1}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\beta_{i}^{\{m\}}\boldsymbol{B}^{\{m\}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2}),

which can be interpreted as a first-order Taylor expansion of the asserted expression.

Theorem 5
.

Consider a hierarchical splitting method
\Phi_{h}
with numerical integration schemes
\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
of order
p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
assigned to nodes
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}
,

\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}=\exp\left(h\mathcal{L}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+h^{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+1}\mathcal{O}_{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+1}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+\mathcal{O}(h^{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+2})\right)\mathrm{Id},

with leading error term
\mathcal{O}_{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+1}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
.
For each node
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}
, we define the mapping

\displaystyle\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\colon\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\to\{0,1\},

\displaystyle\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ $\mathtt{left}(\boldsymbol{w})\in\bar{\mathcal{P}}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}$,}\\
0,&\text{$\mathtt{right}(\boldsymbol{w})\in\bar{\mathcal{P}}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}$.}\end{cases}

(9)

Then,
\Phi_{h}
is at least convergent of order
p:=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{I}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}}p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
,

\Phi_{h}=\exp\left(h\mathcal{L}+h^{p+1}\mathcal{O}_{p+1}+\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2})\right)\mathrm{Id},

with leading error term

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{p+1}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{I}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}\\
p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}=p\end{subarray}}\left[\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\sum_{j=1}^{s^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}}\Big(\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w})a_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}+(1-\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w}))b_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\Big)^{p+1}\right]\mathcal{O}_{p+1}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}.

(10)

Proof.

For each node
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}
, the assigned method
\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
or flow
\varphi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
is employed
\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}s^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}
times with step sizes

\tilde{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}}:=\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\big(\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w})a_{j_{\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}+(1-\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w}))b_{j_{\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\big)h,

for
j_{\boldsymbol{w}}=1,\ldots,s^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}
, and all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
.
Let
H^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
denote the set of all step sizes
\tilde{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}}
.
Note that it holds
\sum_{\tilde{h}\in H^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\tilde{h}=h
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}
, provided that all splitting methods are consistent, i.e.,
\sum_{j}a_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}=\sum_{j}b_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}=1
for all
\boldsymbol{i}\in\boldsymbol{I}
.
As per Gröbner’s Lemma
[
19

, we can formally write
\Phi_{h}
as a product of

\exp(\tilde{h}\mathcal{L}^{\{\boldsymbol{k}\}}),\,\boldsymbol{k}\in\boldsymbol{L}\setminus\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime},\,\tilde{h}\in H^{\{\boldsymbol{k}\}}
, and

\exp\left(\tilde{h}\mathcal{L}^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}+\tilde{h}^{p^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}+1}\mathcal{O}_{p^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}+1}^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}+\mathcal{O}\big(\tilde{h}^{p^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}+2}\big)\right),

for
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{h}\in H^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}
.
Applying Lemma
4
for
p:=\min_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}}p^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}
yields

\Phi_{h}=\exp\left(h\tilde{\mathcal{L}}+h^{p+1}\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{p+1}+\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2})\right)\mathrm{Id},

where

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{p+1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}\\
p^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}=p\end{subarray}}\Bigg[\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}}\sum_{j=1}^{s^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}}\Big(\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}(\boldsymbol{w})a_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}+(1-\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}(\boldsymbol{w}))b_{j}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\Big)^{p+1}\Bigg]\mathcal{O}_{p+1}^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}},

and
\tilde{\Phi}_{h}=\exp(h\tilde{\mathcal{L}})\mathrm{Id}
denotes the splitting method
\Phi_{h}
where all elementary subsystems are solved exactly, i.e., to each leaf node
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\boldsymbol{L}
its exact flow
\varphi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}
is assigned.
We now define the set
\boldsymbol{A}\subset\boldsymbol{I}

\boldsymbol{A}=\emptyset

Then, we can now interpret
\tilde{\Phi}_{h}=\exp(h\tilde{\mathcal{L}})\mathrm{Id}

Proposition 9
(Self-adjoint hierarchical splitting methods).

A hierarchical splitting method
\Phi_{h}
is self-adjoint, i.e., it holds
\Phi_{h}=\Phi_{-h}^{-1}=:\Phi_{h}^{*}
, provided that all methods assigned to nodes in the splitting tree are self-adjoint.

Proof.

A self-adjoint composition of self-adjoint maps is self-adjoint.
As per assumption, all (numerical) flows computed in the leaf nodes
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\boldsymbol{L}
are self-adjoint.
Therefore, we know that the (numerical) flows computed in the nodes

\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}\colon\mathtt{left}(\boldsymbol{v})\in\boldsymbol{L}\text{ and }\mathtt{right}(\boldsymbol{v})\in\boldsymbol{L}\}

are self-adjoint as a symmetric composition of self-adjoint maps.
This argument can be repeated iteratively until we reach the root node
\boldsymbol{r}
, concluding that the overall hierarchical splitting method is self-adjoint.

2.3
Composition methods

A common approach to designing higher-order numerical integration schemes is provided by composition techniques
[
43
,
38
]
.
Given a base method
\Phi_{h}
of order
p
, a composition scheme (
13
)

\Psi_{h}=\Phi_{\gamma_{s}h}\circ\ldots\circ\Phi_{\gamma_{1}h}

(13)

with weights
\gamma_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,s
is at least convergent of order
p+1
, provided that it satisfies
\sum_{i}\gamma_{i}=1
and
\sum_{i}(\gamma_{i})^{p+1}=0
.
One example is given by the
triple-jump composition

[
43
]

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{3}=1/(2-2^{1/3}),

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=1-2\gamma_{1}.

(14)

When choosing the Strang splitting (
3
) as the base method, the composition scheme (
13
) with weights (
14
) is convergent of order
p=4
and reads

\Phi_{h}=\varphi_{\gamma_{1}h/2}^{\{1\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{1}h}^{\{2\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{1}h/2}^{\{1\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{2}h/2}^{\{1\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{2}h}^{\{2\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{2}h/2}^{\{1\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{1}h/2}^{\{1\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{1}h}^{\{2\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{1}h/2}^{\{1\}}.

(15a)

If the exact flows of the subsystems are computed, (
15
) is equivalent to

\Phi_{h}=\varphi_{\gamma_{1}h/2}^{\{1\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{1}h}^{\{2\}}\circ\varphi_{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})h/2}^{\{1\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{2}h}^{\{2\}}\circ\varphi_{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})h/2}^{\{1\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{1}h}^{\{2\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{1}h/2}^{\{1\}}.

(15b)

Hierarchical splitting methods
\Phi_{h}
can also be employed as a base method for the composition scheme (
13
) to derive higher-order splitting methods for
N
-split systems.
In particular, a composition method (
13
) based on a hierarchical splitting method is once again a hierarchical splitting method.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\Phi_{h}
be a hierarchical splitting method. Then, any composition method (
13
) using
\Phi_{h}
as the base method is again a hierarchical splitting method.
The hierarchical splitting representation of the composition method differs from the base method
\Phi_{h}
only in the root node.
In particular, the method
\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}
is replaced with its composition using the same weights as for the overall composition.

Proof.

The composition of the full hierarchical splitting method reads

\Psi_{h}=\Phi_{\gamma_{s}h}\circ\Phi_{\gamma_{s-1}h}\circ\ldots\circ\Phi_{\gamma_{1}h}

As per construction, any application of the base method starts with an application of the method
\Phi_{\gamma_{i}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}
assigned to the root node.
Consequently, the initial step of the iterative splitting process yields

\Phi_{\gamma_{s}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}\circ\Phi_{\gamma_{s-1}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}\circ\ldots\circ\Phi_{\gamma_{1}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}},

which is equivalent to directly applying the composition technique to the method
\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}
assigned to the root node.

When considering compositions of self-adjoint splitting methods, the last flow of
i
-th application and the first flow of the
(i+1)
-th application of the base scheme coincide.
As it holds
\varphi_{\gamma_{i+1}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\circ\varphi_{\gamma_{i}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}=\varphi_{(\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{i+1})h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
for the exact flows, these subsequent flow evaluations are frequently combined to reduce computational cost, see for example (
15
).
In a hierarchical splitting method, however, we do in general not assign the exact flows to the child nodes of the root node.
Consequently, combining subsequent flows would result in a different hierarchical splitting method that potentially exhibits a lower order of convergence, as illustrated in the following example.

Example 11
.

Consider the hierarchical splitting method (
7
) and its splitting tree from
Figure

1
.
When replacing the Strang splitting in the root with the composition method (
15a
),
Theorem

5
states that all error terms
\mathcal{O}_{3}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}
for
\boldsymbol{i}\in\boldsymbol{I}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{r}\}
are either scaled by

\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\right)^{3}+2\left(\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2}\right)^{3}+2\left(\frac{\gamma_{3}}{2}\right)^{3}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\gamma_{1}^{3}+\gamma_{2}^{3}+\gamma_{3}^{3}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\text{or}

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}^{3}+\gamma_{2}^{3}+\gamma_{3}^{3}=0,

i.e., the third-order error terms are all vanishing. Due to the inherent symmetry of the method, the hierarchical splitting method is convergent of order
p=4
.
Alternatively, we may replace the Strang splitting in the root node with the composition method (

The leading error terms in the right branch of the splitting tree are scaled by

\gamma_{1}^{3}+\gamma_{2}^{3}+\gamma_{3}^{3}=0

and thus vanish. However, the leading error terms in the left branch are scaled by

\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\right)^{3}+\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}{2}\right)^{3}+\left(\frac{\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{3}}{2}\right)^{3}+\left(\frac{\gamma_{3}}{2}\right)^{3}=\frac{3}{8}\left(\gamma_{1}^{2}\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2}\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{2}\gamma_{3}^{2}\right)\neq 0.

3
Multirate hierarchical splitting methods

When considering
N
-split systems, one frequently encounters that computationally expensive parts of the right-hand side do not contribute much to the dynamics of the solution, suggesting the use of
multiple time stepping techniques
that evaluate the expensive part of the vector field less often.
Multiple time stepping methods can be constructed within the framework of splitting methods
[
6
]
.
A common example is a multirate generalization of the Strang splitting that is known as the
impulse method

[
19
]
.
It is given by

\Phi_{h}=\Phi_{h/2}^{\{\mathfrak{s}\}}\circ\left(\Phi_{h/M}^{\{\mathfrak{f}\}}\right)^{M}\circ\Phi_{h/2}^{\{\mathfrak{s}\}},\quad M\in\mathbb{N},

(16)

where
\Phi_{h}^{\{\mathfrak{f}\}}
and
\Phi_{h}^{\{\mathfrak{s}\}}
are numerical integration schemes consistent with the
\mathfrak{f}
ast subsystem
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{f}^{\{\mathfrak{f}\}}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathfrak{s}
low subsystem
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{f}^{\{\mathfrak{s}\}}(\mathbf{x})
, respectively.
It was already noted in
[
19
]
that multiple time stepping techniques can be iteratively extended to problems with
N>2
partitions with different time scales.
The idea is to further split the fast partition into
\mathbf{f}^{\{\mathfrak{f}\}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{f}^{\{\mathfrak{f,f}\}}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{f}^{\{\mathfrak{f,s}\}}(\mathbf{x})
, and to replace the numerical integration scheme
\Phi_{h}^{\{\mathfrak{f}\}}
with another multiple time stepping method.
This approach is widely utilized in molecular dynamics simulations
[
40
,
32
,
41
]
and aligns with the splitting process of a hierarchical splitting method.
We can incorporate this multiple time stepping approach into the framework of hierarchical splitting methods by introducing multirate splitting trees.

Definition 12
(multirate splitting tree).

A multirate splitting tree is a splitting tree
\boldsymbol{G}=(\boldsymbol{V},\boldsymbol{E})
with weighted edges where for any node
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{r}\}
, the respective multirate factor
M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\in\mathbb{N}
is assigned to the edge connecting the node
\boldsymbol{v}
with its parent node
\mathtt{parent}(\boldsymbol{v})
.

Based on a multirate splitting tree, a first multirate version is obtained by extending (
6
) to

\displaystyle\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}=

\displaystyle\Big(\varphi_{b_{s^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}h\big/M^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\Big)^{M^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}}\circ\Big(\varphi_{a_{s^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}h\big/M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\Big)^{M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\circ\ldots

(17)

\displaystyle\circ\Big(\varphi_{b_{1}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}h\big/M^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\Big)^{M^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}}\circ\Big(\varphi_{a_{1}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}h\big/M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\Big)^{M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}},

with
\boldsymbol{v}:=\mathtt{left}(\boldsymbol{i})\in\boldsymbol{V}
and
\boldsymbol{w}:=\mathtt{right}(\boldsymbol{i})\in\boldsymbol{V}
.
In fact, (

) will introduce a modification only if numerical flow approximations are used. For any analytically computed flow, we can select

.

The generalization of the impulse method (

In particular, a nested integration scheme assigns the kinetic part to node

Typically, we determine the multirate factor

such that the step size

is sufficiently small to meet the stability and accuracy requirements.
For any node
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{r}\}

\frac{\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w})a_{j_{\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}+(1-\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w}))b_{j_{\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}}{\prod_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\bar{\mathcal{P}}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}M^{\{\boldsymbol{u}\}}},

(18)

where

, and

These step sizes can be vastly different in magnitude.
For instance, the fourth-order
(s=6)
-stage splitting method with coefficients
[
26
]

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=b_{6}=0.083983152628767,

(19)

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=a_{6}=0.253978510841060,

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle=b_{5}=0.682236533571909,

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=a_{5}=-0.032302867652700,

\displaystyle b_{3}

\displaystyle=b_{4}=0.5-(b_{1}+b_{2}),

\displaystyle a_{4}

\displaystyle=1-2(a_{2}+a_{3}),

satisfies

, and

The requirements for the multirate factor

For the sub-steps with step sizes (

is already sufficient to meet the accuracy and stability requirements.
As a result, the approach (
17
) may cause substantial computational overhead. This overhead can be mitigated by reweighting the multirate factors for each individual sub-step.
Suppose a sub-step requires advancing the solution by
c\cdot h
,
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, and the desired step size is
h/M
,
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
We aim to ensure that each sub-step utilizes a step size that is less than or equal to the desired step size
h/M
. Simultaneously, we strive to maintain a step size close to
h/M
to minimize computational overhead.

Lemma 15
.

The smallest
\tilde{M}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
\lvert c\cdot h\rvert/\tilde{M}\leq h/M
for a given constant
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, step size
h>0
, and multirate factor
M\in\mathbb{N}
is

\tilde{M}:=\lceil\lvert c\rvert\cdot M\rceil.

(20)

Proof.

The choice (
20
) is feasible since

\left\lvert\frac{c\cdot h}{\tilde{M}}\right\rvert=\frac{\lvert c\rvert\cdot h}{\lceil\lvert c\rvert\cdot M\rceil}\leq\frac{\lvert c\rvert\cdot h}{\lvert c\rvert\cdot M}=\frac{h}{M}.

For
\tilde{M}=1
, optimality is evident. For
\tilde{M}>1
,

\frac{h}{M}=\frac{\lvert c\rvert\cdot h}{\lvert c\rvert\cdot M}\leq\frac{\lvert c\rvert\cdot h}{\lfloor\lvert c\rvert\cdot M\rfloor}

proves the optimality of the choice (
20
).

In particular, when computing a sub-step
\Phi_{ch}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
in node
\boldsymbol{v}
, the coefficient
c
reads

c^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}}=\frac{\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w})a_{j_{\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}+(1-\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w}))b_{j_{\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}}{\prod_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}M^{\{\boldsymbol{u}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{u}\}}}}}\,,

where

denotes the reweighted multirate factors of the nodes in

In general, we define

M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}}:=\Big\lceil\lvert c^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}}\rvert\cdot M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\Big\rceil.

For brevity, we write

, respectively.

Theorem 17
.

Consider a multirate hierarchical splitting method
\Phi_{h}
with numerical integration schemes
\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
of order
p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
assigned to nodes
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}
,

\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}=\exp\left(h\mathcal{L}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+h^{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+1}\mathcal{O}_{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+1}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+\mathcal{O}(h^{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+2})\right)\mathrm{Id},

\mathcal{O}_{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+1}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
and let

denote the mappings defined in (

Then,

is at least convergent of order

,

\Phi_{h}=\exp\left(h\mathcal{L}+h^{p+1}\mathcal{O}_{p+1}+\mathcal{O}(h^{p+2})\right),

with leading error term

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{p+1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{I}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}\\
p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}=p\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})\in\mathcal{J}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\frac{\left(\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w})a_{j_{\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}+(1-\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w}))b_{j_{\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\right)^{p+1}}{\left(\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\bar{\mathcal{P}}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}M^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{u}})}\right)^{p}}\right]\mathcal{O}_{p+1}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}},

\mathcal{J}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}:=\left\{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\,:\,1\leq j_{\boldsymbol{w}}\leq s^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\,\text{for all }\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\right\}
.

Proof.

The root node
\boldsymbol{r}

\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}
still computes a single time step of step size

Additionally, all nodes
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\boldsymbol{L}\setminus\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}
remain unaffected, as the exact flow
\varphi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\}}
is assigned to these nodes.
Consider any node
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{I}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}
where a numerical integration scheme
\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
is assigned.
At any occurrence, identified by a tuple
(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})\in\mathcal{J}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
, the method computes
M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}
steps of step size
c_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}h\big/M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}
.
These consecutive applications can be formally expressed as

\displaystyle\Psi_{c_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\Phi_{c_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}h\big/M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\right)^{M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(c_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}h\mathcal{L}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}\left(\frac{c_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}h}{M^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})}}\right)^{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+1}\mathcal{O}_{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+1}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+\mathcal{O}\big(h^{p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}+2}\big)\right)\mathrm{Id}.

We can extend the iterative process outlined in the proof of
Theorem

5
as follows: between steps 1) and 2), we replace the methods
\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
with
\Psi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{V}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{r}\}
.
In the proof of
Theorem

5
, we utilized in (
10
), that the sum and the product were interchangeable.
As the reweighted multirate factors are generally different, we can no longer interchange the sum and product signs.
Consequently, the leading error term of the intermediate steps (
12
) becomes

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{p+1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{A}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}\boldsymbol{L}^{\prime}\\
p^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}=p\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{(j_{\boldsymbol{w}})\in\mathcal{J}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\frac{\left(\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{P}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}}\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w})a_{j_{\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}+(1-\delta^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}(\boldsymbol{w}))b_{j_{\boldsymbol{w}}}^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}\right)^{p+1}}{\left(\prod_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\bar{\mathcal{P}}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}M^{\{\boldsymbol{w}\}}_{(j_{\boldsymbol{u}})}\right)^{p}}\right]\mathcal{O}_{p+1}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}.

The remainder of this proof is identical to the proof of

Remark 18
.

The use of the reweighting (
20
) presents challenges for composition techniques.
In contrast to (singlerate) hierarchical splitting methods, a composition (
13
) based on a multirate hierarchical splitting method is not equivalent to applying the composition (
13
) to the method
\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{r}\}}
assigned to the root node.
In a composition of a multirate hierarchical splitting method, each application of the base scheme utilizes the same reweighted multirate factors, whereas the weights
\gamma_{i}
enter the reweighting when applying the composition to the root node.

Remark 19
.

As the number of applications of a method
\Phi_{h}^{\{\boldsymbol{v}\}}
grows exponentially with the distance of
\boldsymbol{v}
to
\boldsymbol{r}
, a general guideline is to assign elementary subsystems
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{f}^{\{m\}}(\mathbf{x})
that require a smaller step size to leaf nodes with a greater distance to the root node.
Consequently, a natural assumption is that the multirate factors along a path from the root node
\boldsymbol{r}
to a node
\boldsymbol{v}
are monotonically increasing.

In the limit
M^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}\to\infty
, the subsystem
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{f}^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}(\mathbf{x})
will be integrated exactly for any node
\boldsymbol{i}\in\boldsymbol{I}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{r}\}
, provided that all methods assigned to nodes in this branch of the multirate splitting tree are consistent.
Roughly speaking, in this case, the convergence order of the methods assigned to nodes within this sub-tree does not impact the convergence order of
\Phi_{h}
. Therefore, we can assign lower-order schemes to nodes of the sub-tree without altering the convergence order of the overall method.
In practice, however, we will not perform the limit
M^{\{\boldsymbol{i}\}}\to\infty
.
Nonetheless, we would like to impose conditions on the multirate factors ensuring that the scaling behavior for step sizes
h\geq h_{\mathtt{min}}
is superior to the expected scaling based on the order theory.
We refer to this as the
computational order
of a multirate integration scheme.
The conditions on the multirate factors are derived in the following lemma.

Lemma 20
.

Let
h>0
,
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
M\in\mathbb{N}
, and
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
,
p>q
.
It holds

\left\lvert\frac{(ch)^{q+1}}{\lceil\lvert c\rvert\cdot M\rceil^{q}}\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(h^{p+1}),

provided that the multirate factor is bounded from below by

h^{(q-p)/q}\leq M.

Proof.

By Lemma
15
and the lower bound on
M
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{(ch)^{q+1}}{\lceil\lvert c\rvert\cdot M\rceil^{q}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert c\rvert\cdot\left(\frac{\lvert c\rvert\cdot h}{\lceil\lvert c\rvert\cdot M\rceil}\right)^{q}h\leq\lvert c\rvert\cdot\left(\frac{h}{M}\right)^{q}h=\lvert c\rvert\cdot\frac{h^{q+1}}{M^{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\lvert c\rvert\cdot(h^{(p-q)/q})^{q}\cdot h^{q+1}=\lvert c\rvert\cdot h^{p+1}=\mathcal{O}(h^{p+1}),

proving the statement of this lemma.

4.1
Rigid body equations

The motion of a free rigid body is described by the following ODE using respective principal moments of inertia
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}

\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{\prime}\\
x_{2}^{\prime}\\
x_{3}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&x_{3}/I_{3}&-x_{2}/I_{2}\\

Given an initial value

[
13

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&x_{1}/I_{1}\\

0&0&0\\

The flows of the elementary subsystems are also evolving on

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced a novel class of hierarchical splitting methods for
N
-split differential equations.
These methods apply splitting methods for two-split systems in a hierarchical manner.
Since assigning numerical integration schemes of order
p
to all nodes of a splitting tree suffices to obtain a hierarchical splitting method of order
p
, the framework readily yields higher-order schemes for general
N
-split systems.
Furthermore, any hierarchical splitting method for
N
partitions can be straightforwardly extended to methods for systems with more than
N
partitions.
We also examined an extension to multirate hierarchical splitting methods. The resulting multiple time stepping schemes retain the structure-preserving properties and inherit the order theory of their singlerate counterparts.
We discussed a procedure for selecting optimal multirate factors to reduce computational overhead and introduced the notion of computational order, providing sufficient conditions on the multirate factors under which higher-order behavior is observed over a restricted step size range.
We derived and applied hierarchical splitting methods to the rigid body equations, and a modified Fermi–Pasta–Ulam system.
The numerical results confirm the theoretical findings and demonstrate the efficiency of the hierarchical splitting approach compared to a straightforward construction of higher-order splitting methods via composition techniques.

In various applications, commutator-based splitting approaches
[
42
,
22
,
26
,
24
,
29
]
are frequently employed.
Consequently, a natural next step involves investigating commutator-based approaches inside the hierarchical splitting approach.
Furthermore, the hierarchical splitting approach presents a promising strategy for the derivation of
N
-split systems beyond the ODE case, such as partial differential equations (PDEs)
[
9
]
, differential-algebraic equations (DAEs)
[
5
]
, and stochastic differential equations (SDEs)
[
16
]
.
However, depending on the specific class of equations, a more comprehensive investigation of the hierarchical splitting approach is necessary.
For instance, it remains an open question to what extent splitting methods with complex coefficients
[
11
,
20
,
7
]
can be integrated into the hierarchical splitting approach to obtain methods with complex coefficients having positive real part.
