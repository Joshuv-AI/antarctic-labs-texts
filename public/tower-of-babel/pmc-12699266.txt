PanARGMiner (Pan-Genomic Antimicrobial Resistance Gene Miner): An advanced feature selection framework for extracting key resistance genes from pan-genomic datasets
Authors: Yu-Cheng Chen, Ming-Ren Yang, Yu-Wei Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdentifying antimicrobial resistance (AMR)-related biomarkers from large-scale genomic datasets is often akin to finding a needle in a haystack. With pan-genomic data containing more than 100,000 gene sequences, isolating features that truly drive resistance remains a major challenge in computational biology. Here we present PanARGMiner, a machine learning-based feature selection framework designed to robustly extract highly relevant and informative biomarkers from high-dimensional biological data. PanARGMiner uses an ensemble-based feature selection strategy to select highly informative and compact feature subsets. It then utilizes repeated iterations to ensure the stability and reliability of the proposed framework, enabling PanARGMiner to generate significantly reduced features with comparable prediction performance compared to those obtained with other feature selection algorithms. Applying PanARGMiner to bacterial pan-genomic antimicrobial resistance datasets successfully extracted as few as one to ten candidate AMR biomarkers from datasets with more than 100,000 genes for five common pathogens. Although many of the extracted candidate AMR biomarkers are well-known resistance genes, proteins not known to be associated with AMR mechanisms, including functionally uncharacterized hypothetical proteins, were also extracted. This indicates the potential of PanARGMiner in revealing both established and novel mechanisms of antibiotic resistance, thus providing actionable insights for biomarker discovery, functional genomics, and precision medicine based on complex data. Its ability to uncover both known and uncharacterized resistance-related features offers new opportunities for research and clinical applications in combating AMR.Keywords: PanARGMiner, Feature selection, Antimicrobial resistance, Biomarker, Pan-genome

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become a growing threat to global public health [1], with the pace of bacterial resistance development outpacing the rate of discovery and development of new antibiotic drugs. Traditional AMR detection methods, such as broth dilution and agar disk diffusion, are considered the gold standards for AMR testing; however, they are time-consuming and prevent physicians from making rapid and precise decisions regarding appropriate antibiotic treatments. Therefore, empirical administration of first-line antibiotics based on symptoms is often the default approach. However, this practice may delay effective treatment and increase the risk of further resistance development, underscoring the urgent need for alternative strategies [2], [3], [4].

Research on antibiotic resistance across various infectious diseases highlights the risks of empirical therapy and the need for targeted strategies. Several studies (e.g., [5]) have shown that the recommended empirical treatments may fail in some instances, increasing the likelihood of both immediate and long-term complications. This challenge emphasizes the importance of characterizing AMR determinants (or markers) as a highly valuable research direction [6]. By identifying these AMR markers, researchers can obtain a deeper understanding of resistance mechanisms while simultaneously enabling faster and more accurate predictions and interventions [7]. This pressing issue highlights the transformative potential of innovative computational approaches for addressing the growing AMR crisis.

In this context, genotypic characterization of AMR determinants has emerged as a highly valuable approach. This strategy leverages strong associations between specific genes and resistance phenotypes. For instance, the emergence of Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC) variants involves mutations in the blaKPC gene, which reduce the effectiveness of ceftazidime-avibactam (CZA), a key treatment option for carbapenem-resistant members of Enterobacterales [8]. More than 150 variants have been identified globally, with many discovered in recent years [8], highlighting a growing public health concern. These mutations alter the KPC protein structure, increasing its binding with ceftazidime and decreasing its affinity for avibactam, thereby conferring resistance. Despite their threat, no standardized guidelines are available for diagnosing or managing infections caused by KPC variants, underscoring the urgency for further research and interventions [8]. Another example is the role of the PmrAB two-component regulatory system in Acinetobacter baumannii, which is critical for conferring resistance to polymyxins [9]. PmrA, a response regulator, undergoes phosphorylation by the PmrB sensor kinase, triggering conformational changes that enable it to activate the transcription of resistance-related genes, such as pmrC and naxD. Structural studies have revealed that mutations disrupting PmrA phosphorylation and dimerization reduce polymyxin resistance by altering cell surface modifications. These findings suggest the potential of targeting the PmrA system as a promising strategy to combat polymyxin-resistant A. baumannii [9]. These cases demonstrate the potential of genotypic analyses for revealing the molecular basis of resistance and informing the development of diagnostic tools and therapeutic strategies.

In recent decades, comprehensive databases, including CARD [10], ARDB [11], Resfam [12], and ResFinder [13], have been developed to systematically catalog known AMR genes. Although these resources have significantly advanced the existing understanding of AMR, they remain incomplete owing to the ongoing discovery of novel resistance genes [14], [15], [16], [17]. Furthermore, although these databases provide extensive lists of candidate genes, their broad scope often complicates the identification of the most critical AMR-related genes for specific pathogens. To address this limitation, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) is increasingly used to predict AMR in clinical isolates such as Staphylococcus aureus [18], [19], Escherichia coli [20], and K. pneumoniae [20]. WGS enables more accurate predictions of antimicrobial susceptibility and resistance profiles, providing a more precise alternative to traditional culture-based methods. Furthermore, integrating WGS with AMR surveillance databases has facilitated the real-time monitoring and prediction of resistance patterns, thereby improving clinical decision-making. However, despite these advances, identifying the most relevant AMR genes from vast genomic datasets remains challenging, underscoring the need for novel approaches to efficiently identify key genetic markers for AMR prediction.

With the increasing volume of data in public databases such as the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center (BV-BRC) [21] (previously known as the PAThosystems Resource Integration Center, or PATRIC [22]), identifying novel AMR genes by associating them with resistance phenotypes has become feasible. Our previous work [23] employed a pan-genomic approach combined with machine learning (ML) feature selection techniques to identify gene sets predictive of AMR strains. However, the number of features selected using contemporary feature selection algorithms is still too high (usually hundreds, if not thousands, in our pan-genomic datasets [22]), making it difficult to discern their relative importance and highlight the most crucial features.

To address this challenge, we previously developed a Cross-Validated Feature Selection (CVFS) approach to select feature sets with significantly reduced dimensions while maintaining prediction performance [24]. Although the significantly reduced feature numbers allowed better interpretation of resistance mechanisms compared to contemporary feature selection algorithms, the number of features usually ranged between 50 and 100, which did not allow a straightforward explanation of the downstream model.

To extract the most critical and informative features, we developed PanARGMiner, an advanced feature selection algorithm designed to identify the most vital genetic elements from pan-genomic datasets. PanARGMiner employs an ensemble-based feature selection approach to identify the most critical AMR-related features from pan-genomic data. Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of ensemble algorithms for achieving notable success in improving model performance and robustness [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]. PanARGMiner significantly enhances the identification of key features that may serve as protein biomarkers, while maintaining high predictive accuracy, by refining the key steps of feature selection and processing. Additionally, a feature importance estimation technique was developed to integrate feature importance scores from multiple models, enabling a quantifiable assessment of feature importance. A functional analysis of genes selected using the PanARGMiner algorithm revealed strong correlations with known AMR-related proteins, providing deeper insights into resistance mechanisms. Overall, the PanARGMiner algorithm is robust and unbiased in choosing the most precise AMR biomarkers, thus advancing the field of AMR research and facilitating the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

PanARGMiner is an advanced machine learning feature selection approach that integrates multiple feature selection methods to identify the most critical AMR genetic determinants with high precision and predictive accuracy. In this section, we describe the methodological details of the PanARGMiner algorithm to select the most precise AMR features from pan-genomic data. In general, the PanARGMiner algorithm integrates multiple widely used feature selection algorithms, including eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [30], Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) [31], and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) [32] to select features from machine learning datasets in an ensemble-based manner, thereby emphasizing both dimensionality reduction and predictive accuracy. The PanARGMiner algorithm was applied to AMR datasets of five pathogenic species (S. aureus, Salmonella enterica, Shigella sonnei, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli) to identify the key features for each species. The algorithm selected no more than ten features for each dataset, while maintaining strong predictive performance. Its innovative consensus-based approach ensured robust identification of essential features.

Pan-genome construction

In this section, we outline the steps used for constructing AMR pan-genomics datasets for S. aureus, S. enterica, S. sonnei, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli using genomic data from publicly available resources.

Data acquisition and quality control

To construct pan-genomes for five species (S. aureus, S. enterica, S. sonnei, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli), their assembled genomes (.fna files), protein sequences (.faa files), and AMR phenotype metadata (PATRIC_genomes_AMR.txt) were retrieved from the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center (BV-BRC) [22] (https://www.bv-brc.org/) database. Stringent quality control measures were applied to ensure the reliability of genomic data. Genome completeness and contamination were evaluated using CheckM [33]; only genomes with at least 95 % completeness and no more than 5 % contamination were included [24]. Species identities were further verified using BLASTN [34] by aligning all downloaded genomes against 16S rRNA sequences from the reference genomes of S. aureus, S. enterica, S. sonnei, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli (Supplementary Table S1). Genomes with a 16S nucleotide identity of > 99 % with the references were retained. Further, genomes with fewer than 500 predicted proteins were excluded from the dataset to ensure sufficient genomic content for meaningful and unbiased analysis.

Pan-genome assembly and clustering

Protein sequences from the retained high-quality genomes were collected into a single file and clustered using CD-HIT v4.6 [35] with a 95 % amino acid identity threshold [36]. The presence or absence of gene clusters (defined as groups of genes identified by CD-HIT) within the analyzed genomes was encoded using binary values, where "1" indicated the presence of a gene cluster and "0" indicated its absence. This binary representation of pan-genome data was then associated with AMR phenotypes, forming the basis for subsequent analyses to identify the resistance-predictive genetic determinants. The number of core and accessory genes was also determined in this process, wherein core genes were defined as genes that appeared in most (99 %) genomes, whereas accessory genes did not [37].

Subsequently, whether the pan-genome was open or closed [35] (a closed pan-genome refers to a gene pool that is saturated, whereas an open pan-genome indicates a potentially unlimited gene pool) was determined by estimating the pan-genome rarefaction distribution through 10 repeated random samplings of genomes and their cumulative gene numbers. The gene growth rate of the rarefaction curve could be fitted to the Heaps' law [38], [39] to determine whether the pan-genome of a species was open or closed. Specifically, the pan-genome growth curve was fitted to a power law growth model n=κNβ, where n represents the average pan-genome size calculated from 10 random sampling processes, N is the number of genomes, and β is the exponent of the model. The pan-genome was considered open if β > 0, indicating continual growth of the pan-genome size. Conversely, if β ≤ 0, the pan-genome was considered closed, as its size had reached a saturation point.

Integration of pan-genome content with AMR phenotypes

To integrate the pan-genome content with AMR phenotypes, the datasets were integrated with strain phenotypes, including resistant (R) and susceptible (S) (other phenotypes, such as intermediate (I), were excluded from this analysis). We imposed two restrictions on dataset selection. First, only datasets with a more balanced representation of resistant (R) and susceptible (S) phenotypes were selected. Specifically, the ratio of R to S was maintained within the range of 0.1–10 (i.e., the allowed ratio of R:S was between 1:10 and 10:1), preventing class imbalances that could affect downstream analyses [24]. Second, the number of entries within the datasets needed to be at least 500. Only datasets that passed these two criteria were selected for the downstream analysis.

Pan-genomic AMR gene miner (PanARGMiner) algorithm

The PanARGMiner algorithm operates by repeatedly applying multiple machine learning feature selection algorithms twice on the input dataset and identifying features that can be consistently extracted from all repeated runs. Features consistently selected by all algorithms in dual runs were considered robust and retained. To mitigate the effects of algorithmic randomness and improve the stability of selected features, the entire procedure was repeated multiple times, and a consensus-based strategy was used to finalize the feature set. The overall procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Workflow of PanARGMiner.

Below, we describe the detailed feature-selection process using PanARGMiner, which integrates multiple feature-selection algorithms (XGBoost [36], AdaBoost [29], and LASSO [30]), each offering a unique niche for selecting features. XGBoost [40] employs gradient boosting to optimize a loss function using gradient descent and incorporates built-in regularization to enhance efficiency and robustness. AdaBoost [31] adapts by reweighting misclassified samples, focusing on difficult-to-classify cases for improved accuracy. LASSO [32] complements these tree-based methods by introducing sparsity and mitigating multicollinearity through linear regression and regularization, thus promoting simplicity and interpretability. Together, these three models leverage their unique strengths to create a balanced and sharpened feature-selection process.

Feature scoring

The importance score of each feature was calculated by aggregating and weighting the feature importance values derived from multiple selection process iterations. During each of two consecutive runs, the training data were subjected to three distinct feature selection algorithms (XGBoost, AdaBoost, and LASSO) for feature selection purposes, where features with non-zero importance scores were extracted for each algorithm. The importance values assigned to each feature by these algorithms were then weighted based on the predictive performance of the corresponding model, which was quantified by the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC).

To ensure statistical robustness, AMR datasets were included in the study only if (1) the number of genomes associated with each antibiotic was at least 500; and (2) the phenotypic representation was within the 10:1 or 1:10 balance threshold (see Methods). The number of drug resistance datasets for all included pathogenic species ranged from 10 to 18 (the detailed list of selected antibiotics for each species is shown in Supplementary Table S2). Application of PanARGMiner for extracting the gene features from all AMR datasets yielded features with comparable and non-inferior performance compared with those obtained by other feature selection methods (p > 0.05; Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S3). In contrast, the number of features selected using PanARGMiner was significantly lower (p < < 0.005; Wilcoxon rank-sum test) (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S4). These results, consistently observed in almost all bacterium-drug resistance combinations, suggest that PanARGMiner can identify a much smaller yet highly predictive subset of features. We also attempted to select the same number of top features from other feature selection algorithms and found that PanARGMiner consistently presented higher predictive performances (Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Table S5), demonstrating its effectiveness in selecting the most succinct and informative feature subsets. Other prediction performance metrics, including precision, recall, and F1-score, are reported in Supplementary Table S6.

Shared predictive features and potential Co-resistance mechanisms

Repeated identification of the same genes across resistance models for different antibiotics may reflect underlying mechanisms such as co-expression, genetic linkage, or HGT, which enable bacteria to simultaneously resist multiple antibiotics through co-selection [38], [39]. We observed such co-resistance patterns across all five bacterial species. Fig. 5 presents the UpSet plots for visualizing overlaps in predictive genetic features among resistance models of the five pathogens for different antibiotics. These plots highlight shared genes across multiple drug-specific models, supporting the hypothesis that certain genes contribute to multidrug resistance (MDR) through interconnected genetic mechanisms.

UpSet plots showing the overlap of predictive genetic features across antibiotic resistance models in five bacterial species. In each UpSet plot, the top part displays the number of singletons or the shared number of genes, whereas the bottom part illustrates the appearance patterns of genes within different antimicrobial resistance datasets.

Here, we highlight some of the co-resistance patterns observed in different bacterial species.

Staphylococcus aureus

In S. aureus, five genes, including mecA (pbp2a), erm(C), an aminoglycoside acetyltransferase, and two hypothetical proteins, were consistently selected in at least two drug-specific models (Supplementary Table S8).

Salmonella enterica

Within S. enterica, four determinants recurred, most notably a class C β-lactamase spanning four β-lactams, two folate-pathway enzymes linking trimethoprim and sulfonamide resistance, and the integron integrase IntI1 (Supplementary Table S9).

Shigella sonnei

The S. sonnei UpSet plot highlighted nine genes, dominated by β-lactamases (including ESBLs), a tetracycline efflux pump, and multiple mobile-element proteins that co-occurred across the ampicillin, cephalosporin, and tetracycline models (Supplementary Table S10).

Klebsiella pneumoniae

In K. pneumoniae, seven antibiotics shared two highly prevalent features: blaKPC and a large mobile-element protein; transposases, integrases, aminoglycoside acetyltransferases, and hypothetical proteins also appeared in three to five models (Supplementary Table S11).

Escherichia coli

Five co-resistance determinants were observed in the E. coli species: a class C β-lactamase, a mobile-element protein, two aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, and an uncharacterized protein, YmdF (Supplementary Table S12).

Discussion

Understanding the genetic foundations of AMR across diverse bacterial species is crucial for advancing both clinical treatment strategies and resistance surveillance [41]. By applying the PanARGMiner to multiple pathogens and antibiotic classes, this study identified a set of predictive genetic features that aligned with well-established resistance mechanisms and uncovered potentially novel factors. As discussed in previous research, the recurrent selection of specific genes across different antibiotic models suggests the existence of core resistance determinants and shared selective pressures, such as coordinated regulatory responses that transcend individual drug classes [42], [43].

One of the most critical parameters of the PanARGMiner algorithm is the proportion cutoff of the selected genes among repeated runs. To further investigate the effect of the Z parameter, we applied varying Z cutoffs to filter the features before classification. The AUC values fluctuated across different Z-score thresholds for each bacterial species (Supplementary Figure S2 and Supplementary Table S13), suggesting that the choice of Z-score can influence predictive performance. Correspondingly, the number of selected features decreased with more stringent Z-score thresholds (Supplementary Figure S3 and Supplementary Table S14), indicating a trade-off between feature quantity and predictive performance. Eventually, we selected 60 % as the default Z parameter, as it demonstrates a more balanced number of selected genes (all under ten) and their predictive performances. Users may also adjust this parameter to control the behavior of the PanARGMiner pipeline.

In this work, only resistant (R) and susceptible (S) phenotypes were included. One may ask: why not include the intermediate labels (I) in the analysis? The “I” labels were not included for two reasons. First, the proportion of the “I” label is comparably low among the labels (ranging from 0.4 % in Staphylococcus aureus to 4.96 % in Klebsiella pneumoniae); second, the low proportion of the “I” label may confuse machine learning algorithms. Therefore, we elected to keep only the “R” and “S” labels for creating binary classifiers. However, we also agree that the intermediate phenotype is important, as “I” strains may possess critical genes that enable them to transition from susceptible to resistant strains. Given sufficient intermediate data, we can include the intermediate strains in the analysis and examine the genes indispensable for these strains.

To facilitate importance ranking based on the three incorporated machine learning algorithms, we designed a ranking formula to estimate feature importance. The goal and rationale of the formula was to combine signals from different models and repeated runs on the same scale, while exerting a greater influence on the models that better separate resistant strains from susceptible ones. AUROC is used as the performance basis for calculation because it summarizes how well a model performs binary classification across all decision thresholds and is not tied to a particular cutoff or calibration. Feature normalization is performed because feature importance has different meanings (e.g., coefficient-based magnitudes for LASSO or gain/impurity-based scores for tree-based models) and scales across model families. Rescaling raw importance values to a range of 0–1 makes them comparable to each other. We hope that this design can achieve the goal of robust feature comparison, thereby enhancing downstream feature interpretability.

As the goal of PanARGMiner is somewhat similar to that of our previous work (the CVFS algorithm), we specifically highlight the differences and improvements made in this work. The primary difference between PanARGMiner and CVFS is that CVFS employs repeated data partitioning and consensus feature extraction to achieve feature selection, whereas PanARGMiner achieves the same goal by utilizing an ensemble machine learning feature selection algorithm. Further, the design of PanARGMiner involves data partitioning into training and test portions, rather than the utilization of K-fold cross-validation in CVFS. There are three reasons for the PanARGMiner design. First, partitioning the input dataset into training and test sets and then applying feature selection only to the training set tend to circumvent the danger of feature overfitting, which is a minor drawback of our original CVFS design. Second, application of different machine learning algorithms to the training set and extraction of consensus features enable identification of the most important features distinguished by different algorithmic designs. Third, the repeated run design adopted from CVFS helps avoid the influence of random effects. Our test also demonstrates that the design of the PanARGMiner algorithm outperforms other feature selection algorithms, including CVFS, in terms of the most parsimonious number of features. Notably, we have attempted to add the idea of data partitioning (similar to that described in CVFS) into PanARGMiner; however, perhaps because of stringent constraints, wherein features need to be identified using different data partitions and algorithms, the results were not as good as those obtained with PanARGMiner. We therefore only adopted the ensemble algorithm into PanARGMiner.

As PanARGMiner incorporated three machine learning algorithms into the ensemble approach, we evaluated two additional feature selectors (Random Forest and CatBoost) and compared whether adding these algorithms could further enhance performance. We observed that the addition of the two algorithms did not yield similar prediction performances and numbers of features (Wilcoxon rank-sum test; p > 0.05), and the runtimes were significantly longer than the original design of PanARGMiner (by 8–10 folds), except in the case of adding random forest alone (Supplementary Figures S4-S6). Therefore, we conclude that the combination of the three algorithms is sufficient to yield promising results in feature selection.

A comparative ablation study was conducted to identify the importance of the feature selection algorithms used by PanARGMiner. Algorithm removal in a stepwise manner reveals that although all individual algorithms perform comparable to PanARGMiner (Wilcoxon rank-sum test; p > 0.05), the combination of AdaBoost and LASSO works significantly worse with similar feature numbers, suggesting that AdaBoost-LASSO may include some features that hamper the prediction performances (Supplementary Figures S7-S8 and Supplementary Tables S15-S18). We therefore conclude that XGBoost may be the most critical algorithm in determining important features and that both AdaBoost and LASSO algorithm help pinpoint the most critical features among XGBoost-selected features.

Comparing the prediction performances of features extracted using PanARGMiner against other state-of-the-art feature selection algorithms, including Boruta, mRMR, and Shapley additive explanation (SHAP), revealed no significant difference in prediction performance (Supplementary Figure S9; Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p > 0.05). However, PanARGMiner selects significantly fewer features than those selected with other methods (Supplementary Figure S10; Wilcoxon rank-sum test; p < 0.01), yielding more parsimonious and interpretable feature sets that facilitate downstream analysis and potential experimental validation. This again demonstrates the capability of PanARGMiner in selecting minimal but meaningful features.

The potential AMR genes after extraction are listed (Supplementary Tables S7) along with their detailed mechanistic insights and relevant literature evidence for the genes in Supplementary Tables S8-S12. We further discuss several important genetic factors, particularly those associated with multi-antimicrobial resistance, for each of the five species.

In S. aureus, consistent identification of the gene encoding the penicillin-binding protein, PBP2a (1280.10000.peg.2171), in the models for both oxacillin and cefoxitin reinforces its pivotal role in β-lactam resistance [44] and confirms the ability of the models to capture clinically relevant markers. Interestingly, aminoglycoside N(6′)-acetyltransferase (1280.30131.peg.2298) appeared in the models for both gentamicin and ciprofloxacin, indicating potential co-selection or stress-induced cross-resistance pathways that also were observed in the high frequency of co-resistances in E. coli [45].

In S. enterica, key antifolate resistance genes, such as dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) and dihydropteroate synthase (DHPS), were repeatedly selected in models for trimethoprim and sulfonamides, as identified and reviewed previously [46]. Similarly, genes like FloR and CMY-2 β-lactamase were identified across multiple antibiotic classes, highlighting the importance of multidrug efflux and enzymatic degradation mechanisms.

In S. sonnei, the predictive features selected by PanARGMiner reflected a multifaceted resistance architecture involving both well-characterized and potentially novel genetic contributors. Known AMR determinants included drug inactivation-mediating enzymes, such as ANT(3''), APH(3′), Mph(A), and β-lactamases of the TEM and CTX-M families, as well as efflux-associated genes including Tet(A) and QacEΔ1. The identification of Sul2, usually found on small plasmids [47], and the integron integrase IntI1 underscores the role of conjugative plasmids and horizontal gene transfer in facilitating the acquisition and dissemination of resistance traits [48]. Notably, a considerable number of hypothetical or uncharacterized proteins were consistently selected, suggesting that S. sonnei may harbor several unexplored elements involved in antibiotic resistance or adaptive responses.

In K. pneumoniae, the resistance landscape revealed by PanARGMiner was notably complex, featuring a diverse array of β-lactamases, including the KPC, CTX-M, TEM, and OXA families that function as key contributors to β-lactam hydrolysis [49]. Aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (e.g., AAC(6′)-Ib, AAC(3)-IIIb, and APH(6)) further emphasized the role of enzymatic inactivation in resistance [50]. Furthermore, regulators such as AcrR, OqxR, and TetR, along with stress-associated elements like MrkJ and toxin-antitoxin systems (e.g., KacT/KacA), suggested an adaptive resistance network involving efflux regulation, biofilm persistence, and tolerance phenotypes [51]. The frequent appearance of hypothetical proteins among the top predictive features highlights a reservoir of uncharacterized elements that may contribute to AMR, warranting future functional validation.

In E. coli, the identified AMR-related genes span multiple known resistance mechanisms. These include drug-modifying enzymes (AAC(3), AAC(6′), ANT(2''), ANT(3''), and CAT), β-lactamases (e.g., CMY and CTX-M), target protection systems (e.g., Sul2), and active efflux components such as FloR and DMT-family transporters. The diversity of mechanisms observed reflects the genetic plasticity of E. coli and validates the potential of PanARGMiner for capturing a wide spectrum of resistance determinants. Importantly, several genes were shared across models for different antibiotic classes, suggesting either co-resistance or involvement in generalized stress-response pathways.

Co-resistance patterns across pathogens

We identified co-resistance patterns in all five species included in this study, wherein numerous genes appeared across multiple drug resistance profiles. As co-resistance may facilitate the dissemination of pathogenic species and promote the co-expression of genes that enhance resistance mechanisms [52], [53], [54], strains and isolates exhibiting such profiles represent a significant risk. Identifying these co-resistance patterns will help determine the genes potentially associated with dangerous co-resistance schemes, thus highlighting the predictive value of features selected by PanARGMiner. This applies to single-drug resistance as well as to genes likely involved in cross-class resistance and gene mobility. A comparative examination of co-resistant features across S. aureus, S. enterica, S. sonnei, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli reveals convergent resistance strategies underpinned by both well-known and uncharacterized genetic determinants. Despite differences in genomic backgrounds and ecological niches, several common themes emerged, suggesting shared evolutionary pressures shaping MDR.

First, across all five species, multiple resistance models repeatedly identified not only well-characterized antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) but also hypothetical proteins and mobile genetic elements. This suggests that MDR is driven by canonical ARGs as well as lesser-known contributors, likely through regulatory modulation, genetic linkage, or epistatic interactions. For instance, both S. aureus and K. pneumoniae featured recurrent hypothetical proteins in their co-resistance gene sets (1280.8067.peg.175 and 1284797.4.peg.4695, respectively), highlighting their potential roles as novel resistance factors or mediators of resistance gene networks.

Second, consistent identification of mobile elements, such as integrons, transposases, and plasmid-associated genes, underscores the central role of HGT in disseminating co-resistance. In S. enterica, the presence of IntI1, alongside the β-lactamase and antifolate resistance genes, exemplified how resistance to distinct antibiotic classes can be physically linked and co-selected. Similarly, K. pneumoniae and E. coli featured transposase and integrase genes within co-resistance profiles, reinforcing the hypothesis that mobile elements act as genomic vehicles for MDR.

Third, β-lactamase genes were among the most frequently co-selected features across species. Class A, C, and D β-lactamases, such as blaKPC, blaTEM, and CMY-type enzymes, were identified across diverse drug models, reflecting their broad-spectrum activity and centrality in clinical resistance. These enzymes often co-occurred with aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (e.g., AAC(3) and AAC(6’)), especially in K. pneumoniae and E. coli, suggesting that co-resistance to β-lactams and aminoglycosides may be reinforced by shared selective pressures or co-mobilization on the same genetic elements.

Fourth, intersection analyses revealed that many co-resistant features were not species-specific but rather reflected shared functional categories such as efflux pumps, metabolic bypass enzymes (e.g., DHFR and DHPS), and outer membrane porins. For example, both S. sonnei and E. coli contain sulfonamide resistance genes (Sul2) and aminoglycoside resistance markers (AAC variants), suggesting a conserved molecular strategy across members of the Enterobacteriaceae. The Tet(A) efflux pump observed in S. sonnei also mirrored similar findings in other gram-negative bacteria, indicating its utility beyond tetracycline resistance.

Finally, frequent selection of uncharacterized or hypothetical proteins across multiple species and antibiotics suggested a significant reservoir of unexplored resistance biology. Overall, these findings challenge the current reliance on known ARG databases and highlight the importance of using predictive algorithms, such as PanARGMiner, to reveal hidden layers of the resistome. Proteins such as 1328433.3.peg.1098 in E. coli and 624.603.peg.4572 in S. sonnei exemplify candidates that warrant further experimental validation. Their recurrence across unrelated drug models suggests roles beyond random inclusion, possibly as novel resistance effectors or modulators.

In summary, our comparative analysis affirmed the power of PanARGMiner to identify high-confidence resistance determinants and contextually relevant and mechanistically plausible co-resistance genes. The recurring appearance of mobile elements, hypothetical proteins, and broad-spectrum resistance genes across species demonstrated that MDR is a multifactorial phenomenon driven by both conserved and unique genetic architectures. Future research, including elucidation of the functionalities of hypothetical proteins, determining the actual roles of mobile genetic elements, performing network-based analysis for identifying gene-interaction patterns, identifying non-additive or epistasis among genes that co-contribute to resistance, analyzing selection pressure, and experimentally validating novel resistance genes, may allow identification and confirmation of the extracted potential AMR genes. The development of multi-omics datasets, although currently unavailable for most pathogenic strains in the BV-BRC database, may also enhance the performance of PanARGMiner. We hope that the ongoing work may eventually enable the decoding of imminent resistance threats and inform therapeutic countermeasures in an era of escalating antimicrobial resistance.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully demonstrated the applicability of our newly developed and customized feature selection pipeline, PanARGMiner, for extracting a minimal but predictive subset of AMR-associated genomic features. Across all five species tested, PanARGMiner consistently demonstrated the ability to significantly reduce the number of selected features (typically below 10 genes per antibiotic condition) without compromising the model performance. Comparative functional analyses of the extracted genes revealed both species-specific and shared resistance and co-resistance determinants, highlighting the potential of PanARGMiner as a powerful and reliable algorithm for pan-genomic feature selection in AMR research. We hope that this approach can be widened to provide machine learning-based diagnostics in the era of precision antimicrobial stewardship.
