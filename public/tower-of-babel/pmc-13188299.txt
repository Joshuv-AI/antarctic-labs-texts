The prognostic significance of the mechanism of mild to moderate alkalemia at emergency department admission
Authors: Ivan Gur, Amichai Gutgold, Asaf Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPaucity of evidence exists as to the prognostic significance of mild to moderate alkalemia upon presentation to the emergency department (ED).MethodsPatients presenting to the ED of one tertiary hospital over the last 24 years were included in this retrospective cohort study if the serum pH drawn within 3 h of the initial presentation was 7.45–7.55. Alkalemia was defined as primarily metabolic (PM) if PCO2≥35mmHg, and primarily respiratory (PR) if HCO3≤24mEq/L - otherwise, the etiology was considered to be mixed and the case was excluded from this analysis. Background

Alkalemia (a serum pH > 7.45) remains the second most common acid-base disturbance in the emergency department (ED) [1]. Affecting about one in every five patients presenting to the ED, the prognostic significance of this laboratory finding remains to be fully elucidated. In a recent work by Miro et al. [2], a large multicenter cohort of patients presenting to the ED with a presumed diagnosis of acute heart failure were examined. In patients with alkalemia, there was no significant increase in in-hospital mortality compared with unmatched controls with normal pH. The subgroup analysis of a fairly small cohort of patients with acute decompensated heart failure and primarily metabolic (676 patients) and respiratory (937 patients) alkalemia revealed a trend towards better survival in the later group (9.3% versus 6.7%), but these differences did not reach statistical significance [3].

Severe alkalemia (usually defined as serum pH > 7.55) has been consistently associated with increased mortality. In our recent work [4] we detected strong evidence of notably divergent outcomes based on the mechanism of alkalemia. Primarily metabolic (PM) alkalemia was associated with a significantly higher short-term (30-day) mortality risk compared to primarily respiratory (PR) alkalemia, independent of age, comorbidities, or other laboratory parameters. Specifically, patients with PM alkalemia had a 30-day survival rate of 78.9%, while those with PR alkalemia had a notably higher survival rate of 95.3% in a propensity-matched analysis. Evidence from non-ED and mixed cohorts also link severe alkalemia (pH > 7.6) to markedly increased mortality, with the risk escalating as pH rises, particularly in the context of mixed metabolic and respiratory disturbances [5–7]. These findings are limited in generalisability by the relative scarcity of severe alkalemia in the ED, to less than ~ 0.5% in most published cohorts [8].

This study aimed to increase our understanding of the prognostic significance of alkalemia in the ED population as a whole, including mild and moderate cases.

Methods

Setting

The electronic health records (EHR) of all patients presenting to the emergency department of a large tertiary referral hospital from the implementation of the EHR system in November 1, 1999 to December 31 2024. This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee with the requirement for informed consent waived due to the purely observational nature of this study.

Participants

The study population included all adult patients aged 18 years or older evaluated at the emergency department during the study period, provided an arterial or venous blood sample was drawn within 3 h from admission and analysed on sight (RAPIDLab ® 1200, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Included were all patients with an initial serum pH of 7.45 to 7.55. We defined the alkalemia to be primarily metabolic (PM) if PCO2 was ≥ 35 mmHg, and primarily respiratory (PR) if bicarbonate levels were below or equal to 24 mEq/L. Patients who did not meet either criteria were considered to have a mixed etiology and were excluded from this study.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 30 days mortality. In addition, the following data were retrieved from patients’ electronic medical records: (1) Demographics (age and sex); (2) Initial vital signs at admission (heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and temperature during the first 24 h); (3) Comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, smoking status, asthma, COPD, chronic kidney disease and heart failure); (4) Concomitant therapy with medications that could cause alkalemia, including diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and angiotensin receptor blockers; (5) Charlson Comorbidity Index; (6) Laboratory values at admission (pH, bicarbonate, carbon dioxide, lactate, hemoglobin level, white blood cell count, platelet count, plasma creatinine level, blood sodium and potassium levels) and Rapid Emergency Medicine Score (REMS) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores at presentation. Chief complaint at presentation was not collected as the initial diagnostic coding was often non-informative.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (for continuous variables) and proportions for nominal variables. Survival analysis was conducted using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model to evaluate independent predictors of 30-day mortality. The proportional hazards assumption was verified for all covariates using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. Multicollinearity among predictors in the final model was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), confirming no significant collinearity (all VIFs < 2.5). Because missing data accounted for less than 2% of the study cohort, missingness was handled conservatively using a complete case analysis approach. A highly detailed description of model specification, assumption testing, and covariate selection is provided in the Supplementary Material. Model diagnostics, including checks for influential observations and multicollinearity (see the Supplementary Material), were performed to ensure robustness. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.2).

Results

Of 141 thousand patients with blood gases drawn upon admission, 31,272 patients (22.1%) had a pH > 7.45, of whom 14,802 (47.4%) had a primarily metabolic (PM) alkalemia and 11,082 (35.4%) patients had a primarily respiratory (PR) alkalemia. Thus, 25,884 patients were included in the final analysis after excluding 5388 (17.2%) who did not qualify exclusively as either PM or PR. The patient selection flow is presented in Fig. 1.

Study design. The patient selection process depicted in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. Alkalosis was considered primarily metabolic (PM) if PCO2 was ≥ 35 mmHg, and primarily respiratory (PR) if bicarbonate levels were below or equal to 24 mEq/L, and “mixed” if neither conditions were satisfied

Patients with PM tended to be older than those with PR (median age 66.5, IQR [50–77] years, versus 59.5 years, IQR [38–75]) with a slight female predominance in both groups (52% vs. 55%). Patients with PM had a higher proportion of significant chronic diseases, including heart failure (20% vs. 14%), active malignancy (34% vs. 28%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (12% vs. 6%) and smoking (22% vs. 17%). Of note, the prevalence of past diagnosis of asthma (6.8% vs. 6.3%) and chronic kidney disease (25% vs. 24%) was similar between groups. Overall, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI - median 6, IQR [4,8] in both groups) was similar, as were the Rapid Emergency Medicine Scores (REMS - median 5 [3–7] for both groups), the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA - median 1 [0–3] in both groups) and the rates of hyperkalemia (1.1% vs. 2.6%) and hypokalemia (26% vs. 21%). Chronic medications, including diuretics and renin-angiotensin axis inhibitors, were used in similar proportions in both groups. Following a univariate analysis (presented in Supplementary Table 2), a multivariate survival analysis identified pH severity as a robust, independent predictor of 30-day mortality. When analyzed by quartiles, a clear stepwise increase in mortality risk was observed. Compared to the lowest pH quartile (Q1, reference), the adjusted hazard ratio increased progressively from 1.24 (95% CI 1.10–1.40, p < 0.001) in Q2, to 1.52 (95% CI 1.35–1.71, p < 0.001) in the highest quartile (Q4).

The type of alkalemia (PR vs. PM) remained a significant and independent predictor of 30-day mortality. Alkalemia that was primarily respiratory in origin was associated with an increase of 21% in short-term mortality (aHR 1.21, 95% CI [1.12–1.32]) even when adjusted for patient age, co-morbidity (CCI), physiologic derangement at presentation (REMS and SOFA scores), potassium level, pH, and year of admission. Indeed, later admission years were associated with improved survival (aHR 0.98, 95% CI [0.98–0.99]). This multivariate Cox regression model, demonstrating good discrimination (concordance = 0.742) and overall significance (likelihood ratio test p < 0.0001), is presented in Table 2. These findings remained similar when the diagnosis of PM and PR was made using Winter’s formula for expected compensation, as presented in the Supplementary Material.

Discussion

Our analysis shows primarily respiratory (PR) alkalemia to confer a higher adjusted risk of mortality compared to metabolic (PM) alkalemia—a finding that is directly opposite to what was observed in our analysis of cases of severe alkalemia, where PM was associated with substantially greater mortality than PR [4]. The inclusion of venous samples, as well as arterial once, is based upon several studies showing a good correlation between venous and arterial blood samples when it comes to the diagnosis of acid-base disturbances [1, 2, 4], as well as the minute effects venipuncture has on the results of this analysis [1, 6]. In view of the relatively large and representative retrospective cohort from a general ED of a tertiary hospital, this distinct reversal likely reflects fundamental differences in underlying pathophysiology and adaptive reserves between these severity strata [9].

In mild to moderate alkalemia, PR typically results from an acute increase in ventilation (e.g., pain, anxiety, sepsis) and serves as a marker of significant acute physiological stress or underlying illness [10]. Even when not immediately life-threatening, and undoubtedly labile in time, chronic or sustained PR alkalemia can lead to deleterious consequences such as cerebral and coronary vasoconstriction, decreased oxygen delivery due to the leftward shift in the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve, and electrolyte disturbances, notably hypokalemia [11]. Crucially, patients with PR alkalemia often lack chronic physiologic adaptations to maintain homeostasis under acute stress, rendering them more vulnerable to secondary complications and adverse outcomes [12]. However, caution is warranted in inferring causality. The increased short-term mortality in PR alkalemia likely reflects the severity of the underlying trigger rather than the pH disturbance per se. It is plausible that PR alkalemia acts as a “severity marker” for unmeasured confounders—such as subtle hypoxemia, respiratory failure, or early neurological injury—that are not fully captured by the composite indices (REMS, SOFA) used in our survival analysis.

Metabolic alkalemia in mild to moderate forms is often related to chronic disease and medication effects (e.g. diuretics). Individuals affected may possess some degree of chronic adaptation, such as renal bicarbonate retention mechanisms and compensatory hypoventilation, which may mitigate acute adverse effects and temporarily blunt the impact of the disturbance. In other words, in mild to moderate alkalemia PR could be a stronger indicator of acute pathophysiology, even beyond the scores used in our analysis [13].

Conversely, in cases of severe alkalemia (pH ≥ 7.55), metabolic alkalemia is associated with much decreased survival, as reported previously (78.9% at 30 days for PM vs. 95.3% for PR) [14]. At very high pH, the compensatory capacity for both metabolic and respiratory mechanisms becomes overwhelmed [15]. Severe PM alkalemia could signify profound chronic volume depletion, severe hypokalemia, impaired renal function, and often intractable underlying disease, predisposing patients to arrhythmias, hemodynamic instability, and sudden cardiac death. In contrast, PR alkalemia, even when severe, may still represent an acute but reversible response to issues like hyperventilation; and perhaps indicate a better physiological reserve that could facilitate the underlying physiologic insult can sometimes improve with prompt treatment or resolution of the precipitating cause [8].

This study has several important limitations. While the baseline characteristics of our patient population, including the prevalence of background medical conditions, is quite representative of the general ED population, this is a single center study. Chief complaint could not be reliably ascertained. The exclusion of mixed etiology, representing 17% of our cohort, limits the generalizability of our conclusions, as these patients tended to present with baseline characteristics (see Table 1) that is generally in between what we defined as PR and PM. Furthermore, the retrospective design limits our ability to completely account for residual confounding. While we adjusted for major severity scores, specific drivers of hyperventilation—such as pain, anxiety, or specific infection sources—were not individually controlled for. Therefore, the association between PR alkalemia and mortality should be interpreted as a prognostic signal rather than a direct causal pathway. Larger, multicenter cohorts are needed to corroborate these findings, with the eventual goal of including alkalemia severity and etiology in our risk stratification in the ED. Finally, while our dataset includes both venous and arterial samples, the specific source could not be reliably distinguished in the electronic health record for every patient. However, based on institutional protocols, arterial samples comprise less than 5% of the total cohort.

Conclusions

Primarily respiratory alkalemia was associated with increased 30-day mortality compared to primarily metabolic alkalemia in patients presenting to the general ED with a serum pH of 7.45–7.55. This association remained significant even after adjusting for patient age, comorbidities, and the degree of physiological derangement upon presentation. More severe alkalemia (increased pH) was associated with increased 30 days mortality in patients with PM but not in patients with PR alkalemia.
