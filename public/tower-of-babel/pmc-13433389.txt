A rare variant in DPYD c.812delT causes severe adverse events of S-1 in a patient with tongue cancer
Authors: Hiroki Ishimura, Atsushi Suehiro, Daiki Hira, Eiji Hishinuma, Midori Kato, Masamitsu Maekawa, Taishi Yasuda, Yurie Katsube, Yoshiki Katada, Natsuki Imayoshi, Yuki Shigetsura, Shunsaku Nakagawa, Masahiro Tsuda, Masahiro Hiratsuka, Tomohiro Terada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD), which is encoded by the DPYD gene, plays an important role in the metabolism of fluoropyrimidine (FP) drugs, including tegafur, in S-1. A decrease in DPD activity can cause severe FP-related toxicity. The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) guidelines for FP and DPYD polymorphisms recommend FP dose adjustments based on the four major DPYD polymorphisms. However, multiple rare variants of DPYD have been reported. We present the case of a man in his 50s with cT3N0M0 tongue squamous cell carcinoma who developed severe myelosuppression and diarrhea after the initiation of S-1 (tegafur/gimeracil/oteracil) despite appropriate dosing. On day 20 of treatment, the patient developed grade 4 neutropenia and septic shock and required ICU admission. Genetic testing identified a rare heterozygous DPYD variant (c. 812delT) that causes a frameshift and a presumed loss of enzyme function, suggesting an underlying cause of the severe adverse events. Although severe toxicity occurred, marked tumor shrinkage allowed for less invasive surgery. This case highlights the need for expanded genetic screening beyond the guideline-listed variants, particularly in Asian populations, where common variants differ. Combining genotypic and phenotypic evaluations of DPD activity may improve the prediction and prevention of FP-related toxicities, supporting safer and more effective use of FP drugs.

Introduction

Dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD) is an enzyme encoded by the DPYD gene that is involved in the catabolism of the pyrimidine bases uracil and thymine [1]. It is also recognized as the rate-limiting enzyme in the major metabolic pathways of fluoropyrimidine (FP) drugs such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), tegafur, and capecitabine. Decreased DPD activity is associated with increased risk of severe adverse reactions to FP drugs [2]. DPYD loss-of-function variants that cause decreased DPD activity are associated with an increased risk of treatment-related mortality [3]. The DPYD gene harbors multiple genetic mutations, and the guidelines established by the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) recommend dose reduction or avoidance of FP drugs in patients with decreased DPD activity because of four specific polymorphisms: c.1905 + 1G > A (rs3918290, DPYD*2A), c.1679T > G (rs55886062, DPYD*13), c.2846 A > T (rs67376798), and c.1129–5923 C > G (rs75017182) [4]. However, the frequencies and spectra of clinically relevant DPYD variants differ substantially among ethnic groups [5]. In Japan, DPYD genotyping is not currently covered by the national health insurance system, and DPYD is not among the major recommended targets in the pharmacogenetic testing recommendations issued by The Japanese Society of Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics [6]. Therefore, routine testing before the initiation of FP-based chemotherapy has not yet been implemented in Japan. In addition to these polymorphisms, many other rare variants contribute to individual differences in DPD activity [2, 7]. Although many of these variants have been identified through genetic analyses, detailed case reports describing their clinical course remain limited [8–11].

Here, we present a case of severe myelosuppression and diarrhea after the initiation of tegafur, gimeracil, and oteracil potassium combination tablets (S-1). Sequencing analysis of the DPYD gene revealed a rare variant distinct from the four gene polymorphisms described in the CPIC guidelines. To the best of our knowledge, no prior case reports have documented the relationship between this rare variant and its clinical impact. Therefore, we present this case as a valuable report that systematically summarizes the genetic findings, assessment of DPD activity, and clinical course.

Case presentation

A man in his 50s visited a local doctor with tongue pain. The patient had no significant medical history, allergies, medications, or smoking. The patient was referred to our hospital by a local doctor, and an ulcerative lesion measuring 39 mm in length was observed extending from the left margin of the tongue to the floor of the mouth. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evaluation revealed that the estimated depth of invasion (DOI) of the tumor is approximately 12 mm. Based on the biopsy results, the patient was diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma of the tongue (cT3N0M0). S-1 was initiated as neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and hemiglossectomy with free-flap reconstruction was planned. The patient had a body surface area of 1.62 m² and creatinine clearance of 83 mL/min. Following the dosing recommendations in the package insert, 60 mg S-1 was prescribed twice daily as outpatient therapy (day 0). The clinical course after initiation of S-1 therapy is shown in Fig. 1. On day 14, the tumor had visibly shrunk. On day 15, the patient began to develop diarrhea, and on day 18, S-1 was discontinued due to worsening diarrhea (grade 2) and anorexia. Clostridium butyricum MIYAIRI and loperamide were used to treat the diarrhea. On day 19, the patient developed a fever of 38 °C and generalized joint pain. On day 20, the patient developed leukopenia (grade 4), neutropenia (grade 4), fever, and dehydration (grade 3) and was urgently hospitalized. On day 22, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit with hypovolemia and septic shock. The patient was discharged from the intensive care unit on day 25, and his clinical symptoms stabilized thereafter. On day 33, the estimated DOI decreased to approximately 1–2 mm. On day 35, the tumor had markedly shrunk to a long diameter of 8.4 mm. Therefore, partial tongue resection was performed on day 38 as a de-escalation from the initially planned tongue reconstruction procedure. Furthermore, postoperative pathological examination revealed a DOI of 0 mm. The subsequent course was uneventful even with the discontinuation of S-1, and the patient was discharged on day 48. Notably, no recurrence occurred during 2.5 years of postoperative follow-up.

We considered the possibility of an adverse reaction caused by S-1 as the cause of severe myelosuppression and diarrhea observed in the patient. To determine subsequent treatment strategies, we performed a sequencing analysis of the DPYD gene and genes related to S-1 metabolism. FP-related toxicity associated with metabolic abnormalities due to variants in metabolism-related genes, such as DPYD, DPYS, miR27a, and CYP2A6 [12–14]. After extracting genomic DNA from the blood, regions of interest were amplified by PCR, and each fragment was analyzed by Sanger sequencing [5]. Genetic analysis targeted exons 1 through 23, intron 10, and a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) (rs4294451) in the enhancer region of the DPYD gene; exons 1 through 9 of the DPYS gene, which encodes dihydropyrimidinase (DHP); exons 1 through 9 and copy number variations (CNV) of the CYP2A6 gene; and the flanking region of a SNP (rs895819, c.40 A > G) in microRNA 27a (miR27a), which regulates the transcription of the DPYD gene. Sequence analysis revealed two heterozygous SNPs: c.812delT (rs1660305188) and c.1627 A > G (rs1801159, DPYD*5) in DPYD, homozygous c.-1T > C (rs2959023) in DPYS, heterozygous complete deletion variant (CYP2A6*4) in CYP2A6, and homozygous c.40 A > G (rs895819) in miR27a. In addition to the genetic analysis, the concentrations of uracil and dihydrouracil, which serve as endogenous and indirect markers of DPD activity, were measured using a fully validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) [15]. Blood samples were collected during the morning outpatient visit, 63 days after discontinuation of S-1, and stored under refrigerated conditions until analysis. Uracil (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and dihydrouracil (Alfa Aesar, Ward Hill, MA, USA) were used as calibration standards. Stable isotope-labeled internal standards, U-15N2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) and UH2-2H4 (CDN Isotopes, Pointe-Claire, Quebec, Canada), were used for quantification. The uracil concentration of this patient on day 81 was 95.5 ± 6.5 ng/mL, which was higher than that of commercially available pooled plasma control, with a uracil concentration of 11.5 ± 2.4 ng/mL (Table 2). Furthermore, the dihydrouracil/uracil (UH2/U) ratio was 3.7 ± 0.5, below the cutoff value of 6.0 that is associated with severe toxicity [16].

Discussion

Here, we report a patient with DPYD variants who developed severe and prolonged myelosuppression and diarrhea after receiving S-1. However, the tumor significantly shrank, allowing de-escalation of the initially planned surgical procedure. Sequence analysis identified two heterozygous SNPs in DPYD. Because the S-1 dosage was appropriate based on the patient’s body surface area and renal function, the adverse events were likely attributable to impaired drug metabolism due to DPD deficiency.

The genetic variant identified in this case is not included in the DPYD risk variants specified in the CPIC guidelines [4]. However, based on the results of the genetic and phenotypic assessments, DPD activity was markedly reduced in this case. Therefore, not only S-1 but also all fluoropyrimidine therapies were considered inappropriate. After discussion with the attending physician, a decision was made to avoid fluoropyrimidine-based therapy in subsequent treatments.

Fluoropyrimidine-based drugs, including 5-FU, exhibit a dose-response relationship with respect to both efficacy and toxicity [17]. The frequency of serious adverse events has been reported to be 30% for grade 3, 3–5% for grade 4, and 0.1–1% for grade 5 [18]. In the present case, bone marrow suppression was observed 20 days after the initiation of S-1. Previous reports have shown that hematologic toxicity associated with S-1 occurs most frequently after three weeks of treatment [19], and that the median time to the nadir white blood cell count is 27 (range: 4–43) days [20]. Compared with these reports, the timing was generally consistent with that of previous reports. However, because the patient was receiving S-1 on an outpatient basis and blood tests were not performed until hospitalization, the actual onset may have occurred earlier. Diarrhea appeared on day 15 after the initiation of S-1 treatment, which was slightly earlier than the reported median onset time of 24.5 (range: 2-183) days [20]. The patient received G-CSF daily for myelosuppression, which improved over seven days, and extracellular fluid replacement for diarrhea, which resolved after approximately three weeks. The reported time required for improvement of adverse events is seven days for myelosuppression and nine days for diarrhea [20]. However, in this case, the duration of diarrhea was particularly prolonged, which subsequently led to the development of enteritis and the occurrence of Grade 3 dehydration and shock. Patients with decreased DPD activity are known to develop toxicity earlier and more severely than those with normal DPD activity [2]. The early onset, increased severity, and prolonged duration of myelosuppression and diarrhea suggest that the metabolism or excretion of S-1 was impaired, leading to prolonged drug effects.

DPD inactivates 80–90% of 5-FU in the liver [21]. In the DPD-mediated metabolic pathway, 5-FU is first reduced to dihydro-5-fluorouracil by DPD. It is then hydrolyzed by DHP, encoded by the DPYS gene, into the reaction product fluoro-β-ureidopropionic acid (FUPA). Partial or complete DPD deficiency can lead to elevated and accumulated plasma levels of 5-FU, potentially causing severe and prolonged myelosuppression and diarrhea. Evaluation of DPD activity is also useful for predicting toxicity [18]. One of the primary methods for assessing DPD activity is evaluation of polymorphisms in the DPYD gene. However, the types and frequencies of DPYD variants differ among ethnic groups, and the four DPYD polymorphisms for which pretreatment testing is recommended in the CPIC guidelines have not been identified in Asian populations, including the Japanese [5]. As there are currently no recommendations regarding pretreatment screening for these DPYD variants in Japan, pretreatment screening was not performed in the present case. Moreover, genomic analysis studies conducted in Japanese populations have identified several novel DPYD variants, and subsequent in vitro functional analyses have demonstrated markedly reduced DPD activity in some of these variants [5]. Collectively, these findings suggest that, although the major DPYD risk variants recommended in current Western guidelines are rare in Asian populations, other rare or population-specific variants may contribute to severe FP-related toxicity.

In this study, we analyzed DPYD and the genes related to S-1 metabolism. The DPYD c.812delT (p.L271fs) variant detected in this case was reported to have an allele frequency of 0.0147% in a Japanese population [22]. The DPYD c.812delT variant induces a frameshift due to a single-nucleotide deletion, leading to the generation of a premature stop codon around residues 270–271. Consequently, the majority of DPD proteins are predicted to be absent or truncated. Human DPD is an enzyme composed of five major domains [23]. The truncating variant is predicted to result in the complete loss of domain III (residues 287–441), domain IV containing the catalytic center (residues 525–847), and domain V (residues 848–1025) [23]. As domain IV harbors the catalytic center [23–24], its loss in the present variant, this variant is expected to markedly reduce or potentially abolish DPD activity. Consistent with this interpretation, Offer et al. reported that variants on stop codons (E386X) or frameshifts (P633fs) downstream of L271fs result in a complete loss of DPD activity in vitro [25]. Therefore, the present variant, which is located further upstream, is also predicted to result in a complete loss of DPD activity. Accordingly, it is assumed that when a heterozygote carries this gene variant, DPD activity is reduced to approximately half that of the wild-type. DPYD c.1627 A > G is a genetic polymorphism registered as DPYD*5 with an allele frequency of 28.1% in the Japanese population [26]. In vitro analysis of metabolic capacity using 5-FU as a substrate showed intrinsic clearance values of 101% of that of the wild-type, with no effect on DPD activity [7]. Therefore, DPYD c.1627 A > G had little effect on DPD activity, whereas DPYD c.812delT decreased DPD activity.

miR27a downregulates DPD protein expression via the transcriptional regulation of DPYD. The miR27a c.40 A > G polymorphism is energetically stable and significantly downregulates DPD protein expression [12]. miR27a c.40 A > G has been reported to be present at an allele frequency of 36.4% in the Japanese population [27]. Although its association with 5-FU-induced toxicity in clinical practice has not been clarified, we speculate that the presence of miR27a c.40 A > G in this patient was associated with decreased metabolic activity of 5-FU. DPYS c.-1T > C has been reported to be present at an allele frequency of 69.3% in the Japanese population [28]. Polymorphisms in DPYS c.-1T > C have been reported to cause mucositis and gastrointestinal toxicity, possibly due to impaired 5-FU metabolism [13]. However, no association has been established between this polymorphism and its adverse effects.

Decreased DPD activity in this case was also demonstrated by a lower UH2/U ratio than the cutoff value of 6.0, which is associated with severe fluoropyrimidine toxicity [16]. As DPD activity exhibits circadian variations [29], a previous report recommended blood sampling for DPD phenotyping during the morning period, typically around 8–9 a.m [30]. In the present case, blood samples for DPD phenotyping were collected during the morning outpatient visit and stored under refrigerated conditions until analysis. Therefore, the sampling conditions were considered appropriate for phenotypic evaluation of DPD activity, and the measured UH2/U ratio was consistent with the genetic findings and clinical presentation. Dihydrouracil concentrations were also higher than those in the control group, suggesting that this may be due to a reduction in DHP activity resulting from the c.-1T > C polymorphism in DPYS. Plasma UH2/U dynamics correlated with the results of the genetic mutation analysis, suggesting that decreased DPD activity was the cause of the serious adverse events associated with S-1. Although the UH2/U ratio was measured following a period of time after S-1 discontinuation, it is thought to reflect baseline DPD deficiency stemming from genetic mutations. During S-1 therapy, DPD activity could be further reduced compared with the levels reflected by the UH2/U ratio, owing to DPD inhibition by the gimeracil included in S-1.

CYP2A6 has been shown to metabolize tegafur to 5-FU in vitro, which potentially influences the therapeutic efficacy of S-1 [14]. CYP2A6*4 is a polymorphism in which the gene encoding CYP2A6 is completely defective, and is present at a frequency of 4.7% in the Japanese population [31]. It has been suggested that the presence of CYP2A6*4 results in the reduced metabolic activation of 5-FU and inadequate therapeutic effects [32]. However, because the patient was heterozygous, this effect was presumed to be limited. Although DPYD c.812delT was considered the primary genetic factor associated with reduced DPD activity in the present case, the possible contribution of miR27a c.40 A > G should also be considered. As these variants may affect DPD activity through different mechanisms, the severe toxicity observed in our patient may reflect the cumulative effects of these variants.

This case report has certain limitations. First, the expression of the abnormal DPD protein resulting from the DPYD c.812delT variant was not confirmed, and the expressed truncated protein was not functionally evaluated. However, considering the location of the variant, the resulting truncation is predicted to cause loss of domains essential for DPD enzymatic activity. Furthermore, the expression of dysfunctional DPD protein is indirectly supported by the decrease in the UH2/U ratio and the onset of severe FP-related toxicity. Second, genetic testing for TYMS and MTHFR mutations. Although some reports have indicated an association between these genes and increased 5-FU toxicity, [33–34] their clinical utility remains less established in the current CPIC guidelines, in contrast to DPYD [4].

Although the advantages of detecting major variants of DPYD prior to FP administration have been demonstrated, the sensitivity of these tests remains low, and a negative test result cannot rule out the risk of severe adverse events [35]. To improve sensitivity, it is necessary to investigate rare variants that contribute to severe adverse events. Case reports summarizing the relationship between genetic variants, phenotypes, and clinical courses, such as the present case, are considered highly valuable for estimating the risk of adverse events associated with FP drugs. Because DPD activity is also influenced by transcriptional and post-transcriptional effects on the DPYD gene [36], evaluating DPD activity solely through DPYD gene analysis poses the risk of overlooking patients who may develop severe adverse events [37]. Phenotypic assessment of DPD activity has the potential to screen such patients [2], and its implementation in clinical practice under insurance coverage is desirable, although numerous challenges remain.

Conclusion

Here, we report a case of severe toxicity during S-1 treatment in a DPYD c.812delT variant carrier. The patient’s U/UH2 ratio was significantly higher than the reference values, suggesting that increased systemic exposure to fluoropyrimidines driven by reduced DPD activity was the primary cause of the adverse events. These findings suggest that FP-based regimens should be used with extreme caution, and alternative therapies may be considered in patients carrying the DPYD c.812delT variant, particularly when accompanied by phenotypic evidence of decreased DPD activity.
