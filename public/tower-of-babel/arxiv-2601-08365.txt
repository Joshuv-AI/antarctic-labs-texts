Impacted Buildings for GL ⁡ ( 2 ) \GL(2) Journal: arXiv
Malors Espinosa, Zander Karaganis

Abstract
In this paper we define a generating function for buildings of type A ~ 1 \widetilde{A}_{1} (i.e. trees) that are enhanced with a certain filtration structure. We prove that this generating function recovers the zeta function of certain quadratic orders. We do this by studying how the ideals of the orders distribute in the building of S ​ L ​ ( 2 , K ) SL(2,K) .

Introduction

1.1
The goal of this article

This paper is concerned with the way that certain zeta functions can be defined and computed. Let
K
be a
p
-adic field and
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
be its ring of integers. Let
L
be a reduced
K
-algebra of dimension
2
over
K
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
the integral closure of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
in
L
. In
[
13
]
, Yun defines for a given order
{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq L
a zeta function
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}}
and proves it is a rational function

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}}(s)=\dfrac{P(q^{-s})}{V(q^{-s})},

where
q
is the cardinality of the residue field of
K
. The polynomial
V
is determined by
L/K
, while the polynomial
P
is determined by
{\mathcal{O}}
.

Let
E
be a quadratic extension of a number field
F
. For this extension, Langlands in
[
11
]
and Altuğ in
[
1
]
, construct a formula for the evaluation of the product of certain local orbital integrals. This formula is essential for the success of the analysis being carried out. However, in the aforementioned papers, its source is
ad hoc
. Arthur, in
[
2
]
, predicted that the polynomials
P
, as
K
varies on the localization at different primes of
E
(and
L
being the appropriate extension according to the splitting behavior of the prime), recover the Euler factors of the formulas. Consequently, it became of interest to compute these polynomials for the quadratic case. They are computed in
[
6
]
. Then, in
[
7
]
, the explicit polynomials are used to recover the formulas, thus confirming the prediction of Arthur.

The above process leaves two problems to be discussed. On the one hand, in
[
13
]
it is proven that these order zeta functions exists and continue to be rational for
L/K
of dimension
n
. Furthermore, the corresponding polynomials
P
are also expected to be the local factors for formulas that can play the analogous role as those of Langlands and Altuğ did for the quadratic case. The method in
[
6
]
runs into complications when dealing with
n\geq 3
.

To compute the zeta functions in
[
6
]
, one verifies that in the cases of interest, they are the classical ideal counting zeta functions. Then one proves it is enough to study the contribution of the principal ideals to the zeta function. In order to quantify this contribution, the principal ideals are classified in different classes, and then the cardinality of each class is explicitly computed. For higher dimension cases, both steps are unclear for it is not evident how to organize ideals. On the other hand, the importance of these formulas arises from their relationship with orbital integrals for
\GL(n)
. When this group changes, it is unclear what the analogous zeta functions could be.

It is tempting to approach both of these issues through the Bruhat-Tits building alone, however, such an approach has yet to be employed.
The difficulty with this approach is that these zeta functions count ideals, as opposed to rank
2
lattices. This obscures their distribution in the building and hence hinders their counting. Nevertheless, to find such a possibility is desirable because such methods could be transferred to other groups and their Bruhat-Tits building.

The purpose of this article is to give a construction in the building of
\SL(2,K)
(i.e. a tree) and then reinterpret and compute the zeta function
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}}
with these new definitions.

Results and organization of this article

Our first main definition is the following one.

Definition A
.

An

is a pair
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}})

{\mathscr{B}}

{\mathscr{P}}

Given an impacted building we define for

{\mathscr{P}}_{n}=\left\{v\in V({\mathscr{B}})\mid d(x,{\mathscr{P}}_{n-1})\leq 1\right\}.

Consequently, we have a layer structure around the basin
{\mathscr{P}}
, by defining

{\mathscr{R}}_{n}={\mathscr{P}}_{n}\backslash{\mathscr{P}}_{n-1},

and
{\mathscr{R}}_{0}={\mathscr{P}}_{0}={\mathscr{P}}
.

Given a vertex
v
, we denote by

as the unique
n

x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
. With this, we are able to provide our second main definition.

Definition B
.

Let
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}})
be an impacted building and let
v
be any vertex of
{\mathscr{B}}
. We define the
layer generating function from v
as

\zeta_{v}^{{\mathscr{R}}}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\#\left\{x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}\mid x\mbox{ can be reached by a path of length }d\mbox{ from }v\right\}X^{d}.

The definition of impacted building shows that the building by itself is not enough to make the desired computations. A basin, and hence a particular structuring into layers, is also required. This reorganization is captured in the coefficients of the generating functions from the vertices. We will discuss these definitions and related ones, as well as some of their basic properties, in Section
2
.

Once we are in this context, three particular choices for
{\mathscr{P}}
become important. A single vertex, a single edge or a whole apartment. We will call the corresponding impacted buildings the ramified, unramified and split impacted buildings and denote them
{\mathscr{B}}_{R},{\mathscr{B}}_{U},{\mathscr{B}}_{S}
. In Section
3
we will compute the corresponding zeta functions for these three cases. As we will see, the computations are straightforward and do not require any knowledge from arithmetic origin. They belong to the impacted building alone. In particular, at this stage these computations recover the values of
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}}
for the quadratic case, even though we are not yet making the explicit connection between the two perspectives.

Section
4
briefly discusses the results of
[
6
]
. We need these results to be able to establish the connections between the two perspectives. We keep the discussion as brief as possible and refer the reader to the original paper for proofs. In Section
5
we use the reviewed results to explicitly locate the ideals of the orders
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
we care about (and define in Section
4
). Concretely, we prove

Theorem A
.

Let
L/K
be one of our three cases (i.e. ramified, unramified, or split),
n\geq 0
and
{\mathscr{B}}
the corresponding impacted building (i.e. ramified, unramified, or split, respectively). A vertex
v
of
{\mathscr{B}}
represents a (principal, rank
2
) ideal of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
if and only if
v\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
. In here
{\mathscr{B}}
is the corresponding building.

This theorem shows that the impacted buildings that we have independently studied indeed contain among them those required for the study of the zeta functions of orders. We have mentioned above that the study of the zeta function succeeds because it can be reduced to that of the principal ideals. Instead, in the impacted building we have the basin generating functions. The theorem above is qualitatively establishing that the principal ideals are to the ideals what the layer
{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is to
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
.

In order to solidify that these are the same structure seen from two different views, we must prove that quantitatively they also correspond. That is, their contribution to the corresponding zeta functions coincide. That is our main theorem:

Theorem B
.

Let
L/K
be one of the above considered extensions (i.e. unramified, ramified, split extensions) and
{\mathscr{B}}
the corresponding impacted building (i.e.
{\mathscr{B}}_{U},{\mathscr{B}}_{R}
,
{\mathscr{B}}_{S}
). Then we have the equality

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(s)=\zeta_{{\mathscr{B}},{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(q^{-s}),

where
\zeta_{{\mathscr{B}},{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(X)
is the generating function from the vertex associated to
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
for the corresponding impacted building and
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}
is the contribution of the principal ideals to
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(s)
.

This theorem is proven in Section
6
. We have mentioned before that in
[
6
]
the contribution of the principal ideals is computable because one can classify them into different types and then count how many ideals of each type exist. The essential idea to prove the main theorem is to describe precisely which types are equivalent to a given vertex
v
of
{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
. We will prove that is codified by the distance
d(v,{\mathcal{O}}_{n})
. In this way, the types disappear from the picture and everything gets reorganized into the layer zeta functions.

In particular, the main theorem and the computations of Section
3
, give an alternative way to obtain the values of the polynomials
P
we have mentioned before. The main difference lies in the fact that we
do not
require to compute how many ideals are of each individual type. Instead, we count how many vertices are at a given distance within a given layer.

2
Impacted Buildings and their Generating Functions

2.1
The Generating Functions from a Vertex

Let
{\mathscr{B}}
be a building with fundamental apartment of type
\widetilde{A}_{1}
; that is, the apartments are real lines with vertices at each integer, and the building is a tree.

We begin with our construction by introducing a simple but fundamental concept.

Definition 1
.

We define an
impacted building
as a pair
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}})
, where
{\mathscr{B}}
is a building of type
\widetilde{A}_{1}
and
{\mathscr{P}}
is a connected subcomplex of
{\mathscr{B}}
which we call the
basin
.

Given an impacted building we construct a filtration

{\mathscr{P}}={\mathscr{P}}_{0}\subset{\mathscr{P}}_{1}\subset{\mathscr{P}}_{2}\subset{\mathscr{P}}_{3}\subset\cdots,

by declaring inductively for
n\geq 1
,

{\mathscr{P}}_{n}=\left\{v\in V({\mathscr{B}})\mid d(x,{\mathscr{P}}_{n-1})\leq 1\right\}.

(1)

Associated with this filtration, we have an associated layer structure around the basin
{\mathscr{P}}
, by defining

{\mathscr{R}}_{n}={\mathscr{P}}_{n}\backslash{\mathscr{P}}_{n-1},

and
{\mathscr{R}}_{0}={\mathscr{P}}_{0}={\mathscr{P}}
.

Remark 1
.

The name comes from the “graphical” idea of a building
{\mathscr{B}}
being impacted by
\mathscr{P}
creating a crater. The basin is the bottom of the crater formed and the different layers given by our filtration form a topographical map of the crater.

Using this construction, we define the following concept for a vertex.

Definition 2
.

Let
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}})
be an impacted building and let
v
be any vertex of
{\mathscr{B}}
. We define the
height of v
to be minimal
n
such that
v\in{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
. Equivalently, the height of
v
is the unique
n
such that
v\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
. We shall denote the height of
v
by
h(v)
or
h_{{\mathscr{P}}}(v)
if we wish to emphasize the basin.

Using this structure, we now define two generating functions, derived solely from combinatorial data arising in the impacted buildings.

Definition 3
.

Let
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}})
be an impacted building and let
v
be any vertex of
{\mathscr{B}}
. We define the
layer generating function from v
as

\zeta_{v}^{{\mathscr{R}}}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\#\left\{x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}\mid x\mbox{ can be reached by a path of length }d\mbox{ from }v\right\}X^{d}.

(2)

We also define the
basin generating function from v
as

\zeta_{v}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\#\left\{x\in{\mathscr{P}}_{h(v)}\mid x\mbox{ can be reached by a path of length }d\mbox{ from }v\right\}X^{d}.

(3)

To simplify notation, we shall denote
\zeta_{v}^{{\mathscr{R}}}
by
\zeta_{v}
. However, we shall always write
\zeta_{v}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)
to denote the basin generating functions. Also, for the sake of clarity we denote the coefficients of these generating functions by

r(d,v)=\#\{x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x\mbox{ can be reached by a path of length }d\mbox{ from }v\},

and

p(d,v)=\#\{x\in{\mathscr{P}}_{h(v)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x\mbox{ can be reached by a path of length }d\mbox{ from }v\},

Remark 2
.

We emphasize that we are not requiring the path to be nonintersecting nor to be contained entirely
{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}
or
{\mathscr{P}}_{h(v)}
.

Example 1
.

The Coxeter Complex of type
\widetilde{A}_{1}
is the real line with vertices of two alternating types (which we show by painting them of two colors: red and blue) the type of a vertex is determined by the parity of its distance from
0
. Figure

shows two “impacts” on this building.

In Figure

we have

consists of a chamber. The choice of the name “unramified” and “ramified” will become clear in Section

Let us compute some layer generating functions in the “unramified” case. Fix a half apartment with edge at the point on the basin and call
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
its unique vertex in
{\mathscr{R}}_{n}

{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
consists of exactly two vertices of the same color, except for

2.2
The Way Out

The constructions in the previous section only require an impacted building
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}})
. With this information we can construct
\zeta_{v}(X)
for all
v
. We now aim to put more structure to the building by constructing a “bigger” generating function recursively; recall that
{\mathscr{B}}
is of type
\widetilde{A}_{1}
.

Definition 4
.

Let
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}})
be an impacted building and
{\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathscr{P}}
. We define a
way out (from
{\mathcal{O}}
)
as a half apartment
{\mathscr{H}}
such that

1.

its boundary vertex is
{\mathcal{O}}
,

2.

for each
n=0,1,2,...
,
{\mathscr{H}}
there exists a unique vertex
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
of height
h({\mathcal{O}}_{n})=n
.

We denote by
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}},{\mathscr{H}})
a choice of impacted building with a way out.

Remark 3
.

Noitce that a way out does not necessarily exist for every
{\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathscr{P}}
. In all the cases we will work with it is clear that it does.

The purpose of having a way out is to chose, among all the vertices, a particular collection of generating functions from the vertices of
{\mathscr{H}}
. That is, we have highlighted the importance of

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{0}},\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{1}},\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{2}},\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{3}},...

and of

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{0}}^{{\mathscr{P}}},\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{1}}^{{\mathscr{P}}},\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{2}}^{{\mathscr{P}}},\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{3}}^{{\mathscr{P}}},...

Before we can establish the fundamental relationship between the above functions defined in this way, we must discuss some simple combinatorial properties of these generating functions.

2.3
Combinatorics, Rationality and the Recurrence Relation

In Definition
3
the coefficient of
X^{d}
, in either case, requires a path that is not necessarily a geodesic path. This leads naturally to the related generating functions

\displaystyle\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}^{{\mathscr{R}}}(X)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\left\{x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}\mid x\mbox{ can be reached by a geodesic path of length }d\mbox{ from }v\right\}X^{d},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\left\{x\in{\mathscr{P}}_{h(v)}\mid x\mbox{ can be reached by a geodesic path of length }d\mbox{ from }v\right\}X^{d}.

We shall also omit the
{\mathscr{R}}
from the notation and write simply
\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}
. Similar to Definition
3
, we set
\widetilde{r}(d,v)
to be the coefficient of
X^{d}
in
\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}^{{\mathscr{R}}}(X)
and
\widetilde{p}(d,v)
that of
X^{d}
in
\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}})
be an impacted building, and
v
a vertex of
{\mathscr{B}}
, then

\zeta_{v}(X)=\dfrac{\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}(X)}{1-X^{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\zeta_{v}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)=\dfrac{\widetilde{\zeta}^{{\mathscr{P}}}_{v}(X)}{1-X^{2}}.

Proof.

Both equalities are proven using the same method; we will consider only the first. Set
n=h(v)
; then when counting coefficients for
\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}(X)
, for each
x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
, it will only be counted once: exactly when
d=d(x,v)
. Hence, we have that

\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}(X)=\sum_{x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}}X^{d(x,v)}.

By our previous comment, we notice that when
d\in d(x,v)+2{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, there is a path of length
d
connecting
x
and
v
. This shows that

\zeta_{v}(X)=\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}}\sum_{\ell\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}}X^{d(x,v)+\ell}=\dfrac{1}{1-X^{2}}\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}}X^{d(x,v)}=\dfrac{\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}(X)}{1-X^{2}},

concluding the proof.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}})
be an impacted building with
{\mathscr{P}}
finite, then
\zeta_{v}(X)
and
\zeta_{v}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)
are rational functions.

Proof.

If
{\mathscr{P}}
is finite, then every
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
is finite. In particular, each of them has a finite diameter. This implies that
\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}
and
\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}^{{\mathscr{P}}}
are polynomials because the coefficient of
X^{d}
vanishes for
d
greater than the diameter. This concludes the proof.
∎

Example 2
.

Let us return to the constructions of Example
1
, to highlight how Corollary
1
applies to save computational effort. In the “unramified” impact, we have seen that for
n\geq 1
there are two vertices of the same color in
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
. One is at distance
0
from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. The other is at distance
2n
. Thus

\widetilde{\zeta}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}=1+X^{2n}.

Corollary
1
implies

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}=\dfrac{1+X^{2n}}{1-X^{2}},

agreeing with our previous work. We also know that there is exactly one vertex at each of the distances
0,1,2,...,2n
in
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
. Thus

\widetilde{\zeta}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)=1+X+X^{2}+\cdots+X^{2n}=\dfrac{1-X^{2n+1}}{1-X}.

As a consequence, we get

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)=\dfrac{1+X+X^{2}+\cdots+X^{2n}}{1-X^{2}}=\dfrac{1-X^{2n+1}}{(1-X)(1-X^{2})}.

In the “ramified” impact, we have that from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
there is exactly a vertex at distance
0,1,...,2n+1
in
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
. Thus

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)=\dfrac{1+X+\cdots+X^{2n+1}}{1-X^{2}}=\dfrac{(1+X)(1+X^{2}+\cdots+X^{2n})}{1-X^{2}}=\dfrac{1+X^{2}+\cdots+X^{2n}}{1-X}.

\square

The functions
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}
also admit a recursive formula.

Theorem 1
.

Let
({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{P}},{\mathscr{H}})
be an impacted building with way out, then for
n\geq 0
we have

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n+1}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)=\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n+1}}(X)+X\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X).

Proof.

Using Proposition
1
, we see it is enough to prove

\widetilde{\zeta}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n+1}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)=\widetilde{\zeta}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n+1}}(X)+X\widetilde{\zeta}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X).

Let
d\geq 1
be given; comparing coefficients of
X^{d}
and unraveling the definition of
\widetilde{\zeta}_{v}(X)
yields that an equivalent formulation of this is

\widetilde{p}({\mathcal{O}}_{n+1},d)=\widetilde{r}({\mathcal{O}}_{n+1},d)+\widetilde{p}({\mathcal{O}}_{n},d-1).

We now notice that
{\mathscr{P}}_{n+1}={\mathscr{R}}_{n+1}\sqcup{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
. Because we are counting geodesics and
{\mathscr{B}}
is a tree, the only way from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n+1}
to enter
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
is through
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. Moreover, such paths must immediately go from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n+1}
to
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
to prevent self-intersection. This defines a bijection between paths contributing to
\widetilde{p}({\mathcal{O}}_{n+1},d)
not contributing to
\widetilde{r}({\mathcal{O}}_{n+1},d)
and paths contributing to
\widetilde{p}({\mathcal{O}}_{n},d-1)
, yielding our result.
∎

3
Explicit computations of generating functions

The purpose of this section is to study three families of impacted buildings that arise naturally in the study of the zeta functions of orders discussed in the introduction. We call them the “unramified,” “ramified,” and “split” cases due to their connection with the zeta functions of orders. Specifically, the zeta functions of each of these cases corresponds to an impacted building of the corresponding family. In this section, we continue to work exclusively with the buildings without the appearance of arithmetic.

Definition 5
.

Let
m\geq 2
be a positive integer. We denote by
{\mathscr{T}}_{m}
the homogeneous tree of degree
m+1
.

For each of the above trees we now define an impacted building with way out structure. We describe the basins first.

Definition 6
.

Let
m\geq 2
be a positive integer. We define the following basins:

1.

The unramified basin
{\mathscr{P}}_{U}
consists of a single vertex.

2.

The ramified basin
{\mathscr{P}}_{R}
consists of a single edge.

3.

The split basin
{\mathscr{P}}_{S}
consists of a single apartment.

We denote the impacted building
({\mathscr{T}}_{m},{\mathscr{P}}_{U})
,
({\mathscr{T}}_{m},{\mathscr{P}}_{R})
and
({\mathscr{T}}_{m},{\mathscr{P}}_{S})
by
{\mathscr{B}}_{U}(m)
,
{\mathscr{B}}_{R}(m)
and
{\mathscr{B}}_{S}(m)
, respectively. We call them the unramified, ramified and split impacted buildings of degree
m+1
. We will sometimes omit the word “impacted” as there will be no possibility of confusion. When we do not need to specify a particular case we will drop the subindex.

In each of the above cases, we can fix a vertex
{\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathscr{P}}
and follow a half apartment from
{\mathcal{O}}

{\mathscr{P}}
. Notice, we cannot do this if

in the split case, which explains our choice of

. For the ramified and unramified case, we can extend our definitions. We have previously seen these constructions in Examples
1

3.1
The unramified case

In this case, we think of
{\mathscr{B}}_{U}(m)
as a tree with a single root
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
. One immediately sees that
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
consists of all vertices at distance at most
n

{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
. In particular, the vertices at distance exactly

{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
. All of them will be of the same type and the maximum distance between any such vertices is

Lemma 1
.

Let
v\neq{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
be any vertex of
{\mathscr{B}}_{U}(m)
, then

\mid\!{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}\!\mid=(m+1)m^{h(v)-1}

Proof.

In this case,
{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}
consists of the vertices at distance
h(v)
from
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
. By this, we count
m+1
branches out of
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
. Moreover, for the next layer, each vertex adds
m
new vertices per branch. Thus, per possible branch, we get
m^{h(v)-1}
vertices in the
h(v)
-layer; the result follows.
∎

Proposition 2
.

Let
v\neq{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
be a vertex in
{\mathscr{B}}_{U}(m)
. The values
r(d,v)
are given by

r(d,v)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $d$ is odd,}\\
m^{d/2}&\text{if }d<2h(v),\,\text{and $d$ is even,}\\
(m+1)m^{h(v)-1}&\text{if }d\geq 2h(v),\,\text{and $d$ is even.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Because all the vertices in
{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}
have the same color, they must be at even distance one from each other. This proves the statement for
d
odd. On the other hand, when
d\geq 2h(v)
is even, every vertex in
{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}
can be reached. Thus Lemma
1
implies the result in this case.

We proceed now to when
d<2h(v)
and even; write
d=2k
. Then, there exists a unique branch of
{\mathscr{B}}_{U}
whose intersection with
{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}
is at distance exactly
2h(v)
from
v.
By the above logic, we may reach every vertex of distance
v-k
from
k
, except those on the one branch defined by this unique point. This proves the theorem, applying Lemma
1
once more.
∎

We may now compute the basin generating functions associated to our way out.

Proposition 3
.

In the unramified case, we have

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(X)=\dfrac{1+(m-1)X^{2}+m(m-1)X^{4}+\cdots+m^{n-2}(m-1)X^{2(n-1)}+m^{n}X^{2n}}{1-X^{2}}.

and

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)=\dfrac{1+X+m(X^{2}+X^{3})+\cdots+m^{n}(X^{2n-2}+X^{2n-1})+m^{n}X^{2n}}{1-X^{2}}.

Proof.

By Proposition
2
,

\displaystyle\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{h(v)}}

\displaystyle=1+mX^{2}+m^{2}X^{4}+\cdots+m^{n-1}X^{2n-2}+(m+1)m^{n-1}X^{2n}(1+X^{2}+X^{4}+\cdots)

\displaystyle=1+mX^{2}+m^{2}X^{4}+\cdots+m^{n-1}X^{2n-2}+\frac{(m+1)m^{n-1}X^{2n}}{1-X^{2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1+(m-1)X^{2}+m(m-1)X^{4}+\cdots+m^{n-2}(m-1)X^{2(n-1)}+m^{n}X^{2n}}{1-X^{2}}.

Using Theorem
1
recursively gives

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}X^{i}\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n-i}}

Substituting the values for each of
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n-i}}
in the above equation and chasing powers of
X
(which amounts to a tedious but straightforward calculation) yields

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)=\dfrac{1+X+m(X^{2}+X^{3})+\cdots+m^{n}(X^{2n-2}+X^{2n-1})+m^{n}X^{2n}}{1-X^{2}}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4
.

By Proposition
1
, the above implies that in the unramified case

\widetilde{\zeta}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{{\mathscr{P}}}(X)=1+X+m(X^{2}+X^{3})+\cdots+m^{n-1}(X^{2n-2}+X^{2n-1})+m^{n}X^{2n}.

One can thus interpret these coefficients as the number of vertices at the corresponding exact distances from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. We leave to the reader to carry out this interpretation.

3.2
The ramified case

Continuing with the strategy outlined at the start of this section, we note that
\mathscr{B}_{R}(m)
can be thought of as the tree
\mathcal{T}_{m}
with basis the chamber defined by
{\mathcal{O}}_{0},{\mathcal{O}}_{1}
. In particular, we notice that
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
consists of
m
branches from
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
and
m
branches from
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
; if we note that no branches of an
{\mathcal{O}}_{i}
contain an
{\mathcal{O}}_{j}
, this gives a well defined description.

Lemma 2
.

Let
v\not\in\mathscr{P}_{R}
be any vertex of
\mathscr{B}_{R}(m)
, then

|\mathscr{R}_{h(v)}|=2m^{h(v)}.

Proof.

The set of vertices in
{\mathscr{R}}_{h(v)}
is given by
m
branches of the ball
B({\mathcal{O}}_{0};h(v))
and
m
branch of the ball
B({\mathcal{O}}_{1};h(v))
. These sets are clearly disjoint as the distances of their elements from
0
have distinct parity. Hence, the result follows.
∎

Proposition 4
.

Let
v\in{\mathscr{B}}_{R}(m)
and
v\not\in{\mathscr{P}}
be given. The values
r(d,v)
are given by

r(d,v)=\begin{cases}0&d=2k+1,\,k<h(v)\\
m^{k}&d=2k,\,k<h(v)\\
m^{h(v)}&d\geq 2h(v)\\
\end{cases}.

Proof.

To begin, we notice that vertices on opposite sides on the basin
{\mathscr{P}}
have opposite type; hence if
d
is odd, paths must go through the basin, while if
d
they must not. Immediately this gives that if
d
is odd and
d<2k
, then there are no such paths. The case of
d=2k,

k<h(v)
is the same as the proof of Theorem
1
.

Now when
d\geq 2h(v),
the vertices are exactly those contained in the intersection
{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
and of distance
h(v)
from
{\mathcal{O}}_{j(d)}
, where
j(d)=2(\frac{d}{2}-\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor)
,
as the diameter of
{\mathscr{P}}_{n}
is exactly
h(v).
Thus the proof of Lemma
2
applies.
∎

We may now compute the basin generating functions associated to our way out.

Proposition 5
.

In the ramified case, we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-X+mX^{2}-mX^{3}+\cdots+m^{n-1}X^{2n-2}-m^{n-1}X^{2n-1}+m^{n}X^{2n}}{1-X}

\displaystyle\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+mX^{2}+m^{2}X^{4}+\cdots+m^{n}X^{2n}}{1-X}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4
, we find that

\displaystyle\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(X)

\displaystyle=1+mX^{2}+m^{2}X^{4}+\cdots+m^{n-1}X^{2n-2}+m^{n}(X^{2n}+X^{2n+1}+\cdots)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-X}((1-X)(1+mX^{2}+m^{2}X^{4}+\cdots+m^{n-1}X^{2n-2})+m^{n}X^{2n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-X}(1-X+mX^{2}-mX^{3}+\cdots+m^{n-1}X^{2n-2}-m^{n-1}X^{2n-1}+m^{n}X^{2n}).

Moreover, using Theorem
1
yields that

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}X^{i}\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n-i}};

by isolating coefficients in this sum, the result for
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(X)
follows.
∎

3.3
The split case

Before beginning with the split case, we emphasize that here the basin
{\mathscr{P}}
is itself infinite, being an apartment. Moreover, the orders
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
are associated to a half apartment out of
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
, a point in the apartment.

Lemma 3
.

Let
v\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
be given. The numbers
r(d,v)
are given by

r(d,v)=\begin{cases}m^{k-1}&d=2k<2n\\
0&d=2k+1<2n\\
\ell(m-1)m^{n-1}&d=2n+\ell\end{cases}

Proof.

The distance from
v
to the basin is
h(v)
; let us write
n=h(v)
. Hence to enter the basin and leave it again, returning at the same height a distance of at least
2n
must be traveled. In particular, we find that if
d<2n
then the only vertices of
{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
that may be reached are the ones on the same branch as
v.

Now when
d\geq 2n,
one can move
\ell=d-2n
units along the fundamental basin. This leads to
\ell
vertices to leave the basin on (
\ell/2
in each direction). The result follows upon realizing there are
m-1=m+1-2
branches out from each vertex.
∎

Once again, this data is sufficient to compute the zeta functions using our way out.

Proposition 6
.

In the split case, we have

\displaystyle\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-2X+(m-1)X^{2}-2X^{3}+(m-1)X^{4}-\cdots+m^{n-2}(m-1)X^{2(n-1)}-2X^{2n-1}+m^{n}X^{2n}}{(1-X)^{2}}

\displaystyle\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-X+m(X^{2}-X^{3})+\cdots+m^{n}(X^{n}-X^{n+1})}{(1-X)^{2}}.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3
, one computes

\displaystyle\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}

\displaystyle=1+mX^{2}+\cdots+m^{n-1}X^{2n-2}+(m-1)m^{n-1}X^{2n}+2(m-1)m^{n-1}X^{2n+1}+\cdots

\displaystyle=1+mX^{2}+\cdots+m^{n-1}X^{2n-2}+\frac{(m-1)m^{n-1}X^{2n}}{(1-X)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-2X+(m-1)X^{2}-2X^{3}+(m-1)X^{4}-\cdots+m^{n-2}(m-1)X^{2(n-1)}-2X^{2n-1}+m^{n}X^{2n}}{(1-X)^{2}}.

Now, using the recursive formula obtained from Theorem
1
and isolating for coefficients in the numerator yields the result about
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(X)
, concluding our proof.
∎

4
Order polynomials for
\GL(2)
and their computations via ideal types

In this section, we present the constructions used in
[
6
]
to compute the polynomials mentioned in Section
1
, via ideals and their type. Throughout this section, our treatment will be brief, presenting only the main statements; the interested reader can refer to
[
6
]
for these proofs. The results of this section will be used in Section
5
and to prove our main theorem in
6
.

4.1
The setup and the Zeta Function of Orders

Let
K
be a nonarchimedean local field of characteristic zero and
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
its ring of integers. Let
p
be a uniformizer of
K
and
q
the cardinality of the residue field of
K
. Let
L
be a reduced
K
-algebra of dimension
2
over
K
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
the integral closure of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
in
L
. Under these hypotheses, there are three possibilities for
L
: an unramified quadratic extension of
K
, a ramified quadratic extension of
K
, or
K\times K
. We refer to these cases as the
unramified
,
ramified
and
split
case, respectively. When
L
is a field, we shall denote by
\pi
a fixed uniformizer of
L
and by
e
and
f
the ramification and inertia degrees of
L/K
respectively.

The general theory of discrete valuation rings guarantees the existence of
\Delta\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
such that

{\mathcal{O}}_{L}={\mathcal{O}}_{K}[\Delta].

This implies that
L=K(\Delta)
. Based on this we can define

{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\mathcal{O}}_{K}[p^{n}\Delta]\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{L},

for
n=0,1,2,\cdots
. Each of these sets is an order of
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
and we refer to them collectively as the
main sequence of orders
. It is an easy exercise to prove that
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
is independent of
\Delta
. In this section we shall have no need to specify a particular
\Delta
. However, in Section
5
we will pick for each case convenient choices.

In
[
13
]
, Yun defines for (general) orders a zeta function and proves that it is a rational function of
q^{-s}
. For the orders
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
of our main sequence, it can be verified that these zeta functions reduce to

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(s)=\displaystyle\sum_{I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}\dfrac{1}{[{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I]^{s}},

where the sum runs over ideals
I\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
of finite index. One can verify that the ideals of finite index are exactly those that have rank
2
as
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
-modules. We shall always assume that the ideals we are working with satisfy this latter condition.

The aforementioned zeta functions are thus the classical ideal counting functions. In particular, when
L
is a field extension and
n=0
it coincides with its local zeta function.

The rationality of these zeta functions implies there are two polynomials,
P(X)
and
V(X)
such that

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(s)=\dfrac{P(q^{-s})}{V(q^{-s})}.

The denominator polynomial depends uniquely on the extension
L/K
and is easy to compute. On the other hand, the numerator polynomial
P
depends on the particular order
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. Their values are given by the following result.

Theorem 2
.

For each
n\geq 0
define the following polynomials:

\displaystyle R_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=1+qX^{2}+q^{2}X^{4}+...+q^{n}X^{2n},

and for
n\geq 1
define

\displaystyle U_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=(1+X)R_{n-1}(X)+q^{n}X^{2n},

\displaystyle S_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=(1-X)R_{n-1}(X)+q^{n}X^{2n}.

Finally, also put
U_{0}(X)=S_{0}(X)=1
. Explicitly, these polynomials are

\displaystyle R_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=1+qX^{2}+q^{2}X^{4}+...+q^{n}X^{2n},

\displaystyle U_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=1+X+qX^{2}+qX^{3}+...+q^{n-1}X^{2n-2}+q^{n-1}X^{2n-1}+q^{n}X^{2n},

\displaystyle S_{n}(X)

\displaystyle=1-X+qX^{2}-qX^{3}+...+q^{n-1}X^{2n-2}-q^{n-1}X^{2n-1}+q^{n}X^{2n}.

Then we have

\displaystyle(1-q^{-s})\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(s)

\displaystyle=R_{n}(q^{-s}),

\displaystyle(1-q^{-2s})\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(s)

\displaystyle=U_{n}(q^{-s}),

\displaystyle(1-q^{-s})^{2}\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(s)

\displaystyle=S_{n}(q^{-s}).

That is,
R_{n},U_{n}
and
S_{n}
are the numerators of the corresponding zeta functions in the ramified, unramified and split case, respectively.

Proof.

This is Theorem 5.4 of
[
6
]
.
∎

4.2
The Traveling map and the Principal Zeta Function

In
[
6
]
the strategy to prove Theorem
2
requires two steps. The first one establishes a recurrence relation between different zeta functions. This step has as consequence the reduction of the problem to that of computing the contribution from the principal ideals. The second one consists on computing the latter by organizing the principal ideals by their type.
We begin with discussing the recurrence relation.

Definition 7
.

For each integer
n\geq 0
, let
{\mathcal{I}}_{n}
be the set of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
-rank 2 ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. Define the
traveling map

T_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{I}}_{n}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{I}}_{n+1},\quad T_{n}(J)=pJ.

This definition is motivated by the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

The traveling map
T_{n}
is a bijection onto its image, which consists exactly of the non-principal ideals in
{\mathcal{I}}_{n+1}
.

Proof.

This is Theorem 3.22 in
[
6
]
.
∎

This motivates us to isolate the contribution to
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(s)
from the principal ideals.

Definition 8
.

For each
n\geq 0
, we define the
principal ideal zeta function
as

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\\
\text{principal}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{[{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I]^{s}}

A consequence of Theorem
3
, together with some index manipulations, is the recurrence relation we mentioned above.

Theorem 4
.

For
n\geq 1
, the zeta functions of the orders
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
satisfy the recurrence relation

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(s)=\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(s)+q^{-s}\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n-1}}(s).

(4)

Proof.

This is Theorem 4.2 in
[
6
]
.
∎

4.3
Types of Principal Ideals

If one can compute
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(s)
then, by the repeated use of the recurrence relation (
4
), one can compute
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(s)
. In order to do this one introduces the type of an element in
{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
.

Definition 9
.

Let
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{0}={\mathcal{O}}_{L}
be any element. We define its
type
by

\varepsilon(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\valpi(x)&\mbox{in the nonsplit case }\\
(\valp(x_{1}),\valp(x_{2}))&\mbox{in the split case }\end{array}\right.

where in the split case we have
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
. We define a
possible type
as an element of the image of
\varepsilon
. We also define for every
x\in{\mathcal{O}}_{L}

\eta(x)=\min\{\valp(a)\mid a\mbox{ is a coordinate of }\varepsilon(x)\}.

Furthermore, let
I\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
be a principal ideal and
\alpha
one of its generators. We define the
type of
I
by

\varepsilon(I)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varepsilon(\alpha),

and
\eta(I)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\eta(\alpha)
. It is straightforward to verify these definitions are independent of the generator
\alpha
of
I
.

The types thus correspond to the ideals that can contribute to the sum
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}
. In practice, the contribution of ideals is determined by their type. Moreover, the contribution of types is not uniform; rather, they are classified in two main families, depending only on the “magnitude” of a type.

Definition 10
.

Let
\omega
be a possible type. In the nonsplit case, we say
\omega
is a
low type
if
\omega<ne
where
e
is the ramification degree of
L/K
. In the split case, we say
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is a
low type
if
\omega_{1}<n
or
\omega_{2}<n
.
Otherwise, we say
\omega
is a
high type
.

Adapting the above definition to ideals leads to the following definition.

Definition 11
.

Let
\omega
be a possible type of a principal ideal of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. We say
I
is a
low ideal
if
\varepsilon(I)
is a low type. That is, if

\eta(I)<\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}ne&\mbox{in the nonsplit case, }\\
n&\mbox{in the split case. }\end{array}\right.

Otherwise, we say
I
is a
high ideal
. We will refer to
ne
and
n
, respectively in the nonsplit and split cases, as the
ideal threshold
and denote if uniformly by
t_{n}
.

4.4
The computation of the Zeta Function of Orders

With the concept of low and high ideals at hand, we can compute the principal idea zeta function. First, we notice that the index
[{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I]
is uniquely determined by
\epsilon(I)
.

Definition 12
.

Let
\omega
be a possible type of a principal ideal
I\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. We define its
type contribution
as

c(\omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f\omega&\mbox{in the nonsplit case, }\\
\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}&\mbox{in the split case. }\end{array}\right.

In here we are denoting
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
in the split case, where the possible types are pairs of nonnegative integers.

In terms of the type contribution one easily proves

[{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I]=q^{-c(\varepsilon(I))}.

(5)

Let
\omega
be a possible type and define

X_{\omega}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{I\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\mbox{ principal ideal }\mid\;\varepsilon(I)=\omega\}.

Equation (
5
) implies that every ideal in
X_{\omega}
contributes the same to the zeta function. Thus, grouping ideals by type, we deduce

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(s)=\displaystyle\sum_{\omega}\dfrac{|X_{\omega}|}{q^{c(\omega)}}.

With this, we have reduced the computation of the principal zeta function to a two-step-process:

1.

deduce which possible types appear as types of an ideal, and

2.

for each of these types, compute
|X_{\omega}|
.

This process is summarized in the next theorem.

Theorem 5
.

The criteria to determine which possible types are types of principal ideals and their contribution are given by the following facts.

High ideals:

Every possible high type
\omega
is the type of some principal ideal. In such case,

|X_{\omega}|=[{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}].

Low ideals:

In the nonsplit case, a possible low ideal
\omega
is the type of some principal ideal if and only if
n=de
for some integer
d
.

In the split case, a possible low ideal
\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is the type of some principal ideal if and only if
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}=d
.

In any of these cases,

|X_{\omega}|=[{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}]=\dfrac{[{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}]}{[{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}^{*}]}.

Proof.

This is discussed in Section 4 of
[
6
]
.
∎

5
Unit distribution and natural appearance of Impacted Buildings

Notice that the polynomials found in Section
3
coincide with those arising in Theorem
2
. Moreover, the recursive approach in each case is similar.
The Building-theoretic approach in Section
3
uses the recurrence relation appearing from the
n
th layer polynomial and
(n-1)
st basin polynomial to construct the
n
th basin generating function. Similarly, the ideal-theoretic perspective in Section
4
induces this recurrence relation using the traveling map.

This in particular suggests that the principal ideal zeta function
is
a layer generating function evaluated at
X=q^{-s}
. The purpose of Theorem
17
below is to prove this result. The proof of this theorem depends on a dictionary between zeta functions and impacted buildings; this is the objective of the current section. Specifically, we outline how a quadratic reduced algebra
L/K
translates to an impacted building structure (with way out), in the usual Bruhat-Tits building of
\SL(2,K)
.

5.1
The unit structure

The choice of
\Delta
amounts to a choice of basis of
L
over
K
. Thus, it corresponds to a linear isomorphism

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K\times K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x+y\Delta}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(x,y)}

In particular,
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}-
modules of rank
2
on each side correspond with each other; thus, we have an isomorphism of buildings. In particular, we see that we are working on
\mathcal{T}(q)
, the Bruhat-Tits building of
\SL(2,K)
.

The choice of
\Delta
, which puts coordinates on
L
, identifies the
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
-lattices

O_{n}=\{x+yp^{n}\Delta\mid x,y\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}\},\;n\in\mathbb{Z}.

with the vertices of the fundamental apartment (on the right hand side), corresponding to the canonical basis

\{e_{1}=(1,0),e_{2}=(0,1)\},

and whose fundamental chamber (by choice) is given the edge with vertices
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{1}
.

Our goal now is the locate the main sequence of orders, as well as the corresponding ideals in the building of
\SL(2,K)
. In what follows, we shall many times refer to vertices. When we do this we are always thinking on the tree of
\SL(2,K)
under the above identification. In general, we denote the minimal polynomial of
\Delta
by
X^{2}-\tau X+\delta
with
\tau,\delta\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
.

The following families of maps will allow us to understand how the structure of the tree changes in each case to accommodates to the structure of the ideals.

Definition 13
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer. We define the map
M_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/p{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
by

M_{n}(w+zp^{n}\Delta)=zw^{-1}.

We call it the
n
-th slope map
. Notice this is well defined because
w\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{*}
.

Proposition 8
.

For
n\geq 1
, the
n
-th slope map is an epimorphism with
\ker(M_{n})={\mathcal{O}}_{n+1}^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
x_{1}+y_{1}p^{n}\Delta
and
x_{2}+y_{2}p^{n}\Delta
be units of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. Then we have

\displaystyle M_{n}((x_{1}+y_{1}p^{n}\Delta)(x_{2}+y_{2}p^{n}\Delta))

\displaystyle=M_{n}(x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}y_{2}p^{n}\Delta+y_{1}x_{2}p^{n}\Delta+y_{1}y_{2}p^{2n}(\tau\Delta-\delta))

\displaystyle=M_{n}((x_{1}x_{2}-y_{1}y_{2}p^{2n}\delta)+(x_{1}y_{2}+y_{1}x_{2}+y_{1}y_{2}p^{n}\tau)p^{n}\Delta)

\displaystyle=(x_{1}y_{2}+y_{1}x_{2}+y_{1}y_{2}p^{n}\tau)(x_{1}x_{2}-y_{1}y_{2}p^{2n}\delta)^{-1}\pmod{p}

\displaystyle=(x_{1}y_{2}+y_{1}x_{2}))(x_{1}x_{2})^{-1}\pmod{p}

\displaystyle=x_{2}^{-1}y_{2}+x_{1}^{-1}y_{1}\pmod{p}

\displaystyle=M_{n}(x_{1}+y_{1}p^{n}\Delta)+M_{n}(x_{2}+y_{2}p^{n}\Delta).

This proves it is an homomorphism. Furthermore, that is is surjective follows by picking
w=1
and varying
z
in a set of representatives of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/p{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
.

The kernel consists of those units with
M_{n}(x+yp^{n}\Delta)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. That is, such that
yx^{-1}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, which in turn implies
y\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and
R(p)\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{K}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{L}
a fixed set of representatives of
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/p{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. Then the units

u_{t}^{(n)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1+tp^{n}\Delta,\,t\in R(p),

are a set of coset representatives of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}/{\mathcal{O}}_{n+1}^{*}
. In particular, the order of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}/{\mathcal{O}}_{n+1}^{*}
is
q
for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

This is the First Isomorphism theorem applied to Proposition
8
.
∎

When
n=0
, the slope map as stated only makes sense when
L/K
is a totally ramified extension. By Proposition
7
, the quotient
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}/{\mathcal{O}}_{1}^{*}
cannot inject into
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}/p{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
when
L
is unramified, for example.
At this point, we note that the distinction at
n=0
is where the difference in structures arises. Moreover, an explicit map is not necessary; rather, we simply compute the cardinality of the quotient. To do this, Proposition
7
applies at once.

Proposition 9
.

The cardinality of
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}/{\mathcal{O}}_{1}^{*}
is given by

[{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{O}}_{1}^{*}]=\begin{cases}q&\text{in the ramified case,}\\
q+1&\text{in the unramified case,}\\
q-1&\text{in the split case.}\\
\end{cases}

Remark 5
.

The slope maps are used to describe jumps from
{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
to
{\mathscr{R}}_{n+1}
; however, when
n=0,
this descends to describing the different ways to leave the basin
{\mathscr{P}}
.

Definition 14
.

We extend our previous notation and denote by
u_{t}^{(0)}
a fixed set of representatives of
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}/{\mathcal{O}}_{1}^{*}
as
t
varies in
R_{0}(p)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{0,1,...,[{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{O}}_{1}^{*}]-1\}
.

Remark 6
.

When
L/K
is ramified, it is sufficient to take
R_{0}(p)=R(p).

Definition 15
.

Let
1\leq d\leq n
. We call a
d-
tuple,

\alpha=(\alpha_{n-d},\cdots,\alpha_{n-1}),

where
\alpha_{i}\in R(p)
for
0<n-d\leq i\leq n-1
and
\alpha_{0}\in R_{0}(p)
, a
unit index
of length
d
(or simply index if there is no chance for confusion). The set of unit indices of length
d
is denoted by
I_{d}(n)
. In particular,
I(n)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=I_{n}(n)
. Associated to each unit index of length
d
, say
\alpha\in I_{d}(n)
, we define

u_{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=u_{\alpha_{n-d}}^{(n-d)}u_{\alpha_{n-d+1}}^{(n-d+1)}\cdots u_{\alpha_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}.

(6)

With this at hand we can describe a set of representatives of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}^{*}/{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
n\geq 0
be an integer and
0\leq d\leq n
. A set of representatives of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}^{*}/{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}
is

\{u_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in I_{d}(n)\}.

Proof.

For notational simplicity, we will present the proof for
d=n
; the other cases are analogous. Let
\alpha,\beta\in I(n)
be given and suppose
u_{\alpha}
and
u_{\beta}
are equivalent modulo
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}
. Pick
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}\in I_{n-1}(n)
such that

\alpha=(\alpha^{\prime},\alpha_{n-1}),\,\beta=(\beta^{\prime},\beta_{n-1}).

Unraveling Definition
15
yields that
u_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha^{\prime}}u_{\alpha_{n-1}}^{(n-1)},u_{\beta}=u_{\beta^{\prime}}u_{\beta_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}
. Equivalently, we have

u_{\alpha}u_{\beta}^{-1}=u_{\alpha^{\prime}}u_{\beta^{\prime}}^{-1}u_{\alpha_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}\left(u_{\beta_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}\right)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}.

Furthermore,
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{n-1}^{*}
and
u_{\alpha_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}\left(u_{\beta_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}\right)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{n-1}^{*}
(because each of the factors is). From these, we conclude that

u_{\alpha^{\prime}}u_{\beta^{\prime}}^{-1}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{n-1}^{*}.

That is,
u_{\alpha^{\prime}}
and
u_{\beta^{\prime}}
are equivalent modulo
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-1}^{*}
. Repeating this process, we conclude that
u_{\alpha_{0}}^{(0)}
is equivalent to
u_{\beta_{0}}^{(0)}
modulo
{\mathcal{O}}_{1}^{*}
. It follows that
\alpha=\beta
, completing the proof.
∎

5.2
The ramified case

As of yet, we have treated the three cases simultaneously. We now pick a particular choice of
\Delta
in each case to specify our computations. We begin with the ramified case.

Proposition 10
.

In the ramified case
L/K
, we can choose
\Delta=\pi
a uniformizer of
L
satisfying the minimal polynomial

X^{2}-\tau X+\delta=0,

with with
\valp(\tau)=t\geq 1
and
\valp(\delta)=1
(i.e.
\Delta
satisfies an Eisenstein polynomial).

Proof.

For a proof of the existence of such uniformizer as well as for a discussion on Eisenstein polynomials see
[
10
, Chapter II, Section 5]
.
∎

We notice that Proposition
8
applies, irrespective of our choosing
\Delta
. Thus, we set

u_{t}^{(0)}=1+t\pi,t\in R(p).

This choice simplifies computation. We emphasize we can only make this choice in the
ramified
case. Let us now begin locating the ideals.

We begin by understanding the high ideals. Fix a positive integer
n
and let
\omega
be a high type; we recall this means
\omega\geq 2n
. Let
I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
be a high ideal with type
\omega
. Thus, all the generators
\alpha
of
I
satisfy

\valpi(\alpha)=\omega.

In particular, we can write
\alpha=\pi^{\omega}u
, for some unit
u\in{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
(notice the unit is not necessarily in
{\mathcal{O}}_{n})
. We get

I=\alpha{\mathcal{O}}_{n}=\pi^{\omega}u{\mathcal{O}}_{n}.

If we write
\omega=2x+\delta
, with
\delta=0,1
according to whether
\omega
is even or odd, then

I=\alpha{\mathcal{O}}_{n}=\pi^{\omega}u{\mathcal{O}}_{n}=p^{x}\pi^{\delta}u{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\sim\pi^{\delta}u{\mathcal{O}}_{n},

where
\sim
represents equivalence of lattices in the building construction (i.e. homothetic lattices are equivalent). Furthermore, if
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are equivalent modulo
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}
, then we have

u_{1}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}=u_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}.

This leads to the following result.

Proposition 11
.

Let
u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{l}
be a set of coset representatives of
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}/{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}
. The
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
-lattices

u_{1}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\cdots,u_{l}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},u_{1}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\cdots,u_{l}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n},

represent pairwise distinct vertices of the building of
\SL(2,K)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
u_{k}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
and
u_{j}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
represent the same vertex in the building. By definition, there exists
\lambda\in K^{*}
such that

u_{k}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}=\lambda u_{j}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}.

We conclude that
\lambda{\mathcal{O}}_{n}=u_{j}^{-1}u_{k}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
. In particular,
\lambda\in{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
, which is to say,
\lambda\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{*}={\mathcal{O}}_{0}\cap K^{*}
.

Since
\lambda
and
u_{j}^{-1}u_{k}
are equivalent modulo
{\mathcal{O}}_{n},
there exists an
o\in{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
such that
u_{j}^{-1}u_{k}=\lambda o\in{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}.
Noting that
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{*}\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*},
we find that
\lambda,o\in{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}.
In particular,

u_{j}^{-1}u_{k}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}.

As
u_{j},u_{k}
are coset representatives for
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}/{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}
, we find they must be equal, showing that
u_{1}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\cdots
,
u_{l}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
are pairwise different. The same argument “cancelling
\pi
”, shows that

\pi u_{1}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\cdots,\pi u_{l}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}

are also pairwise different.

To conclude the proof we must now show
\pi u_{j}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
is not equivalent to
u_{k}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
for any choice of
u_{j},u_{k}
(possibly equal).
If they were equivalent, once more we can find
\lambda\in K^{*}
with

\pi u_{i}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}=\lambda u_{k}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}.

Just as before, we conclude
\lambda\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
. We also have that there must be an element
o\in{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
with

u_{i}\pi=\lambda u_{k}o.

Taking valuations we get

1=\valpi(\lambda)+\valpi(o)\geq\valpi(\lambda)\geq 0

However,
\lambda\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
and thus its valuation with respect to
\pi
is even (because of ramification). Since
\val_{\pi}(\lambda)\leq 1
, we conclude
\valpi(\lambda)=0
; hence,
\lambda\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{*}
.

The equality
\pi u_{i}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}=\lambda u_{k}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
also implies the existence of
t\in{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
with

\pi u_{i}t=\lambda u_{k},

which implies

\pi t=\lambda u_{k}u_{i}^{-1}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}.

This is impossible because the left hand side is not a unit. This contradiction concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 7
.

We know the explicit value of
l
. Propositions
7
and
9
imply
[{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}]=q^{n}.

We have thus shown the following.

Corollary 3
.

Each high ideal of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
is equivalent to exactly one of the vertices

u_{1}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\cdots,u_{l}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},u_{1}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\cdots,u_{l}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n},

where
u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{l}
be a set of coset representatives of
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}^{*}/{\mathcal{O}}_{n}^{*}
. If
\varepsilon(I)
is even, then
I
is equivalent to one of

u_{1}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\cdots,u_{l}{\mathcal{O}}_{n};

whereas is
\varepsilon(I)
is odd, then
I
is equivalent to one of

\pi u_{1}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\cdots,\pi u_{l}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}.

In order to describe the location of the vertices
u_{1}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\cdots,u_{l}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
we must first find
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. To this end we prove the following result.

Proposition 12
.

We have the equality

\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n}=p^{n+1}{\mathcal{O}}_{-(n+1)}=\{x+yp^{-(n+1)}\pi\mid x,y\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}\}.

In particular,
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{-(n+1)}
represent the same vertex in the building and
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
and
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
are adjacent.

Proof.

We must prove that the equality

\pi(x+yp^{n}\pi)=p^{n+1}\left(a+\dfrac{b\pi}{p^{n+1}}\right)

determines a correspondence of pairs
(a,b)
and
(x,y)
over
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}.
That is, given a pair
(a,b)\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{2}
one may solve uniquely for
(x,y)\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{2}
and vice versa. Expanding the equality and comparing similar powers of
p
we get

\displaystyle-\delta yp^{n}

\displaystyle=ap^{n+1},

\displaystyle x+yp^{n}\tau

\displaystyle=b.

Recall that
\delta=p\delta_{0}
for some
\delta_{0}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{*}
, the system becomes

\displaystyle-\delta_{0}y

\displaystyle=a,

\displaystyle x+yp^{n}\tau

\displaystyle=b.

From this we see that
(a,b)
and
(x,y)
determine each other in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{2}
.
∎

To proceed we need to understand the action of multiplying in
L
by a unit
u_{\alpha}
that we have constructed before. To this end, consider
1+tp^{n}\pi
with
t\in R(p)
with
n\geq 0
. We have

\displaystyle(1+tp^{n}\pi)(x+y\pi)

\displaystyle=x+y\pi+xtp^{n}\pi+typ^{n}\pi^{2}

\displaystyle=x+y\pi+xtp^{n}\pi+typ^{n}(\tau\pi-\delta))

\displaystyle=(x-\delta typ^{n})+(y+xtp^{n}+ytp^{n}\tau)\pi.

The basis elements are
1
and
\pi
, for which the above computations reduce to

\displaystyle(1+tp^{n}\pi)\cdot 1

\displaystyle=1+tp^{n}\pi,

\displaystyle(1+tp^{n}\pi)\cdot\pi

\displaystyle=-\delta tp^{n}+(1+tp^{n}\tau)\pi.

Thus, as a matrix, the above element corresponds to

\begin{pmatrix}1&-\delta tp^{n}\\
tp^{n}&1+tp^{n}\tau\end{pmatrix}.

We shall call this matrix also
u_{t}^{(n)}
. It should always be clear from the context whether we are considering it as a unit or as a matrix. Now with this computation at hand, we can prove the following result.

Proposition 13
.

Let
\alpha\in I(n)
and let
u_{\alpha}
denote both the unit, as defined in Equation (
6
) above as well as the matrix corresponding to its action in
K\times K
. Then
u_{\alpha}
fixes the vertices
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
and
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
.

Proof.

By construction we have that

u_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha_{0}}^{(0)}u_{\alpha_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots u_{\alpha_{n-1}}^{(n-1)},

and we have computed above that each of the matrices on the right is of the form

\begin{pmatrix}1&-\delta tp^{m}\\
tp^{m}&1+tp^{m}\tau\end{pmatrix},

for some
0\leq m\leq n-1
and
t\in R(p)
. Thus, it is enough to prove the result for the matrices of this type.

Because this matrix represents a unit, there is an inverse of it, which shows it belongs to
\GL(2,{\mathcal{O}}_{K})
; equivalently, we see its determinant is a unit and its entries integral. This shows it fixes
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
. Furthermore, because
u_{\alpha}
and
\pi
commute (as elements of
L)
then they commute as matrices too. Thus

u_{\alpha}\left(\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}\right)=u_{\alpha}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}=\pi u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{0}=\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}.

∎

We can now prove the following result; recall that the vertices
u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
and
u_{\alpha}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
are located in the standard apartment.

Theorem 7
.

The set of vertices
\{u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mid\alpha\in I(n)\}
corresponds to the vertices at distance
n
from
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
and whose path connecting it to
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
does not go through
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
. Analogously, the set of vertices
\{u_{\alpha}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mid\alpha\in I(n)\}
corresponds to the vertices at distance
n
from
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
and whose path connecting it to
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
does not go through
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
.

Proof.

The path joining
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
is given precisely by the main sequence of orders. Acting on the building with
u_{\alpha}
sends this path to the path whose endpoints are
u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{0}={\mathcal{O}}_{0}
and
u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. In particular, we see that the distance is preserved. Furthermore, it is not possible that this path goes through
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
because this vertex is also fixed and it does not belong to the main sequence.

Recall that the tree of
\SL(2,K)
is homogeneous of degree
q+1
. Thus, starting from
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
there are
q^{n}
vertices at distance
n
from it that do not go through
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
. We thus conclude that

\{u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mid\alpha\in I(n)\}

correspond to
all
of the vertices at distance
n
that avoid
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. Indeed, both sets have cardinality
q^{n}
and the correspondence is injective (and hence surjective too).

We claim that the result regarding
\{u_{\alpha}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mid\alpha\in I(n)\}
follows from the above results by applying
\pi
to the building. Indeed, the only fact that we must notice is that
\pi
flips
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
and
\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
:

\pi\cdot(\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{0})=\pi^{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{0}=pu{\mathcal{O}}_{0}=p{\mathcal{O}}_{0}\sim{\mathcal{O}}_{0},

where we have used that
\pi^{2}=up
for some unit
u\in{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
.
∎

We immediately have the following result.

Corollary 4
.

Let
n\geq 0
be an integer. The high ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
of even type are equivalent to vertices in
\{u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mid\alpha\in I(n)\}
, while those of odd type are equivalent to vertices in
\{u_{\alpha}\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mid\alpha\in I(n)\}
.

Proof.

Such ideals are classified in Corollary
3
and the association is given by Theorem
7
.

∎

Now that we understand where the high ideals land, let us locate the low ideals. Recall that a principal ideal
I\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
is a
low ideal
if its type
\omega
satisfies
\omega\leq 2n-1
. Furthermore, by Theorem
5
that the low ideals
I
have types
0,2,4,...,2(n-1)
.

Let
I
be a principal low ideal of type
2d
. Furthermore, let
x+yp^{n}\pi
be a generator of
I
. One can verify (see, for example
[
6
, Propositions 4.12, Proposition 4.14]
) that

\valp(x)=d.

Then

I=(x+yp^{n}\pi){\mathcal{O}}_{n}=(p^{d}u+yp^{n}\pi){\mathcal{O}}_{n}=p^{d}(u+yp^{n-d}\pi){\mathcal{O}}_{n}\sim(u+yp^{n-d}\pi){\mathcal{O}}_{n}.

Notice that in the above,
u+yp^{n-d}\pi\in{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}^{*}
. We realize that the vertices that represent the low ideals are

u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\;\alpha\in I_{d}(n),

which is to say,

u_{\alpha_{n-d}}^{(n-d)}u_{\alpha_{n-d+1}}^{(n-d+1)}\cdots u_{\alpha_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\;\alpha\in I_{d}(n).

We now have the following result.

Theorem 8
.

The set of vertices
\{u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mid\alpha\in I_{d}(n)\}
correspond to the vertices at distance
d
from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}
and whose path connecting it to
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}
does not go through
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d-1}
.

Proof.

Notice that all the units

u_{\alpha_{n-d}}^{(n-d)},u_{\alpha_{n-d+1}}^{(n-d+1)},\cdots,u_{\alpha_{n-1}}^{(n-1)}

belong to
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}^{*}
. As a consequence,

u_{\alpha_{i}^{(i)}}{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}={\mathcal{O}}_{n-d},\;n-d\leq i\leq n-1.

We have seen that
u_{\alpha_{i}^{(i)}}
stabilizes
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
. Thus, under the action of
u_{\alpha}
the whole path from
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
to
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}
is fixed. On the other hand, the path from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}
to
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
goes under the same element to the path from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}
to
u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. However, this path cannot touch
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d-1}
because this vertex is fixed.

One concludes that the vertices that can be reached are those at length
d
from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}
, as the distance is preserved, but that avoid
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d-1}
. All such vertices are attained by a unique unit action, as the cardinalities of the sets of coset representatives and vertices are equal.
∎

Almost all of our discussion is summarized in the following result.

Theorem 9
.

Let
n\geq 0
. The (principal rank
2
) ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
are equivalent to the vertices at distance
n
from the set
\{{\mathcal{O}}_{0},\pi{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\}
.

Remark 8
.

The above statement is independent of the type of
I.
A vertex can represent ideals of both high and low type, or only of high type. So, our work is yet to be complete. The description of what types are represented by a single vertex will be answered in Theorem
16
.

5.3
The unramified case

The analysis of the remaining two cases follows the same structure as the ramified case. A common theme of our work is that much of the theory developed overlaps between cases; in what follows proofs that follow such a pattern will be omitted.

Proposition 14
.

If
L/K
is an unramified quadratic extension, we can pick
\Delta=\sqrt{\epsilon}
,
where
\epsilon
is a nonsquare integral unit in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{*}
.

We once again first locate the high ideals. Fix
\omega\geq n
so that if an ideal
I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
is of (high) type
\omega
, then

I=up^{\omega}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\sim u{\mathcal{O}}_{n},

where

. We once again have the following.

Proposition 15

6
Discussion and proof of the main theorem

So far, we have characterized which exact vertices in the building represent ideals from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
, for any given
n
, for all our cases of
L/K
. However, a single vertex can represent several principal ideals with different types. We must describe with precision which types are represented at each vertex in order to be able to make explicit computations.

6.1
Types represented by a vertex

We begin with a general definition, although we use it only in our three quadratic cases.

Definition 16
.

Let
L=L_{1}\times\cdots\times L_{g}
be reduced finite dimensional
K
-algebra. Thus, each of
L_{1},\cdots,L_{g}
are field extensions of
K
and we let
e_{i}
be the ramification degree of
L_{i}/K
. We shall call the vector

\vec{e}=\vec{e}(L/K)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(e_{1},...,e_{g})

the
translation vector
of the extension
L/K
. We also define

\vec{f}=\vec{f}(L/K)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(f_{1},...,f_{g})

and call it the
inertia vector
of the extension
L/K
.

Remark 9
.

The choice for the adjective “translation”, as opposed to, say, “ramification”, will become clear presently.

In particular, for our three quadratic cases we have

\vec{e}(L/K)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }L/K\mbox{ is an unramified field extension},\\
2&\mbox{if }L/K\mbox{ is a ramified field extension},\\
(1,1)&\mbox{if }L=K\times K.\\
\end{cases}

and

\vec{f}(L/K)=\begin{cases}2&\mbox{if }L/K\mbox{ is an unramified field extension},\\
1&\mbox{if }L/K\mbox{ is a ramified field extension},\\
(1,1)&\mbox{if }L=K\times K.\\
\end{cases}

Definition 17
.

Let
x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
be a vertex. We define

\varepsilon(x)=\{\varepsilon(I)\mid I\sim x,I\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}_{n},I\mbox{ principal }\};

that is,
\varepsilon(x)
is the set of types from principal ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
represented by
x
.

We begin by describing the structure of
\varepsilon(x)
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
I
and
J
two (principal, rank 2) ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
with
I\sim J
. Then,

\varepsilon(I)\equiv\varepsilon(J)\pmod{\vec{e}}.

To be precise, what we mean here is that the difference
\varepsilon(I)-\varepsilon(J)
is an integral multiple of the vector
\vec{e}
.

Proof.

Since
I
and
J
are equivalent in the tree, there exists
\lambda\in K^{*}
such that

I=\lambda J.

Thus, the representatives of
I
(as a principal ideal) are shifts of those of
J
by
\lambda
. That is,

R(I)=\lambda\cdot R(J).

By definition of the type of an ideal, it is the constant vector of valuations of its generators (as an ideal). In particular, taking valuations entrywise, we find

\varepsilon(I)=(\val_{L_{i}}(\lambda))_{i=1,...,g}+\varepsilon(J).

Here
g=1
in the field extension case and
g=2
in the split case. Notice that in each case
(\val_{L_{i}}(\lambda))_{i=1,...,g}
is an integer multiple of
\vec{e}
. Indeed, this vector is

(\val_{L_{i}}(\lambda))_{i=1,...,g}=\begin{cases}\valp(\lambda)&\mbox{if }L/K\mbox{ is an unramified field extension},\\
\valpi(\lambda)\ &\mbox{if }L/K\mbox{ is a ramified field extension},\\
(\valp(\lambda),\valp(\lambda))&\mbox{if }L=K\times K.\\
\end{cases}

We only need to notice that in the ramified case,
\valpi(\lambda)
is even because
\lambda\in K^{*}
and that, in the split case, we obtain
(\valp(\lambda),\valp(\lambda))
because
\lambda
acts by entrywise multiplication. This concludes the proof.
∎

The following fact follows easily from what we have done in the above proof; we isolate it due to its relevance.

Lemma 5
.

Let
I
and
J
two (principal, rank 2) ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
with
I\sim J
. Then
I=J
if and only if
\varepsilon(I)=\varepsilon(J)

Proof.

Only the backwards implication needs to be proven. Indeed, if
\varepsilon(I)=\varepsilon(J)
, then by the proof of Lemma
4
, we have that
\lambda
is a unit in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}^{*}
. Since multiplication by units does not change the ideal, we conclude that

I=\lambda J=J,
as desired
∎

Definition 18
.

Let
I
and
J
be two (principal, rank 2) ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. We say
I\leq J
if and only if

1.

I\sim J,
and

2.

\varepsilon(J)-\varepsilon(I)
has nonnegative entries

Proposition 20
.

Let
I
and
J
be two (principal, rank
2
) ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. The following statements hold.

(a)

The relation
\leq
defines a partial order on the set of (principal, rank 2) ideals of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
.

(b)

Let
x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
be a vertex. When restricted to the ideals
I\sim x
,
\leq
becomes a total order.

(c)

Let
x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
be a vertex. Then
\leq
has a unique minimum element.

Proof.

That these conditions constitute a reflexive and transitive relation is straightforward. Lemma
5
implies it is antisymmetric. This proves (a)

If we now restrict to those ideals that are equivalent to a fixed vertex, then, by Lemma
4
and its proof, we conclude we can always compare them. Indeed, its difference is an integer multiple of
\vec{e}
, which is a vector with positive entries. Thus one of

\varepsilon(J)-\varepsilon(I),\,\varepsilon(I)-\varepsilon(J),

has nonnegative entries. This proves
(b)
.

Finally, to prove that a minimum exist, we notice that given any ideal
I\sim x
, the types of the other ideals that are also equivalent to
x
lie in the set

\{\varepsilon(I)+k\vec{e},\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\},

(10)

by Lemma
4
. However, because the entries of ideal types are nonnegative, the actual ideal types that do appear in (
10
) have a lower bound.

Furthermore, by definition, the sign of
k
determines whether these types are smaller or bigger (or equal if
k=0
). In particular,

\varepsilon(I)+k\vec{e}\longrightarrow k,

embeds this total order into the one of
\mathbb{Z}
. Since there is a lower bound,
(c)
follows.
∎

With the above results, we are now allowed to give the following definition and structural result.

Definition 19
.

Let
x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
. The minimum type of the total order proven in part (b) and (c) of Proposition

will be called the
type source of vertex
x
or simply
the source at
x
. We will denote it by

.

Theorem 16
.

Let
x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
be a vertex with source
\omega(x)
, then

\varepsilon(x)=\{\omega(x)+k\vec{e}\mid k=0,1,2,...\}.

In other words, if
\omega(x)
is the “minimum” type represented by the vertex

, then
x
represents ideals of any type “bigger” than
\omega(x)
under

.

Proof.

Let
I
be an ideal that achieves the minimum at
x
. Notice that multiplication by
p
produces another principal ideal, also represented by
x
, and with

\varepsilon(pI)=\vec{e}+\varepsilon(I)=\vec{e}+\omega(x).

With this we see that by successive multiplication by
p
, we can produce all types in
\{\omega(x)+k\vec{e}\mid k=0,1,2,...\}
. Thus

\varepsilon(x)\supseteq\{\omega(x)+k\vec{e}\mid k=0,1,2,...\}.

On the other hand, Lemma
4
and Definition
19
, prove

\varepsilon(x)\subseteq\{\omega(x)+k\vec{e}\mid k=0,1,2,...\},

concluding the proof.
∎

6.2
The equality of zeta functions

In order to make our final result easy to explain we need one last definition, which is justified by our work in the previous section.

Definition 20
.

Let
I\subseteq{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
be a (principal, rank 2) ideal. We define its
vertex-type coordinates
as the pair
(x,\varepsilon(I))
where
x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is the vertex such that
I\sim x
.

The equivalence of generating functions we desire to prove essentially follows from reading the set of coordinates in two different ways. However, before we can do this, we must connect the source at
x
with some property of
x
as a vertex of the impacted building. The next lemma gives this connection.

Lemma 6
.

Let
x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
, then

d(x,{\mathcal{O}}_{n})=c(\omega(x)),

(11)

where
c(\omega(x))
is the type contribution of
\omega(x)
.

Proof.

We have seen that for each case all the vertices in
{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
represent high ideals. However, not all of them represent low ideals. Fortunately, the latter have been described in Theorem
8
, Theorem
11
, and Theorem
13
. This description gives that reaching this vertex
x
from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
requires to go first to
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}
and from there to
x
.

Let
x
be a vertex that represents low ideals and let
I
be a low ideal represented by it with the lowest contribution; thus,
\omega(x)=\omega(I)
. Furthermore, we know this
I
is unique. Concretely,

I\sim u_{\alpha}\cdot{\mathcal{O}}_{n}=x,

for some
\alpha\in I_{d}(n)
with
d
minimal, and in particular, smaller than
n
. In this situation, the two paths mentioned in the above paragraph are disjoint (otherwise we could decrease
d
because the paths would also share
{\mathcal{O}}_{n-(d-1)}
). Consequently,

d(V,{\mathcal{O}}_{n})=d(x,{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d})+d({\mathcal{O}}_{n-d},{\mathcal{O}}_{n})=d+d=2d.

However,
I
is constructed in a very specific way from this information. Concretely,

I={p}^{d}u_{\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}

Notice that this ideal is indeed a principal ideal
of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
because
u_{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{n-d}^{*}
and thus the corresponding power of
p
by which it gets multiplied guarantees that the “representative” is an element of
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
. We now compute the type contribution of this ideal. We have

\omega(x)=\omega(I)=\begin{cases}f(de)&\mbox{in the nonsplit case,}\\
d+d&\mbox{in the split case.}\end{cases}

Using that
ef=2
we get that in all cases

\omega(x)=2d=d(x,{\mathcal{O}}_{n}),

as desired. This settles the situation where
x
represents low ideals.

Let us now take
x
to be a vertex that
does not
represent low ideals. Let
I
be an ideal, necessarily high, that is represented by
x
and with the lowest possible type contribution. We know that

I=u_{\alpha}{\pi}^{\varepsilon(I)}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},\;\;\alpha\in I(n)

(12)

where in the split case
{\pi}^{t_{n}+\varepsilon(I)}=(p^{n+\alpha_{1}},p^{n+\alpha_{2}})
.

In all these cases, the geodesic from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
to
x
goes through
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
. Specifically, this geodesic has three parts: one from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
to
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
, another from
{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
to
{\pi}^{\varepsilon(I)}{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
, and a final one from
{\pi}^{\varepsilon(I)}{\mathcal{O}}_{0}
to
x
. The first and last segments both have length
n
. The middle segment occurs entirely within the basin and its length is determined by
{\pi}^{\varepsilon(I)}
. As we are dealing with high ideals, the type has to be “above” the ideal threshold.

In the field extension case this means
\varepsilon(I)\geq ne
, while in the split case it means each entry is at least
n
. Writing this as
\varepsilon(I)=t_{n}+(m_{1},m_{2})
in the split case and as
\varepsilon(I)=t_{n}+m
otherwise, we see

\omega(x)=\omega(I)=\begin{cases}f(ne+m)\\
n+m_{1}+n+m_{2}\end{cases}=2n+\begin{cases}fm&\mbox{in the nonsplit case}\\
m_{1}+m_{2}&\mbox{in the split case}.\end{cases}

(13)

To conclude we must use the fact that
I
produces the minimal type at the vertex
x
. Notice that equation (
12
) for
I
represents the same vertex whenever we can “factor powers of
p
” from
\pi^{m}
or
\pi^{(m_{1},m_{2})}
. For example, in the unramified case, if
m\neq 0
, then as vertices

u_{\alpha}p^{n}\cdot{p}^{m}{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\sim u_{\alpha}p^{n}\cdot{p}^{m-1}{\mathcal{O}}_{n},

(14)

but as ideals the right hand one has smallest type. This contradicts the minimality of the type. Hence, we necessarily have
m=0
.

In general, by Proposition
20
, we know that any displacement in the type by
\vec{e}
produces the same vertex. In particular, we must be unable to subtract
\vec{e}
. This means in the ramified case that
m\in\{0,1\}
, in the unramified case that
m=0
, and in the split case that
m\in\{(0,m_{2}),(m_{1},0)\}
.

In the unramified case, where
m=0
, equation (
13
) implies

\omega(x)=2n=d({\mathcal{O}}_{n},{\mathcal{O}}_{0})+d({\mathcal{O}}_{0},x).

In the ramified case, the geodesic from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
to
x
has length

d(x,{\mathcal{O}}_{n})=d({\mathcal{O}}_{n},{\mathcal{O}}_{0})+d({\mathcal{O}}_{0},{\pi}^{\varepsilon(I)}{\mathcal{O}}_{0})+d({\pi}^{\varepsilon(I)}{\mathcal{O}}_{0},x)=2n+d({\mathcal{O}}_{0},\pi^{m}{\mathcal{O}}_{0}).

We finally notice that
d({\mathcal{O}}_{0},\pi^{m}{\mathcal{O}}_{0})\in\{0,1\}
according to whether
m
is even or odd. The latter condition is equivalent to whether
I
is of even or odd type, which by comparing values yields

d(x,{\mathcal{O}}_{n})=\omega(x).

Finally, we deal with the split case. The arguments are the same as above. The term “+ 2n” in the contribution matches the distances
d({\mathcal{O}}_{n},{\mathcal{O}}_{0})
and
d({\pi}^{\varepsilon(I)}{\mathcal{O}}_{0},x)
. Thus, we only need to explain the term
d({\mathcal{O}}_{0},{\pi}^{\varepsilon(I)}{\mathcal{O}}_{0})
. In our current case, since one of
m_{1}
or
m_{2}
is zero, what we have is that
{\pi}^{\varepsilon(I)}{\mathcal{O}}_{0}=\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}_{-m_{1}}}
or
\widetilde{{\mathcal{O}}_{m_{2}}}
, according to whether
m_{1}\neq 0
or
m_{2}\neq 0
, respectively. Thus

d({\mathcal{O}}_{0},{\pi}^{\varepsilon(I)}{\mathcal{O}}_{0})=m_{i}.

We see this matches the missing term from the sum giving
\omega(x)
. Thus, once more

d(x,{\mathcal{O}}_{n})=\omega(x).

This concludes the proof.
∎

We finally prove our main result.

Theorem 17
.

Let
L/K
be one of the above considered extensions (i.e. unramified, ramified, split extensions) and
{\mathscr{B}}
the corresponding impacted building (i.e.
{\mathscr{B}}_{U},{\mathscr{B}}_{R}
,
{\mathscr{B}}_{S}
). Then we have the equality

\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(s)=\zeta_{{\mathscr{B}},{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(q^{-s}),

(15)

where
\zeta_{{\mathscr{B}},{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(X)
is the generating function from the vertex associated to
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
for the corresponding impacted building.

Proof.

We begin by noticing that a given
potential
vertex/type-coordinate
(x,\omega)
singles out at most one ideal. Indeed, Lemma
5
implies this ideal is unique if it exists. We conclude that summing over (principal, rank 2) ideals is the same as summing over vertex-type coordinates. Let us denote by
VT
the set vertex type coordinates that do occur.

Our proof consists on rewriting in two ways the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,\omega)\in VT}q^{-c(\omega)s}.

This is an integral over a discrete set where the point
(x,\omega)
has measure
q^{-c(\omega)s}
for a fixed positive
q
and a fixed positive real
s
large enough such that both series converge. If we can prove the equality in the set of real numbers, the functions are equal also for the complex values where both are defined due to uniqueness of analytic continuation. In particular, we will perform iterated integration in any order.

Let us first integrate over the vertex set. For each
x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
the inner integral will be over the coordinates of the the set of ideals equivalent to that vertex. Theorem
16
shows that these coordinates are

(x,\omega(x)),(x,\omega(x)+\vec{e}),(x,\omega(x)+2\vec{e}),...

In turn, the ideals specified by these coordinates contribute

c(\omega(x)),c(\omega(x))+2,c(\omega(x))+4,\cdots

Indeed, the type contribution for a given possible type
\omega
is

c(\omega_{1},...,\omega_{g})=f_{1}\omega_{1}+...+f_{g}\omega_{g},

where
f_{1},...,f_{g}
are the inertia degrees of the extensions
L_{1},...,L_{g}
. In particular, because
e_{i}f_{i}=[L_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}K]
, we have

c(\vec{e})=f_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+f_{g}e_{g}=2.

As we can see, because the type contribution is an additive function, our claim follows. Finally, using Lemma
6
, we get these contributions are

d,d+2,d+4,\cdots

where
d=d(x,{\mathcal{O}}_{n})
. We thus conclude that the iterated integral, obtained by integrating first over the vertex set, is

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}}\left(q^{-d(x,{\mathcal{O}}_{n})}+q^{-(d(x,{\mathcal{O}}_{n})+2)}+q^{-(d(x,{\mathcal{O}}_{n})+4)}+\cdots\right).

(16)

The generating function from the vertex
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
is defined as

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\left\{x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}\mid x\mbox{ can be reached by a path of length }d\mbox{ from }{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\right\}X^{d}

If
x
can be reached by a path of length
d
then by adding an “unnecessary” jump to a neighboring vertex and back (just as in Proposition
1
), we see it can also be reached with a path of length
d+2
. Thus, if
x\in{\mathscr{R}}_{n}
is a vertex at exact distance
d
from
{\mathcal{O}}_{n}
then its contribution to the right hand side is

X^{d}+X^{d+2}+X^{d+4}+\dots...,

which upon evaluation at
q^{-s}
becomes the value (
16
). We conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,\omega)\in VT}q^{-c(\omega)}=\zeta_{{\mathscr{B}},{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}(q^{-s}).

We will now integrate first over the types. Doing so transforms the integral into

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega}\displaystyle\sum_{\varepsilon(I)=\omega}q^{-c(\varepsilon(I))s}.

We know that the index
[{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I]
only depends on the type. Concretely, if
c(\omega)
is the
type contribution
, then

[{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I]=q^{-c(\varepsilon(I))}.

That is,

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega}\displaystyle\sum_{\varepsilon(I)=\omega}q^{-c(\varepsilon(I))s}=\displaystyle\sum_{\omega}\displaystyle\sum_{\varepsilon(I)=\omega}\dfrac{1}{[{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I]^{s}}

As we have said, summing over
VT
is the same as summing over the ideals, we conclude that the above integral is

\displaystyle\sum_{I}\dfrac{1}{[{\mathcal{O}}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}I]^{s}}.

We can do this because we have eliminated the dependence on the type from the integrand. We recognize this as
\zeta_{{\mathcal{O}}_{n}}^{P}(s)
. This concludes the proof.
∎
