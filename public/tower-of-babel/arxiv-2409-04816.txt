Ill-posedness of the Dirichlet problem for 2D Lagrangian mean curvature equations
Wentao Cao, Zhehui Wang

Abstract
. We investigate the Dirichlet problem of the two-dimensional Lagrangian mean curvature equation in bounded domains. Infinitely many C 1 , α ​ ( α ∈ ( 0 , 1 5 ) ) C^{1,\alpha}(\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{5})) very weak solutions are built through Nash-Kuiper construction. Moreover, we note there are infinitely many C 1 , α C^{1,\alpha} very weak solutions that can not be improved to be C 2 , α C^{2,\alpha} .

1.
Introduction

In the present paper, we study the Dirichlet problem for the two-dimensional Lagrangian mean curvature equation

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\cos\Theta\Delta v+\sin\Theta\left(\det\nabla^{2}v-1\right)=0\text{ in }\Omega,\\
v=g\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega\subset\R^{2}
is a bounded domain, and the phase function
\Theta:\overline{\Omega}\to(-\pi,\pi)
is a given
C^{2,\kappa}(\kappa\in(0,1))
function. The equation

(1.2)

\cos\Theta\Delta v+\sin\Theta\left(\det\nabla^{2}v-1\right)=0

has an equivalent form

(1.3)

\arctan\lambda_{1}+\arctan\lambda_{2}=\Theta

with
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
being eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}v
. The phase function
\Theta

is a potential for the mean curvature of the Lagrangian submanifold

\Sigma:=\{(x,\nabla v(x));x\in\Omega\}

or called the gradient graph.

Generally, the
n
-dimensional Lagrangian mean curvature equation is

(1.4)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{k}=\Theta,

where
\lambda_{i}
’s are eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}v
. The mean curvature vector of the
n
-dimensional gradient graph
\Sigma
is equal to
J\nabla_{g}\Theta
, where
g=\mathrm{Id}+(\nabla^{2}v)^{2}
is the induced metric on
\Sigma
, and
J(x,y)=(-y,x)
is a rotation in
\R^{n}\times\R^{n}
.
In particular, when
\Theta
is a constant, (
1.4
) is called the special Lagrangian equation, which originates in the special Lagrangian geometry introduced by Harvey-Lawson
[
18
]
. Note
\Sigma
is a minimal surface in
\R^{n}\times\R^{n}
if and only if
\Theta
is a constant.

1.1.
Background

For the
n
-dimensional equation (
1.4
), Yuan
[
34
]
noticed that the level set
\{\lambda:=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})\in\R^{n}|\lambda\text{ satisfies equation }\eqref{lag-eq1}\}
is convex only when
|\Theta|\geq\frac{(n-2)\pi}{2}
. Thus the phase
\Theta
is called critical if
|\Theta|=\frac{(n-2)\pi}{2}
, and called supercritical (or subcritical) if
|\Theta|>\frac{(n-2)\pi}{2}
(or
|\Theta|<\frac{(n-2)\pi}{2}
). When
n=2
, viewing (
1.2
) as Monge-Ampère type equations, Heinz established some regularity results in
[
19
]
. For higher dimensions, a priori estimates for solutions of the special Lagrangian equation with critical and supercritical phases were obtained by Warren-Yuan
[
31
,
32
]
, Wang-Yuan
[
30
]
, Chen-Warren-Yuan
[
8
]
, respectively. Moreover, the Hessian estimate for Lagrangian mean curvature equation (
1.4
) with
C^{1,1}
critical and supercritical phases was obtained by Bhattacharya
[
2
,
3
]
, while the gradient estimate for
C^{2}
critical and supercritical phases was achieved by Bhattacharya-Shanker-Mooney
[
1
]
. Hence, combining a priori estimates in
[
2
,
3
,
1
]
, they solved the Dirichlet problem for Lagrangian mean curvature equation (
1.4
) in uniformly convex bounded domains with the phase function satisfying
\frac{(n-2)\pi}{2}\leq\Theta<\frac{n\pi}{2}
. Classical solvability of the Dirichlet problem for the Lagrangian mean curvature equation (
1.4
) was also established by Lu
[
24
]
, and Collins, Picard and Wu
[
10
]
. Besides, Harvey and Lawson
[
16
,
17
]
, and Cirant and Payne
[
9
]
proved the existence of the viscosity solution to the Lagrangian mean curvature equation (
1.4
). However, interior estimates fail for the subcritical case due to the examples constructed in Nadirashvili-Vlăduţ
[
26
]
, Wang-Yuan
[
29
]
, and Mooney-Savin
[
25
]
.

In this paper, we consider not only classical solutions but also very weak solutions to (
1.1
). In fact, the very weak solution to the Monge-Ampère equation was first introduced by Lewicka-Pakzad
[
22
]
via very weak Hessian defined by Iwaniec
[
20
]
. The two-dimensional very weak Hessian coincides with the Monge-Ampère measure for any convex solution (
[
13
]
). Moreover, Without boundary conditions, Lewicka-Pakzad
[
22
]
, Székelyhidi and the first author
[
5
]
, and Hirsch-Inauen and the first author
[
4
]
proved that there are infinitely many very weak solutions to the two dimensional Monge-Ampère equation of different regularity. After imposing boundary data, the first author in
[
7
]
studied very weak solutions to the Monge-Ampère equation. Recently, Li-Qiu
[
23
]
also constructed infinitely many very weak solutions of the Dirichlet problem for 2-Hessian equations.
All the above approaches to constructing very weak solutions rely on the
Nash-Kuiper construction, which was originated from isometric embeddings (the Nash-Kuiper theorem; see
[
27
,
21
,
11
,
6
,
12
,
28
]
), and developed to be convex integration to prove the
h
-principle (see
[
15
]
). In recent decades, the technique of convex integration becomes a powerful approach to showing non-uniqueness of nonlinear partial differential equations.

1.2.
Main result and strategies

Along the line of very weak solutions to the Monge-Ampère equation, we also introduce the concept of very weak solutions to the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
).

Definition 1.1

(Very weak solution)
.

v\in C^{1}(\Omega)
is a very weak solution to the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
) in
\Omega
, if
v=g
on
\partial\Omega
, and it satisfies the following equation in the very weak sense in
\Omega
:

(1.5)

\cos\Theta\mbox{ curl curl }(v\cdot\mbox{Id})+\sin\Theta\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mbox{ curl curl }(\nabla v\otimes\nabla v)-1\right)=0;

namely, for any
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{1}v\partial_{2}v\partial_{12}^{2}(\phi\sin\Theta)-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{1}v)^{2}\partial_{22}^{2}(\phi\sin\Theta)-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{2}v)^{2}\partial_{11}^{2}(\phi\sin\Theta)dx

(1.6)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}v\Delta(\phi\cos\Theta)dx-\int_{\Omega}\phi\sin\Theta dx=0.

The operator
\mathrm{curl}
in two-dimension is defined by

\displaystyle\mbox{ curl }\xi

\displaystyle=\partial_{2}\xi_{1}-\partial_{1}\xi_{2}\mbox{ for }\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

\displaystyle\mbox{ curl }A

\displaystyle=(\partial_{2}A_{11}-\partial_{1}A_{12},\partial_{2}A_{21}-\partial_{1}A_{22})\mbox{ for }A=(A_{ij})_{2\times 2}\in\R^{2\times 2}.

In particular,
\mbox{ curl curl }(v\cdot\mbox{Id})
is understood as
\Delta v
in the very weak sense.

Now we are ready to state our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\R^{2}
be a bounded simply connected domain with a
C^{2,\kappa}
boundary
\partial\Omega
for any given
\kappa\in(0,1)
. Suppose
g\in C^{2,\kappa}(\partial\Omega)
and the phase
\Theta:\overline{\Omega}\to(-\pi,\pi)
is a
C^{2,\kappa}
function. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

There exists some small positive number
c_{1}
such that if
|\sin\Theta|\leq c_{1}
, then the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
) admits a unique
C^{2,\kappa}(\Omega)
classical solution.

(2)

Given any constant
c_{2}\in(0,1]
, the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
) admits infinitely many
C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)
very weak solutions for any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{5})
whenever
|\sin\Theta|\geq c_{2}
.

Remark 1.1
.

The assumption of simply connectedness is only needed for applying Lemma
A.2
to the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Noting
c_{2}
is arbitrary, as a quick observation, we have

Corollary 1.2
.

For any
c_{3}\in(0,c_{1})
, if
c_{3}\leq|\sin\Theta|\leq c_{1}
, then there are infinitely many
C^{1,\alpha}
very weak solutions to the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
) which can not be improved to be
C^{2,\alpha}
.

Our strategy to show Theorem
1.1
(1) is considering (
1.2
) as a perturbation of a Laplacian equation and then applying the implicit function theorem.
As for Theorem
1.1
(2), with
|\sin\Theta|\geq c_{2}>0
, the problem is reduced to solve

\cot\Theta\Delta v+\det\nabla^{2}v-1=0\text{ in the very weak sense}.

We will solve the above equation by the Nash-Kuiper construction. The main obstacle is how to treat terms with the phase function, compared with
[
7
]
, which will be regarded as a Laplacian of some function, i.e.,

\cot\Theta\Delta v=\Delta(v\cot\Theta-V),\text{ in the very weak sense,}

with

\Delta V=2\nabla v\cdot\nabla\cot\Theta+v\Delta\cot\Theta.

Hence, the problem is turned to solve

\mathrm{curl}\,\mathrm{curl}\,\big(\mbox{ sym }\nabla w+\frac{1}{2}\nabla v\otimes\nabla v-(v\cot\Theta)\cdot\mathrm{Id}+V\cdot\mathrm{Id}\big)=-1,

in the very weak sense, and then
V\cdot\textrm{Id}
becomes an error term of a lower order. With the elliptic estimate in Lemma
3.4
, we are able to control it and arrive at our conclusion.

We remark that the method proving Theorem
1.1
(2) has more general applications.

Remark 1.2
.

Let

F_{\tau}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}\big(\ln\lambda_{1}+\ln\lambda_{2}\big),\qquad\tau=0,\\
&\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+1}}{2b}\big(\ln\frac{\lambda_{1}+a-b}{\lambda_{1}+a+b}+\ln\frac{\lambda_{2}+a-b}{\lambda_{2}+a+b}\big),\qquad 0<\tau<\frac{\pi}{4},\\
&-\sqrt{2}\big(\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{1}}+\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{2}}\big),\qquad\tau=\frac{\pi}{4},\\
&\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+1}}{b}\big(\arctan\frac{\lambda_{1}+a-b}{\lambda_{1}+a+b}+\arctan\frac{\lambda_{2}+a-b}{\lambda_{2}+a+b}\big),\qquad\frac{\pi}{4}<\tau<\frac{\pi}{2},\\
&\arctan\lambda_{1}+\arctan\lambda_{2},\qquad\tau=\frac{\pi}{2},\end{aligned}\right.

where
a=\cot\tau
, and
b=\sqrt{|\cot^{2}\tau-1|}
. With slight modifications, we can establish the result like Theorem
1.1
(2) for the following Dirichlet problem

(1.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}F_{\tau}(\lambda)=\Theta,&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
\qquad v=g,&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
are eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}v
. We note the equation
F_{\tau}(\lambda)=\Theta
can be formulated as the equation of the following form

P\Delta u+Q\det\nabla^{2}u=R.

Equations in (
1.7
) are originated from the study of the gradient graph
\Sigma
. By Warren
[
33
]
, if
\Sigma
is a minimal Lagrangian graph in
(\R^{2}\times\R^{2},g_{\tau})
with

g_{\tau}=\sin\tau\big(\sum_{i=1}^{2}dx_{i}\otimes dx_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{2}dy_{j}\otimes dy_{j}\big)+\cos\tau\big(\sum_{i=1}^{2}dx_{i}\otimes dy_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{2}dy_{j}\otimes dx_{j}\big),

then

F_{\tau}(\lambda(\nabla^{2}v))=\text{constant}.

We refer readers to the results about gradient graphs in general dimensions in
[
33
]
.

1.3.
Organization and notations

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Theorem
1.1
(1) will be proved in Section
2
. Our stage proposition, which is our main contribution, and the proof for Theorem
1.1
(2) will be presented in Section
3
and Section
4
respectively.

Finally, we provide some notations for convenience. For any function
h:\Omega\to\R
, set

\displaystyle\|h\|_{0}=\sup_{\Omega}|h|,\,\,\|h\|_{m}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\max_{|\beta|=j}\|\partial^{\beta}h\|_{0},

\displaystyle\|h\|_{m+\alpha}=\|h\|_{m}+[h]_{m+\alpha},

where the Hölder semi-norm is defined by

[h]_{m+\alpha}=\max_{|\beta|=m}\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|\partial^{\beta}h(x)-\partial^{\beta}h(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}.

Here
m
is a nonnegative integer,
\alpha\in(0,1]
a constant, and
\beta
be a multi-index. We denote by
B_{r}
the ball in
\R^{2}
with radius
r
centering at
0
. Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{1})
be a standard two-dimensional mollifier. We define
\varphi_{l}(x)=l^{-2}\varphi(l^{-1}x)
, and
l>0
is called the length-scale. We also define

\tilde{h}(x)=h*\varphi_{l}(x)=l^{-2}\int_{\R^{2}}h(x-y)\varphi(\frac{y}{l})dy.

Some properties of mollification can be found in Lemma
A.1
.

Besides, for
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we write
\xi\otimes\eta
as a
2\times 2
-matrix with entries
(\xi\otimes\eta)_{ij}=\xi_{i}\eta_{j}
. By a direct calculation, for a
C^{3}
-function
w
, we have

\det\nabla^{2}w=-\frac{1}{2}\mbox{curl curl }(\nabla w\otimes\nabla w)\mbox{ and }\Delta w=\mbox{curl curl }(w\cdot\mathrm{Id}).

For a
C^{1}
-function
w:\Omega\to\R^{2}
, note
\nabla w=(\partial_{i}w_{j})_{2\times 2}
and

\mathrm{sym}\nabla w=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla w+\nabla w^{T}).

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
(1)

The proof of Theorem
1.1
(1) is based on the application of the implicit function theorem.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{g}\in C^{2,\kappa}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\widetilde{g}=g
on
\partial\Omega
. Set

\mathcal{X}=\{\psi\in C^{2,\kappa}(\overline{\Omega}):\psi=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}.

Define

G:\mathcal{X}\times C^{\kappa}(\overline{\Omega})\rightarrow C^{\kappa}(\overline{\Omega})
by

G(\psi,\Psi)=\Delta\psi+\Psi\cdot(\det\nabla^{2}(\psi+\widetilde{g})-1)+\Delta\widetilde{g}.

Then we have the following conclusion.

Claim
:
There are a unique
\psi\in\mathcal{X}
and a small enough constant
\mu\in(0,1)
such that
G(\psi,\Psi)=0

for any
\Psi
satisfying
\|\Psi\|_{\kappa}\leq\mu
.

In fact, the Fréchet derivative of
G
with respect to
\psi
is given by

\displaystyle G_{\psi}(\psi,\Psi)\vartheta=\Delta\vartheta+\frac{d\,}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\Psi\cdot(\det\nabla^{2}(\psi+\widetilde{g}+t\vartheta)-1),

and hence
G_{\psi}(\psi,0)\vartheta=\Delta\vartheta
for any
\vartheta\in\mathcal{X}
.
Therefore, for any
\psi\in\mathcal{X}
,
G_{\psi}(\psi,0)
is an invertible operator from
\mathcal{X}
to
C^{\kappa}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Next, take
\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\Delta\psi_{0}=-\Delta\widetilde{g}
.
The existence of such
\psi_{0}
is provided by the Schauder theory.
By the implicit function theorem, the equation
G(\psi,\Psi)=0
admits a unique solution
\psi=\psi_{\Psi}\in\mathcal{X}

for each
\Psi
with
\|\Psi\|_{C^{\kappa}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\mu
, where
\mu
is a sufficiently small constant. Moreover,
\psi_{\Psi}
is close to
\psi_{0}
in the
C^{2,\kappa}(\overline{\Omega})
-norm.

Let
\Theta:\overline{\Omega}\to(-\pi,\pi)
be a
C^{1,\kappa}
function. By the interpolation, we note

\|f\|_{C^{\kappa}(B_{r})}\leq\sigma\|f\|_{C^{1,\kappa}(B_{r})}+(\frac{C}{\sigma^{\kappa}}+1)\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}

for any
0<\sigma\leq r
and
f\in C^{1,\kappa}(B_{r})
. Hence, if
|\sin\Theta|\leq c_{1}
with the small constant
c_{1}
to be fixed, then we can get

\|\sin\Theta\|_{\kappa}\leq C(\kappa,\|\Theta\|_{1+\kappa})(c_{1}+c_{1}^{1-\kappa}),

where
C(\kappa,\|\Theta\|_{1+\kappa})
is a constant depending only on
\kappa,\|\Theta\|_{1+\kappa}.

By a direct computation,

\displaystyle\|\tan\Theta\|_{\kappa}

\displaystyle\leq\|\sin\Theta\|_{0}\|\frac{1}{\cos\Theta}\|_{\kappa}+\|\sin\Theta\|_{\kappa}\|\frac{1}{\cos\Theta}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq 4\|\sin\Theta\|_{\kappa}\|\frac{1}{\cos\Theta}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C(\kappa,\|\Theta\|_{1+\kappa})\|\sin\Theta\|_{\kappa}(\|\frac{1}{\cos\Theta}\|_{0}+\|\frac{1}{\cos^{2}\Theta}\|_{0}\|\sin\Theta\|_{0})

\displaystyle\leq C(\kappa,\|\Theta\|_{1+\kappa})(c_{1}+c_{1}^{1-\kappa})(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-c_{1}^{2}}}+\frac{c_{1}}{1-c_{1}^{2}}).

Now, we choose
c_{1}
small enough such that
\|\tan\Theta\|_{C^{\kappa}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\mu
.
By the above Claim, there is a unique
\psi\in\mathcal{X}
such that

\Delta\psi+\tan\Theta(\det\nabla^{2}(\psi+\widetilde{g})-1)+\Delta\widetilde{g}=0.

Then,
v:=\psi+\widetilde{g}
uniquely satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Delta v+\tan\Theta(\det\nabla^{2}v-1)=0\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
&v=g\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Hence, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
(1).
∎

3.
The Stage Proposition

The rest of the paper is devoted to showing Theorem
1.1
(2). Similar to the treatment in
[
7
,
5
,
22
]
for the Monge-Ampère equation, we split (
1.2
) (after dividing
\sin\Theta
) into a differential system

(3.1)

\displaystyle-\mbox{curl curl }A=1,

(3.2)

\displaystyle A=\mbox{ sym }\nabla w+\frac{1}{2}\nabla v\otimes\nabla v-(v\cot\Theta)\cdot\mathrm{Id}+V\cdot\mathrm{Id},

where
V
is the solution to

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta V&=F(v),\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
V&=0,\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

and
F(v)
is defined as

(3.3)

F(v)=2\nabla v\cdot\nabla\cot\Theta+v\Delta\cot\Theta.

In fact, by a direct calculation, we note that
v
satisfying (
3.1
)-(
3.2
) is a solution of the equation (
1.5
) in the very weak sense.

To solve (
3.2
), our strategy is applying the technique of Nash-Kuiper construction to construct a sequence of subsolutions satisfying the boundary condition, whose limit is our desired solution.
Set

(3.4)

D(v,w):=\mbox{ sym }\nabla w+\frac{1}{2}\nabla v\otimes\nabla v-(v\cot\Theta)\cdot\mathrm{Id}+V\cdot\mathrm{Id},

then such subsolutions, which are called adapted subsolutions, are defined as below.

Definition 3.1
.

A pair of mappings
(v,w):\overline{\Omega}\to\R\times\R^{2}
is called a
C^{1,\beta}(\beta<1)
adapted subsolution of (
3.2
) if
(v,w)\in C^{1,\beta}
and

A-D(v,w)=\rho^{2}(\textrm{Id}+H)\mbox{ in }\overline{\Omega},

where
D(v,w)
is as (
3.4
), and
\rho\in C(\overline{\Omega})
is a nonnegative function with
\partial\Omega\subset\{x:\rho(x)=0\}
, and
H\in C(\overline{\Omega};\R^{2\times 2}_{sym})
is a symmetric matrix such that
\textrm{Id}+H
is positive definite.

Note that the definition is a slight modification of that in
[
7
]
.
To construct new adapted subsolutions, we need to add the deficit
A-D(v,w)
to the given adapted subsolution
v.
Since the deficit is of the form
\rho^{2}(\mathrm{Id}+H)
, we require the following stage proposition (in the terminology of Nash
[
27
]
) for iteration, which is parallel to Proposition 3.1 in
[
7
]
and whose proof is different due to the new error terms. The new error terms are then controlled through Lemma
3.4
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
\Sigma\subset\R^{2}
is a simply connected open bounded set with a
C^{2}
boundary. Let
v\in C^{2}(\overline{\Sigma})
and
w\in C^{2}(\Sigma,\R^{2})
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{1}+\|w\|_{1}\leq\gamma,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{2}+\|w\|_{2}\leq\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda,

and let
\rho\in C^{1}(\overline{\Sigma})
,
H\in C^{1}(\overline{\Sigma},\R^{2\times 2}_{sym})
be such that

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{0}\leq\delta^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\|\nabla\rho\|_{0}\leq\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|H\|_{0}\leq\lambda^{-\alpha},\quad\|\nabla H\|_{0}\leq\lambda^{1-\alpha},

for some
\gamma>1
,
0<\delta,\,\alpha<1
and
\lambda>1
with
2\leq\lambda^{\alpha}
and
\delta^{1/2}\lambda>1
. Then for any
\tau>1
, there exist functions
v^{\star}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Sigma})
,
w^{\star}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Sigma},\R^{2})
and a symmetric matrix
\E\in C^{1}(\overline{\Sigma},\R^{2\times 2}_{sym})
such that

(3.9)

\displaystyle D(v^{\star},w^{\star})=D(v,w)+\rho^{2}(\mathrm{Id}+H)+\E,\,\,\mbox{in }\overline{\Sigma},

(3.10)

\displaystyle(v^{\star},w^{\star})=(v,w),\,\,\E=0,\,\,\mbox{on }\overline{\Sigma}\setminus(\mathrm{supp}\rho+B_{\lambda^{-\tau}}),

and they satisfy the following estimates

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|v^{\star}-v\|_{0}+\|w^{\star}-w\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq C\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{-\tau},

(3.12)

\displaystyle\|v^{\star}-v\|_{1}+\|w^{\star}-w\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\delta^{\frac{1}{2}},

(3.13)

\displaystyle\|v^{\star}\|_{2}+\|w^{\star}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda^{2\tau-1},

and

(3.14)

\|\E\|_{0}\leq\bar{C}\delta\lambda^{1-\tau},\quad\|\E\|_{1}\leq\bar{C}\delta\lambda^{\tau},

where constant
C
and
\bar{C}
depend only on
\gamma
and
\alpha
.

Our proof for the proposition above differs from that for Proposition 3.1 in
[
7
]
due to the presence of the phase function
\Theta
. Indeed, the key ingredients of our proof consist of applying the elliptic estimates in Lemma
3.4
to control the error terms arising from
\Theta
.

Proof.

We divide the proof into three steps.

Step 1. Mollification.
Take the lengthscale
\ell=\lambda^{-\tau}
. Set
\tilde{\rho}=\rho*\varphi_{\ell}
and
\tilde{H}=H*\varphi_{\ell}
. Then by (
3.7
), (
3.8
) and Lemma
A.1
, we know,

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\rho}\|_{0}\leq C\delta^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\|\tilde{\rho}\|_{j}\leq C(j)\ell^{1-j}[\rho]_{1}\leq C(j)\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\ell^{1-j},

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\rho}-\rho\|_{0}\leq C\ell[\rho]_{1}\leq C\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\lambda\ell,

\displaystyle\|\tilde{H}\|_{0}\leq C\lambda^{-\alpha},\quad\|\tilde{H}\|_{j}\leq C(j)\ell^{1-j}[H]_{1}\leq C(j)\lambda^{1-\alpha}\ell^{1-j},

\displaystyle\|\tilde{H}-H\|_{0}\leq C\ell[H]_{1}\leq C\lambda^{1-\alpha}\ell,

for
j\in\N_{+}
. Let
\tilde{h}=\tilde{\rho}^{2}(\mathrm{Id}+\tilde{H})
and
h=\rho^{2}(\mathrm{Id}+{H})
, then

\displaystyle\|\tilde{h}-h\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{\rho}^{2}-\rho^{2}\|_{0}+\|\tilde{\rho}^{2}\tilde{H}-\rho^{2}H\|_{0}\leq C\delta\lambda\ell+C\delta\lambda^{1-\alpha}\ell\leq C\delta\lambda^{1-\tau},

\displaystyle\|\tilde{h}-h\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{\rho}^{2}-\rho^{2}\|_{1}+\|\tilde{\rho}^{2}(\tilde{H}-H)\|_{1}+\|(\tilde{\rho}^{2}-\rho^{2})H\|_{1}

Lemma 3.4
.

Let

\quad u=0,&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

\Omega

Proof.

Let

be the solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u_{1}=\Div\zeta,&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
\quad u_{1}=0,&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

and
u_{2}
be the solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta u_{2}=f,&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
\quad u_{2}=0,&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

then

, and

(3.30)

\|u\|_{0}\leq\|u_{1}\|_{0}+\|u_{2}\|_{0}.

By the maximum principle,

(3.31)

\|u_{2}\|_{0}\leq C(n,\mathrm{diam}(\Omega))\|f\|_{0},

where
\mathrm{diam}(\Omega)
is the diameter of
\Omega
.
Now (
3.29
) will follow from (
3.30
), (
3.31
) and the claim below:

Claim
:
\|u_{1}\|_{0}\leq C(n,\Omega)\|\zeta\|_{0}.

Let
\phi
be the solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Delta\phi=u_{1},&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
\quad\phi=0,&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

then

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{1}^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u_{1}\Delta\phi dx=\int_{\Omega}\phi\Delta u_{1}dx=\int_{\Omega}\phi\Div\zeta dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\zeta dx\leq\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}}\|\zeta\|_{L^{2}}.

Besides, the maximum principle tells us that

(3.32)

\|\phi\|_{0}\leq C(n,\mathrm{diam}(\Omega))\|u_{1}\|_{0}.

Noting

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi|^{2}dx=-\int_{\Omega}\phi\Delta\phi dx=-\int_{\Omega}\phi u_{1}dx\leq\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}},

we have

\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}},

and further,

\|u_{1}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leq\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\zeta\|_{L^{2}}.

Therefore, we get

(3.33)

\|u_{1}\|^{\frac{3}{2}}_{L^{2}}\leq\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\zeta\|_{L^{2}}\leq|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{4}}\|\phi\|_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\zeta\|_{0}\leq C(n,\Omega)\|u_{1}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{0}\|\zeta\|_{0}.

Here we apply (
3.32
) in the last inequality.
By Theorem 8.15 in
[
14
]
, we know

which combined with (
3.33
) will imply

(3.34)

\|u_{1}\|_{0}\leq C(n,\Omega)(\|\zeta\|_{0}+\|u_{1}\|_{0}^{\frac{1}{3}}\|\zeta\|_{0}^{\frac{2}{3}}).

Let

and

and the claim follows.

Case 2

which also implies

Then we complete the proof of the claim.

As discussed at the beginning of Section

, we show Theorem

(2) by seeking infinitely many symmetric

\begin{cases}-\mbox{curl curl }A=1,\\

where
V
satisfies the following Dirichlet problem:

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta V&=F(v)=2\nabla v\cdot\nabla\cot\Theta+v\Delta\cot\Theta,\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
V&=0,\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

We will solve the first system about

Proof of Theorem
1.1
(2).

We separate the proof into four steps.

Step 1. Matrix and initial subsolutions.
We construct the matrix
A
and initial subsolutions at the same time.
Let
u
be the solution to the following Dirichlet problem,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta u&=0,\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u&=g,\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

and
\psi
be the solution of the following Dirichlet problem,

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\psi&=1-\det\nabla^{2}u,\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
\psi&=0,\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Define

A=\psi\mathrm{Id}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla u\otimes\nabla u+(-u\cot\Theta+U)\mathrm{Id},

where
U
is the solution to

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta U&=F(u):=2\nabla u\cdot\nabla\cot\Theta+u\Delta\cot\Theta,\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
U&=0,\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Note that
|\sin\Theta|\geq c_{2}>0
as in the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
. So
\cot\Theta
is a
C^{2,\kappa}
bounded function.
The existence and regularity of
u\in C^{2,\kappa}
,
\psi\in C^{2,\kappa}
and
U\in C^{2,\kappa}
are guaranteed by the elliptic theory.

Obviously,
A\in C^{1,\kappa}(\overline{\Omega})
. Besides,

\displaystyle-\mathrm{curl}\,\mathrm{curl}\,A

\displaystyle=-\Delta\psi+\det\nabla^{2}u-\Delta(-u\cot\Theta+U)

\displaystyle=1+\Delta(u\cot\Theta)-F(u)

\displaystyle=1+\cot\Theta\Delta u=1.

With
\Delta u=0
, we know
1-\det\nabla^{2}u=1+(\partial^{2}_{11}u)^{2}+(\partial^{2}_{12}u)^{2}>0
, and hence by the strong maximum principle, we have

\psi(x)>0,\mbox{ for any }x\in\Omega.

Set the initial subsolution

(v_{0},w_{0})=(u,0).

Then,

A-D(v_{0},w_{0})=A-D(u,0)=\psi\mathrm{Id}:=\rho_{0}^{2}(\mathrm{Id}+H_{0}),

where
\rho_{0}=\sqrt{\psi}
and
H_{0}
is the zero matrix. Moreover,
v_{0}|_{\partial\Omega}=u|_{\partial\Omega}=g
.

Note that
(v_{0},w_{0})
and
A
are not unique. For example, taking
v_{0}
as before and
w_{0}\in\R^{2}
as an arbitrary constant vector will also work.

Step 2. Iteration.

We define the amplitude sequence
\delta_{q}
and the frequency sequence
\lambda_{q}
inductively by

\delta_{1}=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\rho_{0}^{2},\quad\lambda_{q+1}=\lambda_{q}^{b},\quad\lambda_{q}=M\delta_{q}^{-\frac{1}{2\beta}},

where
\beta
is a arbitrary fixed constant in
(0,\frac{1}{5})
, and

(4.2)

b=1+\frac{9\sigma}{1-5\beta}\quad(\text{then }5\beta(b-1)+9\sigma=b-1),

and
M
is a large constant to be fixed. We then decomposes
\overline{\Omega}
according to the level sets of
\rho_{0}
,

\Omega_{q}=\left\{x\in\overline{\Omega}:\,\rho_{0}(x)\geq 2\delta_{q+2}^{1/2}\right\},\,\,\tilde{\Omega}_{q}=\left\{x\in\overline{\Omega}:\,\rho_{0}(x)\geq\frac{3}{2}\delta_{q+2}^{1/2}\right\}

for any
q\in\mathbb{N}
.
With Proposition
3.1
, for any
q\in\N
, we inductively construct
(v_{q},w_{q},\rho_{q},H_{q})
such that the following hold:

(1)_{q}

For any
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, it holds that

D_{q}=:A-D(v_{q},w_{q})=\rho_{q}^{2}(\textrm{Id}+H_{q}).

(2)_{q}

If
x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\tilde{\Omega}_{q}
, then
(v_{q},w_{q},\rho_{q},H_{q})(x)=(v_{0},w_{0},\rho_{0},H_{0})(x).

(3)_{q}

The following estimates hold in
\overline{\Omega}
:

\displaystyle\|\rho_{q}\|_{0}\leq 2\delta_{q+1}^{1/2},\quad\|H_{q}\|_{0}\leq 4\lambda_{q+1}^{-\frac{\alpha}{b}}.

(4)_{q}

If
x\in\tilde{\Omega}_{j}
for some
j\geq q,
we get the following estimates:

\displaystyle\rho_{q}\geq\frac{3}{2}\delta_{j+2}^{1/2},\quad|\nabla\rho_{q}|\leq\delta_{j+1}^{1/2}\lambda_{j+2},\quad\frac{|\nabla\rho_{q}|}{\rho_{q}}\leq\lambda_{j+2},

\displaystyle|\nabla^{2}v_{q}|+|\nabla^{2}w_{q}|\leq\delta_{j+1}^{1/2}\lambda_{j+2},\quad|\nabla H_{q}|\leq\lambda_{j+2}^{1-\frac{\alpha}{b}}.

(5)_{q}

For any
x\in\overline{\Omega},

q\geq 1,

(4.3)

\|v_{q}-v_{q-1}\|_{j}+\|w_{q}-w_{q-1}\|_{j}\leq\overline{C}\delta_{q}^{1/2}\lambda_{q}^{j-1},j=0,1,

where
\overline{C}
is the constant in the conclusion of Proposition
3.1
.

Indeed,
(v_{0},w_{0},\rho_{0},H_{0})
has been constructed in Step 1 and it is not hard to verify
(1)_{0}-(4)_{0}
. Similar to Step 2 of the proof of Proposition 2.1 in
[
7
]
, by using Proposition
3.1
we are able to inductively get
(v_{q},w_{q},\rho_{q},H_{q})
for
q\geq 1
. Since the proof is exactly the same as in
[
7
]
, we omit the details.

Step 3. Taking limits.

By
(3)_{q}
and
(5)_{q}
, we know
(\rho_{q},H_{q})
is a Cauchy sequence in
C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
, and
(v_{q},w_{q})
is a Cauchy sequence in
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
, respectively. Hence we can assume

(v_{q},w_{q},\rho_{q},H_{q})\rightarrow(\bar{v},\bar{w},0,0)\text{ uniformly in }\overline{\Omega},

for some functions
\bar{v}
and
\bar{w}
.

Moreover, for any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{5})
and
\varepsilon>0
, we can choose
\beta\in(\alpha,\frac{1}{5})
in Step 2. By
(4)_{q}-(5)_{q}
,
(4)_{q+1}
and interpolation, we have for any
\alpha\in(0,\frac{\beta}{b^{2}})
,

\|v_{q}-v_{q+1}\|_{1+\alpha}+\|w_{q}-w_{q+1}\|_{1+\alpha}\leq 4\delta_{q+1}^{(1-\alpha)/2}\delta_{q+2}^{\alpha/2}\lambda_{q+3}^{\alpha}\leq M^{\beta}\lambda_{q+1}^{\alpha b^{2}-\beta},

which implies
(v_{q},w_{q})
uniformly converges to
(\bar{v},\bar{w})
in
C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
. Since
\alpha
and
b
can be taken arbitrarily close to
0
and
1
by (
4.2
), respectively,
(\bar{v},\bar{w})\in C^{1,\alpha}({\Omega}),
for any
\alpha<\beta
. From
(5)_{q}
, for any
\epsilon>0,
we also have

\displaystyle\|\bar{v}-v_{0}\|_{0}+\|\bar{w}-w_{0}\|_{0}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\|v_{q}-v_{q+1}\|_{0}+\|w_{q+1}-w_{q}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\overline{C}\delta_{q+1}^{1/2}\lambda_{q+1}^{-1}\leq\overline{C}M^{-1}\leq\epsilon,

if
M
is taken larger.

Step 4. Very weak solutions.
We will show
\bar{v}
is a very weak solution to (
1.1
). On the one hand, by construction, it is easy to see
(\bar{v},\bar{w})|_{\partial\Omega}=(v_{0},w_{0})|_{\partial\Omega}
. On the other hand,
\rho_{q}\to 0
implies
A-D(\bar{v},\bar{w})=0.
Thus for any
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, by integration by parts and
\mbox{curl curl }A=-1
, we have

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\phi\sin\Theta dx=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{Trace}(H_{\phi\sin\Theta}\cdot A)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{Trace}(H_{\phi\sin\Theta}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bar{v}\otimes\nabla\bar{v})dx-\int_{\Omega}\bar{v}\Delta(\phi\cos\Theta)dx,

where we have used
\mbox{curl curl}(\mathrm{sym}\nabla\bar{w})=0
and the notation

H_{f}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}\partial_{22}^{2}f&-\partial_{12}^{2}f\\
-\partial_{12}^{2}f&\partial_{11}^{2}f\\
\end{array}\right)\text{ for any $C^{2}$ function }f.

Then it implies

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{1}\bar{v}\partial_{2}\bar{v}\partial_{12}^{2}(\phi\sin\Theta)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{1}\bar{v})^{2}\partial_{22}^{2}(\phi\sin\Theta)-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{2}\bar{v})^{2}\partial_{11}^{2}(\phi\sin\Theta)dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\bar{v}\Delta(\phi\cos\Theta)dx-\int_{\Omega}\phi\sin\Theta dx.

Finally, since
\epsilon
can be arbitrary and the initial subsolution is also not unique (note each
(v_{q},w_{q})
can be an initial subsolution), (
1.1
) admits infinitely many very weak solutions
\bar{v}
’s. We then complete the proof.
∎

Useful lemmas and proofs of Lemmas

Lemma A.1

(
[

]
)
.

For any
r,s\geq 0
and
0<\alpha\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle[h*\phi_{l}]_{r+s}\leq Cl^{-s}[h]_{r};

\displaystyle\|h-h*\phi_{l}\|_{r}\leq Cl^{1-r}[h]_{1},\mbox{ for }0\leq r\leq 1;

\displaystyle\|h-h*\phi_{l}\|_{j}\leq Cl^{2-j}\|h\|_{2},\mbox{ for }j=0,1;

\displaystyle\|(h_{1}h_{2})*\phi_{l}-(h_{1}*\phi_{l})(h_{2}*\phi_{l})\|_{r}\leq Cl^{2\alpha-r}\|h_{1}\|_{\alpha}\|h_{2}\|_{\alpha},

with the constant
C
depending only on
s,r,\alpha,\phi
.

Lemma A.2

(
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\R^{2}
be a simply connected open bounded set with a smooth boundary and
H:\Sigma\to\R^{2\times 2}
a smooth
2\times 2
symmetric positive definite matrix-valued function, such that for some
0<\alpha<1
, and
c,M\geq 1
,

c^{-1}\mathrm{Id}\leq H\leq c\mathrm{Id},\,\,\|H\|_{C^{\alpha}(\Sigma)}\leq M.

Then there exist a smooth diffeomorphism
\Phi:\Sigma\to\R^{2}
and a smooth positive function
\vartheta:\Sigma\to\R
satisfying

H=\vartheta^{2}(\nabla\Phi_{1}\otimes\nabla\Phi_{1}+\nabla\Phi_{2}\otimes\nabla\Phi_{2}).

Moreover the following estimates hold:

\displaystyle\det(\nabla\Phi(x))\geq c_{0},\,\,\vartheta(x)\geq c_{0},\mbox{ for all }x\in\Sigma,

\displaystyle\|\vartheta\|_{j+\alpha}+\|\nabla\Phi\|_{j+\alpha}\leq C(j)\|H\|_{j+\alpha},\,\,j\in\N,

where the constants
c_{0}>0
,
C(j)\geq 1
depend only on
\alpha,c,M
and on
\Sigma
.

A sketch proof of Lemma

.

Recall the definition of

,

For any nonnegative integer

\begin{split}|\partial_{t}^{k}\Gamma_{1}(s,t)|+|\partial_{s}\partial^{k}_{t}\Gamma_{2}(s,t)|&\leq C|s|,\\
|\partial_{s}\partial_{t}^{k}\Gamma_{1}(s,t)|&\leq C,\\
|\partial_{t}^{k}\Gamma_{2}(s,t)|&\leq Cs^{2}.\end{split}

The derivatives of

,

could be calculated directly by the chain rule, then the desired estimates directly follow from the assumptions on

, Lemma
A.2

A.1
). ∎
