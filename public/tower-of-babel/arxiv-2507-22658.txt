Quasiconformal characterization of Schottky sets
Dimitrios Ntalampekos

Abstract
. The complement of the union of a collection of disjoint open disks in the 2 2 -sphere is called a Schottky set. We prove that a subset S S of the 2 2 -sphere is quasiconformally equivalent to a Schottky set if and only if every pair of complementary components of S S can be mapped to a pair of open disks with a uniformly quasiconformal homeomorphism of the sphere. Our theorem applies to Sierpiński carpets and gaskets, yielding for the first time a general quasiconformal uniformization result for gaskets. Moreover, it contains Bonk’s uniformization result for carpets as a special case and does not rely on the condition of uniform relative separation that is used in relevant works.

1.
Introduction

In this work we provide a necessary and sufficient condition so that a compact set in the Riemann sphere is quasiconformally equivalent to a Schottky set. We discuss some background before stating the main result.

1.1.
Background

A
(geometric) disk
in the Riemann sphere
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is an open ball
B(a,r)
in the spherical metric, where
0<r<\pi
. The complement of the union of a collection of disjoint open (geometric) disks in the Riemann sphere is called a
Schottky set
. Special cases of Schottky sets include closures of circle domains and round Sierpiński carpets. A
circle domain
is a domain in the Riemann sphere whose complementary components are closed disks or points. A
Sierpiński carpet
is a compact set in the sphere with empty interior whose complementary components are countably many Jordan regions, called
peripheral disks
, with disjoint closures and with diameters shrinking to zero. A fundamental result of Whyburn
[
37
]
states that all Sierpiński carpets are homeomorphic to each other. A
round
Sierpiński carpet has the additional requirement that its peripheral disks are geometric disks.

It is desirable to find a quasiconformal homeomorphism of the sphere that transforms a compact subset of the sphere to a Schottky set or to a round Sierpiński carpet, due to the strong geometric properties of the latter classes of sets. For example, they enjoy strong rigidity properties and they can be studied using dynamical methods with the aid of groups generated by reflections in the complementary disks; for instance, see
[
14
,
6
,
25
,
26
,
7
,
27
,
30
]
.

A first uniformization result for round carpets was proved by McMullen
[
23
]
, who showed that if the limit set of a convex cocompact Kleinian group is a Sierpiński carpet, then it is quasiconformally equivalent to a round carpet. Herron and Koskela
[
18
]
proved that each uniform domain can be transformed to a circle domain via a quasiconformal homeomorphism of the sphere. In particular, the closure of a uniform domain can be quasiconformally uniformized by a Schottky set. This generalized an earlier result of Herron
[
19
]
.

A few years later Schramm
[
34
]
devised a powerful tool for the study of uniformization and rigidity problems in the plane, known as transboundary modulus; see Section
5.1
. This tool turned out to be of paramount importance for the study of Koebe’s conjecture, a deep open problem in complex analysis that is closely related to the uniformization of Sierpiński carpets.
Koebe’s conjecture
asserts that for every domain
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
there exists a conformal map from
\Omega
onto a circle domain. In contrast, in this work we are interested in quasiconformal homeomorphisms
of the entire sphere
that transform a given set to a Schottky set or a carpet.

Bonk
[
10
]
used the full potential of transboundary modulus in order to provide a sufficient criterion for a Sierpiński carpet to be quasiconformally equivalent to a round carpet. Let
S\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a Sierpiński carpet and let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be its peripheral disks. We say that the peripheral disks of
S
are
uniform quasidisks
if, for each
i\in I
,
U_{i}
can be mapped to a geometric disk by a
K
-quasiconformal homeomorphism of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, where
K\geq 1
is a uniform constant. We say that the peripheral disks of
S
are
uniformly relatively separated
if there exists a constant
\delta>0
such that the
relative distance
of
U_{i}
and
U_{j}
, defined by

\displaystyle\Delta(U_{i},U_{j})=\frac{\dist(U_{i},U_{j})}{\min\{\diam(U_{i}),\diam(U_{j})\}},

is bounded from below by
\delta
for each
i\neq j
. Bonk proved that if the peripheral disks of
S
are uniformly relatively separated uniform quasidisks, then there exists a quasiconformal homeomorphism of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
that maps
S
onto a round carpet. Trivially, the assumption of uniform quasidisks is necessary for the conclusion. The uniform relative separation prevents large peripheral disks from being too close to each other. This condition is obviously not necessary, since round carpets need not satisfy it.

Bonk’s excellent criterion was exploited in rigidity problems that appear in the study of the standard Sierpiński carpet and of Julia sets
[
8
,
7
,
9
]
, and was extended to non-planar carpets
[
24
,
32
]
. However, the removal or replacement of the assumption of uniform relative separation with a weaker assumption remained elusive. It was observed by the author in
[
28
]
*Prop. 3.1.6 that the assumption of uniform relative separation is necessary for the quasiconformal transformation of a round carpet to a square carpet (defined in the obvious manner). This provided some evidence that the result of Bonk is optimal.

Subsequent works of the author
[
28
,
29
]
removed the assumption of uniform relative separation (and also weakened the assumption of uniform quasidisks) at the cost of weakening the regularity of the uniformizing map, which transforms an arbitrary carpet to a round or square carpet. For instance, the uniformizing maps in
[
28
]
are termed
carpet-quasiconformal
and are required to quasipreserve a generalization of transboundary modulus, called
carpet modulus
. In particular, the uniformizing maps that appear in
[
28
,
29
]
are not quasiconformal in the usual sense in general. These results, therefore, provided further evidence that the separation assumption of Bonk cannot be relaxed.

The quasiconformal uniformization of carpets whose peripheral disks are too close to each other, as well as, of limiting objects in which “peripheral disks” can touch each other had withstood extensive investigation. In particular, a general uniformization result for sets that resemble the Sierpiński gasket (in the sense that complementary components can touch each other) seemed to be out of reach.

However, very recently, the author and Luo
[
21
]
characterized, under some mild conditions, Julia sets of rational maps that can be quasiconformally uniformized by round
gaskets
; see
[
21
]
*Definition 1.1 for the definition of a topological gasket. Specifically, it is shown that a Julia set that does not contain critical points can be quasiconformally uniformized by a round gasket if and only if it is a
fat gasket
, i.e., boundaries of Fatou components intersect tangentially. Yet, the result and its proof rely heavily on the structure of Julia sets and on complex dynamical methods, and it is clear that the proof does not extend to arbitrary gaskets not arising from a dynamical framework.

In this work we overcome this obstacle and, as our main theorem, we provide a characterization of compact subsets of the sphere that are quasiconformally equivalent to Schottky sets, removing entirely the separation assumption of Bonk.

1.2.
Main result

Let
U,V\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be disjoint Jordan regions. We say that the pair
U,V
is
quasiconformally circularizable
if there exists a quasiconformal homeomorphism
\phi\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\phi(U)
and
\phi(V)
are (geometric) disks. If
\phi
can be taken to be
K
-quasiconformal for some
K\geq 1
, then we say that the pair
U,V
is
K
-quasiconformally circularizable. Note that the closures of
U
and
V
are allowed to intersect each other.

Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of disjoint Jordan regions in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
. We say that the regions
U_{i}
,
i\in I
, are
uniformly quasiconformally pairwise circularizable
if there exists
K\geq 1
such that every pair
U_{i},U_{j}
,
i\neq j
, is
K
-quasiconformally circularizable. We now state our main theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of at least two disjoint Jordan regions in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
. The following are quantitatively equivalent.

(i)

The regions
U_{i}
,
i\in I
, are uniformly quasiconformally pairwise circularizable.

(ii)

There exists a collection of disjoint disks
\{D_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and a quasiconformal homeomorphism
f\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
that maps the compact set
S=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
onto the Schottky set
T=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}D_{i}
and is
1
-quasiconformal on
S
.

In that case, the map
f|_{S}
is unique up to postcomposition with a conformal automorphism of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
.

Here, a quasiconformal map
f\colon U\to V
between open subsets of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is said to be
1
-quasiconformal on a measurable set
S\subset U
if
\|Df(z)\|^{2}=J_{f}(z)
for a.e.
z\in S
. The statement is quantitative in the usual sense. Namely, the quasiconformal distortion bound of the uniformizing map and the constant of the quasiconformal circularization assumption depend only on each other.

We note that the assumptions of Bonk on uniform quasidisks that are uniformly relatively separated are stronger than the quasiconformal circularization assumption; see Proposition
3.1
. Thus, our result implies Bonk’s theorem
[
10
]
*Theorem 1.1. Also, the uniqueness in the last statement of our theorem is based on and extends results from
[
6
]
, regarding the rigidity of Schottky sets of area zero.

One of the novelties of the proof of existence in Theorem
1.1
is that it relies on properties of groups generated by reflections in disjoint circles, known as Schottky groups; see Section
4.3
. Such techniques have been used extensively in the past for the proof of significant rigidity results (e.g., see
[
14
,
6
]
). It is quite remarkable that a reflection argument can lead to extra mileage in existence results and we expect that the methods introduced here can result in further progress in other uniformization problems in the complex plane. Furthermore, our theorem is expected to have considerable consequences in complex dynamics, yielding uniformization and rigidity results beyond the hyperbolic setting and without any limitation on the separation of Fatou components.

We remark that a priori it is not even clear whether there exists a homeomorphism that maps the set
S
of Theorem
1.1
onto a Schottky set. A necessary condition is that for any
i\neq j
the closures
\overline{U_{i}}
and
\overline{U_{j}}
are either disjoint or intersect at a single point; in our case, this is guaranteed by the assumption that
U_{i}\cup U_{j}
can be mapped to the union of two disks with a homeomorphism of the sphere. A more subtle condition is that no three regions
\overline{U_{i}}
,
i\in I
, can meet at a point; this is also guaranteed by the quasiconformal circularization assumption; see Lemma
3.2
. Yet another requirement is that for each
\varepsilon>0
there exist at most finitely many
i\in I
such that
\diam(U_{i})>\varepsilon
; this follows from the assumption that the regions
U_{i}
,
i\in I
, are disjoint uniform quasidisks. Are these necessary conditions also sufficient?

Problem 1.2
.

Find a topological characterization of Schottky sets.

We also state a consequence of Theorem
1.1
regarding circle domains.

Corollary 1.3
.

A domain
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
can be mapped onto a circle domain by a conformal map that is the restriction of a quasiconformal homeomorphism of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
if and only if the components of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
are Jordan regions that are uniformly quasiconformally pairwise circularizable.

This corollary resolves a problem studied by Herron and Koskela
[
19
,
18
]
, who provided sufficient conditions for the transformation of a domain onto a circle domain with a quasiconformal homeomorphism of the sphere.

1.3.
Proof outline

We outline the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Note that (ii) trivially implies (i), so we sketch the proof of the reverse implication.

Preliminary reductions

The idea of the proof is to consider a finite subset
J
of
I
and find a quasiconformal homeomorphism of the sphere that maps
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in J}U_{i}
onto a Schottky set. Then one wishes to show equicontinuity of such maps over all finite sets
J\subset I
and pass to a subsequential limit in order to obtain the desired uniformizing map.

Even at the very first step we face an obstacle that is not present in other works. Koebe’s uniformization theorem allows one to map a finitely connected domain conformally onto a circle domain. If the regions
U_{i}
,
i\in J
, had disjoint closures, then
G=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in J}\overline{U_{i}}
would be a finitely connected domain. For instance, this is the case in the setting of Sierpiński carpets. However, in our setting the closures
\overline{U_{i}}
,
i\in J
, can intersect. We overcome this by finding an exhaustion
U_{i}(n)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, of each
U_{i}
,
i\in J
, so that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the regions
{U_{i}(n)}
,
i\in J
, have disjoint closures and most importantly they are still uniformly quasiconformally pairwise circularizable; see Proposition
3.3
. This step is technical and the obvious approaches do not seem to work. On the other hand, note that Theorem
1.1
, once proved, implies the existence of such exhaustions immediately.

In the next step, assuming that the closures
\overline{U_{i}}
,
i\in J
, are disjoint, we apply Koebe’s uniformization theorem to find a conformal map from the domain
G=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in J}\overline{U_{i}}
onto a circle domain. This conformal map extends to the closure
\overline{G}
and we assume that
\partial U_{i}
is mapped to a circle that bounds a closed disk
K_{i}\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus f(G)
for each
i\in J
. We wish to find an extension of
f
to a uniformly quasiconformal homeomorphism of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
. Then standard equicontinuity properties of uniformly quasiconformal maps will allow us to pass to the limit and prove the uniformization result.

By a consequence of the Beurling–Ahlfors extension, it suffices to show that, after suitable normalizations, the conformal map
f
is uniformly quasisymmetric in the finitely connected domain
G
.

Proving quasisymmetric behavior via conformal modulus

Classical results (see e.g.
[
16
]
*Theorem 11.14) imply that (quasi)conformal maps are quasisymmetric away from the boundary. This is not quite satisfactory in our case, since we need
f
to be quasisymmetric on all of
G
. However, the method of proof is more valuable.

According to that method, one needs to show that if two continua
E,F\subset G
are relatively close to each other (meaning that the relative distance
\Delta(E,F)
is bounded from above), then the family of curves in
G
connecting them, denoted by
\Gamma(E,F;G)
, has large conformal modulus. On the other hand, if the images
E^{\prime}=f(E)
,
F^{\prime}=f(F)
are not relatively close to each other (that is,
\Delta(E^{\prime},F^{\prime})
is large), then one should prove that the conformal modulus of the family
\Gamma(E^{\prime},F^{\prime};f(G))
is small. Assuming these bounds, the conformal invariance of modulus implies that if
\Delta(E,F)
is small, then
\Delta(E^{\prime},F^{\prime})
must also be small in a quantitative way. This is sufficient to show that
f
is quasisymmetric by a classical result of Väisälä.

The desired upper bound for the conformal modulus of
\Gamma(E^{\prime},F^{\prime};f(G))
is immediate from the fact that the modulus of curves connecting the boundary components of a Euclidean annulus with inner radius
r
and outer radius
R
is
2\pi(\log(R/r))^{-1}
; this converges to
0
as
r\to 0
. However, the modulus of curves in
G
that connect
E
and
F
can be very small even when
\Delta(E,F)
is large. This is because passages between complementary components of
G
can be very narrow; see Figure

Proving quasisymmetric behavior via transboundary modulus

In order to achieve large modulus lower bounds, we need to switch to a different notion of modulus, the transboundary modulus that was introduced by Schramm; see Section
5.1
for the definition. The striking advantage of transboundary modulus is that it allows for curves to exit the domain
G
and travel through complementary components, while maintaining at the same time conformal invariance. If the geometry of the complementary components of
G
is nice, which is the case in our setting since each
U_{i}
,
i\in J
, is a uniform quasidisk, then the transboundary modulus of the family of curves in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
(rather than in
G
) connecting
E
and
F
is large, whenever
\Delta(E,F)
is bounded from above; see Figure
1
. In modern terminology, transboundary modulus has the Loewner property; see Section
5.3
. We prove this in Proposition
5.7
. We note that Bonk obtains a similar result
[
10
]
*Prop. 8.1, but he imposes the uniform relative separation assumption, which is not available here.

So, based on the method we discussed, now one needs to go back to the images
E^{\prime},F^{\prime}
and ensure that whenever
\Delta(E^{\prime},F^{\prime})
is large, then the transboundary modulus of the family of curves
\Gamma(E^{\prime},F^{\prime};\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
is small. Unfortunately, this is not the case in general! It was observed by Bonk that the transboundary modulus can be actually large. This is because there can be some large disks that function as bridges between two relatively small continua
E^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime}
; see Figure

2
) that can serve as bridges between
E^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime}
and cause transboundary modulus to be large. He resolves this issue by restricting to the subfamily of curves in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
connecting
E^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime}
and avoiding a bounded number of large disks that function as bridges.

Proving quasisymmetric behavior without the separation assumption

We remark that the method of Bonk shows that any conformal map is quasisymmetric as long as it maps a finitely connected domain whose complementary components are uniformly relatively separated uniform (closed) quasidisks onto a domain whose complementary components are uniform (closed) quasidisks. Thus, no particular property of geometric disks is used, other than the trivial fact that they are quasidisks. Instead, here we use the full strength of the geometry of disks, which is manifested by the existence of anti-conformal reflections along their boundaries.

We show that if
\Delta(E^{\prime},F^{\prime})
is relatively large, there are
at most two
disks
K_{i_{1}},K_{i_{2}}
that are large and are close to
E^{\prime},F^{\prime}
that could potentially serve as bridges in the argument discussed above. To put it differently, if two disks
K_{i_{1}},K_{i_{2}}
are so large that they intersect both boundary components of an annulus, then
all other disks
that are disjoint from
K_{i_{1}},K_{i_{2}}
must be small in a sense with respect to that annulus; see Proposition
4.2
for a precise statement. Note that the number
2
is important here. If one tried to prove an analogous statement for squares instead of disks, perhaps the appropriate maximum number of squares that can serve as bridges would be
4
.

Let
U_{i_{1}}
and
U_{i_{2}}
be the Jordan regions that correspond to
K_{i_{1}}
and
K_{i_{2}}
under
f^{-1}
, respectively. We now use the quasiconformal circularization assumption to map the pair
U_{i_{1}},U_{i_{2}}
onto geometric disks
V_{i_{1}},V_{i_{2}}
with a quasiconformal homeomorphism
h
of the sphere. The remaining regions
U_{i}

, are transformed to uniform quasidisks
V_{i}=h(U_{i})
. Let
g=f\circ h^{-1}
. Remember, our task is to show favorable distortion bounds for
g
, and thus for
f
. Namely, if
\Delta(h(E),h(F))
is small, we wish to show that
\Delta(E^{\prime},F^{\prime})
is also small.

An important ingredient of the proof is that the reflections of
all other disks

K_{i_{1}}
and
K_{i_{2}}
remain

and do not create new bridges between

and

E^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime}
. In addition, all iterated reflections of

, inside
K_{i_{1}}
and
K_{i_{2}}

consists of favorable disks that do not serve as bridges. This is an elaborate result proved in Proposition

On the other side, the orbit of the quasidisks

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation

If
a
is a parameter we use the notation
C(a),L(a)
, etc. for positive constants that depend only on the parameter
a
. We say that a statement is
quantitative
if the constants or parameters appearing in the conclusions depend only on the constants or parameters in the assumptions.

Throughout the paper we mostly use the spherical metric
\sigma
on the Riemann sphere
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
. For
z,w\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
we have

\sigma(z,w)=\inf_{\gamma}\int_{\gamma}\frac{2|dz|}{1+|z|^{2}},

where the infimum is taken over all rectifiable curves
\gamma
in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
connecting
z
and
w
. For the purpose of estimating the spherical metric, we will also use the chordal metric on
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
defined for
z\in\mathbb{C}
by

\chi(z,w)=\frac{2|z-w|}{\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}}\,\,\,\text{when $w\in\mathbb{C}$ and}\,\,\,\chi(z,\infty)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}}.

Note that
\chi\leq\sigma\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\chi
. We will also use the spherical measure on
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, which is denoted by
\Sigma
and is given by

\Sigma(E)=\int_{E}\frac{4\,dxdy}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}

for a measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{C}
. The Euclidean area of a measurable set
E\subset\mathbb{C}
is denoted by
|E|
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. The open ball of radius
r>0
centered at a point
x\in X
is denoted by
B_{d}(x,r)
and the closed ball is denoted by
\overline{B}_{d}(x,r)
. The diameter of a set
E\subset X
is denoted by
\diam_{d}(E)
. For the Euclidean metric in
\mathbb{C}
we use the subscript
e
when necessary. For example
B_{e}(z,r)=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|z-w|<r\}
. Most of our considerations will be on the sphere
(\widehat{\mathbb{C}},\sigma)
and we will drop the subscript
\sigma
from the notation. For example, if
E\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, then
\diam(E)
denotes the spherical diameter of
E
. Also, when
z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and
0<r<\pi
,
B(z,r)
is called a (geometric) disk because its boundary is a circle. A
region
or a
domain
is a connected open subset of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
.

The Hausdorff distance of two sets
E,F
in a metric space is the infimum of all
r>0
such that
E
is contained in the open
r
-neighborhood of
F
and
F
is contained in the
r
-neighborhood of
E
.

2.2.
Quasiconformal maps

Let
K\geq 1
. An orientation-preserving homeomorphism
f\colon U\to V
between open subsets of
\mathbb{C}
is
quasiconformal
if
f
lies in the Sobolev space
W^{1,2}_{\loc}(U)
and

\|Df(z)\|^{2}\leq KJ_{f}(z)

for a.e.
z\in\mathbb{C}
; here
\|\cdot\|
denotes the operator norm of the matrix
Df
and
J_{f}
is its Jacobian determinant. In that case we say that
f
is
K
-quasiconformal. A homeomorphism
f\colon U\to V
between open subsets of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is quasiconformal if
f|_{U\setminus\{\infty,f^{-1}(\infty)\}}
is quasiconformal in the above sense. We will freely use the fundamental facts that the inverse of a quasiconformal map is quasiconformal, that the composition of two quasiconformal maps is quasiconformal, and that sets of measure zero are mapped to sets of measure zero under a quasiconformal map.
We direct the reader to
[
20
]
for further background.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f\colon U\to V
,
g\colon V\to W
be quasiconformal homeomorphisms between open subsets of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
such that
f
is
1
-quasiconformal on a measurable set
S\subset U
and
g
is conformal on
f(S)
. Then
f^{-1}
is
1
-quasiconformal on
f(S)
and
g\circ f
is
1
-quasiconformal on
S
.

Recall that a quasiconformal map
f
is
1
-quasiconformal on a measurable set
S
if
\|Df(z)\|^{2}=J_{f}(z)
for a.e.
z\in S
. Since
f
is quasiconformal, we have
J_{f}(z)>0
for a.e.
z\in S

[
20
]
*Section IV.1.5. Thus,
f
is
1
-quasiconformal on
S
if and only if
Df(z)
is a conformal linear matrix for a.e.
z\in S
. A
conformal linear matrix
is a positive scalar multiple of a special orthogonal matrix. Note that the inverse of a conformal linear matrix or the product of two such matrices is also a conformal linear matrix.

Proof.

Since
f
is quasiconformal,
f^{-1}
is quasiconformal and we have
Df^{-1}(f(z))=Df(z)^{-1}
for a.e.
z\in U
, as a consequence of the chain rule; see
[
17
]
*Theorem 5.13 for a very general statement. The assumption that
f
is
1
-quasiconformal on
S
implies that
Df(z)
is a conformal linear matrix for a.e.
z\in S
. Thus,
Df(z)^{-1}
has the same property. Since
f
maps sets of measure zero to sets of measure zero, we conclude that
Df^{-1}(w)
is a conformal linear matrix for a.e.
w\in f(S)
, as desired.

For the second assertion, by the chain rule we have
D(g\circ f)(z)=Dg(f(z))\cdot Df(z)
for a.e.
z\in U
. By assumption,
Dg(f(z))
and
Df(z)
are conformal linear matrices for a.e.
z\in U
; here one uses the fact that
f^{-1}
maps sets of measure zero to sets of measure zero. Thus,
D(g\circ f)(z)
is a conformal linear matrix for a.e.
z\in S
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

One can extend the definition of (
1
-)quasiconformal maps and allow them to be orientation-reversing; for example, this is the case for anti-Möbius transformations. Then the conclusion of Lemma
2.1
remains valid for such maps. In particular, if
f
is orientation-preserving as in Lemma
2.1
and
\phi,\psi
are anti-Möbius transformations, then
\psi\circ f\circ\phi^{-1}
is
1
-quasiconformal on
\phi(S)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
U,V\subset\mathbb{C}
be open sets and let
K\geq 1
. Let
f_{n}\colon U\to V
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be a sequence of
K
-quasiconformal homeomorphisms that converge locally uniformly to a quasiconformal map
f\colon U\to V
. If
f_{n}
is
1
-quasiconformal on a measurable set
S\subset U
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
f
is also
1
-quasiconformal on
S
.

Proof.

Let
B\subset U
be a ball such that
\overline{B}\subset U
. By uniform convergence,
f_{n}(B)
is contained in a fixed compact subset of
V
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By the change of variables formula
[
20
]
*Section III.3.4 we have

\int_{B}\|Df_{n}\|^{2}\leq K\int_{B}J_{f}=K|f_{n}(B)|,

which is uniformly bounded in
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, the sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded in
W^{1,2}(B)
. By the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, there exists a subsequence of
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that converges weakly in
W^{1,2}(B)
to an element of
W^{1,2}(B)
. Since
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
f
strongly in
L^{2}(B)
, we see that
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
f
weakly in
W^{1,2}(B)
; see the discussion in
[
33
]
*Section VI.7.8.

We now resort to
[
12
]
*Theorem 6.1, which implies that if
\|Df_{n}(z)\|^{2}\leq M(z)J_{f_{n}}(z)
for a.e.
z\in B
and for some measurable function
M<\infty
, and
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
f
weakly in
W^{1,2}(B)
, then
\|Df(z)\|^{2}\leq M(z)J_{f}(z)
for a.e.
z\in B
. This gives immediately that for
z\in B\cap S
we have
\|Df(z)\|^{2}=J_{f}(z)
. This is true for every ball
B\subset\overline{B}\subset U
, so we obtain
\|Df(z)\|^{2}=J_{f}(z)
for a.e.
z\in S
.
∎

2.3.
Quasi-Möbius and quasisymmetric maps

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space and
a,b,c,d\in X
be distinct points. We define their cross ratio to be

[a,b,c,d]=\frac{d_{X}(a,c)d_{X}(b,d)}{d_{X}(a,d)d_{X}(b,c)}.

Observe that if
X=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, equipped with the chordal metric, and
a,b,c,d\neq\infty
, then

[a,b,c,d]=\frac{|a-c||b-d|}{|a-d||b-c|}.

The factors containing the point
\infty
are omitted. For example,

[a,b,c,\infty]=\frac{|a-c|}{|b-c|}.

A homeomorphism
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is called a
distortion function
. A homeomorphism
f\colon X\to Y
between metric spaces is
quasi-Möbius
if there exists a distortion function
\eta
such that

[f(a),f(b),f(c),f(d)]\leq\eta([a,b,c,d])

for every quadruple of distinct points
a,b,c,d\in X
. In that case, we say that
f
is
\eta
-quasi-Möbius. Suppose that
X,Y\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and
f\colon X\to Y
is
\eta
-quasi-Möbius. Then the cross ratios in
X
and
Y
may be computed with the chordal or the Euclidean distance interchangeably. Thus, we do not need to specify which of the two metrics is used in
X
and
Y
. Also, since cross ratios are invariant under Möbius transformations, the composition of
f
with a Möbius transformation is
\eta
-quasi-Möbius as well. Let
[a,b,c,d]_{\sigma}
be the cross ratio computed with respect to the spherical metric. We have

\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}[a,b,c,d]\leq[a,b,c,d]_{\sigma}\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}[a,b,c,d].

This shows that we have the freedom of switching between the Euclidean, chordal, or spherical metrics when dealing with quasi-Möbius maps between subsets of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
at the cost of changing slightly the distortion function.

A homeomorphism
f\colon X\to Y
between metric spaces is
quasisymmetric
if there exists a distortion function
\eta
such that

\frac{d_{Y}(f(a),f(b))}{d_{Y}(f(a),f(c))}\leq\eta\left(\frac{d_{X}(a,b)}{d_{X}(a,c)}\right)

for every triple of distinct points
a,b,c\in X
. In that case we say that
f
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric.

A homeomorphism
f\colon X\to Y
between metric spaces is
bi-Lipschitz
if there exists
L\geq 1
such that for all
x,y\in X
we have

L^{-1}d_{X}(x,y)\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\leq Ld_{X}(x,y).

In that case we say that
f
is
L
-bi-Lipschitz.

We list some fundamental properties of the above types of maps.

(Q-1)

If
f
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric (resp. quasi-Möbius), then
f^{-1}
is
\eta^{\prime}
-quasisymmetric (resp. quasi-Möbius) for
\eta^{\prime}(t)=1/\eta^{-1}(t^{-1})
,
t>0
. Also, if
f
is
\eta_{1}
-quasisymmetric (resp. quasi-Möbius) and
g
is
\eta_{2}
-quasisymmetric (resp. quasi-Möbius), then
g\circ f
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric (resp. quasi-Möbius) for
\eta=\eta_{2}\circ\eta_{1}
.

(Q-2)

Quasisymmetric maps are quasi-Möbius, quantitatively
[
36
]
*Theorem 3.2. Conversely, quasi-Möbius maps between bounded metric spaces are quasisymmetric
[
36
]
*Theorem 3.12; this statement is not quantitative.

(Q-3)

Quasi-Möbius maps between open subsets of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
are quasiconformal, quantitatively
[
36
]
*Theorem 5.2. Conversely, quasiconformal homeomorphisms of the entire sphere
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
are quasi-Möbius, quantitatively
[
36
]
*Theorem 5.4.

(Q-4)

If
f
is an
L
-bi-Lipschitz map between domains in
\mathbb{C}
, then
f
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric for
\eta(t)=L^{2}t
and thus
f
is
L^{2}
-quasiconformal
[
35
]
*Theorem 34.1.

A
continuum

E
is a compact and connected metric space. If
E
contains more than one points, we say that
E
is a
non-degenerate continuum
. We define the
relative distance
of two disjoint non-degenerate continua
E,F
in a metric space
(X,d)
as

\Delta(E,F)=\frac{\dist_{d}(E,F)}{\min\{\diam_{d}(E),\diam_{d}(F)\}}.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f\colon(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
be an
\eta
-quasi-Möbius homeomorphism between metric spaces. Then there exists a distortion function
\widetilde{\eta}
that depends only on
\eta
such that for every pair of disjoint non-degenerate continua
E,F\subset X
we have

\Delta(f(E),f(F))\leq\widetilde{\eta}(\Delta(E,F)).

Proof.

For distinct points
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in X
, we define

\langle x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\rangle=\frac{\min\{d_{X}(x_{1},x_{3}),d_{X}(x_{2},x_{4})\}}{\min\{d_{X}(x_{1},x_{4}),d_{X}(x_{2},x_{3})\}}.

It is shown in
[
10
]
*Lemma 4.5 that for the functions
\eta_{1}(t)=3^{-1}\min\{t,\sqrt{t}\}
and
\eta_{2}(t)=3\max\{t,\sqrt{t}\}
we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle\eta_{1}([x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}])\leq\langle x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\rangle\leq\eta_{2}([x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}]).

We define
D(E,F)=\inf\{\langle x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\rangle:x_{1},x_{4}\in E\,\,\text{and}\,\,x_{2},x_{3}\in F\}
. For the sake of brevity, we write
D(E,F)=\inf\langle x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\rangle
. It is shown in
[
10
]
*Lemma 4.6 that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Delta(E,F)\leq D(E,F)\leq 2\Delta(E,F).

We use the notation
x^{\prime}=f(x)
. Combining (
2.2
) and (
2.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta(f(E),f(F))

\displaystyle\leq D(f(E),f(F))=\inf\langle x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3}^{\prime},x_{4}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\inf\eta_{2}([x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},x_{3}^{\prime},x_{4}^{\prime}])

\displaystyle\leq\inf\eta_{2}\circ\eta([x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}])

\displaystyle\leq\inf\eta_{2}\circ\eta\circ\eta_{1}^{-1}(\langle x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\eta_{2}\circ\eta\circ\eta_{1}^{-1}(\inf\langle x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\rangle)

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{2}\circ\eta\circ\eta_{1}^{-1}(2\Delta(E,F)).

We obtain the desired conclusion for
\widetilde{\eta}(t)=\eta_{2}\circ\eta\circ\eta_{1}^{-1}(2t)
.
∎

2.4.
Quasidisks and quasicircles

A set
U\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is a
quasidisk
if it is the image of the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
(or equivalently of any other disk) under a quasiconformal homeomorphism
f\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
. If
f
is
K
-quasiconformal for some
K\geq 1
, we say that
U
is a
K
-quasidisk. Note that the complement of a quasidisk is also a quasidisk.

Let
J\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a Jordan curve. We say that
J
is a
quasicircle
if there exists a constant
L\geq 1
such that for every pair of points
x,y\in J
there exists an arc
E\subset J
with endpoints
x,y
such that

(2.3)

\displaystyle\diam(E)\leq L\sigma(x,y).

In that case we say that
J
is an
L
-quasicircle. We note that a subset of
\mathbb{C}
is a quasicircle in the above sense if and only if (
2.3
) holds with the Euclidean metric and with a possibly different constant
L^{\prime}
. This equivalence is quantitative and follows from
[
10
]
*Prop. 4.4 (iv).

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
U\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a Jordan region and
J=\partial U
. Then
U
is a quasidisk if and only if
J
is a quasicircle, quantitatively.

A proof can be found in
[
1
]
,
[
20
]
*Theorem II.8.6 or
[
31
]
*Section 5. A consequence of that result and of the fact that quasiconformal maps preserve sets of measure zero is that quasicircles have measure zero.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a domain such that
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
is the union of finitely many quasidisks with disjoint closures. Then every quasi-Möbius embedding
f\colon\Omega\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
has an extension to a quasiconformal homeomorphism of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, quantitatively.

This follows immediately from the more general result
[
10
]
*Prop. 5.1. Alternatively, a simple proof of Proposition
2.6
can be given based on the Beurling–Ahlfors extension
[
3
]
and on the quasiconformal removability of quasicircles.

3.
Circularizable pairs of Jordan regions

3.1.
Elementary properties

Recall that a pair of Jordan regions
U,V
in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is
K
-quasiconformally circularizable for some
K\geq 1
if there exists a
K
-quasiconformal homeomorphism
\phi\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\phi(U)
and
\phi(V)
are geometric disks. In that case, if
f
is a
K^{\prime}
-quasiconformal homeomorphism of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, then
f(U)
and
f(V)
are
KK^{\prime}
-quasiconformally circularizable.

The next proposition implies that the main assumption of Bonk
[
10
]
on uniformly relatively separated uniform quasicircles is stronger than our assumption in Theorem
1.1
on quasiconformally pairwise circularizable Jordan regions.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
L\geq 1
,
\delta>0
and let
U,V\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a pair of
L
-quasidisks with disjoint closures such that

\Delta(\partial U,\partial V)\geq\delta
.
Then the pair
U,V
is
K(L,\delta)
-quasiconformally circularizable.

The conclusion of Proposition
3.1
in the Euclidean metric was first observed by Herron
[
19
]
*Theorem 2.6 and Corollary 3.5. As a consequence of Lemma
2.4
, the assumptions of the above proposition can be equivalently stated using the Euclidean metric, in a quantitative fashion.

Observe that if
U,V
are quasiconformally (or merely homeomorphically) circularizable Jordan regions, then
\partial U\cap\partial V
contains at most one point. We prove another important consequence of quasiconformal circularizability.

Lemma 3.2

(No room for three)
.

Suppose that a pair of disjoint Jordan regions
U,V\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is quasiconformally circularizable. Let
W\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(U\cup V)
be a Jordan region such that
\partial W\cap\partial U\cap\partial V\neq\emptyset
. Then
W
is not a quasidisk.

Proof.

By assumption, there exists a quasiconformal map of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
that maps
U
and
V
to the half-planes
U^{\prime}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\im(z)<0\}
and
V^{\prime}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\im(z)>1\}
, respectively. Let
W\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0<\im(z)<1\}
be a Jordan region such that
\infty\in\partial W
. By the quasiconformal invariance of quasidisks, it suffices to show that
W
is not a quasidisk. Let
z_{0}\in\partial W\setminus\{\infty\}
be a point and consider two arcs
E_{1},E_{2}
of
\partial W
connecting
z_{0}
to
\infty
that have disjoint interiors. By the connectedness of
E_{1}
, without loss of generality, we may assume that
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\re(z)=t\}
has non-empty intersection with
E_{1}
for each
t\geq\re(z_{0})
.

If
E_{2}
does not have the same property, then the connectedness of
E_{2}
would imply that
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\re(z)=t\}
has non-empty intersection with
E_{2}
for each
t\leq\re(z_{0})
. We will show that
\partial W
separates
U^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
. If not, there exists
\varepsilon>0
and a Jordan arc
\alpha
lying in
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:-\varepsilon<\im(z)<1+\varepsilon\}\setminus\partial W
except for its endpoints
z_{1},z_{2}
, which satisfy
\im(z_{1})=-\varepsilon
and
\im(z_{2})=1+\varepsilon
. By the Jordan curve theorem, the Jordan arc
\alpha
and the lines
\im(z)=-\varepsilon
and
\im(z)=1+\varepsilon
define two disjoint Jordan regions whose boundaries pass through
\infty
. By the properties of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, one of these Jordan regions intersects
E_{1}
and the other intersects
E_{2}
. Thus the connected set
(E_{1}\cup E_{2})\setminus\{\infty\}=\partial W\setminus\{\infty\}
must intersect the common boundary
\alpha
of these Jordan regions. This is a contradiction. Therefore, as claimed,
\partial W
separates
U^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
. The Jordan curve theorem implies that one of
U^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
is a subset of the Jordan region
W
, a contradiction. This contradiction implies that
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\re(z)=t\}
has non-empty intersection with both
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
for each
t\geq\re(z_{0})
.

Let
z_{1},z_{2}\in\partial W
be distinct points with
t=\re(z_{1})=\re(z_{2})\geq\re(z_{0})
. Then

\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})\leq\frac{\pi}{2}\chi(z_{1},z_{2})\leq\frac{\pi}{1+t^{2}}.

Let
E
be the arc of
\partial W\setminus\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
that contains
\infty
. Then

\diam(E)\geq\sigma(z_{1},\infty)\geq\chi(z_{1},\infty)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+|z_{1}|^{2}}}\geq\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+(|t|+1)^{2}}}.

Now, if
F
is the complementary arc of
E
, then

\diam(F)\geq\sigma(z_{1},z_{0})\geq\chi(z_{1},z_{0})\geq\frac{2(t-\re(z_{0}))}{\sqrt{1+(|t|+1)^{2}}\sqrt{1+|z_{0}|^{2}}}.

As
t\to\infty
, we see that the ratio
\min\{\diam(E),\diam(F)\}/\sigma(z_{1},z_{2})
tends to
\infty
, so
\partial W
is not a quasicircle. By Proposition
2.5
,
W
is not a quasidisk.
∎

3.2.
Pulling away Jordan regions

We prove a statement that allows us to reduce Theorem
1.1
to the case of Jordan regions with disjoint closures.

Proposition 3.3

(Exhaustion)
.

Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a finite collection of disjoint Jordan regions in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
that are

-quasiconformally pairwise circularizable for some

. Then for each

there exists a collection

.

(ii)

The regions

(iii)

The closures

Lemma 3.4

(Bi-Lipschitz pushing of a chord onto a circular arc)

For

and maps the segment

,
(0,0)
, and
(-a,b)
.

In particular, if

is the disk whose boundary passes through these points and

, then

maps

onto

See Figure

for an illustration of the behavior of the map given by the lemma.

Proof.

Consider the circle that passes through the points

,
(0,0)
, and
(-a,b)
. Let
r>0
be its radius
(c,0)
be its center. Note that

.
Since

. Also,
(-a,b)=(c+r\cos\delta,r\sin\delta)
for some

and

. By assumption,

\displaystyle\sqrt{3}<\frac{b}{a}=\frac{\sin\delta}{1-\cos\delta}

from which we obtain

. We apply a scaling so that

; the general case can be recovered by suitably scaling the map

constructed below.

In the rectangle

4.
Geometry of annuli and disks

4.1.
Annulus width

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. A
(closed) annulus
in
X
is a set
A\subset X
of the form

A=A(x;r,R)=\{y\in X:r\leq d(x,y)\leq R\},

where
x\in X
,
0<r<R
, and
X\setminus\overline{B}_{d}(x,R)\neq\emptyset
. We define the
width
of the annulus
A
to be

w_{A}=\log(R/r).

Moreover for a set
K\subset X
, the
width of
K
relative to
A
is defined as

w_{A}(K)=\inf\{\log(R^{\prime}/r^{\prime}):A\cap K\subset A(x;r^{\prime},R^{\prime})\subset A\}.

By definition, if
A\cap K=\emptyset
, then
w_{A}(K)=0
. Also, if
w_{A}(K)>0
, then there exists an annulus
A^{\prime}=A(x;r^{\prime},R^{\prime})
, concentric with
A
, such that
A\cap K\subset{A^{\prime}}\subset A
and
w_{A}(K)=w_{A^{\prime}}
. Namely,

\displaystyle r^{\prime}=r_{A}(K)\coloneqq\inf_{y\in A\cap K}d(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad R^{\prime}=R_{A}(K)\coloneqq\sup_{y\in A\cap K}d(x,y)

so we have
A^{\prime}=A(x;r_{A}(K),R_{A}(K))
and

w_{A}(K)=\log\frac{R_{A}(K)}{r_{A}(K)}.

For
x\in\mathbb{C}
and
0<r<R<\infty
we consider the Euclidean annulus

A=A^{e}(x;r,R)=\{y\in\mathbb{C}:r\leq|x-y|\leq R\}.

As a convention, in this case we will use the superscript
e
in the above notions and we write
w^{e}_{A}
,
w^{e}_{A}(K)
,
r^{e}_{A}(K)
,
R^{e}_{A}(K)
. For
x\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and
0<r<R<\pi
consider the spherical annulus

A=A(x;r,R)=\{y\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}:r\leq\sigma(x,y)\leq R\}.

In the case of spherical annuli, we use the original notation
w_{A}
,
w_{A}(K)
,
r_{A}(K)
,
R_{A}(K)
without any alteration. Observe that each Euclidean or spherical annulus has two boundary components that are circles.

The next elementary lemma is a modification of
[
10
]
*Lemma 8.6. It essentially says that given an annulus

and a collection

of continua in

, one can find a large subannulus

of

such that either all sets of
\mathcal{K}
, with the exception of at most one, have small width relative to
A^{\prime}
or (at least) two sets of

intersect both boundary components of

.

.

Let

4.3.
Schottky groups

Let
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of closed disks in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
with disjoint interiors. The
Schottky group
of
\mathcal{K}
is the group of Möbius and anti-Möbius transformations generated by reflections in the disks of
\mathcal{K}
and is denoted by
\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
. Let
\phi_{i}
be the reflection in
K_{i}
,
i\in I
. Each element
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
that is not the identity map can be expressed uniquely as
\phi=\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
i_{j}\neq i_{j+1}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. This is called the
reduced form
of
\phi
. We define the
length
of
\phi
to be
l(\phi)=n
. The length of the identity map is defined to be
0
. See
[
6
]
*Section 3 or
[
27
]
*Section 7 for further details.

The next proposition strengthens the conclusion of Lemma
4.4
and asserts that if two disks
K_{1},K_{2}
are large relative to a fixed annulus
A
, then not only each disk
D\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(K_{1}\cup K_{2})
has small width relative to
A
, but actually the entire orbit of
D
under the Schottky group of
\{K_{1},K_{2}\}
retains the same property.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
A
be an annulus in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and
K_{1},K_{2}
be a pair of disjoint closed disks in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
that intersect both boundary components of
A
. Let
\Gamma
be the Schottky group of
\{K_{1},K_{2}\}
. For each closed disk
D\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(K_{1}\cup K_{2})
and for each
\phi\in\Gamma
we have

w_{A}(\phi(D))\leq\beta_{0},

where
\beta_{0}
is the universal constant appearing in Lemma
4.4

Proof.

Let
\Omega=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(K_{1}\cup K_{2})
. Let
\phi_{i}
be the reflection across
\partial K_{i}
,
i=1,2
. By Lemma
4.4
we have
w_{A}(D)\leq\beta_{0}
for each closed disk
D\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(K_{2}\cup\phi_{1}(K_{2}))
. In particular, this is true for each
D\subset\Omega
, so we have verified the statement when
\phi
is the identity map. Next, for each
D\subset\Omega
we have
\phi_{1}(D)\subset\phi_{1}(\Omega)=\inter(K_{1})\setminus\phi_{1}(K_{2})\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(K_{2}\cup\phi_{1}(K_{2}))
. Thus, by the above,
w_{A}(\phi_{1}(D))\leq\beta_{0}
. With the same argument, one obtains
w_{A}(\phi_{2}(D))\leq\beta_{0}
for each
D\subset\Omega
and we have verified the statement when
l(\phi)=1
.

Let
\phi\in\Gamma
with
l(\phi)=n\geq 2
be represented in reduced form as
\phi=\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n}}
, where
i_{j}\in\{1,2\}
and
i_{j}\neq i_{j+1}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. Note that
\phi(\Omega)
has two complementary disks,
\phi(K_{1})
and
\phi(K_{2})
, and

\phi(\Omega)\subset\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{i_{n}})

because
\phi_{i_{n}}(\Omega)\subset K_{i_{n}}
. If
w_{A}(\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{i_{n}}))=0
, then we also have
w_{A}(\phi(D))=0
for all
D\subset\Omega
. In that case we define
A(\phi)=\emptyset
. Suppose that
w_{A}(\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{i_{n}}))>0
. Then there exists an annulus
A(\phi)
concentric with
A
such that

A\cap\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{i_{n}})\subset A(\phi)\subset A\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,w_{A(\phi)}=w_{A}(\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{i_{n}})).

The disks
\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{1})
and
\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{2})
are disjoint. We show by induction on the length of
\phi
that each of these two disks intersects both boundary components of
A(\phi)
.

For
n=2
, let
\phi=\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\phi_{i_{2}}
and consider the disks
\phi_{i_{1}}(K_{1})
and
\phi_{i_{1}}(K_{2})
. Without loss of generality,
i_{2}=2
, so
i_{1}=1
and we wish to show that
\phi_{1}(K_{1})
and
\phi_{1}(K_{2})
intersect both boundary components of
A(\phi)
. By the definition of
A(\phi)
this is immediate for the disk
\phi_{1}(K_{2})
. Note that
\phi_{1}(K_{1})=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\inter(K_{1})\supset K_{2}
. By assumption,
K_{2}
intersects each of the boundary components of the initial annulus
A
, so the same is true for the subannulus
A(\phi)
. Thus,
\phi_{1}(K_{1})
has the same property, as desired.

Now, suppose that the claim is true whenever
l(\phi)=n
for some
n\geq 2
. Let
\phi=\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n}}\circ\phi_{i_{n+1}}
such that
A(\phi)\neq\emptyset
. Without loss of generality,
i_{n+1}=1
. Let
\psi=\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n}}
. Then the disk
\psi(K_{1})
intersects both boundary components of
A(\phi)
by definition. We have
\psi(K_{2})=\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\inter(K_{2}))
. By the induction assumption, the disjoint disks
\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{1})
and
\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{2})
intersect both boundary components of
A(\psi)
. Thus,
\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{2})
does not contain any of the boundary components of
A(\psi)
and
\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(\partial K_{2})
intersects both boundary components of
A(\psi)
. Hence,
\psi(K_{2})
intersects both boundary components of
A(\psi)
. Observe that
A(\psi)\supset A(\phi)
, since
\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}(K_{2})\supset\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}\circ\phi_{i_{n}}(K_{1})
.
Thus,
\psi(K_{2})
intersects both boundary components of
A(\phi)
, as desired. We have established the inductive claim.

Now, let
\phi\in\Gamma
with
l(\phi)=n\geq 2
and
\phi=\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n}}
. Also, let
\psi=\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n-1}}
. Without loss of generality,
i_{n}=2
. The disks
\psi(K_{1})
and
\psi(K_{2})
are disjoint and intersect both boundary components of
A(\phi)
, by the above claim. The reflection in
\psi(K_{2})
is given by
\psi\circ\phi_{2}\circ\psi^{-1}
. By Lemma
4.4
, each closed disk

D\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\psi(K_{1})\cup\psi\circ\phi_{2}(K_{1}))=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\psi(K_{1})\cup\phi(K_{1}))

satisfies
w_{A(\phi)}(D)\leq\beta_{0}
. If
D\subset\Omega
, then

\phi(D)\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\phi(K_{1})\cup\phi(K_{2}))\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(\phi(K_{1})\cup\psi(K_{1})),

given that
\psi(K_{1})\subset\psi(\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\inter(K_{2}))=\phi(K_{2})
. Therefore,
w_{A(\phi)}(\phi(D))\leq\beta_{0}
. Moreover, since
\phi(D)=\psi\circ\phi_{2}(D)\subset\psi(K_{2})
, by the choice of the annulus
A(\phi)
, we have
w_{A(\phi)}(\phi(D))=w_{A}(\phi(D))
; recall that
A(\phi)
is the smallest subannulus of
A
that contains
A\cap\psi(K_{2})
. This completes the proof.
∎

We include some further properties of Schottky groups in this section that will be used in the proof of the main theorem. Suppose that we are given a bijection from a collection of disks
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
with disjoint interiors to another such collection
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\{K_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i\in I}
so that
K_{i}
corresponds to
K_{i}^{\prime}
for each
i\in I
. If
\phi=\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n}}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
is expressed in reduced form, then we define
\phi^{\prime}=\phi_{i_{1}}^{\prime}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n}}^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K}^{\prime})
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a finite collection of disjoint closed disks in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and let
S=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}\inter(K_{i})
.

(i)

The set
S_{\infty}=\bigcup_{\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})}\phi(S)
is a domain and
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S_{\infty}
is a totally disconnected compact set. If
\#I=2
, then
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S_{\infty}
consists of two points.

Let
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\{K_{i}^{\prime}\}_{i\in I}
be another collection of disjoint closed disks in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and let
f\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a homeomorphism such that
K_{i}^{\prime}=f(K_{i})
for each
i\in I
.

(ii)

There exists an extension of
f|_{S}
to a homeomorphism
F\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
such that
F\circ\phi=\phi^{\prime}\circ F
for each
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
.

Let
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a finitely connected domain such that the components of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Omega
are the disks
K_{i}
,
i\in I
, together with a finite collection of disjoint compact sets
\{L_{j}\}_{j\in J}
.

(iii)

The set

G=S_{\infty}\setminus\bigcup\{\phi(L_{j}):j\in J,\,\,\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})\}

is a domain.

(iv)

If
f
is
H
-quasiconformal in
\Omega
for some
H\geq 1
, then the extension
F
is
H
-quasiconformal in
G
.

Proof.

Let
\phi_{i}
be the reflection in
K_{i}
,
i\in I
. It is shown in
[
27
]
*Corollary 7.4 that the set
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S_{\infty}
is equal to the intersection over
n\in\mathbb{N}
of the disjointed unions

\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{i_{j}\neq i_{j+1}}\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{n}}(K_{i_{n+1}}).

Given that
I
is finite, each such union is compact. Therefore,
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S_{\infty}
is compact. Also, again from
[
27
]
*Corollary 7.4, for each point
z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S_{\infty}
there exists a unique sequence
\{i_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
i_{j}\in I
and
i_{j}\neq i_{j+1}
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and we have

\{z\}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{\infty}\phi_{i_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{i_{j}}(K_{i_{j+1}}).

This implies that
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S_{\infty}
is totally disconnected. Hence, its complement is a domain; see
[
38
]
*Lemma II.5.20. If
\#I=2
, then there are only two sequences
\{i_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
as above, so
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S_{\infty}
has only two elements. This proves

. Part
(ii)
is proved in
[

]
*Lemma 7.5; see also
[

]
*Lemma 5.1.

Now we establish part
(iii)
. First observe that the open sets
\phi(\inter(S))
,
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
, are disjoint. This implies that the compact sets
\phi(L_{j})
,

,
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
, are disjoint. By the argument used in
(i)
, observe that for each

\phi(L_{j})
converges as

to the boundary of the open set

, i.e., to
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus S_{\infty}
. This implies that the set
\bigcup\{\phi(L_{j}):j\in J,\,\,\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})\}
is relatively closed in

. Thus,
G
is open. Also, any two points of
G
can be connected by a path in the domain

. This path intersects at most finitely many sets
\phi(L_{j})
, where
j\in J
,
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
, thus, it can be modified to a path that avoids each of these sets and stays in

. We conclude that
G
is connected. This proves
(iii)
.

Before proving
(iv)
, we show that

agrees with the set

(4.4)

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}=\bigcup\{\phi(\Omega)\cup\phi(\partial K_{i}):i\in I,\,\,\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})\}.

To prove this, observe that

\Omega\cup\bigcup_{i\in I}\partial K_{i}=S\setminus\bigcup_{j\in J}L_{j}=S\setminus\bigcup\{\psi(L_{j}):j\in J,\,\,\psi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})\},

where the last equality follows from the fact that

\displaystyle=\phi(S)\setminus\bigcup\{\phi\circ\psi(L_{j}):j\in J,\,\,\psi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})\}

\displaystyle=\phi(S)\setminus\bigcup\{\psi(L_{j}):j\in J,\,\,\psi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})\}.

. For each

Since

is

-quasiconformal in
\phi(\Omega)
for each
\phi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{K})
.

For each

and

and
\phi(\phi_{i}(\Omega))

such that

5.
Transboundary modulus estimates

5.1.
Classical and transboundary modulus

Let
\Gamma
be a family of curves in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
. A Borel function
\rho\colon\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to[0,\infty]
is
admissible
for
\Gamma
if
\int_{\gamma}\rho\,ds\geq 1
for every locally rectifiable path
\gamma\in\Gamma
. The
(conformal) modulus
of
\Gamma
is defined as

\Mod\Gamma=\inf_{\rho}\int\rho^{2}\,d\Sigma,

where the infimum is taken over all admissible functions
\rho
for
\Gamma
. If there exists no admissible function for
\Gamma
, then by convention
\Mod\Gamma=\infty
.

Let
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a domain and
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of disjoint compact subsets of
\Omega
such that the set
K=\bigcup_{i\in I}K_{i}
is relatively closed in
\Omega
. Let
\gamma\colon[a,b]\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a curve. The
trace
of
\gamma
is the set
\gamma([a,b])
and is denoted by
|\gamma|
. The set
\gamma^{-1}(\Omega\setminus K)
is relatively open in
[a,b]
, so it is equal to the union of countably many intervals
A_{j}
,
j\in J
, that are relatively open in
[a,b]
. We say that
\gamma
is
locally rectifiable
in
\Omega\setminus K
if
\gamma|_{A_{j}}
is locally rectifiable for each
j\in J
. In this case, if
\rho\colon\Omega\setminus K\to[0,\infty]
is a Borel function, we define

\int_{\gamma}\rho\chi_{\Omega\setminus K}\,ds=\sum_{j\in J}\int_{\gamma|_{A_{j}}}\rho\,ds.

A
transboundary mass distribution

\rho
in
\Omega
consists of a Borel function
\rho\colon\Omega\setminus K\to[0,\infty]
and a collection of weights
\rho(K_{i})\geq 0
,
i\in I
. The
mass
of
\rho
is defined as

\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\,d\Sigma+\sum_{i\in I}\rho(K_{i})^{2}.

The transboundary mass distribution
\rho
is
admissible
for a curve family
\Gamma
in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
if

\int_{\gamma}\rho\chi_{\Omega\setminus K}\,ds+\sum_{i:|\gamma|\cap K_{i}\neq\emptyset}\rho(K_{i})\geq 1

for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
that is locally rectifiable in
\Omega\setminus K
. The
transboundary modulus
of
\Gamma
with respect to
\Omega
and
\mathcal{K}
is defined as

\Mod_{\Omega,\mathcal{K}}\Gamma=\inf_{\rho}\left\{\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\,d\Sigma+\sum_{i\in I}\rho(K_{i})^{2}\right\},

where the infimum is taken over all transboundary mass distributions in
\Omega
that are admissible for
\Gamma
. If there is no admissible transboundary mass distribution, we define
\Mod_{\Omega,\mathcal{K}}\Gamma=\infty
.

Transboundary modulus is conformally invariant, as was observed by Schramm
[
34
]
*Lemma 1.1. The next statement is a generalization of
[
10
]
*Lemma 6.3 and can be found in
[
32
]
*Lemma 3.1.3.

Lemma 5.1

(Invariance of transboundary modulus)
.

Let
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be domains and let
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a countable collection of disjoint compact subsets of
\Omega
such that
\bigcup_{i\in I}K_{i}
is relatively closed in
\Omega
. Suppose that
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
is a homeomorphism that is
H
-quasiconformal on
\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}K_{i}
for some
H\geq 1
and let
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\{f(K_{i})\}_{i\in I}
. Then for every family of curves
\Gamma
in
\Omega
and for
f(\Gamma)=\{f\circ\gamma:\gamma\in\Gamma\}
we have

H^{-1}\cdot\Mod_{\Omega,\mathcal{K}}\Gamma\leq\Mod_{\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{K}^{\prime}}f(\Gamma)\leq H\cdot\Mod_{\Omega,\mathcal{K}}\Gamma.

5.2.
Fat sets

Let
\tau>0
. A measurable set
K\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}

\tau
-fat
if for each
x\in K
and for each ball

that does not contain

\Sigma(B(x,r)\cap K)\geq\tau r^{2}

if is
\tau
-fat for some
\tau>0

\tau>0
. A more modern terminology for fatness is Ahlfors
2

[

]
and Bonk
[

]
. In fact, Schramm defines fatness using the Euclidean metric, but a set is fat in the Euclidean metric if and only if it is fat in the spherical metric, quantitatively; see the proof of
[
29
]
*Lemma 2.3.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\tau>0
and
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of disjoint
\tau
-fat continua in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
. Then for each set
A\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and
t>0
the set

\{i\in I:K_{i}\cap A\neq\emptyset\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\diam(K_{i})\geq t\diam(A)\}

has at most
C(\tau,t)
elements.

See
[
22
]
*Lemma 2.6 for the a proof of the statement with the Euclidean metric. The proof remains unchanged if one uses the spherical metric. See also
[

]
*Lemma 4.8 for another argument. A proof of the next elementary lemma can be found in
[
28
]
*Remark 2.3.5 or
[

]
*(42), p. 615.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\tau>0
and
K\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a
\tau
-fat continuum. Let
A(x;r,R)
be an annulus in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
such that
K
intersects both
\partial B(x,r)
and
\partial B(x,R)

\Sigma(A(x;r,R)\cap K)\geq c(\tau)(R-r)^{2}.

An elementary property of (closed) geometric disks in

is that they are

-fat; see the argument in

*p. 609 and adapt it to the spherical metric. Schramm proved the quasiconformal invariance of fatness, which yields the next statement.

Lemma 5.4

(

Each (closed) quasidisk is fat, quantitatively.

Variants of the next lemma have appeared repeatedly in the literature; see e.g.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
a\geq 1
and
\{b_{i}\}_{i\in I}

5.3.
Lower bound for transboundary modulus

The results in this and in the next section provide sufficient conditions for transboundary modulus to behave similarly to conformal modulus. The following proposition appears in a weaker form in
[
32
]
*Lemma 3.3.5 and its proof is essentially a modification of the proof of
[
10
]
*Prop. 8.1.

Proposition 5.6

(Comparison of classical and transboundary modulus)
.

Let
\tau>0
. Let
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a domain and
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a countable collection of disjoint
\tau
-fat continua in
\Omega
such that
K=\bigcup_{i\in I}K_{i}
is relatively closed in
\Omega
. Then for each curve family
\Gamma
in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
we have

\min\{1,\Mod\Gamma\}\leq c(\tau)\Mod_{\Omega,\mathcal{K}}\Gamma.

Proof.

Let
m>0
be a constant to be determined, depending only on
\tau
. If
\Mod_{\Omega,\mathcal{K}}\Gamma\geq m
the statement is true for
c(\tau)\geq m^{-1}
, so suppose that
\Mod_{\Omega,\mathcal{K}}\Gamma<m
. Also, if
\Gamma
contains a constant curve that intersects a set
K_{i}
,
i\in I
, then any transboundary mass distribution
\rho
that is admissible for
\Gamma
must satisfy
\rho(K_{i})\geq 1
, so
\Mod_{\Omega,\mathcal{K}}\Gamma\geq 1
. In that case, the desired statement is true for
c(\tau)\geq 1
. Hence we suppose that no constant curve (if any) in
\Gamma
intersects
K_{i}
,
i\in I
.

Let
J=\{i\in I:\diam(K_{i})>0\}
and let
\Gamma_{0}
be the family of curves in
\Gamma
that intersect some of the countably many singletons
K_{i}
,
i\in I\setminus J
. By our assumption,
\Gamma_{0}
is a family of non-constant curves passing through a countable collection of points. Therefore,
\Mod\Gamma_{0}=0
; see
[
35
]
*Section 7.9.

Let
\rho
be a transboundary mass distribution that is admissible for
\Gamma
with

\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\,d\Sigma+\sum_{i\in I}\rho(K_{i})^{2}<m.

In particular, for each
i\in I
we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle\rho(K_{i})<m^{1/2}

For each
i\in J
consider a point
x_{i}\in K_{i}
and
r_{i}=\diam(K_{i})
, so
K_{i}\subset\overline{B}(x_{i},r_{i})
. The
\tau
-fatness assumption implies that
\Sigma(B(x_{i},2r_{i}))\leq c_{1}(\tau)\Sigma(K_{i})
. We define

\widetilde{\rho}=\rho\chi_{\Omega\setminus K}+\sum_{i\in J}\frac{\rho(K_{i})}{r_{i}}\chi_{B(x_{i},2r_{i})}.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0}
be locally rectifiable. By Lemma
5.2
, applied to the set
A=|\gamma|
, there exists a number
N(\tau)>0
such that the set

J_{1}=\{i\in J:K_{i}\cap|\gamma|\neq\emptyset\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\diam(B(x_{i},2r_{i}))\geq\diam(|\gamma|)\}

has at most
N(\tau)
elements. Let
i\in J\setminus J_{1}
such that
K_{i}\cap|\gamma|\neq\emptyset
, so
|\gamma|
intersects the ball
\overline{B}(x_{i},r_{i})
. Then
\diam(|\gamma|)>\diam(B(x_{i},2r_{i}))
, so
|\gamma|
is not contained in
B(x_{i},2r_{i})
. This implies that

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\frac{\rho(K_{i})}{r_{i}}\chi_{B(x_{i},2r_{i})}\,ds\geq\rho(K_{i}).

We conclude that

(5.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\widetilde{\rho}\,ds\geq\int_{\gamma}\rho\chi_{\Omega\setminus K}\,ds+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in J\setminus J_{1}\\
|\gamma|\cap K_{i}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\rho(K_{i}).

Also, by (
5.1
) and the bound on the cardinality of
J_{1}
, we obtain

\sum_{i\in J_{1}}\rho(K_{i})\leq N(\tau)m^{1/2}.

We now prescribe
m=(2N(\tau))^{-2}
, so that the above sum is less than
1/2
. Given that
\rho
is admissible for the transboundary modulus of
\Gamma
, by (
5.2
) we obtain

\int_{\gamma}\widetilde{\rho}\,ds\geq 1/2.

Therefore
2\widetilde{\rho}
is admissible for the conformal modulus of
\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0}
. By the subadditivity of conformal modulus, the definition of
\widetilde{\rho}
, and Lemma
5.5
, we have

\displaystyle\Mod\Gamma

\displaystyle\leq\Mod\Gamma_{0}+\Mod(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0})=\Mod(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0})\leq 4\int\widetilde{\rho}^{2}\,d\Sigma

\displaystyle\leq 8\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\,d\Sigma+8\int\left(\sum_{i\in J}\frac{\rho(K_{i})}{r_{i}}\chi_{B(x_{i},2r_{i})}\right)^{2}\,d\Sigma

\displaystyle\leq 8\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\,d\Sigma+c_{2}(\tau)\int\left(\sum_{i\in J}\frac{\rho(K_{i})}{r_{i}}\chi_{K_{i}}\right)^{2}\,d\Sigma

\displaystyle=8\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\,d\Sigma+c_{2}(\tau)\sum_{i\in J}\frac{\rho(K_{i})^{2}}{r_{i}^{2}}\Sigma(K_{i})

\displaystyle\leq c_{3}(\tau)\left(\int_{\Omega\setminus K}\rho^{2}\,d\Sigma+\sum_{i\in I}\rho(K_{i})^{2}\right).

Infimizing over
\rho
gives the desired inequality with
c(\tau)\geq\max\{m^{-1},1,c_{3}(\tau)\}
.
∎

Given an open set
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and sets
E,F\subset\overline{\Omega}
, we denote by
\Gamma(E,F;\Omega)
the family of curves
\gamma\colon[a,b]\to\overline{\Omega}
such that
\gamma(a)\in E
,
\gamma(b)\in F
, and
\gamma((a,b))\subset\Omega
. Let
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a domain. If there exists a decreasing function
\phi\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that

\Mod\Gamma(E,F;\Omega)\geq\phi(\Delta(E,F))

for every pair of disjoint non-degenerate continua
E,F\subset\overline{\Omega}
, then we call
\Omega
a
Loewner region
. The function
\phi
is called the
Loewner function
of
\Omega
and we also say that
\Omega
is a
\phi
-Loewner region. In this work we will need the facts that the sphere
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is a Loewner region (see
[
15
]
*Section 6) and that each quasidisk is a Loewner region, quantitatively. The latter follows from
[
5
]
*Remarks 6.6, but it can be derived easily from Lemma
2.4
and the facts that the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
is a Loewner region and that quasiconformal maps of the sphere are quasi-Möbius (see
(Q-3)
).

We now obtain a modified version of
[
10
]
*Prop. 8.1. The main difference is that Bonk’s result assumes the continua of
\mathcal{K}
to be uniformly quasiround and uniformly relatively separated, while here we use the condition of fatness.

Proposition 5.7

(Loewner property of transboundary modulus)
.

Let
\tau>0
. Let
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a Loewner region and
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a countable collection of disjoint
\tau
-fat continua in
\Omega
such that
K=\bigcup_{i\in I}K_{i}
is relatively closed in
\Omega
. Then there exists a decreasing function
\phi\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
that depends only on the Loewner function of
\Omega
and on
\tau
with the following property. If
E,F\subset\overline{\Omega}
are disjoint non-degenerate continua, then

\Mod_{\Omega,\mathcal{K}}\Gamma(E,F;\Omega)\geq\phi(\Delta(E,F)).

Proof.

Suppose that
\Omega
is a
\widetilde{\phi}
-Loewner region. In view of Proposition
5.6
, we may take
\phi(t)=c(\tau)^{-1}\min\{1,\widetilde{\phi}(t)\}
.

5.4.
Upper bound for transboundary modulus

For an annulus
A
in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
we denote by
\Gamma(A)
the family of curves
\gamma\colon[a,b]\to A
such that
\gamma(a)
and
\gamma(b)
lie in distinct components of
\partial A
and
\gamma((a,b))\subset\inter(A)
. The following result is a distilled version of
[
10
]
*Prop. 8.7.

Proposition 5.8

(Transboundary modulus upper bound)
.

Let
a,\tau>0
. Let
\mathcal{K}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a countable collection of disjoint
\tau
-fat continua in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
such that
K=\bigcup_{i\in I}K_{i}
is a closed set. There exists a decreasing homeomorphism
\psi\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)

that depends only on
a,\tau
with the following property. If
A=A(x;r,R)
is an annulus in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
with
w_{A}(K_{i})\leq a\cdot w_{A}^{1/3}
for each
i\in I
, then

\Mod_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}},\mathcal{K}}\Gamma(A(x;r,R))\leq\psi(\log(R/r)).

Compare this inequality to the corresponding well-known estimate for conformal modulus (see
[
16
]
*Lemma 7.18):

\Mod\Gamma(A(x;r,R))\leq C(\log(R/r))^{-1}.

Thus, Proposition
5.8
asserts that if the uniformly fat continua
K_{i}
,
i\in I
, have small width relative to the annulus
A
, then the transboundary modulus of
\Gamma(A)
enjoys an upper bound that is very similar to the upper bound of conformal modulus.

Proof.

Let
A=A(x;r,R)
be an annulus in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
with
w_{A}(K_{i})\leq a\cdot w_{A}^{1/3}
for each
i\in I
. We define a transboundary mass distribution
\rho
by

\displaystyle\rho(z)=\frac{\chi_{A}(z)}{w_{A}\sigma(x,z)}\,\,\text{for}\,\,z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K\quad\text{and}\quad\rho(K_{i})=\frac{w_{A}(K_{i})}{w_{A}}\,\,\text{for}\,\,i\in I.

We show the admissibility of
\rho
for
\Gamma(A)
. Let
\gamma\in\Gamma(A)
be a curve that is locally rectifiable in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K
. Consider the function
\boldsymbol{\pi}\colon A\to[\log r,\log R]
defined by
\boldsymbol{\pi}(z)=\log\sigma(x,z)
. Observe that

(5.3)

\displaystyle[\log r,\log R]=\boldsymbol{\pi}(A)=\boldsymbol{\pi}(|\gamma|)=\boldsymbol{\pi}(|\gamma|\setminus K)\cup\bigcup_{i:K_{i}\cap|\gamma|\neq\emptyset}\boldsymbol{\pi}(A\cap K_{i}).

We break up the curve
\gamma
into countably many non-overlapping pieces, each of which lies in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K
. For each such piece
\alpha
, an elementary inequality
[
10
]
*(37), p. 612 states that

\int_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\sigma(x,\cdot)}\,ds\geq m_{1}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(|\alpha|)).

By applying this to each piece and then summing, we obtain

\int_{\gamma}\rho\chi_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K}\,ds=\frac{1}{w_{A}}\int_{\gamma}\frac{1}{\sigma(x,\cdot)}\chi_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K}\,ds\geq\frac{1}{w_{A}}m_{1}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(|\gamma|\setminus K)).

Also, for
i\in I
, by the definition of
w_{A}(K_{i})
we have

\rho(K_{i})=\frac{w_{A}(K_{i})}{w_{A}}\geq\frac{1}{w_{A}}m_{1}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(A\cap K_{i})).

Summarizing,

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\rho\chi_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K}\,ds+\hskip-1.0pt\sum_{i:K_{i}\cap|\gamma|\neq\emptyset}\rho(K_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{w_{A}}\bigg(m_{1}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(|\gamma|\setminus K))+\hskip-1.0pt\sum_{i:K_{i}\cap|\gamma|\neq\emptyset}m_{1}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(A\cap K_{i}))\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{w_{A}}m_{1}(\boldsymbol{\pi}(|\gamma|))=1,

where the last inequality follows from (
5.3
) and the countable subadditivity of Lebesgue measure. The countability of the index set
I
is used only here.

Finally, we produce a favorable upper bound for the mass of
\rho
. Via integration in spherical coordinates, we have

(5.4)

\displaystyle\int_{A}\frac{d\Sigma}{\sigma(x,\cdot)^{2}}=2\pi\int_{r}^{R}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta^{2}}\,d\theta\leq 2\pi\int_{r}^{R}\frac{d\theta}{\theta}=2\pi\cdot w_{A}.

Therefore,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K}\rho^{2}\,d\Sigma\leq\frac{1}{w_{A}^{2}}\int_{A}\frac{d\Sigma}{\sigma(x,\cdot)^{2}}\leq\frac{2\pi}{w_{A}}.

Regarding the discrete part of
\rho
, let
I_{1}
be the set of indices
i\in I
such that
A\cap K_{i}\neq\emptyset
and
w_{A}(K_{i})\leq\log 2
, and let
I_{2}
be the set of
i\in I
such that
A\cap K_{i}\neq\emptyset
and
w_{A}(K_{i})>\log 2
. Note that
\rho(K_{i})=0
if
A\cap K_{i}=\emptyset
, so

\sum_{i\in I}\rho(K_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\rho(K_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i\in I_{2}}\rho(K_{i})^{2}.

Let
i\in I_{1}
such that
w_{A}(K_{i})>0
and consider an annulus
{A(x;r_{i},R_{i})}\supset A\cap K_{i}
such that
w_{A}(K_{i})=\log(R_{i}/r_{i})
. Since
i\in I_{1}
, we have
R_{i}\leq 2r_{i}
. By Lemma
5.3
,

\displaystyle\bigg(\log\frac{R_{i}}{r_{i}}\bigg)^{2}\leq\left(\frac{R_{i}-r_{i}}{r_{i}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{4(R_{i}-r_{i})^{2}}{R_{i}^{2}}\leq c(\tau)\frac{\Sigma(A\cap K_{i})}{R_{i}^{2}}\leq c(\tau)\int_{A\cap K_{i}}\frac{d\Sigma}{\sigma(x,\cdot)^{2}}.

Therefore, by (
5.4
) we obtain

(5.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\rho(K_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{w_{A}^{2}}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}w_{A}(K_{i})^{2}\leq\frac{c(\tau)}{w_{A}^{2}}\int_{A}\frac{d\Sigma}{\sigma(x,\cdot)^{2}}\leq\frac{2\pi c(\tau)}{w_{A}}.

Next, we treat the sum over
I_{2}
. For
i\in I_{2}
we have
2r_{i}<R_{i}
, so

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\leq\frac{(R_{i}-r_{i})^{2}}{R_{i}^{2}}\leq c(\tau)\frac{\Sigma(A\cap K_{i})}{R_{i}^{2}}\leq c(\tau)\int_{A\cap K_{i}}\frac{d\Sigma}{\sigma(x,\cdot)^{2}}.

As a consequence, by (
5.4
) we have

\frac{1}{4}\#I_{2}\leq c(\tau)\int_{A}\frac{d\Sigma}{\sigma(x,\cdot)^{2}}\leq 2\pi c(\tau)w_{A},

where
\#I_{2}
denotes the cardinality of
I_{2}
, so

\#I_{2}\leq 8\pi c(\tau)w_{A}.

By the assumption that
w_{A}(K_{i})\leq a\cdot w_{A}^{1/3}
for each
i\in I
, we conclude that

(5.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{2}}\rho(K_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{w_{A}^{2}}\sum_{i\in I_{2}}w_{A}(K_{i})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{w_{A}^{2}}\#I_{2}\cdot a^{2}w_{A}^{2/3}\leq\frac{8\pi a^{2}c(\tau)}{w_{A}^{1/3}}.

Combining (
5.5
), (
5.6
), and (
5.7
), we see that the total mass of
\rho
is at most

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{w_{A}}+\frac{2\pi c(\tau)}{w_{A}}+\frac{8\pi a^{2}c(\tau)}{w_{A}^{1/3}}\leq c_{1}(a,\tau)\bigg(\bigg(\log\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{-1}+\bigg(\log\frac{R}{r}\bigg)^{-1/3}\bigg).

This completes the proof with
\psi(t)=c_{1}(a,\tau)(t^{-1}+t^{-1/3})
,
t>0
.
∎

Proof of the main theorem

We first establish a preliminary version of Theorem
1.1
. The proof of Theorem
1.1
is given at the end of the section.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a finite collection of Jordan regions in

K

\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
.

The main ingredient for the proof of Theorem

in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
separates two sets

in
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
if
E
and

\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus A
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let

. Let
A

\widehat{\mathbb{C}}

.
Then there exists an increasing function
\theta\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
that depends only on
K
such that

w_{A}\leq\theta(\Delta(E,F)).

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
, there exists a subannulus
A^{\prime}
of
A
that is concentric with
A
such that
w_{A^{\prime}}\geq\min\{w_{A},w_{A}^{1/9}\}
and one of alternatives
(i)
,
(ii)
holds.

Case (i).
We first assume that alternative
(i)
of Lemma
4.1
holds. That is,
w_{A^{\prime}}(K_{i})\leq w_{A^{\prime}}^{1/3}
for all

, where
I_{0}=\emptyset
or
I_{0}
contains precisely one index of

. Let
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\{K_{i}\}_{i\in I\setminus I_{0}}
,
\Omega^{\prime}=\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I_{0}}K_{i}
