Knowledge, acceptance, and willingness to pay for expanded carrier screening among obstetric patients in China: Implications for genetic counseling practice
Authors: Changhong Wang, Zhonghao Shen, Yang Wan, Hong Li, Shanshan Luan, Jali Lu, Pei Wang, Xiaona Li, Li Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExpanded carrier screening (ECS) enables the identification of couples at increased risk of having offspring affected by serious inherited disorders; however, its effective implementation depends on individuals' understanding, acceptance, and access. This study aimed to assess knowledge, attitudes, acceptance, and willingness to pay (WTP) for ECS among individuals receiving obstetric care, and to examine the implications for genetic counseling practice. We conducted a cross‐sectional survey among 2964 reproductive‐age individuals attending preconception counseling and antenatal care clinics. The questionnaire assessed sociodemographic characteristics, reproductive history, knowledge of ECS, perceived stigma related to carrier status, screening preferences, and the WTP. Multinomial logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with the acceptance of ECS, and ordered logistic regression was used to examine determinants of the WTP. Overall, acceptance of ECS was high, with most participants preferring screening either before conception or during pregnancy. Substantial knowledge gaps were identified, particularly regarding the scope and limitations of ECS, including misconceptions that ECS could exclude chromosomal abnormalities or fully eliminate residual genetic risk following a negative result. After multivariable adjustment, perceived stigma related to carrier status and higher educational attainment were independently associated with acceptance of ECS, whereas household income, age group, and family history of genetic disorders were not. Knowledge accuracy showed a directional but imprecise association with acceptance. WTP for ECS varied across socioeconomic groups. Higher knowledge accuracy, higher educational attainment, and higher household income were independently associated with greater WTP. In contrast, perceived stigma showed only a modest association with WTP and was not independently associated with high WTP in sensitivity analyses. These findings suggest that psychological factors, particularly perceived stigma, are significantly associated with decisions regarding ECS, whereas cognitive understanding and socioeconomic capacity are more closely linked to monetary valuation. Genetic counseling strategies that address misconceptions, mitigate stigma‐related concerns, and consider affordability may be essential for promoting the informed and equitable implementation of ECS in obstetric settings.Keywords: expanded carrier screening, genetic counseling, knowledge and attitudes, perceived stigma, willingness to pay

What is known about this topic

ECS is a tool used in reproductive genetics to identify couples at risk of having children with serious inherited disorders. It offers a broader panel of conditions than traditional genetic screening and is increasingly offered in obstetric settings, including preconception counseling and prenatal care. While ECS is widely accepted in certain clinical contexts, its implementation depends not only on technical accuracy but also on patients' understanding of its limitations, the perceived psychological and social implications, and the accessibility of screening, including the financial aspects. Previous research has demonstrated that knowledge gaps regarding ECS remain prevalent, especially regarding misconceptions about the scope of the screening and the meaning of negative results. Additionally, WTP for ECS has been shown to vary considerably by socioeconomic factors, and psychological factors such as stigma associated with being a carrier may influence acceptance and participation.

What this paper adds to the topic

This study provides new insights into the knowledge, attitudes, acceptance, and WTP for ECS among individuals receiving obstetric care. We highlight substantial knowledge gaps, particularly regarding the scope and limitations of ECS, including misconceptions about the exclusion of chromosomal abnormalities and the residual genetic risk after a negative result. Perceived stigma related to carrier status was significantly associated with ECS acceptance. Educational attainment and higher knowledge accuracy were positively associated with ECS acceptance, while the WTP varied significantly across income and educational groups, indicating potential barriers to equitable access. These findings underscore the critical role of genetic counseling in addressing misconceptions, supporting informed decision‐making, and ensuring equitable access to ECS in obstetric settings.

INTRODUCTION

Expanded carrier screening (ECS) has emerged as an important tool in reproductive genetics, enabling the identification of couples at increased risk of having offspring affected by serious inherited disorders. Advances in next‐generation sequencing technologies have substantially expanded the number of conditions that can be screened simultaneously, allowing ECS to move beyond single‐disease or ethnicity‐based approaches toward more comprehensive panels (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2017; Gregg et al., 2021; Kraft et al., 2019). As a result, professional societies increasingly recognize ECS as a valuable option for individuals or couples planning a pregnancy or already pregnant, particularly when offered in conjunction with appropriate genetic counseling (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2017; Gregg et al., 2021).

Despite its growing availability, the successful implementation of ECS depends not only on technical validity but also on individuals' understanding, attitudes, and engagement with screening. Prior studies have demonstrated that awareness of ECS varies widely and that misunderstandings regarding the scope and limitations of carrier screening are common (Buckley et al., 2023; Ramdaney et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021). In particular, misconceptions that ECS can exclude chromosomal abnormalities or completely eliminate residual risk have been reported in prior empirical studies, including findings that some patients make health decisions based on misunderstandings of negative ECS results (Kraft et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2021).

Beyond knowledge‐related factors, psychological and social considerations may play an important role in shaping acceptance of ECS. Concerns related to stigma, discrimination, or being perceived as “genetically abnormal” have been identified as potential barriers to participation in genetic testing and carrier screening programs (van den Heuvel et al., 2023). In the context of ECS, the perceived stigma associated with carrier status may influence not only whether individuals choose to undergo screening, but also how they interpret results and communicate genetic information within families. However, quantitative evidence examining the role of perceived stigma in ECS acceptance, particularly within real‐world obstetric settings, remains limited (Ramdaney et al., 2022).

Affordability and access represent additional challenges for the implementation of ECS in routine clinical practice. Coverage policies and out‐of‐pocket costs for ECS vary across health care systems, and the WTP for screening differs substantially among individuals (Clarke et al., 2018; van den Heuvel et al., 2023). Previous studies suggest that socioeconomic factors, including education and household income, may influence both the acceptance of ECS and WTP, raising concerns about equitable access to screening (Clarke et al., 2018; Ramdaney et al., 2022). Nevertheless, relatively few studies have examined acceptance and the WTP simultaneously or explored how cognitive factors, such as knowledge accuracy, and psychological factors, such as perceived stigma, jointly shape the perceived value of and access to ECS.

To address these gaps, we conducted a cross‐sectional survey among individuals receiving obstetric care to examine their knowledge, attitudes, acceptance, and the WTP for ECS. Specifically, we aimed to (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2017) assess knowledge accuracy and perceived stigma related to ECS; (Gregg et al., 2021) examine how cognitive and psychological factors influence the acceptance of ECS across different decision stages (preconception, pregnancy, no preference, or refusal); and (Kraft et al., 2019) evaluate the roles of socioeconomic and informational factors in shaping the WTP for ECS. By integrating cognitive, psychological, and socioeconomic factors within a single analytic framework, this study seeks to inform genetic counseling, obstetric care, and broader clinical communication practices to support more equitable and informed implementation of ECS in obstetric settings.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This cross‐sectional survey was conducted among individuals receiving obstetric care at the Obstetrics Department of Fuyang People's Hospital between July 2025 and Sep 2025.

Participants were recruited consecutively from obstetric outpatient clinics, including preconception counseling clinics and antenatal care clinics. Thus, participants were either planning a pregnancy or currently pregnant at the time they completed the questionnaire. Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older, were able to complete a self‐administered questionnaire, and provided informed consent for the anonymous use of their responses for research purposes.

Questionnaires with missing data on both primary outcome variables (acceptance of ECS and the WTP) were excluded from the primary analyses. Questionnaires with partially missing data on covariates were retained and addressed using complete‐case analysis in multivariable models.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuyang People's Hospital (No. 2023‐6, July 13, 2023).

In the study setting, ECS is generally not covered by public health insurance and is therefore primarily paid out‐of‐pocket by patients. The cost of ECS varies depending on panel size but typically ranges from approximately 1200 to 2400 RMB.

Participants completed the questionnaire based on their existing knowledge and understanding of ECS. No standardized educational explanation was provided immediately before survey completion, although participants may have been exposed to general information about ECS through routine clinical encounters, informational posters in clinic waiting areas, physician recommendations, or public media sources. The questionnaire did not systematically assess whether participants had previously received formal pretest genetic counseling related to ECS.

Survey instrument and measures

The questionnaire was developed based on previously published surveys assessing knowledge, attitudes, and acceptance of ECS among reproductive‐age individuals and healthcare providers and was adapted for the local clinical context (Clarke et al., 2018; Ramdaney et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021). The survey items were informed by prior literature on ECS awareness, decision‐making, and WTP and were refined through expert review by obstetricians and genetic counselors. The questionnaire comprised eight sections assessing sociodemographic characteristics, reproductive history, knowledge of ECS, attitudes and psychological concerns related to carrier status, acceptance and preferences for ECS, WTP, anticipated responses to high‐risk results, and ethical and privacy considerations (Supporting Information).

Multiple‐response items were recoded into binary indicators (selected vs. not selected) for statistical analysis.

Sociodemographic and reproductive characteristics

Participants reported age, sex, marital status, ethnicity, educational attainment, occupation, and household income. Educational attainment was dichotomized as college‐level education or higher versus high school or lower for multivariable analyses.

Reproductive characteristics included current fertility plans or parental status, a family history of genetic disorders, and a history of adverse reproductive outcomes (e.g., miscarriage or fetal anomalies). The history of adverse reproductive outcomes was considered during model development but was not retained in the final multivariable analyses because it was not significantly associated with ECS acceptance or WTP.

Knowledge of expanded carrier screening

Knowledge of ECS was assessed through items evaluating prior awareness of ECS and an understanding of its scope and limitations. Two items were used to assess knowledge accuracy: whether ECS could exclude chromosomal abnormalities such as Down syndrome, and whether a negative ECS result could completely eliminate the risk of having a child with a serious genetic disorder. Responses were summed to construct a knowledge accuracy score ranging from 0 to 2, with higher scores indicating more accurate understanding of ECS.

Because the questionnaire was designed as a brief survey for use in a busy clinical setting, knowledge accuracy was assessed using two key items reflecting common misconceptions regarding the scope and residual risk of ECS.

Attitudes, psychological, and ethical concerns

Participants' initial attitudes toward ECS were assessed using a Likert‐scale item ranging from strong interest to strong opposition. Psychological concerns were evaluated through an item assessing the perceived stigma associated with being identified as a genetic carrier. Perceived stigma was assessed using a single survey item asking participants whether being identified as a genetic carrier might lead to stigma or negative social consequences. For analytic purposes, perceived stigma was dichotomized as high stigma (responding “yes” or “possibly”) versus low or no stigma (responding “no” or “uncertain”), consistent with prior research examining psychosocial responses to carrier screening (Ramdaney et al., 2022; van den Heuvel et al., 2023). Concerns regarding genetic privacy and potential misuse of genetic information were assessed descriptively.

Acceptance and preferences for expanded carrier screening

Acceptance of ECS was assessed by participants' stated preference regarding whether and when they would choose to undergo ECS, categorized as preconception screening, screening during pregnancy, no preference, or refusal of screening. The response option “no preference” represented participants who did not indicate a clear preference regarding the timing of screening or reported that screening timing was not important to them. Participants were also asked whether ECS results would influence their reproductive decision‐making. Reasons for supporting or declining ECS were assessed using multiple‐response items. In the multinomial regression analysis, refusal of screening was used as the reference category, allowing comparisons across the different decision stages of ECS acceptance (preconception screening, screening during pregnancy, and no preference).

WTP and affordability

WTP for ECS was assessed by asking participants to select the maximum out‐of‐pocket cost they would be willing to pay for ECS from four predefined categories (<500 RMB, 500–1000 RMB, 1000–2000 RMB, and >2000 RMB). WTP was examined as an indicator of perceived value and potential barriers to access, rather than as a formal economic evaluation, consistent with prior studies of carrier screening uptake and affordability. Participants were also asked whether ECS should be covered by health insurance and whether the costs of downstream interventions (e.g., prenatal diagnosis or preimplantation genetic testing) would be financially affordable.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics, knowledge of ECS, attitudes, acceptance, and WTP. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Acceptance of ECS was analyzed using multinomial logistic regression models, with screening preference categorized as preconception screening, screening during pregnancy, no preference, or refusal of screening. Refusal of screening was used as the reference category.

Independent variables included knowledge accuracy regarding ECS, perceived stigma related to carrier status, educational attainment, household income, a family history of genetic disorders, and age group. These variables were selected a priori based on theoretical relevance and previous literature on the determinants of genetic screening uptake.

To improve model stability and interpretability, categories with small sample sizes were combined where appropriate. In particular, educational attainment was collapsed into two categories (≤ high school vs. ≥ college). All selected variables were entered simultaneously into the multivariable multinomial logistic regression model because they were considered conceptually important determinants of screening acceptance and potential confounders.

Factors associated with WTP were examined using ordered logistic regression models, treating willingness‐to‐pay categories as an ordinal outcome. The proportional odds assumption was assessed prior to model interpretation.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. These included alternative categorizations of screening acceptance (e.g., combining preconception and pregnancy screening as “acceptance” and comparing it with refusal), as well as alternative specifications of key variables. In addition, willingness to pay (WTP) was dichotomized as ≥1000 RMB versus <1000 RMB, and multivariable binary logistic regression was performed using the same set of predictors. Knowledge accuracy was categorized into three levels (low, moderate, and high) based on the number of correct responses, and responses of “uncertain” were treated as incorrect in sensitivity analyses. These categorization thresholds were predefined based on the questionnaire scoring framework and analytical design.

Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for regression models. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p value <0.05. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1).

Ethical considerations

Participation in the survey was voluntary and anonymous. No identifiable personal information was collected. Participants were informed that their responses would be used solely for research purposes and would not affect their clinical care.

Knowledge of expanded carrier screening

Despite generally high awareness of ECS, substantial knowledge gaps regarding its scope and limitations were identified. 87.66% of participants incorrectly believed that ECS could exclude chromosomal abnormalities such as Down syndrome, and 82.96% of participants incorrectly believed that a negative ECS result could completely eliminate the risk of having a child with a serious genetic disorder. Based on responses to these two items, a composite knowledge accuracy variable categorized as low, moderate, or high was constructed.

Acceptance of expanded carrier screening

Acceptance of ECS varied across decision stages. Most participants preferred screening either before conception or during pregnancy, whereas a smaller proportion reported no preference regarding the timing of screening or declined screening altogether (Table 1).

Results from the multivariable multinomial logistic regression analysis are presented in Table 2. In this model, the refusal of ECS served as the reference category. Odds ratios represent the relative likelihood of belonging to each ECS acceptance category compared with refusal of screening. In this model, the other response options (preconception screening, screening during pregnancy, and no preference) were analyzed as distinct outcome categories. After adjustment for sociodemographic and reproductive factors, perceived stigma related to carrier status emerged as a strong and consistent correlate of ECS acceptance. Compared with individuals reporting high perceived stigma, those reporting low or no perceived stigma had significantly lower odds of belonging to the preconception screening category (OR = 0.26, 95% CI 0.17–0.41, p < 0.001) and the pregnancy screening category (OR = 0.31, 95% CI 0.19–0.49, p < 0.001), relative to the refusal of screening. Educational attainment was independently associated with ECS acceptance. Participants with a college‐level education or higher were more likely to accept ECS during the preconception period (OR = 2.84, 95% CI 1.65–4.89, p < 0.001) and during pregnancy (OR = 2.02, 95% CI 1.16–3.51, p = 0.013) compared with those with a high school education or lower. The association between educational attainment and the “no preference” category did not reach statistical significance.

Higher knowledge accuracy regarding ECS was directionally associated with acceptance across decision stages; however, category‐specific estimates were imprecise, with wide confidence intervals. In contrast, household income, family history of genetic disorders, and age group were not independently associated with ECS acceptance after multivariable adjustment.

WTP for expanded carrier screening

Participants' WTP for ECS varied across predefined cost categories (Table 1). In ordered logistic regression analyses examining factors associated with WTP (Table 3), knowledge accuracy, educational attainment, household income, acceptance of ECS, age group, and family history of genetic disorders were independently associated with WTP.

Compared with participants with high knowledge accuracy, those with low knowledge accuracy were significantly less likely to report higher WTP (OR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.51–0.99), whereas those with moderate knowledge accuracy were more likely to report higher WTP (OR = 2.09, 95% CI 1.65–2.65). Participants with a college‐level education or higher demonstrated greater WTP than those with a high school education or lower (OR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.01–1.38).

A clear income gradient was observed, with progressively lower WTP among lower‐income groups compared with the highest income category. Acceptance of ECS was also associated with greater WTP (OR = 1.74, 95% CI 1.36–2.21). Younger participants were more likely to report higher WTP compared with participants older than 40 years, while family history of genetic disorders showed an overall association with WTP. Lower perceived stigma was modestly associated with higher WTP (OR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.98).

Sensitivity analyses

As a sensitivity analysis, WTP for ECS was dichotomized as ≥1000 RMB versus <1000 RMB, and multivariable binary logistic regression was performed using the same set of predictors. The direction and magnitude of associations for the key variables were largely consistent with the primary ordinal logistic regression results. In particular, a lower knowledge accuracy was associated with substantially lower odds of a high WTP, while a moderate knowledge accuracy was associated with higher odds of a high WTP compared with the high knowledge group. Higher educational attainment and higher household income remained significantly associated with greater WTP.

In contrast, perceived stigma and acceptance of ECS were not independently associated with high WTP in the dichotomized model. Overall, these findings support the robustness of the primary analyses and suggest that the observed associations are not sensitive to alternative specifications of the WTP outcome.

DISCUSSION

Principal findings

In this obstetric clinic‐based survey, acceptance of ECS was high, with most participants preferring screening either before conception or during pregnancy, consistent with current professional recommendations (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2017; Gregg et al., 2021). Acceptance, however, varied substantially according to psychological and cognitive factors. Perceived stigma related to carrier status appeared to be one of the strongest and most consistent correlates of ECS acceptance, whereas higher educational attainment was associated with greater acceptance. In contrast, household income, age group, and a family history of genetic disorders were not independently associated with ECS acceptance after multivariable adjustment.

WTP for ECS followed a different pattern. WTP was primarily associated with knowledge accuracy and socioeconomic factors, including education and household income. Perceived stigma showed a weaker and less consistent association with WTP and was not independently associated with high WTP in sensitivity analyses, suggesting that its influence on monetary valuation may be context‐ or threshold‐dependent.

Perceived stigma and ECS acceptance

A central finding of this study is the prominent association between the perceived stigma related to carrier status and ECS acceptance. Participants reporting low or no perceived stigma demonstrated lower odds of belonging to the preconception and pregnancy screening categories relative to the refusal of screening, even after adjustment for sociodemographic factors, knowledge accuracy, and reproductive history. This direction of association was unexpected and should be interpreted cautiously. Although the direction and magnitude of the associations were consistent across decision stages, this association was not observed for the “no preference” category.

Previous qualitative and survey‐based studies have suggested that concerns about being labeled as genetically abnormal, a fear of discrimination, and potential impacts on family or partner relationships may discourage engagement with genetic screening (Ramdaney et al., 2022; van den Heuvel et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). However, the direction of the association observed in the present study differed from these prior assumptions. Several explanations are possible, including contextual differences in the perceptions of reproductive risk, a greater perceived salience of genetic conditions among participants reporting stigma‐related concerns, or limitations related to the simplified measurement of perceived stigma in this study. Individuals who perceived greater stigma‐related implications of carrier status may also have viewed genetic risk as more personally or socially consequential, thereby increasing their motivation to engage with screening. Notably, although perceived stigma was strongly associated with ECS acceptance preferences, its association with WTP was modest and attenuated at higher payment thresholds. This pattern suggests that perceived stigma may influence ECS decision‐making differently from the willingness to pay, while willingness to pay for ECS appeared to be more strongly associated with knowledge accuracy and socioeconomic capacity (Clarke et al., 2018; van den Heuvel et al., 2023).

Knowledge accuracy and WTP

Despite generally high awareness of ECS, misconceptions regarding its scope and limitations were common. 82.96%–87.66% of participants incorrectly believed that ECS could exclude chromosomal abnormalities or eliminate the risk of serious genetic disorders following a negative result, consistent with previous reports (Kraft et al., 2018, 2019; Zhang et al., 2021).

In multivariable analyses, a higher knowledge accuracy was directionally associated with ECS acceptance, but the category‐specific estimates were imprecise and the confidence intervals were wide, indicating uncertainty in the magnitude of these associations. In contrast, knowledge accuracy demonstrated a strong and consistent association with WTP. Individuals with a more accurate understanding of ECS may therefore be better positioned to recognize both its benefits and limitations and to assign greater value to screening. These findings underscore the importance of genetic counseling that emphasizes residual risk, the distinctions between ECS and other forms of prenatal testing, and realistic expectations regarding screening outcomes (Kraft et al., 2018).

Socioeconomic factors and equity considerations

Educational attainment was independently associated with ECS acceptance, and both educational attainment and household income were strongly associated with WTP. Similar socioeconomic gradients in WTP for carrier screening have been reported previously (Clarke et al., 2018). Acceptance of ECS was also associated with higher WTP, suggesting that readiness to engage with screening may translate into greater perceived value.

In contrast, age group and a family history of genetic disorders were associated with WTP but not with ECS acceptance, indicating that the factors influencing the decision to undergo screening may differ from those shaping perceived affordability or value. However, these associations should be interpreted cautiously given that some estimates were close to the null value. Together, these findings highlight potential equity concerns and emphasize the importance of considering both informational and financial barriers when integrating ECS into routine clinical practice (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2017; Gregg et al., 2021; van den Heuvel et al., 2023).

Clinical implications

From a clinical communication and genetic counseling perspective, these findings suggest that stigma‐related concerns should be explicitly addressed during pretest counseling, particularly in time‐sensitive obstetric settings. Normalizing carrier status and proactively addressing concerns related to discrimination or relational harm may support informed decision‐making regarding ECS (Ramdaney et al., 2022; van den Heuvel et al., 2023) Communication and counseling strategies used by both genetics and non‐genetics healthcare professionals should also prioritize the correction of key misconceptions rather than exhaustive technical detail, with particular attention to individuals with lower educational attainment or baseline knowledge (Kraft et al., 2018, 2019; Zhang et al., 2021) Transparent discussions regarding cost and downstream testing options may further support informed and equitable decision‐making (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2017; Clarke et al., 2018; Gregg et al., 2021).

In China, ECS is typically offered as an optional service in obstetric clinics and is not routinely covered by public health insurance. Genetic counseling services are still developing in many regions, and counseling is often integrated into routine obstetric consultations with limited time for detailed discussions. These contextual factors may influence patients' knowledge, perceived stigma, and willingness to pay for ECS.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include a large sample size, recruitment from real‐world obstetric clinics, and the integrated assessment of knowledge, psychological factors, acceptance, and WTP. Limitations include the cross‐sectional design, reliance on hypothetical scenarios rather than observed uptake, and conduct at a single center, which may limit generalizability. The questionnaire did not assess whether participants had previously undergone carrier screening or ECS. Prior screening experience may influence knowledge, attitudes, and WTP, and future studies should consider including this variable. In addition, the study did not systematically assess whether participants had previously received formal pretest genetic counseling related to ECS. Prior counseling exposure may influence participants' understanding, psychological perceptions, and decision‐making regarding ECS. Perceived stigma was assessed using a single survey item rather than a validated multi‐item stigma scale, which may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of stigma. Because participants responded based on their prior knowledge rather than after standardized genetic counseling, their responses may reflect the baseline awareness of ECS and may not fully represent attitudes formed after comprehensive counseling in clinical practice. Knowledge accuracy was assessed using two items focusing on key misconceptions about ECS rather than a comprehensive multi‐item knowledge scale. As a result, the three‐level knowledge accuracy score used in the analyses represents a simplified measure and may not fully reflect participants' true understanding of ECS.

CONCLUSIONS

Acceptance of ECS in obstetric settings is shaped not only by knowledge and socioeconomic factors but also by the perceived stigma related to carrier status. While knowledge accuracy and socioeconomic advantage are more closely linked to WTP, psychological factors appear closely associated with the initial decision to engage with screening. Integrating psychosocial support with clear, accurate information may therefore be critical to promoting the informed and equitable use of ECS.
