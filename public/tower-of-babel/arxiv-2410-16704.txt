Resolvability of classical-quantum channels
Masahito Hayashi, Hao-Chung Cheng, Li Gao

Abstract
Channel resolvability concerns the minimum resolution for approximating the channel output. We study the resolvability of classical-quantum channels in two settings, for the channel output generated from the worst input, and form the fixed independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) input. The direct part of the worst-input setting is derived from sequential hypothesis testing as it involves of non-i.i.d. inputs. The strong converse of the worst-input setting is obtained via the connection to identification codes. For the fixed-input setting, while the direct part follows from the known quantum soft covering result, we exploit the recent alternative quantum Sanov theorem to solve the strong converse.

In this paper, we consider the optimal rate of channel resolvability, namely, the resolution rate required to approximate the channel output via optimal codes with asymptotically vanishing error.
This problem admits two settings.
The first one considers channel output being generated from a memoryless and stationary channel with i.i.d. input from a fixed distribution, for which we call the

The second one is the

In other words, the worst-input setting concerns the channel output that
is the hardest to approximate.
Originally, the worst-input setting for classical channels was introduced for the strong converse of identification codes
[
6

That is, by deriving the direct part of the worst-input channel resolvability, the paper
[
6

showed the strong converse part of identification codes. At the same time, the strong converse of the worst-input channel resolvability was also obtained by the achievability of identification capacity.
An essential tool introduced in above analysis is the information spectrum method, which enables us to handle a general sequence of channels and sources.
The other setting regarding the fixed-input formulation was used for achieving strong secrecy for classical wiretap channel, which derives
the exponential decreasing rate for the amount of information leakage
[
10

[

,

The strong converse and second order asymptotics for the fixed-input setting was shown in
[
53
]

The main contributions of this paper is to establish the optimal rate of CQ channel resolvability for both the worst-input and the fixed-input settings.
For the worst-input setting, we show that the optimal rate is given by the maximum mutual information between the CQ channel input-output over all input distributions.
This quantity coincides with the channel capacity of message transmission over the CQ channel
[
54
,
55
,
56
]
and also with its identification capacity
[
31
,
32
]
.
We establish the result by employing two key tools: CQ channel version of information spectrum
[
57
]
for the direct part, and the identification codes for CQ channel for its strong converse.

For the fixed-input setting, we show that the optimal rate is given by the minimum mutual information between the CQ channel input-output over all input distributions yielding the same target output state.
Here we focus on the strong converse, as the direct part can be shown via the classical-quantum soft covering lemma
[
30
,
37
,
38
]
.
Our strategy is an extension of the method by the paper
[
53
]
for the classical case, where the key idea is a separation of good codewords and bad codewords, classified based on the empirical distribution. Nevertheless, a direct adaption to quantum setting does not works. The main issue is the failure of Lemma 2 in
[
53
]
, which states if a codeword has the empirical output distribution away from the target distribution (hence a bad codeword), then its typical set is disjoint of the typical set of the target distribution so that one distinguishes the good and bad codes by some natural test. Such a nice separation does not exist in quantum because the output quantum states have different eigenbasis to the target state, then one cannot easily conclude the orthogonality of typical subspaces based on the separation of codewords.

We note that the classical approach in
[
53
]
implicitly uses the Sanov theorem, which states that in the i.i.d. asymptotic setting,
the probability that the empirical distribution is different from the true distribution approaches zero exponentially.
However, a well-known quantum version of the Sanov theorem
[
58
,
59
]
does not discuss a quantum counterpart of empirical distributions.
Armed with the latest paper
[
60
]
formulating
another quantum version of the Sanov theorem, we manage to adopt the quantum analogue of the strategy in
[
53
]
.
We remark that the strong converse for the fixed-input setting is more technical challenging as the method for the worst-input setting and the standard strong converse analysis for packing problems via data-processing inequality (see e.g.,
[
61
]
) do not apply here.

Lastly, we explain why channel resolvability of a fully quantum channel does not cover that of a CQ channel.
An entanglement breaking (EB) channel is given by a measurement and state preparation process.
When using a projective measurement over a computation basis,
an EB channel becomes essentially a CQ channel for the aim of message transmission.
That is, a code for message transmission with such an EB channel
can be considered as a code for message transmission with the corresponding CQ channel.
However, this relation does not hold
for channel resolvability. This is because,
a pure input state for an EB channel
often works as a randomized input over a CQ channel.
That is, a channel resolvability code over an EB channel
cannot be simulated by a code over CQ channel of the same rate.
Therefore, the result in
[
52
]
for fully quantum channels does not directly apply to
channel resolvability for CQ channels.
We refer the readers to Remark
11
for a more detailed discussion.

II
Formulation of Channel Resolvability and our results

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite alphabet corresponding to the input system.
and
\mathcal{H}
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
corresponding to the output system, which is described by
B
.
We denote the set of density matrices over
\mathcal{H}

by
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
.
Let
W:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}),x\mapsto W_{x}
be a classical-quantum channel, where
\{W_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})
is the family of output states on
\mathcal{H}
. We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
the set of probability distribution on
X
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})=\left\{p:\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)\ \Big|\ \sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)=1\right\}.

Given an input distribution
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we write

W(p):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)W_{x}

as the induced output state, and

\displaystyle W\times p

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x}p(x)|{x}\rangle\langle{x}|\otimes W_{x},

\displaystyle\rho\times p

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x}p(x)|{x}\rangle\langle{x}|\otimes\rho,

for the joint input-output states.
The key information quantities is quantum relative entropy
and mutual information, which are defined as follows.
Given two density matrices
\rho
and
\sigma
with
\text{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\text{supp}(\sigma)
,
the quantum relative entropy
[
62
]
is defined as

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\sigma):=\Tr\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma).

(1)

The mutual information under the joint state
W\times p

is given as

\displaystyle I(X;B)_{W\times p}:=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)D(W_{x}\|W(p)).

(2)

Let
M\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
be a positive integer.
We define the
optimal resolution error
of
M
-types to the input
p
as

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p,W,M):=\inf_{|\mathcal{C}|=M}\frac{1}{2}\parallel\!W_{\mathcal{C}}-W(p)\!\parallel_{1},

(3)

where
the infimum is over all codebook
\mathcal{C}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{M})\in\mathcal{X}^{M}
of cardinality
M
and
W_{\mathcal{C}}:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}W_{x_{m}}
.
Denote the set of
M
-types probability distributions

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{M}(\mathcal{X})=\{p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\ |\ Mp(x)\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X}\}.

The optimal resolution error can be equivalently formulated as

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p,W,M)=\inf_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{M}(\mathcal{X})}\frac{1}{2}\parallel\!W(p)-W(q)\!\parallel_{1},

\displaystyle\varepsilon(W,M):=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}\inf_{q\in\mathcal{P}_{M}(\mathcal{X})}\frac{1}{2}\parallel\!W(p)-W(q)\!\parallel_{1},

where
\varepsilon(W,M)
is the
worst case error
over all input distributions.

In the i.i.d.
n
-shot setting, a memoryless and stationary channel maps each
n
-letter input
x^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
to an output product state, namely,

\displaystyle W^{\otimes n}:x_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{n}\mapsto W^{\otimes n}_{x^{n}}:=W_{x_{1}}\otimes W_{x_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes W_{x_{n}}.

Then, we define the corresponding optimal resolution error as

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p^{n},W^{\otimes n},M)=\inf_{q^{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{M}(\mathcal{X}^{n})}\frac{1}{2}\parallel\!W^{\otimes n}(p^{n})-W^{\otimes n}(q^{n})\!\parallel_{1},

\displaystyle\varepsilon(W^{\otimes n},M)=\sup_{p^{n}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}^{n})}\inf_{q^{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{M}(\mathcal{X}^{n})}\frac{1}{2}\parallel\!W^{\otimes n}(p^{n})-W^{\otimes n}(q^{n})\!\parallel_{1}.

The
resolvability rate
for a fixed input
p
(resp. the worst-input) are given by the minimum achievable rate such that the optimal resolution error vanishes, i.e.,

\displaystyle R(p,W)

\displaystyle:=\inf\{R>0\ |\ \forall\gamma>0,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon(p^{\otimes n},W^{\otimes n},\lfloor 2^{n(R+\gamma)}\rfloor)=0\},

\displaystyle R(W)

\displaystyle:=\inf\{R>0\ |\ \forall\gamma>0,\liminf_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon(W^{\otimes n},\lfloor 2^{n(R+\gamma)}\rfloor,n)=0\}.

The quantity
R(p,W)
corresponds to the optimal rate of CQ channel resolvability for the fixed-input setting (see Figure
1
), while
R(W)
is the optimal rate for the worst-input setting (see Figure
2
).

The goal is to employ

-bit uniform randomness and an optimal codebook of size

to simulate the target state

, which is generated from a fixed i.i.d. input distribution

.
The minimum asymptotic rate
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log M
for an
\epsilon
-approximation is
R_{\epsilon}(p,W)
defined in (

).

A schematic diagram of channel resolvability for the worst-input case.
The goal is to
nR

2^{nR}
to simulate the target state
W^{\otimes n}(p^{n})
, which is generated from a worst (probably non-i.i.d.) input distribution

The minimum asymptotic rate
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log M
for an
\epsilon

defined in (

).

Our main results can be stated as follows

\displaystyle R(p,W)=\inf_{q:W(q)=W(p)}I(X;B)_{W\times q},

(4)

\displaystyle R(W)=C(W):=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}I(X;B)_{W\times p},

(5)

where
C(W)
is channel capacity of
W
, and the infimum in (
4
) is over all input distribution
q
whose output
W(q)
coincides with the target state
W(p)
.
Moreover, we have the strong converse,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon(p^{\otimes n},W^{\otimes n},\lfloor 2^{nR}\rfloor)\to 1\hskip 2.84544pt,\hskip 2.84544pt\text{ if }R<R(p,W),

(6)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon(W^{\otimes n},\lfloor 2^{nR}\rfloor)\to 1\hskip 2.84544pt,\hskip 2.84544pt\text{ if }R<R(W).

(7)

To state the strong converse in another word,
for fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we define

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}(p,W):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log M_{\epsilon}(p^{\otimes n},W^{\otimes n})\hskip 2.84544pt,\quad

\displaystyle M_{\epsilon}(p,W):=\min\{M\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\ |\ \varepsilon(p,W,M)\leq\epsilon\},

(8)

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}(W):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log M_{\epsilon}(W^{\otimes n})\hskip 2.84544pt,\quad

\displaystyle M_{\epsilon}(W):=\min\{M\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\ |\ \varepsilon(W,M)\leq\epsilon\}.

(9)

Clearly, for all
0<\epsilon<1
, we have

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}(W)

\displaystyle\leq R(W),

(10)

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}(p,W)

\displaystyle\leq R(p,W).

(11)

Therefore, the strong converse parts (
6
) and (
7
)
can be restated as the equalities in (
10
) and (
11
),
which can be derived by showing

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}(W)

\displaystyle\geq C(W),

(12)

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}(p,W)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{q:W(q)=W(p)}I(X;B)_{W\times q}.

(13)

That is, the aim of the remaining part of the paper is to prove
the part
\leq
in (
4
),(
5
) (i.e., the direct part)
and to prove (
12
), (
13
) (i.e., the strong converse). Note that here we do not consider the zero error resolvality
\epsilon=0
, which is different with the vanishing error case
R(W)
, and in most of case, an exact resolvability is not feasible for finite code length
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

The following theorems summarize our main results.

Theorem 1

(Worst-Input Setting)
.

For any classical-quantum channel
W:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{S(H)}
, the resolvability rate for the worst-input setting, defined in (
5
) and (
9
), is given by

\displaystyle R(W)=R_{\epsilon}(W)=C(W):=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}I(X;B)_{W\times p},\quad\forall\epsilon\in(0,1).

Theorem 2

(Fixed-Input Setting)
.

For any classical-quantum channel
W:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{S(H)}
and any input distribution
p\in\mathcal{P(X)}
,
the resolvability rate for the fixed-input setting, defined in (
4
) and (
8
), is given by

\displaystyle R(p,W)=R_{\epsilon}(p,W)=\inf_{q:W(q)=W(p)}I(X;B)_{W\times q},\quad\forall\epsilon\in(0,1).

Moreover, for
R<R(p,W)
, we have
\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\varepsilon(p^{\otimes n},W^{\otimes n},\lfloor 2^{nR}\rfloor)\to 1
exponentially.

III
Classical Channel Resolvability

For classical channels, i.e.,
W:x\mapsto W_{x}
with
W_{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
being probability distributions over some finite alphabet
\mathcal{Y}
, the worst-input channel resolvability rate was shown to coincide with Shannon capacity (see
[
6
,
63
]
and also
[
10
]
)

\displaystyle R(W)=C(W)=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P(X)}}I(X;Y)_{W\times p}

(14)

where
I(X;Y)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)D(W_{x}\|W(p))
is the mutual information of the joint input-output distribution
W\times p
. Later,
the reference
[
53
]
proved that for a fixed input
p\in\mathcal{P(X)}
,

\displaystyle R(p,W)=\inf_{q:W(q)=W(p)}I(X;Y)_{W\times q}.

(15)

Here, the infimum is over all possible input
q
such that
W(q)=W(p)
. Such infimum is needed as the resolution error only depends the output
W(p)
but not directly depend on the input
p
.
Moreover, the reference
[
53
]

actually proved the strong converse that for any
R<R(p,W)
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}1-\varepsilon(p,W^{\otimes n},\lfloor 2^{nR}\rfloor)=0\text{ exponentially }.

For the relation between
R(p,W)
and
R(W)
, one has

\displaystyle\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P(X)}}R(p,W)=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P(X)}}\inf_{q:W(q)=W(p)}I(X;Y)_{W\times q}\leq\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P(X)}}I(X;Y)_{W\times p}=R(W).

(16)

Here we emphasis that (the converse of) the fixed-input case (
15
) does not imply (the converse of) the worst case (
14
). One can consider the case that there are two distributions
p
and
q
such that

W(p)=W(q),\quad I(X;Y)_{W\times q}<I(X;Y)_{W\times p}=C(W).

which gives a strict inequality
\sup_{p\in\mathcal{P(X)}}R(p,W)<R(W)
in (
16
).
Hence, even taking maximum of fixed-input rate
R(p,W)
over all inputs
p\in\mathcal{P(X)}
, one cannot recover
the channel resolvability rate
R(W)
for the worst input (
14
).
For this reason, we cannot say that
the converse for
R(W)
is an easier problem than
the converse for
R(p,W)
.

IV
Direct part for CQ-Channel Resolvability

The direct part of the fixed-input setting (which is also called the achievability part) follows from random coding strategies studied in
[
30
, § IV]
,
[
37
, § III]
,
[
38
, § IV]
under the name
quantum soft covering
.
The idea is to employ a random codebook
\mathcal{C}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{M}\}
of size
M
, where each codeword
x_{m}
is pairwise-independently drawn from a distribution
q\in\mathcal{P(X)}
satisfying
W(q)=W(p)
.
Then, one can relax the optimal resolution error introduced in (
3
) as follows:

\displaystyle\varepsilon(p,W,M)\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathds{E}_{\mathcal{C}\sim q}\parallel\!W_{\mathcal{C}}-W(q)\!\parallel_{1}.

It was proved in
[
37
, Theorem 1]
that for any
\alpha\in(1,2]
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathds{E}_{\mathcal{C}\sim q}\left\|W_{\mathcal{C}}-W(q)\right\|_{1}\leq 2^{\frac{2}{\alpha}-2}2^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(X;B)_{W\times q}-\log M},

(20)

where
I_{\alpha}(X;B)_{W\times q}:=\inf_{\sigma}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log(\sum_{x}q(x)\|\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}W_{x}\sigma^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha})
is the sandwiched Rényi mutual information
[
64
,
65
,
66
,
67
]
.
Since
\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 1}I_{\alpha}(X;B)_{W\times q}=I(X;B)_{W\times q}
, (
20
) implies (see also
[
30
, Lemma 10]
and
[
38
, Proposition 14]
) that, if
R>I(X;B)_{W\times q}
,

\varepsilon(p^{\otimes n},W^{\otimes n},\lceil 2^{nR}\rceil)\leq 2^{-n\sup_{\alpha\in(1,2]}\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}(R-I_{\alpha}(X;B)_{W\times q})}\to 0.

Minimizing all input distribution
q
satisfying
W(q)=W(p)
gives the direct part for the fixed-input setting, i.e.,
the part of
\leq
in (
4
) and Theorem
2
.

The remaining part of this section studies the part of
\leq
in (
5
) and Theorem
1
.
In the worst-input setting, the analysis for
R(W)
is more involved because

Moreover, as illustrated in (

Strong converse for Channel Resolvability with fixed input

V-A

Quantum Sanov theorem

The goal of this section is to show the
strong converse for channel resolvability with fixed-input, basically, the inequality (
13
)

R_{\epsilon}(p,W)\geq\inf_{q:W(q)=W(p)}I(X;B)_{W\times q},\quad\forall\epsilon\in(0,1)

in Theorem
2
.
For this aim, we employ an alternative quantum version of Sanov theorem by
[
60
]
, which is different from quantum Sanov theorem by
[
70
,
71
]
.
The quantum extension by
[
60
]

is based on empirical distributions
in the same spirit as the original Sanov theorem in the classical system

[
72
,
73
]
.
To state the above type of quantum Sanov theorem,
we prepare several notations.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{|v_{j}\rangle\}_{j=1}^{d}
be the basis that diagonalizes the state
W(p)
.
We assume that our observation is based on
the basis
\mathcal{B}
, and
when our system is
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
,
our observation is given as a basis

|v[x^{n}]\rangle:=|v_{x_{1}},\ldots,v_{x_{n}}\rangle

with a data
x^{n}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
In this case, we obtain the data
x^{n}
.
The state
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}|v_{x_{j}}\rangle\langle v_{x_{j}}|

is called its empirical state under the basis
\mathcal{B}
,
and is denoted by
e_{\mathcal{B}}(x^{n})
.
When
\mathcal{H}
is a classical system, we only have state vectors like
|x^{n}\rangle:=|{x_{1}},\ldots,{x_{n}}\rangle
.
The state
e_{\mathcal{B}}(x^{n})
is simplified to

e(x^{n})
and can be considered as the empirical distribution.

In the
n
-fold tensor product case,
we denote the set of empirical states under the basis
\mathcal{B}

by

\mathcal{S}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}|v_{x_{j}}\rangle\langle v_{x_{j}}|\,\Big|(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}^{n}\right\}.

Given an empirical state
\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]
,
we consider the projection

T_{\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}:=\sum_{x^{n}:e_{\mathcal{B}}(x^{n})=\rho}|v[x^{n}]\rangle\langle v[x^{n}]|.

When
\rho\notin\mathcal{S}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]
,

T_{\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}
is defined to be
0
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the permutation group over
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
For
g\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we denote the unitary operation for changing the order of the tensor product based on
g

by
U_{g}
.
We define the twirling
\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\rho):=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{g\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}U_{g}\rho U_{g}^{\dagger}.

(41)

Next,
similar to the papers
[
74
,
75
,
76
,
77
,
78
,
79
,
80
,
81
,
70
,
82
,
83
,
84
]
,
we employ Schur duality of
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
.
A sequence of monotone-increasing non-negative integers

\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
is called Young index.
Although many references
[
83
,
85
,
86
]
define Young index as
monotone-decreasing non-negative integers, here
we define it in the opposite way for notational convenience.
We denote the set of Young indices
\bm{\lambda}
with condition

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}=n
by
Y_{d}^{n}
.
Also, the set of probability distributions
p=(p_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
with the condition

p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots\leq p_{d}
by
\mathcal{P}_{d}
.
For any density
\rho
on
\mathcal{H}
,
the eigenvalues of
\rho
forms an element of
\mathcal{P}_{d}
, which
is denoted by
p(\rho)
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{d}^{n}
be the set of elements
p
of
\mathcal{P}_{d}
such that

p_{j}
is an integer times of
1/n
.
We define the majorization relation
\succ
in two elements of

\mathcal{P}_{d}
and
Y_{d}^{n}
.
For two elements
p,p^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{P}_{d}
, we say that

p\succ p^{\prime}
when

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}^{\prime}

(42)

for
k=1,\ldots,d-1
,
and the majorization relation
\succ
for two elements of
Y_{d}^{n}
is defined
in the same way.

As explained in
[

, Section 6.2]

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}=\bigoplus_{\bm{\lambda}\in Y_{d}^{n}}\mathcal{U}_{\bm{\lambda}}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{\bm{\lambda}},

(43)

Denote

As shown in

\displaystyle d_{\bm{\lambda}}\leq(n+1)^{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}}.

P_{\bm{\lambda}}

For
\bm{\lambda}\in Y_{d}^{n}
and

\displaystyle T_{\bm{\lambda},\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}:=P_{\bm{\lambda}}T_{\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}.

(45)

Note that

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}\succ np(\rho).

(46)

Since
\bm{\lambda}
is the highest weight vector,

contained in the space
\mathcal{U}_{\bm{\lambda}}

needs to satisfy the condition (

We also define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{B}]

\displaystyle:=\{(p,\rho)\in\mathcal{P}_{d}\times\mathcal{S}[\mathcal{B}]\ |\ p\succ p(\rho)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{B}]\cap(\mathcal{P}_{d}^{n}\times\mathcal{S}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]).

That is, the decomposition

and the Schur sampling

For

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr\rho^{\otimes n}\left(\sum_{(p^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\in S_{\rho,r}^{c}\cap\mathcal{R}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]}T_{np^{\prime},\rho^{\prime},\mathcal{B}}^{n}\right)=r.

To study this case, we recall the sandwiched Rényi relative entropy

\displaystyle D_{1+s}(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\phi(-s|\rho\|\sigma)}{s},

(52)

\displaystyle\phi(s|\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\log\Tr(\sigma^{\frac{s}{2(1-s)}}\rho\sigma^{\frac{s}{2(1-s)}})^{1-s}.

(53)

The quantum relative entropy

is characterized as

\displaystyle D(\rho\|\sigma)=\lim_{s\to 0}D_{1+s}(\rho\|\sigma).

(54)

\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(X):=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]}T_{\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n}XT_{\rho,\mathcal{B}}^{n},

\displaystyle-\phi(s|\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(\sigma^{\otimes n})\|\rho^{\otimes n})\leq-\phi(s|\sigma^{\otimes n}\|\rho^{\otimes n})\leq-\phi(s|\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(\sigma^{\otimes n})\|\rho^{\otimes n})+s(d-1)\log(n+1).

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr\sigma^{\otimes n}T_{\bm{\lambda}_{n},\rho_{n},\mathcal{B}}^{n}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{-(1-s)r_{n}-\phi(1-s|\sigma\|\rho)}{s}-\frac{d(d+3)}{2sn}\log(n+1),

where

Strong converse of fixed-input resolvability

This subsection aims to prove the

Theorem 10

Let

be an input distribution satisfying the condition

.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{n}
be a sequence of codebook
\mathcal{C}_{n}\subset X^{n}
with size
|\mathcal{C}_{n}|=M_{n}
. Suppose that
\displaystyle R_{1}:=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log M_{n}<I(X;B)_{W\times p}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\|W^{\otimes n}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}-W(p)^{\otimes n}\|_{1}=1.

(58)

We divide our proof into 6 steps.
The initial two steps are preparations for latter steps.

Step 1:
Throughout the proof, we fix two positive numbers
R_{2}
,
R_{3}
such that
I(X;B)_{W\times p}>R_{3}>R_{2}>R_{1}
.

In the first step, we will divide our codes into two subsets using information quantities.
For an element
x^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
,
we write
W[x^{n}]:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{x_{j}}
as the empirical output, and define the set

\displaystyle B_{n}(\delta,p):=\{x^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}\ |\ \|W[x^{n}]-W(p)\|_{1}\geq\delta\}.

B_{n}(\delta,p)
is the set of bad codewords whose empirical output states are away from
W(p)
.

Since
p
satisfies
I(X;B)_{W\times p}=\inf_{q:W(q)=W(p)}I(X;B)_{W\times q}
,
we can choose
\delta>0
small enough to satisfy the following conditions:
for any element

,
the empirical distribution

defined in the beginning of
Section

satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}D(W_{x_{j}}\|W(p))=I(X;B)_{W\times e(x^{n})}>R_{3}>R_{2}.

(60)

Furthermore, by the continuity of sandwiched Rényi relative entropy over the parameter
s\mapsto D_{1+s}
,
we choose
s_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}-\frac{1}{s_{0}}\phi(s_{0}|W_{x_{j}}\|W(p))=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}D_{1-s_{0}}(W_{x_{j}}\|W(p))>R_{2}

(61)

for any element

.

We define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\delta,p):=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\ |\ \|\sigma-W(p)\|_{1}\geq\delta\}.

(62)

Then, for any
s\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle-\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,p)}\phi(1-s|\sigma\|W(p))>0.

(63)

Given the chosen

we choose

\displaystyle R_{4}:=\frac{-(1-s_{0})r-\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,p)}\phi(1-s_{0}|\sigma\|W(p))}{s_{0}}>0.

(64)

Step 2:

In this proof, two projections play key roles.
The aim of this step is to introduce these two

We fix the basis

Define the first projection:

In particular, the trace

Step 3:

The aim of this step is to show the traces

\displaystyle Q_{n}(I-T_{r,n})=(I-T_{r,n}).

8

\displaystyle\Tr W(p)^{\otimes n}(I-T_{r,n})Q_{n}\leq\Tr W(p)^{\otimes n}(I-T_{r,n})\leq\beta_{2,n}:=(n+1)^{\frac{(d+4)(d-1)}{2}}2^{-nr}.

(68)

Step 4:

The aim of this step is to show the trace

\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}(I-Q_{n})
is small
for a bad codeword

x^{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\cap B_{n}(\delta,p)

Since
|S_{W(p),r}\cap\mathcal{R}_{n}[\mathcal{B}]|\leq(n+1)^{2(d-1)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(n+1)^{2(d-1)}(n+1)^{-\frac{d(d+3)}{2s_{0}}}2^{-nR_{4}}.

(69)

For an element

(70)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}T_{r,n}=\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(T_{r,n})=\Tr\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n})T_{r,n}=\Tr W^{\otimes n}(\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(|x^{n}\rangle\langle x^{n}|))T_{r,n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle(n+1)^{|\mathcal{X}|-1}\Tr W^{\otimes n}(e(x^{n})^{\otimes n})T_{r,n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+1)^{|\mathcal{X}|-1}\Tr W(e(x^{n}))^{\otimes n}T_{r,n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle(n+1)^{|\mathcal{X}|-1}(n+1)^{2(d-1)}(n+1)^{-\frac{d(d+3)}{2s_{0}}}:=\beta_{1,n}2^{-nR_{4}}.

(71)

Here,
(a)
follows from (
70
),

(b)
follows from (

) and

\parallel\!e(x^{n})-W(p)\!\parallel_{1}>\delta
by the assumption that

For a bad codeword

\displaystyle\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}Q_{n}\geq\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}(I-T_{r,n})Q_{n}=\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}(I-T_{r,n})\geq 1-\beta_{1,n}.

(72)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}(I-Q_{n})\leq\beta_{1,n}.

(73)

Step 5:

The aim of this step is the evaluation of

\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}(I-Q_{n})

for a good codeword

x^{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\cap B_{n}(\delta,p)^{c}

Define the pinching

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n}(X):=Q_{n}XQ_{n}+(I-Q_{n})X(I-Q_{n}).

(74)

Denote

\displaystyle M_{n}\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W^{\otimes n}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}})\geq\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W^{\otimes n}_{x^{n}}),

(75)

and the application of the pinching

\displaystyle M_{n}\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W^{\otimes n}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}})\geq\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W^{\otimes n}_{x^{n}}).

(76)

Thus

\displaystyle I-Q_{n}=\{\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W^{\otimes n}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}})<2^{\alpha}T_{r,n}W(p)^{\otimes n}\}\leq\left\{\frac{1}{M_{n}}\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W^{\otimes n}_{x^{n}})<2^{\alpha}T_{r,n}W(p)^{\otimes n}\right\}.

Because

\displaystyle(\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}))^{-s}(T_{r,n}W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}))^{-s}(W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s/2}(\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}))^{-s}(W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s/2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle 2^{-s}(W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s/2}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}))^{-s}(W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s/2}.

(78)

Here,
(c)
follows from the pinching inequality

\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n})\leq 2\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n})

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}(I-Q_{n})

(79)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}\left\{\frac{1}{M_{n}}\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W^{\otimes n}_{x^{n}})<2^{\alpha}T_{r,n}W(p)^{\otimes n}\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle 2^{s_{0}\alpha}M_{n}^{s_{0}}\Tr W^{\otimes n}_{x^{n}}(\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}))^{-s_{0}}(T_{r,n}W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s_{0}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(f)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle 2^{-s_{0}}2^{s_{0}\alpha}M_{n}^{s_{0}}\Tr W^{\otimes n}_{x^{n}}(W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s_{0}/2}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}))^{-s_{0}}(W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s_{0}/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-s_{0}}2^{s_{0}\alpha}M_{n}^{s_{0}}\Tr\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W^{\otimes n}_{x^{n}})(W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s_{0}/2}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}))^{-s_{0}}(W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s_{0}/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-s_{0}}2^{s_{0}\alpha}M_{n}^{s_{0}}\Tr(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}))^{1-s_{0}}(W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-s_{0}}2^{s_{0}\alpha}M_{n}^{s_{0}}2^{\phi(s_{0}|\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n})\|W(p)^{\otimes n})}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(g)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle(n+1)^{s_{0}(d-1)}2^{-s_{0}}2^{s_{0}\alpha}M_{n}^{s_{0}}2^{\phi(s_{0}|W_{x^{n}}^{n}\|W(p)^{\otimes n})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n+1)^{s_{0}(d-1)}2^{-s_{0}}2^{s_{0}\alpha}M_{n}^{s_{0}}2^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\phi(s_{0}|W_{x_{j}}\|W(p))}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(h)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle(n+1)^{s_{0}(d-1)}2^{-s_{0}}2^{s_{0}\alpha}M_{n}^{s_{0}}2^{-ns_{0}R_{2}}=:\beta_{3,n}.

(80)

Here,
(d)
follows from (
77
),

(e)
follows from the fact that
the condition

\frac{1}{M_{n}}\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W^{\otimes n}_{x^{n}})<2^{\alpha}T_{r,n}W(p)^{\otimes n}\Longrightarrow I\leq 2^{s\alpha}M_{n}^{s_{0}}(\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}))^{-s_{0}}(T_{r,n}W(p)^{\otimes n})^{s_{0}},

(f)
follows from (
78
),

(g)
follows from (
56
), and

(h)
follows from (

).
By the choice of

, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\beta_{3,n}=s(R_{2}-R_{1})>0\Rightarrow\liminf_{n\to\infty}\beta_{3,n}=0.

(81)

Step 6:
As the final step, we evaluate the desired trace norm by using
the above evaluations.
We have

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{2}\|W^{\otimes n}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}-W(p)^{\otimes n}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1-\Tr(W^{\otimes n}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}-W(p)^{\otimes n})Q_{n}

(82)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr W^{\otimes n}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}(I-Q_{n})+\Tr W(p)^{\otimes n}Q_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{n}}\sum_{x^{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\cap B_{n}(\delta,p)^{c}}\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}(I-Q_{n})+\frac{1}{M_{n}}\sum_{x^{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\cap B_{n}(\delta,p)}\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}(I-Q_{n})

\displaystyle+\Tr W(p)^{\otimes n}T_{r,n}Q_{n}+\Tr W(p)^{\otimes n}(I-T_{r,n})Q_{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{n}}\sum_{x^{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\cap B_{n}(\delta,p)^{c}}\beta_{3,n}+\frac{1}{M_{n}}\sum_{x^{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}\cap B_{n}(\delta,p)}\beta_{1,n}+2^{-\alpha}+\beta_{2,n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max(\beta_{1,n},\beta_{3,n})+2^{-\alpha}+\beta_{2,n}.

(83)

(i)
follows from (

), (
68
), (
73
), and (

).
Therefore, combining

), (

), (

),
we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}1-\frac{1}{2}\|W^{\otimes n}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}-W(p)^{\otimes n}\|_{1}\leq 2^{-\alpha}.

(84)

Since

is arbitrary, we obtain

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}1-\frac{1}{2}\|W^{\otimes n}_{\mathcal{C}_{n}}-W(p)^{\otimes n}\|_{1}=0.

(85)

Although the paper
[
53
]
discussed the second-order asymptotics for classical channels,
our method does not work with
the second-order asymptotics for the CQ case in the following reason.
First, in our method, we apply the pinching map
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.
There is a possibility that
the pinching map
\mathcal{E}_{n}
might cause an unexpected behavior of the probability

\Tr W_{x^{n}}^{\otimes n}\{\frac{1}{M_{n}}\mathcal{E}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{B}}(W^{\otimes n}_{x^{n}})<2^{\alpha}T_{r,n}W(p)^{\otimes n}\}
.
To avoid this problem,
we evaluate the Rényi relative entropy, which enables us to show the strong converse.
However, it cannot derive the second-order asymptotics.

Remark 11
.

Here we discuss the relation with the approach by
[
52
]
.
The paper
[
52
]
studied the channel resolvability for fully quantum channels.
Theorem 31 of
[
52
]
gives a lower bound of

R_{\epsilon}(p,W)
for a quantum channel from system
A
to the system
B
by the smoothing of conditional entropy
-H(A_{R}|B)
, where
A_{R}
is the reference system of the input system
A
.

Nevertheless, the channel resolvability of a fully quantum channel does not cover that of a CQ channel. For the aim of message transmission, a CQ channel is equivalent to an
entanglement breaking (EB) channel
given by a projective measurement over the computation basis and a state preparation process. Nevertheless, this equivalence does not hold
for channel resolvability. When we treat a CQ channel as an entanglement breaking channel in quantum to quantum channel setting, we are allowed to input a superposition pure state for the computation basis as a deterministic input. However, such an input works as a randomized input over a CQ channel because
the outcome of the projective measurement over the computation basis
is probabilistic.
Hence it cannot be considered as a deterministic input in the framework of CQ channel. Therefore, a quantum resolvability code over an EB channel
cannot be simulated by a resolvability code over CQ channel at the same rate. This is one of the difficulties of channel resolvability.

Indeed, for the entanglement-breaking channel,
the state over the joint system between
the output system
B
and
the reference
A_{R}
is a separable state.
Hence, the conditional entropy
H(A_{R}|B)
is always
a non-negative value
[
87
]
. Hence,
-H(A_{R}|B)
is not even a positive value.
Thus, the method
[
52
]
does not give an effective lower bound for

R_{\epsilon}(p,W)
for classical-quantum channels.

VI
Strong converse for channel resolvability of worst input

The goal of this section is to show the
strong converse for channel resolvability with worst-input, i.e., inequality (
12
)

R_{\epsilon}(W)\geq C(W),

by using identification codes.
For classical channels, the channel resolvability was introduced to show the strong converse of the identification code, and
the converse of channel resolvability can be proved using the direct part of identification code. For CQ channels, the identification codes and its capacity have been studied in
[
31
]
.

VI-A

Identification codes

The aim of this subsection is to summarize basic facts
for identification codes for CQ-channels, which works as
our preparation for strong converse of channel resolvability for the worst-input setting.

Let
W:x\mapsto W_{x}
be a CQ channel and
0<\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}<1
.
An
(N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
identification (ID) code
(p_{i},D_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
of
W
consists of a family of probability distribution
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P(X)}
and corresponding measurement operator
D_{i}
on
\mathcal{H}
with
0\leq D_{i}\leq I
such that for all
i,j=1,\cdots,N
and
i\neq j

\displaystyle\Tr(W(p_{i})D_{i})\geq 1-\lambda_{1}\ ,\ \Tr(W(p_{i})D_{j})\leq\lambda_{2}.

If in additional, there exists a common refinement of
D_{i}
, that is, there exists a POVM
\{E_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
,
0\leq E_{j}\leq I,\sum_{j}E_{j}=I
such that

D_{j}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}E_{j}\text{ for some subset }\mathcal{A}_{j}\subset[k]\hskip 2.84544pt,

then
(p_{i},D_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
is called a simultaneous
(N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
ID code for
W
. In the
n
-shot setting, a
(N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
code for the product channel
W^{\otimes n}
is called a code
(n,N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
for
W
.

Denote
N(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
(resp.
N_{\text{sim}}(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
) as the maximum
N
such that there exists a
(n,N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
(resp. simultaneous) ID-code and the (resp. simultaneous) ID capacity is defined as

\displaystyle C_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(W)=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\log N(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}{n}

\displaystyle C_{\text{sim},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(W)=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\log N_{\text{sim}}(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}{n}.

From the definition, we find that

\displaystyle C_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(W)\geq C_{\text{sim},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(W).

(86)

It was proved by
[
31
,
32
]
that for a CQ channel,the simultaneous identification capacity and the identification capacity coincides and are given by

\displaystyle{C}_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(W)={C}_{\text{sim},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(W)=C(W)

(87)

for any
0<\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}<1
.

The connection between ID code and overall channel resolvability is provided by the following lemma, whose classical version is due to Han and Verdú
[
6
]
, and the quantum version is used by Loeber
[
31
]
in his proof of achievability of ID code.

Lemma 12
.

Let
W:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}),x\mapsto W_{x}
be a classical quantum channel.
If there exists an
(N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
identification code
\mathcal{C}=(p_{i},D_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
for
W
and an integer
M
satisfy

1-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}>\varepsilon(W,M)

then

|\mathcal{X}|^{M}\geq N.

Proof.

The proof is essentially in Lober’s paper
[
31
, Lemma 4.10 and Theorem 4.11]
. We repeat it here for completeness. Denote

\mathcal{D}_{i}:\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H})\to l_{\infty}(\{0,1\})
as the binary measurement

\mathcal{D}_{i}(\rho)=(\Tr(\rho D_{i}),\Tr(\rho(I-D_{i}))).

Then for any
i\neq j
, we have

\parallel\!W(p_{i})-W(p_{j})\!\parallel_{1}\geq\parallel\!\mathcal{D}_{i}\circ(W(p_{i}))-\mathcal{D}_{i}\circ W(p_{j})\!\parallel_{1}\geq 2(1-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})>2\varepsilon.

By the definition of
\varepsilon(M,W)
, there exists
M
-type distribution
(q_{1},\cdots,q_{N})
such that

\frac{1}{2}\parallel\!W(p_{i})-W(q_{i})\!\parallel_{1}\leq\varepsilon.

In total, there are less than
|\mathcal{X}|^{M}
many
M
-types in
P(\mathcal{X})
. Now, suppose
N>|\mathcal{X}|^{M}
, there must be some
i\neq j
such that
q_{i}=q_{j}
. It follows that

\frac{1}{2}\parallel\!W(p_{i})-W(p_{j})\!\parallel_{1}\leq\parallel\!W(p_{i})-W(q_{i})\!\parallel_{1}+\parallel\!W(p_{j})-W(q_{j})\!\parallel_{1}\leq 2\varepsilon

which is a contradiction. This finishes the proof.
∎

The above lemma says if there exists an
(N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
identification code
\mathcal{C}=(p_{i},D_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
with

N>|\mathcal{X}|^{M},

then

\varepsilon(M,W)\leq 1-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}.

Let us denote the optimal error for identification code as

\varepsilon_{\text{ID}}(N,W):=\inf\{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\hskip 2.84544pt|\hskip 2.84544pt\exists(N,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\text{ ID code}\hskip 2.84544pt\}.

Then the above lemma gives the following one shot estimate
between the minimum error of identification codes and
the minimum error of channel resolvability for CQ-channels;

\varepsilon\Big(\Big\lfloor\frac{\log N}{\log|\mathcal{X}|}\Big\rfloor,W\Big)\geq 1-\varepsilon_{\text{ID}}(N,W).

VI-B

Strong converse for Channel Resolvability

Next, we proceed to the strong converse for
channel resolvability for CQ channels.
To prove this statement, we show the following relation between
the identification capacity and the capacity for channel resolvability for CQ channels.

Theorem 13
.

For
\epsilon\geq 1-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle C_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(W)\leq R_{\epsilon}(W).

(88)

Thus, for
0<\epsilon<1
,

\displaystyle C(W)\leq R_{\epsilon}(W).

(89)

The above theorem implies the strong converse for
channel resolvability for CQ channels as follows.
Summarizing Theorems
7
and
13
with (
10
), we obtain our main claim in Theorem
1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle R(W)=R_{\epsilon}(W)=C(W)

(90)

for
0<\epsilon<1
.

Proof.

Recall the notation that

M_{\epsilon}(W):=\min\{M\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\ |\ \varepsilon(W,M)\leq\epsilon\}.

Lemma
12
implies that

\displaystyle N(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\leq(|\mathcal{X}|^{n})^{M_{\epsilon}(W^{\otimes n})}.

(91)

Taking the logarithm, we have

\displaystyle\log N(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\leq M_{\epsilon}(W^{\otimes n})\log(|\mathcal{X}|^{n})=nM_{\epsilon}(W^{\otimes n})\log|\mathcal{X}|.

(92)

Taking the logarithm, again, we have

\displaystyle\log\log N(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\leq\log n+\log M_{\epsilon}(W^{\otimes n})+\log\log|\mathcal{X}|.

(93)

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\log N(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\leq\frac{1}{n}\log n+\frac{1}{n}\log M_{\epsilon}(W^{\otimes n})+\frac{1}{n}\log\log|\mathcal{X}|.

(94)

Taking the limit, we have

\displaystyle C_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(W)=

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\log N(n,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log M_{\epsilon}(W^{\otimes n})=R_{\epsilon}(W).\qed

VII
Discussion

We have studied channel resolvability for CQ channels under the two problem settings,
the worst-input formulation and the fixed-input formulation.
To address the worst-input formulation, similar to existing studies
[
6
]
,
we focus on its relation to identification codes for CQ channels.
While the simultaneous identification codes is a special case of identification codes,
we have observed that the direct part of identification codes with both simultaneous and non-simultaneous settings
works as the strong converse of
the worst-input channel resolvability for CQ channels.
The direct part of worst-input is shown by employing the information spectrum method for CQ channels formulated in
[
57
]
.
To address the fixed-input formulation,
we extend the approach from
[
53
]
to the case with CQ channels,
in which we have used the latest result for a new version of quantum Sanov theorem, which
focuses on a quantum version of empirical distributions.

Here, we emphasize the difference between two settings.
The fixed-input formulation with the input
p

has the minimum rate given by
\inf_{q:W(q)=W(p)}I(X;B)_{W\times q}
,
whereas the worst-input formulation
has the minimum rate
\sup_{p}I(X;B)_{W\times p}
.
That is, the maximum value of the minimum rate of the fixed-input formulation
does not equal the minimum rate of the worst-input formulation, in general (see Example
3
).
This fact means that the worst-input in the
n
-shot setting could be achieved by a non-i.i.d. input, which shows the difficulty of the worst-input formulation. The ideas and techniques used in the work may shed a light on other converse for covering type problems such as quantum decoupling.

Recently, the paper
[
52
]
studies channel resolvability for quantum-quantum channels.
This topic is more difficult and challenging so that
the results
[
52
]
, when restricted to CQ channels, are limited to partial results. Our results of channel resolvability for CQ channels cannot be included in that for fully quantum channels
as explained in Remark
11
.
Therefore, further development of covering lemma and channel resolvability for fully quantum channels is an interesting open problem.
