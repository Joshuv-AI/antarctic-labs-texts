Synthesis of defect-rich La2O2CO3 supports for enhanced CO2-to-methanol conversion efficiency
Authors: Rui Zhang, Xiao Wang, Ke Wang, Huilin Wang, Xudong Sun, Weidong Shi, Shuyan Song, Hongjie Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Converting CO2 to methanol is crucial for addressing fuel scarcity and mitigating the greenhouse effect. Cu-based catalysts, with their diverse surface states, offer the potential to control reaction pathways and generate reactive H* species. However, a major challenge lies in oxidizing active Cu0 species by water generated during the catalytic process. While nonreducible metal oxides are beneficial in stabilizing metallic states, their limited capability to generate surface oxygen vacancies (OV) hinders CO2 activation. Herein, we present a strategy by doping Nd into a La2O2CO3 (LOC) support, enhancing OV formation by disrupting its lattice dyadicity. This leads to higher Cu0 concentration and improved CO2 activation. The resulting Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst notably outperforms Cu/LOC and CuZnAl catalysts, achieving a methanol yield of 9.9 moles of methanol per hour per mole of Cu. Our approach opens up possibilities for enhancing Cu-based catalysts in CO2 conversion.

INTRODUCTION

Methanol (MA), a promising liquid energy carrier, energy resource for direct methanol fuel cells, and indispensable platform for the synthesis of dimethyl ether, olefins, etc., has witnessed a surge in global demand, exceeding 107 million tons in 2023 (1, 2). Selective CO2 hydrogenation opens up a sustainable route to produce MA, holding immense significance in reducing our dependency on fossil fuels and mitigating the greenhouse effect (3). Among various options, Cu has emerged as a front-runner for CO2-to-MA conversion due to its diverse surface states, which enable the coexistence of Cu+ and Cu0 species (4–7). According to the prior literature, Cu+ greatly enhances the stability of the formate and methoxy, the key intermediates in CO2-to-MA conversion (8, 9). Meanwhile, Cu0 exhibits remarkable activity in H2 activation (10, 11). Up to now, CuZnAl stands as the only industrially viable catalyst for CO2-to-MA conversion (12). However, it is still facing pressing challenges about the stability of Cu0 species. During the catalytic process, water generated is easily adsorbed onto Cu0 sites, leading to undesired surface oxidation and particle aggregation (13–15). These phenomena rapidly deactivate the catalysts, posing a major hurdle in the efficient production of MA.

To tackle these challenges, a range of strategies have been developed, including modifying the Cu surface with functional groups, the creation of multimetal alloys, and the modulation of the coordinate structures of Cu (16–19). Despite the fact that great progress has been made, these advancements often come at the cost of reducing catalytic activity. Recently, the interactions between supports and metals garnered increasing attention as they offer a promising approach to enhancing the adjustability of metal centers (20, 21). Generally, reducible oxides exhibit stronger metal-support interactions, which are more likely to cause the ionization of the supported metals (22–24). In contrast, nonreducible oxides, which have weak metal-support interactions, are beneficial for stabilizing the metallic surface states (25, 26). However, they have limited capability to generate surface oxygen vacancies (OV), hampering the low-temperature activation of CO2 molecules (27). Therefore, finding an optimal balance between the metal-support interaction and OV creation remains a critical challenge in the pursuit of more efficient and stable catalysts for CO2-to-MA conversion.

In this study, we selected La2O2CO3 (LOC), a nonreducible, stable, and basic metal oxide, as the carrier to support active Cu species (28, 29). The introduction of Nd atoms into the Cu/LOC system induces a remarkable enhancement in overall efficiency. Specifically, the spatial and temporal yield (STY) of MA over the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst reaches 9.9 molMA/(hour·molCu) at 260°C and 3 MPa, nearly 1.4 times that of the Cu/LOC catalyst [7.2 molMA/(hour·molCu)]. Further mechanistic investigations reveal that the incorporation of Nd efficiently promotes the generation tendency of OV on LOC. The effect not only facilitates the adsorption of CO2 but also expedites the dissociation of H2 by increasing the Cu0 proportion.

RESULTS

Catalyst screening

Initially, rare earth atoms were doped into LOC supports through a modified solvothermal method previously reported (28). Subsequently, these doped supports were combined with Cu(NO3)2·3H2O to introduce Cu species. The x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the prepared samples reveal their hexagonal LOC structure, with no observable diffraction peaks corresponding to Cu and CuOx (fig. S1). This indicates the high dispersity of Cu species within the samples. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) indicates that the ratio of Ln (wt %)/(La + Ln) (wt %) is ~10%, and the actual Cu content is around 2 wt %, which closely aligns with the feeding amounts (table S1).

The catalytic performance was evaluated at 260°C, 3 MPa, weight hourly space velocity (WHSV): 12,000 ml gcat−1 hour−1, and gas composition: CO2/H2/Ar = 24/72/4 (v/v/v). The STY [molMA/(hour·molCu)] of MA follows such a sequence: Cu/LOC:Nd (4.39) > Cu/LOC:Dy (4.03) > Cu/LOC:Tm (3.86) > Cu/LOC:Lu (3.72) > Cu/LOC:Sm (3.64) > Cu/LOC:Eu (3.33) > Cu/LOC:Pr (3.12) > Cu/LOC:Gd (2.75) > Cu/LOC:Tb (2.70) > Cu/LOC (2.25) > Cu/LOC:Ho (2.24) > Cu/LOC:Ce (2.02) > Cu/LOC:Yb (1.93) > Cu/LOC:Er (1.78) (Fig. 1A and fig. S2). Except for Ho, Ce, Er, and Yb, other lanthanum atoms are instructive to the STY. The highest value appears at Cu/LOC:Nd, which is almost twofold than that of Cu/LOC. Afterward, the performance for the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst was evaluated under various conditions, ultimately achieving peak performance at a pressure of 3 MPa and a WHSV of 36,000 ml gcat−1 hour−1 (figs. S3 and S4). Figure 1B presents the curves of conversion and selectivity versus different temperatures by Cu/LOC:Nd and Cu/LOC catalysts. Compared with Cu/LOC, the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst shows a noteworthy rise in activity in the range from 220° to 300°C (fig. S5). The support influence was also considered. A series of catalysts, including Cu/CeO2, Cu/TiO2, Cu/m-ZrO2, Cu/ZnO, and CuZnAl catalysts, was applied instead of the LOC-based catalysts (figs. S6 to S9). None of them is comparable to the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst, indicating the great potential of the LOC:Nd support for MA production (Fig. 1C and table S2). Furthermore, we summarized the catalytic performance of the reported state-of-the-art Cu-based and rare earth–based catalysts; our Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst exhibits a competitive STY (fig. S10 and table S2). Figure 1D presents the long-term stability test of the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst; nearly 100% of the original MA selectivity and 87% of the original activity remained after 100 hours of continuous operation at 260°C, indicating its exceptional stability.

Fine structure characterizations

To gain a profound understanding of the promotion effect of Nd, we investigated the fine structure of the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images show that Cu/LOC:Nd presents a rod-like morphology, and the characteristic lattice distance is 0.353 nm, closely matching with the (100) plane of hexagonal LOC (Fig. 2A and fig. S11). The HAADF-STEM and energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping images reveal that Cu and Nd elements are dispersed homogeneously (Fig. 2, B and C, and fig. S12). The N2O titration results reveal that the Cu dispersions for the Cu/LOC:Nd and Cu/LOC catalysts are 40.2 and 39.7%, respectively (fig. S13 and table S3). The surface charge and coordinate structure of Cu species were initially studied by x-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) (30). The Cu average valence state of Cu/LOC:Nd is lower than that of Cu/LOC (fig. S14), which is consistent with the Cu 2p x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterizations (fig. S15) (31). To investigate the evolution of the Cu surface state during catalyzing, we also collected the quasi–in situ XAS spectra of the samples (fig. S16). The x-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra reveal that both the average valance states of Cu in Cu/LOC:Nd and Cu/LOC are between Cu(II) and Cu(0) (Fig. 2D) (32). Meanwhile, in the pre-edge region, the white line intensity of Cu/LOC:Nd is lower than that of Cu/LOC, indicating that the Cu average valence state of Cu/LOC:Nd is lower than that of Cu/LOC during the catalyzing. The extended x-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra simultaneously present obvious Cu-Cu coordination at 2.2 Å and Cu-O coordination at 1.5 Å on both Cu/LOC and Cu/LOC:Nd catalysts, which gives strong evidence for the coexistence of metallic and oxidized Cu species in both Cu/LOC:Nd and Cu/LOC catalysts (Fig. 2E). Meanwhile, the higher Cu-Cu coordination peak of Cu/LOC:Nd catalysts also validates its higher Cu0 ratio. We then used the quasi–in situ XPS spectra to explore the surface state of Cu species during catalyzing. With the data shown in Fig. 2F, the Cu L3VV Auger XPS spectra of Cu/LOC:Nd and Cu/LOC show two peaks with binding energies of 913.6 and 918.7 eV, corresponding to Cu+ and Cu0, respectively (33). Notably, the calculated ratio of Cu0/(Cu0 + Cu+) of Cu/LOC:Nd is 82.5%, considerably higher than that of Cu/LOC (67.7%), suggesting that the incorporation of Nd facilitates Cu0 stabilization. Furthermore, a remarkable diversity in the surface state of the O element was also observed (fig. S17). The asymmetrical O 1s spectra of both samples could be divided into two categories: OV centered at 531.5 eV and oxygen-containing contaminant CO32− at 533.0 eV (34). Clearly, Cu/LOC:Nd exhibits a higher OV concentration than that of Cu/LOC (68.2 versus 61.9%), implying that Nd doping is also beneficial to OV generation.

Reaction pathway identification

To figure out the reaction pathways, we carried out the temperature-programmed operando diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) of the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst at 3 MPa (Fig. 3A). At 30°C, a distinct band was observed at 1660 cm−1, which can be assigned to the antisymmetric OCO stretching [νas(OCO)] of bicarbonate peaks (HCO3*) (35, 36). The emergence of carbonaceous oxide at such low temperatures underscores the great potential of Cu/LOC:Nd for CO2 activation. As the temperature rose to 140°C, additional bands emerged at 1600, 1330, and 2850 cm−1, corresponding to the νas(OCO), symmetric OCO stretching [νs(OCO)], and CH stretching [ν(CH)] vibrations of HCOO* (formate), respectively. Upon further heating to 180°C, the peaks situated at 2930 and 1460 cm−1 appeared, which can be attributed to the ν(CH3) and ν(CO) of methoxy (H3CO*), respectively (37, 38). Figure 3B diagrams a plot of peak intensity versus temperature for the aforementioned intermediates. As the temperature rises, the peak intensity of HCO3* gradually decreases, concomitant with the appearance of the signals belonging to HCOO* (~140°C) and H3CO* (~180°C). Furthermore, to further corroborate the HCOO*-to-H3CO* conversion process, we conducted additional DRIFTs experiments by bubbling liquid formic acid as reported previously (fig. S18) (39). Besides HCOO*, the peaks of H3CO* were also detected, confirming the existence of a typical formate mechanism (CO2 → HCO3* → HCOO* → H3CO* → CH3OH) in our catalytic system (40). Notably, we also observed three additional peaks at 2129, 2094, and 2077 cm−1 associated with CO adsorption (COad) (Fig. 3C). For verifying whether an alternative CO hydrogenation pathway exists, we performed DRIFTS experiments under a CO + H2 sequence (Fig. 3D). The peaks of H3CO* (2932 and 2933 cm−1) were clearly observed after switching CO to H2, indicating the accessibility of the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction + CO hydrogenation pathway (41). Similar DRIFTS spectra were also observed for Cu/LOC, suggesting that Nd doping does not alter the fundamental reaction pathways (fig. S19).

Promotion effect investigations

To explore the impact of Nd doping on catalytic efficiency in the formate pathway, we operated transiently reduced experiments to simulate the transformation process of HCOO* over the Cu/LOC:Nd and Cu/LOC catalysts (fig. S20). The DRIFTS were collected at 260°C, initially using the reaction gas for 30 min, followed by a switch to a 5% H2/Ar mixed gas for an additional 110 min. For both catalysts, the peaks of HCOO* (1600, 2859, and 2971 cm−1) and H3CO* (2930 cm−1) are gradually intensified over time, ultimately reaching saturation within 30 min (22, 37). After transitioning to the H2/Ar mix, the peak intensity of HCOO* declines rapidly. Figure 4A presents a comparison of the normalized peak intensity of HCOO* versus time. During the first 30 min, the Cu/LOC catalyst exhibits a higher HCOO* generation rate. The sequential CO2 adsorption and H2 purging experiments also reveal that the net HCOO* generation rate of Cu/LOC is much higher than that of Cu/LOC:Nd (fig. S21). However, in the HCOO* consumption stage, the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst exhibits a notably faster rate. This consumption rate aligns closely with the overall catalytic performance (Fig. 1B), suggesting that the hydrogenation of HCOO* should be the rate-determining step, consistent with the previous reports (42, 43). Generally, the hydrogenation of HCOO* is heavily dependent on the catalysts’ ability to dissociate H2 molecules (44, 45). The Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst with a more metallic surface state typically exhibits superior H2 dissociation kinetics, which is evidenced by the H-D exchange and H spillover experiment (figs. S22 and S23) (44). Therefore, for the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst, the higher Cu0 concentration likely accounts for its superior catalytic efficiency within the formate pathway.

To further study how Nd doping affects catalytic efficiency in the RWGS + CO hydrogenation pathway, we investigated the adsorption behavior of CO by CO temperature-programmed desorption mass spectrometry (CO-TPD-MS) experiments (fig. S24). Both samples display comparable profiles, characterized by a prominent desorption peak at 110°C (46). Noteworthy, the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst exhibits a higher desorption amount of CO, suggesting its enhanced ability to adsorb CO. We also evaluated the activity of CO hydrogenation to MA (Fig. 4B). As expected, the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst exhibited a higher STY than Cu/LOC [5.35 versus 3.24 molMA/(hour·molCu)]. These results highlight the significance of Cu0 species on the catalytic activity in the RWGS + CO hydrogenation pathway.

In addition, we further carried out the CO2-TPD-MS characterization to study the influence of the surface state of LOC supports for CO2 activation (Fig. 4C). The peak at around 350° to 500°C can be assigned to the desorption of CO2 molecules on OV (47). The peak intensity of Cu/LOC:Nd is higher than that of Cu/LOC, indicating that the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst has higher CO2 adsorption capacity. Meanwhile, the adsorption configuration of CO2 was also studied. The infrared spectra of the adsorbed CO2 on Cu/LOC and Cu/LOC:Nd catalysts show four bands at around 1610 and 1300 cm−1, which can be assigned to the antisymmetric and symmetric OCO stretching vibrations [νas(OCO) and ν(OCO)] of bicarbonate species (HCO3*) (fig. S25). Notably, Cu/LOC:Nd exhibits a blue shift in the HCO3* peak compared to Cu/LOC, indicating its stronger interactions with the adsorbed HCO3* species. This enhanced interaction is conducive to catalytic efficiency, as demonstrated in a previous study (48).

The root of the higher Cu0 concentration of Cu/LOC:Nd was comprehensively investigated by density functional theory (DFT) simulations. Given that (110) faces are known to be more active in rod-like LOC, we constructed a (110) surface-exposed model for computational simulations (fig. S26). For the (110) surface-exposed LOC:Nd surface model, the surface La atom was replaced with a Nd atom. When removing the Nd-nearest O, the OV-containing (110) surface-exposed LOC:Nd surface model was established (Fig. 4D and figs. S27 and S28). As shown in Fig. 4E, the OV generation energy is decreased from 0.62 of LOC to 0.04 eV of LOC:Nd, underscoring the substantial enhancement in the possibility of OV formation through Nd doping, which is further verified by the corresponding O2-TPD characterizations (fig. S29) (49, 50). Therefore, the abundance of electron-rich OV can serve as the electron donors for stabilizing metallic Cu species.

For better illustration, we visualized the promotion effect of Nd doping in Fig. 5. The introduction of Nd heteroatoms enhances the generation of OV by efficiently disrupting the lattice dyadicity of pure LOC. Consequently, this augmentation triggers a notable boost in the number of OV and enhancement in the concentration of Cu0 (Fig. 5A). The overall reaction loop was also proposed (Fig. 5B), mainly involving the CO2 adsorption and hydrogenation of HCOO* and COad intermediates. For the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst, the more vital Cu0 sites and higher OV concentration boost the transformation of intermediates and facilitate CO2 adsorption, thus concurrently resulting in improving the STY.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we have demonstrated an effective doping strategy, which is accomplished by using Nd as a dopant to modify the Cu/LOC catalyst, effectively accelerating catalysis. This doping approach effectively enhances the OV generation tendency and thereby stabilizes the Cu0 sites, leading to a considerable acceleration in CO2 activation and H2 dissociation rates. The obtained Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst exhibits a drastic improvement in the STY of MA [9.9 molMA/(hour·molCu)], substantially surpassing both Cu/LOC [7.2 molMA/(hour·molCu)] and CuZnAl [3.3 molMA/(hour·molCu)] catalysts. This work underscores the practical application of heteroatom doping in nonreducible support-based catalytic systems; the enhanced concentration of defects on supports not only facilitates reactant activation but also optimizes the surface state of metals. We anticipate that this facile doping strategy can be widely applied to various catalyst designs, offering a promising avenue for the development of more efficient and sustainable catalytic systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthesis of Cu/LOC:X

Take the synthesis of Cu/LOC:Nd as an example. Typically, 250 mg of LOC:Nd was dispersed in 20 ml of deionized water under ultrasonication for 5 min. An appropriate amount of a CuN2O6·3H2O solution (871 μl with 0.1 M) was then added, followed by ultrasonication for an additional 10 min. The sample was then placed in an oil bath pot and kept at 70°C until all the water evaporated. The as-prepared sample was then reduced under 5% H2/Ar mixed gas in a tube furnace at 300°C for 2 hours to obtain the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst. The preparation method for the rest of the Cu/LOC:X samples is similar to the Cu/LOC:Nd catalyst, requiring only a change of support from LOC to LOC:X.

Catalyst characterization

The XRD patterns were collected on a Bruker D8 Advance, which is operated at 10 mA and 40 kV using Cu Kα (λ = 0.15406 nm) radiation. The scanning rate was 7° min−1 in the 2θ range of 10° to 80°. The ICP-OES was performed with a Varian Liberty 200 spectrophotometer to measure actual metal and lanthanide amounts of samples. The TEM was carried out using the FEI Tecnai G2 F20 field-emission transmission electron microscope and operated at 200 kV. High-resolution TEM and high-resolution STEM were performed on an atomic resolution analytical microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific Themis Z) and operated at 300 kV. The XPS measurements were carried out on an ESCALAB-MKII 250 photoelectron spectrometer (VG Co.) with Al Kα x-ray radiation as the x-ray source for excitation, and the binding energies were corrected by the C 1s peak of contaminant carbon. The quasi–in situ XPS measurements were carried out on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Escalab 250Xi with 1486.6-eV Al Kα. Before the quasi–in situ XPS data collection, all the samples were treated in the reaction gas at 260°C for 60 min and then transferred to the vacuum chamber for testing. The CO2-TPD-MS was carried out on a ChemStar TPx chemisorption analyzer with an online quadrupole mass spectrometer. In detail, the 50-mg sample was first prereduced in the reaction gas at 260°C for 30 min. After cooling to 50°C, the gas was switched to CO2 (30 ml min−1) and kept for 60 min. Last, the TPD process was performed with a ramp rate of about 5°C min−1 up to 800°C. The CO-TPD-MS was also carried out on a ChemStar TPx chemisorption analyzer with an online quadrupole mass spectrometer. In detail, the 100-mg sample was purged by the reaction gas (30 ml min−1) at 300°C for 30 min. Then, the sample was cooled by air cooling to 50°C. Subsequently, the sample was saturated with CO/He (10%, 30 ml min−1) at room temperature for 0.5 hours and then flushed with He (30 ml min−1) for 0.5 hours to remove all physically adsorbed CO molecules. Afterward, the experiment was started over the sample with the temperature increasing from 50° to 300°C at a speed of 5°C min−1 with a He flow (30 ml min−1). The O2-TPD was carried out on a ChemStar TPx chemisorption analyzer with a thermal conductivity detector. In detail, 50 mg of carriers was heated to 400°C with He (30 ml min−1) for 1 hour and then cooled to 50°C. The O2 (3.85% saturated with He, 30 ml min−1) was purged for 1 hour, and the excess O2 was purged with He purging for 30 min. Last, the TPD process was performed with a ramp rate of about 5°C min−1 up to 600°C. The in situ DRIFTS was performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iz10. Considering the evolution of Cu species during the reaction process, all samples were treated with the reaction gas at 260°C for 30 min before DRIFTS testing and the gas flow rates were all 30 ml min−1. XAS measurements at the Cu k-edge were performed in fluorescence mode at the BL11B of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. XAFS data were analyzed with the ATHENA code of Ravel. The EXAFS signals χ(k) were obtained after the removal of the pre-edge and post-edge background.

Catalytic activity testing

CO2 hydrogenation was carried out on a tubular fixed-bed reactor. Catalytic performance was tested using a 100-mg catalyst with a range of gas flow (5 to 60 ml min−1) of 72% H2, 24% CO2, and 4% Ar (v/v/v) to reach the WHSV from 3000 to 36,000 ml gcat−1 hour−1.
