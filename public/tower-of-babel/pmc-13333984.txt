Inference of latent epidemic regimes and generative simulations reveal how inequality and mobility shape COVID-19 transmission
Authors: Mauricio Herrera-Marín, Constanza Neira-Urrutia, Fernando Lagos-Alvarado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epidemic waves in large metropolitan areas unfold heterogeneously across territories shaped by persistent socioeconomic inequalities. Explaining how transmission intensifies, stabilises, and shifts across the urban landscape remains a central challenge in epidemiology. This study develops a covariate-dependent, non-homogeneous Hidden Markov Model (nHMM) to infer latent transmission regimes from municipality-level COVID-19 incidence in Santiago, Chile. The framework links daily case dynamics to mobility flows and structural socioeconomic indicators within a hierarchical specification that captures inter-municipal heterogeneity. Model selection identifies three statistically distinct and epidemiologically interpretable regimes corresponding to moderate, severe, and critical transmission phases. Transition dynamics reveal marked spatial asymmetries: while increases in mobility consistently elevate escalation risk, structural conditions—such as overcrowding and deficits in urban infrastructure—substantially influence both the probability of entering and the persistence within high-severity regimes. To ensure epidemiological interpretability, regime-conditioned incidence trajectories are mapped to the time-varying reproduction number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}) through a renewal formulation, enabling coherent propagation of uncertainty from latent-state inference to transmission estimates. By integrating latent regime inference with hierarchical transition modelling and renewal-based transmission analysis, this study distinguishes structural phase shifts from stochastic variability in urban epidemic dynamics. The findings clarify how mobility and entrenched inequality jointly structure transmission risk, providing a scalable and transferable framework for monitoring and anticipating epidemic regime transitions in complex metropolitan systems.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by SARS-CoV-2, has been one of the most consequential global public health crises of the twenty-first century, leaving a profound and uneven mark on health systems, economic activity, and social structures1,2. Following its initial identification in Wuhan, China, in December 20193, the outbreak rapidly evolved into a global emergency, leading the World Health Organisation to declare a pandemic in March 20204. While rapid vaccine development substantially altered the course of the crisis, the continued emergence of viral variants and successive epidemic waves have underscored the ongoing need for analytical frameworks capable of detecting, interpreting, and anticipating shifts in epidemic intensity5,6.

Urban epidemics unfold within complex social and infrastructural environments in which transmission is neither temporally stationary nor spatially uniform. Large metropolitan areas have shown pronounced asynchrony in epidemic growth, peak timing, and decline across neighbourhoods with differing socioeconomic profiles—even when governed by the same regional containment policies7,8. In Santiago, Chile, empirical studies demonstrated that structural inequality fundamentally shaped both incidence and mortality patterns; socioeconomically disadvantaged municipalities faced disproportionately severe outcomes despite strict mobility restrictions9–12. These findings indicate that mobility alone cannot fully explain epidemic dynamics; structural determinants also condition exposure, susceptibility, healthcare access, and the practical capacity to adhere to non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs). Evidence from health services, transport studies, and environmental epidemiology further supports this perspective by documenting how persistent inequalities influence healthcare-seeking behaviour13, contact opportunities14, environmental risk exposure15,16, and behavioural adaptation17,18. Together, these findings motivate modelling approaches that explicitly account for contextual heterogeneity.

Mechanistic epidemic models, such as spatial S(E)IR and metapopulation formulations, remain indispensable for quantifying transmission processes and evaluating interventions19–21. Their parameters provide biological interpretability and support counterfactual analysis when calibrated to mortality and mobility data10. Nevertheless, such models often rely on structural assumptions regarding transmission flows and tend to treat heterogeneity parametrically rather than as an inferential target. Conversely, data-driven forecasting and machine learning approaches enhance short-term predictive performance22–26, yet frequently remain agnostic to the mechanisms governing regime escalation and decline. Fractional-order epidemic formulations introduce memory and non-local effects27–29, but generally focus on transmission persistence rather than identifying discrete epidemic phases or quantifying the drivers of transitions between them.

When the objective is to detect and characterise discrete regimes of epidemic intensity—phases that persist, recur, or regress heterogeneously across space—and to understand how mobility and structural inequality shape those transitions, a complementary statistical framework is required. Hidden Markov models (HMMs) provide a principled probabilistic structure for separating observation processes from latent state dynamics30–35. This paper develops a covariate-dependent non-homogeneous HMM (nHMM)36,37 in which transition probabilities are modelled as functions of time-varying covariates through multinomial logit links. By incorporating internal and external mobility indicators alongside structural socioeconomic measures directly into the transition mechanism, the model allows these factors to alter the odds of regime escalation or regression. To capture inter-municipal heterogeneity, transition logits are embedded within a mixed-effects specification that estimates municipality-level random intercepts and slopes, yielding interpretable odds ratios and quantifying systematic deviations from metropolitan averages.

To ensure epidemiological interpretability, these latent regimes are connected to standard transmission metrics through the renewal framework, which links expected incidence to the time-varying reproduction number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}):1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {E}[I_t \mid R_t] = R_t \Lambda _t, \quad \Lambda _t = \sum _{s \ge 1} I_{t-s} w_s, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_t$$\end{document} denotes daily incidence, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_s$$\end{document} the discretised serial interval (SI) distribution, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Lambda _t$$\end{document} total infectiousness38–40. Both instantaneous (Cori) and retrospective (Wallinga–Teunis) estimators are employed. Embedding latent regimes within this framework enables transmission dynamics to be interpreted in standard epidemiological terms while propagating uncertainty from regime inference directly into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimates.

This study pursues four objectives. First, it infers municipality-specific latent epidemic regimes using a covariate-dependent mixed-effects nHMM, quantifying how transition probabilities respond to mobility and structural determinants. Second, it constructs regime-conditioned generative reconstructions of incidence trajectories using two complementary engines designed to capture dependence structure, tail behaviour, and temporal variability. Third, it maps observed and simulated trajectories through the renewal identity to estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}, thereby assessing epidemiological coherence and uncertainty calibration. Fourth, it compares municipalities with respect to regime persistence, transition propensities, and threshold crossings of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}, elucidating how structural inequality and mobility jointly shape escalation and recovery.

The methodological contribution lies in integrating (i) covariate-dependent mixed-effects regime inference; (ii) regime-conditioned generative reconstruction; and (iii) explicit renewal-based epidemiological mapping within a unified inferential pipeline. Unlike prior studies in Santiago that primarily documented inequality gradients9–12,41, this work models the latent phase process through which structural determinants operate and quantifies their influence on regime transitions.

By combining probabilistic regime inference, hierarchical modelling, generative simulation, and renewal-based transmission estimation, this framework advances a phase-aware perspective that complements mechanistic epidemic models. It is transparent, reproducible, and readily generalisable to other urban systems characterised by spatial and socioeconomic heterogeneity.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows: the Results section presents regime identification, transition dynamics, generative reconstructions, and renewal-based transmission comparisons; the Methods section details data sources, model specification, and estimation procedures. Sensitivity analyses and further technical details are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Full-curve reconstructions with a heteroscedastic, regime-conditioned CVAE

To reconstruct complete epidemic trajectories for each municipality, this paper employs a conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE) with an LSTM encoder and a heteroscedastic decoder43–49.

Training objective and calibration

On day t, the input sequence comprises the previous \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L=7$$\end{document} scaled feature vectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}_{t-L+1:t}\in \mathbb {R}^{3}$$\end{document}: (i) daily cases (7-day moving average), (ii) internal mobility, and (iii) external mobility. Conditioning variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{c}_t$$\end{document} concatenate a one-hot code for the municipality and a one-hot code for the nHMM regime at time t. The encoder defines \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_\phi (\textbf{z}_t\mid \textbf{x}_{t-L+1:t},\textbf{c}_t)=\mathcal {N}(\boldsymbol{\mu }_t^z,\textrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma }_t^{z\,2}))$$\end{document}. The decoder outputs a mean vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{\mu }_t^{x}\in \mathbb {R}^{3}$$\end{document} for all features and a dedicated log-variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log \sigma _{t,\text {case}}^{2}$$\end{document} for the case dimension, enabling state- and context-dependent dispersion. Latent variables are drawn using the reparameterisation trick, and cases are simulated via\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{x}_{t,\text {case}} \sim \mathcal {N}\!\big (\mu _{t,\text {case}},\, \sigma _{t,\text {case}}^{2}\big ),$$\end{document}while mobility components are set to their observed values at time t to preserve exogenous consistency. The model is trained (on a pre-tail slice to avoid leakage) by minimising\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {L} \;=\; \underbrace{\textrm{NLL}_{\mathcal {N}}(x_{t,\text {case}}\mid \mu _{t,\text {case}},\sigma _{t,\text {case}}^{2})}_{\text {heteroscedastic case likelihood}} \;+\; \alpha \,\Vert \boldsymbol{\mu }_t^{x}-\textbf{x}_t\Vert _2^2 \;+\; \beta \,\textrm{KL}\!\big (q_\phi (\textbf{z}_t\mid \cdot )\,\Vert \,\mathcal {N}(\textbf{0},\textbf{I})\big ),$$\end{document}with small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} (feature-reconstruction aid) and moderate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} (latent regularisation) to balance sharpness and coverage. After checkpoint selection, a short fine-tuning pass calibrates the variance head. This combination proved important to avoid under-dispersed intervals at low incidence and over-dispersed intervals near peaks. Regime conditioning and piecewise behaviour

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_t\in \{1,\ldots ,K\}$$\end{document} denote the nHMM regime at day t, and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{c}_t=[\textrm{onehot}(\text {municipality}),\,\textrm{onehot}(s_t)]$$\end{document}. Because \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_t$$\end{document} is piecewise constant over contiguous intervals, trajectories are state-consistent within each regime, the conditional mean is governed by a fixed decoder gate while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_t$$\end{document} continues to evolve with new observations and mobility. Accordingly, the apparent “piecewise” morphology reflects regime-specific dynamics rather than step functions; when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_t\ne s_{t-1}$$\end{document}, abrupt changes in level and trend are permitted, as expected at intervention boundaries or behavioural shifts.

(i) State-specific elasticities: because mobility enters the input window, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\partial \mu _{t,\text {case}}/\partial \textrm{IM}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\partial \mu _{t,\text {case}}/\partial \textrm{EM}$$\end{document} can be probed by small, regime-held perturbations of mobility within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}_{t-L+1:t}$$\end{document}. Uncertainty quantification and metrics

For each time point, the ensemble is summarised by the 50% central band \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[Q_{0.25},Q_{0.75}]$$\end{document}, the 90% band \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[Q_{0.05},Q_{0.95}]$$\end{document}, the median \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{0.5}$$\end{document}, and the mean. Reported metrics include: (i) average width (AW) of the bands; (ii) empirical coverage (POC), defined as the fraction of observations within the band; (iii) average asymmetry degree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{AAD}=\big ((Q_{0.95}-Q_{0.5})-(Q_{0.5}-Q_{0.05})\big )/(Q_{0.95}-Q_{0.05})$$\end{document} (and its absolute value); (iv) the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS); and (v) median-based MAE and RMSE. Across municipalities, 90% coverage is close to nominal (typically \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 0.97$$\end{document}–1.00), indicating well-calibrated dispersion, including in low-incidence periods; 50% coverage typically lies around 0.65–0.75, consistent with sharper central bands. Interval widths scale with incidence: high-throughput municipalities (e.g., La Pintana, Renca) exhibit larger AW90 (often 35–42 cases) than low-incidence municipalities (e.g., Vitacura, Las Condes, often 10–16). Central-tendency errors remain small relative to peak magnitudes (pooled MAE \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 4$$\end{document} cases; RMSE \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 9$$\end{document}), and CRPS improves where empirical coverage aligns closely with nominal rates, consistent with coherent probabilistic fits rather than overconfident means.

Municipality heterogeneity captured by conditioning

Municipality effects enter through the one-hot code in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{c}_t$$\end{document}, while mobility affects both the autoregressive input and the regime process. This dual pathway allows the decoder to learn municipality-specific baselines and differential sensitivity to mobility changes. In Fig. 5, La Florida and Santiago show wider bands around growth and decline fronts. In contrast, Vitacura and Las Condes show narrower central bands and near-symmetric AAD, consistent with lower and steadier incidence. The heteroscedastic variance head inflates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{t,\text {case}}$$\end{document} around rapid transitions, producing wider yet calibrated intervals at surge onset and regime switches.

Because regime information gates the decoder, within-regime credible intervals typically contract and AAD approaches zero, whereas around regime boundaries intervals widen and may transiently skew during explosive growth or rapid decline. These features support operational interpretation: wide, mildly right-skewed bands indicate elevated upward risk near inflection points; narrow, symmetric bands indicate stable control. The municipality-conditioned structure ensures that these signals are not averaged across heterogeneous populations.

Overall, the regime-conditioned heteroscedastic CVAE provides (i) calibrated uncertainty (near-nominal 90% coverage), (ii) incidence-adaptive widths without excessive peak smoothing, (iii) limited systematic asymmetry outside genuine transitions, and (iv) low central-tendency error. These properties hold across municipalities with markedly different epidemiological profiles, indicating that combining LSTM context, nHMM regime conditioning, and an explicit variance head suffices to reconstruct full epidemic curves with epidemiologically meaningful uncertainty. Wallinga–Teunis (WT)

WT reconstructs the case reproduction number by probabilistic infector assignment, with weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i\rightarrow j}^{(c)} \propto w_{i-j}$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i>j$$\end{document}, normalised over all potential infectors of case j. The reproduction number for onsets at time t is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{(c)}_{t,\textrm{WT}}=\sum _{j>t} p^{(c)}_{j\rightarrow t}$$\end{document}, with right-censoring correction near the series end achieved by dividing by the surviving SI mass \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1-\sum _{s=1}^{T-t} w_s$$\end{document}. WT is retrospective and therefore exhibits a characteristic phase lead during growth and lag during decline; agreement with Cori provides an informative robustness check.

Across six representative municipalities (La Florida, Cerrillos, Vitacura, Providencia, Las Condes, and Santiago), the Cori median computed from CVAE trajectories closely tracks the Cori median computed from observed data and lies within the latter’s 90% credible interval for most days. Full-curve reconstructions with a regime-conditioned Copula–GPD generator

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_t^{(c)}=(Y_t^{(c)},M^{(c)}_{t,\textrm{int}},M^{(c)}_{t,\textrm{ext}})$$\end{document} denote daily incidence (7-day moving average) and internal/external mobility for municipality c on day t. Conditional on the nHMM regime \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_t^{(c)}\in \{1,\ldots ,K\}$$\end{document}, the joint distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_t^{(c)}$$\end{document} is represented using a state-specific Sklar factorisation,5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{S}(x_1,x_2,x_3)\;=\;C_S\!\Big (F_{1,S}(x_1),F_{2,S}(x_2),F_{3,S}(x_3);\theta _S\Big ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_S$$\end{document} is a t-copula with unstructured correlation and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu _S$$\end{document} degrees of freedom, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{j,S}$$\end{document} are state-specific marginal distributions. For the incidence marginal, a body–tail construction is adopted: with a state-specific threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_S$$\end{document} (90th percentile),6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{Y,S}(y)\;=\; {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \widehat{F}_{\textrm{body},S}(y), & y\le u_S,\\ 1-\big (1-\widehat{F}_{\textrm{body},S}(u_S)\big )\,\big (1+\xi _S\frac{y-u_S}{\beta _S}\big )^{-1/\xi _S}_{+}, & y>u_S, \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the bulk is modelled using the empirical CDF and the tail using a Generalised Pareto Distribution (GPD) with parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\xi _S,\beta _S)$$\end{document} estimated via peaks over threshold. Within each state, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(C_S,\theta _S)$$\end{document} are estimated by maximum likelihood using pseudo-observations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{U}_t=\big (F_{1,S}(x_{1t}),F_{2,S}(x_{2t}),F_{3,S}(x_{3t})\big )$$\end{document}. Daily simulations are generated conditionally independently across t given the state path. For each day: (i) draw \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{U}_t\sim C_{S_t^{(c)}}(\theta _{S_t^{(c)}})$$\end{document}; (ii) invert the marginals, using the GPD for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_t^{(c)}$$\end{document} whenever \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{t,1}>\widehat{F}_{\textrm{body},S_t}(u_{S_t})$$\end{document}; and (iii) record \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_t^{(c)}$$\end{document} (mobility is sampled latently to preserve cross-sectional dependence during generation). Concatenating daily draws along the observed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_t^{(c)}$$\end{document} sequence yields a full synthetic curve for each municipality.

CVAE versus Copula–GPD: complementarity under the renewal mapping

Both simulators are conditioned on the nHMM regime process and municipality identity and generate ensembles of daily incidence trajectories \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{\tilde{I}_t^{(c,m)}\}$$\end{document}. Each ensemble is mapped to the time-varying reproduction number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t^{(c)}$$\end{document} through the renewal formulation, and summarised using Cori’s estimator38; WT estimates are provided as a robustness check39. The unified \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} panels for the CVAE (Fig. 6) and Copula–GPD (Fig. 8) show close agreement between renewal-based medians and those computed from observed incidence, and consistent timing of threshold crossings with WT.

Calibration versus sharpness. Across municipalities, the CVAE yields 50% predictive intervals with near-nominal empirical coverage (pooled \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{COV}_{50}\approx 0.68$$\end{document}) and widths that appropriately expand and contract with regime volatility. In contrast, the Copula–GPD produces substantially tighter 50% intervals but with systematic under-coverage (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{COV}_{50}\approx 0.40$$\end{document}–0.51 in the six-municipality panel). This pattern is consistent with the sharpness–calibration trade-off formalised by strictly proper scoring rules51,52: the Copula-based generator is sharper and performs well in low-variance settings, but can become overconfident during surge periods, while the CVAE provides better-calibrated uncertainty at the cost of moderately wider intervals, particularly around regime transitions.

Temporal structure and piecewise appearance. The CVAE uses an LSTM encoder and a heteroscedastic decoder, yielding locally smooth temporal dynamics with regime-dependent variance. By contrast, the Copula–GPD model is static conditional on the regime: within each state, it samples from a state-specific multivariate distribution, preserving cross-sectional dependence while introducing limited serial structure beyond the exogenous regime path. After renewal mapping, both simulators produce epidemiologically standard \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} trajectories: CVAE medians are smoother, whereas Copula medians may show small-amplitude oscillations; in both cases, the growth/decline signal relative to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t=1$$\end{document} remains coherent.

Extremes and peak representation. The Copula component represents the upper tail explicitly via peaks over threshold and a GPD, with severity-weighted fitting to emphasise high-incidence regimes53. The CVAE captures peak variability through its heteroscedastic variance head and tempered latent sampling, without specifying explicit thresholds. In the reported metrics, Copula bands are narrower (smaller AW50) but can under-cover; CVAE bands widen around transitions and remain close to symmetric unless the data support pronounced skew.

Practical use. In practice, the two approaches are complementary: the CVAE is advantageous when calibrated uncertainty and short-horizon temporal coherence are central (nowcasting, monitoring, and regime-change detection), whereas the Copula–GPD generator is advantageous when explicit tail behaviour and dependence with covariates are central (stress testing and peak-focused scenario design). A principled ensemble approach (e.g., stacking based on a proper score such as CRPS) can combine these strengths. Methods

Data

Mobility data were publicly released by the Instituto de Data Science (IDS) and Telefónica Movistar54. The mobility indicators are derived from passive signalling recorded by the cellular network infrastructure in the Metropolitan Region (MR) of Santiago. The infrastructure comprises georeferenced Base Transceiver Stations (BTS) with non-overlapping coverage polygons55. Each time a device switches its serving BTS, the event is recorded and interpreted here as a proxy for a trip. According to Chile’s telecommunications regulator (SUBTEL), there were 25,731,160 mobile subscriptions in Chile during the study period, with Telefónica Movistar accounting for approximately 28%56. The dataset contains anonymised traces from approximately 1.4 million users. Following54, two municipality–day indices were used in this study: (i) an Internal Mobility Index, defined as BTS-to-BTS transitions within the same municipality, normalised by the number of resident devices; and (ii) an External Mobility Index, defined as entries plus exits across municipality boundaries, again normalised by resident devices. Both indices are interpreted as behavioural proxies of compliance with, and response to, non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs)57.

Socioeconomic covariates were assembled from the 2017 National Census58 and the 2012 Territorial Well-being Index59. Variables included per-capita income (block-level values aggregated to municipalities), overcrowding, completed higher education, housing precariousness, average household size, and the proportion of households with immigrants. In addition, the 2019 Urban Quality of Life Index (ICVU 2019)42 was incorporated. The ICVU comprises six dimensions (Employment Conditions; Business Climate; Sociocultural Factors; Connectivity and Mobility; Health and Ecology; and Housing and Urban Space). All socioeconomic covariates were standardised (mean 0, standard deviation 1) prior to modelling.

Daily COVID-19 incidence time series were computed as reported cases per 100,000 inhabitants at the municipality level using official surveillance data. To attenuate high-frequency reporting noise and day-of-week effects, figures in the main text display a 7-day moving average, whereas the nHMM was fitted to the corresponding daily series; sensitivity analyses considered both smoothed and unsmoothed variants. Missing days (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<1\%$$\end{document}) were imputed by linear interpolation when isolated (gaps \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le 2$$\end{document} days) and otherwise left missing for models that can accommodate missingness. In particular, forward–backward smoothing in the HMM can accommodate missing emissions by marginalisation; in practice, missing days were handled consistently with the software implementation (Supplementary Information).

Only aggregated, de-identified indices were used throughout. No individual trajectories were accessed, and no re-identification attempts were made. All analyses were conducted at the municipality–day level.

Non-homogeneous Hidden Markov model (nHMM)

Each municipality’s incidence series was modelled with a time-inhomogeneous hidden Markov model with K discrete latent states and covariate-dependent transitions. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_t^{(c)}$$\end{document} denote the daily case rate per 100,000 in municipality c on day t, and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_t^{(c)} \in \{1,\dots ,K\}$$\end{document} denote the latent regime. The joint distribution factorises as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\!\left( Y_{1:n}^{(c)}, S_{1:n}^{(c)}\right) = P\!\left( S_1^{(c)}\right) \prod _{t=2}^{n}P\!\left( S_t^{(c)} \mid S_{t-1}^{(c)}, X_t^{(c)}\right) \prod _{t=1}^{n} P\!\left( Y_t^{(c)} \mid S_t^{(c)}\right) ,$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_t^{(c)}$$\end{document} denotes the covariate vector (mobility and structural indices).

Covariate-dependent transitions

Non-homogeneity is introduced via multinomial logistic links. Conditional on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{t-1}^{(c)}=j$$\end{document} and covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_t^{(c)} \in \mathbb {R}^p$$\end{document},\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\!\left( S_t^{(c)}=i \mid S_{t-1}^{(c)}=j, X_t^{(c)}=x\right) = \frac{\exp \!\left( \alpha _{j,i} + \beta _{j,i}^{\top }x\right) }{\sum _{k=1}^{K}\exp \!\left( \alpha _{j,k} + \beta _{j,k}^{\top }x\right) }.$$\end{document}Both contemporaneous covariates and distributed lags (0–7 days) were considered for internal and external mobility. The lag specification was selected by BIC within each transition block after standardising covariates. To mitigate label switching and to preserve interpretability across fits and restarts, an ordering constraint on state means was enforced (see below).

Emission distributions

The primary specification assumes Gaussian emissions:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_t^{(c)} \mid S_t^{(c)}=k \sim \mathcal {N}(\mu _k,\sigma _k^2).$$\end{document}Although Gaussian emissions are supported on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {R}$$\end{document}, they provide an accurate approximation at the fitted scales; positivity was additionally assessed via log-Gaussian emissions (to enforce strictly positive support). Sensitivity analyses also explored Laplace emissions (to increase robustness to outliers and heavier tails). Qualitative conclusions were unchanged across emission families (Supplementary Information).

Estimation and decoding

Parameters were estimated via the EM algorithm using depmixS437. For each K, we ran 50 random initialisations and retained the solution with the best BIC. To limit label switching across restarts, we imposed the ordering \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _1< \mu _2 < \mu _3\;(\le \mu _4 \le \mu _5)$$\end{document} during EM by relabelling states at each iteration according to the ordered state means. Convergence was declared when the relative change in log-likelihood was below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-6}$$\end{document} or after 1,000 iterations. Posterior state probabilities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _t^{(c)}=\Pr (S_t^{(c)}=k\mid Y_{1:n}^{(c)})$$\end{document} were obtained using forward–backward smoothing, and most probable state sequences were obtained via Viterbi decoding.

Model order selection and state-assignment uncertainty

Models with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K \in \{2,\dots ,8\}$$\end{document} were fitted and compared using AIC, BIC, and log-likelihood. Although AIC decreased monotonically with K, BIC typically favoured \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K=3$$\end{document}–5. The main analyses adopt \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K=3$$\end{document} for epidemiological clarity, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K=4$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K=5$$\end{document} reported as robustness checks (Tables S1–S2). State-assignment uncertainty was summarised by the maximum posterior probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max _k \gamma _t^{(c)}$$\end{document} (state confidence) and by the posterior entropy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-\sum _k \gamma _t^{(c)}\log \gamma _t^{(c)}$$\end{document} (Fig. S1).

Goodness-of-fit and diagnostics

Posterior predictive checks (PPCs) were conducted by simulating 1,000 trajectories per municipality from the fitted nHMM and comparing distributional summaries (mean, variance, upper deciles) and turning-point counts to the observed series. State-conditioned residuals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_t^{(c)} - \mathbb {E}[Y_t^{(c)}\mid S_t^{(c)}]$$\end{document} were examined for autocorrelation (Ljung–Box) and approximate normality. Emission choices passed standard diagnostic checks.

Diagnostics and implementation details

Thresholds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{c,s}$$\end{document} were selected using quantile rules and checked using mean residual life and parameter-stability plots; copula adequacy was assessed using PIT uniformity and Kendall’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} concordance diagnostics53,60. The Student–t copula was selected for its symmetric tail dependence (finite \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu$$\end{document}), which is appropriate for co-movement between large incidence values and mobility deviations. The simulator is state-static (no internal autoregressive component), so serial coherence arises from the external nHMM regime path; this design isolates regime-specific cross-sectional dependence and tail risk while retaining temporal phase information via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{S_t\}$$\end{document}.

This severity-weighted, state-conditioned Copula–GPD framework preserves the empirical marginal structure of mobility, provides an EVT-consistent incidence tail, and reproduces regime-specific dependence among variables. Ensembles are subsequently mapped through the renewal equation to obtain reproduction numbers and uncertainty bands consistent with transmission dynamics (see Sec. 2.7).

Random effects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{b}_{ij}^{(c)}$$\end{document} include municipality-specific random intercepts and random mobility slopes; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon _{ij}^{(c)}(t)$$\end{document} denotes residual variability on the logit scale. All analyses were implemented in R (nHMM and GLMM components) and Python/PyTorch (CVAE–LSTM). Reproducible scripts and data-processing pipelines are available in the repository referenced in the Additional information section.

Discussion

This study develops a regime-based, covariate-dependent framework that integrates three core components: (i) a mixed-effects non-homogeneous hidden Markov model (nHMM) for latent phase detection and transition attribution; (ii) a severity-aware Copula–GPD generator to capture cross-variable dependence and tail behaviour; and (iii) a heteroscedastic CVAE–LSTM for short-horizon temporal structure and calibrated variability. When applied to municipality-level COVID-19 data in a socioeconomically stratified metropolitan area, the framework demonstrates that complex epidemic trajectories can be parsimoniously represented as transitions between a small number of latent transmission regimes. These shifts between escalation and decline are not merely random fluctuations, but are systematically associated with and structured by mobility patterns and structural inequalities.

A central contribution of this framework is the formal integration of latent regime inference with renewal-based epidemiological interpretation. Under the renewal identity,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {E}[I_t \mid R_t] = R_t \sum _{s \ge 1} I_{t-s} w_s,$$\end{document}posterior-predictive incidence trajectories can be mapped directly to the time-varying reproduction number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}) using both the Cori and Wallinga–Teunis estimators38,39. This construction propagates uncertainty from latent-state estimation and generative reconstruction through the nonlinear renewal operator, yielding transmission summaries that are uncertainty-calibrated by design. The resulting linkage preserves epidemiological interpretability in standard \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} terms while accommodating substantial spatial and temporal heterogeneity in observed dynamics.

Why regimes when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} can be estimated directly?

Renewal estimators recover transmission intensity from incidence given a serial-interval kernel, yet they are generally agnostic to phase structure and to the drivers underlying shifts in transmission. The present framework complements renewal-based monitoring in four key respects.

First, the nHMM formalises phase detection and dwell-time structure. It simplifies noisy trajectories into recurrent, data-driven regimes with clearly interpretable severity levels, revealing duration asymmetries—such as prolonged residence in high-severity regimes—that may be obscured in raw \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} curves63. Second, it enables attribution: covariate-dependent mixed-effects transition logits quantify how internal and external mobility, along with structural indicators, shape the odds of escalation or regression, while partial pooling stabilises municipality-specific estimates even under limited data. Third, it provides early-warning signals: increases in the posterior probability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Pr (S_{t+1}>S_t)$$\end{document} under observed covariates may precede visible peaks in incidence, offering valuable lead time. Fourth, it supports generative inference: regime-conditioned reconstructions produce ensembles that can be propagated through the renewal identity, yielding distributional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} trajectories rather than single-path estimates. In this context, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} measures the velocity of transmission, whereas regimes and their transitions clarify the underlying phase structure and its covariate-dependent modulation.

Interpreting regimes through the renewal lens

The inferred regimes exhibit consistent renewal-based semantics. Low-severity regimes typically concentrate below the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t=1$$\end{document} threshold with negative growth, intermediate regimes cluster near criticality, and high-severity regimes align with sustained supercritical transmission39,40. Because the renewal mapping is applied to posterior-predictive trajectories, uncertainty in latent states, covariate effects, and tail behaviour is consistently reflected in the resulting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} uncertainty bands. This integration produces municipality-specific, uncertainty-calibrated summaries of time spent above and below control thresholds, allowing comparisons across heterogeneous contexts without conflating persistent structural vulnerability with transient observation noise.

Relation to mechanistic and endemic–epidemic models

Mechanistic models encode biological processes and remain essential for counterfactual policy analysis. The present approach is intentionally phenomenological, yet statistically principled and complementary.Regime transitions can inform time-varying parameters in mechanistic formulations, provide duration-aware constraints via dwell-time estimates, and supply state-conditioned ensembles for testing intervention triggers. Conversely, mechanistic priors may help regularise regime discovery when data are limited. A promising synthesis involves regime-switching renewal or endemic–epidemic specifications64,65, in which infectiousness \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Lambda _t$$\end{document} is retained while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} evolves on a covariate-dependent Markov backbone with mixed effects. Such integration would combine biologically grounded timing with phase-aware covariate attribution.

Implications for practice

Three outputs have direct operational relevance. First, escalation probabilities provide early indicators for surge preparedness and adaptive communication strategies. Second, regression odds quantify the barriers to exiting high-severity regimes; their association with housing precariousness, overcrowding, and urban quality highlights municipalities where mobility reductions may yield comparatively smaller gains—an equity-relevant finding. Third, regime-conditioned scenario envelopes generate credible incidence and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} bands, enabling probabilistic trigger evaluation rather than reliance on volatile point estimates.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

The strengths of this approach include explicit modelling of covariate dependence with hierarchical partial pooling, epidemiological grounding via renewal-based \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document} estimation, and tail-aware generative reconstruction that preserves cross-variable dependence. Several limitations suggest avenues for refinement rather than undermining the conclusions. The first-order Markov assumption may be extended to hidden semi-Markov or dwell-time models66,67. Mobility indices serve as behavioural proxies; incorporating origin–destination networks could further refine modelling of inter-municipality transmission pathways68. Renewal-based summaries inherit assumptions regarding the serial-interval distribution, which may vary due to behavioural change or variant replacement69. Future work could explore regime-specific or hierarchical estimation of serial-interval distributions70, as well as fully Bayesian hierarchical formulations of the nHMM71,72 and the integration of causal mediation frameworks to assess behavioural and structural pathways formally73,74.

Conclusion

Epidemic dynamics in large urban systems are not driven solely by short-term behavioural fluctuations. Still, they are structured by phase dynamics shaped jointly by mobility patterns and entrenched socioeconomic conditions. Framing transmission as covariate-dependent switching among latent regimes provides a coherent statistical representation of these dynamics and complements traditional renewal-based monitoring centred on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t$$\end{document}.

The findings indicate that increases in mobility elevate escalation risk, yet structural inequalities substantially condition both the probability of entering and the persistence within high-severity regimes. Municipalities characterised by overcrowding, housing precariousness, and deficits in urban quality exhibit greater difficulty exiting severe phases, even under comparable reductions in mobility. These results indicate that behavioural interventions and structural vulnerability interact to shape transmission trajectories rather than operate independently.

Methodologically, this study establishes a unified inferential pipeline linking surveillance data to interpretable, uncertainty-calibrated risk metrics. By distinguishing persistent structural vulnerability from transient stochastic variation, the framework enables more precise attribution of escalation dynamics and more informed evaluation of policy thresholds. Beyond the specific case examined here, the approach is transferable to other pathogens and metropolitan contexts characterised by spatial and socioeconomic heterogeneity. As urban populations continue to face recurrent epidemic threats, phase-aware statistical frameworks that integrate behavioural signals, structural determinants, and epidemiological metrics will remain essential for anticipatory and equity-sensitive public health governance. By formalising epidemic phase dynamics within a statistically identifiable and covariate-responsive structure, the present framework contributes a generalisable methodology for analysing transmission variability in complex urban systems.
