Bone-Modifying Agents in Metastatic Castration-Resistant Prostate Cancer
Authors: Zeynep Irem Ozay, Yeonjung Jo, Georges Gebrael, Micah Ostrowski, Vinay Mathew Thomas, Haoran Li, Ryon P. Graf, Soumyajit Roy, Benjamin L. Maughan, Avirup Guha, Irbaz Bin Riaz, Emmanuel S. Antonarakis, Rana R. McKay, Neeraj Agarwal, Umang Swami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study examines patterns of bone-modifying agent use from 2013 to 2024 among men with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer.

Introduction

Bone metastases are common in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), affecting more than 90% of patients, and are associated with a substantial risk of skeletal-related events (SREs), with up to one-third of patients with prostate cancer and bone metastases experiencing such events.1,2 Androgen deprivation therapy accelerates bone loss, compounding SRE risk.3 Bone-modifying agents (BMAs) denosumab and zoledronic acid reduce or delay SREs in mCRPC with bone metastases; however, prior population-based studies have shown underuse,4,5,6 and contemporary data remain limited. Herein, we evaluated contemporary patterns in BMA use among patients with mCRPC.

Methods

Patient-level data were extracted from the Flatiron Health Research Database, which contains deidentified data from approximately 280 US cancer clinics, curated through technology-enabled abstraction.7 The University of Utah Institutional Review Board deemed this cohort study exempt from ethics review and informed consent requirement because deidentified data were used. We followed the STROBE reporting guideline.

Patients diagnosed with mCRPC (data cutoff date: May 30, 2024) were included; patients with mCRPC diagnosis 30 days before data cutoff and those missing a diagnosis or BMA administration or order date were excluded (eFigure in Supplement 1). The primary outcome was evidence of BMA receipt after mCRPC diagnosis; use before diagnosis was excluded. Patients were categorized into 2 groups: with vs without BMA receipt. The index was mCRPC diagnosis date, and baseline characteristics were measured before this date. Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous values and Pearson χ2 test for categorical values were used to assess statistical differences between groups.

The proportion of patients with mCRPC receiving BMAs was calculated annually using all patients diagnosed with mCRPC in that year as the denominator. BMA use was further categorized by agent type and assessed over time. Among BMA recipients, time from mCRPC diagnosis to initiation was summarized as median (IQR). Two-sided P <.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R 4.5.1 (R Core Team).

Results

Overall, 14 076 patients with mCRPC (median [IQR] age, 75 [68-81] years) were included, 83.1% of whom were treated in community practice. Among these patients, 7969 (56.6%) had evidence of BMA receipt and 6107 (43.4%) did not. Differences in baseline characteristics between cohorts were observed (Table). Patients with BMA receipt were more likely to be treated in community practices (6959 [87%] vs 4737 [78%) and have commercial insurance (6368 [80%] vs 4653 [76%]).

Discussion

Randomized trials have shown that BMAs reduce SREs in mCRPC treated with novel therapies and combinations. In a post hoc analysis of the phase 3 ERA-223 trial, BMA use was associated with fewer fractures compared with no BMA use (abiraterone plus radium-223: 15% vs 37%; abiraterone plus placebo: 7% vs 15%).8 In the EORTC 1333/PEACE-3 phase 3 trial, after mandated BMA use, 1-year fracture rates decreased compared with no BMA use (enzalutamide plus radium-223: 37.1% to 2.7%; enzalutamide monotherapy: 15.6% to 2.6%).9

Clinical data show this gap has persisted for more than a decade. One study (2007-2015) revealed that up to one-third of patients with mCRPC did not receive BMAs.5 Similarly, in a VA cohort (2010-2017) of more than 3900 patients with mCRPC, only 47% received BMAs.6

To our knowledge, this study is one of the largest contemporary analyses evaluating BMA use in mCRPC. Clinical guidelines, including the 2020 American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines, recommend denosumab or zoledronic acid for men with mCRPC with bone metastases.10 In our cohort, 43.4% of patients had no BMA receipt, and BMA use was higher in the mid-study years than later years overall, highlighting a gap between recent recommendations and clinical practice. Differences in baseline characteristics suggest structural contributors to BMA use variation. This pattern may reflect implementation barriers, including competing treatment priorities, toxic effect concerns, uncertainty regarding duration, and variation in supportive care delivery. Given the high morbidity and mortality associated with SREs, efforts are urgently needed to increase bone health awareness and facilitate timely BMA initiation.

Study limitations include potential residual confounding, selection bias, and misclassification due to technology-enabled abstraction. Missing BMA order or administration data may have resulted in underestimation of use. As this was a descriptive study, differences between groups may reflect underlying patient, clinician, and practice-level factors. Data on bone mineral density testing and bone metastases were unavailable, and BMA use outside Flatiron’s network could not be captured.
