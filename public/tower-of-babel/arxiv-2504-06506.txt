Some Remarks on the Product Formula for Defect Numbers of Closed Operators
Christoph Fischbacher, Fritz Gesztesy, Lance L. Littlejohn

Abstract
. This largely pedagogical note recalls some facts on defect numbers of products of closed operators employing results from the theory of semi-Fredholm operators and then applies these facts to positive integer powers of symmetric operators and subsequently to certain minimal Sturm–Liouville and minimal higher even-order ordinary differential operators. We also point out some unexpected missed opportunities when comparing the work of different groups on this subject.

This primarily pedagogical note originated with the following explicit question: “What are the deficiency indices of positive integer powers of of the minimal Legendre operator in
L^{2}((-1,1))

In 1950 (Engl. translation in 1953) I. M. Glazman
[
40

determined the deficiency indices of products of densely defined and closed linear operators
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
in a complex separable Hilbert space assuming finite defect numbers of
T_{1},T_{2}

\defe(T_{j},0)=\dim(\ker(T_{j}^{*}))<\infty,\quad j=1,2,

(1.1)

and
0

T_{1},T_{2}
, that is, there exists constants
c_{T_{j}}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\|T_{j}f\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}\geqslant c_{T_{j}}\|f\|_{{\mathcal{H}}},\quad f\in\dom(T_{j}),\;j=1,2.

(1.2)

Under these hypotheses, Glazman proved that

\defe(T_{2}T_{1},0)=\defe(T_{1},0)+\defe(T_{2},0).

(1.3)

In addition, Glazman proved the result that if
S
is symmetric with with finite deficiency indices

n_{\pm}(S)=\dim(\ker(S^{*}\mp iI_{{\mathcal{H}}}))\in{\mathbb{N}},

(1.4)

then

n_{\pm}\big(S^{2}\big)=n_{+}(S)+n_{-}(S)

(1.5)

(These results extend of course to higher positive integer powers of

[

R. M. Kauffman, T. Read, and A. Zettl, in their 1977 lecture notes
[
54

on the deficiency indices of powers of ordinary differential expressions are citing Glazman’s 1950 paper, but not his 1965 book.

At the same time, H. Behncke and H. Focke also derived (
1.5

In addition, Behncke and Focke also deal with the case of possibly infinite deficiency indices. Interestingly, Behncke and Focke do not cite Glazman and, being essentially simultaneous with Kauffman, Read, and Zettl, neither group appears to have been aware of the activities of the other.

Next, we recall the notion of Fredholm and left semi-Fredholm operators and their index:

Suppose

integral power of each of these expressions is explicitly known in Lagrangian symmetric form. We indicate what the deficiency indices are for the corresponding minimal operators generated by each these powers.

(i)
The Laguerre expression in
L^{2}\big((0,\infty);x^{\alpha}e^{-x}dx\big)
:

For
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)
, the Laguerre differential expression is given by

\tau_{\alpha,Lag}=-x^{-\alpha}e^{x}\frac{d}{dx}x^{\alpha+1}e^{-x}\frac{d}{dx},\quad x\in(0,\infty).

(3.70)

For this expression,
\tau_{\alpha,Lag}
is in the limit circle case at the singular endpoint
x=0
in
L^{2}\big((0,\infty);x^{\alpha}e^{-x}dx\big)
when
\alpha\in(-1,1)
and in the limit point case when
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
. At
x=\infty
,
\tau_{\alpha,Lag}
is in the limit point case for all
\alpha\in(-1,\infty)
. Consequently, the minimal operator
T_{\alpha,Lag,min}
has deficiency indices given by

n_{\pm}(T_{\alpha,Lag,min})=\begin{cases}1,&\alpha\in(-1,1),\\
0,&\alpha\in[1,\infty).\end{cases}

(3.71)

Details can be found in
[
37
]
,
[
39
, Example 13.7.4.]
. By Theorem
2.6
, the deficiency indices of
(T_{\alpha,Lag,min})^{m}
are give by

n_{\pm}\big((T_{\alpha,Lag,min})^{m}\big)=\begin{cases}m,&\alpha\in(-1,1),\\
0,&\alpha\in[1,\infty),\end{cases}\quad m\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(3.72)

The form of the minimal operator
(T_{\alpha,Lag,min})^{m}
is given by

(T_{\alpha,Lag,\min})^{m}f=x^{-\alpha}e^{x}\tau_{Lag}^{m}f=x^{-\alpha}e^{x}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j}S_{m}^{(j)}\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}x^{\alpha+1}e^{-x}\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}f,

(3.73)

for
f\in\dom\big((T_{\alpha,Lag,min})^{m}\big)
, where
S_{m}^{(j)}
is the classical Stirling number of the second kind defined by

S_{m}^{(j)}=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\frac{(-1)^{i+j}}{j!}\binom{j}{i}i^{m}.

(3.74)

For more details, see
[
60
, Section 12]
.

(ii)
The Hermite expression in
L^{2}\big((-\infty,\infty);e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)
:

The Hermite differential expression is given by

\tau_{Her}=-e^{x^{2}}\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x^{2}}\frac{d}{dx},\quad x\in(-\infty,\infty).

(3.75)

For this expression,
\tau_{Her}
is in the limit point case at both singular endpoints
x=\pm\infty
in
L^{2}\big((-\infty,\infty);e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)
. Hence the minimal operator
T_{Her,min}
has deficiency indices
(0,0)
and thus coincides with the corresponding maximal operator
T_{Her,max}
; in particular, the operator
T_{Her,min}=T_{Her,max}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}\big((-\infty,\infty);e^{-x^{2}}dx\big)
. Consequently, by Theorem
2.6
, the deficiency indices of
(T_{Her,min})^{m}
are also given by

n_{\pm}\big((T_{Her,min})^{m}\big)=0,\quad m\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(3.76)

In this case, the form of the minimal operator
(T_{Her,min})^{m}
is given by

(T_{Her,min})^{m}f=e^{x^{2}}\tau_{Her}^{m}f=e^{x^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j}S_{m}^{(j)}2^{n-j}\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}e^{-x^{2}}\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}f,

(3.77)

for
f\in\rm{dom}
(T_{Her,min})^{m}
, where
S_{m}^{(j)}
is the classical Stirling number of the second kind as defined above. For full details, see
[
30
]
.

(iii)
The Jacobi expression in
L^{2}\big((-1,1);(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}dx\big)
:

For
\alpha,\beta\in(-1,\infty)
, the Jacobi differential expression is defined by

\tau_{\alpha,\beta,Jac}=-(1-x)^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\frac{d}{dx}(1-x)^{\alpha+1}(1+x)^{\beta+1}\frac{d}{dx},\quad x\in(-1,1).

(3.78)

Of course, the Legendre expression is a special case of the Jacobi expression when
\alpha=\beta=0
. We refer the reader to
[
28
]
,
[
38
]
, and
[
39
, Example 13.5.4]
for full details of the analytic study of
\tau_{\alpha,\beta,Jac}
. The singular endpoints
x=\pm 1
satisfy the following limit point/limit circle criteria:

(a)

the endpoint
x=1
is limit point if
\alpha\in[1,\infty)
; if

\alpha\in(-1,0)
,
x=1
is regular, and if
\alpha\in[0,1)
,
x=1
is limit circle,
nonoscillatory.

(b)

the endpoint
x=-1
is limit point if
\beta\in[1,\infty)
; if

\beta\in(-1,0)
,
x=-1
is regular, and if
\beta\in[0,1)
,
x=-1
is limit circle, nonoscillary.

From this, we see that the deficiency indices of the minimal operator
T_{\alpha,\beta,Jac,min}

are given by

n_{\pm}(T_{\alpha,\beta,Jac,min})=\begin{cases}0,&\alpha,\beta\in[1,\infty),\\
1,&\text{$\alpha\in[1,\infty)$ and $\beta\in(-1,1)$}\\
&\text{or $\beta\in[1,\infty)$ and $\alpha\in(-1,1)$,}\\
2,&\alpha,\beta\in(-1,1).\end{cases}

(3.79)

Consequently, by Theorem
2.6
, the deficiency indices of
(T_{\alpha,\beta,Jac,min})^{m}
are given by

n_{\pm}\big((T_{\alpha,\beta,Jac,min})^{m}\big)=\begin{cases}0,&\alpha,\beta\in[1,\infty),\\
m,&\text{$\alpha\in[1,\infty)$ and $\beta\in(-1,1)$}\\
&\text{or $\beta\in[1,\infty)$ and $\alpha\in(-1,1)$,}\\
2m,&\alpha,\beta\in(-1,1),\end{cases}\quad m\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(3.80)

Moreover, the form of the minimal operator
(T_{\alpha,\beta,Jac,min})^{m}=(1-x)^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\tau_{Jac}^{m}
is explicitly given by

\displaystyle(T_{\alpha,\beta,Jac,min})^{m}f

(3.81)

\displaystyle\quad=(1-x)^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j}P^{(\alpha,\beta)}S_{m}^{(j)}\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}(1-x)^{\alpha+j}(1+x)^{\beta+j}\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}f,

for
f\in\rm{dom}
(T_{\alpha,\beta,Jac,min})^{m}
, where
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}S_{m}^{(j)}
is the Jacobi-Stirling number defined by

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}S_{n}^{(j)}=\sum_{r=1}^{j}(-1)^{r+j}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+r+2)(\alpha+\beta+2r+1)(r^{2}+(\alpha+\beta)r+r)^{n-1}}{(r-1)!(j-r)!\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+j+r+2)}.

(3.82)

\ddagger

Next, we turn to some illustrations of Theorems
2.8
and
2.9
in the PDE context.

3.3.
Homogeneous perturbations of the Laplacian on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

In this subsection we transition to some PDE applications in connection with (minus) the Laplacian
-\Delta_{n}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and some of its (homogeneous) perturbations of the form
c|x|^{-2}
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
n\geqslant 2
, and some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. This requires some preparations to which we turn next.

Consider the Bessel operator on
(0,\infty)
generated by the differential expression

\tau_{\gamma}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{\gamma^{2}-(1/4)}{x^{2}},\quad\gamma\in[0,\infty),\;x\in(0,\infty).

(3.83)

The associated maximal and minimal operators
T_{\gamma,max}
and
T_{\gamma,min}
in
L^{2}((0,\infty);dx)
associated with
\tau_{\gamma}
are then given by (see, e,g.,
[
39
, Ch. 13]
)

\displaystyle T_{\gamma,max}f=\tau_{\gamma}f,\quad\gamma\in[0,\infty),

\displaystyle f\in\dom(T_{\gamma,max})=\big\{g\in L^{2}((0,\infty);dx)\,\big|\,g,g^{\prime}\in AC_{loc}((0,\infty))

(3.84)

\displaystyle\hskip 179.25244pt\tau_{\gamma}g\in L^{2}((0,\infty);dx)\big\}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&T_{\gamma,min}f=\tau_{\gamma}f,\quad\gamma\in[0,1),\\
&f\in\dom(T_{\gamma,min})=\big\{g\in\dom(T_{\gamma,max})\,\big|\,\widetilde{g}(0)=\widetilde{g}^{\,\prime}(0)=0\big\},\end{split}

(3.85)

\displaystyle T_{\gamma,min}=T_{\gamma,max},\quad\gamma\in[1,\infty),

(3.86)

where the generalized boundary values
\widetilde{g}(0)
and
\widetilde{g}^{\,\prime}(0)
are of the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{g}(0)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\lim_{x\downarrow 0}g(x)\big/\big[x^{1/2}\text{\rm ln}(1/x)\big],&\gamma=0,\\
\lim_{x\downarrow 0}g(x)\big/\big[(2\gamma)^{-1}x^{(1/2)-\gamma}\big],&\gamma\in(0,1),\end{cases}

(3.87)

\displaystyle\widetilde{g}^{\,\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\big[g(x)-\widetilde{g}(0)x^{1/2}\text{\rm ln}(1/x)\big]\big/x^{1/2},&\gamma=0,\\
\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\big[g(x)-\widetilde{g}(0)(2\gamma)^{-1}x^{(1/2)-\gamma}\big]\big/x^{(1/2)+\gamma},&\gamma\in(0,1).\end{cases}

(3.88)

Then

\displaystyle T_{\gamma,max}^{*}=T_{\gamma,min},\quad T_{\gamma,min}^{*}=T_{\gamma,max},\quad\gamma\in[0,1),

(3.89)

\displaystyle T_{\gamma,max}=T_{\gamma,max}^{*}=T_{\gamma,min}^{*}=T_{\gamma,min},\quad\gamma\in[1,\infty).

(3.90)

In particular,
\tau_{\gamma}
is in the limit circle case at
x=0
for
\gamma\in[0,1)
and in the limit point case at
x=0
for
\gamma\in[1,\infty)
; in addition,
\tau_{\gamma}
is in the limit point case at
x=\infty
for
\gamma\in[0,\infty)
. Consequently, taking into account that
\big[\gamma^{2}-(1/4)\big]x^{-2}
,
x\in(0,\infty)
, is real-valued, one obtains (cf. (
2.71
))

n_{\pm}(T_{\gamma,min})=\begin{cases}1,&\gamma\in[0,1),\\
0,&\gamma\in[1,\infty),\end{cases}\quad n_{\pm}\big((T_{\gamma,min})^{m}\big)=\begin{cases}m,&\gamma\in[0,1),\\
0,&\gamma\in[1,\infty),\end{cases}\quad m\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(3.91)

In addition we introduce the following family of Bessel differential expressions

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\tau_{n,\ell,L,\alpha}=-\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{\ell(\ell+n-2)-L(L+n-2)}{r^{2}},\\
&n\in{\mathbb{N}},\,n\geqslant 2,\;\ell,L\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\;\alpha\in[-1/4,3/4),\;r\in(0,\infty),\end{split}

(3.92)

such that once again

\displaystyle n_{\pm}(T_{n,\ell,L,\alpha,min})=\begin{cases}1,&0\leqslant\ell\leqslant L,\\
0,&\ell\in[L+1,\infty),\end{cases}

(3.93)

\displaystyle n_{\pm}\big((T_{n,\ell,L,\alpha,min})^{m}\big)=\begin{cases}m,&0\leqslant\ell\leqslant L,\\
0,&\ell\in[L+1,\infty),\end{cases}\quad m\in{\mathbb{N}},

(3.94)

\displaystyle n\in{\mathbb{N}},\,n\geqslant 2,\;\ell,L\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\;\alpha\in[-1/4,3/4).

Here, in obvious notation,
T_{n,\ell,L,\alpha,min}
denotes the minimal operator associated with
\tau_{n,\ell,L,\alpha}
in
L^{2}((0,\infty);dr)
, for the parameter ranges

n\in{\mathbb{N}},\,n\geqslant 2,\;\ell,L\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\;\alpha\in[-1/4,3/4),

(3.95)

which we keep throughout the rest of Subsection
3.3
.

Next, we pivot to the PDE context and consider the pre-minimal operator

\bigg(-\Delta_{n}-\frac{[(n-1)(n-3)/4]+L(L+n-2)}{|x|^{2}}\bigg)\bigg|_{C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash\{0\})}

(3.96)

in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n};d^{n}x)
. Here
-\Delta_{n}
represents (minus) the Laplacian differential expression in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, which, in spherical coordinates
(r,\omega)
,
r\in(0,\infty)
,
\omega\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
, amounts to

-\Delta_{n}=\bigg(-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}-\frac{n-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{B}\bigg),

(3.97)

where
\Delta_{B}
abbreviates the Laplace-Beltrami differential expression on the
(n-1)
-dimensional unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(cf., e.g.,
[
39
, Sect. 15.5]
for details).

As a final ingredient we introduce the unitary map

U_{n}\colon\begin{cases}L^{2}((0,\infty);dr)\to L^{2}\big((0,\infty);r^{n-1}dr\big),\\
f\mapsto M_{(\,\cdot\,)^{-(n-1)/2}}f,\end{cases}

(3.98)

where
M_{\psi}
denotes the maximally defined operator of multiplication in the space
L^{2}((0,\infty);dr)
by the (Lebesgue) measurable function
\psi
, that is,

(M_{\psi}f)(r)=\psi(r)f(r)\,\text{ for a.e.~$r\in(0,\infty)$},\;f\in L^{2}((0,\infty);dr).

(3.99)

One then infers that (see, e.g.,
[
66
, p. 160–161]
)

\displaystyle T_{n,L,\alpha,min}

\displaystyle=\overline{\bigg(-\Delta_{n}-\frac{[(n-1)(n-3)/4]+L(L+n-2)}{|x|^{2}}\bigg)\bigg|_{C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\backslash\{0\})}}

(3.100)

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}U_{n}T_{n,\ell,L,\alpha,min}U_{n}^{-1},

(3.101)

where, again in obvious notation,
T_{n,L,\alpha,min}
is the minimal operator associated with the partial differential expression employed in (
3.96
).

Exploiting (
3.94
) (and noting
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}=\{0\}\cup{\mathbb{N}}
) one infers that

n_{\pm}(T_{n,L,\alpha,min})=n_{\pm}\Bigg(\bigoplus_{\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}T_{n,\ell,L,\alpha,min}\Bigg)=L+1,

(3.102)

implying

n_{\pm}\big((T_{n,L,\alpha,min})^{m}\big)=m(L+1),\quad m\in{\mathbb{N}},

(3.103)

again by an application of Theorems
2.8
and
2.9
(resp., (
2.71
)).

3.4.
On singularly perturbed Dirichlet Laplacians

In this subsection we discuss an applicaton of the abstract results in
[
34
]
to singularly perturbed Dirichlet Laplacians.

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an open bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. We will employ the usual
(n-1)
-dimensional surface measure
d\omega^{n-1}
on
\partial\Omega
and introduce the Dirichlet and Neumann traces on the boundary
\partial\Omega
following Grubb
[
44
]
(see also the summary in
[
6
, Example 3.5]
) as follows: Consider

\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{\gamma}_{k}\colon\begin{cases}C^{\infty}(\Omega)\to C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega),\\
u\mapsto\big(\partial_{n}^{k}\big)\big|_{\partial\Omega},\end{cases}\quad k=0,1,

(3.104)

with
\partial_{n}
denoting the interior normal derivative. By continuity,
\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{\gamma}_{k}
extends to a bounded operator,

\gamma_{k}\colon H^{s}(\Omega)\to H^{s-k-(1/2)}(\Omega),\quad s\in(k+(1/2),\infty),

(3.105)

such that the map

\gamma^{(1)}=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\colon H^{s}(\Omega)\to H^{s-(1/2)}(\Omega)\times H^{s-(3/2)}(\Omega),\quad s\in(3/2,\infty),

(3.106)

satisfies

\ker\big(\gamma^{(1)}\big)=H^{s}_{0}(\Omega),\quad\ran\big(\gamma^{(1)}\big)=H^{s-(1/2)}(\Omega)\times H^{s-(3/2)}(\Omega),\quad s\in(3/2,\infty).

(3.107)

In the following we employ the more suggestive notation

\gamma_{D}=\gamma_{0},\quad\gamma_{N}=\gamma_{1}

(3.108)

for the Dirichlet and Neumann trace operators.

Introducing the minimal Laplace operator in
L^{2}(\Omega)
by

\displaystyle T_{\Omega,min}f=-\Delta f,

(3.109)

\displaystyle f\in\dom(T_{\Omega,min})=\big\{g\in H^{2}(\Omega)\,\big|\,\gamma_{D}g=\gamma_{N}g=0\big\},

(3.110)

the corresponding maximal operator
T_{\Omega,max}
is given by

\displaystyle T_{\Omega,max}f=-\Delta f,

(3.111)

\displaystyle f\in\dom(T_{\Omega,max})=\big\{g\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,\big|\,\Delta g\in L^{2}(\Omega)\big\},

(3.112)

and one infers

T_{\Omega,min}^{*}=T_{\Omega,max},\quad T_{\Omega,max}^{*}=T_{\Omega,min}.

(3.113)

Since the open set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
was assumed to be bounded,
T_{\Omega,min}
is a strictly positive symmetric operator in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, whose Friedrichs extension is given by the self-adjoint Dirichlet realization
T_{\Omega,D}
of the Laplacian,

\displaystyle T_{\Omega,D}f=-\Delta f,

(3.114)

\displaystyle f\in\dom(T_{\Omega,D})=\big\{g\in H^{2}(\Omega)\,\big|\,\gamma_{D}g=0\big\},

(3.115)

In
[
34
, Example 6.1]
, the following restrictions of
T_{\Omega,D}
are constructed: Fix

h\in L^{2}(\Omega),\quad k\in C(\partial\Omega)\backslash\{0\},

(3.116)

and define

\displaystyle\begin{split}&T_{\Omega,h,k}f=-\Delta f,\\
&f\in\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k})=\{g\in\dom(T_{\Omega,D}))\,|\,(h,g)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=(k,\gamma_{N}g)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\}.\end{split}

(3.117)

Then
T_{\Omega,h,k}
is closed in
L^{2}(\Omega)
by
[
34
, Theorem 2.11]
.
For convenience, and without loss of generality, we employ the normalization

\|k\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=1

(3.118)

from now on (after all, one can absorb any normalizing factor for
k
in the function
h
). Moreover, we note that

T_{\Omega,min}\subseteq T_{\Omega,h,k}\,\text{ if and only if $h=0$.}

(3.119)

In addition, if one temporarily only assumes that
k\in C(\partial\Omega)
, it was shown in
[
34
, Example 6.1]
that

T_{\Omega,h,k}
in (
3.117
) is densely defined if and only if
k\neq 0
,

(3.120)

explaining our choice of hypothesis on
k
in (
3.116
).

One observes that
T_{\Omega,h,k}
as a restriction of
T_{\Omega,D}
is also bounded from below with a strictly positive lower bound.

The adjoint
T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
is then given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*}f=-\Delta f+(k,\gamma_{D}f)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}h,\\
&f\in\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*})=\big\{g\in H^{2}(\Omega)\,\big|\,\gamma_{D}g=(k,\gamma_{D}g)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}k\big\}.\end{split}

(3.121)

Next, let
\eta_{k}
be the unique solution of the Cauchy problem

\Delta\eta_{k}=0,\quad\gamma_{D}\eta_{k}=k,\,\text{ implying }\,\eta_{k}\in\ker(T_{max}).

(3.122)

From results in
[
34
, Example 6.1]
, it follows that

\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*})=\dom(T_{\Omega,D})\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{+}{\rm lin.span}\{\eta_{k}\},

(3.123)

and hence,

\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*})\subseteq\dom(T_{\Omega,max}),

(3.124)

and

T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*}\subseteq T_{\Omega,max}\,\text{ if and only if $h=0$.}

(3.125)

Finally, we note that the deficiency indices and subspaces are given by

n_{\pm}(T_{\Omega,h,k})=1,\quad\ker(T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*}\mp iI)={\rm lin.span}\{(T_{\Omega,D}\mp iI)^{-1}(h\mp i\eta_{k})-\eta_{k}\}.

(3.126)

Using von Neumann’s theory of self-adjoint extensions of a closed, symmetric operator, the following description of all self-adjoint extensions of
T_{\Omega,h,k}
(by (
3.126
) necessarily a real one-parameter family of self-adjoint extensions) was obtained in
[
34
, Example 6.1]
,

\displaystyle T_{\Omega,h,k,\alpha}f=T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*}f,\quad\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle f\in\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k,\alpha})=\{g\in\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*})\,|\,(k,\gamma_{N}g)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}-(h,f)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(3.127)

\displaystyle\hskip 213.39566pt=\alpha(k,\gamma_{D}g)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega}\},

\displaystyle T_{\Omega,h,k,\infty}=T_{\Omega,D},\quad\alpha=\infty.

(3.128)

Next, we determine the Friedrichs and Krein–von Neumann extensions of
T_{\Omega,h,k}
and also all nonnegative self-adjoint extensions of
T_{\Omega,h,k}
.

We start with the the Krein–von Neumann extension
T_{\Omega,h,k,K}
of
T_{\Omega,h,k}
. Since
T_{\Omega,h,k}
is strictly positive, the domain
\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k,K})
is given by

\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k,K})=\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k})\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{+}\ker(T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*}),

(3.129)

and hence it remains to determine the one-dimensional kernel of
T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*}
. From a straightforward calculation, it follows that

\displaystyle\begin{split}T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*}\big((T_{\Omega,D})^{-1}h-\eta_{k}\big)&=-\Delta\big((T_{\Omega,D})^{-1}h-\eta_{k}\big)+\big(h,\gamma_{D}\big((T_{\Omega,D})^{-1}h-k\big)\big)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega}h\\
&=\big[1-\|k\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}\big]h=0.\end{split}

(3.130)

Thus, the operator
T_{\Omega,h,k,K}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&T_{\Omega,h,k,K}f=T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*}f,\\
&f\in\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k,K})=\dom(T_{\Omega,h,k})\overset{\textbf{\Large.}}{+}{\rm lin.span}\big\{(T_{\Omega,D})^{-1}h-\eta_{k}\big\}.\end{split}

(3.131)

Next, we turn to the Friedrichs extension
T_{\Omega,h,k,F}
of
T_{\Omega,h,k}
. In this context we will employ the following general result in the theory of nonnegative self-adjoint extensions of a given closed, strictly positive, symmetric operator
S
in the complex, separable Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
to the following effect:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{If $\widetilde{S}$ is a nonnegative self-adjoint extension of $S$, then}\\
&\quad\text{$\widetilde{S}=S_{F}$ if and only if $\dom\Big(\big(\widetilde{S}\big)^{1/2}\Big)\cap\ker(S^{*})=\{0\}$.}\end{split}

(3.132)

An application of (
3.132
) will show that
T_{\Omega,h,k,F}=T_{\Omega,D}
as follows:
First, one observes that by construction,
T_{\Omega,D}
is a strictly positive self-adjoint extension of
T_{\Omega,h,k}
, with form domain given by
\dom\big((T_{\Omega,D})^{1/2}\big)=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. Next, since
\ker(T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*})={\rm lin.span}\big\{(T_{\Omega,D})^{-1}h-\eta_{k}\big\}
and

\gamma_{D}\big((T_{\Omega,D})^{-1}h-\eta_{k}\big)=-k\neq 0
, one concludes that
(T_{\Omega,D})^{-1}h-\eta_{k}\notin H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Consequently,

\dom\big((T_{\Omega,D})^{1/2}\big)\cap\ker(T_{\Omega,h,k}^{*})=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap{\rm lin.span}\big\{(T_{\Omega,D})^{-1}h-\eta_{k}\big\}=\{0\},

(3.133)

implying
T_{\Omega,D}=T_{\Omega,h,k,F}
, independently of
h\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in C(\partial\Omega)
.

We conclude these observations by next determining all nonnegative self-adjoint extensions of
T_{\Omega,h,k}
. The largest is, of course, the Friedrichs extension
