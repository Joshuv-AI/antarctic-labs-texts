Nationwide whole-genome sequencing reveals local transmission of Mycobacterium tuberculosis in Poland, 2003–2020
Authors: Alina Minias, Lidia Fiedorowicz, Monika Kozińska, Anna Zabost, Jakub Lach, Marcin Słomka, Dominik Strapagiel, Jarosław Dziadek, Ewa Augustynowicz-Kopeć
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains highly prevalent in many countries and is a significant contributor to infectious disease mortality. Combating TB requires monitoring the global circulation of strains, and analyzing the prevalence of specific strains within populations. Currently, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) enables the precise tracking of TB transmission. Here, we utilized spoligotyping combined with whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to identify cases of M. tuberculosis transmission in Poland. Our study aimed to identify M. tuberculosis clones transmitting in Poland before the beginning of the war in Ukraine and the influx of refugees.MethodsWe used spoligotyping data to identify M. tuberculosis isolates that were possibly transmitted. We used WGS to confirm transmission, and to identify transmission chains.ResultsA total of 163 isolates were successfully sequenced with high-quality genomic data. Two significant clusters were identified: a pan-susceptible Euro-American lineage cluster (WGS-Cluster 3, n = 32, 19.6%) associated with patients experiencing homelessness and centered in Bydgoszcz (2006–2014); and a multidrug-resistant Beijing lineage cluster (WGS-Cluster 13, n = 32, 19.6%) emerging between 2016 and 2020 across five cities.ConclusionM. tuberculosis transmission should be monitored with routine WGS for better TB control in Poland.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12438-5.

Background

Mycobacterium tuberculosis is the causative agent of tuberculosis (TB). The World Health Organization (WHO) reported that TB is the 13th leading cause of death overall, the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent worldwide, after being temporarily surpassed by COVID-19 during the pandemic. An estimated 10 million people fall ill with TB each year [1].

Poland is currently a low-incidence country, with an incidence rate of 10 per 100,000 in 2022. The incidence is slightly higher than that in neighboring Germany (5 per 100 000), Czechia (4 per 100 000), Slovakia (3 per 100 000), and lower than that in the Eastern Bloc countries, Belarus (28 per 100 000), Ukraine (99 per 100 000), and Lithuania (30 per 100 000), based on data presented by the WHO [1].

The Polish National Reference Laboratory for Mycobacteria at the Institute of Tuberculosis and Lung Diseases in Warsaw conducted a cyclic four-year study according to the WHO protocol, the last of which was published in 2022 [2]. In the last study, the laboratory performed molecular analysis of approximately 20% of the drug-susceptible isolates and all of the drug-resistant strains to determine the frequency of specific molecular patterns of the shared international type (SIT) spoligotype. These cyclic studies highlight the current epidemiological situation of TB in Poland, including the identification of possible TB transmission.

The definition of a TB outbreak is currently not standardized and may vary depending on the incidence of TB in the region. According to previous definitions of TB outbreaks in the literature, an outbreak involves at least three cases of infection with evidence of serial transmission or a cluster consisting of at least three strains [3–6]. Historically, TB transmission has been monitored with spoligotyping [7], but currently, it has been replaced with whole-genome sequencing (WGS), which is a more reliable method of determining recent transmission. Several factors are thought to contribute to the occurrence of TB transmission host factors (e.g., sex, immune status, and comorbidities [1]), environmental factors (e.g., humidity and temperature [8]), socioeconomic [1], and bacterial factors [9–11]. The weight of each factor in global TB transmission is currently elusive.

Here, we utilized spoligotyping combined with WGS to identify cases of M. tuberculosis transmission in Poland. Our study aimed to identify M. tuberculosis clones transmitting in Poland before the beginning of the war in Ukraine and the influx of refugees.

Materials and methods

M. tuberculosis strains

The strains were identified in the collection of the National Reference Laboratory of Tuberculosis in Poland, located at the Institute of Tuberculosis and Lung Diseases (Table S1). The isolates were isolated from 183 unique patients between 2003 and 2020.

From 2003 to 2020, there were approximately 57,500 bacteriologically confirmed cases of TB. Spoligotyping was performed for 1,785 of the MDR and randomly chosen strains, which constituted 2%. A total of 45.8% of the strains (n = 817) were clustered by spoligotyping. The spoligotypes of all the isolates were determined using a standard protocol [7]. The spoligotype lineage was assigned with the TBLineage tool [12], and family determination was performed with SITVIT2. Conventional drug susceptibility testing (DST) was performed on a liquid medium using a Bactec MGIT 960 system (Becton Dickinson Diagnostic Systems, Sparks, MD, USA), following the WHO recommendations [13]. The M. tuberculosis H37Rv reference strain was used as a quality control.

For whole-genome sequencing, we prioritized M. tuberculosis isolates that were most likely to represent recent transmission events. Selection was based on three criteria: (i) identical spoligotype patterns, (ii) identical city of residence, and (iii) the presence of at least three putative epidemiologically linked cases. This targeted approach was chosen to maximize the likelihood of capturing local transmission clusters within Poland, thereby providing insight into community-level spread and the identification of potential outbreak strains.

DNA isolation and sequencing

Aliquots of M. tuberculosis frozen cultures were thawed and cultured on Löwenstein–Jensen medium until visible growth occurred. DNA was isolated as previously described [14]. All procedures were conducted in a biological safety level 3 laboratory under WHO protocol recommendations. The sequencing libraries were constructed following the manufacturer’s instructions with a Nextera XT DNA library preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) and a TruePrep DNA Library Prep Kit V2 for Illumina (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Sequencing was performed using the Illumina NextSeq 500 or NovaSeq 6000 platform. The sequences were deposited in the SRA database (National Library of Medicine, National Center for Biotechnology Information), under the PRJNA1216520 and PRJNA1216515 studies.

Sequencing analysis

Data analysis was carried out following the previously described pipeline MTBSeq [15] (Table S1 List of strains and patient data and Table S2 Mapping and variant statistics). The transmission and clustering cutoff was set at 12 SNPs pairwise comparisons [15]. (Table S3 SNP distances and Table S4 SNP list). The estimated mutation rate of M. tuberculosis, has been reported at approximately 0.6 SNPs per genome per year [16]. On this basis, isolates differing by ≤ 5 SNPs are commonly regarded as representing recent transmission events, whereas distances above 12 SNPs effectively exclude transmission [17]. Lineage and family calling was done with MtbSeq (Table S5 Strain classification). Drug resistance of particular SNPs was assessed according to the WHO Catalog of Drug Resistance Mutations [18].

The phylogenetic tree was built using Geneious Tree Builder (Dotmatics, United Kingdom) using the using the maximum-likelihood method as implemented in RAxML, withing Geneious software, with the GTR substitution model and 100 bootstrap replicates. The median-joining network was built with POPArt (University of Otago, New Zealand) [19, 20].

Results

Patient data and M. tuberculosis outbreak strains

Based on spoligotyping data, we recovered 181 M. tuberculosis isolates, which were grouped into 23 clusters (Table 1; Table S1). A total of 38 strains were isolated from female patients, and 143 strains were isolated from male patients, scattered across Poland. A total of 54 (30%) patients involved in our study reported experiencing homelessness; nine were classified as foreigners, and one patient had a positive HIV status. The spoligotyping clusters contained three to 35 isolates (median 4, Q1-Q3 = 3–8). 70% of the isolates in our study were Lineage 4, and the rest represented Lineage 2, mostly from spoligotype-based families H1 (36%, n = 66) and Beijing (30%, n = 55). A total of 122 isolates were pansusceptible, whereas 59 were resistant to at least one antibiotic—isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, or a fluoroquinolone (Table 1, Table S1).

Whole-genome sequencing

We retrieved WGS data of sufficient quality for 163 isolates. The minimum mean coverage was 30x, and the median mean coverage was 60x (Q1-Q3 = 50x-90x) (Table S2).

Drug resistance determination via the phenotypic method and WGS was concordant at 95% for isoniazid, 93% for rifampicin, 82% for ethambutol, and 88% for fluoroquinolones (Table S1).

WGS identified 21 clusters among our collection of strains, with a maximum of 12 SNP difference criteria. With more strict criteria, there were 387 pairwise comparisons between isolates (Table S3), where isolates differed by 0–2 SNPs, and 793 pairwise comparisons, where isolates differed by 0–5 SNPs.

Generally, WGS clusters based on the 12-SNP rule of pairwise comparisons match those identified by traditional spoligotyping. Twenty isolates were ungrouped, and for 18 isolates, we did not retrieve sufficient quality sequencing results. Spoligotype-based Clusters 12 and 22 were grouped into Cluster 12 by WGS. Clusters 13, 14, 15, and 16, identified by spoligotyping, were grouped into a large Cluster 13 and a smaller Cluster 14 identified via WGS. There were three large clusters containing ≥ 10 isolates. Each of the two largest clusters, cluster 3 and cluster 13, contained 32 isolates. Cluster 3 consisted of strains collected in a single geographic location, whereas cluster 13 isolates were spread across a vast part of Poland (Fig. 1).

Following the genomic SNP-based classification scheme of Coll et al. [21], five WGS-based families were identified, each represented by several clusters (Table S1, Table S5). There were six Haarlem family clusters (4.1.2.1), five Beijing family clusters (2.2.1), four Ural family clusters (4.2.1), three mainly T family clusters (4.8), and three designated as Euro-American (4.1 or 4.1.2).

WGS Cluster 3, consisted of 32 isolates of the Euro-American lineage differing by no more than 12 SNPs (Fig. 2). The isolates were collected in the city of Bydgoszcz from people reporting homelessness (Fig. 1). The first isolate was collected in 2006, and the last isolate in 2014. The isolates were determined to be pansusceptible by both phenotypic and genotypic testing. Upon investigation, we found that isolate G197, which was isolated from a person experiencing homelessness in Bydgoszcz, was also linked with Cluster 3, separated by the 21 − 25 SNP.

WGS Cluster 13, consisted of isolates of the Beijing lineage and was collected across the country of Poland—in Gdańsk and Gdynia (these cities are part of the Tri-cities agglomeration, pol. Trójmiasto), Otwock, Szczecin, and Poznań (Figs. 1 and 2). The first isolate was collected in 2016, and the last isolate in 2020. The isolates within this cluster were resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin, two isolates from Otwock and one from Szczecin developed resistance to ethambutol, and 65% of the isolates from distinct cities developed resistance to fluoroquinolones. Cluster 13 was linked to two clusters : Cluster 14 originating from Otwock, and Cluster 16 originating from Poznań. Furthermore, Cluster 13 was linked with isolates G50 and G53 by approximately 31 (range: 29 − 37), and 26 (range: 25 − 32) SNPs. The other two clusters of the Beijing lineage, Clusters 8 and 15, also showed linked transmission between Polish cities, specifically Kraków and Otwock, as both clusters were separated by 22 − 26 SNPs. Another case of intercity transmission was L4 Cluster 12 - WGS revealed a link between isolates from people in the city of Lodz and a man experiencing homelessness in Bydgoszcz.

The third-largest cluster, WGS Cluster 1, was collected in the city of Bydgoszcz, and the 14 isolates were prevalent among individuals reporting homelessness. The cluster consisted of strains belonging to the Euro-American lineage and were collected between 2007 and 2017. This cluster was further linked to isolate G188, which was also isolated in Bydgoszcz from a person experiencing homelessness − 25 SNPs from strains of Cluster 1.

Discussion

We confirmed several events of M. tuberculosis transmission in Poland. We identified linked isolates of the Beijing lineage (WGS Clusters 13, 14, and 15) that are effectively spreading across a vast part of the territory of Poland. These strains are of particular concern due to their drug resistance and ability to acquire drug resistance. The frequent identification of isolates of these closely related clusters across various communities in Poland suggests that the isolates might be exceptionally transmissive. Further, we identified one ungrouped L2 ‘Europe/Russian W148 Outbreak strain isolated from a foreigner in Otwock [22, 23]. We conclude that the Beijing lineage M. tuberculosis population in Poland should be monitored closely, with routine WGS.

Our findings indicate active TB transmission among the homeless population in Bydgoszcz. We found two large L4 clusters spreading within this population and one incidence of relatedness with a cluster from Lodz. People experiencing homelessness are generally considered vulnerable and highly susceptible to TB due to frequent health-related problems, comorbidities, and malnutrition [24, 25]. Sleeping in homeless shelters increases the risk of TB exposure among vulnerable individuals. We suspect that in these cases, TB transmission is facilitated by the state of the vulnerable hosts. Identifying outbreak clusters suggests that additional measures are needed to control the spread of TB in these particular settings.

The remaining clusters contained two to eight isolates, and the WGS clusters reflected the clusters detected by spoligotyping, with 12% (n = 20) of the strains remaining unclustered. The size of the outbreak clusters remains in line with previous reports, where the median size of the local outbreak was five isolates [3]. The drug resistance of the strains was concordant between the phenotypic and genotypic methods, as reported previously [26–28].

Recent data from Central and Eastern Europe provide a framework for contextualizing our findings. In Poland, the overall notification rate of rifampicin- and multidrug-resistant TB remains low; for 3,388 cases of TB reported in Poland in 2020, 38 cases were multidrug-resistant [29]. Nevertheless, molecular studies demonstrate that resistance-associated mutations in rpoB (most frequently S450L) and katG (notably S315T) continue to dominate among Polish isolates, consistent with our observations. Moreover, genotyping and spoligotyping analyses of isolates from Poland and Lithuania reveal a heterogeneous population structure, with a predominance of the Beijing and Euro-American lineages [30]. These findings align with our data in demonstrating the genetic diversity in the region. Of note, nine patients in our study were identified as foreigners, accounting for approximately 5% of all cases. Five of these strains were ungrouped, hence might represent the introduction of novel M. tuberculosis strains to the territory of Poland. This finding is consistent with the role of migration in shaping the epidemiology of TB across Europe, where cross-border transmission contributes to the genetic diversity of circulating M. tuberculosis strains [31–33]. Of note, the ongoing war in Ukraine has created conditions that may facilitate displacement-related transmission and alter the epidemiological landscape of TB in bordering countries. Systematic genomic surveillance will be critical to detect such changes in real time.

The major limitation of our study is that although efforts were made to select strains based on epidemiological indications of transmission, the coverage might have underestimated the variability of circulating clones. Second, while spoligotyping was useful for initial clustering, it has lower discriminatory power compared with WGS. Although WGS was subsequently used to confirm clusters, some transmission links may have been missed due to the inability to sequence all strains originally selected in our study, or the use of a 12-SNP cutoff, which, despite being widely applied, may not capture all epidemiologically relevant events. Finally, detailed clinical, demographic, and social data were not available for all patients. For example, while homelessness was identified as a risk factor in Bydgoszcz, other factors such as HIV status, comorbidities, or migration history could not be systematically assessed. Based on our results, we recommend the introduction of WGS in the routine monitoring of TB transmission in Poland, especially for L2 isolates and isolates collected in shelters for people experiencing homelessness and other public facilities struggling with TB control.

WGS allows for determining both relatedness between the isolates and possible divergence of strains on the basis of the molecular clock [34], which helps to monitor and control TB transmission over longer periods of time. Close monitoring of TB transmission will enable better TB control in Poland. Although whole-genome sequencing has been adopted for TB surveillance in several European countries, its implementation in Poland remains limited to research settings, with routine diagnostics still relying on molecular assays such as GeneXpert and line-probe assays. However, broader adoption will require significant investment in sequencing infrastructure, bioinformatics expertise, and integration with existing surveillance workflows. Strengthening these capacities would bring Poland’s practices in line with WHO recommendations.
