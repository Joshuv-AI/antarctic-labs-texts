1.
Introduction

For an irrational number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q},
the sequence
(\{n\alpha\})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of fractional parts is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, in contrast to the rational case where the sequence is periodic. In 1900, Minkowski
[
52
]
proved that for any irrational
\alpha
and any
\beta\not\in\mathbb{Z}+\alpha\mathbb{Z}
, the inequality

(1)

|\!|n\alpha-\beta|\!|<\frac{1}{4n}

holds for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N},
where

|\!|\alpha|\!|
denotes the distance from
\alpha
to the nearest integer. The constant
\frac{1}{4}
was later shown by Khintchine
[
41
]
to be optimal.

Minkowski’s theorem above provides a uniform rate of approximation valid for all real numbers. Replacing the right-hand side of (
1
) by a more rapidly decaying function
\psi:\N\to(0,\frac{1}{2})
leads naturally to the study of the size of the set

\mathcal{W}(\psi):=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in[0,1)^{2}\colon|\!|n\alpha-\beta|\!|<\psi(n)\text{ i.m. }n\in\N\},

where i.m. means ‘for infinitely many’. Fixing
\beta
and varying
\alpha
corresponds to classical
inhomogeneous Diophantine approximation
(with
\beta=0
giving the homogeneous case), while fixing
\alpha
and varying
\beta
falls within the framework of
twisted Diophantine approximation

[
5
,
14
]
.

1.1.
Homogeneous and inhomogeneous Diophantine approximation

The
\psi
-well approximable set is defined as

\mathcal{W}_{\beta}(\psi):=\{\alpha\in[0,1)\colon|\!|n\alpha-\beta|\!|<\psi(n)\text{ i.m. }n\in\N\}.

The study of the metric properties of
\mathcal{W}_{\beta}(\psi)
dates back a century to Khintchine
[
40
]
, who proved for
\beta=0
that

\mathcal{L}^{1}\big(\mathcal{W}_{\beta}(\psi)\big)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)<\infty$},\\[5.69054pt]
1,&\text{if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)=\infty$ and $\psi$ is non-increasing}.\end{cases}

Here and throughout, we write
\mathcal{L}^{d}(A)
to denote the Lebesgue measure of
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Duffin and Schaeffer
[
25
]
later demonstrated that the monotonicity condition is essential: they constructed
\psi
supported on highly composite integers such that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)=\infty,\qquad{\mathcal{L}}^{1}(\mathcal{W}_{0}(\psi))=0.

Duffin and Schaeffer conjectured that for almost all
\alpha,
the inequality
|n\alpha-m|<\psi(n)
has infinitely many coprime solutions
(n,m)
almost surely if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\phi(n)}{n}\psi(n)=\infty,

where
\phi
is Euler’s totient function. Following important contributions by Gallagher
[
30
]
, Erdős
[
26
]
, Vaaler
[
62
]
, and Pollington–Vaughan
[
54
]
, the conjecture was solved affirmatively by Koukoulopoulos and Maynard
[
44
]
. A quantitative version was later given by Aistleitner
et al.

[
1
]
, with a subsequent refinements to the error term by Hauke–Vazquez Saez–Walker
[
33
]
and an almost-sharp estimate by Koukoulopoulos–Maynard–Yang
[
45
]
.

An inhomogeneous generalisation of Khintchine’s theorem was established by Szüsz
[
59
]
.
The non-monotonic scenario is more complicated. Counterexamples to relaxing monotonicity were given in
[
16
,
55
]
. Despite rumours of its falsity, the most direct analogue of the Duffin–Schaeffer conjecture remains formally open. The so-called weak Duffin–Schaeffer conjecture also remains open, see
[
7
]
. The latter was solved for a wide class of non-monotonic functions in
[
15
,
17
,
18
]
. Results with extra divergence were established by Yu
[
65
,
66
]
.
For systems of linear forms, see
[
3
,
42
,
66
]
.

Under the assumption that
\psi
is monotonic,
Jarník’s theorem
[
39
]
implies that

\dim_{\rm H}W_{\beta}(\psi)=\frac{2}{\tau+1},\ \ \text{where }\tau=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{-\log\psi(n)}{\log n}.

This result can also be derived from Khintchine’s theorem via the mass transference principle of Beresnevich and Velani
[
9
]
. For a general function
\psi,
the Hausdorff dimension of
W_{\beta}(\psi)
was studied in depth by Hinokuma and Shiga
[
37
]
.

1.2.
Twisted Diophantine approximation

The twisted framework differs from classical inhomogeneous Diophantine approximation. Here, we consider the distribution of the Kronecker sequence

(n\alpha{{\,\,\mathrm{mod}\,\,1}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

on the unit interval. The diophantine properties of
{\alpha}
strongly influence this
distribution
[
4
,
20
]
. A natural generalization is to replace the upper bound
\frac{1}{4n}
in (
1
) by a general non-increasing function
\psi\colon\mathbb{N}\to(0,\frac{1}{2}),
investigating the set

\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\psi):=\left\{\beta\in[0,1]\colon|\!|n\alpha-\beta|\!|<\psi(n)\quad\text{ i.m. }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

In 1955, Kurzweil made a foundational contribution to this question. Recall that an irrational
\alpha
is
badly approximable
if there exists a constant
c(\alpha)>0
such that

|\!|n\alpha|\!|\geq\frac{c(\alpha)}{n}\quad\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Theorem 1

(Kurzweil
[
47
]
)
.

Let
\psi\colon\mathbb{N}\to(0,\frac{1}{2})
be non-increasing and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
irrational. Then
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\psi))\in\{0,1\}
. Moreover, if
\alpha
is badly approximable, then

\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\psi))=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)<\infty$},\\[5.69054pt]
1,&\text{if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\psi(n)=\infty$}.\end{cases}

Kurzweil also showed that the divergence part of the statement fails whenever
{\alpha}
is not badly approximable. Nonetheless,
other irrational
{\alpha}
can still be studied. Fuchs and Kim
[
29
, Theorem 1.2]
refined Kurzweil’s theorem by incorporating information about the continued fraction convergents of
\alpha
, and Simmons made further progress in
[
56
]
. When
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\psi)
has zero Lebesgue measure —as occurs for
\psi_{\tau}(n)=n^{-\tau}
with
\tau>1
— finer geometric tools are needed to distinguish such null sets. In this direction, Bugeaud
[
13
, Theorem 1]
and Schmeling–Troubetzkoy
[
61
, Theorem 3.2]
independently obtained the following result.

Theorem 2

(
[
13
,
61
]
)
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
and
\tau>1,
we have

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\psi_{\tau})=\frac{1}{\tau},

where
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}
denotes Hausdorff dimension.

We note that an early result in this direction was established by Bernik and Dodson
[
11
, p. 105]
, who proved a version of the above statement for Lebesgue almost all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Subsequent work has extended this result in several directions, including generalisations to wider classes of approximation functions
\psi
and restricted sets of
\alpha,
where the exponent
\tau
in the dimension formula is replaced by the lower order at infinity of
\psi
, see
[
28
, Theorem 3]
. Notably, it was also shown in
[
28
, Theorem 2]
that there exist irrationals
\alpha
for which the expected dimension result fails to hold. For more about twisted Diophantine approximation, we refer to
[
8
,
19
,
21
,
32
,
38
,
46
]
.

1.3.
Dynamical Diophantine approximation

Let
T_{\alpha}:x\mapsto x+\alpha
denote the rotation on the unit circle. Minkowski’s theorem
studies the approximation properties of the orbit
\{T_{\alpha}^{n}(0)\}_{n\geq 1},
motivating the general problem of quantifying the rate at which orbits in a dynamical system approach a target with a prescribed speed. Let
(X,T,\mu)
be a measure-preserving dynamical system, where
(X,d)
is a metric space,
T\colon X\to X
is a Borel transformation, and
\mu
is a
T
-invariant Borel probability measure on
X.
By Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem
[
63
]
, when the system is ergodic, the set

\left\{\alpha\in X\colon\liminf_{n\to\infty}d(T^{n}(\alpha),\beta)=0\right\}

has full
\mu
-measure for
\mu
-almost every
\beta\in X.
The gives a qualitative description of orbit distribution, analogous to the density of rationals in
\mathbb{R}
, and naturally leads to quantitative refinements.

The classical
shrinking target problem
in dynamical system
(X,T)
provides a quantitative refinement of Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem. It concerns the size of the set

\mathcal{W}_{\beta}(T,(E_{n}))=\{\alpha\in X\colon T^{n}(\alpha)\in\beta+E_{n}\text{ for infinitely many }n\in\N\},

where
(E_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of subsets of
X
and
\beta\in X.
This concept, introduced by Hill and Velani
[
35
]
, generalises the framework of metric Diophantine approximation to a dynamical setting. A direct application of the first Borel–Cantelli lemma shows that

(2)

\mu(\mathcal{W}_{\beta}(T,(E_{n})))=0\ \text{ if }\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(\{\alpha\in X\colon T^{n}(\alpha)\in\beta+E_{n}\})<\infty.

Two natural questions then arise
\colon

•

What is
\mu\big(\mathcal{W}_{\beta}(T,(E_{n}))\big)
when the above series (
2
) diverges?

•

What is the Hausdorff measure/dimension of
\mathcal{W}_{\beta}(T,(E_{n}))
when it is
\mu
-null?

Since its introduction, the set
\mathcal{W}_{\beta}(T,(E_{n}))
and its variants have been studied in many contexts. These include mixing systems, automorphisms, beta-transformations, Gauss maps, and fractal-restricted targets
[
2
,
24
,
48
,
49
,
51
,
53
,
57
,
64
]
.

Let
A
be an integral matrix defining an endomorphism

T:\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d},\qquad T({\bf x})=A{\bf x}\penalty\ \!\!\!\!\pmod{1}.

In recent work, Li
et al.

[
50
]
revisited the shrinking target problem for such toral endomorphisms, originally studied by Hill and Velani
[
36
]
. For broad class of target sets
(E_{n})_{n\geq 1}
— including metric balls, rectangular regions, and hyperboloidal neighbourhoods in algebraic varieties — they determined the Lebesgue measure and Hausdorff dimension of the shrinking target set
\mathcal{W}_{\beta}(T,(E_{n})),
under the so-called summable-mixing property. Their results provide quantitative refinements beyond the classical zero–full law, substantially extending the dichotomy established in
[
36
]
. Li
et al.
also allowed the matrix to have real coefficients.

For
i=1,2,\ldots,d
, let
r_{i}\colon\N\to(0,\frac{1}{2})
, and define
{\bf r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}).
For each
n\in\N,
define the target set

\mathcal{R}_{n}({\bf r}):=\{{\bf x}\in[0,1)^{d}\colon|\!|x_{i}|\!|\leq r_{i}(n),\ 1\leq i\leq d\}.

For
{\bm{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d})\in[0,1)^{d},
the shifted set
{\bm{\beta}}+\mathcal{R}_{n}({\bf r})
is a box centred at
{\bm{\beta}}
. Let
\sigma(A)
denote the smallest singular value of a matrix
A
, i.e.,

\sigma(A):=\inf\big\{|\!|Ax|\!|_{2}\colon|\!|x|\!|_{2}=1\big\}.

For
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
write
|{\bf x}|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}|x_{i}|.

The Fourier transform of a non-atomic probability measure
\mu
is given by

\widehat{\mu}({\bf t}):=\int e^{-2\pi i\langle{\bf t},{\bf x}\rangle}{\rm d}\mu({\bf x})\ \ \ \ ({\bf t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where the scalar product
\langle{\bf t},{\bf x}\rangle
is defined by

\langle{\bf t},{\bf x}\rangle=t_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+t_{d}x_{d}.

Recently, Tan and Zhou
[
60
]
extended the Lebesgue-measure analysis in
[
50
]
to general Fourier-decaying measures. Let
\mathcal{A}=(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of expanding integral matrices — meaning that the absolute determinant exceeds 1 — and fix
{\bm{\beta}}\in[0,1)^{d}.

They studied the lim sup set

\mathcal{W}_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r}):=\left\{{\bm{\alpha}}\in[0,1)^{d}\colon A_{n}{\bm{\alpha}}\in{\bm{\beta}}+\mathcal{R}_{n}({\bf r})\!\!\!\!\!\pmod{1}\text{ i.m. }n\in\N\right\}.

Theorem 3

(
[
60
, Theorem 1.10]
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{r}}:{\mathbb{N}}\to(0,1/2)^{d}
.
Let
\mu
be a probability measure supported on
[0,1)^{d}
. Let
\mathcal{A}=(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of expanding integral matrices such that
\sigma(A_{n+1}A_{n}^{-1})\geq K>1\penalty\ (n\geq 0),

where
A_{0}
is the identity matrix in
d
dimensions. Suppose there exists
s>d+1
such that

\widehat{\mu}({\bf t})=O\Big((\log|{\bf t}|_{\infty})^{-s}\Big)\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{as}\penalty\ |{\bf t}|_{\infty}\to\infty.

Then

\mu(\mathcal{W}_{\bm{\beta}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r}))=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)<\infty,\\
1,&\text{if }\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)=\infty.\end{cases}

Surprisingly, nothing seems to be known about twisted Diophantine approximation for the matrix-generated dynamical systems. The purpose of this work is to fill this gap.

1.4.
Results

To state our results, we require some additional notation and terminology. A
d
-tuple of non-increasing functions

{\bf r}\colon\N\to\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)^{d}

is
doubling
if there exists
C>1
such that for all
1\leq i\leq d
and all
n\in\N,

r_{i}(n)\leq Cr_{i}(2n).

Thus, when we write of doubling functions, we implicitly assume their monotonicity.
For
{\bm{\alpha}}\in[0,1)^{d},
define

\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})=\left\{{\bm{\beta}}\in[0,1)^{d}\colon A_{n}{\bm{\alpha}}\in{\bm{\beta}}+\mathcal{R}_{n}({\bf r})\!\!\!\!\!\pmod{1}\text{ i.m. }n\in\N\right\}.

A
dimension function
is a continuous, non-decreasing function

f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)

such that
f(\rho)\to 0
as
\rho\to 0^{+}.

For
s\geq 0,
we write
f\preceq s
if

\frac{f(y)}{y^{s}}\leq\frac{f(x)}{x^{s}}\text{ for all }0<x<y.

Observe that if
f\preceq s
then

\lim_{\rho\to 0^{+}}\frac{f(\rho)}{\rho^{s}}>0.

We now state our first result: a Khintchine-type dichotomy for twisted approximation for general Fourier-decaying measures.

Theorem 4
.

Let
{\bf r}\colon\N\to(0,\frac{1}{2})^{d}
be doubling, and let
\mu
be a probability measure on
[0,1)^{d}
.
Let
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of integral
d\times d
matrices,
and let
A_{0}
be the identity matrix in
d
dimensions.
Assume that, for some
c,s,{\varepsilon}>0,
we have

(3)

\sigma(A_{n}-A_{m})\geq c|n-m|^{\frac{1}{s}}(\log_{+}|n-m|)^{\frac{1}{s}+{\varepsilon}}

whenever
m\neq n
. Assume also that

\widehat{\mu}({\bf{t}})=O(|{\bf t}|_{\infty}^{-s})\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{as}\penalty\ |{\bf t}|_{\infty}\to\infty.

Then, for
\mu
-almost all
{\bm{\alpha}}
,

\mathcal{L}^{d}\big(\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})\big)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)<\infty$},\\
1,&\text{if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)=\infty$}.\end{cases}

Example 5
.

Let
\mu
be the induced Lebesgue measure on an open ball intersected with a smooth submanifold of

that is not contained in any affine hyperplane. Then
\mu
has polynomial Fourier decay rate
[

.

\mathcal{L}^{d}\big(\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})\big)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)<\infty$},\\

Theorem 8
.

Let
{\bf r}\colon\N\to(0,\frac{1}{2})^{d}

\mathcal{L}^{d}\big(\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})\big)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)<\infty$},\\

Remark 9
.

The determinant condition is weaker than either of the conditions (
3
), (
4
). Indeed, (
3
) implies the determinant condition trivially, since it gives

{\sigma}(A_{n}-A_{m})>0
whenever
m\neq n
. Moreover, if we instead assume (

), then for

we have

\sigma(A_{n}-A_{m})\geq\sigma(A_{m})\sigma(A_{n}A_{m}^{-1}-I)\geq\sigma(A_{m})(K^{n-m}-1)>0,

where

Let
{\bm{\omega}}=({\bm{\omega}_{n}})_{n\geq 1}
be sequence of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d},
wherein we abbreviate
{\bm{\omega}_{n}}=(\omega_{n,1},\ldots,\omega_{n,d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
Define the twisted lim sup set

\mathcal{T}({\bm{\omega}},{\bf r}):=\left\{{\bm{\beta}}\in[0,1)^{d}\colon{\bm{\omega}_{n}}\in{\bm{\beta}}+\mathcal{R}_{n}({\bf r})\!\!\!\!\!\pmod{1}\text{ i.m. }n\in\N\right\}.

González Robert
et al.

[
31
]
proposed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 10

(
[
31

)
.

For almost every sequence
{\bm{\omega}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d},
for every

{\bf r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}),
we have

\mathcal{L}^{d}\big(\mathcal{T}({\bm{\omega}},{\bf r})\big)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)<\infty$},\\
1,&\text{if $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)=\infty$}.\end{cases}

Let
(A_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of integral matrices satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
8
, and let
{\bm{\omega}}=(A_{n}{\bm{\alpha}})_{n\geq 1}
. We see that
Theorem

solves, affirmatively, a variant of Conjecture

. Moreover, the result remains valid for general

, relaxing monotonicity.

Returning to the convergence case of Theorem
8
, the Lebesgue measure of
\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})
is zero, offering no further insight into its size. Intuitively, the size of
\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})
should decrease as the rate of approximation governed by the function
{\bf r}
increases.
In short, we require a more delicate notion of size than simply Lebesgue measure. Generalised Hausdorff measures serve as suitable framework for capturing the fine metric structure of
\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})
.
The Hausdorff
f
-measure
\mathcal{H}^{f},
defined via a dimension function
f,
naturally extends the Lebesgue measure. We refer the reader to §
2.1
for standard definitions of
\mathcal{H}^{f}
and Hausdorff dimension.

In view of the development of the Lebesgue theory, it is natural to ask for conditions under which
\mathcal{H}^{f}\big(\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})\big)
can be determined. The following result provides a simple criterion.

Theorem 11
.

Let
{\bf r}\colon\N\to(0,\frac{1}{2})^{d}
with
|{\mathbf{r}}(n)|_{\infty}\to 0
as

. Let

be a sequence of integral

be the identity matrix in
d
dimensions. Assume that

whenever

. Let

be a dimension function such that

Then, for

\mathcal{H}^{f}\big(\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})\big)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}s_{n}({\bf r},f)<\infty,\\
\mathcal{H}^{f}([0,1)^{d}),&\text{ if }\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}s_{n}({\bf r},f)=\infty,\end{cases}

where

s_{n}({\bf r},f)=\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\left\{f(r_{i}(n))\prod_{j\colon r_{j}(n)\geq r_{i}(n)}\frac{r_{j}(n)}{r_{i}(n)}\right\}.

Applying Theorem

Corollary 12
.

Let
{\bf r}\colon\N\to(0,\frac{1}{2})^{d}
with

r_{i}(n)=n^{-\tau_{i}}\penalty\ \penalty\ (1\leq i\leq d)\penalty\ \text{ and }\penalty\ \sum_{i=1}^{d}\tau_{i}>1.

Then for Lebesgue almost all
{\bm{\alpha}}\in[0,1)^{d}

\dim_{\rm H}\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})=\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\left\{\frac{1+\sum_{j\colon\tau_{j}<\tau_{i}}(\tau_{i}-\tau_{j})}{\tau_{i}}\right\}.

Remark 13

Let us compare this to Theorem

2.1.
Hausdorff measure and content

Let
f
be a dimension function. For
E\subset\R^{d}
and
\eta>0
, define

\mathcal{H}_{\eta}^{f}(E)=\inf\bigg\{\sum_{i}f(|B_{i}|):E\subset\bigcup_{i\geq 1}B_{i},\quad|B_{i}|\leq\eta\bigg\},

where
|B|
denotes the diameter of a ball
B
. The definition also applies when
\eta=\infty
, and the quantity
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{f}(E)
is called the
Hausdorff
f
-content
of
E
.
The
Hausdorff
f
-measure
of
E
is

\mathcal{H}^{f}(E):=\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{H}_{\eta}^{f}(E).

Lemma 14

(
[
12
, Lemma 1.2.5]
)
.

Let
f
and
g
be dimension functions such that the ratio
f(r)/g(r)\to 0
as
r\to 0
.
If
\mathcal{H}^{g}(E)<\infty
,
then
\mathcal{H}^{f}(E)=0.

When
f(r)=r^{s}
with
s\geq 0
, the measure
\mathcal{H}^{f}
coincides with the standard

s
–dimensional Hausdorff measure

\mathcal{H}^{s}.
When
s\in\N
, the measure

\mathcal{H}^{s}
is proportional to

s
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. The
Hausdorff dimension
of a set
E
is

\dim_{\rm H}E:=\inf\left\{s\colon\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=0\right\}=\sup\left\{s\colon\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)=\infty\right\}.

For more background, see
[
23
,
27
]
.

A classical and widely-used method for establishing a lower bound on the Hausdorff
f
-measure of a set
E
is the mass distribution principle.
The following version is a slight generalisation of
[
12
, Lemma 1.2.8]
and follows from the same argument.

Lemma 15
.

Let
E
be a Borel subset of
\R^{d}
. If
E
supports a Borel probability measure
\mu
that satisfies

\mu(B)\leq cf(|B|),

for some constant
0<c<\infty
and for every ball
B
, then

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{f}(E)\geq 1/c
.

A key tool in proving the divergence part of Theorem
11
is the following ‘balls-to-open sets’ mass transference principle. Originally established by Koivusalo and Rams
[
43
]
in the context of Hausdorff dimension, this principle was later extended to Hausdorff measures by Zhong
[
67
]
and by He
[
34
]
, with conditions on the singular value function and on the Hausdorff content, respectively.

Lemma 16

(
[
34
, Theorems 2.4 and 2.5]
)
.

Let
f
be a dimension function such that
f\preceq d
. Let
(B_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of balls in
[0,1]^{d}
with radii tending to 0 such that
\mathcal{L}^{d}(\limsup B_{k})=1
. Let
(E_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of open sets such that
E_{k}\subseteq B_{k}
for all
k
. If there exists a constant
c>0
such that for any
k\geq 1
,

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{f}(E_{k})>c\mathcal{L}^{d}(B_{k}),

then

\mathcal{H}^{f}\Big(\limsup_{k\to\infty}E_{k}\Big)=\mathcal{H}^{f}([0,1]^{d}).

Equipped with these results, the main strategy for proving the divergence part of Theorem
11
is to verify that the relevant sets satisfy suitable lower bounds on their Hausdorff
f
-content. Lemma
15
will play a crucial role.

2.2.
Auxiliary results

The following lemmas are widely used to estimate the measure of a lim sup set in a probability space.

Lemma 17

(Borel–Cantelli lemma)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{B},\nu)
be a probability space. Let

(E_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of measurable sets, and let

E=\limsup E_{n}=\bigcap_{N=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty}E_{n}.

If
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\nu(E_{n})<\infty
then
\nu(E)=0
. If instead
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\nu(E_{n})=\infty
, and the sets
E_{n}
are pairwise independent, then

\nu(E)=1
.

We note that the divergence part of the above lemma requires the independence of the sets
E_{n}
. In applications where pairwise independence fails, the following lemma is often used as a proxy.

Lemma 18

(Chung–Erdős inequality
[
22
]
)
.

If
\sum_{n\geq 1}\nu(E_{n})=\infty
, then

\nu(E)\geq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{(\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\nu(E_{n}))^{2}}{\sum_{1\leq m,n\leq N}\nu(E_{m}\cap E_{n})}.

This is also known as the divergence Borel–Cantelli lemma
[
10
]
. In many cases, the Chung–Erdős inequality only tells us that
\nu(E)>0
. To obtain a full measure result for
E
, one may apply the inequality locally and then deduce that
\nu(E)=1

via the following lemma.

Lemma 19

(
[
6
, Lemma 6]
)
.

Let
(\Omega,d)
be a metric space with a finite measure
\nu
for which every open set is measurable. Suppose
E\subseteq\Omega
is a Borel set and
h:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is an increasing function such that
h(x)\to 0
as
x\to 0^{+}
. If the inequality

\nu(E\cap U)\geq h(\nu(U)),

holds for every open set
U\subset\Omega
, then
\nu(E)=\nu(\Omega)
.

The next lemma establishes a fundamental connection between the Fourier decay of a measure and the equidistribution properties of sequences of expanding integral matrices.

Lemma 20
.

Let

be a probability measure on

(A_{n})_{n\in\N}
be a sequence of expanding integral matrices. Assume that the inequality (
3

(A_{n}{\bm{\alpha}})_{n\geq 1}

for

Proof.

A proof follows along similar lines to that of
[
60

.
∎

The following calculation will play a pivotal role in the estimation procedure.

Lemma 21
.

For

S:=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{{\bf k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}}(m^{\frac{1}{s}}(\log_{+}m)^{\frac{1}{s}+{\varepsilon}}|{\bf k}|_{\infty})^{-s}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\min\{1,|k_{i}|^{-1}\}<\infty.

We compute that

3.
Proof of Theorem

Recall that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})

\displaystyle=\{{\bm{\beta}}\in[0,1)^{d}\colon A_{n}{\bm{\alpha}}\in{\bm{\beta}}+\mathcal{R}_{n}({\bf r})\!\!\!\!\pmod{1}\text{ i.m. }n\in\mathbb{N}\}

3.1.
Convergence part

We assume that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)<\infty.

It is readily checked that

\mathcal{L}^{d}(E_{n})=2^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n).

Now Lemma
17
yields

{\mathcal{L}}^{d}(\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r}))=0

for any
{\bm{\alpha}}\in[0,1)^{d}.

3.2.
Divergence part

In this case, we assume that the series

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)=\infty.

We proceed in two stages:

(1)

Construct a suitable set
G\subseteq[0,1)^{d}
such that
\mu(G)=1
.

(2)

Show that
{\mathcal{L}}^{d}(\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r}))=1
for every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in G.

For

define

B_{N}(p)=\left\{\begin{split}{\bm{\alpha}}\in[0,1)^{d}:&\sum_{1\leq m,n\leq N}\mathcal{L}^{d}(E_{m}\cap E_{n})\\
&>p\int\sum_{1\leq m,n\leq N}\mathcal{L}^{d}(E_{m}\cap E_{n}){\rm d}\mu\end{split}\right\}.

Chebyshev’s inequality yields
\mu(B_{N}(p))\leq 1/p
, whence

\mu(B_{N}^{c}(p))\geq 1-\frac{1}{p},

where
B_{N}^{c}(p)
is the complement of

Defining

B(p)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}B_{N}^{c}(p),\qquad B=\bigcup_{p=1}^{\infty}B(p),

we have
\mu(B)=1
by the reverse Fatou lemma.

Lemma 22
.

In the context of Theorem
4

Now

\displaystyle\int\sum_{1\leq m<n\leq N}\mathcal{L}^{d}(E_{m}\cap E_{n}){\rm d}\mu({\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq m<n\leq N}\mathcal{L}^{d}(E_{n})\int{\bm{1}}_{\mathcal{R}_{m}(2{\bf r})}((A_{n}-A_{m}){\bm{\alpha}}){\rm d}\mu({\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq m<n\leq N}\mathcal{L}^{d}(E_{n})\sum_{{\bf k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{\bm{1}}_{\mathcal{R}_{m}(2{\bf r})}({-\bf k})\hat{\mu}((A_{n}-A_{m})^{T}{\bf k}).

∎

We see from Lemma

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n)=\infty.

To complete the proof of Theorem

Since
f\preceq d
, for large
n
we have
f(r_{i}(n))\gg r_{i}(n)^{d}
, hence

(9)

s_{n}(\mathbf{r},f)\gg\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\left\{r_{i}(n)^{d}\prod_{j\colon r_{j}(n)\geq r_{i}(n)}\frac{r_{j}(n)}{r_{i}(n)}\right\}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}r_{i}(n).

Since the lim sup set
\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},\bf r)
is unaffected by the alteration of finitely many
n\in\N,
we will henceforth assume that the inequality (
9
) holds for all
n\in\N.

For each
n\in\N,
we rearrange
r_{1}(n),\ldots,r_{d}(n)
as

r_{i_{1}}(n)\leq r_{i_{2}}(n)\leq\cdots\leq r_{i_{d}}(n).

Note that the indices
i_{1},\ldots i_{d}
depend on
n,
although we suppress this dependence in the notation. One may readily verify that the inequalities

\prod_{j=1}^{d}r_{i_{j}}\leq\cdots\leq r_{i_{k}}(n)^{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{d}r_{i_{j}}\leq\cdots\leq r_{i_{d}}(n)^{d}

hold for
k=1,2,\ldots,d
.
By (
9
), there exists
k=k(n)\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
such that

r_{i_{k}}(n)^{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{d}r_{i_{j}}\ll s_{n}({\bf r},f)\ll r_{i_{{k+1}}}(n)^{k+1}\prod_{j=k+2}^{d}r_{i_{j}},

where
r_{i_{d+1}}=\infty
. Let

(10)

b_{n}=\left(\frac{s_{n}({\bf r},f)}{\prod_{j=k+1}^{d}r_{i_{j}}(n)}\right)^{1/k},

and note that

r_{i_{k}}\ll b_{n}\ll r_{i_{k+1}}.

Define
\Psi=(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{d})
by

\psi_{i_{j}}(n)=\begin{cases}r_{i_{j}}(n),&\text{if $j>k=k(n)$}\\
b_{n},&\text{if $j\leq k=k(n)$}.\end{cases}

Then

\psi_{i_{1}}(n)=\cdots=\psi_{i_{k}}(n)=b_{n}\ll\psi_{i_{k+1}}(n)\leq\cdots\leq\psi_{i_{d}}(n).

Lemma 24
.

Let
\Psi
be the function defined as above. Then, for
\mathcal{L}^{d}
-almost every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in[0,1)^{d}
,

\mathcal{L}^{d}\big(\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},\Psi)\big)=1.

Proof.

By Theorem
8
, it suffices to show that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\psi_{j}(n)=\infty.

Note that

\prod_{j=1}^{d}\psi_{j}(n)=\prod_{j=1}^{k(n)}b_{n}\cdot\prod_{j=k(n)+1}^{d}r_{i_{j}}(n)=s_{n}({\bf r},f).

The conclusion now follows from (
8
).
∎

Recall that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},\Psi)=\bigcap_{N=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty}G_{n},

where

G_{n}=\{{\bm{\beta}}\in[0,1)^{d}:A_{n}{\bm{\alpha}}\in{\bm{\beta}}+\mathcal{R}_{n}(\Psi)\!\!\!\!\pmod{1}\}.

For each
n
, the set
G_{n}
has a finite cover
\mathcal{C}_{n}

by balls of radius
Cb_{n}
centred at points in
G_{n}
, where
C=C_{d}
is a large, positive constant. Consequently,

\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},\Psi)\subseteq\bigcap_{N=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty}\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}B.

By Lemma
24
, the right-hand side has full Lebesgue measure for
\mathcal{L}^{d}
-almost every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in[0,1)^{d}
. Moreover,

\mathcal{T}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r})\supseteq\bigcap_{N=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty}\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}B\cap E_{n}.

In light of Lemma
16
, to establish that

\mathcal{H}^{f}(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A},{\bf r}))=\mathcal{H}^{f}([0,1)^{d})

holds for
\mathcal{L}^{d}
-almost every
{\bm{\alpha}}\in[0,1)^{d}
, it suffices to show that for every such covering
\mathcal{C}_{n}
and every ball
B\in\mathcal{C}_{n},

(11)

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{f}(B\cap E_{n})\gg\mathcal{L}^{d}(B)\asymp b_{n}^{d}.

Since the radius of
B
is
Cb_{n}
, the set
B\cap E_{n}
contains an axis-aligned box
H_{n}=H_{n}(B)

with side lengths

r_{i_{1}}(n),\ldots,r_{i_{k}}(n),\underbrace{b_{n},\ldots,b_{n}}_{d-k\text{ terms}}.

The following estimate for the Hausdorff
f
-content of
H_{n}
implies (
11
), and will therefore complete the proof of Theorem
11
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
H_{n}
be as above. Then

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{f}(H_{n})\gg_{f}b_{n}^{d}.

Proof.

We will apply Lemma
15
to bound the Hausdorff
f
-content from below. Define a probability measure
\mu
on
H_{n}
by

\mu:=\frac{\mathcal{L}^{d}\big|_{H_{n}}}{\mathcal{L}^{d}(H_{n})}=\frac{\mathcal{L}^{d}\big|_{H_{n}}}{r_{i_{1}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)b_{n}^{d-k}}.

We estimate
\mu\big(B({\bf x},\rho)\big)
for an arbitrary ball
B({\bf x},\rho)
with
{\bf x}\in H_{n}
and
\rho>0.
For this proof only, we redefine
r_{i_{k+1}}=b_{n}
. We divide the analysis into three cases.

Case 1.
Suppose
0<2\rho\leq r_{i_{1}}(n).
Then

\mu(B({\bf x},\rho))\leq\frac{(2\rho)^{d}}{r_{i_{1}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)b_{n}^{d-k}}.

Since
s\preceq f\preceq s+1
for some
s\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
, we get

l\preceq f\text{ for all }l\leq s,\text{ and }f\preceq l\text{ for all }l\geq s+1.

In particular, since
s+1\leq d
we have
f\preceq d,
so

\frac{f(r_{i_{1}}(n))}{r_{i_{1}}(n)^{d}}\leq\frac{f(2\rho)}{(2\rho)^{d}}\ \Longrightarrow\ (2\rho)^{d}\leq\frac{r_{i_{1}}(n)^{d}f(2\rho)}{f(r_{i_{1}}(n))}.

Hence,

\mu(B({\bf x},\rho))\leq\frac{f(2\rho)}{f(r_{i_{1}}(n))r_{i_{1}}(n)^{1-d}r_{i_{2}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)b_{n}^{d-k}}.

Case 2.
Suppose
r_{i_{j}}(n)<2\rho\leq r_{i_{j+1}}(n)
for some
1\leq j\leq k\leq d
.
Then either

d-j\preceq f\text{ or }f\preceq d-j.

We compute that

\mu(B({\bf x},\rho))\leq\frac{r_{i_{1}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{j}}(n)(2\rho)^{d-j}}{r_{i_{1}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)b_{n}^{d-k}}=\frac{(2\rho)^{d-j}}{r_{i_{j+1}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)b_{n}^{d-k}}.

If
d-j\preceq f
, then from
r_{i_{j}}(n)<2\rho
we obtain

\frac{(2\rho)^{d-j}}{f(2\rho)}\leq\frac{r_{i_{j}}(n)^{d-j}}{f(r_{i_{j}}(n))}\ \Longrightarrow\ (2\rho)^{d-j}\leq\frac{r_{i_{j}}(n)^{d-j}f(2\rho)}{f(r_{i_{j}}(n))},

whence

\mu(B({\bf x},\rho))\leq\frac{f(2\rho)}{f(r_{i_{j}}(n))r_{i_{j}}(n)^{j-d}r_{i_{j+1}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)b_{n}^{d-k}}.

If instead
f\preceq d-j,
then
2\rho\leq r_{i_{j+1}}(n)
gives

\frac{f(r_{i_{j+1}}(n))}{r_{i_{j+1}}(n)^{d-j}}\leq\frac{f(2\rho)}{(2\rho)^{d-j}}\ \Longrightarrow\ (2\rho)^{d-j}\leq\frac{r_{i_{j+1}}(n)^{d-j}f(2\rho)}{f(r_{i_{j+1}}(n))},

and thus

\mu(B({\bf x},\rho))\leq\frac{f(2\rho)}{f(r_{i_{j+1}}(n))r_{i_{j+1}}(n)^{j+1-d}r_{i_{j+2}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)b_{n}^{d-k}}.

In either of these first two cases,

\mu(B({\bf x},\rho))\leq\frac{f(2\rho)}{\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq k+1}\left\{f(r_{i_{j}}(n))r_{i_{j}}(n)^{j-d}r_{i_{j+1}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)b_{n}^{d-k}\right\}}.

For
1\leq j\leq k
,

\displaystyle f(r_{i_{j}}(n))r_{i_{j}}(n)^{j-d}r_{i_{j+1}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)b_{n}^{d-k}

\displaystyle=f(r_{i_{j}}(n))\prod_{s=j+1}^{d}\frac{r_{i_{s}}(n)}{r_{i_{j}}(n)}\cdot\frac{(b_{n})^{d-k}}{\prod_{s=k+1}^{d}r_{i_{s}}(n)}

\displaystyle\geq s_{n}({\bf r},f)\frac{(b_{n})^{d-k}}{\prod_{s=k+1}^{d}r_{i_{s}}(n)}=b_{n}^{d},

where the inequality uses the definition of
s_{n}({\bf r},f),
and the last equality follows from (
10
). For
j=k+1
,

\displaystyle f(r_{i_{j}}(n))r_{i_{j}}(n)^{j-d}r_{i_{j+1}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)b_{n}^{d-k}

\displaystyle=f(b_{n})r_{i_{j}}(n)\cdots r_{i_{k}}(n)=f(b_{n}).

The upshot is that, in Cases 1 and 2,

\mu(B({\mathbf{x}},\rho))\leq\frac{f(2\rho)}{b_{n}^{d}}.

Case 3.
Suppose
2\rho>b_{n}
. Then

\mu(B({\mathbf{x}},\rho))\leq 1\leq\frac{f(2\rho)}{f(b_{n})}\ll\frac{f(2\rho)}{b_{n}^{d}},

since
f\preceq d
.

We have

\mu(B({\mathbf{x}},\rho))\ll\frac{f(2\rho)}{b_{n}^{d}}

in all three cases so, by Lemma
15
,

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{f}(H_{n})\gg b_{n}^{d}.

∎
