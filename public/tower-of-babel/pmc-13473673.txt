Flavonoids of Rosa sterilis S. D. Shi alleviate high-fat diet-induced hyperlipidemia in mice by activation of autophagy
Authors: Ji-Qiu Wang, Rui-Xue Yu, Xu-Lu Luo, Qiu-Ye Liu, Yan Feng, Jiao-Jiao Li, Hejiang Zhou, Liang Ma, Yong-He Li, Ling-Yan Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity and hyperlipidemia are global health crises linked to metabolic disorders, characterized by an abnormal accumulation of triglycerides in fat cells. Autophagy, an essential cellular degradation mechanism, plays a key role in maintaining adipocyte balance and lipid metabolism. Recent studies have found that polysaccharides and the juice of Rosa sterilis S. D. Shi (RS) have hypolipidemic effects. However, the effects of flavonoids from RS (FRS) on lipid metabolism and their potential hypolipidemic and hypoglycemic roles remain unclear. Here, we found that FRS alleviated obesity symptoms and corrected glycolipid metabolism in mice induced by a high-fat diet. Further studies revealed that FRS reduced liver injury and fat accumulation, accompanied by counteracting oxidative stress in the liver of high-fat diet-induced mice. Additionally, FRS inhibited the expression of genes related to fat synthesis and up-regulated the expression of genes related to fat degradation. We also confirmed that FRS significantly influenced lipid metabolism by activating autophagy, promoting lipolysis, and inhibiting adipocyte differentiation. In 3T3-L1 adipocytes, autophagy was suppressed during differentiation, FRS treatment activated autophagy, reduced lipid accumulation, and downregulated adipogenic genes. Blocking autophagy with BAFA1 abolished the effect of FRS. In conclusion, these results suggest that FRS plays a crucial role in lipid metabolism by inhibiting lipid synthesis and increasing autophagy in adipocytes. FRS shows promise as a novel, non-toxic natural product for addressing obesity and hyperlipidemia linked to high-fat diets. Exploring bioactive compounds from natural foods that modulate the autophagy pathway may offer a new therapeutic strategy for treating obesity and hyperlipidemia.

Introduction

Globally, obesity and its related metabolic disorders, including insulin resistance, hyperlipidemia, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, have become epidemics, posing serious financial and public health challenges1,2. It is a chronic illness with a complicated pathophysiology that includes environmental, genetic, and epigenetic elements3. Comprehending the fundamental mechanisms is essential for devising efficacious prevention and therapeutic approaches to obesity. Bariatric surgery is the most effective, but not scalable globally. Many anti-obesity medications for the treatment of obesity can lead to resistance, poor efficacy, accompanied with many side effects4,5. Searching for natural and effective food-based ingredients without side effects to prevent and cure obesity is becoming more and more important.

Adipose tissue modulates lipid metabolism and energy equilibrium by sequestering surplus nutrients and liberating energy substrates via the process of lipolysis6,7. Dysfunction of adipocytes within fat tissues is pivotal in the development of obesity, leading to metabolic dysregulation and inflammatory responses associated with this condition8. Obesity is associated with diminished activity of antioxidant enzymes, as evidenced by increased levels of oxidative stress biomarkers and impaired antioxidant capacity9,10. Oxidative stress promotes adipogenesis and the differentiation of mature adipocytes by modulating the expression of mitochondrial activity regulators and altering the concentrations of inflammatory mediators associated with adipocytes, ultimately facilitating the onset of obesity.

Autophagy, a lysosome degradation pathway, has been recognized as a critical factor in the realm of obesity, especially for adipose tissue homeostasis and systemic energy balance11,12. Recent studies have demonstrated that autophagy is dysregulated in individuals with obesity and corresponding animal models13,14. Autophagy contributes to adipocyte homeostasis and lipid metabolism within adipose tissue14. Reduced autophagy may facilitate lipid accumulation and the hypertrophy of adipose tissue, while enhanced autophagy can have the potential to reverse these processes15. Autophagy modulates lipid homeostasis, enhances insulin sensitivity, and mitigates oxidative stress16. Hepatic steatosis, characterized by abnormal accumulation of lipids in the liver, induces inflammatory oxidative stress and leads to cellular injury17. Autophagy provides cellular protection by mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation18,19. The human immune system is negatively impacted by the autophagy inducer rapamycin20. Developing drugs that selectively stimulate autophagic processes for the management of hepatic steatosis continues to pose significant challenges.

Rosa sterilis S. D. Shi is a wild fruit indigenous to southwestern China, known for its wealth of bioactive substances, including polysaccharides, flavonoids, superoxide dismutase (SOD), and vitamin C21. Previous studies have demonstrated that the aqueous extracts and polysaccharides of Rosa sterilis S. D. Shi can enhance conditions related to type 2 diabetes and hyperlipidemia22–24, yet the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Additional studies are required to gain a comprehensive understanding of these mechanisms and to investigate the potential clinical uses of Rosa sterilis S. D. Shi in combating obesity and hyperlipidemia. We hypothesize that the flavonoids of Rosa sterilis S. D. Shi (FRS) can mitigate obesity and hyperlipidemia in mice caused by a high-fat diet (HFD). Additionally, we aim to explore the multi-targeted mechanisms through which FRS prevents obesity, thereby providing strong evidence for the role of dietary supplementation in obesity management.

In this study, we found that FRS had the potential protective effects on obesity and hyperlipidemia caused by an HFD. By using mouse animal models and cells, we discovered that FRS mitigated HFD-induced hyperlipidemia by suppressing oxidative stress, activating autophagy within adipocytes, thereby suppressing lipid accumulation and adipogenesis, promoting lipid mobilization, and restoring metabolic homeostasis. Our study suggested that FRS may serve as a novel and promising natural antioxidant that is safe for oral consumption in the prevention of obesity.

Results

FRS attenuated the obesity phenotype and hyperlipidemia in mice fed with HFD

To evaluate the effects of FRS on HFD-induced obesity and hyperlipidemia in mice, male C57BL/6 mice were fed an HFD for 12 weeks to establish the model (Fig. 1A). After 12 weeks on an HFD, mice showed increased body weight (Fig. 1B) and weight gain (Fig. 1C) relative to controls, in line with prior reports25,26. These parameters were significantly reduced by 12 weeks of FRS treatment (Fig. 1B, C). The excessive accumulation of triglycerides in adipocytes is a characteristic that sets obesity apart27. Subsequently, serum total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) concentrations were quantified in each group of mice (Fig. 1D–G). Neither HFD nor FRS significantly affected serum TC in mice (Fig. 1D). In comparison to control mice, HFD increased the level of TG (Fig. 1E), LDL-C (Fig. 1F), and LDL-C/HDL-C (Fig. 1H). Meanwhile, HFD decreased the level of HDL-C (Fig. 1G) in mice. FRS treatment may effectively counteract the effects of HFD. FRS improved glucose homeostasis and mitigated hepatic injury induced by an HFD

Lipid metabolism has been linked to glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity28. We found that HFD disturbed the glucose metabolism of mice, and FRS treatment had alleviated these effects (Fig. 2A). The oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) showed a markedly elevated area under the glucose curve (AUC) with HFD, while the AUC was significantly lower with FRS treatment (Fig. 2B). The fasting glucose levels in mice provided additional validation of their glucose metabolic activity29. It was found that FRS-treated mice had a lower fasting glucose level (Fig. 2C) compared to the control group. Given the critical role the liver plays in both human and mouse metabolism, we detect the markers of liver function in the serum of mice from different groups. We found that FRS gavage distinctly reduced the enzyme activity of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (Fig. 2D) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (Fig. 2E). Consistent with the results in plasma, the levels of TC and TG in the livers of mice with HFD were much higher than those in the normal control group (Fig. 2F, G). FRS intervention did not influence TC levels but significantly decreased TG concentrations in the hepatic tissue of mice (Fig. 2F, G). To further investigate the function of FRS, we stained the liver of mice. Histological analysis showed that hepatic tissue damage was characterized by pronounced vacuolar degeneration of hepatocytes, perivenous hepatocyte swelling, and a more diffuse cytoplasmic appearance in mice from the HFD group compared to control mice (Fig. 2H). Mice subjected to the HFD exhibited a markedly elevated accumulation of lipid droplets within hepatic tissue, as evidenced by Oil Red O staining (Fig. 2H). FRS treatment significantly remodeled the hepatocytes and diminished hepatic lipid accumulation. (Fig. 2H). Altogether, these findings imply that FRS intervention attenuated HFD-induced hepatic injury and lipid droplet increase in mice.

FRS treatment attenuates hepatic oxidative stress and chronic inflammation induced by HFD

To further investigate whether HFD-induced hyperlipidemia is accompanied by HDL cholesterol-induced chronic inflammation, we subsequently assessed the concentrations of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines in the hepatic tissue of murine models. We found there were significantly increased expression of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (Fig. 3A) and decreased expression of interleukin-10 (IL-10) (Fig. 3B) in the livers of mice from the HFD group compared to control mice. In contrast, FRS treatment counteracted the HFD-induced increase in TNF-α and the reduction of IL-10 levels in the hepatic tissue of mice (Fig. 3A, B). Subsequently, the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), a biomarker indicative of lipid peroxidation, along with the enzymatic activity of antioxidant defense mechanisms, were assessed. Compared with the control mice, the elevated MDA (Fig. 3C) content, decreased SOD (Fig. 3D), catalase (CAT) (Fig. 3E), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) (Fig. 3F) activities were observed in the livers of mice from the HFD group. Notably, FRS treatment reversed these effects induced by HFD (Fig. 3C–F). FRS reduces the fat accumulation induced by HFD

Consistent with previous studies, a 12-week regimen of HFD administration led to a more pronounced augmentation of epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT), subcutaneous white adipose tissue (sWAT, encompassing inguinal WAT and anterior WAT), inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT), and brown adipose tissue (bWAT) (Fig. 3G) when compared to the control group. These parameters were significantly reduced by 12 weeks of FRS treatment (Fig. 3G). Epididymal fat stained with HE revealed. In contrast, the FRS therapy restored adipocyte organization and considerably decreased the diameter and area of adipocytes, while the HFD group’s adipocytes were much larger than those in the control group (Fig. 3I). The control mice had regularly arranged adipocytes with uniform cells, while HFD mice had disorganized adipocytes with enlarged cells (Fig. 3H). Meanwhile, the adipocyte diameter and surface area in the HFD group were significantly greater than those in the control group. However, treatment with FRS effectively normalized the adipocyte architecture and markedly decreased both the diameter and surface area of the adipocytes (Fig. 3I). Note that 25 mg/kg FRS also significantly decreased HFD-induced crown-like structures (CLS) of adipocyte (Fig. 3I). Similarly, Oil Red O staining demonstrated that a HFD resulted in an enlargement of adipocyte area and diameter in epididymal fat of mice, an effect that could be mitigated by FRS (Fig. 3J). These results suggest that FRS intervention attenuated HFD-induced adipocyte hypertrophy in mice. The lipid metabolism of adipose tissue may be significantly impacted by FRS therapy.

Treatment with FRS inhibits adipocyte differentiation

An investigation into the potential involvement of FRS in the differentiation of adipocytes was prompted by the observed increase in adipose tissue mass in mice subjected to a high-fat dietary regimen. Preadipocytes are stimulated to develop into mature adipocytes when exposed to high energy30. It is possible to induce 3T3-L1 fibroblasts, which are extensively characterized preadipocyte cell lines, to develop into normal white adipocytes31. Figure 4A shows the 3T3-L1 adipocyte differentiation process. To evaluate the function of FRS in the process of adipogenesis, FRS was added to the medium during adipogenesis in 3T3-L1 cells when exposed to adipogenic stimuli. Firstly, we aimed to test the cellular viability after FRS treatment by using the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Analysis revealed no significant variation in cell viability of 3T3-L1 cells subjected to FRS treatment for 24 h compared to untreated controls (Fig. S1), indicating that the cells exhibit a tolerance to the specified concentration of FRS utilized in our study. Using Oil Red O staining, we discovered that FRS treatment in 3T3-L1 preadipocytes reduced lipid accumulation with adipogenic stimuli (Fig. 4B). Compared to control group, the adipocyte area in the 3T3-L1 cell with adipocyte differentiation (MDIR group) was significantly increased, whereas FRS treatment reduced the area of adipocytes (Fig.4C). Next, we assessed the total TC and TG levels in 3T3-L1 cells with or without FRS administration. To gain a deeper insight into the impact of FRS interventions on lipid metabolism, we conducted a further analysis of the mRNA expression levels of pertinent genes. Our analysis revealed that the MDIR group exhibited a notably elevated expression of genes associated with lipogenic pathways and cholesterol biosynthesis, including acetyl-CoA carboxylase (Acc, Fig. 4F), fatty acid synthase (Fasn, Fig. 4G), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (Pparγ, Fig. 4H), sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (Srebp-1c, Fig. 4I), sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2 (Srebp-2, Fig. 4J), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (Hmgcr, Fig. 4K), and liver X receptor (Lxr, Fig. 4L). Additionally, we delineate the expression of genes implicated in lipolytic processes, notably peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (Pparα, Fig. 4M) and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (Cpt-1a, Fig. 4N). The mRNA expression of Pparα was significantly downregulated in the MDIR group in comparison to the control group (Fig. 4M). In contrast to the MDIR group, the FRS treatments decreased Acc, Fasn, Pparγ, Srebp-1c, and Srebp-2 and increased the Pparα (Fig. 4F–M) mRNA expressions. This data indicates that FRS may serve as a novel factor for inhibiting adipogenic programming.

To investigate the regulatory effect of FRS on autophagy, cells were treated with 15 μg/mL FRS. These findings suggested that FRS activates autophagy to mitigate lipid accumulation within adipocytes.

To further confirm the impact of FRS on autophagy, we introduced bafilomycin A1 (BAFA1), a specific inhibitor of the vacuolar (V)-type ATPase, which prevents autophagosome-lysosome fusion in our system33,34. Pretreatment with BAFA1 (Fig. S2) resulted in a significantly greater accumulation of LC3B-II compared to control cells, irrespective of FRS treatment status (Fig. 6A, B), suggesting FRS treatment increased autophagy in adipocytes. Oil Red O staining results showed that differentiated adipocytes had more accumulation of lipid than sham control cells, but FRS treatment reduced lipid accumulation in adipocytes (Fig. 6C, D). Pretreatment with BAFA1 prevents the restorative effects of FRS (Fig.6C, D). Next, the levels of TC and TG were measured in adipocytes. FRS treatment decreased the TC (Fig. 6E) and TG (Fig. 6F) levels in differentiated adipocytes, while this was partially abolished by pretreatment with BAFA1. Taken together, pretreatment with BAFA1 partially abolished FRS-induced activation of autophagy (Fig. 6A, B) and lipid accumulation (Fig. 6C–F) in adipocytes. Discussion

Human health is seriously threatened by obesity, as seen by the higher rates of cancer, diabetes mellitus, insulin resistance, hypertension, systemic inflammation, sleep-disordered breathing, and various other chronic diseases35. Although the management of obesity has improved over the years, therapies aimed at its underlying mechanisms remain limited. Drug therapy that aids in weight loss is gaining popularity. Our investigation revealed that oral administration of FRS resulted in a substantial reduction in body weight gain and ameliorated glucose metabolism and liver damage in mice with HFD-fed. We further demonstrated that FRS can deregulate the expression of genes involved in fatty acid synthase and inhibit adipocyte differentiation. FRS alleviates lipid metabolism dysfunction in adipocytes via activation of autophagy (Figs. 5 and 6).

Natural products, which often exhibit favorable efficacy and safety profiles, may accelerate the development of improved obesity-management strategies36,37. Rosa sterilis S. D. Shi is rich in nutrients and bioactive constituents, including flavonoids, organic acids, polysaccharides, triterpenes, and the antioxidant enzyme SOD38. In vivo, FRS confers protection against radiation-induced, ROS-mediated DNA damage in thymocytes, regardless of PARP‑1 expression39,40. However, the functions of FRS in lipid metabolism haven’t been reported until now. Obesity is characterized by heightened oxidative stress and weakened antioxidant defenses, including decreased antioxidant enzyme activities41,42. Oxidative stress promotes adipogenesis and the differentiation of mature adipocytes by altering the expression of regulators of mitochondrial activity and the levels of inflammatory mediators associated with adipocytes, thereby promoting the development of obesity43,44. The current study mainly found that FRS treatment can reverse the hepatic oxidative stress and inflammation induced by HFD. FRS plays a key role in lipid metabolism by activating autophagy in adipocytes and the liver. During the experiments, we observed that pretreatment with FRS decreased the level of MDA, increased the activity of antioxidant enzymes, increased autophagy activity, and decreased lipid accumulation. Furthermore, FRS improved the hyperlipidemia and obesity phenotypes induced by HFD (Fig. 4).

Adipocyte differentiation and maturation were accompanied by adipogenesis and fat degradation, which are closely related to obesity37,45. Here, we focus on these signaling pathways to elucidate how FRS exerts anti-obesity and anti-hyperlipidemic effects. Our results indicate that FRS reverses the dysregulation of genes involved in fatty acid synthesis, adipogenesis, triglyceride synthesis, and fatty acid oxidation, including Acc1, Fasn, Pparγ, Cebpa, Srebp-1c, Srebp-2, Pparα, and Lxr (Figs. 4 and 5). In this study, we found that FRS reduces hyperlipidemia in vitro and in vivo by inhibiting adipogenesis and lipogenesis, partly through regulating lipid‑metabolism gene expression and promoting autophagy.

Autophagy maintains cellular homeostasis and organ function by degrading potentially toxic proteins and lipids and removing damaged organelles46. Autophagy dysregulation contributes to metabolic disorders, such as obesity, atherosclerosis, diabetes mellitus, and insulin resistance35,47. Previous studies revealed that HFD-fed mice inhibited the autophagy in the liver of mice48,49. Recent evidence suggests that autophagy helps maintain intracellular lipid‑droplet (LD) homeostasis in cultured cells by regulating LD biogenesis, facilitating fatty‑acid mobilization, and promoting LD degradation (lipophagy)49,50. Accumulating evidence indicates that autophagy regulates glucose and lipid metabolism in mammalian systems in vivo50. Therefore, we speculate that FRS might be an autophagy inducer. In the present study, we show that the FRS-induced reduction in adipocyte lipid accumulation is partially dependent on autophagy. However, which type of autophagy could be induced by FRS in adipocytes needs to be further confirmed, which is an attractive research direction inspiring us to further investigate. As well as its lipid‑metabolic functions, autophagy regulates inflammatory and redox pathways, thereby potentially mitigating obesity-related complications.

In summary, we show that FRS, a bioactive fraction derived from Rosa sterilis S.D. Shi attenuates HFD-induced obesity and hyperlipidemia by suppressing lipid accumulation and enhancing autophagy in the liver (Fig. 7). To our knowledge, the role of FRS in autophagy has not been reported. This study provides further evidence that autophagy is a viable therapeutic target for anti-hyperlipidemic drug development and supports FRS as a promising, well‑tolerated candidate for counteracting diet‑induced obesity. The potential pleiotropic roles of FRS in humans still need to be addressed in future research. Methods

Preparation of FRS

We prepared FRS according to our previous study51. Briefly, the fruits of Rosa sterilis S. D. Shi were dried and ground to produce fruit powder, which was then processed using an enzyme-assisted ultrasound extraction technique that involved ethanol along with specific parameters for time, temperature, and enzyme dosage. The flavonoids obtained from the extraction were purified with AB-8 macroporous resin and subsequently freeze-dried to achieve a high-purity flavonoid powder. The purity of FRS was 80%, and the bioactive ingredients of FRS were summarized in our previous study51.

3T3-L1 cell cultures, cell viability assay, and differentiation

Mouse embryo fibroblast preadipocyte 3T3-L1 cells were procured from Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco-BRL, 11965092) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco-BRL, 10099-141) and 1×penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122) under a controlled environment of 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity. Pharmacological agents were introduced directly into the culture medium for therapeutic applications. Each experimental procedure was conducted in triplicate at a minimum.

3T3-L1 cells (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in a 96-well plate (100 μL/well). Cells were treated with or without FRS (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40 μg/mL) for 24 h. Next, 10 μL of CCK-8 assay solution (Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., China) was added to each well at the indicated time. After incubation for 2 h at 37 °C, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using an automatic microplate reader (Synergy H1, Biotek, Vermont, USA)52.

Inoculate 2 × 105 cells/mL into a 6-well plate and culture 3T3-L1 cells for 48 h of cell adhesion, then induce differentiation of the cells. Then, replace the culture medium with one containing 1 μM Dexamethasone (DEX), 0.5 mM 3-Isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX), 10 μg/mL Insulin (INS), and 1 μM Rosiglitazone (ROS), and culture for another 48 h. Following this, replace the culture medium with one containing only 10 μg/mL INS and continue culturing for 48 h. Replace the medium with DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% PS and continue culturing for 96 h. The medium was changed every 48 h. Throughout the induction process, 5, 15, and 30 μg/mL of FRS were added along with the changes in the medium. The entire induction process lasted for 10 days.

Animal experiments

Male C57BL/6J mice, 6–8 weeks old, were sourced from Sibeifu (Beijing) Biology Technology Co Ltd. They were kept in an environment with access to food and water at 22 ± 2 °C, 50% humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. A double-blind, controlled design was applied for data collection and analysis in behavioral trials. All procedures were approved by the local animal research committee (review number: APYNAU202411006).

They were split into four groups at random, each consisting of six animals, following 7 days of adaptive feeding. The control group (CON) received 10% low-fat feed and physiological saline via gavage; the high-fat model group (HFD) was given a 60% HFD and saline; and the HFD with FRS group (HFD + FRS) received a 60% HFD along with 25 mg/kg FRS solution daily at 1:00 pm for 12 weeks. Each group of mice was kept in a separate cage with unrestricted access to food and water. Mice’s weights were recorded daily, and feed was replenished every 2 days. Throughout the experiment, the mice’s physiological status was continuously monitored to evaluate any side effects of FRS on their development. During the experiment, the physiological status of the mice was continuously observed during the trial to ascertain whether the FRS has any side effects on their development.

Tissue preparation

Two days prior to gastric lavage, mice were fasted for 12 h overnight, and their fasting blood glucose and oral glucose tolerance were assessed the following morning. At the end of the treatment cycle, the mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital, blood was collected via heart puncture, and they were perfused intracardially with ice-cold saline. Tissues were isolated on ice, weighed, and divided; one portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, while the other was frozen in a sterile centrifuge tube at −80 °C for storage. For the blood sample, centrifuge at 4 °C and 3000 r/min for 15 min after standing at room temperature −4 °C for 2 h, collect the supernatant, and store it in a −80 °C freezer for later use.

Determination of biochemical indicators and antioxidant indicators in cells, serum, and liver of the mouse

On the 10th day of culturing 3T3-L1 cells, measure the concentrations of triglycerides (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) according to the instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Retrieve the collected mouse serum from the −80 °C freezer, thaw it on ice, and centrifuge at 4 °C and 3000 rpm for 15 min to obtain the supernatant. This supernatant was used to measure TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C in serum samples from each group of mice utilizing a fully automated biochemical analyzer. Additionally, the serum levels of liver function markers, ALT and AST, were determined in accordance with the kit instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China)53. Next, retrieve the mouse liver from the −80 °C freezer, thaw it on ice, weigh it, and homogenize it while maintaining an ice bath as requirements of the kit. After homogenization, the levels of MDA, SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT were measured (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China).

Histopathological evaluation

On day 10, differentiated 3T3-L1 cells were subjected to Oil Red O staining to assess lipid accumulation in adipocytes. First, the DMEM cell culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. After fixing the cells in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 8–10 min, they were washed twice with PBS to eliminate the fixative. A small volume of 60% isopropanol was then added to the plate to cover the cells for 15–20 s, after which the isopropanol was discarded, and the cells were allowed to dry slightly. The Oil Red O working solution was added to the well plate to cover the cells and was kept in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. After removing the staining solution, 60% isopropanol was used for rapid differentiation for 3–5 s, followed by three washes with pure water, each lasting 5 min. Finally, PBS was added to cover the cells for microscopic observation.

The liver and epididymal fat were preserved in 4% neutral formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin to a thickness of approximately 4 μm (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co Ltd, China). Following dewaxing, the tissue samples were analyzed using light microscopy with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Specimens of fixed liver and epididymal fat were sectioned with a cryostat for oil-red staining in accordance with established protocols. Microscopic images of the liver and epididymal fat were captured through scanning, and the diameters of adipocytes, along with the number of CLS per adipocyte area, were measured using ImageJ (Media Cybernetics, USA)54.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis

The mRNA expressions of lipid metabolism-related genes in 3T3-L1 cells and liver tissues were assessed using qPCR. Total RNA was extracted from liver samples with TRIzol reagent (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The mRNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Revert Aid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and qPCR was performed to measure the expression of lipid metabolism-related mRNA in 3T3-L1 cells and mouse liver by using qPCR. The primer pairs utilized for quantifying the expression levels in both 3T3-L1 cells and liver samples are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Subsequently, the relative mRNA expressions of the target mRNA were calculated relative to Gapdh expression.

Western blotting

Western blot analyses for the target proteins were conducted following the methodology outlined in our earlier research55,56 In brief, cell lysates from mouse liver tissues and cultured 3T3-L1 cells were prepared using protein lysis buffer (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, P0013). The protein concentration was assessed with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, P0012). A total of 25 μg of protein was resolved via 12% or 15% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Bio-Rad, L1620177 Rev D). The membrane was incubated in a 5% (w:v) skim milk solution for 2 h at room temperature, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting LC3B (Abmart, T55992, 1:1000), SQSTM1 (HUABIO, HA721171, 1:5000), and GAPDH (Servicebio, GB12002-100, 1:5000). The membranes were washed three times for 5 min each with Tris-buffered saline (Cell Signaling Technology, 9997) containing 0.1% Tween 20 (Sigma, P1379), and then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse (KPL, NCM Biotech, 474-1806; 1:10,000) or anti-rabbit (NCM Biotech, 474-1516; 1:10,000) IgG. The epitope was detected using an ECL Western Blot Detection Kit (Millipore, WBKLS0500). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA). GAPDH western blotting served as a loading control for measuring the densitometric ratios of LC3B-II to LC3B-I and SQSTM1.

Statistical analysis

All experimental results are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The figures were created using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Variance statistical significance was assessed using a one-way ANOVA, followed by the least significant difference post-hoc test or a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, as previously outlined in our previous study55. P value less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant14.
