Error-In-Variables Methods for Efficient System Identification with Finite-Sample Guarantees
Yuyang Zhang, Xinhe Zhang, Jia Liu, Na Li

Abstract
This paper addresses the problem of learning linear dynamical systems from noisy observations. In this setting, existing algorithms either yield biased parameter estimates or have large sample complexities. We resolve these issues by adapting the instrumental variable method and the bias compensation method, originally proposed for error-in-variables models, to our setting. We provide refined non-asymptotic analysis for both methods. Under mild conditions, our algorithms achieve superior sample complexities that match the best-known sample complexity for learning a fully observable system without observation noise.

In the context of linear systems, recent research primarily focuses on two classes of systems, i.e. fully observable and partially observable systems. In the fully observable setting, the entire state vectors are observed without observation noises. To learn the linear system models, it suffices to perform least-squares on the state and input trajectories. Recent studies

15

16

For an intermediate class of systems where the entire state vectors are observed but subject to observation noises, the aforementioned algorithms do not perform ideally. For detailed illustrations, consider the following linear system with
n
-dimensional states and
m
-dimensional inputs

\begin{split}x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t}+w_{t},\quad\widehat{x}_{t}=x_{t}+\eta_{t}.\end{split}

(1)

Here

denote the state, input and process noise, respectively.
\widehat{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\eta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the state observation and observation noise.
On one hand, directly applying least-squares on
(\widehat{x}_{t},u_{t})
and
\widehat{x}_{t+1}

and

Because both

are contaminated by observation noises, the above problem becomes an error-in-variable (EIV) problem
[
17
,
18
]
where the least-squares estimator is known to be biased and does not converge to the true parameter regardless of the number of samples.
On the other hand, we can treat the system as a partially observable system with an identity observer matrix

and apply Ho-Kalman-based algorithms. However, this approach leads to a suboptimal estimation error.
This is because under the standard controllability assumption, Ho-Kalman-based algorithms need to estimate the Markov parameters up to order

In this paper, we propose sample-efficient algorithms to learn the system in
Equation

1
. Specifically, we adapt two classical methods in the EIV problems, i.e. the instrumental variable (IV) method and the bias-compensation (BC) method
[
17
]
, to our setting. We provide finite-sample analysis of the proposed algorithms, establishing an error upper bound of order
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{(m+n)/T})
on the estimated system matrices under mild assumptions. Here
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
hides constants and logarithmic factors. Notably, this rate matches the error upper bound for learning a fully observable linear system without any observation noises
[
12
]
. Namely, our algorithms effectively mitigate the influence of the observation noises while only sacrificing some noise-related constants and logarithmic factors.
Achieving this tight upper bound requires careful non-asymptotic analysis, which is fundamentally different from the asymptotic analysis in existing EIV literature
[
17
,
18
]
.

II-C

The Naive Least-Squares Estimator

To better motivate our algorithms, we first analyze the naive least-squares estimator and illustrate the reason why it is biased in our setting.
For simplicity, we introduce the following auxiliary notations:

\begin{split}E=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix},\quad z_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}\\
u_{t}\end{bmatrix},\quad\widehat{z}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{x}_{t}\\
u_{t}\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

The system dynamics can then be rewritten as
x_{t+1}=Ez_{t}+w_{t}
.
Ideally, one would aim to solve for
\arg\min_{E}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|x_{t+1}-Ez_{t}\right\|^{2}
. However, because we only have access to noisy observations
\{\widehat{x}_{t+1}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
and
\{\widehat{z}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
, we could only resort to the alternative least-squares problem
\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{E}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\widehat{x}_{t+1}-E\widehat{z}_{t}\right\|^{2}}

The close-form solution to the alternative problem is

\begin{split}\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}=\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{x}_{t+1}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}.\end{split}

(2)

Notice that
\widehat{x}_{t+1}=E\left(\widehat{z}_{t}-\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{t}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right)+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1}=E\widehat{z}_{t}-A\eta_{t}+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1}
. Substituting this into
Equation

2
gives

\begin{split}\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}={}&E+\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(-A\eta_{t}+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1}\right)\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}.\end{split}

We note that the noise
-A\eta_{t}+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1}
is correlated with the measurement
\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}
, since both terms have
\eta_{t}
as a component. To see how this correlation influences the performance of
\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}
, we further decompose
\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}
as follows

\begin{split}\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}={}&E+\underbrace{\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}-A\eta_{t}z_{t}^{\intercal}+w_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}+\eta_{t+1}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}}_{\Delta_{1}}\\
{}&-\underbrace{\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}A\eta_{t}\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{t}\\
0\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}}_{\Delta_{2}}.\end{split}

(3)

For term
\Delta_{1}
,
\eta_{t}
is independent of
z_{1:t}
and
w_{t},\eta_{t+1}
are independent of
\widehat{z}_{1:t}
. Leveraging this independency, we can prove by the well-established self-normalizing bound
[
20
, Theorem 1]
that
\left\|\Delta_{1}\right\|\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\sqrt{T})
, which decays as the number of samples
T
grows. In contrast, for term
\Delta_{2}
, both
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\eta_{t}^{\intercal}
and
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}
are as large as
\mathcal{O}(T)I
. This implies that
\Delta_{2}
doesn’t vanish regardless of
T
. This persistent offset term leads to the bias in
\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}
.

To eliminate this bias, we introduce two methods in the following sections, i.e. the
instrumental variable (IV) method
and the
bias compensation (BC) method
, both adapted from previous research on single-input-single-output auto-regressive error-in-variable models
[
17
]
.

III
The Instrumental Variable (IV) Estimator

III-A

The IV Estimator and Its Design Rationale

The IV method eliminates the bias in
\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}
, or equivalently, eliminates the correlation between measurement
\widehat{z}_{t}
and noise
-A\eta_{t}+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1}
, by replacing
\widehat{z}_{t}
with an instrumental variable

\widehat{i}_{t}
and modifying the estimator as follows

\begin{split}{}&\widehat{E}_{\text{IV}}=\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{x}_{t+1}\widehat{i}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{i}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\\
={}&E+\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(-A\eta_{t}+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1})\widehat{i}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{i}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}.\end{split}

(4)

On the high level, we require instrumental variables
\widehat{i}_{1:t}
to be uncorrelated with
-A\eta_{t}+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1}
so that the norm of covariance
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(-A\eta_{t}+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1})\widehat{i}_{t}^{\intercal}
is small
2
2

2

Asymptotically, for this norm to be small, it only requires
\widehat{i}_{t}
to be uncorrelated with
-A\eta_{t}+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1}
. However, here we aim for a non-asymptotic upper bound and adopt a slightly strong requirement for technical reasons.
. Moreover, we require
\widehat{i}_{t}
to be well correlated with
\widehat{z}_{t}
so that the norm of
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{i}_{t}^{\intercal}
is large. Combining the two requirements would lead to a small error term
\widehat{E}_{\text{IV}}-E
.

It turns out that choosing

\begin{split}\widehat{i}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{x}_{t-1}\\
u_{t}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\end{split}

(5)

suffices.
To see this, we first note that
\widehat{i}_{1:t}
is uncorrelated with
-A\eta_{t}+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1}
. We can then prove
\left\|\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(-A\eta_{t}+w_{t}+\eta_{t+1})\widehat{i}_{t}^{\intercal}\right\|\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{T}\right)
.
Secondly, we show
\widehat{i}_{t}
strongly correlates with measurement
\widehat{z}_{t}
by analyzing their covariance
\mathbb{E}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{i}_{t}^{\intercal}=\mathbb{E}\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{x}_{t}\widehat{x}_{t-1}^{\intercal}&\widehat{x}_{t}u_{t}^{\intercal}\\
u_{t}\widehat{x}_{t-1}^{\intercal}&u_{t}u_{t}^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}
.
For the upper left block, we know
\mathbb{E}\left(\widehat{x}_{t}\widehat{x}_{t-1}^{\intercal}\right)=\mathbb{E}(Ax_{t-1}+Bu_{t-1}+w_{t-1}+\eta_{t})\left(x_{t-1}+\eta_{t-1}\right)^{\intercal}=A\mathbb{E}\left(x_{t-1}x_{t-1}^{\intercal}\right)
because
u_{t-1},w_{t-1},\eta_{t-1}
and
\eta_{t}
are independently sampled from zero-mean distributions. Similarly, we conclude that the off diagonal blocks are
0
. It then naturally follows that,
\mathbb{E}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{i}_{t}^{\intercal}=\begin{bmatrix}A\mathbb{E}\left(x_{t-1}x_{t-1}\right)&0\\
0&\Sigma_{u}\end{bmatrix}
, which is invertible as long as
A
is invertible. Utilizing this key insight, we can prove that
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{i}_{t}^{\intercal}\succeq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T)I
for any invertible
A
. Combining the above two inequalities, we can conclude
\left\|\widehat{E}_{\text{IV}}-E\right\|\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\sqrt{T})
, which decays to
0
as
T
tends to infinity.

Remark 1
.

If the target system is autonomous, i.e.
x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+w_{t},\widehat{x}_{t}=x_{t}+\eta_{t}
, we can adapt the above IV estimator as follows

\begin{split}\widehat{A}_{\text{IV}}=\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\widehat{x}_{t+1}\widehat{x}_{t-1}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\widehat{x}_{t}\widehat{x}_{t-1}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}.\end{split}

The theoretical results in the next subsection can be extended to this setting with minor modifications.∎

IV
The Bias Compensation (BC) Estimator

IV-A

The BC Estimator and Its Design Rationale

In the bias compensation method, we assume access to an estimation of the observation noise covariance
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta}
that satisfies the following inequality for some constant
\epsilon_{\eta}

\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta}-\Sigma_{\eta}\right\|\leq\epsilon_{\eta}.

(18)

With
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta}
, we can directly estimate the bias in the naive least-squares estimator
\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}
and compensate for it by designing a multiplicative correction term.

To derive the correction term, we take a deeper look into
\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}
(
Equation

3
)

\begin{split}\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}={}&E+\Delta_{1}-E\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{t}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{t}\\
0\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\\
={}&E+\Delta_{1}-E\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{\eta}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\\
{}&+E\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{\eta}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\eta_{t}^{\intercal}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\\
\end{split}

As we discussed in
Section

II-C
, we can show that
\left\|\Delta_{1}\right\|\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\sqrt{T})
. Moreover, by a standard covariance concentration lemma, e.g.
[
19
, Lemma A.5]
, we can show that
\|\Sigma_{\eta}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t-0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\eta_{t}\|\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\sqrt{T})
. Therefore, we can rewrite
\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}
as follows

\begin{split}\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}={}&E\left(I-\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{\eta}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\right)I.\end{split}

Therefore, with
\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta}
(
Equation

18
), we can compensate for the bias as follows

\displaystyle\widehat{E}_{\text{BC}}=\widehat{E}_{\text{LS}}\left(I-\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\right)^{-1}.

(19)

Remark 2
.

If the target system is autonomous, i.e.
x_{t+1}=Ax_{t}+w_{t},\widehat{x}_{t}=x_{t}+\eta_{t}
, we can easily adapt the above estimator as follows

\begin{split}\widehat{A}_{\text{BC}}={}&\widehat{A}_{\text{LS}}\left(I-\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{x}_{t}\widehat{x}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}\right)^{-1},\\
\widehat{A}_{\text{LS}}={}&\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{x}_{t+1}\widehat{x}_{t}^{\intercal}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{x}_{t}\widehat{x}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}.\end{split}

Our proof techniques can be extended to this setting with minor modifications.∎

In the BC estimator, the invertibility of matrix
I-\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\Sigma}_{\eta}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}
can be ensured if the minimal singular value
\sigma_{m+n}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\widehat{z}_{t}\widehat{z}_{t}^{\intercal}\right)
is large. Intuitively, this can be easily satisfied by injecting large enough inputs
\{u_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
into the system. The exact requirements are summarized in

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we focus on learning linear dynamical systems with observation noises. We propose two estimators and provide non-asymptotic guarantee of their performance. The IV estimator requires the dynamic matrix
A
to be invertible and achieves an error upper bound of order
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{(m+n)/T})
. This order matches the best-known error upper bound for learning a fully observable system without observation noises. The BC estimator requires an estimation of the observation noise covariance and achieves an error upper bound of order
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{(m+n)/T}+\epsilon_{\eta})
, where
\epsilon_{\eta}
is the error of the observation noise covariance estimation.
