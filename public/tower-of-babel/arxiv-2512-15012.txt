Isomorphism between Jacobi forms of index D 2 ​ n + 1 D_{2n+1} and elliptic modular forms of level 2 2
Shuichi Hayashida

Abstract
. There are three aims in this paper: (i) We show an isomorphism between Jacobi forms of index D 2 ​ n + 1 D_{2n+1} (lattice index) and elliptic modular forms of level 2 2 . (ii) We give an explicit formula of Fourier coefficients of Jacobi-Eisenstein series of index D 2 ​ n + 1 D_{2n+1} . (iii) We construct a holomorphic modular form of weight 3 / 2 3/2 of level 8 8 from the Zagier Eisenstein series ℱ \mathscr{F} of weight 3 / 2 3/2 of level 4 4 . Moreover, we show that the four functions E 2 ∗ E^{*}_{2} , η 3 \eta^{3} , θ 3 \theta^{3} and ℱ \mathscr{F} have essentially the same Hecke eigenvalue 1 + p 1+p for any odd prime p p , where E 2 ∗ E^{*}_{2} is the non-holomorphic Eisenstein series of weight 2 2 , η \eta is the Dedekind eta-function and θ \theta is the usual theta function. This fact follows from a special case of the isomorphism of (i). As an application, we give a formula for a sum of the numbers r 3 ​ ( n ) r_{3}(n) , where r 3 ​ ( n ) r_{3}(n) is the number of representations of an integer n ≥ 0 n\geq 0 as a sum of 3 3 squares.

2.
Jacobi forms of lattice index

2.1.
Basic properties

We denote by
{\mathfrak{H}}
the Poincare upper half space.
We put
e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}
.
We write
q:=e(\tau)
for
\tau\in{\mathfrak{H}}
.
For each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we take the argument
-\pi<\arg(z)\leq\pi
and set

z^{m/2}:=(z^{1/2})^{m}
for
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let
L
be a free
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module of finite rank
n
equipped with a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-valued symmetric binary form
\beta
.
In this paper we assume that
\beta
is positive-definite.
Such a pair
\underline{L}=(L,\beta)
is called an
integral lattice
of rank
n
.
By abuse of language, we define
\beta(x):=\frac{1}{2}\beta(x,x)
.
Sometimes we use
x\mapsto\beta(x)
, instead of
(x,y)\mapsto\beta(x,y)
.
If
\beta(x)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for any
x\in L
, then
\underline{L}
is called an
even
integral lattice.

For example, if
M
is a positive-definite half-integral symmetric matrix of size
n
,
then

\displaystyle({\mathbb{Z}}^{n},(x,y)\mapsto x(2M){{}^{t}y})

is an even integral lattice.

We put

\displaystyle L^{\sharp}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{x\in L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}\ :\ \beta(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\mbox{ for any $y\in L$}\right\}.

We fix a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-basis
\{e_{i}\}_{i}
of
L
, and we identify
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
with
L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{C}}

by
(x_{1},...,x_{n})\mapsto\sum_{i}x_{i}e_{i}
.

Let
k
be an integer

Definition 2.1
.

A holomorphic function
\phi(\tau,z)
on
{\mathfrak{H}}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is called a Jacobi form of weight
k
and index
\underline{L}
,
if
\phi
satisfies the following three conditions:

(i)

For all
A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mbox{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
,
the function
\phi
satisfies

\displaystyle(\phi|_{k,\underline{L}}A)(\tau,z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\phi\!\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\frac{1}{c\tau+d}z\right)e\!\left(\frac{-c\beta(z)}{c\tau+d}\right)(c\tau+d)^{-k}\ =\ \phi(\tau,z).

(ii)

For all
\lambda
,
\mu

\in L
, the function
\phi
satisfies

\displaystyle(\phi|_{k,\underline{L}}(\lambda,\mu))(\tau,z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\phi(\tau,z+\lambda\tau+\mu)e(\tau\beta(\lambda)+\beta(\lambda,z))\ =\ \phi(\tau,z).

(iii)

The function
\phi(\tau,z)
has a Fourier expansion of the form

\displaystyle\phi(\tau,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\sum_{r^{\prime}\in L^{\sharp}}c(n^{\prime},r^{\prime})e(n^{\prime}\tau+\beta(r^{\prime},z)),

where
c(n^{\prime},r^{\prime})=0
unless
n^{\prime}-\beta(r^{\prime})\geq 0
.

The complex numbers
c(n^{\prime},r^{\prime})
are called the
(n^{\prime},r^{\prime})
-th Fourier coefficients of
\phi
.

If
c(n^{\prime},r^{\prime})=0
unless
n^{\prime}-\beta(r^{\prime})>0
in
(\rm{iii})
, then
\phi
is called a Jacobi cusp form.

We denote by
J_{k,\underline{L}}

(
resp.
J^{cusp}_{k,\underline{L}}
)
the space of all Jacobi forms

(
resp. Jacobi cusp forms
)
of weight
k
of index
\underline{L}
.

In Definition
2.1
, if the lattice
\underline{L}=(L,\beta)
coincides with

({\mathbb{Z}}^{n},x\mapsto\beta(x)=xM{{}^{t}x})

with a positive-definite half-integral symmetric matrix
M
of size
n
,
then
\phi
is called a Jacobi form of weight
k
of index
M
(see
[
Zi 89
]
).
We denote by
J_{k,M}
the space of all Jacobi forms of weight
k

of index
M
.

Let
\underline{L}=(L,\beta)
be an integral lattice with a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-basis
\{e_{1},...,e_{n}\}
of
L
.
The matrix
2M=(\beta(e_{i},e_{j}))_{i,j}
is called the
Gram matrix
of
\beta
with respect to
\{e_{1},...,e_{n}\}
.
Then
L\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
,
L^{\sharp}\cong(2M)^{-1}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and
L^{\sharp}/L\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}/(2M{\mathbb{Z}}^{n})
.
Moreover, if
\underline{L}
is an even integral lattice, then we have
J_{k,\underline{L}}\cong J_{k,M}
(see
[
B-S 23
, §1]
).

2.2.
Discriminant modules

For an even integral lattice
\underline{L}=(L,\beta)
,
the discriminant module

D_{\underline{L}}
of
\underline{L}
is a pair defined by

\displaystyle D_{\underline{L}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left(L^{\sharp}/L,x+L\mapsto\beta(x)+{\mathbb{Z}}\right)

(see
[
B-S 23
, §2]
).
We remark that
L^{\sharp}/L
is a finite abelian group and
x+L\mapsto\beta(x)+{\mathbb{Z}}

is the associated quadratic form.

Two discriminant modules
D_{\underline{L_{i}}}=(L_{i}^{\sharp}/L_{i},x+L_{i}\rightarrow\beta_{i}(x)+{\mathbb{Z}})

(i=1,2)
are called isomorphic,
if there exists a homomorphism
j:L_{1}^{\sharp}/L_{1}\rightarrow L_{2}^{\sharp}/L_{2}

which satisfies
\beta_{2}(j(x))+{\mathbb{Z}}=\beta_{1}(x)+{\mathbb{Z}}
.

2.3.
Theta functions

For

The following formulas are well known.

.

For

\displaystyle\theta_{\underline{L},r_{i}}(\tau+1,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e(\beta(r_{i}))\theta_{\underline{L},r_{i}}(\tau,z)

and

(2.1)

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\theta_{\underline{L},r_{i}}(-\tau^{-1},\tau^{-1}z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\tau}{i}\right)^{n/2}e(\tau^{-1}\beta(z))\frac{1}{\sqrt{|L^{\sharp}/L|}}\sum_{r_{j}\in L^{\sharp}/L}e(-\beta(r_{i},r_{j}))\theta_{\underline{L},r_{j}}(\tau,z).

(
See
[
Ar 98
, page 100]
,
[
Aj 15
, Theorem 2.3.3]
,
[
Boy 11
, Cor. 3.34]
,
[
B-S 23
, page 16]
, etc.
)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\phi\in J_{k,\underline{L}}
be a Jacobi form. Then,
\phi
has the decomposition:

\displaystyle\phi(\tau,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in L^{\sharp}/\penalty L}h_{x}(\tau)\theta_{\underline{L},x}(\tau,z)\mbox{ with }h_{x}(\tau)\ =\ \sum_{n^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c(n^{\prime},x)e^{2\pi i(n^{\prime}-\beta(x))\tau}.

We denote by

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e(m^{2}\tau).

\displaystyle A\mapsto(A,j(A,\tau))

We set

\frac{A(0)}{2(1+\left(\frac{8}{r}\right)2^{k})}L(1-k,\left(\frac{(-1)^{k}d_{0}}{*}\right))

due to the definition of the map
S_{d_{0}}
.
On the other hand, the constant term of
A(d_{0})\mathbb{G}_{2k}
is

A(d_{0})\frac{\zeta(1-2k)}{2}
.
Thus we need to show

(9.8)

\displaystyle\frac{A(d_{0})}{A(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L(1-k,\left(\frac{(-1)^{k}d_{0}}{*}\right))}{(1+\left(\frac{8}{r}\right)2^{k})\zeta(1-2k)}.

We recall that
A(0)=e_{r,k}(0,0)
and
A(d_{0})=e_{r,k}(n^{\prime},r^{\prime})
for
d_{0}=8(n^{\prime}-\beta(r^{\prime}))
.
By virtue of Theorem
8.4
and Lemma
8.2
, and by assumption on
d_{0}
,
we have
A(0)=1
and
A(d_{0})=\frac{H(k,(-1)^{k}d_{0})}{(1+\left(\frac{8}{r}\right)2^{k})\zeta(1-2k)}
.
Since,
H(k,(-1)^{k}d_{0})=L(1-k,\left(\frac{(-1)^{k}d_{0}}{*}\right))
, the identity (
9.8
) holds.
Therefore we have
S_{d_{0}}(E_{k+\frac{r+1}{2},D_{r}})=A(d_{0})\mathbb{G}_{2k}\in M_{2k}(1)
.

Since
S_{d_{0}}
is a linear map,
for any
\phi\in J_{k+\frac{r+1}{2},D_{r}}=J_{k+\frac{r+1}{2},D_{r}}^{cusp,new}\oplus J_{k+\frac{r+1}{2},D_{r}}^{cusp,old}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}E_{k+\frac{r+1}{2},D_{r}}
we obtain
S_{d_{0}}(\phi)=A(d_{0})f

with a
f\in M_{2k}^{new,\epsilon_{2}}(2)\oplus M_{2k}^{\epsilon_{1}}(1)
.
We conclude Theorem
1.8
.

Proof of Theorem
1.9
.

The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
1.8
.
By virtue of the identity (
5.5
), we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{p}A(m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{-4}{p}\right)\left\{A(p^{2}m)+\left(\frac{(-1)^{k}m}{p}\right)p^{k-1}A(m)+p^{2k-1}A(m/p^{2})\right\},

for
m\equiv-r\mod 8
and for any odd prime
p
, where
\lambda_{p}
denotes
the Hecke eigenvalue of
\phi
for
T^{J}(p)
.

Let
f\in S_{2k}^{new,\epsilon_{2}}(2)
be a Hecke eigenform which corresponds to
\phi\in J_{k+\frac{r+1}{2},D_{r}}^{cusp,new}

by Theorem
1.3
.
We take the Fourier expansion
f=\sum a_{f}(n)q^{n}
and assume
a_{f}(1)=1
.

By an argument analogous to the calculation of (
9.3
), if
m
is not divisible by
p^{2}
for any odd prime
p
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{-4}{n_{1}}\right)\sum_{d|n_{1}}d^{k-1}\left(\frac{(-1)^{k}m}{d}\right)A(n^{2}d^{-2}m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A(m)a_{f}(n_{1})

for any odd integer
n_{1}
.

On the other hand, since
f
belongs to
S_{2k}^{new,\epsilon_{2}}(2)
, we have

f|_{2k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\epsilon_{2}i^{-2k}f
.
And it is known that

f|_{2k}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)=-2^{1-k}a_{f}(2)f
(see
[
Zh 13
, Introduction]
).
Thus we have

\displaystyle a_{f}(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon_{2}i^{-2k}2^{k-1}\ =\ \left(\frac{-8}{r}\right)(-1)^{k}2^{k-1}\ =\ \left(\frac{-8}{r}\right)(-1)^{\frac{r-1}{2}}2^{k-1}\ =\ \left(\frac{8}{r}\right)2^{k-1}.

Here we used the assumption
k+\frac{r+1}{2}\equiv 1\mod 2
.
The arithmetic function
a_{f}(*)
is multiplicative and the identity
a_{f}(2^{e_{2}})=a_{f}(2)^{e_{2}}
holds.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\left(\left(\frac{8}{r}\right)2^{k-1}\right)^{e_{2}}\left(\frac{-4}{n_{1}}\right)\sum_{d|n_{1}}d^{k-1}\left(\frac{(-1)^{k}d_{0}}{d}\right)A(n^{2}d^{-2}d_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A(d_{0})a(2^{e_{2}})a(n_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A(d_{0})a(2^{e_{2}}n_{1})

for any fundamental discriminant
(-1)^{k-1}d_{0}
such that
d_{0}\equiv-r\mod 8
and
d_{0}>0
.

We conclude
S_{d_{0}}(\phi)=A(d_{0})f\in S_{2k}^{new,\epsilon_{2}}(2)
for any Hecke eigenform
\phi\in J_{k+\frac{r+1}{2},D_{r}}^{cusp,new}
.
Since the map
S_{d_{0}}
is linear,
we conclude Theorem
1.9
.
