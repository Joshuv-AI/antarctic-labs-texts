Epidemic Spreading with Co-infection on Long-Range Random Networks
Frank Namugera

Abstract
We study a discrete-time co-infection epidemic model on spatial networks incorporating both random and long-range interactions. Using numerical analysis, we investigate how the co-infection recovery rate ( μ \mu ), the interaction distance decay exponent ( α \alpha ), and network connectivity parameters affect epidemic persistence. Our results show that lower values of μ \mu facilitate long-term survival, and that the presence of long-range connections ( α > 0 \alpha>0 ) robustly enhances persistence, often outweighing the effects of co-infection dynamics and local connectivity. These findings highlight nontrivial threshold behavior and the critical role of long-range structure in heterogeneous, nonlocal epidemic systems.



Key words
: Co-infection, Epidemic Spreading, Random environment, Long range decay.

1
Introduction

The spread of infectious diseases is strongly influenced by the underlying structure and heterogeneity of contact networks
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Network-based models have become essential tools for understanding and predicting epidemic outcomes, particularly under conditions of spatial randomness, long-range interaction, and stochastic transmission. These models not only offer theoretical insights but also guide real-world interventions, as evidenced by their widespread use during the COVID-19 pandemic
[
11
]
.

In a graph-based framework, individuals are represented as nodes in a graph
G=(V,E,W)
, where edges
E
denote potential contacts and weights
W
represent heterogeneous transmission intensities or environmental variability. The state of the system evolves stochastically over time, typically governed by discrete-time or continuous-time Markovian dynamics over a finite state space.

While classical epidemic models have focused on single-strain dynamics, real-world epidemics often involve co-circulating pathogens. Co-infections, which are simultaneous infections by multiple strains, are known to modulate transmissibility, recovery, and disease severity
[
12
,
15
]
. These interactions may be competitive, where one pathogen suppresses another, or synergistic, where co-infection increases transmission or virulence. Despite their epidemiological relevance, most existing models treat co-infection in simplified settings, typically on homogeneous or locally connected graphs
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

In this work, we develop a discrete-time, dual-strain epidemic model that integrates co-infection
[
16
]
, long-range transmission
[
5
]
, and environmental heterogeneity
[
4
]
. Each node occupies one of four states: susceptible (S), infected by strain A, infected by strain B, or co-infected (AB), yielding a state space
\mathcal{S}=\{S,A,B,AB\}
. Transmission occurs through long-range interactions governed by a distance-weighted kernel:

P_{ij}\propto\frac{\omega_{ij}}{d_{ij}^{\alpha}},

where
d_{ij}
is the shortest-path distance between nodes
i
and
j
,
\alpha>0
controls the spatial decay, and
\omega_{ij}\sim\mathcal{U}(a,b)
represents random environmental variability from a uniform distribution. This model generalizes distance-based epidemic models
[
7
]
and aligns with recent efforts to model mobility-driven contagion
[
11
]
.

The model exhibits rich dynamical behavior, including phase transitions between extinction and persistence, as well as regimes of strain dominance and coexistence. These transitions depend on parameters such as the infection rate
\lambda
, the co-infection recovery rate
\mu
, the spatial decay exponent
\alpha
, and the underlying network connectivity.

To analyze these phenomena, we study the spectral properties of the effective transmission operator and derive thresholds for epidemic survival. Our numerical simulations validate these theoretical predictions and reveal nontrivial survival behavior driven by the interplay of coinfection dynamics, network structure, and spatial decay. Overall, this work offers a general and flexible framework for understanding multi-strain epidemic processes in spatially structured and stochastic environments.

The following sections present the model descriptions, analysis, simulation and results.

2
Model Description

We extend our earlier formalism in
[
16
]
for the discrete-time symbiosis model for an SIS (Susceptible-Infected-Susceptible) model where no permanent recoveries are possible.
Consider a finite graph
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where each node
i\in\mathcal{V}
represents an individual and edges
\mathcal{E}
encode potential transmission pathways. As already noted, each node may occupy one of four epidemic states: susceptible (S), infected by strain A, infected by strain B, or coinfected by both (AB), corresponding to a state space
\mathcal{S}=\{S,A,B,AB\}
. Let
p_{i,t}^{A}
,
p_{i,t}^{B}
, and
p_{i,t}^{AB}
denote the probabilities that node
i
is in state A, B, or AB at discrete time
t
, respectively. The system evolves via a nonlinear, stochastic difference equation, capturing both transmission and recovery processes. An infected node with either
A
or
B
recovers at a uniform rate of
\delta
however the co-infected node recovers at a rate
\mu<\delta
. The general update rules for the infection probabilities are given by:

\displaystyle p_{i,t+1}^{A}

\displaystyle=(1-p_{i,t}^{A})(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{A})+(1-\delta)\zeta_{i,t+1}^{B}p_{i,t}^{A}+\delta p_{i,t}^{A}(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{A})+\tfrac{1}{2}\mu p_{i,t}^{AB},

(2.1)

\displaystyle p_{i,t+1}^{B}

\displaystyle=(1-p_{i,t}^{B})(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{B})+(1-\delta)\zeta_{i,t+1}^{A}p_{i,t}^{B}+\delta p_{i,t}^{B}(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{B})+\tfrac{1}{2}\mu p_{i,t}^{AB},

(2.2)

\displaystyle p_{i,t+1}^{AB}

\displaystyle=(1-p_{i,t}^{AB})(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{AB})+(1-\mu)p_{i,t}^{AB}+\mu p_{i,t}^{AB}(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{AB}).

(2.3)

The dynamics in Eqs. (
2.1
)–(
2.3
) reflect: (i) primary infection from susceptible to A or B; (ii) secondary infections converting A to AB or B to AB; (iii) coinfection recovery transitions from AB to A or B at rate
\mu
; and (iv) persistence or reinfection processes modeled via
\delta\in[0,1]
, the reinfection sensitivity parameter.

Transmission is mediated by a long-range kernel, with the avoidance probability from strain
K\in\{A,B\}
defined by:

\displaystyle\zeta_{i,t+1}^{K}=\prod_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{LR}(i)}\left(1-\beta_{ji}^{K}p_{j,t}^{K}\right)\left(1-\beta_{ji}^{AB}p_{j,t}^{AB}\right),

(2.4)

where
\mathcal{N}_{LR}(i)
denotes the set of all long-range potential contacts of node
i
, and:

\displaystyle\beta_{ji}^{K}=\lambda_{K}\cdot\omega_{ji}\cdot d_{ji}^{-\alpha},

(2.5)

represents the effective transmission probability from node
j
to node
i
, decaying with distance
d_{ji}
, scaled by a strain-specific infectivity
\lambda_{K}
, and modulated by random environmental weight
r_{ji}\in[0,1]
, drawn from a uniform distribution. For coinfection, the probability of avoiding AB infection is governed by a nonlinear coupling:

\displaystyle\zeta_{i,t+1}^{AB}=\prod_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{LR}(i)}\left(1-\beta_{ji}^{AB}\left(p_{i,t}^{A}\rho_{j}^{B}+p_{i,t}^{B}\rho_{j}^{A}\right)\right),

(2.6)

where

\displaystyle\rho_{j}^{A}=p_{j,t}^{A}+p_{j,t}^{AB},\quad\rho_{j}^{B}=p_{j,t}^{B}+p_{j,t}^{AB},

(2.7)

encode the total infectious pressure from strain A and B at node
j
, including coinfected individuals.

2.1
Nonlinear Analysis

We now analyze the discrete-time dynamics of the nonlinear contact process defined in Eqs. (
2.1
)–(
2.7
). Let the infection state at time
t
be:

\mathbf{p}_{t}:=\left(p_{i,t}^{A},p_{i,t}^{B},p_{i,t}^{AB}\right)_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\in[0,1]^{3n},

where
n=|\mathcal{V}|
is the number of nodes. Define the feasible state space as the compact hypercube
\Omega:=[0,1]^{3n}
. The first proposition evaluates if the model is a well-constructed probability measure.

Proposition 2.1

(Well-Posedness of the Dynamics)
.

Let
\mathbf{F}:\Omega\to\Omega
be the update map defined by the system (
2.1
)–(
2.7
). Then:

1.

(Continuity)
The map
\mathbf{F}
is continuous on
\Omega
.

2.

(Forward-Invariance)
The image of
\Omega
is contained in
\Omega
, i.e.,
\mathbf{F}(\Omega)\subseteq\Omega
.

Proof.

(1) Continuity.

Each component of the map
\mathbf{F}
involves finite sums and products of continuous functions of the infection probabilities
p_{j,t}^{A},p_{j,t}^{B},p_{j,t}^{AB}\in[0,1]
. The probabilities
\zeta_{i,t+1}^{A},\zeta_{i,t+1}^{B},\zeta_{i,t+1}^{AB}
are finite products over the (long-range) neighborhood:

\zeta_{i,t+1}^{K}=\prod_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{LR}(i)}(1-\beta_{ji}^{K}p_{j,t}^{K})(1-\beta_{ji}^{AB}p_{j,t}^{AB}),\quad K\in\{A,B\},

\zeta_{i,t+1}^{AB}=\prod_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{LR}(i)}\left(1-\beta_{ji}^{AB}\left(p_{i,t}^{A}\rho_{j}^{B}+p_{i,t}^{B}\rho_{j}^{A}\right)\right),

with
\rho_{j}^{A}=p_{j,t}^{A}+p_{j,t}^{AB}
, and
\rho_{j}^{B}=p_{j,t}^{B}+p_{j,t}^{AB}
. Each term in the product is continuous in its arguments, and the product of finitely many continuous functions is continuous. Therefore,
\zeta_{i,t+1}^{K}
and
\zeta_{i,t+1}^{AB}
are continuous in
\mathbf{p}_{t}
. Since all updates in
\mathbf{F}
are composed of continuous operations (addition, multiplication, scalar multiplication, subtraction), it follows that
\mathbf{F}
is continuous on
\Omega
.

(2) Forward-Invariance.

We show that for any
\mathbf{p}_{t}\in\Omega
, the updated state
\mathbf{p}_{t+1}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{p}_{t})\in\Omega
, i.e., all updated infection probabilities remain in
[0,1]
.

Fix
i\in\mathcal{V}
, then each term in the update equations for
p_{i,t+1}^{A}
,
p_{i,t+1}^{B}
, and
p_{i,t+1}^{AB}
is a product of values in
[0,1]
, and all coefficients such as
\delta,\mu,\lambda_{K},r_{ji},\beta_{ji}^{K}
are also in
[0,1]
.

Let us analyze each update:

For strain A:

\displaystyle p_{i,t+1}^{A}

\displaystyle=(1-p_{i,t}^{A})(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{A})+\delta p_{i,t}^{A}(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{A})+(1-\delta)\zeta_{i,t+1}^{B}p_{i,t}^{A}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mu p_{i,t}^{AB}.

Each term on the right-hand side is a product of quantities in
[0,1]
. Therefore,

0\leq p_{i,t+1}^{A}\leq(1-p_{i,t}^{A})+p_{i,t}^{A}+p_{i,t}^{AB}\leq 2,

but this upper bound is loose. More sharply, since all coefficients are convex weights
(\leq 1)
and
\zeta\in[0,1]
, we find that the sum of these four terms is at most:

(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{A})+\zeta_{i,t+1}^{B}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mu\leq 2.

But importantly, all terms involve existing probabilities and weights in [0,1], and no negative terms are present. Moreover, note that:

p_{i,t+1}^{A}\leq(1-p_{i,t}^{A}+\delta p_{i,t}^{A})(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{A})+(1-\delta)p_{i,t}^{A}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mu p_{i,t}^{AB}\leq 1.

Hence,
p_{i,t+1}^{A}\in[0,1]
. The same reasoning applies to
p_{i,t+1}^{B}
.

For coinfection AB:

p_{i,t+1}^{AB}=(1-p_{i,t}^{AB})(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{AB})+(1-\mu)p_{i,t}^{AB}+\mu p_{i,t}^{AB}(1-\zeta_{i,t+1}^{AB}).

Again, all terms lie in
[0,1]
, and:

p_{i,t+1}^{AB}\leq(1-p_{i,t}^{AB})+p_{i,t}^{AB}=1,

and clearly
p_{i,t+1}^{AB}\geq 0
.

Therefore, for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
,
\left(p_{i,t+1}^{A},p_{i,t+1}^{B},p_{i,t+1}^{AB}\right)\in[0,1]^{3}
, and hence
\mathbf{p}_{t+1}\in\Omega
. This proves forward-invariance.
∎

2.2
Linearization at the Disease-Free Equilibrium

Define the disease-free equilibrium (DFE) as
\mathbf{p}^{*}=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3n}
, corresponding to zero prevalence of all strains. To study local stability of this point, we compute the Jacobian matrix
J:=D\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{0})
of the discrete-time map
\mathbf{F}
, linearized about
\mathbf{p}=0
.

Let
W_{K}:=\lambda_{K}(R\circ D^{-\alpha})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denote the weighted infection matrix for strain
K\in\{A,B,AB\}
, where
R\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
contains the random environmental weights, and
D^{-\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
encodes the distance-based decay.

The Jacobian matrix
J\in\mathbb{R}^{3n\times 3n}
, acting on the state vector
\mathbf{p}=(p^{A},p^{B},p^{AB})
, has the following block upper-triangular form:

J=\begin{bmatrix}W_{A}+(1-\delta)\mathbf{I}&0&W_{AB}\tfrac{1}{2}\mu I_{n}\\
0&W_{B}+(1-\delta)\mathbf{I}&W_{AB}\tfrac{1}{2}\mu I_{n}\\
0&0&(1-\mu)I_{n}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where
W_{A}
,
W_{B}
and
W_{AB}
describe the linearized infection propagation from neighbors for strains A, B and AB,
\mu\in[0,1]
is the rate at which individuals with co-infection resolve into single-strain infections,
I_{n}
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix.

2.3
Epidemic Threshold and Stability

Theorem 2.2

(Epidemic Threshold and Local Stability)
.

Let
W_{K}:=\lambda_{K}(R\circ D^{-\alpha})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denote the infection matrix for strain
K\in\{A,B\}
, and let
\rho(W_{K})
denote its spectral radius. Consider the linearized map at the disease-free equilibrium
\mathbf{p}^{*}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{3n}
. Then,
the disease-free equilibrium is
locally asymptotically stable
if

\rho(W_{A}+(1-\delta)\mathbf{I})<1\quad\text{and}\quad\rho(W_{B}+(1-\delta)\mathbf{I})<1.

otherwise, it’s linearly unstable.

Proof.

We analyze the linear stability of the discrete-time nonlinear system
\mathbf{p}_{t+1}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{p}_{t})
near the disease-free fixed point
\mathbf{p}^{*}=0
. Since
J
is upper triangular, its eigenvalues are the union of the spectra of the diagonal blocks and the eigenvalues of a block upper-triangular matrix are the eigenvalues of its diagonal blocks, we have:

\operatorname{spec}(J)=\operatorname{spec}\big((1-\delta)I_{n}+W_{A}\big)\cup\operatorname{spec}\big((1-\delta)I_{n}+W_{B}\big)\cup\{1-\mu\}.

For discrete-time dynamics, local stability of
\mathbf{p}^{*}
requires all eigenvalues of
J
to lie strictly inside the unit circle, i.e.,
\rho(J)<1
. Since
1-\mu<1
whenever
\mu>0
, it does not affect stability. Thus, stability depends on the spectral radii of the top-left blocks.

Therefore,
\mathbf{p}^{*}
is locally asymptotically stable if and only if both
\rho((1-\delta)I_{n}+W_{A})<1
and
\rho((1-\delta)I_{n}+W_{B})<1
; otherwise,
\rho(J)>1
and
\mathbf{p}^{*}
is unstable.

∎

The threshold takes the form
\tau=1/\rho(J)
, and persistence occurs if
\lambda_{K}/\delta>\tau
. Increasing distance or decay exponent
\alpha
suppresses transmission by reducing
\rho(W_{K})
, while higher randomness in
R
amplifies spectral weight and lowers the threshold. The coinfection rate
\mu
influences later dynamics but does not affect the initial instability of the disease-free state. Thus, epidemic onset is controlled by the interplay of spatial structure, environmental variability, and intrinsic transmission-to-recovery ratios.

3
Numerical results and Discussion

We tune the infection strength
\lambda
and simulate the spread of two interacting infection strains (A and B) on a stochastic
long-range network
that combines spatial structure with random connections. The network is generated using an Erdos-Renyi model
G(n,p)
with
n=200
nodes and the probability of connection
p_{\text{edge}}
. Pairwise distances
D_{ij}
are computed for all node pairs, and random weights are assigned to each edge from a uniform distribution, introducing environmental heterogeneity into the transmission dynamics.

We adopt the simulation framework introduced by
[
16
]
, modeling the epidemic dynamics on three coupled networks corresponding to strains A, B, and the co-infection strain AB. The initial condition assigns infection to
5\%
of the nodes independently in the networks corresponding to strains A and B. The system then evolves according to the probabilistic update rules defined in Equations (
2.1
)–(
2.3
), which incorporate contributions from all nodes in a long-range network structure, distance-dependent transmission, stochastic environmental weights, and nonlinear co-infection dynamics.

At each discrete time step, the infection probability at each node is updated based on interactions with all other nodes, modulated by the spatial decay exponent
\alpha
, random transmission weights, and the co-infection recovery parameter
\mu
. We systematically vary the parameters
\alpha
,
\mu
, and the network connectivity probability
p_{\text{edge}}
to investigate how spatial coupling, recovery dynamics, and topological sparsity influence the evolution and persistence of infection.

3.1
Coinfection Recovery Effects in Long-Range Epidemics

For fixed infection rate
\lambda
, edge connectivity
p_{\text{edge}}
, and decay exponent
\alpha
, decreasing
\mu
i.e., slowing recovery from the coinfected state substantially increases the probability of epidemic survival
\phi
, even when the baseline recovery rate
\delta
remains fixed. Formally, we observe that if

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\phi(\mu_{1})>\phi(\mu_{2}),\quad\text{for fixed }\lambda,\alpha,p_{\text{edge}}.

This reflects an intrinsic asymmetry in the dual-strain model that is, coinfected individuals act as disproportionately strong sources of infection due to their higher transmissibility and delayed recovery.

The effect of
\mu
is most pronounced in the lower-
\alpha
regime, where long-range edges promote rapid spatial mixing. As
\alpha
decreases, infection can bypass local spatial constraints, and persistent coinfection pockets (with small
\mu
) rapidly seed distant nodes, accelerating epidemic spread. This results in near-instantaneous saturation of the infected population when both
\mu
is small and
\alpha
is small.

p_{\text{edge}}
), the coinfection recovery rate (
\mu
), and the long-range decay exponent (
\alpha
) over extended ranges in order to characterize their individual and combined effects on the mean infection prevalence.

The fitted generalized linear model summaried in Table

\mu

\alpha

4
Conclusion

This study reveals that the coinfection recovery rate
\mu
plays a critical role in the persistence of dual-strain epidemics on long-range networks. Lowering
\mu
significantly enhances survival probability, especially in regimes with strong long-range interactions (small
\alpha
), where rapid mixing amplifies the effect of coinfection. Unlike traditional models, the epidemic threshold depends asymmetrically on
\mu
, with coinfected individuals sustaining transmission disproportionately. We also find that long-range links extend the survival region without inducing a sharp transition, and that increased network connectivity consistently lowers the critical infection rate
\lambda_{c}
. These findings suggest that targeting coinfection recovery may be an effective control strategy and motivate further analytical work to characterize thresholds in heterogeneous, nonlocal epidemic models.
