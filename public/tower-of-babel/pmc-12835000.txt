Quantitative paleoclimate reconstruction in the Yangtze River Delta since the Last Glacial Maximum based on pollen records
Authors: Feng Chen, Xue Ke, Tinglu Yang, Zongyan Zhang, Ren Jiang, Junjie Yu, Jianlei Xie, Jing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe climate dynamics and driving mechanisms of the East Asian Summer Monsoon (EASM) in the Yangtze River Delta (YRD) since the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) remain uncertain. We reconstruct annual mean temperature (Tann), mean temperature of the warmest month (MTwa), and annual mean precipitation (Pann) since 19.4 ka BP in the Yangtze River Delta (YRD), using pollen records from three sediment cores (LZK1, CSJA6, ZKA4), constrained by AMS ¹⁴C and OSL dating. Reconstructions were performed with a pollen–climate model based on the Locally-Weighted Weighted-Averaging (LWWA) method. Additional sedimentary proxies (grain size and magnetic susceptibility) were integrated to evaluate climate dynamics and forcing factors. Five climatic stages were identified: the LGM (19.4–18.0 ka, cold–dry), deglaciation (18.0–11.7 ka, fluctuating warming), early Holocene (11.7–8.2 ka, warm–humid), mid-Holocene (8.2–4.2 ka, warm–humid), and late Holocene (after 4.2 ka, fluctuating cooling). These stages broadly align with EASM intensity variations. Abrupt events (Heinrich 1, Bølling–Allerød, Younger Dryas, 8.2 ka, and 4.2 ka) were synchronous with Greenland ice core and Yangtze River stalagmite records, reflecting global climate teleconnections. On the millennial scale, EASM variability was controlled by Northern Hemisphere summer insolation, Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), and ice-volume/sea-level feedbacks. On the centennial scale, it was influenced by solar activity, volcanic eruptions, and the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO). These findings provide high-resolution evidence of EASM dynamics and offer a valuable reference for understanding regional climate adaptation in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33476-8.Keywords: Yangtze River Delta, Late Quaternary, East Asian Summer Monsoon, Pollen, Paleoclimate reconstructionSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The Last Glacial Maximum (LGM, ~ 21 ka BP) was the peak of the most recent glaciation and marked the critical threshold for the global transition from glacial to interglacial conditions1. Subsequent large-scale ice sheet retreat, significant sea-level rise, and atmospheric circulation reorganization reshaped ecosystems2,3, altered vegetation patterns, and even influenced the dispersal of early human population4–6. During this period, global sea level was ~ 120 m lower than it is at present, and its rapid rise dramatically altered fluvial dynamics and land–sea interactions7. Although the Holocene brought generally warmer and more stable conditions, abrupt climate fluctuations persisted on millennial–centennial timescales. Well-known examples include Heinrich 1 (H1), Bølling–Allerød (B/A), Younger Dryas (YD), and the 8.2 ka and 4.2 ka cold events. These rapid changes are widely regarded as “natural experiments” that provide critical insights into climate system sensitivity and serve as valuable analogs for understanding ecosystem responses under future climate change8,9.

The Yangtze River Delta (YRD), located at the land–sea transition of the East Asian summer monsoon (EASM) domain, provides high-resolution archives of past environmental change. Its thick Quaternary deposits record responses to sea-level fluctuations, fluvial dynamics, and marine influences10,11. As one of China’s most densely populated and economically dynamic regions, understanding the paleoclimate history of YRD is essential for assessing regional climate resilience.

The YRD lies between 30°–32°N and 119°–122°E, covering about 5.2 × 10⁴ km², across both subaerial and subaqueous settings (Fig. 1). The region is characterized by low-lying plains (< 5 m a.s.l.) and a subtropical monsoon climate, with mean annual temperature of 14–18 °C and precipitation of 1100–1400 mm. Its modern landscape reflects strong fluvial–tidal interactions, with the Yangtze River delivering about 4.8 × 10⁸ t of suspended sediment annually. Further detailed descriptions of the study area’s geomorphology, hydrology, vegetation, and climatic background, are provided in Supplementary Materials (Section S1).

Recent advances in palynology and quantitative reconstruction techniques have refined estimates of past temperature and precipitation14–16. However, most reconstructions are limited to single-core or pollen-only records, restricting spatial coverage and increasing uncertainty. Traditional methods such as Weighted Averaging (WA) are prone to edge effects, whereas machine learning approaches such as Random Forests offer higher accuracy but lower interpretability17. Consequently, an optimal reconstruction framework for the East Asian monsoon region has yet to be established.

Understanding the detailed history of temperature and precipitation in the YRD since the LGM and identifying the dominant mechanisms driving EASM variability at millennial and centennial scales remain key challenges for regional paleoclimatology. To address these questions, we analyzed three well-dated sedimentary cores from the YRD (LZK1, CSJA6, and ZKA4), constrained by robust AMS ¹⁴C and OSL chronologies11–13,18. By integrating pollen assemblages with auxiliary sedimentary proxies, we reconstructed quantitative variations in temperature and precipitation since the LGM. We also examined major abrupt climate events—including H1, the YD, and the 8.2 and 4.2 ka cold events—and assessed their links to global teleconnections recorded in ice cores and speleothems. We further investigated the driving mechanisms of EASM variability at millennial–centennial scales and evaluated different reconstruction approaches to identify the most suitable framework for the East Asian monsoon region. This multicore, multiproxy study provides new insights into EASM evolution and offers a robust historical perspective for regional climate resilience.

Results

Results of spearman rank correlation analysis

Bilinear interpolation of climate variables at modern pollen sampling sites revealed substantial climatic variability. Tann ranged from − 12.2 to 22.7 °C (median 1.9 °C), MTwa ranged from 1.45 to 41.3 °C (median 18.6 °C), and MTco ranged from − 31.3 to 10.6 °C (median − 18.7 °C). Pann varied between 22 and 1801 mm (median 398 mm), with MPwet ranging from 4 to 377 mm (median 90 mm) and MPdry ranging from 0 to 28 mm (median 3 mm). These results indicate that the sampling sites capture a wide range of climatic conditions, from cold to warm and arid to humid environments.

Spearman correlation analysis (Fig. 2) highlighted strong collinearity among several variables: Pann and MPwet (ρ = 0.958, p < 0.001), Pann and MPdry (ρ = 0.922, p < 0.001), and MTco and Tann (ρ = 0.960, p < 0.001). To minimize multicollinearity in subsequent models, one representative variable from each correlated group was retained. MPwet and Tann were prioritized for ecological relevance, whereas MPdry and MTco were excluded due to redundancy or limited ecological significance.

Spearman’s rank coefficients among environmental variables.

Results of ordination analysis

Canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) of the pollen percentage data (Table 1) revealed substantial multicollinearity among the climatic variables. The initial variance inflation factor (VIF) values for MPwet, Tann, Pann, MTco, and MTwa all exceeded 10, indicating significant information overlap. Considering the proportion of explained variance of climatic variables as the sole predictor (λ1/λ2), Pann and Tann exhibited greater explanatory power than the other variables did, so Pann and Tann were prioritized for retention, whereas MTco was excluded. Subsequent removal of MPwet and MPdry based on the VIF and Spearman correlation reduced multicollinearity, yielding three variables—Pann, Tann, and MTwa—with VIF values < 10. Together, these variables explained 49.3% of the total variance in the pollen data and were used for subsequent pollen–climate quantitative reconstruction.

Temporal ordination along the CCA axes (Fig. 3) indicated that Axis 1 (eigenvalue = 35.4%) accounted for 58.3% of the environmental variance, whereas Axis 2 (eigenvalue = 12.6%) explained 21.8%. Pann primarily aligned with Axis 1, representing a precipitation-dominated gradient, whereas MTwa aligned with Axis 2, representing a temperature-dominated gradient.

CCA ordination of modern samples showing (A) sampling sites and (B) taxa in relation to environmental variables (arrows).

The CCA results for the modern samples (Fig. 3A) indicated that the pollen site distribution was mainly consistent with that of MTwa and Tann, suggesting that temperature was the primary driver of modern pollen site variation. Most samples clustered in the negative direction, implying relatively low ambient temperatures. The CCA results for pollen taxa (Fig. 3B) revealed that Tann broadly aligned with the distribution of most taxa, confirming its role as the main climatic driver of long-term vegetation changes. Moisture-loving taxa such as Ficus, Mallotus, and Lithocarpus were distributed in the positive direction of Axis 1, whereas heat-tolerant taxa, including Tamaricaceae, Urticaceae, and Cannabaceae, were distributed in the positive direction of Axis 2.

Performance of quantitative paleoclimate reconstruction models

Quantitative paleoclimate reconstructions were conducted for cores LZK1, CSJA6, and ZKA4. For Tann, six pollen-based methods were tested (Fig. 4), with the Locally-Weighted Weighted-Averaging (LWWA) algorithm performing best. While the 1400 km radius yielded R² = 0.870 and RMSE = 2.351 °C, an 800 km radius was selected for reconstruction, providing a balance of accuracy and spatial resolution (R² = 0.845, RMSE = 1.287 °C).

Model performances comparison among different approaches under multiple radius ranges from 500 to 1500 km in 100 increments. The dots represent R²; bars represent RMSE.

For MTwa, both LWWA and Locally-Weighted Weighted-Averaging Partial Least Squares (LWWAPLS) performed optimally within a 700 km radius, with LWWA slightly outperforming LWWAPLS (R² = 0.866 vs. 0.865; RMSE = 1.376 vs. 1.379 °C) and was therefore selected.

For Pann, Random Forests achieved the highest accuracy (R² = 0.950, RMSE = 131.959 mm) within 700 km, followed by LWWA (R² = 0.913, RMSE = 167.845 mm). LWWA was employed for Pann reconstruction to maintain methodological consistency across climate variables and to leverage its ecological interpretability, which provides a more reliable basis for paleoclimatic inference than the less transparent Random Forests algorithm19.

Accordingly, the LWWA algorithm with radii of 800 km, 700 km, and 700 km was applied for the Tann, MTwa, and Pann reconstructions, respectively, providing robust and interpretable quantitative paleoclimate estimates for the YRD.

Results of quantitative paleoclimate reconstruction

The results of the quantitative reconstructions from cores LZK1, CSJA6, and ZKA4 reveal multistage climate evolution since the LGM (Fig. 5). In the LZK1 core (Hengsha, Shanghai), seven stages are recognized: 19.4–18.0 ka, which was characterized by cooling and low precipitation (cold–dry), 18.0–12.6 ka showed warming with initially reduced then gradually increasing precipitation (still cold–dry), 12.6–8.0 ka experienced rapid cooling followed by warming and increasing precipitation (cool), 8.0–4.4 ka was characterized by stable warmth and abundant rainfall (warm–humid), 4.4–3.0 ka was characterized by warming but reduced precipitation (warm–dry), 3.0–2.0 ka was characterized by high temperature and abundant precipitation (warm–humid), and after 2.0 ka the climate was characterized by cooling followed by stabilization and wetter conditions (relatively cool).

Climate reconstruction results for cores LZK1, CSJA6 and ZKA4 (0.3 loess smoothing). The shading indicates the uncertainty range of the reconstructions.

The CSJA6 core (Nantong, Jiangsu) also records seven stages: 19.0–18.0 ka was cold–dry, 18.0–12.9 ka saw gradual warming with precipitation decreasing and then increasing (transition to warm–humid), 12.9–10.9 ka was warm–dry, 10.9–7.9 ka was warm–humid, 7.9–5.4 ka was relatively warm–dry, 5.4–1.6 ka was cold–dry, and after 1.6 ka, warming and subsequent cooling with increasing precipitation reflected a relatively cool climate.

The ZKA4 core (Jiangdu, Jiangsu) similarly reveals seven stages: 17.4–13.7 ka was cold–dry, 13.7–12.9 ka was cold–arid, 12.9–10.9 ka was warm–dry, 10.9–8.2 ka was warm–humid, 8.2–5.0 ka was warm–humid with increasing precipitation, 5.0–3.1 ka was cold–dry, and 3.1–1.7 ka was relatively cool.

Overall, although the three sites differ in the exact timing and intensity of changes, they consistently demonstrate multistage climatic fluctuations since the LGM, which is broadly consistent with major East Asian climate transitions. The reconstructions are robust, as confirmed by significance tests and ecological plausibility. Tann and Pann from LZK1 and CSJA6 are statistically significant (p < 0.05), MTwa from CSJA6 provides reference information, and the overall trends align with ecological expectations (see Supplementary Materials Section S4).

Discussion

Climate succession and regional comparison in the Yangtze River Delta since the Last Glacial Maximum

Quantitative pollen reconstructions from three sediment cores (LZK1, CSJA6, ZKA4), together with lithological, grain-size, and magnetic susceptibility data (Fig. 6), delineate five stages of climate evolution in the Yangtze River Delta since the LGM. The Tann, Pann, and MTwa values reported here are averages from the three cores.

Comparison of the sedimentary records from the Yangtze River Delta with other climatic records since 19.4 ka BP. a, b and c Quantitative reconstructions of Tann, MTwa and Pann based on pollen from the YRD (in this study); d EASMI of Chaohu Lake20; e Summer insolation at 30°N53; f. δ¹⁸O values of stalagmites from Luoshui Cave21; g Susceptibility of LZK112; h Grain size of LZK111.

During the LGM (19.4–18.0 ka), the region experienced cold–dry conditions (Tann < 14 °C, Pann < 900 mm, and MTwa ~30.6 °C). Notably, climate minima occurred at ~ 18.0–18.2 ka (Tann = 13.22 °C, MTwa = 28.3 °C) and again at ~ 18.6–18.9 ka (Pann = 749.3 mm). The vegetation was dominated by coniferous forest and shrubland (Pinus and Picea > 40%; Chenopodiaceae and Artemisia > 15%), reflecting cold and arid environments. Sea level stood ~ 120 m below present3,7, and extensive incised valleys across the delta were infilled with coarse sands and gravels (sand > 40%), while the mean magnetic susceptibility was ~ 40 × 10⁻⁸ SI, together indicating strong aeolian input and proximal coarse-grained transport. These signals are consistent with stalagmite δ¹⁸O records from the middle Yangtze and EASMI reconstructions from Chaohu20,21, suggesting that basin-wide EASM weakening was linked to the “ice-sheet cold-source effect” of the expanded Laurentide and Scandinavian ice sheets22.

During the subsequent deglaciation (18.0–11.7 ka), the climate gradually warmed and became wetter (Tann ~14.6 °C, Pann ~1003 mm, MTwa ~ 30.9 °C), punctuated by three millennial-scale events. H1 (17.5–14.7 ka) was cold and dry (Tann min. 13.1 °C, Pann min. 780 mm), with mixed conifer–broadleaf vegetation, sand > 50%, and magnetic susceptibility fluctuating 40–120 × 10⁻⁸ SI. During the B/A (14.7–12.9 ka), the conditions shifted to warm and humid (Tann >15 °C, Pann >1200 mm), with evergreen–deciduous broadleaf forest (Quercus up to 30%), clay–silt > 80%, and magnetic susceptibility < 40 × 10⁻⁸ SI, which is consistent with ~ 60 m sea-level rise23. The YD (12.9–11.7 ka) produced abrupt cooling (Tann drop ~ 2.2 °C), Pann fluctuations ~ 400 mm, vegetation shift to deciduous broadleaf forest–meadow (Poaceae up to 30%), sand content rising to 40–80%, and magnetic susceptibility exceeding 80 × 10⁻⁸ SI, in phase with Greenland ice-core and North Atlantic coral δ¹⁸O records24,25, suggesting a possible link to AMOC disruption26.

The early Holocene (11.7–8.2 ka) was the warmest and wettest period since the LGM (Tann ~15.2 °C, Pann ~1200 mm, MTwa ~31.1 °C). The vegetation was dominated by mixed deciduous–conifer forest, and clay–silt deposits > 80% indicate stable runoff. Short-lived events include 9.6–9.2 ka cooling (~ 1.3 °C) and precipitation fluctuations (~ 200 mm) which may be linked to reduced solar activity and weakened AMOC27. The 8.2 ka event (8.2–7.8 ka) saw rapid Tann decline from 15.2 to 12.7 °C, with Pann increasing by ~ 300 mm, consistent with freshwater input into the North Atlantic, potential AMOC weakening, and southward Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) shift28,29.

During the mid-Holocene (8.2–4.2 ka), warm‒humid conditions continued (Tann ~15.3 °C, Pann ~1170 mm, MTwa ~30.3 °C), with evergreen–deciduous broadleaf forest and strongly fluctuating sand content. The 4.2 ka event (4.4–3.7 ka) caused cooling and drying (Tann = 14.1 °C, Pann = 1017 mm), broadly synchronous with Mawmluh Cave speleothems and volcanic crater lake sediments30,31.Potential mechanisms include Arctic sea-ice expansion, weakened AMOC, and enhanced ENSO activity32–34, which together act to suppress monsoon rainfall35. Despite declining summer insolation at 30°N, the retreat of Northern Hemisphere ice sheets and high western Pacific SSTs (~ 30 °C) enhanced evaporation and helped sustain the EASM via ocean–atmosphere compensation36–38.

In the late Holocene (after 4.2 ka), climatic variability increased (Tann ~14.8 °C, Pann ~1061 mm, MTwa ~30.5 °C). LZK1 experienced warm–humid conditions during 3.5–1.9 ka (Tann ~16.8 °C, Pann ~ 995 mm, sand 20–30%, magnetic susceptibility < 20 × 10⁻⁸ SI), which was consistent with flooding, freshwater lake formation, and agricultural expansion39–42. In contrast, the CSJA6 and ZKA4 cores recorded cooler–drier conditions (Pinus > 30%), which may reflect vegetation disturbance and soil erosion associated with intensified human land use, whereas LZK1 maintained < 10% Pinus. Alternating cold and warm intervals are evident, including the Dark Age Cold Period (1.8–1.3 ka, Tann = 13.1 °C) and the Medieval Warm Period (1.0–0.8 ka, Tann = 15.5 °C, Pann = 1100 mm). Magnetic susceptibility (20–60 × 10⁻⁸ SI) and sand content (10–40%) fluctuations indicate strong variability in wind and hydrodynamic processes.

These centennial-scale oscillations were broadly synchronous with solar activity cycles and deep-sea records from the North Atlantic and the northern Yangtze Delta40,43–48, whereas intensified land clearance and associated soil erosion could have amplified sedimentary fluctuations49–51. Taken together, these findings highlight a dual natural–anthropogenic control on regional environmental evolution during this stage.

Driving mechanism of the EASM since the LGM

On the basis of the climatic evolution records from the LZK1, CSJA6, and ZKA4 cores in the Yangtze River Delta, together with regional proxies and global paleoclimate archives (Fig. 6), the driving mechanisms of the EASM clearly differ across temporal scales, with millennial- and centennial-scale variations controlled by distinct factors under the combined influence of external forcing and internal feedbacks.

At the millennial scale, the EASM variability was likely influenced by Northern Hemisphere summer insolation, AMOC strength, and ice-volume–sea-level feedbacks52. During the early to mid-Holocene (11.7–4.2 ka BP), summer insolation at 30°N reached a Holocene peak53. The maximum land‒sea thermal contrast during this period led to a record intensity of the EASM20, corresponding to the warmest and wettest phases in the region. However, in the late Holocene, the continuous decline in summer insolation may have provided the fundamental background for the weakening of the EASM and the cooling of the climate. AMOC acted as a key interhemispheric link, with its weakening associated with the YD and 8.2 ka cold events28,29,54,55, while its recovery during the B/A period enhanced the EASM56. In the late LGM, the “ice-sheet cold-source effect” of the expanded Laurentide and Scandinavian ice sheets reinforced the Siberian High and suppressed the northward penetration of the monsoon52,57–61. During deglaciation, rapid sea-level rise (e.g., ~ 60 m in the B/A) altered land–sea configurations and atmospheric circulation53, potentially promoting EASM strengthening and forming a positive feedback loop of “ice-sheet melting–sea-level rise–monsoon intensification.”

At the centennial scale, the EASM variability was probably more strongly modulated by solar activity cycles, volcanic eruptions, and ocean–atmosphere modes such as ENSO62. The Medieval Warm Period coincided with higher solar activity and reduced volcanic frequency, which may have favored stronger EASM, whereas the Dark Age Cold Period corresponded to weaker solar activity, intensified volcanism, and a reduced EASMI (~ 0.1)20. Volcanic aerosols could have amplified cooling and weakened the short-term monsoon intensity. ENSO may have exerted indirect control by modulating tropical Pacific SSTs. For example, the 4.2 ka event was linked to Arctic sea ice expansion47, the end of the Green Sahara31, and AMOC weakening, together with increased El Niño activity32–34, potentially contributing to drought in East Asia. During the 8.2 ka event, freshwater discharge from the collapse of remnant Laurentide ice sheets disrupted AMOC and displaced the ITCZ southward28,29,63,64, producing a “cooling–humidification” pattern in the Yangtze Delta, which is consistent with stalagmite δ¹⁸O records21.

Conclusions

This study provides a systematic reconstruction of the climate evolution in the Yangtze River Delta since 19.4 ka BP, based on pollen records from three sediment cores (LZK1, CSJA6, ZKA4) combined with AMS ¹⁴C and OSL dating and sedimentary proxies (grain size, magnetic susceptibility). Model evaluation via modern pollen datasets, training set optimization, and cross-validation indicates that the Locally-Weighted Weighted-Averaging (LWWA) approach yields the most robust results, producing a continuous and reliable regional climate sequence.

The reconstructed climate evolution can be divided into five characteristic stages. The Last Glacial Maximum was dominated by cold‒dry conditions with coniferous forest and shrubland vegetation. During deglaciation, climate gradually warmed and became wetter, punctuated by millennial-scale events (H1, Bølling–Allerød, and Younger Dryas). The early Holocene represents the warmest and wettest interval since the last glacial period, with peak EASM strength. The mid-Holocene was generally warm and humid, with western Pacific warm pool–driven ocean–atmosphere compensation mitigating the effect of declining summer insolation. Since the late Holocene, climatic fluctuations have intensified, including centennial-scale events such as the Dark Age Cold Period and the Medieval Warm Period, with increasing human impacts on vegetation and sedimentary processes.

Climate drivers exhibit clear scale-dependent characteristics. On millennial timescales, EASM variability was primarily controlled by Northern Hemisphere summer insolation, AMOC strength, and ice volume–sea level feedbacks, whereas on centennial-scale, climate anomalies were modulated by solar activity, volcanic eruptions, and ENSO. The reconstructed rapid climate events (H1, B/A, YD, 8.2 ka, and 4.2 ka) are highly synchronous with Greenland ice cores, stalagmite records from the middle Yangtze, and North Atlantic sedimentary archives, highlighting teleconnections within the global climate system and the combined influence of external forcing and internal feedbacks on regional climate evolution.

Materials and methods

Core materials and chronological framework

This study is based on three pollen records from the Yangtze River Delta: core LZK1 (Hengsha Island, Shanghai; 403.83 m), core CSJA6 (Nantong, Jiangsu; 118.0 m), and core ZKA4 (Jiangdu, Jiangsu; 234.0 m). For quantitative analysis, the upper Quaternary sections were selected (LZK1 above 78.5 m, CSJA6 above 66.6 m, and ZKA4 above 67.15 m), yielding a total of 150 pollen horizons. Chronologies were constrained by AMS ¹⁴C and OSL ages, and Bayesian age–depth models were developed using the R Bacon package. The deepest ages of the three cores span ~ 19.4–17.4 ka BP, providing continuous records since the Last Glacial Maximum. Detailed descriptions of cores, vegetation sequences, and dating results are provided in Supplementary Materials (Section S2.1, S3.1, Tables S1–S2, Fig. S2).

Modern pollen dataset and climate variables

To calibrate pollen–climate relationships, 2,303 modern surface pollen samples within a 1,500 km radius of the study area were compiled from Chinese and Asian pollen datasets. Climate variables were extracted from the WorldClim 2.1 database at 30″ resolution, including mean annual temperature (Tann), maximum temperature of the warmest month (MTwa), and mean annual precipitation (Pann), among others. Correlation analysis and ordination (DCA, CCA) were applied to assess collinearity and select the most explanatory climatic variables for reconstruction. Further details on modern pollen site distribution, climate variable extraction, and preprocessing are given in Supplementary Materials (Section S2.2, Fig. S1).

Spearman rank correlation and ordination analysis

To identify the climatic variables most strongly influencing pollen composition, Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed to detect collinearity. Detrended Correspondence Analysis (DCA) showed a gradient length of 4.72, indicating unimodal ordination was appropriate. Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) was then used to assess the explanatory power of climatic variables. Variables with Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) > 10 were iteratively removed to minimize multicollinearity. The ratio of constrained to unconstrained eigenvalues (λ₁/λ₂) quantified explanatory strength. Analyses were conducted in R (GGally, vegan) and Canoco 5. Detailed scripts and summaries are provided in Supplementary Materials (Section S3.2).

Quantitative reconstruction models

Prior to reconstruction, pollen data were square-root transformed to stabilize variance. Six transfer function approaches were tested: Modern Analogue Technique (MAT), Weighted Averaging (WA), Weighted Averaging Partial Least Squares (WAPLS), Locally-Weighted Weighted-Averaging (LWWA), Locally-Weighted Weighted-Averaging Partial Least Squares (LWWAPLS), and Random Forests. Model performance was evaluated under different training radii (500–1500 km) using cross-validation statistics (R², RMSE). Among these, the LWWA model showed the most robust results for Tann, MTwa, and Pann, and was adopted for paleoclimate reconstruction. Full model descriptions, algorithm details, and performance comparisons are provided in Supplementary Materials (Section S3.3).
