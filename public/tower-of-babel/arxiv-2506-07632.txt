Notes on Real Quantum Mechanics in a Kähler Space
Irina Aref’eva, Igor Volovich

Abstract
The necessity of complex numbers in quantum mechanics has long been debated. This paper develops a real Kähler space formulation of quantum mechanics [ 19 ] , asserting equivalence to the standard complex Hilbert space framework. By mapping the complex Hilbert space ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} to a real Kähler space 𝒦 2 ​ n \mathcal{K}^{2n} , i.e. ℝ 2 ​ n \mathbb{R}^{2n} equipped with a metric, a symplectic structure and an automorphism, we establish a correspondence between Hermitian operators in ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} and real operators in 𝒦 2 ​ n \mathcal{K}^{2n} . While the isomorphism appears straightforward some subtleties emerge: (i) the overcounting of composite system states under tensor products in ℝ 2 ​ n \mathbb{R}^{2n} , and (ii) the double degeneracy of operator spectra in the real formulation. Through a systematic investigation of these challenges, we clarify the structural relationship between real and complex formulations, resolve ambiguities in composite system representations, and analyze spectral consequences. Our results reaffirm the equivalence of the two frameworks while highlighting nuanced distinctions with implications for foundational debates on locality, phase invariance, and the role of complex numbers in quantum theory.

To a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
with inner product
\langle\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\rangle
,

\psi_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n},i=1,2,
one can associate a real Kähler space
\mathcal{K}^{2n}
consisting of pairs
\begin{pmatrix}q\\

\psi=q+ip
and forming a real Hilbert space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
with inner product
g\left(\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\

which is also equipped with a symplectic form

\omega\left(\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\

p_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\Im(\langle\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\rangle)

J\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-p\\

Therefore, there is a following relation between the complex Hilbert space and the real Kähler space

\langle\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\rangle=g\left(\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
p_{1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}q_{2}\\
p_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)+i\omega\left(\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
p_{1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}q_{2}\\
p_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right),\quad\psi_{i}=q_{i}+ip_{i},\quad i=1,2.

(1.1)

Therefore, there is a natural isomorphism between operators in the Hilbert
\mathbb{C}^{n}
space and in the Kähler space
\mathcal{K}^{2n}
.
At first glance, the connection (
1.1
) suggests equivalent formulations of quantum mechanics in Hilbert and Kähler spaces. However, several subtleties arise:
i) The first subtlety concerns the treatment of quantum states. In standard formulations, states correspond to equivalence classes of vectors distinguished only by multiplication by a phase factor.
ii) The second subtlety involves defining states for composite systems. Specifically, naively interpreting the tensor product in Kähler space as a tensor product in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
leads to an overcounting of composite system states in the real Kähler space compared to the conventional Hilbert space formulation.
iii) The third subtlety concerns the spectrum of the Hermitian Kähler operator
\mathcal{L}
compared to the spectrum of the corresponding Hermitian operator
L
: it is doubly degenerate relative to the spectrum of the corresponding Hermitian operator

L
.
The purpose of this paper is to systematically study these subtleties.

By reformulating quantum mechanics in the real Kähler space
\mathcal{K}^{2n}
, we preserve all essential features of the theory, including the consistent treatment of composite systems via tensor products and adherence to the standard postulates. Crucially, we resolve ambiguities arising in two key areas:
1) The avoidance of overcounting in composite systems through careful treatment of tensor products in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
2) The interpretation of spectral degeneracy in real operator representations.

2
Kähler Spaces

Here and thereby we consider a finite dimensional Kähler space.

2.1
Finite dimensional Kähler space

A finite dimensional Kähler space is a real finite dimensional Hilbert space equipped with a symplectic form and an automorphism called complex structure.
A Kähler space is a quadruplet

\mathcal{K}^{2n}=(\mathbb{R}^{2n},g,\omega,J)
, where:

•

g:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a positive defined
symmetric, non-degenerate bilinear form,

•

\omega:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a skew-symmetric, non-degenerate bilinear form (i.e., a symplectic form),

•

J:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is an automorphism satisfying
J^{2}=-\mathrm{id}
(referred to as a complex structure),

•

the following relations hold

\displaystyle g(\cdot,\cdot)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega(\,\cdot\,,J\,\cdot\,),

(2.1)

\displaystyle\omega(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(J\,\cdot\,,\cdot\,),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\omega(J\,\cdot\,,J\cdot\,)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega(\,\cdot\,,\cdot\,);

(2.3)

see, for example,
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.

2.2
Kähler structure in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}

Since
\mathbb{R}^{2n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n}

and we represent elements of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
as pairs

\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},\quad q,p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.4)

Vectors
p
and
q
in (
2.4
) can be expand into bases as

q=\sum_{a=1}^{n}q_{a}e_{a},\quad q_{a}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad p=\sum_{a=1}^{n}p_{a}h_{a},\quad p_{a}\in\mathbb{R},

(2.5)

For simplicity, we can take the same bases in both
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.
e_{a}=h_{a}
.

Now we introduce the Kähler structure in

\mathbb{R}^{2n}
:

•

i) the scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}

we define as

g\left(\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
p_{1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}q_{2}\\
p_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\sum_{a=1}^{N}\,(q_{1,a}q_{2,a}+p_{1,a}p_{2,a})

(2.6)

•

ii) the symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2N}

we define as

\omega\left(\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
p_{1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}q_{2}\\
p_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\sum_{a=1}^{N}\,(q_{1,a}p_{2,a}-q_{2,a}p_{1,a})

(2.7)

•

iii) the complex structure we define
as

\mathcal{J}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{J}\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-p\\
q\end{pmatrix},

(2.8)

where
I
is the
n\times n
identity matrix.

One can check that relations
(
2.1
), (
2.2
)
and (
2.3
) hold.

2.3
Operators

In this subsection, we consider linear operators on the Kähler space
({\mathbb{R}}^{2n},\omega,\mathcal{J},g)
.

Since elements of
\mathcal{K}
are represented as pairs of vectors (
2.5
) linear operators
{\cal L}
on
\mathcal{K}
can be represented as block matricies

\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}S_{1}&A_{1}\\
A_{2}&S_{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{L}\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}S_{1}q+A_{1}p\\
A_{2}q+S_{2}p\end{pmatrix}.

(2.9)

2.3.1
Double degeneration of spectrum of
\mathcal{K}
-Hermitian operators

We define a
\mathcal{K}
-Hermitian operator in the Kähler space
(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\omega,\mathcal{J},g)
as a real
2n\times 2n
matrix
\mathcal{L}
that satisfies the conditions

\mathcal{L}^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}=\mathcal{L},\quad\mathcal{L}\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{L},

(2.10)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}_{ij}=\mathcal{L}_{ji}
and
\mathcal{J}
is defined as in (
2.8
). Matrices satisfying (
2.10
) admit the block decomposition:

\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}S&-A\\
A&S\end{pmatrix},

(2.11)

where

\displaystyle A^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}

\displaystyle=-A,

(2.12)

\displaystyle S^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}

\displaystyle=S.

(2.13)

To demonstrate spectral degeneracy, consider the eigenvalue problem for
\mathcal{L}
:

(\mathcal{L}-\lambda I)\begin{pmatrix}q_{\lambda}\\
p_{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}=0.

(2.14)

Since
\mathcal{L}
satisfies (
2.10
), multiplying (
2.14
) by
\mathcal{J}
yields

(\mathcal{L}-\lambda I)\,\mathcal{J}\begin{pmatrix}q_{\lambda}\\
p_{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}=0,

(2.15)

revealing a new eigenvector
\mathcal{J}\begin{pmatrix}q_{\lambda}\\
p_{\lambda}\end{pmatrix}
with identical eigenvalue
\lambda
. Thus, the spectrum of any
\mathcal{K}
-Hermitian operator
\mathcal{L}
is doubly degenerate.

The
\mathcal{K}
-Hermitian operators in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
admit the following spectral representation (resolution of the identity):

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\mathcal{P}_{1i}+\mathcal{P}_{2i})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L},

(2.16)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathcal{P}_{1i}+\mathcal{P}_{2i})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I},

(2.17)

where
\mathcal{I}
is the
2n\times 2n
identity matrix, and
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha i}
(
i=1,\ldots,n
,
\alpha=1,2
) are projectors with block form

\mathcal{P}_{1i}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{i}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{P}_{2i}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&P_{i}\end{pmatrix},\quad P_{i}^{2}=P_{i},

(2.18)

satisfying the conditions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\alpha i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\alpha i},\quad\mathcal{P}_{1i}\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{P}_{2i},

(2.19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{1i}\mathcal{P}_{2i}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\mathcal{P}_{\alpha i}\mathcal{P}_{\beta j}=0\quad(\alpha\neq\beta).

Equations (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathcal{E}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L},

(2.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{I},

(2.21)

where
\mathcal{E}_{i}
relates to
\mathcal{P}_{1i}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2i}
through

\mathcal{E}_{i}=\mathcal{P}_{1i}+\mathcal{P}_{2i},

(2.22)

and satisfies the properties:

\mathcal{E}_{i}^{2}=\mathcal{E}_{i},\quad\mathcal{E}_{j}\mathcal{E}_{i}=0\quad(j\neq i).

(2.23)

2.3.2
Kähler unitary group
\mathcal{U}(n)
, symplectic group
Sp(2n,\mathbb{R})
, and orthogonal group
O(2n)

A Kähler unitary group
\mathcal{U}(n)
consists of matrices
\mathcal{U}
in the form

\mathcal{U}=\begin{pmatrix}X&Y\\
-Y&X\end{pmatrix},

(2.24)

where
X,Y
are real
n\times n
matrices, and
\mathcal{U}
matrices are subject to the relation

\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}=\mathcal{I}.

(2.25)

Relations (
2.25
) give the following conditions:

XX^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}+YY^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}=X^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}X+Y^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}Y=I,

(2.26)

The matrices in the form (
2.24
) are also elements of
O(2n,\mathbb{R})
, i.e.,
\mathcal{V}\in O(2n,\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, they are elements of the symplectic group
Sp(2n,\mathbb{R})
.
Indeed, a symplectic matrix
\mathcal{S}
of the symplectic group
Sp(2n,\mathbb{R})
satisfies the relation

\mathcal{S}^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{J},

(2.27)

where
\mathcal{J}
is given by (
2.8
). Representing the
2n\times 2n
matrix
\mathcal{S}
in the block form

\mathcal{S}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix},

where
A,B,C,D
are
n\times n
matrices, we obtain the following conditions on
A,B,C
, and
D
:

\displaystyle A^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}C,\quad B^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}D,\quad AB^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}\,\mbox{and}\quad CD^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}\quad\mbox{ are symmetric},

(2.28)

\displaystyle A^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}D-C^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}B=I,\quad AD^{\mathrm{T}}-BC^{\mathrm{T}}=I.

(2.29)

If we assume
A=X=D
and
B=-C=Y
, as in (
2.24
), the listed conditions reduce to:

\displaystyle Y^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}X=X^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}Y,\quad XY^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}=YX^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}},

(2.30)

\displaystyle X^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}X+Y^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}Y=I,\quad XX^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}+YY^{\mbox{\footnotesize T}}=I.

(2.31)

This implies the fundamental relation:

U(n)=\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})\cap\mathrm{O}(2n,\mathbb{R}),

(2.32)

where
U(n)
is the unitary group,
\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})
is the symplectic group, and
\mathrm{O}(2n,\mathbb{R})
is the orthogonal group.

2.4
Tensor products on Kahler spaces

In this subsection we consider two natural definitions of tensor products of real Kähler spaces. We will elaborate explicit constructors using bases in corresponding spaces.

In
\mathbb{R}^{2n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n}
there is a basis,

\{\ell_{A},A=1,...2n\}=\{e_{a}|+\rangle,\,h_{b}|-\rangle,a,b=1,...n\},

(2.33)

where
|+\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
,
e_{a}|+\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}e_{a}\\
0\end{pmatrix}
, and
|-\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}
,
h_{b}|-\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
h_{b}\end{pmatrix}
;
as noted above we take
h_{a}=e_{a}
.

To define the tensor product of two linear vector spaces, it is sufficient to define the tensor product on the elements of the bases.

2.4.1
The tensor product operator

\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\otimes}
on Kähler spaces

We can write

\displaystyle\ell_{A}\otimes\ell_{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{a}\otimes e_{b}\Big[\sum_{i,j=1,2}|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle\Big]

(2.34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{a}\otimes e_{b}\Big[\,|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle+|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle+|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle+|-\rangle\otimes|-\rangle\Big]

Denoting

\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
p_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\eta_{1};\quad\begin{pmatrix}q_{2}\\
p_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\eta_{2},

(2.35)

we consider the tensor product in the sense of (
2.34
) that is indicated as
\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\otimes}
and we get

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\eta_{1}\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\otimes}\eta_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
p_{1}\end{pmatrix}\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\otimes}\begin{pmatrix}q_{2}\\
p_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(2.36)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\sum_{a}\left(q_{1a}e_{a}|+\rangle+p_{1a}e_{a}|-\rangle\right)\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\otimes}\sum_{b}\left(q_{2b}e_{b}|+\rangle+p_{2b}e_{b}|-\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b}\Big(q_{1a}q_{2b}\,|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle+p_{1a}q_{2b}|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle+q_{1a}p_{2b}\,|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle+p_{1a}p_{2b}|-\rangle\otimes|-\rangle\Big)e_{a}\otimes e_{b}.

The scalar product and the symplectic form of two vectors in the form (

) are given by the formula

\displaystyle g\left(\eta_{1}\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\otimes}\eta_{2},\chi_{1}\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\otimes}\chi_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g\left(\eta_{1},\chi_{1})\cdot g(\eta_{2},\chi_{2}\right)

(2.37)

\displaystyle\omega\left(\eta_{1}\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\otimes}\eta_{2},\chi_{1}\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\otimes}\chi_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega\left(\eta_{1},\chi_{1})\cdot g(\eta_{2},\chi_{2}\right)+g\left(\eta_{1},\chi_{1})\cdot\omega(\eta_{2},\chi_{2}\right).

(2.38)

\underset{\cal K}{\otimes}
on
Kahler Spaces

\displaystyle\eta_{1}\underset{\cal K}{\otimes}\eta_{2}=\mathbb{P}\,\eta_{1}\underset{\mathbb{R}}{\otimes}\eta_{2},

\mathbb{C}^{n}
and Kähler

3.1
From Hilbert to Kähler

To a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
we associate a real Kähler space consisting of a real Hilbert space
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
with inner product
g(\cdot,\cdot)
which is also equipped with a symplectic form
\omega
and an automorphism
J
. There is a following relation between the complex Hilbert space and the real Kähler space:

•

we write
\psi=q+ip
,
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

Taking in mind that elements of

\mathcal{K}
consist of pairs
\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}
it is convenient to introduce an operation
\gamma
that maps pairs of vectors
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
and
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
to vector in the form
q+ip\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and the operation
\gamma^{-1}
maping the complex
\psi=q+ip
to the pair of its real and imaginary parts

\gamma\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}=q+ip,\quad\gamma^{-1}(q+ip)=\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}.

(3.1)

Therefore, we have

\gamma\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}=\psi,\quad\gamma^{-1}\psi=\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}.

(3.2)

•

To specify the structure of the Kähler space on pairs
\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}
we have to define the scalar product and
\omega
form on these pairs as well as automorphism
J
. We do this using the real and imaginary part of the corresponding scalar product in
\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle g\left({\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
p_{1}\end{pmatrix}},\begin{pmatrix}q_{2}\\
p_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)

6
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, we have demonstrated the equivalence between the complex Hilbert space formulation of quantum mechanics and its real Kähler space counterpart.
Our Kähler-space framework resolves ambiguities in debates on real vs. complex formulations by:

1.

Avoiding ad hoc assumptions: Composite systems are treated via standard tensor products in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, without introducing hidden variables or nonlocal maps, but by introducting the projection operator that makes one to one correspondence between wave function of composite system in the Hilbert and Kähler spaces.

2.

Preserving spectral equivalence: Operator degeneracy in
\mathcal{K}^{2n}
mirrors the algebraic structure of Hermitian operators in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

While debates on the ”reality” of complex numbers persist, our results demonstrate that real Kähler spaces provide a consistent, self-contained framework for real quantum mechanics. Future directions include:

•

Experimental tests of real-complex discrepancies in network scenarios.

•

Computational advantages of the Kähler formulation.
