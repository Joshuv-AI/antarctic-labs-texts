From paper to practice: What AJPC owes its readers
Authors: Khurram Nasir
Published: 2026

In 2008, around this time of the year, my daughter was born. A few weeks later, low on sleep and high on coffee, I submitted a paper I was convinced would change how people would practice.

Eventually, it got published in 2009 [1]. The paper examined the prognosis associated with the absence of coronary artery calcium (CAC) and promoted the concept “power of zero” [1]. Since its publication this concept has garnered meaningful interest, but equally also received widespread skepticism and resistance.

In mid-March 2026, CAC reached a major milestone, receiving a Class I recommendation in the cholesterol management guideline for refining uncertain treatment decisions [2]. CAC had appeared in earlier guidelines, but this was a stronger endorsement. Two weeks later, I also learned that my daughter had been accepted to college.

Do the math with me. From birth to college admission. This is the interval between submitting the paper and a guideline milestone.

I bring this up because my experience is a common theme. Seventeen years is the usual estimate for how long good evidence takes to reach routine practice, something that we have been frequently quoting. At the recent American Society for Preventive Cardiology (ASPC) Congress I used this example to contextualize a broader question: What role and responsibility should a modern journal, such as the American Journal of Preventive Cardiology (AJPC), have after a paper has been accepted and published?

We proposed five responsibilities that can accelerate the journey for our aspiration of ‘Paper to Practice’ at AJPC. This includes the framework of curate, accelerate, contextualize, translate, activate and learn. A recent editorial experience at AJPC illustrates the first four changes being actively adopted in our editorial process. The last require a partnership with ASPC that we still need to develop.

Actively curate the questions that matter

For most of the history of medical publishing, a journal's job is very simple, attract the best work, vet it, print it. A passive record of what the field already knows. That is no longer enough. At AJPC we feel, that as a journal we can become an active participant rather just a mirror reflecting what we receive.

This conversation was prompted by the 2026 ACC/AHA/ASPC multi-society dyslipidemia guidelines focusing on a lifelong management strategy centered on three cardinal messages: start screening early, initiate treatment threshold earlier, and maintain lower levels for extended durations.2 At AJPC we recognized the life-course LDL-cholesterol question as a priority, so rather than waiting for it to land in our inbox, we actively started having conversation with a prominent academic known to actively lead the efforts in this space.

I wish to state clearly upfront that the purpose of inviting such a submission is certainly not to publish work supporting a predetermined conclusion. Instead, it is meant to emphasize an important clinical issue worthy of investigation. The invitations should never carry any presumption that submission will lead to publication, let alone agreement with authors’ conclusions. That is how AJPC can contribute in actively recruiting the domains that will critically impact our preventive practices.

Accelerate without lowering standards

Initial communications with the authors commenced on March 30. Manuscript submission date was April 19. Following a highly expedited review and revisions, the publication was simultaneously published with presentation at the European Atherosclerosis Society Congress on May 25 [3].

I feel that while important, interval from submission to acceptance alone cannot necessarily serve as a benchmark for measuring quality; neither should there be an expectation across all submissions. However, for high impact papers, coordination with conference presentations is being increasingly adopted at various journals, and we will continue this practice at AJPC. Acceleration towards timely publication does not mean loosening our rigorous evaluation metrics, rather requires a dedicated activation of the entire editorial team

Contextualize before recommending action

Karungi et al. pooled data from 17 randomized trials involving 105,879 patients experiencing 6076 cardiovascular events [3]. By conducting meta-regression analysis on pooled data, authors estimated a relative risk reduction of 36% with 1 mmol/L decrease in LDL-C level for those with an initial incidence rate of 1%, whereas the reduction was 13% for 3% annual event rate [3].

Notwithstanding the importance of the study findings, there are caveats to consider before drawing clinical implications. While peer review ensures scientific integrity in evaluating evidence, editorial stewardship plays a critical responsibility in explaining the significance of evidence, to whom evidence applies, what needs to be changed, and, equally importantly, what should not be modified in light of evidence.

Editorial comment by Alexander Razavi and Roger Blumenthal clarified the clinical perspectives [4]. A lower relative effectiveness observed at high risk does not equate a lesser degree of urgency in treating [4]. Neither does early initiation of therapy translate into treating all comers. The determination of treating and timing of treatment depends on individual risk stratification and preferences. This is precisely what editorials aim to accomplish in interpreting evidence beyond its summary description.

Translate for the people who will use the evidence

Based on my experience thus far Published ≠ Understood ≠ Used.

At AJPC we don’t think that publication is the sole endpoint, but probably that is where our work is beginning, because the end users do not benefit from evidence merely because it exists. The evidence has to travel and be translated. So, we built four doorways into the same source of truth, each aimed for a different audience.

To begin, an easy-to-understand infographic designed specifically for our busy readership/clinicians that delivers the message concisely. Second, Dr Kausik Ray provided his interpretation of the results via social media outreach, allowing us to expand our reach beyond just the journal’s readership. Third, at AJPC, we positioned our story by posing a simple question “are we acting late?” to highlight the preventive implications of the findings. Fourth, via our press and our video interview format, we delivered key take-home messages of treat earlier, treat longer, think beyond the traditional primary-versus-secondary prevention divide, and approach prevention across the life course.

Activate & learn through partnership

But I want to be honest about what isn't finished. Activate and learn are still unfinished business for us. While AJPC has a wide outreach, it cannot convene a community at scale. For that to happen, ASPC’s chapters, committees, and educational programs need to take the lead and evaluate whether these efforts changed what should be published, how it should be disseminated.

That is why we need a genuine ‘partnership bridge’ between AJPC and ASPC to finish what we started, turning paper into practice.

So, what would such a collaboration involve?

ASPC can potentially consider concurrent journal clubs presenting original articles and associated editorials; web-based symposia allowing communication between authors and practicing clinicians; and patient-oriented briefs facilitating shared decision making as well as policy briefs analyzing the implications of findings on existing guidelines, costs, and access issues. These suggestions are merely conceptual starting points, and the impact of these collective efforts is still up for discussion.

Conclusion

The concluding remarks of Razavi et al.’s editorial are quite fitting here regarding timeliness issue by noting that timing is everything in golf, and now in preventive cardiology [4]. I would add one more place: it is also everything in publishing with the goal of taking a paper beyond publication and into practice. At AJPC our ambition is not simply to move papers, but to help move trustworthy evidence through the world. And I don't think we can do that alone. Whether papers such as Karungi et al. and the efforts built around them will help change practice by 2030 depends on what AJPC and ASPC choose to build together around it.
