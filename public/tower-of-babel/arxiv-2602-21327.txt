Abstract
Individuals with similar qualifications and skills may vary in their demeanor, or outward manner: some tend toward self-promotion while others are modest to the point of omitting crucial information. Comparing the self-descriptions of equally qualified job-seekers with different self-presentation styles is therefore problematic. We build an interactive AI for skill elicitation that provides accurate determination of skills while simultaneously allowing individuals to speak in their own voice. Such a system can be deployed, for example, when a new user joins a professional networking platform, or when matching employees to needs during a company reorganization. To obtain sufficient training data, we train an LLM to act as synthetic humans. Elicitation mitigates endogenous bias arising from individuals’ own self‑reports. To address systematic model bias we enforce a mathematically rigorous notion of equitability ensuring that the covariance between self-presentation manner and skill evaluation error is small.

Introduction

It is widely known that biases in data yield biased systems. Self-descriptions are not exempt from bias: data about individuals that are provided by the individuals themselves exhibit cultural biases.
More broadly, individuals, even within the same culture, differ in their levels of modesty and tendency (or ability) to self-promote. These differences have measurable downstream effects on outcomes, such as recruitment
38
.

If the problem were simply one of tone, existing language models could mitigate the effect by homogenizing the presentation of facts supplied by the individuals, for example, by using a prompt of the form “Rewrite this in the style of a white cisgender male in an individualistic culture.” However, the LLM cannot compensate for relevant information that the user
fails to supply
, which studies show to exhibit pronounced cultural and gender disparities
(
35
;
38
;
11
)

For example, in their official blog, LinkedIn reports

even at similar occupations and experience levels
” (emphasis added; see also

These
endogenous biases
arising from individuals’ self presentation styles cannot be addressed by post-hoc fairness techniques such as multicalibration
22
, outcome indistinguishability
13
, and learning fair representations
50
because the necessary data may simply not be present, or because the semantics of identical text for people with different styles may differ.

For concreteness (and availability of data), we focus on job-seekers’ self-generated professional profiles, for example, as on LinkedIn, but our proof of concept can extend to many other settings. Our key contribution is an interactive tool for accurate and equitable elicitation of skill signals in job candidates across a cultural spectrum. That is, we determine whether or not a person has a specific skill based on their initial self-description and answers to a few questions.
Crucially – and this is a principal design goal – our tool allows individuals to speak in the style in which they are comfortable. The pressure, and even well-intentioned advice, to conform to a specific interaction style is widespread, burdening even highly competent but conversationally more restrained individuals

;
3
. To our knowledge, ours is the first work to actively elicit information while respecting personal style.

For our purposes, a

is a function that maps a transcript to a score in
[0,1]
. Speaking intuitively and informally, one can think of this score as a ‘‘probability’’ that the individual in the transcript has a stated skill. For our purposes, it is helpful to think of the algorithm as trying to learn a ground truth, rather than to predict the future

Elicitation mitigates self-presentation bias. To avoid systematic
model

Multi-accuracy, which curtails the correlation between prediction error and self-presentation style, can be enforced at any point, including on the individuals’ initial self-descriptions. In our system, the trainings of the

Beyond articulating the problem of equitable elicitation while preserving individual voice, our work has several notable features:

1.

We provide a novel example of the use of LLMs for computational social science research. Drawing on a publicly available dataset of individual profiles, we build an LLM to create synthetic humans on which we train an accurate and equitable scoring function for skills.

3

3

2.

In many commercial settings, the training of predictors is a dynamic process, with systems trained to improve themselves in a sequence of epochs. Let
p_{i}
denote the predictor in the
i

p_{i}
satisfies our model fairness constraints.

To our knowledge, our system is the first in the algorithmic fairness literature to address inadequacies in the data by actively eliciting more information. We see this as a crucial subject for further study.

1.1
High-Level Overview

We use a structured approach to building an elicitation model based on
45
and Sutton
et al.
style reinforcement learning
44
.

We determine whether or not a person has a specific skill based on their initial self-descriptions and their answers to a series of adaptively selected questions. The question selection algorithm is trained to minimize uncertainty of the predictions, with the questions belonging to a prespecified and human-created set: no user will ever be asked a question that was directly generated by an LLM, and the pool of possible questions can be audited beforehand by any relevant parties
4
4

4

These aspects are relevant to deployment in the EU.

Given a question-and-answer conversation prefix, the selection of the next question is done via a fixed machine-learning-based function, which maps the transcript of user responses to a probability distribution on the next question. This
question selector
converts the user’s responses into vectors using an embedding model and then feeds them into our pre-trained transformer-based machine learning system. We use a transformer architecture because that allows us to input a variable-length conversation prefix; this is needed because, for example, on the first question we only have the user’s background information or profile, while on the fifth we also have 4 previous answers. This system is static and reproducible: given the same answers, it will produce the same distribution

on outputs (questions).

We construct our
skill predictor
using a technique similar to the one used to build the question selector, and the two functions are trained together (see Section
3
). We formulate the skill prediction task as a scoring function that deterministically maps a transcript of questions and responses to a score in
[0,1]
.
Training proceeds in a series of rounds and multi-accuracy is imposed in each one using a technique

from
17
.
5
5

5

As noted above, stronger guarantees, such as multi-calibration and moment multicalibration
27
can also be ensured, but these algorithms are more complicated.

We remark that neither of the two functions is generative.
The training process for our elicitation model
uses generative AI (specifically, our trained LLM) to bootstrap and expand the training data, but no generative processes are deployed at runtime.

2.1
Algorithmic Fairness

The literature on algorithmic fairness is broad and rapidly growing, encompassing notions such as individual (aka metric) fairness
(
12
)
and group fairness
(
12
;
21
)
.
Protections defined with respect to a single group can be too weak
(
12
)
, and desirable group-based fairness desiderata can be mutually inconsistent for imperfect classifiers
10

(
30
)
, while finding a metric for individual fairness can be problematic (but see
(
24
;
37
;
40
)
for positive results).
Bracketing the consistency concerns, the literature explored fixing a single group-fairness condition and requiring that it hold simultaneously for a rich collection of arbitrarily overlapping (sufficiently large) groups

(
22
;
28
;
29
)
.
Most pertinent to our work are the frameworks of multi-calibration and multi-accuracy
(
22

29

, which require predictors to be, respectively, accurate
in expectation
or calibrated simultaneously on each group in the collection.
By enforcing multi-accuracy with respect to presentation styles, we extend these fairness-as-accuracy goals into the domain of
information elicitation
, adding proactive interaction to ensure that stylistic self-presentation variation does not systematically affect errors in skill estimation.

A wide range of related approaches highlight complementary fairness notions.
Fair representation learning methods aim to construct latent spaces invariant to sensitive attributes
50
;
46
.

The problem of model multiplicity, as highlighted by

4
, emphasizes that among equally accurate models, one should select those that are less discriminatory.
Additional perspectives include
de-biasing
6
, corrective transformations
14
, and work that seeks to move beyond explicit definitions of fairness altogether
26
.

2.2
Endogenous Biases: Empirical Evidence

A substantial body of empirical evidence shows that individuals’ self-presentation styles introduce systematic disparities in evaluation outcomes.
For example, East Asian employees tend to exhibit a modesty bias in self-ratings of job performance, leading to lower self-assessments despite comparable actual performance
16
;
25
;
8
.
Such disparities highlight how cultural norms of modesty versus self-promotion can shape the information available to evaluators, and thereby affect professional advancement.

Research on self-presentational behavior shows that applicants strategically adjust how they present themselves during hiring processes, often influencing evaluations in ways that are independent of their actual skills
32
.
More recently, studies have explored how AI systems interact with such biases, showing both risks and opportunities.
For instance,
23
and
20
analyze the role of AI in recruitment and assessment, noting that algorithmic tools can either amplify existing disparities or help to mitigate them if designed carefully.

Relatedly,
15
investigates biases in chatbot responses linked to users’ names, illustrating how seemingly innocuous identity cues can alter model behavior.

6
shows that widely used word embeddings encode gender stereotypes, raising concerns that linguistic representations themselves can reproduce and amplify social biases.
Most recently,
2
demonstrates that Western-centric AI suggestions led Indian participants to adopt Western writing styles, homogenizing their work toward Western norms and diminishing cultural nuance.

LLMs as Interviewers

In recent years, AI has increasingly been employed as an interviewer across a range of contexts, including the elicitation of political opinions

, qualitative interviews

, quantitative telephone surveys

, and even in assisting with questionnaire design

Methods

3.1
Problem Setup

We study the
skill elicitation problem
: given an individual
j
, the goal is to determine whether they possess a target skill. The ground-truth label is denoted
y^{j}\in\{0,1\}
, and our predictor produces an estimate
\hat{y}^{j}\in[0,1]
.

The elicitation is interactive. At each round
i
, the system asks a question
q_{i}
from a fixed bank
\mathcal{Q}
(appendix
E
), and the individual responds in free text
s_{i}
. The dialogue history up to round
i
is called the
transcript
:

x_{i}^{j}=(q_{1}^{j},s_{1}^{j},\ldots,q_{i}^{j},s_{i}^{j}).

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the space of transcripts. Our system learns two components:
1) A
question selector

q_{\psi}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Q})
, parameterized by
\psi
, which maps the transcript
x_{i}^{j}
to a probability distribution over the remaining questions.
2) A
score function

f_{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
, parameterized by
\phi
, which outputs the predicted probability
\hat{y}^{j}
of skill possession.

Each response
s_{i}
is embedded using a pretrained text encoder
\operatorname{BERT}()
, concatenated with its question index, and aggregated with a GPT-style attention mechanism
\operatorname{ATTN}()
. For example:

\displaystyle q_{\psi}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ATTN}_{\psi}(q_{1}\circ\operatorname{BERT}(s_{1}),\dots,q_{i}\circ\operatorname{BERT}(s_{i})),

\displaystyle f_{\phi}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ATTN}_{\phi}(q_{1}\circ\operatorname{BERT}(s_{1}),\dots,q_{i}\circ\operatorname{BERT}(s_{i})),

where
\circ
denotes concatenation.

3.2
Optimization Objectives

Our learning problem can be cast in an actor–critic framework
(
43
)
, where the question selector
q_{\psi}
plays the role of the
actor
and the score function
f_{\phi}
serves as the
critic
. The actor is responsible for deciding which question to ask next, while the critic evaluates the quality of the interaction by predicting whether the individual possesses the target skill. The two models are updated jointly and influence one another during training. Below we detail the objectives for each component.

Certainty-maximizing policy learning.

A naïve approach would be to update the question selector using the final prediction error
|y^{j}-\hat{y}^{j}|
as the reward. However, this leads to very sparse and delayed feedback: a sequence of questions may only be judged good or bad once the entire interview is complete. To provide a more informative training signal, we instead optimize the question selection policy for immediate
information gain

(
45
)
.

For a transcript
x_{i}^{j}
and candidate next question
q_{i+1}^{j}
, we simulate
M
steps into the future using the response simulator
s()
. This is a generative model of an individual, provided by our trained language model; see Section
4.1.1
. We use
x_{i+M}^{j}
to compute the rollout-based
certainty
measure

Z({q^{j}_{i+1}})=2\big|f_{\phi}(x_{i+M}^{j})-0.5\big|.

Intuitively,
Z({q^{j}_{i+1}})
is large when asking
{q^{j}_{i+1}}
leads to states in which the score function
f_{\phi}
makes more confident predictions. In other words, the policy is rewarded for selecting questions that are expected to most reduce predictive uncertainty, forcing prediction errors to be quickly corrected.
Algorithm
1
outlines this rollout-based calculation for
{q^{j}_{i+1}}
: it samples a length
M
extension of the current transcript, for some parameter
M
, beginning with
{q^{j}_{i+1}}
and running for
M-1
more steps, and calculates the certainty of
q^{j}_{i+M}
.
We call this the
rollout-based certainty (for
{q^{j}_{i+1}}
).

Supervised training of the score function.

The critic
f_{\phi}
is trained to approximate the ground-truth label
y^{j}
. Unlike the policy, the critic has access to the true skill label during training and can therefore be updated with a direct supervised loss. Specifically, we use mean squared error (MSE):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(\phi)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\big(f_{\phi}(x_{i}^{j})-y^{j}\big)^{2}.

The gradient update for
\phi
is

\phi\leftarrow\phi-\beta(f_{\phi}(x_{i}^{j})-y^{j})\nabla_{\phi}f_{\phi}(x_{i}^{j}),

with learning rate
\beta
. This ensures that
f_{\phi}
gradually improves its skill predictions as more rich transcripts are observed.

The objectives of
q_{\psi}
and
f_{\phi}
are interdependent. As the critic improves, its uncertainty estimates
Z
become sharper, which provides the actor with a more reliable reward signal. Conversely, as the actor learns to ask more informative questions, the critic observes richer transcripts and thus achieves higher predictive accuracy. This coupling drives a virtuous cycle: the actor guides exploration, while the critic supplies feedback, leading to a jointly optimized interactive elicitation process.

3.3
Model Fairness

While the optimization objectives above reduce endogenous (self-reporting) bias and ensure that the score function
f_{\phi}
becomes accurate on average, they do not guarantee that the learned model is free of bias. In particular, even after successful elicitation, skill evaluation may correlate with personality traits or communication styles that are irrelevant to the actual skill. If left unchecked, the model could systematically overestimate or underestimate individuals from certain personality groups, leading to biased outcomes.

Multi-Accuracy.

To mitigate this, we require the predictor to be
multi-accurate
with respect to a family of auxiliary functions. Let
\mathcal{C}
denote this family
7
7

7

In our proof of concept

|\mathcal{C}|=6
.
. Each
c\in\mathcal{C}
maps a transcript
x_{i}^{j}
to a real-valued score in
[0,1]
, representing, for instance, how strongly the individual exhibits a certain trait.

A predictor
f_{\phi}
is said to be
(\mathcal{C},\epsilon)
-multi-accurate if

\displaystyle\sup_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\Big|\mathbb{E}_{j}\big[c(x_{i}^{j})\cdot(f_{\phi}(x_{i}^{j})-y^{j})\big]\Big|\leq\epsilon.

(1)

This condition ensures that the prediction error of the score function is approximately uncorrelated with all functions
c\in\mathcal{C}
: no personality dimension (or their linear combinations) can explain away the residual bias.

Enforcing Multi-Accuracy.

To enforce multi-accuracy, we adapt the procedure of
(
18
)
. The idea is to correct the raw predictor
f_{\phi}
by adding a linear combination of the auxiliary functions, followed by a transfer function. Concretely, we define

f_{\phi}^{*}(x)=\sigma\!\left(\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}l_{c}\,c(x)+\sigma^{-1}(f_{\phi}(x))\right),

where
\sigma(t)=1/(1+\exp(-t))
is the sigmoid function,
\sigma^{-1}
is its logit, and
\{l_{c}\}
are calibration weights. Intuitively, the correction shifts the logit of
f_{\phi}(x)
depending on the personality scores
c(x)
so that the residual correlation between errors and traits is minimized. The weights
\{l_{c}\}
are obtained by solving a convex optimization problem derived from Theorem 5.6 of
(
18
)
(see the theorem statement in Appendix
D.1
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{g}(y,z)

\displaystyle=-\bigl(y\log(z)+(1-y)\log(1-z)\bigr)

(2)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Biggl[y^{j}\log\Biggl(\sigma\Bigl(\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}l_{c}c(x_{i}^{j})+\sigma^{-1}(f(x_{i}^{j}))\Bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+(1-y^{j})\log\Bigl(1-\sigma\Bigl(\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}l_{c}c(x_{i}^{j})+\sigma^{-1}(f(x_{i}^{j}))\Bigr)\Biggr)\Biggr]

\displaystyle\quad+\epsilon\cdot\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}|l_{c}|

This yields a predictor
f_{\phi}^{*}
that is provably multiaccurate with respect to
\mathcal{C}
.

Integration with training.

Fairness calibration is interleaved with the actor-critic updates described earlier. After a number of standard training steps, we perform a calibration phase: estimate
\{l_{c}\}
on a batch of transcripts, update the calibrated predictor
f_{\phi}^{*}
, and then use
f_{\phi}^{*}
as
f_{\phi}
for the next batch. This alternating procedure ensures that improvements in predictive accuracy are continually sharpened by multiaccuracy post-processing
8
8

8

The imposition of multiaccuracy
improves
predictive accuracy: since
f_{\phi}^{*}
is the optimal solution to the optimization problem,
f_{\phi}^{*}
has smaller or equal cross entropy loss than
f_{\phi}
. This implies that the KL divergence with the ”ground truth”
p^{*}
is shrinking:
KL(p^{*}||f_{\phi}^{*})\leq KL(p^{*}||f_{\phi})
. If the calibration condition is ever violated (i.e., the bound above exceeds
\epsilon

3.5
Theoretical Guarantee

We have implicitly assumed that our hypothesis class is sufficiently rich to capture the output of the multi-accuracy algorithm. Formally, we have the following result, whose proof appears in Appendix

A Specific Instantiation

We have created a prototype
elicitation model
that interviews (synthetic) people of differing presentation style to elicit information about their skills. The particular setting we have in mind is joining a professional networking platform, at which time users provide employment-relevant information. The goal is to mitigate self-presentation differences and capture relevant facts, creating for the platform an internal representation of skills
9
9

9

The question of whether the platform shares this with the user is orthogonal to our work.
.

We evaluate the prototype on users synthesized with our trained language model, measuring both accuracy loss (

To our knowledge, no previous work in reinforcement learning measures, let alone guarantees, multi-accuracy as a core requirement of the training.

Our procedure is inspired by standard interview practices, to wit we frame the interview as a sequence of questions, where each question is chosen from a fixed set of possible questions based on the current state of the interview (see Appendix

This work is motivated by the empirically observed endogenous biases in self-description during on-boarding on hiring platforms

Model Training Procedures

4.1.1
Language Model Fine-Tuning

We start with a set of sample profiles collected from public profiles on a large professional networking site. These profiles were then filtered to remove entries that were missing fields or in which the summary is too short (fewer than 50 characters).
The remaining profiles were then split into training, validation, and test sets.

The profiles were converted into a structured text format, with a fixed set of key-value pairs, with each key representing a field in the profile (e.g.,
"full_name"
,
"gender"
,
"industry"
,
"skills"
,
"summary"
, etc.). This structured text format was then used to fine tune

, creating a model that generates profile summaries based on the other fields in the profile. This is not a general purpose language model, but rather a method of sampling from the space of valid profile summaries; nothing is done to remove the biases inherent in the dataset at this stage.
We use this language model to ”respond” to questions by generating counterfactual responses to questions, see Appendix
E
for the list of possible counterfactuals.

4.1.2
RL Model Training Procedure (
f
and
q
)

Conversation Format.
Our structured conversations are a series of counterfactual questions
10
10

10

This design choice too is expedient, as these are compatible with our particular language model and the data on which it is trained.
. We model truthful users who manifest no intended deception. We start with a base profile that represents the user’s ground truth. We then ask questions by appending skills to the profile, having the language respond with a summary that incorporates these skills. Thus we can construct a series of self-summaries that represent the user under different (self-assigned) skill sets. The training objective is to determine if the user possesses a specific skill based on their sequence of summaries. Note that this creates the need for the two decision making processes as outlined in Algorithm

(the question model) selecting which sequence of self-summaries

(the score function) will encounter.

Our structured conversation format allows us to frame the process as the reinforcement learning problem described in section
3.2
. Given a base profile, it either does or does not possess a target skill. Our end goal is to create a fair scoring function (
f_{\phi}(x_{i})
) that takes the sequence of self summaries and predicts whether or not the interviewee has the target skill.

Given

we can then assign the correct answer to have a reward of +1, allowing us to attempt to learn a policy that maximizes the accuracy of the final answer. We use a simple policy gradient method,

(

and the critic is

5
Discussion

In this work we address two sources of bias: endogenous biases arising in self-reporting, and systematic model biases arising during training.
The former is accomplished via an AI that interacts with the users and permits them to respond in their own voice, the latter is achieved by imposing

post-hoc theoretical guarantees of fairness.
Our work presents an existence proof that fairness can be improved, beyond simply ensuring multi-accuracy, in an interactive social setting.

Our key contributions are:

1.

In
computational social science
we provide a novel example of the use of LLMs, finetuning an existing language model to create synthetic humans on which we train an equitable and fair scoring function.

2.

We fine-tune an existing LLM to obtain a large language model that can accurately reproduce a complex dataset and allows us to test our methods without risking harm to individuals.
12
12

12

We started from a public dataset. Starting from confidential data would require privacy protections, for example, via differential privacy.
That said, we are not advocating for replacing human subjects research with LLMs, and as we noted in Section
4
, these methods should be used in addition to real human interviewers’ analysis.

3.

We address endogenous biases in individuals’ self-reports
by eliciting additional information
.

4.

Our technique permits individuals to
speak in their own voice
.

5.

We incorporate model bias correction, specifically, multi-accuracy with respect to personality types, at regular intervals during training, improving accuracy over what is obtained with no model bias correction.

To our knowledge, all prior work on algorithmic fairness ”makes do” with whatever data are available. Ours is the first system to actively elicit additional information in order to address feature-insufficiency for fairness.

Future Work.

While we focus on the interview context with a single skill, equitable elicitation can be applied to estimating multiple skills simultaneously. Moreover, chat bots are growing in usage
(
42
)
, and equitable elicitation would be beneficial in many other realms, such as making medical or financial recommendations, which often rely on analysis of transcripts.

Looking through the lens of theory, how should we formalize the goal of equitable elicitation? In our setting, we were able to assume there is a ground truth that can be discovered through interaction with the user; there is no aleatoric uncertainty. In such a setting we might aim for a condition analogous to standard multi-group fairness conditions, eg, that the absolute or squared error have low covariance with the distinguishing functions
c\in C
.

Although we expect our approach also to be applicable for improving predictors of
future
events, for example, whether a candidate will be promoted within 2 years of hiring, the definition of equitable elicitation in this case now would have to handle differences in inherent uncertainty that truly are related to the functions in
C
– even if the uncertainty does not inhere in the individual but rather in the interaction between the individual and the work environment.

Finally, our work was inspired by research showing that endogenous biases in self-presentation are gendered. Looking more broadly, how can we design algorithms to detect when collected features are insufficient for fairness?
