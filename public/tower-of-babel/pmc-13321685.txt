The dynamic expression pattern of signaling pathways involved in early embryo development
Authors: Zifan Song, Shi Song, Nan Wang, Xiaomeng Wang, Liying Yan, Jie Qiao, Peng Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early embryonic development in mammals involves extensive intercellular communication and interaction. The rapidly changing signaling pathways, governed by signaling pathway-related genes (SPGs), underlie these intricate communication networks and mediate a series of developmental events, including blastulation and gastrulation. However, the detailed expression patterns of SPGs remain to be clearly illustrated. In this study, we used mouse and human transcriptomic and epigenomic data to systematically depict the dynamics of signaling pathway networks during early embryonic development. Our results indicate that zygotic genome activation (ZGA) triggers considerable remodeling of SPGs transcriptional patterns, which coincides with noticeably elevated promoter accessibility after ZGA in both humans and mice. In addition, most SPGs are maternally inherited and are more conserved between humans and mice compared to those activated by the zygotic genome. Interestingly, we found that various extracellular matrix (ECM)-related signaling pathways were highly enriched during early embryogenesis. Two enriched and conserved receptors in several ECM-related pathways, SDC1 and SDC4, were expressed on the cell membrane from oocyte to blastocyst stage both in humans and mice. Knockdown of Sdc1 and Sdc4 in mice resulted in an impaired developmental rate from the 8-cell stage via different mechanisms. Collectively, our study provides new insights into understanding the underlying mechanisms of early embryo development.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02909-w.

Introduction

Early embryo development is a complex process that requires strict genetic and epigenetic regulation in a spatial-temporal manner. During pre- and peri-implantation development, embryonic cells undergo two successive lineage segregations to generate the original three lineages, epiblast (EPI), primitive endoderm (PE), and trophectoderm (TE). Next, these three cell lineages rapidly expand and derive divergent embryonic structures with distinct functions to form the gastrula. The rapid diversification of cell fates is mirrored by the fast-changing signaling network, regulated by the dynamic expression of signaling pathway-related genes (SPGs). Successful embryonic development requires functional signaling networks, and disruption of these networks can lead to abnormal embryo development [1, 2].

During early mammalian embryonic development, extensive activation of signaling pathways plays a key role in regulating embryonic fate choices [3–5]. Although pre-implantation events in mammalian embryo development, such as zygotic genome activation (ZGA), compaction, and blastocyst formation, have been extensively studied, little is known about the intercellular signaling pathways that regulate these events [6, 7].

In the past, elucidation of cellular signaling networks in early embryos was hindered by methodological constraints: the limited number of cells hampers the acquisition of sufficient gene products for downstream assays. The advent of single-cell omics sequencing technology has dismantled this barrier, enabling high-resolution reconstruction of communication networks within the early embryo in diverse species, including humans [8, 9]. Integration of multi-omics data, such as transcriptomics and epigenomics, further provides more comprehensive molecular portraits of individual cells [10–12]. Over the past decade, single-cell multi-omics analysis has become a prevalent strategy for dissecting cellular heterogeneity and dynamic state transitions [13, 14], thereby offering a great opportunity to uncover the underlying mechanisms of early embryogenesis [9, 15, 16]. Given the strong heterogeneity of early embryonic cells, studying signaling networks at the single-cell level furnishes a unique and indispensable perspective for developmental biology.

In this study, we utilized single-cell transcriptome and methylome data from pre-implantation [16–18], peri-implantation [19], and gastrulation stages [20] in humans and mice to systematically elucidate the regulatory features of signaling networks during early embryonic development. We comprehensively depicted dynamic gene expression patterns of SPGs and identified the role of different cells in specific signaling pathways. Furthermore, by illustrating ligand-receptor (L-R) cross-talk network, we found that two critical conserved receptors, syndecan (SDC) 1 and SDC4, are shared across several extracellular matrix (ECM)-related signalings and play important roles in pre-implantation development. These findings will not only deepen our understanding of the dynamic signaling networks and intercellular communication during early embryogenesis, meanwhile, but also provide a valuable resource to identify key ligands or receptors that orchestrate development processes.

Methods and materials

Data acquisition and processing

We used publicly available datasets containing single-cell transcriptome and epigenome data from human and mouse pre-implantation embryos and human gastrulation embryos [16, 17, 19, 20]. All single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data were normalized using transcripts per million (TPM). The methylation and chromatin accessibility of gene promoter regions (from upstream 1500 bp to downstream 500 bp of transcription start site) during the pre-implantation stages in humans and mice were used. The histone modification datasets, including H3K4me and H3K27ac, were obtained from the ‘DevOmics’ project for analysis [21].

Pseudo-time analysis

We used the R package ‘Monocle2’ following a standard protocol to conduct pseudo-time analysis [22]. Expressed SPGs were used as input for Monocle2. Dimensionality reduction was conducted by the DDRTree reduction method and visualized.

UMAP dimensionality reduction analysis

The R package Seurat was employed to conduct UMAP reduction analysis. The log2(TPM + 1) value was used as the input for transcriptome data. After normalizing via the ‘ScaleData’ command, the runPCA and runUMAP functions were executed in succession to obtain the UMAP reduction result. Methylation data of the SPGs promoters, including WCG and GCH, were sourced from COOLseq data and served as inputs. The sample correlations were computed using the ‘cor’ command in R, with the parameter set to “pairwise.complete.obs”. Subsequently, according to Seurat’s protocol, UMAP dimensionality reduction analysis was conducted utilizing the resulting correlation value (detailed methods can be viewed in Li’s publication [18]).

Cell-cell communications analysis

To characterize cell-cell communications specifically involved in human peri-implantation and gastrulation stages at the single-cell level, the ‘CellChat’ package was used [23]. The ‘computeCommunProb’ function was generally used to compute the communication probability and infer the cellular communication network. Next, the ‘aggregateNet’ function was used to calculate the aggregated cell-cell communication networks. Detailed analysis and visualization protocol were referred to the standard pipeline published at https://github.com/sqjin/CellChat.

Define constant expressed and epigenetically active genes

Based on the transcriptome data, we used the ‘FindVariableFeatures’ command in the ‘Seurat’ package to calculate the variance of SPGs. We defined the top 100 ‘variance.standardized’ genes, for which the mean log2(TPM + 1) value is greater than 1 as ‘constantly’ expressed genes. Based on the ‘COOLseq’ data, we selected the epigenetically active genes that met the following four criteria: [1] Top 200 lowest promoter WCG methylation level; [2] Top 200 highest GCH methylation level; [3] Top 200 H3K4me modification level; [4] Top 200 H3K27ac modification level.

Identify maternal factors and zygotic-activated genes

According to the transcriptome data, we defined genes that are expressed log2(TPM + 1) ≥ 1 in all zygotes as maternal genes [24], and those with an average log2(TPM + 1) < 0.5 at the zygotic stage, meanwhile, an average log2(TPM + 1) > 1 at any other stages were defined as ZGA genes.

Differentially expressed genes analysis

After normalizing the input transcriptome data with ‘ScaleData’ command in ‘Seurat’, the ‘FindAllMarkers’ command was used to analyze the differentially expressed SPGs in different types of cells with default parameters.

Human embryo collection

In a routine in vitro fertilization cycle, after selecting high-quality embryos for clinical use, the residual embryos were collected to perform our study. The use of these embryos was authorized by informed written consent under the supervision of the Peking University Third Hospital ethical committee (no. M2024147).

Mouse embryo collection and culture

All animal experiments were performed according to the ethical guidelines developed by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University Health Science Center (no. A2024012). ICR female mice (6–8 weeks old) were superovulated by intraperitoneally injecting 10 IU pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (San Sheng) followed by 10 IU human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG, San Sheng) 42–48 h later, and then housed overnight with ICR males for mating. The next morning, pick out the female mice with vaginal plugs to harvest oviducts for zygote collection. Zygotes with two pronuclei were cultured in KSOM medium (Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology Co., Ltd). To record embryo developmental rate or sample at different stages, embryos were checked under stereoscope at the following time points: 36–40 h post hCG (two-cell embryos), 54–58 h post hCG (four-cell embryos), 66–70 h post hCG (eight-cell embryos), 78–82 h post hCG (morula), 94–98 h post hCG (blastocyst).

Microinjection of siRNAs

Fertilized one-cell embryos were picked out after 20 h post hCG and randomly allocated into four groups for siRNA injection: NC, SDC1_KD, SDC4_KD, Simultaneous knockdown of SDC1 and SDC4 (hereafter referred to as SDC1+4_KD). The siRNAs were purchased from HanBio Technology and diluted into 20 µM using nuclease-free water according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequences of siRNAs can be found in Supplementary Table S1. To ensure the efficiency of knockdown, three siRNAs of one target gene were mixed equally, and 5 pl of the mixture was injected into the cytoplasm of zygotes using a microinjection glass capillary. For the NC group, the same amount of siRNA NC was injected. After microinjection, the zygotes were washed three times with PBS drops, then cultured in KSOM medium at 37℃, 5% CO2.

Time-lapse imaging

The time-lapse images of mouse embryos were captured by Harioculture TL-16 (HARIOMED). The timing of development events were annotated by viewing time-lapse videos. The kinetic parameters are defined according to previous studies [25, 26]. Briefly, t2-t8 correspond to the times of dividing into two to eight blastomeres; tSC represents the first evidence of compaction; tM represents the completion of compaction; tSB represents the initiation of blastulation. Compacted morulae were identified into three types: [1] fully compacted morula (FCM), which includes all blastomeres; [2] partial compacted morula (PCM), which can be further divided into two subtypes: with blastomeres (i) excluded from the outset (excluded-PCM) or (ii) extruded after compaction (extruded-PCM).

Immunofluorescence staining

Human and mouse embryos at each developmental stage were individually collected using a mouth pipette and sequentially transferred into 4% formaldehyde for fixation at room temperature for 30 min. The embryos were then washed three times with 30 µL drops of 0.1% BSA. Permeabilization was performed using 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Following three washes with 0.1% BSA, the embryos were incubated in 5% BSA at 37 °C for 1 h to block nonspecific binding sites. The embryos were then transferred directly into primary antibody diluent for overnight incubation at 4 °C. The following day, embryos were washed three times with 0.1% BSA and incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 2 h. Nuclei were stained with 1 µg/mL DAPI (Abcam, ab285390). All antibodies used in this study were diluted in 5% BSA. The dilution ratios were as follows: mouse anti-SDC1 (Biodragon, BD-PA0643, monoclonal, 1:200), rabbit anti-SDC4 (Abmart, PHX6046S, polyclonal, 1:200), Alexa Fluor Plus 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Invitrogen, A32740, 1:500), and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Abcam, ab150113, 1:500). Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope was used for imaging. Image processing and analysis were performed with Imaris 9.0.1 software.

Quantitative Real-time PCR analysis

To examine the effect of siRNA, RNA samples were collected at 48 h after microinjection. Four-cell embryos were carefully transferred into lysis buffer, with five embryos per tube. The samples were incubated at 72℃ for 3 min and then quickly placed on ice. 5.7 µl of Reverse Transcription (RT) mix was added and gently mixed. The lysis buffer and RT mix were prepared according to a smart-seq2 protocol [27]. After RT procedure, the cDNA product was purified by Agencourt Ampure XP beads (Beckman, A63881) and then used for qPCR analysis. Each group has three biological replicates. Each sample was analyzed with four technical repetitions. The expression level of mRNA was calculated by the relative quantitation method using GAPDH as an endogenous control. The primers used in PCR were listed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Smart-seq2 protocol was employed to construct the RNA-Seq library of the mouse morula. To ensure the comparable completion of compaction across different groups of embryos, late-stage morulae were collected at 75 h after fertilization and transferred into 0.04% hydrochloric acid for zona pellucida removal. After washing with PBS, the embryos were transferred into PCR tube containing lysis buffer (three embryo per tube). Each group contained 7–9 technical replicates. The following steps for generating RNA-Seq library were identical to those previously described [17, 27]. STAR was used for genome alignment. Genome assembly GRCm38 was downloaded as a reference genome to conduct read mapping. Genes with average count < 10 and transcripts with the “Gm” prefix were excluded from the count matrix [28, 29]. The minimum TPM values of Sdc1 and Sdc4 in NC group were used as sample-level thresholds to exclude samples exhibiting insufficient knockdown efficiency in the knockdown groups [30, 31]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the DESeq2 package in R, with screening criteria of | log2 (Fold Change) | ≥ 0.6 and P adjust < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the DAVID database, with P < 0.05 considered significant. The genes present in our filtered count matrix were used as the background genes for GO analysis, following a previously published study [32]. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed based on all genes ranked by DESeq2 via the clusterProfiler package in R, with default parameters [33].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of animal experiments were performed with GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). An unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for qPCR analysis of siRNA efficiency. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multiple comparisons. The results were presented as mean ± SEM. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The transcriptional characterization of SPGs during pre-implantation stage

To investigate the cell communication among cell lineages during early embryogenesis, we used multi-omics data of human and mouse, including pre-implantation [16–18], peri-implantation [19, 34], and gastrulation stage [20], to depict the gene regulatory dynamics of SPGs (Fig. 1A). SPGs were collected by the R package ‘Cellchat’ [23], which comprehensively covers the L-R interactions involved in different signaling pathways.

The pseudo-time trajectory based on SPGs expression was in accordance with the developmental timeline (Fig. 1B and S1A) in both human and mouse, suggesting a sequential changing expression pattern of SPGs across pre-implantation stage. The expression ratio of SPGs was high before ZGA (with the highest level in zygotes) and gradually decreased afterward (Fig. 1C and S1B), and the differentially expressed SPGs show a similar pattern before ZGA (Fig. 1D and S1C), thus suggesting that most of the SPGs were maternally inherited (Fig. 1C and S1B, D). Furthermore, the total expression ratio of SPGs (the ligands and receptors involved in the corresponding signaling pathway), including all pathways, showed a gradually decreasing trend from zygote to blastocyst (Fig. S1D). We found that 221 SPGs in human and 229 SPGs in mouse were maternally inherited, and 106 maternally reserved SPGs were shared in human and mouse (Fig. S1E, G). According to Sha et al., we categorized the 229 maternal mouse SPGs into four clusters (Supplementary Table 6) [35]. Most maternal SPGs were categorized into cluster II, which were dramatically degraded from the zygote to 2-cell stage. 40 maternal SPGs were overlapped with cluster IV, which represents the stable transcripts during maternal-to-zygotic transition. Notably, several key ECM signaling-related SPGs, including ITGA6 and ITGB1, were allocated into cluster IV, which suggesting the importance of ECM signaling during early embryogenesis. KEGG enrichment analysis of these 106 conserved SPGs indicated that PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, cell adhesion molecules, and ECM-receptor interaction were the top three significantly enriched pathways (Supplementary Table S2). Notably, many ECM components, including ITGB1, FN1, and COL6A6, were shared in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. On the other hand, 92 SPGs in human and 66 SPGs in mouse were ZGA genes, while only seven genes were conserved (Fig. S1F, G). These results suggested that maternally inherited SPGs were more conserved between human and mouse, while ZGA SPGs were relatively species-specific.

The epigenetic characterization of SPGs during pre-implantation stage

After profiling the transcriptomic landscape of SPGs, we further investigated the epigenetic patterns in pre-implantation embryos. By analyzing DNA methylome data from human and mouse pre-implantation samples, we identified two major clusters based on the promoter methylation status of SPGs. The difference between the clusters mainly occurred before and after blastocyst formation (Fig. 2A). We also analyzed the dynamic changes in average methylation level of SPGs during pre-implantation stage. Our results indicated that the methylation level of SPGs gradually decreased from zygote to blastocyst in human, while in mouse peaked at 4-cell stage before continuing to decline (Fig. 2B). Nevertheless, a significant decrease in ICM and TE was observed compared to other stages both in human and mouse, which is consistent with the general DNA demethylation tendency in pre-implantation embryo [16, 36].

In contrast, both human and mouse showed no distinct clustering patterns of chromatin accessibility across all pre-implantation stages, yet the promoter accessibility level of SPGs reached the bottom around the major ZGA stage, then soared considerably after ZGA (Fig. S2A, B). To further investigate the variation of these two epigenetic modifications, we visualized the modification level using a combination of a heatmap plot and barplot (Supplementary Fig. S2C, D). The average methylation level of different SPGs showed dramatic variations across the pre-implantation stage, while chromatin accessibility exhibited no obvious fluctuations (Fig. S2C, D). This highlights the significant role of promoter methylation in shaping the dynamic epigenetic landscape of SPGs. In conclusion, the dynamic epigenetic modifications of SPGs in human and mouse are consistent with the general trend of global epigenome change, exhibiting reduced DNA methylation and increased chromatin accessibility [36, 37]. Among these, the promoter accessibility of SPGs continuously decreased before major ZGA, then rapidly increased after ZGA.

Next, we analyzed the H3K4me3 and H3K27ac datasets of human and mouse pre-implantation cells [38–40] and discussed the relationship between epigenetically active SPGs (genes with high accessibility and low methylation of the promoter region, accompanied by high modification level of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac) (Fig. S2E) and constantly expressed SPGs (genes with high mRNA expression level and low expression variation, see methods) (Fig. S2F). We found that the constant SPGs significantly overlapped with epigenetically active SPGs and were involved in signaling pathways such as WNT, ncWNT, NOTCH, and BMP, which play critical roles in embryo development (Fig. 2C). In addition, we found six epigenetically active and highly expressed genes were conserved in both human and mouse (Fig. 2D). Interestingly, half of them were ECM-related genes, including LAMC1, ITGB1, and ITGAV, suggesting an important role of ECM in regulating cell communication during early embryogenesis.

The expression and interaction of ligand-receptor pairs in signaling network during peri-implantation stage

As the embryo progresses into post-implantation stage, it gradually differentiates into multiple lineages. Correspondingly, the signaling communication within the embryo becomes increasingly complex and lineage-specific. Based on the scRNA-seq data of human [19] and mouse [34] peri-implantation embryos, we found that SPGs have distinct expression patterns in the EPI, PE, and TE (Fig. 3A and S3A). We further used the ‘Cellchat’ tool to analyze the cell communication within peri-implantation embryo. Among EPI, PE, and TE, 1738 significant L-R pairs involved in 78 signaling pathways were identified in human, while 374 significant L-R pairs involved in 61 signaling pathways were identified in mice (Fig. 3B, C and Fig. S3B, E). We also found 47 conserved pathways shared in human and mouse blastocysts, including ECM-related pathways such as COLLAGEN, FN1, LAMININ, and CDH, re-emphasizing the essential role of ECM in embryonic development (Fig. 3B, E and S3E).

In addition to the differences among lineages, the expression pattern of SPGs also varies at different developmental time points. Therefore, we further explored the transcriptome of SPGs at embryonic day 6, 8, 10, 12 in humans, and at day 3.5, 4.5, 5.5 in mice. Results showed that the number and intensity of L-R pairs in human embryos progressively increased from day 6 to 10, whereas the total number of L-R pairs decreased after day 10 (Fig. 3C). Even so, the overall intensity of L-R pairs still increased. By contrast, the number and intensity of L-R pairs in mouse embryos first decreased from day 3.5 to 4.5, then rebounded from day 4.5 to 5.5 (Fig. S3B). Intriguingly, we noticed that the dramatic increase in the number and intensity of L-R pairs from day 6 to 8 in humans and from day 4.5 to 5.5 in mice exactly covered the implantation window (Fig. 3C and S3B) [19, 41]. This suggests that peri-implantation embryo needs a well-prepared signaling network for post-implantation development.

To illustrate the specific variation trajectory of each signaling pathway, we drew relative information flow from consecutive developmental days. In humans, we found 40 significantly increased pathways on day 8 when compared with day 6, whereas only 8 pathways showed significantly decreased relative information flow (Fig. 3D). A similar pattern was found in day 8 versus day 10, with 33 increased pathways and 17 decreased pathways. However, there were 34 pathways decreased and 22 pathways increased from day 10 to 12, consistent with the decreased trend of L-R pairs shown in Fig. 3C. From day 6 to 12, no pathway showed consecutive decreased relative information flow, while 2 ECM-related pathways, COLLAGEN and CDH5, continuously upregulated. In general, there were 42 pathways increased and 11 pathways decreased during the human peri-implantation stage. Interestingly, many ECM-related signaling pathways, such as COLLAGEN, LAMININ, FN1, and THBS, were upregulated according to comparison between day 6 and 12 (Fig. 3D). Moreover, these pathways were also enriched in human gastrula and placenta, suggesting that ECM-receptor interaction is stably active during early embryo development (Fig. S3F, G). Likewise, in mice, we identified 13 increased and 10 decreased pathways from day 3.5 to 4.5, while 21 increased and seven decreased pathways from day 4.5 to 5.5 (Fig. S3C). Among these, only one pathway showed a consecutive decline in relative information flow, while three pathways, including one ECM-related pathway, L1CAM, exhibited a consecutive increase in activity (Fig. S3D). Overall, according to a comparison between day 3.5 and 5.5, there were 17 pathways upregulated and 7 pathways downregulated during the mouse peri-implantation stage. Considering the prominent epigenetic and transcriptional activity of ECM-related signalings during peri-implantation stage, we decided to dissect their specific contribution among different lineages.

Analysis of canonical signaling pathways during the first and second lineage segregation

In addition to screening for potential critical signalings, we also reviewed several established pathways that drive the first and second lineage segregations, including Hippo [42], FGF-ERK [43–45], p38-MAPK [46–48], PDGFRa [49] and BMP-TGFβ [50]. Our analysis suggested that the regulation of Hippo signaling on the first lineage segregation exhibits different features between human and mice. In mice, the median modulescore of Hippo signaling was higher in TE than in ICM, while this trend was not significant in humans (Fig. S4A). DEG analysis also revealed species differences in the expression of Hippo signaling components. Tead4 and Lats2 were significantly upregulated in mouse TE, whereas TEAD1, YAP1 and MOB1A were highly expressed in human TE (Fig. S4B). These findings are in consistent with the results of a previous study [51]. In comparison, the expression pattern of ECM-related SPGs were more conserved between humans and mice. Two ECM receptors, SDC4 and DAG1, were significantly enriched in both human and mouse TE, whereas two major subunits of type IV collagen, COL4A1 and COL4A2, were expressed relatively highly in ICM (Fig. S4B). Besides, we noticed that FN1, the encoding gene of fibronectin-1, was prone to conservatively enriched in both human and mouse ICM, despite without statistical significance in human. Given that fibronectin-mediated adhesion induces YAP nuclear accumulation, we hypothesized that differential expression of ECM-related L-R pairs likely contribute to the opposing regulation of Hippo signaling between ICM and TE [52].

Similarly, we also found species-specific differences in the regulatory mechanisms of signalings involved in the second lineage segregation also indicated difference between humans and mice. In both humans and mice, the module score of FGF pathway remained unchanged around implantation, though a significant decline was observed specifically in human day 12 (Fig. S4C). By contrast, the module score of BMP/TGFβ pathway exhibited a general upward trend during peri-implantation stage in both humans and mice. Intriguingly, the expression patterns of FGF-MEK-ERK and p38-MAPK signaling in epiblast (EPI) and primitive endoderm (PE) exhibit clear differences after implantation in mouse (day 3.5 to day 4.5), showing a gradually established FGF signal flow from EPI to PE and enhanced p38-MAPK signaling in PE (Fig. S4D). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in human (day 6 to day 8). However, in human, most FGF ligands were still relatively upregulated in EPI, while FGF receptors tend to highly expressed in PE. These results are consistent with previous findings demonstrating that human hypoblast (HYP) lineage specification also requires FGF signaling, which challenges the old concept that FGF dependence is not exist in human HYP formation [53–55]. Apart from that, BMP/TGFβ and PDGFRa signaling also exhibited species difference during the second lineage bifurcation. In human, BMP2 and BMP6 were stably upregulated in PE, while BMPR1A tends to be enriched in EPI (Fig. S4D). By contrast, no BMP ligand showed significantly different expression between mouse EPI and PE, while various BMP receptors, including Bmpr1a, Bmpr2, Acvr1 and Acvr2a, were inclined to enriched in PE after implantation. PDGFRa signaling was stably transducted from EPI to PE during the peri-implantation stage in human (Fig. 4D). In contrast, in mouse EPI, the initially high expression of Pdgfa progressively declined. Collectively, these findings advance our understanding of the signaling mechanisms during the two primary lineage segregation events.

ECM-related signaling interaction during peri-implantation stage

To specify the role of each lineage in ECM-mediated signal transduction, we first conducted network centrality analysis on the selected ECM-related pathways with the highest relative strength, as shown in Fig. 3B and S3E. As shown in Fig. 4A, ligands and receptors of THBS signaling were enriched in both PE and TE. Moreover, THBS1-SDC4 and THBS1-SDC1 were identified as the main L-R pairs in the THBS pathway in humans. However, the THBS pathway was not enriched in mouse blastocyst, as the key ligands were poorly expressed in EPI, PE, and TE (data not shown). Notably, both in humans and mice, PE served as the main sender and receiver among the FN1, COLLAGEN, and LAMININ pathways, stressing its central role in regulating ECM-related signaling (Fig. S4A, B). What’s more, we found that two conserved membrane proteoglycans, SDC1 and SDC4, were shared in FN1 and COLLAGEN pathways as the receptors with the highest relative contribution (Fig. S4A). By contrast, integrins and dystroglycan 1 were the main receptors in LAMININ pathways. Although the expression patterns of SDC1 and SDC4 were not similar between human and mice during pre-implantation stage, they both were highly expressed among EPI, PE, and TE in peri-implantation stage (Fig. S4B, C). The prediction of communication probability further indicated their interaction with ECM ligands, including COL4A1, COL4A2, and FN1 as universally expressed receptors among all three lineages (Fig. 4B). Overall, these results imply that SDC1 and SDC4 may play an important role in regulating cell communication within pre- and peri- implantation embryos.

The expression and function of SDC1 and SDC4 during pre-implantation stage

To validate and investigate the role of SDC1 and SDC4 in pre-implantation embryos, we performed immunofluorescence staining and a gene knockdown experiment. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that SDC1 and SDC4 were consistently expressed on the cell membrane from zygote to blastocyst stage in both human and mouse embryos (Fig. 5A). qPCR analysis confirmed the effective knockdown of Sdc1 and Sdc4 by siRNA (Fig. 5B). As shown in Fig. 5C, the knockdown of Sdc1 and Sdc4 markedly lowered the development rate of 8-cell embryos, morula, and blastocyst by approximately 20–30% relative to NC group. Given that the 8-cell stage coincides with the onset of compaction, these findings suggest that these two transmembrane proteoglycans may influence embryo development primarily by regulating the compaction process (Fig. 5C, D).

RNA-seq analysis reveals the molecular mechanisms of SDC1 and SDC4 in regulating morula development

To further investigate the underlying mechanisms of SDC1 and SDC4 in regulating embryonic development around compaction stage, we collected mouse morulae to conduct RNA-seq analysis.

Thirty-one samples that met stringent quality control standards were retained for further analysis, including 7, 7, 8, and 9 samples in NC, si1 (Sdc1 knockdown), si4 (Sdc4 knockdown), and si1 + 4 (Sdc1 and Sdc4 knockdown) groups, respectively (Supplementary Table S3). In contrast to the NC group, 88 genes were upregulated and 276 were downregulated in the si1 group, 226 genes were upregulated and 959 genes were downregulated in the si4 group, 221 genes were upregulated and 578 genes were downregulated in the si1 + 4 group (Fig. S6A). As shown in the Venn plot, si4_vs_NC shared more DEGs with si1+4_vs_NC than with si1_vs_NC, suggesting a prominent role for Sdc4 in regulating gene expression (Fig. S6B). Moreover, 156 DEGs were commonly identified across all three comparisons. A detailed list of DEGs was deposited in Supplementary Table S4.

To investigate the potential mechanism by which the knockdown of Sdc1 and Sdc4 affects embryo development, we conducted KEGG and GO enrichment analyses. Surprisingly, despite the primary localization of SDC1 and SDC4 are on the plasma membrane, enrichment results of GO biological process (BP) category revealed that the knockdown of their transcripts remarkably downregulated chromatin origanization and remodeling, with notably greater effects observed upon Sdc4 knockdown (Fig. 7A). On the other hand, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that both individual and combined knockdown of Sdc1 and Sdc4 resulted in significant dysregulation of MAPK, TGF-β, and Wnt signaling pathway, (Fig. 7A). Besides, the knockdown of Sdc4, irrespective of Sdc1 co-knockdown, caused marked upregulation of metabolic pathways, while many pivotal genes related to Hippo signaling and adherens junction, including Yap1, Tead1 and Rock2, were significantly downregulated. These findings suggest that both SDC1 and SDC4 are involved in the regulation of MAPK, TGF-β, Wnt signaling and chromatin remodeling, which may be important for morula to prepare for the incoming lineage specification.

To better elucidate the underlying pathways and biological processes, we performed GSEA. GSEA revealed significant enrichment of regulation of actin cytoskeleton, cadherin signaling, Hippo signaling, and adherens junction across all three knockdown groups (Fig. 7B). Beyond these structural functions, depletion of Sdc1 and Sdc4 also perturbed cellular metabolism, leading to the upregulation of various gene sets related to metabolism. Moreover, consistent with the KEGG and GO enrichment analyses, all the knockdown groups exhibited dysregulated gene sets associated with MAPK, TGF-β, and Wnt signaling pathway, as well as ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling and polycomb repressive complex. Intriguingly, we noticed that the transcripts of several chromatin remodeling proteins, including SMARCA5 and INO80, have been significantly downregulated after the knockdown of Sdc4 (Fig. 7C and S6D). Considering the important role of these chromatin remodeling proteins in the lineage commitment [56–61], we further examine the expression of lineage markers. Compared with the NC group, the expression of Klf4 and Gata6 were significantly decreased in all three knockdown groups, while the expression of Klf5 were markedly downregulated only in si1 + 4 group (Fig. 7D). Notably, two TE markers, Gata3 and Eomes, were both significantly downregulated by the knockdown of Sdc4. This observation is reminiscent of the downregulated Hippo signaling pathway identified in the functional enrichment analysis, since TE lineage specification largely depends on the appropriate regulation of Hippo signaling. Coincidentally, several key components of the Hippo signaling, including Yap1 and Tead1, were downregulated upon Sdc4 knockdown (Fig. S6E). Although Lats1 and Lats2 showed no statistically significant change in the si4 versus NC comparison (P.adjust > 0.05), their expression was markedly reduced upon combined knockdown of Sdc1 and Sdc4 (Fig. S6E). Taken together, our data suggest that Sdc4 and Sdc1 may have implications on the subsequent lineage differentiation via multiple molecular mechanisms.

Discussion

During early embryo development, a wide range of secretory ligands and their receptors are dynamically expressed, building a complex signaling network among different lineages [4]. Based on the published data, we have mapped the temporal expression profile of SPGs from zygote to blastocyst stage. Notably, the expression patterns of SPGs in humans and mice are similar before ZGA, while become considerably different after ZGA. Additionally, we found that the expression ratio of SPGs gradually decreased from zygote to blastulation stage, which was consistent with the degradation of maternal materials. Although the proportion of ligands and receptors gradually decreased, the total number of expressed ligands and receptors peaked in human 8-cell and mouse 2-cell stages, which corresponded to the ZGA stage in these two species, respectively. These findings indicate that maternal and zygotic SPGs have distinct expression patterns. However, during pre-implantation stage, we found six SPGs not only with stable expression in humans and mice, but also active at the epigenetic level. Interestingly, three of them (LAMC1, ITGB1, ITGAV) are closely related to ECM-receptor interaction, suggesting a constant important role of ECM in early embryogenesis. Besides, we also characterized the change of L-R interactions and signaling pathways from day 6 to 12 in the human embryo. Intriguingly, the number and intensity of L-R interactions have nearly doubled from day 6 to 8. Since this period is the critical time window for embryo implantation, and each lineage begins to exhibit significant differences in transcriptome, the enhanced communication among signaling pathways may be a key characteristic of cell lineage differentiation. We also noticed that several ECM-related signalings, including LAMININ, COLLAGEN, and FN1, are continuously upregulated during post-implantation stage, which further suggests the vital role of ECM in early embryonic development.

ECM serves as a complex reservoir of various signaling molecules and growth factors, occupying a central role in the signaling network [62, 63]. ECM is mainly comprised of glycoproteins, collagens, glycosaminoglycans, and proteoglycans. Its receptors, including integrin and non-integrin, are located on the surface of plasma membrane [64]. These extracellular components and other ligands, such as growth factors, bind to the extracellular domain of ECM receptors, triggering various cascade reactions within the cytoplasm to regulate cell migration, cell-cell adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and other biological processes [65]. Previous studies have found that ECM-related genes are expressed at the very early stage of embryogenesis, which is closely relative to lineage differentiation, implantation, and morphogenesis [66–68]. Deletion of key ECM ligands or receptors, such as laminin (Lama1, Lamb1, Lamc1), collagen (Col4a1, Col4a2, and Col5a1), fibronection, and integrin, will cause abnormal development or malformation, leading to embryonic lethality [69–77]. Our analysis also supports that these ECM signaling pathways are highly activated during early embryonic development, with extensive L-R cross-talk among different lineages. Among these ECM-related pathways, we noticed that SDC1 and SDC4, two co-receptors of integrin, were constantly expressed in pre-implantation stage and had prominent L-R contribution among different lineages, especially in PE and TE. Our experimental results also prove their expression in human and mouse embryos and demonstrate their critical roles in pre-implantation embryonic development.

Syndecans are an ancient family of transmembrane proteoglycans that belong to type I transmembrane heparan sulphate proteoglycans (HSPGs), with four family members expressed in vertebrates: syndecan-1, -2, -3, and − 4 (SDC1, 2, 3, 4). Previous studies have elucidated the function and mechanism of SDC1 and SDC4 in adult tissue. Sdc1 is expressed predominantly in epithelial cells as the dominant syndecan, whereas SDC4 is almost ubiquitous in most nucleated cells, yet at low levels [78]. The knockout of Sdc1 or Sdc4 in mice causes no severe defect, but shows impaired function under diverse challenges, including wound healing and inflammatory diseases [79]. These disease phenotypes can be well-explained by the vital role of SDC1 and SDC4 as regulated receptors for extracellular effectors to fine-tune adhesion formation, migration, polarity, and differentiation (reviewed in [78, 80]). However, little attention has been paid to their roles during early embryogenesis.

In our study, we demostrated that SDC1 and SDC4, especially SDC4, are involved in compaction and blastulation process of embryo. RNA-Seq analysis provided preliminary insights into the molecular mechanisms by which these two syndecans regulate embryonic development. On the one hand, GSEA results suggest that SDC1 and SDC4 are likely required for the proper regulation of actin cytoskeleton organization, cadherin signaling, and adherens junction, which are essential for embryonic compaction. On the other hand, the depletion of these two syndecans was associated with altered signaling dynamics in late-stage morulae, including MAPK, TGF-β, Wnt and Hippo signaling pathway, which have been reported to participate the lineage specification during peri-implantation stage [42, 81–83]. Suggested by previous studies, the regulation on these signalings may be attributed to the SDC-mediated tension at the cell-extracellular matrix interface, leading to the activation of MAPK signaling and YAP, which subsequently serves as a nexus for the integration of Hippo, TGF-β, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways [84–86]. However, it’s elusive to explain why the knockdown of Sdc1 and Sdc4 downregulates chromatin reorganization and polycomb repressive complex, as syndecans were widely considered as transmembrane proteoglycans located at the plasma membrane. Interestingly, Rønning et al. found that SDC4 could be internalized from the plasma membrane to the nuclear envelope during myogenesis [87]. Our immunofluorescent staining also captured localization of SDC1 on nuclear membrane in mouse pre-implantation embryos, suggesting their potential role in regulating chromatin remodeling and the transcription of lineage-specific genes.

Although our study demonstrates that the knockdown of Sdc1 and Sdc4 will impair pre-implantation development, previous researches have found that the knockout of these genes does not significantly change Mendelian inheritance [88, 89]. Interestingly, many other studies have also reported the phenotypic disjunction between knockdown and knockout [90, 91]. Rønning et al. reported that Sdc4-/- had also increased mRNA levels of Sdc2, suggesting that the effects of loss-of-function mutations may be compensated through genetic compensation [92, 93]. However, we observed that the combined knockdown of Sdc1 and Sdc4 did not exacerbate either the morphological or transcriptomic abnormalities, and no reciprocal compensatory upregulation was observed under single knockdown condition. Therefore, these phenotypic discrepancies between in vivo knockout and in vitro knockdown cannot be explained by genetic compensation. Coincidently, a recently published article reported similar phenomenon that despite miR-203 ablation caused profound developmental retardation and global transcriptional perturbation during preimplantation development, no difference was observed in the ratio of miR-203-null pups [94]. This suggests that early embryos may possess a robust and complex compensatory system capable of overcoming minor developmental perturbations.

Conclusions

In summary, we investigated the dynamic expression patterns of SPGs in human and mouse early embryonic development from pre-implantation to gastrulation stage and delineated the signaling pathway patterns coordinated by different cell types. DNA methylation and histone modifications may potentially regulate the dynamics of SPGs expression. Furthermore, enhanced intensity and activity of signaling pathways before and after implantation may be essential for lineage differentiation and maturation. These findings provide novel perspectives for understanding ligand-receptor interactions of signaling pathways during early embryo development. Based on the SPGs analysis, we identified two potential key receptors in ECM-related signaling pathways, SDC1 and SDC4, and confirmed their crucial roles in pre-implantation embryos. Transcriptomics results revealed their multifaceted functions not only as cell-membrane receptors for ECM ligands to mediate cell adhesion and signaling pathways, but also participate in regulating chromatin remodeling and lineage-specific gene expression. Future research should further explore other enriched L-R pairs to identify robust signaling molecules that have a considerable impact on early embryogenesis.
