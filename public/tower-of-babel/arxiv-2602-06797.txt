Optimal Learning-Rate Schedules under Functional Scaling Laws: Power Decay and Warmup-Stable-Decay
Binghui Li, Zilin Wang, Fengling Chen, Shiyang Zhao, Ruiheng Zheng, Lei Wu

Abstract
We study optimal learning-rate schedules (LRSs) under the functional scaling law (FSL) framework introduced in 26 , which accurately models the loss dynamics of both linear regression and large language model (LLM) pre-training. Within FSL, loss dynamics are governed by two exponents: a source exponent s > 0 s>0 controlling the rate of signal learning, and a capacity exponent β > 1 \beta>1 determining the rate of noise forgetting. Focusing on a fixed training horizon N N , we derive the optimal LRSs and reveal a sharp phase transition. In the easy-task regime s ⩾ 1 − 1 / β s\geqslant 1-1/\beta , the optimal schedule follows a power decay to zero, η ∗ ​ ( z ) = η peak ​ ( 1 − z / N ) 2 ​ β − 1 \eta^{*}(z)=\eta_{\mathrm{peak}}(1-z/N)^{2\beta-1} , where the peak learning rate scales as η peak ≂ N − ν \eta_{\mathrm{peak}}\eqsim N^{-\nu} for an explicit exponent ν = ν ⁡ ( s , β ) \nu=\nu(s,\beta) . In contrast, in the hard-task regime s < 1 − 1 / β s<1-1/\beta , the optimal LRS exhibits a warmup–stable–decay (WSD) ( 20 ) structure: it maintains the largest admissible learning rate for most of training and decays only near the end, with the decay phase occupying a vanishing fraction of the horizon. We further analyze optimal shape-fixed schedules, where only the peak learning rate is tuned–a strategy widely adopted in practice–and characterize their strengths and intrinsic limitations. This yields a principled evaluation of commonly used schedules such as cosine and linear decay. Finally, we apply the power-decay LRS to one-pass stochastic gradient descent (SGD) for kernel regression and show the last iterate attains the exact minimax-optimal rate, eliminating the logarithmic suboptimality present in prior analyses. Numerical experiments corroborate our theoretical predictions.

Introduction

Learning-rate schedules (LRSs) are a fundamental component of stochastic optimization, governing the trade-off between optimization progress and statistical noise during training, and thereby shaping convergence behavior. They play a central role in both theoretical analyses and practical algorithm design in modern machine learning.

The formal study of learning-rate schedules dates back to the seminal work of
42
, which established sufficient conditions for convergence of stochastic approximation, namely
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\eta_{k}=\infty
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\eta_{k}^{2}<\infty
. A canonical realization satisfying these conditions is the
polynomial-decay
schedule
\eta_{k}\propto k^{-\kappa}
with
\kappa\in(1/2,1)
, which has been extensively adopted in the analysis of both convex and non-convex optimization
(
25
;
7
)
. In linear regression, such schedules—when combined with iterate averaging
(
43
)
—can achieve minimax-optimal convergence rates in a statistical sense
(
3
;
10
;
37
)
.
More recently, a line of work has focused on the performance of the last
iterate, showing that
exponential-decay
schedules can
attain nearly optimal rates without averaging

(
13
;
51
;
57
)
.

Despite the rich theoretical literature, two notable gaps remain.
First, the learning-rate schedules that are theoretically optimal or near-optimal in classical analyses—such as polynomial or exponential decay—are rarely used in modern large-scale training. Second, practical training overwhelmingly relies on alternative schedules, most notably cosine decay
(
34
;
19
;
48
)
and warmup–stable–decay (WSD)
(
56
;
20
;
33
;
46
)
. WSD schedules typically keep the learning rate constant for the majority of the training horizon (often up to 80%) and defer decay to a short final annealing phase.
This stark contrast raises a fundamental theoretical question:
why can schedules that delay decay until the very end of training still perform well?

A key reason for these gaps is that most existing analyses do not derive learning-rate schedules from a principled approach. Instead, they follow a “propose-and-verify” paradigm: a specific schedule—typically motivated by classical sufficient conditions or heuristics—is posited a priori and then shown to achieve a desired convergence rate under certain conditions. While this approach provides useful guarantees, it decouples performance analysis from schedule design and offers limited guidance on important questions such as which decay shapes are preferable and how optimal schedules depend on problem characteristics like model capacity and task difficulty.

Recent work by
26
offers a complementary perspective in a controlled yet expressive setting.
Under feature-space linear regression with power-law structure
(
4
;
6
;
32
;
40
;
26
)
, they derive a
functional scaling law (FSL)
that expresses the loss as an explicit and analytically tractable functional of the learning-rate schedule. Moreover, empirical evidence in
26
shows that this functional characterization remains accurate in large language model (LLM) pre-training, despite the substantial gap between the theoretical setting and practical LLM training.
While the FSL provides a precise description of how a
given
learning-rate schedule shapes the loss, it does not address the problem of learning-rate schedule design, nor the characterization of optimal schedules.

In this paper, we focus on the problem of
optimal learning-rate schedules (LRSs)
.
Specifically, we characterize the LRS that minimizes the final-step loss over a fixed training horizon
N
(equivalently, a fixed data budget).
Under the FSL framework, this problem can be formulated as a constrained variational optimization problem, in which the loss is governed by a competition between
signal learning
, controlled by the source exponent
s>0
, and
noise forgetting
, determined by the capacity exponent
\beta>1
. Our main contributions are summarized as follows.

Optimal learning-rate schedules (LRSs).

We derive that the optimal LRS depends critically on the task difficulty. In the
easy-task regime
(
s\geqslant 1-1/\beta
), the optimal LRS follows a
power decay
to zero:

\eta_{*}(z)=\eta_{\text{peak}}\left(1-z/N\right)^{2\beta-1},

where peak learning rate
\eta_{\text{peak}}\eqsim N^{-\frac{1+s\beta-\beta}{1+s\beta}}
.
In contrast, in the
hard-task regime
(
s<1-1/\beta
), the optimal LRS exhibits a
WSD-like
structure: the decay phase occupies only a
o_{N}(1)
fraction of the training horizon, while retaining the same power-decay profile as the easy-task regime. Figure
1
(left) provides an illustration of these optimal LRSs.

Shape-fixed optimality and capacity saturation.

To isolate the essential structure underlying optimal LRSs, we consider a class of
fractional
schedules of the form

\eta(z)=\eta_{0}\,\zeta(z/N),

which depend on training steps only through the relative progress
z/N
and exhibit a power-decay tail near the end of training:

\zeta(x)\propto(1-x)^{\gamma}

as
x\to 1
.
Within this shape-fixed setting—where the decay shape is fixed and only the peak learning rate is tuned—we uncover a
capacity saturation
phenomenon: such schedules adapt to model capacity only up to
\beta\leqslant\gamma+1
, beyond which the achievable convergence rate saturates regardless of peak-rate tuning.
Figure
1
(right) summarizes this behavior via a phase diagram of convergence rates over the
(\beta,s)
plane.
This characterization clarifies the strengths and limitations of widely used practical schedules; in particular, Figure
1
(middle) shows that cosine LRS (
\gamma=2
; see Section
5
) indeed exhibits the predicted saturation.

Improved convergence rates for kernel regression.

Finally, to substantiate the predictions of the continuous-time FSL analysis, we provide a rigorous discrete-time analysis showing that one-pass SGD with a power-decay LRS attains the
exact
optimal convergence rate at the last iterate. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such result without logarithmic factors, improving upon prior analyses based on exponential-decay LRS
(
51
;
32
;
26
)
.

Neural scaling laws.

18

.
These laws have since guided large-scale training and been refined across architectures and training regimes

Our work fits into this line of research by providing a scaling-law analysis of the design of learning-rate schedules. In particular, we study the structure of optimal schedules by leveraging the FSL framework introduced by
26
.

Optimal learning-rate schedules for linear regression.

Previous analyses of optimal learning-rate schedules for linear regression often formulate the problem as an optimal control problem.
However, such analyses are technically challenging and typically restricted to low-dimensional settings or isotropic Hessians
(
28
;
12
)
. By contrast, leveraging the FSL framework, we develop a principled variational approach to derive and analyze optimal LRSs.

Understanding cosine and WSD schedules.

For cosine schedules,
30
attributes their empirical success to a
duration-aware
property: the cumulative learning rate scales linearly with the total training horizon while the learning rate itself decays to a horizon-independent minimum. Standard polynomial decay schedules fail to satisfy this dual requirement. In this work, we generalize this property through a class of
fractional
LRS and identify the regimes in which it attains optimal rates and those in which it does not.

For WSD schedules,
50
provide a river-valley landscape interpretation of their dynamical behavior;
44
;
26
offer theoretical evidence for the benefits of delayed decay but do not characterize the role of the
decay shape
itself. Meanwhile, existing empirical studies reach seemingly conflicting conclusions:
9
and
5
advocate linear decay, whereas
16
report superior performance for concave decay profiles such as
1-\sqrt{x}
. In this work, we show that the optimal decay shape is determined by the profile of the forgetting kernel, which governs the rate of noise forgetting. In addition,
35
numerically solve a variational problem based on a multipower-law model for LLM pre-training and find that the resulting LRS takes a WSD form with a decay shape approximately
(1-x)^{1.5}
. Our analysis provides theoretical support for this empirical observation.

One-pass SGD for kernel regression.

The convergence of one-pass SGD for kernel regression—often formulated as high-dimensional feature-space linear regression—has received considerable attention. In particular,
10
and
37
showed that averaged SGD attains the minimax-optimal rates
\mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{s\beta}{s\beta+1}})
in the easy-task regime and
\mathcal{O}(N^{-s})
in the hard-task regime. Subsequent work demonstrated that iterate averaging can be replaced by the more practical last iterate when learning-rate schedule is adopted, but the resulting rates typically incur logarithmic factors
(
51
;
32
;
26
)
.
In this work, we remove these logarithmic factors by employing power-decay schedules.

Preliminaries

Notation.

We write
\eqsim
to denote equivalence up to a multiplicative constant factor, and
\lesssim
(resp.
\gtrsim
) to denote an inequality up to a multiplicative constant factor.
For two nonnegative functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
, we write
f(t)\eqsim g(t)
if there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
(independent of
t
) such that

C_{1}f(t)\leqslant g(t)\leqslant C_{2}f(t),\;\forall\,t\geqslant 0.
We write a sequence
a_{N}=o_{N}(1)
if
\lim_{N\to\infty}a_{N}=0
.

3.1
Feature-Space Linear Regression

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
and denote by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}
the marginal on input domain
\mathcal{X}
. Samples
(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mathcal{D}
satisfy
y=f^{*}(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon
, where

\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
is independent of
\mathbf{x}
. We assume
f^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\langle\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\theta_{j}^{*}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x})
for some feature map
\bm{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\to\ell^{2}
and
\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\ell^{2}
.

Assumption 3.1

(Hypercontractive features)
.

For any
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\ell^{2}
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\langle\mathbf{u},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle^{2}\langle\mathbf{v},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle^{2}]\eqsim\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[\langle\mathbf{u},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle^{2}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[\langle\mathbf{v},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle^{2}].

Define the feature covariance operator by

\mathbf{H}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}\big[\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\big].

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\mathbf{H}
is diagonalized. Let

\lambda_{1}\geqslant\lambda_{2}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant 0
denote its eigenvalues, ordered
non-increasingly. This assumption is without loss of generality due to the
rotation equivariance of SGD.

Let
\{(\mathbf{x}_{k},y_{k})\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
be
N
samples drawn independently from
\mathcal{D}
.
To learn the target function, we consider the linear model
f(\mathbf{x};\bm{\theta})=\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle
trained by
one-pass SGD
:

\bm{\theta}_{k+1}=\bm{\theta}_{k}-\eta_{k}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}(f(\mathbf{x}_{k};\bm{\theta}_{k})-y_{k})^{2}\Big),\qquad\bm{\theta}_{0}=\mathbf{0},

(1)

where
(\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N-1})
is the
learning-rate schedule (LRS)
. We measure the performance using the excess risk

\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}\!\left[(f(\mathbf{x};\bm{\theta})-f^{*}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\right].

The choice of the LRS plays a crucial role in determining the final-step performance. Our goal is to characterize the
optimal
LRS for a fixed
N
.

3.2
Functional Scaling Laws

We adopt the FSL framework of
26
to characterize the impact of LRS on the
final-step loss
and to identify optimal schedules.
This framework operates under a power-law data assumption:

Assumption 3.2

(Power-law structures)
.

The following conditions hold for each
j\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
:

\lambda_{j}\eqsim j^{-\beta},\qquad\lambda_{j}|\theta^{*}_{j}|^{2}\eqsim j^{-(1+s\beta)}.

(2)

The condition
\lambda_{j}\eqsim j^{-\beta}
is referred to as the
capacity condition
, where the exponent
\beta
controls the decay rate of the feature spectrum. A smaller
\beta
yields slower eigenvalue decay, corresponding to a higher capacity.
The condition
\lambda_{j}\lvert\theta^{*}_{j}\rvert^{2}\eqsim j^{-(1+s\beta)}
is referred to as the
source condition
, which quantifies the alignment between the target function and the feature space. The capacity exponent
s
measures the
relative difficulty
: smaller values of
s
correspond to more challenging problems, in which a larger fraction of the signal energy is concentrated in high-frequency components.

In the FSL framework, rather than working directly with the discrete SGD, one adopts a continuous-time modeling perspective. Specifically, the discrete SGD is modeled by an Itô stochastic differential equation (SDE)
(
29
;
39
)
.
A central concept in FSL is the
intrinsic time
, which encodes the effect of the LRS. At iteration
k
, the intrinsic time is given by

t_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\eta_{j}.

At the continuous level,
t=\Gamma(z):=\int_{0}^{z}\eta(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z
, where
z
and
\eta(z)
denote the continuous step and learning rate
z
step, respectively.
Under this intrinsic-time parametrization, the SGD dynamics are modeled by the
Itô SDE

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=-\nabla\mathcal{E}(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{t})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\varphi(t)}\,\bm{\Sigma}(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{t})^{1/2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t},

(3)

where
\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
denotes the parameter at intrinsic time
t
,

\varphi(t):=\eta(\Gamma^{-1}(t))
is the learning rate in intrinsic time,

\mathbf{W}_{t}
is a standard Wiener process, and
\bm{\Sigma}(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
denotes the
covariance of gradient noise.

Theorem 4.4 of
26
shows that, in the label-noise–dominated regime
\sigma\gtrsim 1
and under the stability condition
\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\varphi(t)\leqslant C
for a sufficiently small constant
C>0
, the solution to the SDE (
3
) satisfies, for all
t\gtrsim 1
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{t})]\eqsim\mathcal{F}[t]:=\underbrace{(1+t)^{-s}}_{\text{signal learning}}+\int_{0}^{t}\underbrace{\mathcal{K}(t-\tau)}_{\text{forgetting kernel}}\varphi(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau,

(4)

where
\mathcal{K}(t):=(1+t)^{-(2-1/\beta)}
.
This FSL establishes a functional-level map from the LRS function to the loss at intrinsic time
t
and notably, the two terms exhibits a clean interpretation:

•

The
signal-learning
term corresponds to learning under full-batch gradient descent, capturing the rate at which SGD extracts signal
f^{*}
. This rate is determined by the relative difficulty
s
.

•

The
noise-accumulation
term characterizes how the LRS changes the accumulation and dissipation of gradient noise. The forgetting kernel
\mathcal{K}(t-\tau)
characterizes how the noise injected at time
\tau
still affects the loss at time
t
. Due to
\mathcal{K}(t)=(t+1)^{-(2-1/\beta)}
, a higher-capacity model (smaller
\beta
) tends to forget noise more slowly.

Remark 3.3
.

The theoretical and empirical evidence in
26
demonstrates that the FSL (
4
) can accurately model the effect of LRSs in feature-space linear regression, and even in large-scale LLM pre-training. In this work, we take this FSL as a starting point for studying optimal LRS—a direction that lies outside the scope of
26
.

4
Optimal Learning Rate Schedules

We begin by asking the following natural question:

Given a data budget
N
, what is the optimal LRS when the loss dynamics follows the FSL (
4
)?

To formalize the resource constraint, we move to domain of training steps. Let
\eta(z)
denote the learning rate at (continuous) step
z
and define the intrinsic time
t(z)=\int_{0}^{t}\eta(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z
. Then,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=t^{\prime}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z
and
\eta(\tau)=t^{\prime}(z)
. For a fixed
N
, by a change of variable, the noise term becomes

\int_{0}^{t(N)}\mathcal{K}(t(N)-\tau)\varphi(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{0}^{N}\mathcal{K}(t(N)-t(z))\,\bigl(t^{\prime}(z)\bigr)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z.

(5)

Hence, seeking the optimal LRS can be formulated as the resource-constrained
variational problem
:

\displaystyle\min_{t\in\mathrm{AC}([0,N])}

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{F}}[t]\coloneqq\left(1+t(N)\right)^{-s}\;+\;\int_{0}^{N}\mathcal{K}(t(N)-t(z))\,\bigl(t^{\prime}(z)\bigr)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z

(6)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle t(0)=0,

\displaystyle 0\leqslant t^{\prime}(z)\leqslant\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}\quad\text{for a.e. }z\in[0,N].

where
\mathrm{AC}([0,N])
denotes the set of absolutely continuous function over
[0,N]
.
The constraint
0\leqslant t^{\prime}(z)\leqslant\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}
enforces the nonnegativity of the learning
rate and an upper bound required for training stability

(
52
;
53
)
.

Before stating our characterization of the optimal LRS, we first clarify what constitutes a good LRS.
From (
6
), the signal-learning term favors a large intrinsic time
t(N)
, which corresponds to using a larger learning rate, whereas the noise-accumulation term favors a smaller learning rate.
A good LRS must balance these two competing effects.

Theorem 4.1

(Optimal learning-rate schedules)
.

Let
t_{*}
be a minimizer of (
6
) and define

\eta^{*}(z)\coloneqq t_{*}^{\prime}(z)
and
\mathcal{E}_{N}^{*}=\bar{\mathcal{F}}[t_{*}]
be the final-step loss. Then the following holds.

•

Easy-task regime
(
s\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{\beta}
): the optimal LRS follows a power decay to zero:

\eta^{*}(z)=\eta_{\mathrm{peak}}\left(1+o_{N}(1)-\frac{z}{N}\right)^{2\beta-1},\qquad\eta_{\mathrm{peak}}\eqsim N^{-\frac{1+s\beta-\beta}{1+s\beta}},

(7)

under which, the final-step loss satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{N}^{*}\eqsim N^{-\frac{s\beta}{s\beta+1}}.

•

Hard-task regime
(
s<1-\frac{1}{\beta}
): the optimal LRS is a WSD form:

\eta^{*}(z)=\begin{cases}\eta_{\mathrm{stability}},&0\leqslant z\leqslant N_{1},\\[4.0pt]
\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}\left(1+o_{N}(1)-\frac{z-N_{1}}{N-N_{1}}\right)^{2\beta-1},&N_{1}<z\leqslant N,\end{cases}

(8)

where the ratio of the decay phase satisfies

r^{*}_{N}\coloneqq\frac{N-N_{1}}{N}\eqsim N^{-\frac{(1-1/\beta)-s}{\,2-1/\beta\,}}=o_{N}(1).

(9)

Under this optimal LRS, the final-step loss scales as

\mathcal{E}_{N}^{*}\eqsim N^{-s}.

This theorem provides a characterization of the optimal LRS under the assumption that FSL correctly describes the loss dynamics. The proof is deferred to Appendix
A
.

We see that the optimal LRS depends critically on the task difficulty.
In the
easy-task regime
, the optimal LRS decreases monotonically from the beginning of training and follows a power decay to zero. Moreover, the peak learning rate scales with the training horizon according to a power law. In contrast, in the
hard-task regime
, the optimal LRS exhibits a WSD structure: it maintains the largest admissible learning rate
\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}
for most of training and decays only near the end. Notably, the decay phase occupies only a vanishing fraction of the training horizon as
N\to\infty
.

We next provide intuition for explaining these results through the trade-off between signal learning and noise forgetting:

•

The optimal decay shape.

In both regimes, the decay phase follows a power-decay profile whose exponent depends only on the capacity exponent
\beta
.
Intuitively, higher-capacity models forget noise more slowly and therefore require a faster decay of the learning rate to ensure that injected noise dissipates sufficiently.
More precisely, the optimal decay profile satisfies

\varphi^{*}(t)\propto\mathcal{K}(T-t)^{-1/2}
in intrinsic time; see (
19
) and the accompanying derivation.
The power-decay form arises from the power-law behavior of the forgetting kernel
\mathcal{K}(\cdot)
.

•

The emergence of a prolonged stable phase for hard tasks.

When
s
is small, the learning problem is hard: signal learning progresses very slowly and requires a large intrinsic time.
In contrast, the rate of noise forgetting is governed by the capacity exponent
\beta
and is independent of the task difficulty
s
.
As a result, the optimal strategy is to maintain the largest stable learning rate
\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}
for an extended period, and to allocate only a short decay phase at the end to eliminate accumulated noise.
As predicted by (
9
), harder tasks (smaller
s
) indeed need longer stable phases in the optimal LRS.

5
Shape-Fixed Learning-Rate Schedules: Optimality and Limitations

The analysis in Section
4
provides a complete characterization of the optimal learning-rate schedule (LRS) and the best achievable performance. Beyond optimality, these schedules share two salient structural features: a
fractional
form, in which the schedule depends on training progress only through the normalized step
z/N
, and a
power-decay to zero
in the terminal phase. To disentangle the contributions of these two structures, we consider the following family of LRSs.

Definition 5.1

(Fractional LRS with power-decay tail)
.

Given a training horizon
N
, a LRS

\eta_{N}:[0,N]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
is called
fractional
if it can be written as

\eta_{N}(z)=\eta_{0}\,\zeta\!\left(\frac{z}{N}\right),\qquad z\in[0,N],

(10)

where
\eta_{0}>0
is the initial learning rate and the profile function

\zeta:[0,1]\to[0,1]
satisfies
\zeta(0)=1
.
It is said to have a
power-decay tail
if there exist
constants
\gamma>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

\zeta(x)\eqsim(1-x)^{\gamma},\qquad\forall\,x\in[\delta,1].

(11)

Examples.

This class of schedules includes nearly all commonly used in practice:

•

Constant LRS

\eta_{N}(z)\equiv\eta_{0}
:

\zeta(x)\equiv 1
, corresponding to
\gamma=0
.

•

Cosine decay

\eta_{N}(z)=\tfrac{1}{2}\eta_{0}(1+\cos(\pi z/N))
:
here
\zeta(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\cos(\pi x))
. A Taylor expansion at
x=1

yields

\zeta(x)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}(1-x)^{2}+O\!\left(|1-x|^{4}\right),

and hence
\gamma=2
.

•

1-
sqrt decay

\eta_{N}(z)=\eta_{0}\bigl(1-\sqrt{z/N}\bigr)
:

\zeta(x)=1-\sqrt{x}
admits the expansion

\zeta(x)=\frac{1}{2}(1-x)+O\!\left(|1-x|^{2}\right),

implying
\gamma=1
.

•

Power decay

\eta_{N}(z)=\eta_{0}(1-z/N)^{\gamma}
:

\zeta(x)=(1-x)^{\gamma}
, which satisfies
Definition
5.1
for any
\gamma>0
.
The case
\gamma=1
is commonly referred to as
linear decay
in the literature.

First, for a fractional LRS, the total intrinsic time is given by

t(N)=\int_{0}^{N}\eta(u)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u=\eta_{0}\,N\int_{0}^{1}\zeta(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

This reveals a key property of fractional schedules: when
\eta_{0}
is fixed, the total intrinsic time grows
linearly
with the training horizon
N
. As a result, fractional LRSs can provide sufficient intrinsic time for
signal learning
.
Second, the power-decay tail ensures that the learning rate vanishes smoothly in the terminal phase, allowing SGD to forget accumulated noise at a controlled rate.

Theorem 5.2

(Scaling law for fractional LRS)
.

Let
\eta_{N}
be a fractional LRS,
\alpha=\min\{\beta,\;\gamma+1\}
and
T_{N}=\eta_{0}N\int_{0}^{1}\zeta(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
.
Then the final-step loss satisfies

\mathcal{F}[\eta_{N}]\;\eqsim\;T_{N}^{-s}+\eta_{0}T_{N}^{-(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})}\bigl(\log T_{N}\bigr)^{\mathbf{1}\{\beta=\gamma+1\}}.

(12)

The quantity
T_{N}
represents the total intrinsic training time.
The second term in (
12
) exhibits a

capacity saturation
effect: when the LRS decays more slowly than the
forgetting kernel, noise forgetting is governed by the decay rate of the LRS
rather than the model’s intrinsic capacity.
As a result, the exponent

\alpha=\min\{\gamma+1,\beta\}

acts as an
effective capacity exponent
governing noise forgetting. In particular, choosing a fast-decay tail
\gamma>\beta-1
recovers the intrinsic capacity
\alpha=\beta
. At the boundary case
\gamma+1=\beta
, an additional logarithmic factor appears.

Fix the tail exponent
\gamma
and optimize only over the peak learning rate
\eta_{0}
on the right-hand side of (
12
).
Let
\eta_{0}^{\star}
denote the minimizer, and let
\mathcal{E}_{N}^{\star}
be the resulting final-step excess risk.

Theorem 5.3

(Optimal fractional LRS)
.

Consider a fractional LRS with fixed tail exponent
\gamma
, and let

\alpha=\min\{\beta,\gamma+1\}
. Then:

•

Easy-task regime
(
s\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{\alpha}
):

–

If
\beta=\gamma+1
, then

\eta_{0}^{*}\;\eqsim\;N^{-\frac{s-1+1/\alpha}{s+1/\alpha}}\bigl(\log N\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{\,s+1/\alpha\,}},\;\mathcal{E}_{N}^{*}\;\eqsim\;N^{-\frac{s\alpha}{s\alpha+1}}\bigl(\log N\bigr)^{\frac{s\alpha}{s\alpha+1}}.

–

If
\beta\neq\gamma+1
, then

\eta_{0}^{*}\;\eqsim\;N^{-\frac{s-1+1/\alpha}{s+1/\alpha}},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{N}^{*}\;\eqsim\;N^{-\frac{s\alpha}{s\alpha+1}}.

•

Hard-task regime
(
s<1-\frac{1}{\alpha}
):

\eta_{0}^{*}\eqsim 1,\qquad\mathcal{E}_{N}^{*}\eqsim N^{-s}.

Comparing Theorem
5.3
with Theorem

, we observe that fixing the decay shape and optimizing only the peak learning rate shifts the boundary between the easy- and hard-task regimes from
s=1-\tfrac{1}{\beta}
to
s=1-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}
.
Figure

(right) provides a diagrammatic illustration of how the convergence rate under fractional LRSs varies with
(s,\beta)
for different
\gamma

Remark 5.4
.

We observe that, in both regimes, a fractional LRS with decay exponent
\gamma>\beta-1
is sufficient to attain the same convergence rates as those achieved by the exact optimal LRS.
By contrast, the latter employs the sharper decay exponent
\gamma=2\beta-1
and, in the hard-task regime, requires a prolonged stable phase.
This comparison highlights that an exact optimal LRS can differ substantially from schedules that merely achieve optimal convergence rates.

Practical implications.

The above analysis mirrors a common practice in large-scale training, where one fixes the shape of LRS and tunes only the peak learning rate. Theorem
5.3
therefore provides a principled understanding of both the strengths and limitations of this approach. In particular, for a fractional LRS with power-decay exponent
\gamma
, tuning only the peak learning rate achieves the
optimal
convergence rate whenever the model capacity satisfies
\beta\leqslant\gamma+1
. In contrast, when
\beta>\gamma+1
, corresponding to lower-capacity models, this strategy can lead to suboptimal rates.

As concrete examples, cosine decay (
\gamma=2
) achieves optimal rates for

\beta\leqslant 3
, while linear decay and
1-
sqrt decay (
\gamma=1
) achieve optimal rates for
\beta\leqslant 2
. Since modern deep learning models typically operate in high-capacity regimes, these results may provide an explanation of why shape-fixed LRSs perform well in practice.

SGD for Kernel Regression

In this section, we show that the optimal LRS derived within the continuous-time FSL framework can be indeed transferred to discrete-time SGD for kernel regression.

A function
K:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be a kernel if there exists a feature map
\phi:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{H}
such that

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\langle\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}),\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},

where
\mathbb{H}
is a separable Hilbert space. Consider the hypothesis class

\mathcal{H}\coloneqq\{f_{\bm{\theta}}=\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}:\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{H}\}.

Then
\mathcal{H}
is the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(
2
)
corresponding to the kernel
K
.
Suppose
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}[K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})]<\infty
. Then,
there exist nonnegative eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

and an orthonormal system
\{\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}})
such that

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

where the convergence is in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}})
.

Assumption 6.1

(Capacity condition)
.

There exists a
\beta>1
such that
\lambda_{j}\lesssim j^{-\beta}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.

Assumption 6.2

(Source condition)
.

f^{*}=\sum_{j}a_{j}\lambda_{j}^{s/2}\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j}
with
\sum_{j}a_{j}^{2}\leqslant 1
for some
s>0
.

These are classic capacity and source conditions, widely used in the study of kernel methods
(
55
;
10
;
11
;
41
;
15
;
14
;
36
)
. Assumption
3.2
can be viewed as a power-law variant of these conditions. Analogously, the capacity condition controls the effectively size of the hypothesis space through the eigenvalue decay.
The source condition captures the regularity of the target function

. The parameter

measures how well the target aligns with the spectral geometry of the kernel and a larger

The following result from
8

established a lower bound on the best achievable convergence rate, independent of the choice of estimator.
Suppose Assumption
6.1
and
6.2
hold. Let

\mathcal{D}_{N}

We now present our main result. Through a detailed analysis of the dynamics of SGD with the power decay LRS, we derive the convergence rate of SGD with power decay.

Theorem 6.3

(Convergence rate of SGD with power decay)

Suppose Assumptions

and

\eta_{k}=\eta_{0}(1-k/N)^{\gamma}

•

Easy-task regime
(

Proposition 6.4

Suppose that
\max_{i\in[N]}\eta_{i}\lesssim 1

To the best of our knowledge, Theorem

Notably, this improvement is achieved by employing a power-decay LRS, which is inspired by our optimal LRS analysis under the FSL framework.
By contrast, the analyses in

Conceptually, power-decay LRSs precisely match the power noise-forgetting dynamics, striking the optimal balance between signal learning and noise dissipation; in contrast, exponential decay over-regularizes late iterations, leading to logarithmic suboptimality for last-iterate SGD.

7.1
Proof Sketch of Theorem
4.1

Directly solving the variational problem (
6
) is hard due to the non-linearity. To address this, we adopt a decoupled approach:

Step 1: Deriving the optimal profile under a fixed intrinsic time budget.

We first fix the total intrinsic time
t(N)=T
and optimize the schedule profile subject
solely
to this constraint. Noting
t=\Gamma(z):=\int_{0}^{z}\eta(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u
and
\varphi(t)=\eta(\Gamma^{-1}(t))
, the intrinsic-time constraint becomes

N=\int_{0}^{N}1\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\eta(z)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\varphi(\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

For fixed intrinsic time, the signal-learning term is constant; we only need to minimize noise term:

\displaystyle\min_{\varphi:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)\varphi(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\varphi(\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=N.

(14)

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields

\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)\varphi(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\left(\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\varphi(\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\geqslant\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{2}.

Consequently, the noise term (
14
) is minimized when the equality condition holds, which yields the optimal intrinsic-time profile:

\varphi^{*}(\tau)=\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(T-u)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{N\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)}}\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)}}=(1+T-\tau)^{1-\frac{1}{2\beta}}.

(15)

This also translates to a power-decay in training steps:
\eta(z)\propto\left(1-z/N\right)^{2\beta-1}
.

Step 2: Determining the optimal intrinsic time budget.

Substituting (
15
) into (
14
), the noise term under optimal LRS becomes

\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)\varphi(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=\frac{1}{N}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{2}\eqsim\frac{T^{\frac{1}{\beta}}}{N}.

Consequently, the total excess risk is given by
\mathcal{E}_{N,T}\eqsim T^{-s}+\frac{T^{\frac{1}{\beta}}}{N}
. Minimizing this risk with respect to
T
, we obtain the optimal intrinsic time horizon
T^{*}\eqsim N^{\frac{\beta}{1+s\beta}}
.

Step 3: Incorporating the peak learning-rate constraint.

For the unconstrained solution, the implied peak learning rate scales as

\varphi^{*}(0)\eqsim N^{-\frac{1+\beta(s-1)}{s\beta+1}}.

This scaling exhibits two distinct regimes.
(i) When
s>1-1/\beta
, the exponent is negative, and hence
\varphi^{*}(0)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.
In this case, the stability constraint
\eta_{\text{peak}}\leqslant\eta_{\text{stability}}
is asymptotically inactive.
(ii) When
s<1-1/\beta
, the unconstrained peak diverges as
N\to\infty
, violating the stability constraint.
Consequently, the peak constraint becomes
active
and must be explicitly enforced.

To handle this regime, we apply the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions to the fully constrained variational problem.
Using Lagrange multipliers and exploiting the monotonicity of the optimal profile, we show that the resulting optimal LRS exhibits a WSD-like structure.

7.2
Proof Sketch of Theorem
5.2

We first transform the training-step LRS into its intrinsic-time counterpart, denoted as
\varphi(\tau)\eqsim\eta_{0}\bar{\zeta}(\tau/T)
. Crucially, the fractional structure preserves in intrinsic time. Specifically, if the training-step profile exhibits a power-decay tail, the intrinsic-time profile also possesses a power-decay tail, satisfying
\bar{\zeta}(x)\eqsim(1-x)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}
for
x\in[\bar{\delta},1]
and some
\bar{\delta}\in(0,1)
. Applying the FSL (
4
), we derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\eta_{N}]

\displaystyle\eqsim T_{N}^{-s}+\int_{0}^{T_{N}}\mathcal{K}(T_{N}-\tau)\eta_{0}\bar{\zeta}\left(\frac{\tau}{T_{N}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(16)

\displaystyle\eqsim T_{N}^{-s}+\frac{\eta_{0}}{T_{N}^{1-1/\beta}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\bar{\zeta}(x)}{(T_{N}^{-1}+1-x)^{2-1/\beta}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

We decompose the integral into two regions:
[0,\bar{\delta}]
and
[\bar{\delta},1]
. The asymptotic behavior is
dominated by the tail integral
over
[\bar{\delta},1]
. We analyze the convergence based on the exponent comparison:

•

Fast decay regime
(
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}>1-\frac{1}{\beta}
): In this case, the combined exponent satisfies
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}-(2-\frac{1}{\beta})>-1
, ensuring the integral converges absolutely:

\int_{\bar{\delta}}^{1}\frac{\bar{\zeta}(x)}{(T_{N}+1^{-1}-x)^{2-1/\beta}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\eqsim\int_{\bar{\delta}}^{1}(1-x)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}-(2-\frac{1}{\beta})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

•

Slow decay regime
(
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}<1-\frac{1}{\beta}
): Here, the integral diverges as
x\to 1
. The term
T_{N}^{-1}
acts as a cutoff, yielding:

\int_{\bar{\delta}}^{1}\frac{\bar{\zeta}(x)}{(T_{N}^{-1}+1-x)^{2-1/\beta}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\eqsim\int_{\bar{\delta}}^{1}(T_{N}^{-1}+1-x)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}-(2-\frac{1}{\beta})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\eqsim T_{N}^{(1-\frac{1}{\beta})-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}.

Combining the two regimes gives the rate
T_{N}^{-\min\{1-\frac{1}{\beta},\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}\}}
. At the boundary
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}=1-\frac{1}{\beta}
, the tail integral contributes an additional
\log T_{N}
factor.

Intuitively, when the LRS decays rapidly, the noise injection diminishes faster than the forgetting kernel can dissipate it; consequently, the bottleneck becomes the decay rate of the forgetting kernel itself. In contrast, when the LRS decays more slowly, the noise accumulation overwhelms the forgetting mechanism, leading to a sub-optimal total noise forgetting.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we study optimal learning rate schedules (LRSs) under a fixed training horizon within the functional scaling law framework. We derive optimal LRSs, identify their essential structural properties, and use these insights to explain the empirical success of cosine decay and warmup–stable–decay (WSD) schedules as well as their limitations. This characterization explains why different schedules can behave similarly in some regimes, yet diverge sharply in others, as a function of task difficulty and model capacity. Finally, we further leverage this structural understanding to improve the last-iterate convergence rate of SGD for kernel regression.

The core insight underlying our analysis is a fundamental trade-off between

signal learning
and
noise forgetting
.
This perspective provides a unified lens for many existing LRSs and offers principled guidance for designing new ones, thereby bridging theoretical and practical training regimes.
A promising direction for future work is to extend this analysis to more realistic optimization settings, incorporating adaptivity, momentum, and batch-size effects.

Appendix A
Proof for Theorem
4.1

In this section, we provide a detailed proof of Theorem
4.1
, structured into the following three steps. We define the auxiliary functions:

\Phi(T)=(1+T)^{-s},\quad L(T,t,t^{\prime})=\mathcal{K}(T-t)(t^{\prime})^{2}.

The objective functional in training step is then:

\displaystyle\min_{t\in\operatorname{AC}([0,N]),T}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}[t,T]:=\Phi(T)+\int_{0}^{N}L(T,t(z),t^{\prime}(z))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

(17)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle t(0)=0,\;t(N)=T,

\displaystyle 0\leqslant t^{\prime}(z)\leqslant\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}\quad\text{for a.e.\ }z\in[0,N].

Directly solving the variational problem (
17
) is challenging due to the non-linearity of the functional with respect to the LRS function. To address this, we adopt a decoupled approach and decompose the proof into three steps.

A.1
Step 1: Deriving the Optimal Profile under a Fixed Intrinsic Time Budget

We first fix the total intrinsic time
t(N)=T
and optimize the schedule profile subject
solely
to this constraint (temporarily omitting the peak learning rate constraint). Formally, we apply the change of variables
z=t^{-1}(\tau)
. By the inverse function theorem, the differential transforms as:

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau}{t^{\prime}(z)}=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau}{\eta(z)}=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau}{\varphi(\tau)}.

Then, the requirement imposes the following integral constraint:

N=\int_{0}^{N}1\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\varphi(\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau,

which leads to the following variational problem of noise term:

\displaystyle\min_{\varphi:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)\varphi(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

(18)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\varphi(\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=N.

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields

\left(\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)\varphi(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\left(\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\varphi(\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\geqslant\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{2}.

Consequently, the noise term is minimized when the equality condition holds, which yields the optimal intrinsic profile:

\displaystyle\varphi(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(T-u)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}{N\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)}}

(19)

\displaystyle=a_{N,T}(1+T-\tau)^{1-\frac{1}{2\beta}}

where
a_{N,T}:=\frac{2\beta}{N}((1+T)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}-1)
. We have used
\mathcal{K}(t)=(1+t)^{1/\beta-2}
.

Now, we proceed to derive the LRS in terms of training steps
z
. Recall that
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}=\varphi(\tau)
. Substituting the profile from (
19
), we have

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}=\varphi(\tau)=a_{N,T}(1+T-\tau)^{1-\frac{1}{2\beta}},

(20)

with boundary condition
\tau=0
when
z=0
.

Solving the ODE (
20
), we derive

z=a_{N,T}^{-1}2\beta(1+T)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}-a_{N,T}^{-1}2\beta(1+T-\tau)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}},

which implies the following intrinsic time function:

t(z)=1+T-\left((1+T)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}-\frac{a_{N,T}}{2\beta}z\right)^{2\beta}.

Differentiating with respect to
z
gives the training-step LRS:

\eta(z)=t^{\prime}(z)=\frac{2\beta}{N}\left((1+T)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}-1\right)^{2\beta}\left(1+\frac{1}{(1+T)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}-1}-\frac{z}{N}\right)^{2\beta-1}.

Step 2: Determining the Optimal Intrinsic Time Budget

Substituting the optimal intrinsic profile (
19

18
), we derive the accumulated variance:

\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)\varphi(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=\frac{1}{N}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(T-\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{2}\eqsim\frac{T^{\tfrac{1}{\beta}}}{N}.

Consequently, the total excess risk is given by

\mathcal{E}_{N,T}\eqsim T^{-s}+\frac{T^{\tfrac{1}{\beta}}}{N}.

Minimizing this risk with respect to

T_{\text{opt}}\eqsim N^{\frac{\beta}{1+s\beta}}

A.3
Step 3: Incorporating the Peak Learning Rate Constraint

Based on the unconstrained solution, the implied peak learning rate scales as:

\eta(0)\eqsim N^{-\frac{1+\beta(s-1)}{s\beta+1}}.

This scaling behavior reveals two distinct regimes:

•

Easy-task regime (
s>1-1/\beta

In this case, the exponent is negative, meaning
\eta(0)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Consequently, the physical constraint
\eta_{\text{peak}}\leqslant\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}
is naturally satisfied (inactive) for sufficiently large

•

Hard-task regime (
s<1-1/\beta

Conversely, the optimal unconstrained peak diverges as
N\to\infty
. This violates the stability constraint
\eta(z)\leqslant\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}

To address the hard-task regime, we apply the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions to solve the fully constrained variational problem.

To explicitly incorporate the maximal learning rate constraint
t^{\prime}(z)\leqslant\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}
, we formulate the Lagrangian in the training step domain. We introduce the Lagrange multipliers:

\lambda(z)\geqslant 0\quad(\text{dual for }t^{\prime}(z)\leqslant\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}),\qquad\mu\in\mathbb{R}\quad(\text{dual for }t(N)-T=0).

The generalized Lagrangian functional
\mathcal{J}
is defined as:

\mathcal{J}[t,T,\lambda,\mu]\;=\;\Phi(T)\;+\;\int_{0}^{N}\Bigl[L\!\left(T,t,t^{\prime}\right)+\lambda(z)\bigl(t^{\prime}(z)-\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}\bigr)\Bigr]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\;+\;\mu\bigl(t(N)-T\bigr).

Theorem A.2

(KKT condition)
.

A feasible pair
(t^{\star},T^{\star})
is optimal only if there exist multipliers

\lambda^{\star}\in L^{\infty}([0,N])
and
\mu^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(1) Euler–Lagrange stationarity:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}L-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\!\Bigl(\partial_{t^{\prime}}L+\lambda^{\star}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\qquad z\in(0,N);

(2) Boundary stationarity:

\displaystyle\Bigl[\partial_{t^{\prime}}L+\lambda^{\star}\Bigr]_{z=N}+\mu^{\star}

\displaystyle=0;

(3) Scalar stationarity (w.r.t.
T
):

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(T^{\star})+\int_{0}^{N}\partial_{T}L\,\mathrm{d}z-\mu^{\star}

\displaystyle=0;

(4) Primal feasibility:

\displaystyle t^{\star}(0)=0,\quad t^{\star}(N)=T^{\star},\quad t^{\star\,\prime}(z)\leqslant 1;

(5) Dual feasibility:

\displaystyle\lambda^{\star}(z)\geqslant 0;

(6) Complementary slackness:

\displaystyle\lambda^{\star}(z)\,\bigl(t^{\star\,\prime}(z)-\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}\bigr)=0;

\displaystyle\qquad z\in[0,N].

If
t^{\star\,\prime}(z)<\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}
at some point, condition (6) forces
\lambda^{\star}(z)=0
;
where the derivative saturates the bound (
t^{\star\,\prime}=\eta_{\mathrm{stability}}
),

\lambda^{\star}
may be positive.

Direct analysis of the above KKT condition is still complicated. A key observation that make the analysis easier is the following observation:

Proposition A.3

(Monotonicity)
.

Let

be the solution of (

Proof.

To prove this, we only need to show for any fixed

\displaystyle G_{T}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{N}\bigl(1+T-t(z)\bigr)^{-(2-1/\beta)}\,\bigl(t^{\prime}(z)\bigr)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}(1+T-\tau)^{-(2-\frac{1}{\beta})}\varphi(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\int_{0}^{T}w(\tau)\varphi(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau,

where the weight function
w(\cdot)
is increasing.

A simple “bubble-sort” argument suffice to show that the optimal

\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}

\varphi(\tau_{1})<\varphi(\tau_{2})

\tilde{\varphi}
by swapping the values on small intervals around
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
.

•

The constraint
\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{\tilde{\varphi}(\tau)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=N
and boundedness constraint are unaffected by the swaps.

•

The change of integral:

\Delta=[\varphi(\tau_{2})-\varphi(\tau_{1})](w(\tau_{1})-w(\tau_{2}))<0.

Thus the swap strictly lowers the objective. Repeating finitely many swaps (or taking a limit) yields a decreasing function with no larger cost, contradicting optimality. Hence a minimiser must be decreasing.
∎

Remark A.4

Throughout this section, let

\eta_{N}(z)=\eta_{0}\,\zeta\!\left(\frac{z}{N}\right),\qquad z\in[0,N],

Learning rate in intrinsic time.

Following the FSL notation in (

\varphi(t)\coloneqq\eta_{N}(z(t)),\qquad t\in[0,T_{N}].

We introduce the intrinsic-time profile function

\bar{\zeta}(y)\coloneqq\zeta\!\Bigl(\rho^{-1}(y\rho_{1})\Bigr),\qquad y\in[0,1].

(22)

Then for any

Tail exponent under intrinsic-time

The power-decay tail in Definition

\zeta(x)\eqsim(1-x)^{\gamma},\qquad x\in[\delta,1].

Lemma B.1

(Intrinsic-time tail exponent)
.

Assume
\zeta(x)\eqsim(1-x)^{\gamma}
for all

with some
\gamma>0
.
Let

\bar{\delta}\coloneqq\frac{\rho(\delta)}{\rho_{1}}\in(0,1).

Then for all
y\in[\bar{\delta},1]

\bar{\zeta}(y)\eqsim(1-y)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}.

Proof.

Fix
y\in[\bar{\delta},1]
and define

.
Then
\bar{\zeta}(y)=\zeta(x)

Let

Since
\zeta(u)\eqsim(1-u)^{\gamma}

Moreover,

Hence, for
y\in[\bar{\delta},1]

1-x\eqsim(1-y)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}.

Finally, using
\zeta(x)\eqsim(1-x)^{\gamma}
on
[\delta,1]
and
\bar{\zeta}(y)=\zeta(x)
,
we obtain

\bar{\zeta}(y)=\zeta(x)\eqsim(1-x)^{\gamma}\eqsim\Bigl((1-y)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}\Bigr)^{\gamma}=(1-y)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}},

which holds for all
y\in[\bar{\delta},1]
.

\{(\lambda_{j},\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j})\}_{j\geqslant 1}
be the spectral system of
\mathcal{I}
in

L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}})
, so that

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

with convergence in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}})
.
Let
\{\mathbf{v}_{j}\}_{j\geqslant 1}\subset\mathbb{H}
be eigenvectors of
\mathcal{T}
such that

\mathcal{T}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\lambda_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j},\qquad\langle\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\delta_{ij}.

Then the two bases are linked by the standard relation

\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_{j}^{-1/2}\,\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},

(35)

where the equality holds in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}})
.

Capacity and source conditions.

We recall the capacity and source condition in Assumption
6.1
and
6.2
:

•

(Capacity condition)
There exists
\beta>1
such that
\lambda_{j}\lesssim j^{-\beta}
.

•

(Source condition)
There exists
s>0
and coefficients
\{a_{j}\}_{j\geqslant 1}
with
\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}^{2}\leqslant 1
such that

f^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}\,\lambda_{j}^{\frac{s}{2}}\,\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j}(\mathbf{x}).

We note that the source condition can be equivalently stated in the space
\mathbb{H}
. That is,

\bm{\theta}^{*}=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}\,\lambda_{j}^{\frac{s-1}{2}}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\theta^{*}_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}.

where the coefficient
\theta^{*}_{j}=a_{j}\,\lambda_{j}^{\frac{s-1}{2}}
. Note that when
s<1
, the sequence
\{\theta^{*}_{j}\}
may not be square-summable, so the corresponding
\bm{\theta}^{*}
may not belong to
\mathbb{H}
as a vector; however, each coefficient
\theta^{*}_{j}
is well-defined,
and this is sufficient for our coordinate-wise SGD analysis below.
We also assume w.l.o.g. that
f^{*}
lies in the closure of
\mathrm{span}\{\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\}
(otherwise its orthogonal component is not learnable by any
f_{\bm{\theta}}
and only contributes an irreducible constant error).

C.2
Analysis of One-Pass SGD

We consider the standard kernel regression model

y=f^{*}(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\mid\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

and let
\{(\mathbf{x}_{k},y_{k})\}_{k=0}^{N-1}
be i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{D}
.
Define the population squared risk by

\mathcal{R}(f)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[(f(\mathbf{x})-y)^{2}\big].

Using
y=f^{*}(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon
and
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0
,
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^{2}]=\sigma^{2}
, we have

\mathcal{R}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[(f(\mathbf{x})-f^{*}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\big]+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}.

The last term is the irreducible noise level. Accordingly, we define the excess risk

\mathcal{E}(f)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[(f(\mathbf{x})-f^{*}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\big].

Recall the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
induced by
K
, where each
f\in\mathcal{H}
satisfies the reproducing property
f(\mathbf{x})=\langle f,K(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.

One-pass SGD in RKHS.

For a single sample
z=(\mathbf{x},y)
, define the instantaneous loss

\ell(f;z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\,(f(\mathbf{x})-y)^{2},\qquad f\in\mathcal{H}.

Its gradient in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
is given by

\nabla\ell(f;(\mathbf{x},y))=(f(\mathbf{x})-y)\,K(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}.

Indeed, for any
g\in\mathcal{H}
, the directional derivative satisfies

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\ell(f+tg;(\mathbf{x},y))\right|_{t=0}=(f(\mathbf{x})-y)\,g(\mathbf{x})=\langle(f(\mathbf{x})-y)K(\mathbf{x},\cdot),\,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

where we used the reproducing property
g(\mathbf{x})=\langle g,K(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Starting from
f_{0}=0\in\mathcal{H}
, one-pass SGD updates
f_{k}
by

f_{k+1}=f_{k}-\eta_{k}\nabla\ell(f_{k};(\mathbf{x}_{k},y_{k}))=f_{k}-\eta_{k}\big(f_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{k})-y_{k}\big)\,K(\mathbf{x}_{k},\cdot),\qquad k=0,\dots,N-1.

(36)

Translating the update to
\mathbb{H}
.

Under the feature representation
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)=\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
, the update (
36
) is equivalent to the parameter update

\bm{\theta}_{k+1}=\bm{\theta}_{k}-\eta_{k}\big(\langle\bm{\theta}_{k},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-y_{k}\big)\,\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\qquad k=0,\dots,N-1.

(37)

with
\bm{\theta}_{0}=0
. For each
j\geqslant 1
, define the coordinates

\theta_{k,j}\coloneqq\langle\bm{\theta}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},\qquad u_{k}^{j}\coloneqq\theta_{k,j}-\theta^{*}_{j}.

Using
y_{k}=f^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{k})+\epsilon_{k}
and taking inner products with
\mathbf{v}_{j}
in (
37
), we obtain

u_{k+1}^{j}=u_{k}^{j}-\eta_{k}\Big(\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\epsilon_{k}\Big)\,\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}},

(38)

where
\mathbf{u}_{k}
denotes the error vector in eigen-coordinates, i.e.,
\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=u_{k}^{j}
for all
j\geqslant 1
.

Conditioned on
\mathbf{u}_{k}
, the drift satisfies

\mathbb{E}\!\big[\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\,\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\,\big|\,\mathbf{u}_{k}\big]=\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\mathcal{T}\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\lambda_{j}u_{k}^{j}.

Define the centered noise

\xi_{k}^{j}\coloneqq\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\,\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\lambda_{j}u_{k}^{j}-\epsilon_{k}\,\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}.

(39)

Then
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}^{j}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}]=0
, and (
38
) can be rewritten as

u_{k+1}^{j}=(1-\eta_{k}\lambda_{j})u_{k}^{j}-\eta_{k}\xi_{k}^{j}.

(40)

Our goal is to control the excess risk

\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big[(f_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})-f^{*}(\mathbf{x}))^{2}\big].

Using (
35
), we have the
L^{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}})
expansion

f_{\bm{\theta}_{k}}(\mathbf{x})-f^{*}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\,u_{k}^{j}\,\mathrm{\mathbf{e}}_{j}(\mathbf{x}),

and therefore

\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}(u_{k}^{j})^{2}.

(41)

In particular, bounding
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})]
reduces to bounding the second moments

\mathbb{E}[(u_{k}^{j})^{2}]
for all
j\geqslant 1
.
Squaring (
40
) and conditioning on
\mathbf{u}_{k}
, the cross term vanishes since
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}^{j}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}]=0
, yielding

\mathbb{E}\big[(u_{k+1}^{j})^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}\big]=(1-\eta_{k}\lambda_{j})^{2}(u_{k}^{j})^{2}+\eta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[(\xi_{k}^{j})^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}\big].

(42)

Taking expectation gives

\mathbb{E}\big[(u_{k+1}^{j})^{2}\big]=(1-\eta_{k}\lambda_{j})^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[(u_{k}^{j})^{2}\big]+\eta_{k}^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[(\xi_{k}^{j})^{2}\big].

(43)

Proposition C.1
.

For any
k\geqslant 1
and any
j\geqslant 1
,

\mathbb{E}\big[(u_{k}^{j})^{2}\big]=\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(1-\eta_{i}\lambda_{j})^{2}(u_{0}^{j})^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\eta_{i}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\xi_{i}^{j})^{2}\big]\,\prod_{\ell=i+1}^{k-1}(1-\eta_{\ell}\lambda_{j})^{2}.

Proof.

Iteratively unroll (
43
).
∎

Intrinsic time and excess risk bound

Define the intrinsic time

t_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\eta_{i},\qquad k\geqslant 1,\qquad t_{0}\coloneqq 0.

Assume
\eta_{\max}\coloneqq\max_{0\leqslant i\leqslant N-1}\eta_{i}\leqslant\lambda_{1}^{-1}
.
Then for any
0\leqslant i<k
and any
j\geqslant 1
,

\prod_{\ell=i}^{k-1}(1-\eta_{\ell}\lambda_{j})^{2}\leqslant\exp\!\Big(-2\lambda_{j}\sum_{\ell=i}^{k-1}\eta_{\ell}\Big)=\exp\!\big(-2\lambda_{j}(t_{k}-t_{i})\big).

Combining this with Proposition
C.1
and (
41
) yields

2\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})]\leqslant\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}e^{-2\lambda_{j}t_{k}}(u_{0}^{j})^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\eta_{i}^{2}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}e^{-2\lambda_{j}(t_{k}-t_{i+1})}\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\xi_{i}^{j})^{2}\big].

(44)

Recall the hypercontractivity condition in Assumption
3.1
. It gives that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}\!\big[\langle\mathbf{u},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\langle\mathbf{v},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\big]\leqslant C\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\!\big[\langle\mathbf{u},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\big]\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\!\big[\langle\mathbf{v},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\big].

(45)

Proposition C.2
.

Suppose (
45
) holds and
\epsilon_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
is independent of
\mathbf{x}_{k}
.
Then for any
k\geqslant 0
and any
j\geqslant 1
,

\mathbb{E}\big[(\xi_{k}^{j})^{2}\big]\leqslant\lambda_{j}\big(2C\,\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})]+\sigma^{2}\big).

Proof.

Recall the definition

\xi_{k}^{j}=\Big(\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\epsilon_{k}\Big)\,\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}-\lambda_{j}u_{k}^{j}.

Let
A_{k}\coloneqq\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}\,\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
and

B_{k}\coloneqq\epsilon_{k}\,\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
.
Then
\xi_{k}^{j}=(A_{k}-\mathbb{E}[A_{k}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}])-B_{k}
since
\mathbb{E}[A_{k}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}]=\lambda_{j}u_{k}^{j}
.

Conditioned on
\mathbf{u}_{k}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[B_{k}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[A_{k}-\mathbb{E}[A_{k}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}]\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}]=0
.
Moreover,
\epsilon_{k}
is independent of
\mathbf{x}_{k}
and
\mathbf{u}_{k}
, hence the cross term vanishes:

\mathbb{E}\!\big[(A_{k}-\mathbb{E}[A_{k}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}])\,B_{k}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}\big]=0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\xi_{k}^{j})^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[(A_{k}-\mathbb{E}[A_{k}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}])^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[B_{k}^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}[A_{k}^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}]+\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{k}^{2}]\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\big].

The second term equals
\sigma^{2}\lambda_{j}
because

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\big]=\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\mathcal{T}\mathbf{v}_{j}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\lambda_{j}.

For the first term, apply (
45
) with
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{k}
and
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{j}
:

\mathbb{E}[A_{k}^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}]=\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\,\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}\big]\leqslant C\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}\big]\,\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\big].

Using
\mathbb{E}[\langle\mathbf{v}_{j},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}]=\lambda_{j}
and

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}\big]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}}\big[\langle\mathbf{u}_{k},\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\big]=2\,\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k}),

we obtain
\mathbb{E}[A_{k}^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}]\leqslant 2C\,\lambda_{j}\,\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})
.
Combining the two bounds yields

\mathbb{E}\big[(\xi_{k}^{j})^{2}\mid\mathbf{u}_{k}\big]\leqslant\lambda_{j}\big(2C\,\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})+\sigma^{2}\big).

Taking expectation over
\mathbf{u}_{k}
gives the desired bound.
∎

Proposition C.3
.

Assume
\eta_{\max}\leqslant\lambda_{1}^{-1}
and (
45
) holds.
Then for any
k\geqslant 1
,

2\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})]\leqslant\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}e^{-2\lambda_{j}t_{k}}(u_{0}^{j})^{2}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\eta_{i}^{2}\,\big(2C\,\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{i})]+\sigma^{2}\big)\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{j}(t_{k}-t_{i+1})}.

Proof.

Plug Proposition
C.2
into (
44
) and use

\lambda_{j}e^{-2\lambda_{j}(t_{k}-t_{i+1})}\cdot\mathbb{E}[(\xi_{i}^{j})^{2}]\leqslant\lambda_{j}e^{-2\lambda_{j}(t_{k}-t_{i+1})}\cdot\lambda_{j}\big(2C\,\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{i})]+\sigma^{2}\big).

∎

The following proposition provides a uniform bound on the excess risk, when the maximal learning rate is smaller than a certain constant. We note that
\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{T})=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}=\mathbb{E}[K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})]<\infty
.

Proposition C.4

(Uniform boundedness of the excess risk)
.

Assume (
45
) holds, and

\eta_{\max}<\frac{1}{2C\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{T})}.

Then

\sup_{k\leqslant N}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})]\lesssim 1.

Proof.

From Proposition
C.3
, the first term satisfies

\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}e^{-2\lambda_{j}t_{k}}(u_{0}^{j})^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}(u_{0}^{j})^{2}=2\,\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{0}).

For the second term, use
\eta_{i}^{2}\leqslant\eta_{\max}\eta_{i}=\eta_{\max}(t_{i+1}-t_{i})
and obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\eta_{i}^{2}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{j}(t_{k}-t_{i+1})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\eta_{\max}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{k}}e^{-2\lambda_{j}(t_{k}-t)}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\eta_{\max}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{2}\big(1-e^{-2\lambda_{j}t_{k}}\big)\leqslant\frac{\eta_{\max}}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{T}).

Let
M_{k}\coloneqq\max_{0\leqslant i\leqslant k}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{i})]
.
Then Proposition
C.3
implies

2\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})]\leqslant 2\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{0})+\frac{\eta_{\max}\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{T})}{2}\Big(2C\,M_{k-1}+\sigma^{2}\Big).

Taking maximum over
k
and using
\eta_{\max}<(2C\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{T}))^{-1}
yields
M_{k}\lesssim 1
by induction.
∎

With this uniform bound, we obtain the following bound on the excess risk.

Lemma C.5
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
C.4
, for any
k\geqslant 1
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{k})]\lesssim\underbrace{\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}e^{-2\lambda_{j}t_{k}}(u_{0}^{j})^{2}}_{S_{k}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\eta_{i}^{2}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{j}(t_{k}-t_{i+1})}}_{\mathcal{N}_{k}}.

Proof.

By Proposition
C.4
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{i})]\lesssim 1
uniformly in
i\leqslant k
.
Hence in Proposition
C.3
, the factor
(2C\,\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{i})]+\sigma^{2})
is bounded by a constant and can be absorbed into
\lesssim
.
∎

C.3
Bounding Signal Learning and Noise Accumulation

We now bound the two terms
S_{k}
and
\mathcal{N}_{k}
in Lemma
C.5
. They correspond to the signal-learning term and the noise-accumulation term in the FSL (
4
). Since our goal is the final-step excess risk
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\bm{\theta}_{N})]
, it suffices to bound
S_{N}
and
\mathcal{N}_{N}
. We denote the total intrinsic time by
T=t_{N}
.

C.3.1
Signal-Learning Term
S_{N}

Proposition C.6

(Bound on
S_{N}
)
.

Assume the source condition in Assumption
6.2
. Then

S_{N}\coloneqq\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}e^{-2\lambda_{j}t_{N}}(u_{0}^{j})^{2}\;\lesssim\;T^{-s},

where
T=t_{N}
.

Proof.

Since
\bm{\theta}_{0}=0
, the source condition implies
u_{0}^{j}=-\theta_{j}^{*}=-a_{j}\lambda_{j}^{\frac{s-1}{2}}
, hence

S_{N}=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}^{2}\,\lambda_{j}^{s}e^{-2\lambda_{j}T}.

Using
\sum_{j\geqslant 1}a_{j}^{2}\leqslant 1
and
\sup_{\lambda\geqslant 0}\lambda^{s}e^{-2\lambda T}\leqslant(s/(2eT))^{s}
,
we obtain
S_{N}\lesssim T^{-s}
.
∎

C.3.2
Noise-Accumulation Term
\mathcal{N}_{N}

We first provide a lemma that will be useful in the bound of
\mathcal{N}_{N}
.

Lemma C.7
.

Assume the capacity condition
\lambda_{j}\lesssim j^{-\beta}
for some
\beta>1
.
Then for all
\tau>0
,

\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{j}\tau}\;\lesssim\;\min\Big\{1,\;\tau^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\Big\}.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{j}\leqslant c\,j^{-\beta}
. Then

\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{j}\tau}\leqslant c^{2}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}j^{-2\beta}e^{-2c\tau j^{-\beta}}\;\lesssim\;\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-2\beta}e^{-2c\tau x^{-\beta}}\,\mathrm{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}x.

With the change of variables
u=x^{-\beta}
(so
x=u^{-1/\beta}
,
\mathrm{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}x=\frac{1}{\beta}u^{-1/\beta-1}\mathrm{d}u
),
the integral is bounded by

\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{1}u^{1-\frac{1}{\beta}}e^{-2c\tau u}\,\mathrm{d}u\;\leqslant\;\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{1-\frac{1}{\beta}}e^{-2c\tau u}\,\mathrm{d}u\;\eqsim\;\tau^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}.

This yields the
\tau^{-2+1/\beta}
bound. The constant bound follows from

\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}^{2}\leqslant\lambda_{1}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{1}\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{T})<\infty
.
∎

Noise term
\mathcal{N}_{N}
for power decay.

We consider the power decay schedule

\eta_{i}=\eta_{0}\Big(1-\frac{i}{N}\Big)^{\gamma},\qquad i=0,1,\dots,N-1,

and the intrinsic time
t_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\eta_{i}
.

Lemma C.8

(Intrinsic time for power decay)
.

For
\gamma>0
, we have

t_{N}\eqsim\eta_{0}N,\qquad t_{N}-t_{i+1}\eqsim\eta_{0}N\Big(\frac{N-i-1}{N}\Big)^{\gamma+1},\quad i=0,\dots,N-1.

Proof.

Both relations follow from comparing the sums with the corresponding integrals:

\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\Big(1-\frac{i}{N}\Big)^{\gamma}\eqsim N\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)^{\gamma}\,\mathrm{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\sum_{\ell=i+1}^{N-1}\Big(1-\frac{\ell}{N}\Big)^{\gamma}\eqsim N\int_{(i+1)/N}^{1}(1-x)^{\gamma}\,\mathrm{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}x.

∎

Proposition C.9

(Noise term for power decay)
.

Assume the capacity condition and
\eta_{0}\lesssim 1
.
Let
\eta_{i}=\eta_{0}(1-i/N)^{\gamma}
with
\gamma>0
. Suppose
\beta\neq\gamma+1
. Then

\mathcal{N}_{N}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\eta_{i}^{2}\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{j}(t_{N}-t_{i+1})}\;\lesssim\;\eta_{0}\,T^{-\min\left\{1-\frac{1}{\beta},\;\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}\right\}},

where
T=t_{N}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
C.7
,

\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\lambda_{j}^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{j}(t_{N}-t_{i+1})}\lesssim\min\Big\{1,\;(t_{N}-t_{i+1})^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\Big\}.

Let
m=N-i-1
and choose a cutoff
m_{0}
such that

m_{0}\eqsim N\,t_{N}^{-\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}\qquad\iff\qquad t_{N}\Big(\frac{m_{0}}{N}\Big)^{\gamma+1}\eqsim 1.

We split the sum into
m\leqslant m_{0}
(near the end, use the bound
\leqslant 1
) and
m>m_{0}
(use the polynomial bound).
Using
\eta_{i}=\eta_{0}((m+1)/N)^{\gamma}
and Lemma
C.8
, we have the following bound.

(i) Tail part
m\leqslant m_{0}
.

\sum_{m=0}^{m_{0}}\eta_{0}^{2}\Big(\frac{m+1}{N}\Big)^{2\gamma}\;\lesssim\;\eta_{0}^{2}\,N^{-2\gamma}\,m_{0}^{2\gamma+1}\;\eqsim\;\eta_{0}\,t_{N}^{-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}.

(ii) Head part
m>m_{0}
.

Using
t_{N}-t_{i+1}\eqsim t_{N}(m/N)^{\gamma+1}
and
\eta_{i}=\eta_{0}(m/N)^{\gamma}
, we have

\eta_{i}^{2}(t_{N}-t_{i+1})^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\;\lesssim\;\eta_{0}^{2}\Big(\frac{m+1}{N}\Big)^{2\gamma}\cdot t_{N}^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\Big(\frac{m}{N}\Big)^{(\gamma+1)\left(-2+\frac{1}{\beta}\right)}=\eta_{0}^{2}\,t_{N}^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\Big(\frac{m}{N}\Big)^{-2+\frac{\gamma+1}{\beta}}.

Let
\nu\coloneqq-2+\frac{\gamma+1}{\beta}
. Then

\sum_{m>m_{0}}\eta_{i}^{2}(t_{N}-t_{i+1})^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\;\lesssim\;\eta_{0}^{2}\,t_{N}^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\sum_{m>m_{0}}\Big(\frac{m}{N}\Big)^{\nu}.

The head part
We then consider two cases of the head part.

•

Case 1:
\gamma>\beta-1
(i.e.,
\nu>-1
). Since
(m/N)^{\nu}\leqslant 1
for
m\leqslant N
, we have
\sum_{m>m_{0}}(m/N)^{\nu}\lesssim N
, and hence

\sum_{m>m_{0}}\eta_{i}^{2}(t_{N}-t_{i+1})^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\;\lesssim\;\eta_{0}^{2}\,t_{N}^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\cdot N\;\eqsim\;\eta_{0}\,t_{N}^{-\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\right)},

where we used
t_{N}\eqsim\eta_{0}N
.

•

Case 2:
\gamma<\beta-1
(i.e.,
\nu<-1
). In this case,
\sum_{m>m_{0}}(m/N)^{\nu}\lesssim N\,(m_{0}/N)^{\nu+1}
, so

\sum_{m>m_{0}}\eta_{i}^{2}(t_{N}-t_{i+1})^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\;\lesssim\;\eta_{0}^{2}\,t_{N}^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\cdot N\Big(\frac{m_{0}}{N}\Big)^{\nu+1}.

Since
m_{0}\eqsim N\,t_{N}^{-1/(\gamma+1)}
, we have

\Big(\frac{m_{0}}{N}\Big)^{\nu+1}=t_{N}^{-\frac{\nu+1}{\gamma+1}}=t_{N}^{-\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{1}{\gamma+1}\right)},

and therefore

\sum_{m>m_{0}}\eta_{i}^{2}(t_{N}-t_{i+1})^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\;\lesssim\;\eta_{0}^{2}\,N\,t_{N}^{-2+\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}\;\eqsim\;\eta_{0}\,t_{N}^{-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}},

again using
t_{N}\eqsim\eta_{0}N
.

Combining the two cases yields the following bound on the head part:

\sum_{m>m_{0}}\eta_{i}^{2}(t_{N}-t_{i+1})^{-2+\frac{1}{\beta}}\;\lesssim\;\eta_{0}\,t_{N}^{-\min\left\{1-\frac{1}{\beta},\,\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}\right\}}.

Then we finish the proof by combining (i) and (ii).
∎

D.1
A Quick Introduction to Variational Calculus

Consider the simplest setting

\mathcal{F}[f]\;=\;\int_{a}^{b}L\bigl(z,\,f(z),\,f^{\prime}(z)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z,

where
f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
is admissible
1
1

1

For instance,

f\in C^{1}([a,b])
with prescribed boundary values

f(a)=A,\;f(b)=B
.
and
L
(the
Lagrangian
) is smooth.

Euler–Lagrange equation.

Take a smooth perturbation
h
with
h(a)=h(b)=0
and set

f_{\varepsilon}=f+\varepsilon h
. The first variation is

\delta\mathcal{F}[f;h]=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\mathcal{F}[f_{\varepsilon}]\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=\int_{a}^{b}\Bigl(\partial_{f}L\,h+\partial_{f^{\prime}}L\,h^{\prime}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}z=\int_{a}^{b}\Bigl(\partial_{f}L-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\left(\partial_{f^{\prime}}L\right)\Bigr)h(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,

where the last step uses the integration by part and
h(a)=h(b)=0
).

Because this must vanish for
all
such
h
, the integrand is zero:

{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\left(\,\partial_{f^{\prime}}L\right)-\partial_{f}L=0}

Beltrami identity.

If
L=L(f,f^{\prime})
has no explicit
z
-dependence, multiply the
Euler–Lagrange equation by
f^{\prime}
and integrate once:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\bigl(f^{\prime}\,\partial_{f^{\prime}}L-L\bigr)=0\quad\implies\quad{L-f^{\prime}\,\partial_{f^{\prime}}L=C}

where
C
is a constant.
The quantity
L-f^{\prime}\partial_{f^{\prime}}L
is (up to sign) the Legendre transform
of
L
with respect to
f^{\prime}
and is conserved – an analogue of energy conservation.

Let us start by fixing the total intrinsic time

\displaystyle\min_{t}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{N}L(T,t(z),t^{\prime}(z))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

\varphi(t)=t^{\prime}=C(1+T-t)^{1-\frac{1}{2\beta}}=\varphi(0)\left(1-\frac{t}{1+T}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{2\beta}}.

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}=\varphi(0)\left(1-\frac{t}{1+T}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{2\beta}}

\displaystyle\implies-\varphi(0)^{-1}2\beta(1+T)\left(1-\frac{t}{1+T}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}}=z-C

Differentiating with respect to

\partial_{t}L-\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z}(\partial_{t^{\prime}}L)=0
