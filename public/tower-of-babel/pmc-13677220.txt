Epimeric Vitamin D is Not Associated With Arterial Stiffness or Cardiovascular Outcomes in Patients With Advanced CKD
Authors: Monique O. Campos, Chien-Yao Sun, Heather N. Burney, Yang Li, Gayatri Narayanan, Olivia Carlton, Tzong-Shi Lu, Glenville Jones, Martin Kaufmann, Stephen M.S. Ting, Daniel Zehnder, Thomas Hiemstra, Kenneth Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale & Objective3-epi-25(OH)D3, an epimeric form of 25(OH)D3 generated by C-3 epimerization, declines with advancing chronic kidney disease (CKD), yet its role in vascular health and related outcomes remains largely unknown. We aimed to determine whether circulating 3-epi-25(OH)D3 levels are associated with arterial stiffness and longitudinal cardiovascular (CV) outcomes in advanced CKD (stages 5 and 5D).Study DesignA prospective observational study.Setting & ParticipantsA total of 270 patients with advanced CKD using the Cambridge Coventry Vascular cohort.Exposure (s) or Predictor (s)Baseline 3-epi-25(OH)D3 measured by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry.Outcome (s)Arterial stiffness was assessed by carotid–femoral pulse wave velocity (PWV) and augmentation index (AIx75). Longitudinal outcomes included a composite of major CV events and all-cause mortality.Analytical ApproachBaseline 3-epi-25(OH)D3 was categorized into quartiles. Associations were assessed using multivariable regression adjusted for age, race, and dialysis status. Kaplan–Meier and Cox models assessed longitudinal outcomes.ResultsAmong 270 participants, quartiles were Q1 < 0.30 ng/mL (n = 64), Q2: 0.30-0.456 ng/mL (n = 71), Q3: 0.457-0.729 ng/mL (n = 68), and Q4 ≥ 0.73 ng/mL (n = 67). Strata were similar in age (44.9 years), sex, and BMI (all P > 0.05). Race differed by quartile (P < 0.001); 87.4% were White. 24.8% were pre-dialytic, 61.1% treated with hemodialysis, and 14.1% treated with peritoneal dialysis. Arterial stiffness was similar across quartiles (AIx75 ∼20-21%, P = 0.99; PWV: 7.8-8.0 m/s, P = 0.94). 3-epi-25(OH)D3 showed no association with AIx75 or PWV before or after adjustment using multiple regression modeling. Over 9.3 years, 115 composite events occurred (97 CV events, 18 deaths). Kaplan–Meier analysis did not discriminate risk across 3-epi-25(OH)D3 quartiles for the composite outcome (log-rank P = 0.23), and adjusted Cox models showed no association.LimitationsObservational design with possible residual confounding.ConclusionIn advanced CKD, 3-epi-25(OH)D3 showed no independent association with arterial stiffness or CV events and mortality. Further studies are needed to determine the role of epimeric vitamin D in patients with CKD.

Epimeric vitamin D (3-epi-25-hydroxy vitamin D3 or 3-epi-25(OH)D3 is generated through C-3 epimerization of 25(OH)D3), involving inversion of the C-3 hydroxyl group from β to α.1 3-epi-25(OH)D3 typically represents a small fraction of total 25(OH)D, although values ranging from 0 to 54% have been reported in patients with cardiovascular disease,2 underscoring its potential biological and clinical relevance to human health, although little is presently known about its biological role. Advances in mass spectrometry now enable reliable separation of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 from other vitamin D metabolites.3 Moreover, because epimeric forms of vitamin D constitute a meaningful fraction of total 25(OH)D, this distinction is crucial in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) in which profoundly altered vitamin D metabolism may influence epimeric and nonepimeric metabolites differentially. In the Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing in Renal Failure and After Kidney Transplantation (CAPER) cohort,4 our group previously demonstrated that low 3-epi-25(OH)D3 (<0.4 ng/mL) in advanced CKD was significantly associated with impaired cardiovascular (CV) functional capacity, as assessed by maximal oxygen consumption (VO2Max) during incremental exercise stress testing. We reported that 1 year after kidney transplantation, 3-epi-25(OH)D3 increased substantially, whereas its levels declined in non-transplanted patients with advanced CKD. In contrast, no association was observed with resting left ventricular mass index, suggesting that 3-epi-25(OH)D3 is linked to functional rather than structural cardiac parameters. Of note, other studies have now shown that patients with CV disease without kidney impairment also exhibit lower circulating of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 when compared with healthy individuals.5,6 However, data specifically in patients with CKD remain limited. These findings suggest that 3-epi-25(OH)D3 may play an important role in the regulation of CV health and as a potential biomarker for CV disease. Despite this, the physiological role of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 in vascular health remains poorly understood, particularly in CKD, in which disrupted vitamin D metabolism has been tightly linked to the development of an unacceptably high vascular disease burden.

As CKD progresses, endothelial dysfunction, medial calcification, and arteriosclerosis develop, leading to arterial stiffening and hypertension.7, 8, 9 Loss of aortic compliance and coronary calcification also lead to adverse cardiac remodeling that includes left ventricular hypertrophy and the development of diastolic heart failure.10,11 These structural changes underlie the high rates of CV events such as arrhythmias, progression of heart failure, and CV death in the CKD population.7,9 Deficiency of traditional vitamin D metabolites—25(OH)D3 and 1,25(OH)2D3—has been consistently associated with measures of vascular dysfunction, including higher pulse wave velocity and augmentation index (indices of arterial stiffening), and endothelial dysfunction and accelerated progression of carotid intima–media thickness in patients with CKD.12, 13, 14 Experimental evidence has shown that vitamin D receptor (VDR) knockout mice develop increased vascular platelet aggregation, reduced endothelial nitric-oxide synthase expression, accelerated inflammation, and endothelial injury, suggesting that vitamin D plays a critical regulatory role in maintaining vascular integrity.15, 16, 17 Moreover, 1,25(OH)2D3 has been shown to directly exert vascular protective effects, including anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative actions at vascular smooth muscle cells, improve endothelial function, and reduce vascular calcification in both experimental and human studies.13,18 Conversely, supplementation with inactive 25(OH)D3 yields inconsistent vascular benefits in CKD in clinical studies,19 and this could be due in part to impaired renal conversion to its active form in states of reduced kidney function. Critically, the vascular role of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 remains unknown in patients with CKD, despite reports that this epimer may constitute a substantial proportion of total circulating 25(OH)D and is biologically active.2

Given the substantial contribution of the 3-epi-25(OH)D3 isoform to overall vitamin D status,2 failure to quantify the epimeric fraction may mask clinically meaningful alterations in vitamin D metabolism as CKD progresses. Unfortunately, our biological understanding of the 3-epi-25(OH)D3 isoform is compounded by the severe paucity of studies that have investigated its role in CKD. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, no clinical studies have examined the association between 3-epi-25(OH)D3 with critical vascular endpoints or prospective CV outcomes in this population. Understanding this isoform is particularly important, as it may clarify critical uncertainties underlying the physiological actions of vitamin D metabolites, address divergent CV findings with inactive vitamin D supplementation, and refine how best to assess overall vitamin D status. Although we previously demonstrated in the CAPER cohort that low 3-epi-25(OH)D3 is associated with impaired CV functional capacity, it remains unclear whether this reflects underlying vascular dysfunction or translates into worse CV risk. Therefore, in this study we hypothesized that lower circulating 3-epi-25(OH)D3 levels are associated with increased arterial stiffness and higher risk of cardiovascular events and mortality in patients with advanced CKD. Accordingly, we examined the associations of circulating 3-epi-25(OH)D3 with (1) arterial stiffness and (2) longitudinal cardiovascular outcomes in patients with CKD stages 5 and 5D. We used the Cambridge–Coventry Vascular cohort—a large, well-characterized validated cohort— in which we quantified vitamin D metabolites using gold-standard liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. In addition, the cohort provides a unique opportunity to delineate these relationships with clinically important vascular endpoints and long-term CV outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a cross-sectional and longitudinal secondary analysis of the Cambridge Coventry Vascular cohort. This cohort was a prospective, nonrandomized study that recruited a total of 614 patients, comprised of 3 groups: patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (n = 84), individuals with advanced CKD stage 5 (n = 270), and healthy controls consisting of living kidney donors (n = 260).20,21 In this study, only participants with advanced CKD stage 5 were included, as our focus was on determining the role of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 in patients with CKD. Eligible participants recruited in the cohort were adults aged ≥18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of stage 5 CKD who were receiving routine nephrology care at University Hospital Coventry and Warwickshire National Health Service (NHS) Trust, Coventry, UK. Individuals with a history of chronic lung disease were excluded. Patients receiving hemodialysis (n = 165) underwent conventional thrice-weekly sessions. Those receiving peritoneal dialysis (n = 38) were treated with either automated peritoneal dialysis, involving 5 nightly exchange cycles, or continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis, consisting of 4 exchanges over a 24-hour period. All participant data were deidentified before analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Black Country research ethics committee and conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.22 Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Study Endpoints

The primary intermediate endpoint was arterial stiffness as assessed by applanation tonometry using 2 derived measures: the augmentation index adjusted to a heart rate of 75 beats per minute (AIx75) and pulse wave velocity (PWV) derived from applanation tonometry. For longitudinal analysis, our primary outcome was a composite time-to-event variable that includes adjudicated CV events, all-cause mortality, and CV-specific mortality. The CV events included myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest (ventricular fibrillation or asystole), ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, heart failure, arrhythmia, non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction, ST-elevation myocardial infarction, worsening hypertension, and acute decompensation of heart failure. Participants were followed for a median of 9.3 years. Long-term outcomes were identified through manual review of clinical records and when necessary, additional information was obtained from primary care physicians and death certificates to clarify the cause of death.

Arterial Stiffness Assessment

Applanation tonometry was performed according to a standardized protocol, as previously described.4 Briefly, pulse wave analysis was conducted at the radial artery and aortic (carotid–femoral) PWV was assessed by sequential electrocardiogram-gated recordings of pressure waveforms at the carotid and femoral arteries using a high-fidelity micromanometer (SPC-301; Millar Instruments). The derived measures included PWV, a marker of aortic stiffness, and the AIx75, which reflects wave reflection and systemic arterial tone. Central aortic waveforms were derived using a validated radial-to-aortic transfer function (SphygmoCor; AtCor Medical), averaging 3 recordings. In patients treated with hemodialysis, assessments were performed the day after dialysis.

Vitamin D Metabolite Analysis

Vitamin D metabolites were quantified using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Serum concentrations of 25(OH)D2, 25(OH)D3, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, 1α,25(OH)2D3, and 24,25(OH)2D3 were extracted from human serum spiked with deuterated internal standards (d3-25(OH)D2, d6-25(OH)D3, d3-3-epi-25(OH)D3, d6-1,25(OH)2D3, and d6-24,25(OH)2D3) using a combination of liquid–liquid extraction and immunoextraction. Extracts were then derivatized with 4-(2-[6,7-dimethoxy-4-methyl-3-oxo-3,4-dihydroquinoxalinyl]ethyl)-1,2,4-triazoline 3,5-dione (DMEQ-TAD), as previously described.23,24 Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analyses were performed on Waters ACQUITY UPLC/Xevo TQ-S systems (Waters Corporation) using both rapid and extended chromatographic methods. All other biochemical tests were performed at the University Hospitals Coventry and Warwickshire NHS Trust clinical laboratory using standard protocols.25 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and PWV/AIx75 were measured on the same day in 81.9% of participants and within 7 days in 11.8%; sensitivity analyses were performed excluding participants with longer intervals.

Statistical Analyses

Baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively by quartiles of 3-epi-25(OH)D3. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%), and continuous variables as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate. Group comparisons across 3-epi-25(OH)D3 quartiles used ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Associations between 3-epi-25(OH)D3 quartiles and AIx75 and PWV were assessed using multiple linear regression models adjusted for age, race, and dialysis status.

The primary outcome was a composite of CV events and all-cause mortality. Associations with outcomes were evaluated using Cox models adjusted for age, race, and dialysis status, with Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests for group comparisons. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4.

The distribution of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and smoking status did not differ significantly across 3-epi-25(OH)D3 quartiles (all P ≥ 0.05). Dialysis status varied significantly (P = 0.016) across the quartiles. The proportion of patients receiving hemodialysis increased progressively across quartiles (Q4: 74.6%; Q3: 63.2%; Q2: 52.1%; and Q1: 54.7%), although peritoneal dialysis was more frequently observed in the lower quartiles (Q4: 3.0%; Q3: 10.3%; Q2: 21.1%; and Q1: 21.9%). However, among those undergoing hemodialysis, there were no significant differences in the median dialysis vintage (Q4: 17.0 [2.0-72.0] months; Q3: 15.0 [0.0-49.0]; Q2: 16.0 [1.0-60.0]; Q1: 24.0 [2.5-60.0]; P = 0.77). With regard to medication use, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blocker (Q4: 31.3%; Q3: 26.5%; Q2: 45.1%; Q1: 46.9%; P = 0.033) were more commonly used in participants in the lowest 3-epi-25(OH)D3 quartiles. Conversely, the use of calcium channel blockers, diuretics, and β-blockers did not differ significantly across the groups (all P > 0.05). Similarly, the use of vitamin D analogs (alfacalcidol, calcitriol, and paricalcitol) was comparable among groups. No significant differences were observed across quartiles in serum calcium, phosphate, albumin, hemoglobin, HbA1c, C-reactive protein, parathyroid hormone, or fibroblast growth factor 23 (all P > 0.05).

Discussion

This study is the first to comprehensively evaluate the relevance of the 3-epi-25(OH)D3 in advanced CKD with clinically important vascular endpoints and prospective CV outcomes in a well-characterized advanced CKD cohort. We found that circulating 3-epi-25(OH)D3 was not independently associated with arterial stiffness, whether evaluated by PWV or AIx75. We reported a lower overall concentration range (0.0-4.70 ng/mL) of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 as expected in patients with advanced CKD when compared with healthy patients.4,26 Although studies of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 are scarce, particulary in patients with advanced stages of CKD, its proportion relative to total 25(OH)D remains unclear, and our null findings may reflect the distinct biological role of this epimer when compared with nonepimeric metabolites. It has been postulated that 3-epi-25(OH)D3 might be converted to 3-epi-1,25-(OH)2D3 and transduce its biological effects through the VDR. However, experimental data indicate that this epimer has markedly reduced the VDR affinity and transcriptional activity when compared with its nonepimeric counterpart.2,27 In vitro studies, 3-epi-25(OH)D3 binds the VDR with only ∼2-3% of the affinity (representing a >95% reduction in receptor binding) when compared with its nonepimeric counterpart.1 However, 3-epi-25(OH)D3 has a lower affinity for the vitamin D binding protein (∼36-46% of 25(OH)D3), increasing its free fraction but potentially shortening half-life or altering tissue distribution.28 The 3-epimeric form of calcitriol (3-epi-1,25(OH)2D3) also exhibits reduced biologic activity, inducing VDR-regulated genes at ∼15% of native calcitriol, and functions overall as a weakly active form despite increased resistance to CYP24A1 degradation.28

Substantial evidence links nonepimeric vitamin D metabolites to vascular health in CKD,12, 13, 14 with studies in the general population and in early or patients with CKD not treated with dialysis consistently associating lower 25(OH)D levels—and related metabolites such as 24,25(OH)2D3 and its ratio—with increased arterial stiffness and impaired endothelial function as kidney function declines.29, 30, 31, 32 However, in advanced CKD, these associations are inconsistent. In patients with CKD not treated with dialysis(stages 3b-5), one study reported higher AIx75 with vitamin D deficiency,30 whereas the IMPROVE-CKD trial (stages 3b-4) found no association with PWV, AIx75, or aortic calcification.33 Consistently, randomized trials of inactive vitamin D supplementation (ergocalciferol or cholecalciferol) have not improved arterial stiffness or vascular calcification in CKD, and their impact on vascular health remains unresolved.19,33, 34, 35, 36 Together, these findings suggest that modifying inactive vitamin D status with current supplements may not be therapeutic for vascular health in CKD and that vitamin D–related vascular effects, including those of epimeric metabolites, may be more evident in early CKD and limited in advanced stages.

Similarly, baseline 3-epi-25(OH)D3 was not associated with CV events or all-cause death in our 9.3-year CKD cohort, although higher levels have been linked to favorable CV phenotypes in other populations.4, 5, 6 In a large Chinese case–control study of >7,000 adults across 32 disease groups and healthy controls, mean 3-epi-25(OH)D3 was lower in participants with CV disease than in controls (0.77 vs 1.07 ng/mL; P < 0.05).6 Notably, the mean 3-epi-25(OH)D3 in our CKD cohort was ∼0.55 ng/mL, lower than both groups in that study.5,6 Although we observed no association with indices of arterial stiffness or clinical outcomes, this suggests that the association between 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and CV risk may vary across patient populations and disease stages. In a smaller case–control study of patients with coronary artery disease or venous thrombosis (n = 27) versus healthy controls (n = 35), 3-epi-25(OH)D3 was ∼70% lower in cases despite similar total 25(OH)D3; however, the inverse association with CV disease was not statistically significant.5 Both studies were conducted in non-CKD populations using case–control designs prone to disease enrichment, confounding, and reverse causation, fundamentally differing from our prospective CKD cohort.

In contrast, in the CAPER cohort, higher 3-epi-25(OH)D3 was associated with better CV functional capacity in advanced CKD.4 Participants in the lowest epimer quartile (<0.4 ng/mL) had lower VO2Max than those in the highest quartile (18.4 vs 20.8 mL/kg/min; P = 0.009), and each 1 ng/mL higher baseline 3-epi-25(OH)D3 predicted ∼2.5 mL/kg/min higher VO2Max in longitudinal models, without differences in cardiac structure.4 Given that this study found no association with vascular endpoints (a well-established determinant of VO2Max), these findings suggest that 3-epi-25(OH)D3 may influence CV functional capacity through other mechanisms such as at the musculoskeletal or pulmonary systems rather than vascular effects. This is supported by evidence linking low 3-epi-25(OH)D3 to reduced muscle mass, strength, and physical performance.37 Experimental studies show that the C3-epimer retains biological activity, including partial suppression of parathyroid hormone synthesis38,39 and stimulation of pulmonary surfactant production.28,40 Although parathyroid hormone regulation can influence vascular physiology, these data suggest the epimer also acts on nonvascular targets more relevant to exercise capacity. Although the biological role of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 remains incompletely understood, evidence indicates effects may be mediated primarily through pulmonary and musculoskeletal pathways, key determinants of VO2Max, rather than vascular structure or stiffness. Consistent with our null findings, randomized trials41, 42, 43 of vitamin D receptor agonists in CKD failed to improve cardiac structure or major CV events. Although epimers were not measured,41, 42, 43 these results suggest that even strong VDR activation provides limited CV benefit in CKD, supporting the biological plausibility that the weakly active metabolite 3-epi-25(OH)D3 does not meaningfully modify long-term CV risk in advanced CKD, despite associations with more favorable CV phenotypes in other settings.

The results of this study should be interpreted against its strengths and limitations. This represents the first and largest evaluation of circulating 3-epi-25(OH)D3 in advanced CKD, enabling assessment of vascular associations and long-term cardiovascular relevance within a prospective cohort with 9.3 years of follow-up. However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, we did not measure other vitamin D epimers, limiting insight into vitamin D catabolism in CKD. Despite being the largest CKD cohort evaluating 3-epi-25(OH)D3, the moderate number of CV events or deaths over 9.3 years limited power to detect small effect sizes, particularly for infrequent outcomes. Given the observational design, causality cannot be inferred and will require further interventional studies. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors (eg, sun exposure, physical activity, and diet) cannot be excluded, although medication use was comparable across quartiles. Finally, the exclusive inclusion of advanced CKD and ESKD limits generalizability to earlier disease stages.

In conclusion, in this study, circulating levels of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 were not associated with arterial stiffness and composite outcome (CV events/all-cause death) in patients with advanced CKD. These findings suggest that the epimeric form of vitamin D3 does not play a major role in vascular dysfunction or adverse outcomes (CV events/all-cause death) over time in this population. Further studies are needed to validate these findings and comprehensively evaluate the role of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 in patients with CKD.
