Curving transparent ceramics via force-driven sintering
Authors: Xincheng Cai, Xiaoqiang Li, Tiecheng Lu, Zhuoying Jia, Shengquan Yu, Bin Kang, Qiwu Shi, Jianqi Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransparent ceramics are critical for advanced optics and armor, but shaping them into highly curved geometries without degrading their optical and mechanical properties remains a formidable challenge. Here, we introduce a force-driven sintering strategy that harnesses force-induced creep to dynamically control microstructure and curvature through continuous stress release. A hallmark of this process is a dynamic curvature reversal phenomenon, governed by cyclic stress accumulation and release. Using force-driven sintering, we fabricated large-scale (0.5*2*22 cm3) curved MgAl2O4 ceramics exhibiting a high curvature (>5.36 m−1), and exceptional transmittance exceeding 85% (approaching the theoretical limit), while matching the best-reported mechanical properties. We also demonstrate the versatility of force-driven sintering by producing curved Al2O3 transparent ceramics. This method synchronizes external mechanical forces with intrinsic material creep and stress relaxation, enabling the single-step fabrication of complex-shaped, high-performance transparent components. force-driven sintering establishes a scalable and versatile manufacturing paradigm for transparent ceramics in demanding applications.Subject terms: Ceramics, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Transparent ceramics represent a class of advanced structural and functional materials that combine excellent optical transparency with outstanding mechanical strength, thermal stability, and chemical resistance1–3. These properties have enabled their broad application across high-technology fields including aerospace (e.g., missile domes and infrared windows), defense (transparent armor systems), lighting technology, and medical devices4,5. Particularly under extreme service environments, there is a growing demand for components that are not only optically and mechanically superior but also exhibit complex geometric profiles, such as highly curved surfaces for improved aerodynamics, impact resistance, or optical focusing6.

However, a significant challenge persists in fabricating transparent ceramics with high curvature without sacrificing their optical and mechanical performance. Conventional shaping techniques, such as machining of pre-sintered blanks or sintering in rigid molds, often introduce microstructural defects (including pores, and surface cracks) that severely degrade both transparency and strength7–9. The inherent brittleness and high hardness of ceramics further complicate high-precision shaping, making it difficult to achieve large, highly curved, and defect-free transparent components10–12. For example, the high cost associated with diamond tooling and specialized equipment, the significant time investment required for multi-step processing, the complexity of achieving uniform material removal without introducing subsurface damage or residual stresses, and the geometric constraints that make certain highly curved or non-axisymmetric shapes difficult or impossible to achieve via machining alone. These post-sintering shaping steps often introduce microstructural defects, such as surface cracks or localized residual porosity, that can degrade both optical transparency and mechanical strength, partially negating the hard-won properties of the sintered ceramic. Although advanced forming methods like slip casting, gel casting, injection molding, or additive manufacturing, allow some geometric freedom, they typically involve complex steps, suffer from low dimensional accuracy after sintering, and are difficult to scale for large and curved parts13–19. Consequently, there remains a critical technology gap in simply and efficiently manufacturing transparent ceramics that fulfill simultaneous requirements in curvature, optical quality, mechanical reliability, and dimensional scale.

To overcome these challenges, we propose a simple, interesting, and readily implementable force-driven sintering (FDS) strategy, which enables dynamic control over the microstructure and curvature through the regulation of force-induced creep and continuous stress release. This method utilizes forces (specifically, gravity and the opposing reaction force from the supports) at elevated temperatures to dynamically regulate both the macroscopic curvature and the underlying microstructure (primarily the development of a grain size gradient across the sample thickness), thereby tuning the densification behavior and shaping the ceramic in a single step. A distinctive feature observed in this process is a dynamic curvature reversal phenomenon, governed by cyclic accumulation and release of internal stress alongside force-induced creep, allowing unprecedented control of the final geometry. Unlike natural bending, our Force-Driven Sintering (FDS) is a controllable non-equilibrium process. By engineering boundary conditions, we transform external force (such as gravity) into a programmable tool for predictable curvature. The novelty lies in revealing the mechanism: stress gradients modulate grain boundary migration, creating a thickness-dependent grain-size gradient that drives curvature evolution. This enables “programming” shape during sintering. We demonstrate large-size, highly curved transparent ceramics with preserved optical quality, establishing FDS as a mold-free, net-shape manufacturing pathway beyond conventional limits.

In this work, employing the FDS approach with deliberately designed microstructural gradation, we have successfully fabricated highly curved MgAl2O4 spinel transparent ceramics that demonstrate exceptional overall performance: a transmittance exceeding 85% (approaching the theoretical limit for the material), a curvature exceeding ~5.36 m−1, a thickness of ~5 mm (Fig. S1), and a length surpassing 22 cm, demonstrating the unique capability of FDS in forming thick-walled components without compromising optical quality. This technology holds potential for applications in aerospace and high-speed flight vehicles, such as reconnaissance domes, sensor windows, or cockpit canopies. For more demanding optical imaging or sensing windows requiring higher curvature, the FDS process parameters can be further adjusted (e.g., by applying additional external force to the green body) to achieve greater curvature, thereby broadening the application scope. To demonstrate the versatility of this method, we have also produced highly curved Al2O3 ceramics.

The FDS process fundamentally operates by synchronizing external mechanical forces with intrinsic material creep and stress relaxation mechanisms. This synergy ensures that high optical quality and mechanical strength are maintained while achieving significant geometric curvature. Our method establishes a simple, scalable, and versatile manufacturing route for transparent ceramics with complex shapes. Beyond the demonstrations in this work, the FDS strategy offers considerable potential for further development. By integrating FDS with tailored mold designs (for example, using supports with specific curvatures or profiles), it should be possible to directly sinter ceramics into more complex, non-uniform shapes beyond simple arcs. Furthermore, combining FDS with multi-field assistance, such as the application of an external electric field (field-assisted sintering/spark plasma sintering), could potentially enhance stress-modulated grain growth or enable lower-temperature shaping. These combined approaches open promising avenues for fabricating transparent ceramic components with sophisticated architectures, such as compound-curved domes or asymmetric lenses, in a single sintering step. This work introduces alternative avenues for designing and manufacturing advanced transparent ceramic components, with broad implications for their use in extreme and high-precision environments.

The grain size evolution across the sintered curved ceramics showed distinct spatial anisotropy due to the asymmetric stress field. The grain size difference ΔGS between the tensile and compressive zones increased with curvature depth. A histogram of ΔGS versus vertical position (Fig. 1i) showed a continuous gradient, with a clear bias toward coarsening on the tensile side. This observation is consistent with classic sintering theory, where tensile stress enhances grain boundary migration by increasing the chemical potential at triple junctions18.

To further elucidate the evolution of grains and pores under different stress states, phase-field simulations were performed, as shown in Fig. 2b. The results reveal that along the longitudinal direction, grain growth is suppressed in the upper (concave) region under compressive stress due to inhibited diffusion, while enhanced diffusion in the lower (convex) region under tensile stress leads to larger grains. These findings indicate that the finite element and phase-field simulations are consistent with the experimental observations.

To validate the effect of stress release driven by force-induced creep on microstructural evolution, we performed SEM characterization on a post-sintered body following high-temperature creep sintering, oriented in both longitudinal and transverse directions (Fig. 2c and Fig. S7). Notably, the average grain size in the longitudinal sections was significantly smaller than that in the transverse sections, indicating pronounced anisotropy in grain growth behavior. This anisotropy arises from the stress release driven by force-induced creep during sintering: compressive stress was aligned with the longitudinal axis, restricting grain boundary migration, whereas tensile stress along the transverse axis promoted accelerated grain growth20,21,27

These findings provide direct experimental evidence that force-driven creep and stress release modulates grain boundary kinetics during FDS, leading to orientation-dependent microstructure development. Moreover, they confirm that the underlying mechanism of FDS (external force (e.g., gravity, pressure) induce creep for continuous stress release, enabling control of microstructure evolution) is not limited to transparent ceramics, but is potentially applicable across a broader class of polycrystalline materials where directional properties are desirable22,28

A significant feature of FDS is the curvature reversal phenomenon driven by force-driven creep and stress accumulation-release during FDS, accompanied by a corresponding change in curvature evolution. As shown in Fig. 3a and S3, Supplementary Movies 4–6, thin green bodies (0.75 mm thick and L/t = 40:1) initially exhibited downward bending at ~1200 °C, reached peak downward curvature near 1350 °C, and then reversed to form upward arches at around 1450 °C. During the FDS process, the accumulation of stress-induced microstructural evolution, primarily affecting grain growth, as demonstrated in Fig. 2, Supplementary Movies 2 and 5, leads to a temporal asymmetry in the state of the strip. This results in the shape evolution observed in Fig. 3, where the strip initially bends downward, then arches upward, and finally returns to a horizontal state. This behavior cannot be explained by conventional densification kinetics alone. Finite element simulations incorporating temperature-dependent modulus softening and shrinkage kinetics successfully reproduce the reversal, suggesting that feedback among differential densification, evolving elastic moduli, and stress-modulated grain boundary migration shifts the neutral axis of stress from the lower surface to the upper surface (Fig. 3b). A measurable difference in displacement velocity between the downward bending and upward recovery stages was recorded, which may affect defect accumulation and lead to distinctive microstructural features (e.g., grain size distribution). The dynamic coupling between force thermal fields, creep mechanisms, stress accumulation-release, and microstructural evolution lies at the heart of this phenomenon (Fig. 3c).

At the mechanistic level (Fig. 4a), curvature reversal arises from the competition between grain boundary migration kinetics in compressive and tensile regions. The local effective migration rate D can be expressed as:

where NGB is the grain boundary density, and MGB is the migration factor, which depends exponentially on temperature (T) and linearly on local stress (σ)23,29. In the early sintering stages, tensile regions exhibit elevated migration rates due to higher MGB, thereby accelerating grain growth and favoring downward curvature. As FDS progresses, the high-temperature force generates an asymmetric stress field across the sample thickness. Consequently, grain growth on the compressive side is slower than on the tensile side, leading to a grain size gradient with finer grains in the compressed region and coarser grains in the tensioned region. This differential growth accompanies the progressive increase in curvature, with stress accumulation ultimately culminating in release. This ultimately enhances grain boundary density (NGB) and migration factor (MGB) in compressed regions. This compensates for the initially lower migration, resulting in a net increase in migration flux. Consequently, the balance of migration shifts, redistributing mass transport across the cross-section and driving an inversion of curvature. This effect is particularly pronounced in thin green bodies, where the bending moment is relatively weak, allowing the stress-modulated migration kinetics to dominate shape evolution. Under sufficiently high stresses, grain boundary sliding may occur in polycrystalline materials, which can suppress the stress effect on grain growth dominated by grain boundary diffusion. To investigate this issue, we carefully examined the stress levels experienced by samples during force-driven sintering using finite element simulations. The results indicate that the peak stress during the entire FDS process does not exceed 0.5 MPa (Supplementary Movies 1 and 4). This value is an order of magnitude lower than the critical stress threshold (~30 MPa) reported in the literature for the transition from grain boundary diffusion to grain boundary sliding in polycrystalline ceramics30–32 Therefore, within the stress range of our experiments, grain boundary diffusion remains the dominant mechanism, while grain boundary sliding is not activated. This explains the observed differential effects of tensile and compressive stresses on grain growth.

As shown in Fig. 4b, the influence of stress release from force-driven creep on grain boundary migration can be understood by considering the coupling between mechanical fields and the system’s free energy20. Under tensile stress (σ > 0), grain boundaries experience an additional positive driving force, as the elastic strain energy stored in the material is partially released during boundary migration. This release of elastic energy increases the overall free energy of the system, thereby promoting grain boundary migration28,33, Conversely, under compressive stress (σ < 0), migration of grain boundaries continuously accumulates the local strain energy, reducing the overall free energy and rendering boundary migration unfavorable. This results in a negative contribution to the effective driving pressure, thereby suppressing grain growth in compressive regions27. This formulation captures the stress-strain coupling that modulates grain boundary migration, and aligns with classical models of stress-assisted microstructural evolution20,33. Functionally, such stress-mediated grain gradients driven by force-driven creep can enable directional thermal conductivity due to phonon scattering differences or even refractive index tuning for optical applications29,34. Importantly, the ability to generate such gradients in a single-phase, single-composition ceramic without multilayer assembly or dopant modification, provides a powerful route for fabricating functionally graded materials using bulk sintering alone35.

When the cumulative grain boundary migration rate in the compressive region (Dc) surpasses that in the tensile region (Dt), i.e., ΔD = Dt − Dc < 0, a reversal in grain growth is triggered, favoring macroscopic upward deformation. If the green sheet is sufficiently thin, the upward deformation can overcome gravitational sagging, resulting in net upward bending. This dynamic competition between force-driven creep, stress-modulated migration and microstructure-driven migration establishes a feedback loop that leads to the observed curvature reversal. As densification progresses and grain boundary density decreases due to grain growth, the migration balance shifts again, restoring curvature and stabilizing the sintered geometry. The establishment of this dynamic equilibrium between tensile and compressive migration regimes (Fig. 4c) marks a key mechanistic signature of FDS.

This stress inversion bears strong resemblance to the Bauschinger effect in metals, wherein prior plastic deformation alters the directionality of subsequent yield due to internal stress redistribution36. While classical ceramics do not plastically deform under these conditions, the stress-modulated grain boundary rearrangement observed here reveals a form of microstructural memory—where local stress history dynamically governs mass transport and curvature evolution. Unlike metallic systems, FDS couples this curvature reversal with densification-driven grain migration, enabling a programmable route to spatially graded ceramics.

Beyond mechanistic interest, this curvature reversal acts as a controllable lever for shaping ceramics during FDS. It enables bidirectional curvature control from a single geometric input, simply by tuning parameters such as initial thickness, thermal profile, or support conditions. The ability to program curvature without molds or external forces opens avenues for freeform ceramic architectures, including domes, optical lenses, and multi-curve components.

The broader significance of the observed phenomena extends beyond ceramics. Analogous memory effects driven by microstructural stress fields are known in ferroelastic and ferroic materials, suggesting a universal principle of stress-mediated evolution in polycrystalline solids37,38. Furthermore, curvature reversal provides a promising strategy for mitigating warpage and engineering gradient microstructure during high-temperature processing, a longstanding challenge in advanced transparent ceramics manufacturing.

The intrinsic mechanism of FDS involves a green compact bending under its force-driven creep to enable continuous stress release during densification, with tensile stress promoting grain boundary migration and compressive stress inhibiting it. A gradient in grain size is established, and as temperature rises, a dynamic curvature reversal occurs through the interplay of force-driven migration and evolving grain boundary density. Ultimately, as densification progresses, the system reaches a mechanically stabilized state, fixing the final sintered shape. These findings introduce possibilities for stress-programmed shaping of ceramics and reveal a previously underappreciated level of history dependence in high-temperature ceramic microstructures.

Despite the introduction of curvature and stress gradients, FDS-fabricated ceramics retained excellent mechanical and optical integrity (Fig. 5). Three-point bending tests of curved MgAl2O4 samples (0.5*2*22 cm3, curvature >5.36 m-1) yielded a flexural strength of 260 ± 15 MPa. These strength results are interpreted as a result of microstructural duality: Large grains in tensile regions increase crack path tortuosity by promoting intergranular deflection and microcrack bridging, mechanisms that absorb fracture energy and enhance toughness. In contrast, fine grains in compressive zones raise the local critical stress for crack initiation by minimizing stress concentration at grain boundaries and impeding flaw propagation. This microstructural duality enables simultaneous enhancement of damage tolerance and structural integrity, mimicking the functional gradient seen in metal laminates—yet achieved here within a monolithic ceramic without multilayer construction39–41

Optical transmittance was also preserved. As shown in Fig. 5b, all positions across the curved MgAl2O4 arc showed transmittance >85 %@1.064 μm (Theoretical transmittance of 87%@1.064 μm). There was no measurable birefringence or scattering, even in regions of grain size transition. This suggests that the force-induced grain growth is not accompanied by residual porosity or orientation-induced anisotropy. The successful preservation of both strength and transparency in curved parts indicates the robustness of FDS and its potential for practical deployment in optical-grade ceramics. The fine speckles in Fig. 5c stem from limitations of the commercial powder, not from FDS (the transmittance in Fig. 5b, from a speckle-free area, better reflects FDS’s potential). Moreover, the optical uniformity across curved shapes opens possibilities for high-curvature optical windows, infrared domes, or waveguides that are otherwise difficult to machine post-sintering.

To evaluate the generality of FDS, we applied the same processing sequence to Al2O3 ceramics. Similar curvature trends were observed, with moderate transparency (Fig. S4). This demonstrates that FDS is not limited to MgAl2O4 or spinel-type systems but is likely applicable to a wide range of materials. A comparison with existing ceramic shaping technologies highlights the distinct advantages of FDS. Although additive manufacturing (AM) enables geometric complexity, it is frequently constrained by issues such as limited material purity, high residual porosity, and inherently slow build rates42,43 Similarly, robocasting demands precise control over slurry rheology and often necessitates extensive post-processing to achieve structural integrity44,45 Laser sintering techniques, while promising for local consolidation, face challenges in scalability and maintaining compositional and microstructural homogeneity over large areas46,47 In contrast, FDS achieves tool-free, in-situ shaping during densification, relying solely on stress-field engineering and geometric design—eliminating the need for molds, complex feedstocks, or layer-by-layer deposition (Fig. 5). FDS also opens a route toward mechanically programmed sintering, where green body geometry, sintering path, and support configuration define not only the shape but also the microstructure. This paradigm could extend into stress-directed texture formation, spatially graded refractive index ceramics, or even multiphase stress-coupled architectures where different phases respond uniquely to stress fields. Future work should explore combining FDS with external fields (electric/magnetic), more complex 3D green body configurations, and modeling tools to predict curvature outcomes in multi-material systems. Incorporating FDS into existing ceramic production lines would require only minimal equipment modification, as the process relies on force and sintering path design rather than specialized tooling. Ultimately, FDS represents a scalable, controllable, and generalizable advance in ceramic processing—redefining the role of stress from a problem to a design variable.

In conclusion, this study establishes Force-Driven Sintering (FDS) as a transformative strategy for shaping high-performance transparent ceramics. By leveraging force-induced creep and the dynamic release of internal stress during densification, FDS enables the direct, in situ fabrication of highly curved components, eliminating the need for complex molds or post-sintering machining. A central finding is the curvature reversal phenomenon, which reveals a previously unrecognized microstructural memory effect in ceramics, driven by the competition between stress-modulated grain boundary migration in tensile and compressive zones. Through in situ experiments, finite element simulations, and microstructural analysis, we have demonstrated that FDS not only dictates macroscopic shape but also engineers beneficial grain size gradients. These gradients enhance fracture resistance without compromising the exceptional optical transparency of the ceramics. The method’s generality is confirmed by its successful application to both MgAl2O4 and Al2O3 systems. Beyond transparent ceramics, FDS provides a foundational framework for stress-programmed fabrication, opening pathways to create functionally graded and architecturally complex polycrystalline materials. It should be noted that FDS enables the fabrication of curved transparent ceramics, but achieving the required surface quality for optical focusing remains a challenge that necessitates further finishing. By redefining stress as an active design variable rather than a processing flaw, this work lays the groundwork for ceramic manufacturing where geometry, microstructure, and performance are co-optimized from within the sintering process itself.

Methods

Fabrication of force-driven sintered (FDS) ceramics

High-purity MgAl2O4 powders (≥99.99%, Aladdin, China) were used to prepare green compacts with varying aspect ratios. The powders were uniaxially pressed at 20 MPa for 5 min using a hydraulic press (YQ32-100t, China). The green bodies were placed on alumina platforms, chosen for their high thermal conductivity and low thermal expansion, ensuring uniform heating. Sintering was conducted in air with a two-step heating profile: a 10 °C/min ramp to 1000°C, followed by a 3 °C/min ramp to 1600 °C with a 5 h dwell time. The furnace was then cooled to 1000 °C at 3 °C/min, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. This process (Fig. 5a and S5) yielded pre-sintered curved ceramics with ~96% theoretical density. Final densification was achieved via hot isostatic pressing (HIP) (HIPEX1850, China) at 1700 °C and 150 MPa for 6 h in an inert atmosphere. FDS was also applied to Al2O3 ceramics, demonstrating its versatility for complex geometries.

Characterization of FDS-fabricated ceramics

The bending behavior of FDS-processed ceramics was evaluated for green bodies with varying aspect ratios. Bending moment measurements were conducted to analyze deformation during FDS. Microstructural analysis was performed on different regions of both pre-sintered and fully densified ceramics. After thinning and polishing via focused ion beam (FIB), the specimen was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using a JEM−2100F instrument (JEOL, Japan). Samples were mechanically polished using diamond pastes and thermally etched at 1000 °C for 3 h in air before scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Inspect F50, USA). To analyze force-driven deformation, finite element modeling (FEM) was performed in COMSOL. The phenomenon of curvature reversal during FDS was investigated through in situ visual sintering experiments (TA-Z16A01, Tianjin Zhonghuan Electric Furnace Co., Ltd, China), employing high-temperature cameras to capture real-time sintering body evolution. The schematic diagrams were created using Blender and PowerPoint. Transmittance was measured using a spectrophotometer with a relatively large aperture, averaging the optical response over macroscopic regions. Minor localized pores/inclusions, intrinsic to commercial powders48, scatter visible light. To obtain the material’s intrinsic optical transmittance, we selected regions that avoided these local spots.

Statistics and reproducibility

Grain size distributions were obtained from SEM images using Nano Measurer software, with more than 1000 grains analyzed per region. Relevant data are presented as mean values +/- SEM. Each experiment was independently repeated ≥ 3 times, yielding similar results.
