Chinese Expert Consensus on ultrasound anatomical examination of fetuses at 11 + 0 to 13 + 6 weeks of gestation based on the Delphi method
Authors: Guorong Lyu, Ruibi Liao, Qiuxia Jiang, Xiaoqin He, Zongjie Weng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFirst-trimester fetal malformation screening via ultrasound has become increasingly important. This study aims to establish a standardized expert consensus on the ultrasound anatomical examination of fetuses during the first trimester.MethodsA comprehensive literature review was conducted on first-trimester fetal anatomical examinations and malformation screening. A draft of the Chinese Expert Consensus on Fetal Anatomical Examination at 11 + 0 to 13 + 6 Weeks of Gestation was initially prepared by eight core committee members. Using the Delphi method, three rounds of online voting were conducted by 35 expert panel members, and feedback was solicited from 122 experts across 27 representative medical institutions of various levels and regions in China. If three-fourths of the expert panel members agreed to categorize the first-trimester fetal anatomical ultrasound examination into three types, these classification methods were adopted. Similarly, if three-fourths of the experts agreed that a specific item or plane in the consensus should be a mandatory component of the examination, it was designated as a recommended item or plane; otherwise, it was classified as a suggested item or plane.ResultsThis expert consensus categorizes the first-trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical examination into basic (Class I), detailed (Class II), and targeted (Class III) examinations. The examination includes one measurement item (with six sub-items) and nine anatomical examination items (with 26 sub-items). The basic and detailed fetal anatomical examinations involve largely consistent measurement parameters and target organs. However, the detailed examination includes a greater number of sub-items. In the basic examination, biometric parameters such as biparietal diameter (BPD), head circumference (HC), and abdominal circumference (AC) are considered suggested items (not mandatory). Similarly, sub-items such as the primary palate, kidneys, and genital tubercle are also classified as suggested items. In contrast, all of these parameters are regarded as recommended items (mandatory) in the detailed examination. Both the basic and detailed fetal anatomical examinations are indicated for low-risk pregnancies. The primary distinction between the two lies in the level of healthcare facility where they are conducted. Basic examinations are suitable for county-level hospitals with screening qualifications, whereas detailed examinations should be performed at municipal-level hospitals with equivalent screening credentials. In the targeted examination of the fetal central nervous system (FCNS), a total of 11 ultrasound planes are included. Among these, 4 are designated as recommended planes, and the remaining 7 as suggested planes. In the targeted fetal cardiac examination, 5 planes are evaluated, with 4 being recommended and the bilateral subclavian artery plane classified as a suggested plane. In the targeted early pregnancy screening for cleft lip and palate, 5 planes are assessed, including 3 recommended and 2 suggested planes. Targeted examinations are primarily indicated for high-risk or suspected anomalous fetuses and should be performed at qualified prenatal diagnostic centers by certified physicians.ConclusionThis consensus provides a robust framework for the standardized ultrasound anatomical examination of fetuses in the first trimester across medical institutions of varying levels and capacities. It also offers evidence-based support for future first-trimester fetal malformation screening.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00404-025-08231-z.Keywords: Ultrasound, The first trimester, Fetal anatomical examination, Consensus

What does this study adds to the clinical work

This consensus serves as a valuable expert guideline that supports medical professionals across different levels of healthcare institutions in conducting standardized first trimester fetal anatomical ultrasound examinations. Furthermore, it offers evidence-based guidance for future screening of fetal abnormalities in the first trimester.

Introduction

Fetal ultrasound anatomical examination is a critical tool in prenatal diagnosis. Growing clinical evidence indicates that ultrasound observation of fetal anatomy between 11 + 0 and 13 + 6 weeks of gestation can detect 33–50% of major fetal structural abnormalities [1–4]. Additionally, the false-positive rate for first-trimester structural abnormality screening is less than 0.5%, significantly lower than that of second-trimester screenings [4]. Standardized training further improves the accuracy of first-trimester fetal structural malformation screening [5].

The advantages of conducting anatomical examinations in the first trimester include: (1) facilitating earlier detection of structural malformations; (2) allowing more time for genetic testing and counseling; (3) enabling earlier interventions. Most importantly, early diagnosis, decision-making, and treatment can help reduce both physical and psychological harm to the pregnant woman [4].

However, fetal structures and organs are still small in the first trimester, making it challenging to obtain complete and accurate ultrasound images. Moreover, certain progressive or late-onset malformations may only be diagnosed during the mid-trimester. Factors such as maternal obesity, uterine fibroids, and surgical scars can also affect the detection of fetal anomalies [4, 5].

In recent years, rapid advancements in ultrasound technology have significantly improved the accuracy of first-trimester anatomical examinations, enabling the early detection of more structural abnormalities. Despite this progress, variations in examination protocols, procedural practices, and diagnostic criteria across regions, institutions, and healthcare professionals of different qualifications have led to inconsistencies in screening quality. These discrepancies contribute to uneven detection rates for fetal anomalies in the first trimester [5], resulting in delays in early diagnosis and intervention that may impact pregnancy outcomes.

To address these issues, we propose a standardized protocol for first-trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical examination based on the Delphi method, ultimately culminating in an expert consensus. This consensus aims to guide healthcare professionals across institutions of varying levels and qualifications in performing standardized first-trimester fetal anatomical examinations. It also provides evidence-based support for future first-trimester fetal malformation screening.

Methods

First, a review of recent literature on first-trimester ultrasound anatomical examinations was conducted. A core committee consisting of eight experts from prenatal diagnostic centers, prenatal screening centers, university-affiliated hospitals, and district-level hospitals was established to draft the Chinese Expert Consensus on Fetal Ultrasound Anatomical Examination for Fetuses at 11 + 0 to 13 + 6 Weeks of Gestation. The Delphi method was employed to conduct a three-round survey process, focusing on the following topics: (1) whether the expert consensus and suggested revisions were agreed upon; (2) Whether to agree on classifying first-trimester fetal anatomical ultrasound examinations into three categories: basic (Class I), detailed (Class II), and targeted (Class III) examinations. The recommended or suggested planes for basic (Class I), detailed (Class II), and targeted (Class III) first-trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical examinations.

In the first round, the draft consensus and survey were distributed via email to a panel of 35 prenatal ultrasound experts. Feedback was collected by staff members who were not involved in drafting the consensus. The feedback was categorized and organized. Survey items that did not reach consensus in the first round were revised by the core committee based on the collected feedback. Any items that were not supported by at least 25% of the expert panel were removed. A virtual meeting was held to clarify and explain the revisions.

The revised expert consensus draft and survey were then distributed to the expert panel for a second round of consecutive voting. Feedback was again collected, categorized, and reviewed. The core committee made further revisions accordingly. The updated draft was also sent to an additional 122 prenatal ultrasound experts from various regions of China to solicit broader opinions and suggestions. Based on their feedback, additional modifications were made, and another virtual meeting was held to discuss and clarify key points.

Finally, the revised draft was submitted to the 35-member expert panel for a third round of voting. If 75% or more of the panel members agreed, the draft was accepted. For the classification of planes related to the basic (Class I), detailed (Class II), and targeted (Class III) examinations, any item receiving 75% or more approval was designated as a recommended item; items with less support were considered suggested. The final consensus was then established.

Results (consensus reached)

Qualifications

First-trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical examinations must be conducted at institutions qualified for prenatal ultrasound screening and/or diagnosis. These institutions should also establish consultation networks and quality assurance systems.

Ultrasound physicians performing first-trimester fetal anatomical examinations must meet the following qualifications: (1) A minimum of five years of experience in obstetric ultrasound and formal training in prenatal ultrasound diagnostic systems; (2) Participation in at least one specialized continuing medical education activity related to first-trimester fetal anatomical ultrasound examination, with satisfactory performance; (3) Independent completion of at least 12 cases of first-trimester fetal anatomical ultrasound examinations in each category, with acceptable image quality, under the supervision of a qualified ultrasound physician.

Equipment

A high-resolution color Doppler ultrasound diagnostic system of medium or high grade should be used. The system must be equipped with both abdominal and transvaginal probes, capable of adjusting ultrasound output power, and able to display the mechanical index and/or bone thermal index. Alternatively, the system should include obstetric-specific software with appropriate safety certification.

A complete image storage and image management system must be in place.

For targeted first-trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical examinations, high-end color Doppler ultrasound machines with three-dimensional ultrasound capabilities and highly sensitive color Doppler blood flow imaging technology should be employed.

Examination principles

The ALARA principle (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) should be strictly followed, meaning that the required diagnostic information should be obtained using the lowest possible ultrasound exposure dose [6–8]. Particular attention should be paid to the following points: (1) The scanning area should be kept as small as possible, with appropriate control of the region of interest (ROI); (2) Ultrasound exposure should not be focused on a single area, organ, or target zone—especially on sensitive structures such as the eyes and reproductive organs; (3) B-mode and M-mode ultrasound should be the primary imaging modalities, with minimal use of pulsed Doppler or color Doppler techniques.

Ultrasound equipment should be configured specifically for first-trimester obstetric examinations, with the thermal index (TI) and mechanical index (MI) adjusted to values below 1.0.

The duration of basic and detailed ultrasound examinations should be limited to under 10 min. For targeted examinations, the duration may be extended as needed but should not exceed 20 min.

Classification and applicable institutions for the first trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical examination

Classification and indications

First-trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical examinations are divided into three categories: basic (Class I), detailed (Class II), and targeted (Class III) examinations. In cases where abdominal ultrasound yields suboptimal images, transvaginal ultrasound may be performed—provided there are no contraindications and informed consent is obtained from the pregnant woman.

It is applied for screening low-risk fetuses and is suitable for county-level central hospitals and maternal and child health hospitals with screening or diagnostic qualifications, as well as their certified physicians. First-trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical targeted examination (Class III)

This examination is indicated in specific clinical scenarios and is typically performed between 13 + 0 and 13 + 6 weeks of gestation. It is intended for high-risk fetuses or those suspected of having anatomical abnormalities and should be performed at a prenatal diagnosis center or institution with prenatal diagnostic screening qualifications. It consists of both recommended and suggested items. Recommended items are mandatory, whereas suggested items are optional. If image quality is compromised due to technical limitations, suggested items may be omitted. The indications for targeted first-trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical examinations include the following [9, 10]:

Clearly or potentially abnormal fetal findings detected through ultrasound and clinical evaluation, including: Increased nuchal translucency; Structural malformations of the fetus; High risk on the Down syndrome screening; Abnormal results from chorionic villus sampling or Non-Invasive Prenatal Testing (NIPT);

Quality control

To reduce the rate of missed diagnoses and misdiagnoses in first-trimester prenatal ultrasound examinations, strict quality control and management are essential for improving the overall quality of first-trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical assessments.

Relevant standardized images of each plane should be collected and stored according to the category of ultrasound examination and the corresponding observation items. Prenatal ultrasound examinations between 11 + 0 and 13 + 6 weeks of gestation should be performed in a standardized manner, and ultrasound reports should be written accordingly (see Appendix 1).

If satisfactory ultrasound images cannot be obtained due to fetal position or maternal obesity, and detailed evaluation is necessary, it is recommended to adjust the maternal position or have the pregnant woman move and repeat the scan. If image quality remains suboptimal, this should be clearly documented in the ultrasound report, and a follow-up examination should be recommended within a short period.

Multidisciplinary Consultation: If fetal abnormalities are suspected during the first-trimester ultrasound examination, referral to a prenatal diagnostic center for further evaluation is recommended. If needed, a multidisciplinary consultation should be conducted. It is advised that the pregnant woman’s family participate in the multidisciplinary discussion, which should involve ultrasound physicians, obstetricians, pediatricians, geneticists, radiologists, and other specialists to provide a comprehensive evaluation and management plan.

Four-Step Management: Establish a system of follow-up ultrasound and obstetric clinical tracking during the first, second, and third trimesters, as well as after birth. For cases with uncertain diagnoses in the first trimester, a follow-up examination in the second trimester is recommended.

Basic and detailed the first trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical examination (11 + 0 to 13 + 6 weeks of gestation)

Basic and detailed ultrasound anatomical examinations should be conducted by qualified institutions and trained personnel. These examinations are intended for first-trimester ultrasound screening of low-risk fetuses. The examination planes (Fig. 1) and corresponding content are outlined in Tables 1 and 2. For multiple pregnancies, it is important to document the number of chorionic membranes and amniotic sacs. In cases of monochorionic multiple fetuses, special attention should be given to capturing images of the placental cord insertion site. If there are concurrent uterine adnexal conditions, such as uterine fibroids (especially those attached to the placenta) or ovarian cysts, these findings should also be clearly reported [10].

Targeted the first trimester fetal ultrasound anatomical examination (11 + 0 to 13 + 6 weeks of gestation)

Targeted fetal ultrasound anatomical examinations are performed when indicated. Currently, there are three well-established anatomical sites or organs for targeted ultrasound evaluation, though these are not limited to only these areas.

Targeted examinations must utilize advanced ultrasound technologies—such as three-dimensional ultrasound, four-dimensional spatiotemporal image correlation, volume imaging, and microvascular flow imaging—to conduct comprehensive, multi-planar assessments of potentially abnormal organs or regions. These examinations are primarily indicated for high-risk fetuses, including: (1) those with confirmed or suspected structural abnormalities identified during basic or detailed first-trimester examinations; (2) increased nuchal translucency or elevated risk of chromosomal abnormalities; and (3) abnormal findings from chorionic villus sampling or NIPT. Since fetal malformations are often multiple, especially in cases of syndromes or sequences, targeted examinations are performed on the basis of the detailed first-trimester fetal anatomical ultrasound (Class II), focusing further on organs or regions with suspected anomalies.

Discussion

Perinatal Medicine Foundation (PMF) [4], the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG) [6, 10], and the American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine (AIUM) [9]—have issued guidelines, consensus statements, and protocols on first-trimester fetal anatomical ultrasound examinations. However, the screening items, content, requirements, and operational standards differ among these organizations and professional bodies. This study presents the expert consensus in China on first-trimester fetal anatomical ultrasound examinations developed using the Delphi method.

The consensus involved experts from diverse regions and institutions across China, encompassing various levels of qualifications. It was formulated based on a comprehensive review of the literature, clinical practice experience and three rounds of expert voting, with the goal of standardizing and enhancing the effectiveness and consistency of early fetal anatomical ultrasound screening. The establishment of this consensus is expected to harmonize examination content, procedures, and operational standards across different healthcare settings, thereby reducing variability in practice and improving the accuracy and reliability of first-trimester screening for fetal structural anomalies.

The key features of this expert consensus are as follows:

The first-trimester fetal anatomical ultrasound examinations are classified into three categories—basic, detailed, and targeted. This tiered system is designed to support standardized practice across regions, institutions, and professionals with varying levels of expertise, promoting more consistent and structured clinical implementation.

The consensus introduces the concept of targeted examinations focusing on three critical systems or anatomical regions: the central nervous system, the heart, and cleft lip and palate. Malformations of these three organs or regions account for the majority of severe fetal anomalies in early pregnancy. They may be lethal anomalies or serve as clues for the screening of chromosomal abnormalities and their associated malformations (sequences).

Despite the detailed examination scheme outlined in this consensus, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the expert panel consisted solely of specialists from China. As a result, the feedback and recommendations may reflect regional clinical practices, variations in expertise, and local healthcare infrastructure. This could limit the generalizability of the consensus to other international contexts. Additionally, given the inherent complexity of fetal anatomical assessments, particularly in high-risk or complicated pregnancies, some cases may still require further evaluation through advanced modalities such as molecular genetic testing or specialist consultation. To enhance the robustness and global applicability of these standards, future work should integrate broader clinical data, drawing from evidence-based medicine and emerging technologies. Furthermore, some progressive or late-onset fetal malformations (such as digestive tract obstruction, heart valve stenosis, tumors, etc.) do not manifest until the second trimester or third trimester. Therefore, it is important to pay attention to the re-examination and follow-up of ultrasound. This would help refine and validate the standardized examination protocols and expand their use in diverse healthcare settings.

In conclusion, this expert consensus presents a scientifically grounded and standardized approach to first-trimester fetal anatomical ultrasound examinations. It provides a valuable framework to support the early detection of fetal structural abnormalities and improve the consistency of prenatal screening. Continued evaluation in clinical practice is essential to assess the effectiveness of these guidelines and to update and optimize the examination standards in accordance with advances in prenatal imaging and diagnostic technologies.
