Aggressive Periodontitis and Palmoplantar Keratoderma: Papillon–Lefèvre Syndrome in an 11‐Year‐Old Nepalese Girl
Authors: Jenisha Bhattarai, Sanish Pokhrel, Yogendra Poudel, Ujjwal Rimal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPapillon–Lefèvre syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by palmoplantar keratoderma and aggressive periodontitis. We report an 11‐year‐old Nepalese girl presenting with severe periodontal destruction and characteristic cutaneous manifestations. Clinical and radiographic findings established the diagnosis. Early multidisciplinary management is essential to preserve function and improve quality of life.Keywords: cathepsin‐C, palmoplantar keratoderma, Papillon–Lefèvre syndrome, periodontitis, retinoid

Introduction

Papillon–Lefèvre syndrome was first described by MM Papillon and P Lefèvre (French pediatricians) in 1924, reportedly seen in a brother and sister who were the offspring of first cousins. This is characterized by palmoplantar keratosis of the palms and soles caused by thickening of the stratum corneum layer of the epidermis and aggressive periodontitis [1]. It is an autosomal recessive disorder affecting one to four persons per million that results from mutation of the cathepsin C66 gene encoding [2]. Haneke reviewed 124 cases and settled they were equally distributed among males and females, there was no racial predominance, one‐third of Papillon–Lefèvre syndrome patients were offspring of blood relatives and were susceptible to infectious diseases alongside periodontal disease [3]. Parents of a child with PLS are informed that there is a 25% probability of PLS in each subsequent pregnancy. Also, it is extremely rare for PLS patients to pass this disorder to their own children [4].

Case Presentation

An 11‐year‐old female patient presented to our tertiary care center with a chief complaint of loosening of the lower anterior teeth, associated with pain for the past 7 months.

The pain was gradual in onset and was intermittent, lasting for a few seconds. It was sharp and pricking in nature, non‐radiating, and was not associated with any identifiable aggravating or relieving factors. The patient also had discomfort while biting and chewing food. There was gum bleeding while brushing. Past dental history revealed that the patient had undergone professional dental cleaning 5 months earlier. No significant medical history was reported. There was no history of similar dental manifestations in other family members.

Clinical Examination

Extraoral Examination

The face was apparently symmetrical. The lips were dry, patchy in texture, and competent. Temporomandibular joint examination revealed bilaterally symmetrical and synchronous movements without deviation or deflection during mouth opening or closing. No joint sounds were detected. Submental and bilateral submandibular lymph nodes were non‐palpable. Dermatological examination revealed diffuse symmetrical erythema, marked xerosis, superficial scaling, and fine reticular fissuring involving the palmar and plantar surfaces of the hands and the dorsal surfaces of the legs. Massive bilateral hyperkeratosis of the soles was present, with underlying raw erythematous areas in regions where the plaques had exfoliated. The nails showed hyperkeratosis, thickening, and yellow‐brown discoloration (Figures 1 and 2).

Intraoral Examination

Soft Tissue Findings

The gingiva appeared red, soft, and edematous, scalloped with blunt edges, inflamed with loss of stippling. Generalized bleeding on probing was present. Position of gingiva apical to the cementoenamel junction (gingival recession) was observed with respect to teeth 31, 33, 36, and 41. Periodontal pocket depths of 7, 5, and 7 mm were recorded with respect to teeth 31, 36, and 41, respectively. The oral mucosa appeared dry and sticky on palpation (Figure 3).

Radiographic Findings

Orthopantomogram (OPG) revealed severe generalized horizontal alveolar bone loss involving both the maxillary and mandibular arches. Bone loss extended up to the root apex of tooth 36, producing a characteristic floating tooth appearance (Figure 4). Several permanent teeth were present in different stages of root development. No periapical pathology, cystic lesions, or other jaw abnormalities were detected.

(A) Horizontal bone loss, (B) floating tooth appearance.

Treatment

Supragingival scaling was done. Mouth gargle with chlorhexidine gluconate 0.2% was prescribed 10 mL for 30 s for 2 weeks. The patient was advised for modified bass brushing technique and to maintain oral hygiene. Further treatment plans were made to extract the hopeless teeth and proceed with dental implants.

Dermatology consultation was done, and the patient was treated with oral acitretin and topical moisturizer containing 12% urea and 6% lactic acid.

Discussion

PLS is commonly caused by mutations in the cathepsin C (CTSC) gene, resulting in reduced or absent cathepsin C enzyme activity [5]. The major gene locus for PLS is located to a 2.8‐cm interval on chromosome 11q14, where homozygous mutations in the cathepsin C (CTSC) gene have been identified in affected individuals [6]. PLS is a rare genetic disorder affecting 1–4 people per million, with equal occurrence in all sexes and races. Around 2–4 per 1000 people are carriers of the gene [7]. The gene is predominantly expressed in the epithelial tissues including palms, soles, gingiva, and immune cells [8]. In our case, genetic testing could not be performed because of low socioeconomic status of the patient, but dermatological with oral and radiological features strongly suggested the diagnosis of PLS. PLS is characterized by diffuse palmoplantar keratoderma, early loss of both primary and permanent teeth, and a predisposition to recurrent pyogenic skin infections [9]. Patients with PLS may have defects in neutrophil, lymphocyte, or monocyte function, making them more susceptible to bacterial infections and recurrent pyogenic conditions such as pyoderma, furunculosis, pneumonia, and liver abscesses [10]. Besides skin and oral manifestations, patients may have immune dysfunction due to cathepsin C deficiency, affecting granzyme B activation and natural killer (NK) cell activity [11]. Just like the Classical symptoms, in our patient there was recurrent erythematous lesions in hands, palms, legs, and soles with areas of scarring. Recurrent gingivitis and multiple loose teeth were present. No skin infection or liver abscess was present. Other features of PLS include calcification of the choroid plexus and tentorium seen on radiographic examination, along with excessive sweating of the palms and soles (palmoplantar hyperhidrosis) [12]. Similarly, our patient developed severe periodontal destruction and characteristic cutaneous manifestations during childhood. However, unlike reports with prolonged follow‐up documenting periodontal progression and response to therapy, our report describes the initial presentation and early multidisciplinary management in a resource‐limited setting.

Differential diagnosis that closely resembles Papillon–Lefèvre syndrome are Haim‐Munk syndrome and prepubertal periodontitis [9]. Histopathological findings of skin lesions in PLS are generally nonspecific; therefore, the diagnosis is primarily based on the patient's history and clinical features [13]. Early diagnosis of PLS and a multidisciplinary treatment approach can significantly improve patient outcomes. Systemic antibiotics help control active periodontitis, while regular oral preventive care can improve the patient's quality of life [14].

Management of PLS is challenging because of its unpredictable course and poor periodontal prognosis. Treatment requires a multidisciplinary approach involving dermatologists, pediatricians, and dental specialists [7]. The primary goal is to eliminate periodontal pathogens through oral hygiene measures, scaling and root planing, chlorhexidine mouth rinses, and systemic antibiotics to control active periodontitis and preserve teeth [15]. PLS can significantly affect patients physically, psychologically, and socially, making oral rehabilitation essential. Age appropriate prosthetic treatment, including dentures and later implant‐supported prostheses, helps restore function, esthetics, and self‐confidence, improving the quality of life of both patients and their families [16]. Low‐dose acitretin (0.4–1 mg/kg/day) is a safe and effective treatment for palmoplantar keratoderma associated with PLS, providing significant improvement, although it is not curative [17]. The severity of keratoderma can be further reduced with the use of emollients and keratolytic agents [18].

Acitretin therapy successfully reduced inflammatory markers like IL‐12B, IL‐17C, IL‐26, and IL‐36. Topical tazarotene helps regulate keratinocyte growth and differentiation while delivering an anti‐inflammatory effect, making it highly effective at managing PLS‐related keratoderma [19]. Oral retinoids, such as acitretin, etretinate, and isotretinoin, serve as highly effective interventions generally started before the successor teeth begin to erupt, continuing the regimen until the entire dental development process is complete. These agents function by regulating vital cellular processes, including apoptosis, differentiation, and proliferation. By actively promoting keratinocyte growth, retinoids strengthen the protective barrier of the epidermis, reduce transepidermal water loss, preserve structural collagen, and block destructive metalloproteinase activity [20]. Working within these clinical constraints, we focused our treatment primarily on providing immediate relief for the patient's periodontal and skin conditions. Early diagnosis, timely intervention, and regular follow‐up are essential for improving long‐term outcomes. However, long‐term follow‐up was unavailable in our case; the effectiveness of treatment cannot be evaluated. Stem cell therapy may offer promising new possibilities for the dental management of children with PLS in the future [21].

Conclusion

This case underscores the diagnostic value of correlating clinical, dermatological, and radiographic findings when molecular confirmation is unavailable. Early diagnosis and a coordinated multidisciplinary treatment approach are crucial for minimizing tooth loss, managing cutaneous manifestations, and improving functional, psychological, and social well‐being. Retinoids such as acitretin remain an important adjunct in the management of palmoplantar keratoderma, providing significant clinical improvement despite not being curative. Future rehabilitation with osseointegrated implants remains an excellent option, providing critical psychosocial benefits by restoring both functional capacity and dental esthetics. Regular follow‐up, preventive periodontal care, and oral rehabilitation are essential components of long‐term management. Furthermore, emerging regenerative approaches, including stem cell therapy, may offer promising future possibilities for dental rehabilitation and improved outcomes in patients with PLS.
