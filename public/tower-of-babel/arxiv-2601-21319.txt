Alliance Mechanisms in General Lotto Games
Vade Shah, Jason R. Marden

Abstract
How do different alliance mechanisms compare? In this work, we analyze various methods of forming an alliance in the Coalitional General Lotto game, a simple model of competitive resource allocation. In the game, Players 1 and 2 independently compete against a common Adversary by allocating their limited resource budgets towards separate sets of contests; an agent wins a contest by allocating more resources towards it than their opponent. In this setting, we study three alliance mechanisms: budget transfers (resource donation), contest transfers (contest redistribution), and joint transfers (both simultaneously). For all three mechanisms, we study when they present opportunities for collective improvement (the sum of the Players’ payoffs increases) or mutual improvement (both Players’ individual payoffs increase). In our first result, we show that all three are fundamentally different with regards to mutual improvement; in particular, mutually beneficial budget and contest transfers exist in distinct, limited subsets of games, whereas mutually beneficial joint transfers exist in almost all games. However, in our second result, we demonstrate that all three mechanisms are equivalent when it comes to collective improvement; that is, collectively beneficial budget, contest, and joint transfers exist in almost all game instances, and all three mechanisms achieve the same maximum collective payoff. Together, these results demonstrate that differences between mechanisms depend fundamentally on the objective of the alliance.

Among the mechanisms studied in the literature, perhaps the most natural is the

II
Model

In this section, we introduce the model of the Coalitional General Lotto game in which two Players independently compete against a common Adversary across disjoint sets of contests. For ease of exposition, we begin with a discussion of the classical one-versus-one General Lotto game between a single Player and the Adversary.

II-A

General Lotto Game

In the
General Lotto
game, two agents (say, Player 1 and the Adversary) compete across a set of
n

X_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

k^{\mathrm{th}}

v^{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}

D_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}

D_{1}^{k}

k

U_{1}^{\rm GL}(D_{1},D_{A})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}v^{k}\cdot I\{D_{1}^{k}\geq D_{A}^{k}\},

where

U_{A}^{\rm GL}(D_{1},D_{A})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}v^{k}-U_{1}^{\rm GL}(D_{1},D_{A})

A
strategy
for Player 1 is a
n

F_{1}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}_{D_{1}\sim F_{1}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}D_{1}^{k}\right]\leq X_{1},

(1)

meaning that Player 1 meets their budget constraint
in expectation
. The Adversary’s strategy
F_{A}
is defined similarly.
The agents’ Nash equilibrium strategies and payoffs in this setting are well-understood and have been characterized in
[
28
]
. For ease of exposition, we omit the equilibrium strategies and instead present only the equilibrium payoffs in the following Corollary from
[

]
:

(Kovenock and Roberson, 2021)
.

For any General Lotto game with budgets
X_{1}
,
X_{A}
and contest valuations
v^{1},\dots,v^{n}
, a Nash equilibrium exists, and the unique Nash equilibrium payoffs for Player 1 and the Adversary are

\displaystyle U_{1}^{\rm NE}(\phi,X_{1},X_{A})

\displaystyle\triangleq\begin{cases}\phi\left(\frac{X_{1}}{2X_{A}}\right)&X_{1}\leq X_{A}\\
\phi\left(1-\frac{X_{A}}{2X_{1}}\right)&X_{1}>X_{A}\end{cases}

(2)

\displaystyle\text{and }\;U_{A}^{\rm NE}(\phi,X_{1},X_{A})

\displaystyle\triangleq\phi-U_{1}^{\rm NE}(\phi,X_{1},X_{A}),

(3)

respectively, where
\phi\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{n}v^{k}
.

II-B

Coalitional General Lotto Game

To study alliance mechanisms, we analyze the
Coalitional General Lotto game
, a variant in which Players 1 and 2 compete against a common Adversary in separate standard General Lotto games. Our focus is on opportunities for the Players to form alliances, where informally, an alliance consists of transferring contests, resources, or both from one game to the other. Formally, the interaction unfolds over multiple stages, as illustrated in Figure

Stage 0 (Initialization):

Stage 1 (Alliance Formation):
The Players may form an alliance through a transfer of contests, budgets, or both.

Definition 1

(Types of transfers)

a)

A

III-B

Collectively Beneficial Alliances

Next, we consider alliances formed through a collectively beneficial transfer, which we define as follows:

Definition 3

(Collectively beneficial transfers)

A transfer
(\tau^{*},\nu^{*})

U_{1}(\tau^{*},\nu^{*})+U_{2}(\tau^{*},\nu^{*})>U_{1}(0,0)+U_{2}(0,0).

(8)

As the name suggests, collectively beneficial transfers improve the Players’ collective payoff, but they need not be mutually beneficial. Now, recall our second main question (Q2): do budget, contest, and joint transfers differ in their ability to generate
collectively

For any Coalitional General Lotto game
G\in\mathbf{G}

\displaystyle\max_{\tau}\ U_{1}(\tau,0)+U_{2}(\tau,0)=

\displaystyle\max_{\tau,\nu}\ U_{1}(\tau,\nu)+U_{2}(\tau,\nu)

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\nu}\ U_{1}(0,\nu)+U_{2}(0,\nu).

Theorem
2
highlights that when it comes to maximizing the collective payoff, there is effectively no difference between budget, contest, and joint transfers. Note that this Theorem says two things: first, opportunities for collective improvement arise in the same subsets of games for all three mechanisms, and second, that the magnitude of improvement is also the same. The key implication of this result is that performing budget and contest transfers simultaneously cannot enhance collective performance; forming an alliance through either mechanism alone is sufficient. IV
Conclusion

In this paper, we study three alliance mechanisms—resource sharing (budget transfers), non-compete agreements (contest transfers), and combinations of the two (joint transfers)—using the framework of the Coalitional General Lotto game, a competitive resource allocation problem where two players compete against a common Adversary. We compare these transfers on two different performance objectives, collective benefit and mutual benefit. While intuition might suggest that different transfer mechanisms would consistently yield different outcomes, our results demonstrate otherwise: although the mechanisms are equivalent when it comes to collective benefit (Theorem
2
), they differ greatly in their ability to generate opportunities for mutual benefit (Theorem
1
).

Returning to our opening question about comparing alliance mechanisms: the answer depends entirely on what the alliance seeks to achieve. Practically, this implies that organizations seeking to maximize joint performance can choose any alliance mechanism based on convenience or feasibility. However, when individual partners require guaranteed improvement, the choice of mechanism becomes crucial. In this setting, our results suggest that joint approaches should be the default consideration, as they offer far greater flexibility than single-mechanism alliances.

Several interesting directions emerge from this work. An important future study entails extending these results to the network setting with several competitors and contests; a similar study has been performed in
[
24
]
for a different payoff model. Another question is to analyze whether other strategic maneuvers, such as information sharing (if the Players hold priors on the Adversary’s allocation strategy, for example), would also create distinct alliance opportunities.

Proof of Theorem
2

The proof requires analyzing the change in the collective payoff
U_{1+2}(\tau,\nu)\triangleq U_{1}(\tau,\nu)+U_{2}(\tau,\nu)
as a function of the contest transfer amount
\nu
for fixed
\tau=0
in each of the four Cases; the comparison with budget and joint transfers is shown at the end of the proof. A transfer from Player 1 to Player 2 effectively translates the game
G
in the parameter space to a new game
\overline{G}
, meaning that the game
\overline{G}
may belong to a Case different from that of
G
; this is detailed in Figure
6
.

First, consider any game
G\in\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{1}
. In this case, the Adversary allocates all of their budget to General Lotto game 1, meaning that the alliance wins a portion of the contests in Lotto game 1, and they win all the contests in Lotto game 2. Thus, it is clear that the alliance can improve their collective payoff by transferring contest valuation from Player 1 to Player 2, increasing the value of the contests won in the game 2 (i.e., they win
\phi_{2}+\nu
); conversely, it is also clear that the alliance cannot improve their payoff by transferring valuation from Player 2 to Player 1. The alliance can improve their payoff by transferring contests from Player 1 to Player 2 until either
\overline{G}\in\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{2}
, which we address next, or
\overline{G}\in\mathbf{C}^{4}
.

Second, consider any game
G\in\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{2}
. In this case, the alliance payoff
U_{1+2}(0,\nu\,|\,G)
is given by

\left(1-\frac{1}{2X_{2}}\right)(\phi_{2}+\nu)+\left(\frac{X_{1}(\phi_{1}-\nu)(\phi_{2}+\nu)}{X_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and its derivative
\frac{d}{d\nu}U_{1+2}
is given by

1-\frac{1}{2X_{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{X_{1}}{X_{2}(\phi_{1}-\nu)(\phi_{2}+\nu)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}-2\nu).

It is readily shown that
\frac{d}{d\nu}U_{1+2}|_{\nu=0}>0
when
X_{2}>\frac{\phi_{2}}{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}}
, which is true for all
G\in\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{2}
. Hence, we have that collectively beneficial transfers exist for every game in
\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{2}
. Furthermore, since
\frac{d}{d\nu}U_{1+2}|_{\nu=0}>0
and
U_{1+2}
is concave in
\nu
, we also have that collectively beneficial transfers must be positive. The alliance can thus improve its payoff by performing a transfer, which yields
\overline{G}\in\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{3}
if
X_{1}+X_{2}<1
, or
\overline{G}\in\mathbf{C}^{4}
otherwise—we address both Cases next.

Third, consider any game
G\in\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{3}
. In this case, the alliance payoff
U_{1+2}(0,\nu\,|\,G)
is given by

\frac{1}{2}X_{1}(\phi_{1}-\nu)+\frac{1}{2}X_{2}(\phi_{2}+\nu)+\left(X_{1}X_{2}(\phi_{1}-\nu)(\phi_{2}+\nu)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

and its derivative
\frac{d}{d\nu}U_{1+2}
is given by

\frac{1}{2}(X_{2}-X_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{X_{1}X_{2}}{(\phi_{1}-\nu)(\phi_{2}+\nu)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}-2\nu).

It is straightforward to show that
\frac{d}{d\nu}U_{1+2}|_{\nu=0}
is nonnegative for all games in
\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{3}
. Furthermore, simple algebraic substitution shows that the unique transfer
\nu^{**}
that solves
\frac{d}{d\nu}U_{1+2}=0
satisfies
\frac{X_{1}}{\phi_{1}-\nu^{**}}=\frac{X_{2}}{\phi_{2}+\nu^{**}}
. Thus, collectively beneficial transfers exist for every game in
\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{3}
, and they must go from Player 1 to Player 2.

Last, consider any game
G\in\mathbf{C}^{4}
. The previous arguments concerning Cases 1 and 2 establish that the alliance payoff increases only via positive transfers approaching Case 4. Furthermore, these arguments are symmetric for games
G\in\mathbf{G}\setminus\mathbf{G}_{1\leq 2}
. This, along with the fact that
U_{1+2}
is continuous in
\nu
, implies that the alliance payoff is maximized when
G\in\mathbf{C}^{4}
. By extension, for any game
G\in\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{1}\,\cup\,\mathbf{C}_{1\leq 2}^{2}
, the transfer
\nu^{**}
that maximizes the alliance payoff must be positive and must induce
\overline{G}\in\mathbf{C}^{4}
, meaning that
\frac{X_{1}}{\phi_{1}-\nu^{**}}=\frac{X_{2}}{\phi_{2}+\nu^{**}}
.

The previous arguments establish that the collectively beneficial transfer that maximizes the collective payoff is such that
\frac{X_{1}}{\overline{\phi}_{1}}=\frac{X_{2}}{\overline{\phi}_{2}}
. To complete the proof, we must simply compare the payoff resulting from maximizing budget, contest, and joint transfers. First, observe that if
X_{1}+X_{2}\geq 1
, then from the above analysis and from
[
16
]
, the maximizing budget or contest transfer is such that
\overline{G}\in\mathbf{C}^{4}
, meaning that the collective payoff is

\frac{2(\overline{X}_{1}+\overline{X_{2}})-1}{2(\overline{X}_{1}+\overline{X_{2}})}\left(\overline{\phi}_{1}+\overline{\phi}_{2}\right)=\frac{2(X_{1}+X_{2})-1}{2(X_{1}+X_{2})}\left(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}\right),

which is a fixed constant regardless of the transfer type. Otherwise, if
X_{1}+X_{2}<1
, then
\overline{G}\in\mathbf{C}_{3}
, and the collective payoff is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{X}_{1}\overline{\phi}_{1}+\overline{X}_{2}\overline{\phi}_{2}\right)+\left(\overline{X}_{1}\overline{X}_{2}\overline{\phi}_{1}\overline{\phi}_{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

When
\frac{\overline{X}_{1}}{\overline{\phi}_{1}}=\frac{\overline{X}_{2}}{\overline{\phi}_{2}}
(which is true for any maximizing contest or budget transfer
[
16
]
), this expression can be rewritten as
\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}\right)\left(X_{1}+X_{2}\right)
, which is also a constant.
