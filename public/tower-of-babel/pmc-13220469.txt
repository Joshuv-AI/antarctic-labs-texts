Spatiotemporal modeling of Under-Five Pneumonia (U-5P) risk in Ghana using Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations, 2018–2022
Authors: Moses Asori, Ali Musah, Monica Ahiadorme, Emmanuel Nakua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Ghana, disparities in health risks and child mortality, mainly attributed to socioeconomic and environmental factors, exhibit significant geographic characteristics. We employed a comprehensive approach that integrated advanced spatiotemporal modeling techniques to address the intricate interplay between spatial and temporal dynamics underlying the risk of under-five pneumonia (U-5P) in Ghana.MethodsPneumonia cases for children under five from 2018 to 2022 were obtained from the District Health Information Management System-2 (DHIMS-2). We coupled an integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) with an intrinsic conditional autoregressive (ICAR) model based on the Besag-York-Mollie (BYM2) model to estimate the relative risk of U-5P between 2018 and 2022. Temporal dependences were handled using a random walk order 1. Furthermore, we used Local Indicators of the Spatial Association (LISA) to detect spatial clusters of the relative risk estimates calculated with INLA.ResultsOverall, there was statistically significant variation in cases across years (K2 = 115.63, p-value < 0.0001). The spatial distribution of the relative risk of U-5P was not random across all the years studied: 2018 (z-score = 2.7917; p = 0.00523), 2019 (z-score = 3.48; p = 0.0048), 2021 (z-score = 3.83; p = 0.000126), and 2022 (z-score = 5.50; p < 0.0001), except for 2020 (z-score = 0.38; p = 0.699). Specifically, there was high spatial variability in risk, with districts in the Greater Accra region consistently experiencing lower-than-expected risk over the years studied. In contrast, those in the Volta region had a consistently higher-than-expected risk.ConclusionOur findings have two main takeaways. First, there is spatial heterogeneity in U-5P risk levels, with statistically significant clusters. Second, the spatial pattern may persist or even intensify with time. Therefore, a real-time surveillance system that integrates time and geography must be developed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27299-z.

Introduction

Pneumonia is an acute respiratory infection of one or both lungs caused by bacteria, viruses, or fungi [1–3]. The disease can lead to inflammation, resulting in hypoxia, dyspnea, and death [4]. People of all ages can be affected by pneumonia, although infants and children under five are more susceptible to infection and its complications [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), pneumonia is the leading infectious cause of mortality in children worldwide, with the highest rate found in southern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa [1]. WHO in 2019 estimated that pneumonia killed 740,180 children under the age of 5 years old, accounting for 14% of deaths of children in this age group [1]. Annually, the disease has also contributed to more than 10 million hospital admissions among the under-five-year-old population [5, 6], posing a significant burden on the healthcare infrastructure [7, 8] and household incomes [9] most especially in the African regions.

Streptococcus pneumoniae and Respiratory syncytial virus are the most frequent causes of bacterial and viral pneumonia in children [1, 3]. However, Pneumocystis jiroveci is responsible for pneumonia infection in HIV-positive newborns, accounting for at least 25% of all pneumonia deaths in HIV-infected infants [1]. Pneumonia can spread to the lungs when bacteria or viruses in the nose or throat are inhaled. The infection can also spread through blood during and soon after birth, and air-borne droplets from a cough or sneeze [1]. Children with compromised immune systems due to malnutrition and the presence of comorbid conditions, including HIV infection, measles, and malaria, are more susceptible to pneumonia infection compared to healthy children [1]. Sociodemographic characteristics such as maternal education, region of residence, rural-urban residence, water and sanitation facilities, and household income are other identified risk factors for childhood pneumonia [10–12]. A child’s risk of contracting pneumonia is further increased by environmental factors such as living in crowded households, having parents who smoke, indoor air pollution due to biomass, and outdoor air pollution due to vehicular emissions [13–15]. Environmental and socioeconomic risk factors exhibit substantial spatial heterogeneity. Consequently, the inherent geographic distribution of these risk factors likely influences pneumonia risk differently across regions. This emphasizes the importance of adopting innovative methodologies that account for geographic and spatial effects. Doing so will enable the development of targeted interventions sensitive to local conditions and contexts.

Based on the 2017 data on the cause of death in children under five years, pneumonia was identified as the fourth leading cause of mortality globally [16]. In Africa, the disease is life-threatening and a significant cause of mortality, morbidity, and hospitalization among children. Results from various studies on pneumonia infection indicated that the infection is temporally seasonal [17, 18]. Therefore, this underscores the need to adopt spatio-temporal models that capture both temporal and spatial effects at different levels. Such approaches can spatially target the most vulnerable locations while making future-oriented inferences to support proactive planning. Even though the reported incidence and mortality of pneumonia in Ghana are lower than in other sub-Saharan countries, the annual record of incidence of deaths due to pneumonia in the country remains > 100 per 100,000 in children less than five years of age, which makes the infection the 3rd most common cause of death in this age population [19]. Few studies on childhood pneumonia have been conducted in Ghana. In one such study, it was shown that pneumonia accounted for deaths in children less than five years old, which was 21.7% in 2011 [20], stressing the high impact pneumonia poses on the already poor local healthcare facilities. Therefore, attention is given to the etiology and epidemiology of pneumonia in children under 5 years in Ghana to ensure effective treatment and preventive interventions. This is particularly relevant for Ghanaians, as reducing under-five pneumonia aligns with Sustainable Development Goal (SDG)-3 (ensuring healthy lives and promoting well-being for all, targeting under-5 mortality below 25 per 1000 live births by 2030) and African Union Agenda 2063 Priority 2 (strengthening resilient health systems to address communicable diseases like pneumonia) [21].

Despite the public health importance of pneumonia, most studies conducted in Ghana have relied on descriptive analysis and traditional statistical techniques, with limited exploration of its geographical patterns and drivers. Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations (INLA) has emerged as an efficient Bayesian approach for fitting complex spatial and spatiotemporal models, and it has been applied in studies examining respiratory infections, environmental exposures, and childhood diseases [22–24]. INLA is a Bayesian inference-based approach that can handle hierarchical analytical structure. Studies involving high-dimensional spatio-temporal data typically generate large latent Gaussian fields. Therefore, using traditional Bayesian approaches based on Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) can become computationally demanding and difficult to converge, particularly when spatially conditional autoregressive models are employed. Variational Bayes Inference (VBI), although fast, can underestimate posterior uncertainty and yield less accurate estimates for hierarchical spatial random models. INLA, on the other hand, provides a more deterministic, exact, and computationally efficient option for latent Gaussian models by leveraging the Gaussian Markov random field (GMRF) structure. This made it ideal for handling our data, which involves disease across districts and over time. However, its application to childhood pneumonia in Ghana remains rare. This represents a methodological and contextual gap, particularly given the spatial heterogeneity of pneumonia risk factors. To address this gap, the present study applies INLA to model spatiotemporal patterns in under-five pneumonia incidence across Ghana from 2018 to 2022 and to identify how the infection’s relative risk evolves over time and space. The goal is to generate evidence to inform targeted, data-driven interventions and support Ghana’s progress toward achieving national, regional, and global child health goals.

Materials and methods

Study Area

Ghana (Fig. 1) has a projected population of approximately 33.7 million people. Recent demographic data indicate that children under five years of age constitute a significant proportion of this population. Although the under-five mortality rate has declined steadily over the past decades, it remains a pressing public health concern, with current estimates at about 37 deaths per 1,000 live births. This demographic profile, marked by a large cohort of young children, highlights both the scale of vulnerability to childhood illnesses, such as pneumonia, and the critical importance of interventions aimed at reducing under-five morbidity and mortality [25, 26].

Childhood pneumonia continues to impose a considerable burden in Ghana. A recent meta-analysis reported a pooled prevalence of 17.8% among children under five, while overall pneumonia prevalence across all age groups was estimated at 22.0%. Notably, substantial regional variation in prevalence rates exists, with rates exceeding 30% in some areas, such as Greater Accra and the Northern Region, but considerably lower in others, including the Upper West Region [27]. Hospital-based and district-level analyses consistently confirm pneumonia as one of the leading causes of under-five morbidity and a major contributor to child mortality nationwide [28].

Regional disparities further complicate the national picture. Differences in pneumonia prevalence across regions reflect variations in environmental exposures (e.g., housing quality, indoor air pollution), health infrastructure, access to care, immunization coverage, and socioeconomic conditions [27, 29]. For instance, in the Greater Accra Region, an urbanized and relatively well-resourced area, one study documented more than 1,900 confirmed cases of under-five pneumonia between 2016 and 2020. Interestingly, incidence was higher in peri-urban communities compared to strict urban settings, underscoring the heterogeneity of risk even within the same region [28]. The interaction between undernutrition and respiratory infections adds another layer of complexity. Children experiencing wasting or underweight are significantly more susceptible to pneumonia, a relationship well established in the literature [30]. When compounded by environmental exposures such as household air pollution, overcrowding, reliance on biomass fuels, and gaps in immunization and care-seeking practices, these factors converge to create a diverse yet substantial burden of under-five pneumonia across Ghana.

Data and Data Sources

Pneumonia case data for children under five years of age were extracted from the District Health Information Management System-2 (DHIMS-2) in CSV format and integrated into spatial units (districts) using tabular joins for geospatial analysis. DHIMS-2, managed by the Ghana Health Service (GHS), is the national health information system designed to collect, report, analyze, and disseminate health service data across multiple levels: national, regional, district, and community. This study employed a retrospective observational design, relying on routinely collected health service data reported by health facilities into DHIMS-2. No sampling was conducted, as DHIMS-2 captures complete census data from all reporting health facilities nationwide. Each facility serves as a reporting unit and must summarize and submit all relevant health indicators, including pneumonia cases, derived directly from patient encounters and facility registries. The study population comprised all reported pneumonia cases among children under five years of age across Ghana, and the dataset included all pneumonia cases reported by all health facilities for the specified years. Consequently, the sample size reflects the total number of under-five pneumonia cases recorded nationally during the study period, ensuring comprehensive coverage without selection bias. The acquired pneumonia data were the total number of confirmed positive cases at the district level nationwide between 2018 and 2022.

The two covariates, the child dependency ratio (CDR) and the infant mortality rate (IMR), were obtained from the Socioeconomic Data and Applications Center at Columbia University (https://sedac.ciesin.columbia.edu/data/set/povmap-grdi-v1/data-download). The Child Dependency Ratio (CDR) represents the proportion of the population aged 0 to 14 relative to the working-age population aged 15 to 64. A higher CDR indicates greater reliance on the working-age population to support dependents. This ratio reflects a dimension in which elevated dependency ratios, typically associated with younger age distributions, indicate greater reliance on the working population and are often linked to higher levels of socioeconomic deprivation. Studies have found a significant association between childhood pneumonia burden and socioeconomic deprivation, such as low household income, illiteracy rates, and childhood pneumonia [31]. Similarly, socioeconomic deprivation, child well-being index (CWI), the number of children needing support from local authorities, and proximity to hospitals are also related to childhood pneumonia. As these variables were unavailable, we used CDR as a proxy for socioeconomic deprivation [32]. Also, higher IMR was associated with greater deprivation and was therefore included in our spatio-temporal model. These were acquired in raster format with a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds (~ 1 km). These raster datasets were projected to UTM Zone 30 N using bilinear resampling. National-level shapefiles containing district and regional boundaries were obtained from the Human Data Exchange (HDX: https://data.humdata.org/dataset/cod-ab-gha) and projected into a linear coordinate system.

Preprocessing

Acquired raster datasets (IMR & CDR) were both projected and resampled. Afterward, zonal statistics tools were used to estimate the means for each district. Furthermore, the discrepancy between distinct names in DHIS-2 data and the HDX boundary shapefiles was corrected to permit accurate spatial tabular joins. Since CDR and IMR were temporal averages (2010–2020), we treated them as temporally fixed variables in our model.

Analysis

Descriptive Analysis

We reported descriptive statistics using the median and interquartile range for count variables and the mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. We used Bartlett’s test of equality of variances to assess variation in U-5P cases across regions. The variance was statistically significant at p < 0.05.

The INLA Implementation

Using INLA, we fitted our U-5P cases to spatiotemporal models, with and without covariates. The INLA estimates the posterior marginals for the hyperparameters (including the precision parameters) and the latent fields by using the nested Laplace approximations. First, the joint posterior estimate of these hyperparameters is obtained using the Laplace approximation. This is achieved by leveraging the GMRF’s sparse precision matrix. Secondly, depending on the hyperparameter configuration, INLA computes the posterior marginals of the latent field using a combination of numerical integration and Gaussian approximation. This integrated system allows you to estimate accurate posteriors without running a long MCMC chain, making it faster and more efficient. A penalized complexity prior was specified as (1, 0.01), while default INLA priors were used for the remaining fixed effect and BYM2 hyperparameters. We used a grid-based optimization approach to numerically approximate the INLA integration strategy. While it can be computationally intensive, it provides high accuracy for the posterior marginals of likelihood and distributions. To ensure the limitations of INLA did not bias our results, we: (1) tested the GMF assumptions of the spatial and spatiotemporal random effects (supplementary material 1 Figs. 1, 2 and 3), (2) visualized the posterior distribution of the hyperparameters to ensure there was no evident skewness or multimodal distribution (supplementary material 1 Figs. 4, 5 and 6), and (3) checked for sensitivity using different penalized complexity priors. The strong prior (1, 0.1) and the weak prior (0, 0.001) were compared with our base prior (1, 0.01). The hyperparameter posterior densities were well behaved, and prior sensitivity analysis yielded near-identical results (ΔDIC < 0.3 and ΔWAIC < 0.6), confirming that our findings are robust to alternative complexity priors. Additionally, we compared a simpler Poisson GLM that lacked a spatial random effect with the full spatiotemporal models. The Poisson GLM performed poorly (–2 log-likelihood = 735,371.3) as compared with the INLA (-2 log-likelihood = 18,484.34). The consistency of the INLA results gave us confidence that INLA is better suited to our study.

Cluster Detection

To assess the strength of the global clustering of the RR, we utilized the Global Moran’s Index (Moran’s I), as outlined by [34, 35]. Positive values of Moran’s I indicate the clustering of similar values; negative values suggest the concentration of dissimilar values, while zero denotes no spatial autocorrelation [34–36]. Global Moran’s I extends Karl Pearson’s statistic into a spatial context [36, 37], expressed as follows: [8].

Where Wij​ represents the spatial weight, corresponding to the observation pair (i) and (j), autocorrelation [34–36]. These spatial weights are established using a neighborhood-based approach, in which values beyond a neighborhood threshold are set to zero. Our study used the Queen Contiguity (edge only) method to define neighborhood relationships. Spatial autocorrelation analysis typically yields five statistical indices: Moran’s I, expected index, variance, z-score, and p-value. Moran’s index ranges from 1 to -1, with positive values indicating clustering and negative values indicating dispersion of the phenomenon of interest [34, 37]. We conducted spatial autocorrelation analysis for all years (2018 to 2022) to assess the consistency of spatial autocorrelation patterns over time. Since Global Moran’s I does not account for local clustering, we also incorporated the local version, Local Indication of Spatial Association (LISA). The base mathematical formulation remains unchanged, except that the global effect is partitioned into local regions based on neighborhood relations. The LISA has five main divisions: Low-Low (low risk), High-High (high risk), Low-High (risk outlier), High-Low (risk outlier), and not significant. For LISA, we used 499 permutations to estimate the statistical significance of the local clustering.

Descriptive Statistics

Cluster Analysis of the Risk Distribution

The cluster for the RR was statistically significant in 2018 (z-score = 2.67; p = 0.007), 2019 (z-score = 3.40; p < 0.001), 2020 (z-score = 0.38; p = 0.699), 2021 (z-score = 4.179; p < 0.001), and 2022 (z-score = 6.030; p < 0.001). Our clustering results suggest that spatial clustering strength increases over time, with a rise in the magnitude of Z-scores (from 2.67 in 2018 to 6.03in 2022). In 2018, we identified three cluster outliers in which lower-risk districts bordered higher-risk zones: one in the Northeast region and one in the Volta/Oti region. Low-low risk zones were found in the Greater Accra region; high-low risk zones were seven, distributed sporadically across the Brong-Ahafo and Volta regions; whereas high-high clusters were found in the Volta and Oti regions (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Despite the substantive investment, pneumonia continues to present a ravaging impact on the health of millions of people around the world. In Sub-Saharan African nations, such as Ghana, children under the age of five are at an increased risk of pneumonia, with annual incidence exceeding 100/100,000 [19], making it the 3rd cause of death in Ghana among this demographic group [19]. Considering its annualized increment, existing intervention systems have been ineffective. This underscores the dire need to adopt innovative, temporally effective local surveillance systems to support adequate resource allocation and proactive planning for future scenarios. Therefore, to spatially model and temporally infer the risk of U-5P, our current study relied on a combination of rigorous spatial statistical approaches with explicit spatiotemporal effect-capture capabilities. Our study found that U-5P is generally spatially heterogeneous, with statistically significant clusters. The RR cluster was statistically significant across all years, with the spatial clustering strength increasing over time. For example, the magnitude of the Z-scores rose from 2.67 in 2018 to 6.30 in 2022.

Sociodemographic characteristics such as maternal education, region of residence, rural-urban residence, water and sanitation facilities, and household income are identified as risk factors for childhood pneumonia [10–12]. A child’s risk of contracting pneumonia is further increased by environmental factors, including living in crowded households, having parents who smoke, indoor air pollution from biomass burning, and outdoor air pollution from vehicular emissions [13–15]. The environmental and socioeconomic risk factors are inherently geographic. In Ghana, there is substantial inequality in income, access to health care, environmental risk factors, and sociodemographic factors, often evident along the South-North divide and between districts [38–40]. Consequently, the inherent geographic distribution of these risk factors likely influences pneumonia risk differently across different geographies. The increased risk clustering between 2018 and 2022 suggests that U-5P risk factors are becoming even more heterogeneous and spatially clustered. In other words, poorer districts may have an adverse spillover effect on neighboring districts, whereas more affluent districts that share neighborhoods may become even more prosperous. Such anticipated spatial polarization may make combating under-five pneumonia even more challenging. Adapting the existing surveillance system to a spatiotemporal one is urgently needed. Nevertheless, because studies that account for spatial effects in risk-factor identification are virtually nonexistent, it is difficult to determine which combination of factors drives the spatiotemporal polarization of U-5P risk. It is strongly recommended for research and policy.

However, our regional-level RR observations from 2018 to 2022 may strongly correlate with socioeconomic status and health systems infrastructure. For example, we found that in the Volta and Oti regions, the U-5P showed a clear upward trend over the period, indicating a growing vulnerability among children in these areas. A pronounced shift was also seen in the Ahafo region, where an initially lower-than-expected risk rose to levels well above expectations by 2022. On the contrary, regions such as Greater Accra and Ashanti, where business and administrative investment has been concentrated over the years, exhibited lower-than-expected risk throughout the period studied. Evidence from a study investigating the prevalence of under-five pneumonia in Tema Metropolis, similarly, showed a steady decline in under-five pneumonia cases, reinforcing the pattern of persistently lower risk in Greater Accra [40]. This suggests that although the under-five pneumonia risk is generally lower in Greater Accra, it will remain significantly lower. The same pattern has been observed for the Ashanti region. Of course, this is unsurprising, as there is clear inequality in the intensity and scale of socioeconomic development and health systems infrastructure between Ghana’s northern and southern flanks, with current investment concentrated disproportionately in the Greater Accra and Ashanti regions. We found that CDRs are associated with increased U-5P risk, and areas with higher IMR also show a corresponding rise in pneumonia vulnerability. A comprehensive list of social, economic, and environmental risk factors must be integrated into the spatial modeling framework to identify geographically adjusted risk factors for critical policing. This, however, will require improvements in surveillance and ongoing sociodemographic assessments nationwide.

Socioeconomic inequalities and poor access to health may predispose children to various kinds of diseases, of which pneumonia is a major one. In Northern Ghana, the comorbidity of childhood pneumonia and diarrhea has intensified, and both are strongly associated with socioeconomic deprivation and poor access to health services [41]. Previous studies have also shown that under-five mortality risk in the Upper West and Upper East regions is considerably higher than that of children in Greater Accra, highlighting persistent regional disparities in child health outcomes [39]. Other global studies have found a significant association between childhood pneumonia burden and socioeconomic deprivation, such as low household income, illiteracy rates, and childhood pneumonia [31]. Similarly, socioeconomic deprivation, the CWI score in the health domain, the number of children requiring local authority support, and proximity to hospitals are associated with childhood pneumonia. It is not surprising that most deprived regions in our study may continue to see an increased risk of under-five pneumonia over time. In contrast, relatively more prosperous areas may continue to see reductions in risk. Our findings have two main takeaways. First, there is spatial heterogeneity in risk levels of under-five pneumonia, with statistically significant clusters. This suggests increased spatial polarization, with prosperous regions likely to experience a lighter burden than the average, whereas poorer areas may continue to bear a heavier burden. Second, the spatial pattern may persist or even intensify with time. This underscores the need for a real-time surveillance system that integrates geographic and temporal data. We argue that geographically balanced socioeconomic and healthcare investments, combined with improved surveillance, have strong potential to reduce the disparities in U-5P risk observed across the country.

Limitation

Despite the rigorous methodology and comprehensive spatial analysis, we acknowledge some limitations. First, the disease cases were aggregated annually, preventing us from assessing inter-monthly variability and disease evolution. Secondly, analysis was only possible at the district level due to data limitations. Therefore, patterns at the sub-district level could not be analyzed. Thus, interpretation of results, including fixed and random effects, needs to be done cautiously. Thirdly, factors associated with changes in pneumonia patterns between 2018 and 2022 in Ghana were not analyzed in this study because obtaining spatiotemporally consistent predictors proved challenging. Therefore, we adjusted the pure spatial random effect using the child dependency ratio (CDR) and the infant mortality rate (IMR). These were selected to define the overall socioeconomic context under which the children lived. Since the data for CDR and IMR were available only as mean values for 2010–2020, they were treated as temporally invariant in the analysis, and their fixed effects were reported. Although we included a time indicator in the adjustment, we acknowledge the limitations. However, during cluster detection (using Moran’s I) of the spatial effect residuals, Moran’s I was not statistically significant (p = 0.7919), indicating that, despite these limitations, the model was, on average, well behaved.

Conclusion

As childhood pneumonia continues to increase worldwide, particularly in poorer geographies, there is a need to adapt the existing surveillance systems. Monitoring systems should be locationally and temporally meaningful for targetable resource allocation and proactive planning. In Ghana, health risks and mortality, especially among children, are mainly driven by socioeconomic and environmental disparities, which are inherently geographic. To address the complex spatiotemporal interactions driving under-five pneumonia in Ghana, we used a combination of spatiotemporal models. We found statistically significant spatial clustering in the risk of U-5P across all years, except for 2020. In particular, spatial risk will likely intensify over time, with high-burden areas likely to experience even greater impacts. In contrast, lower-risk areas may maintain their risk levels in the foreseeable future. This observed spatiotemporal polarization suggests that the current surveillance system should be adapted to address any explicit and implicit spatial disparities.
