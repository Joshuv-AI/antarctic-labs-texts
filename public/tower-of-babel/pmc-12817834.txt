Factors associated with death anxiety among nurses working in the earthquake region: a cross-sectional correlational study
Authors: Rukiye Turk Delibalta, Nevra Karaca Bicakci, Zehra Coktay, Kadriye Tuzcu, Yeliz Akkus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis is a descriptive-correlational study and aims to determine the factors associated with death anxiety of nurses in earthquake zones.Methods404 nurses working in 11 earthquake-affected provinces in Türkiye. Data were collected using “Questionnaire on Sociodemographic Characteristics and Earthquake Experience”, Spielberger State and Trait Anxiety Scale (STAI), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Spiritual Well-being Scale (SWBS), Thorson Powel’s Revised Death Anxiety Scale (RDAS).ResultsRespectively, the mean scale score of RDAS (62.13 ± 21.030) was above the intermediate level, BDI (17.74 ± 11.328) at moderate level, STAI-S (48.90 ± 10.305) and STAI-T (49.70 ± 8.664) at moderate level. It can be said that SWBS (116.26 ± 13.318) was at a high level. A relationship was found statistically significantly between RDAS and BDI, STAI-S, STAI-T, SWBS sub-dimensions and Transcendence sub-dimension. Model 8, which includes all the independent variables analyzed (gender, having religious belief, STAI-S, STAI-T, Harmony with Nature, and Anomie), explained 33.2% of the variance in death anxiety.ConclusionsBased on these results, developing intervention a program for nurses to cope with fear of death and anxiety was recommended, especially in crisis periods such as earthquakes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03745-2.Keywords: Anxiety, Depression, Disaster, Nurse, Spiritual-well-being

Background

Disaster is an unpredictable and often sudden event that exceeds local response capacity, requires national or international assistance, and causes major damage, destruction and human suffering [1]. Earthquakes have an important place among disasters, and their destructive effects are still continuing for human beings all over the world. In Türkiye, two major earthquakes occurred on 6 February 2023, the first with a magnitude of 7.7 and the second with a magnitude of 7.6, centred in Kahramanmaraş. Over 50 thousand people lost their lives due to these earthquakes that affected many cities [2].

Earthquakes pose a serious threat to the lives of people all over the world. It is known that psycho-social problems such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression and anxiety, sleep disorders, substance abuse and mental anguish, as well as physical health problems, occur in both earthquake victims and rescuers and the health care team providing care after the earthquake [2–4]. Due to the increased burden on the health system after the earthquake and its aftermath, health professionals and nurses are affected the most [5]. Studies have shown that nurses experience higher levels of anxiety and depression than physicians in crisis situations [6]. It was found that 6 years after the Sichuan 2008 earthquake, nurses still experienced many mental symptoms [7]. In a study conducted in Turkey after the earthquake, it was determined that nurses experienced severe anxiety and depression [2, 3, 8].

Spirituality is often characterised as a person’s views and behaviour that are influenced by their connection with God or a higher power in the world. Spirituality is particularly important when facing stressful situations such as life-threatening illnesses, death threats or moments of crisis and is one of the variables contributing to the resilience of survivors of [9] natural and technological disasters [10]. In a study conducted on 176 Emergency relief and disaster management students after the Kahramanmaraş earthquake in Turkey, spiritual gratitude and religious coping levels of the students were associated with psychological resilience coping one year after the earthquakes [11]. However, it is observed that studies on spirituality in both earthquake victims and rescuers, especially nurses, are limited and have started to be studied in recent years with the COVID-19 outbreak. Spirituality is a mechanism to counteract occupational stress and burnout syndrome in many professions, including health professionals [12]. In a study, it was concluded that spirituality is critical for nurses to overcome the numerous negativities they face while undertaking important roles during the pandemic [13]. Due to the changing world conditions, patient demands are increasing, exceeding the care management capacities of nurses and thus negatively affecting their productivity [14]. Therefore, spiritual well-being (SWB) can increase psychological and physical stress tolerance in individuals [15].

Nurses play a critical role in the prevention of adverse health events globally by helping patients and families with advanced guidelines [16, 17]. Furthermore, nurses’ encounters with mass death and dying individuals following traumatic life events such as natural disasters may increase death anxiety [18, 19]. Death anxiety plays a role in the development of many mental disorders [20]. In the literature review, no studies evaluating nurses’ fear of death after the earthquake experience were found. In a study conducted in Türkiye, fear of death was found to be high in midwifery students after the earthquake [21]. Moreover, studies have shown that nurses have high death anxiety [19, 22, 23].

In Türkiye, the earthquake that occurred on 6 February (the disaster of the century) affected an unusually large area. This situation made transportation conditions difficult, and many earthquake victims died under the rubble or before reaching the hospital, leading to an increase in casualties. The health personnel on duty at the hospital were also affected by the earthquake to a great extent. The survivors quickly started to help the earthquake victims. In this process, a state of emergency was declared in order to ensure that health and security services were not disrupted and to deploy personnel from other provinces on temporary duty, and health professionals started to work in the earthquake zone on temporary duty. The trauma has doubled for healthcare workers due to both earthquake trauma and continuing rescue operations. In addition, problems of shelter, nutrition and safety for all workers in the earthquake zone also emerged. One of the professional groups present at every stage during the earthquake is nurses, and studies on the psychosocial effects of earthquake in nurses are limited. Nursing profession plays a critical role in coping with the negative effects of disasters. In order for nurses to cope with major disasters such as earthquakes, they should have sufficient knowledge and equipment about care before, during and after the disaster as well as psychological stability [24]. In addition, since disasters are a non-stationary process that causes large-scale destruction with mass deaths, it is important for nurses to cope with death and fear of death in order to maintain the care of both those affected by the disaster and the survivors. Hence, in this study, the aim was to determine the factors associated with the death anxiety of nurses working in an earthquake zone.

In this study, the Biopsychosocial and Spiritual Model was used as a guiding framework to evaluate the stress responses of nurses working in the earthquake zone in relation to the biological, psychological, social, and spiritual conditions they experienced. The model was first developed by [25], who emphasized that health and illness cannot be explained solely through biological processes, but that psychological and social factors also play a significant role. Later [26], expanded the model by adding the spiritual dimension. Accordingly, it was emphasized that the spiritual aspects of the individual should also be taken into consideration. Although this model is commonly used in end-of-life care contexts, it offers a valuable framework for evaluating the experiences of nurses working in earthquake-affected regions. Nurses working in disaster zones may experience serious challenges, including loss of self-efficacy and resources, due to the impact of a life-threatening event that strains both physiological and psychological capacities [27]. This loss of resources may challenge nurses’ coping strategies depending on their sociodemographic characteristics, potentially leading to state and trait anxiety, as well as depression, and is therefore expected to increase death anxiety. Within this framework, components such as death anxiety and spirituality, which may influence one another, are evaluated together. A study conducted by [28] reported the biological, psychological, and social difficulties experienced by nurses working during the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes. In addition [29], addressed the psychosocial challenges faced by volunteer nurses in the earthquake-affected regions. Another study by [30] discussed the use of the Biopsychosocial and Spiritual Model as an approach to prevent post-traumatic stress disorder among first responder teams. Within this framework, each of the study variables was conceptually mapped to one of the model’s dimensions: the biological dimension, while not directly measured through physiological indicators, was indirectly represented by sociodemographic characteristics such as age which serve as proxies of biological vulnerability; the psychological dimension included state and trait anxiety (STAI), depressive symptoms (BDI), and death anxiety (RDAS); the social dimension was reflected in relational and contextual variables within the sociodemographic questionnaire (by coping experiences); and the spiritual dimension was operationalized through the Spiritual Well-being Scale (SWBS), which addresses religious and existential well-being, harmony with nature, and anomie. By organizing the study variables according to these dimensions, the model provides a holistic framework for understanding how biological, psychological, social, and spiritual factors interact to influence death anxiety in nurses working under disaster conditions.

When the above literature information is evaluated holistically, it is seen that fear of death in nurses is related to socio-demographic characteristics, earthquake experience, state and trait anxiety, depression, and spiritual well-being, and one or more of these variables are addressed together in the studies, but there is no study that addresses all variables together. It is important to reveal the fear of death and the factors affecting it in nurses working in the earthquake zone in order to prevent psychosocial problems such as post-traumatic stress that nurses may experience in the working environment after the earthquake. In addition, this study will lead to sustainable goals in terms of making plans to cope with the fear of death in the challenging process such as earthquake in the education processes of nurses and early diagnosis of this problem in the work environment. In this context, this study was conducted to eliminate this deficiency in the field and to examine the correlational power of socio-demographic characteristics, earthquake experience, state and trait anxiety, depression, and spiritual well-being in the fear of death of nurses with earthquake experience. Methods

Aim and research questions

This study was conducted to determine the factors associated with death anxiety of nurses working in an earthquake zone.

Design

This study was descriptive and correlational.

Participants

The sample of the study consisted of nurses working in 11 provinces declared as earthquake zones after the earthquake that occurred on 6 February 2023 in Türkiye. Since the number of nurses working in the provinces where the earthquake occurred is not known exactly. The power analysis was conducted using the G*Power software. In the a priori power analysis, the study by [19] was taken as a reference. Based on an effect size of 0.17, a significance level of 0.05, and a desired power of at least 95%, it was calculated that a minimum of 370 nurses would be required for the sample. Nevertheless, considering the possible losses, the study was completed between 04 May − 7 July 2023 with 404 nurses working in the earthquake zone either permanently or by assignment. A post hoc power analysis was conducted using the G*Power program. For the study completed with 404 participants, assuming a Type I error rate of 0.05 (α = 0.05) and an effect size of d = 0.50, the power for detecting a significant difference in test scores was calculated as Power (1 − β) = 1.0000000 (100%).

Data collection

The data collection forms were prepared with Google Docs programme and sent to the nurses working in the earthquake zone via e-mail and social media (Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp) with the link created online. The objective, content and inclusion criteria of the study were explained, and the nurses participated in the study after giving informed consent. During the data collection period, the study was conducted with nurses who were over the age of 18, who agreed to participate, and who were either actively working or assigned as volunteers in the earthquake-affected regions. Due to damage to institutional infrastructures and the lack of precise information regarding the number of nurses working or volunteering in the affected cities, data were collected using a purposeful sampling method. This approach was chosen to allow for the inclusion of participants directly involved in the disaster response and to support the interpretability of the findings, even in the absence of comprehensive national data.

Instruments

Data were collected using the “Questionnaire on Sociodemographic Characteristics and Earthquake Experience”, Spielberger State and Trait Anxiety Scale (STAI), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Spiritual Well-being Scale (SWBS), Thorson Powel’s Revised Death Anxiety Scale (RDAS).

Questionnaire on sociodemographic and some other characteristics

This form, which was prepared by the researchers after the literature review, consists of a total of 14 questions including questions such as gender, age, marital status, educational status, unit of employment, previous disaster training, previous disaster experience, being in one of the affected provinces during the earthquake, experiencing material loss or loss of life, working in the earthquake zone, defining the earthquake, what the earthquake makes one feel, coping strategies, acceptance of the earthquake, having any religious belief and whether or not they would like to receive disaster training.

The Spielberger State-Trait anxiety inventory

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) was developed by Spielberger et al. and adapted for Turkish by Öner and Le Compte [31]. STAI consists of 40 items and includes two separate scales. The “Trait Anxiety Scale” was developed as a means to identify how an individual felt in general. The reliability coefficient determined by the alpha correlation was between 0.83 and 0.87. The “State Anxiety Scale” is a scale that shows how an individual feels in a situation and in particular conditions. The reliability coefficient was found between 0.94 and 0.96. Oner & Lecompte (1983) defined the normal anxiety level between 36 and 42 [31]. Higher scores indicate higher levels of anxiety and the lower scores lower levels of anxiety. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha values were found to be 0.87 for state and 0.82 for trait, respectively. In this study, the mean STAI-S (48.90 ± 10.305) and STAI-T (49.70 ± 8.664) were found to be above the medium level.

The Beck depression inventory

BDI consists of 21 items, each corresponding to a specific category of symptoms and attitudes, scored on a range from 0 to 3. This inventory had been translated into Turkish by Hisli in 1989 [32]. Total scoring ranges from 0 to 63. The scores of all questions in the scale are totalled and the depression score is determined. The higher the total score, the more severe the depression. If the total score of BDI is between 0 and 9, it means “no depression”, between 10 and 16 means “mild depression”, between 17 and 29 means “moderate depression”, between 30 and 63 means “severe depression”. In this study, the mean level of depression was found to be moderate (17.74 ± 11.328). In this study, Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.92.

The Thorson-Powell’s Revised Death Anxiety Scale (RDAS)

The scale developed by Thorson and Powell (1992) was adapted into Turkish by Karaca & Yıldız [33]. Death Anxiety Scale is a 5-point Likert scale (0 = strongly disagree-4 = strongly agree) and consisting of 25 items and 4 sub-dimensions. Seventeen items (1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, 22 and 24) contain positive statements, while eight items (4, 10, 11, 13, 17, 21, 23 and 25) contain negative statements, which are reverse-scored. Each item score is summed, and then the total score is converted to a 0-100 scale. Higher scores indicating higher death anxiety. The results can be categorised into four levels of death anxiety according to the range of scores: very low (0–25), mild (25–50), moderate (51–75) and very high (76–100). In this study, the mean RDAS score (62.13 ± 21.030) was found to be above the medium level. Cronbach’s alpha value of RDAS is 0.84. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.90.

The Spiritual Well-being Scale (SWBS)

The scale was developed for adults in order to make sense of one’s life in line with one’s values. It has 3 sub-dimensions: transcendence, harmony with nature and anomie. The validity and reliability studies of SWBS show that it is a valid and reliable scale capable of measuring the spiritual well-being of adults. Cronbach’s Alpha values for the three sub-dimensions of the scale and the scale as a whole are: transcendence (0.95), harmony with nature (0.86), anomie (0.85) and total (0.88). In this study, on the other hand, the Cronbach’s Alpha values for the three sub-dimensions of the scale and the overall scale were found to be: transcendence (0.92), harmony with nature (0.89), anomie (0.82) and total (0.88). In this study, the mean SWBS score (116.26 ± 13.318) was found to be at a high level. At the same time, it was determined that the mean scores of the sub-dimensions of the scale; transcendence (64.66 ± 9.862), harmony with nature (31.89 ± 3.863), anomie (22.29 ± 6.636) were at a high level [34].

Data evaluation

The data were analyzed using SPSS for Windows (version 23.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). In this study, Skewness and Kurtosis values were examined, and it was determined that the study showed normal distribution [35]. While evaluating the study data, frequency (number, percentage) was given for categorical variables and descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation) was given for numerical variables. The relationship between two independent numerical variables was interpreted by Pearson Correlation Coefficient. One of the assumptions of hierarchical regression analysis is the absence of multicollinearity among the variables. To determine whether multicollinearity was present in the models, Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values were examined. A VIF value below 10 is considered acceptable [36]. In addition, to test for the presence of autocorrelation in the models, Durbin-Watson values were evaluated, with values between 1 and 3 indicating the absence of autocorrelation [37]. The VIF values in the models were found to be below 10, indicating no multicollinearity problem. Similarly, Durbin-Watson values were below 3, suggesting no autocorrelation issue. As all assumptions were met, an eight-step hierarchical regression analysis was conducted in the study. Statistical significance was checked at 0.05 level in the analyses.

Ethical considerations

Written permission was obtained from the authors to use the scales in this study. Approval was obtained from the University’s Non-Interventional Studies Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences. A form containing detailed information about the purpose and method of the study was sent online to the nurses who agreed to participate in the study and their consent was obtained. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Voluntary participants were included in the study and their personal identity information was kept confidential.

In the first step, the gender variable was entered (Model 1). Gender explained 2.8% of the variance in the dependent variable (death anxiety). It was found that gender was a significant correlate of death anxiety (β = –0.166, p = .001).

In the next step, the variable of having a religious belief was added (Model 2). The inclusion of this variable did not lead to a significant change in the explained variance (ΔR² = 0.001). Having a religious belief was not found to be a significant factor associated with death anxiety (β = –0.036, p = .471).

In the third step, the interaction effect (Gender × Religious Belief) was entered (Model 3). The addition of this interaction did not produce a significant change in the proportion of explained variance (ΔR² = 0.000). The Gender × Religious Belief interaction was not a significant correlate of death anxiety (β = −0.024, p = .698).

In the fourth step, the variable of depression was added (Model 4). The inclusion of depression led to a significant increase in the explained variance (ΔR² = 0.056). Moreover, depression was found to be a significant correlate of death anxiety (β = 0.239, p = .001).

In the fifth step, the variable of state anxiety was added (Model 5). The inclusion of state anxiety did not result in a significant change in the explained variance (ΔR² = 0.001). Furthermore, state anxiety was not found to be a significant factor associated with death anxiety (β = 0.038, p = .545).

In the sixth step, the variable of trait anxiety was added (Model 6). The inclusion of trait anxiety led to a significant increase in the explained variance (ΔR² = 0.135). Trait anxiety was found to be a significant correlate of death anxiety (β = 0.525, p = .000).

In the seventh step, the variable of transcendence was added (Model 7). The inclusion of transcendence did not result in a significant change in the explained variance (ΔR² = 0.006). Transcendence was not found to be a significant factor associated with death anxiety (β = 0.087, p = .071).

In the eighth step, the variable of harmony with nature was added (Model 8). The inclusion of this variable led to a significant increase in the explained variance (ΔR² = 0.021). Harmony with nature was found to be a significant factor associated with death anxiety (β = 0.189, p = .001).

In the final step, the variable of anomie was added (Model 9). The inclusion of anomie resulted in a significant increase in the explained variance (ΔR² = 0.083). Anomie was found to be a significant correlate of death anxiety (β = 0.368, p = .000). Additionally, in the final model, gender (β = –0.102), state anxiety (β = –0.151), trait anxiety (β = 0.285), harmony with nature (β = 0.145) and anomie (β = 0.348) were found to be significant factors. The independent variables in the final model explained 33.2% of the variance in death anxiety. Based on this result, it can be concluded that the final model is the strongest among the nine models. The analyses showed that Hypothesis 2 (trait anxiety is significantly related to death anxiety), Hypothesis 3 (depression is significantly related to death anxiety), and Hypothesis 4 (spiritual well-being is significantly related to death anxiety) were supported. Hypothesis 1 (state anxiety is significantly related to death anxiety) was only partially supported, as the relationship was significant but in the negative direction, contrary to our initial assumption.

Discussion

This study is conducted to determine the factors associated with death anxiety in nurses working in the earthquake zone in 11 provinces of Türkiye centred on Kahramanmaraş on 6 February; therefore, it is thought that the findings of this study will make important contributions to the literature.

In the first regression model, gender was found to be associated with 2.8% of the variance in death anxiety. A review of the literature reveals mixed findings: while some studies report that gender does not significantly influence death anxiety [19, 38, 39], others indicate that it does [22, 40]. These discrepancies may be related to women having less knowledge about post-earthquake interventions or to their maternal roles. The gender roles imposed by society may lead men and women to express fear and anxiety differently, thereby being associated with differences in death anxiety [39].

In the third step, depression, and in the fifth step, trait anxiety were found to be significantly associated with death anxiety. In the studies conducted during the COVID-19 process, it was found that anxiety and death anxiety were high in those working in the COVID-19 service [19, 41]. Coping with end-of-life situations is related to the ability to control stress and anxiety. Coping is the feelings and thoughts that will enable the person to reduce and tolerate everything that exceeds his/her limits with his/her actions [42]. However, the earthquake experience may be linked to a decrease in coping strategies and an increase in anxiety and depression in nurses. Especially nurses are expected to have a higher fear of death because they experience important jobs such as search and rescue, care, education, management and counselling in every disaster period including earthquake. The aftershocks that occurred during and after the earthquake, the rescue teams’ working under difficult conditions, and the fact that more than one city was affected may have contributed to higher levels of anxiety and thus death anxiety. In previous studies, it has been reported that personal characteristics and anxiety are related to self-report death anxiety [43, 44]. However, in Model 4, state anxiety alone was not significantly associated with death anxiety. This may be related to the fact that, for this group, the earthquake event has already passed and the process has moved into the rehabilitation phase.

In Model 7 and 8, the SWBS sub-dimensions of harmony with nature and anomie significantly associated with RDAS. Fisher [45] SWB’s personal domain is the relationship with the self, social domain is the relationship with society, environmental domain is the relationship with the environment and relation with transcendent. Other, in the transcendental domain [45]. Harmony with Nature determines the individual’s attachment and relationship with nature and other living things in nature, and the individual’s harmony with nature can make it easier to accept death as the end of life. Harmony with nature may be associated with lower death anxiety by fostering the belief that the earthquake is a natural event, that ultimately everything will return to nature, and that humans should live in harmony with nature in the future. Anomie, on the other hand, is dissatisfaction, discontent, which determines the negative thoughts of individuals regarding the meaning and purpose of life. Additionally, it also reflects a loss of meaning in life, which may intensify in disaster contexts, reducing life satisfaction and increasing death anxiety. Social support and spiritual resources may act as protective factors against these negative effects. In this study, RDAS increased as the SWB sub-dimension anomie score increased. Individuals with meaningful lives have fewer unfinished tasks and are exposed to fewer factors that reduce life satisfaction [46]. Therefore, anomie may be related to lower life satisfaction, resulting in increased anxiety or fear, which in turn associated with death anxiety. Interestingly, while state anxiety (STAI-S) initially showed a positive association with death anxiety, its coefficient became negative in the final model. This unexpected direction may be due to a suppression effect caused by the high overlap between state and trait anxiety (STAI-T), as STAI-T remained a strong positive predictor. Another possibility is that in disaster contexts, nurses’ elevated state anxiety reflects immediate situational demands, while death anxiety is more strongly linked to enduring trait anxiety and existential factors. These findings highlight the need for future research to disentangle the unique contributions of state and trait anxiety in post-disaster populations.

Awareness and fear of death is not a foreign concept for humanity. It has long been argued that there is a relationship between religion and the fear of death, and even that individuals turn to religion due to the fear of death [47, 48]. Traditionally, individuals have used religious knowledge to help reduce anxiety arising from the reality of death and to understand the unpredictability and certainty of death. Furthermore, the world’s major belief systems (Islam, Judaism and Christianity) promise individuals true immortality [49]. The majority of Turkish society is Muslim, and in Islam, the Holy Qur’an states that there is no escape from death: “Every living creature will taste death”. Previous studies have reported conflicting results, with some indicating that increased religious belief is associated with decreased death anxiety [39], while others report an increase in death anxiety [50]. Although the majority of the Turkish population is Muslim, this study did not assess adherence to religious rituals. The incomplete practice of religious rituals may be associated with heightened death anxiety among believers due to fears of indebtedness to the afterlife, post-mortem judgment, divine wrath, or insufficient religious coping [51]. In addition, religion can be a reinforcing and healing force associated with overcoming or minimising individual and social difficulties, problems and negativities that arise in difficult periods such as epidemics and earthquakes [52]. In a study conducted with individuals in the community after the 6 February earthquakes in Turkey, post-earthquake stress was found to be lower in individuals with high SWB [53].

In the final model, gender (β = –0.102), having a religious belief (β = –0.090), state anxiety (β = –0.151), trait anxiety (β = 0.285), and harmony with nature (β = 0.145) were found to be significant factors. The independent variables in this model explained 33.2% of the variance in death anxiety. To our knowledge, these variables have not been previously studied as correlates of death anxiety following earthquakes or major disasters in Turkey or globally. Additionally, although state anxiety was not significant in Model 4, it was found to negatively correlate death anxiety in the final model when combined with other variables. A study by [19]. also found a relationship between death anxiety and anxiety among nurses during the COVID-19 pandemic. As mentioned above, although there may be residual effects, this finding may be related to the conclusion of the major earthquake, the beginning of rehabilitation processes, and prior professional training received by nurses. In the current post-earthquake study, 66.8% of death anxiety was explained by other variables. This highlights the need for further research on post-disaster processes, especially among nurses who play critical roles in the healthcare system. In this study, the mean DA score was 62.13 ± 21.03 and was determined at a moderate level. In studies conducted before and during COVID-19, the mean RDAS score of nurses was 51.17 ± 8.18–57.33 ± 16.20 and was found to be at a moderate level [19, 41, 54]. Again, in studies conducted using various scales during the COVID-19 pandemic, moderate [23, 55] or high level of death anxiety was detected [22, 56]. Despite the mean RDAS score was higher than other studies in this study, a moderate level of fear of death was determined. This result may be explained by its association with the occurrence of earthquakes after a major epidemic such as COVID-19 and the empowerment of nurses to cope with the fear of death in this process, the majority of them having religious beliefs and half of them having previous earthquake experience.

In this study, STAI-S (48.90 ± 10.305) and STAI-T (49.70 ± 8.664) were found to be at high level. The literature shows that the studies determining the level of depression and anxiety are often conducted following a certain period of time after the earthquake experience. In a study conducted to determine the cognitive and mental states of nurses 1 year after the 2011 Great Japan Earthquake using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale, it was determined that 11.9% of nurses developed high levels of anxiety and depression [57]. When compared with these studies, it is seen that the STAI-T score of nurses working in the earthquake zone is higher. Similarly, in studies conducted after the 6 February earthquake, anxiety was found to be quite high in nurses working in the region [2, 4, 29]. Nonetheless, it is thought that STAI-T is higher in nurses since the earthquake affected 11 provinces, health institutions turned into rubble, and many health professionals and people lost their lives. In a study conducted 14 months after the Great Japan Earthquake, the prevalence of depression was found to be higher in healthcare workers than in firefighters and municipal workers [58].

In this study, the mean BDI score was 17.74 ± 11.328 and it can be said that there was moderate depression. In a study conducted with Turkish nurses after the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes reported that the nurses who participated the study had severe depression levels [4]. Depression in nurses caring for war victims is 13.7% [59]. In the literature review, it was observed that studies evaluating depression after earthquake experience were limited. In another study, it was stated that being a nurse is a risk factor for PTSD [3].

In a study conducted with isolation nurses in COVID-19, it was found that 58.9% of nurses experienced mild depression [60]. During the COVID-19 period, 19.6% of psychiatric nurses experienced mild to severe depression [61]. In this study, it was observed that the BDI score of nurses was higher when compared with other studies. Similar to anxiety, this result may be related to the fact that nurses in Türkiye experienced the earthquake disaster after the prolonged COVID-19 pandemic, traumatised by the losses experienced, and uncertainty about the future.

In this study, the mean SWBS score was found to be 116.26 ± 13.318. Reviewing the literature, it was found that the mean SWBS score varied between 84.3 ± 13.1-120.57 ± 11.43 in the pre-COVID-19 period in nurses [59, 62, 63]. When compared with the studies mentioned above, the SWB score was found to be high but in a similar range. Spirituality is known to be important for people in traumatic situations, and this can also be explained by the increase in religious beliefs in people after disasters [64] and SWB help the individual develop coping methods. Thus, the fact that nurses experiencing earthquake experience witness many moments of death and its aftermath may be associated with questioning their own existence and higher SWB.

Study limitations

The most important limitation of this study is that there was no control or comparison group and anxiety, depression and fear of death were not evaluated before the study or before the earthquakes. Due to the cross-sectional design, the identified associations should be interpreted as correlational and not as evidence of causality. Future studies should employ more representative sampling methods, include pre-disaster baseline measures, and incorporate comparison groups to strengthen causal interpretation and external validity. In addition, this study was collected online due to the difficult conditions in the earthquake region, and the number of nurses who continued to work in the region after the earthquake could not be reached. Nurses with limited internet access or connectivity issues were particularly difficult to reach. In addition, the use of online self-report questionnaires may have introduced response bias, as participants might have provided socially desirable answers or underreported their psychological distress. The predominance of Muslim nurses participating in this study limits the generalizability of the findings to non-Muslim populations and necessitates cautious interpretation of the results in other cultural contexts. Although death anxiety was assessed based on the nurses’ self-reports, the use of valid and reliable measurement instruments enhanced the reliability of the study. Although the hierarchical regression explained 33.2% of the variance, it is likely that other factors such as social support and coping strategies also contribute to death anxiety; future studies should incorporate these variables for a more comprehensive understanding. Additionally, the study did not account for potential confounding variables such as previous trauma exposure, personal losses related to the earthquake, or pre-existing mental health conditions, which may have influenced the psychological outcomes observed. Future studies could benefit from employing longitudinal or qualitative designs to obtain more effective results. Additionally, assessing social support and coping mechanisms in subsequent research may provide valuable insights into predicting death anxiety. Nurses were provided with information and contact details for psychological support services at the end of the survey; however, no formal debriefing session was conducted, which may be considered a limitation and should be addressed in future research.

Conclusion

It was determined that the nurses participating in this study experienced moderate depression, STAI-S and STAI-T anxiety and death anxiety at levels above the moderate. It was determined that the hierarchical regression model established to examine the associations of STAI-S and STAI-T, SWBS sub-dimensions of harmony with nature and anomie, gender and having a religious belief on RDAS was significant. In the final model, these variables, namely gender, depression, STAI-T, harmony with nature, and anomie, were together explained 33.2% of the variance in death anxiety. According to these results, it is recommended that intervention programs such as mindfulness-based stress reduction, spiritual counseling, and psychological support services be developed and implemented to help nurses cope with depression, anxiety and death anxiety, particularly in disaster-affected settings. At the same time, since there is a traumatic situation in the earthquake zone, different mental problems that may arise in the long term should be questioned and determined in the later period. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs and qualitative approaches to provide deeper insights into the predictors and dynamics of death anxiety among nurses.
