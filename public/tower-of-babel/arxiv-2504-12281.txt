A Near-Optimal Kernel for a Coloring Problem
Ishay Haviv, Dror Rabinovich

Abstract
For a fixed integer q q , the q ​ -Coloring q\textsc{-Coloring} problem asks to decide if a given graph has a vertex coloring with q q colors such that no two adjacent vertices receive the same color. In a series of papers, it has been shown that for every q ≥ 3 q\geq 3 , the q ​ -Coloring q\textsc{-Coloring} problem parameterized by the vertex cover number k k admits a kernel of bit-size O ~ ​ ( k q − 1 ) \widetilde{O}(k^{q-1}) , but admits no kernel of bit-size O ⁡ ( k q − 1 − ε ) O(k^{q-1-\varepsilon}) for ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 unless 𝖭𝖯 ⊆ 𝖼𝗈𝖭𝖯 / 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 \mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{coNP/poly} (Jansen and Kratsch, 2013; Jansen and Pieterse, 2019). In 2020, Schalken proposed the question of the kernelizability of the q ​ -Coloring q\textsc{-Coloring} problem parameterized by the number k k of vertices whose removal results in a disjoint union of edges and isolated vertices. He proved that for every q ≥ 3 q\geq 3 , the problem admits a kernel of bit-size O ~ ​ ( k 2 ​ q − 2 ) \widetilde{O}(k^{2q-2}) , but admits no kernel of bit-size O ⁡ ( k 2 ​ q − 3 − ε ) O(k^{2q-3-\varepsilon}) for ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 unless 𝖭𝖯 ⊆ 𝖼𝗈𝖭𝖯 / 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 \mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{coNP/poly} . 1
Introduction

Graph coloring is a fundamental concept in graph theory that has garnered significant attention from a computational perspective.
In particular, coloring problems have been thoroughly investigated within the realm of parameterized complexity, with respect to a range of parameterizations that capture diverse aspects of the instances (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
3
,
4
]
).
For a fixed integer
q
, let
q\textsc{-Coloring}
denote the problem that given an input graph
G
asks to decide if
G
is
q
-colorable, that is, if there exists a vertex coloring of
G
with
q
colors such that no two adjacent vertices receive the same color. Such a coloring is called a proper
q
-coloring of
G
.
It is well known that the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem is solvable in polynomial time for
q\in\{1,2\}
and is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete for every
q\geq 3
.
The present paper focuses on structural parameterizations of the problem that are characterized by the distance of the input graph from a prescribed graph family
{\cal G}
.
Specifically, borrowing a terminology of Cai
[
1
]
, we consider the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem on
{\cal G}+k\mathrm{v}
graphs, defined as follows.

Input: A graph
G=(V,E)
and a set
X\subseteq V
such that
G\setminus X\in{\cal G}
.

Question: Is
G

q
-colorable?

Parameter: The size
k=|X|
of the set
X
.

Note that
G\setminus X
stands for the graph obtained from
G
by removing the vertices of
X
.

A primary theme in parameterized complexity is kernelization, also known as data reduction, where the objective is to efficiently transform a given instance of a parameterized problem into an equivalent instance, whose size is bounded in terms of the involved parameter (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
). Formally, a kernel for a parameterized problem
Q\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
is an algorithm that given an instance
(x,k)
, where
k
is the parameter, runs in time polynomial in
|x|+k
and returns an instance
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})
, such that
(x,k)\in Q
if and only if
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in Q
, with
|x^{\prime}|+k^{\prime}\leq f(k)
for some computable function
f
. The function
f
is referred to as the bit-size of the kernel.

The kernelizability of the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problems on
{\cal G}+k\mathrm{v}
graphs was systematically studied in 2013 by Jansen and Kratsch
[
8
]
, who provided a hierarchical classification of graph families
{\cal G}
according to whether the problems admit a kernel and whether the kernel is of polynomial size (see also
[
7
, Chapter 7]
). In particular, for the family
Empty
of edgeless graphs, which corresponds to the well-studied parameterization by the vertex cover number, they proved that for every
q\geq 3
, the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem on
\textsc{Empty}+k\mathrm{v}
graphs admits a kernel with
O(k^{q})
vertices and bit-size
O(k^{q})
. This result was improved in 2019 by Jansen and Pieterse
[
9
]
, who proved that the problem admits a kernel with
O(k^{q-1})
vertices and bit-size
O(k^{q-1}\cdot\log k)
. On the other hand, it was shown in
[
8
,
9
]
that for every integer
q\geq 3
and for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is no kernel for the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem on
\textsc{Empty}+k\mathrm{v}
graphs with bit-size
O(k^{q-1-\varepsilon})
unless
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{coNP/poly}
. Since this containment would imply the unlikely collapse of the polynomial-time hierarchy
[
13
]
, these results resolve the kernelization complexity of the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem on
\textsc{Empty}+k\mathrm{v}
graphs up to a
k^{o(1)}
multiplicative term for all integers
q\geq 3
.

In 2020, Schalken
[
12
]
investigated the kernelizability of the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problems with respect to parameters that are bounded above by the vertex cover number.
Specifically, he considered the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problems on
\textsc{Independent Edge}+k\mathrm{v}
graphs, where
Independent Edge
denotes the family of all graphs that form a disjoint union of edges and isolated vertices or, equivalently, the graphs whose maximum degree does not exceed
1
.
It was proved in
[
12
]
that for every integer
q\geq 3
, the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem on
\textsc{Independent Edge}+k\mathrm{v}
graphs admits a kernel with
O(k^{2q-2})
vertices and bit-size
O(k^{2q-2})
, whereas for any
\varepsilon>0
, it admits no kernel of bit-size
O(k^{2q-3-\varepsilon})
unless
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{coNP/poly}
. While these results leave a multiplicative gap of roughly
k
between the upper and lower bounds, it was further shown in
[
12
]
that for
q\in\{3,4\}
, the problem admits a kernel with
O(k^{2q-3})
vertices and bit-size
O(k^{2q-3}\cdot\log k)
. The question of whether such a kernel exists for integers
q\geq 5
was left open in
[
12
]
. The present paper resolves this question affirmatively, thereby providing a near-optimal kernel for the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem on
\textsc{Independent Edge}+k\mathrm{v}
graphs for all integers
q\geq 3
, as stated below.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every integer
q\geq 3
, the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem on
\textsc{Independent Edge}+k\mathrm{v}
graphs admits a kernel with
O(k^{2q-3})
vertices and bit-size
O(k^{2q-3}\cdot\log k)
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
employs a powerful sparsification technique that was introduced by Jansen and Pieterse
[
10
]
for kernels of constraint satisfaction problems and found various applications since (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
6
,
9
,
11
]
).
In a nutshell, their method involves expressing combinatorial constraints associated with a given instance as polynomials equated to zero and reducing the number of constraints by considering only a subset of these polynomials, which linearly spans all the others. The smaller the degree of the polynomials used, the smaller the resulting kernel size is.
Based on this machinery, it was shown in
[
12
]
that in order to establish a near-optimal kernel for the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem on
\textsc{Independent Edge}+k\mathrm{v}
graphs, it suffices to construct a polynomial of degree
2q-3
that expresses some specified constraints on a collection of colors, encoded as vectors of
\mathbb{F}^{q}
for a field
\mathbb{F}
. The polynomials needed for
q\in\{3,4\}
were found in
[
12
]
through a computer search. In the present work, we provide a rigorous construction of the desired polynomials for all integers
q\geq 3
, allowing us to establish Theorem
1.1
.

We conclude by noting that the main idea behind our application of the sparsification technique of
[
10
]
lies in encoding colors as vectors that satisfy certain linear-algebraic constraints, enabling us to capture combinatorial coloring properties via low-degree polynomials. It would be interesting to explore whether this approach can be applied more broadly. Possible directions include the development of improved kernels for the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem under alternative parameterizations, or for other problems of a similar nature.

The remainder of this short paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we introduce the notion of a
q
-palette over a field
\mathbb{F}
as a set of
q
vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{q}
that exhibit some specified properties and are used to encode colors. After determining the integers
q
and fields
\mathbb{F}
for which a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}
exists, we use this object to construct low-degree polynomials that meet the constraints identified in
[
12
]
.
Then, in Section
3
, we combine these polynomials with the approach of
[
12
]
to confirm Theorem
1.1
.

2
Color Palettes and Low-degree Polynomials

In this section, we construct the low-degree polynomials needed for our kernelization result.
The polynomials are defined on variables representing colors from a prescribed set, where the colors are encoded as vectors over some field
\mathbb{F}
.
We refer to such a set of vectors as a palette over
\mathbb{F}
and require its members to satisfy the conditions outlined in the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

For an integer
q\geq 2
and a field
\mathbb{F}
, a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}
is a set
C=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{q}\}
of
q
vectors of
\mathbb{F}^{q}
satisfying that

1.

for each
i\in[q]
, the first entry of
c_{i}
is
1
,

2.

C
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}
, and

3.

for every set
S\subseteq[q]
of size
|S|=q-1
, the vectors of
\mathbb{F}^{q-1}
obtained from the vectors
c_{i}
with
i\in S
by omitting their last entry are linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}
.

For a
q
-palette
C
over
\mathbb{F}
and for an integer
m
, a matrix
M=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times m}
is said to be
C
-colored if every column of
M
is a member of
C
, that is,
a_{i}\in C
for all
i\in[m]
.

The following lemma offers sufficient and necessary conditions for the existence of a
q
-palette over a field
\mathbb{F}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For every integer
q\geq 2
and for every field
\mathbb{F}
, there exists a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}
if and only if
q
is even or
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 3
.

Let
q\geq 2
be an integer and
\mathbb{F}
be a field.
We first show that if
q
is even or
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 3
, then there exists a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}
.
Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
be a field element such that
\alpha\notin\{0,4-q\}
, where
4
stands for the sum of the identity element of
\mathbb{F}
with itself four times. Such an element clearly exists when
|\mathbb{F}|\geq 3
. Such an element also exists when
\mathbb{F}
is the binary field, provided that
q
is even (because in this case
4-q=0
).

For each
i\in[q]
, let
e_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{q}
denote the vector with
1
in the
i
th entry and
0
elsewhere.
We define
q
vectors
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{q}
in
\mathbb{F}^{q}
as follows:
c_{1}=e_{1}
,
c_{i}=e_{1}+e_{i}
for each
2\leq i\leq q-1
, and
c_{q}=e_{1}+\alpha\cdot e_{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{q}{e_{i}}
. Namely,

(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{q})=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots&1&1\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0&\alpha\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1&1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}_{q\times q}.

We claim that the set
C=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{q}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{q}
forms a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}
.
To this end, let us verify the three requirements of Definition
2.1
.
Firstly, by definition, the first entry of
c_{i}
is
1
for each
i\in[q]
, as required for Item
1
.
Secondly, the
q\times q
matrix
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{q})
is an upper triangular matrix with nonzero elements on the diagonal, hence
C
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}
, as required for Item
2
.
Thirdly, for each
i\in[q]
, let
d_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{q-1}
denote the vector obtained from
c_{i}
by omitting its last entry. We shall prove that any
q-1
of the vectors
d_{1},\ldots,d_{q}
are linearly independent. Consider the
(q-1)\times(q-1)
matrix
D=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{q-1})
.
The columns of
D
are linearly independent, because it is an upper triangular matrix with ones on the diagonal.
It thus remains to show that
d_{q}
cannot be represented as a linear combination of fewer than
q-1
of the columns of
D
.
Indeed, it is not difficult to verify that the only solution of the linear system
D\cdot x=d_{q}
for
x\in\mathbb{F}^{q-1}
is
x=(4-q-\alpha,\alpha,1,\ldots,1)^{t}
.
By our choice of
\alpha
, all the entries of
x
are nonzero, so we are done.

We next show that the stated conditions are necessary for the existence of a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}
, namely, for every odd integer
q\geq 3
, there is no
q
-palette over the binary field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.
Suppose for contradiction that there exists a
q
-palette
C=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{q}\}
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
for an odd
q\geq 3
.
For each
i\in[q]
, let
d_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{q-1}
denote the vector obtained from
c_{i}
by omitting its last entry.
Since
C
is a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, by Item
3
of Definition
2.1
, the vectors
d_{1},\ldots,d_{q-1}
are linearly independent. It follows that they span the entire vector space
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{q-1}
, so in particular, the vector
d_{q}
forms a linear combination of them. We claim that in this linear combination, at least one of the coefficients must be zero. Indeed, by Item
1
of Definition
2.1
, the first entry of each of the vectors
d_{1},\ldots,d_{q-1}
is
1
. Hence, if all the coefficients are
1
, then the first entry of the linear combination is
q-1
, which is
0
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
for an odd
q
, whereas the first entry of
d_{q}
is
1
. We obtain that
d_{q}
is a linear combination of fewer than
q-1
of the vectors
d_{1},\ldots,d_{q-1}
, hence
q-1
of the vectors
d_{1},\ldots,d_{q}
are linearly dependent, in contradiction to Item
3
of Definition
2.1
. This completes the proof.

Remark 2.3
.

We provide here another construction of a
q
-palette over a field
\mathbb{F}
, applicable when
|\mathbb{F}|\geq q
.
Under this assumption, there exist
q
distinct field elements
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{q}\in\mathbb{F}
.
For each
i\in[q]
, consider the column vector
c_{i}=(1,\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i}^{2},\ldots,\alpha_{i}^{q-1})^{t}\in\mathbb{F}^{q}
.
We claim that the set
C=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{q}\}
forms a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}
.
First, by definition, the first entry of
c_{i}
is
1
for each
i\in[q]
, as required for Item
1
of Definition
2.1
.
Next, the vectors of the set
C
are distinct columns of a
q\times q
Vandermonde matrix, hence
C
is linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}
, as required for Item
2
.
Finally, for every set
S\subseteq[q]
of size
|S|=q-1
, omitting the last entry of the vectors
c_{i}
with
i\in S
results in distinct columns of a
(q-1)\times(q-1)
Vandermonde matrix, which are linearly independent over
\mathbb{F}
, as required for Item
3
.

We next provide constructions of low-degree polynomials that, when evaluated on the entries of a
C
-colored matrix
M
for a given palette
C
, determine whether the columns of
M
fulfill certain prescribed conditions. To this end, we will repeatedly use, for an integer
q
and a field
\mathbb{F}
, the determinant polynomial
\det:\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
. This polynomial, which is defined on
q^{2}
variables corresponding to the entries of a
q\times q
matrix, maps any matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}
to its determinant
\det(M)
. Note that the polynomial
\det
is a linear combination of
q!
monomials, with each monomial being a product of
q
variables, one taken from each row of the matrix. As is well known, a matrix
M\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}
satisfies
\det(M)=0
if and only if its columns are linearly dependent over
\mathbb{F}
.

We start by constructing a polynomial of degree
q-1
that determines whether
q
given vectors of a
q
-palette include two identical vectors.

Lemma 2.4
.

For every integer
q\geq 2
and for every
q
-palette
C
over a field
\mathbb{F}
, there exists a polynomial
f:\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
of degree
q-1
, defined on
q^{2}
variables corresponding to the entries of a
q\times q
matrix, such that for every
C
-colored matrix
M=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}
, it holds that
f(M)=0
if and only if there exist distinct
i,j\in[q]
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}
.

For an integer
q\geq 2
and a field
\mathbb{F}
, let
C
be a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}
, and consider the determinant polynomial
\det:\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
defined on
q\times q
matrices over
\mathbb{F}
.
Let
f:\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
be the polynomial obtained from
\det
by substituting
1
for the variables that correspond to the first row of the matrix (see Example
2.5
).
Recall that each monomial of
\det
is a product of
q
variables, one taken from each row of the matrix. This implies that the degree of
f
is
q-1
.

Now, let
M=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}
be a
C
-colored matrix.
The columns of
M
are members of
C
, hence by Item
1
of Definition
2.1
, the first entry of each of them is
1
. This implies that
f(M)=\det(M)
, hence
f(M)=0
if and only if the columns of
M
are linearly dependent. By Item
2
of Definition
2.1
, the
q
vectors of
C
are linearly independent. It thus follows that the columns of
M
are linearly dependent if and only if two of them are identical.
We conclude that
f(M)=0
if and only if there exist distinct
i,j\in[q]
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}
, as desired.

Example 2.5
.

For
q=4
, the proof of Lemma
2.4
produces the polynomial
f:\mathbb{F}^{4\times 4}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
defined by

f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&1\\
(x_{1})_{2}&(x_{2})_{2}&(x_{3})_{2}&(x_{4})_{2}\\
(x_{1})_{3}&(x_{2})_{3}&(x_{3})_{3}&(x_{4})_{3}\\
(x_{1})_{4}&(x_{2})_{4}&(x_{3})_{4}&(x_{4})_{4}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
(x_{i})_{j}
stands for the
j
th entry of the variable vector
x_{i}
.
Note that the degree of
f
is
3
.

We proceed by constructing a polynomial of degree
q-2
that determines whether
q-1
given vectors of a
q
-palette include two identical vectors.

Lemma 2.6
.

For every integer
q\geq 2
and for every
q
-palette
C
over a field
\mathbb{F}
, there exists a polynomial
g:\mathbb{F}^{q\times(q-1)}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
of degree
q-2
, defined on
q\cdot(q-1)
variables corresponding to the entries of a
q\times(q-1)
matrix, such that for every
C
-colored matrix
M=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q-1})\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times(q-1)}
, it holds that
g(M)=0
if and only if there exist distinct
i,j\in[q-1]
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}
.

For an integer
q\geq 2
and a field
\mathbb{F}
, let
C
be a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}
, and consider the determinant polynomial
\det:\mathbb{F}^{(q-1)\times(q-1)}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
defined on
(q-1)\times(q-1)
matrices over
\mathbb{F}
. Let
g:\mathbb{F}^{q\times(q-1)}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
be the polynomial obtained by applying
\det
to the first
q-1
rows of the matrix and by substituting
1
for the variables that correspond to its first row (see Example
2.7
).
Since each monomial of
\det
is a product of
q-1
variables, one taken from each row, it follows that the degree of
g
is
q-2
.

Now, let
M=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q-1})\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times(q-1)}
be a
C
-colored matrix, and let
M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{(q-1)\times(q-1)}
denote the restriction of
M
to its first
q-1
rows.
The columns of
M
are members of
C
, hence by Item
1
of Definition
2.1
, the first entry of each of them is
1
.
This implies that
g(M)=0
if and only if
\det(M^{\prime})=0
.
By Item
3
of Definition
2.1
, every
q-1
vectors of
C
, restricted to their first
q-1
entries, are linearly independent. It thus follows that
\det(M^{\prime})=0
if and only if two of the columns of
M
are identical.
We conclude that
g(M)=0
if and only if there exist distinct
i,j\in[q-1]
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}
, as desired.

Example 2.7
.

For
q=4
, the proof of Lemma
2.6
produces the polynomial
g:\mathbb{F}^{4\times 3}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
defined by

g(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\
(x_{1})_{2}&(x_{2})_{2}&(x_{3})_{2}\\
(x_{1})_{3}&(x_{2})_{3}&(x_{3})_{3}\\

Note that the degree of

is

.

With Lemmas
2.4
and
2.6
in place, we turn to the construction of a polynomial of degree
2q-3
that, given two sequences of
q-1
vectors from a
q
-palette, determines whether the first sequence contains two identical vectors or the two sequences consist of distinct vectors.

Lemma 2.8
.

For every integer
q\geq 2
and for every
q
-palette
C
over a field
\mathbb{F}
, there exists a polynomial
h:\mathbb{F}^{q\times(2q-2)}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
of degree
2q-3
, defined on
q\cdot(2q-2)
variables corresponding to the entries of a
q\times(2q-2)
matrix, such that for every
C
-colored matrix
M=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q-1},b_{1},\ldots,b_{q-1})\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times(2q-2)}
, it holds that
h(M)=0
if and only if

1.

there exist distinct
i,j\in[q-1]
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}
, or

2.

\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{q-1}\}\neq\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{q-1}\}

For an integer

and a field

, let

be a
q
-palette over
\mathbb{F}
.
By Lemma

, there exists a polynomial

of degree

-colored matrix

, it holds that

if and only if there exist distinct

such that

.
By Lemma

, there exists a polynomial

of degree

, such that for every
C
-colored matrix
M=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q-1})\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times(q-1)}
, it holds that

if and only if there exist distinct
i,j\in[q-1]
such that
a_{i}=a_{j}
.
Let
c=\sum_{i=1}^{q}{c_{i}}
denote the sum of the vectors of

, and define a polynomial

by

h(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q-1},y_{1},\ldots,y_{q-1})=g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{q-1})\cdot f(y_{1},\ldots,y_{q-1},c-\sum_{i=1}^{q-1}{x_{i}})

(see Example

).
The above substitution of linear terms into the polynomial

clearly does not increase its degree. In fact, it follows from the definition of

, as evidenced by expanding the determinant given in

.

We now prove that the polynomial

satisfies the assertion of the lemma.
Consider a
C
-colored matrix
M=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q-1},b_{1},\ldots,b_{q-1})\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times(2q-2)}

.
We shall prove that
h(M)=0
if and only if either there exist distinct

such that

, or it holds that
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{q-1}\}\neq\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{q-1}\}
.

Suppose first that

. By the definition of

, it follows that
g(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q-1})=0
or

. If the former condition holds, then by Lemma
2.6
, there exist distinct

such that
a_{i}=a_{j}
, and we are done.
Otherwise, using Lemma
2.6
again, the vectors

are pairwise distinct, hence

is the unique vector of
C
that does not lie in
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{q-1}\}
, and it holds that

.
By Lemma

, it follows that two of the vectors

are equal. This implies that either

for some distinct

, or for some

and thus

. In both cases, it holds that
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{q-1}\}\neq\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{q-1}\}

For the converse direction, suppose that

, which by the definition of

and

.
The first inequality implies, using Lemma

, that the vectors

are pairwise distinct, and thus,

, that the vectors

This implies that

Proof of Theorem

In this section, we establish Theorem
1.1
by combining the polynomials constructed in the previous section with the approach of
[

]
.
We provide the full proof here primarily for completeness.
Let us begin with the following lemma, which resembles a statement in
[
12
]
.
Here, for a graph
G=(V,E)
, let

denote the set of neighbors of a vertex

in
G
, and let

denote the subgraph of

induced by a set

.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
12
, Lemma 7]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, and let
X\subseteq V
be a set of vertices for which
G\setminus X
is a disjoint union of edges and isolated vertices.
For every integer
q
, the graph
G
is
q
-colorable if and only if there exists a proper
q
-coloring
c:X\rightarrow[q]
of
G[X]
such that

1.

for every vertex
v\in V\setminus X
and for every set
S\subseteq X
of size
|S|=q
with
S\subseteq N_{G}(v)
, there exist distinct vertices
z_{1},z_{2}\in S
such that
c(z_{1})=c(z_{2})
, and

2.

for every edge
\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
of
G\setminus X
and for every two sets
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq X
of size
|S_{1}|=|S_{2}|=q-1
with
S_{1}\subseteq N_{G}(u_{1})
and
S_{2}\subseteq N_{G}(u_{2})
, either there exist distinct vertices
z_{1},z_{2}\in S_{1}
such that
c(z_{1})=c(z_{2})
, or
\{c(z)\mid z\in S_{1}\}\neq\{c(z)\mid z\in S_{2}\}
.

To prove the forward direction of the lemma, assume that the graph
G
is
q
-colorable, and consider a proper
q
-coloring
c:V\rightarrow[q]
of its vertices.
The restriction of
c
to the vertices of
X
is clearly a proper
q
-coloring of
G[X]
. We claim that it satisfies the two conditions of the lemma.
For the first, consider a vertex
v\in V\setminus X
and a set
S\subseteq X
of size
|S|=q
with
S\subseteq N_{G}(v)
. Since the coloring
c
is proper, the color
c(v)
differs from the colors of all the vertices of
S
. By
|S|=q
, it follows that two distinct vertices of
S
are assigned by
c
the same color, as required.
For the second condition, consider an edge
\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
of
G\setminus X
and two sets
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq X
of size
|S_{1}|=|S_{2}|=q-1
with
S_{1}\subseteq N_{G}(u_{1})
and
S_{2}\subseteq N_{G}(u_{2})
. If two vertices of
S_{1}
are assigned by
c
the same color, then we are done. Otherwise, the vertices of
S_{1}
are assigned
q-1
distinct colors, so
c(u_{1})
is the unique color of
[q]\setminus\{c(z)\mid z\in S_{1}\}
. Further, either two vertices of
S_{2}
are assigned by
c
the same color, or
c(u_{2})
is the unique color of
[q]\setminus\{c(z)\mid z\in S_{2}\}
. Using
c(u_{1})\neq c(u_{2})
, we obtain that in both cases it holds that
\{c(z)\mid z\in S_{1}\}\neq\{c(z)\mid z\in S_{2}\}
, so we are done.

For the converse implication, assume that there exists a proper
q
-coloring
c:X\rightarrow[q]
of
G[X]
satisfying the two conditions of the lemma. We extend this coloring to a proper
q
-coloring of
G
as follows.
The graph
G\setminus X
is a disjoint union of edges and isolated vertices.
We first claim that for every vertex
v\in V\setminus X
, there exists a color that does not appear on its neighbors in
X
. Indeed, otherwise there would exist a set
S\subseteq X
of size
|S|=q
with
S\subseteq N_{G}(v)
such that
\{c(z)\mid z\in S\}=[q]
, in contradiction to the first condition of the lemma.
Therefore, every isolated vertex of
G\setminus X
can be assigned a color that does not appear on its neighbors (which all lie in
X
).
Consider now an edge
\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
of
G\setminus X
. As shown above, for each of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
there exists a color that does not appear on its neighbors in
X
. If for either
u_{1}
or
u_{2}
there exist two colors that do not appear on its neighbors in
X
, then it is possible to assign to
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
distinct colors that differ from the colors of their neighbors. Otherwise, we assign to each of them the unique color of
[q]
that does not appear on its neighbors in
X
. The colors of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are distinct, because otherwise there would exist two sets
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq X
of size

with

and
S_{2}\subseteq N_{G}(u_{2})
, such that
\{c(z)\mid z\in S_{1}\}=\{c(z)\mid z\in S_{2}\}
, in contradiction to the second condition of the lemma. The proof is now complete.

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Fix an integer

.
Let
\mathbb{F}
be some field for which there exists a

over

, where field operations and Gaussian elimination can be performed in polynomial time.

The input of the

•

with

Consider an input of the
q\textsc{-Coloring}
problem on

We now turn to analyzing the size of the algorithm’s output.
The algorithm initializes the graph

To construct the collection of polynomials
