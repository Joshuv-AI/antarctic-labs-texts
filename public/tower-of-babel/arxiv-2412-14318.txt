Long-Time Accuracy of Ensemble Kalman Filters for Chaotic and Machine-Learned Dynamical Systems
Daniel Sanz-Alonso, Nathan Waniorek

Abstract
Filtering is concerned with online estimation of the state of a dynamical system from partial and noisy observations. In applications where the state is high dimensional, ensemble Kalman filters are often the method of choice. This paper establishes long-time accuracy of ensemble Kalman filters. We introduce conditions on the dynamics and the observations under which the estimation error remains small in the long-time horizon. Our theory covers a wide class of partially-observed chaotic dynamical systems, which includes the Navier-Stokes equations and Lorenz models. In addition, we prove long-time accuracy of ensemble Kalman filters with surrogate dynamics, thus validating the use of machine-learned forecast models in ensemble data assimilation.



keywords

ensemble Kalman filter, long-time accuracy, dissipative chaotic dynamical systems, surrogate models, Navier-Stokes equations

Introduction

The filtering problem involves the sequential estimation of the state of a dynamical system from partial and noisy observations. For high-dimensional problems, particularly those arising in the geophysical sciences
[
48
,
68
,
46
]
, the ensemble Kalman filter
[
34
]
is often the method of choice. The algorithm propagates an ensemble of particles under the dynamics and nudges the ensemble to new observations using empirical covariances in a Kalman style update.
In the linear-Gaussian setting, the ensemble Kalman filter converges to the Kalman filter as the ensemble size grows
[
61
,
54
]
, thus fully characterizing the filtering distribution and yielding the optimal estimator of the state
[
74
]
. Furthermore, non-asymptotic theory shows that an ensemble size much smaller than the state dimension often suffices to approximate the Kalman filter
[
5
]
. For nonlinear dynamics, however, the ensemble Kalman filter does not reproduce the filtering distribution even in the large ensemble limit
[
60
,
33
,
59
]
. Despite this caveat, this paper rigorously justifies the use of ensemble Kalman filters in practical settings with nonlinear dynamics, partial observations, and an ensemble size smaller than the state dimension. We do so by providing conditions on the dynamics and the observations that guarantee the long-time accuracy of the ensemble Kalman filter as a state estimator. Our conditions cover a wide class of partially-observed chaotic dissipative dynamical systems, which includes the Navier-Stokes equations and Lorenz models.

Motivated by recent advancements in machine learning emulation of geophysical systems, this paper additionally studies ensemble Kalman filters that use surrogate models for the dynamics. Examples of machine learning models for global weather forecasting include ClimaX
[
67
]
, FourCastNet
[
70
]
, GraphCast
[
55
]
, Pangu
[
11
]
, and FengWu
[
26
]
, among others. Due to the chaotic nature of the dynamical systems of interest, it is unreasonable to expect these surrogate models to provide accurate state predictions over long time scales, motivating work on learning surrogates that preserve the time-invariant statistical properties of the dynamics
[
47
,
69
,
79
]
. However, in data assimilation, long-time filter accuracy may only need forecasts to be accurate over one assimilation cycle
[
1
]
. Consequently, cheap-to-evaluate machine learning surrogate models have the potential to enormously speed up data assimilation algorithms and allow for the generation of much larger ensembles than is possible with traditional numerical approximations of the true dynamics
[
53
]
. This paper establishes precise mathematical conditions for long-time accuracy of ensemble Kalman filters with surrogate models.

Ensemble Kalman filters

Much of the work on the theoretical foundations of ensemble Kalman filters has focused on the linear-Gaussian setting. In this setting, it is known that the ensemble Kalman filter converges to the Kalman filter in the large ensemble asymptotic
[
61
,
54
]
. In the nonlinear setting, a similar line of work assumes the
mean-field
perspective
[
20
,
45
,
32
,
31
]
and analyzes the large ensemble limits of the ensemble Kalman filter. Recent results using this approach have shown that the mean-field ensemble Kalman filter is near the filtering distribution in the near-Gaussian setting
[
23
]
and in the near-linear setting
[
19
]
. These results only hold over a finite-time horizon and do not guarantee accurate state estimation. A complementary line of work provides a non-asymptotic analysis of ensemble Kalman updates, explaining how the ensemble Kalman filter can well approximate the mean-field updates with an ensemble size much smaller than the dimension of the state space
[
5
,
76
,
2
]
. This perspective helps explain why localization techniques are so successful within ensemble Kalman filters and extends notions of localization and sparse covariance estimation to the function space setting
[
3
,
4
]
. However, none of these non-asymptotic analyses hold over an infinite-time horizon, and they do not consider state estimation accuracy. A number of previous works have considered the state estimation accuracy of the ensemble Kalman filter, largely using the theory of synchronization in dynamical systems
[
71
]
. The work
[
50
]
proves stability and accuracy of the ensemble Kalman filter with covariance inflation, but only in the setting with complete observations of the system. They provide numerical evidence of filter accuracy with partial observations, but do not extend the theorems to the partially-observed setting. Recently,
[
78
]
proves accuracy of the ensemble Kalman filter with multiplicative covariance inflation, but again requires full observations and the results are restricted to finite-dimensional dynamical systems. Notable works in the partially-observed setting include
[
83
,
39
,
13
]
. In
[
83
]
, the authors prove long-time stability of the ensemble Kalman filter with an adaptive variance inflation scheme, but do not prove filter accuracy. For hyperbolic dynamical systems,
[
39
]
proves accuracy of the ensemble Kalman filter with partial observations using a shadowing argument. The work most similar to ours is
[
13
]
, which proves accuracy of a continuous-time variant of the ensemble Kalman filter for the Navier-Stokes equations in the partially-observed setting. Their algorithm includes variance inflation and a somewhat atypical localization procedure where the unobserved components of the forecast covariance are projected to be zero. In contrast, our results hold in the discrete-time setting and do not require this localization procedure.

Main results

2.1
Long-time accuracy of ensemble Kalman filters

In this subsection, we show long-time accuracy for a standard implementation of the ensemble Kalman filter. Our setting covers a wide range of partially-observed dissipative chaotic dynamical systems often used to benchmark filtering algorithms.

2.1.1
Set-up

We study the filtering problem of sequentially estimating a time-evolving hidden signal as new observations become available.
Suppose that the signal process
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{H}
lies on a separable Hilbert space
(\mathcal{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,\|\cdot\|)
and its evolution is governed by a discrete-time dynamical system given by
u_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{j}&=\Psi(u_{j-1}),\qquad j=1,2,\ldots\end{split}

(1)

The map
\Psi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is assumed to be known, but the initial condition
u_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
—and hence the signal process— are assumed to be unknown.

To estimate the signal, we are given observations
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbbm{R}^{k}
that comprise finite-dimensional noisy measurements of the state

\displaystyle y_{j}=Hu_{j}+\varepsilon\eta_{j},\qquad j=1,2,\ldots

Here,
H:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbbm{R}^{k}
is assumed to satisfy
HH^{*}=I_{k}
and we define
P:=H^{*}H:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
, which is an orthogonal projection onto a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
. We are interested in the case where
k\ll\text{dim}(\mathcal{H})\in\mathbbm{N}\cup\{\infty\}

and assume that the observation errors
\{\eta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
are independent, with
\mathbb{E}[\eta_{j}]=0
and
\text{Cov}[\eta_{j}]=R\in\mathbbm{R}^{k\times k}.
The parameter
\varepsilon>0
controls the size of the observation noise, which is often small in applications. Filtering concerns sequentially estimating the state
u_{j}
given all the observations
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{j}
available at time
j.

The ability of filtering algorithms to accurately estimate the signal over a long time window necessarily hinges on control-theoretic conditions on the signal and observation processes. We will adopt the following assumption, also used in
[
75
]
, on the dynamics and the observations. As discussed in Subsection
2.1.3
below, this assumption is satisfied by important test filtering problems, including partially-observed Lorenz models and Navier-Stokes equations. For a constant
\beta\geq 0,
we define
V:\mathcal{H}\to[0,\infty)
by

\displaystyle V(u):=\Bigl(\|u\|^{2}+\beta\|Pu\|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

The norm
V
will act as a Lyapunov function in the theory.

Assumption 1
.

The following properties hold:

(Absorbing ball property)
There exist a constant
r>0
such that the ball

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}:=\left\{u\in\mathcal{H}:\|u\|\leq r\right\}

(2)

is absorbing and forward invariant for the dynamical system (
1
).

(Local Lipschitz continuity)

There exists
L>0
such that, for any
u,v\in\mathcal{B},

\displaystyle V^{2}\bigl(\Psi(u)-\Psi(v)\bigr)\leq LV^{2}(u-v).

(3)

(Squeezing property)

There exists
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that, for any
u,v\in\mathcal{B},

\displaystyle V^{2}\Bigl((I-P)\bigl(\Psi(u)-\Psi(v)\bigr)\Bigr)\leq\alpha V^{2}\left(u-v\right).

(4)

The absorbing ball property and local Lipschitz continuity are naturally satisfied by dissipative dynamical systems where there is an energy-loss mechanism. In such systems, the bounded invariant sets of the dynamics contain all the information about asymptotic behavior
[
77
, Section 1.8]
; therefore, these sets play a key role in the study of long-time filter accuracy. The squeezing property can be interpreted as a detectability condition that ensures that the dynamics contract in the unobserved part of the system
[
75
]
.

2.1.2
Square-root ensemble Kalman filter

Ensemble Kalman filters sequentially estimate the state using an ensemble of interacting particles. At time
j=0,
we generate an
initial ensemble

\mathsf{U}_{0}=\{u_{0}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
of
N
particles; for concreteness, we assume that
u_{0}^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(m_{0},C_{0}),

1\leq n\leq N,
for given mean
m_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and covariance operator
C_{0}.
Then, at each time
j\geq 1,
we estimate the hidden state
u_{j}
by the empirical mean
\widehat{m}_{j}
of an ensemble
\mathsf{U}_{j}=\{u_{j}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. Particles are updated from time
j-1
to time
j
in two steps, referred to as
prediction
and
analysis
, which utilize respectively the dynamical system (
1
) and the observation
y_{j}
acquired at time
j.
Thus, each update can be schematically represented by:

\mathsf{U}_{j-1}=\bigl\{u_{j-1}^{(n)}\bigr\}_{n=1}^{N}\xrightarrow[\text{Dynamical System}]{\text{Prediction}}\mathsf{V}_{j}=\bigl\{v_{j}^{(n)}\bigr\}_{n=1}^{N}\xrightarrow[\text{Observation}]{\text{Analysis}}\mathsf{U}_{j}=\bigl\{u_{j}^{(n)}\bigr\}_{n=1}^{N}\,.

(5)

In the prediction step, we use the dynamics governing the evolution of the state and set

v_{j}^{(n)}=\Psi\bigl(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u_{j-1}^{(n)}\bigr)+\xi_{j}^{(n)},\qquad\xi_{j}^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,Q),\qquad 1\leq n\leq N.

(6)

Here, we follow
[
75
]
and project each particle
u_{j-1}^{(n)}
into the absorbing ball
\mathcal{B}
in Assumption
1
; specifically,
P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{B}
in the
V
-norm.
To promote spread of the prediction ensemble and avoid downward bias of estimates of the covariance of the state
[
38
]
, we introduce additional randomness
\xi_{j}^{(n)}
in the propagation of each particle
u_{j-1}^{(n)}
. This is a standard procedure in data assimilation known as
covariance inflation
; see for instance
[
35
, Chapter 10]
,
[
49
, Section 4]
, and the discussion in
[
9
]
.
We take the covariance inflation operators to be of the form
Q=aP
for some constant
a>0.
Since
P
is an orthogonal projection onto a
k
-dimensional subspace,
Q
is trace-class and positive semi-definite, and hence a valid covariance operator in
\mathcal{H}
. It will be relevant to note that for
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q)
, it holds that
\text{Cov}[H\xi]=HQH^{*}=aHPH^{*}=aI_{k\times k}.

In the analysis step, we update the prediction ensemble
\mathsf{V}_{j}=\{v_{j}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
into an analysis ensemble
\mathsf{U}_{j}=\{u_{j}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
by imposing that the empirical mean and covariances are consistent with Kalman-type formulae for linear-Gaussian inverse problems. To that end, we define the
Kalman gain
associated to covariance operator
\Sigma
by

\mathscr{K}(\Sigma):=\Sigma H^{*}(H\Sigma H^{*}+\varepsilon R)^{-1}.

(7)

Then, denoting
by
(\widehat{\mu}_{j},\widehat{\Sigma}_{j})
the empirical mean and covariance of
\mathsf{V}_{j}
and
by
(\widehat{m}_{j},\widehat{C}_{j})
the empirical mean and covariance of
\mathsf{U}_{j},
we transform the particles so that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{m}_{j}&=\widehat{\mu}_{j}+\mathscr{K}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{j})\left(y_{j+1}-H\widehat{\mu}_{j}\right),\\
\widehat{C}_{j}&=\bigl(I-\mathscr{K}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{j})H\bigr)\widehat{\Sigma}_{j}.\end{split}

(8)

As described in
[
82
]
and reviewed in
[
5
]
, many deterministic maps can be used to transform the prediction ensemble
\mathsf{V}_{j}
into an analysis ensemble
\mathsf{U}_{j}
that satisfies the moment constraints in (
8
), leading to different square-root ensemble Kalman filters. Notable examples include the Ensemble Transform Kalman Filter
[
12
]
and the Ensemble Adjustment Kalman Filter
[
6
]
. Our theory is agnostic to the choice of transformation, and holds for the general formulation of square-root ensemble Kalman filters summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Square-Root Ensemble Kalman Filter

1:

Initialization
: Draw

u_{0}^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(m_{0},C_{0}),\qquad 1\leq n\leq N.

2:

For
j=1,2,\ldots
do the following:

3:

Prediction
: Compute

\displaystyle\begin{split}v_{j}^{(n)}&=\Psi\bigl(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u_{j-1}^{(n)}\bigr)+\xi_{j}^{(n)},\qquad\xi_{j}^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,Q),\qquad 1\leq n\leq N,\\
\widehat{\mu}_{j}&=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{n=1}v_{j}^{(n)},\qquad\widehat{\Sigma}_{j}=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{n=1}\bigl(v^{(n)}_{j}-\widehat{\mu}_{j}\bigr)\otimes\bigl(v^{(n)}_{j}-\widehat{\mu}_{j}\bigr).\end{split}

4:

Analysis
: Transform
\{v_{j}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\mapsto\{u_{j}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{m}_{j}&:=\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{n=1}u_{j}^{(n)}=\widehat{\mu}_{j}+\mathscr{K}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{j})\left(y_{j+1}-H\widehat{\mu}_{j}\right),\\
\widehat{C}_{j}&:=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigl(u^{(n)}_{j}-\widehat{m}_{j}\bigr)\otimes\bigl(u^{(n)}_{j}-\widehat{m}_{j}\bigr)=\bigl(I-\mathscr{K}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{j})H\bigr)\widehat{\Sigma}_{j}.\end{split}

5:

Output
: Means and covariances
\{\widehat{m}_{j},\widehat{C}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}.

We are ready to state our first main result, which establishes long-time accuracy of the ensemble Kalman filter. We show that both observed and unobserved parts of the state can be estimated at the level
\varepsilon
of the observation noise. The proof will be deferred to Section
3
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds and that
N\geq 6k.
Then, if the inflation parameter
a>0
is sufficiently large, there is a constant
\mathsf{C}
independent of
\varepsilon
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\sup\mathbb{E}\|\widehat{m}_{j}-u_{j}\|\leq\mathsf{C}\varepsilon.

Remark 3
.

Our theory requires the ensemble size
N
to scale with the dimension
k
of the observations, but
not
with the state dimension. The assumption that
N\geq 6k
is a requirement of the results in
[
65
]
that we use to lower bound the smallest eigenvalue of
H\widehat{\Sigma}_{j}H^{*}
.
Instead of performing variance inflation by randomly perturbing each particle in (
6
), one could consider deterministic variance inflation by noiselessly propagating each particle through the dynamics, i.e. setting
v_{j}^{(n)}=\Psi\bigl(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u_{j-1}^{(n)}\bigr),
and then deterministically and additively inflating the empirical covariance.
Then, our proof techniques suggest that
N\geq k
suffices.

Lemma 9

Under Assumption
1

\displaystyle{\rm{Tr}}\left(C_{j}\right)\leq\alpha^{j}{\rm{Tr}}\left(C_{0}\right)+(1+\alpha\beta)\frac{1-\alpha^{j}}{1-\alpha}\varepsilon^{2}{\rm{Tr}}(R),

(15)

Proof.

Since
P
is an orthogonal projection and
Q=aP,
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm{Tr}}(C_{j})&={\rm{Tr}}(PC_{j}P^{*})+{\rm{Tr}}\bigl((I-P)C_{j}(I-P)^{*}\bigr)\\
&\leq{\rm{Tr}}(PC_{j}P^{*})+{\rm{Tr}}\bigl((I-P)\Sigma_{j}(I-P)^{*}\bigr).\end{split}

(16)

We next bound in turn the two terms in the right-hand side.

For the first term, let

be an orthonormal basis for the range of

\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm{Tr}}\left(PC_{j}P^{*}\right)&=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\langle\varphi_{i},PC_{j}P^{*}\varphi_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle\varphi_{i},PC_{j}P^{*}\varphi_{i}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle H\varphi_{i},HC_{j}H^{*}H\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\mathbbm{R}^{k}}={\rm{Tr}}\left(HC_{j}H^{*}\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}{\rm{Tr}}\left(R\right),\end{split}

(17)

where we have used that
\{H\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
and that
\epsilon^{2}R-HC_{j}H^{*}
is positive semi-definite in the last inequality.

For the second term in the right-hand side of (
16

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm{Tr}}\bigl((I-P)\Sigma_{j}(I-P)^{*}\bigr)&=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}(m_{j-1},C_{j-1})}\Bigl[\bigl\|(I-P)(\Psi(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u)-\mu_{j})\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr]\\
&\hskip-28.45274pt\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}(m_{j-1},C_{j-1})}\Bigl[\bigl\|(I-P)\bigl(\Psi(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u)-\Psi(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}m_{j-1})\bigr)\bigr\|^{2}\Bigr]\\
&\hskip-28.45274pt\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}(m_{j-1},C_{j-1})}\Bigl[\alpha\left(\bigl\|P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u-P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}m_{j-1}\bigr\|^{2}+\beta\bigl\|P(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u-P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}m_{j-1})\bigr\|^{2}\right)\Bigr]\\
&\hskip-28.45274pt\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{N}(m_{j-1},C_{j-1})}\Bigl[\alpha\left(\|u-m_{j-1}\|^{2}+\beta\|P(u-m_{j-1})\|^{2}\right)\Bigr]\\
&\hskip-28.45274pt=\alpha{\rm{Tr}}\left(C_{j-1}\right)+\alpha\beta{\rm{Tr}}\left(PC_{j-1}P^{*}\right).\end{split}

(18)

Combining (

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}{\rm{Tr}}(R)+\alpha{\rm{Tr}}(C_{j-1})+\alpha\beta{\rm{Tr}}(PC_{j-1}P^{*})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\alpha\beta)\varepsilon^{2}{\rm{Tr}}(R)+\alpha{\rm{Tr}}(C_{j-1}).

Applying the discrete Gronwall lemma
[

14

\displaystyle{\rm{Tr}}\bigl((I-P)\widehat{\Sigma}_{j}(I-P)^{*}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Bigl\|(I-P)(v_{j}^{(n)}-\widehat{\mu}_{j})\Bigr\|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Bigl\|(I-P)\bigl(\Psi(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u^{(n)}_{j-1})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Psi(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u^{(n)}_{j-1})\bigr)\Bigr\|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\leq\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Bigl\|(I-P)\bigl(\Psi(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u^{(n)}_{j-1})-\Psi(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}\widehat{m}_{j-1})\bigr)\Bigr\|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\leq\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha\left(\bigl\|P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u^{(n)}_{j-1}-P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}\widehat{m}_{j-1}\bigr\|^{2}+\beta\bigl\|P(P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}u^{(n)}_{j-1}-P_{\mathcal{B}_{V}}\widehat{m}_{j-1})\bigr\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\leq\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha\left(\bigl\|u^{(n)}_{j-1}-\widehat{m}_{j-1}\bigr\|^{2}+\beta\bigl\|P(u^{(n)}_{j-1}-\widehat{m}_{j-1})\bigr\|^{2}\right)

6
Conclusions

This paper established long-time accuracy of ensemble Kalman filters, introducing conditions on the dynamics and the observations under which the estimation error remains small in the long-time horizon. We have shown that these conditions can be verified for many partially-observed dynamical systems, such as the Lorenz models and the 2-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations. In addition, we have rigorously established and empirically demonstrated that ensemble Kalman filters can provide long-time accurate state estimation even when employing machine-learned surrogate models that only give accurate short-term forecasts.

There are several natural research directions that stem from this work. First, our analysis focuses on linear observations; the nonlinear case could be studied by working on an extended state-space, as in
[
6
]
. Second, it would be interesting to extend the theory beyond the case of autonomous dynamical systems and fixed observation operators considered here. Third, our work does not account for covariance localization, which can improve the empirical performance of the algorithm and reduce the required ensemble size. Lastly, there are numerous opportunities to leverage the ideas in this paper to co-learn the state, the dynamics, and the filtering algorithms in data assimilation tasks across many application domains.
