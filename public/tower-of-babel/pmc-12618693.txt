Structures of CRP antigen-antibody complexes provide insights into the mechanism of specific recognition
Authors: Yijian Li, Qianxi Yu, Mengtan Du, Jian Zhang, Tonggong Liu, Jingzhe Wang, Qingchun Wei, Yong Peng, Chaohui Duan, Dayong Gu, Fuxing Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antigen-antibody specific recognition constitutes fundamental research in molecular drug design and immune diagnostics, where cumulative non-covalent interactions critically determine binding affinities. Current mechanistic understanding of antibody affinity optimization remains incomplete, hindering rational structure-based design of therapeutic antibodies and bispecific variants. This study presents four cryo-EM structures of C-reactive protein (CRP) complexed with heavy-chain antibodies (HCAbs) of varying affinities, resolved at 3.0-3.4 Å resolution. Comparative structural analysis reveals pronounced variations in binding modalities among affinity-differentiated HCAbs, while identifying critical determinants of engagement conformations, providing mechanistic insights for rational optimization of CRP-specific antibodies.

Introduction

The specific antigen–antibody recognition mechanism constitutes a cornerstone of immunological science, governing critical biological processes that underpin immune diagnostic applications1–5. Antibody-based therapeutics have gained substantial prominence in pharmacotherapy because of their exceptional target binding affinity and specificity. However, conventional antibody discovery pipelines remain constrained by prolonged development timelines, high costs, and suboptimal success rates6. Furthermore, structure-guided antibody engineering, particularly the systematic optimization of paratope‒epitope interactions for enhanced specificity and affinity, continues to face formidable technical challenges, including conformational flexibility prediction and developability profiling7–11.

The thermodynamic stability conferred by synergistic noncovalent interactions constitutes the molecular basis of antibody–antigen binding specificity and affinity12. In vitro antibody mutagenesis has emerged as a principal strategy for affinity maturation, primarily implemented through two distinct paradigms: (i) structure-guided targeted mutagenesis of complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) and (ii) error-prone PCR-driven random mutagenesis13. Nevertheless, critical challenges persist: (a) precise identification of paratope residues requiring optimization14, and (b) insufficient mechanistic understanding of the cooperation between somatic hypermutation and affinity maturation, substantially hindering structure-guided engineering of therapeutic antibodies and vaccine immunogens8,10,15–19. Consequently, precision modulation of antibody affinity has become a central paradigm in the development of next-generation antibody-based biotherapeutics and diagnostic platforms20,21.

C-reactive protein (CRP), a pentameric acute-phase reactant (115 kDa), is synthesized predominantly by hepatocytes under proinflammatory conditions triggered by tissue injury or microbial invasion22,23. This evolutionarily conserved pattern recognition molecule serves as a cardinal biomarker for clinical inflammation monitoring24,25. Recent advances in high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) assays have revealed significant associations between minimally elevated concentrations and the subclinical pathogenesis of atherosclerosis26,27, insulin resistance28, and tumor microenvironment remodelling29,30. Contemporary clinical CRP quantification primarily employs antibody-mediated immunoturbidimetric assays. However, these methods present significant limitations: (a) insufficient sensitivity for subclinical detection, (b) prohibitive monoclonal antibody production costs, and (c) Inter-Lot variability exceeding 20%31. Consequently, structure-guided antibody engineering strategies combining computational epitope mapping and paratope optimization through in silico affinity maturation are essential for the development of next-generation assays capable of pan-CRP quantification with ≤5% Inter-Assay variability32,33.

Recent technological advancements in single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), particularly in terms of direct electron detector sensitivity and image processing algorithms, have established it as the gold-standard technique for determining the near-atomic-resolution structures of macromolecular complexes34–36. This study establishes a methodological framework to decipher the structural basis of CRP–antibody affinity modulation through the resolution of four cryo-EM structures of CRP–antibody complexes with affinities ranging from 60.7 to 277 nM.

Results Antibody affinity analysis and structural determination of CRP–HCAb complexes

Interactions between these four heavy chain antibodies and the CRP antigen

To elucidate the structural basis of affinity modulation, we systematically analyzed the interdomain interactions and conformation differences in these CRP–HCAb complexes. The buried interface areas of the CRP–HCAb complexes were quantified using PDBePISA (Fig. 2a), revealing the order CRP–HCAb2 > CRP–HCAb4 > CRP–HCAb3 > CRP–HCAb1. Notably, CRP–HCAb2 had the largest interface area despite its lower binding affinity. Molecular dynamics simulations were also performed, and these four CRP–HCAb complexes were in a stable state at approximately 5 ns, indicating that their intersection was stable (Supplementary Fig. 10a–d). The binding free energy was calculated using MM/PBSA based on the conformation obtained by molecular dynamics simulation, in which CRP–HCAb3 < CRP–HCAb4 ≈ CRP–HCAb2 < CRP–HCAb1 (Fig. 2b). Energy decomposition was subsequently performed to analyse the key amino acids in these four CRP–HCAb complexes. The high-affinity CRP–HCAb3 complex (KD = 70 nM) exhibited three hydrogen-bonding interactions: Glu81 (CRP)–Gln55 (HCAb3), Thr76 (CRP)–Asn59 (HCAb3), and Gly79 (CRP)–Asn59 (HCAb3), which were stabilized by a salt bridge between Glu85 (CRP)–Arg33 (HCAb3) (Fig. 2i, Table 2). The high-affinity CRP–HCAb4 complex (KD = 60.7 nM) contained (i) four hydrogen bonds (Asp70 (CRP)–Arg57 (HCAb4), Val86 (CRP)–Arg57 (HCAb4), Glu81 (CRP)–Arg99 (HCAb4), and Asn61 (CRP)–Asn101 (HCAb4) and (ii) three salt bridges forming an electrostatic network (Glu81 (CRP)–Arg33 (HCAb4), Asp70 (CRP)–Arg57 (HCAb4), and Glu81 (CRP)–Arg99 (HCAb4)) (Fig. 2j, Table 2). In the CRP–HCAb3 and CRP–HCAb4 complexes, the binding sites were concentrated not only in the CDR2 and CDR3 regions of antibodies but also in the CDR1 region.

Structural superposition analysis revealed that all HCAbs targeted the same binding epitope on CRP but adopted divergent binding orientations (Supplementary Fig. 11a–b). The high-affinity complexes CRP–HCAb3 (KD = 70 nM) and CRP–HCAb4 (KD = 60.7 nM) both exhibited distinguishing features compared with those of CRP–HCAb1 and CRP–HCAb2, including supplementary salt bridges in complementarity-determining region 1 (CDR1) involving Arg33 (HCAb) and enhanced intermolecular interactions in CRP–HCAb4 (four hydrogen bonds vs. three in HCAb1 or HCAb2; three salt bridges vs. one in HCAb1 or HCAb2) concentrated at the paratope-epitope interface (Fig. 2, Table 2). However, there was no clear correlation between the hydrogen bond lengths and binding affinity (Supplementary Fig. 11c).

Different particle components in these four CRP–HCAb complexes

Cryo-EM single-particle analysis revealed eight distinct stoichiometric configurations of CRP pentamer–HCAb complexes, classified by bound antibody valency (1–5 HCAbs per pentamer), with all HCAbs targeting the same binding epitope on CRP (Supplementary Figs. 4–7). Strikingly, the high-affinity CRP–HCAb4 complex (KD = 60.7 nM) exhibited predominant pentamer saturation (Type I: 59.6% occupancy, Student’s t test, p < 0.01 vs. other classes), demonstrating enhanced multivalent binding capability (Table 3, Supplementary Fig. 7). The distributions of the lower-affinity complexes CRP–HCAb1 (KD = 143 nM) and HCAb2 (277 nM) were bimodal, with partial pentamer saturation (23.4% and 28.9% for 5 HCAbs, respectively) coexisting with the dominant 4:1 binding mode (35.7% and 25.9%, respectively) (Fig. 3, Table 3, Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). Although the high-affinity CRP–HCAb3 complex (KD = 70.0 nM) was an exception to the observed trend, it exhibited two predominant stoichiometric configurations (Fig. 3): (i) bis-pentameric assemblies with 5 HCAb3 dimers bridging two CRP pentamers (approximately 22.4% occupancy) and (ii) asymmetric complexes with four HCAb3 dimers per pentamer pair (approximately 32.5% occupancy) (Table 3; Supplementary Fig. 6). Key factors involved in the different binding modes of the CRP–HCAb complexes

To elucidate the structural basis of the differential binding modalities between HCAb3 (KD = 70.0 nM) and the other HCAbs (HCAb1, HCAb2, and HCAb4; KD = 60.7–277 nM), we further analysed the structural differences among all four CRP–HCAb complexes. The CRP–HCAb3 complex uniquely formed symmetric bis-pentameric decamers through VHH dimers engaging two CRP pentamers (C5 symmetry), with CH2–CH3 domains (AlphaFold 3 predicted, Supplementary Fig. 14a) fitting at the end of VHH and oriented outwards (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 14b). Although dimers of CRP pentamers were not observed directly in 2D class averages for HCAb1–CRP, HCAb2–CRP, and HCAb4–CRP (Supplementary Fig. 3), these modelled dimeric pentamer complexes (Fig. 4a) suggested that the orientation of CH2–CH3 domains may promote or hinder HCAb-mediated CRP dimerization.Fig. 4Analysis the key factors for different binding mode involved in the CRP antigen-antibody binding complexes.a Spliced CRP-HCAb1, CRP-HCAb2, CRP-HCAb4 of their own two CRP-HCAb complexes into one complex based on the symmetry of heavy chain antibodies. In contrast, the CRP–HCAb1, CRP–HCAb2 and CRP–HCAb4 complexes did not form decamers because of the inwards orientation of the CH2–CH3 domains. Inwards-oriented CH2–CH3 domains in the CRP–HCAb1, CRP–HCAb2, and CRP–HCAb4 complexes could cause steric clashes with the CRP pentamer, generating repulsive forces that destabilize symmetric decamer assembly.

Structural analysis of the CRP–HCAb3 complex monomer revealed two distinct interaction zones: Area1, a salt bridge network centred on Glu85 (CRP)–Arg33 (HCAb3) in CDR1; and Area2, a hydrogen-bond cluster involving Glu81 (CRP)–Gln55 (HCAb3), Thr76 (CRP)–Asn59 (HCAb3), and Gly79 (CRP)–Asn59 (HCAb3) in CDR2. This bipartite interaction geometry dictated the VHH orientation (Fig. 4b, bottom left). In contrast, the CRP–HCAb1 and CRP–HCAb2 complexes exhibited CDR3-dominated binding only in Area2 (Fig. 4b, upper panels). This restricted binding mode forced CH2–CH3 domains inwards, creating steric clashes that destabilized symmetric bis-pentameric assemblies. The CRP–HCAb4 complex uniquely combined the following: Area1, salt bridge network Asp70 (CRP)–Arg57 (HCAb4), Val86 (CRP)–Arg57 (HCAb4) via CDR2; and Area2, extended hydrogen-bond network Glu81 (CRP)–Arg33 (HCAb4), Glu81 (CRP)–Arg99 (HCAb4), and Asn61 (CRP)–Asn101 (HCAb4) through CDR1/CDR3 (Fig. 4b, bottom right). These AlphaFold-based models illustrated how CH2–CH3 orientation may promote or inhibit CRP dimerization. Whether interactions between specific residues in CDRs and CRP promote or inhibit dimerization remains unclear.

To confirm the roles of specific amino acids, we introduced alanine mutations at R99, N101, and D103 in HCAb1; at S55, G56, and R101 in HCAb2; at R33, Q55, and N59 in HCAb3; and at R33, R57, R99, and N101 in HCAb4 (Supplementary Fig. 12). These mutations resulted in negligible CRP binding for HCAb1, 3, and 4 and reduced binding for HCAb2, as measured by BLI (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 13). Similar trends were observed by ELISA when the wells were coated with CRP (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, when HCAbs were used to coat the wells, binding was abolished for all the mutants (Fig. 5c). Discussion

The affinity conferred by interactions between CDRs constitutes the molecular basis for antigen‒antibody recognition, with direct implications for rational drug design and diagnostic assay development. Elucidating the structure‒activity relationship (SAR) governing paratope–epitope recognition affinity is essential for structure-guided antibody engineering. We present four cryo-EM structures of CRP in complex with heavy-chain antibodies (HCAbs) spanning a 4-fold affinity range, resolved at 3.0–3.4 Å map resolution (tight mask) using cryoSPARC v4. Our study highlights the importance of Arg33 from CDR1 binding to glutamates on CRP, as this binding mode was utilized by the tightest binders, HCAb3 and HCAb4. Notably, the highest-affinity antibody, HCAb4, was the only one to interact with CRP via all three CDRs, possibly suggesting that engagement through all three regions may contribute to enhanced affinity.

Current antibody discovery pipelines remain constrained by conventional approaches, such as animal immunization or library screening6. Emerging computational platforms (e.g., AbLang and IgLM) predominantly focus on CDR3 optimization (weight ratio of CDR3:CDR1:CDR2 = 5:1:1)37–40 because of the inherently restricted recognition capacity of CDR1 and CDR2 relative to that of CDR341. The buried interface area of the CRP–HCAb2 complex was abnormally greater than those of the other three CRP–HCAb complexes (Fig. 2a), whereas the binding free energy was greater than those of the CRP–HCAb3 and CRP–HCAb4 complexes (Fig. 2b). This may imply an extensive network of weaker intermolecular interactions within the complex: van der Waals forces, which scale with the contact surface area and the complementarity of the interacting surfaces, are a primary component strengthened by this increased burial.

High-affinity CRP–HCAb3 and CRP–HCAb4 complexes also exhibit additional salt bridges in CDR1 (Glu85 (CRP)–Arg33 (HCAb3) and Glu81 (CRP)–Arg33 (HCAb4)) (Fig. 2g–j, Table 2). In the CRP–HCAb3 complex, a single HCAb3 dimer molecule simultaneously binds to two CRP epitopes located on two distinct CRP pentamers, forming a pentamer dimer. We hypothesize that this binding is facilitated by interactions between Arg33 (HCAb3-CDR1) in Area1 and the CDR2 residues Gln55 and Asn59 in Area2, which may help dictate a specific orientation for the VHH domain of HCAb3 during CRP engagement.

In contrast, CRP–HCAb1 and CRP–HCAb2 complexes exhibit CDR3-restricted binding to Area2, whereas CRP–HCAb4 shows multidomain engagement (Area1:CDR2; Area2:CDR1/CDR3) (Fig. 4b). These alternative binding topologies internalize constant domains, generating substantial steric hindrance that destabilizes symmetric decamer assembly. Finally, the roles of specific residues (R99, N101, and D103 in HCAb1; S55, G56, and R101 in HCAb2; R33, Q55, and N59 in HCAb3; and R33, R57, R99, and N101 in HCAb4) in determining differences in affinity were also confirmed by mutagenesis of the HCAbs, which disrupted their effects on CRP binding affinity (Fig. 5).

However, in the present study, we did not modify the antibodies to optimize their affinities, nor did we evaluate their functional performance. This is due to the lack of established, robust structure–function relationships linking specific CDR residues to CRP binding affinity. Nonetheless, we believe that the cryo-EM and biophysical workflow presented here provides a platform for developing such structure–function relationships, ultimately enabling the generation of modified HCAbs with enhanced affinity for CRP. These improved antibodies could then be evaluated in in vivo disease models.

Materials and methods

Expression and purification of heavy-chain antibodies (HCAbs)

All 8 HCAbs were generated by gene synthesis (Secretory peptides & VHH & CH2-CH3 & 6x his-tag) (Genscript, Nanjing, China) and subsequently subcloned and inserted into pcDNA3.1+ plasmids. Plasmids (2 mg each, sterile-filtered) encoding HCAb were mixed with PEI (5.4 mL, 1 g/L, pH 7.0; Polysciences) in OPM-293 CD05 medium (66.6 mL; OPM Biosciences). The mixture was then added to HEK293F cells (ATCC, CRL-3249) (density: 5 × 105 cells/mL in 2000 mL of medium). Supplemental OPM-293 CD05 medium (100 mL) was added at 24 and 72 h post-transfection. The supernatants containing HCAb were harvested at 96 h post-transfection, centrifuged, and sterile-filtered. The HCAbs were then purified using Ni-NTA resin (Cytiva). The resin was equilibrated with 20 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4; Gibco™), and the HCAbs were eluted with 20 mM PBS containing 250 mM imidazole (pH 7.4). Finally, the eluted HCAbs were dialyzed against 20 mM PBS (pH 7.4) to obtain the purified products42 by Beijing Siodax Biotechnology.

Characterization of the size and purity of HCAb

The purity of the HCAbs was assessed by SDS‒PAGE and Coomassie blue staining. The nondenatured/denatured (heating to 100 °C) and nonreduced/reduced (adding 10 mM TCEP) HCAb samples were analyzed by SurePAGE, with 10 cm × 8 cm Bis-Tris gels (Genscript) running in Tris-MOPS-SDS Running Buffer (Genscript) on ice at 25 mA and 100 mA for the stacking and resolving stages, respectively, and stained with an eStain™ L1 Protein Staining System (Genscript).

Biolayer interferometry (BLI) binding assays

BLI was carried out on an Octet® RED96 device (ForteBio), and assays were carried out in 96-well black plates. The CRP antigen was biotinylated with an EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotinylation Kit (Thermo Fisher) and diluted to concentrations of 500, 250, 125, 62.5, 31.3, 15.6, 7.8, and 0 nmol/L with kinetic buffer (PBS supplemented with 0.02% Tween-20 and 0.1% BSA (w/v)). The following steps were then performed at 25 °C: (1) Octet® SA Biosensor (ForteBio) was hydrated in 200 µL of kinetic buffer for 10 minutes; (2) the biosensor was exposed to 250 µL of buffer for 120 seconds to obtain an initial baseline reading; (3) the biosensor was exposed to 200 µL of biotinylated CRP antigen for 300 seconds to obtain a loading curve; (4) the biosensor was exposed to 200 µL of buffer for another baseline measurement; (5) the biosensor was exposed to 200 µL of different HCAb dilutions for 180 seconds to obtain an association curve; and (6) following the association phase, the biosensor was transferred to a well containing buffer (250 μL) for 180 seconds to initiate the dissociation phase by removing free analyte and establishing conditions in which bound complexes may dissociate. All the curves were fitted by a 1:1 binding model using Data Analysis software (ForteBio), and the KD values were determined by binding curves within a dilution series with R2 values greater than the 95% confidence level.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The four CRP–HCAb complex cryo-EM-derived models were used for simulation. The CHARMM36 force field was applied to both the HCAb and the CRP antigen, and topology files were generated using the pdb2gmx module in GROMACS. After topology generation, the complexes were solvated in a cubic water box using the TIP3P water model, with a buffer distance of 10 Å from the solute in all three dimensions. Counterions were added to neutralize the system. Prior to the production simulation, a three-step preparation protocol was followed. First, energy minimization was performed using 50,000 steps of the steepest descent algorithm to remove steric clashes and relax the system. Second, temperature equilibration was conducted for 50,000 steps under an NVT (constant volume and temperature) ensemble, gradually heating the system to 300 K using a velocity rescaling thermostat. Third, pressure equilibration was carried out under an NPT (constant pressure and temperature) ensemble for an additional 50,000 steps, maintaining the pressure at 1 atm using a Parrinello–Rahman barostat. Following equilibration, a 100 ns production run was performed under NPT conditions (T = 300 K, P = 1 atm). The LINCS algorithm was used to constrain all the bonds involving hydrogen atoms, allowing a time step of 2 fs. Long-range electrostatics were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, and a cut-off of 10 Å was applied for short-range van der Waals and Coulomb interactions. System snapshots were saved every 10 ps for subsequent trajectory and structural analysis.

ELISA binding assays

ELISA plates (YEASEN # 83650ES20) were coated with CRP antigen (BBI Solutions) at 4000, 2000, 1000, 500, 250, 125, 62.5, and 0 ng/well in PBS overnight at 4 °C and blocked with PBS buffer containing 3% milk powder (w/v) at 37 °C for 60 mins. Afterwards, 1000 ng/ml HCAbs were added and incubated with the CRP antigen-coated plates for 2 h at 25 °C, after which they were detected with peroxidase-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-Alpaca IgG and the VHH domain (200 ng/mL; Jackson) for 1 h at 37 °C. Following TMB (Beyotime) colorization (100 μL/well) and concentrated sulfuric acid (Beyotime) termination (100 μL/well), the specific recognition activity was measured, with the signal read at 450 nm using an automated ELISA Reader (BioTek).

ELISA plates (YEASEN # 83650ES20) were coated with HCAb at 1000 ng/ml in PBS overnight at 4 °C and blocked with PBS buffer containing 3% milk powder (w/v) at 37 °C for 60 mins. The CRP antigen was biotinylated with an EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotinylation Kit (Thermo Fisher). Next, biotinylated CRP was added to the ELISA plate at concentrations of 2000, 200, 20, 2, 0.2, 0.02, and 0 ng/ml and incubated for 1 hour at 37 °C with the HCAb-coated wells. After the samples were washed, bound CRP was detected by incubation with HRP-labelled streptavidin (1:5000; Sangon Biotech) for 1 hour at 37 °C. Following TMB (Beyotime) colorization (100 μL/well) and concentrated sulfuric acid (Beyotime) termination (100 μL/well), the specific recognition activity was measured, with the signal read at 450 nm using an automated ELISA reader (BioTek).

Structural prediction by AlphaFold3

The CH2–CH3 domain of HCAb (Supplementary Fig. 14a) was obtained via AlphaFold 3 prediction43. The prediction was performed using the AlphaFold-Server (https://alphafoldserver.com), generating five structures for each complex sequence. The evaluation of these structures was conducted using the predicted template modelling (pTM) score and the interface-predicted template modelling (ipTM) score. The pTM score represents the likelihood that the overall predicted folding of the complex is similar to that of the actual structure, whereas the ipTM score reflects the accuracy of the predicted relative positions of the subunits within the complex. Structural models with an ipTM score above 0.7 and a pTM score above 0.5 were selected for further analysis.

Complex formation

The CRP antigen and HCAbs were buffer-exchanged from PBS to 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) separately using an Amicon Ultra-0.5 Centrifugal Filter 10 kDa MWCO (Merck Millipore). The CRP antigen (50 μg/mL) was subsequently incubated with excess HCAbs (100 μg/mL) in 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer at 37 °C for 120 min to generate CRP–HCAb complexes.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

CRP antigen and HCAbs were incubated at 37 °C for 120 min, after which 2.5 µL of each CRP antigen‒antibody complex was applied to an R1.2/1.3 GO 400 grid from QUANTIFOIL (Germany), whose grids were glow discharged (negative polarity, air atmosphere at 0.5 mbar vacuum, 10 mA for 5 seconds). After 30 seconds of incubation, the grids were blotted in Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher) with a blotting time of 3 s at 4 °C and 100% humidity, after which the grids were plunged into liquid ethane cooled with liquid nitrogen. The cryogrids were loaded onto a Titan Krios electron microscope (300 kV; Thermo Fisher), which was equipped with a Gatan K3 summit camera for data collection. Gain-normalized movies of 30 frames were collected with a total dose of 30 e-/pix. Images were recorded at a nominal magnification of 130,000×, corresponding to a pixel size of 0.92/0.855 per pixel. Images were recorded with a set defocus range of 0.5–1.5 µm.

Image processing

Image processing, including motion correction, contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation, particle selection, 2D classification, 3D classification, and final reconstruction, was performed in cryoSPARC v444, the details of which are described in Supplementary Figs. 4–7. Briefly, all movies acquired by cryo-EM were imported, patch motion correction was performed. Afterward, the Blob picker was used to pick particles, which underwent two rounds of 2D classification to reject bad particles, followed by 3D classification. Low-resolution classes were discarded based on visual inspection. Finally, good 3D classes were pooled for 3D auto-refinement.

Model refinement

Initial atomic models for the VHH of HCAb (Supplementary Fig. 14a–d) were obtained from AlphaFold 3 predictions43, which were superimposed on the cryo-EM maps and then refined iteratively through rounds of manual adjustment in Coot45, real space refinement in Phenix46, and structure validation using MolProbity47. The local resolution map was calculated using cryoSPARC v4. Finally, the map resolution (tight mask) estimations of all cryo-EM maps were based on a Fourier shell correlation (FSC) value of 0.143 with tight masking (dynamic mask threshold: 0.2; dynamic mask near: 6 Å; dynamic mask far: 14 Å; and dynamic mask start resolution: 12 Å). The cryo-EM data collection and refinement statistics are provided in Table 1.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis and product of graphs were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 and Adobe Illustrator CS6. The Biolayer interferometry (BLI) binding assays were performed with three independent biological replicates. The ELISA binding assays were performed with two independent biological replicates. To determine the statistical significance of the differences between the groups, Student’s t test was employed. Values of p ≤ 0.01 and p ≤ 0.001 were considered to indicate statistical significance.
