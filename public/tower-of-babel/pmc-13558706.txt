Silicate-bound iron drives abiotic solar energy conversion in terrestrial environments
Authors: Zehong Zhang, Chengpeng Yuan, Wenfeng Huang, Wenyan Wu, Zhemin Shen, Hailiang Dong, Xinde Cao, Xiaoyun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solar energy conversion mediated by natural semiconducting minerals constitutes an abiotic pathway in terrestrial environments, but the drivers and biogeochemical consequences of this process remain poorly understood. Here, based on national-scale field and laboratory measurements, we identify silicate-bound iron, particularly a goethite–kaolinite association, as the primary photoelectrochemical response driver. An S-scheme heterojunction at the goethite–kaolinite interface enhances photogenerated carrier separation, yielding a photocurrent density four times that of pure goethite. A model predicts higher soil photogenerated electron fluxes in warm-humid regions, where intense weathering promotes secondary silicate-bound iron formation. Process-specific theoretical upper-bound estimates suggest that these photogenerated electrons could support alternative metabolic pathways in microorganisms (supplying ~0.01–3.49% of the metabolic electron demand), drive elemental cycling (e.g., donating ~0.16–9.49% of the electrons required for the reductive dissolution of Fe oxides), and promote pollutant degradation (e.g., contributing ~8.20–49.4% of the electrons required for Cr(VI) reduction), highlighting a potential energy flow in surficial Earth systems. These findings reveal a mineral-mediated abiotic pathway for solar energy conversion that fuels biogeochemical processes and sustains ecosystem functions.

Introduction

Solar radiation governs energy exchange within the Sun–Earth system and fundamentally shapes Earth’s surface environment, profoundly influencing the emergence of life1,2, regulating global biogeochemical cycles3,4, and driving long-term climatic evolution5–7. For centuries, biological photosynthesis, which is mediated by plants, cyanobacteria, and other photosynthetic organisms, has been regarded as the dominant natural mechanism for solar energy conversion, serving as the principal driver of energy flow in the biosphere8,9. However, recent studies have demonstrated that natural semiconducting minerals, owing to their intrinsic photoelectric properties, constitute a previously underappreciated yet significant abiotic pathway for solar energy conversion in terrestrial systems10–12.

Soils serve as a major reservoir of natural semiconducting minerals (e.g., hematite [Hem, α-Fe2O3], goethite [Gt, α-FeOOH], and anatase [Ant, TiO2]), which accumulate through weathering and pedogenic processes13,14. Upon solar irradiation, these minerals generate electron-hole pairs capable of driving redox reactions at mineral–water interfaces, effectively converting solar energy to chemical energy15,16. Although semiconducting minerals initiate the photoelectrochemical response, its magnitude and expression are strongly modulated by soil physicochemical properties, particularly mineralogical composition and organic matter (OM). However, the complex heterogeneity of soil matrices has hindered the identification of dominant controls and consequently obscured mechanistic understanding. Moreover, the absence of quantitative frameworks has impeded systematic evaluation of its environmental impact.

Here, we collected soil samples from diverse climatic and geographic regions across China and systematically characterized their physicochemical properties (e.g., pH, ζ potential, soil texture, elemental content, mineral composition, and organic constituents) and photocurrent density (Jph). Combining correlation analysis with machine learning, we identified the key components governing soil photoelectrochemical response and mapped the spatial distribution of soil photogenerated electron flux (Φph) nationwide. After establishing silicate-bound iron (silicate-bound Fe; defined by sequential extraction of soil iron species) as the primary driver of soil photoelectrochemical response, we further resolved its mineral phase composition and analyzed the structural association between silicates and iron minerals. In addition, we synthesized a goethite–kaolinite association (Gt–Kln–A) via hydrothermal treatment as a simplified model designed to mimic the dominant silicate–iron mineral interface identified in soils, and subsequently analyzed its band structure to elucidate the mechanism by which interactions between silicates and iron minerals in soils enhance the photoelectrochemical response. Finally, we discussed the implications of soil photoelectrochemical response for key biogeochemical processes and ecosystem functions.

Results and discussion

Key components governing soil photoelectrochemical response

Several soil components showed significant correlations with Jph (|Pearson’s r| > 0.3, P < 0.001), including Si, P, Fe, Ti, quartz (SiO2), Gt, Ant, structural iron in highly crystalline Fe (hydr)oxides, and silicate-bound Fe (Fig. 1a). Among these, silicate-bound Fe exhibited the strongest positive correlation with Jph (r = 0.59) (Supplementary Fig. 1a), suggesting its dominant role in driving soil photoelectrochemical response. To account for the potential confounding or moderating effects of OM, we performed partial correlation and interaction analyses, which indicated that the silicate-bound Fe–Jph relationship remained robust after adjusting for OM-related variables, with no statistically significant interaction detected after false discovery rate correction (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Moreover, all silicate-bound Fe–enriched soils exhibited increased Jph compared to their pristine counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 1b), further evidencing that soil photoelectrochemical response is determined by silicate-bound Fe. By definition, silicate-bound Fe refers to iron in the crystal lattice of silicate minerals, as well as iron in the lattice of other iron minerals associated with silicates17. Since no diagnostic X-ray diffraction peaks attributable to typical iron-bearing silicate minerals (e.g., fayalite [Fe2SiO4], biotite [Bt, K(Mg, Fe)3AlSi3O10(OH, F)2], and epidote [Ca2Al2Fe3+(SiO4)(Si2O7)O(OH)]) were detected in our soil samples, we infer that the operationally defined silicate-bound Fe fraction is dominated by Fe (hydr)oxides associated with silicates, although trace amounts of iron-bearing silicate minerals cannot be completely ruled out. These associations, ubiquitous in soils18, are formed through electrostatic attraction, ligand exchange, surface complexation, and hydrogen bonding19–21. Kaolinite (Kln, Al2Si2O5(OH)4; r = 0.53, P < 0.001) and Gt (r = 0.77, P < 0.001) showed the strongest correlations with silicate-bound Fe among all silicates and iron minerals, implying that Gt–Kln–A represents the predominant form of silicate-bound Fe in soils. The abundance of silicate-bound Fe varied spatially within the sampled regions, increasing with longitude (r = 0.31, P < 0.001) and decreasing with latitude (r = −0.33, P < 0.001). Rather than representing a universal longitudinal or latitudinal pattern across the entire China, this trend reflects the climatic gradient captured by our sampling design, which ranges from relatively cold-dry to warm-humid environments (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Along this sampled climatic gradient, warm and humid conditions favor chemical weathering and iron leaching–reprecipitation processes, thereby promoting the formation of secondary silicate-bound Fe22–24. This is supported by significant positive linear correlations between silicate-bound Fe and the chemical index of alteration (r = 0.49, P < 0.001), mean annual temperature (r = 0.29, P < 0.001), mean annual precipitation (r = 0.38, P < 0.001), and the aridity index (r = 0.29, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 4). Our Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model, incorporating soil components significantly correlated with Jph (|r| > 0.3, P < 0.001), explained 71% of the variation in Jph with a root mean squared error of 7.43 (Fig. 1b). This model effectively captures the nonlinear relationship between soil components and Jph, demonstrating both robust fitting and generalization capabilities. The contributions of various features to the model output were quantified using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis, with silicate-bound Fe exhibiting a mean |SHAP| value of ~1.9 that significantly surpassed those of other features (Fig. 1c), further indicating its dominant role in regulating soil photoelectrochemical response. Based on the above model, we generated spatial predictions of soil Φph (converted from Jph divided by the elementary charge [e = 1.602 × 10−19 C]) across China (Fig. 1d). Prediction uncertainty ranged from 0.00–0.20 near sampling sites, indicating relatively reliable predictions; from 0.20–0.60 in most regions far from sampling sites, sufficient for trend assessment; and exceeded 0.60 only in extremely remote or topographically complex regions such as the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and the Tarim Basin (Supplementary Fig. 5). The coastal regions of South China, the middle–lower Yangtze River Plain and the hilly areas of Southwest China exhibited higher Φph (predominantly > 1.25 × 1015 e m−2 s−1), whereas the Northeast Plain, the Inner Mongolia Plateau and the arid zones of Northwest China showed lower Φph (predominantly < 1.00 × 1015 e m−2 s−1). This regional heterogeneity of Φph is primarily attributable to variations in soil composition: low-latitude soils exhibit a stronger photoelectrochemical response because of their higher silicate-bound Fe content, as discussed above.

Microstructure of silicate-bound iron in soils

An intimate association between Gt/Hem and Kln was clearly observed in multiple micro-areas of the selected silicate-bound Fe–enriched soil (Fig. 2a–e). Similar silicate-bound Fe structures, where Fe (hydr)oxides are associated with silicates, have been documented in many soils before17,25,26. Such associations reflect the coupled processes of silicate weathering and Fe (hydr)oxide precipitation during pedogenesis. Primary iron-bearing silicate minerals (e.g., Bt, hornblende [(Ca, Na)2–3(Mg, Fe, Al)5(Si, Al)8O22(OH, F)2], and pyroxene [XY(Si, Al)2O6, where X and Y represent cation sites]) undergo chemical weathering, releasing Fe(II) and H4SiO4 into the soil solution. Under oxic conditions, Fe(II) is oxidized to Fe(III) and hydrolyzed to form Fe (hydr)oxides (e.g., ferrihydrite [Fh, Fe10O14(OH)2], Gt, and Hem), which nucleate directly on silicate grain surfaces to produce iron coatings and cementing rims27,28. The iron mineral phase associated with Kln was further identified as Gt rather than Hem (Fig. 2f), which was consistent with the results of correlation analysis. Moreover, Gt–Kln–A was also detected in the soil prior to silicate-bound Fe enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 6), which ruled out the influence of sequential extraction treatments on the identification of mineral components, confirming their natural occurrence and dominance in soils. In typical weathering environments (pH ~5–7), the nucleation and growth of Gt are kinetically favored over those of Hem29,30. The presence of H4SiO4 further inhibits the direct nucleation of Hem, while promoting Gt crystallization by redirecting the transformation pathway of Fh31,32. Simultaneously, Kln is one of the most stable and abundant secondary aluminosilicates formed under intense chemical weathering33,34, providing extensive reactive surfaces for Gt nucleation and adhesion35,36. Recent nanoscale observations further show that clay minerals can regulate the Fe(II)-catalyzed transformation of Fh by serving as templates that favor the oriented attachment and dendritic growth of Gt in Fh–clay mineral heteroaggregates37. An infrared vibrational peak at ~930 cm−1 was observed at all tested silicate-bound Fe-rich sites (Fig. 2g), which was attributed to the presence of inner-sphere Fe–O–Si coordination bonds31,38 and provided direct evidence that the interaction between Kln and Gt involved chemical bonding rather than mere physical adsorption. The Fe–O–Si fitted peaks observed at ~712.3 eV (Fe 2p3/2) and ~725.9 eV (Fe 2p1/2) in X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra for all tested sites further confirm the presence of chemical bonding between Kln and Gt39,40 (Fig. 2h). At the nanoscale, the intimate contact between Kln and Gt facilitated the formation of a heterointerface (Fig. 2i–m and Supplementary Fig. 7), where the extension of Gt lattice fringes to the interface suggested structural continuity, supporting the formation of inner-sphere Fe–O–Si bonds. Unlike physical adsorption, which relies on weak outer-sphere interactions, the formation of inner-sphere Fe–O–Si bonds at the Kln–Gt interface establishes a chemically bonded network that resists desorption and dissolution, thereby imparting enhanced mechanical and thermal stability19,24. Collectively, these results identify Gt–Kln–A as a prevalent and chemically robust form of silicate-bound Fe in soils.

Mechanism of enhanced photoelectrochemical response via mineral interactions

The synthesized Gt–Kln–A sample exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to the (001), (002), and (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{2}02$$\end{document}2¯02) planes of Kln and the (110), (130), and (111) planes of Gt (Fig. 3a), confirming the growth of Gt on Kln. In high-resolution images, nanoscale Gt crystals were observed to nucleate and grow on natural Kln substrates, resulting in the formation of Gt–Kln–A (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c and Supplementary Fig. 9). Similar to Gt–Kln–A in soils, the synthesized Gt–Kln–A sample also formed a heterointerface through the intimate contact between Kln and Gt, making it an ideal model for investigating the mechanism by which mineral interactions enhance the photoelectrochemical response. Under identical testing conditions, the average steady-state Jph of Gt–Kln–A (~400 nA cm−2) was significantly higher than those of both natural Kln (~15 nA cm−2) and synthesized pure Gt (~100 nA cm−2) alone, and its photocurrent response remained highly reproducible and stable over multiple cycles (Fig. 3b), indicating that the separation and transport of photogenerated electron-hole pairs were more efficient and stable in Gt–Kln–A. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy further supported this interpretation, as Gt–Kln–A exhibited a smaller Nyquist semicircle than either Kln or Gt under irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 10), consistent with lower apparent interfacial charge transfer resistance and more efficient photogenerated carrier transport. Although intrinsic Kln is electrically insulating, natural Kln exhibits a weak but detectable photoresponse that may be attributed to the isomorphous substitution of Al by Fe. Specifically, the Fe content measured in our Kln phase (~0.32 wt%) (Supplementary Fig. 8d–g) falls within the typical range reported for natural Kln samples41, and such Fe incorporation may introduce defect states and narrow the band gap (Eg). Both Gt and Gt–Kln–A showed a fluorescence emission peak at ~425 nm (Fig. 3c), which could be attributed to the radiative recombination of electron-hole pairs in surface or crystal defect states42. The absorption of light by Kln was limited to the ultraviolet region, whereas Gt and Gt–Kln–A exhibited broad absorption extending into the visible region (Supplementary Fig. 11a). The Eg values of Kln, Gt, and Gt–Kln–A were further determined by the Tauc method to be ~3.85, ~2.22, and ~2.11 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 11b). The work function (Φ) values of Kln and Gt, calculated from the secondary electron cutoff edge, were ~4.37 and ~4.15 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 11c). The lower Φ of Gt implies that its Fermi level (EF) lies closer to the vacuum level than that of Kln, which is conducive to the transfer of electrons from Gt to Kln across the interface, forming a built-in electric field. In addition, the Φ values of Kln (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0\bar{1}0$$\end{document}01¯0) and Gt (110), calculated by density functional theory (DFT), were 7.82 and 5.33 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 12). Although these values are higher than those obtained from ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements, likely reflecting differences between the ideal facet-specific slab models used in the DFT calculations and the real, heterogeneous sample surfaces probed by UPS, they still show the same trend, with Gt exhibiting a lower Φ than Kln. The valence band maxima (VBMs) of Kln and Gt were positioned at ~3.77 and ~2.01 eV below the EF, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 11d). Based on the Eg, EF, and VBM of Kln and Gt, the band structures of the two minerals in their isolated (non-contact) state were constructed (Fig. 3g).

The charge density difference at the Kln/Gt heterojunction interface demonstrated an electron transfer that occurred to achieve EF equilibration upon contact between the two minerals (Fig. 3d). Therefore, at the Kln/Gt heterojunction interface of Gt–Kln–A, a built-in electric field oriented from Gt toward Kln was formed as a result of electron transfer (Fig. 3g). Under dark conditions, the Fe 2p XPS peak of Gt–Kln–A shifted to higher binding energy relative to that of Gt by 0.18 ± 0.04 eV (mean ± standard deviation, n = 3 independent interfacial sites) (Fig. 3e), whereas the Si 2p and Al 2p XPS peaks shifted to lower binding energy relative to those of Kln by 0.14 ± 0.06 and 0.12 ± 0.06 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 13). These opposite shifts further support the conclusion that, upon contact between the two minerals, electron transfer occurred from Gt to Kln across the Kln/Gt heterojunction interface. Under irradiation, the Fe 2p XPS peak of Gt–Kln–A shifted to lower binding energy relative to that under dark conditions by 0.14 ± 0.04 eV, whereas the Si 2p and Al 2p XPS peaks shifted to higher binding energy relative to those under dark conditions by 0.11 ± 0.05 and 0.15 ± 0.08 eV, respectively. These light-induced shifts indicate that photogenerated electrons were transferred from Kln to Gt under the influence of the built-in electric field, consistent with the characteristics of an S-scheme heterojunction43–45. Briefly, under irradiation, electrons in both the Kln and Gt phases of Gt–Kln–A underwent transitions from the valence band to the conduction band, generating electron-hole pairs, and the built-in electric field drove the transfer of photogenerated electrons from Kln to Gt for interfacial recombination with Gt holes (Fig. 3g). The S-scheme heterojunction formed at the Kln/Gt interface effectively promotes the spatial separation of photogenerated carriers between the two phases, thus significantly improving the photoelectric conversion efficiency of Gt–Kln–A. To distinguish this pathway from a conventional type-II pathway, we evaluated whether the separated photogenerated carriers retained sufficient redox potentials for radical generation. In a type-II pathway, photogenerated holes and electrons would be transferred to the less oxidizing valence band and the less reducing conduction band, respectively, thereby weakening the system’s ability to simultaneously generate hydroxyl (·OH) and superoxide (·O2–) radicals. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spin-trapping analysis revealed signals associated with light-induced ·OH and ·O2– radicals in Gt–Kln–A (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). Relative quantification further showed that, under identical measurement conditions, the EPR signal intensity of the 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO)–·OH adduct for Gt–Kln–A was 1.56 times that of Kln, whereas the EPR signal intensity of the DMPO–·O2– adduct for Gt–Kln–A was 1.63 times that of Gt. These observations indicate that Gt–Kln–A retains the oxidative capacity of the photogenerated holes in Kln and the reductive capacity of the photogenerated electrons in Gt, a feature characteristic of an S-scheme pathway but not readily explained by a conventional type-II pathway. Coupled with the charge transfer evidence described above, these results further confirm that the Kln/Gt interface constitutes an S-scheme heterojunction.

Environmental implications of soil photoelectrochemical response

Soil photoelectrochemical response is primarily mediated by silicate-bound Fe in the form of Gt–Kln–A, which may substantially influence terrestrial biogeochemical processes (Fig. 4). To quantify this influence, we used the predicted soil Φph to estimate the potential contribution of this response to microbial metabolism, elemental cycling, and pollutant degradation (“Methods” and Supplementary Table 4). These estimates should be interpreted as process-specific theoretical upper bounds rather than as actual in situ reaction fractions, because the calculations assume that soil Φph is fully allocated to a given target process without explicitly accounting for additional process-level factors, such as the optical and reactive accessibility of photoactive mineral surfaces, competition among electron sinks, the interfacial effects of OM or biofilm coatings under natural soil conditions, and, for microbial processes, the presence and efficiency of extracellular electron-uptake pathways. In microbial systems, photogenerated electrons can be captured by microbial outer-membrane c-type cytochromes and funneled into their electron transport chains, effectively acting as an auxiliary electron donor alongside traditional organic substrates46,47. This electron influx can drive alternative metabolic pathways, boosting proton-motive force (PMF), adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, and supporting the regeneration of reducing cofactors such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH)11,48–51. Under typical irradiance, photogenerated electrons can supply ~0.01–3.49% of the electrons required for microbial metabolic processes (“Methods” and Supplementary Table 4). Although this fraction appears modest, it preferentially feeds into high-energy, low-flux pathways (e.g., electrotrophic respiration and anabolic NADPH-dependent biosynthesis)49–52, thereby shaping microbial community structure and metabolism. Even minimal electron input can shift redox balance, upregulate outer-membrane c-type cytochromes, and redirect metabolic flux toward pathways conserving PMF and maximizing ATP production47,53. During the diurnal cycle, this supplemental electron supply can induce oscillations in gene transcription related to carbon fixation, nitrogen assimilation, and oxidative stress responses, ultimately enhancing the metabolic flexibility and community resilience of soil microorganisms54–56.

Aerobic conditions at the Earth’s surface limit the biogeochemical cycling of key elements by inhibiting their reduction processes (e.g., the reductive dissolution of Fe and Mn oxides and denitrification), leading to decreased bioavailability of Fe and Mn and hindered removal of nitrogen14,57–59. As a sustainable and renewable electron source, photogenerated electrons can compensate for the deficiency of natural reductants in surface environments, thereby playing a pivotal role in the reduction processes of the aforementioned elements60–62. Based on estimates of the reduction fluxes of Fe and Mn oxides and nitrate, photogenerated electrons can supply ~0.16–9.49% and ~0.22–11.4% of the electrons required for the reductive dissolution of Fe and Mn oxides, respectively, and ~0.34–3.64% of those required for denitrification in surface soils (“Methods” and Supplementary Table 4). Despite these substantial contributions, their role remains underrepresented in current models of surface biogeochemical cycles63.

Furthermore, photogenerated electrons enhance soil self-purification by facilitating the transformation of pollutants17. Solar irradiation significantly promotes the reduction of Cr(VI) in surface soils, as photogenerated electrons can supply ~8.20–49.4% of the electrons required for Cr(VI) reduction (“Methods” and Supplementary Table 4), resulting in diurnal fluctuations in its concentration64. Apart from heavy metal pollutants, the photogenerated electrons of Gt–Kln–A (with a conduction band at −0.50 V vs. the normal hydrogen electrode [NHE]) are thermodynamically capable of reductively degrading organic pollutants like nitrobenzene (with the C6H5NO2/C6H5NH2 redox couple at 0.81 V vs. NHE)65, trichloroethylene (with the C2HCl3/C2H2Cl2 redox couple at 0.42 V vs. NHE)66, and methyl orange (with the C14H14N3NaO3S/C14H16N3NaO3S redox couple at 0.96 V vs. NHE)67. Meanwhile, the photogenerated holes (with a valence band at 3.70 V vs. NHE) can directly oxidize organic pollutants such as phenol (with the C6H5O·/C6H5OH redox couple at 1.40 V vs. NHE)68, aniline (with the C6H5NH·/C6H5NH2 redox couple at 1.02 V vs. NHE)69, and bisphenol A (with the C15H15O2·+/C15H16O2 redox couple at 1.12 V vs. NHE)70, or indirectly degrade them through the generation of reactive oxygen species (Fig. 4).

In conclusion, we identify silicate-bound Fe (predominantly in the form of Gt–Kln–A) as the key driver of soil photoelectrochemical response, underscoring its pivotal biogeochemical role in regulating soil ecosystem functions, such as microbial metabolism, elemental cycling, and pollutant degradation. In natural soils, while this response is primarily governed by silicate-bound Fe, it may also be influenced by OM, microorganisms, and other soil components. These components may accumulate on mineral surfaces as natural interfacial coatings (e.g., OM-rich films, biofilms, extracellular polymeric substance layers, and adhering fine mineral particles), thereby altering the local microenvironment in which soil photoelectrochemical processes occur. Although OM-related variables did not materially confound or significantly modify the silicate-bound Fe–Jph relationship in the present dataset (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3), this statistical result does not preclude a mechanistic role for OM at mineral interfaces. For example, OM can act as a light absorber or an electron donor or acceptor, forming interfacial complexes with silicate-bound Fe71. These complexes can either inhibit soil photoelectrochemical response by shielding mineral surfaces from light absorption or promote it by mediating electron transfer through active functional groups (e.g., phenolic hydroxyls and quinone moieties)72,73. Similarly, microorganisms and their metabolites can modify the surface properties of silicate-bound Fe, thereby influencing electron transfer efficiency, while certain microorganisms may directly exchange electrons with silicate-bound Fe via extracellular electron transfer, significantly modulating soil photoelectrochemical response46,74,75. Furthermore, adhering mineral particles can physically mask photoactive sites, as well as alter local light absorption and scattering; depending on their composition and interfacial contact with silicate-bound Fe, they may also create additional mineral–mineral interfaces, potentially modifying photogenerated carrier separation and recombination pathways76,77. In future work, elucidating these complex co-regulatory mechanisms will be crucial to advancing our understanding of soil photoelectrochemical response. Additionally, future studies should expand the predictive model’s dataset, refine its parameters, and optimize the quantitative framework for soil photoelectrochemical response, thereby enabling more accurate assessments of environmental impacts. Despite certain limitations, our findings reshape the understanding of abiotic solar energy conversion pathways in terrestrial environments.

Methods

Sample collection and preparation

During the summer of 2024 (June–August), we collected 205 surface no-till soil samples (0–2 cm depth) from multiple sites across Hainan, Yunnan, Guizhou, Sichuan, and the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China (Supplementary Fig. 16). These five regions were selected to capture contrasting climatic zones and pedogenic environments relevant to natural soil iron geochemistry, rather than to provide an area-weighted administrative representation of China. Specifically, the sampling sites span tropical, temperate, and arid climates and encompass diverse soil types, including Ferralsols, Acrisols, and Calcisols, thereby establishing a broad environmental framework for evaluating soil photoelectrochemical response across various natural settings. Although this field sampling has limited geographical coverage at the national scale, with the sites restricted to five provinces/regions rather than being uniformly distributed across China, this gradient-oriented design provides sufficient environmental contrast for identifying cross-environmental relationships between soil properties and photoelectrochemical response, which constitutes the primary role of the field dataset in this study. We targeted the 0–2 cm horizon because it is rich in organic matter (OM) and semiconducting minerals, and because light penetration is limited to this thin surface layer, which most directly reflects soil photoelectrochemical response. All samples were air-dried at room temperature, passed through a 100-mesh sieve, and stored in a desiccator.

Sample characterization

All samples were systematically characterized in terms of their physicochemical properties (including pH, ζ potential, soil texture, elemental content, mineral composition, and organic constituents) and photocurrent density (Jph). pH was measured in a 1:2.5 (w/v) soil-water suspension using a calibrated pH meter after stirring and equilibration for 30 min. ζ potential was determined using a nanoparticle analyzer (Anton Paar Litesizer 500, Austria) in a 1:100 (w/v) soil-water suspension following 30 min of ultrasonic dispersion. Particle size fractions (clay, silt, and sand) were quantified in a 1:100 (w/v) soil-water suspension using a laser particle size analyzer (Malvern Panalytical Mastersizer 3000, UK) after ultrasonic pretreatment to minimize aggregation. Elemental content was analyzed using an X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (Rigaku ZSX Primus III+, Japan) equipped with a Rh target X-ray source, applying theoretical α-coefficients to correct for inter-element absorption-enhancement effects and quantifying major and minor constituents from fluorescence intensities. Prior to analysis, samples were fused at 1050 °C with a Li2B4O7–LiBO2 flux (1:10 [w/w] sample-to-flux ratio), using LiNO3 as an oxidizer and a trace of LiBr as a fluxing/releasing agent, to produce homogeneous glass disks.

Given the widespread distribution and high abundance of iron-based semiconducting minerals in soils, we applied a sequential extraction method to operationally fractionate soil iron into four species: ion-exchangeable iron (i.e., iron bound by ion exchange), surface-bound iron (i.e., iron complexed by surface hydroxyl groups) and structural iron in poorly crystalline Fe (hydr)oxides (e.g., ferrihydrite, Fe10O14(OH)2), structural iron in highly crystalline Fe (hydr)oxides (oxide-structural Fe; e.g., hematite [Hem, α-Fe2O3] and goethite [Gt, α-FeOOH]), and silicate-bound iron (silicate-bound Fe; i.e., iron in the crystal lattice of silicate minerals and iron in the lattice of other iron minerals associated with silicates)78–80. Samples were sequentially extracted with 1 M CaCl2, 1 M HCl, 6 M HCl, and a mixed solution of 1.3 M HF–1.8 M H2SO4 at a 1:40 (w/v) soil-to-extractant ratio. After each extraction, concentrations of each iron species were quantified using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific iCAP PRO, USA). Mineral composition was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Ultima IV, Japan) equipped with a Cu Kα X-ray source, and each mineral phase was quantified via the reference intensity ratio method with corundum (Al2O3) as the standard.

Organic constituents were assessed in terms of soil organic carbon (SOC), dissolved organic carbon (DOC), and dissolved organic matter (DOM) optical indices, including: the absorption coefficient at 355 nm (α355, reflecting the concentration of chromophoric DOM); the specific ultraviolet absorbance at 254 nm (SUVA254, calculated as the absorption coefficient at 254 nm [m−1] divided by the DOC concentration [mg C L−1], indicating DOM aromaticity); and the ratio of absorption coefficients at 250 and 365 nm (E2/E3, serving as a proxy for DOM molecular weight). SOC was measured using an elemental analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific FlashSmart NC SOIL, USA), with samples pre-acidified with 10 wt% HCl to remove inorganic carbon before analysis. DOC (extracted at a 1:10 [w/v] soil-to-water ratio) was measured using a total organic carbon analyzer (Analytik Jena multi N/C 2100S, Germany). DOM optical indices were determined using an ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2600, Japan) with water as a blank, and scattering effects were corrected during calculations by applying the absorption coefficient at 700 nm.

Jph was measured without external bias using a computer-controlled electrochemical workstation (Metrohm Autolab PGSTAT302N, Switzerland) in a standard three-electrode configuration under air mass 1.5 global (AM 1.5G) simulated solar irradiation provided by a xenon lamp–filter system. AM 1.5G irradiation was selected because it represents the standardized terrestrial solar spectrum at mid-latitudes (as defined by the American Society for Testing and Materials G173 standard), ensuring that the measured Jph are representative of real-world conditions and directly comparable across different studies. The setup included a sample-coated indium tin oxide (ITO) working electrode, a platinum foil counter electrode, an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, and 0.5 M Na2SO4 as the supporting electrolyte. Working electrodes were prepared by ultrasonically dispersing 10 mg of sample in 1 mL of 0.2 wt% Nafion aqueous solution for 30 min to yield a homogeneous slurry, which was then drop-cast onto a 1 cm × 1 cm ITO substrate (2 mg loading) and air-dried at room temperature.

Machine learning model construction

We compiled geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude), physicochemical properties, and Jph of all samples into a dataset, and then split it into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets based on their geographic distribution. An Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model was trained to predict Jph from soil compositional and physicochemical features. XGBoost was selected for its robust performance on relatively small datasets and its built-in regularization mechanisms that effectively mitigate overfitting82.

The XGBoost model was constructed through the following steps: (i) features exhibiting a strong correlation with Jph (|r| > 0.3) were identified through a correlation heatmap, including Si, P, Fe, Ti, quartz (Qtz, SiO2), Gt, anatase (Ant, TiO2), oxide-structural Fe, and silicate-bound Fe (Fig. 1a); (ii) the XGBoost model was constructed and evaluated in Python using the scikit-learn package, with the training dataset employed for hyperparameter optimization and final model fitting, and the test dataset used to assess the generalization capability of the trained model. Specifically, we conducted a grid search combined with five-fold cross-validation (CV) using scikit-learn, with the root mean squared error (RMSE) from the CV serving as the evaluation criterion to identify the optimal hyperparameter configuration among 60 candidate combinations (Supplementary Table 5). After determining the optimal hyperparameters, we trained the corresponding XGBoost model for final prediction and performance validation. The optimized XGBoost model demonstrated a strong correlation between the observed and predicted Jph on the test set, explaining 71% of the variation with an RMSE of 7.43 (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, we used the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) library in Python to visualize the effects of various features on Jph (Fig. 1c).

The spatial mapping of soil photogenerated electron flux (Φph) in China was performed using a predictor-based spatial modeling framework comprising the following procedures: (i) data on soil elemental and mineral contents were collected from public sources: elemental data from the Geochemical Atlas of China (published by the China Geological Survey), and mineral data from the global mineral abundance maps in ref. 83; (ii) since data for Ant, oxide-structural Fe, and silicate-bound Fe were not available from public sources, three models were developed to impute these values based on the collected soil elemental and mineral content data (details in Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 17); (iii) the trained XGBoost model was applied to predict soil Jph using Si, P, Fe, Ti, Qtz, Gt, Ant, oxide-structural Fe, and silicate-bound Fe as input features; (iv) Jph was converted to Φph by dividing it by the elementary charge (e = 1.602 × 10−19 C), and the spatial distribution of soil Φph was then mapped at a 1 km × 1 km resolution using kernel density analysis in ArcGIS (Fig. 1d).

Additionally, uncertainty analysis of the predictions was conducted through the following process: (i) model-inherent uncertainty was evaluated using 1000 propagated realizations under an end-to-end bootstrap framework with upstream residual propagation. In each realization, the upstream imputation models for Ant, oxide-structural Fe, and silicate-bound Fe, together with the downstream Jph model, were refitted using bootstrap-resampled training data. Empirical held-out residual pools from the three upstream imputation models were mean-centered and resampled, and the resulting perturbations were propagated through the stacked workflow into the final Jph predictions. The variance of the fully propagated Jph prediction distribution was used to represent model-inherent uncertainty; (ii) feature-extrapolation uncertainty was measured as the squared Mahalanobis distance of each prediction grid cell relative to the multivariate training-feature distribution; (iii) spatial-residual uncertainty was quantified as the ordinary kriging variance of the training-dataset residuals from the Jph model across the prediction domain; (iv) penalty terms were computed for sparse spatial sampling and extreme feature values, using the normalized mean distance to the five nearest training samples and the maximum standardized deviation of selected feature values from the corresponding training-data means, respectively; (v) all uncertainty components and penalty metrics were min–max scaled to [0, 1], and then blended using grid-cell-specific weights that interpolate between interpolation- and extrapolation-favoring schemes. The extrapolation degree of each grid cell was defined as the maximum of its normalized feature-extrapolation uncertainty and normalized spatial-sampling penalty. The fused uncertainty score was then further adjusted by the feature-penalty term; (vi) the spatial distribution of uncertainty was mapped at a 1 km × 1 km resolution using kernel density analysis in ArcGIS (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Soil silicate-bound iron identification

Based on the correlation heatmap, kaolinite (Kln, Al2Si2O5(OH)4; r = 0.53) and Gt (r = 0.77) exhibit the strongest associations with silicate-bound Fe among all silicates and iron minerals. To elucidate the speciation of silicate-bound Fe in soil systems, we selected a soil sample from Hainan Province, China, which is rich in silicate-bound Fe, Kln, and Gt. After further enriching silicate-bound Fe by sequential extraction, we conducted a comprehensive characterization, including phase identification of the silicate-bound Fe mineral components and systematic analysis of the association patterns between silicate and iron phases.

After carbon coating, mineral compositional mapping of the sample was performed using a scanning electron microscope (Tescan MIRA3, Czech Republic) equipped with a Tescan Integrated Mineral Analyzer (TIMA) liberation analysis module (Fig. 2a–e). To obtain a flat cross-section for observing the internal structure of soil particles, the sample was prepared through cold mounting followed by polishing. Because Hem and Gt share nearly identical Fe/O ratios, and their energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) spectral signatures and backscattered-electron intensities strongly overlap, the TIMA system’s EDS-based classification algorithm lacks sufficient contrast to reliably differentiate between these two phases. Therefore, a 1 mm × 1 mm localized region rich in silicate-bound Fe within the sample (Fig. 2b) was analyzed using a micro-area X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku SmartLab SE, Japan) equipped with a Cu Kα X-ray source to further identify the iron mineral phases present (Fig. 2f). The iron phases were subsequently confirmed by comparison with the standard powder diffraction files from the International Centre for Diffraction Data. Moreover, the sample was scanned prior to silicate-bound Fe enrichment using a confocal Raman microscope (Renishaw inVia, UK) to confirm that the silicate-bound Fe mineral components remained unaffected by the sequential extraction treatments (Supplementary Fig. 6). Kln was recognized by the O–Al–O symmetric bending mode at ~141 cm−184, while Gt was recognized by the Fe–OH symmetric bending mode at ~300 cm−1 and the Fe–OH symmetric stretching mode at ~395 cm−185. Selected sites within the silicate-bound Fe-rich localized region of the sample (Fig. 2b) were examined using a micro-Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iN10, USA) to determine the bonding mode of the silicate and iron phases (Fig. 2g). Kln was identified by the Si–O–Al bending vibration at ~694 cm−1, the Al–OH bending vibration at ~912 cm−1, the Si–O–Al stretching vibration at ~1010 cm−1, and the Si–O–Si stretching vibration at ~1030 cm−186. Gt was identified by the Fe–OH out-of-plane bending vibration at ~795 cm−1 and the Fe–OH bending vibration at ~890 cm−187. The Fe–O–Si stretching vibration at ~930 cm−131,38 confirmed the chemical bonding between Kln and Gt. To further verify the chemical bonding, these sites were also tested using a micro-focused X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific K-Alpha, USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source, followed by peak fitting analysis performed in the XPS Peak 4.1 software with the Gaussian-Lorentzian model (Fig. 2h). The Fe 2p spectrum was deconvoluted into the following components39,40: the Fe–O–Fe 2p3/2 peak at ~711.0 ± 0.2 eV, the Fe–O–Si 2p3/2 peak at ~712.3 ± 0.2 eV, the Fe 2p3/2 satellite at ~718.0–721.0 eV, the Fe–O–Fe 2p1/2 peak at ~724.6 ± 0.2 eV, the Fe–O–Si 2p1/2 peak at ~725.9 ± 0.2 eV, and the Fe 2p1/2 satellite at ~731.0–734.0 eV. To observe the goethite–kaolinite association (Gt–Kln–A) in soils at the nanoscale, a cross-sectional lamella was prepared from site 1 of the sample using a focused ion beam–scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific Scios 2 DualBeam, USA) equipped with a Ga ion source (Fig. 2i). Prior to ion-beam cutting, a W/C protective layer was deposited at the cutting site. Subsequently, the Kln–Gt interface of Gt–Kln–A in the cross-sectional lamella was observed using a transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos F200X G2 TEM, USA) (Fig. 2j–m and Supplementary Fig. 7).

Mineral interaction-enhanced photoelectrochemical response mechanism

To elucidate the mechanism by which interactions between silicates and iron minerals enhance the photoelectrochemical response, we synthesized Gt–Kln–A via a hydrothermal method as a simplified model designed to mimic the dominant silicate–iron mineral interface identified in soils, and subsequently analyzed its band structure. The hydrothermal synthesis procedure was adapted from the previously reported Gt synthesis method88, using natural Kln as the substrate to enable the growth of Gt on Kln at a theoretical mass ratio of 1:1. Additionally, pure Gt was synthesized as a control in the absence of Kln, following the same procedure. Morphological characteristics of Gt–Kln–A were observed using the transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos F200X G2 TEM, USA) (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). To minimize electron-beam damage to Kln, the Kln–Gt interface of Gt–Kln–A was further observed using a spherical-aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (JEOL JEM-ARM200F, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV (Supplementary Fig. 9). Phase composition of Gt–Kln–A was identified using an X-ray diffractometer (Shimadzu XRD-6100, Japan) equipped with a Cu Kα X-ray source (Fig. 3a).

The separation and transport of photogenerated electron-hole pairs in Kln, Gt, and Gt–Kln–A were evaluated by transient photocurrent measurements, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. Transient photocurrent responses were recorded using the same method as that employed for the Jph testing of soil samples (Fig. 3b). To elucidate the possible reasons for the weak photoresponse of natural Kln, the isomorphous substitution by Fe ions in the Kln phase of Gt–Kln–A was investigated using the transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos F200X G2 TEM, USA) (Supplementary Fig. 8d–f). Furthermore, after digestion, the Fe content of natural Kln was quantified using the inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific iCAP PRO, USA) to confirm the occurrence of isomorphous substitution (Supplementary Fig. 8g). EIS spectra were collected in the dark and under AM 1.5G simulated solar irradiation using the computer-controlled electrochemical workstation (Metrohm Autolab PGSTAT302N, Switzerland) with the same three-electrode configuration at 1.0 V vs. the reversible hydrogen electrode, over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz, with an alternating current perturbation amplitude of 10 mV (Supplementary Fig. 10). PL spectra were acquired using a steady-state/lifetime spectrofluorometer (Edinburgh FLS1000, UK) at room temperature, with the samples excited by a 340 nm pulsed laser (Fig. 3c). The band structure of Gt–Kln–A was elucidated by calculating the band gap (Eg), Fermi level (EF), and valence band maximum (VBM) values for Kln and Gt, and by identifying the heterojunction type at the Kln/Gt interface. Absorbance was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 950, USA) equipped with an integrating sphere detector (Supplementary Fig. 11a), and Eg was subsequently calculated from the absorbance data by the Tauc method89 (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Work function (Φ) was measured using an ultraviolet photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB XI+, USA) equipped with a He I ultraviolet source (21.22 eV), and the EF of the samples was thereby determined (Supplementary Fig. 11c). Samples were spin-coated onto ITO substrates and cleaned via atomic argon-ion bombardment prior to measurement. VBM, referenced to EF, was measured using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi, USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (Supplementary Fig. 11d). Spin-polarized DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package, employing plane-wave basis sets and projector augmented-wave potentials. The exchange-correlation potential was described using the generalized gradient approximation with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof parametrization, combined with Grimme’s DFT-D3 van der Waals correction. The cutoff energy for the plane-wave basis set was set to 400 eV. The electronic convergence criterion was set to 1 × 10−5 eV for the self-consistent solution of the Kohn-Sham equations. The Brillouin zone was sampled at the Γ point. All structures were fully relaxed until the residual forces on each atom were less than 0.05 eV Å−1. A Hubbard U correction was applied to the Fe 3d states using the Dudarev approach, with an effective U value of 4.0 eV. Electron transfer at the Kln/Gt heterojunction interface was monitored in situ under dark conditions and 300 W xenon lamp irradiation using the X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi, USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 13). To assess the reproducibility and uncertainty of the binding energy shifts, measurements were performed at three independent Gt–Kln–A interfacial sites. The shifts under dark conditions were calculated by comparing Gt–Kln–A with the corresponding reference minerals, with Fe 2p referenced to Gt, and Si 2p and Al 2p referenced to Kln. The light-induced shifts were calculated by comparing each irradiated spectrum with its paired dark spectrum at the same interfacial site. The shifts reported in the main text are presented as mean ± standard deviation across three independent interfacial sites. Furthermore, the generation of hydroxyl (·OH) and superoxide (·O2−) radicals by Gt–Kln–A was evaluated in situ under both 300 W xenon lamp irradiation and dark conditions using an electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrometer (Bruker ELEXSYS-II E500, Germany) to determine whether the redox capacities of its valence band holes and conduction band electrons conform to the characteristics of the previously confirmed Kln/Gt heterojunction (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). The samples were ultrasonically dispersed for 10 min, after which 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) was added as a ·OH and ·O2− spin-trapping agent. For a relative quantitative comparison of the light-induced EPR signals, all sample suspensions were prepared at the same concentration of 0.5 mg mL–1 and analyzed under identical spin-trapping, irradiation, and instrumental conditions. After baseline correction, the light-induced DMPO–·OH and DMPO–·O2− signals were quantified by a fixed-window double integration of the resolved signal features, followed by the summation of the absolute integrated areas. The resulting values were used for a mass-normalized relative comparison of the detectable EPR spin-adduct signals.

Soil photoelectrochemical response environmental implications assessment

We presented a schematic illustration showing how soil photoelectrochemical response participates in terrestrial biogeochemical processes (Fig. 4). Moreover, using the predicted soil Φph values, we estimated the potential contribution of soil photoelectrochemical response to microbial metabolism, elemental cycling, and pollutant degradation in surface soils, including heterotrophic respiration, the reductive dissolution of Fe and Mn oxides, denitrification, and Cr(VI) reduction (Supplementary Table 4). The predicted soil Φph was derived from a machine learning model trained on experimentally measured soil photocurrent responses under standardized photoelectrochemical testing conditions. Therefore, this flux reflects the integrated soil photoelectrochemical response, encompassing the effects of soil composition and the collective dynamics of carrier generation, separation, transport, and recombination, which ultimately determine the net electron output. To the extent that sample-intrinsic factors, including mineral associations, surface shielding, and retained organic coatings, modulate the experimentally measured photocurrent responses, their effects are implicitly incorporated into the predicted soil Φph. These contribution rates were then estimated by converting the predicted soil Φph to reaction-specific photogenerated electron redox fluxes based on reaction stoichiometry, and by comparing them with reported redox fluxes under dark conditions. These calculations aim to establish process-specific theoretical upper bounds, assuming that soil Φph is fully allocated to a given target process, rather than to determine its exact in situ partitioning among competing electron sinks. Accordingly, they do not explicitly resolve additional process-level factors, such as the optical and reactive accessibility of photoactive mineral surfaces, competition among electron sinks, the interfacial effects of OM or biofilm coatings under natural soil conditions, or, for microbial processes, the presence and efficiency of extracellular electron-uptake pathways.

The estimation of each reaction was carried out through the following steps: (i) the thermodynamic feasibility of photogenerated electrons participating in the reaction was evaluated by comparison with the standard electrode potential; (ii) according to the reaction stoichiometry, the soil Φph (ranging from [0.56–2.07] × 1015 e m−2 s−1) was converted to a photogenerated electron redox flux (in units of mol m−2 s−1); (iii) the reported reaction rate (under dark conditions) was converted to a redox flux (in units of mol m−2 s−1) according to its reaction system; (iv) the contribution of soil photoelectrochemical response was calculated by dividing the photogenerated electron redox flux by the total redox flux (i.e., the sum of redox fluxes under light and dark conditions).
