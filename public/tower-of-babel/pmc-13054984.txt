Decoding anoikis-related genes in lung adenocarcinoma brainmetastasis via single-cell RNA sequencing: CD44-mediated functions
Authors: Zheng Tao, Shuang Li, Haifeng Xia, Zhengzheng Ni, Kai Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnoikis, a form of cell death triggered by detachment from cell-cell contact, is closely associated with tumor invasion and metastasis. After LUAD has metastasized to the brain, the prognosis is extremely poor. This study aims to investigate the role of Anoikis-related genes (ARGs) in the progression of lung cancer brain metastasis.MethodsWe analyzed six lung cancer and six brain metastasis samples from a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset. Potential molecular mechanisms were explored through cell communication and transcription factor regulation. AddModuleScore was applied to score cell types based on 338 ARGs. A cluster with the most significant score difference between the lung tumor (tumor group) and brain metastasis (mBrain group) was selected for pseudotime analysis. Epithelial differentiation genes were identified and intersected with ARGs. Using bulk RNA sequencing and patient survival data, a prognostic model was developed. Clinical samples were collected to validate differential expression between the two groups. ARGs associated with metastasis were knocked down to confirm their molecular functions.ResultsEpithelial cells exhibited the greatest difference between the two groups in terms of AddModuleScore and cell proportions. Cell communication analysis revealed strong enrichment of VEGF signaling. Transcription factor analysis identified distinct regulatory networks. A prognostic model based on bulk RNA data was constructed, with key genes including ITGA2, ITGA3, CD44, and BIRC3. ARGs were significantly associated with immune infiltration and immune checkpoint modulation. Clinical validation revealed that CD44 was significantly overexpressed in brain metastasis tissues. Knocking down CD44 significantly impaired cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.ConclusionOur findings indicate that epithelial cells undergo profound changes during lung cancer brain metastasis. ARGs, particularly CD44, play a critical role in this process and have prognostic significance for lung cancer brain metastasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15485-y.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide, characterized by high mortality and incidence rates [1]. It is highly aggressive and prone to recurrence, posing significant clinical challenges [2]. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common histological subtype of lung cancer and is notably inclined to metastasize to the brain, which leads to a particularly poor prognosis [3]. Despite substantial advancements in screening and treatment methods for NSCLC, the first-line treatment efficacy remains limited, especially in metastatic cases, resulting in suboptimal cure and survival rates [4]. There is an urgent need to identify novel molecular biomarkers and elucidate the mechanisms of metastasis to develop more effective therapeutic strategies for patients with advanced-stage disease.

Evasion of programmed cell death and dissemination to distant sites are hallmark characteristics of metastatic tumor cells. Anoikis, a form of programmed cell death triggered when cells lose attachment to the extracellular matrix (ECM) or neighboring cells, plays a critical role in maintaining tissue homeostasis [5].

It serves as a defense mechanism, leading to the death of displaced cells, which is essential for cellular plasticity and adhesion.

However, metastatic cancer cells acquire resistance to anoikis, enabling their survival during detachment and subsequent transport through the circulation. This resistance arises from alterations in metabolic pathways (e.g., glucose metabolism, fatty acid uptake/synthesis), activation of pro-survival signaling, and induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), all of which confer the ability to survive and metastasize within the hostile tumor microenvironment [6]. Consequently, malignant cells exemplify anoikis resistance. Increasing evidence underscores the crucial link between tumor invasion, metastasis, and anoikis resistance [7, 8]. For example, genes such as Solute Carrier Family 2 Member 1 (SLC2A1) and Sphingosine Kinase 1 (SPHK1) have been implicated in regulating tumor cell viability, migration, anoikis, and anoikis resistance [9]. In addition, modulation of anoikis sensitivity has been shown to enhance immune cell-mediated cytotoxicity [10]. Although some studies have explored correlations between Anoikis-Related Genes (ARGs) signatures and cancer prognosis [7, 10, 11], the specific roles and underlying molecular mechanisms of ARGs in the context of LUAD brain metastasis remain poorly understood and warrant further investigation.

By reviewing methods for predictive modeling in published studies [12–14], we developed appropriate models aligned with our research objectives. Similar to previous approaches [15], constructing such models can facilitate clinical translation in the future. To explore tumor heterogeneity between primary LUAD tumors and their brain metastases, we employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology. We compared scRNA-seq data from six primary lung tumor samples and six brain metastasis samples, both obtained from the GEO database. Our specific objectives were to: (1) Identify cell types and states undergoing significant changes during brain metastasis, with a particular focus on anoikis resistance, as indicated by the ARG activity score (AddModuleScore for 338 ARGs); (2) Map alterations in cell-cell communication networks and transcription factor (TF) regulatory landscapes between primary and metastatic sites; (3) Identify metastasis-relevant ARGs by intersecting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in key cell clusters (identified by ARG scoring) with ARGs; (4) Construct and validate a prognostic model using bulk RNA-seq data combined with survival information based on these ARGs; (5) Investigate the correlations between the prognostic ARG signature, immune infiltration, and drug sensitivity; and (6) Clinically validate key model genes and functionally characterize their roles in proliferation, migration, and invasion through patient samples and in vitro knockdown experiments.

By integrating multi-omics data and functional validation, this study aims to uncover new insights into the role of ARGs, particularly CD44, in LUAD brain metastasis. Ultimately, we aim to establish a clinically valuable prognostic model and identify potential therapeutic targets to improve survival outcomes for LUAD patients, especially those with brain metastases.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and pre-processing

The scRNA-seq dataset (GSE131907) was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), which includes six lung adenocarcinoma (tumor group) samples and six brain metastasis (mBrain group) samples. The data were analyzed using the R packages Seurat and harmony. The bulk RNA-seq dataset was retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). consisting of 477 LUAD patient cases. An additional bulk RNA-seq dataset, GSE41271, also sourced from GEO, includes survival and RNA-seq data from 182 LUAD patients. The TCGA dataset was used to construct the prognostic model, while GSE41271 served for model validation. A total of 338 genes associated with anoikis were downloaded from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) and cited in a previous study [16]. The gene name was provided in Supplementary File 1.

Workflow for cell clustering and annotation

Initial data processing involved log normalization of all samples to ensure uniformity. Distinct cell subpopulations were characterized using cluster-specific marker genes identified via the FindAllMarkers algorithm. Cell type annotation was performed by referencing marker genes from the Cell Markers database and relevant literature [4, 17]. Cross-dataset consistency was maintained by applying identical computational parameters to each dataset.

Evaluation of immune feature

Multiple computational methods were employed to quantify immune cell infiltration within tumors., including the TIDE [18], TIMER [19], ESTIMATE [20], and CIBSORT [21] algorithms. These tools utilize gene expression deconvolution and predefined gene set scores to estimate the abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune cells (TIICs). RNA-seq data were normalized using standard methods, while microarray data underwent quantile normalization.

Cell-cell communication

Cell-cell communication networks were analyzed using the CellChat platform (v1.6.0) for paired primary LUAD (tumor group, n = 6) and brain metastasis (mBrain group, n = 6) samples. The default ligand-receptor interaction database (CellChatDB.human) was used to quantify signaling probabilities (α = 0.05) between characterized cellular subpopulations. Differentially signaling pathways between the tumor and mBrain groups were identified using permutation testing (1,000 iterations), with FDR-adjusted p-values < 0.01 and |log2(fold-change)| >0.5 as significance thresholds.

Pseudo-temporal trajectory analysis

Trajectory analysis, a key methodology in single-cell RNA sequencing, was employed to deduce potential dynamic temporal relationships based on the transcriptomic profiles of cells. Unlike traditional time-series experiments, which rely on physical time markers, this technique reconstructs temporal dimensions by simulating continuous cell-state lineages representing developmental or differentiation processes [22].

Prognostic model construction and validation

Initial candidate screening for survival-associated transcripts was performed using univariable Cox proportional hazards regression (P < 0.01) [23]. Feature selection was conducted using LASSO-penalized regression with the glmnet package (v4.1–8) in R [21].

The risk score was calculated as:

Risk Score = ∑i₌₁ᵏ (βi·Expression_i) [24].

Where βi denotes regression coefficients and Expression_i represents normalized expression. The transcriptomic data from 477 patients were sorted based on model gene expression and risk coefficients to calculate risk scores. Patients were classified into high-risk and low-risk groups using the median as a cutoff. Patients with risk scores above the median were assigned to the high-risk group, while those below the median were assigned to the low-risk group.

Drug sensitivity analysis and molecular docking

Drug response phenotypes were predicted using the oncoPredict R package (v0.2.2), leveraging GDSC2 training data (n = 969 cell lines; 198 compounds). Transcriptomic risk scores from our model were integrated as covariates for IC50 prediction via elastic-net regression (α = 0.5, 10-fold cross-validation).

Transcriptomic risk scores from our model were incorporated as covariates for IC50 prediction via elastic-net regression (α = 0.5, 10-fold cross-validation).

Protein targets were obtained from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/) using canonical gene identifiers, with structures filtered by ≥ 95% sequence identity. Ligand 3D conformations of the screened compounds were acquired from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Semi-flexible docking simulations were conducted using the CB-Dock 2.0 web server.

Cell culture and transfection

Normal bronchial epithelial cells (HBE) and lung adenocarcinoma cell lines (A549, H1299, PC9, and HCC827) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). All cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS (Excell) and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. SiRNA targeting CD44 (Si-CD44#1 and Si-CD44#2) was obtained from Fubio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China. A control siRNA (si-NC) was used as a negative control.

qRT-PCR assay

RNA was extracted from lung tumor and brain metastasis tissues using the TRIzol method. Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a qRT-PCR Kit. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed with gene-specific primers [25].

Wound healing assay

HCC827 and A549 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and incubated until adherent. Cells were then scraped using a 1000 µL pipette tip. The cells were divided into untreated and Si-CD44-transfected groups. After scratching, initial images were captured. At 0 h and 24 h, additional images were taken. The wound area was quantified using ImageJ software, and the percentage of wound closure was calculated to assess cell migration capacity.

Transwell assay

Transwell assays were performed using a 24-well chamber system. For each well, 600 µL of DMEM medium containing 3 × 10⁴ HCC827 or A549 cells (untransfected and transfected) was added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with medium containing 10% FBS. Cells were incubated for 24 h. Non-migrated cells were removed, and the migrated cells were fixed with 4% formalin for 10 min, followed by crystal violet staining for 15 min. Three randomly selected fields were photographed. For cell invasion assays, the transwell chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence staining of paraffin sections, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections underwent antigen retrieval. After washing with PBS, sections were blocked with 10% normal serum and incubated with an anti-CD44 primary antibody (1:100-1:400 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washing, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody (1:400) and DAPI (for nuclear staining) for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were mounted with anti-fade medium, coverslipped, and imaged using a confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.3.3) and SPSS (v23.0), with graphs generated using GraphPad Prism (v10.0). The normality of continuous data (e.g., gene expression levels, cell counts) was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data that followed a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

For group comparisons, the independent samples t-test or paired samples t-test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with an FDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships between gene expression and immune infiltration indices.

Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves, with differences in overall survival (OS) between high-risk and low-risk groups evaluated using the Log-rank test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to assess the prognostic performance of the gene model, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. All tests were two-tailed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Cell annotation and visualization

To examine the cellular heterogeneity within the lung tumor and brain metastasis microenvironments, we conducted scRNA-seq analysis on 12 samples, comprising 6 primary lung tumor tissues (tumor group) and 6 brain metastatic tissues (mBrain group) from GSE131907. Clinical data for these samples are summarized in Supplementary file 2. Quality control (QC) was performed using Seurat’s NormalizeData function to filter out low-quality cells (e.g., cells with > 10% mitochondrial gene expression, < 200 detected genes, or < 500 unique molecular identifiers). After batch effect correction, normalization, and QC, we retained 26,332 high-quality cells from an initial 40,307 for further analysis. Dimensionality reduction and clustering revealed 20 distinct cellular clusters (Fig. 2A, UMAP), which were classified into 8 broad cell types. These clusters were annotated using canonical marker genes: B cells (CD79A, MS4A1), T cells (CD3D, CD3E, TRAC), macrophages (CD163, CD14), fibroblasts (FGF7, MME), NK cells (FGFBP2, CX3CR1), mast cells (TPSAB1, TPSB2), endothelial cells (PECAM1, VWF), and epithelial cells (CDH1, CLDN4). Figure 2B indicates the differential expression profiles of these marker genes within the 20 clusters. From these expression profiles, the clusters were unmistakably annotated into eight major cell types, whose final classification are depicted in UMAP space (Fig. 2C). Figure 2D graphs expression levels of lineage-defining markers among the eight annotated cell types. Figure 2E then identifies the top four most highly expressed genes in each cellular compartment.

Transcription factor regulatory network by SCENIC

To compare the transcription factor (TF) regulatory networks of epithelial cells in the tumor and mBrain groups, we analyzed a total of 5,751 epithelial cells across all 12 samples, with 6 samples from each group. This approach allowed us to investigate cell-type-specific (epithelial) regulatory programs during the progression of lung cancer to brain metastasis.

We first plotted regulon filtering curves to assess the reliability of TF regulons (Fig. 5A). By plotting regulon rank against the AUCell activity metric, we were able to eliminate low-confidence regulons, ensuring that only robust TF-target gene modules were retained for further analysis. These high-confidence regulons provided a solid foundation for examining regulatory differences between the two groups.

Overall, Fig. 5 highlights that brain metastasis of lung cancer is associated with extensive transcriptional regulatory reprogramming.

Construction of a prognostic model related to lung cancer brain metastasis and ARG

To identify significant genes associated with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and brain metastasis, we first screened for differential expressed genes (DEGs) between mBrain group epithelial cells and tumor group epithelial cells. With a cutoff fold - change of 1.2 (logFC = 0.286) and p < 0.05, we discovered 1776 DEGs. We then intersected the 1776 DEGs with angiogenesis - related genes (ARG) and identified 73 overlapping genes (Fig. 6A).

Drug sensitivity analysis and molecular docking

To explore potential therapeutic strategies targeting the risk model, we first performed drug sensitivity analysis between low risk and high group. As illustrated in Fig. 8A, several candidate drugs (PRT06607, AZD046, AMG319, AZD8186, CZC24832, and SB216763) were identified, and significant differences in drug sensitivity were observed between the high and low risk group. Among these drugs, PRT06607 and SB216763 showed the most prominent differences between the two groups, so they were selected for subsequent molecular docking experiments. The 3D molecular structures of PRT06607 and SB216763 are presented in Figs. 8B-C, respectively. Molecular docking results (Figs. 8D-K) revealed the binding affinities of these two drugs with different target molecules (ITGA2, CD44, ITGA3, BIRC3). Specifically, ITGA3, CD44, and BIRC3 exhibited relatively strong binding capacities with both PRT06607 and SB216763, with binding free energies (e.g., for ITGA3-PRT06607: −8.4 kcal/mol; ITGA3-SB216763: −8.6 kcal/mol) indicating favorable interactions. In contrast, ITGA2 showed relatively weaker binding affinities with the two drugs (e.g., ITGA2-PRT06607: −7.5 kcal/mol; ITGA2-SB216763: −8.0 kcal/mol). These findings suggest that ITGA3, CD44, and BIRC3 may be potential therapeutic targets for PRT06607 and SB216763 in the context of our risk model.

Discussion

This study leveraged single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to investigate the role of Anoikis-Related Genes (ARGs) in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) brain metastasis. By analyzing paired primary tumors and brain metastases, we identified epithelial cells as the predominant cell type undergoing significant alterations in ARG activity and proportion during metastasis. Cell-cell communication analysis revealed that VEGF signaling is a central pathway enriched in both primary and metastatic sites, primarily mediated by epithelial-endothelial interactions. Through differential gene expression and ARG intersection, we constructed a four-gene prognostic model (ITGA2, ITGA3, CD44, BIRC3), which was validated in TCGA and GEO cohorts. Functional experiments demonstrated that CD44, significantly upregulated in brain metastases, drives proliferation, migration, and invasion in LUAD cells.

Brain metastasis in lung cancer is associated with an extremely poor prognosis [13]. Research into the mechanisms underlying lung cancer progression and brain metastasis is ongoing [26, 27]. Traditional cytotoxic agents exhibit limited efficacy in managing brain metastases, primarily due to poor penetration of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the presence of efflux pumps [28]. Consequently, targeted therapies represent a crucial treatment modality for NSCLC patients with brain metastases. Activating epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations are detected in 15–35% of primary NSCLC cases [29], with a higher prevalence observed in Asian populations. NSCLC patients harboring EGFR mutations have a higher propensity for developing brain metastases. However, their prognosis is generally more favorable compared to those with EGFR wild-type brain metastases. Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) rearrangements occur in approximately 4% of NSCLC patients, with a reported ALK fusion positivity rate of 3–11% in the Chinese NSCLC population [30]. A randomized phase II trial [31] demonstrated that brigatinib achieved an intracranial response rate of 78% in patients with ALK-rearranged NSCLC brain metastases, significantly higher than the 29% rate observed with crizotinib. Furthermore, a phase III clinical trial compared lorlatinib to crizotinib as first-line therapy in patients with ALK fusion-positive NSCLC. ROS1 fusions are found in 1–2% of NSCLC patients. Crizotinib, the only ROS1 tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) approved in China, is a viable treatment option for NSCLC patients with brain metastases [32]. Despite multiple therapeutic strategies for lung cancer brain metastases, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and anti-angiogenic therapy, treatment outcomes often remain suboptimal [33]. Therefore, identifying critical genes involved in survival at the single-cell level and uncovering the core genes driving lung cancer brain metastasis is of paramount importance and represents an urgent clinical need.

Anoikis is a distinct form of programmed cell death, first described by Frisch and Francis in 1994 [11]. It refers to cell death triggered by the loss of proper connections between cells and the extracellular matrix (ECM) or adjacent cells. Anoikis plays a crucial role in maintaining tissue homeostasis and preventing aberrant cell proliferation and is closely associated with the development and progression of various cancers [34, 35]. Research indicates that the ECM, primarily composed of proteoglycans, elastic fibers, adhesive glycoproteins, and selectins [36], serves as the physical scaffold for cells. Integrins are a class of transmembrane glycoprotein in receptors that specifically recognize and bind adhesive glycoproteins within the ECM, such as fibronectin and laminin, mediating cell-ECM adhesion.

The molecular mechanisms underlying anoikis involve activation of the mitochondrial-dependent pathway (Bcl-2/Bax signaling), activation of death receptor pathways (e.g., caspase family activation), inhibition of integrin-related signaling, and suppression of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) expression. Among these, integrin-related signaling is particularly critical. When anoikis is inhibited, certain tumor cells can develop resistance, enabling them to survive in the circulatory system despite losing ECM attachment, ultimately forming metastatic lesions in distant organs [37].

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) describes the process by which cells transition from a stationary epithelial state to a motile mesenchymal state, marked by loss of cell polarity and weakening of intercellular tight junctions. EMT facilitates ECM degradation, promotes tumor cell dissemination, and thereby increases metastatic potential. Studies demonstrate that tumor cells, upon detachment from the ECM, can maintain a “bubble-like” morphology (spherical with small hemispherical protrusions) and activate pro-survival signals, enabling them to evade anoikis [37].

Tumor cells achieve this evasion through various mechanisms, including activation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [38], non-coding RNAs, and multiple signaling pathways, a phenomenon termed anoikis resistance.

In recent years, a growing body of research has used bioinformatics approaches to identify anoikis-related gene (ARG) signatures in lung cancer and develop prognostic models based on these signatures. For example, Pan et al. [39] identified several anoikis-associated genes, including sirtuin 3 (SIRT3), phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PIK3CA), integrin alpha 3 (ITGA3), death-associated protein kinase 1 (DAPK1), p21-activated kinase 1, and caspase-3. These genes were suggested to facilitate tumor cells in evading immune surveillance, contributing to the development of anoikis resistance and promoting tumor cell survival and metastasis. Similarly, Li et al. [40] established a novel anoikis-associated prognostic model based on the expression patterns of ARGs such as CD36, transforming growth factor-beta 2 (TGF-β2), WW and C2 domain containing 3, and C-C motif chemokine ligand 2. This model stratified patients into different risk groups, with those in the low-risk group showing heightened sensitivity to several commonly used chemotherapeutic agents, including oxaliplatin, bortezomib, and temozolomide.

In the present study, we adopted a single-cell perspective to investigate the association between ARGs and LUAD brain metastasis, with the aim of exploring key genes and pathways involved. This approach brings novelty to our research by focusing on a relatively understudied aspect of ARG function in the context of brain metastasis, distinguishing it from previous studies primarily focused on prognostic modeling. Consistent with this, a prior study on ARGs in LUAD highlighted the critical role of ARGs in tumor progression and prognosis, specifically identifying the anoikis-related gene CDKN3 as an oncogene and a prognostic marker associated with immunotherapy response [8].

The discrepancies observed in characteristic genes identified across studies can be attributed to variations in sample datasets, tumor types, gene selection algorithms, sources of anoikis-related genes, and the inherent heterogeneity of the LUAD tumor microenvironment, which may lead to divergent immune profiles among patients. Nonetheless, these studies collectively underscore the pivotal role of anoikis in lung cancer prognosis and treatment response.

Furthermore, our study employed paired tissue samples from both primary tumors and metastatic brain (mBrain) tissues, complemented by in vitro cellular assays, to validate the efficacy of the ARGs in our model from multiple perspectives. By integrating comprehensive bioinformatics analyses with experimental validation, our study achieves a higher level of rigor.

We identified and validated CD44 as a critical molecule in LUAD brain metastasis [41]. CD44, a transmembrane glycoprotein receptor and a well-established cancer stem cell (CSC) marker, mediates tumor progression through multiple pathways, including cell adhesion, migration, and anoikis resistance [42]. CD44 is highly expressed in various cancers, including colorectal cancer, where its expression correlates with clinicopathological parameters [43]. Additionally, CD44 is associated with cancer stemness [44]. (CD44/PD-L1-mediated networks in drug resistance and immune evasion of breast cancer stem cells: Promising targets of natural compounds), CD44 overexpression in tumors (e.g., > 30% in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma) [45] and its activated state in cancer cells (vs. quiescence in normal cells) make it an ideal therapeutic target. Recent studies developed anti-CD44 v5 antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), demonstrating potent tumor suppression in preclinical models. Current research highlights its dual roles [46]. These findings align with emerging evidence that CD44 is a multifunctional hub in cancer progression, supporting its potential as both a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The clinically validated samples are limited in number, as obtaining both brain metastasis and primary lung tissue samples is challenging. Larger, prospective cohorts are needed to further validate the prognostic model and the differential expression of CD44. The specific molecular mechanisms downstream of CD44 (and other model genes) in conferring anoikis resistance and promoting brain metastasis require deeper investigation, potentially involving specific signaling pathways or interactions with the unique brain microenvironment. Additionally, the functional role of the other model genes (ITGA2, ITGA3, BIRC3), despite their prognostic value in bulk RNA-seq data, and their lack of significant differential protein expression in our specimen data, warrants further exploration in different contexts or with larger sample sizes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our integrated multi-omics approach reveals the significant changes epithelial cells undergo during LUAD brain metastasis and highlights the critical role of Anoikis-Related Genes in this process. We identified and validated CD44 as a key functional driver of metastasis. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the biology underlying LUAD brain metastasis but also provide a valuable tool for risk stratification. Furthermore, they position CD44 as a promising therapeutic target, warranting further investigation to address this devastating complication.
