Investigation of enhanced thermoelectric response in the extended Lieb lattice using the Hubbard model
Authors: Erfan Norian, Bandar Astinchap, Mona Abdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We investigate the thermoelectric properties of the extended Lieb lattice under the influence of intrinsic spin-orbit coupling, on-site Coulomb interaction, and an external magnetic field using the Hubbard model and linear response theory. The unique electronic structure of this system, characterized by two tunable flat bands, provides a fertile platform for controlling charge and heat transport. By systematically analyzing the electronic contributions to thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, Seebeck coefficient, and the thermoelectric figure of merit (ZTe), we reveal how spin-orbit coupling and strong electron correlations suppress thermal conductivity while enhancing thermoelectric efficiency. Our results show that increasing the on-site Coulomb interaction and magnetic field strength significantly improves ZTe, particularly at low to intermediate thermal energy. The modulation of flat bands via spin-orbit and interaction effects plays a critical role in enhancing the density of states near the Fermi level, thereby optimizing the Seebeck response. These findings highlight the role of flat-band engineered lattices in shaping thermoelectric response and provide theoretical insights into band-structure engineering of correlated low-dimensional materials with tunable transport behavior.

Introduction

In recent years, materials and lattice systems hosting flat electronic bands have become a central topic in condensed matter physics. Unlike conventional dispersive bands, flat or nearly flat bands feature localized electronic states arising from destructive quantum interference within the lattice1. This suppression of carrier kinetic energy results in vanishing group velocity and makes such systems a fertile ground for strong correlation effects and unconventional electronic phases. Flat-band physics has thus opened avenues toward correlated insulating states, unconventional magnetism2,3, and topologically nontrivial phases4,5. Experimental and theoretical studies have confirmed the presence of flat bands in a variety of platforms, ranging from nickelate-based superconductors6–8 and porous organic frameworks9,10 to engineered lattices such as kagome11,12, Mielke checkerboard13, and pyrochlore geometries14. A remarkable aspect of flat bands is the existence of compact localized states15,16, which have been observed in photonic and optical realizations of Lieb17,18 and kagome lattices19, further underscoring their technological potential. Among these geometries, the Lieb lattice has attracted particular attention owing to its non-Bravais structure with three sites per unit cell, which naturally supports a flat band at zero energy20–22. This feature has inspired the exploration of higher-order edge-centered square lattices with larger unit cells. One such structure is the extended Lieb lattice with five atomic sites per unit cell (the Lieb-5 lattice), which hosts multiple flat bands and enriched electronic behavior23–25. Recent studies have shown that the inclusion of intrinsic spin–orbit coupling within the tight-binding framework drives this lattice into topologically nontrivial phases characterized by finite spin Chern numbers23,26. These results point to the potential realization of exotic phases, including fractional quantum spin Hall states, in experimentally feasible systems such as photonic waveguide arrays and ultracold atomic lattices. A notable example is the work of Bhattacharya et al., who demonstrated the emergence of multiple perfectly flat bands and spin–orbit coupling-induced topological gaps in the Lieb-5 lattice27. In parallel, thermoelectric power generation has received increasing attention due to growing concerns about fossil fuel consumption and environmental impact. Thermoelectric devices exploit the Seebeck effect to directly convert waste heat into electricity28,29, with efficiency quantified by the ZT. Achieving high ZT requires a delicate balance of enhanced thermopower and electrical conductivity alongside suppressed thermal conductivity30,31. However, these transport coefficients are interdependent, often showing competing trends with carrier concentration and electronic structure. A promising route to overcome this challenge lies in materials with unconventional band structures—such as high-valley-degeneracy bands, low effective mass states, and flat or pudding-mold bands—that enhance thermopower while simultaneously modifying electronic transport32–34. Motivated by these considerations, the present work explores the role of flat bands in determining thermoelectric properties. Specifically, we investigate how the proliferation of flat bands in the extended Lieb lattice influences thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, the Seebeck coefficient, and overall energy conversion efficiency. To our knowledge, no prior study has systematically examined the thermoelectric response of the extended Lieb lattice within the framework of the Hubbard model. Here, we address this gap by analyzing the interplay between on-site Coulomb interaction and intrinsic spin-orbit coupling, as well as their combined effect on fundamental thermoelectric parameters. This approach provides insights into the microscopic mechanisms governing heat and charge transport in flat-band systems and offers a pathway toward the design of high-performance thermoelectric materials based on engineered lattice geometries. The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the theoretical framework and computational methodology for modeling the extended Lieb lattice with Hubbard interaction and spin-orbit coupling. Section 3 presents and discusses the results, focusing on the influence of these interactions on thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, the Seebeck coefficient, and ZTe. Finally, Sect. 4 summarizes the key findings and outlines prospects for future research, both theoretical and experimental.

Method of calculations

In 2017, Zhang and colleagues introduced a novel class of edge-centered photonic square lattices, designated as Lieb-5 and Lieb-7 lattices, which are distinguished by the presence of multiple flat bands in their band structures35. These lattices, composed of 5 and 7 sites per unit cell, respectively, exemplify a broader family of structures where the number of sites N adheres to an odd-integer sequence. Importantly, the number of flat bands, m, supported by these systems satisfies the relation m = (N − 1)/2, providing a systematic foundation for engineering flat-band-rich lattices. These configurations serve as highly tunable platforms for investigating a wide range of physical phenomena, including light localization, photonic topological insulator phases, and vortex generation driven by quasi-spin dynamics. In this work, we begin by constructing the tight-binding Hamiltonian for the extended Lieb lattice configuration shown in Fig. 1. This geometry is realized by periodically replicating a fundamental building block across a two-dimensional plane, resulting in a lattice where each unit cell comprises five atomic sites, highlighting the extended nature of the system.

The Hubbard model serves as a fundamental and flexible framework for studying the interplay between electron mobility and correlation effects in systems with multiple energy bands. By combining a kinetic energy term that captures the hopping of electrons between lattice sites with a local interaction term representing on-site Coulomb repulsion, the model effectively bridges the physics of itinerant electrons and localized interactions39,40. Its broad applicability has made it central to understanding a wide spectrum of collective phenomena, ranging from charge and spin ordering to various types of superconductivity and topologically nontrivial states. The Hubbard interaction is treated at the mean-field level, which provides qualitative insight into correlation-induced trends but cannot capture quantum fluctuations or many-body effects beyond static density correlations. Consequently, our results should be viewed as the electronic baseline, suitable for describing the weak-to-intermediate correlation regime.

Particularly in flat band systems, where kinetic energy is quenched, the Hubbard interaction becomes the dominant energy scale, offering an ideal setting to probe strongly correlated phases. In this context, the electron–electron interaction is introduced via an on-site repulsive term, mathematically formulated as:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{u} = U\mathop \sum \limits_{j} n_{{j \uparrow }} n_{{j \downarrow }}$$\end{document}

The Seebeck coefficient S, also known as thermopower, quantifies the voltage generated in response to a temperature gradient across a material. It serves as a key indicator of the dominant charge carriers responsible for thermoelectric transport. Specifically, the sign of S reveals the nature of the charge carriers: a positive value indicates hole-dominated conduction, whereas a negative value signifies that electrons are the primary carriers43,44. The temperature dependence of the Seebeck coefficient provides valuable insights into the interaction between thermal excitation and carrier dynamics. As temperature increases, changes in the magnitude and sign of S can reflect complex band structure effects and scattering mechanisms. The mean-field Green’s function equations were iterated to self-consistency, with convergence achieved when the relative change in electron density between successive steps fell below 10⁻⁶. Although more sophisticated methods, such as DMRG or QMC, can treat correlations beyond mean-field, they are limited to one-dimensional or small clusters. Our mean-field framework enables tractable calculations on extended two-dimensional lattices while capturing the essential physics of correlation-driven transport49,50.

Results and discussion

In this section, we present the numerical analysis of the thermoelectric properties of the extended Lieb lattice based on the Hubbard model and Green’s function approach. Specifically, we evaluate the thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, Seebeck coefficient, and the ZTe under the combined influence of spin-orbit coupling, on-site Coulomb interaction, and an external magnetic field. To begin, we calculate the electronic band structure of the extended Lieb lattice as derived from Eq. (8). A distinguishing feature of the extended Lieb lattice, as compared to the conventional Lieb lattice, is the presence of two flat bands in its spectrum. This unique band structure significantly enhances the potential of this material for thermoelectric applications, offering distinct advantages over many other two-dimensional systems due to the associated high density of states and localization effects. The band dispersions corresponding to the extended Lieb lattice geometry depicted in Fig. 2 exhibit two nondispersive, completely flat bands located at energies E = ± 1. These flat bands are a direct consequence of the lattice topology and the underlying symmetry of the system. In our calculations, we have set the intra-cell and inter-cell hopping parameters to t = t1 = 1, ensuring a symmetric and balanced hopping configuration throughout the lattice. The presence of flat bands in a lattice is often rooted in its unique geometry, which can create conditions for wave localization through destructive interference. In the extended Lieb lattice, this architectural feature plays a pivotal role, giving rise to multiple flat bands as seen in Fig. 2. Specifically, two perfectly flat bands emerge symmetrically at energy levels E = ± 1, each intersected by dispersive bands in between. This behavior marks a clear departure from the traditional Lieb lattice, which typically hosts a single flat band flanked by two dispersive bands that meet at a triply degenerate Dirac point, resulting in a spin-1, cone-like energy structure51,52. It’s important to emphasize that the flat bands observed in the extended Lieb model are not merely a modification of those in the conventional case; rather, they represent a fundamentally distinct configuration that cannot be continuously morphed into the standard Lieb lattice band structure, as detailed in Refs. 53.

The emergence of flat bands can be understood from both momentum-space and real-space perspectives. In momentum space, particles in a flat band exhibit an infinite effective mass, rendering them effectively immobile; this leads to a completely dispersionless energy band. Conversely, from the real-space viewpoint, particle hopping between different regions of the lattice becomes suppressed due to interference effects. This suppression gives rise to compact localized states, where the particle remains confined to a limited region of the lattice without spreading54,55. In the following, we explore how spin-orbit coupling influences the electronic properties of the extended Lieb lattice. Figure 3 illustrates the band structure under varying strengths of spin-orbit coupling. As the intrinsic spin-orbit coupling strength (λ)is increased from zero, the band structure of the system undergoes a significant transformation, resulting in the opening of a gap, as illustrated in Fig. 3. When λ = 0, the system supports completely flat bands; however, introducing a nonzero λ generally disrupts these flat bands, as seen in Fig. 3a. Interestingly, at the particular value λ/t = 0.5, a new flat band emerges precisely at the band center, E = 0, as depicted in Fig. 3b. In this tight-binding model, the intrinsic spin-orbit coupling acts analogously to an effective magnetic flux. Typically, such a flux tends to break the flatness of bands, but under certain fine-tuned conditions, such as λ/t = 0.5, it instead enables the reformation of a completely flat, nondispersive band. This phenomenon results from destructive quantum interference that confines electronic states, leading to localization and the formation of a flat band at E = 0. Thus, despite the general tendency of intrinsic spin-orbit coupling to destroy flat bands, this specific tuning restores one through a subtle interplay between geometry and interference effects56,57.

In solid-state systems, thermal conductivity arises from two principal contributions: phononic, originating from lattice vibrations, and electronic, stemming from the movement of charge carriers. In the case of the extended Lieb lattice, characterized by its geometric complexity, flat bands, spin-orbit coupling, and strong electronic correlations, the electronic contribution becomes particularly relevant. In this study, we focus exclusively on the electronic part of the thermal conductivity, using the tight-binding model to incorporate the effects of spin-orbit coupling, on-site Coulomb repulsion, and external magnetic fields. In the present study, we concentrate exclusively on the electronic component of transport coefficients, leaving out the influence of phonons. This simplification allows a clear examination of purely electronic effects, facilitating analysis of how electrons contribute to conductivity and other transport properties. By separating electronic and lattice contributions, we can focus on intrinsic electronic behavior without the additional complexity introduced by phonon-mediated thermal transport59,60. Several recent studies have investigated the thermoelectric properties of two-dimensional materials by focusing exclusively on the electronic contribution, neglecting the phonon part of thermal conductivity46,61–63. Calculating ZTe provides a comprehensive evaluation of a material’s thermoelectric response. This is particularly important in low-dimensional and correlated electron systems, where external parameters such as spin-orbit coupling, Coulomb interaction, and magnetic field can significantly influence the electronic structure and carrier dynamics. By analyzing ZTe under varying conditions, researchers can identify strategies to enhance thermoelectric efficiency through band structure engineering, quantum confinement, or suppression of thermal conductivity. Therefore, the calculation of ZTe is not only essential for characterizing the intrinsic performance of novel materials but also serves as a guide for designing next-generation thermoelectric devices. For comparison, the two-dimensional Bi2Te3 material exhibits a ZT ∼ 1 at 300 K, serving as a benchmark for high-performance thermoelectrics. While the extended Lieb lattice studied here is in the early stages of investigation, our results indicate qualitatively tunable thermoelectric behavior. Future work, including phonon contributions, doping effects, and advanced correlation treatments, will allow a more quantitative comparison with established materials such as Bi2Te3 64,65.

Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the thermoelectric properties of the extended Lieb lattice using a Hubbard and Green’s function framework that incorporates spin–orbit coupling, on-site Coulomb interaction, and magnetic field effects. By analyzing thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, the Seebeck coefficient, and the ZTe, we demonstrated how these external parameters strongly influence thermoelectric behavior. A defining feature of the extended Lieb lattice is its pair of flat bands, which are highly sensitive to spin–orbit coupling and Coulomb interactions. We found that tuning spin–orbit coupling can suppress or regenerate flat bands, directly affecting carrier localization and the density of states near the Fermi level. This restructuring modifies transport balance: while stronger correlations and magnetic fields reduce electrical conductivity and thermal conductivity, they enhance the magnitude of the Seebeck coefficient and the overall efficiency captured by ZTe. These findings suggest that flat-band engineering, in combination with external field control, can serve as a conceptual mechanism for tuning the electronic thermoelectric response. This work provides theoretical guidance for exploring materials where flat-band physics and strong correlations play a central role in shaping thermoelectric behavior. However, because this method treats electron correlations approximately, future work could employ non-perturbative techniques such as Quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) or Dynamical Mean-Field Theory (DMFT) to quantitatively validate the results and capture stronger correlation and fluctuation effects, particularly near flat-band regimes. A more comprehensive investigation, including electron–phonon interactions and phonon thermal transport, will be pursued in future work to achieve a quantitative understanding of the total thermoelectric efficiency.
