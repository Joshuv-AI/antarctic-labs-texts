Real-Time Generation of Near-Minimum-Energy Trajectories via Constraint-Informed Residual Learning
Domenico Dona’, Giovanni Franzese, Cosimo Della Santina, Paolo Boscariol, Basilio Lenzo

Abstract
Industrial robotics demands significant energy to operate, making energy-reduction methodologies increasingly important. Strategies for planning minimum-energy trajectories typically involve solving nonlinear optimal control problems (OCPs), which rarely cope with real-time requirements. In this paper, we propose a paradigm for generating near minimum-energy trajectories for manipulators by learning from optimal solutions. Our paradigm leverages a residual learning approach, which embeds boundary conditions while focusing on learning only the adjustments needed to steer a standard solution to an optimal one. Compared to a computationally expensive OCP-based planner, our paradigm achieves 87.3% of the performance near the training dataset and 50.8% far from the dataset, while being two to three orders of magnitude faster.



I
Introduction

The emergence of “green” policies in industry is crucial for mitigating climate change. In the context of robotics, enhancing the efficiency of robotic systems can significantly reduce energy consumption and operational costs
[
1
]
.

Trajectory Optimization (TO) has emerged as a cost-effective approach for reducing energy consumption of robotic systems, such as single-axis systems
[
2
]
, quadrotors
[
3
]
, and manipulators
[
4
]
.
TO is used to plan trajectories that minimize total energy expenditure by solving the associated Optimal Control Problem (OCP). However, closed-form solutions for OCPs are rarely available, requiring the use of numerical routines. Consequently, these numerical routines limit the Real-Time (RT) deployment of TO, confining its applicability to precomputed (off-line) trajectories, thus posing a significant limitation in adaptive scenarios.

In this paper, we address this limitation by introducing a surrogate model paradigm. Trained on precomputed optimal trajectories, the surrogate model inherently enables RT deployment.

Instead of learning the whole solution, we learn only the quantity (or
residual
) that steers a standard (or prior) solution toward the optimal one, leveraging the idea of
residual learning
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. The key idea is that the solution of the planning problem for a fixed-time-point-to-point motion is rapidly available, e.g., cubic law; what remains is the (residual) component required for achieving optimality.

By proper regressor design, the proposed paradigm (i) allows the embedding of Boundary Conditions (BCs) as hard constraints, (ii) requires less training data, and (iii) allows deciding the resolution of the solution.
Moreover, by using probabilistic regressors, the performance is enhanced by selecting the solution that best satisfies the optimality requirements from multiple samples; the uncertainty measure is used in an Active Learning (AL) data aggregation routine. The idea is depicted in Fig.
2
.

Fig. 1:
Schematic of the proposed
residual
learning paradigm. Offline, a dataset of residuals is generated and used to train a specifically designed probabilistic regressor that embeds the boundary conditions.

In summary, this work proposes a
real-time
capable planner for (near) minimum-energy fixed-time-point-to-point planning problems. This is achievedusing a
residual learning
paradigm that allows the embedding of BCs as
hard constraints
and reduces the number of required training data.
The proposed paradigm is tested using two probabilistic regression frameworks, namely Neural Network (NN) ensembles and Gaussian Processes.

The proposed paradigm is validated through simulated experiments on three robotic systems, namely a pendulum, a SCARA robot, and a six-axis manipulator

III
METHOD

The paradigm can be divided into the following steps:

1.

Formulate the minimum-energy problem as an OCP and solve it numerically to create a dataset of residuals.

2.

Define a structure for the regressors that (i) embeds the BCs for the residuals and (ii) gives uncertainty information of the output.

3.

When a new set of problem variables is given, generate a near-optimal solution using the selected regressor and (eventually) evaluate the uncertainty.

4.

If needed, upgrade the training set by generating new data based on uncertainty information, i.e., active learning.

III-A

Optimal Control Problem formulation

We are interested in fixed-time-point-to-point (FT-PTP) trajectories for manipulators, as usually task time is decided externally by production constraints. The study case can be formalized as a fixed-time fixed-endpoint OCP.
The functional that we want to minimize is the energy expenditure, i.e. the integral over time of the electrical power. We take as state the vector of configurations and velocities
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}},\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and as control the torque exerted by the motors
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n
is the number of degrees-of-freedom of the system. Using the DC equivalent circuit model, the electrical power takes the following quadratic form:

\mathcal{P}=\sum_{i}^{n}\mathcal{P}_{i}=\sum_{i}^{n}\left(r_{i}u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}u_{i}\right)=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}

(1)

where first term is due to Joule losses and the second is due to the mechanical power. The control weight matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}
is a diagonal matrix whose
i-th
diagonal term is:

r_{i}=\frac{R_{a}^{i}}{{k_{t}^{i}}^{2}}

(2)

where
R_{a}^{i}
is the armature resistance and
k_{t}^{i}
the torque constant of the
i-th
motor, respectively.
The mixed state-control term matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}
only selects the velocities from the state vector:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{N}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{0}_{n}\\
\mathbb{1}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

(3)

where
\mathbb{0}_{n}
and
\mathbb{1}_{n}
are the
n\times n
zero and identity matrices, respectively. With the convenient definitions above, it is possible to state the desired problem as following:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}(\cdot),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot)}

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{E}=\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}(t),\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t

(4a)

subject to:

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}(0)=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{0},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}(t_{f})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{f}

(4b)

\displaystyle\quad\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}},t)

(4c)

where
t_{f}
is the prescribed total time,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{0}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}_{0},\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{0})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{f}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}_{f},\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{f})
are the BCs. Since it is a PTP motion the velocities at the boundaries are zero (
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}_{f}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
).
The dynamics
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{f}}
can be explicited by splitting the position and velocity components as follows,

\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}=\begin{cases}\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}}=\texttt{FD}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{u}})\end{cases}

(5)

where
FD(
\cdot
)
is the
F
orward
D
ynamics map.

III-B

Regressor design

The
explicit
solution of the OCP can be seen as a map from the inputs of the problem, i.e. the time
t
and the trajectory parameters
\mathcal{X}
to the trajectory
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}(t)
. In particular,
t\in[0,\;t_{f}]
and
\mathcal{X}=(t_{f},\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}_{0},\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}_{f})
.

In this work, we parameterize the solution of the optimal control problem (OCP) in the
explicit
manner where we focus solely on predicting the position over time,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}(t)
, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}(t,\mathcal{X}).

(6)

This choice alleviates the curse of output dimensionality of the regressor; nevertheless, the velocity can still be retrieved numerically or analytically from the position.

Using a dataset of optimal trajectories, one can learn an approximation
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}}
for the mapping
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{g}}
. However, this ‘‘naive’’ approach has the following problems

III-B
1
Residual Learning of explicit solutions

The key idea is that we know how to plan a FT-PTP trajectory, but we do not know how to plan it optimally. Therefore, we want to learn only the
residual
component that steers our standard (prior) solution towards the optimal one. This reduces the burden of the regressor by learning only a part of the solution. Mathematically, let us define the
prior
as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}(t)
and the
residual
as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}(t)
:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{q}}(t)=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}(t)+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}(t)

(7)

where the prior
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{p}}(t)
can be, for instance, a cubic polynomial that satisfies the BCs in (
4b
).

III-B
2
Boundary Conditions Satisfaction

Since the prior satisfies the BCs, the residual must have zero values at the boundaries, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}(0)=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}(t_{f})=\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}(0)=\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}(t_{f})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}.

(8)

At this point, even if the values at the boundaries are the same, their position depends on the value of
t_{f}
. For overcoming this issue, we can define the normalized time
\xi
as the ratio between the physical time
t
and the total time
t_{f}
, such that the boundaries are at
\xi=0
and
\xi=1
. The mapping
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}(\cdot)
can be approximated using a regressor
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}(\xi,\mathcal{X})
that, according to (
8
), must satisfy the following:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}(0)=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}(1)=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}^{\prime}(0)=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}^{\prime}(1)=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}

(9)

where
\square^{\prime}
denotes the partial derivative with respect to
\xi
. The second argument has been omitted for brevity. Depending on the type of nonlinear regressor chosen, different techniques can be applied to satisfy (
9
). In the following, NNs and GPs are considered, as they are two of the most established nonlinear regressors.

To enforce property (
9
) using a NN, it is sufficient to multiply the output of the NN element-wise by a “scaling term”
s
, as shown in Fig.
3
. One possible choice for
s
is:

s(\xi)=\xi^{2}(1-\xi)^{2}

(10)

Fig. 3:
Proposed neural network scheme. The scaling function is applied element-wise.

and calling
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{o}}
the output of the NN, then, the regressor:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}(\xi,\mathcal{X})=s(\xi)\odot\boldsymbol{\mathbf{o}}(\xi,\mathcal{X})

(11)

satisfies (
9
). The symbol
\odot
is used for element-wise multiplication.

Recalling the objectives outlined at the beginning of Section
III
, at this stage, we lack uncertainty information for the output. This issue can be addressed by using an ensemble of neural networks, providing two main benefits: (i) estimation of the epistemic uncertainty using the standard deviation of outputs, and (ii) selection of the best solution (in energy sense) among the set of predictions. The ensemble approach introduces only a minor computational cost, as it can be easily parallelized.

Another common nonlinear regression model is given by GPs. Similar to NNs, GPs do not satisfy property (
9
) out-of-the-box. However, although the process is less straightforward, one can modify the GP kernel to enforce it.
To do so, we propose taking a general-purpose kernel
k_{g}(\cdot,\cdot)
, such as a Radial Basis Function (RBF), and multiplying it by two “scaling” functions
s(\cdot)
that depends only on the normalized time
\xi
. This custom kernel is defined as:

k(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=s(\xi_{1})k_{g}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})s(\xi_{2});

(12)

with
s(\xi)
being the scaling function introduced in (
10
). Here,
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
are the normalized time points at which the function is evaluated. The kernel (
12
) satisfies (
9
), as proven in the Appendix. Unlike NNs, where an ensemble was needed to estimate uncertainty, this regressor inherently provides epistemic uncertainty information. Additionally, multiple solutions are sampled from the distribution, and the one with the lowest energy consumption is selected.

III-B
3
Active Learning

Our paradigm reduces data quantity by enforcing structure through the prior. However, solution quality may vary across the input space due to limited data, as data generation is costly.
However, we can query the optimal planner as needed to update the dataset. We propose employing an AL strategy based on the idea that the further the input is from the existing dataset, the higher the uncertainty in the output. As a result, the epistemic uncertainty of the output serves as a measure of the quality of the solution, allowing us to generate new data where the uncertainty is higher. The idea is depicted in Fig.
4
.

Fig. 4:
Active learning approach: the uncertainty of the solution is used to generate new data.

The way new data is incorporated differs significantly between the types of regressors used. GPs can easily incorporate new data by updating their training dataset. On the other hand, NNs do not possess the same flexibility when it comes to incorporating new information.
One common strategy for NNs is fine-tuning, which involves training the model for additional epochs while enriching the original batches with new data.

V
CONCLUSIONS

In this paper, by focusing only in the residual between an optimal planner solution and a given standard planner, the objective of learning optimal solutions has been achieved. Through numerical simulation, the advantage of the proposed paradigm has been shown to be twofold. Firstly, it possible to embed boundary conditions and secondly reduce the quantity of training data required.

By using variational regressors, it is possible to select the best output from the samples and using uncertainty information to upgrade the training dataset.
Between the two regressor frameworks used, Gaussian Processes turned out to be more flexible in incorporating new data during the active learning phase, while the ensemble of neural networks demonstrated better results before adding new evidence.

Future work will consider the inclusion of other types of constraints, such as torque or speed limits, to make the paradigm applicable in more demanding situations.

\begin{gathered}k_{10}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2})=\frac{\partial k(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}_{2})}{\partial\xi_{1}}\\

Now it is possible to prove the following Theorem.

Theorem V.1

A symmetric function of the type (
12
) generates a family of functions that satisfy (
9
).

Proof:

We consider some training points consistent with the BCs,
\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}\}
, as well as the vector of test points
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}=[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{0},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{1}]
, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}=[i,\,\mathcal{X}],\;i=0,1
. The GP mean
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}
at the two test points
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}
can be computed as:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})+\mathbb{1}_{n}\sigma_{n}^{2})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}

(17)

We will show that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}}
by showing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})=\mathbb{0}
, i.e. the
cross-correlation
between the training points and the test points is zero. From (
12
), it is evident that the kernel is zero
\forall\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}
with respect to the test points
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}
. The same holds for the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
by using its definition:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})+\mathbb{1}_{n}\sigma_{n}^{2})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})

(18)

Using the fact that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})=\mathbb{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})=\mathbb{0}
, also
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
is zero. This shows that the samples converge to the mean at the boundaries, since:

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\textup{s}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}_{\textup{s}}
denotes the samples at the boundaries,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}
is the mean at the boundaries, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}
is the lower triangular matrix from the Cholesky decomposition of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{0}},\mathbb{1})
is a Gaussian distributed random vector. Since
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\mathbf{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{0}
.

For derivative information, it is sufficient to use Lemma
V.1
and observe that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}_{11}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}})

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}_{10}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})

\xi=0

\xi=1

\begin{gathered}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\mu}}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}_{10}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{K}}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}},\,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}})+\mathbb{1}_{n}\sigma_{n}^{2})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}},\\

(20)

∎
