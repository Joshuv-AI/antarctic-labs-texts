Metatarsal coalition with pseudarthrosis mimicking bone tumor: two case reports and literature review
Authors: Dongping Yu, Zhen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetatarsal coalition is an extremely rare congenital anomaly. Its complication with pseudarthrosis is even more exceptional and may present as a palpable dorsal mass or chronic pain, often mimicking a bone tumor. Awareness of this condition is essential for accurate diagnosis and to avoid unnecessary biopsies or overtreatment.Case presentationWe report two cases of metatarsal coalition complicated by pseudarthrosis. Background

Metatarsal coalition is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by abnormal osseous or fibrous fusion between adjacent metatarsals [1]. Unlike the more commonly reported tarsal coalitions, metatarsal variants are rarely documented [2]. Tarsal coalition is relatively uncommon. Its prevalence is reported to be less than 1% in the general population. In cadaveric or advanced imaging studies, the prevalence may reach 11.5% ~ 13%. In contrast, epidemiological data on metatarsal coalitions are lacking, likely due to their extreme rarity and underreporting [3, 4]. The additional complication of pseudarthrosis at the coalition site is even more rare [5]. Pseudarthrosis refers to the failure of osseous union resulting in the formation of a false joint, typically lined by fibrous tissue. Clinically, such lesions may present with chronic dorsal foot pain or palpable mass-like abnormalities, often mimicking neoplastic processes [1, 6]. Metatarsal coalition with pseudarthrosis is exceedingly rare and poorly documented, often leading to misdiagnosis due to nonspecific clinical and radiologic features. A lack of clinical awareness further contributes to delayed treatment or inappropriate interventions [1]. By presenting two such misdiagnosed cases, this report aims to raise awareness of this underrecognized entity and help prevent diagnostic errors.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 25-year-old woman presented with a 7-year history of a gradually enlarging dorsal mass on the medial aspect of her right foot, associated with walking-related pain. She had no significant medical or genetic history. Physical examination revealed a firm, non-mobile, mildly tender subcutaneous mass measuring approximately 1.5 × 1.0 × 0.5 cm (Fig. 1A). Mild restriction of motion was noted at the right first tarsometatarsal joint, while other joints and neurovascular findings were unremarkable. A smaller, non-tender nodule (0.8 × 0.5 × 0.5 cm) was also palpated over the medial dorsal aspect of the contralateral foot (Fig. 1B). Imaging of the left foot also revealed subtle structural alterations at the corresponding metatarsal region, suggestive of a similar coalition. However, the patient denied any symptoms on the contralateral side and declined further surgical intervention. Therefore, the left-sided lesion was not resected.

Plain radiographs of the right foot demonstrated a bony prominence between the first and second metatarsals, with features suggestive of pseudarthrosis (Fig. 1C). CT confirmed an irregular bony outgrowth with marginal sclerosis at the metatarsal junction (Fig. 1D). MRI revealed subtle signal alterations consistent with pseudarthrosis, without soft-tissue abnormalities (Fig. 1E).

The patient underwent surgical excision of the right dorsal mass under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, a 2.0 × 1.8 × 1.0 cm osseous structure was identified bridging the medial and lateral aspects of the first and second metatarsals (Fig. 1F). The lesion had clear proximal and distal boundaries and was completely excised; the final specimen measured 2.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 cm. A fibrous pseudarthrotic interface with slight mobility was noted, and surrounding bone appeared intact without cortical destruction or periosteal reaction (Fig. 1G). Histopathology confirmed bone-like tissue consistent with pseudarthrosis and localized vascular proliferation (Fig. 1H).

The postoperative course was uneventful. At 41-month clinical follow-up, the patient remained symptom-free. As she resided in another province, follow-up was conducted via structured telephone interviews. No recurrence of symptoms or new mass was reported. Recurrence was defined as the reappearance of pain or a newly palpable lesion. However, as no imaging was performed during follow-up, asymptomatic recurrence could not be entirely excluded. Because the patient was asymptomatic and lived remotely, follow-up was carried out clinically through structured telephone interviews rather than repeat imaging.

Case 2

A 54-year-old man presented with a 10-year history of intermittent dorsal pain in his left foot, which had worsened over the past week and was associated with discomfort during ambulation. He had no significant past medical history or known genetic disorders. Physical examination revealed a firm, immobile mass measuring approximately 1.5 × 1.0 × 0.5 cm over the distal lateral dorsum of the left foot, with mild tenderness on deep palpation. The overlying skin appeared normal in both color and texture. No abnormalities were noted in the contralateral (right) foot.

Plain radiographs of the left foot demonstrated bony overgrowth and joint space fusion between the third and fourth metatarsals, suggestive of pseudarthrosis formation (Fig. 2A). The right foot was radiographically unremarkable (Fig. 2B). CT confirmed localized bony hypertrophy and a pseudarthrotic articulation, without other degenerative changes (Fig. 2C). MRI revealed osseous proliferation at the affected site with mild signal alterations in the surrounding soft tissue, but no marrow or soft-tissue abnormalities were detected (Fig. 2D).

Surgical excision was performed under spinal anesthesia. Intraoperatively, a bone-like mass measuring 2.0 × 1.0 × 0.8 cm was identified between the third and fourth metatarsals (Fig. 2E). The lesions were interconnected by fibrous tissue without detectable mobility and were anchored to the adjacent metatarsal shafts. The abnormal tissue was completely excised, and the main excised lesion measured approximately 1.5 × 0.8 × 0.5 cm. The surrounding bone and periosteum appeared intact, with no signs of cortical destruction or reactive changes. Histopathologic examination confirmed dense lamellar bone with nodular cartilaginous proliferation and interspersed fibrous tissue, consistent with pseudarthrosis within a metatarsal coalition (Fig. 2F).

The patient’s postoperative recovery was uneventful. At 21 months of telephone follow-up, the patient remained symptom-free without recurrence. Follow-up methods and definitions of recurrence were identical to those used in Case 1. Although no imaging was performed to assess for asymptomatic recurrence—a limitation also noted in Case 1—the absence of clinical signs suggests a favorable short-term outcome. Because the patient remained asymptomatic and lived remotely, follow-up was performed clinically through structured telephone interviews rather than repeat imaging.

Discussion

Metatarsal coalition is a rare congenital anomaly. It results from incomplete mesenchymal segmentation during embryologic development. Compared to tarsal coalitions, metatarsal coalitions are extremely rare. Only isolated cases have been described in the literature [2, 7]. Coalitions involving the central rays, particularly the third and fourth metatarsals, are especially rare. The concomitant presence of pseudarthrosis is even more uncommon. Pseudarthrosis is defined as a non-union with a fibrous-lined false joint. It may occur because of incomplete ossification and chronic mechanical stress [1].

Reports of metatarsal coalition with concurrent pseudarthrosis are very limited. No consensus exists on imaging standards or management strategies. Rare cases of non-osseous intermetatarsal coalitions have been reported. Lim et al. described an instance of incomplete fusion with fibrous bridging that mimicked pseudarthrosis [1]. Similarly, Vun et al. described a distal 4th—5th metatarsal coalition with fibrous, cartilaginous, and osseous tissue. This may have represented early pseudarthrotic remodeling [7]. Our report adds to the limited literature. It documents a clear case of pseudarthrosis formation associated with metatarsal coalition. This rare but clinically significant variant may radiographically mimic a bone tumor.

Histopathology and etiopathogenesis

Histologically, both cases showed dense lamellar bone, fibrous bridging, and nodular cartilaginous components. These findings support a benign, likely congenital lesion. Pseudarthrosis may result from failed bony fusion during skeletal maturation. Repeated microtrauma or abnormal biomechanical stress can aggravate this failure. Instability may trigger a chronic inflammatory response. This can lead to fibrous tissue interposition and the development of false joint. These findings are consistent with prior reports of coalition-associated pseudarthrosis [7].

Radiologic features and diagnostic challenges

Standard radiographs may demonstrate irregular ossification, cortical disruption, or bony outgrowths. CT provides high-resolution detail on osseous bridging and the extent of pseudarthrotic change. MRI can exclud soft tissue masses, cartilage-capped lesions, and marrow abnormalities [8, 9]. However, overlapping radiologic features often lead to diagnostic confusion with neoplastic conditions.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis includes osteochondroma [10], bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation (BPOP), fibrous dysplasia [11], myositis ossificans [12], and ossifying fibroma [13]. Notably, BPOP is a benign but locally aggressive lesion. It can closely mimic coalition with pseudarthrosis because of its exophytic growth and irregular ossification [14]. However, it typically lacks a coalition-like bridging structure and often recurs after incomplete excision [15]. Ganglion cysts are soft tissue lesions. They can present as dorsal foot masses with vague radiological findings, especially when chronic or partially ossified [16]. Low-grade surface-based malignancies, including parosteal osteosarcoma and low-grade chondrosarcoma, may also resemble coalition or pseudarthrosis. Their slow growth, well-demarcated margins, and periosteal origin make them difficult to distinguish in atypical cases [17, 18] (Table 1).

Surgical considerations

Surgically, both lesions were immobile, well-circumscribed, and tightly anchored to adjacent metatarsals without true articulation. Dissection was difficult because no clear cleavage plane was present. Fibrous tissue interdigitated into surrounding bone. Resection required piecemeal removal and careful curettage. The aim was to achieve complete excision while preserving cortical integrity. These features make surgery challenging in small bones, where the margin for error is narrow. Preoperative recognition is critical. It allows for conservative osteotomy planning and helps avoid unnecessarily wide resection.

In Case 1, the lesion was located between the first and second metatarsals. This region contains critical neurovascular structures. Dorsally, it includes the dorsalis pedis artery and branches, the deep peroneal nerve, and accompanying veins. Plantarly, it contains the medial plantar artery and nerve, along with the tendons of the flexor hallucis longus and brevis. Coalition-related dorsal masses in this region may compress nearby structures. They may also produce inflammation or neuropathic pain from direct impingement or friction with footwear.

A dorsal approach was chosen in our case to minimize the risk of injury to major plantar neurovascular bundles while providing direct access to the symptomatic lesion. However, the constrained anatomy and lack of joint space still made resection technically demanding. These findings emphasize the need for anatomical familiarity and careful surgical planning when managing metatarsal coalition with pseudarthrosis.

Prognosis and clinical implications

Both patients achieved complete symptom resolution with no recurrence during follow-up. This outcome supports prior reports that symptomatic metatarsal coalitions, especially those with pseudarthrosis, respond well to surgical excision [19, 20]. Long-term outcomes, however, remain poorly documented due to the rarity of this condition. Our cases expand the limited evidence base and underscore the importance of considering this entity in the differential diagnosis of dorsal foot masses. Future prospective multi-center studies that collect imaging and outcome data will be essential to define optimal management strategies for both symptomatic and asymptomatic cases.

Conclusions

Metatarsal coalition with pseudarthrosis is uncommon. However, it is clinically significant. It should be considered in the differential diagnosis of patients who present with chronic dorsal foot pain or a palpable bony mass [7, 21]. Its ability to mimic bone tumors—both clinically and radiographically—poses substantial diagnostic challenges. Careful radiologic evaluation, particularly with CT and MRI, is essential to assess lesion morphology and exclude more aggressive pathologies. When imaging findings remain inconclusive, histopathologic confirmation remains the definitive method to establish the diagnosis and guide management. This condition is rare but benign. Early recognition is important. It can help avoid misdiagnosis and may also prevent unnecessary interventions. In addition, it allows for individualized treatment planning based on symptom severity and lesion characteristics.
