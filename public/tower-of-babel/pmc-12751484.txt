From victimization of traditional bullying to perpetration of cyberbullying: the roles of moral disengagement, social support, and gender
Authors: Ping Jia, Chengfeng Li, Xin Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The global concern over bullying has spurred extensive research among scholars on both traditional bullying and cyberbullying. This study aims to further explore the intrinsic connection between traditional bullying and cyberbullying, as well as the underlying mechanisms linking the two. A comprehensive survey was conducted among 705 young students from various universities across China. Through the analysis of questionnaire data, this study further investigated how moral disengagement, social support, and gender function in the relationship between being a victim of traditional bullying and perpetrating cyberbullying. The findings revealed that victims of traditional bullying are at a higher risk of engaging in cyberbullying. The analysis indicated that moral disengagement plays a mediating role in the relationship between traditional bullying victimization and cyberbullying perpetration. Social support acts as a moderator, such that the relationship between traditional bullying victimization and cyberbullying perpetration is stronger when the level of social support is higher. Additionally, gender moderates the relationship from moral disengagement to cyberbullying perpetration. The results of this study underscore the necessity for cyberbullying prevention programs to focus on victims of traditional bullying to prevent the escalation from traditional bullying to cyberbullying.

Introduction

Bullying is a significant social issue currently receiving global attention. In 2018, UNESCO indicated that nearly one-third of adolescents worldwide experience bullying in school settings [42]. A comprehensive study involving student populations from 13 Asian and European countries revealed an average prevalence rate of 28.9% for both school bullying and cyberbullying [12]. In China, the situation is also concerning. A sample survey of 104,825 primary and secondary school students across 29 counties and cities found that the incidence rate of school bullying was 33.36%, with 47% reporting frequent bullying and 28.66% reporting occasional bullying [51]. Bullying negatively impacts adolescents' quality of life, significantly hindering their social progress, educational outcomes, and physical and mental health [55]. Thus, bullying has emerged as a malignant social issue requiring urgent resolution globally. Given college students' prolonged online presence—especially their extensive use of social media—the prevalence of cyberbullying among them exceeds that of primary and secondary school students, reaching 25.9% [40]. To further address bullying among college students and explore the potential mechanisms facilitating the transition from traditional bullying to cyberbullying, we aim to contribute to curbing the spread of cyberbullying on university campuses.

The relationship between traditional bullying victimization and cyberbullying perpetration

Olweus [31] defines bullying as a phenomenon where an individual is repeatedly or continuously subjected to intentional harm by other individuals or groups without the ability to protect themselves, such as being physically assaulted or verbally insulted by peers, which is distinct from other forms of school violence [37]. Bullying manifests in two primary forms: traditional bullying and cyberbullying. Traditional bullying victimization refers to adolescents repeatedly or continuously experiencing physical, verbal, and relational harm from peers [9], typically occurring in face-to-face interactions, especially in school environments [26], often manifesting as school bullying. Cyberbullying perpetration involves deliberate aggressive behavior conducted via the internet against individuals who cannot easily defend themselves [21]. Notably, the roles of bully or victim in traditional bullying scenarios can extend to other contexts, such as cyberbullying [32]. Research indicates a high correlation between traditional bullying and cyberbullying [16, 17, 29, 47], with relatively high overlap rates [43]. Cyberbullying is viewed as an extension of traditional bullying in the online domain, with overlap rates as high as 88% and 93% in samples from different countries [32]. Therefore, the coexistence and harmfulness of traditional bullying victimization and cyberbullying perpetration among adolescents in school settings prompt global researchers to delve into the intrinsic connections between these phenomena and explore the mechanisms of transition from traditional to cyberbullying.

The mediating role of moral disengagement

The General Aggression Model (GAM, [1]) posits that bullying behavior is first closely related to individual and situational factors of both the bully and the victim, which indirectly influence aggressive behavior through personal cognition. In bullying scenarios, individuals with higher levels of moral disengagement are more likely to become bullies. Moral disengagement explains why normal individuals engage in unethical behavior without apparent guilt or self-blame. It refers to specific cognitive tendencies that include redefining one's actions to minimize perceived harm, minimizing responsibility for consequences, and reducing empathy for the victim's pain [2–5, 52]. Moral disengagement can disable the self-regulatory function of an individual's moral mechanism, enabling unethical behavior without guilt [52, 53]. A survey of Chinese college students found that traditional bullying victimization significantly predicts cyberbullying perpetration, with all eight mechanisms of moral disengagement playing a partial mediating role [50]. In adolescent bullying phenomena, victims in one scenario often become perpetrators in another [38], meaning that the roles of bully and victim can coexist in the same individual. This phenomenon can be explained by the GAM: for adolescents, being bullied in a school environment is an unpleasant situational factor that greatly harms their cognition and emotions; thus, after experiencing bullying, adolescents often seek to relieve psychological pressure by bullying others in other scenarios.

Social support and gender as moderators

Social support refers to the psychological and material resources provided by a social network to help individuals effectively cope with stress [13], regulating the relationship between stress and physical and mental health [27]. For college students, social support primarily comes from family, friends, and classmates [54]. The Buffering Hypothesis [46] suggests that social support can alleviate or mitigate the negative impacts of stress in negative events or stressful situations. Social support from family, friends, and peers has emerged as a critical moderating factor capable of buffering the negative impacts of bullying [19]. A survey of Chinese college students found that social support moderates the mediating pathway between traditional bullying victimization and cyberbullying victimization [49], and social support negatively predicts cyberbullying perpetration [28]. Additionally, relevant studies indicate that gender significantly influences both traditional bullying victimization and cyberbullying perpetration [11]. There are significant gender differences in both moral disengagement levels and cyberbullying perpetration [50, 53].

In light of these findings, the present study aims to deepen our understanding of the relationship between traditional bullying victimization and cyberbullying perpetration by examining the mediating role of moral disengagement in the link between traditional bullying victimization and cyberbullying perpetration, as well as the moderating effects of social support and gender. Drawing on the aforementioned literature review and theoretical explanations, the present study formulates the following four hypotheses:

Research methods

Procedure and participants

In September 2023, we conducted a questionnaire survey among 1,055 students from two secondary vocational schools and one undergraduate institution in Beijing using a convenience sampling method. The three participating schools comprised the researcher's affiliated institution and closely collaborating units, with respondents including secondary vocational school students, higher vocational school students, college students upgrading from junior college to undergraduate programs, and postgraduate students. After excluding questionnaires with incorrect responses to lie-detection questions or response times under 100 s, 705 valid questionnaires were retained (effective response rate: 66.82%). Among the respondents, there were 346 males (49.08%) and 359 females (50.92%), with a mean age of 21.66 ± 1.97 years. All participants were first-year students enrolled in 2023, coming from the same grade level. Among them, 49.8% were from urban areas, and 50.2% were from rural areas.

Prior to the survey, all participants received a comprehensive explanation regarding the study's anonymity, purpose, and data usage protocols. The initial instructions in the questionnaire explicitly emphasized that participation was entirely voluntary, all questionnaire data would remain anonymous, personal privacy would not be compromised, no risks were involved, and participants had the right to withdraw at any time. Questionnaire completion commenced only after participants confirmed their informed consent. This study obtained ethical approval from the research institution before initiation, and all procedural operations strictly adhered to relevant guidelines. Data collection was conducted via an online platform.

Measure

Traditional bullying victimization

The Bullying Participation Behavior Questionnaire [14], which has been validated among Chinese college students by Qiu et al. [35], was employed in this study. This questionnaire comprises 50 items distributed across five subscales: bully, co—bully, victim, defender, and bystander. For the assessment of traditional bullying victimization, the victim subscale with 10 items was utilized. The items cover a range of bullying forms, including but not limited to physical (e.g., "Someone forcibly takes my things","I have been pushed, hit, or slapped by others"), verbal (e.g., "Someone gives me a nickname"; "I have been made fun of by others"), and relational (e.g., "I have been intentionally isolated and left out by others"; "Someone tries to get others to exclude me"). Scoring is conducted on a 5—point scale, ranging from "Never" to "7 times or more." Higher scores indicate higher levels of traditional bullying victimization, with a maximum possible score of 50. The internal consistency coefficient in this study was 0.973, indicating satisfactory internal consistency.

Cyberbullying perpetration

The Cyberbullying Questionnaire, developed by Wright et al. [48] and revised and validated by Wang et al. [44], comprises 9 items. These items are designed to investigate different forms of cyberbullying perpetration behaviors across various platforms. For instance, in the item "Do you often make malicious jokes about your peers online or via text messages?", "online" pertains to public bullying, while "via text messages" refers to malicious private teasing. The questionnaire employs a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from "Never" to "Always". Higher scores denote more frequent cyberbullying behaviors, with a maximum score of 45. The internal consistency coefficient of this scale in the present study was 0.982.

Moral disengagement

The Moral Disengagement Scale, developed by Bandura et al. [6] and revised by Wang and Yang [45], was utilized. This scale comprises 32 items, including statements such as "If students cause trouble at school, it's their teachers' fault," "A child should not be punished for the mistakes made by their group," "Hitting classmates who annoy everyone is just giving them 'a lesson,'" and "Verbal abuse of classmates is not a big deal compared to physical assault." The scale employs a 5-point Likert rating (1 = strongly disagree, …, 5 = strongly agree). Higher scores indicate a higher level of moral disengagement, with a maximum possible score of 160. In this study, the internal consistency coefficient of the scale was 0.948.

Social support

The Social Support Rating Scale for College Students, developed by Ye & Dai [54], was utilized in this study. This scale is categorized into three dimensions: subjective support, objective support, and support utilization. Subjective Support: This dimension reflects an individual's perceived emotional support and a sense of being cared for and respected. Items such as "Most of my classmates are very concerned about me" and "I often receive emotional help and support from my classmates and friends" are included. These items assess the subjective feelings of being supported and the quality of interpersonal relationships,Objective Support: It refers to the visible and tangible support that an individual receives, encompassing material assistance and the presence of a support network. Examples of items in this dimension include "When needed, I can receive financial support from my family and relatives" and "During difficult times, I can rely on my family or relatives". These items measure the actual availability of support resources; Support Utilization: This dimension evaluates an individual's ability and willingness to seek and utilize support. Items like "When encountering difficulties, I often seek help from my family and relatives" and "When in trouble, I usually take the initiative to seek help from others" are part of this dimension. It reflects the individual's proactive behavior in dealing with problems through support. The scale consists of 17 items and employs a 5-point Likert rating, ranging from "does not apply" to "applies." Higher scores indicate a higher level of perceived social support, with a maximum possible score of 85. In this study, the internal consistency coefficient of the scale was 0.979.

Data processing

To ensure data quality, questionnaires with response times under 100 s (indicating potential random answering) or inconsistent responses to lie-detection items were excluded. Future studies may consider adopting more stringent controls, such as adding filler items or collecting data at multiple time points, to further reduce common method bias. All data were managed and analyzed using SPSS 27.0. Moderated mediation effects were tested using PROCESS macro for SPSS [18],hereafter referred to as PROCESS). Significance tests of regression coefficients were conducted using the bias-corrected percentile Bootstrap method (5000 resamples). Although incorporating different dimensions of variables into the analysis may yield more specific results regarding the roles of various dimensions, this study ultimately opted to use the total scores of each variable as manifest variables for analysis. This decision was made due to its operational simplicity, suitability for researchers familiar with regression analysis methods, and the relatively straightforward model structure. Additionally, the sample size was sufficient to support bootstrapping sampling for the process model, enabling stable parameter estimation without requiring a large sample.

For the path "moral disengagement → cyberbullying perpetration," among male college students, when social support levels are high, moral disengagement significantly and positively predicts cyberbullying perpetration (βsimple = 0.34, t = 5.92, p < 0.001), with a 95% confidence interval of [0.23, 0.46]. By observing the slope values and the upper and lower bounds of the 95% confidence intervals for the two groups under different paths, it is evident that the slope values differ significantly across different groups with high and low social support levels in several paths. This indicates that as social support levels increase, the predictive effect of traditional bullying victimization on moral disengagement and cyberbullying perpetration gradually strengthens, while the predictive effect of moral disengagement on cyberbullying perpetration gradually weakens.

In summary, the impact of traditional bullying victimization on cyberbullying perpetration among college students is mediated by moral disengagement. Social support moderates three paths within this mediating effect, and gender additionally moderates the path from "moral disengagement → cyberbullying perpetration." Based on the above results, Hypotheses 1–4 are all supported.

Discussion

Relationship between traditional bullying victimization and cyberbullying perpetration

The research findings indicate a significant positive correlation between traditional bullying victimization and their engagement in cyberbullying perpetration. Specifically, traditional bullying victimization can directly and positively predict cyberbullying perpetration, a result that aligns with previous relevant studies [23, 24]. This suggests that the higher the level of traditional bullying victimization experienced by college students, the more likely they are to engage in bullying behaviors online. It is also plausible that college students who perpetrate cyberbullying may have been victims of traditional bullying themselves [32]. In other words, with the continuous advancement of internet technology, online platforms have emerged as a new tool for bullying, facilitating the transition from traditional bullying to cyberbullying.

The mediating role of moral disengagement

The research findings reveal a positive correlation between traditional bullying and moral disengagement. Traditional bullying can predict cyberbullying perpetration through the mediating effect of moral disengagement. Moral disengagement can render an individual's internal moral mechanisms ineffective in self-regulation, thus enabling them to engage in unethical behavior without feeling guilty or responsible [52, 53]. Acting as a bridge between traditional bullying and cyberbullying, moral disengagement undermines the function of moral self-regulation [4], allowing individuals to easily violate their internal moral standards. Consequently, moral disengagement has a significant positive impact on aggressive behavior [33]. Moreover, numerous empirical studies have shown that adolescents with high levels of moral disengagement generally exhibit higher aggressive tendencies and are more prone to bullying, delinquent, and aggressive behaviors, while being less likely to engage in prosocial behaviors [30, 34]. When individuals engage in aggressive behavior, they first need to disengage from feelings of guilt and self-reproach through moral disengagement mechanisms. Therefore, adolescents with high levels of moral disengagement tend to display more aggressive behavior [10, 22, 33].

The moderating roles of social support and gender

The research findings indicate that social support can moderate the relationship between traditional bullying and cyberbullying. Social support positively moderates the relationships from "traditional bullying victimization → cyberbullying perpetration" and "traditional bullying victimization → moral disengagement," while negatively moderating the relationship from "moral disengagement → cyberbullying perpetration." As social support increases, the positive link between moral disengagement and cyberbullying perpetration weakens, potentially serving as a protective factor to alleviate and reduce the impact of cyberbullying perpetration. This study has a particularly counterintuitive finding: College students with higher levels of social support actually exacerbate the impact of traditional bullying victimization on cyberbullying perpetration and moral disengagement. According to the Social Support Deterioration Theory (arrera, 1986), when individuals are under low levels of stress, social support generally exerts a positive influence on mental health. However, when stress levels escalate, excessive social support may have an adverse effect, not only intensifying stress but also leading to more severe psychological issues [8]. In college student bullying incidents, high social support for victims may be accompanied by "higher social expectations," causing victims to feel guilty for "failing to meet the expectations of support providers." This guilt, in turn, may drive them to transfer negative emotions through cyberbullying perpetration behaviors as a way to alleviate the pressure brought about by excessive social support. The Social Identity Theory [39] and the Moral Disengagement Theory [7] may offer another explanation for this phenomenon. High social support may lead victims to become overly reliant on external support and develop a strong identification with the supportive group. When the group (or its members) suffers harm, retaliatory actions against outsiders are framed as "justice for the group," significantly reducing the moral burden associated with such actions. This "group identification → retaliation legitimization" mechanism provides a richer theoretical framework for explaining how high social support can inadvertently foster an environment where cyberbullying perpetration is rationalized. Under such circumstances, the supportive group environment may not only fail to curb cyberbullying perpetration but could also have the opposite effect. Therefore, appropriate social support serves as an important means for alleviating the psychological distress caused by bullying behaviors.

Additionally, gender factors play a moderating role in the pathway from "moral disengagement → cyberbullying perpetration." Although boys indeed exhibit significantly higher levels of moral disengagement than girls—implying that, compared to girls, boys are more inclined to downplay aggressive behaviors through moral neutralization [36] and are more prone to forming unethical cognitive tendencies, such as redefining their own behaviors to minimize their harmfulness, maximally reducing responsibility for the consequences of their actions, and lowering empathy for the victims' suffering [53]—the gender role expectation of "men should be strong" in the Chinese cultural context often suppresses emotional expression and vulnerability. This may further weaken men's empathy for bullying victims, thereby fostering a higher tendency toward moral disengagement among men and, consequently, leading to cyberbullying perpetration behaviors. Notably, when comparing our research findings with those of similar studies, certain inconsistencies emerge: For instance, some studies indicate that females' cyberbullying relies more on relational aggression, whereas males' cyberbullying relies more on verbal aggression [20, 25]. Such differences underscore the necessity of considering the unique sociocultural backgrounds and psychological characteristics of different groups when analyzing gender disparities in cyberbullying perpetration. By revealing the moderating effects of social support and gender on cyberbullying perpetration, this study provides novel insights into the existing literature but also acknowledges its limitations. Future research should further explore the complex interactions among psychological, social, and cultural factors to gain a deeper understanding of and effectively address the issue of cyberbullying.

Implications and limitations

This study delves deeply into the relationship between traditional bullying victimization and cyberbullying perpetration, offering crucial insights for the prevention and intervention of bullying issues among college students. On one hand, college students should be guided to continually elevate their own standards of moral behavior, distinguishing right from wrong and good from evil. Schools, families, and society should collaborate to foster an environment that promotes good moral conduct, strengthening moral education in schools, conducting cognitive development training, and establishing a multidimensional moral education system. Simultaneously, psychological counseling should be provided to college students who have experienced bullying to mitigate the impact of bullying as much as possible and reduce the severity of their negative emotions. On the other hand, considering the significant finding that gender moderates the relationship between moral disengagement and cyberbullying perpetration, specific and distinct intervention strategies should be implemented for males and females. For males, given that they may be more prone to externalize their frustration and engage in aggressive behaviors as a result of moral disengagement [50], intervention programs should concentrate on enhancing their emotional regulation skills. This can be accomplished through workshops that impart techniques such as deep breathing, mindfulness meditation, and impulse control. Additionally, male students could gain advantages from group counseling sessions where they can share their experiences in a secure and supportive setting, aiding them in recognizing and challenging their moral disengagement mechanisms. For females, who are more sensitive to the moral wrongness of bullying and rule-violating behaviors [41], intervention strategies should underscore the importance of building self-esteem and resilience. One-on-one counseling sessions can be particularly efficacious in this regard, enabling female students to delve into their emotions and devise coping strategies. Schools and parents should also focus on enhancing their own qualities, avoiding patronizing forms of social support, and instead providing students with the genuine support and encouragement they need. Effective and beneficial interventions for bullied students are expected to reduce the risk of Chinese college students further engaging in cyberbullying as a result of experiencing campus bullying.

However, this study also has certain limitations. Firstly, all data in this study were collected through self-reports provided by college students, which may introduce certain reporting biases. This suggests that future research could strive to gather data from multiple sources (e.g., teachers and peers) to enhance the objectivity of the findings. Additionally, the sample scope of this study was relatively limited, as it did not clearly distinguish between relevant academic majors, whether participants were only children, or other demographic information, thereby restricting the generalizability of the research conclusions. Subsequent studies could further expand the sample survey scope to address these limitations. Secondly, the factors influencing cyberbullying encompass external environments, personal traits, emotions, and other aspects. Cross-sectional studies struggle to elucidate the dynamic relationships among variables. Future research should incorporate experimental designs or longitudinal tracking to thoroughly explore the mechanisms underlying the occurrence and development of cyberbullying. Thirdly, the study's discussion considered the influence of the Social Support Deterioration Theory on the moderating effect of social support.
