Correlation Between Self-Perceived Aging and Oral Frailty in Elderly Chinese Patients with Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Fen Ye, Hui Lu, Ling-Yun Zhu, Yang-Yang Shao, Yu-Ling Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOral frailty (OF) is defined as an age-related decline in physiological reserve resulting from the accumulation of oral functional deterioration and oral health problems, typically manifested as impairments in mastication, swallowing, tongue pressure, and salivary secretion. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between self-perceived aging (SPA) and OF in elderly Chinese patients with ischemic stroke.MethodsA total of 500 elderly ischemic stroke patients were included from the neurology outpatient clinic and inpatient ward of the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University from January to September 2025. Patients completed general demographic questionnaires, the Brief Ageing Perceptions Questionnaire (B-APQ), and the Oral Frailty Index-8 (OFI-8). Participants were classified into OF and Non-OF groups. Baseline demographics, clinical data, B-APQ and OFI-8 scores were compared between groups. Logistic regression analysis identified independent risk factors for OF, while Pearson correlation analysis assessed the relationship between SPA and OF.Results488 elderly patients with ischemic stroke were ultimately included in this study. The average B-APQ score among elderly ischemic stroke patients was 53.18±11.16. The prevalence of OF was 74.6%, with an average OFI-8 score of 6.14±3.53. Logistic multivariate regression analysis identified age (OR=1.156), score of National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (OR=1.298), infarct area (OR=2.340), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (OR=1.183), and B-APQ score (OR=1.423) were factors significantly associated with OF (P<0.05). Spearman correlation analysis demonstrated a negative correlation between B-APQ and OFI-8 scores (P<0.05).ConclusionElderly Chinese patients with ischemic stroke have a relatively high prevalence of OF. Advanced age, severity of baseline illness, and SPA significantly influence OF. Higher SPA levels correspond to more severe OF.Keywords: ischemic stroke, self-perceived aging, oral frailty, elderly, association

Background

According to the China Stroke Surveillance Report 2021, the incidence of ischemic stroke among residents aged 40 and older in China was 538.1 per 100,000 in 2020, with an age-standardized incidence of 413.3 per 100,000.1 Ischemic stroke accounts for approximately 80% of all stroke cases, characterized by high disability and mortality rates, and ranks as the third leading cause of death globally.2 Influenced by lifestyle and dietary pattern changes, the incidence of ischemic stroke has gradually increased, creating significant burdens for patients, families, and society.2

Oral frailty (OF) is recognized as an essential aspect of healthy aging.3 Studies indicate that elderly ischemic stroke patients commonly exhibit poor oral health.4 Due to the complexity and multidimensional nature of oral health, scholars proposed the concept of OF. OF emphasizes the progressive decline in oral function with aging, accompanied by deterioration in both physical and mental capacities.5 As a geriatric syndrome, OF correlates with adverse health outcomes such as sarcopenia, malnutrition, cognitive impairment, and frailty, substantially impairing patients’ quality of life and prolonging disease progression.6–8 Stroke care guidelines from Australia, the United States, and Canada highlight the importance of assessing oral function and providing post-stroke oral care.9–11

Self-perceived aging (SPA) refers to older adults’ subjective perceptions and emotional responses when confronting physiological, psychological, and social aspects of aging.12 These perceptions and responses originate from age-related stereotypes and self-evaluation, subsequently influencing behaviors among older adults.12 Positive SPA improves physical function and mental health, facilitating successful adaptation to aging. Conversely, negative SPA adversely affects physical and mental health, potentially shortening lifespan.13 Research indicates that older adults with positive SPA environments readily accept long-term medical care and proactively manage personal finances. However, those in negative SPA environments often refuse extended medical treatment.14

Current research in China mainly examines relationships between factors such as age, disease severity, and comorbid conditions and OF in elderly ischemic stroke patients. The association between SPA and OF in this patient population remains unclear.15 The potential mechanisms linking SPA to OF may involve both behavioral and psychological pathways. At the behavioral level, elderly individuals with more negative perceptions of aging tend to exhibit lower health awareness and self-management motivation, which may manifest as neglect of daily oral hygiene, reduced frequency of dental visits, and avoidance of professional oral care. These behaviors may directly accelerate the deterioration of oral function, including mastication, swallowing, and salivary secretion.16 At the psychological level, negative SPA is often accompanied by emotional distress such as anxiety and depression, which can activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and increase cortisol secretion. Sustained cortisol elevation may suppress immune function and promote systemic inflammation, both of which have been shown to adversely affect oral mucosal integrity and salivary gland function.17,18 Furthermore, chronic inflammation is a known contributor to conditions such as xerostomia and oral mucosal lesions, which are closely related to the development of OF.19 Collectively, these behavioral and psychological mechanisms may interact and cumulatively predispose elderly stroke patients to OF. This study analyzed the relationship between SPA and OF among elderly ischemic stroke patients to provide guidance for clinical nursing care.

Objects and Methods

Research Objects

A total of 500 elderly ischemic stroke patients were recruited from the outpatient clinic and inpatient ward of the Department of Neurology at the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University, between January 2025 and September 2025.

Inclusion Criteria

①Patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke according to the Chinese Stroke Association guidelines for ischemic cerebrovascular disease20 and confirmed by cranial CT or MRI; ②Stable clinical condition at least 3 months after stroke onset; ③Conscious and capable of cooperating with investigators; ④Aged ≥ 65 years; ⑤ Patients who provided informed consent voluntarily.

Exclusion Criteria

① Patients diagnosed with severe cognitive or psychiatric disorders; ②Patients with severe communication impairments.

Research Tools

General Information Questionnaire

The researchers designed this questionnaire based on a literature review and study objectives. Information collected included patient gender, age, marital status, educational level, disease duration, smoking and drinking habits, chronic diseases, and place of residence. Patients were required to fast on the day of data collection. Venous blood samples were obtained to measure high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides, total cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels. Neurological deficits were assessed using the NIHSS scale. Infarct sizes were determined by CT imaging and classified as follows: Large infarct (maximum lesion diameter ≥5 cm); Moderate infarct (maximum lesion diameter 3–4 cm); Small infarct (maximum lesion diameter 1.5–2 cm); Lacunar infarction (maximum lesion diameter <1.5 cm).

B-APQ

This scale, developed by Sexton et al, measures elderly patients’ perceptions of aging.21 The questionnaire includes five domains: negative outcomes and control (5 items), positive outcomes (3 items), chronic timeline (3 items), positive control (3 items), and emotional representation (3 items). Each of the 17 items is scored using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Total scores range from 17 to 85, with higher scores indicating more negative perceptions of aging. It has been translated into simplified Chinese and validated among Chinese older adults, demonstrating good construct validity and internal consistency, supporting its applicability in this cultural context. The Scale-level Content Validity Index of the scale was 0.970, the split-half reliability was 0.738, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale is 0.914.21,22

OFI‐8

Tanaka et al developed this 8-item questionnaire to evaluate OF.18 Items 1–3 are scored 2 points for “Yes” and 0 points for “No”, items 4–5 are scored 1 point for “Yes” and 0 points for “No”, items 6–8 are scored 0 points for “Yes” and 1 point for “No”. Higher scores represent poorer oral health. Patients with a total OFI-8 score ≥ 4 were classified as having OF. This questionnaire has been scientifically validated in the Chinese population. The Scale-level Content Validity Index of the scale was 0.934, the split-half reliability was 0.889, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale is 0.949.23,24

Sample Size Calculation

The primary measurement tools used in this study (General Information Questionnaire, B-APQ, and OFI-8) included 40 variables. The sample size calculation followed a standard of 10 times the number of variables in the scales, with an additional 10% attrition rate. Thus, the calculated sample size required at least 440 elderly ischemic stroke patients.

Research Methods

This was a cross-sectional study. Convenience sampling was employed: eligible patients who presented to the neurology outpatient clinic or were admitted to the inpatient ward of the Department of Neurology at the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University between January 2025 and September 2025 were invited to participate based on their availability and accessibility during the study period. Eligible patients were identified by reviewing hospital records, and trained researchers subsequently contacted them by telephone. After obtaining informed consent, all questionnaire assessments and clinical measurements were collected at the Outpatient Health Service Center. The study protocol received approval from the hospital’s Ethics Committee (No. LS2023064) and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (No. ChiCTR2400085278) on June 4, 2024. The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The research flowchart is shown in Figure 1.

Statistical Methods

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Count data were presented as percentages (%) and analyzed by chi-square tests. Ordinal data were analyzed using rank-sum tests. Continuous variables with normal distributions were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using t-tests. Logistic regression models were employed to identify independent factors influencing OF in patients. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between OFI-8 and B-APQ scores. The statistical significance level was set at P = 0.05.

Results

Comparison of Baseline Characteristics Between the Two Patient Groups

A total of 500 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding incomplete responses, 488 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of 97.6%. Among these 488 elderly ischemic stroke patients, the mean B-APQ score was 53.18±11.16. The mean OFI-8 score was 6.14±3.53, with an OF incidence of 74.6%. Compared to the Non-OF group, the OF group had significantly higher age, NIHSS scores, infarct areas, hs-CRP levels, and B-APQ scores (P<0.05). Detailed results are provided in Table 1.

Discussion

Elevated of Score in Elderly Ischemic Stroke Patients

Results from this study indicate an average OFI-8 score of 6.14±3.53 among elderly ischemic stroke patients, with an OF prevalence of 74.6%. These findings exceed those previously reported among elderly individuals in Chinese communities.25 One possible explanation is that elderly ischemic stroke patients experience greater systemic inflammatory responses than the general elderly population. This increased inflammation may lead to oral conditions such as dry mouth and oral mucosal ulcers, thereby elevating the risk of OF.26 This inflammatory state is objectively reflected by elevated levels of hs‑CRP, a well‑established marker of systemic inflammation.27 Persistent elevation of hs‑CRP may contribute to damage of oral mucosal integrity, impairment of salivary gland function, and diminished tissue repair capacity, thereby exacerbating xerostomia and mucosal lesions and further increasing susceptibility to OF.19 The relatively high prevalence of OF (74.6%) observed in this study warrants further discussion. Several factors may explain this finding. First, all participants in this study were elderly patients with ischemic stroke, a population that commonly presents with varying degrees of neurological impairment, which may affect the coordination and sensory function of orofacial muscles, leading to compromised mastication, swallowing, and salivary secretion. Second, post-stroke declines in cognitive function and limitations in activities of daily living may hinder patients’ ability to maintain adequate daily oral hygiene practices. Third, the mean age of our study population was relatively advanced, and aging itself is a well-established independent risk factor for OF. Beyond systemic inflammation, several other factors may contribute to the elevated OF prevalence in this population. Age-related physiological changes, including progressive decline in oral motor function, reduced salivary flow, and diminished sensory acuity, are well-documented determinants of OF.5 In addition, social and behavioral factors—such as suboptimal oral hygiene practices, reduced dental attendance, and limited health literacy—may further compound the risk.5 Moreover, the presence of multimorbidity and polypharmacy, common in this population, may exacerbate xerostomia and impair oral tissue integrity, creating a vicious cycle that accelerates functional decline.5 Collectively, these multifaceted factors, along with heightened systemic inflammation, likely explain the particularly high OF burden observed in elderly patients with ischemic stroke. The combination of these factors likely contributes to the higher prevalence of OF observed in our study compared to that reported in general community-dwelling elderly populations.25 Healthcare providers should therefore pay close attention to the oral functional status of elderly stroke patients. When clinically indicated, multidisciplinary collaboration involving dentists, speech therapists, and rehabilitation specialists may be considered to facilitate comprehensive oral function evaluation and management.

Logistic multivariate regression analysis demonstrated that age, NIHSS score, infarct area, and hs-CRP levels significantly influence oral function in elderly ischemic stroke patients (P<0.05). The results confirm that advanced age, severity of baseline disease, and inflammatory markers adversely affect oral health. These findings align with previous research indicating that older age is associated with increased OFI-8 scores and poorer oral health.28 With aging, conditions such as dental wear, gum recession, periodontal disease, and tooth loss become increasingly common. These oral health issues may result in decreased chewing function, salivary gland atrophy, and an elevated risk of OF.29,30 The NIHSS score is commonly used to assess neurological deficits following stroke.10 Higher scores indicate more severe neurological impairment, often resulting in compromised swallowing coordination and impaired oral sensory-motor control. Consequently, stroke severity directly increases the risk of OF and aspiration.31 A larger infarct area typically indicates greater brain tissue damage, potentially disrupting neural pathways responsible for swallowing, chewing, and oral hygiene, significantly impairing oral motor function and accelerating OF progression.32 Due to the complex relationships between baseline conditions and OF in elderly ischemic stroke patients, further research on the underlying pathological mechanisms is necessary.

Elevated SPA Score in Elderly Ischemic Stroke Patients

The multifaceted sequelae of stroke—encompassing physical disability, social role changes, and emotional distress—not only contribute to negative SPA but also substantially impair the overall quality of life (QoL) in elderly survivors.33 Lourenço et al demonstrated that chronic functional dependency, as measured by the modified Rankin Scale, was a strong predictor of poorer physical, psychological, and general QoL domains. Furthermore, lower family income and comorbidities were also significantly associated with reduced QoL.33 These findings highlight that the burden of stroke extends beyond objective functional deficits, and that subjective perceptions of aging and health are closely intertwined with multidimensional QoL outcomes. Therefore, interventions aimed at improving SPA and oral health may have the potential to positively influence the broader QoL in this vulnerable population, although this hypothesis requires further investigation.

Results indicate that elderly ischemic stroke patients scored 53.18±11.16 on the B-APQ scale, reflecting moderately high levels of SPA. Stroke is a chronic condition characterized by persistent effects and challenges in achieving full recovery. It often results in sequelae, including long-term impairment of physical function, reduced mobility, changes in social roles, diminished social support, and ongoing medical needs. These persistent burdens may cause patients to perceive accelerated physical decline, triggering negative emotions such as anxiety and depression, thus intensifying their aging perceptions.34 Therefore, healthcare professionals should closely monitor SPA levels among elderly ischemic stroke patients, providing health counseling and psychological support to reduce the negative impacts of SPA.

Positive Correlation Between of and SPA in Elderly Chinese Ischemic Stroke Patients

Results indicate a positive correlation between OFI-8 scores and B-APQ scores among elderly ischemic stroke patients (P<0.05), suggesting that higher SPA is associated with more severe OF. Additionally, the OF group had significantly higher B-APQ scores compared to the non-OF group. Multivariate analysis revealed that each one-point increase in B-APQ score increased the risk of OF by 1.423-fold, representing a moderate‑strength effect that remained significant after adjustment for multiple confounders. Studies suggest that patients with higher SPA are more sensitive to negative aging effects, influencing their behaviors and lifestyle choices. These may include reduced attention to oral hygiene and avoidance of regular dental check-ups and cleanings, consequently worsening oral health and exacerbating OF severity.7,16 Moreover, it is possible that SPA may influence health through psycho-immunological pathways.35 Some studies have suggested that negative psychosocial factors may be associated with immune alterations,17,18 which could theoretically increase susceptibility to oral infections and exacerbate oral health deterioration.36–38 However, as immune function was not directly measured in the present study, this potential pathway remains speculative and should be investigated in future research with direct assessments of immunological parameters. In interpreting the observed association between SPA and OF, the possibility of reverse causation should also be considered. It is plausible that objective declines in oral function—such as impaired mastication, swallowing difficulties, and oral discomfort—may directly affect patients’ daily living and psychological well-being, thereby intensifying their negative perceptions of aging. Due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, the temporal direction of the observed association cannot be determined. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the temporal and causal relationships between SPA and OF.

Limitations

This study was a single-center cross-sectional investigation. Future research may enrich methodologies by conducting multicenter, large-sample prospective cohort studies, qualitative research, or even mixed-method studies. These approaches would provide a multimodal understanding of oral frailty and self-perceived aging among elderly ischemic stroke patients. Consequently, a predictive model correlating oral frailty with self-perceived aging risks in this population could be established, offering reference for developing relevant management and intervention strategies. In addition, several clinically important variables that have been shown to be associated with OF—including dysphagia, nutritional status, depression, and functional disability—were not assessed in this study due to the constraints of available data collection in this single-center cross-sectional survey. The absence of these variables may introduce residual confounding, and future studies with more comprehensive data collection are warranted to further elucidate the determinants of OF in this population.

Conclusion

OF prevalence among elderly Chinese ischemic stroke patients is relatively high, and is associated with advanced age, baseline disease severity, and SPA levels. Higher SPA levels are associated with greater OF severity. Healthcare professionals should be aware of the oral health status and SPA conditions in this population. Future research, particularly multicenter longitudinal studies, is warranted to further explore the temporal relationship between SPA and OF, and to investigate whether interventions targeting SPA may improve oral health outcomes in this population.
