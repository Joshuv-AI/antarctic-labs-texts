Post-infection colonization and recurrent infections by ST11-KL64 carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae: a study of within-host evolution
Authors: Jiao Xu, Yaxu Leng, Qiao Chen, Quan Guo, Ying Li, Zehui Yu, Enrique Joffré, Yue O. O. Hu, Huan Li, Renjing Hu, Jianhua Su, Yingshun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBacteria pose a serious threat to hosts through adaptive mutations that confer stress resistance and promote persistent colonization. Here, we describe an adaptive evolution event involving eight highly similar ST11-KL64 carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) strains, isolated from a non-infected inpatient who acquired two distinct CRKP strains, CRKP-F1 and CRKP-S2 during the first hospitalization, recovered, and was discharged after receiving antimicrobial therapy but subsequently experienced two additional recurrent febrile episodes and re-admission. The strain CRKP-S2 showed significantly enhanced resistance to oxidative stress, survival within macrophages, and internalization ability, and carried an additional ~72 kb fragment containing oxidative stress response factors (including NAD(P)-dependent oxidoreductases), and a ~ 19kb plasmid fragment harboring catA2, sul2, umuC/D genes, compared to the initial strain CRKP-F1. All four strains, CRKP-B3, CRKP-U4, CRKP-F5 and CRKP-S6, from the second hospitalization exhibited higher genetic similarity to CRKP-S2 than each other, and each of these strains has its own unique mutations compared to CRKP-S2. The third-hospitalization strain CRKP-U7 displayed the highest average nucleotide identity (ANI) with CRKP-S2, and possessed unique mutations in cecR, rlmA1, and selB, distinct from second-hospitalization strains. However, the last strain, CRKP-B8, carries a new gene mutation based on CRKP-U7 and exhibits greater host adaptability than all other isolates. While these findings are suggestive, whether the ~72-kb and ~19-kb fragments and mutations in CRKP-S2 drove enhanced colonization, and whether subsequent mutations contributed to subclones linked to recurrent febrile, or merely coincided, remains unclear. The possibility of mixed colonization by co-circulating subclones cannot be excluded, and functional validation is needed.

Introduction

As a core representative of Gram-negative “superbugs,” Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) has been classified as an “urgent threat” by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) due to its extensive drug resistance, high mortality rate, and rapid spread [1,2]. Currently, ST11-KL64 is the predominant CRKP clone in clinical settings in China [3]. However, this strain exhibits both carbapenem resistance and hypervirulent phenotypes by acquiring virulence plasmids (e.g., encoding rmpA and iucA) alongside resistance plasmids with KPC-2 or a hybrid plasmid containing both. These strains can evade host immune defenses and cause fatal infections, posing a significant challenge to clinical treatment [4,5].

CRKP has long been prevalent in hospitals, where it serves as a leading cause of hospital-acquired infection (HAI) among immuno-compromised inpatients, particularly the elderly and diabetic patients [6]. Additionally, in cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, the risk of hospital-acquired CRKP infection is significantly elevated as a consequence of prolonged neutropenia and mucosal barrier injury [7]. Alarmingly, persistent CRKP colonization may continue to be observed in a subset of patients with hospital-acquired CRKP infections, particularly in the intestinal, respiratory, and urinary tracts, despite clinical recovery following standard antimicrobial therapy [8]. Once established, K. pneumoniae employs various evolutionary strategies to evade host defenses and immune clearance, ultimately creating a cycle of chronic colonization and reinfection [9–11].

Previous studies have demonstrated that pathogens often adapt to their hosts through genomic mutations, such as recombination, point mutations, insertions or deletions, to evade immune responses [12,13]. For example, a KPC-producing K. pneumoniae ST258 strain was reported to persist in a patient for 4.5 years, during which it accumulated mutations at an average rate of 7.5 substitutions per genome per year, leading to substantial genetic and phenotypic diversity [11]. The capsule-deficient mutant (Δwbap) demonstrates attenuated capsular polysaccharide synthesis, with significantly enhanced epithelial cell invasion and robust in vitro biofilm formation, which together result in improved host adaptation [14]. Whole-genome sequencing of strains repeatedly isolated from the same patient has identified mutations in virulence genes (such as rmpA and rmpA2) and capsular polysaccharide synthesis genes (such as wcaJ and wbap), which are associated with enhanced immune evasion and host adaptation [15]. Pathogens also enhance genomic plasticity through mutations in DNA mismatch repair genes. The mutS mutation, for instance, enables Salmonella to adapt to antimicrobial pressure and host environments [12]. Moreover, Helicobacter pylori displays distinct mutational signatures in different gastric compartments, indicating that its evolution within the host is site-specific and may involve multiple clonal subpopulations [16].

Klebsiella pneumoniae, a major hospital-acquired pathogen, can establish persistent colonization within patients, as evidenced by repeated isolation of the bacterium from the same inpatient [17]. This persistent colonization is maintained by phenotypic adaptations that enhance host colonization and survival, including reduced capsular polysaccharide synthesis, hypermucoid phenotype, increased biofilm formation, enhanced epithelial cell invasion, oxidative stress tolerance, and lipopolysaccharide structural modifications [17–19]. For example, some microevolved strains exhibit mutations in capsule and siderophore-encoding genes, as well as alterations in the mucoid phenotype, thereby enhancing host adaptation and antioxidant capacity [20]. In ST11-KL64 CR-hvKP, the loss of the virulence gene cluster iroBCDN has been associated with enhanced antioxidant capacity and improved survival in macrophages, thus providing an evolutionary advantage [21]. A recC point mutation has been reported to increase recombination efficiency, thereby promoting the acquisition of rmpA-positive pLVPK-like plasmids that confer resistance to phagocytosis. In addition, the presence of an IncFII-type multidrug resistance plasmid was found to accelerate the spread of KL64-hvKP [13].

In our study, eight similar CRKP isolates were obtained from an anti-tumor patient who, after initially contracting a hospital-acquired CRKP infection, experienced recurrent febrile episodes over the course of one year, resulting in a total of three hospitalizations. Whole genome sequencing (WGS), CPS quantification, macrophage survival assay, in vitro oxidative stress assay, anti-phagocytosis assay, and invasion assay were performed to investigate factors potentially associated with the enhanced host adaptation and colonization capacity of CRKP-S2 compared with CRKP-F1, which may be linked to recurrent febrile episodes and repeated hospitalizations. Additionally, based on the string test, biofilm formation, siderophore production, serum killing assay, animal infection models, phylogenetic tree analysis, and ANI/SNP analyses, our data suggest that the four isolates from the second hospitalization and CRKP-U7 are phylogenetically related to CRKP-S2, while CRKP-B8 is related to CRKP-U7.

Materials and methods

Patient and eight clinical isolates of CRKP

In March 2021, a patient was hospitalized due to a tumor. Following the initial CRKP infection, the patient recovered after antimicrobial treatment and was discharged. However, the patient was again hospitalized twice for recurrent febrile episodes and other symptoms. Across the three hospitalizations, a total of eight CRKP strains were isolated (Figure 1). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The broth microdilution method was used to investigate the antimicrobial susceptibility of the strains in vitro according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). The control strains for drug susceptibility tests were E. coli ATCC 25922 and K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603. Interpretation of results follows CLSI M100 33rd edition 2023 with reference to the European Commission for Antimicrobial Sensitivity Testing (EUCAST) [22].

String test

The string test was performed using 5% sheep blood agar, as this medium has been reported to exhibit the highest diagnostic accuracy for detecting the hypermucoviscous (HMV) phenotype [5,23]. All isolates were inoculated onto an agar plate containing 5% sheep blood and cultured overnight at 37°C. The string test was positive when a single colony formed a viscous string > 5 mm in length, contacting and pulling upward with a standard inoculation loop.

Chrome azurol S (CAS) plate assay for iron carrier content

The CAS plate assay was employed to evaluate siderophore secretion. The strains were inoculated into LB liquid medium and cultured overnight. Bacteria were then harvested, washed twice, and adjusted OD600 to 0.5 McFarland. A 5 μL aliquot of the bacterial solution was spotted onto CAS plates. Plates were incubated at 30°C for 48 h [14].

Growth curve

The growth curves were investigated as described previously with some modifications [24]. Bacteria were incubated overnight and a total volume of 200 μL (0.5 McFarland) was transferred to a 96-well polystyrene microtiter plate (Falcon), incubated at 37°C for 12 h and absorbance (OD600) was measured every 1 h.

Biofilm formation assay

The biofilm biomass of isolated strains was analyzed by crystal violet staining. Overnight culture was diluted 1:100 and grown to mid-log phase [25]. Bacterial solution with a total volume of 200 μL (0.5 McFarland) was transferred to a 96-well polystyrene microtiter plate (Falcon). After incubation at 37°C for 48 h, bacteria were washed twice with 200 µL of distilled water. Each well was added with 200 μL 0.1% crystal violet solution, incubated for 15 min, washed 3 times with PBS, and dried at room temperature. Binding dyes were released from stained cells using 95% v/v ethanol (95% absolute ethanol and 5% glacial acid), and absorbance was measured at OD600.

Capsular polysaccharide

CPS extraction used a modified carbazole assay [26,27]. Overnight bacterial cultures (OD600 = 1.0, ~1 × 109 CFU/mL) were mixed with 1% (w/v) amphoteric detergent (100 μL per 500 μL suspension) and incubated at 50°C for 20 min. Following centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 min), 500 μL supernatant was precipitated with 1 mL cold absolute ethanol overnight at −20°C. After centrifugation, the pellet was air-dried at room temperature. The pellet was resuspended in 200 μL distilled water, mixed with 1.2 mL sodium tetraborate-sulfuric acid solution, heated in a boiling water bath (5 min), and immediately cooled on ice. After adding 20 μL hydroxybiphenyl (1.5 mg/mL) and vortex-mixing (5 min), 200 μL of the mixture was used for OD520 measurement. CPS content was quantified against a glucuronic acid standard curve.

Cell adhesion and invasion assay

A549 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 2.5 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight. Logarithmic-phase bacteria (OD600 = 0.1) were used to infect cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 for 2 h (37°C, 5% CO2). After PBS washing, extracellular bacteria were eliminated with 100 μg/mL polymyxin B (1 h). Cells were lysed with 0.25% Triton X-100, and lysates were serially diluted on LB agar for CFU quantification. For the invasion assay, cells were gently washed three times with PBS and lysed with 0.25% Triton X-100 after 2 h of infection [21,28].

Survival in macrophage assay

RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded in 24-well plates at 2.5 × 105 cells/well. After 24 h, cells were infected (MOI = 20:1) for 2 h (37°C, 5% CO2). Extracellular bacteria were eliminated with 100 μg/mL polymyxin B (1 h). Cells were lysed in 0.25% Triton X-100 (5 min), and lysates were serially diluted on LB agar for CFU enumeration [29]. For survival in the macrophage assay, Klebsiella pneumoniae (MOI = 20:1) was added to macrophages and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Extracellular bacteria were then eliminated by adding 100 μg/mL polymyxin B. After 2 h incubation, cells were lysed with 0.25% (v/v) Triton X-100 [30]. Bacterial survival within macrophages was visualized using inverted fluorescence microscopy. Logarithmic-phase bacteria were labeled by incubating with 100 μL of 20 mg/mL FITC for 1 h in darkness. Macrophages were infected at MOI 20 for 2 h. After eliminating extracellular bacteria with polymyxin B, F-actin was stained with 200 μL Actin-Tracker Red-Rhodamine (45 min, dark), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 min). Images were acquired using ImageView x64 software [30].

Measurement of antioxidant capacity in vitro

Logarithmic-phase bacterial suspensions were adjusted to OD600 = 0.1. Equal volumes (200 μL) of bacterial suspension and H2O2 solution were mixed to achieve a final concentration of 5 mM. After incubation with shaking at 150 rpm for 30 min, samples were serially diluted and plated on LB agar for viable count determination [30].

Serum killing assay

Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to 1 × 107 CFU/mL during the logarithmic growth phase. A 90 μL aliquot of pooled normal human serum was mixed with 30 μL of bacterial suspension at a 3:1 (v/v) ratio. The mixtures were incubated at 37°C with continuous shaking (150 rpm). Viable colony counts were determined after 0, 60, 120, and 180 minutes of incubation. Serial dilutions of the mixtures were plated onto LB agar plates and cultured overnight at 37°C to quantify bacterial survival rates [31].

Murine infection model

The female BALB/c mice (aged 6–8 weeks) used in this study were procured from the Experimental Animal Center of Southwest Medical University. A total of 70 mice were randomly divided into ten groups with seven mice per group, comprising one blank control group (PBS group), eight experimental groups, and a NTUH-K2044 group. Briefly, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 100 µL (0.05 McFarland) of exponential phase bacterial suspension or sterile PBS. At 24 hours post-infection, the liver, kidney, spleen, and lung tissues were aseptically collected and weighed. Subsequently, the tissues were then homogenized for bacterial enumeration. The results are expressed as colony-forming units (CFU) per 0.1 gram of tissue. Since NTUH-K2044 is a highly virulent strain, when mice were injected intraperitoneally with 1 × 106 CFU/mL, they all died within less than 24 h. Therefore, the organ load of NTUH-K2044 was not considered as a control.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and bioinformatic analysis

All strains were sequenced using Illumina HiSeq and assembled using SOAPdenovo v2.04. On this basis, CRKP-F1, CRKP-S2, and CRKP-B8 strains were sequenced using PacBio RSII and assembled using Unicycler v0.4.7 to obtain complete chromosome and plasmid sequences. SOAPdenovo v2.04 and Unicycler v0.4.7 were used to perform de novo assembly for both the draft and complete genome sequences. Genes were annotated using the RAST online tool (https://rast.nmpdr.org/FIG/rast.cgi) [32] and subsequently verified through BLASTn (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) [33,34]. MLST analysis was carried out using MLST v2.0 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/MLST/) [35]. Antimicrobial and virulence genes were identified using the ResFinder 4.1 and VFDB 2019 databases, respectively [36,37]. ANI plots were plotted using ChiPlot online platform (https://chiplot.online/). Easyfig was used to compare the various mobile genetic elements [38]. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using a core-genome single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based approach. SNPs were identified using Snippy v4.6.0. A core SNP alignment was generated using the snippy-core utility. Subsequently, a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was reconstructed from this alignment using IQ-TREE v2.2.0 with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. The final tree was visualized and annotated using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) platform. In silico functional enrichment analysis was performed using DAVID (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/). The results were visualized using R v4.2.0 with the ggplot2 package.

Informed consent

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient. Consent for publication was granted by the patient herself.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Student’s t-test (Prism v9 software). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. Non-parametric tests were used when these assumptions were violated. Each experiment was performed with three independent biological replicates (n = 3), and data are shown as mean ± SD. No multiple-testing correction was applied, as each phenotype comparison was performed independently with pre-defined hypotheses based on specific experimental questions. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

CRKP persistently colonizes the host and causes recurrent infections in an uninfected tumor patient following an initial hospital-acquired CRKP infection

On 4 March 2021, a 77-year-old woman hospitalized with abdominal pain and distension was diagnosed with gallbladder cancer at the Department of Hepatobiliary Surgery, Wuxi Second People’s Hospital. Subsequently, the patient underwent anti-tumor therapy. On 23 March 2021, the patient presented with a febrile episode and diarrhea. The first CRKP strain, designated CRKP-F1, was isolated from fecal samples on 25 March 2021. Following cefoperazone-sulbactam treatment, the patient’s infection symptoms improved. A second strain (CRKP-S2) was still detected in sputum on 30 March 2021. After receiving continuous cefoperazone-sulbactam treatment, the patient’s condition stabilized and was discharged smoothly. On 17 November 2021, the patient was hospitalized again due to a febrile episode accompanied by dyspnea. On 19 November 2021, CRKP-B3 was detected in the blood culture. On 6 December 2021, the patient underwent a urine culture test owing to dysuria, and CRKP-U4 was identified. On 17 December 2021, the patient presented with new symptoms of cough and expectoration, along with a febrile episode, and then, CRKP-F5 and CRKP-S6 were detected in feces and sputum samples, respectively. Following ceftazidime-avibactam therapy, the patient was discharged again. On 2 March 2022, the patient was admitted to the hospital for the third time with dysuria and a febrile episode. On 8 March 2022, the urine culture results revealed the presence of CRKP-U7. During tigecycline-polymyxin combination therapy, blood culture detected CRKP-B8 again on 18 March 2022. The patient was discharged following ceftazidime treatment (Figure 1).

As shown in Table 1, eight isolates all expressed resistance to amikacin, norfloxacin, ampicillin, cefuroxime sodium, ceftazidime, imipenem, meropenem, ciprofloxacin, tetracycline, yet retained susceptibility to ceftazidime-avibactam and polymyxin B. Notably, except for the CRKP-F1 strain, all remaining strains exhibited resistance to chloramphenicol. WGS confirmed that all isolates belonged to the ST11-KL64 and harbored the KPC-2 gene. Association of additional ~72-kb and ~19-kb fragments and mutations in CRKP-S2 with enhanced antioxidant capacity

To investigate the biological characteristics exerted on the host by two distinct CRKP strains acquired during the patient’s first hospitalization, a comprehensive phenotypic analysis was first performed. No significant difference was observed between the two strains in growth rate or colony morphology, and both were non-HMV (Fig. S2a-c). Next, the virulence and pathogenicity of these two strains were investigated. As shown in Figure 2(a,b) the second strain CRKP-S2 exhibited significantly enhanced invasion of human A549 but reduced resistance to phagocytosis by RAW264.7, compared to CRKP-F1 (Figure 2(a,b)). Notably, CRKP-S2 survival within macrophages was markedly higher than CRKP-F1 (Figure 2(c,d)). To further assess macrophage survival, an oxidative stress assay was performed under H2O2 induction. The test showed that CRKP-S2 exhibited pronouncedly enhanced antioxidant capacity (Figure 2(e)). In addition, a marked reduction in CPS production was observed in CRKP-S2 compared with CRKP-F1 (Figure 2(f)). CRKP-S2 also exhibited markedly lower adherence to host cells than CRKP-F1 (Figure 2(g)), indicating attenuated pathogenicity, as determined by cellular adhesion assay. Next, to investigate the potential causes of these phenotypic variations, we conducted a systematic genomic analysis of these two strains. Genomic analysis revealed that the second strain CRKP-S2 had substantial genetic differences compared to the initially isolated strain CRKP-F1, mainly including SNPs, an additional ~72 kb chromosomal fragment, and an additional ~19 kb fragment on an IncFII-type plasmid. Mutations in fyuA and iutA were observed in CRKP-S2, relative to CRKP-F1 (Fig. S3). Interestingly, the ~72-kb fragment was located between the ATP-binding protein of the ABC transporter and the aldehyde/ketone reductase, encompassing 62 open reading frames (ORFs) (Figure 2(h)). These ORFs include NAD(P)-dependent oxidoreductases and transcriptional regulatory factors related to oxidative stress (ramA, tetR, rpoN, KPHS_13290, lacI, and luxR families) (Figure 2(i), Table S1), which are closely associated with oxidative stress responses [39,40]. Structural characterization of the ramA gene within this island was performed using NCBI CD-Search and InterProScan to assess its regulatory potential. The analysis indicated that this allele encodes a structurally intact RamA-family transcriptional regulator with a highly conserved helix-turn-helix (HTH) DNA-binding domain (E-value = 1.27 ×10−81). Lacking disruptive mutations or premature stop codons, the intact domain structure suggests its functional capacity to regulate the adjacent NAD(P)-dependent oxidoreductases within the ~72 kb adaptive module. We next performed GO enrichment analysis to gain further insight into the functions of these additional genes. As shown in Fig. S4, the analysis revealed significant enrichment of genes involved in import across plasma membrane (p = 0.025), carbohydrate transmembrane transport (p = 0.050), and transaminase activity (p = 0.050). The transmembrane transport functions may help the bacteria acquire nutrients in the host environment. Transaminase activity is linked to amino acid metabolism and redox balance. This is consistent with the presence of NAD(P)-dependent oxidoreductases and oxidative stress-related regulators on this fragment. The ~19 kb fragment on the plasmid exhibits a linear structure, flanked on one side by a removable IS26 element and on the other side by a truncated ltrA gene (IS26-catA2-ORF-IS26-ORFs-TnpA-glmM-sul2-ORF-IS5075-Tn3-IS26-ORF-IntI1-ORF-IS26-mcp-umuC-umuD-ltrA). It carries 21 ORFs, including catA2, sul2, five transposases (three IS26, a Tn3 and an IS5075), umuC and umuD (Fig. S5). To further explore the potential impact of the ~19-kb fragment, we performed functional annotation of its gene content through bioinformatics analysis. As shown in Table S2, the fragment carries genes predicted to be involved in oxidative stress survival (umuC/umuD), metabolic flexibility (catA), cell envelope biosynthesis (glmM), chemotaxis (mcp), and transcriptional regulation (ltrA). Combined with phenotypic assays and bioinformatics analysis, the ~72-kb and ~19-kb fragments were associated with enhanced macrophage survival, increased antioxidant capacity, and reduced CPS production. Thus, together with mutations in fyuA and iutA, these additional fragments were associated with the superior fitness of CRKP-S2 within the host and with recurrent infection, though functional experiments are needed for confirmation.

Genetic mutations and subtle phenotypic differences of four second-hospitalization CRKP strains relative to CRKP-S2

To investigate the relationship between CRKP-S2 and the strains isolated during the patient’s second hospitalization, we performed a systematic comparative phenotypic analysis of five isolates. As shown in Figure 3, despite sharing the same host origin, four CRKP strains of the second hospitalization displayed subtle phenotypic differences. Firstly, string assays revealed that CRKP-F5 and CRKP-S6 exhibited a hypermucoid (HMV) phenotype, whereas CRKP-S2, CRKP-B3 and CRKP-U4 were non-HMV (Fig. S2b). However, when cultured on blood agar plates, all five strains showed similar colony morphology, with no significant differences (Fig. S2c). To determine whether the different mucoid phenotypes correlated with CPS levels, we quantified CPS production. The capsule content of the four strains from the second hospitalization was less than that of CRKP-S2, however, the difference was not statistically significant (Figure 3(a)). To further characterize these isolates, the five isolates were evaluated for their ability to produce siderophore, form biofilm, resist serum, and tolerate in vitro stress conditions. As shown in Figure 3(b), the siderophore secretion capacity of CRKP-U4, CRKP-F5, and CRKP-S6 was significantly lower than that of the CRKP-S2 isolate. In contrast, while CRKP-B3 showed slightly higher secretion relative to CRKP-S2, the difference was not statistically significant (Figure 3(b)). Consistently, it was found that the CRKP-U4, CRKP-F5, and CRKP-S6 had a markedly reduced biofilm-forming capacity compared to the CRKP-S2 isolate. Nevertheless, no significant difference in biofilm formation ability was observed between CRKP-B3 and CRKP-S2 (Figure 3(c)). What’s more, the second-hospitalization isolates were more susceptible to serum-mediated killing compared with CRKP-S2, as determined by serum killing assay (Fig. S2d). All four strains showed comparable resistance to oxidative stress in vitro (Figure 3(d)). Additionally, the growth curves of the five strains were similar (Fig. S2a). To assess the pathogenicity of these strains in vivo, a mouse model of intraperitoneal infection was employed. All strains were detected in liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues 24 hours post-infection. Among the strains, CRKP-B3 and CRKP-U4 exhibited lower bacterial loads than CRKP-S2, whereas CRKP-F5 and CRKP-S6 (HMV phenotypes) had bacterial loads one to two orders of magnitude higher than CRKP-S2, CRKP-B3, and CRKP-U4 (Figure 3(e–h)). Subsequently, to gain insight into the genomic characteristics of the five bacterial isolates and CRKP-F1, whole-genome sequencing was performed. Analysis of genomic data revealed that ANI values ranged from 98.86% to 99.15% when comparing the four strains from the second hospitalization with the strains CRKP-F1 and CRKP-S2, which were obtained from the first hospitalization. Besides, the ANI between CRKP-S2 and the strains from the second hospitalization was higher than the ANIs between CRKP-B3, CRKP-U4, CRKP-F5, and CRKP-S6 (Fig. S1). Interestingly, similar to CRKP-S2, all four strains were found to carry an additional ~72 Kbp chromosomal segment and a ~ 19 Kbp plasmid fragment, which were absent in the first isolate (CRKP-F1). To further investigate the correlation between phenotypic variations and genomic mutations, SNP analysis was performed. It was found that four second-hospitalization isolates harbored unique mutations compared to strain CRKP-S2. As shown in Fig. S3 and Table S1, CRKP-B3 harbored mutations in ymdB (H64P) and ruvX (K82N, D95V, E96D); CRKP-U4 carried mutations in wzc (A651V), fyuA (M320L), qnrs (H11R), wecG (R236C), and citF (A300T); CRKP-F5 exhibited mutations in wzc (S475F, G663E) and lgoR (L172F); and CRKP-S6 possessed only a wzc (G663E) mutation (Fig. S3, Table S1). Notably, there were no identical mutations among CRKP-B3, CRKP-U4, CRKP-F5, and CRKP-S6. SNP analysis using CRKP-S2 as the reference revealed that the four second-hospitalization isolates carried 5–7 SNPs relative to CRKP-S2 (Figure 5(a)). Phylogenetic analysis further showed that CRKP-S2 clustered together with these four isolates within the ST11 clade, suggesting a close genetic relationship (Figure 5(b)). These data are consistent with the possibility that the four second-hospitalization strains are phylogenetically related to CRKP-S2, each acquiring distinct mutations and subtle phenotypic variations. Additionally, isolates from different anatomical sites carried distinct mutation profiles, which may be associated with different host niches.

CRKP-U7 isolated in third hospitalization shows genomic and phenotypic links to CRKP-S2, and CRKP-B8 shows similar links to CRKP-U7

Despite receiving extensive antimicrobial agents during the second hospitalization, which temporarily suppressed the CRKP infection and alleviated symptoms, the patient developed a third febrile episode and other symptoms after discharge. During the third hospitalization, two CRKP strains, CRKP-U7 and CRKP-B8, were isolated.

Firstly, the virulence phenotypes of the isolates were systematically analyzed. As shown in Fig. S2c, there were no significant differences in colony morphology between CRKP-U7 and CRKP-B8 (Fig. S2c). Siderophore assays showed no difference in secretion between the two strains and CRKP-S2 (Figure 4(a)). When assessed for biofilm formation capacity, CRKP-U7 showed comparable performance to CRKP-S2 and CRKP-B8 (Figure 4(b)). No significant differences were observed in antioxidant stress resistance or CPS content between CRKP-U7, CRKP-B8, and the first-hospitalization isolate CRKP-S2 (Figure 4(c,d)). It was found that CRKP-U7 showed enhanced resistance relative to CRKP-B3, CRKP-U4, and CRKP-S6, whereas CRKP-B8 and CRKP-S2 displayed the highest resistance among all tested isolates (Fig. S2d). In liver and kidney tissues, the bacterial burdens of CRKP-U7 and CRKP-B8 were found to be significantly lower than that of the first-hospitalization CRKP-S2 isolate (Figure 4(e–h)). In addition, in vitro antioxidant assays revealed that CRKP-B8 had enhanced antioxidant defense, as indicated by its increased survival rate under menadione-induced oxidative stress (Fig. S2e). Discussion

Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) is a major cause of hospital-acquired infections worldwide, posing high morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [41]. After infection, CRKP can persistently colonize the host and often resists clearance by immunity or antimicrobial agents [11]. During colonization, it undergoes microevolution, including gene mutations, recombination and horizontal gene transfer, which enable adaptation and recurrent infections [42–44]. Currently, studies have shown that capsule modifications allow K. pneumoniae to avoid recognition and phagocytosis by macrophages, highlighting the role of phenotypic adaptations in persistent infections [42,45]. In this study, we analyzed the host adaptation of eight ST11-KL64 CRKP strains isolated from a cancer patient who, after an initial hospital-acquired CRKP infection, experienced recurrent febrile episodes and three hospitalizations. Following the patient’s initial CRKP infection, the second isolated strain, CRKP-S2, was recovered from sputum despite clinical improvement following antimicrobial therapy. Phenotypic profiling revealed that CRKP-S2 displayed significantly enhanced oxidative stress tolerance, macrophage survival capacity, and epithelial cell invasiveness compared to CRKP-F1. Furthermore, comparative genomic analysis revealed significant genomic differences between the second and first isolates. Specifically, CRKP-S2 harbored an additional ~72 kb chromosomal fragment and a ~ 19 kb plasmid fragment compared to CRKP-F1, which may be associated with rapid host adaptation. The ~72 kb chromosomal fragment carries multiple genes primarily associated with host adaptation and oxidative stress defense. These include the transcriptional regulator rpoN (involved in nitrogen metabolism, oxidative stress, and virulence gene regulation) [39,46]; luxR, a quorum sensing regulator controlling virulence, biofilm formation, resistance dissemination, and environmental fitness; and ramA, a global regulator modulating drug resistance, environmental adaptability, and virulence via efflux pumps and membrane proteins [9,47]. NAD(P)-dependent oxidoreductases are also encoded within the fragment and are responsible for providing NADPH-dependent antioxidant defense, which is critical for survival and proliferation within the host [40].

In addition to the ~72-kb gene fragment, the strain also exhibited several other genomic variations. These genomic variations comprised an additional tRNA-Arg and a truncated putative cysteine hydrolase. tRNA-Arg is often recognized as a preferential integration hotspot for exogenous genetic elements, which may be associated with bacterial evolution and environmental adaptation [44,48]. Mutations in the siderophore receptor genes fyuA and iutA were observed in CRKP-S2. Recent studies have shown that host adaptability and persistent colonization of CRKP can be enhanced through the modulation of virulence gene expression [14,17]. Although no significant differences in siderophore secretion were detected between CRKP-F1 and CRKP-S2, we speculate that these mutations may represent a compensatory mechanism by which metabolic resources are reallocated. CRKP-S2 fine-tuned its iron acquisition systems without reducing siderophore production. This may have lowered the metabolic burden of virulence expression, freeing resources for oxidative stress resistance and intracellular survival. At the plasmid level, significant alterations were also observed, with the most notable being an additional ~19-kb gene fragment accompanied by an extra ISKpn26 element. This ~19-kb fragment, flanked by IS26 on one side and the ItrA gene on the other, exhibited a distinct linear structure. Notably, the umuC and umuD genes, key components of the DNA damage repair system, may play a role in CRKP survival under host inflammatory stress, particularly in ROS-rich environments [49]. Sequence alignment showed 100% identity and coverage with a region of plasmid pKP20194c4-p3 (accession CP054747.1), suggesting that transposon-mediated insertions may facilitate cross-strain genetic exchange [44]. Together, these findings deepen our understanding of CRKP genetic diversity and provide new insights into its resistance mechanisms and evolutionary trajectories.

An interesting phenomenon was observed in CRKP-S2 and its subsequent isolates, which exhibited reduced CPS production, decreased adhesion, and weakened serum resistance, characteristics consistent with classic patterns of virulence attenuation. However, these strains also demonstrated enhanced macrophage survival and antioxidant capacity. This paradox can be explained by the evolutionary trade-off between acute virulence and long-term survival. High-virulence traits, such as strong capsular production and high serum resistance, may promote rapid disease progression but also trigger a strong host immune response, leading to faster infection clearance [10,50]. In contrast, reduced acute virulence facilitates bacterial persistence, immune evasion, and intracellular survival [50]. Clinically, some CR-hvKP strains carrying wild-type rmpA exhibit low virulence, with CPS abundance dissociated from the hypermucoid phenotype [27,51], This suggests that reduced CPS, while associated with decreased virulence, may favor long-term bacterial survival [17,27]. Similar phenomena have been reported during within-host evolution of ST11-KL64 CRKP [17]. Mutation in wzc or wcaJ that alter CPS production have been shown to simultaneously reduce biofilm formation and epithelial cell adhesion while enhancing resistance to macrophage phagocytosis or increasing epithelial cell invasion [18]. Moreover, different pathogenic types of Klebsiella pneumoniae exhibit varying adhesion and invasion abilities [52]. While capsule reduction often enhances adhesion and invasion in some strains, the concurrent decrease in CPS and adhesion with enhanced invasion in our isolates may represent an evolutionary strategy to evade immune recognition and improve host adaptation [53,54]. Although serum resistance is diminished, the survival of Klebsiella pneumoniae in blood depends on multiple tissue-specific adaptive factors, rather than solely on serum resistance [40,52]. Bacteria are able to survive in the host through other mechanisms, such as enhanced antioxidant capacity, reflecting an optimization strategy where limited resources are redirected from high-energy-cost serum resistance to oxidative stress tolerance and intracellular survival [30]. Therefore, the reduced CPS production, decreased adhesion, and weakened serum resistance observed in CRKP-S2 and its subsequent isolates may not represent a loss of adaptation, but rather an optimized strategy for achieving persistent colonization within the host. This allows the bacteria to sacrifice some acute virulence in favor of enhanced colonization and persistence.

After bacteria adapt to their hosts, they may establish colonization while undergoing parallel microevolutionary changes [11,12,55,56], which can be influenced by the host infection environment. Colonizing bacteria are thus important drivers of recurrent infections [42]. In this study, the patient experienced two recurrent episodes, during which strains with high genetic similarity but distinct mutations were isolated. Notably, CRKP-U4, CRKP-F5, and CRKP-S6 harbored mutations in the capsule synthesis gene wzc, which likely contributed to reduced CPS production. Despite this suppression, CRKP-F5 and CRKP-S6 exhibited a hypermucoid phenotype (HMV) and showed higher bacterial loads in mouse tissues in the intraperitoneal infection model. These observations are consistent with a possible association between wzc mutations and hypermucoidosis, but further investigation is needed. However, the murine intraperitoneal infection model does not fully replicate the natural colonization sites such as the gastrointestinal, urinary, or respiratory tracts, which limits the direct applicability of these findings to real-world infections. The use of NTUH-K2044 as a comparator also has limited relevance to ST11-KL64 evolution. Bearing these limitations in mind, our in vivo data showed that HMV phenotype strains (CRKP-F5 and CRKP-S6) had higher bacterial loads in multiple organs compared to other isolates. These results are consistent with enhanced bacterial survival within the model, but should be interpreted as supportive evidence rather than definitive proof of host adaptation. Further studies using more representative infection models (e.g. intragastric or intratracheal inoculation) are needed to validate these findings in natural infection contexts.

Recent studies indicate that multiple factors influence HMV, with CPS exerting a stronger effect than pLVPK-like virulence plasmids, which typically carry regulatory genes rmpA and rmpA2 [19,31]. The S682N mutation in wzc has been reported to induce HMV while impairing biofilm formation [18]. In this study, the wzc gene was found to carry the mutations A651V, S475F, and G663E, with CRKP-U4 carrying A651V, CRKP-F5 the double mutations S475F and G663E, and CRKP-S6 G663E. Combined with string test and biofilm assays, these findings suggest that, beyond S682N, other wzc mutations may be linked to the emergence of the HMV phenotype, which is associated with enhanced resistance to phagocytosis, increased transmission efficiency, and higher mortality in animal models. Notably, the HMV phenotype occurred without increased CPS production in CRKP-F5 and CRKP-S6, suggesting that mechanisms beyond CPS synthesis, possibly mutations in the wzc gene, may correlate with this phenotype, consistent with previous reports [18].

Recent studies have found that CRKP incurs fitness cost after acquiring tigecycline resistance, resulting in reduced virulence [27,57]. In our study, the CPS content of later isolates was reduced compared with the initial strain. Although CRKP-S2, CRKP-B3, CRKP-U7, and CRKP-B8 carried no detectable mutations in CPS synthesis genes, their phenotypic differences may relate to the presence of additional gene fragments. Such additional fragments may correlate with host adaptation and reduced virulence as a compensatory trade-off [57,58]. This phenotypic shift likely represents an adaptive strategy to evade host immune clearance and promote long-term persistence within the host [57,59]. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear.

Furthermore, the distinct mutations detected in isolates from different anatomical sites (blood, urine, feces, and sputum) suggest that strains from distinct microenvironments within the same patient may undergo adaptive evolutionary trade-offs in response to niche-specific selective pressures [18]. Through this process, isolates may acquire key survival advantages, such as enhanced colonization and improved immune evasion strategies [14]. The observation that no identical mutations were found among the second-hospitalization isolates, combined with their distinct phenotypic profiles, suggests that these genetic differences may contribute to the observed phenotypic diversity. The close relationship between CRKP-U7 and CRKP-S2, along with the unique mutations found in isolates from different anatomical sites, supports the idea that the adaptive Klebsiella pneumoniae population may consist of multiple subpopulations evolving along distinct evolutionary trajectories. Our findings are consistent with the hypothesis that CRKP-U7, with enhanced survival compared to the second-hospitalization isolates, is phylogenetically related to CRKP-S2. Furthermore, CRKP-B8 is phylogenetically related to CRKP-U7, as suggested by their shared mutations and enhanced survival phenotype. These conclusions are supported by two complementary genomic metrics. ANI values (98.84%–99.15%) and SNP distances (0–10 SNPs) provide complementary views of genomic relatedness. ANI is calculated using whole-genome sequences, including all conserved and variable regions, plasmid sequences, and additional genomic fragments. In contrast, SNP analysis is restricted to core-genome positions relative to CRKP-S2, excluding mobile genetic elements and recombinant regions. The slightly lower ANI values in some isolates are largely attributable to the presence of additional ~72-kb and ~19-kb fragments in CRKP-S2 and its descendants, which reduce the overall whole-genome identity. Furthermore, ANI and SNP analyses were performed using different software (ANI: ChiPlot; SNP: Snippy v4.6.0) and different reference points (ANI: pairwise genome comparison; SNP: CRKP-S2 as the reference). These methodological differences mean that ANI reflects large-scale genomic changes (e.g. fragment gains/losses), while SNP analysis captures fine-scale point mutations. Importantly, no assembly fragmentation was observed, and all isolates were processed consistently within each analytical pipeline. Thus, the two metrics are not contradictory but complementary: ANI captures large-scale genomic changes, while SNP analysis reveals fine-scale mutational events that have accumulated during within-host evolution.

However, an alternative explanation for the genetic relationships observed among these isolates should be considered. While the acquisition of unique mutations over time is consistent with within-host evolution from CRKP-S2, we cannot rule out the possibility of mixed colonization by multiple CRKP subclones. The patient may have harbored a diverse population of CRKP strains during the infection, with different subclones sampled at different time points instead of a single lineage evolving linearly. Our sampling strategy was longitudinal but not exhaustive, so we acknowledge that unsampled diversity may exist. To distinguish between linear evolution and co-circulating subclones, higher-resolution sampling (e.g. multiple colonies per time point) is needed and should be explored in future studies.

Additionally, because the ~72 kb chromosomal fragment, the ~19 kb plasmid fragment, and specific point mutations occurred concurrently in CRKP-S2, distinguishing the individual contribution of each genetic event remains a challenge. Consequently, the links identified between these acquired elements and enhanced antioxidant capacity or macrophage survival are correlative rather than causal. Although in silico predictions suggest the RamA HTH domain is functionally intact and GO enrichment analysis associates this island with transmembrane transport and transaminase activity, definitive causal validation requires systematic wet-lab experimentation, such as gene knockouts, complementation assays, and transcriptomic profiling, in future studies.

In summary, our findings are consistent with the hypothesis that the second isolated strain, CRKP-S2, established host colonization, possibly aided by the additional ~72-kb and ~19-kb genomic fragments and specific mutations. After colonization, adaptive genetic mutations may have generated multiple subclones from CRKP-S2, which may have been associated with the recurrent infections. However, these observations are correlative, and the possibility of mixed colonization by co-circulating subclones cannot be excluded. These observations nevertheless suggest that enhanced surveillance of hospitalized patients could be valuable for early intervention to reduce the potential risk of CRKP infection, though functional validation and larger-scale studies are needed to test these hypotheses.
