Vitamin D, premature mortality, and life expectancy among US adults
Authors: Qiong Wang, Che Li, Ning Ma, Fangjieyi Zheng, Bochun Wang, Qiushi Lin, Xiaoqun Dong, Wenquan Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVitamin D deficiency is prevailing worldwide and linked to multiple diseases; however, its association with premature mortality remains unknown in the US.MethodsTo investigate the association between serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D or 25(OH)D concentrations and premature mortality and life expectancy in a nationally representative cohort of US adults. We analyzed data of adults aged ≥ 18 years included in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey from the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination (NHANES III) (1988 − 1994) and NAHANES (2001 − 2018) with linkage to the National Death Index through December 31, 2019 were investigated. Data were analyzed between July and November in 2024. Serum 25(OH)D was computed as the sum of 25(OH)D3 and 25(OH)D2 concentrations measured by standardized liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry.ResultsTotally 62,704 adults (weighted mean [SE] age: 46.0 [0.19] years; 51.2% women; 26,971 non-Hispanic White adults, 14,228 non-Hispanic Black adults, and 12,436 Mexican American adults) were followed up over a median (IQR) of 12.2 (12.0 to 12.5) years, with 6,323 documented premature deaths. Compared to adults with sufficient 25(OH)D (≥ 75.0 nmol/L), multi-adjusted hazard ratio (HR) was 1.18 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.07 to 1.30), 1.60 (1.42 to 1.79), and 2.29 (1.92 to 2.72) in those with insufficient (50.0 − 74.9 nmol/L), moderately deficient (25.0 − 49.9 nmol/L), and severely deficient 25(OH)D (< 25.0 nmol/L), respectively (Ptrend < 0.001). Corresponding life expectancy at age 50 in each group was 31.3 (31.0 to 31.6), 30.6 (30.3 to 30.9), 29.2 (28.9 to 29.4), and 26.9 (26.7 to 27.2) years, respectively. Equivalently, adults in insufficiency, moderate deficiency, and severe deficiency groups lived on average 0.7 (95% CI: 0.1 to 1.3), 2.1 (1.6 to 2.4), and 4.4 (3.8 to 4.9) fewer years, respectively, at age 50 compared to those in sufficiency group. The association was stronger in women than in man (HRs comparing severely deficient to sufficient 25(OH)D concentrations, 2.67 [2.14 to 3.34] in women and 1.92 [1.42 to 2.58] in men), and in White adults than in Black adults (2.64 [1.95 to 3.56] in White adults and 1.55 [1.20 to 2.01] in Black adults) or in Mexican American adults (2.30 [1.53 to 3.46]) (all Pinteraction < 0.001).ConclusionsIn this national cohort, our findings indicated that compared to sufficiency, deficiency, even moderate, in serum 25(OH)D concentrations could correlate with a significantly increased risk of premature mortality and shortened life expectancy, especially among women and White individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01785-z.

Introduction

Vitamin D is vital for maintaining health and prolonging lifespan. Its deficiency is highly prevalent and is established as a key driver of many chronic diseases and even premature mortality [1–3]. Currently, vitamin D deficiency affects over 1 billion people worldwide and up to 50% of older individuals [4]. An analysis of 307,601 UK Biobank participants of White European ancestry revealed that the association between genetically predicted 25-hydroxyvitamin D or 25(OH)D and all-cause mortality was L-shaped. Specifically, genetic analysis indicated that all-cause mortality was estimated to be 25% higher in participants with a measured 25(OH)D concentration of 25 nmol/L compared with those with 50 nmol/L [5]. Additionally, it was proposed that per 20 nmol/L reduction in plasma 25(OH)D was causally associated with a significant increase in mortality risk: 21% for all-cause mortality, 13% for cardiovascular mortality, and 10% for cancer mortality, respectively [6]. Hence, to maintain vitamin D within a sufficient range could be more beneficial for primary prevention of chronic diseases and mortality, as well as longevity.

However, the association between vitamin D and mortality remains a subject of intense debate. Prior evidence-based studies examining vitamin D supplementation and all-cause mortality demonstrated that two meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials yielded separate effect size estimates of 0.97 and 0.98, respectively, with no detectable statistical significance [7, 8]. By contrast, in a systematic review and meta-analysis of 50 trials including 74,655 participants, vitamin D supplementation significantly reduced cancer mortality by 15%, yet its association with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality was nonsignificant [9]. As reported by a long-term follow-up study, patients with post-treatment 25(OH)D concentrations at or above 30 ng/mL had a lower incidence of myocardial infarction and a lower risk of all-cause mortality [10]. The explanations for discrepant results are multifaceted, possibly due to heterogeneity from sex and race/ethnicity, adopting risk as a relative metric to predict mortality, and lack of sufficient period of follow-up. Thus far, no study has quantified potential linkage of vitamin D to life expectancy, an absolute quantitative metric for population health. To fill in gaps in knowledge, we aimed to investigate how serum 25(OH)D concentrations could correlate with premature mortality and life expectancy in a nationally representative cohort of US adults.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was longitudinal in design using data from the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination (NHANES III) (1988 − 1994) and NAHANES (1999 − 2018) and Linked Mortality File (LMF) including death records in the National Death Index (NDI) dataset until December 31, 2019 [11]. The NHANES was implemented by the National Center for Health Statistics to monitor healthy and nutritional status of civilian, non-institutionalized US population using a multistage probability sampling strategy on a series of cross-sectional surveys. It was conducted periodically before 1994 (NHANES III) and continuously in 2-year cycles from 1999 onwards as previously described [12]. The NHANES collected data from household interviews and visits conducted at mobile test centers, including information on demographics, physical examinations, and laboratory results. The institutional review board of the National Center for Health Statistics, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention had approved the implementation of NHANES, and all respondents provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Because serum 25(OH)D concentrations were not measured in the 1999 − 2000 cycle, only data from NHANES III and nine cycles of NHANES (2001 − 2018) were analyzed in our study. A total of 62,704 adults aged 18 years or more with information on 25(OH)D concentrations were eligible for inclusion. Serum 25(OH)D measurement

In the NHANES III and NHANES, serum concentrations of 25(OH)D2 and 25(OH)D3 were measured by DiaSorin RIA kit (Stillwater MN) during 2001–2006 and by standardized liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) from 2007. Concentrations of 25 (OH)D2 and 25(OH)D3 were converted by regression method [13] to equivalent ones using the LC–MS/MS, as recommended by the National Center for Health Statistics. In this study, 25(OH)D was computed as the sum of 25(OH)D3 and 25(OH)D2. According to the Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guidelines, status of 25(OH)D was divided into four categories: severe deficiency (< 25.0 nmol/L), moderate deficiency (25.0 − 49.9 nmol/L), insufficiency (50.0 − 74.9 nmol/L), and sufficiency (≥ 75.0 nmol/L) [14].

Assessment of mortality

Primary outcomes were premature mortality (death at the age of ≤ 80 years, as the approximate life expectancy of US adults in 2022 [15]) and life expectancy. Secondary outcomes included CVD mortality, cancer mortality, and non-CVD/non-cancer mortality. Survival time (in months) was defined as the interval from the date of participation to the date of death or the date of last follow-up on December 31, 2019, whichever occurred first. The specific causes of mortality were recorded according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th version (ICD-10), including CVD (I00 − I99) and cancer (C00 − C97).

Assessment of covariates

In this study, a wide panel of covariates were selected [16, 17], including season (November 1 through April 30 and May 1 through October 31) when blood was drawn, age, sex, race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, or other), marital status (married, living with a partner, or not married nor living with a partner), education level (less than high school, high school or equivalent, or college or above), family income level (family income-to-poverty ratio calculated by dividing family income by a family size-specific poverty threshold and categorized into 0 − 1.0, 1.0 − 3.0, or > 3.0), leisure-time moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (no or yes), healthy eating index (HEI), smoking status (no or yes), and chronic diseases (diabetes, hypertension, and CVD collected from household interviews via standardized questionnaires). Body weight, height and drinking status (no or yes) were collected in physical examinations at mobile examination centers with standardized techniques and equipment. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m)2, and categorized as < 25.0, 25.0–29.9, or ≥ 30.0 kg/m2. Diabetes was defined as a self-reported history of diabetes or a fasting plasma glucose level of 126 mg/dL or greater or a hemoglobin A1c level of 6.5% or greater or taking antidiabetic medications. Hypertension was defined as a self-reported history of hypertension or a systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher or a diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or higher or taking antihypertensive medications. CVD was defined as a self-reported history of coronary heart disease, heart attack, or stroke.

Statistical analysis

Sampling weights, primary sampling units, and strata were applied to infer nationally representative estimates. The relation of serum 25(OH)D categories with premature mortality and cause-specific mortality was investigated by selecting appropriate models. The proportional hazards assumption of Cox model was examined by Schoenfeld test and Kaplan–Meier curve, and the assumption of Weibull model was examined by checking the linearity between ln(-ln[S(t)]) and ln(t) [S(t) denotes survival function; t denotes follow-up time; ln denotes logarithmic function)]. Next, using follow-up intervals as the time metric and under the proper model, effect-size estimates expressed as hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were derived by adjusting covariates in a graded increasing manner. In detail, covariates under adjustment included season in model 1; additional age, sex, race and ethnicity, marital status, education level, and family income-poverty ratio in model 2; additional smoking status, drinking status, leisure-time moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, HEI, and BMI in model 3. Missing records in categorical covariates were coded as an additional category. Percentages of missing values are in the eTable 1. The continuous scale of serum 25(OH)D was used to test the linear tendency in association with premature mortality. The dose–response effect on premature mortality was explored using restricted cubic spline curve after adjusting for all covariates. Subgroup analyses by sex and race and ethnicity were performed for linkage of serum 25(OH)D categories to premature mortality. The product term of serum 25(OH)D categories with sex or race and ethnicity was used to gauge possible interaction.

To make reliable estimates, sensitivity analyses were conducted after adjusting for season when blood was drawn (data only available in the NHANES III 1988–1994, n = 16,294); adjusting for dietary supplement use (data only available in the NHANES 2007–2018, n = 32,074) and serum calcium and magnesium (in tertiles); after filling in missing values of covariates with multiple imputation method; after excluding mortality cases that occurred within the first three years of follow-up; and after additionally adjusting for NHANES cycles to control the potential impact of shifting economic conditions.

Life tables were constructed starting at age 40 and ending at age 100 based on the following variables: (1) age-specific multi-adjusted HRs (in 20-year’s age intervals) of premature mortality associated with different serum 25(OH)D categories (with “sufficient 25(OH)D” as the reference) overall and by sex or race and ethnicity derived from the NHANES; (2) age-specific population mortality rates overall and by sex or race and ethnicity derived from the National Vital Statistics System of National Center for Health Statistics (available at the website https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/nvsr.htm, in 2019, from 40 to 100 years); (3) age-specific weighted population prevalence of different serum 25(OH)D categories overall and by sex or race and ethnicity derived from the NHANES. Because NHANES respondents aged ≥ 80 years at the time of examination are top-coded at 80, mortality rates over 80 years old were not available and thus estimated using the Poisson regression model. This model included both linear and quadratic terms for the midpoints of single-year age groups minus 39.5 years, as illustrated in eFigure 2. The 95% CIs of life expectancy estimates were derived as described by the South East Public Health Observatory (SEPHO) [18]. The Arriaga’s decomposition method [19, 20] was employed to assess mortality inequalities in premature mortality (deaths from CVD, cancer, or other causes) between respondents with severely deficient 25(OH)D and sufficient 25(OH)D.

All statistical analyses were conducted using the STATA software (version 14.0) and R programming environment (version 4.3.3). All statistical tests were 2-sided; probability < 5% was indicative of statistical significance. Data were analyzed between July 1 and November 30 in 2024.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study involved 62,704 adults (weighted mean [SE] age: 46.0 [0.19] years; 51.2% women) from the NHANES III and NHANES 2001 − 2018. There were 26,971 (70.0%) non-Hispanic White adults, 14,228 (14.2%) non-Hispanic Black adults, and 12,436 (12.4%) Mexican American adults. Characteristics of this cohort at the time of enrollment were presented in Table 1. Percentage of adults with sufficient, insufficient, moderately deficient, and severely deficient serum 25(OH)D was 34.5%, 39.3%, 23.6%, and 2.6%, respectively. Adults with lower concentrations of 25(OH)D tended to be younger, be more likely non-Hispanic Black and Mexican Americans, physically inactive, and obese, and have lower levels of education and family income, whereas have higher prevalence of self-reported hypertension, diabetes, and CVD. There was a dose–response linear relation between serum 25(OH)D concentrations and premature mortality in all US adults (eFigure 5). By sex, premature mortality risk was more evident in women relative to men across serum 25(OH)D categories (Table 2). Specifically, women with insufficient, moderately deficient, and severely deficient concentrations of serum 25(OH)D were 1.32 (95% CI: 1.13 to 1.54), 1.77 (1.51 to 2.08), and 2.67 (2.14 to 3.34), respectively, more likely of dying prematurely than those with sufficient ones in fully-adjusted models. For men, significance was observed in moderate deficiency (1.47, 1.26 to 1.71) and severe deficiency (1.92, 1.42 to 2.58) groups. There was significant interaction between sex and serum 25(OH)D categories in association with premature mortality (P < 0.001).

By race/ethnicity, premature mortality risk was increased significantly by 18% (fully-adjusted HR, 95% CI: 1.18, 1.06 to 1.32), 68% (1.68, 1.47 to 1.90), and 164% (2.64, 1.95 to 3.56) for non-Hispanic White adults in insufficiency, moderate deficiency, and severe deficiency groups relative to sufficiency group (Table 2). For adults of non-Hispanic Black and Mexican American origins, significance was noted for moderately and severely deficient groups, with fully-adjusted HRs of 1.55 (95% CI: 1.20 to 2.01) and 2.30 (1.53 to 3.46), respectively. Significant interaction was observed between White individuals with Black or Mexican American individuals and premature mortality across serum 25(OH)D categories (P < 0.001).

Reliability was tested in sensitivity analyses. No material changes in fully-adjusted HRs for premature mortality after filling in missing values using multiple imputation method (eTable 3), after additionally adjusting for survey cycles (eTable 4), after separate analyses in NHANES III and NHANES 2007 − 2018 (eTable 5 and eTable 6), and after excluding those participants who died within the first three years of follow-up (eTable 7) or cause-specific mortality by serum 25(OH)D categories (eTable 8).

Serum 25(OH)D and life expectancy

As shown in Fig. 1, average life expectancy at age 50 was estimated at 31.3 (95% CI: 31.0 to 31.6), 30.6 (95% CI: 30.3 to 30.9), 29.2 (95% CI: 28.9 to 29.4), and 26.9 (95% CI: 26.7 to 27.2) in US adults with sufficient, insufficient, moderately deficient, and severely deficient concentrations of serum 25(OH)D, respectively. Equivalently, adults in insufficiency, moderate deficiency, and severe deficiency groups lived on average 0.7 (95% CI: 0.1 to 1.3), 2.1 (1.6 to 2.4), and 4.4 (3.8 to 4.9) fewer years at age 50, respectively, compared to sufficiency group. Cause-specific decomposition analyses of life expectancy differences between severe deficiency and sufficiency groups indicated that 20.4% of estimated life expectancy lost at age 50 was attributed to CVD, 31.3% to cancer, and 48.3% to other causes.

By sex, as shown in Fig. 2, estimated life expectancy at age 50 was shortened by 4.4 (95% CI: 3.8 to 4.9), 2.2 (1.6 to 2.7), and 0.8 (0.2 to 1.4) years, respectively, in women with severely deficient, moderately deficient, and insufficient serum 25(OH)D relative to sufficient group. For men, the corresponding reduction was 3.8 (3.3 to 4.3), 2.0 (1.6 to 2.5), and 0.6 (0.1 to 1.1) years, respectively. Compared severe deficiency to sufficiency group, the percentage of premature mortality at age 50 because of CVD, cancer, and other causes was 19.1%, 38.1%, and 42.8% in women, whereas 19.9%, 24.3%, and 55.8% in men, respectively. Discussion

In this nationally representative cohort of 62,704 US adults, (even moderate) deficiency, compared to sufficiency, in serum 25(OH)D was associated with a significantly increased risk of premature mortality, especially among women and White individuals. We estimated an average of 4.4 years of life expectancy lost at age 50 among adults with severely deficient (compared to sufficient) serum 25(OH)D. This loss was up to 4.4 years in women and 5.2 years in White individuals. To our knowledge, this is to date the first study on vitamin D linked to life expectancy.

The association of low vitamin D with chronic diseases as well as all or specific causes of mortality has been widely examined, however, no definitive consensus is reached. Consistent with our findings, analysis of 411,436 UK Biobank participants indicated vitamin D deficiency significantly associated with 24 − 42% increased mortality risk of stomach, colorectal, lung, and prostate cancer. In addition, vitamin D insufficiency was closely related to colorectal and lung cancer mortality [21]. In another study on 307,601 participants of White European ancestry from UK Biobank, genetically predicted 25-(OH)D was associated with significant all-cause mortality risk in an L-shaped fashion, with the risk decreasing steeply with increasing concentrations of vitamin D up to 50 nmol/L.5Among 4,651 participants with fatty liver disease from NHANES III, there was a nonlinear inverse association between serum 25(OH)D status and all-cause mortality [22]. Importantly, data from the Third National Health and Nutrition Survey (NHANES III) reported a significant increase in mortality at 25(OH)D concentrations below 16 ng/mL [23]. By contrast, in a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, vitamin D supplementation alone was not associated with all-cause mortality in adults compared with placebo or no treatment. Vitamin D supplementation reduced the risk of cancer death by 15% [9]. Most previous studies on vitamin D and chronic disease or mortality outcomes were mainly based on specific cohort populations and vitamin D classifications, so extrapolation of those results to the more general populations deserves further investigation. Moreover, nearly all studies quantified the association using professional relative estimates, which were not intuitive. Extending previous studies, we have provided the first-ever evidence on potential association between vitamin D status and life expectancy, an absolute metric that can better assess the general health of a population.

It is worth noting that life expectancy differed widely by race and ethnicity in this study. Specifically, for severe deficiency vis-à-vissufficiency in serum 25(OH)D, life expectancy at age 50 was estimated to be shortened by 5.2 years in White adults, which was about 1.6 times higher than that in Black adults (3.2 years) origins. Notably, prevalence of vitamin D moderate or severe deficiency was remarkably more common in Black adults (64.5%) and Mexican Americans (42.3%) than in White adults (16.8%), as supported by previous studies [24]. Besides the overall mortality rate by age, life expectancy depends on the prevalence of exposure (vitamin D categories) and the specific HRs for mortality by exposure. The multivariable-adjusted hazard of premature mortality was significantly lower in Black adults than in White adults (1.55 versus 2.64), in line with the previous studies. Black adults who exposed lower concentrations of 25(OH)D had a lower risk of negative health outcomes than White adults [25, 26]. Above lines of evidence collectively suggest that vitamin D deficiency is particularly harmful for White individuals, highlighting necessarily continued and enhanced monitoring of serum vitamin D status in the US. The molecular mechanisms driving these race- and ethnicity-specific disparities remain incompletely understood, but they may be attributed to differences in genotypes or vitamin D-binding protein concentrations between White and Black populations [17]. Specifically, Black individuals generally have lower concentrations of vitamin D-binding proteins than White individuals, and these proteins may protect against the adverse effects of vitamin D deficiency [17]. Beyond biological factors, major unfavorable social determinants of health, including low income, unemployment, less or no access to healthcare, no health insurance, are more prevalent among Black adults than White adults. These factors are strongly linked to premature death and fully account for the disparities in premature death between the two groups [27]. Additionally, Black individuals may exhibit an adaptive response to vitamin D deficiency, which could further contribute to observed differences [28, 29]. Finally, future studies should aim to validate findings on global mortality disparities and identify key determinants to inform targeted interventions.

Moreover, serum vitamin D concentrations correlate with premature mortality and life expectancy more evident in women than in men. This finding was supported by previous studies on higher prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in women [30–32], which could be interpreted by the different subcutaneous fat between sex. Vitamin D is fat-soluble mainly stored in subcutaneous adipose tissue [33]. Considering the fact that women generally have more subcutaneous fat than men [34], bioavailability of circulating vitamin D for metabolism is relatively lower in women than in men with the same height and weight [35, 36]. Additionally, significant interaction between sex hormones and vitamin D might be interpretation [37]. Elucidating sex-specific heterogeneity in life expectancy is beyond the scope of this study but certainly requires further investigation.

The present findings may have a great public health implication. The significant impact of serum vitamin D on life expectancy and heterogeneity by sex or race and ethnicity observed in this study would provide scientific clue for formulating strategies and policies in fighting against health inequalities and lengthening average lifespans.

Limitations

Several limitations in this study should be acknowledged. First, due to the lack of repeated 25(OH)D measurements, we were unable to analyze the relationship between dynamic changes in 25(OH)D concentrations over time and mortality. However, existing evidence indicates that a single measurement can serve as an adequate indicator of an individual’s longer-term vitamin D status [38]. Second, serum 25(OH)D concentrations were measured using different methods across NHANES cycles, which could yield variability despite standardization was adopted. Third, vitamin D production varies with geographic latitudes because of different duration and strength of ultraviolet rays from sunlight. Data on region-specific vitamin D and sun exposure were not available in the NHNAES, which might bias the association between vitamin D and premature mortality. Fourth, according to NHANES sampling protocols, chemicals with seasonal variability—measured in urine or blood samples collected under NHANES—may be prone to both temporal and spatial biases [39]. These biases, in turn, could lead to inaccurate interpretations of population exposure levels and health data. Fifth, this study is observational by nature. Although we controlled for a wide panel of confounding factors, potential unmeasured or residual confounding may still remain. Sixth, the analyses in this study are based on long follow-up periods, yet our results may be underestimated to some extent. Seventh, life expectancy varies substantially by sex and race/ethnicity, and so the uniform use of 80 years as the cutoff age across all subgroups could introduce bias. Last but not the least, only US adults were analyzed; it was unclear whether our findings could be extrapolated to other national populations.

Conclusions

In this national cohort study, our results indicated that deficiency, even moderate, vis-à-vis sufficiency, in serum 25(OH)D was related to significantly increased risk of premature mortality and shortened life expectancy, especially among women and White individuals. This finding underscores the importance of monitoring vitamin D in circulation for primary prevention of chronic diseases and premature mortality in the US. This is a call for action that renewed focus on vitamin D and life expectancy is warranted.
