Geometrically robust least squares through manifold optimization
Jeremy Coulson, Alberto Padoan, Cyrus Mostajeran

Abstract
This paper presents a methodology for solving a geometrically robust least squares problem, which arises in various applications where the model is subject to geometric constraints. The problem is formulated as a minimax optimization problem on a product manifold, where one variable is constrained to a ball describing uncertainty. To handle the constraint, an exact penalty method is applied. A first-order gradient descent ascent algorithm is proposed to solve the problem, and its convergence properties are illustrated by an example. The proposed method offers a robust approach to solving a wide range of problems arising in signal processing and data-driven control.



keywords

least squares, optimization, differential geometry

Introduction

The
least squares problem
is a cornerstone of science and engineering, classically defined by the optimization problem

\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}{\textup{min}}\;\|Ax-b\|_{2}^{2},

(1)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is an unknown coefficient vector, while
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are given data which usually represent a
linear model
(often termed the
regressor matrix
or the
design matrix
) and a vector of observations, respectively.
The historical origins of the least squares problem can be traced back to its early applications in celestial mechanics
(
Gauss 1809
;
Gauss 1857
)
and it has since become indispensable across various scientific and engineering fields. In control theory, least squares problems are ubiquitous and play a critical role in numerous applications, including identification, filtering, and optimal control, among others.

The least squares problem (
1
) is an essential tool to approximate solutions of the linear system
{Ax=b}
. By minimizing the residual
\Delta b=Ax-b
, it determines a solution that closely matches the observations in the Euclidean
2
-norm.
The
total least squares problem
discussed in
(
Golub and Van Loan 1980
)
extends this paradigm
to address the presence of
perturbations
(errors or uncertainties) in both the dependent and independent variables. This extension involves minimizing residuals
(\Delta A,\Delta b)
in the observations
b
and in the matrix
A
, thereby refining the accuracy of the model. Given that accuracy is the primary aim of both least squares and total least squares, it is not surprising that both solutions may exhibit sensitive behavior to perturbations in the data matrices
(A,b)

(
Golub and Van Loan 2013
)
.

Sensitivity issues and robustness to perturbations in the data
(A,b)
can be effectively mitigated in different ways.
One strategy is to consider the
robust
least squares problem

\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}{\textup{min}}\;\underset{A\in\mathbb{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{F}}_{\rho}(\hat{A})}{\textup{max}}\;\|Ax-b\|_{2}^{2},

(2)

where

\mathbb{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{F}}_{\rho}(\hat{A})=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\,|\,\|A-\hat{A}\|_{\text{F}}\leq\rho\}

is the ball centered at
\hat{A}
with radius
\rho
defined by the Frobenius norm.
This approach is intimately connected to (Tikhonov)
regularization

(
Golub and Van Loan 2013
)
.

The perturbation model defined implicitly by (
2
) allows the perturbed data matrix
A
to vary in a neighbourhood of the matrix
\hat{A}
. The parameter
\rho
controls the size of the neighbourhood and can be interpreted as a penalty that promotes solutions whose norms are small, thereby trading accuracy for robustness at the expense of introducing bias.
Consequently, the formulation (
2
) serves a double purpose: it enhances the robustness of the original problem and it incorporates prior knowledge into the model.

Building on these ideas, alternative perturbation models have been explored, yielding different robust versions of the least squares problem. For example,
(
El Ghaoui and Lebret 1997
)
considered the robust least squares problem

\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}{\textup{min}}\;\underset{[\,A\ b\,]\in\mathbb{B}^{\scriptscriptstyle\text{F}}_{\rho}([\,\hat{A}\ \hat{b}\,])}{\textup{max}}\;\|Ax-b\|_{2}^{2}.

(3)

This approach is motivated by scenarios where the exact data
(A,b)
are unknown, but belong to a family of matrices
(\hat{A}+\Delta A,\hat{b}+\Delta b)
and the residual
[\,\Delta A\ \Delta b\,]
lies in a norm-bounded matrix ball. The paper also considers a structured variant of (
3
) by considering matrix box constraints of given radii
(
El Ghaoui and Lebret 1997
)
.

In this extended abstract, we introduce a robust least squares problem that accounts for the geometric nature of perturbations found in diverse instances of the problem. Specifically, we consider the optimization problem

\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\textup{min}}\;\underset{S\in\mathbb{B}^{d}_{\rho}(\hat{S})}{\textup{max}}\;\|P_{S}x-b\|_{2}^{2},

(4)

where
P_{S}
is the orthogonal projection matrix onto the
k
-dimensional subspace
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{d}(\hat{S})
is the ball centered at
\hat{S}
with radius
\rho
defined by the metric
d
on the
Grassmannian

\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)
, i.e., the set of all
k
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
endowed with the structure of a smooth Riemannian manifold. This approach is motivated by a diverse range of applications where the linear model
A
is a matrix representation of a subspace subject to bounded perturbations (due to uncertainty or approximations errors), quantified naturally in terms of distances between subspaces. In what follows, we present some examples to motivate (
4
).

Subspace tracking:
In signal processing, communications, and computer vision, the problem of tracking a time-varying
k
-dimensional subspace
S_{t}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is common and arises in various contexts
(
Delmas 2010
)
. Subspace tracking algorithms aim to provide an estimate
\hat{S}_{t}
of the true subspace from a sequence of possibly noisy samples in
S_{t}
. The projection matrix
P_{\hat{S}_{t}}
onto the subspace estimate
\hat{S}_{t}
is then used as a linear model, e.g., for foreground-background separation in noisy free-motion camera videos
(
He et al. 2012
)
or for Direction of Arrival (DOA) tracking in blind channel estimation and equalization
(
Delmas 2010
)
. Subspace tracking algorithms often guarantee local
(
Balzano et al. 2010
)
or global
(
Zhang and Balzano 2016
)
convergence with respect to a given metric on the Grassmannian (e.g., the chordal metric in
(
Zhang and Balzano 2016
)
).
Consequently, quantifying the distance between the estimate and the true subspace after a finite number of iterations of a subspace tracking algorithm is naturally expressed by a metric ball. This, in turn, leads to the robust least squares problem (
4
) when using the projection matrix
P_{\hat{S}_{t}}
as a linear model, which needs to be robustified up to the maximum distance within the metric ball.

Data-driven control:

The availability of large datasets coupled with unprecedented storage and computational power has recently reignited interest in direct data-driven control methods, which aim to infer optimal decisions directly from measured data (bypassing system identification). At the heart of this emerging trend lies the behavioral approach to system theory
(
Willems 2007
)
and a seminal result by Willems and his collaborators
(
Willems et al. 2005
)
, commonly referred to as the
fundamental lemma
. The lemma establishes that finite-horizon behaviors of Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) systems can be represented as images of raw data matrices. As a result, various data-driven modeling, estimation, filtering, and control problems can be formulated as weighted or constrained least squares problems
(
Markovsky and Dörfler 2021
)
. Moreover, being finite-dimensional subspaces, finite-horizon LTI behaviors can be identified with points on the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)
and uncertainty can be naturally quantified using Grassmannian (subspace) metrics. This approach has demonstrated its effectiveness in data-driven mode recognition and control applications and shows promise to open new avenues in adaptive control
(
Padoan et al. 2022
)
.

The key contribution of this paper is the formulation of a methodology for solving the geometrically robust least squares problem (
4
), grounded in the framework of optimization on manifolds
(
Absil et al. 2008
;
Boumal 2023
)
and enriched by recent progress in minimax optimization techniques
(
Han et al. 2023
)
.
Due to the presence of a (ball) constraint in the second variable, problem (
4
) cannot be solved directly using the framework presented in
(
Han et al. 2023
)
. We resolve this issue using an exact penalty method. In particular, we augment the original cost function with a penalty term to enforce the ball constraint. We then apply a smoothing technique
(
Liu and Boumal 2020
)
to transform the resulting non-smooth penalized cost function into a smooth one. Finally, we introduce a first-order gradient descent ascent algorithm, motivated and inspired by the TSRGDA algorithm detailed in
(
Han et al. 2023
)
. The convergence properties of the algorithm are demonstrated by means of an illustrative example.

2
Geometrically robust least squares

We adopt optimization on manifolds as our framework
(
Absil et al. 2008
;
Boumal 2023
)
, given that one optimization variable of the geometrically robust least squares problem (
4
) naturally lies in a nonlinear space. Our goal is to turn (
4
) into a minimax optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y),

(5)

where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are subsets of smooth finite-dimensional Riemannian manifolds
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, respectively.

Problem (
4
) fits into this minimax framework with the cost function given by

f(x,y)=\|P_{y}x-b\|_{2}^{2}.

(6)

The manifold
\mathcal{M}
is the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
endowed with the Riemannian metric defined by the standard inner product.
The manifold
\mathcal{N}
is the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)
endowed with the Riemannian metric
defined in
(
Boumal 2023
, Section 9.16)
.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{B}_{\rho}^{d}(\hat{y})
is the ball centered at
\hat{y}
with radius
\rho
defined by the metric
d
(not necessarily the Riemannian distance).

Since the cost function (
6
) is smooth and convex in
x
, but non-concave in
y
, we assume that the cost function
f:\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}\to\mathbb{R}
in (
5
) is smooth, but in general non-convex and non-concave (in both Euclidean and geodesic senses). As a result, the existence of saddle points is not guaranteed
(
Jin et al. 2020
)
. Consequently, we adopt the notion of solution defined by a local minimax point from
(
Han et al. 2023
)
, where
d_{\mathcal{M}}
denotes the Riemannian distance in
\mathcal{M}
(i.e., the length of shortest path connecting
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
).

Definition 2.1

(Local minimax point)

A point
(x_{*},y_{*})
is called a
local minimax point
of
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
if there exists a constant
\delta_{0}>0
and a function
h
satisfying
\lim_{\delta\to 0}h(\delta)=0
for any
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0}]
and any
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
satisfying
d_{\mathcal{M}}(x,x_{*})\leq\delta
and
d_{\mathcal{N}}(y,y_{*})\leq\delta
, we have
f(x_{*},y)\leq f(x_{*},y_{*})\leq\textup{max}_{y^{\prime}:d_{\mathcal{N}}(y^{\prime},y_{*})\leq h(\delta)}f(x,y^{\prime})
.

A necessary condition for
(x_{*},y_{*})
to be a local minimax point is that the Riemannian (partial) gradients denoted by
\textup{grad}_{x}f(x_{*},y_{*})
and
\textup{grad}_{y}f(x_{*},y_{*})
are zero
(
Han et al. 2023
, Proposition 3)
.
Sufficient conditions in terms of the Hessian can be found in
(
Han et al. 2023
)
.

Existing algorithms for solving unconstrained minimax optimization problems on manifolds
(
Han et al. 2023
)
do not explicitly accommodate (metric ball) constraints. To overcome this limitation, we use an exact penalty method
(
Liu and Boumal 2020
)
. Introducing a penalty weight
{\lambda\geq 0}
, we relax the original problem and consider the unconstrained minimax optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{N}}f(x,y)+\lambda\min\{0,\rho^{2}-d(y,\hat{y})^{2}\}.

(7)

Given that this lifting results in a nonsmooth objective, we employ a smoothing technique inspired by
(
Liu and Boumal 2020
)
to obtain a smooth relaxation of the minimax optimization problem (
7
), obtaining

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\max_{y\in\mathcal{N}}f(x,y)-\lambda u\log\left(1+\textup{exp}\left(\tfrac{d(y,\hat{y})^{2}-\rho^{2}}{u}\right)\right)

(8)

where
{u>0}
serves as the smoothing parameter.
For the specific case of the geometrically robust least squares problem (
4
), these transformations yield

\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\textup{min}}\;\underset{y\in\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)}{\textup{max}}\;\|P_{y}x-b\|_{2}^{2}-\lambda u\log\left(1+\textup{exp}\left(\tfrac{d(y,\hat{y})^{2}-\rho^{2}}{u}\right)\right).

(9)

The minimax optimization problem can then be solved using any first- or second-order algorithm presented in
(
Han et al. 2023
)
. For simplicity, we opt for the
timescale-separated Riemannian gradient descent ascent
(TSRGDA) algorithm, which is based on the update rule

(x_{k+1},y_{k+1})=\textup{Exp}_{(x_{k},y_{k})}\begin{pmatrix}-\eta_{x}\textup{grad}_{x}f(x_{k},y_{k})\\
\eta_{y}\textup{grad}_{y}f(x_{k},y_{k})\end{pmatrix},

(10)

where
\textup{Exp}_{z}
denotes the exponential map of the product manifold
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}
, and
\eta_{x},\eta_{y}>0
are step sizes.

In our numerical implementations, each point
y\in\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)
is identified with an equivalence class represented by the image of an orthonormal matrix
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
, treated as an element of the
Stiefel manifold

\textup{St}(k,n):=\{Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\,|\,Y^{\top}Y=I\}.

The Stiefel manifold is viewed as a Riemannian submanifold of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
, endowed with Riemannian metric inherited by the standard Euclidean inner product

\langle Z_{1},\,Z_{2}\rangle=\operatorname{trace}(Z_{1}^{\top}Z_{2}).

This approach is standard and the interested reader is referred to
(
Absil et al. 2008
)
for further detail.

The metric
d
used to describe the ball constraint in (
9
) is chosen as the
chordal distance
between subspaces

d_{\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)}^{\kappa}(y_{1},y_{2})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\sin\theta_{i})^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
\theta_{i}
denotes the
i
-th principal angle between the subspaces
y_{1}\in\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)
and
y_{2}\in\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)
(see
(
Golub and Van Loan 2013
)
for definition of principal angles). Given
Y_{1},Y_{2}\in\operatorname{St}(k,n)
such that
y_{1}=\operatorname{im}(Y_{1})
and
y_{2}=\operatorname{im}(Y_{2})
, the chordal distance can be computed as

d_{\operatorname{Gr}(k,n)}^{\kappa}(y_{1},y_{2})=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\lVert Y_{1}Y_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}-Y_{2}Y_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rVert_{F}.

(11)

This distance does not correspond to the standard Riemannian distance, but it satisfies the four metric requirements while being more numerically stable than the geodesic distance. Thus, it is preferred over the Riemannian distance here (and in most applications).

4
Conclusion

We have introduced a robust least squares problem tailored to accommodate geometric perturbations commonly encountered in various scenarios. Leveraging the framework of optimization on manifolds and recent advances in minimax optimization, we have addressed the problem through an exact penalty method and a smoothing technique. We proposed a first-order gradient descent ascent algorithm to solve the problem, illustrating its convergence properties through a numerical example. Our methodology not only provides a new robust least squares formulation but also expands its applicability to a diverse range of problems where the geometric nature of the linear model is essential.
