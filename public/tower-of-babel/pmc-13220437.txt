Combining ultrastructure expansion microscopy with immunofluorescence and Oligopaint DNA FISH
Authors: Lorielle M Raab, Clio B Hockens, Ling Sze Lee, Rose K Runyan, Emma E Burns, Nasser M Rusan, Leah F Rosin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExpansion microscopy (ExM) enlarges biological samples by embedding them in a swellable hydrogel, enabling nanoscale imaging of subcellular structures with standard light microscopes. This offers an accessible alternative to super-resolution methods. ExM has yet to be combined with the Oligopaint DNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) technology. We present an optimized ExM workflow for simultaneous Oligopaint DNA FISH and immunofluorescence (IF) in intact Drosophila ovaries. The protocol incorporates nucleic-acid anchoring, reliable protein retention, and digestion conditions that preserve chromatin while maintaining probe accessibility. Our approach achieves ~ 5 × expansion and strong signal retention, enabling high-resolution studies of nuclear organization.Keywords: Expansion microscopy, ExM, Synaptonemal complex, Meiosis, Oligopaints

Introduction

Expansion microscopy (ExM) is a technique which magnifies biological specimens by embedding them in a hydrogel matrix that swells isotropically to enable nanoscale visualization of sub-cellular structures in intact cells and tissues. Since its introduction in 2015 (Chen et al. 2015), ExM has rapidly transformed numerous areas of biological study, from cell biology to neurobiology. Despite its growing success, ExM applications in the context of chromosome biology, particularly in visualizing specific DNA sequences or DNA features, remain extremely limited. Chromatin is dense, fragile, and chemically heterogeneous, presenting unique challenges for expansion. Preserving chromatin architecture while retaining epitopes for immunolabeling and accessibility for nucleic-acid probes requires careful optimization of anchoring chemistries, digestion conditions, and labeling strategies. Moreover, whole tissues serve as powerful models for studying chromosome biology in the native cellular context, but they can be difficult to permeabilize and process without compromising nuclear integrity.

Instead, researchers typically turn to super-resolution microscopy for imaging DNA below the diffraction limit. These super-resolution microscopy approaches, such as stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM; Heilemann et al. 2005, 2008; Rust et al. 2006)), photo-activated localization microscopy (PALM; (Betzig et al. 2006)), structured illumination microscopy (SIM; (Gustafsson 2000, 2005)), or stimulated emission microscopy (STED; Hell and Wichmann 1994; Klar et al. 2000)) depend on specialized, expensive instrumentation, limiting their accessibility.

Conversely, ExM samples can be imaged with conventional light microscopes, offering a robust, cost-effective, and accessible alternative for super-resolution imaging. While studies to date directly visualizing DNA by ExM are limited, recent work in Drosophila and other systems have shown that ExM can be readily adapted for imaging nuclear proteins (Cahoon et al. 2017; Zwettler et al. 2020; Gaudreau-Lapierre et al. 2021; Faulkner et al. 2022; Pownall et al. 2023; Day et al. 2024). While these studies were ultimately successful, they also highlighted methodological challenges associated with ExM, including inconsistent expansion, loss of nuclear morphology, and reduced fluorescence signal. Thus, there is an increasing need for optimized ExM protocols, not just for the detection of nuclear proteins, but especially for the visualization of specific genomic loci of interest using DNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)-based methodologies.

Here, we provide a new, detailed, and reproducible protocol that combines Oligopaint DNA FISH with previously verified methods of immunofluorescence (IF) and Ultrastructure Expansion Microscopy (U-ExM; Gambarotto et al. 2019; Burns et al. 2026)). U-ExM differs from original ExM protocols in several important ways. Most notably, the fixation and crosslinking conditions employed in standard ExM are comparatively stringent, whereas U-ExM adopts milder conditions that intentionally sacrifice some sample rigidity to better preserve native cellular architecture. U-ExM also implements a reduced anchoring step. While traditional ExM methods emphasize strong chemical anchoring of proteins to the gel matrix to maximize protein retention, U-ExM uses a gentler anchoring strategy that prioritizes the preservation of spatial organization. Differences between protocols extend to the polymer chemistry of the hydrogel, where U-ExM fine-tunes the relative proportions of the monomers and adjusts the concentrations of initiators (APS/TEMED) to promote a more controlled and isotropic expansion. Finally, whereas classical ExM protocols typically rely on aggressive enzymatic digestion (e.g., with proteinase K) to enable expansion, U-ExM employs a more controlled, heat-based denaturation step for better ultrastructure preservation. Through these changes, U-ExM reduces competition among antibodies and minimizes fluorophore depletion associated with labeling after expansion (Gambarotto et al. 2021; Hinterndorfer et al. 2022).

The combination of U-ExM with IF-FISH in intact insect germline tissues provides a powerful new avenue for exploring nuclear architecture during meiosis and beyond. By physically magnifying specimens in a controlled manner while preserving both protein localization and DNA sequence information, our method expands the experimental possibilities for studying the synaptonemal complex (SC) and related chromosomal structures. This work underscores how U-ExM can overcome long-standing limitations in imaging resolution and opens the door to new investigations into the dynamic and intricate organization of meiotic chromosomes.

Schematic of U-ExM with IF-FISH in Drosophila ovaries. 1) Approximately 10 pairs of ovaries of the same genotype are dissected and placed in a tube with 1X PBS. Ovaries are then fixed. 2) Ovaries are immunostained with primary and secondary antibodies. 3) Ovaries are then post-fixed and incubated overnight in an anchoring solution (Acrylamide mix). 4) Samples (10 ovary pairs each) are embedded in a monomer solution (MS-Gel-Solution) on a pre-chilled humidified chamber and covered with a coverslip to initiate gelation. Polymerization is completed by incubating at 37 °C. 5) A biopsy punch tool is used to excise gel pieces that contain the embedded tissue. 6) Gel punches are transferred to a dish and then a tube for protein denaturation. 7) Gels undergo the first expansion in water, then are washed in 2X SSC-T to prepare for Oligopaint FISH. 8) Gels are denatured and subject to Oligopaint FISH hybridization. 9) Gels are then incubated with a fluorescent DNA stain such as DAPI. 10) A second expansion is performed by incubating the gels in water. A fully expanded gel is mounted onto a glass-bottom imaging dish for imaging

Other methods

Insects and husbandry

D. melanogaster w1118 strain was obtained from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center (stock 3605). Stocks were kept at 25 °C on standard molasses formula.

Oligopaint design and synthesis

Oligo probes for the AACAC and dodeca heterochromatic repeats (Dernburg et al. 1996; Dernburg 2011; Jagannathan et al. 2016) were synthesized with a 5′ Alexa-488 or Cy3 fluorescent dye, respectively, by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). Probe sequences were designed to be relatively short in length (25—35 bp) for efficient nuclear incorporation and to withstand stringent washes. The exact probe sequences are as follows: 488-AACAC:/5Alex488N/AACACAACACAACACAACACAACACAACACAACAC, and Cy3-dodeca:/5Cy3/ACGGGACCAGTACGG. Oligo probes were resuspended in 1 × TE at 100 µM concentration and stored at − 20 °C.

For euchromatic Oligopaint probes, Oligopaints were designed using the Dm6 genome (Hoskins et al. 2015) and were generated in the lab using the T7 method (Nguyen and Joyce 2019). Briefly, oligo pools were ordered from IDT and were amplified by PCR using primers containing forward and reverse barcode sequences that were added to oligos during the design process. The T7 sequence was added to the reverse primer (TAATACGACTCACTATAGGG), and secondary oligo binding sites were added to the forward primer. T7-labeled PCR products were then in vitro transcribed using the Hi-Scribe T7 RNA synthesis kit (NEB). RNA was converted to RNA:DNA duplexes by reverse transcription using the Maxima™ H Minus Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Scientific) with a fluorescently-labeled forward primer that recognizes the secondary oligo binding site (IDT). RNA was degraded to leave single-stranded DNA FISH probes using alkaline hydrolysis, and probes were purified using the Clean and Concentrator-100 Kit (Zymo Research). The paint used in this study was designed to have maximum probe density (~ 5 probes per kb) and 80 bp of homology and labels 200 kb at the following genomic locus: Chr 2L: 13,000,000—13,200,000.

Antibodies

Mouse anti-C(3)G antibodies was a gift from Scott Hawley (Page and Hawley 2001) and were used together (IA8, IG5, 5G4) at concentrations of 1/100 in these experiments. Rabbit anti-Corolla was also a gift from Scott Hawley (Collins et al. 2014) and was used at a concentration of 1/500. Secondary antibodies were as follows, all used at concentration of 1/250: Donkey anti-mouse 488 (Invitrogen; A21202), Donkey anti-mouse 555 (Invitrogen; A31570), or Donkey anti-rabbit 488 (Invitrogen; A21206).

Ovaries from mated adult females were dissected in 1X PBS at RT. Dissected ovaries were placed in 1X PBS on ice until all dissections were complete. Ovaries were then splayed open with forceps and fixed in 4% formaldehyde in 0.1% PBS-T for 15 min at RT. Ovaries were then washed thrice in 0.1% PBS-T for 5 min each. Subsequently, a dual permeabilization was performed: (1) 10 min in 100% methanol on ice followed by two 5 min washes in 0.1% PBS-T, then (2) 0.5% PBS-T (PBS with 0.5% Triton) for 15 min at RT. After permeabilizations, ovaries were blocked in 2% BSA in 0.1% PBS-T for 1 h at RT. The next day, ovaries were washed thrice in 0.1% PBS-T for 20 min before incubating in secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT (Donkey anti-mouse 488 or Donkey anti-rabbit 488;1/500). After secondary staining, ovaries were washed thrice in 0.1% PBS-T for 20 min and post-fixed in 4% formaldehyde in 0.1% PBS-T for 10 min at RT. After fixation, ovaries were rinsed twice in 2X SSC-T before performing the following wash steps: 10 min in 2X SSC-T, 10 min in 20% formamide in 2X SSC-T, and 10 min in 50% formamide in 2X SSC-T. In fresh 50% formamide in 2X SSC-T, ovaries were pre-denatured in a thermocycler as follows: 3 h at 37 °C, 3 min at 92 °C, and 20 min at 60 °C. Primary probe mix was made as follows: 300 pmol 2L-mid probe, 25 μL 100% formamide, 12.5 μL DNA hybridization buffer (see above), 10.5 μL PVSA, 2 μL RNaseA, and 2.8 μL 25 mM each dNTP mix. Ovaries were resuspended in this primary probe mix and denatured at 91 °C for 3 min before being incubated at 37 °C overnight. The next day, ovaries were washed twice in 50% formamide in 2X SSC-T for 30 min each at 37 °C and then resuspended in secondary probe mix (2 μL of 10 μM ATTO-565-labeled secondary probe stock, 25 μL formamide, 12.5 μL DNA hybridization buffer, and 10.5 μL PVSA). Ovaries in secondary probe mix were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. After incubation, the following washes were performed: twice for 30 min each in 50% formamide in 2X SSC-T at 37 °C, once for 10 min in 50% formamide in 2X SSC-T at RT, once for 10 min in 20% formamide in 2X SSC-T at RT, and once for 10 min in 2X SSC-T at RT. Results and discussion

As described above, U-ExM has previously been shown to minimize the technical artifacts associated with conventional ExM. The technique supports improved structural preservation and reduced fluorophore depletion after expansion (Gambarotto et al. 2019; Burns et al. 2026). However, adding DNA FISH to the protocol provides an additional level of complexity and new challenges. For example, fixation and DNA denaturation conditions must be balanced to maintain protein architecture. Additionally, the harsh denaturation conditions associated with FISH can result in epitope masking, loss, or structural distortion, impacting the visualization and spatial interpretation of antibody signals.

To overcome these obstacles, our U-ExM method is adapted from the recent Burns et al. (Burns et al. 2026) U-ExM method, which already improves many issues associated with previous iterations of U-ExM. For example, thinner gels are used to decrease the working distance between sample and microscope objective, allowing objects of interest to more easily be brought into focus on conventional microscopes. Additionally, biopsy gel punches are used to allow for easier handling of the thinner gel matrix. To prevent antibody or epitope denaturation during the DNA FISH process, we perform IF on intact gonads prior to the FISH and expansion processes. This allows us to crosslink the primary and secondaries antibodies to their epitopes prior to anchoring the cells within acrylamide, reducing IF signal loss and structural distortion during the FISH process. We then take advantage of the high denaturation temperature used in U-ExM to simultaneously begin the denaturation of DNA along with the proteins in preparation for expansion and FISH. FISH is performed with fluorescently labeled DNA oligos and was carried out after gel polymerization and initial expansion but before the second expansion (Fig. 1). This specific sequence of steps preserves the structural integrity of protein/antibody complexes while allowing for highly specific DNA labeling and visualization.

We use this newly adapted U-ExM-IF-FISH method to effectively capture high resolution images of the synaptonemal complex (SC; Fig. 2A) and either heterochromatic DNA repeats or single-copy euchromatic loci in whole ovaries from Drosophila melanogaster (Fig. 2B). We find that in using this protocol with an antibody targeting the C-terminal region of the transverse filament protein C(3)G (Fig. 2A), non-expanded samples show only a single “track” of SC (Fig. 2C, 2H, 2I) while samples after U-ExM show two distinct tracts (Figs. 2D-F, H, I). Using this protocol, our gels expanded by a factor of 4—5x (from 4 mm in diameter to ~ 1.5—2 cm in diameter; Fig. 2G). Consistent with the expanded size of our gels, the total width of the SC after U-ExM is around 800 nm, which is ~ 5 × larger than the previous measurements of SC size in Drosophila based on electron and super-resolution microscopy (~ 100—150 nm) (Carpenter 1975; Collins et al. 2014). Importantly, the addition of DNA FISH to our U-ExM-IF-FISH protocol does not significantly reduce fluorescence intensity or the structure of SC proteins compared to IF + U-ExM alone (Fig. 2J-K).

Our combination of U-ExM with IF-FISH in intact insect germline tissues offers a powerful approach for examining nuclear architecture during meiosis. By physically expanding the specimen in a controlled manner, this technique enables simultaneous visualization of protein localization and DNA sequence information. This integration broadens the range of experimental possibilities for investigating the SC and other chromosomal structures.

While U-ExM-based methods like the one we present here offer numerous advantages over other super-resolution imaging approaches, they are not without limitations. Expansion-based protocols are inherently restricted to fixed (dead) samples, precluding their use in live-cell imaging. Moreover, sample size is constrained by the practical challenges of handling numerous expanded specimens, as well as the substantially higher antibody requirements relative to conventional methods (two- to four-fold increase in concentrations). Reports from other laboratories have also highlighted concerns regarding reproducibility, potential structural distortions, and diminished antibody performance in ExM workflows (Ku et al. 2016; Gambarotto et al. 2021), although such issues were not apparent in our hands. Notably, U-ExM presents additional technical demands compared to standard ExM, as it relies on thinner, more fragile gels and involves a somewhat more time-intensive protocol.

Despite these challenges, our U-ExM-IF-FISH protocol represents a new and improved method for imaging DNA and DNA-associated protein complex at nanoscale resolution. Our work here highlights how U-ExM can overcome traditional limitations in imaging resolution and paves the way for new studies into the dynamic and complex organization of meiotic chromosomes.
