Safe to Stay: Psychological Safety Sustains Participation in Pull-based Open Source Projects
Emeralda Sesari, Federica Sarro, Ayushi Rastogi

Abstract
Background: Psychological safety refers to the belief that team members can speak up or make mistakes without fear of negative consequences. While it is recognized as important in traditional software teams, its role in open-source software development remains understudied. Open-source contributors often collaborate without formal roles or structures, where interpersonal relationships can significantly influence participation. Code review, a central and collaborative activity in modern software development, offers a valuable context for observing such team interactions. Aims: This study investigates whether team-level psychological safety, inferred from code review activities, is associated with contributors’ sustained participation in open-source projects. Method: Using data from 60,684 pull requests across multiple repositories, we developed a psychological safety index based on observable cues such as merge decisions, comment activity, interaction diversity, and mentions. We analyzed the relationship between this index and contributors’ short-term (within 1 year) and long-term (over 4-5 years) sustained participation using three logistic regression models. Results: Contributors are more likely to remain active in repositories with higher levels of psychological safety. Psychological safety is positively associated with both short-term and long-term sustained participation. However, prior participation emerges as a stronger predictor of future engagement, reducing the effect of psychological safety when accounted for. Conclusions: This study introduces a scalable, data-driven approach to measuring psychological safety through pull request data and provides new empirical evidence of its relevance in sustaining participation within open-source development.



I
Introduction

Software Engineering (SE) is a highly team-intensive discipline that requires effective collaboration, communication, and coordination among developers to successfully build and maintain software systems. Studies have shown that factors such as team diversity, cohesion, and human factors impact software development outcomes
[
1
,
2
]
. One of the key elements that fosters such teamwork is psychological safety (PS). Psychological safety is a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking
[
3
]
. It allows individuals to speak up, ask questions, admit mistakes, and offer new ideas without fear of negative consequences. Research has shown that PS is a key factor in high-performing teams
[
3
]
.

Google’s Project Aristotle examined 115 engineering teams and 65 sales teams to determine what makes a team effective
[
4
]
. Their findings revealed that teams with strong PS were more effective, as members felt comfortable sharing ideas, taking risks, and collaborating openly. Their results showed that even highly skilled employees needed a psychologically safe environment to contribute their best work. Project Aristotle also revealed that employees do not want to suppress their personalities or emotions at work. PS allows individuals to bring their entire self to work, to allow honest discussions about challenges, frustrations, and uncertainties about their work.

In SE, studies suggest that PS can be indicated by open communication, constructive feedback, willingness to admit mistakes, and engagement in discussions
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. Santana et al. highlight that software teams benefit from open communication, constructive feedback, and a culture where mistakes are seen as learning opportunities
[
5
,
6
]
. Recent work also suggests that interpersonal fairness, such as being treated with respect and dignity, is not only central to job satisfaction
[
8
]
, but also underpins PS in software teams
[
9
]
. Lenberg and Feldt examined PS in open-source communities by linking it to sustained participation measuring factors like discussion engagement and re-engagement after PR rejections
[
7
]
. GitLab emphasizes the negative effects of punishment culture, blame, and poor communication, which can reduce trust and collaboration
[
10
]
.
PS has also been linked to high team performance in software teams
[

,

.

Despite these insights, most empirical studies of PS in SE have focused on company environments and rely primarily on qualitative methods, such as interviews and surveys

However, software development also takes place in open-source software (OSS) communities, which differ from traditional workplaces.

OSS contributors span diverse time zones, cultures, and experience levels, and they participate voluntarily or as part of their professional work

. Because participation is self-directed and non-contractual, contributors are especially sensitive to the social dynamics they encounter. Prior studies have shown that motivation, task characteristics, social capital, and experiences of rejection shape contributor engagement

. Given this, psychological safety, the sense that it is safe to ask questions and make mistakes, may be particularly important in OSS, where contributors often decide to stay or leave based on how they are treated and whether their work is valued.

In OSS development, pull requests (PRs) are central to collaboration. PRs are not only technical data but also a source of social interaction, where contributors receive feedback, negotiate changes, and seek acceptance from project maintainers
[
25
]
. These interactions can be constructive, supportive, or discouraging, and they offer insights into how teams communicate and whether a psychologically safe environment exists
[
26
]
. For this reason, we focus on code review as the collaborative activity through which PS may be reflected. We hypothesize that contributors are more likely to remain engaged when PR activities reflect higher PS.

This study focuses on PR interactions as a cue for team-level PS in OSS, examining whether these observable signals are associated with the continued participation of contributors. This study takes an initial step toward empirically examining how PS during code review influences sustained participation in OSS. Specifically, we investigate the following research questions:

RQ1: How is psychological safety associated with contributors’ sustained participation in a repository?

We modeled the relationship between repository-level PS and sustained participation, defined as whether a contributor remains active within 12 months after their PR. The results show that contributors are more likely to remain active in repositories where the team exhibits a higher PS.

RQ2: How is psychological safety associated with contributors’ future sustained participation in a repository?

We then tested whether PS predicts longer-term participation, measured as participation beyond the 12-month window. We found that team-level PS measured at the time of the contributor’s PR significantly predicts future sustained participation.

RQ3: How are psychological safety and contributors’ prior sustained participation associated with their future sustained participation in a repository?

To further examine the relative importance of PS, we extended the model in RQ2 by including prior participation as a predictor. The findings show that prior participation is a stronger predictor of future participation, and that the effect of PS is weaker once past activity is taken into account.

Through this study, we contribute a data-driven approach to operationalizing PS in OSS by developing a proxy based on PR cues. We test its association with sustained participation across three statistical models. Our findings suggest that PS serves as a meaningful signal that supports contributor sustained participation. However, for contributors who have already established a participation pattern in a project, long-term participation appears to be driven more by their previous activity than by the psychologically safe team.

II
Background

II-A

Sustained Participation in Open Source

Sustained participation is important for the continuation and success of OSS projects, as frequent turnover can affect project stability and quality
[
24
]
. One factor influencing OSS participation is rejection experiences. Negative feedback or rejection of contributions can discourage contributors, leading to disengagement. Jiang et al. examined patch acceptance rates in the Linux kernel and found that long approval times and frequent rejections negatively impact contributor sustained participation
[
19
]
. Similarly, Hellendoorn et al. suggested that contributor’s perception of peer feedback affects their willingness to continue participation
[
20
]
. Conversely, Tao et al. emphasized that contributors who receive constructive feedback and guidance tend to participate longer
[
22
]
.

Previous studies also indicate that social factors play a crucial role in retention. Lin et al. found that contributors who join early, engage in coding rather than documentation, and assume responsibilities such as modifying code have a higher probability of continued participation
[
17
]
. Furthermore, Zhou and Mockus identified key characteristics of new contributors who are likely to become long-term participants, including participation through discussions and integration within peer groups
[
27
]
. Furthermore, Qiu et al. found that contributors who collaborate with familiar team members tend to stay engaged longer
[
24
]
.

When contributors work with familiar peers, they are more likely to communicate openly, exchange constructive feedback, and collaborate without fear of negative consequences. We propose that this familiarity in a team (that is, a repository) could reinforce PS by fostering an environment where individuals feel comfortable taking risks, expressing concerns and contributing consistently. Given these connections, we hypothesize that PS serves as a key mechanism facilitating sustained participation in OSS. By examining how PS correlates with contributor participation, we aim to provide empirical insights into the role of PS in shaping long-term participation in OSS projects.

II-B

Psychological Safety

The concept of PS was introduced by Amy Edmondson in 1999 through her study of clinical teams
[
3
]
. She highlighted the importance of a shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking
[
3
]
. Edmondson identified key indicators of PS, including whether team members feel comfortable raising concerns, taking risks, and seeking help without fear of embarrassment or punishment
[
3
]
.

Subsequent research, such as Google’s Project Aristotle, found PS as a key determinant of team effectiveness
[
4
]
. After studying 180 teams comprising 115 engineering teams and 65 sales teams, Google found that team composition, such as shared hobbies, personalities, or management styles, did not predict team effectiveness. Instead, Google’s study identified five key factors of high-performing teams: impact, meaning, structure & clarity, dependability, and PS, with PS being the strongest predictor of team success. Given its strong influence on team effectiveness, PS may also play a crucial role in sustaining participation in OSS teams.

II-C

Psychological Safety in Software Teams

In recent years, researchers have examined PS impact in various S contexts, from Stack Exchange discussions to Agile team dynamics. Previous studies
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
11
,
12
]
together observed that PS is a vital component of successful software development teams, which foster an environment where developers feel comfortable sharing ideas, asking questions, and embracing innovation.

Santana et al. investigated PS in SE by analyzing discussions on Stack Exchange
[
5
]
. Their findings suggest that a psychologically safe environment enables developers to ask questions and share knowledge more freely, leading to enhanced learning and problem-solving efficiency
[
5
]
. Additionally, they identified potential indicators of a lack of PS in software development teams through various interpersonal scenarios, such as communication of opinions on work issues, and valuation of expressed opinion.

Further studies by Santana et al. highlight the significance of PS in software workplaces
[
6
]
. Their research emphasizes that teams with higher levels of PS exhibit improved communication, reduced stress, and greater openness to innovation, which are important for maintaining productivity and software quality
[
6
]
. They observed that in a psychologically safe workplace, the following should not be challenging: disagreeing with suggestions or ideas, identifying problems, recommending changes, sharing negative feedback, seeking help, drawing attention to errors, and admitting errors. They also shared some takeaways for software teams to foster a psychologically safe workspace, such as for software project leaders to ensure that the team is comfortable admitting errors.

Additionally, Lenberg and Feldt explored the relationship between PS and norm clarity within SE teams
[
7
]
. They collected industry survey data from practitioners (N = 217) in 38 development teams working for five different organizations. The result of multiple linear regression analyses indicates that both PS and team norm clarity predict team members’ self-assessed performance and job satisfaction.

The role of PS in Agile teams has also been a subject of interest. Alami et al. conducted interviews with 20 practitioners and surveyed 423 participants
[
11
]
. They observed that psychologically safe Agile software development teams seem to capitalize on four main quality-related behaviors: speaking up about software quality problems, admitting software quality mistakes, learning from mistakes, helping each other to enhance quality, collective problem-solving to solve quality issues, and taking initiatives to promote better software quality
[
11
]
.

Moreover, Kakar examined the interplay between PS, team cohesion, and knowledge sharing in software development projects
[
12
]
. This study theorizes and tests the direct and interactional impacts of team cohesion and PS on knowledge sharing. They found that high levels of team cohesion can leverage the positive impact of PS on knowledge sharing, ultimately enhancing collaboration and team efficiency
[
12
]
.

Most research on PS in software teams has focused on company environments using qualitative methods such as interviews and surveys. However, software development also occurs in OSS communities. Given its established role in fostering communication and learning within software teams, we investigate whether PS similarly influences sustained participation in OSS teams using empirical data to support our analysis.

III
Methodology

To examine the relationship between PS and sustained participation in OSS, we analyze a dataset of pull requests and assess contributor participation based on their last active date. Our methodology includes selecting and filtering a dataset, defining sustained participation, identifying PS cues, calculating PS index, identifying control variables, and applying statistical modeling.

III-A

Dataset

We used the dataset provided by Zhang et al.
[
28
]
, which is the largest dataset for pull-based development research to date. This dataset contains 11,230 OSS projects, including 96 metrics and 3,347,937 pull requests which was compiled using the latest version of Mysql data dump created on 1 June 2019 and MongoDB dump created on 30 June 2019. It is the most comprehensive and largest dataset toward a complete picture of pull-based development research
[
28
]
. The feature selection is based on Gousios et al.’s dataset
[
29
]
, as well as studies on pull request development from 2009 until 2019. These features broadly fall into three categories: those related to the contributor, the project, and the pull request, though some features lie at their intersection. The dataset consisting of 69 new features in addition to the 27 features reported in Gousios et al.’s dataset.

III-B

Filtering Repositories

To ensure a relevant dataset for our analysis, we applied a filtering process to select repositories with active engagement. Prior work suggests that starring can be a useful proxy for project engagement, as popular repositories tend to attract more contributors and sustained development activity
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.

However, recent research has highlighted concerns regarding fake stars on GitHub, where artificially inflated popularity has been linked to scams, malware, and deceptive ranking tactics
[
33
]
. To mitigate this risk, we initially selected the 200 most starred repositories but further refined the repositories by removing repositories that did not primarily focus on active software development contributions. Specifically, we excluded:

•

code learning (e.g., coding challenges, tutorials);

•

resource lists (e.g., curated lists of tools, frameworks, or libraries);

•

educational content (e.g., repositories intended for teaching rather than active software development);

•

non-English repositories, as language barriers limit our understanding about the context of the repository;

•

documentation-only repositories that do not involve active software development.

After applying these criteria, we retained 26 repositories, comprising a total of 60,684 PRs for further analysis. The complete list of selected repositories, along with the PR distribution, is presented in Table

. We categorized the repositories based on PR activity into three groups: large (more than 1,000 PRs), medium (between 101 and 1,000 PRs), and small (fewer than 100 PRs).
This distribution emerged naturally from our filtering process, which focused on highly starred repositories engaged in active software development. Since activity size may influence contributor dynamics, we included repository activity size as a control variable to account for potential differences in participation patterns (see Section

).

III-C

Sustained Participation as Outcome Variable

Our goal is to understand the role of PS in sustaining contributor participation in OSS projects. To measure sustained participation, we follow the approach by Qiu et al.
[
24
]
, which defines disengagement based on GitHub activity.

A contributor is considered disengaged if they have not made any commits for 12 consecutive months. This threshold accounts for normal breaks in participation while ensuring that long-term disengagement is accurately identified. Contributors whose last commit falls near the end of the study period are not classified as disengaged since they may still return. Additionally, contributors who resumed activity after 12 months of inactivity were excluded, as their participation patterns did not align with long-term disengagement.

To align the participation measurement with the timeframe of the dataset by Zhang et al.
[
28
]
, which are derived from 2019 pull request interactions, we mainly considered commit history up to June 30, 2020 (i.e., a 12-month window following June 30, 2019, the timestamp of the MongoDB snapshot used in the dataset). A contributor is marked as a sustained contributor (
sustainedp_or_not_12
= 1) if they remained active during this period, and as non-sustained (
sustainedp_or_not_12
= 0) if they became inactive before June 30, 2019.
In addition to this, we also computed a more recent sustained participation variable (
recent_sustainedp_or_not
) based on commit activity up to December 2024, to explore longer-term participation.

III-E

Calculating Psychological Safety Index as Predictor Variable

To examine how PS influences sustained participation, we constructed a PS index using cues observed during PR activities as provided in Table

. Specifically, we first defined the PS index at the PR level (ranging from 0 to 10). We considered a PR as psychologically safe (i.e., assigned a value of 1 for a specific cue) if the contributor remained active after the PR (i.e., sustained participant) and one of the following interaction conditions was met:

•

PR was merged or not merged (
merged_or_not = 1
or
= 0
)

•

number of comments in the PR (
pr_comment_num
) was high

•

PR received contributor’s and reviewers comment (
has_exchange = 1
)

•

PR received contributor’s comment (
contrib_comment = 1
)

•

number of contributor comments (
num_comments_con
) was high

•

PR received an integrator’s comment (
inte_comment = 1
)

•

PR received a reviewer’s comment (
reviewer_comment = 1
)

•

PR received a comment from others outside the team (
other_comment = 1

•

number of participants in PR comment (
num_participant
) was high

•

existence of mention in a PR
comment (

For each PR, we calculated the total number of PS-aligned cues, resulting in a PS index between 0 and 10.

We used the mean to aggregate PS indexes at both the contributor and repository levels. The mean enables us to interpret the PS index as the average level of psychologically safe behavior observed in interactions. It retains more information than the median, aligns with common practices in behavioral and organizational research, and is well-suited for use as a continuous predictor in regression models

We then calculated the repository-level PS index by averaging the contributor-level PS scores for each repository. This repository-level index serves as the main predictor in our statistical models.

III-F

Control Variables

We identified a set of control variables based on factors known to influence sustained participation in open-source development to better isolate the role of PS.
By including these variables in our statistical models, we account for other possible influences on sustained participation.
If PS remains a significant predictor after controlling for these factors, it strengthens the credibility of its association with sustained participation.

These variables were inspired by the study of Qiu et al.
[
24
]
, and adapted based on the data available in our dataset
[
28
]
. Below, we describe each of the seven control variables and its corresponding operationalization in our study.

Is Project Owner and Is Project Major Contributor.
Qiu et al.
[
24
]
used project ownership and major contributor status, which is defined as those who authored at least 5% of the project’s commits during a specific window. In our study, we operationalized this using two variables: whether a contributor is a
core_member
, and their commit proportion as
contrib_rate_author
.

Number of Followers and Number of Repository Stars.
Contributor and project visibility were also considered important controls
[
47
]
. Qiu et al.
[
24
]
used the number of followers (developer visibility) and number of repository stars (project visibility). In our dataset, we use contributor
followers
for contributor visibility and repository
watchers
to represent repository stars.

Niche Breadth.
Contributors knowing multiple languages are more versatile and less likely to disengage
[
24
]
. Qiu et al.
[
24
]
used the number of programming languages of the developer’s past commits are spread across. We calculated this by counting the number of unique repository
language
s that a contributor has contributed to as
num_languages
.

Recurring Cohesion.
To approximate the strength of prior collaboration ties, Qiu et al.
[
24
]
incorporate a network-based measure known as recurring cliques. Following the approach of Lutter
[
48
]
and de Vaan et al.
[
49
]
, this measure captures groups of at least three developers who have previously collaborated and then worked together again. Such recurring triads are seen as more cohesive than teams where interactions are limited to individual pairwise connections.

In our study, we used a proxy for recurring cohesion: whether the contributor had followed the integrator prior to creating the pull request (
contrib_follow_integrator
).

Team Familiarity.
The concept of team familiarity captures the extent of a contributor’s prior social ties to the team
[
24
]
. We built on Newman’s
[
50
]
concept of average interpersonal familiarity within a team, which reflects how frequently current team members have worked together in the past. This idea is conceptually aligned with Casalnuovo et al.’s
[
51
]
measure of a developer’s social connection to a project. In our study, we used
social_strength
, which represents the fraction of team members a contributor has interacted with in the past three months.

Repository Activity Size.
We also controled for the overall level of activity within a repository, as projects with more PRs may provide greater opportunities for engagement, which potentially affect sustained participation. Following our categorization (see Section
III-B
and Table
I
), we classify repositories based on the number of PRs into three categories: large, medium, and small. This classification is captured in the categorical variable
repo_size
.

III-G

Statistical Modeling

To examine how PS relates to contributors’ sustained participation, we developed three logistic regression models, each designed to address a different research question. All models estimate the probability of sustained participation given selected predictors and control variables, using a binary outcome variable (1 = sustained participation, 0 = disengagement).

Model 1 tests the association between repository-level PS and sustained participation. This model tests whether a contributor remained active within a 12-month window following their 2019 activity given the repository PS index. Model 2 examines whether PS predicts long-term sustained participation. Here, the model indicates whether a contributor remained active up to December 2024 given the repository PS index. Model 3 extends Model 2 by including prior participation (12-month window following their 2019 activity) as an additional predictor, to evaluate whether repository PS index has a unique contribution to long-term participation beyond a contributor’s past participation.

To ensure model stability, we separately assessed skewness and multicollinearity. We evaluated skewness using
skewness()
function from the
e1071
package in R and found that the variable
watchers
remained highly skewed even after log transformation (from –2.75 to –3.39), so we excluded it from further analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), and all remaining predictors had VIF values below 5 which indicates acceptable levels of multicollinearity.

After fitting the models, we also examined the effect sizes by computing odds ratios for each predictor.

RQ1: How is psychological safety associated with contributors’ sustained participation in a repository?

Model 1 examines whether PS at the repository level is associated with developers’ sustained participation (i.e., being active within 12 months following their pull request). The variable
PS_index_repository
is positively and significantly associated with sustained participation (
\beta=1.03
,
p<0.001
) even after accounting for various control variables. The corresponding odds ratio is 2.80, indicating that for every one-unit increase in repository-level PS, the odds of sustained participation increase by 180%.
This result supports our hypothesis that contributors are more likely to remain active in repositories where the overall team environment exhibits higher PS.

In addition, the results show that contributors in medium-sized repositories are significantly more likely to sustain participation compared to those in small repositories (
\beta=1.12
,
p<0.001
; OR = 3.08), and those in large repositories even more so (
\beta=3.84
,
p<0.001
; OR = 46.5). This implies that contributors are more likely to remain active in repositories that exhibit higher levels of PS and that operate at larger scales.

RQ2: How is psychological safety associated with contributors’ future sustained participation in a repository?

Model 2 assesses whether repository-level PS is associated with developers’ future sustained participation. The variable
PS_index_repository
is positive and statistically significant (
\beta=0.32
,
p<0.01
). The corresponding odds ratio is 1.38, meaning that a one-unit increase in the PS index increases the odds of a contributor remaining active in the long term by 38%.

This result suggests that PS, as observed at the time of a PR, has a meaningful and positive association with contributors’ long-term engagement, independent of their past behavior.
Compared to RQ1, where PS increased the odds of short-term sustained participation by 180%, this finding suggests that the influence of psychological safety diminishes over time. While PS clearly supports initial and near-term engagement, its effect on long-term participation appears to be more modest.

Additionally, the results reveal that contributors in medium repositories have significantly higher odds of long-term participation than those in small ones (
\beta=2.15
,
p<0.001
; OR = 8.63), and the effect is slightly larger for contributors in large repositories (
\beta=2.38
,
p<0.001
; OR = 10.8). These findings suggest that contributors are more likely to remain active in repositories that are not only psychologically safe but also more active or established in terms of PR volume.

RQ3: How are psychological safety and contributors’ prior sustained participation associated with their future sustained participation in a repository?

The reduced impact of PS in the long term (RQ2) raises the question of what other factors sustain contributor participation over extended periods. To explore this, RQ3 examines whether prior participation history explains continued participation beyond the influence of PS. Here, model 3 investigates whether contributors’ prior sustained participation (
sustainedp_or_not_12
) and repository-level PS (
PS_index_repository
) predict continued engagement in the project.

The results show that prior participation is a strong and highly significant predictor (
\beta=1.86
,
p<0.001
; OR = 6.43), indicating that contributors who have previously remained active are over six times more likely to continue participating. PS remains statistically significant in this model (
\beta=0.05
,
p<0.01
; OR = 1.05), though its effect size is notably smaller. This suggests that while PS has a positive association with future participation, its influence is more modest when contributors’ participation history is accounted for.

Additionally, repository activity size (
repo_size
) continues to be relevant, with contributors in medium and large repositories more likely to sustain participation compared to those in small ones.

Together, the three models offer an understanding of how PS relates to developers’ sustained participation in open source repositories based on the PR activities.
Overall, the findings indicate that developers working in repositories with higher levels of PS are more likely to remain active, both in the short (RQ1) and longer term (RQ2). However, once contributors have already established a pattern of participation, their future participation is largely shaped by that prior activity (RQ3). In other words, while PS supports initial and continued participation, long-term participation appears to be reinforced more by previous engagement.

V
Discussion

V-A

Role of PS in Open Source Participation

Our study highlights the role of PS in influencing contributor participation in open source projects. We find that repository-level PS, operationalized using observable cues from pull request interactions, is significantly associated with sustained participation. Contributors in repositories with higher PS are more likely to continue contributing. This reinforces the idea that fostering a psychologically safe environment plays a meaningful role in retaining contributors in the near term.

This finding aligns with prior research in traditional SE settings, where PS has been recognized as a key factor in team effectiveness and collaboration. Previous work, mostly qualitative in nature, such as interviews and surveys, has explored the impact of PS in various contexts, from Stack Exchange discussions to Agile team dynamics. Studies by Santana et al.
[
5
,
6
]
, Lenberg et al.
[
7
]
, Alami et al.
[
11
]
, and Kakar
[
12
]
consistently suggest that PS fosters environments where developers feel comfortable sharing ideas, asking questions, and engaging in innovation.

However, while PS continues to be a significant predictor of future contributor participation independent of prior participation (Model 2), its effect reduces once we include whether a contributor had already been active (Model 3). In Model 3, prior participation emerges as a stronger predictor of future activity, suggesting that developers who have previously remained active are likely to continue contributing regardless of the perceived level of PS.

This shift aligns with findings from prior large-scale studies. For instance, Xiao et al.
[
52
]
found that early contributions from experienced and consistent contributors strongly predicted future activity within GitHub projects. Their work suggests that early sustained participation acts as a powerful signal of long-term engagement, potentially outweighing social signals such as PS. Similarly, Bao et al.
[
53
]
demonstrated that prior behavior, including frequency of commits, collaboration patterns, and overall GitHub activity, was the strongest predictor of contributor retention, while social or psychological factors played a comparatively smaller role over extended time.

Taken together, these findings suggest that PS may act as an important signal, one that shapes sustained participation within a certain period. However, for contributors who are already embedded in the project, other factors such as experience, consistency, collaboration patterns, and overall activity may take more precedence over team PS.

V-B

Implications

Our work introduces a novel empirical approach to measuring PS in open source repositories using PR cues. This contributes to the growing interest in social and human aspects of SE by offering an accessible, reproducible method to infer PS from repository activity which is to draw on empirical data rather than relying on self-reported perceptions. In doing so, we shift the lens from how developers say they work to how they actually work in collaborative settings. This approach opens opportunities for future research to refine or validate PS measurement using qualitative or survey-based methods.

Several closely related works support the premise that social dynamics and interpersonal signals are embedded in PR processes. The following studies affirm the feasibility and importance of our approach. For instance, Alami et al. identified different styles of PR governance that emphasize not only technical quality but also trust, mentorship, and inclusion
[
25
]
. These findings suggest that PR interactions are reflective of deeper team-level social norms. Similarly, Golzadeh et al.
[
54
]
demonstrate that PR discussions and reactions carry interpersonal meaning, from empathy and encouragement to approval and disapproval, such cues influence contributor experience and behavior. This further supports the idea that the social climate of a project is embedded in its review processes.

For project maintainers, our findings suggest that creating psychologically safe interactions, through tagging, engaging in comments, and inclusive responses, can positively influence sustained participation. These cues not only serve as observable indicators of team PS but are also modifiable through community guidelines and review practices. Several prior studies reinforce this insight, showing that inclusive and respectful communication from maintainers improves contributor retention and enhances perceptions of welcomeness, particularly among newcomers and underrepresented contributors
[
55
]
. Tools like
Climate Coach
further demonstrate that communication tone and inclusion signals are actionable elements of team climate that can be monitored and improved by maintainers
[
56
]
.

Contributors may also benefit from recognizing and seeking out team environments that support PS. For example, repositories where PS discussions involve frequent, constructive interactions, such as comments from multiple participants, exchanges of feedback between contributors, reviewers, and integrators, which may indicate a more psychologically safe environment. Contributing to such repositories could increase the likelihood of sustained participation.

This is particularly important given that many potential contributors are discouraged from further participation when their initial pull requests are rejected without constructive feedback
[
57
]
. Moreover, developers frequently report that one of the greatest challenges in pull-based development is the lack of meaningful responses from integrators, which can reduce motivation and discourage future contributions
[
58
]
. On the other hand, projects that foster open dialogue and collaborative evaluation during PR reviews, especially when contributors feel their ideas are heard, tend to encourage repeated participation and long-term community engagement
[
59
]
.

VI
Limitations

While our study provides an empirical approach to understanding PS in OSS, several limitations should be noted.
First, PS is measured indirectly using cues extracted from PR interactions. Although these cues are grounded in prior research, they do not directly capture contributors’ perceptions or experiences of PS and future studies could complement this approach with survey-based or qualitative methods to validate these indicators. Future research could also explore additional indicators, such as the tone and content of feedback contributors receive, or other outcomes, such as team learning and team effectiveness.

Second, our analysis relies solely on communication data from GitHub PR interactions. This excludes informal communications via other channels, such as messaging platforms, emails, or offline discussions, which potentially overlook important social dynamics. Prior research has noted the risks of incomplete insights when mining only GitHub data
[
60
]
. Future studies could address this by incorporating external communication sources.

Third, our study focuses on 26 highly starred repositories with varying PR activities. We also introduced repository activity size as a control variable to account for potential differences in contributor dynamics across project scales. However, the sample may still be biased toward highly rated repositories. Lower rated projects, which may operate under different social and technical conditions, remain underrepresented. Future work could further extend this line of research by incorporating a more diverse and representative set of open-source projects.

Fourth, despite the inclusion of multiple control variables, unobserved factors such as individual contributor circumstances, specific project governance practices, or external factors may also affect participation outcomes. While we cannot account for all possible influences, we selected control variables based on prior empirical studies that identified them as important predictors of sustained participation in open source. This approach enhances the validity of our findings, though some residual confounding may remain.

VII
Conclusion

This study explored the role of
psychological safety
in sustaining contributor participation in open-source projects. Using cues from
pull request interactions
, we developed a data-driven proxy to quantify PS and tested its association with participation across three statistical models. We found that repository-level PS is significantly associated with sustained participation. It also predicts future participation when prior activity is not included in the model. However, once prior sustained participation is accounted for, psychological safety has a lower effect.
These findings highlight psychological safety as an important social factor in the different stages of participation, aligning with prior work on team dynamics in software engineering. Our approach provides a quantitative way to study psychological safety from pull request activity data, which offers a foundation for future research on psychological safety in open source software and software engineering.
