Deep Brain Stimulation after Subthalamic Focused Ultrasound for Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Ayaka Ichise, Naoki Tani, Yuko Ushida, Takeshi Shimizu, Koichi Hosomi, Satoru Oshino, Keita Kakuda, Kousuke Baba, Manabu Kanemoto, Hideki Mochizuki, Haruhiko Kishima
Published: 2026

Subthalamic nucleus (STN) deep brain stimulation (STN‐DBS) is an established treatment for medication‐refractory motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease (PD). 1 In contrast, unilateral magnetic resonance‐guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) lesioning of the STN (STN‐FUS) is an incisionless treatment that does not require implanted hardware and has been investigated for markedly asymmetric PD. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 However, electrophysiological characteristics of chronic STN‐FUS lesions and the effects of STN‐DBS in surrounding regions remain poorly described. We report a case of a patient with tremor‐dominant PD who underwent right STN‐DBS for recurrent symptoms after right STN‐FUS.

Case Report

A 57‐year‐old man was diagnosed with PD at 51 years of age after developing left upper limb‐predominant tremor and bradykinesia at 50 years of age. He previously participated in our clinical trial of STN‐FUS for PD, the overall outcomes of which were reported by Saeki et al. 5 He underwent right STN‐FUS at 53 years of age. The preoperative skull density ratio was 0.48. The target was set 13.1 mm lateral, 1.0 mm posterior, and 3.0 mm inferior to the right midcommissural point. At the first target, sonication at 59/54°C improved rigidity and bradykinesia. At the second target, the sonication point was adjusted 0.5 mm laterally and posteriorly, but the procedure was discontinued because of dyskinesia of the right foot during sonication.

Tremor nearly resolved after STN‐FUS but gradually recurred, and right VIM‐FUS was subsequently performed at 54 years of age for recurrent tremor. Despite VIM‐FUS, rest, re‐emergent postural, and kinetic tremors characteristic of PD persisted. Magnetic resonance imaging obtained 4 years later showed a chronic residual fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR)‐hyperintense lesion within the right STN (Fig. 1A). Right STN‐DBS was performed at 57 years of age when the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS‐UPDRS) part III score was 31 (tremor subscore, 13). Lead implantation was performed under total intravenous anesthesia, targeting 12.8 mm lateral, 2.0 mm posterior, and 4.0 mm inferior to the midcommissural point. Microelectrode recording (MER) was performed along the planned trajectory before placement of a directional lead (SenSight 33,005, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Increased background activity with an irregular bursting pattern was observed approximately 5 mm dorsal to the target. Activity decreased across the 3–4‐mm segment corresponding to the chronic STN‐FUS lesion on FLAIR imaging before resuming ventrally, suggesting incomplete ablation with viable STN tissue on both sides of the lesion (Fig. 1A,B). Postoperative computed tomography–magnetic resonance imaging fusion (Elements, Brainlab SE, Munich, Germany) confirmed that the lesion was adjacent to contacts 9 and 10 of the STN‐DBS lead, predominantly in the b/c direction (Fig. 1C–E).

Local field potentials (LFPs) were recorded using the STN‐DBS lead and implantable pulse generator (Medtronic Percept PC). Because recordings were bipolar, signals reflected relative activity between contact pairs. 6 Data were analyzed offline in MATLAB according to Sanger et al, 7 including demeaning, detrending, 58–62 Hz notch filtering, multitaper power spectral density (PSD) estimation, and artifact‐window rejection.

Beta‐band PSD varied across montages, suggesting a local beta‐band LFP gradient adjacent to the chronic STN‐FUS lesion (Fig. 2). During monopolar review, stimulation at levels 8–10 improved tremor and rigidity, although the b‐direction was slightly less effective. Fractionated stimulation through contacts 10a, 10c, 9a, and 9c was selected, improving motor symptoms, including tremor. The early postoperative MDS‐UPDRS part III score improved to 15 (tremor subscore, 7), with tremor resolution and improved activities of daily living.

Postoperative LFP findings obtained using BrainSense Electrode Survey. PSD was calculated from time‐domain LFPs extracted from Percept BrainSense JSON reports and analyzed offline. (B) PSDs from directional segment‐pair montages. Segment pairs involving contact 9a showed relatively prominent beta‐band PSD. Because BrainSense Electrode Survey uses bipolar derivations, these findings reflect relative activity between contact pairs rather than absolute activity at a single contact, suggesting spatial heterogeneity or a local beta‐band LFP gradient near the STN‐FUS lesion. Discussion

This case demonstrates that MER and postoperative LFPs can be obtained along a trajectory through or near a chronic STN‐FUS lesion, revealing altered neuronal activity within a previously ablated region, and that subsequent STN‐DBS through this trajectory can be clinically effective.

A related observation was reported by De Vloo et al, who described ventral intermediate nucleus DBS after ventral intermediate nucleus MRgFUS thalamotomy for recurrent tremor. 8 Their FUS procedure involved 14 sonications (maximum energy, 14.1 kJ; maximum temperature, 60°C), compared with eight sonications (maximum energy, 10.1 kJ; maximum temperature, 59°C) in the present case. De Vloo et al reported absence of neuronal firing and markedly reduced background activity within the MRgFUS‐induced lesion, whereas the present case showed reduced, but not absent, MER activity within the chronic STN‐FUS lesion. Lower delivered energy may have contributed, although direct comparison is limited by differences in target, lesion morphology, and treatment strategy.

These findings warrant cautious interpretation. Given the bipolar recordings, the beta peak near the lesion likely reflects relative intercontact differences, consistent with local heterogeneity or a gradient of beta‐band activity near the lesion, rather than absolute beta power at the lesion site.

The selection of contacts 9a, 9c, 10a, and 10c for chronic stimulation likely reflects reduced neural density within and adjacent to the lesion, limiting the therapeutic response in the b‐direction and directing stimulation toward more intact STN tissue. Further studies are needed to confirm this interpretation.
