EdgeSSVEP: A Fully Embedded SSVEP BCI Platform for Low-Power Real-Time Applications
Manh-Dat Nguyen 1, Thomas Do 1, *, Nguyen Thanh Trung Le 1, Xuan-The Tran 1, Fred Chang 1, Chin-Teng Lin 1

Abstract
Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs) enable users to interact with machines directly via neural activity, yet their real-world deployment is often hindered by bulky and power-hungry hardware. We present EdgeSSVEP, a fully embedded microcontroller-based Steady-State Visually Evoked Potential (SSVEP) BCI platform that performs real-time EEG acquisition, zero-phase filtering, and on-device classification within a low-power 240 MHz MCU operating at only 222 mW. The system incorporates an 8-channel EEG front end, supports 5-second stimulus durations, and executes the entire SSVEP decoding pipeline locally, eliminating dependence on PC-based processing. EdgeSSVEP was evaluated using six stimulus frequencies (7, 8, 9, 11, 7.5, and 8.5 Hz) with 10 participants. The device achieved 99.17% classification accuracy and 27.33 bits/min Information Transfer Rate (ITR), while consuming substantially less power than conventional desktop-based systems. The system integrates motion sensing to support artifact detection and improve robustness and signal stability in practical environments. For development and debugging, the system also provides optional TCP data streaming to external clients. Overall, EdgeSSVEP offers a scalable, energy-efficient, and secure embedded BCI platform suitable for assistive communication and neurofeedback applications, with potential extensions to accelerometer-based artifact mitigation and broader real-world deployments.

II
Related Work

II-A

SSVEP Decoding Algorithms and Systems

SSVEP based BCIs exploit periodic neural responses elicited by visual stimuli flickering at specific frequencies. These responses, typically observed in the occipital cortex, exhibit spectral peaks at the stimulation frequency and its harmonics, enabling frequency-based identification of user intent
[
38
]
. SSVEP BCIs are popular because of their relatively high information transfer rate, minimal training requirements, and robustness to noise under controlled conditions.

A variety of signal processing algorithms have been developed to detect SSVEPs. One of the most widely used methods is CCA, introduced by Lin
et al.

[
26
]
, which measures the correlation between multichannel EEG recordings and a set of reference sine–cosine signals. CCA provides high classification accuracy without requiring any subject-specific training data, making it well suited for calibration-free SSVEP-based BCI applications. However, its reliance on multivariate matrix operations can make real-time computation challenging on low-power embedded systems.

To reduce complexity and improve performance, Power Spectral Density Analysis (PSDA) is often employed, particularly in early or resource-constrained systems
[
10
,
39
]
. PSDA detects the dominant frequency components using Fourier-based methods. While computationally lightweight, it generally suffers from lower accuracy, particularly in the presence of noise or non-stationary EEG signals.

More recent approaches include Filter Bank Canonical Correlation Analysis (FBCCA), proposed by Chen
et al.

[
8
]
, which decomposes EEG signals into multiple frequency bands using filter banks prior to applying CCA. FBCCA enhances detection performance by capturing frequency-specific information across multiple sub-bands, but further increases computational demands.

Another powerful algorithm is Task-Related Component Analysis (TRCA), introduced by Nakanishi
et al.

[
29
]
, which maximizes the reproducibility of task-related components across multiple trials. TRCA has demonstrated superior performance compared to CCA and FBCCA in multi-trial scenarios and is particularly effective when temporal correlation within EEG signals is important. However, TRCA requires supervised training and relies on multi-trial covariance estimation as well as class-specific spatio-temporal templates, which substantially increase both memory usage and computational load. During online classification, TRCA must also correlate each incoming EEG segment with these full-length templates, resulting in significantly heavier real-time processing compared to the lightweight sinusoidal reference correlations used in CCA. These factors make TRCA less suitable for systems with strict latency constraints or limited memory capacity.

In parallel, deep learning-based methods have shown promising results in recent years. For instance, EEGNet
[
22
]
and SSVEPNet
[
32
]
are compact convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures tailored for EEG classification tasks, including SSVEP decoding. These models can automatically learn spatial and spectral features from raw EEG data and outperform traditional methods in accuracy. Despite their effectiveness, deep models require substantial computational resources and memory, making them unsuitable for most microcontroller-based implementations.

In recent years, lightweight and wearable EEG devices such as Mentalab Explore, OpenBCI Cyton, and Muse S have emerged as more practical alternatives for real-world BCI applications. While these devices offer greater portability and lower power consumption, they primarily function as acquisition units rather than fully autonomous BCI systems, since signal processing and classification still rely on external computing resources. As such, there is a growing need for low power embedded BCI platforms that can maintain high decoding accuracy while operating autonomously and efficiently in mobile environments.

II-B

Gaps in Embedded SSVEP BCI Deployment and On-Device Decoding

Translating advanced SSVEP decoding methods to embedded platforms presents significant challenges due to limited computational and memory resources. However, enabling on-device processing is essential for building autonomous, real-time BCI systems suitable for mobile or wearable applications.

A related limitation in existing embedded BCIs concerns data streaming and interoperability. Many laboratory and commercial systems rely on the Lab Streaming Layer (LSL)
[
21
]
framework for synchronized multimodal acquisition, yet LSL is rarely implemented directly on microcontroller-class hardware due to its computational and memory overhead. Consequently, embedded devices often depend on simplified or proprietary communication protocols, which restrict compatibility with established research toolchains.

The proposed system addresses these issues by performing CCA-based SSVEP decoding entirely on a low-power MCU, while providing a lightweight TCP-based streaming interface that allows a host computer to forward the recorded EEG data into LSL when required. This design maintains the efficiency needed for on-device real-time classification while preserving interoperability with standard BCI software ecosystems for offline analysis and dataset generation. Together, these capabilities offer a practical foundation for real-time portable SSVEP BCI applications.

Several embedded SSVEP BCI systems have been proposed in recent years, varying in processing platforms, signal processing methods, and system capabilities. However, many of these require external processing or lack real-time performance. For example, Acampora
et al.

used a Raspberry Pi for external FFT and SVM computation, and Lin
et al.

[

III
System Design

This section provides a comprehensive description of the embedded SSVEP BCI system, covering the hardware platform, signal acquisition pipeline, noise performance, real time firmware architecture, zero phase filtering, CCA based frequency recognition, and the lightweight communication protocol used for external interfacing. The system is designed as a fully self contained microcontroller based platform capable of real time EEG acquisition, on device signal processing, and continuous data streaming under tight computational and power constraints.

III-D

Zero-Phase Bandpass Filtering for SSVEP Signals

To isolate the neural frequencies relevant for SSVEP decoding, the system applies a bandpass filter using a zero-phase forward–backward IIR method. The filter is a third-order Butterworth design with a 2–45 Hz passband, which suppresses slow drifts and high-frequency noise while preserving the harmonics that characterize SSVEP responses.

The implementation is specifically optimized for embedded deployment on the ESP32-S3. A Direct Form II transposed structure minimizes memory usage, and signal padding is used to suppress boundary transients. All filtering is performed in-place within statically allocated buffers. Although the forward–backward operation increases computational cost (346.71 ms for a 5 s window sampled at 500 Hz), it provides distortion-free temporal responses that are essential for reliable CCA-based classification.

Figure
4
compares the embedded zero-phase output with a standard causal bandpass filter. The causal filter introduces a clear phase delay relative to the underlying oscillatory features, whereas the embedded implementation preserves temporal alignment. This validation confirms that the proposed firmware achieves true zero-phase behavior comparable to offline processing methods.

III-E

Real-Time CCA-Based SSVEP Classification

CCA was implemented as a lightweight classification technique for real-time SSVEP detection on the ESP32-S3 microcontroller. To accommodate the limited computational resources of the embedded platform, a custom embedded C implementation was developed and optimized for single-precision floating-point arithmetic, fixed memory allocation, and deterministic execution time.

The core idea behind CCA is to identify linear projections of the multichannel EEG data and a set of reference signals such that the correlation between the projected components is maximized. Given an input EEG matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
and a reference signal matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times T}
, where
n
is the number of EEG channels,
m
is the number of reference signals, and
T
is the number of samples per epoch, CCA finds canonical vectors that maximize the correlation between
\mathbf{w}_{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{w}_{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}
.

To improve sensitivity to the multi-harmonic structure of SSVEP responses, the reference matrix
\mathbf{Y}_{i}
for each stimulation frequency
f_{i}
includes two harmonic components (
N_{h}=2
). Specifically, four basis functions are constructed:

\mathbf{Y}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\sin(2\pi f_{i}t)\\
\cos(2\pi f_{i}t)\\
\sin(4\pi f_{i}t)\\
\cos(4\pi f_{i}t)\end{bmatrix},

where
t
is the time vector defined by the sampling rate (500 Hz) and the duration of the analysis window.

For each candidate frequency, the canonical correlation coefficient between
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{i}
is computed, and the frequency yielding the maximum correlation is selected as the output of the classifier.

This is formulated as the following optimization problem in Equation
1
:

\max_{\mathbf{w}_{X},\mathbf{w}_{Y}}\text{corr}(\mathbf{w}_{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X},\mathbf{w}_{Y}^{\top}\mathbf{Y})

(1)

Solving this leads to a generalized eigenvalue problem for
\mathbf{w}_{X}
as Equation
2
:

\mathbf{C}_{XY}\mathbf{C}_{YY}^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{YX}\mathbf{w}_{X}=\lambda\mathbf{C}_{XX}\mathbf{w}_{X}

(2)

where:

•

\mathbf{C}_{XX}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the covariance matrix of EEG signals,

•

\mathbf{C}_{YY}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is the covariance matrix of the reference signals,

•

\mathbf{C}_{XY}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is the cross-covariance matrix between
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
,

•

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is the eigenvalue corresponding to the canonical correlation.

To simplify computation for embedded implementation, we convert this generalized eigenvalue problem into a standard form by multiplying both sides of Equation
2
from the left with
\mathbf{C}_{XX}^{-1}

[
17
]
:

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{C}_{XX}^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{XY}\mathbf{C}_{YY}^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{YX}

(3)

Here,
\rho
denotes the canonical correlation coefficient, representing the maximum correlation achievable between the projected EEG signals and reference signals. It is computed as the square root of the largest eigenvalue
\lambda
of
\mathbf{M}
as Equation
4
:

\rho^{2}=\lambda\quad\Rightarrow\quad\rho=\sqrt{\lambda}

(4)

In our implementation, the input EEG data
\mathbf{X}
consists of epochs, with each epoch’s duration configurable (defaulting to 5 seconds). The raw epoch is first processed using a band-pass filter to isolate the relevant frequency components. From the filtered signal, the last 4 seconds are extracted to ensure signal stability and minimize transient artifacts. This segment is then zero-meaned across all channels to remove DC offsets and normalize the input before applying CCA.

•

Covariance matrices
\mathbf{C}_{XX}
,
\mathbf{C}_{YY}
, and
\mathbf{C}_{XY}
are computed using efficient custom matrix multiplication routines.

•

Whitening of the EEG and reference signals is performed by computing the inverse square roots of
\mathbf{C}_{XX}
and
\mathbf{C}_{YY}
.

•

These matrix inverses are calculated using a tailored Gauss-Jordan elimination algorithm implemented in C, which avoids recursion and dynamic memory usage to ensure deterministic execution on the ESP32-S3.

•

The whitened cross-covariance matrix is then formed and subjected to eigenvalue decomposition.

Eigenvalue decomposition is performed using the power iteration
[
3
]
method to extract the largest eigenvalue, which corresponds to the maximum canonical correlation coefficient. The final classification decision is made by selecting the frequency whose corresponding reference signal yields the highest canonical correlation with the EEG input.

All linear algebra operations, including matrix transpose, multiplication, and inversion, were developed from scratch for embedded use with statically allocated memory buffers and loop-level optimizations. This efficient implementation supports real-time classification of 8-channel EEG signals sampled at 500 Hz, relying solely on the onboard computing resources of the ESP32-S3.

III-F

Communication and Streaming Stack

A lightweight TCP communication layer is used to support continuous real time streaming of EEG and auxiliary data. Each acquired sample is encoded into a compact frame that contains the EEG channels, three axis accelerometer readings, battery status, and an event marker. Packing all modalities within the same frame ensures that the signals share a unified device clock domain and remain temporally aligned.

Frames are accumulated in a shared streaming buffer and forwarded in fixed size packets by a dedicated TCP task running on Core 1. This asynchronous design decouples network transmission from the acquisition loop on Core 0, allowing sampling to remain stable even under variable network conditions. On the receiver side, the client parses the incoming packets and forwards the data to LSL in real time, enabling synchronized multimodal analysis for downstream BCI applications.

IV-C

Embedded device configuration

The embedded system was configured to acquire EEG signals from 8 channels at a sampling rate of 500. A programmable gain amplifier (PGA) with a gain of 12 was selected to prevent signal saturation, considering the use of low-impedance electrodes. These electrodes, with impedance typically ranging from 0.2 to 0.5
\Omega
, provide relatively large signal amplitudes compared to traditional high-impedance electrodes, requiring a lower gain setting for optimal dynamic range utilization.

Spring-loaded, low-impedance electrodes were mounted onto a flexible sensor pad designed to ensure stable mechanical contact and consistent electrode positioning throughout the recording sessions. This configuration allowed reliable and high-fidelity EEG acquisition under naturalistic conditions. The sensor pad included eight electrodes positioned according to the international 10–20 system at the following locations: O2 (Ch1), PO4 (Ch2), P2 (Ch3), Oz (Ch4), POz (Ch5), P1 (Ch6), PO3 (Ch7), and O1 (Ch8). V
Results

Ten healthy participants (aged 28–35 years) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision participated in online experiments. All recordings were conducted at the Computational Intelligence and Brain-Computer Interfaces Lab, University of Technology Sydney. Participants had no known history of neurological or psychiatric conditions and gave informed consent prior to participation. V-B

Offline decoding performance across window lengths

In addition to real-time operation, the proposed system supports a
high-rate streaming mode that records continuous multi-channel EEG while
the online classifier runs on the microcontroller. This feature enables
offline evaluation using data captured under the same conditions as the
online experiments, providing a consistent basis for algorithm
comparison.

Using the streamed recordings, three widely adopted calibration free SSVEP decoding algorithms were benchmarked: CCA, FBCCA and PSDA. These methods were chosen because they do not require subject specific training and can be applied directly to the recorded epochs, which enables a fair and reproducible comparison of decoding performance under conditions suitable for embedded deployment. EEG segments with window lengths ranging from 0.5 s to 5 s were extracted and processed using the same zero-phase bandpass filter employed in the online system. FBCCA was implemented with a standard ten band filter bank
[
8
]
, whereas CCA and PSDA operated on the same bandpass-filtered signal used in the online system.

Figure
9
summarizes classification accuracy and
ITR across all window lengths. Accuracy
improves monotonically with longer windows for all three methods. CCA
and FBCCA exhibit similar performance for windows of 3 s and above,
reaching accuracy above 85% and approaching their peak values at 4–5 s.
For short windows (1–2 s), FBCCA provides only a modest improvement of
approximately 2–3% over CCA, while PSDA remains substantially lower
across all window lengths. ITR peaks at approximately 2.5–3.5 s for both
CCA and FBCCA (around 30–35 bits/min), reflecting the expected trade-off
between speed and accuracy.

These results indicate that CCA offers the most practical balance between decoding performance and computational efficiency for the proposed ESP32-S3–based system. FBCCA offers slightly higher performance at
very short windows but incurs substantially higher computational cost
due to multi-band filtering and repeated CCA computations, which
introduce additional latency on MCU-class hardware. PSDA offers low
complexity but significantly lower accuracy. Overall,
the analysis demonstrates that CCA is the most effective method for the
proposed system, delivering high accuracy with deterministic and
consistently low processing latency on MCU-class hardware.

V-C

Power consumption

To assess the energy efficiency of the proposed embedded SSVEP BCI system, we measured the total power consumption of the device at different CPU clock frequencies (80, 160, and 240 MHz) under two distinct operational modes: Normal Mode and Streaming Mode.

In Normal Mode, no LSL client is connected. The embedded system operates autonomously, performing real-time EEG acquisition, bandpass filtering, and classification entirely on-device. Classification results are sent to the connected mobile application over a TCP socket. This mode represents the standard use case for self-contained, low-power BCI operation without external data consumers.

In contrast, Streaming Mode includes an active connection from an external LSL client. While the device continues to execute its full on-device processing pipeline, including signal filtering and classification, it simultaneously streams raw EEG data over Wi-Fi to the connected LSL client. This dual operation leads to increased power consumption, primarily due to the continuous wireless transmission. Streaming Mode is typically used for validation, debugging, or scenarios requiring real-time monitoring on external systems.

VI
Conclusion

This work presents a fully embedded SSVEP brain computer interface that performs EEG acquisition, signal conditioning, and classification entirely on a low power controller. A central contribution is the development of a deterministic signal processing pipeline for zero phase bandpass filtering and CCA that operates fully within the controller. This design preserves the temporal structure of the SSVEP waveform, ensures stable decoding across analysis windows, and provides predictable timing for real time operation. By removing the need for external computers or auxiliary processors, the approach supports practical deployment of SSVEP based brain computer interfaces in mobile and energy constrained settings.

The proposed EdgeSSVEP system achieves an average accuracy of 99.17% across six targets using eight EEG channels, with a decision latency of 0.48 seconds and a total power consumption of 222 mW. These results place it among the most capable controller based SSVEP systems reported in recent literature.

When viewed alongside prior embedded and portable SSVEP systems, the proposed device presents a distinct balance of accuracy, decision time, and energy usage. Embedded designs such as the RX210 together with an FPGA
[
23
]
, the Olimex EEG board together with a Raspberry Pi
[
2
]
, and active electrode controllers such as the Mr Wolf processor
[
19
]
achieve strong performance but require higher power budgets or additional processors to complete the signal processing pipeline. Several high performing systems still rely entirely on PC based computation, including works that use FBCCA, CNN based decoding, or multistage classifier structures
[
34
,
46
,
43
,
6
,
5
]
. These designs limit mobility and increase system complexity because they cannot operate independently. In contrast, the EdgeSSVEP system processes all filtering and classification locally on a single controller while supporting eight EEG channels, and it maintains accuracy and responsiveness that are comparable with PC assisted designs. This positions the system as one of the few fully self contained SSVEP platforms capable of real time operation without external hardware.

The controller architecture provides predictable execution for signal processing tasks and careful management of available memory resources. Tests across several CPU frequency settings confirm that the design can sustain short decision times while satisfying the energy requirements of portable and wearable scenarios.

Although the results are promising, several challenges remain for real world conditions. Individual variability in SSVEP responses and sensitivity to motion related artifacts, including disturbances caused by head movement or changes in viewing angle, may reduce robustness. These issues motivate further research into adaptive filtering and artifact suppression strategies. Future development will explore lightweight learning models to improve tolerance to noise, more refined scheduling to reduce energy usage, and the use of Bluetooth as an alternative communication mode. Additional enhancements such as SD card data logging and improved software level noise handling will further strengthen system reliability and versatility.

In summary, the EdgeSSVEP system demonstrates that high accuracy, short decision time, and low energy usage can be achieved within a controller only design. This provides a practical foundation for the development of portable and wearable brain computer interfaces suitable for research, assistive technology, and daily applications.
