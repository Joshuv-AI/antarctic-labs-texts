Exploring the mitochondrial genomes of the Indian dhole and wolf: a phylogenetic and conservation perspective
Authors: Aparna Suresh, Karippadakam Bhavana, Muniyandi Nagarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
India harbours two ecologically and evolutionarily significant canids: the dhole and wolf. Both species are increasingly threatened by habitat fragmentation, declining prey availability, and anthropogenic pressures. Despite their conservation importance, the complete mitochondrial genome structure and organization of the Indian dhole and wolf have remained unreported. In this study, we characterized the structure and organization of the complete mitochondrial genomes of the Indian dhole and wolf for the first time in order to examine their genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships. The mitochondrial genome measured 16,754 bp in the Indian dhole and 16,727 bp in the Indian wolf. Both species showed conserved gene content and organization, including 22 tRNA genes, 13 protein-coding genes, two rRNA genes, and a control region. Maximum parsimony and Bayesian phylogenetic analyses consistently recovered the dhole as a well-supported monophyletic clade in which the two Indian dhole samples clustered together and were clearly separated from the Chinese dhole. The Indian wolf formed a well-supported distinct clade within the Canis lupus complex. The distinct phylogenetic placement of the Indian dhole and wolf emphasizes their evolutionary significance and conservation relevance. The mitochondrial genome resources generated in this study provide a foundation for future population and comparative genomic analyses of these threatened canids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47957-x.

Introduction

The dhole (Cuon alpinus), is a highly social canid inhabiting fragmented forest landscapes across South, East, and Southeast Asia1,2. The Indian wolf (Canis lupus pallipes) is a subspecies of C. lupus, commonly known as the Indian grey wolf. It inhabits dry deciduous forests, semi-arid grasslands, scrublands, and agro-pastoral landscapes of peninsular and central India3. The global population of the dhole is estimated at 4,500 − 10,500 individuals4,5, while the Indian wolf population is estimated at approximately 3,000 adult individuals6. Although both species are ecologically important, they have experienced significant population declines and marked contractions in their geographic ranges. Major threats to both species include habitat loss and fragmentation, prey depletion, diseases, human-wildlife conflict, and persecution5,6. Consequently, the dhole is listed as endangered by the IUCN and protected under India’s Wildlife Protection Act (Schedule I) and CITES Appendix II, whereas the Indian wolf is categorised as vulnerable by the IUCN and receives protection under India’s Wildlife Protection Act (Schedule I) and CITES Appendix I5,7–9. Despite their conservation importance, insights into the genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships of these species remain limited. Earlier studies have largely relied on short mitochondrial regions such as the control region or cytochrome b, or on microsatellite markers, which often provide insufficient resolution to accurately infer phylogenetic relationships and genetic diversity4,10–15.

Complete mitochondrial genomes offer enhanced resolution for species identification, phylogenetic relationships, evolutionary history, and wildlife trade monitoring16–20. In species with wide geographic distributions and fragmented habitats, such as the dhole and wolf, complete mitogenomes are particularly valuable for assessing region-specific haplotypes, intraspecific genetic variation, population structure, and patterns shaped by geographic isolation and historical demographic processes21–25. Available mitogenomic information on the dhole is primarily based on Southeast Asian populations, while mitochondrial genome sequences of the Indian wolf remain partial or partially annotated14,23,25–28. Thus, sequencing the complete mitochondrial genome of the Indian dhole and Indian wolf is essential to address existing knowledge gaps in phylogenetic inference and genetic diversity. Given the dhole’s endangered status and the Indian wolf’s vulnerable status, such genomic resources are critical for evidence-based conservation planning. In this study, we present the complete mitochondrial genome structure of Indian dhole and wolf for the first time and examine their phylogenetic relationship.

Results

Mitochondrial genome organization

Complete mitochondrial genomes of the dhole and wolf sampled from India were sequenced in this study. A total of 4,235,938 and 15,015,022 raw sequencing reads were generated for the dhole and wolf respectively (Table 1). The mitochondrial genome length was 16,754 bp in the dhole and 16,727 bp in the wolf. The two dhole mitochondrial genome sequences were identical. Both species possessed a circular, double-stranded mitochondrial genome comprising 22 tRNA genes, 13 protein-coding genes, two rRNA genes, and a noncoding control region (Table 2). The gene order and arrangement were identical in both species (Fig. 1). The average nucleotide composition of the dhole mitochondrial genome was 31.6% A, 24.7% C, 14.3% G, and 29.4% T while wolf exhibited 31.5% A, 25.4% C, 14.3% G, and 28.8% T (Fig. 2).

Protein coding genes (PCGs)

The mitochondrial genomes of both the dhole and wolf contained 13 PCGs accounting for 68.08% and 68.19% of the total mitochondrial genome length respectively. The number, and arrangement of PCGs were identical between the two species. The AT content of the PCGs was 61% in the dhole and 60.22% in the wolf (Fig. 2). In both species, the heavy strand encoded six NADH genes (nad1-5 and nad4L), three cytochrome c oxidase genes (cox1-3), two ATPase genes (atp6 and atp8), and the cob gene, while nad6 was encoded on the light strand. Among the PCGs nad5 was the longest gene (1,821 bp) and atp8 was the shortest (204 bp) in both species. Negative AT and GC skews were observed across the PCG regions in both species (Fig. 2). A total of 3,802 codons were identified in the PCGs of both species (Fig. 3). Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) analysis showed differences in codon usage patterns between the dhole and wolf (Fig. 4). In both species, CUA (L), CGA (R), GUA (V), and CCU (P), exhibited high RSCU values indicating preferential usage.

Ribosomal RNA and transfer RNA

The mitochondrial genomes of both species contained 12 S and 16 S rRNA genes between trnF and trnL2, with trnV situated between them (Fig. 1). In the dhole, the 16 S rRNA was 1,582 bp and the 12 S rRNA was 955 bp in length. In the wolf, the 16 S rRNA measured 1,580 bp and 12 S rRNA measured 954 bp. The 12 S and 16 S rRNA genes exhibited a positive AT skew and negative GC skew in both species (Fig. 2). Both mitochondrial genomes encoded 22 tRNA genes ranging in length from 60 to 75 bp. Of the 22 tRNA genes, 14 namely trnF, trnV, trnL2, trnI, trnM, trnW, trnD, trnK, trnG, trnR, trnH, trnS1, trnL1, and trnT were found on the heavy strand. The predicted secondary structures of all tRNAs exhibited the typical cloverleaf structure, except for trnS1 which lacked the dihydrouridine stem and loop (Supplementary Figure S1 & S2).

Control region

The control region was located between trnF and trnP in both species. Its length was 1,292 bp in the dhole and 1,268 bp in the wolf. A total of 138 variable sites were identified between the two species within the control region. The AT content was 57.9% in the dhole and 56.5% in the wolf. The AT skew was positive in the dhole and negative in the wolf, whereas the GC skew was negative in both species (Fig. 2). Detailed sequence analysis revealed the presence of multiple repetitive motifs within the control regions of both species. The motif 5′-ACACGTACGT-3′ was repeatedly observed in both species, whereas its variant, 5′-ACACGTGCGT-3′, was exclusive to the wolf. Likewise, the repetitive motif 5′-GTACACGTAC-3′ occurred at a higher frequency in the dhole, while its variant, 5′-GTACACGTGC-3′, was observed at a higher frequency but only in the wolf. Further, the motif 5′-CACGTACACG-3′ was identified exclusively in the dhole. Furthermore, palindromic sequences, including 5′-TACAT-3′ and its reverse complement 5′-ATGTA-3′, were identified at multiple positions in both species.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic relationships among the Indian dhole and wolf were inferred alongside other canid species using maximum parsimony and Bayesian methods. The African wild dog (Lycaon pictus) was used as the outgroup to root the phylogenetic tree. Both methods yielded nearly identical tree topologies, showing consistent branching patterns and concordant relationships among major clades, thereby supporting the reliability of the phylogenetic inferences. The phylogenetic trees clearly resolved the separation between the genera Cuon and Canis (Fig. 5). A well-supported distinct clade was observed for the dhole, in which the two Indian dholes grouped together and were clearly distinct from the Chinese dhole. The Indian wolf formed a well-supported clade within the C. lupus complex, while the Himalayan wolf was clearly distinct, forming a separate and divergent clade.

Discussion

The present study analysed complete mitochondrial genomes of the Indian dhole and Indian wolf, contributing valuable region-specific genomic data for two ecologically and evolutionarily important but understudied canids. The overall mitochondrial genome organization observed in both species was highly conserved, comprising 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 22 tRNAs, two rRNAs, and a control region, consistent with the canonical vertebrate mitochondrial genome structure reported across canids22,23,26,29. The mitochondrial genome sizes of the dhole and wolf fall within the range reported for other canids, with minor length variation largely attributable to differences in the control region. Both species exhibited similar nucleotide composition and AT bias, consistent with patterns reported for mammalian mitochondrial genomes16,17. The PCGs constituted approximately 68% of the mitochondrial genome in both species, consistent with other members of Canidae21,22,29,30. RSCU analysis revealed distinct codon usage patterns in both species, with certain codons being preferentially used. Similar codon usage biases have been reported in other vertebrate mitochondrial genomes and are thought to result from nucleotide composition bias18,20.

The mitochondrial control region is the most variable segment of the mitochondrial genome and plays a central role in the initiation of replication and transcription31. In both the dhole and the wolf, the control region was located between trnF and trnP in agreement with the conserved mitochondrial genome organization observed across canids22,23,26. Although the location was conserved, the control region was slightly longer in the dhole than in the wolf. Length variation in the control region is commonly attributed to insertions, deletions, and expansions of repetitive elements21,31. The presence of 138 variable sites between the dhole and the wolf further supports the rapid evolutionary rate of this region and emphasizes its usefulness in differentiating closely related taxa and assessing interspecific divergence10–13,15. Another notable feature of the control region was the abundance of repetitive motifs, which are known to contribute to high levels of length and sequence variability21,24,26,31. The repeated occurrence of the motif 5′-ACACGTACGT-3′ in both species suggests its conserved nature. In contrast, the presence of species-specific variants and differences in motif frequency, such as the high copy number of 5′-ACACGTGCGT-3′ uniquely observed in the wolf, indicates species-specific evolution of repetitive elements following divergence. Further, the exceptionally high copy number of the motif 5′-CACGTACACG-3′ in the dhole, coupled with its complete absence in the wolf, represents a striking molecular signature that may serve as a useful marker for species identification and population genetic studies, particularly in closely related or morphologically similar taxa.

Phylogenetic analysis based on complete mitochondrial genome sequences improved our understanding of the evolutionary relationships of both species within the family Canidae. The Indian dhole formed a well-supported, monophyletic clade within the genus Cuon, clearly separated from members of the genus Canis. This pattern is consistent with earlier molecular and fossil-based studies that recognize the dhole as an evolutionarily ancient canid lineage32–34, thereby reinforcing its long-established taxonomic status and evolutionary distinctiveness. Similarly, the Indian wolf formed a distinct clade within the C. lupus complex in agreement with previous studies that identify the Indian wolf as one of the early-diverging lineages of C. lupus11,12,15. This phylogenetic placement further supports its recognition at the subspecies level within the wolf lineage9,14,25. The two dhole mitogenomes generated in the present study clustered together in the phylogenetic tree. No differences were identified upon sequence comparison. As the samples were obtained from a zoo population, it is likely that they originated from the same genetic lineage, which could account for the lack of mitochondrial diversity. Similar patterns of low mitochondrial diversity have been reported in other mammalian studies involving limited or captive sampling35,36. Therefore, broader sampling across multiple geographic regions would be required to draw robust population-level inferences. In contrast, comparative sequence analyses revealed significant genetic variation between the Indian dhole mitochondrial genomes and those reported from other geographic regions. Such variation likely reflects intraspecies diversity shaped by factors such as geographic isolation, and historical demographic processes as reported in earlier studies10,11,13,15. Our findings indicate that the Indian dhole represents a genetically distinct population compared to conspecific populations from other regions.

Conclusion

This study reports, the complete mitochondrial genome structure and organization of the Indian dhole and the Indian wolf for the first time. Phylogenetic analyses clearly distinguished the dhole from Canis species and the Indian wolf . from the Himalayan wolf. The mitochondrial control region of both species offers important information on their evolutionary history and serves as a useful genetic marker for population genetic and phylogeographic studies. The study enhances our understanding of their genetic diversity and evolutionary relationships and provides a foundation for future conservation and genomic research.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Blood samples were collected from two dholes and one wolf at Sri Chamarajendra Zoological Gardens, Mysore, Karnataka, India. Approximately 0.5 ml of blood was drawn from the saphenous vein (leg) of each animal and preserved in vials with 5 ml of ethanol. Sampling was conducted with the assistance of zoo veterinarians and caretakers, following all necessary approval from the respective state forest department. Collected samples were stored at − 40 °C until further processing.

Mitochondrial genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from blood samples using phenol-chloroform method following the protocol described by Sambrook & Russell37. Two overlapping primer sets were designed using Primer3web version 4.1.038 based on reference mitochondrial genomes of the dhole (GenBank Accession No. GU063864) and the wolf (GenBank Accession No. KF661043) to amplify their complete mitochondrial genomes. The dhole was amplified using primer pairs P291 (5′-GGTTTGGTCCTAGCCTTCCT-3′) and P292 (5′-AAGGGCTACTAGGAGTGGGA-3′), as well as P293 (5′-ACTCGGACTACTACCCCACT-3′) and P294 (5′-ACCTTTGCACGGTCAGGATA-3′). For the wolf, primer pairs P291 (5′-GGTTTGGTCCTAGCCTTCCT-3′) and P292 (5′-AAGGGCTACTAGGAGTGGGA-3′), along with P297 (5’-TTCCTCATCGTGTGCTTCCT-3’) and P298 (5’-ATTGGTTGAAGGAGGCCGTA-3’) were employed. Long-range PCR was carried out in a final volume of 50 µl, comprising 10 µl of 5X PrimeSTAR GXL Buffer (Takara, Japan), 4 µl of dNTPs (2.5 mM each), 4 µl of each primer (15 pmol), 1 µl of PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase (Takara, Japan), 4 µl of genomic DNA (300 ng), and 23 µl of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min followed by 28 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 68 °C for 1 min, and 68 °C for 10 min with a final extension at 68 °C for 10 min. The amplified PCR products were then sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina, USA) as described previously by Kamalakannan et al.17.

Data analysis

Raw sequencing reads were first assessed for quality using FastQC v0.12.139. Illumina adapter sequences and low-quality reads were removed using FastP v1.23.240. High-quality filtered reads were then assembled de novo using GetOrganelle v1.7.7.141 to reconstruct the complete mitochondrial genomes. The assembled sequences were manually curated and aligned using MEGAv7.042. Gene annotation was performed using the MitoFish web server43, and the circular mitochondrial genome map was generated using OGDRAW v1.3.144 with default settings. The secondary structure of tRNAs was predicted using MITOS2 web server45. Further analyses, nucleotide composition and Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) values of each codon, were estimated using MEGA v7.042. Phylogenetic tree construction was carried out in MEGAv7.042 using the maximum parsimony method. The close-neighbor interchange algorithm was applied for heuristic tree searching, and node support was evaluated using 1,000 bootstrap replicates. A Bayesian phylogenetic tree was inferred using MrBayes v3.246 applying the general time-reversible model with invariant sites and gamma-distributed rate variation across eight categories. The analysis employed a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approach run for 10 million generations, with trees sampled at intervals of 500 generations. After discarding the first 5,000 trees as burn-in, the remaining trees were used to construct a 50% majority-rule consensus tree. The analysis was conducted three times independently to confirm convergence.
